Pulmonary botryomycosis in a cat
Authors: Takayuki MINESHIGE, Naoyuki AIHARA, Takuma AOKI, Eijiro HANAOKA, Natsuno TAKASE, Maoko YAMAGUCHI, Takanori SHIGA, Junichi KAMIIE
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pulmonary botryomycosis is an extremely rare bacterial infection that has not been previously documented in cats. This report describes a case of pulmonary botryomycosis in a 10-year-old spayed female domestic shorthair cat with chronic pulmonary lesions refractory to long-term corticosteroid therapy. Surgical lobectomy of the right cranial and middle lung lobes was performed. Histopathological examination revealed extensive necrotizing and fibrosing pyogranulomatous inflammation with characteristic club colonies composed of Gram-positive cocci surrounded by eosinophilic Splendore–Hoeppli material. On molecular analysis of formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue, the highest 16S rDNA sequence homology was Staphylococcus spp. This case represents the first documented instance of pulmonary botryomycosis in a cat and highlights the diagnostic importance of combining histopathological and molecular approaches.

Botryomycosis is a rare chronic bacterial infection characterized in humans [1, 5] and animals [3, 6, 8] by pyogranulomatous inflammation and the formation of sulfur granules, most commonly caused by Staphylococcus spp. In the veterinary field, botryomycosis has been sporadically reported, primarily involving the skin or soft tissues, mainly in horses and pigs [6]. Botryomycosis is exceedingly rare in cats [7, 9, 10], and pulmonary involvement has not been documented. This report describes the histological findings of pulmonary botryomycosis in a cat.

A 10-year-old spayed female domestic shorthair cat was referred to the Azabu University Veterinary Teaching Hospital for the evaluation of a persistent pulmonary lesion. The cat was kept strictly indoors in a three-cat household and had no known history of trauma. The cat tested negative for feline immunodeficiency virus and feline leukemia virus. Thoracic radiographs obtained 14 months before referral revealed increased opacity in the right cranial lung lobe. Eight months before referral, the cat developed coughing. Radiographs revealed increased opacity in the right cranial lung lobe. The lesion in the right cranial lung lobe was initially interpreted as an airway disease-associated inflammatory change. Based on a presumptive diagnosis of feline asthma, the cat had received long-term prednisolone therapy (initially 0.10 mg/kg, gradually increased to 0.21 mg/kg) for 13 months. Although the clinical signs transiently improved, coughing recurred and further diagnostic investigations were performed.

The excised lung tissue was fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE), Gram, periodic acid–Schiff (PAS), Grocott methenamine silver, and Ziehl–Neelsen stains. Immunohistochemical analysis was performed using the immunoenzyme polymer method with an anti-feline coronavirus antibody (Clone FIPV3-70; Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Characteristic club colonies were identified within the necrotic foci, consisting of centrally located bacterial colonies surrounded by radiating eosinophilic club-like material (Fig. 3c). The bacteria were morphologically consistent with cocci. Additional findings included hemorrhage, congestion, pulmonary edema, and thickening of the walls of medium-sized arteries. In some areas, inflammation was centered on the bronchi and bronchioles, with the accumulation of necrotic debris and inflammatory cells within the airway lumina (Fig. 3d). Hyperplasia of the bronchial glands and type II pneumocytes were observed. However, no cytological atypia was identified.

Gram staining demonstrated that the bacteria within the club colonies were Gram-positive cocci, which were also scattered throughout the lesion (Fig. 3c inset). PAS, Grocott methenamine silver, and Ziehl–Neelsen staining did not reveal any fungal organisms, mycobacteria, or other significant pathogens (data not shown). Immunohistochemistry with anti-feline coronavirus antibody revealed no positive staining, excluding feline infectious peritonitis (data not shown).

DNA was extracted from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections using the ReliaPrep FFPE gDNA Miniprep System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted DNA was used as a template for polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of the bacterial 16S rDNA region using a commercial bacterial 16S rDNA PCR kit (TaKaRa Bio, Kusatsu, Japan). As no amplification product was obtained with the primer mix provided in the kit, PCR was subsequently performed using the sequencing primers, F2 and R2, included in the same kit, yielding an approximately 550-bp amplicon (Supplementary Fig. 1). PCR products were purified using the NucleoSpin® Gel and PCR Clean-up kit (MACHEREY-NAGEL, Düren, Germany) and subjected to direct sequencing in both forward and reverse directions. The obtained sequences were submitted to BLASTn searches in the National Center for Biotechnology Information nucleotide database, which revealed Staphylococcus spp. as having the highest homology (approximately 95%), suggesting that this genus was the most likely causative agent.

Histopathologically, the lesion was characterized as severe necrotizing pyogranulomatous pneumonia with intralesional Gram-positive cocci. Based on these findings, the lesion was diagnosed as pulmonary botryomycosis. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of pulmonary botryomycosis in a cat. The histopathological presence of club colonies composed of Gram-positive cocci embedded within an eosinophilic club-like material is a hallmark of botryomycosis [6]. Botryomycosis is most commonly associated with Staphylococcus spp.; however, other bacteria have also been implicated [6]. Infections caused by Staphylococcus spp. and Streptococcus spp. typically involve Gram-positive cocci, whereas Pseudomonas spp. and Proteus spp. are Gram-negative organisms [2, 6]. In the present case, fungal and mycobacterial infections were excluded by special stains, and feline infectious peritonitis was ruled out by negative immunohistochemistry for feline coronavirus. Along with the molecular confirmation of Staphylococcus spp. from FFPE tissue, these results supported a diagnosis of pulmonary botryomycosis. Although extensive differential diagnoses were not required in this case, the clear presence of the Splendore–Hoeppli phenomenon and the identification of Staphylococcus spp. effectively excluded other infectious diseases, including actinomycosis [6].

Botryomycosis arises from a dysregulated host response to low-virulence bacteria, often in the context of chronic inflammation or immunomodulation [6]. The disease typically affects subcutaneous tissues; however, visceral forms have also been reported, particularly in humans, in whom pulmonary involvement is the most common manifestation, followed by lesions in the liver, spleen, kidneys, and brain [1]. Pulmonary botryomycosis has been reported in several species, including dogs [3], cattle [8], guinea pigs [2], and humans [1]. To the best of our knowledge, pulmonary lesions have not been described in cats.

In cats, botryomycosis has been described primarily in the skin and subcutaneous tissues [6, 7, 9, 10]. Several reported cases have been associated with immunosuppressive conditions, such as FIV infection [9]. However, botryomycosis has also been indicated in cats without an apparent underlying disease, in which the cause of onset remained unclear [7, 10]. Histologically, the lesions are characterized by pyogranulomatous inflammation with bacterial colonies surrounded by Splendore–Hoeppli material [6, 7, 9, 10]. Regardless of the affected organ, botryomycosis is frequently associated with immunosuppressive conditions or impaired host defense mechanisms [1, 6, 9].

In the present case, feline asthma was suspected as the underlying disease, and the cat had received long-term corticosteroid therapy for approximately 13 months. Prolonged immunomodulatory treatment may have contributed to the development, persistence, and progression of pulmonary botryomycosis in this cat, even in the absence of overt systemic immunodeficiency. The association between corticosteroid administration and the occurrence of botryomycosis has been documented previously [5], supporting the hypothesis that local or systemic immunosuppression plays a critical role in the disease pathogenesis. Staphylococcus spp., potentially part of the normal flora, has frequently been described as a causative agent. In cats, Staphylococcus spp. is recognized as a lower respiratory tract infection-associated bacterium. Small numbers of aerobic bacteria, including Staphylococcus spp., have been cultured from the lower airways of healthy cats [4]. These results suggest that in the present case, resident airway bacteria might have induced pulmonary botryomycosis via localized colonization or opportunistic infection. The lesion appeared to be largely confined to the right cranial and middle lung lobes instead of a multifocal systemic involvement. This distribution might reflect localized infection upon airway colonization or aspiration rather than hematogenous dissemination. A similar mechanism has been suggested in pulmonary botryomycosis in cattle [8], with the aspiration of oropharyngeal bacteria being considered a possible route of infection.

Botryomycosis is characterized by bacterial colonies embedded within granules surrounded by host-derived materials, a feature that often limits the penetration and efficacy of antimicrobial therapy [3]. Although antimicrobial therapy alone is often insufficient for the complete resolution of botryomycosis, the present case suggests that surgical resection combined with antimicrobial treatment may result in a favorable outcome.

To our knowledge, this is the first documented case of pulmonary botryomycosis in cats. These findings emphasize the importance of recognizing botryomycosis as a rare but significant differential diagnosis in feline chronic pulmonary disease, and demonstrate the diagnostic value of integrating histopathology with molecular techniques, even when limited to FFPE-derived DNA. Pulmonary botryomycosis should be considered in cats with chronic inflammatory pulmonary lesions, particularly when club colonies are histologically identified. Combined histopathological and molecular analyses can facilitate accurate diagnoses and expand our understanding of rare infectious diseases in veterinary pathology.
