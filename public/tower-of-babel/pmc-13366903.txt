Astrocyte-microglia crosstalk via CSF1 and IFN-β promotes central nervous system repair
Authors: Tuoxin Cao, Matin Hemati-Gourabi, Yongliang Liu, Anna E Mills, Lauren Baur, Ellie P Rice, Theo Klippel, Yiwen Yan, Xiu Xu, William K Fenske, Jean X Jiang, Meifan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYDespite accumulating evidence of functional interactions between astrocytes and microglia in central nervous system (CNS) injury and disease, mechanisms coordinating their response to CNS insults remain incompletely understood. We report that injury-reactive astrocytes at the lesion border upregulate colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF1) required for microglial proliferation, wound closure, and motor recovery after focal spinal cord injury (SCI). Intriguingly, astrocyte-targeted deletion of CSF1 also reduces the cell number of border-forming astrocytes, revealing positive feedback regulation between astrocytes and microglia. We further show that microglia produce interferon β (IFN-β), which reciprocally supports astrocyte survival. Genetic disruption of interferon signaling in astrocytes in turn impairs astrocytic border formation, coordination with microglia in wound healing, and motor recovery. This work uncovers astrocyte-microglia crosstalk via CSF1 and IFN-β that synergizes the acute injury response of these cells for neural repair, providing insights into fundamental biology of astrocyte-microglia communication and its therapeutic potential.

INTRODUCTION

Nearly all forms of central nervous system (CNS) pathology are accompanied by glial responses that critically modify histological and neurological outcomes.1-8 Astrocytes and microglia have been independently demonstrated to react rapidly to focal CNS injury to contain tissue damage.9-16 While there is some evidence, primarily from microglia depletion studies that implicate functional interaction between these cell types,10-12,17-19 the knowledge of mechanisms that coordinate their actions in shaping the injury microenvironment for wound repair remain incomplete. Defining molecular signals of astrocyte-microglia crosstalk in this context will provide insights into developing strategies that enhance neural repair following CNS insults.

In this study, we address this important question in a focal spinal cord injury (SCI) model. Evidence from neonatal to adult mammals shows that microglia drive spinal cord wound repair by remodeling the extracellular matrix, compartmentalizing infiltrating immune cells, and resolving inflammation.9-12 Notably, microglia-mediated wound closure critically requires cell proliferation of perilesional microglia that occurs within the first week of injury.10 The cellular sources of mitogenic signals and their respective contributions to microglial proliferation acutely after injury, however, remain to be empirically defined. Here, we demonstrate that lesion border astrocytes rapidly upregulate colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF1) to stimulate cell division of perilesional microglia. Striking reduction of not only perilesional microglia but also astrocytes in astrocyte-targeted Csf1 knockout (KO) mice further unveiled positive astrocyte-microglia feedback regulation via CSF1 and interferon β (IFN-β) that synergize the injury response of these glial cells in spinal cord wound healing, with broad translational implications for neural repair.

RESULTS

Lesion border astrocytes upregulate CSF1 early after SCI

Microglia depletion by pharmacological inhibition of CSF1 receptor (CSF1R) has been widely used to characterize the role of microglia in CNS injury.10,11,17-19 While this approach has demonstrated that CSF1R-dependent microglia proliferation in the acute phase of injury is critical to their tissue protective function,10 the endogenous cellular source(s) of the CSF1 ligand and associated functional consequences are unexplored. Based on spatial co-segregation of microglia and astrocytes into adjoining border structures around the primary lesion following SCI,10,12,20 we hypothesized that astrocytes produce CSF1 to support microglia survival, proliferation, and function at the lesion. To test this, we first examined injury-dependent effects on CSF1 expression at the primary lesion following complete crush of the spinal cord at thoracic level T8 of adult wild-type mice. Compared to the uninjured (naive) spinal cord, injury increased the gene transcription of Csf1 at the lesion epicenter by 2-fold on 7 days post injury (dpi), which remained elevated chronically up to at least 60 dpi (Figure 1A). Having determined injury-dependent upregulation of Csf1 transcription, we next sought to identify the cellular source of CSF1 protein in the absence or presence of injury. In the uninjured naive mouse spinal cord, co-immunofluorescence staining showed colocalization of CSF1 with glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) in astrocytes (Figure 1B). CSF1 was also present in GFAP− cells in the gray matter, whose cell morphology resembled neurons (Figure 1B). After SCI, CSF1 was strongly upregulated in GFAP+ astrocytes lining the lesion border on 7 dpi (Figure 1C) as well as in some interspersed GFAP− cells. While CSF1 was also detected in neurons located in spared tissue on 7 dpi, its cellular origin was largely non-neuronal in the injured spinal cord (Figure S1). To further ascertain that astrocytes at the primary lesion produce CSF1, we isolated astrocytes from naive and injured mouse spinal cords using microbeads that recognize astrocyte cell surface antigen 2 (ACSA2).21,22 To assess the purity of astrocytes isolated by anti-ACSA2 microbeads, eluted astrocytes were subsequently stained for ACSA2, and the percentage of ACSA2+ cells in total singlets was quantified by flow cytometry to be 95.8% (Figure 1D). By this approach, astrocytes purified from the spinal cord lesion were found to upregulate Csf1 expression by more than 3-fold on 7 dpi, compared to astrocytes isolated from naive spinal cords (Figure 1E). These findings together demonstrate that lesion border astrocytes robustly upregulate CSF1 at the transcription and protein expression levels.

Astrocytic Csf1 deletion reduces cell number and proliferation of perilesional microglia and astrocytes

To test the effects of astrocyte-derived CSF1 on microglia and macrophages of the injured CNS, we generated astrocyte-targeted, tamoxifen-inducible CSF1 KO (GFAP-CreERT2; CSF1f/f) mice or referred to as Astro-CSF1-KO from here on, by crossing Csf1f/f mice23 to mice expressing GFAP-CreERT2.24 Csf1f/f littermates lacking GFAP-CreERT2 were used as the genotype control. Prior to performing SCI of complete crush at thoracic level T8, both Astro-CSF1-KO mice and genotype controls were treated with tamoxifen (75 mg/kg intraperitoneal once daily, consecutively for 5 days, followed by 10 days of clearance) (Figure 2A). In a subset of tamoxifen-treated and spinally injured mice whose spinal cords were processed for downstream cytological analyses, astrocytes were also purified from their brains to verify Csf1 gene deletion. Quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) showed a 70% reduction in Csf1 transcript levels in astrocytes of Astro-CSF1-KO mice compared to genotype controls (Figure 2B, left graph). In addition, to directly assess Csf1 KO efficiency in astrocytes of the injured spinal cord, a separate cohort of animals was subjected to tamoxifen treatment followed by SCI. Astrocytes isolated from the spinal cord lesion of Astro-CSF1-KO mice showed 74% reduction in Csf1 transcript levels compared to genotype control on 7 dpi (Figure 2B, right graph). Together, these results validate the efficiency of tamoxifen-induced Csf1 gene deletion in brain and spinal cord astrocytes of injured Astro-CSF1-KO mice.

Focal CNS injury results in rapid microglia cell death at the lesion epicenter. Surviving perilesional microglia surrounding the necrotic lesion core, however, undergo cell proliferation that peaks on 7 dpi,10 and compartmentalize to form a microglial border around the lesion to compact the wound.10,12,20 To assess effects of astrocytic Csf1 deletion on injury-induced proliferation of microglia, therefore, we injected 5-ethynyl 2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) into injured mice on 2–6 dpi to label proliferating cells and collected spinal cords on 7 dpi to examine the cytoarchitecture of the injury site (Figure 2A). By histology, microglia can be identified based on IBA1 expression and cellular localization outside the GFAP-negative lesion core.12,25 Within a perilesional zone of 100–300 μm rostral or caudal to the lesion edge, away from the GFAP-negative lesion epicenter, Astro-CSF1-KO mice exhibited a 40% decrease in the number of IBA1+ cells (Figures 2C and 2D), concomitant with a 28% decrease in proliferating EdU+IBA1+ cells (Figures 2C and 2E) at the lesion periphery, supporting a requirement for astrocyte-derived CSF1 in injury-induced cell division of perilesional microglia.

Flow cytometry was additionally used to examine cellular dynamics of microglia, which can be identified by cell surface markers CD45LOWCD11b+Ly6G− and distinguished from monocyte-derived macrophages that are CD45HIGHCD11b+Ly6G− (Figure S2A). Flow cytometric analyses of cells dissociated from 0.5 to 0.8 cm of the spinal cord centered at the lesion epicenter on 7 dpi showed injury-induced microglia cell loss (Figure S4B) that was accompanied by proliferation of surviving microglia based on EdU incorporation (Figures S2B and S4E). Reduced microglia cell number and proliferation were evident in Astro-CSF1-KO mice (Figures S3, S4I, and S4L), confirming histological findings of dampened injury-dependent proliferative response of microglia (Figures 2C and 2E). Compared to flow cytometry, histology revealed a stronger cellular phenotype because histological preservation of the cytoarchitecture of the injury site enabled quantification with precise spatial specification to within 300 μm of the lesion border, where proliferating microglia are localized,10,12 whereas such spatial resolution is lost with flow cytometry. Flow cytometry also revealed a reduction in population size and proliferation of infiltrating macrophages in Astro-CSF1-KO mice (Figures S2, S3, S4J, and S4M), supporting that CSF1R-expressing macrophages are also target cells of astrocyte-derived CSF1 in the lesion environment.

Next, we assessed whether astrocytic Csf1 deletion impacts the injury response of astrocytes themselves. To accurately quantify astrocytes at the lesion border, where GFAP+ astrocytic processes densely overlap, astrocytes were identified by the astrocyte nuclear protein SOX926 instead. Like microglia, border-forming astrocytes undergo robust cell proliferation within 7 days following injury to form a protective cellular border to contain tissue damage.13,27 Such astrocytic proliferative response exhibits a spatial gradient with respect to distance from the lesion. In a complete crush SCI model as used here, astrocyte proliferation has been shown to be the highest within 250 μm of the lesion border and returns to baseline 1–2 mm rostro-caudally away from the lesion border.27 By histology, Astro-CSF1-KO mice showed a 36% reduction in SOX9+ astrocyte cell number within 100–300 μm of the lesion border (Figures 2F and 2G), accompanied by more than 50% decrease in the number of injury-induced EdU+SOX9+ proliferating astrocytes (Figures 2F and 2H). Flow cytometry using cell surface marker ACSA2 to identify astrocytes confirmed reduction of astrocytes at the spinal cord lesion of Astro-CSF1-KO mice (Figures S3A, S4A, S4D, and S4H), though decreased astrocyte proliferation did not reach statistical significance (Figure S4K). These findings demonstrate positive effects of astrocytic CSF1 on cell survival and proliferation of lesion border astrocytes.

Altogether, histology and flow cytometry show that astrocyte-derived CSF1 supports injury-induced cell proliferation of microglia, macrophages, and, somewhat surprisingly, astrocytes themselves at the lesion. Unlike the former two cell types, astrocytes are not known to be direct cellular targets of CSF1. To test if CSF1 can act on astrocytes, we treated primary cortical astrocytes with recombinant mouse CSF1 at increasing doses and found no effect of CSF1 on the proliferation of primary astrocytes (Figure S5). Functional interaction and spatial colocalization of microglia and astrocytes at the lesion border10,12,20 raise the possibility that astrocyte loss in Astro-CSF1-KO mice results from the loss of pro-survival signals originating from microglia at the lesion.

Impaired motor recovery of Astro-CSF1-KO mice is associated with compromised wound closure

Studies using pharmacological inhibitors of CSF1R have yielded opposite outcomes on motor recovery in rodent models of SCI.10,11,17,18 To determine the effects of astrocytic Csf1 gene deletion on functional recovery, we assessed gross hindlimb motor performance of Astro-CSF1-KO mice after thoracic SCI by Basso mouse scale (BMS)28 and skilled hindlimb stepping by a regular horizontal ladder.29 Pre-injury baseline hindlimb performance was comparable between genotype control and Astro-CSF1-KO mice (Figure 3A). Severity of injury, assessed on 2 dpi by BMS, was similar between the two groups (Figures 3A and 3B, left). Over 8 weeks of post-injury recovery period, astrocytic Csf1 deletion had an overall statistically significant effect of reducing motor recovery in both BMS and horizontal ladder (Figures 3A and 3C). A breakdown of the percentages of mice able to achieve each BMS score at 2 weeks after injury—a time point at which the difference in performance between genotype controls and Astro-CSF1-KO was statistically significant based on post hoc pairwise comparison—showed that whereas nearly 80% of the control mice were able to score 2 or above, none of the Astro-CSF1-KO mice was able to do so (Figure 3B, middle). At 8 weeks after injury, 44% of the control mice, but none of the Astro-CSF1-KO mice, scored 3 or above, regaining weight support and even occasional plantar stepping of the hindlimbs (Figure 3B, right). On the regular horizontal ladder, Astro-CSF1-KO mice consistently underperformed compared to genotype controls throughout the post-injury survival period (Figure 3C). These results demonstrate that astrocyte-derived CSF1 is beneficial to motor recovery after SCI.

The extent of neural recovery is determined by neuronal/axonal preservation and the ability of remaining neurons to reconnect with denervated targets. Quantification of axon fibers based on neurofilament 200 revealed approximately 50% decrease in axon density at the lesion epicenter of Astro-CSF1-KO mice at 8 weeks after injury (Figures 3D and 3E). Loss of wound repair microglia and astrocytes in Astro-CSF1-KO mice points to compromised tissue protection as a contributing factor to reduced axon counts in these mice. We therefore examined lesion size based on the area enclosed by the GFAP+ astrocytic border 14 days after injury. Our data show that Astro-CSF1-KO mice had 31% larger lesions lined with disorganized astrocytes and notably fewer microglia at the lesion border (Figures 3F and 3G), indicative of defective wound closure early after injury that compromised tissue preservation. Altogether, these findings demonstrate a requirement for astrocyte-derived CSF1 in organizing CNS wound closure, with positive effects on motor recovery.

Dampened IFN-β response and dysregulation of wound healing in the injury environment of Astro-CSF1-KO mice

To gain insights into how loss of astrocytic CSF1 impacts the lesion microenvironment in an unbiased manner, which may further provide clues to explain diminished astrocyte population in Astro-CSF1-KO mice, we performed bulk RNA sequencing on injured spinal cord tissues. Because astrocytes upregulate Csf1 by 7 dpi (Figures 1C and 1E), and astrocytic Csf1 deletion produced cellular phenotypes on 7 dpi (Figure 2), we chose this early injury time point for tissue collection. Across four experimental groups—(1) naive controls, (2) controls with SCI, (3) naive Astro-CSF1-KO, and (4) Astro-CSF1-KO with SCI—the total number of genes detected in every group was comparable (Figure S6). High dimensionality reduction analysis showed separation of transcriptional features of the four experimental groups (Figure 4A), indicating differences in transcriptional profiles. On 7 dpi, the main effect of injury was gene upregulation, as about 80% of all differentially expressed genes (DEGs defined by ∣log2FC∣ > 1, p < 0.05) were upregulated by injury in genotype control and Astro-CSF1-KO mice (Figures 4B and 4C; Tables S1 and S2). In contrast, the main effect of astrocytic Csf1 deletion was gene downregulation, as 86% and 71% of all DEGs were downregulated in the naive spinal cord and injured spinal cord, respectively, compared to the genotype control (Figures 4D and 4E; Tables S3 and S4).

To identify mechanisms that contribute to the cellular phenotypes of Astro-CSF1-KO mice, we reasoned that the determination of the top pathways that are differentially regulated in genotype control and Astro-CSF1-KO mice after injury would offer insights. To do this, we first used the 1,499 injury-induced genes (log2FC > 1, p < 0.05) to identify more than 1,000 injury-induced biological processes in genotype control mice (false discovery rate [FDR] < 0.05) (Figures 4B and 4F red; Tables S1 and S5). Separately, we used the 387 genes downregulated in Astro-CSF1-KO compared to genotype controls after injury (log2FC < − 1, p < 0.05) to identify 28 corresponding biological processes (Figures 4E and 4F blue; Tables S4 and S5). Between these two sets of biological processes, 22 are shared and represent processes that are upregulated by injury but downregulated by astrocytic Csf1 deletion after injury. When ranked based on fold enrichment, response to IFN-β was identified as the top two pathways (Figure 4G; Table S6), suggesting that astrocyte-derived CSF1 supports IFN-β response in the injury environment.

IFN-β belongs to the type I IFN family of cytokines. It is best known for its anti-inflammatory effects in CNS autoimmunity and is used clinically to treat relapsing remitting multiple sclerosis.30 Given the initial identification of differential regulation of this pathway in Astro-CSF1-KO mice, we next examined the expression of genes that contribute to IFN-β response in greater detail. There are 81 genes that belong to the Gene Ontology biological process (GO-BP) of “response to IFN-β“ (GO: 0035456). Injury significantly upregulated 35 of them in genotype controls (log2FC > 1, p < 0.05). While they were also induced by injury in Astro-CSF1-KO mice, the levels of their induction were largely reduced. Plotting injury-induced fold change (FC) of expression of each gene in genotype control against Astro-CSF1-KO revealed that 66% of them, or 23 of 35 genes, exhibited reduced injury-dependent induction in Astro-CSF1-KO mice (Figure 4H red dots, 4I red; Table S7). These findings show that deletion of astrocytic Csf1 diminished IFN-β response and identify this pathway as a candidate mechanism underlying injury-dependent cellular phenotypes of Astro-CSF1-KO mice. Detection of reduced IFN-β response in the injury environment following Csf1 deletion in astrocytes alone, despite presence of other CSF1-producing cells, suggests that astrocytic CSF1 locally elicits IFN-β response, likely in lesion border astrocytes themselves. This raises the possibility that astrocytic response to IFN-β contributes to positive feedback regulation between astrocytes and microglia at the lesion border.

Cellular and histological evidence of disrupted wound repair in Astro-CSF1-KO mice led us to also examine whether the wound response is differentially regulated at the transcriptional level in these mice. Genes associated with wound repair are represented in the GO-BP of “wound healing” (GO: 0042060) that has been used to transcriptionally assess wound repair of the injured spinal cord.31 GO-BP of wound healing was highly enriched in injured spinal cords of genotype controls on 7 dpi (enrichment FDR of 4.47 × 10−32). Of the genes annotated to wound healing, 98% or 404 genes have detectable expression across our four experimental conditions (Figure 4J; Table S8). Injury significantly upregulated 184 wound healing genes in genotype controls (FC > 1.5, p < 0.05), 55% of which were down-regulated or showed reduced induction in injured Astro-CSF1-KO mice (Figure 4K red; Table S9), whereas the remaining 45% showed equal or increased induction in the mutant (Figure 4K blue; Table S9). These results demonstrate transcriptional dysregulation of the wound healing response in Astro-CSF1-KO mice. Together with anatomical and behavioral outcomes previously measured in Astro-CSF1-KO mice that showed enlarged lesion size, exacerbated axonal loss, and worsened motor recovery (Figures 2 and 3), transcriptional indications of diminished IFN-β response, and disrupted wound healing provide molecular evidence for a dysregulated injury microenvironment that is unfavorable to neural repair.

Microglia produce IFN-β in the injured spinal cord and are reduced in Astro-CSF1-KO mice

Dampened IFN-β response to injury in Astro-CSF1-KO mice prompted us to test its role in feedback regulation between astrocytes and microglia. Analyses of available transcriptomics datasets (including single-cell RNA sequencing [RNA-seq], astrocyte-specific RNA-seq, and microglia-specific RNA-seq)31-36 first confirmed that SCI upregulates IFN-β—encoded by the gene Ifnb1—in microglia33 (Figure 5A, left). Second, multiple datasets show that lesion border astrocytes upregulate transcription of both subunits of the IFN-β receptor—encoded by genes Ifnar1 and Ifnar2—in the injured spinal cord31,34,36 (Figure 5A, right), supporting their capacity to directly engage in IFN-β signal transduction. Further GO analysis of DEGs (log2FC > 1, p < 0.05) of these datasets revealed significant enrichment of IFN-β response in lesion border astrocytes early after injury31,36 (Figure 5B), strongly implicating microglia-astrocyte communication via IFN-β signaling in wound closure.

To determine the cellular source of IFN-β and its spatial distribution in the injured spinal cord, we used IFN-β-EYFP knockin reporter mice that allow fluorescent labeling of cells that endogenously produce IFN-β in vivo.37 In the healthy spinal cord, EYFP+ IFN-β-producing cells were hardly detectable (Figure 5C, no injury). In the injured spinal cord, strong IFN-β-EYFP signal colocalized with IBA1+ cells at the lesion border, but not with GFAP+ cells (Figure 5C, SCI 7 dpi). Surprisingly, even in spared tissue at 2 mm away from the lesion, where astrocytes appear non-reactive morphologically, IBA1+ microglia still expressed IFN-β-EYFP in the gray matter (Figure 5D, bottom). These findings demonstrate that microglia in the injured spinal cord produce IFN-β, from the lesion border to distal spared tissue. Quantification of IFN-β+IBA1+ cells based on immunodetection of IFN-β in the control and Astro-CSF1-KO mice showed ~50% reduction of IFN-β+ microglia at the lesion border and at 3 mm from the lesion (Figure 5E). Because the level of reduction of IFN-β+IBA1+ population is comparable to that of the total IBA1+ population (Figure 2D), loss of IFN-β+IBA1+ cells in Astro-CSF1-KO mice largely results from the loss of microglia, not their ability to produce IFN-β per se.

Exogenous IFN-β rescues astrocyte cell loss in Astro-CSF1-KO mice

Based on evidence supporting microglia-dependent regulation of astrocytes via IFN-β signaling that is disrupted in Astro-CSF1-KO mice, we next sought to functionally determine effects of IFN-β on astrocytes and microglia, specifically by assessing the ability of exogenous IFN-β to rescue the phenotypes of astrocyte and microglia cell loss in Astro-CSF1-KO mice after injury. To do so, astrocytic Csf1 gene deletion was induced before injury, then immediately following spinal cord surgery, the first dose of purified recombinant mouse IFN-β protein was delivered (104 IU/animal at 2.5–5 × 105 IU/kg, subcutaneous, as previously described38), followed by repeated delivery once every other day for a total of four treatments before spinal cord collection on 7 dpi for flow cytometric analysis (Figure 6A). In injured genotype controls, IFN-β treatment did not significantly increase astrocyte or microglia populations at the lesion (Figures 6B and 6C). As observed before, the population of border-forming astrocytes was reduced by 34% in vehicle-treated Astro-CSF1-KO mice, compared to vehicle-treated genotype controls (Figure 6B). IFN-β treatment rescued astrocyte cell loss in Astro-CSF1-KO mice to a comparable level as in vehicle-treated control (Figure 6B) but did not rescue cell loss of microglia (Figure 6C).

To determine whether rescue of astrocyte cell number by IFN-β is sufficient to improve repair and recovery of Astro-CSF1-KO mice, we applied IFN-β treatment in a second cohort of mice that were collected for histological examination of wound closure, and a third cohort of mice that were collected at 8 weeks after injury for motor assessments. While exogenous IFN-β increased GFAP+ cellular density at the lesion border of Astro-CSF1-KO mice compared to saline-treated Astro-CSF1-KO mice (Figure 6D left red and 6E), it did not statistically significantly increase IBA1+ cellular density (Figure 6D right green and 6F) or improve wound closure (Figure 6G). While IFN-β-treated Astro-CSF1-KO mice displayed a trend toward improved motor performance on the BMS and horizontal ladder, it was nevertheless statistically insignificant (Figure S7). Insufficiency of IFN-β to rescue microglia cell loss or neural recovery in Astro-CSF1-KO mice is likely due to deficiency of astrocytic CSF1.

Upregulation of IFN-β receptor subunits in lesion border astrocytes, together with the ability of exogenous IFN-β to prevent astrocyte cell loss in Astro-CSF1-KO mice when administered in the acute phase of injury, supports pro-survival effects of direct microglia-astrocyte signaling via IFN-β on lesion border astrocytes.

Astrocytic IFNAR1 deletion disrupts wound repair and motor recovery

To directly assess effects of astrocytic IFN signaling on wound healing, we deleted IFN-α/-β receptor subunit 1 (Ifnar1) that mediates signaling of type I IFN, including IFN-β, in astrocytes (Figure 7A). This was achieved by tail vein injection of AAV-PHP.eB-GFAP-Cre, a blood brain barrier crossing adeno-associated virus (AAV) that expresses Cre under the GFAP promoter, into Ifnar1-floxed (Ifnar1f/f) mice to generate Astro-IFNAR1-KO mice. Age-matched wild-type mice with tail vein injection of AAV-PHP.eB-GFAP-Cre were used as controls. Two weeks after AAV injection, mice were subjected to SCI. By this approach, deletion efficiency of Ifnar1 in astrocytes isolated from the spinal cord lesion on 7 dpi averaged at 50% (Figure 7B). A separate delivery into Cre-dependent tdTomato reporter mice39 showed transduction of astrocytes in both white and gray matters of the spinal cord Figure S8). Examination of the spinal cord lesion of Astro-IFNAR1-KO mice revealed over 2-fold reduction in SOX9+ astrocyte cell number on 7 dpi (Figures 7C and 7D) with a corresponding 40% reduction in GFAP+ cellular density at the lesion border (Figures 7E left red and 7F). This was accompanied by a 2-fold decrease in IBA1+ cellular density (Figures 7E right green and 7G) and impaired wound closure (Figures 7E and 7H). Ifnar1 deletion in astrocytes hindered spontaneous motor recovery, with plateau of performance on the BMS at 2 weeks after injury that averaged under a score of 3, in contrast to progressive improvement toward an average score of 4 in controls (Figure 7I). Horizontal ladder similarly showed a lack of spontaneous recovery in skilled hindlimb stepping in Astro-IFNAR1-KO mice (Figure 7J). These observations demonstrate positive regulation of astrocyte survival, coordination with microglia in wound repair, and motor recovery by astrocytic IFN signaling that is attributed to, at least in part, activation by IFN-β based on evidence from transcriptomics analyses (Figure 4) and IFN-β rescue experiments (Figure 6).

In summary, our findings of lesion border astrocytes as a cellular origin of CSF1, microglia as producers of IFN-β in the injured spinal cord, repressed IFN-β response in the injury environment of Astro-CSF1-KO mice, and loss of border-forming astrocytes and microglia associated with adverse effects on wound repair and motor recovery in Astro-CSF1-KO mice and Astro-IFNAR1-KO mice after SCI altogether uncover astrocyte-microglia crosstalk via CSF1 and IFN-β that is tissue protective following CNS injury.

DISCUSSION

Microglia and astrocytes have been separately demonstrated to regulate wound healing in the injured spinal cord.9-16,40 While such functional interaction suggests coordinated regulation of these cell types, the underlying mechanism is not well understood. This study expands knowledge of intercellular communication between astrocytes and microglia that is beneficial to CNS repair. We identified lesion border astrocytes as a cellular source of CSF1 required for injury-induced microglial cell proliferation and IFN-β production that, in turn, supports astrocyte survival at the lesion early after injury. Disruption of this crosstalk by knocking out Csf1 or the IFN-α/-β receptor subunit Ifnar1 in astrocytes resulted in a loss of border-forming astrocytes and microglia, impaired wound closure, and worsened motor deficits after injury, demonstrating its protective function.

Compared to previous studies that primarily aimed to determine the role of microglia in CNS injury by CSF1R inhibition,10,11,17-19 this study, utilizing astrocyte-targeted deletion of Csf1, offers additional biological insights. First, it identified astrocytes as an endogenous source of CSF1 with functional consequences on microglial response to injury and neurological recovery. Second, it uncovered positive astrocyte-microglia feedback regulation via CSF1 and IFN-β that is beneficial to CNS repair. Third, it suggests that microglia and astrocytes react concurrently, rather than sequentially, to focal CNS injury. Fourth, it identified pro-survival effects of IFN-β-IFNAR signaling in wound repair astrocytes. Lastly, it identified potential molecular targets of therapeutic intervention for synergizing the neuroprotective functions of these cells.

Previous use of CSF1R inhibitor and targeted candidate testing identified microglia-derived IGF1 to be required for astrocytic proliferation at the lesion.10 Here, using Astro-CSF1-KO mice and an unbiased transcriptomics approach, we identified microglia-derived IFN-β as a pro-survival factor for lesion border astrocytes early after injury. In assessing IGF1 expression in Astro-CSF1-KO mice, we observed colocalization of IGF1 with IBA1+ cells in the injured spinal cord (Figure S9A); while IGF1+ cellular density was lower in Astro-CSF1-KO mice (Figure S9B top), the percentage of IBA1+ cells that produce IGF1 was comparable to genotype controls (Figure S9B bottom). This indicates that reduced expression of IGF1 in Astro-CSF1-KO mice largely results from loss of microglia in these mice, not from the inability of microglia to produce IGF1. Combined loss of microglia-derived IFN-β and IGF1 likely jointly diminish astrocyte population in Astro-CSF1-KO mice.

Besides astrocytes, SCI also increases transcription of Csf1 in microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons,32,41,42 the latter of which we also observed by histology. The effects of these other cellular sources of CSF1 on CNS repair remain to be determined. Contribution of each CSF1-expressing cell type to the regulation of myeloid cells in the injured CNS likely depends on its respective ability to survive the insult, proximity to target cells, timing of expression, and/or level of CSF1 production.

Effects of IFN-β on astrocytes have been traditionally studied in the interest of the protective effects of IFN-β in multiple sclerosis. IFN-β promotes astrocyte survival via phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase activation,43 elevates astrocytic production of nerve growth factor,44 limits CNS inflammation in an astrocyte-dependent manner in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE),45 upregulates anti-inflammatory signaling pathways in astrocytes,46 and inhibits astrocyte proliferation in response to mitogen47 and in experimental neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder.48 IFN response defines a transcriptionally unique subset of astrocytes that are early strong responders to neuroinflammation in mouse and human cell-based models.49,50 Parallels can also be drawn between our observations and those from studies of CNS viral infection, the latter of which reported that IFN-β is primarily produced by microglia,51,52 IFN-β increases astrocyte reactivity,53,54 and astrocytic type I IFN signaling is required for microglia activation and protection.55 In SCI, IFN-β exerts acutely anti-inflammatory effects,56 based on assessments of selective pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines57 and neutrophil infiltration,58 though the cellular targets of IFN-β were unknown. Our study provides the first evidence that IFN-β-mediated IFNAR signaling in astrocytes promotes wound repair in vivo in a focal CNS injury model.

While our findings support microglia as primary producers of IFN-β and astrocytes as its cellular targets in the injured spinal cord, IFN-β has been shown to act on microglia themselves in CNS physiology and pathology. In brain development, IFN-β promotes microglial phagocytosis of neurons59; whereas in EAE, IFN-β suppresses phagocytic activity, MHC class II expression, and chemokine production by microglia and macrophages to limit neuroinflammation.60 A broad spectrum of microglia-mediated, protective effects of IFN-β has been described in CNS autoimmunity.61 In traumatic brain injury, constitutive deficiency of IFN-β reduces microglia activation and functional deficits62; and STING-mediated type I IFN signaling in microglia is proinflammatory.63,64 Following SCI, IFN-responsive microglia are detected from acute to sub-acute phases of injury32,41,42,65 and proposed to be derived from proliferating microglia in the process of injury-induced microglia activation and differentiation.41 Functions of IFN-responsive microglia in the injured spinal cord remain to be examined.

In summary, this work advances understanding of how astrocytes and microglia are coordinated to promote CNS repair via direct intercellular communication. Positive feedback regulation of these two cell types via CSF1 and IFN-β signaling described here has important implications for therapeutic strategies aimed at synergizing the injury response of these cells for neural repair.

Limitations of the study

Border-forming astrocytes are one of the several cell types that upregulate Csf1 in the injured spinal cord, based on findings of this study and analyses of available transcriptomics datasets.31,32,42 Cell type-specific contributions to CSF1 protein production in the injured CNS and effects of non-astrocytic CSF1 on the injury response remain to be determined. Detection of diminished IFN-β response in the injury microenvironment of Astro-CSF1-KO mice by transcriptomics, despite the presence of Csf1 derived from non-astrocytic sources, suggests that astrocytic CSF1 locally stimulates IFN-β response that primarily occurs or is strongest in border forming astrocytes and their immediate vicinity. Our findings demonstrate that astrocytic type I IFN signaling is important for neural repair; identification of other cellular responders to IFN-β and their spatial proximity to the astrocytic border in situ may be facilitated by spatial single cell transcriptomics. We did not assess immunomodulatory effects of astrocytic IFN-β response following SCI. We did not test for the ability of CSF1 to directly regulate type I IFN response of astrocytes; it is possible that these two signaling pathways interact in astrocytes. Lastly, how IFN-mediated signaling of astrocytes regulates astrocytic functions, behaviors of other cell types, and injury/disease outcome likely depends on CNS insult-specific contexts that remain to be elucidated.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Mice

All mouse husbandry and experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Kentucky (2019–3333). All mice are in C57BL/6 background. Male and female mice at age of 8–10 weeks were used for experiments. Influence of sex was not specifically examined because primary interests of this study were to identify effects of genotypes. Homozygous Csf1f/f mice23 were obtained from Dr. Jean Jiang (University of Texas Health Science Center at San Antonio), and crossed to GFAP-CreERT224 in house to generate GFAP-CreERT2; CSF1f/f mice, referred to as Astro-CSF1-KO mice in this study. Littermate Csf1f/f mice lacking GFAP-CreERT2 were used as genotype control. To induce gene deletion in Astro-CSF1-KO mice, 75mg/kg of tamoxifen was given by i.p. injection once daily, consecutively for five days, followed by 10 days of clearance before spinal cord surgery. Genotype controls were also treated with tamoxifen. Cre-dependent tdTomato reporter mice39 were bred in house. Homozygous IFNB1-EYFP knockin reporter mice37 (JAX 010818), homozygous Ifnar1f/f mice68 (JAX 028256), and wildtype mice (JAX 000664) were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory and bred in house. All experimental procedures, including all treatments, surgery, tissue processing, and data analyses were performed by lab members blinded to mouse genotypes.

Primary astrocyte cell culture

Mouse use for and procedures of primary astrocyte isolation were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (2019–3333) and Institutional Biosafety Committee (IBC-25-149) at the University of Kentucky. Primary cortical astrocytes freshly purified from wildtype C57BL/6J mouse pups of both sexes on postnatal day 2 (P2) were cultured as previously described69; purity of primary astrocytes was verified by GFAP expression. Briefly, prior to astrocyte isolation, T-25 flasks were coated with 50μg/mL poly-d-lysine (Sigma) overnight, washed three times with Dulbecco’s Phosphate-Buffered Saline DPBS (Sigma), one time with sterilized water, then left to dry in biosafety cabinet. P2 mouse cerebral cortices were dissected, meninges removed, and minced with a razor blade. Dissociated tissue was then washed with DMEM (Thermo Fisher) supplemented with 1% L-Glutamine (Thermo Fisher), and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (Thermo Fisher), followed by incubation in 2.5% trypsin (Thermo Fisher) and 100μL/mL DNase I (StemCell Technologies) for 5 min at 37°C. Tissue was then washed with culture media (DMEM supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 1% L-Glutamine, and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin) to inactivate and remove trypsin and DNaseI and triturated with a 1mL open ended pipette in culture media. Dissociated cells were passed through a 40μM cell strainer to remove aggregates, pelleted by centrifugation, resuspended in culture media, and seeded at 1.5 × 106 cells/25cm2 flask. Cells were maintained in tissue culture incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. Culture media was replaced every two to three days. After 7 days in culture, astrocytes were enriched by flask shaking at 180 rpm for 48 h to remove non-adherent cells. Remaining adherent astrocytes were re-seeded at 0.5 × 106 cells/75cm2 flask or 0.1 × 106 cells/well in a 12-well plate for subsequent assays. Primary astrocytes were not tested for mycoplasma.

METHOD DETAILS

Surgical procedures and post-injury treatments

Mice underwent general anesthesia by intraperitoneal injection of ketamine and xylazine. Complete crush of the spinal cord at thoracic level T8-T9 was performed under a surgical microscope (Zeiss Stemi 2000-C). Similar to previously described,70 following laminectomy of a single vertebra, complete crush of the spinal cord was made by compressing the cord at T8/T9 bilaterally for 5 s using Dumont #5 SuperFine forceps (Fine Science Tools). For treatment of recombinant mouse IFNβ (IFNβ carrier-free, PBL assay science, 12410–1),10,000 IU IFNβ in saline was delivered by s.c. injection38 at wound site immediately following spinal cord injury and then once every other days for a total of 4 treatments over 7 days after injury. For 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine labeling (5′-Edu, APExBIO), mice received i.p. injection of 50mg/kg of on 2-6dpi.

Tail vein injection of AAV-PHP.eB

Mice in home cages were placed on heating pads for 1h to facilitate vein visualization, followed by tail vein injection on unanesthetized mice contained in a mouse restrainer. 5–7×1010 genome copies of AAV-PHP.eB-GFAP-Cre (Addgene 105550-PHPeB) diluted in 100μL total volume of saline was injected into the tail vein of each mouse using Easy Touch diabetic syringe (MedLab, 1cc/mLx27gx1/2). To validate astrocyte targeting in the healthy spinal cord, virus was tail vein-injected into Cre-dependent tdTomato reporter mice39 and spinal cords were harvested two weeks after injection for histological examination. For conditional gene deletion of Ifnar1 in astrocytes, virus was tail vein-injected into homozygous IFNAR1f/f mice68 (JAX 028256) to generate Astro-IFNAR1-KO mice. Wildtype mice with tail vein injection of this virus served as controls. Two weeks after injection, control and Astro-IFNAR1-KO mice underwent spinal cord injury.

Tissue collection

At 7 days or 8 weeks after complete spinal cord crush, terminal anesthesia was performed on mice by isoflurane overdose. For fresh tissue collection, mice were perfused intracardially with cold PBS with heparin (10units/mL) for 3 min 0.5–0.8cm of spinal cords centered at the lesion epicenter were rapidly dissected and either kept on ice for cell isolation, or flash frozen in liquid nitrogen for qRT-PCR. For immunohistology, mice were perfused intracardially first with ice-cold PBS containing heparin for 3 min, followed by perfusion of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 7 min. Tissues were post-fixed in 4% PFA at 4°C overnight. Tissues were then cryoprotected in 30% sucrose at 4°C for 48 h. Spinal cords were embedded on dry ice in O.C.T compound (Tissue-Tek). Embedded spinal cords were cryo-sectioned (Leica CM1860) into 15μm thick horizontal sections and stored in 0.01% sodium azide in PBS.

Histology

Immunofluorescence staining of free-floating horizontal sections were performed as previously described.16 Tissues were permeabilized by washing three times with wash buffer (0.2% Triton in PBS) and then blocked with blocking buffer (0.4% Triton and 5% bovine serum albumin in PBS with 1× sodium azide) for 1 h at room temperature. Spinal cord sections were stained in primary antibody mixtures diluted in antibody incubation buffer (0.2% Triton and 1% BSA in PBS with 1× sodium azide) at 4°C with rotation overnight. The next day, tissues were washed three times in wash buffer and then incubated in secondary antibody solution for two hours at room temperature with rotation. Tissues were washed twice with 3% BSA in PBS after secondary antibody application. EdU detection was carried out with Click-iT EdU Cell Proliferation Kit for Imaging, Alexa Fluor 488 (ThermoFisher C10637). Briefly, tissues were incubated in a freshly prepared Click-iT reaction cocktail for 30 min at room temperature following manufacture protocol. After two washes, spinal cords were incubated with 1μg/mL DAPI (Sigma) solution for 20 min. Spinal cord slices were mounted onto glass slides with Fluoromount-G (ThermoFisher).

Astrocyte isolation from adult mouse spinal cords

Mice were perfused intracardially with ice-cold heparin in PBS (10units/mL) for 3 min. Naive spinal cord, or approximately 0.5–0.7cm of spinal cords centered at the lesion epicenter were rapidly harvested. Spinal cords are immediately homogenized and dissociated into single cell suspension using Neural Tissue Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec). Myelin debris and red blood cells were removed by Debris Removal Solution (Miltenyi Biotec) and Red Blood Cell Lysis Solution (Miltenyi Biotec) following manufacturer’s protocols. ACSA2+ astrocytes were labeled by the anti-ACSA2 Microbead Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) and magnetically isolated by MACS columns following the manufacturer’s protocol.

RT-qPCR

Target specificity of all RT-qPCR primers used in this study was validated by the appearance of a single peak on melting curve analysis. Transcriptomic analyses

Mice were perfused intracardially with ice-cold heparin in PBS (10units/mL). Injured spinal cord tissues (0.5–0.7cm centered on the lesion site) or corresponding spinal cord fragments in naive spinal cord were collected 7 days post-injury. Spinal cord tissue was mechanically dissociated by homogenizer on ice. Total RNA isolated from the spinal cord lesion center or equivalent spinal cord region from naive control were isolated using the RNA isolation kit (Takara). RNA concentration and RNA integrity number (RIN) were measured using an RNA Nano chip kit and Bioanalyzer. Samples that passed quality control (RIN>7) were submitted to Novogene for library preparation (polyA selected, non-strand specific) and RNA sequencing on Novaseq 6000 S4, with sequencing depth of 20 million paired-end 150bp reads per sample. Gene expression was normalized based on Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM). High dimensionality reduction was performed on all detectable transcripts by sparse partial least-squares discriminant analysis (sPLS-DA) (mixOmics v6.18.0). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) are identified based on statistical significance (p < 0.05) and fold change (∣Log2FC∣>1). DEGs of four pairwise comparisons – (i) genotype control, naive vs. 7dpi; (ii) Astro-CSF1-KO, naive vs. 7dpi; (iii) naive, genotype control vs. Astro-CSF1-KO; (iv) 7dpi, genotype control vs. Astro-CSF1-KO – were used to generate volcano plots by ggplot2 package in R. DEGs were subjected to analyses by ShinyGO to identify significantly enriched Gene Ontology biological processes (GO-BPs) based on enrichment FDR<0.05. To generate heatmap of detectable genes in our dataset belonging to GO:0042060 “Wound healing,” a GO-BP that is significantly enriched following injury (enrichment FDR = 4.47 × 10−32), gene list of GO:0042060 was first extracted, then those with detectable expression across our four experimental conditions were presented (total of 404 genes) in heatmap using R packages pheatmap and RColorBrewer.

Mouse behavioral analyses

Mice were assessed for their hindlimb motor recovery for 8 weeks after spinal cord injury. All experimenters were blinded throughout the behavioral assessment. Mice were acclimated to test conditions 7 days prior to injury. Basso Mouse Scale (BMS)28 was used to assess open-field gross hindlimb function. Regular horizontal ladder29 modified for SCI69 was used to assess skilled hindlimb stepping. Each animal underwent three trials per time point. Number of successful steps, defined by paw placement on a rung that provides weight support and balance. Paw slip below the rung in a forward movement was counted as failed step. The number of successful steps was normalized to total steps taken, and averaged across three trials per animal per time point.

CSF1 treatment in primary astrocytes

Primary astrocytes were plated on PDL-coated coverslips. Astrocytes at 50% confluency were treated with recombinant mouse CSF1 (PeproTech, 315–02), at 30 or 50ng/mL,72,73 for 48h. Cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde and stained for astrocyte and proliferation markers, GFAP and Ki67, respectively (see below). Astrocyte cell proliferation was assessed by counting number of Ki67+GFAP+ cells as a percentage of GFAP+ cells in a sampling frame of 1300 × 1300μm2. Quantification was based on 5 sampling frames per well, 3 wells per treatment.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

All data are presented as mean values with SEM. Unpaired, parametric, two-tailed t test was used for comparison of two independent groups. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc analysis was used for comparison of more than two groups with one variable. two-way ANOVA with repeated measures was used to analyze effects of two variables. In case of unexpected death of animals before end of study, two-way mixed effects ANOVA was used. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed only if main genotype/treatment effect or interaction was statistically significant, see corresponding figure legends for test used and statistics. GraphPad Prism software was used for all statistical analysis. n represents the number of animals per group. p < 0.05 defines statistical significance. n.s. indicates not significant or p ≥ 0.05 based on the statistical test applied.
