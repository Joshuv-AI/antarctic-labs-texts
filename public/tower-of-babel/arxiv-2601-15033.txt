Generic real Jordan canonical forms
Fernando De Terán, Froilán M. Dopico

Abstract
We obtain the generic real Jordan canonical forms for n × n n\times n matrices with real entries. More precisely, we prove that the set of n × n n\times n real matrices is the union of the closures of ⌊ n / 2 ⌋ + 1 \lfloor n/2\rfloor+1 sets, which are called generic bundles , as they are particular “bundles”. In general, a bundle is the set of n × n n\times n real matrices with the same real Jordan canonical form, up to the values of the eigenvalues, provided that the eigenvalues which are distinct in one matrix of the bundle remain distinct in any other matrix of the same bundle. The k k th generic bundle, for 0 ≤ k ≤ ⌊ n / 2 ⌋ 0\leq k\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor , contains the n × n n\times n real matrices having k k different pairs of non-real conjugate eigenvalues and n − 2 ​ k n-2k different real eigenvalues. We prove that each of the ⌊ n / 2 ⌋ + 1 \lfloor n/2\rfloor+1 generic bundles is an open subset of the set of n × n n\times n real matrices. Some numerical experiments are carried out with large sets of random matrices of different sizes to confirm that all the generic bundles show up, and only these ones.



Keywords
. Real square matrices; similarity orbit; similarity bundle; real Jordan canonical form; closure; Schur form; random matrices; real eigenvalues.

1
Introduction

It is well-known that most
n\times n
matrices with complex entries have
n
different eigenvalues. In the terminology of the present work, this can be rephrased as follows: the “generic” Jordan canonical form for
n\times n
complex matrices consists of a direct sum of
n
Jordan blocks of size
1\times 1
corresponding to different eigenvalues. Recall that the Jordan form is a canonical form under
similarity
of complex matrices, where two matrices
A
and
B
are
similar
if there is some invertible matrix
P
such that
B=P^{-1}AP
. It is natural to ask: what happens when the matrices have real entries and the allowed similarities are restricted to be real? Namely: which are the generic Jordan canonical forms of real
n\times n
matrices under real similarity? It is known that the eigenvalues of a real matrix are either real numbers or pairs of non-real conjugate numbers, but, how many of each kind appear generically in an
n\times n
matrix? This is the question we aim to answer in this work from a topological point of view.

The
real Jordan canonical form
of
A
(denoted by RJCF(
A
)) is the representative of the real matrix
A
under real similarity. It is a direct sum of blocks of two different kinds which correspond to, respectively, real eigenvalues and pairs of non-real conjugate eigenvalues of
A
(see
[
6
, Th. 3.4.1.5]
and Section
2
for the definitions). In this work, we determine the generic real Jordan canonical form of real
n\times n
matrices. More precisely, we prove that there is a finite number of
n\times n
RJCFs (which we explicitly describe) such that the set of matrices having these RJCFs is open and dense in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, that is, in the set of real
n\times n
matrices. Each of these RJCFs is determined by the number of real eigenvalues.

Let us provide a more detailed description of the contribution of this work. The main result is Theorem
3.2
, where we show that
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the union of the closures of
\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1
bundles under real similarity, which are different to each other (we prove a stronger fact, namely that the closure of a given generic bundle has empty intersection with any other generic bundle), and that these bundles are open in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
(in the standard topology). Therefore, the union of these bundles is an open dense set in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and, so, it is generic in the standard topological sense. We call these
\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1
bundles
generic
under real similarity. Each bundle corresponds to the set of real
n\times n
matrices having the same number of different real eigenvalues, and the rest of eigenvalues being different couples of non-real conjugate eigenvalues. In terms of RJCFs, the
t
th bundle is a direct sum of
t
real Jordan blocks of size
2\times 2
associated with different couples of non-real conjugate eigenvalues together with
n-2t
Jordan blocks of size
1\times 1
associated with different real eigenvalues. Since
t
ranges from
0
to
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
, all possible numbers of real eigenvalues appear in the union. In other words, each generic bundle corresponds to an integer
k
, for
0\leq k\leq n
, with the same parity as
n
, and contains all the
n\times n
real matrices having exactly
k
different real eigenvalues and
(n-k)/2
different couples of non-real conjugate eigenvalues.

It is expected that the dimension of each generic bundle is
n^{2}
, which coincides with the dimension of
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. By introducing an appropriate notion of dimension for the similarity bundles, and using the
codimension
within
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
instead, we prove that this is indeed the case, namely we show that the codimension of each generic bundle is
0
.

The problem we address in this paper is related to the one of determining which is the expected RJCF of random matrices. Mimicking the previous considerations for the generic RJCF, it is natural to expect that a random real matrix has all its eigenvalues different to each other, so the remaining question is: how many of them are real? This question has been addressed in several previous works. In
[
3
]
, Edelman obtained, for every
0\leq k\leq n
, the probability of a random
n\times n
real matrix to have exactly
k
real eigenvalues, whereas in
[
4
]
the expected number of real eigenvalues was calculated (random matrices in these works are matrices whose entries follow independent standard normal distributions, though it is mentioned that, after extensive numerical experience, similar results can be obtained for other distributions). According to our results, all values
0\leq k\leq n
having the same parity as
n
must have a positive probability, and they should show up experimentally in a sufficiently large number of tests. We confirm this fact with several numerical experiments.

Notation and basic definitions

Following the notation in
[
6
]
, by
C(a,b):=\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}a&\phantom{!}b\\
-b&\phantom{!}a\end{bmatrix}
we denote a
2\times 2
real Jordan block associated with a couple of non-real conjugate eigenvalues
a\pm b\mathfrak{i}
, with
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
and
b>0
(from now on,
\mathfrak{i}
denotes the imaginary unit). Also, we use the following notation for, respectively, a
k\times k
Jordan block associated with the eigenvalue
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
and a
2k\times 2k
real Jordan block associated with a couple of non-real conjugate eigenvalues
a\pm b\mathfrak{i}
, with
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
and
b>0
:

J_{k}(\mu)=\begin{bmatrix}\mu&1\\
&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&\mu&1\\
&&&\mu\end{bmatrix}_{k\times k},\qquad C_{k}(a,b)=\begin{bmatrix}C(a,b)&I_{2}\\
&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&C(a,b)&I_{2}\\
&&&C(a,b)\end{bmatrix}_{2k\times 2k}.

(1)

When
k=1
, the block
J_{1}(\mu)
will be written as
[\mu]
for simplicity, as it is just a number.

For
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, its
real orbit under similarity
is the set of real matrices which are similar to
A
, namely,

{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)=\{P^{-1}AP:\ \ \mbox{$P\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ invertible}\}.

(2)

The orbit
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
is a differentiable manifold over
\mathbb{R}
, and its tangent space at
A
is the set (see, for instance,
[
2
, §4.1]
):

{\cal T}_{A}=\{XA-AX:\ X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\}.

Therefore, the dimension of
{\cal T}_{A}
, denoted by
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}({\cal T}_{A})
, is the dimension of the real vector space of matrices of the form
XA-AX
. This is the dimension of the orbit
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
. We will consider instead the
codimension
of
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
, namely the dimension of the normal space to
{\cal T}_{A}
, which is equal to
{\rm codim}_{\mathbb{R}}{\cal T}_{A}=n^{2}-\dim_{\mathbb{R}}{\cal T}_{A}
, as
n^{2}
is the dimension of the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Moreover,

{\rm codim}_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\,:\,XA-AX=0\},

(3)

namely the codimension of the orbit
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
is the dimension of the solution space of the linear equation
XA-AX=0
, which is a real vector space (see, for instance,
[
2
, p. 71]
).

The
real bundle under similarity
of
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, denoted by
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
, is the set of real matrices which have the same RJCF as
A
, up to the specific values of the eigenvalues, provided that the eigenvalues which are distinct in one matrix remain distinct in the other ones. More precisely, if

{\rm RJCF}(A)=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}\left(\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d_{i}}C_{\ell_{j,i}}(\tilde{a}_{i},\tilde{b}_{i})\right)\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{s}\left(\bigoplus_{j=1}^{e_{i}}J_{k_{j,i}}(\tilde{c}_{i})\right),

(4)

with
\tilde{a}_{i},\tilde{b}_{i}>0,\tilde{c}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\tilde{c}_{i}\neq\tilde{c}_{i^{\prime}},\ (\tilde{a}_{i},\tilde{b}_{i})\neq(\tilde{a}_{i^{\prime}},\tilde{b}_{i^{\prime}})
, for
i\neq i^{\prime}
, then the real bundle of
A
is

{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbb{R}}(A):=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{i},\,b_{i}>0,\,c_{i}\,\in\,\mathbb{R},\\
c_{i}\neq c_{i^{\prime}},\,\,\mathrm{if}\,i\neq i^{\prime},\\
(a_{i},b_{i})\neq(a_{i^{\prime}},b_{i^{\prime}}),\,\,\mathrm{if}\,i\neq i^{\prime}\end{subarray}}{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}\left(\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d_{i}}C_{\ell_{j,i}}(a_{i},b_{i})\right)\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{s}\left(\bigoplus_{j=1}^{e_{i}}J_{k_{j,i}}(c_{i})\right)\right).

(5)

Following Arnold,
[
1
, §5.5]
, the codimension of the bundle
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
is defined as the difference between the codimension of the orbit and the number of different eigenvalues in any matrix of the bundle. More precisely, if RJCF(
A
) is as in (
4
), then

{\rm codim}_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)={\rm codim}_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)-(2r+s).

(6)

Since bundles under similarity are subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, the topological notions (like the
closure
or the openness) of bundles are considered in the standard (Euclidean) topology of
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. The closure of
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
will be denoted by
{\overline{\mathcal{B}}}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
.

3
The main result

In Theorem
3.2
we provide the generic RJCFs of real
n\times n
matrices. This is done by showing that
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the union of the closures of
\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1
bundles, which correspond to some particular RJCFs. These are the “generic” bundles (in each of these RJCFs the eigenvalues are different to each other and the number of real eigenvalues determines each RJCF). Moreover, we see that the intersection of the closure of any of the generic bundles with any other generic bundle is empty, which implies that none of these closures is included in any other. We also prove that the generic bundles are open sets. Note that this, in particular, implies that the union of these bundles is an open and dense set in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Finally, we show that the codimension of each of the generic bundles is equal to zero.

To prove Theorem
3.2
we will use the following technical lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
M\in{\overline{\mathcal{B}}}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}C(a_{i},b_{i})\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n-2t}[c_{i}]\right)
, for some
0\leq t\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor
, with
a_{i},b_{i},c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
,

(a)

M
has, at least,
n-2t
real eigenvalues, counting multiplicities, and

(b)

if
M
has more than
n-2t
real eigenvalues, counting multiplicities, then at least one of the eigenvalues of

is multiple.

Proof.

If
M\in{\overline{\mathcal{B}}}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}C(a_{i},b_{i})\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n-2t}[c_{i}]\right)

&\ddots&*&*&\cdots&*\\
&&T_{t,m}\\
&&&c_{1,m}&\ddots&\vdots\\
&&&&\ddots&*\\
0&&&&&c_{n-2t,m}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},

(7)

where

is real similar to

for

To prove (b), assume that

For this to happen, it must be

is a multiple (at least double) eigenvalue of

.
∎

t^{\prime}>t
, then part (b) in Lemma
3.1
implies that
A
has at least one multiple eigenvalue, which is not the case. Therefore, it must be
t^{\prime}=t
.

Let us now prove claim (iii). For simplicity, set
{\mathcal{B}}_{t}:={\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}C(a_{i},b_{i})\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n-2t}[c_{i}]\right)
, with
a_{i},b_{i}
, and
c_{i}
as in the statement, for
0\leq t\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor
. Let
M\in{\mathcal{B}}_{t_{0}}
, for some
0\leq t_{0}\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor
. We are going to see that there is some
\varepsilon>0
such that
B(M,\varepsilon)\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}_{t_{0}}
, where
B(M,\varepsilon)=\{A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}:\ \|M-A\|_{2}<\varepsilon\}
is the (open) ball of radius
\varepsilon
centered at
M
(and where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denotes the spectral norm, see, for instance,
[
6
, Example 5.6.6]
). First, by the continuity of the eigenvalues (see, for instance,
[
6
, Th. D.2]
), there is some
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that all matrices in
B(M,\varepsilon_{0})
have
n
different eigenvalues, and this implies that
B(M,\varepsilon_{0})\subseteq\bigcup_{t=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}{\mathcal{B}}_{t}
. Now, assume, by contradiction, that, for all
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
, the open ball
B(M,\varepsilon)
is not contained in
{\mathcal{B}}_{t_{0}}
, which implies, since all matrices in
B(M,\varepsilon)
have
n
different eigenvalues, that
B(M,\varepsilon)\cap\left(\bigcup_{t\neq t_{0}}{\mathcal{B}}_{t}\right)\neq\emptyset
. As a consequence,
M
belongs to the closure of
\bigcup_{t\neq t_{0}}{\mathcal{B}}_{t}
, which is equal to
\bigcup_{t\neq t_{0}}\overline{{\mathcal{B}}_{t}}
. Hence,
M\in{\mathcal{B}}_{t_{0}}\cap\overline{{\mathcal{B}}_{t}}
, for some
t\neq t_{0}
, which is in contradiction with part (ii).

Finally, let us prove claim (i) in the statement. For this, we strongly rely on the developments in
[
5
, Ch. VIII]
. More precisely, the dimension of the solution space of
XA-AX=0
depends on RJCF(
A
) and, for RJCF
(A)=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}C(a_{i},b_{i})\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n-2t}[c_{i}]
, with the parameters
a_{i},b_{i},
and
c_{i}
as in the statement, it is equal to

\sum_{i=1}^{t}\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}\,:\,XC(a_{i},b_{i})-C(a_{i},b_{i})X=0\}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-2t}\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\{x\in\mathbb{R}\,:\,xc_{i}-c_{i}x=0\}.

Since
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\{x\in\mathbb{R}\,:\,xc_{i}-c_{i}x=0\}=1
and, by a straightforward calculation,
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}\,:\,XC(a_{i},b_{i})-C(a_{i},b_{i})X=0\}=2
, we conclude from (
3
) that

{\rm codim}_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}C(a_{i},b_{i})\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n-2t}[c_{i}]\right)=2t+n-2t=n,

and, as a consequence of (
6
),
{\rm codim}_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}C(a_{i},b_{i})\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n-2t}[c_{i}]\right)=n-n=0
.
∎

% counts the number of real evals of m random nxn matrices
counter=zeros(m,1);
for i=1:m
a=randn(n);
e=eig(a);

end
