Prevalence and its associated factors of cancer-related fatigue among liver cancer patients: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Xuejiao Xiong, Qian Wang, Li Wang, Biying Fu, Min Tu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to identify the prevalence of CRF and its associated factors in liver cancer patients. A cross-sectional study was conducted at a major teaching hospital in Sichuan Province, China, between October and December 2025. A total of 315 liver cancer patients were recruited to complete a survey comprising the demographic and clinical information form, the Cancer Fatigue Scale (CFS), the FRAIL Scale, the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). Univariate analysis, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, and binary logistic regression were employed to determine the factors associated with CRF. Among 315 liver cancer patients, 206 (65.4%) participants were diagnosed with CRF. BCLC stage (A: OR = 2.050, 95% CI: 0.889–4.727, P = 0.092; B: OR = 2.921, 95% CI: 1.260–6.772, P = 0.012; C: OR = 5.247, 95% CI: 2.094–13.150, P < 0.001; D: OR = 7.334, 95% CI: 1.300-41.389, P = 0.024), chemotherapy history (OR = 2.202, 95% CI: 1.153–4.197, P = 0.017), physical frailty (OR = 3.343, 95% CI: 1.880–5.946, P < 0.001), depression (OR = 1.133, 95% CI: 1.063–1.208, P < 0.001), and the PSQI score (OR = 1.278, 95% CI: 1.172–1.394, P < 0.001) were identified as significantly associated factors of CRF in liver cancer patients in the binary logistic regression analysis. CRF was highly prevalent among liver cancer patients and was significantly associated with BCLC stage, chemotherapy history, physical frailty, depression, and PSQI score. Healthcare professionals should consider these critical factors when assessing and managing CRF in liver cancer patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54457-5.

Introduction

Liver cancer remains a major cause of death worldwide. It is estimated that there were 483,875 liver cancer-related deaths in 20211. Furthermore, liver cancer constitutes a significant global health challenge. From 2010 to 2021, the worldwide incidence and mortality rates of liver cancer increased by 26% and 25%, respectively, revealing an alarming upward trend that can’t be ignored2. Despite the growing array of treatment options for liver cancer, including surgical resection, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, those therapies prolong patient survival while also imposing a range of symptom burdens and treatment-related adverse reactions, substantially impairing their quality of life3,4.

Cancer-related fatigue (CRF) is one of the most common and distressing symptoms in liver cancer patients in terms of various outcomes reported by them5. A meta-analysis involving 84 original studies revealed that the global pooled prevalence of CRF among cancer patients was 52%6. CRF is generally defined as a distressed subjective experience associated with cancer or cancer treatment, encompassing physical, emotional, or cognitive exhaustion7,8. In addition, it is characterized by a sustained, multidimensional sense of weakness that significantly impairs daily functioning and health outcomes among cancer patients9. Specifically, earlier empirical evidence demonstrated that CRF is significantly associated with a variety of adverse health outcomes, including falls, mortality, and cardiovascular events10–12. These findings highlight the urgent need to identify CRF and deliver targeted interventions.

With increasing attention to the quality of life for cancer patients, CRF has gradually become a clinical and research priority. It is worth noting that although CRF is prevalent among cancer patients, reported prevalence rates vary considerably across studies due to differences in assessment tools, cultural contexts, tumor types, and clinical settings6. Current research on CRF has primarily focused on patient populations with lung cancer, gastric cancer, and breast cancer, while its prevalence among liver cancer patients remains understudied13–15. Furthermore, although several factors related to CRF in liver cancer patients, such as gender, chemotherapy, exercise frequency, and self-care, have been identified, comprehension of its multifaceted determinants remains incomplete6. In particular, the combined effects of various symptoms ( frailty, sleep quality, anxiety, depression) on the severity of fatigue in liver cancer patients have not been fully elucidated. A comprehensive assessment tailored to specific clinical contexts is critically indispensable to determine the prevalence of CRF among liver cancer patients and its associated factors, thereby providing crucial evidence for developing targeted fatigue management strategies and personalized interventions in the future. Therefore, this study aimed to identify the prevalence of CRF among liver cancer patients and its associated factors.

Methods

Study design, setting and participants

A cross-sectional design with convenience sampling was employed in this study. Participants were liver cancer patients recruited from a major teaching hospital in Sichuan Province, China, between October and December 2025. The inclusion criteria included: (1) diagnosed with liver cancer via pathological examination; (2) aged ≥ 18 years; (3) without significant communication barriers. The exclusion criteria were: (1) having a mental illness or cognitive impairment; (2) an anticipated survival estimated to be less than 3 months.

Given that the principal statistical method employed in this study was binary logistic regression, a minimum of 10 events per predictor variable is required to ensure reliable estimates14. This study initially selected 18 candidate predictors. Accounting for a potential attrition rate of 20%, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 216 participants.

Measurements

Demographic and clinical information form

The structured form includes fourteen demographic and clinical variables: gender, age, marital status, educational background, place of residence, work situation, income status, nature of cancer, Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage, Child-Pugh score, disease duration, chemotherapy history, and body mass index (BMI), nutritional risk.

Cancer fatigue scale (CFS)

The CFS was initially compiled by Okuyama16 and adapted into Chinese by Zhang17. The scale consists of 15 items across three dimensions: “physical fatigue (7 items),” “emotional fatigue (4 items),” and “cognitive fatigue (4 items).” The CFS is a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all, 5 = very much), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 60. The CRF was classified into four categories based on the total score: no fatigue (≤ 5), mild fatigue (6–15), moderate fatigue (16–30), and severe fatigue (31–60). In this study, the Cronbach’s α of CFS was 0.852.

FRAIL scale

The FRAIL scale, developed by the International Society of Nutrition and Aging18, is a validated instrument comprising five items 5-item for assessing physical frailty. It consists of five components: “fatigue,” “resistance (difficulty climbing stairs),” “ambulation (slow walking speed),” “illness (number of chronic conditions),” and “loss of weight (≥ 5% in the 6 months).” Each item is scored categorically (0 = no, 1 = yes), and a total score of ≥ 3 indicates a diagnosis of physical frailty. In the present study, the scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.736.

Hospital anxiety and depression scale (HADS)

The HADS, created by Zigmond and Snaith19, has been widely utilized to measure the levels of anxiety and depression in hospital patients. The scale comprises 14 items divided into two subscales: anxiety (7 items) and depression (7 items). Each item is rated on a Likert 4-point scale ranging from 0 to 3. In this study, the HADS exhibited good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α of 0.836 (total scale), 0.801 (anxiety subscale), and 0.813 (depression subscale).

Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI)

The PSQI, compiled by Buysse20, comprises 19 items that assess sleep quality across seven dimensions: “subjective sleep quality”, “sleep latency”, “sleep duration”, “sleep efficiency”, “sleep disturbances”, “use of sleep medication”, and “daytime dysfunction”. The total score, defined as the sum of all item scores (0–3 per item) across a 0–21, increases with poorer sleep quality. In this study, the Cronbach’s α of PSQI was 0.812.

Data collection

Three nurses were recruited and trained as investigators, responsible for distributing, collecting, and verifying the questionnaires. All participants were informed of the study’s purpose and significance, along with their right to withdraw voluntarily at any point. Clinical data were extracted from the electronic medical records, while the remaining self-reported sections were completed by the participants themselves via scanning the QR code on the Questionnaire Star platform. Moreover, we implemented a dual-review process in which two investigators independently checked 336 returned questionnaires for completeness. The exclusion of invalid responses resulted in 315 valid questionnaires, yielding an effective response rate of 93.8%.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS 27.0 and R 4.4.1. For statistical description, continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages. Chi-square tests and t-tests were used to examine differences between participants with and without CRF. To identify the most parsimonious factors from multiple candidate variables while handling potential multicollinearity, the LASSO regression was employed for variable selection. This approach shrinks regression coefficients and can set some coefficients exactly to zero, thereby selecting only the most informative variables and automatically handling multicollinearity. Although the LASSO regression is effective for variable selection and coefficient shrinkage, standard logistic regression provides conventional odds ratios (ORs) and confidence intervals (CIs), which are more interpretable. Therefore, after LASSO variable selection, a binary logistic regression was conducted to explore factors associated with CRF. This binary classification (CRF group and non-CRF group) was adopted for three reasons. First, even mild CRF is clinically meaningful and warrants attention or intervention. Second, the sample sizes for moderate (n = 34, 10.8%) and severe (n = 20, 6.3%) CRF were relatively small, limiting the feasibility of stable subgroup or ordinal analyses. Third, the binary definition of CRF has been commonly used in recent CRF studies. Multicollinearity among the variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with VIF > 5 considered indicative of significant collinearity. Model calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical consideration

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. It was approved by the Ethics Committee of First Affiliated Hospital of Chengdu Medical College (No. 2025CYFYIRB-BA-070). Informed consent was secured from all participants before the survey’s commencement, ensuring strict confidentiality of their personal data.

Prevalence of CRF among liver cancer patients

Among 315 liver cancer patients, a total of 206 participants were diagnosed with CRF, comprising 152 cases (48.3%) of mild CRF, 34 cases (10.8%) of moderate CRF, and 20 cases (6.3%) of severe CRF, while the remaining 109 cases (34.6%) exhibited no evidence of CRF. The associated factors of CRF among liver cancer patients

Univariate analysis (Table 1) was conducted to compare demographic and clinical characteristics between liver cancer patients with and without CRF. The results demonstrated that participants with CRF were significantly older and exhibited higher BCLC stage and Child-Pugh score, greater exposure to chemotherapy, primary liver cancer, physical frailty, nutritional risk, anxiety, and depression, and poor sleep quality (all P < 0.05). No significant differences were detected in gender, marital status, educational background, place of residence, work situation, income status, and disease duration (all P > 0.05).

LASSO regression was employed to select candidate factors. The findings are illustrated in Figs. 2 and 3, and Table 2. LASSO regression identified an optimal λ value of 0.018 and determined five predictors with non-zero regression coefficients: BCLC stage (0.413), chemotherapy history (0.288), physical frailty (0.594), depression (0.315), and the PSQI score (0.454).

Binary logistic regression was performed to determine the associated factors and their corresponding odds ratios for CRF in patients with liver cancer (Table 3). All VIF values < 5, indicating no significant collinearity. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test yielded a χ2 of 8.63 (p = 0.382), suggesting good calibration. BCLC stage (A: OR = 2.050, 95% CI: 0.889–4.727, P = 0.092; B: OR = 2.921, 95% CI: 1.260–6.772, P = 0.012; C: OR = 5.247, 95% CI: 2.094–13.150, P < 0.001; D: OR = 7.334, 95% CI: 1.300-41.389, P = 0.024), chemotherapy history (OR = 2.202, 95% CI: 1.153–4.197, P = 0.017), physical frailty (OR = 3.343, 95% CI: 1.880–5.946, P < 0.001), depression (OR = 1.133, 95% CI: 1.063–1.208, P < 0.001), and the PSQI score (OR = 1.278, 95% CI: 1.172–1.394, P < 0.001) were identified as significantly associated factors of CRF in liver cancer patients.

Discussion

Given metabolic disorders and toxin accumulation, patients with liver cancer are generally considered a susceptible population for CRF21,22. This study determined the prevalence of CRF among liver cancer patients and its associated factors. Specifically, CRF was identified as prevalent in this population and was significantly associated with multidimensional factors including age, nutritional risk, anxiety, depression, and sleep quality. These findings could contribute to the implementation of early symptom screening and targeted management strategies by clinical healthcare providers.

Our findings indicated that more than half of liver cancer patients exhibited CRF, which is consistent with previous research findings. The high prevalence of CRF in liver cancer patients could be attributed to the interaction of multiple biological, psychological, and social factors. Firstly, as the metabolic organ of the human body, the liver is responsible for the metabolism of proteins, carbohydrates, and fats23. Liver cancer disrupts these essential metabolic processes, leading to impaired energy production, which directly contributes to fatigue. Secondly, liver cancer patients suffer from severe symptom burden and are frequently distressed by emotional issues24. These negative emotions constitute a significant source of fatigue and mutually exacerbate physiological fatigue. Thirdly, the financial toxicity associated with the high costs of cancer treatment could exhaust patients, while the inability to work leads to a loss of self-worth, further exacerbating emotional fatigue25. These findings highlight the importance of screening for CRF among patients with liver cancer, as well as the need to train healthcare providers to improve their ability to assess CRF.

In this study, BCLC stage and chemotherapy history were found to be significantly associated with CRF in patients with liver cancer. Specifically, liver cancer patients with higher BCLC stage and greater exposure to chemotherapy are more likely to experience CRF. This association could be attributed to two primary pathways. Firstly, an advanced BCLC stage reflects a greater tumour burden and systemic inflammation, both of which are known to directly drive fatigue through mediated sickness behaviour and increased metabolic demand26. Secondly, chemotherapy induces fatigue through direct toxic effects on cellular metabolism and indirect consequences21. Consequently, the management of fatigue in advanced liver cancer patients requires increased attention to preserve their quality of life during the terminal phase.

In our study, physical frailty was observed to be associated with CRF in patients with liver cancer. Physical frailty is characterized by the loss of skeletal muscle mass and function, which directly impairs activity endurance and leads to physical exhaustion27. Furthermore, the chronic inflammation and metabolic disorder accompanying frailty, combined with liver cancer, contribute to an energy metabolism crisis26. As a result, the body struggles to convert nutrients into energy. Therefore, identifying interventions that mitigate physical frailty among liver cancer patients may serve as a critical breakthrough for alleviating CRF in the future.

Depression is one of the emotional disturbances generally experienced by cancer patients. This study found that liver cancer patients with depression had a higher odds of experiencing CRF. The relationship between depression and CRF has been extensively discussed in previous studies6. Resource Conservation Theory holds that an individual’s psychological resources are limited. When these resources are constantly depleted without replenishment, the individual may experience feelings of exhaustion28. Cancer is a debilitating chronic disease, and depression further exacerbates the consumption of patients’ psychological energy, directly leading to subjective fatigue29. Cognitive behavioral therapy is considered a prospective treatment for alleviating depression in cancer patients30. Therefore, healthcare providers should enhance the assessment of depression among liver cancer patients and implement positive psychological interventions to reduce their risk of suffering from CRF.

Our findings revealed that poorer sleep quality is significantly associated with CRF in patients with liver cancer. Sleep disorders have been determined to be prevalent among cancer patients, increasing their susceptibility to CRF. Specifically, sleep disorders induce circadian rhythm disruption in patients31. Circadian rhythm disruption is one of the characteristic responses to inflammation in the body, promoting neural inflammatory reactions and disrupting neurotransmitter release, which contributes to the onset of depressive moods32. These factors consequently increase the risk of developing CRF. These insights highlight the importance of incorporating routine sleep quality assessments and targeted interventions into the overall care of liver cancer patients. Improving sleep quality could be an effective strategy for reducing CRF.

Limitations

Some limitations should be considered. Firstly, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference regarding the relationships between the independent variables of interest in this study and CRF. Secondly, the external validity of our findings is limited by the sampling method. We used a convenience sampling strategy and recruited participants from a single center. This approach may introduce selection bias and reduce the generalizability of our results. Specifically, our sample may not be representative of liver cancer patients in other regions of China, other healthcare settings with different patient populations or treatment protocols, or other distributions of disease severity. Therefore, caution is needed when applying our conclusions to broader clinical contexts. Thirdly, potential conceptual overlap should be acknowledged between our outcome variable and some of the associated variables. Specifically, the FRAIL scale includes a “fatigue” component, the HADS contains items related to energy loss and tiredness, and the PSQI is closely linked to daytime fatigue. Therefore, the observed associations between these factors and CRF may partly reflect construct redundancy rather than independent causal pathways. Fourthly, although we measured CRF at three severity levels, we collapsed it into a binary outcome for analysis. This may result in loss of information regarding the gradation of fatigue severity and may also overlook the heterogeneous nature of CRF, as different fatigue subtypes could have distinct associated factor profiles. Therefore, latent class analysis could be employed in future studies to explore distinct CRF profiles and their associated factors23,33. Finally, the assessment of many variables, including fatigue, depression, sleep quality, and frailty, relied on self-reported scales. This may introduce recall bias, as participants might overestimate or underestimate their symptoms based on memory inaccuracies.

Conclusion

CRF was highly prevalent in liver cancer patients and was significantly associated with BCLC stage, chemotherapy history, physical frailty, depression, and sleep quality. Therefore, these critical factors should be considered together when assessing and managing the CRF in patients with liver cancer.
