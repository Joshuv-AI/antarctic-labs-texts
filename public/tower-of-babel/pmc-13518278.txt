Clinical Predictors of Tic‐Related Impairment in Children and Adults with Tourette Syndrome
Authors: Wm Jeptha Davenport, Davide Martino, Christos Ganos, Christelle Nilles, Tamara Pringsheim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTic‐related impairment drives treatment decisions for Tourette syndrome and related tic disorders. The clinical features most strongly associated with impairment remain unclear.ObjectiveThe aim of this analysis is to identify clinical predictors of tic‐related impairment in children and adults with tic disorders in a prospective longitudinal study.MethodsParticipants were drawn from the Calgary Tic Registries, undergoing prospective structured assessments. Baseline demographic and psychiatric data, psychotropic medication use, Yale Global Tic Severity Scale (YGTSS) dimension scores, and the number of different tics (NDT) were analyzed. Predictors of impairment were examined using Spearman's correlations, univariate associate screening with false discovery rate correction, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression with ordinary least squares refitting, and linear mixed‐effects (LME) models to evaluate within‐person changes over time.ResultsThe sample included 496 participants (848 total assessments). Motor tic interference showed the strongest correlation with impairment (ρ = 0.50), followed by motor tic intensity and frequency. The NDTs correlated with impairment more strongly than most individual YGTSS dimensions. In LASSO regression, seven predictors remained significant: motor tic interference, frequency and complexity, phonic tic interference, older age, female sex, and absence of autism. The LME models analyzed data from all participants with at least one follow‐up visit. LME models showed that increases in total tic score predicted higher impairment and that impairment decreased modestly over time.DiscussionMotor tic interference is the most robust predictor of tic‐related impairment. Older age and female sex further contribute to functional burden. © 2026 The Author(s). Tourette syndrome (TS) is a childhood‐onset neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by the presence of multiple motor and at least one vocal tic for more than a year. 1 There are several closely related tic disorders, including persistent motor tic disorder (PMTD), persistent vocal tic disorder (PVTD), and provisional tic disorder (PTD). People with tic disorders experience high rates of comorbid conditions, including attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD), generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), and major depressive disorder (MDD). 2 The Yale Global Tic Severity Scale (YGTSS) is a clinical rating scale of tic severity and tic‐related impairment and is frequently used as an outcome in randomized controlled trials of treatments for tics. 3 , 4 The YGTSS consists of a total tic severity score, which measures number, frequency, intensity, complexity, and interference of motor and vocal tics, and a tic‐related impairment score, which measures the impact of tics on self‐esteem, family life, social acceptance, or school or job functioning, in reference to the past week.

Although clinical trials typically choose the total tic severity score on the YGTSS as the primary outcome, in clinical practice, tic‐related impairment is often the main factor driving treatment decisions. People with tics experiencing minimal impairment rarely pursue pharmacological interventions due to possible long‐term adverse effects. Although the YGTSS total tic score and tic‐related impairment score show positive correlations, 3 tic‐related impairment ratings may be driven by other clinical or individual factors, with some affected individuals reporting marked or severe tic‐related impairment despite minimal scores on the YGTSS total tic severity scale, or vice versa. A reduction in the YGTSS total tic severity score of 25% has been the recommended minimal clinically important difference to define clinically meaningful improvement, 5 , 6 yet up to 11% of individuals with TS with at least this level of symptom improvement are not rated as much improved or very much improved on the Clinical Global Impression–Improvement Scale. 5 This suggests that tic severity is not the only important predictor of global functioning in people with TS.

The aim of this study is to determine the main clinical factors associated with tic‐related impairment ratings on the YGTSS in children and adults with TS, PMTD, PVTD, and PTD. Are there specific dimensions on the YGTSS or other clinical factors that have a high correlation with tic‐related impairment scores? Could the presence of such risk factors for tic‐related impairment help guide treatment decisions?

Participants and Methods

This study included participants from the Calgary Child and Adult Tic Registries, two prospective longitudinal studies conducted at the University of Calgary, Canada. The study was approved by the Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board (REB16‐1116, REB20‐1643), and informed consent was obtained from all participants or guardians. Only participants with TS, PMTD, PVTD, and PTD were included in the analysis. Registry participants undergo a diagnostic evaluation at baseline and take part in three assessments of tic severity and tic‐related impairment over a period of 1 year, with pediatric participants undergoing a fourth assessment after their 18th birthday. At baseline, demographic information (sex, age), psychiatric comorbidities (ADHD, OCD, GAD, MDD, autism, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, substance use disorder, personality disorder), and current psychotropic medication by class (α agonists, antipsychotics, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors [SSRI], psychostimulants) are recorded. Diagnoses are made based on the Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM‐5) criteria by the neurologist assessing the participant, or confirmed through diagnostic reports from other physicians involved in participant care. Assessments of tic severity and tic‐related impairment include the YGTSS and the associated motor and phonic tic symptom checklists. Most assessments are performed by the same treating neurologist who administers the YGTSS during routine clinical visits or otherwise by a clinical research nurse (who underwent rater training with the senior author) during research visits. The YGTSS is completed on paper and then transferred to the database. Baseline YGTSS scores are not reviewed prior to reassessment. Using the YGTSS tic symptom checklists, we determined the number of different tics (NDT) each participant had at each visit, including by category: simple motor, complex motor, simple vocal, and complex vocal. For example, if a participant reported and/or the clinician observed eye blinking, eye movements, copropraxia, throat clearing, and echolalia, the total NDTs was 5. For the YGTSS rating scale, we recorded the score on each dimension and each subscale score (total motor tic severity, total vocal tic severity, total tic severity, impairment) at each visit. The impairment dimension relates to the association of tics with difficulties in self‐esteem, family life, social acceptance, or school or job functioning, rather than comorbid conditions.

Data Analysis

All analyses were conducted using R (version 2024.09.1+394) 7 within the RStudio environment. 8 The cleaned dataset included dimension‐level and subscale scores from the YGTSS, along with demographic and diagnostic information.

For the current study, analyses were conducted on baseline assessments for descriptive and correlation studies and on repeat assessments for the linear mixed‐effects (LME) models. The full dataset included 848 observations, of which 496 represented baseline visits and were used for primary analyses. Based on the age range of all participants (4–72 years), age was binned into three categories (4–11, 12–17, and 18–72 years) to reflect childhood, adolescence, and adulthood, respectively. Sex was reported as male or female.

Spearman's Correlations

To understand if the analysis of the original psychometric testing of the YGTSS performed by Leckman has remained consistent over the past three decades, we repeated the correlation analysis between YGTSS dimensions and impairment scores with our dataset. Correlations between individual YGTSS dimensions and impairment scores were computed using Spearman's correlation. All correlation tests used pairwise‐complete observations. r2 (variance explained) and P‐values were reported.

To understand the relationship between the NDTs present and impairment, correlations between NDTs on the YGTSS symptom checklist and the YGTSS impairment score were computed using Spearman's correlation. NDTs were measured in seven overlapping subsets: total NDTs (motor and vocal), total motor NDTs (simple or complex), or total vocal NDTs (simple or complex). All correlation tests used pairwise‐complete observations. r2 (variance explained) and P‐values were reported.

Univariate Association Screen

To identify individual predictors with strong associations to YGTSS impairment, we conducted screens according to predictor type, using baseline visits only (n = 496). We excluded identifier, date fields, and the composite YGTSS global tic score (as this score includes a summation with our target value, YGTSS impairment). For continuous predictors and binary indicators, we used a simple linear model (lm) of the form impairment i = α + βx i + ε i . We report β, which represents a slope or change in impairment per 1‐unit increase in the predictor; 95% confidence interval; two‐sided P‐value; and R 2, the proportion of variance in the impairment score explained by the unique predictor alone. Pairwise‐complete cases were used and varied by predictor in number (n used). Because many predictors were tested, we chose to adjust P‐values using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to address the false discovery rate (FDR) and report FDR‐adjusted P‐values, with FDR <0.05 considered significant. 9 All tests were two sided with α = 0.05. Predictors were analyzed on their original scales.

Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator Regression Predicting Tic‐Related Impairment

To identify the strongest predictors of YGTSS impairment, we conducted least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression using baseline data. The model included 24 candidate predictors. These were numeric YGTSS dimension and subscale scores, age at time of enrollment (in years), sex (encoded as male = 1, female = 0), binary indicators for comorbidity (ADHD, OCD, MDD, GAD, and autism) and binary indicators for psychotropic medication use (α agonists, antipsychotics, SSRIs, and psychostimulants). Four predictors were excluded due to low prevalence (<5): schizophrenia, personality disorder, bipolar disorder, and substance use. The outcome variable was the YGTSS impairment score. A 10‐fold cross‐validation procedure selected the optimal penalty parameter (λ), and predictors with nonzero coefficients at this value were retained. To assess the statistical significance of the LASSO regression, which may bias coefficients downward, an ordinary least squares (OLS) model using the selected predictors was refit.

LME Models

To model longitudinal change in impairment, we considered whether to include participant‐specific random slopes for time in addition to random intercepts. An initial model, including random intercepts and random slopes for months since baseline, provided a marginally better fit than an intercept‐only model (likelihood‐ratio test: χ 2(2) = 4.83, P = 0.089), but the random‐slopes model failed to converge fully. Because nonconvergent models cannot be interpreted reliably, and because the fixed‐effect estimates were nearly identical across the two specifications, we retained the simpler random‐intercept model for all subsequent analyses of impairment over time.

We examined how functional impairment changes over time, independent of tic severity. To do this, we fit a “time‐only” LME model in which the YGTSS impairment score was regressed on months since baseline, with a random intercept for each participant. This model included all individuals with a baseline visit and at least one follow‐up, and it estimates the average longitudinal trajectory of impairment while accounting for repeated measurements within participants.

We then examined whether tic severity explains additional variability in impairment beyond the effect of time. The same random‐effects structure was evaluated before fitting the model that included tic severity; because fixed‐effect estimates and model diagnostics were similar and the slopes model again exhibited instability, the random‐intercept specification was retained for both analyses. For this analysis, we fit a second LME in which YGTSS impairment score was regressed on both YGTSS total tic score and months since baseline, again with participant‐level random intercepts. This model tests whether higher tic severity predicts higher functional impairment after adjusting for the longitudinal trend.

Locally Estimated Scatterplot Smoothing Curves

To visualize age and sex effects on tics and impairment, scatterplots were constructed, with age at enrollment treated as a continuous variable and separate locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) curves for males and females plotted against YGTSS impairment, YGTSS total tic score, and NDTs. In each plot, the shaded areas represent pointwise confidence bands.

Mean total tic severity on the YGTSS was 19.4/50, with a mean impairment score of 17.5/50. ADHD was the most common comorbid condition, followed by GAD. LASSO Regression Predicting Impairment

Sixteen (of 24) predictors were retained by the penalized model after elimination of predictors whose coefficients had shrunk to zero (see Fig. 1). Using LASSO for variable selection followed by OLS refitting helps address the shrinkage bias introduced in LASSO coefficients. 10 We therefore assessed statistical significance by refitting an OLS model on the 16 predictors retained by LASSO. Seven significant predictors (P < 0.05) were found: motor tic interference, age at time of enrollment, motor tic frequency, phonic tic interference, motor tic complexity, female sex, and absence of autism (see Table 5). LME Models

The LME models analyzed data from all participants with at least one follow‐up visit (n = 229 participants, 566 visits, range: 2–4 visits, median: 2 visits). Across participants impairment tended to decrease over time (Fig. 2). In the “time‐only” LME, months since baseline was a significant negative predictor of YGTSS impairment score (β = −0.23, standard error [SE] = 0.04, t = −53.87, P < 0.001). This indicates that, on average, impairment decreased by ~0.23 points per month, although individual trajectories varied widely, showing patterns ranging from marked improvement to relative stability or fluctuation over time; in 1 year (12 months), the expected change is ~−2.8 points.

YGTSS (Yale Global Tic Severity Scale) impairment trajectories over time. [Color figure can be viewed at wileyonlinelibrary.com]

The second LME included both YGTSS total tic score and months since baseline. Tic severity was a strong positive predictor of impairment (β = 0.71, SE = 0.05, t = 13.92, P < 0.0001), indicating that each 1‐point increase in tic score was associated with a 0.71‐point increase in impairment, on average. Variation with months since baseline remained significant (β = −0.14, SE = 0.04, t = −3.97, P < 0.001), reflecting modest longitudinal improvement even after accounting for tic burden. The strength and consistency of the tic severity effect are visible in the unadjusted longitudinal relationship between tic score and impairment (see Fig. 3), where higher tic severity is associated with higher impairment scores across participants and across visits. The statistical model included months since baseline as a covariate to adjust for longitudinal trends. LOESS Curves

The LOESS curves (see Figs. [Link], [Link]) are descriptive and illustrate patterns consistent with the analyses reported earlier. All three curves show increasing impairment, tic severity, and NDTs in the first two decades of life in males and females. In males, there is a downward slope in impairment, tic severity, and NDTs after age 20, whereas in females, there is stability in NDTs, and an upward slope in tic severity and impairment after age 20.

Discussion

This prospective cohort study of children, youth, and adults with TS and related tic disorders found that the YGTSS motor tic interference dimension score had the strongest correlation with tic‐related impairment, followed by the motor tic intensity dimension score. The NDTs on YGTSS inventory had a higher correlation with tic‐related impairment than the remaining dimension scores. Older age at time of enrollment and female sex emerged as important clinical predictors of tic‐related impairment. The strengths of this study include the large number of participants, reflecting the full spectrum of disease severity, who were prospectively and systematically assessed using identical procedures. Limitations of the study are its single‐center design and varying number of follow‐up assessments of tic severity per participant depending on time of entry into the cohort. Due to the variable methods used to assess symptom severity of comorbid conditions, only the presence but not the severity of comorbidity could be included in our analyses.

The initial testing of the YGTSS was performed by Leckman and colleagues on a sample of 105 children and adults with tic disorders between 1987 and 1988. 3 Mean YGTSS total tic score in the sample was 21.9 (standard deviation [SD] 8.7), with a mean impairment score of 22.9 (SD 10.2). This study calculated Pearson's r correlation scores between YGTSS dimension scores and overall impairment and found that motor tic number had the highest correlation (0.64), followed by motor tic interference (0.63) and motor tic complexity (0.61). In comparison, our analysis found that motor tic interference had the highest correlation with impairment, followed by motor tic intensity and motor tic frequency, bearing in mind that we used the revised version of the YGTSS. The YGTSS‐R has minor changes in the anchors for frequency, complexity, and interference, to promote use of the full range of scores in each dimension. 4 At the time of their initial testing, Leckman stated that the value of the separate ratings for each of the elements of tic severity had not been firmly established, and that some elements, such as frequency or intensity, may be more physiologically meaningful than other dimensions. 3 Our analysis suggests that although the number of tics on the scale matters, the number of interruptions caused by tics is the most important determinant of impairment. Furthermore, a simple tally of the total number of tics on the YGTSS inventory in the past week also appears to correlate with impairment at a similar magnitude as the YGTSS number dimension and may be a simple method of quantifying tics and associated impairment.

Our results could also mean that interference and impairment are measuring a similar construct and are interpreted by clinicians and patients to mean the same thing. Considering directly the scale dimensions and how these are phrased, this seems less likely. The interference dimension measures how frequently tics, when present, interrupt the flow of behavior or speech, whereas the impairment dimension measures how the presence of tics impacts self‐esteem, family life, social acceptance, or school/job functioning. An evaluation of the factor structure of the YGTSS in the European Multicentre Tics in Children Study found that the loadings of the overall impairment rating were low, suggesting that impairment does not sufficiently fit the other dimensions and probably does not measure the same construct as the other dimensions. 11

Our LASSO regression found a significant effect for age, with increasing age associated with increasing impairment. Comparative data on tic‐related impairment across the lifespan are limited. Clinic‐ and community‐based studies using common measures demonstrate mild‐to‐moderate functional impairment in both youth and adults with chronic tic disorders, with impairment generally following tic severity in both groups. 12 , 13 , 14 However, in childhood, disability is often driven primarily by comorbid ADHD and OCD, particularly in academic and family domains, 12 , 15 , 16 whereas in adults whose tics persist, tic severity itself—together with mood and anxiety symptoms—is more strongly associated with social and occupational impairment. 13 , 15 , 17 In a comparative study of children to adults with TS, despite similar tic phenomenology and severity between groups, adults were significantly more likely to receive treatment for tics, suggesting greater tic‐related impairment among adults despite similar tic severity. 14

In univariate models, depression and SSRI use were strongly associated with impairment. However, when age was included in multivariable models, the effects of depression and SSRI use were attenuated and no longer statistically significant. Variance inflation factors were low (<1.4), indicating that this pattern was not due to multicollinearity; rather, age accounted for variance in impairment that overlapped with variance captured by depression and SSRI use, reducing their unique contributions to the multivariable model. The simplest explanation is that over time, with cumulative symptom burden, there is more chance to develop depression, and the older a person is, the more time (risk) he or she has been exposed to an SSRI. Comorbid depression may affect perceptions of tic‐related impairment in people with TS. Cognitive therapy addressing negative biases and depression‐related cognitive distortions regarding tics may help address this issue. 18 Earlier studies have also found an important moderating role of depression on impairment. In a study of 85 children with tics evaluated at a tertiary referral center, Feldman Sadeh and colleagues found that depressive symptoms on the Child Depression Inventory had a moderating role that strengthened the adverse impact of tic severity on tic‐related functional impairment on the YGTSS. 19 Lewin and colleagues found that depressive symptoms on the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale moderated the relationship between self‐reported tic severity and functional impairment on the Sheehan Disability Scale in a nonclinical sample of 500 adults with tics completing an online survey. 20

Our analysis found a significant association between biological sex and tic‐related impairment, with female sex associated with higher impairment. This association may be confounded by the higher lifetime prevalence of mood disorders in females with TS. 2 Although females remain the minority of individuals with tics, this finding suggests that greater attention is needed on the treatment and psychosocial needs of females with TS. Previous studies have found that females report greater tic‐related impairment than males, despite having similar ratings of tic severity. 21 , 22 A qualitative study of self‐identity and psychological well‐being in young women with TS found difficulties with self‐acceptance that were intensified by participants' perceptions of stereotypical gender roles. 23

The presence of an autism diagnosis was associated with lower tic‐related impairment in our sample. Estimates of the prevalence of tics in individuals with autism vary but generally fall in the range of 10% to 20%. 24 Previous studies have suggested that tic severity among individuals with autism may fall in the mild to moderate range. 25 , 26 Canitano and Vivanti found that tic‐related impairment scores were mostly determined by adaptive difficulties that tics created in daily life and to a lesser extent the social environment. 26 Kahl reported that although tic phenomenology between individuals with and without autism in their sample was similar, individuals with autism were less aware of their tics. They postulated that this may relate to a deficit in self‐referential cognitive processing or altered perception of the social environment. All registry participants recruited to our study were referred specifically for evaluation and treatment of tics. Although autism severity was not documented among our registry participants, the vast majority were high functioning and able to communicate independently. Our finding of lower impairment among individuals with autism suggests further exploration of impairment in this population is warranted using qualitative and quantitative methods.

Reassuringly, tic‐related impairment improved with prospective longitudinal follow‐up, suggesting that continued clinical involvement with a specialist improves quality of life over the long term, possibly related to treatment or psychosocial support.

Sir William Osler urged doctors to “care more particularly for the individual patient than for the special features of the disease.” 27 This sage advice remains relevant today in our approach to providing care for people with TS. In addition to age, sex, and comorbidity, clinicians should consider the number of individual tics, motor tic interference, and motor tic intensity as possible predictors of tic‐related impairment in children, youth, and adults with TS. Clinicians and researchers must periodically reevaluate and refine measurement tools for tics and tic‐related impairment to ensure these assessments meaningfully guide clinical decision‐making.
