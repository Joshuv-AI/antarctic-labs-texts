Rational corticosteroids administration and antibiotic treatment is key to managing cutaneous anthrax
Authors: Luo Xia, Zhou Yan, Liu Shuai Wei, Liu Huijuan, Jiang Cai Hong, Ma Li Na, Ding Xiang Chun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnthrax is a global health concern, with cutaneous anthrax accounting for over 95% of cases and generally promising outcomes. Nonetheless, the absence of timely intervention can result in mortality rates of 10–40%. This research aims to explore the clinical presentations and phenotypic characteristics of cutaneous anthrax patients and evaluate the efficacy of various therapeutic approaches.MethodsA retrospective study was performed on 76 cutaneous anthrax patients identified at three hospitals from 2017 to 2022. Patients were categorized based on their hospital stay into two groups: those hospitalized for at least seven days and those for shorter durations. We assessed their clinical and phenotypic profiles, including symptoms, general health status, and laboratory findings, alongside treatment outcomes, focusing on corticosteroids therapy and antibiotic regimens.ResultsThe study encompassed 76 diagnosed individuals, predominantly young adult males (78.9%). A significant gender disparity was noted. Hormonal treatment markedly improved edema regression in patients (P < 0.002), highlighting its therapeutic value. The impact of various antibiotic treatments on disease progression differed significantly based on corticosteroids treatment status, with specific combinations showing more effectiveness in non-corticosteroids-treated patients.ConclusionsThe predominance of young male adults among cutaneous anthrax cases was observed, with corticosteroids treatment significantly reducing edema duration. In cases where corticosteroids therapy is not utilized, employing piperacillin-tazobactam alone or in combination with quinolones effectively shortens the illness duration, suggesting a tailored approach to treatment can enhance patient outcomes.

Introduction

Anthrax is an infectious disease caused by Bacillus anthracis, which can be transmitted from herbivorous animals to humans. The disease can be transmitted through various methods, such as contact, ingestion, inhalation, or injection [1]. Globally, around 2,000 cases of anthrax are reported annually, with sporadic cases occurring in Europe, North America, and Australia. However, most cases in the last few years have been reported in Asia, including eastern India, South Korea, the Philippines, Mongolia, and the mountainous areas of western China [1–3]. In China, anthrax mainly occurs in the western and northern regions; Sichuan, Gansu, Qinghai, Xinjiang, and Inner Mongolia were found to have the highest incidence rates in recent years [4]. According to the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, 193–392 cases of anthrax have been reported annually since 2011, with an incidence rate ranging from 0.015/100,000 to 0.030/100,000 and a mortality rate of 0.3–1.2%. Although anthrax cases are relatively rare, they pose a significant threat to public health. For example, the anthrax leak in Sverdlovsk, Russia, in 1979 caused the death of around 100 people [5], while the 2001 anthrax terrorist attack in the United States affected nearly 30,000 people, resulting in five deaths and 17 infections [6, 7]. Therefore, further research is needed on anthrax, and prevention measures need to be improved to protect public health.

Anthrax has different clinical presentations based on the route of infection and bacterial virulence. It can be classified into five main clinical types: cutaneous anthrax, pulmonary anthrax, gastrointestinal anthrax, anthrax sepsis, and anthrax meningitis [8–11]. Cutaneous anthrax is the most prevalent type, accounting for over 95% of all anthrax cases [11, 12]. The initial symptoms of cutaneous anthrax include rash, papules, and blisters of unknown origin on exposed areas like the hands, forearms, face, and neck. The surrounding tissue becomes swollen and infiltrated, and central necrosis gives rise to an ulcerative black eschar. The skin surrounding the eschar becomes red, swollen, and slightly itchy but not significantly painful. Typical skin lesions include shallow ulcers with black eschar and small blisters on the periphery, accompanied by extensive non-depressed edema of nearby tissues. Besides getting skin lesions, patients often experience symptoms such as fever, headache, joint pain, malaise, and local lymph node and spleen enlargement. In severe cases, extensive edema and necrosis may occur in the affected area, which, in the absence of appropriate treatment, can lead to secondary pulmonary anthrax, anthrax sepsis, anthrax meningitis, etc. The mortality rate for anthrax can be as high as 10–40%; however, pulmonary anthrax may have a mortality rate as high as 85% [9–12]. Therefore, early diagnosis and appropriate treatment of cutaneous anthrax are crucial for a favorable prognosis.

According to the recommendations of the World Health Organization (WHO), penicillin G is the preferred drug for treating anthrax [13]. However, studies have shown that although Bacillus anthracis is highly susceptible to penicillin in laboratories, patients with high levels of the bacteria, such as those with inhalational anthrax, may develop penicillin resistance due to the production of beta-lactamase [14–17]. Therefore, some researchers suggested that quinolone antibiotics, such as ciprofloxacin or doxycycline, might be more appropriate initial treatments for patients before drug sensitivity results are available [15–17]. Additionally, cephalosporins, aminoglycoside antibiotics, and carbapenems are also effective against B. anthracis [15–17]. For patients with cutaneous anthrax who have severe edema, incision and drainage should be avoided, and tracheotomy should be performed with caution [16, 17]. A dministration of corticosteroids might help reduce inflammatory edema in patients with severe toxemia symptoms or malignant edema [17]. However, further studies are needed to confirm the effectiveness of combining antibiotics and glucocorticoids to treat anthrax. To investigate the effects of different antibiotic therapeutic regimens and glucocorticoids on the prognosis of patients with cutaneous anthrax, we retrospectively analyzed the clinical characteristics and treatment of 76 patients diagnosed with cutaneous anthrax who were admitted to the hospital between 2018 and 2022.

Materials & methods

Criteria for diagnosis, inclusion, and exclusion

In this study, 76 patients admitted to three municipal hospitals in Ningxia were included. The patients exhibited epidemiological characteristics and clinical symptoms that were consistent with cutaneous anthrax, such as fever, maculopapular rash, blistering, skin scabbing, and localized skin edema. Microscopic examinations of skin secretions, sputum, vomit, feces, blood, cerebrospinal fluid, and bacterial isolation and culture confirmed the presence of Gram-positive bacilli (Bacillus anthracis) in all patients. Patients diagnosed with other diseases were excluded from the study. All patients have no underlying diseases and no history of using immunosuppressants. The endpoint of the study was either patient death or hospital discharge.

Clinical data collection

In this study, we collected comprehensive medical records and test results of patients, which included the general condition of the patients, routine blood tests, biochemical tests, blood culture, secretion culture, Bacillus anthracis PCR test, clinical symptoms (such as temperature, edema, pruritus, pain, and site of onset), treatment details (including whether corticosteroids were used and the type of antibiotics administered), and the length of hospitalization. The method of using glucocorticoids is to administer 10 mg intravenously daily for 3 consecutive days. This project was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University, and all participants or their guardians provided informed consent before participation.

Blood routine and serum biochemical tests [18]

After fasting overnight, 3–5 mL of venous blood was collected from the elbow vein of all patients using an ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid dipotassium anticoagulation vacuum blood collection tube. The samples were sent to the Laboratory Department within 2 h. Using the Japanese Sysmex Corporation XN-9000 hematology analyzer, the platelet count, lymphocyte count, neutrophil count, and monocyte count of the patient were determined. The cell count was reported as 109/L. Additionally, venous blood was collected from the patient using vacuum-dried blood collection tubes, and serum indicators, such as alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bilirubin, direct bilirubin, indirect bilirubin, total protein, albumin, and chloride, were measured using a Siemens ADVIA 2400 automatic biochemical analyzer.

Blood culture

To prepare for blood collection, the puncture site on the skin was disinfected using iodine tincture. Then, blood was collected from the veins in both upper limbs using a “double vial and double side” technique. The specimens were carefully inoculated into culture bottles, gently mixed to prevent clotting, and sent to the laboratory within 2 h for analysis. The BacT/ALERT VIRTUO automated blood culture system from bioMérieux (France) was used to test the specimens, and the results were recorded for further analysis.

PCR test for patients with cutaneous anthrax

The blood samples and secretions from the skin lesions of the patients were collected and sent to the Ningxia Center for Disease Control and Prevention for Bacillus anthracis PCR testing. The test data were recorded for further analysis.

Patient grouping

After analyzing the treatment duration for the patients diagnosed with cutaneous anthrax in the field of infectious diseases, the study population was stratified into two subgroups: those who were hospitalized for at least seven days and those who were hospitalized for less than seven days [18].

Disease severity grading [19]

Mild: Skin lesions had a diameter of less than 4 cm, with narrow erythema around them and no systemic symptoms.

Moderate: Skin lesions had a diameter of 2–4 cm, with papular and edematous reactions around them, and with or without systemic symptoms, such as fever, tachycardia, and dyspnea.

Severe: The edema and inflammation around the skin lesions were extensive, accompanied by severe systemic symptoms or skin lesions with a diameter greater than 4 cm, accompanied by high fever, toxicity or sepsis, and involvement of any organ.

Fever classification and criteria

We classified 37.3 °C to 38 °C as low fever, 38.1 °C to 39 °C as moderate fever, 39.1 °C to 41 °C as high fever; and 41 °C or more as super high fever.

Statistical analysis

We used SPSS 23.0 (SPSS Inc., USA) to statistically analyze the data. For the data that followed a normal distribution, the results were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The differences in the rates between multiple groups were determined by conducting the χ2 test. We calculated two-tailed p-values and considered P < 0.05 to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Discussion

Cutaneous anthrax is a zoonotic infection caused by the bacteria Bacillus anthracis, which is responsible for over 95% of all anthrax cases [11]. In China, the growth of the livestock industry and the rise in cattle and sheep farming have led to an increase in localized outbreaks of cutaneous anthrax, causing panic among people and imposing a significant burden on socioeconomic development [20–23]. Thus, the characteristics of concentrated cutaneous anthrax cases in endemic regions need to be analyzed, and effective treatment strategies need to be investigated for the diagnosis and management of this disease.

The World Health Organization recommends a 7–10 days antibiotic treatment duration for cutaneous anthrax [13]. However, in this study conducted with 76 patients, the average hospital stay was 40.5 days, which was considerably longer than the recommended treatment duration. We considered longer hospital stays to indicate poorer outcomes and slower recovery, regardless of external factors, such as economic conditions. To better understand the factors affecting treatment outcomes, we divided patients into two groups based on the duration of hospitalization: those with seven or more days (group A) and those with less than seven days (group B). We analyzed the clinical phenotypes of both groups and found that only gender was statistically significant (χ2 = 5.709, P = 0.017), with more male patients than female patients in both groups (male: female = 2.16:1), which was similar to the results of another study [24]. Men are affected more often, probably because they are mainly responsible for animal husbandry, slaughter, and processing in pastoral areas, where cutaneous anthrax is endemic. We recommend annual vaccination of key populations, such as herders, veterinarians, livestock slaughterers, fur processors, and those participating in prevention and control programs. Additionally, health education and operational training should be conducted to improve awareness and emergency response capabilities for all personnel.

Edema is the primary clinical manifestation of cutaneous anthrax, and its duration determines the length of hospital stay [3, 10–12]. In this study, 76 patients were evaluated, 55 of whom had moderate or greater perifocal edema, and 35 patients also experienced significant systemic toxic symptoms, such as fever. This patient group was treated with corticosteroids (dexamethasone 10 mg intravenous push) for three days. The results of statistical analysis indicated that edema was resolved within 14 days in 80% of patients and within seven days for 37% of patients who received corticosteroids therapy. In contrast, patients who were not administered corticosteroids therapy had a prolonged time to the resolution of edema; only 20% of cases were resolved within seven days, and over 50% of patients had edema lasting longer than 14 days. Our results showed that administering corticosteroids accelerated the regression of edema at the site of infection, leading to a shorter hospital stay and promote the rehabilitation of patients.

Bacillus anthracis is a Gram-positive bacterium whose exotoxins increase the permeability of capillary walls, causing local edema [1]. An uncontrolled release of pro-inflammatory Cytokines was hypothesized to contribute to the cardiovascular collapse during anthrax in a manner similar to endotoxin-mediated sepsis. Presumably, this relates to macrophage death induced by B. anthracis and anthrax toxin with consequent release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Inflammation as manifested by necrosis and increased peripheral WBC is often present in affected patients and animal models of anthrax. Increased levels of IL-1beta and IL-6 have been found in some, but not all murine strains experimentally infected with B. anthracis [25]. Likewise, an increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines has been described following intravenous injection of B. anthracis in non-human primate model of anthrax [26]. LeTx has also been reported to cause a release of IL-1 beta and TNF- alpha by murine macrophages in vitro [27–29]. A role for cytokine storm in the pathogenesis of anthrax is also supported by the observation that TNF receptor 1, IL-1 receptor and iNOS deficient mice are less susceptible to infection than wild type mice [30]. Glucocorticoids (GCS) are steroid corticosteroids secreted by the adrenal cortex [31]. Inflammation as manifested by necrosis and increased peripheral WBC is often present in affected patients and animal models of anthrax GCS can produce strong pharmacological effects. GCS inhibits the synthesis and secretion of inflammatory factors such as IL-1, IL-3, IL-2, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, TNFα, GM-CSF, and NO, while inducing the synthesis of anti-inflammatory factors such as quercetin, ACE, inflammatory proteins, and IL-10. GCS also induces the apoptosis of inflammatory cells, constricts blood vessels, and inhibits the release of proteolytic enzymes, as well as, the recruitment and phagocytosis of monocytes, neutrophils, and MΦ at the site of inflammation, exerting its rapid and potent anti-inflammatory effects. In the early stages of inflammation, GCS can relieve symptoms by inhibiting capillary dilation, reducing exudation and edema, and suppressing leukocyte infiltration and phagocytosis [31]. However, in some studies, glucocorticoids were not routinely used for cutaneous anthrax. This might be because most patients with mild conditions can be treated with effective antibiotics alone; although glucocorticoids inhibit inflammation and relieve symptoms, they also lower the body’s defense function, leading to the spread of inflammation and worsening of the original condition.

Bacillus anthracis is susceptible to penicillin and other β-lactam-containing compounds, making penicillin G the drug of choice for treating naturally occurring anthrax [17]. However, resistance to penicillin, gentamicin, chloramphenicol, and ampicillin has been observed in clinical and non-clinical isolates of B-anthrax [17]. In certain anthrax-endemic areas, a percentage of carcasses and soil isolates were found to be resistant to penicillin G. Resistance to β-lactam drugs is usually mediated by producing one or more β-lactamases, which inactivate the antimicrobial drug. Quinolone antibiotics are recommended as the initial treatment regimen for anthrax until the results of antimicrobial drug sensitization and β-lactamase tests are obtained [28]. In our study, different antibiotic treatments significantly affected the length of stay for patients who did not use corticosteroids, and the use of piperacillin-tazobactam alone resulted in a shorter length of stay for 80% of patients. The combination of piperacillin-tazobactam and levofloxacin also resulted in a shorter length of stay for patients who were not administered corticosteroids, with 66.7% of patients being discharged from the hospital within seven days. In contrast, administering penicillin alone resulted in a shorter length of stay for only 38.5% of patients, and administering penicillin combined with levofloxacin resulted in a shorter length of stay for only 12.5% of patients. Piperacillin-tazobactam is a type of combination antibiotic, in which piperacillin sodium acts as a broad-spectrum semi-synthetic penicillin and exhibits antibacterial activity by inhibiting the synthesis of bacterial cell walls. Tazobactam is a β-lactamase inhibitor that effectively inhibits various bacterial β-lactamases and enhances antibacterial activity by avoiding the hydrolysis of the chemical structure of piperacillin by bacterial β-lactamases.

Conclusions

Our results indicated that administering corticosteroids to patients with moderate-to-severe oedema and severe systemic symptoms of infectious toxicity may accelerate the regression of edema at the site of infection, leading to a shorter hospital stay for the patient. When selecting an antibiotic regimen, the potential for β-lactamase production and antibiotic resistance among clinical isolates should be considered. Piperacillin-tazobactam may be a viable option for initial treatment. Our study had some limitations. For example, we collected cases where corticosteroids were given on the day of admission and no cases where corticosteroids were given at a later stage. Comparison of the effect of corticosteroids given at different times is lacking. In addition, we speculated that there were also penicillin-resistant strains in our study patients; however, as our study lacked the culture results of all strains, the information was unavailable.
