The VCPKMT-VCP-HDAC1 axis inhibits colorectal cancer by conferring sensitivity to ferroptosis
Authors: Cijun Huang, Jinlong Lin, Shuidan Xu, Yongrui Lv, Jiewei Chen, Jinghua Cao, Dan Xie, Fengwei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFerroptosis, a regulated form of cell death driven by lipid peroxidation and iron dysregulation, plays a critical role in tumor suppression. The expression of certain genes determine ferroptosis sensitivity and may serve as biomarkers to identify patients who could benefit from ferroptosis-promoting therapies. The role of Methyltransferase-like (METTL) family proteins in colorectal cancer (CRC) tumorigenesis has gained increasing attention, yet the functional relevance of METTL21D (also known as VCPKMT) remains largely unexplored.MethodsCCK8 assays, colony formation assays, and xenograft tumor experiments were used to investigate the role of VCPKMT in proliferation and ferroptosis sensitivity in CRC. RNA sequencing, IP-MS, and RT-qPCR were used to identify the relevant genes of VCPKMT. Co-IP and subcellular fractionation assays, western blotting and immunofluorescence (IF) staining were used to explore the underlying molecular mechanisms of actions of VCPKMT.ResultsHerein, we identified that VCPKMT is downregulated in CRC tissues and lower VCPKMT expression correlates with ferroptosis resistance and poorer prognosis. Mechanistically, VCPKMT-induced VCP methylation facilitates its nuclear translocation, a process that enhances VCP’s interaction with HDAC1. This interaction promotes the degradation of HDAC1 via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, leading to reduced expression of FTH1, a key inhibitor of ferroptosis. Combined targeting of HDAC1 and ferroptosis synergistically suppressed the growth of tumors with low VCPKMT expression.ConclusionsOur findings reveal a novel role for VCPKMT as a regulator of ferroptosis sensitivity in CRC, highlighting the VCPKMT-VCP-HDAC1 axis as a potential therapeutic target for CRC treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-04044-x.

Introduction

Ferroptosis is a recently identified form of regulated cell death, primarily driven by iron-catalyzed Fenton reactions, which induce the peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA)-containing phospholipids (PUFA-PLs) within the cell membrane [1]. In cancer, ferroptosis plays a dual role, acting as both a suppressor and a promoter of tumor progression [2, 3]. Given the role of iron metabolism, gene expression, and mutational status in determining ferroptosis sensitivity, these factors may serve as biomarkers to identify patients who could benefit from ferroptosis-promoting therapies [4]. Notably, pro-ferroptotic agents such as statins have shown potential in improving survival in multiple cancers, including CRC [5]. CRC is the third most common malignancy worldwide and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality, affecting approximately 150 000 new individuals each year [6]. Accumulating evidence suggests that ferroptosis activation inhibits CRC progression and overcomes therapeutic resistance [7, 8]. However, the intricate crosstalk between ferroptosis and tumorigenesis remains poorly understood, limiting the clinical application of ferroptosis-based strategies in CRC [9].

METTL proteins are a highly conserved family of seven-beta-stranded methyltransferases, which modify DNA, RNA, and proteins through their S-adenosyl methionine-binding domains. With over 30 members identified, METTL proteins play diverse epigenetic roles, exerting profound effects on molecular processes and cellular phenotypes [10, 11]. Among them, METTL3 and METTL14 are well-characterized regulators of N6-methyladenosine (m6A) RNA modification [12, 13], while METTL17 has been implicated in ferroptosis regulation in CRC by modulating m4C and m5C methylation of mitochondrial rRNA [14]. Beyond their canonical methyltransferase functions, some METTL proteins, such as METTL, participate in translation initiation and tumorigenesis [15]. However, the biological functions of many METTL family members remain largely unexplored [16]. METTL21D, also known as valosin-containing protein lysine methyltransferase (VCPKMT), is solely responsible for the methylation of VCP, a key regulator of diverse cellular processes. It seems to involve in tumor cell proliferation and metastasis according to limited researches [17]. Despite its established role in VCP modification and deeply analyzed structure binding with VCP, the functional relevance of VCPKMT together with VCP remains unknown.

In this study, through systematic screening of METTL family members in CRC, we identified METTL21D as a potential regulator of ferroptosis. Subsequent experiments revealed that VCPKMT-mediated tri-methylation of VCP facilitates its nuclear translocation, leading to HDAC1 degradation and enhanced ferroptosis sensitivity in CRC. Importantly, combined targeting of HDAC1 and ferroptosis synergistically suppressed the growth of tumors with low VCPKMT expression. These findings suggest that VCPKMT upregulation or HDAC1 inhibition may serve as a promising strategy to promote ferroptosis and hinder CRC progression.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

DLD-1 and SW480 cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, NY, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), while 293 T cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Gibco) with the same serum. These cell lines, purchased from ATCC, were authenticated 3 months prior to the start of the study. All cells were kept at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment. Before use, the cell lines were checked for myeloma contamination and were not in culture for more than 2 months.

Patients and samples

Tissue samples were gathered from patients who had surgery at the Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (SYSUCC) in Guangzhou, China between 2013 and 2014. Inclusion criteria for colorectal cancer (CRC) cases included a confirmed pathological diagnosis, comprehensive follow-up information, and no prior treatment. Tumor grades were determined based on the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines and the sixth edition of the TNM classification from the International Union Against Cancer. Approval for this study was granted by the institutional review board at SYSUCC.

Plasmids, lentivirus, and cell transfection

The genes VCPKMT, VCP HDAC1 and their mutated forms were cloned into the pLVX-3×Flag/HA/MYC-Puro vector. Beijing Tsingke Biotech Co. synthesized shRNAs targeting VCPKMT and HDAC1, which were inserted into the pLKO.1-Puro vector. Lentiviral packaging in HEK293T cells was facilitated by two additional vectors, pMD2.G and psPAX2. Stable cell lines were established through puromycin selection lasting 2–3 weeks. Oligonucleotides necessary for vector cloning can be found in the Supplementary Information, Table S1. Transfection of plasmids was carried out using LIPOFECTAMINE 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA).

Cell proliferation

CRC cells were cultured in 96-well plates with a density of 1000 cells per well. To evaluate their proliferation rates, a combination of 90 µL serum-free media and 10 µL CCK8 solution (MCE, NJ, USA) was used. Absorbance readings were taken at 450 nm after a 2-hour incubation using a VersaMax spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices, CA, USA). For colony formation studies, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a concentration of 500 cells per well and allowed to grow for 2 weeks. The resulting colonies were stained with crystal violet (Solarbio, Beijing, China), and the colony count was determined using Image J software.

Measurement of cell viability

Cell viability was assessed in colorectal cancer cell lines (DLD-1, SW480) through exposure to varying doses of the ferroptosis inducer erastin, either alone or in combination with other cell death inhibitors. To begin, 5000 cells per well were seeded in CCK8 assays, or 1000 cells per well were utilized in colony formation assays within 96-well plates and incubated overnight. Following a 48-hour exposure period, cell viability was measured using CCK8 reagents or crystal violet staining, with absorbance at OD 450 nm recorded or colonies quantified using Image J software.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

RNA extraction was conducted using TRIzol reagent (TaKaRa, Dalian, China), while cDNAs were generated with PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (TaKaRa). Subsequently, qPCR was performed using 2x Color SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (TaKaRa). GAPDH served as the internal control for RNA expression normalization. The primer details can be found in Supplementary Information, Table S2.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

RNA sequencing was performed using the Illumina HiSeq platform. Raw image data files were converted into raw sequencing data through CASAVA base calling analysis, with the results stored in FASTQ file format. Clean reads were aligned using Hisat2 and StringTie to obtain positional and sequence information specific to the sample. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq R package (1.10.1). DESeq provides statistical routines for determining differential expression in digital gene expression data using a model based on the negative binomial distribution. Fold Change ≥ 2 and FDR < 0.01 were used as the screening criteria of differential expression. Each group included three biological replicates.

Western blotting and immunoprecipitation

Cell lysates were prepared using a lysis buffer containing essential components such as NaCl, Tris-HCl, EDTA NP-40 protease inhibitor cocktail, and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail for optimal protein extraction. After a 30-minute lysis period, protein lysates were loaded onto SDS-PAGE gels, followed by transfer to PVDF membranes (Millipore, MA, USA). Primary antibodies were then applied and left overnight at 4 °C, while secondary antibodies were incubated at room temperature for 1 h. The membranes were visualized using a ChemistarTM High-sig ECL kit (GLPBIO, CA, USA) for chemiluminescent detection on a Bio-Rad system. Specific details of the antibodies used can be found in the Supplementary Information, Table S3.

The IP assay involved lysing cells in the provided buffer, followed by incubation with anti-Flag/Myc immunomagnetic beads (MCE) overnight at 4 °C. After washing and boiling, the samples were analyzed using Western blot, silver staining, and MS.

Mass spectrometry analysis

Binding proteins were captured via IP utilizing an anti-Flag antibody and subsequently separated by SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis. Detection of interacting proteins was achieved through silver gel staining (#P0017S, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). The immunoprecipitated proteins were then analyzed by mass spectrometry for proteomic analysis (BGI Genomics Co., Ltd., China). In detail, raw mass spectrometry data from the tandem mass spectrometer Q-Exactive HF X were converted into mass spectrometry peak files, which were then searched and matched against sequences in the UniProt database. In this pipeline, results from search engine were pre-processed and re-scored using Percolator to improve the matching accuracy. The output was then filtered by FDR 1% at spectral level (PSM level FDR ≤ 0.01) to obtain a significant identified spectrum and peptide list. For protein quantification, BGI’s own software was used to calculate the iBAQ value for each protein. This process involved the extraction of peptide XIC and calculation of the peak area. The total peak area in each protein group was divided by the number of theoretical peptides to obtain the final iBAQ value. Each group included two biological replicates.

CUT&Tag sequencing

According to the instructions of the NovoNGS CUT&Tag High-Sensitivity Kit (for Illumina) (N259-YH01, NovoProtein), counted 10 000 cells were immobilized on Concanavalin A magnetic beads and permeabilized. Then cells were incubated with the primary antibody, secondary antibody, transposable body and fragmented in turn. DNA fragments were then purified and utilized for qPCR analysis. The primer sequences are shown in Supplementary Information, Table S4.

IF staining

The cells underwent a precise fixation process using a 4% formaldehyde solution for 20 min, followed by three washes with a PBS solution at room temperature. Subsequently, the cells were carefully permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 30 min. Upon blocking with 5% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature, the cells were exposed to a primary antibody solution overnight at 4℃. The next day, the cells were treated with secondary antibodies for Alexa Fluor 488 (#SA00003, Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA) and Alexa Fluor 594 (#SA00006, Proteintech) at RT for 1 h. DAPI staining was then applied for 10 min followed by three 5-minute washes with PBS. Microscopic imaging was conducted using a Zeiss LSM880 microscope (Zeiss, White Plains, NY, USA).

Reagents and treatments

To search cell ferroptosis, the ferroptosis inducer erastin (#HY-15763, MCE) was used at specified concentrations; the ferroptosis inhibitors ferrostatin-1 (#HY-100579, MCE) were used at 0.5 µmol/L and 10 µmol/L respectively; apoptosis inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK (#HY-16658B, MCE) at 20 µmol/L, autophagy inhibitor 3-methylademine (3-MA) (#HY-19312, MCE) at 1 mmol/L, and necroptosis inhibitor ncerostatin-1 (Nec-1) (#HY-15760, MCE) at 10 µmol/L. To search proteasome-dependent degradation, MG132 (#HY-13259, MCE) was used at 10 µmol/L.

Animal study

BALB/nude mice aged four weeks were procured from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Ltd and housed in the Center of Experimental Animal of SYSUCC under standard conditions. Xenograft models were established by injecting control and experimental cells (5 × 106 SW480 cells per mouse) subcutaneously into the mice’s flanks. Tumor growth was monitored every 5 days using calipers, with sizes calculated using a specific formula. Once the average tumor volume reached 50 mm3, the mice were treated with intraperitoneal injections of erastin 30 mg/kg every 3 days for 5 cycles. Following the treatment, the mice were euthanized, and their tumors were collected and weighed. The maximum tumor size did not exceed 500 mm3, in accordance with the predefined criteria. Prior approval for the protocols and animal usage were obtained from the Sun Yat-sen University Animal Care and Use Committee, ensuring ethical standards were met throughout the study.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (California, USA). Two-tailed Student’s t-tests compared two groups, while ANOVA was used for multiple comparisons with more than two groups. Data were reported as mean ± SD, with significance set at P < 0.05. The relationship between VCPKMT expression and patient clinicopathologic parameters was examined using the chi-square test or Welch t-test. Survival analyses were performed using the COX proportional hazards regression model for univariate and multivariate assessments. For stratification, samples were stratified into high and low groups based on the median expression level of VCPKMT.

Results

VCPKMT is downregulated and inhibits proliferation in CRC

To screen potential METTL family members related to CRC carcinogenesis, we estimated the expression of METTLs between CRC tumor and normal tissues from UCSC Xena database. Apart from METTL family members previously implicated in tumor progression, VCPKMT was notably downregulated in CRC compared to normal tissues (Fig. 1A). Clustering analysis further revealed its close association with METTL3 and METTL17, two well-established CRC regulators (Fig. 1A). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis indicated a potential diagnostic performance of VCPKMT (Fig. 1B). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis based on public datasets indicated that higher VCPKMT expression was associated with improved recurrence-free survival (Fig. 1C) [18]. This observation was further validated in a cohort of 88 CRC specimens, where elevated VCPKMT protein levels correlated with longer overall survival (Fig. 1D). Multivariate Cox regression analysis further demonstrated that high VCPKMT expression was an independent predictor of favorable prognosis (HR = 0.432, 95% CI 0.264–0.709, p < 0.001, Table 1) (Clinicopathological information is provided in Table 2). Consistently, immunoblotting of paired CRC and adjacent normal tissues demonstrated a marked reduction of VCPKMT protein levels in tumors (Fig. 1E). Collectively, these data identify VCPKMT as a previously underappreciated factor in CRC and suggest its potential as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker.

To functionally characterize VCPKMT, we examined its endogenous expression across several CRC cell lines (Fig. S1A). Based on the relative expression levels, we stably overexpressed VCPKMT in DLD-1 cell line and knocked down VCPKMT in SW480 cell line (Fig. S1B). CCK8 and colony formation assays showed that VCPKMT depletion enhanced CRC cell proliferation, whereas its overexpression suppressed tumor cell growth (Fig. 1F and G). These data demonstrated that VCPKMT exerts a tumor-suppressive effect in CRC in vitro.

(GSEA) further revealed that genes downregulated upon VCPKMT upregulation were significantly enriched in ferroptosis-associated term (Fig. 2B). Therefore, we performed a series of assays to investigate whether VCPKMT influences tumor proliferation by regulating ferroptosis. First, VCPKMT overexpression sensitized cells to erastin-induced ferroptosis, an effect reversible by the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) (Fig. 2C). Notably, inhibitors of apoptosis (Z-VAD-FMK), autophagy (3-MA), and necroptosis (Nec-1) failed to rescue the decrease in cell viability induced by erastin (Fig. 2C), confirming that ferroptosis is the predominant cell death mechanism regulated by VCPKMT in CRC cells. Further, increasing concentrations of erastin exacerbated ferroptosis, which was rescued by Fer-1 (Fig. 2D). Conversely, VCPKMT depletion conferred resistance to ferroptosis in SW480 cells (Fig. 2E). Liperfluo staining, a method to assess cellular lipid peroxidation, revealed a marked reduction in lipid peroxide accumulation upon VCPKMT depletion (Fig. 2F). Similarly, intracellular labile iron (Fe2+) levels, measured by FerroOrange staining, were significantly decreased in VCPKMT-depleted cells (Fig. 2G). In contrast, VCPKMT overexpression in DLD-1 cells resulted in increased lipid peroxide and higher Fe2+ levels (Fig. S1C-D). Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) imaging revealed typical mitochondrial alterations associated with ferroptosis in VCPKMT-overexpressing DLD-1 cells, including smaller size, increased membrane density, and reduced cristae, even in the absence of erastin treatment (Fig. 2H). These collective findings suggest a plausible association between VCPKMT expression and sensitivity to ferroptosis in CRC.

VCPKMT facilitates nuclear translocation of VCP to degrade HDAC1

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying VCPKMT function, we performed silver.

staining and mass spectrum (MS) analysis following VCPKMT immunoprecipitation (IP) to identify its potential binding partners. In addition to its known methylation substrate VCP, we identified HDAC1, a histone deacetylase with a critical role in transcriptional regulation, as a novel interacting partner of VCPKMT (Fig. 3A). Co-IP assays confirmed their mutual interaction (Fig. 3B), and IF staining revealed colocalization of VCPKMT and HDAC1 predominantly in the nucleus (Fig. 3C). Previous studies have shown that VCP extracts ubiquitinated HDAC1 from chromatin, promoting its degradation and facilitating the transcription of fatty acid β-oxidation-related genes [19], which prompted us to delve further into the interplay among VCPKMT, VCP and HDAC1. Through endogenous IP assays, we found VCPKMT knockdown decreased the binding between VCP and HDAC1, along with the reduced methylation of VCP, increased ubiquitylation and protein expression of HDAC1 (Fig. 3D and E). Conversely, VCPKMT overexpression resulted in enhanced VCP-HDAC1 interaction, elevated VCP methylation, and decreased HDAC1 levels (Fig. S2A and B).

Given the observed interaction between VCPKMT and HDAC1, we considered the possibility that HDAC1 could be a direct substrate of VCPKMT. However, our results showed no detectable changes in either tri-methyllysine or mono/di-methyllysine levels of HDAC1 (Fig. S2C), suggesting that HDAC1 might not be a direct substrate of VCPKMT. Notably, VCP seems to be a crucial nexus protein in this pathway, as it is a well-known ubiquitin-selective chaperone. To provide clearer evidence that VCPKMT mediates HDAC1 degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, we further examined the specific types of ubiquitin chains on HDAC1 mediated by VCPKMT. Our results revealed that K48-linked ubiquitylation of HDAC1 significantly increased upon VCPKMT knockdown, while K63-linked ubiquitylation remained largely unchanged (Fig. S2C). Additionally, MG132, a proteasome inhibitor, enhanced the protein levels of HDAC1 and restored the VCPKMT-induced reduction in HDAC1 protein levels, suggesting that VCPKMT indeed promotes the proteasomal degradation of HDAC1 (Fig. S2D).

Based on the above results and aforementioned research, we hypothesized that VCPKMT facilitates the nuclear translocation of VCP and promotes the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of HDAC1. To test this, we conducted subcellular fractionation assay and IF staining, which revealed that VCPKMT overexpression did not alter total VCP protein levels but significantly promoted its nuclear translocation (Fig. S2E and F). This was accompanied by a marked reduction in nuclear HDAC1 levels. In contrast, VCPKMT depletion impaired VCP nuclear translocation and resulted in HDAC1 accumulation in the nucleus (Fig. 3F and G), further supporting our hypothesis. Previous research have shown that mutations at D96 and D144 abolish the methyltransferase activity of VCPKMT, and that K315 is the methylation target on VCP [17]. To determine whether VCPKMT enhances the nuclear translocation of VCP via its enzymatic activity, we constructed two mutant plasmids: VCPKMT-D96V/D144V (2DV) and VCP-K315L (KL). Co-IP assays confirmed that VCP tri-methylation was abolished by either the KL or 2DV mutation, both of which led to increased HDAC1 protein levels, enhanced HDAC1 ubiquitination, and reduced interaction between VCP and HDAC1 (Fig. S2G). Furthermore, subcellular fractionation assay (Fig. 3H) and IF staining (Fig. 3I) demonstrated that these mutations impaired VCP nuclear localization and led to nuclear accumulation of HDAC1. Collectively, these results demonstrate that VCPKMT facilitates the tri-methylation and nuclear translocation of VCP, thereby promoting the degradation of HDAC1.

VCPKMT suppresses HDAC1 expression to promote ferroptosis via FTH1 downregulation

To investigate whether HDAC1, regulated by VCPKMT, modulates ferroptosis-related gene expression, we analyzed key genes identified in KEGG and GSEA analyses. Among these, phospholipase A2, group IIA (PLA2G2A), Ferritin heavy chain (FTH1), Glutamate–cysteine ligase catalytic subunit (GCLC), and Long-chain-fatty-acid–CoA ligase 1 (ACSL1) exhibited significant changes upon VCPKMT knockdown in SW480 cells and overexpression in DLD-1 cells (Fig. 4A and S3A). PLA2G2A encodes a phospholipase A2 enzyme that releases arachidonic acid, a lipid highly susceptible to peroxidation [20, 21], and was positively regulated by VCPKMT. Conversely, FTH1, GCLC and ACSL1, which limit ferroptosis, were increased with the depletion of VCPKMT, and decreased with the upregulation of VCPKMT in CRC cells [22, 23]. Notably, FTH1, which sequesters iron and protects cells from ferroptosis [24, 25], displayed the most pronounced changes in protein levels (Fig. 4B and S3B). To further investigate the role of VCPKMT in regulating ferroptosis, we evaluated the protein levels of GPX4 and SLC7A11, which are canonical ferroptosis regulators. Our results showed that while FTH1 protein levels increased upon VCPKMT knockdown, the levels of GPX4 and SLC7A11 remained largely unchanged (Fig. S3C). Furthermore, TCGA data also supports a negative correlation between VCPKMT and FTH1 expression (Fig. S3D). Consistently, immunoblotting of paired CRC and adjacent normal tissues confirmed a marked negative correlation between VCPKMT expression and the protein levels of both HDAC1 or FTH1 (Fig. 4C and S3F). Considering the role of HDAC1 in transcriptional regulation, we investigated its involvement in FTH1 expression. As shown in Fig. 4D, HDAC1 co-expression or co-knockdown effectively reversed the impact of VCPKMT on FTH1 expression. To explore whether HDAC1 directly targets FTH1 transcription, we performed CUT&Tag assays using an HDAC1 antibody. However, our results did not provide evidence of direct binding between HDAC1 and the FTH1 promoter (Fig. S3E). These results suggest that the regulation of FTH1 by HDAC1 is likely indirect, which requires further investigation.

Functionally, HDAC1 depletion impaired the proliferation of VCPKMT-knockdown cells (Fig. 4E-F), while HDAC1 overexpression reversed the VCPKMT-induced suppression of CRC cell proliferation (Fig. S3G-I). Furthermore, HDAC1 depletion restored sensitivity to erastin-induced ferroptosis in VCPKMT-knockdown cells (Fig. 4G), while HDAC1 overexpression reinstated resistance to ferroptosis in VCPKMT-overexpressing cells (Fig. S3J). Consistently, lipid peroxide accumulation and intracellular Fe2+ levels remained unchanged when both VCPKMT and HDAC1 were depleted, suggesting that HDAC1 mediates VCPKMT-induced ferroptosis sensitivity (Fig. 4H-I). TEM imaging revealed characteristic mitochondrial morphological changes associated with ferroptosis, which reoccurred upon HDAC1 depletion in VCPKMT-knockdown cells treated with erastin (Fig. S3K). To further verify that VCPKMT enhances ferroptosis sensitivity through FTH1 downregulation, we performed rescue experiments by re-expressing FTH1 in VCPKMT-overexpressing DLD-1 cells. FTH1 overexpression reversed the VCPKMT-induced suppression of CRC cell proliferation (Fig. S4A-C) and restored resistance to ferroptosis in VCPKMT-overexpressing cells (Fig. S4D). Consistently, lipid peroxide accumulation and intracellular Fe2+ levels changed slightly when both VCPKMT and FTH1 were overexpressed (Fig. S4E and F). Together, these findings indicate that VCPKMT enhances ferroptosis sensitivity and suppresses CRC cell proliferation by promoting HDAC1 degradation, leading to the downregulation of FTH1.

VCPKMT relies on HDAC1 to regulate ferroptosis sensitivity in vivo

To further elucidate the role of HDAC1 in mediating the effect of VCPKMT on CRC, we performed rescue experiments in a xenograft model. Xenograft growth was promoted upon VCPKMT silencing, an effect that was reversed by HDAC1 depletion (Fig. 5A-C). Additionally, VCPKMT knockdown induced resistance to erastin-induced ferroptosis, whereas silencing HDAC1 restored sensitivity to ferroptosis (Fig. 5A-C). IHC analysis of Ki-67, a marker of cell proliferation, demonstrated that HDAC1 silencing significantly reversed the increase propagation caused by VCPKMT knockdown (Fig. 5D). Moreover, FTH1 protein levels within tumors displayed a similar trend to the in vitro findings (Fig. 5E). Additionally, 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), a lipid peroxidation end product and a biomarker of ferroptosis, was assessed by IHC staining [27]. As depicted in Fig. 5F, tumors with VCPKMT silencing exhibited lower 4-HNE levels compared to control tumors, and this decrease was reversed upon HDAC1 silencing. Taken together, these data implied that HDAC1 mediates the effect of VCPKMT on ferroptosis sensitivity and tumor suppression in CRC in vivo.

Discussion

Ferroptosis has garnered significant interest in cancer research due to its distinct mechanistic and morphological characteristics, setting it apart from other forms of cell death [26]. Accumulating evidence supports ferroptosis as a critical tumor-suppressive mechanism that is often evaded by cancer cells through several strategies: impairing PUFA-PL synthesis and lipid peroxidation, decreasing labile iron availability, and enhancing cellular defenses against ferroptosis [27, 28]. Targeting these evasion mechanisms offer a promising direction for developing ferroptosis-inducing cancer therapies. A recent study have highlighted that tumors with a high-mesenchymal state exhibit resistance to various cancer treatments by inhibiting lipid peroxidation pathways, yet they remain susceptible to ferroptosis inducers [29]. Furthermore, CD8+T cell-mediated immunotherapy has been shown to enhance ferroptosis-specific lipid peroxidation, contributing to the anti-tumor effects of immunotherapy [30]. Ferroptosis activators also induce synthetic lethality in CRC cells that rapidly acquire resistance to hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)−2α inhibitors [31]. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of ferroptosis in CRC treatment, despite the limited clinical application of current ferroptosis-inducing agents [32]. Our research suggested that small molecule, such as mimetic peptides of VCPKMT or HDAC1 inhibitors, could enhance the sensitivity of CRC cells to ferroptosis and inhibit tumor progression. Interestingly, a recent study have demonstrated that HDAC inhibitors sensitize cells to ferroptosis through FTO- and ALKBH5-dependent downregulation of FSP1 [33]. Our data similarly showed that HDAC1 depletion downregulates FTH1 expression, a ferroptosis inhibitor, suggesting a similar indirect regulatory mechanism. Given HDAC1’s role in chromatin compaction and transcriptional repression, we hypothesize that additional mediators link HDAC1 to FTH1 regulation, an area that warrants further investigation [34].

VCP serves as an integral chaperone for many quality control pathways, including macroautophagy, DNA damage repair, endoplasmic reticulum–associated degradation (ERAD) and chromatin-associated protein degradation [35, 36]. As an ubiquitin-selective chaperone, VCP facilitates the extraction of ubiquitylated proteins from interacting partners or subcellular components for proteasomal degradation [37, 38]. While increased ubiquitylation typically signals for degradation, VCP recognizes ubiquitylated proteins and plays a role in their removal, which in turn leads to a decrease in their overall ubiquitylation levels, as supported by previous studies [39, 40]. In addition, VCP also binds several deubiquitinating enzymes, which remove ubiquitin to promote substrate recycling and act as positive regulators of protein degradation, possibly by shortening and removing branches to make the ubiquitin chains appropriate for proteasome recognition and subsequent degradation [40–42]. Generally, VCP removes K48-linked ubiquitylation of substrates, but recently some researches have also found VCP involves in regulating other specific linkage ubiquitin [44, 45]. Pathogenic VCP variants cause multisystem proteinopath (MSP), featured by multiple clinical phenotypes including inclusion body myopathy, Paget’s disease of the bone, and frontotemporal dementia [46, 47]. Noticeably, knock-in cell lines harboring MSP variants exhibit a reduction in nuclear VCP levels [48], suggesting that VCP’s nuclear functions are crucial yet not fully understood. In cancer, the role of VCP is increasingly recognized, though the mechanisms regulating its nuclear translocation remain unclear. Previous studies have shown that p37 impedes VCP’s nuclear translocation, while the loss of VCF1/2 also results in a reduction of nuclear VCP levels [49, 50]. In addition to protein interactions, post translational modification also plays an important role in subcellular localization. SUMOylation at the N-domain, phosphorylation at Ser612 and Thr613, and acetylation at Lys-614, all promote nuclear localization of VCP [51, 52]. Our study demonstrated that VCPKMT-mediated tri-methylation of VCP facilitates its nuclear translocation, promoting the crosstalk between VCP and HDAC1, which in turn led to the downregulation of FTH1 expression in CRC. Structural studies of VCP and VCPKMT further support our findings. VCP contains two ATPase domains, with the N-terminal D1 domain interacting with various adaptor proteins [53, 54]. Disease-causing mutations in this domain lead to conformational changes that impair VCP’s interaction with its cofactors, disrupting downstream processes [46, 55]. VCPKMT, a lysine-specific methyltransferase, catalyzes the tri-methylation of VCP at Lys315 within the D1 domain [17, 56], which may alter VCP’s structural integrity and facilitating its association with HDAC1. Interestingly, previous studies have demonstrated that nuclear VCP induces the degradation of HDAC1, promoting FAO-associated gene expression and contributing to CRC progression [19]. Our findings suggested that VCPKMT precisely controls VCP’s subcellular localization, rather than its overall expression, which may account for the differential phenotypic outcomes observed in studies where VCP expression was altered.

VCPKMT, initially named “novel metastasis-promoting gene 1” (NVM-1), was first identified as a promoter of metastasis. Early studies indicated that VCPKMT was overexpressed in various cancer tissues compared to non-transformed counterparts, although these findings were based on limited clinical samples [57]. Subsequent research identified VCPKMT as a VCP methyltransferase, highlighting its role in regulating VCP function. However, studies on VCPKMT’s role in cell proliferation have yielded varying results [17, 57], likely due to the use of different cell lines and experimental conditions. These observations suggest that VCPKMT’s function is context-dependent, and further exploration of its potential in cancer treatment and prognosis is warranted.

Conclusion

Our study establishes a novel molecular model in which VCPKMT promotes the nuclear translocation of VCP, leading to HDAC1 degradation and subsequent suppression of FTH1 expression, ultimately sensitizing CRC cells to ferroptosis (Fig. 6). We propose that VCPKMT expression may serve as a biomarker for predicting responses to ferroptosis inducers. For patients with low VCPKMT expression, dual targeting of HDAC1 and ferroptosis may enhance therapeutic efficacy. These findings underscore the functional importance of the VCPKMT-VCP-HDAC1 axis in ferroptosis sensitivity and suggest that targeting this axis may be a viable strategy for CRC therapy.
