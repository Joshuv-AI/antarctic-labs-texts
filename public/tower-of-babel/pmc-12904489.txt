Brown tumor of mandible: A conundrum towards the diagnosis
Authors: Sayani Shome, Manorama Dutta, Aritra Laskar, Rudra P. Chatterjee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Brown Tumor is a rare, reactive, giant cell associated, late-stage bone lesion occuring as a result of long term untreated primary or secondary hyperparathyroidism (HPT). It clinically manifests as a high turnover bone disease, which typically shows increased parathyroid hormone (PTH) secretion in response to low serum calcium concentration thereby enhancing calcium reabsorption and osteoclastic bone resorption, hence called osteoclastoma or giant cell reparative granuloma or osteitis fibrosa cystica.Brown tumor associated with HPT can be seen in any part of the human skeleton, However clinically significant lesion involving the craniofacial skeleton is quite rare. Here we report a rare case of Brown tumor associated with HPT in a 28 year old female involving the right side of mandible.

INTRODUCTION

Brown tumor, also known as osteitis fibrosa cystica, osteoclastoma or giant cell reparative granuloma represents a reparative cellular process rather than true neoplasia, which is caused by rapid osteoclastic activity and peri-trabecular fibrosis leading to erosive bony lesion in response to primary and rarely secondary hyperparathyroidism (HPT).[1]

The literature reveals reported incidence of brown tumor is 4.5% in primary and 1.5%–1.7%[1] in association with secondary HPT. A slight female predilection and increased incidence is seen around 5th decade of life.[2]

CASE REPORT

A 28 years old female patient reported to the department of Oral and Maxillofacial Pathology and Microbiology, Haldia Institute of Dental Sciences and Research with the chief complaint of gradually expansile, diffuse, painless intrabony swelling on the right side of the mandible in the back teeth region for two years. There was no relevant history of any trauma or injury. Her medical and allergic histories were not significant. Patient gives no history of any kind of deleterious oral habits.

Extraoral examination revealed diffuse, large, bony hard, non-tender swelling over the wide area of right sided angle and ramus of the mandible causing ipsilateral facial asymmetry [Figure 1a and b]. Overlying skin was free and no regional lymphadenopathy was noted. No deviation of mandible was noted on vertical and lateral excursion. Intraoral examination revealed a diffuse, solitary, hard, non-tender, non-fluctuant and non-pulsatile swelling with vestibular obliteration in relation to the anatomical region of 43 to 47 (approx. 1.5 cm × 2.0 cm in dimension) [Figure 2]. Overlying mucosa was free and normal in colour and texture. 47 was extracted earlier due to dental caries. Rest of the regional teeth were mobile. Oral hygiene was moderately good. Orthopantomogram [Figure 3] revealed irregular mixed radiopaque and radiolucent appearance over the parasymphysis, angle and entire ramus including parts of condyle on the right side of the mandible mimicking ground glass appearance. Advanced imaging with Computed tomography (CT) both in conventional (axial, coronal, and sagittal view) [Figure 4a] and contrast enhanced 3D CT [Figure 4b] showed large expansile and osteolytic lesion over the aforesaid anatomical location. Based on clinic-radiological parameters provisional diagnosis was made as fibro-osseous lesion considering other differential diagnosis simulating benign odontogenic neoplasms.

Haematological parameters were within normal limit and pre-operative serology was non-reactive for HIV I, HIV II, HBV, and HCV. Incisional biopsy was performed under local anaesthesia via intraoral approach after obtaining written consent from the patient.

Under low-magnification (10×, H&E) histopathological sections stained with haematoxylin and eosin revealed un-encapsulated fibrovascular soft tissue mass intermingled with multiple irregular woven bony trabeculae having entrapped osteocytes and often with osteoblastic rimming [Figure 5a]. The stroma was moderately cellular with plump and spindle shaped fibroblasts. The most striking feature was the concomitant presence of numerous large rounds to ovoid osteoclastic giant cells either focally or in clusters throughout the stromal component [Figure 5b]. Scattered area of haemorrhage interspersed with extravasated erythrocytes and chronic inflammatory cell infiltrates were also noted. No atypia, abnormal mitosis and necrosis were found. Higher magnification (40x, H&E) exhibited large eosinophilic giant cells having average 15–30 nuclei/cell with both hyperchromatic, as well as vesicular nuclei and prominent nucleoli being arranged in clusters [Figure 6a and b]. The overall light microscopic features were suggestive of giant cell tumour.

Serum biochemistry for parathyroid profile was assessed which showed elevated/raised serum parathyroid hormone (450.7 mg/mL), highly serum alkaline phosphatase (325 IU/L) and serum calcium (14.5 mg/dL). Compiling clinic-pathological and biochemical findings, it was finally diagnosed as brown tumour associated with hyperparathyroidism.

Patient was referred to the Dept. of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery for surgical resection and Dept. of Endocrinology for the subsequent correction of increased parathyroid hormone. No anomaly was detected in parathyroid gland as per endocrinologist’s opinion. The occurrence of this entity was presumably due to secondary hyperparathyroidism in response to hypocalcaemia owing to chronic vitamin D deficiency elicited through blood biochemistry.

DISCUSSION

Brown tumor is a non-neoplastic, rather reactive skeletal manifestation with osteolytic features seen as a consequence of primary or secondary hyperparathyroidism.[3] De novo pathology in relation to parathyroid gland predominates as the etiology of primary HPT, while secondary HPT is most commonly associated with end-stage renal disease, while vitamin D deficiency being the second one.[34] Brown tumor or osteitis fibrosa cystica is an intrabony giant cell lesion the origin of that is due to bone resorbing osteoclastic activity by overexpression of parathyroid hormone.[4] Parathyroid hormone is considered as the principal hormone for bone remodeling being coupled with other chemokines and molecules like RANK (Receptor Activator of Nuclear factor kappa β).[4] Though PTH has both anabolic and catabolic activity on bone, precisely it potentiates chemokines like MCP-1 (monocyte chemoattractant protein 1) to initiate monocytic aggregation prior to formation of osteolytic giant cells.[5] Giant cells are the defining histopathological feature of brown tumor raising chance of allocating similar giant cell-rich lesions as differential diagnosis.[45]

The term “giant cell tumour of bone” (GCTB) was first declared in the year 1818.[6] A giant cell is a result of many conglomerated macrophages undergoing defined set of intercellular interactions forming a multinucleated single cytoplasmic compartment. Giant cell tumours (GCT) of bones affect individuals during the 3rd and 4th[7] decades of life unlike brown tumour. The incidence of GCT is quite higher in the Asian population[7] as compared to the western population with a slight prevalence of primary bone tumours of about 20% in Southern India and China. The female-to-male ratio being 1.3 and 1.5 to 2.[7] The ala of the sacrum in the axial skeleton the vertebral body in the spine and the maxilla and mandible in the head region are most affected. Here we report a case of brown tumour involving the right side of mandible in contrary to the age prevalence in the previous cases of brown tumour, in a 28 years old female patient with no classic signs of bones, stones, abdominal groans and psychic moans[10] associated with the disease. On thorough clinical examination, a diffuse, solitary, hard, nontender, nonfluctuant and non-pulsatile swelling, not crossing the midline was elicited intraorally oblitering the vestibule while the extraoral examination revealed an ipsilateral facial asymmetry over the right sided angle and ramus of the mandible. Brown tumour is a rare giant cell lesion of bone associated with primary or secondary HPT. The term “brown tumour” is a misnomer because of its brown macroscopic appearance owing to vascularity, haemorrhage, and deposits of hemosiderin.[11] Jaffe was the first person to coin the term “brown tumour” for bony lesions occurring in association with HPT.[12] Parathyroid adenoma or hyperplasia whether syndromic or nonsyndromic is considered as one of the important causes of primary HPT, while hypocalcaemia attribute to the occurrence of secondary hyperparathyroidism. Raised serum PTH level leads to exaggerated osteoclastic activity leading to increased bone resorptions. Other systemic symptoms involve bone pain, kidney stones and neurological symptoms. Brown tumour can be the first clinical manifestation in primary HPT[1314] demanding the need for biopsy to establish diagnosis unlike secondary HPT where the clinical findings usually helps in detection.[15] Brown tumour can be a manifestation of both primary or secondary HPT, while only 5% cases of primary HPT presents with this.[16] It often regresses and resolves spontaneously if normal PTH level is achieved. On radiological examination, mixed radio-opacities and radiolucency is of parasymphysis, angle and the entire ramus with parts of condyle of right-side mandible was found to be involved whereas the advanced imaging with CT and contrast enhanced 3DCT reveals non uniform bone lysis, irregular areas of bone mineralisation with large expansile osteoclastic lesion. Imaging modalities like CT and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) reveal typical subchondral region associated with GCTs within the bone whereas radiograph reveals characteristic radiolucent appearance with a narrow transition zone in the lesion margin.[7] CT reveals cortical thickening, neocortex formation, bone mineralisation which further helps in distinguishing it from similar lesions. Serum biochemistry of parathyroid profile revealed association of hyperparathyroidism along with increased serum alkaline phosphatase and serum calcium. Histopathological examination showing typical large ovoid osteoclastic giant cell, large eosinophilic giant cells without any evidence of cellular atypia or, abnormal mitosis along with characteristic scattered areas of haemorrhage interspersed with extravasated erythrocytes. It also revealed the presence of around 15–30 hyperchromatic, vesicular nuclei/cell sometimes distinguishing brown tumour from other giant cell lesion of bone like cherubism, Noonan syndrome and reparative granuloma.[1718] Unlike brown tumour, presence of giant cells in less dense stroma but more vascularised one is a characteristic feature of reparative granuloma. The only difference between brown tumour and reparative granuloma is the non-association of HPT in reparative granuloma. After all the clinical, radiological, and histopathological examinations the treatment was planned in conjunction with Department oral and maxillofacial surgery and Department of endocrinology. Wide local excision of the lesion along with right-sided hemi-mandibulectomy were performed and patient was referred to endocrinologist for further evaluation. Further follow up showed excellent healing and overall good response towards HPT management post-treatment.

CONCLUSION

Brown tumour, infrequently found in head and neck area looks forward to comprehensive and critical knowledge of histopathology corroborative to clinical findings. Similar giant cell lesions in the absence of stimulated parathyroid activity must be excluded with rational diagnostic approach. In this case, we have correlated all the clinical, radiological, histopathological features in combination with relevant serum biochemistry to render final diagnosis. Periodic post-treatment follow-up should be an indelible part of overall management of brown tumour. Aforesaid case exhibited favourable outcome post-treatment that had been followed up thoroughly.
