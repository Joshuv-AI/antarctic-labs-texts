Association of blood miR-15a, miR-146a and miR-200b levels with stages of diabetic retinopathy
Authors: Liora Yesharim, Amir Mohammad Salimi Sharif, Amir Hozhabrpour, Khalil Ghasemi Falavarjani, Pasha Anvari, Alireza Zolfagharinia, Faeze Hashemi Rahbarian, Reza Mirshahi, Golnaz Khakpour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a serious complication of diabetes mellitus (DM). MicroRNAs (miRNAs) play crucial roles in DR pathogenesis, but their expression patterns across different DR stages remain unclear. To investigate the expression levels of hsa-miR-200b-5p, hsa-miR-146a-5p, and hsa-miR-15a-5p in the blood of patients in different stages of diabetic retinopathy. The research involved a sample size of 92 patients who had been diagnosed with type 2 diabetes. These patients were classified into three distinct groups: 50 patients with proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR), 30 patients with non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR), and 12 patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus without retinopathy (DM) as a control group. The extraction of total RNA from whole blood samples was followed by RT-qPCR analysis to assess miRNA expression levels. miR-15a showed significantly higher expression in PDR compared to NPDR (P < 0.05), but no significant differences between NPDR vs. DM or PDR vs. DM. miR-146a expression was significantly increased in PDR compared to both NPDR and DM, with no significant difference between NPDR and DM. miR-200b expression was significantly higher in PDR compared to DM but showed no significant differences between NPDR vs. DM or PDR vs. NPDR. Our findings suggest that miR-15a and miR-146a are promising minimally invasive biomarkers for tracking the progression from NPDR to PDR, and distinguishing between these two stages. While elevated levels of miR-200b in PDR indicate potential utility in identifying at-risk patients. These insights may guide early therapeutic interventions and better diabetic retinopathy management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-28368-w.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic disease caused by insulin deficiency or resistance, which results in high blood glucose levels. According to the 2021 report from the International Diabetes Federation, approximately 537 million adults worldwide are suffering from diabetes, and it is expected that this number will increase to 783 million by 20451. Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is one of the main complications of DM, which occurs when a person is exposed to high glucose for a long time2,3. DR is highly prevalent worldwide, making it the leading cause of blindness among working-age adults. The WHO predicts that the number of patients with diabetic retinopathy will increase to 300 million by 20254. In general, retinopathy has 2 stages: non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR) and proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR). In the first stage (NPDR), the blood vessels of the retina are damaged and lead to leakage of blood or fluids inside them, which causes swelling and inflammation of the retina. In the more advanced stage (PDR), abnormal and fragile blood vessels develop inside the retina, which greatly increases the risk of hemorrhage and severe loss of vision5. Higher levels of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) have been linked to increased permeability and growth of retinal endothelial cells in the development of PDR, which shows up as new blood vessel formation (neovascularization) in the retina6,7. However, the mechanisms that control the expression of proteins and metabolites involved in the angiogenic pathways responsible for PDR pathogenesis are still unknown8.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are short, non-coding RNA molecules of approximately 22 nucleotides that regulate gene expression by binding to complementary sequences within the 3′ untranslated regions (3′-UTRs) of target messenger RNAs (mRNAs), ultimately modulating their translation into proteins9,10. They are key post-transcriptional regulators involved in a broad spectrum of biological processes, including cellular proliferation, apoptosis, differentiation, secretion of inflammatory cytokines, tumorigenesis, organogenesis, hematopoiesis, and immune defense against infections11. Previous research has shown the significant involvement of miRNAs in the regulation of angiogenesis12,13. Recent advances in miRNA research have highlighted their potential as noninvasive biomarkers for the diagnosis and monitoring of DR progression, For example, miR-211 has been proposed as a novel clinical biomarker due to its increased expression in DR patients compared with healthy controls and its direct targeting of Sirtuin 1 (SIRT1), a key regulator of cell survival and oxidative stress14. Similarly, miR-126, which is associated with increased retinal endothelial damage especially in the proliferative stages was found to gradually decrease across four groups: healthy individuals, DM patients without retinopathy, NPDR and PDR patients, offering high sensitivity and specificity for the early diagnosis of PDR15. Furthermore, miR-93 has been implicated in the inflammatory and angiogenic cascades characteristic of DR, it was evaluated in plasma from T2DM patients with DR, DM, and healthy controls, with its elevated levels positively correlated with VEGF and TNF-α, and is promising as a diagnostic marker16.

The miR-200 family showed a close association with vascular endothelial cells and the formation of blood vessels17. In a human study, plasma levels of miR-200b were found to be lower in diabetic patients with PDR compared with DM group. McArthur et al. highlighted the role of miR-200b in regulating VEGF signaling in diabetic retinopathy. Their study found that lower levels of miR-200b in diabetic retinas and endothelial cells exposed to high glucose led to increased VEGF and its receptors18. Further human and animal studies have confirmed the regulatory effect of miR-200b on the vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA) gene. In both patient samples and streptozotocin-induced diabetic rat models, reduced miR-200b levels were associated with increased VEGFA expression. Experimental upregulation of miR-200b led to downregulation of VEGFA, transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF-β1), and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), alongside upregulation of pigment epithelium-derived factor (PEDF), suggesting a protective role of miR-200b in diabetic retinopathy through modulation of angiogenic pathways19. miR-146a is mainly expressed in endothelial cells and white blood cells, where it acts to repress inflammation by negatively regulating the NF-κB pathway. The NF-κB pathway is considered one of the key mediators of inflammatory responses, and this modulation by miR-146a reduces chronic inflammation associated with diabetes complications20. Recent findings have shown that serum levels of miR-146a increase progressively with the severity of diabetic retinopathy. Its early upregulation, even in mild stages of the disease, suggests that miR-146a may serve as a sensitive biomarker for early detection and monitoring of DR progression21. miR-15a may also play a role in the pathogenesis of DR. Recent research has also highlighted the potential role of miR-15a as an early biomarker for retinal damage in individuals with impaired glucose regulation. Elevated levels of circulating and extracellular vesicle (EV)-associated miR-15a were found in subjects with impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), even before the onset of clinically detectable retinopathy, miR-15a levels were inversely associated with ganglion cell complex (GCC) thickness, an early indicator of retinal neurodegeneration, suggesting that may reflect clinical retinal changes in the early stages of diabetic retinal pathology22. Another study also reported increased levels of circulating plasma miR-15a in T2DM patients without retinopathy compared to healthy controls; however, this elevation was not statistically significant23.

Taken together, these findings suggest that circulating miRNAs, mirror the underlying molecular alterations driving diabetic retinopathy and hold promise as accessible clinical tools for disease classification and early intervention. Their remarkable stability in blood, conferred by encapsulation within exosomes and micro vesicles, further enhances their reliability as biomarkers under various physiological conditions24.

To our knowledge, this is among the first study which evaluates the expression of miR-15a in whole blood samples from diabetic patients at different stages of retinopathy. Based on previous studies and the need for a reliable non-invasive biomarker for diabetic retinopathy, we aimed to investigate the differences in the expression of these miRNAs among three patient groups. While previous studies have investigated the role of specific miRNAs in diabetic retinopathy, many have only examined one or two stages of the disease. In contrast, our study assesses miR-15a, miR-146a, and miR-200b in whole blood samples, providing a more comprehensive view of systemic expression. Importantly, by analyzing patients in three distinct clinical stages DM, NPDR, and PDR we provide a specific understanding of miRNA dynamics at each stage, which may enhance diagnostic accuracy and biomarker development (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Whole blood samples and RNA extraction

A total of 92 patients diagnosed with type 2 diabetes (DM) were enlisted from Rasoul Akram Hospital, Tehran, Iran, following the protocols outlined by the American Diabetes Association29. All participants had a minimum diabetes duration of 12 years. Among them, 50 patients had PDR, 30 had NPDR, and 12 had diabetes without any signs of retinopathy (DM group), who were considered the retinopathy-free control group. Details of FBS, blood sugar, BMI, age, and sex for the three study groups are presented in Supplementary Table 1. Patients were categorized based on comprehensive ophthalmic evaluation. Age, and medication were carefully matched for all patients.

The informed consent form, approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Iran University of Medical Sciences IR.IUMS.REC.1400.021, was signed by all participants. The extraction of total RNA, including small RNA, was performed from a 500 µL whole blood sample following the guidelines provided in the Pishgaman Sanjesh (Tehran, Iran) Column-based RNA extraction kit # 658,114,223,255 specifically designed for whole blood samples. The volume of the final elution was 40 µl. Quantification of all whole blood RNA preparations was performed using a NanoDrop. Following the extraction process, the RNAs were stored at a temperature of -80 degrees Celsius.

Reverse transcription

A total of 180 ng of RNA was subjected to reverse transcription using miR-specific primers designed via the stem-loop method (Table 1). The schematic of the stem loop structure is shown in Fig. 2. The reverse transcription was performed using the SMO-BIO (Hsinchu, Taiwan) reverse transcription kit, with a reaction volume of 15 µl. The reaction was carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions, following a temperature protocol of 16 ◦C for 30 min, 42 ◦C for 50 min, and 85 ◦C for 5 min.

Real time-quantitative PCR

Real-time PCR was performed using the SYBR™ Green Master Mix (Yekta Tajhiz Azma Tehran, Iran). The relative expression levels of miR-200b, miR-146a, and miR-15a were normalized to U6 small nuclear RNA (snRNA) as an internal reference gene. All reactions were conducted in duplicate, with non-template controls and RT negative controls included. Quantification of gene expression was carried out using the 2 − ΔΔCt method. The primer used was from Pishgam Company (Tehran, Iran). Specific primers for each miRNA, including the stem-loop primers used for cDNA synthesis and reverse primers for the Real-Time PCR, are listed in Table 1. The sequence of the universal forward primer used is AGTGCGTGTCGTGGAGTC.

Statistical analysis

PCR efficiency for each reaction was calculated using the LinRegPCR software, which estimates efficiency by analyzing the linear phase of each amplification curve. After amplification, data were analyzed using the Relative Expression Software Tool (REST) (V2.0.13). REST employs the Pair-Wise Fixed Reallocation Randomization Test, a non-parametric statistical method based on randomization and bootstrapping. This method compares the expression of target genes between sample groups while accounting for PCR efficiency and reference gene normalization. REST is specifically designed for analyzing relative gene expression data, particularly from qRT-PCR experiments with small sample sizes, and it does not assume a normal distribution25. Given the small size of our groups and the expected variability in miRNA expression levels, REST was chosen as an appropriate and robust tool for this analysis.

A p-value of 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To maintain data integrity, outliers (data points significantly deviating from the mean) were removed. The graphs were drawn using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software.

Result

Our primary focus in this study was on examining the blood expression level changes of miRNAs known to control diabetic retinopathy, given that they may partially regulate the expression of various important genes.

In the case of miR-15a, its expression levels were not significantly different between NPDR and DM patients. Likewise, no significant differences existed between PDR and DM groups. However, a significant increase in miR-15a expression was seen when comparing NPDR patients with those suffering from PDR. The expression of miR-15a was significantly increased by 33.38 fold in PDR patients compared to the DM group (p < 0.005) ( Fig. 3A).

While miR-146a expression did not differ significantly between NPDR and DM patients, it was significantly upregulated in PDR patients. Compared to DM, a 15.33 fold increase was observed (p < 0.01), and compared to NPDR, a 17.87 fold increase was noted (p < 0.003) ( Fig. 3B).

For miR-200b, no significant difference in expression was observed between NPDR and DM patients (P < 0.05). However, PDR patients showed a significant 5.57-fold increase in miR-200b expression compared to the DM group (p < 0.033). There was no significant difference in miR-200b expression between PDR and NPDR groups ( Fig. 3C).

To assess whether the expression levels of miR-15a, miR-146a, and miR-200b were influenced by general metabolic factors, we performed Pearson correlation analysis with body mass index (BMI) and fasting blood sugar (FBS) blood glucose, and age. The results showed no significant correlation between the expression of any of the three miRNAs and the mentioned parameters (p < 0.05) (Table 2). This suggests that the observed changes in miRNA expression are more closely related to the retinal pathology of diabetic retinopathy rather than general metabolic control or age.

Discussion

One of the major complications of prolonged hyperglycemia is oxidative stress and inflammation, which cause the endothelial cells that line these blood vessels to become dysfunctional26. This dysfunction increases permeability, allowing fluid and proteins to leak into the retina. High glucose levels can also lead to the formation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), which further contribute to vascular damage and inflammation27. The combination of these factors disrupts the normal blood supply to the retina, leading to complications such as retinal ischemia and the growth of abnormal blood vessels, a condition known as diabetic retinopathy28. The risk of developing DR increases with the duration of diabetes and poor glycemic control. Fortunately, up to 90% of blindness caused by retinopathy could be prevented through early detection and treatment29.

There has been significant interest in exploring the potential use of circulating miRNAs as non-invasive biomarkers for various diseases, including metabolic disorders like DM30. Many miRNAs have been thoroughly studied in DM patients, and their roles in the disease’s progression have been established31,32. In this study, we investigated the expression of miR-200b, miR-146a, and miR-15a at different stages of diabetic retinopathy.

We found that miR-15a expression is significantly higher in the blood of PDR patients compared to NPDR, but not significantly different between DM and NPDR or PDR and DM. Prior in vitro and in vivo research has established that miR-15a plays a critical role in modulating key pathological processes including inflammation33, angiogenesis34, and mainly through its regulatory effects on VEGF and Robo435, which are implicated in the formation of abnormal vessels grow known as fibrovascular membranes, which is a hallmark of progression of NPDR to PDR36. Our findings showed a significant increase in miR-15a only in the PDR group, suggesting its involvement in late-stage DR. In contrast, another study reported a non-significant increase in miR-15a in T2DM patients without DR, indicating early changes. Taken together, these results suggest that miR-15a may be gradually increased during the development of DR and more strongly dysregulated in advanced stages23. The lack of significant differences in miR-15a expression between NPDR vs. DM and PDR vs. DM suggests that miR-15a may become dysregulated only at more advanced stages of DR, particularly when proliferative changes occur.

We observed a significant increase in miR-146a expression in patients with PDR compared to both the DM and NPDR groups. This progressive increase from DM to NPDR to PDR suggests that miR-146a could serve as a predictive biomarker for the progression of diabetes to retinopathy. Our finding appears to be in contrast to previous reports showing decreased miR-146a levels in DR patients, particularly in association with increased VEGF and inflammatory cytokines, A possible explanation for this discrepancy may lie in the expression of miR-146a at each stage or segment, as well as differences in study design and patient population37. While previous studies have emphasized decreased miR-146a expression as a factor in retinal inflammation, our results may indicate compensatory increased expression in advanced stages of the disease, possibly as a feedback response to ongoing vascular and inflammatory stress. Further research is needed to determine whether this increased expression represents a protective mechanism or a secondary effect of disease progression.

We also found a significant increase in miR-200b expression in the blood of patients with PDR compared to the diabetic mellitus DM group. However, there was no significant difference between the PDR and NPDR groups, or between the NPDR and DM groups. Previously, McArthur et al. (2011) reported decreased miR-200b expression in the retinas of diabetic rats, accompanied by increased VEGF levels18. This is the first study, to our knowledge, to directly compare these three groups using blood samples. We assume that the increased miR-200b level in the blood of PDR patients compared to the DM group may be due to retinal cells secreting miR-200b into the blood in response to diabetic stress, or passive leakage of this miRNA to the bloodstream as a result of retinal damage. Investigating this hypothesis will be challenging, as it requires accurate measurement of miRNA expression in both human retina and blood samples from the same individuals at different stages of diabetes. This hypothesis is supported by studies showing increased levels of miR-200b in the vitreous of PDR patients, independent of VEGF levels. This may indicate active secretion or passive leakage from damaged retinal tissue38. In addition, the possibility of active release via exosomes should be considered, as retinal cells under hyperglycemic and hypoxic stresses may utilize extracellular vesicles to export regulatory miRNAs such as miR-200b. This mechanism suggests a deliberate cellular response rather than being simply a consequence of tissue injury31. Recent studies have provided additional evidence supporting the involvement of exosomal miRNAs in the pathogenesis of diabetic retinopathy. Chen et al. demonstrated that mesenchymal stem cell–derived exosomes carrying miR-21 protect retinal cells from hypoxia-induced apoptosis by inhibiting the p38 MAPK signaling pathway39. Li et al. reported that exosomal miR-486-3p alleviates oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis in diabetic mice through inhibition of the TLR4/NF-κB axis40. These findings highlight the potential contribution of exosome-mediated miRNA transport in regulating retinal stress responses, supporting our assumption that the elevated circulating miR-200b observed in PDR may partly result from active exosomal secretion rather than passive leakage. Future studies focusing on isolating exosomal miR-200b and comparing it to intracellular levels could help clarify this distinction and improve our understanding of its role in the pathogenesis of diabetic retinopathy.

In summary, our findings indicate that miR-15a and miR-146a could serve as valuable minimally invasive biomarkers for tracking disease progression, particularly in the transition from NPDR to PDR. The significant increase in miR-15a expression in PDR compared to NPDR, along with the elevated levels of miR-146a in PDR patients compared to both NPDR and DM, highlights their potential role in monitoring disease severity. While miR-200b may not effectively differentiate between the earlier stages of the disease, its higher expression in PDR compared to DM suggests it could still be useful for identifying patients who have progressed to PDR. This insight could help clinicians pinpoint individuals at risk for vision loss and guide early therapeutic interventions, ultimately improving patient outcomes. However, the lack of significant differences between NPDR vs. DM for the three implies that early-stage DR may not yet exhibit detectable dysregulation in these three miRNAs. These findings offer potential clinical applications, particularly in the field of early diagnosis and monitoring of diabetic retinopathy progression. The distinct expression patterns of miR-146a, miR-15a, and miR-200b in different stages of diabetic retinopathy suggest that these circulating miRNAs could serve as noninvasive biomarkers to stratify patients based on disease severity. Given the increasing need for accessible and affordable screening tools, especially in populations with limited access to retinal imaging, blood-based miRNA profiling may complement current diagnostic methods. Moreover, integrating these circulating miRNAs with routine clinical indicators such as HbA1c levels, duration of diabetes, and retinal imaging findings could enhance their clinical relevance as predictive biomarkers. The combined assessment of molecular and clinical data may provide a more accurate picture of disease progression and help identify patients at higher risk for developing advanced stages of DR, enabling earlier and more personalized management strategies. Furthermore, understanding the regulatory roles of these miRNAs in inflammation, angiogenesis, and metabolic dysfunction may help develop targeted therapies to halt or slow the progression of diabetic retinopathy. However, further studies in larger groups and over a longer period of time are necessary.

We acknowledge that our study has some limitations. Our sample of choice was blood since attaining samples from the retina is impossible, and also because we wanted to assess the potential of the three miRNAs as minimally invasive biomarkers. The use of blood samples is easy and non-invasive, its results may not accurately represent the expression profiles of miRNA within the retinal tissue. Further investigation is necessary to determine whether the circulating miRNAs is correlated with the tissue-specific expression in diabetic retinopathy. Additionally, our study was limited by a relatively small sample size, which may have reduced the statistical power to detect significant associations and increased the likelihood of Type II errors. There was also smaller sample size in the DM group compared to the NPDR and PDR groups. This imbalance reflects the clinical rarity of long-term diabetic individuals (≥ 12 years) who have not developed any form of retinopathy. Due to this low prevalence, recruiting such individuals is inherently difficult, and their underrepresentation may limit statistical power. Nonetheless, the use of REST software — a non-parametric method that is robust for small and unequal sample sizes — helps address this limitation in the analysis of qRT-PCR expression data.
