Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation Combined With Esophageal Pressure-Guided Mechanical Ventilation as Salvage Therapies in Postpneumonectomy Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome
Authors: Emmanuelle Guérin, Jade Roland, India Sourieau, Alex Arame, Juliette Didier, Alexandre Prieu, Bertrand Hermann, Nicolas Bréchot, Daniel Talmor, Jean-Luc Diehl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Massive hemoptysis and postpneumonectomy acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) are rare but life-threatening conditions with limited therapeutic options. This case highlights the feasibility of combining venovenous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO) with esophageal pressure-guided ventilation to support both surgical and respiratory management in a critically ill patient.CASE SUMMARY:A 67-year-old woman developed massive hemoptysis after bronchoscopic biopsy of a left hilar lung mass. Despite bronchial artery embolization and selective right lung ventilation, severe ARDS ensued. A salvage left pneumonectomy was performed under VV-ECMO due to refractory bleeding. Postoperatively, the patient developed right-lung ARDS with total airway closure and high elastance. Esophageal pressure monitoring enabled safe adjustment of ventilator settings. She was weaned from ECMO on postoperative day 16 and later discharged from ICU. Final pathology confirmed an atypical carcinoid tumor with negative margins.CONCLUSION:VV-ECMO can enable life-saving pneumonectomy in select patients. Esophageal pressure monitoring may optimize ventilation in postpneumonectomy ARDS with severe mechanical impairment.

KEY POINTS

Question: Can extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) combined with esophageal pressure-guided mechanical ventilation be used as a salvage strategy in severe postpneumonectomy acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)?

Findings: ECMO enabled ultraprotective ventilation, whereas esophageal pressure monitoring allowed individualized positive end-expiratory pressure titration adapted to single-lung physiology, resulting in improved oxygenation, and respiratory mechanics.

Meaning: Combining extracorporeal support with physiology-guided ventilation may represent a feasible rescue approach in complex ARDS cases after pneumonectomy when conventional ventilation strategies are insufficient.

Massive hemoptysis and postpneumonectomy acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) are both major clinical challenges. We report a unique case of left pneumonectomy performed under venovenous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO) in a severe ARDS patient who further benefited from esophageal pressure measurements.

A 67-year-old woman (body mass index [BMI] 27.9 kg/m2) was admitted for massive hemoptysis after undergoing bronchoscopic biopsy of a left mass involving both upper and lower lobes. A chest CT showed a 5.3-cm left hilar and sub-hilar pulmonary mass. Pulmonary function tests were normal, with negative staging evaluations.

Flexible bronchoscopy revealed a highly vascularized tumor. A biopsy triggered significant bleeding, initially controlled with cold saline and topical epinephrine. After laryngeal mask removal, the patient developed recurrent hemoptysis (~50 mL of bright red blood) and acute respiratory failure. She was intubated with selective right mainstem ventilation and required vasopressor support, fluid resuscitation, and adjunctive therapies including tranexamic acid. She was then transferred to the medical ICU. Bronchial artery embolization was performed shortly after admission.

By day 2 after admission, she met Berlin criteria for severe ARDS (Pao2/Fio2 nadir at 89 mm Hg), with bilateral infiltrates on chest radiography (1). Lung-protective ventilation (tidal volume 6 mL/kg, positive end-expiratory pressure [PEEP] set at 8 cm H2O), neuromuscular blockade, and prone positioning were initiated. A ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) caused by oropharyngeal flora was treated with appropriate antibiotics.

Attempts to resume bilateral ventilation repeatedly triggered recurrence of hemoptysis and severe desaturation, necessitating return to selective right lung ventilation. On day 7 after admission, after multidisciplinary discussion and due to refractory hemoptysis without effective bronchoscopic or radiological interventions, a salvage left pneumonectomy was considered and finally performed at day 11 under VV-ECMO, since the patient could not tolerate right lateral decubitus, making surgical access impossible without extracorporeal support. The patient then underwent successful left pneumonectomy and lymph node dissection through a left posterolateral thoracotomy. After pneumonectomy, anticoagulation was provided by unfractionated heparin titrated to anti-Xa levels.

Severe ARDS of the remaining lung progressively worsened until day 8 after pneumonectomy. Airway pressure release ventilation (APRV) was selected as part of our standardized lung-protective ventilation strategy under VV-ECMO support, aiming to maintain alveolar recruitment through sustained mean airway pressure (AP) while limiting driving pressure and cyclic lung stress, in line with current concepts of ultraprotective ventilation during VV-ECMO. The patient was ventilated with the following parameters: APRV mode, low AP: 10 cm H2O, with a respiratory system elastance of 200 cm H2O/L, respiratory rate of 20/min. The CareScape R860 ventilator (General Electric Healthcare, Madison, WI) indicated very low native lung oxygen consumption and CO2 production, with end-tidal expiratory CO2 partial pressure as low of 11 mm Hg. Chest x-ray displayed a near-complete loss of aeration of the remaining right lung.

The respiratory mechanics were therefore more deeply investigated under deep sedation, with the need of IV paralytics if necessary. We first recorded AP and volume signals during a low-flow inflation, by using the proximal D-Lite (+) captor of the ventilator and the OhmedaCom Research Tool software (General Electric Healthcare). We observed at day 8 postpneumonectomy very high respiratory system elastance values with a complete airway closure (Fig. 1A). Airway opening pressure (AOP) was approximately 12 cm H2O, measured near the Y-piece, thereby minimizing the effect of tubing compliance. A lower inflection point was identified by visual inspection near to 20 cm H2O by two independent experienced intensivists. No upper inflection point was observed until pressures as high as 40 cm H2O; raising the hypothesis of substantial pulmonary recruitment during inflation and/or of high pleural pressures protecting the lung for hyperdistention. We therefore planned to repeat the investigation at day 9 and to additionally monitor esophageal pressure in the semirecumbent position, as an indicator of pleural pressure (2). In the meantime, the low AP was gradually increased to 20 cm H2O. At day 9 postpneumonectomy, whereas the AP-volume curve displayed an improvement as compared to day 8 (Fig. 1B), a Nutrivent catheter (Sidam, Mirandola, Italy) was inserted for esophageal pressure measurements and calibrated at a pressure of 20 cm H2O, above the AOP (3, 4). Key ventilatory settings, respiratory mechanics, blood gas analyses, and VV-ECMO parameters over the clinical course are summarized in Table 2. The AP-volume curve displayed a further improvement at day 11, as well as corresponding chest x-ray (Fig. 1). The low AP was gradually increased from day 9 to day 14 postpneumonectomy. We tolerated a negative transpulmonary pressure from day 9 to day 11, aiming to obtain a safe balance between recruitment and overdistension. This was done in association with a careful hemodynamic monitoring and a close bronchoscopic control of bronchial sutures. Right ventricular (RV) function was simultaneously assessed by daily transthoracic echocardiography, including tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion measurement, tissue Doppler S′ velocity at the tricuspid annulus, evaluation of RV size, and screening for interventricular septal flattening, which showed transient RV dilation without systolic dysfunction. From day 14 after pneumonectomy, ventilator mode was switched to volume-assist control and PEEP value was gradually reduced to 8 cm H2O. ECMO was successfully weaned by day 16 postpneumonectomy, after 17 days of total support. A progressive decrease in esophageal pressure occurred until day 30 after pneumonectomy, where an end-expiratory value of 15 cm H2O was recorded.

No further hemoptysis occurred postoperatively. On day 5 after pneumonectomy, a new VAP was treated successfully. Gradual clinical and radiographic improvement followed, and invasive mechanical ventilation weaning was achieved at day 31. The patient was further discharged from ICU and from the hospital. Pathologic examination of the resected specimen confirmed a carcinoid tumor, with safe surgical margins. Written informed consent for publication was obtained from the patient.

This case confirms that, in selected patients, VV-ECMO can be a viable therapeutic option both for permitting salvage pneumonectomy and for management of very severe postpneumonectomy ARDS (5, 6). It also reports for the first time a total airway closure in a postpneumonectomy ARDS patient (7). Different phenomenon could have favor airway closure. It was difficult to formally distinguish between massive compressive atelectasis of the entire solitary lung and other mechanisms such as air-liquid interface creating high surface tension in the small airways inducing closure, as favored by surfactant depletion (8). The fact that the patient suffered from severe ARDS before pneumonectomy could explain a certain level of surfactant depletion, therefore favoring the later hypothesis. On the other side, we further observed both high chest wall elastance and esophageal pressures, making the first hypothesis also conceivable. The case also illustrates the potential of esophageal pressure monitoring in the specific context of postpneumonectomy ARDS, a condition associated with high mortality, even with the use of VV-ECMO (5, 9). Esophageal pressure monitoring provided a physiologic basis for setting expiratory and inspiratory pressures, aiming to obtain slightly positive transpulmonary pressure and to avoid overdistension in a highly noncompliant, postpneumonectomy lung. However, a crucial point was the ability of the measured esophageal pressure to reflect the mean pleural pressure, or at least the pleural pressure in the dependant and middle lung (10). Indeed, pneumonectomy patients are exposed to mediastinal shifts, which by itself can i) favor postpneumonectomy respiratory failure and ii) modify the relationship between the measured esophageal pressure and the mean pleural pressure surrounding the resting lung (11, 12). Notably, Pecchiari et al (11) found in an animal model without marked mediastinum distortion that transpulmonary pressure curves were unchanged by chest removal, suggesting a preserved ability of esophageal pressure to represent the mean pleural pressure. Accordingly, we checked both i) for lack of frank esophageal distortion on daily x-rays and further on CT studies and ii) for a positive occlusion test for all measurements (3, 4, 13). The mechanical profile was first characterized by a very high lung elastance, in relation with the consequences of massive hemoptysis, of pneumonectomy, of VAPs, of ventilator-induced lung injury and of important fluid overload. It also associated an increase in chest wall elastance most likely explained by the consequences of thoracic surgery. Indeed, it was important to distinguish as much as possible between the different components participating to chest wall elastance, that is, right and left chest walls, diaphragm and upper abdominal content. In the present case, we believe that the main contributing factors in the increases in chest elastance and pleural pressure were chest wall edema in a compliant mediastinum patient benefiting from an adequate postoperative management of the left thoracic space. Chest wall edema is classically attributable to thoracic surgery and could have been also favored by important fluid overloard. The mechanical profile was also characterized by high esophageal pressures, a profile frequently observed in morbid obesity ARDS patients (14, 15). In the present case, the relatively low BMI permitted to reasonably exclude major mechanical consequences of obesity. The observed mechanical profile led to an increase in expiratory pressures. Altogether, these data could support a broader application of esophageal pressure monitoring in postpneumonectomy ARDS patients, as well as in VV-ECMO ARDS patients, whatever the etiology (16).
