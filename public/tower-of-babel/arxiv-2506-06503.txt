Equivariant periodic cyclic homology for ample groupoids
Francesco Pagliuca, Christian Voigt

Abstract
. We define and study bivariant equivariant periodic cyclic homology for actions of ample groupoids. In analogy to the group case, we show that the theory satisfies homotopy invariance, stability, and excision in both variables. We also prove an analogue of the Green-Julg theorem for actions of proper groupoids.



1.
Introduction

Cyclic homology is a homology theory for algebras which was introduced independently by Connes and Tsygan. It can be viewed as an analogue of de Rham cohomology in the realm of noncommutative geometry
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
.
Groupoids, in turn, provide a vast range of examples of noncommutative spaces. Among other things, they arise naturally in topological dynamics and the classification of simple
C^{*}
-algebras, see for instance
[
15
]
.

The aim of this paper is to introduce a version of cyclic homology which takes into account groupoid symmetries. More precisely, we shall define and study bivariant equivariant periodic cyclic homology with respect to actions of ample Hausdorff groupoids on complex algebras. This is based on the Cuntz-Quillen approach to cyclic homology
[
11
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
13
]
, and the group equivariant cyclic theory introduced in
[
27
]
,
[
28
]
.

Our main motivation comes from topological dynamics. Matui’s HK-conjecture
[
17
]

asserts that the
K
-theory of a certain class of ample groupoids is given by groupoid homology in the sense of Crainic and Moerdijk
[
9
]
. This conjecture is known to hold in a range of cases, see for instance

[
16
]
,
[
17
]
,

[
14
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
6
]
.
As highlighted in
[
20
]
, it is natural to approach the HK-conjecture, and more generally the problem of calculating the
K
-theory of ample groupoids, through the Baum-Connes conjecture
[
23
]
. From this perspective, one may hope to gain insight into the
K
-theory of ample groupoids via a bivariant Chern character as in
[
30
]
. This is precisely where equivariant cyclic homology enters the picture.

An important feature of group equivariant cyclic homology is that the basic object of the theory, the equivariant
X
-complex, is not a chain complex in the usual sense of homological algebra. More precisely, the square of the differential of the equivariant
X
-complex fails to be zero. This failure is controlled in a precise way, and after passing to mapping complexes one obtains honest chain complexes. The same is true in the groupoid case, and for this reason we only define a generalisation of bivariant periodic cyclic homology, and not of ordinary cyclic homology.

We remark that the constructions in
[
28
]
, on which our work builds, are carried out in the category of bornological vector spaces. In the present paper we restrict ourselves to the purely algebraic framework of complex vector spaces without additional structure.
This makes the algebraic nature of the theory as transparent as possible. However, let us remark that the constructions carry over to the bornological setting with minimal changes.
In contrast, in order to treat more general classes of étale groupoids some of our techniques would need to be modified, and working with bornological vector spaces would then be the most natural starting point.

Let us now explain how this paper is organised. In section
2
we collect some background material on groupoids and their actions. This includes a review of basic properties of locally constant functions on totally disconnected locally compact Hausdorff spaces and balanced tensor products. Section
3
is an exposition of key facts about the category of
\mathcal{G}
-modules for an ample groupoid
\mathcal{G}
and its monoidal structure. In particular, we provide an alternative description of
\mathcal{G}
-modules in terms of what we call
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-comodules. We define
\mathcal{G}
-algebras, give some examples, and introduce the notion of a
\mathcal{G}
-anti-Yetter-Drinfeld module.
In section
4
we first review some facts about pro-categories and the concept of a paracomplex. We then introduce the main ingredients in the construction of equivariant cyclic homology, namely equivariant differential forms, the periodic tensor algebra, and the equivariant
X
-complex. After these preparations we state the definition of the bivariant equivariant cyclic homology groups
HP^{\mathcal{G}}_{*}(A,B)
for a pair of
\mathcal{G}
-algebras
A,B
. Section
5
is devoted to establishing fundamental properties of the resulting homology theory. We show that
HP^{\mathcal{G}}_{*}
is homotopy invariant with respect to smooth homotopies, stable under tensoring with algebras of finite rank operators on
\mathcal{G}
-modules with invariant pairings, and satisfies excision in both variables. The proofs are similar to the group equivariant case, but additional arguments are required in particular in connection with stability. Finally, in section
6
we establish an analogue in periodic cyclic homology of the Green-Julg theorem for the
K
-theory of proper groupoids, see
[
24
]
. Here we make use of localisation techniques, which allows us to reduce the assertion to the group equivariant case studied in
[
27
]
.

We conclude with some comments on notation and terminology. In this paper we work over the complex numbers, and we write
\otimes
for the algebraic tensor product of complex vector spaces. Throughout, by a locally compact space we mean a topological space with a locally compact Hausdorff topology. In particular, all groupoids are assumed to be Hausdorff.

2.1.
Groupoids

By definition, a groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is a small category in which all morphisms are invertible. We will frequently identify the set
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
of objects of
\mathcal{G}
with the set of identity morphisms via the unit map
u:\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
. The source and range maps are denoted by
s,r:\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, respectively. The
composition
m:\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
, given by
m(\alpha,\beta)=\alpha\beta
, is defined on the
set
\mathcal{G}^{(2)}=\{(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}\mid s(\alpha)=r(\beta)\}
of composable morphisms. Finally, the inverse map
i:\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
is given by
i(\alpha)=\alpha^{-1}
. We will use the notations
\mathcal{G}_{x}=s^{-1}(x),\mathcal{G}^{x}=r^{-1}(x)
and
\mathcal{G}^{y}_{x}=\mathcal{G}_{x}\cap\mathcal{G}^{y}
for
x,y\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. Moreover, if
U,V\subseteq\mathcal{G}
are subsets we write
UV=\{\alpha\beta\mid\alpha\in U,\beta\in V,s(\alpha)=r(\beta)\}
.

A topological groupoid is a groupoid
\mathcal{G}
such that
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
are equipped with topologies and all structure maps
u,s,r,m,i
are continuous. In this paper we will only consider topological groupoids which are Hausdorff and locally compact. A topological groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is étale if the range map
r
is a local homeomorphism. In this case
the unit map identifies
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
with an open subset of
\mathcal{G}
, and the maps
u,s,m
are local homeomorphisms as well.

Definition 2.1
.

An ample groupoid is an étale groupoid
\mathcal{G}
such that the base space
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is totally disconnected.

An open source section of an étale groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is an open subset
U\subseteq\mathcal{G}
such that the restriction of the source map induces a homeomorphism
U\rightarrow s(U)
.
Similarly one defines open range sections. An open bisection of
\mathcal{G}
is an open subset of
\mathcal{G}
which is both a source and a range section. Ample groupoids can be equivalently characterised as those étale groupoids for which the set of all compact open bisections forms a basis for the topology.

Let
X,Y,Z
topological spaces and let
p:X\rightarrow Z
and
q:Y\rightarrow Z
be continuous maps. The fibre product

X\times_{p,q}Y=\{(x,y)\in X\times Y\mid p(x)=q(y)\}

is equipped with the subspace topology from
X\times Y
. If
X,Y
are locally compact and
Z
is Hausdorff then
X\times_{p,q}Y
is again locally compact.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an étale groupoid and let
X
be a locally compact space. A left action of
\mathcal{G}
on
X
consists of continuous maps

(i)

\pi:X\rightarrow\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, called the anchor map,

(ii)

m:\mathcal{G}\times_{s,\pi}X\rightarrow X
, denoted
m(\alpha,x)=\alpha\cdot x
,

such that if
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{G}^{(2)}
and
(\alpha,x)\in\mathcal{G}\times_{s,\pi}X
then
(\beta,\alpha\cdot x)\in\mathcal{G}\times_{s,\pi}X
and
\beta\cdot(\alpha\cdot x)=(\beta\alpha)\cdot x
, and
u(\pi(x))\cdot x=x
for all
x\in X
.

One defines right actions of
\mathcal{G}
in an analogous fashion. A locally compact space with an action of
\mathcal{G}
will be called a
\mathcal{G}
-space.
A basic example of a left
\mathcal{G}
-space is
X=\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
with anchor map
\pi=\id
and the action
\alpha\cdot s(\alpha)=r(\alpha)
for
\alpha\in\mathcal{G}
.

Let
X
be a left
\mathcal{G}
-space
X
with anchor map
\pi
. One defines
\mathcal{G}\backslash X
as the quotient of
X
by the equivalence relation
\sim
such that
x\sim y
if and only if there exists an element
\alpha\in\mathcal{G}
such that
\pi(x)=s(\alpha)
and
y=\alpha\cdot x
. For an étale groupoid
\mathcal{G}
the projection map
X\rightarrow\mathcal{G}\backslash X
is always open, and the quotient topology on
\mathcal{G}\backslash X
is locally compact but not necessarily Hausdorff.

2.2.
Algebras and multipliers

By an algebra we shall mean an associative but not necessarily unital algebra over the complex numbers. We will typically work with algebras
A
which are essential in the sense that the multiplication map induces an isomorphism
A\otimes_{A}A\cong A
. We will also be mostly interested in algebras with a nondegenerate multiplication in the sense that
ab=0
for all
a\in A
implies
b=0
and
ab=0
for all
b\in A
implies
a=0
. Note that every unital algebra is essential and has a nondegenerate multiplication.

The algebraic multiplier algebra
M(A)
of an algebra of
A
consists of all two-sided multipliers
(L,R)
of
A
, compare for instance
[
26
, Appendix]
. By definition, a two-sided multiplier
(L,R)
of
A
is a pair of a left multiplier
L
, that is, a right
A
-linear map
L:A\rightarrow A
, and a right multiplier
R
, that is, a left
A
-linear map
R:A\rightarrow A
, such that
R(a)b=a(L(b))
for all
a,b\in A
. The vector space
M(A)
becomes a unital algebra via composition of maps and unit
1=(\id,\id)
. Every element
a\in A
defines a two-sided multiplier
\iota(a)=(L_{a},R_{a})
where
L_{a}(b)=ab,R_{a}(b)=ba
. If the multiplication in
A
is nondegenerate then the canonical homomorphism
\iota:A\rightarrow M(A)
is injective, in which case we identify
A
with a subalgebra of
M(A)
in this way.

A (left)
A
-module
M
is called essential if the canonical map
A\otimes_{A}M\rightarrow M
induced by the module action is an isomorphism. Note that in this case
AM
, the linear span of all elements
a\cdot m
for
a\in A
and
m\in M
, equals
M
.
An algebra homomorphism
f:A\rightarrow M(B)
is called essential if
B
becomes an essential left and right module via
f
.
If
A
is unital then an
A
-module
M
is essential iff it is unital in the sense that
1\cdot m=m
for all
m\in M
,
and an algebra homomorphism
f:A\rightarrow M(B)
is essential iff it is unital. For the following fact compare
[
26
, Proposition A.5]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
f:A\rightarrow M(B)
be an essential algebra homomorphism. If the multiplication in
B
is nondegenerate there exists a unique unital algebra homomorphism
F:M(A)\rightarrow M(B)
such that
F\iota=f
.

We say that an algebra
A
has local units if for every finite set of elements
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
of
A
there exists
e\in A
such that
ea_{i}=a_{i}=a_{i}e
for all
i
. The multiplication in such an algebra is clearly nondegenerate. Let us record the following well-known fact.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
A
be an algebra with local units. Then a left
A
-module is essential iff
AM=M
. A similar statement holds for right modules.

Proof.

We consider only the case of left modules. Let
M
be an essential left
A
-module and let
m\in M
. By assumption, there are elements
a_{i}\in A
and
m_{i}\in M
such that
\sum a_{i}\cdot m_{i}=m
. Since
A
has local units we find
e\in A
such that
ea_{i}=a_{i}
for all
i
, and hence the canonical map
m:A\otimes_{A}M\rightarrow M
sends
\sum_{i}e\otimes a_{i}\cdot m
to
m
. We conclude that
m
is surjective.

Now assume that
\sum a_{i}\otimes m_{i}\in A\otimes_{A}M
satisfies
\sum a_{i}\cdot m_{i}=0
. By assumption we find
e\in A
such that
ea_{i}=a_{i}
for all
i
and hence

\sum a_{i}\otimes m_{i}=\sum ea_{i}\otimes m_{i}=\sum e\otimes a_{i}m_{i}=0

in
A\otimes_{A}M
. This means that the canonical map is injectve.
∎

Note that Lemma
2.3
implies in particular that an algebra with local units is essential.

2.3.
Function spaces

Let
X
be a totally disconnected locally compact space. We define
C_{c}^{\infty}(X)

as the space of all locally constant functions
X\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
with compact support.
This is equal to the linear span of all characteristic functions
\chi_{K}
for compact open subsets
K\subseteq X
. More precisely, we have the following basic fact.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
X
be a totally disconnected locally compact space.
Then every element
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X)
can be written as a linear combination
f=\sum_{i}c_{i}\chi_{U_{i}}
for a finite family of pairwise disjoint compact open subsets
U_{k}\subseteq X
and coefficients
c_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

Since
f
is locally constant and the support of
f
is compact, the image
f(X)
is a finite subset of
\mathbb{C}
. If we denote by
c_{1},\dots c_{n}
the nonzero
elements of
f(X)
and set
U_{k}=f^{-1}(c_{k})
then each
U_{k}\subseteq X
is compact open, the sets
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
are pairwise disjoint, and we
have
f=\sum_{i}c_{i}\chi_{U_{i}}
.
∎

The vector space
C_{c}^{\infty}(X)
is naturally an algebra with pointwise multiplication. This algebra has local units,
and hence is essential, see the remark after Lemma
2.3
. We will write
C^{\infty}(X)
for the algebraic multiplier algebra of
C_{c}^{\infty}(X)
. This can be identified canonically with the algebra of all locally constant functions
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
.

Recall that a continuous map
\varphi:X\rightarrow Y
between locally compact spaces
X,Y
is proper iff
\varphi^{-1}(K)
is compact for every compact subset
K\subseteq Y
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
X,Y
be totally disconnected locally compact spaces and let
\varphi:X\rightarrow Y
be a continuous map. Then
\varphi^{*}:C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)\rightarrow C^{\infty}(X)=M(C_{c}^{\infty}(X)),\varphi^{*}(f)=f\circ\varphi
is a well-defined essential algebra homomorphism. If
\varphi
is proper then the image of
\varphi^{*}
is contained in
C_{c}^{\infty}(X)
.

Proof.

For
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)
the function
\varphi^{*}(f)=f\circ\varphi
is locally constant since it is the composition of a continuous function and a locally constant function. It follows that
\varphi^{*}:C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)\rightarrow C^{\infty}(X)=M(C_{c}^{\infty}(X))
is well-defined. Moreover this map is clearly an algebra homomorphism.

In order to show that
\varphi^{*}
is essential let
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X)
. Since
K=\operatorname{supp}(f)
is compact the same is true for
\varphi(K)
. We can
cover
\varphi(K)
by finitely many compact open subsets of
Y
, and if
\chi
denotes the characteristic function of the union of these sets then
f=\varphi^{*}(\chi)f

is contained in
\varphi^{*}(C_{c}^{\infty}(Y))C_{c}^{\infty}(X)
.

Finally, assume that
\varphi
is proper and let
g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)
. Then the preimage
\varphi^{-1}(K)
of the compact open set
K=\operatorname{supp}(g)
is compact open. If we write
e\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X)
for the characteristic function of
\varphi^{-1}(K)
then we get
\varphi^{*}(g)=\varphi^{*}(g)e=e\varphi^{*}(g)\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X)
as required because
\varphi^{*}
is essential.
∎

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
X,Y
be totally disconnected locally compact spaces. Then the canonical linear map

\gamma:C_{c}^{\infty}(X)\otimes C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(X\times Y),

given by
\gamma(f\otimes g)(x,y)=f(x)g(y)
, is an isomorphism.

Proof.

Assume
F=\sum f_{i}\otimes g_{i}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X)\otimes C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)
satisfies
\gamma(F)=0
. By Lemma
2.4
we can write each
f_{i}
as a linear combination of characteristic functions
\chi_{U_{ij}}
for mutually disjoint compact open subsets
U_{ij}\subseteq X
, and similarly each
g_{i}
as a linear combination of characteristic functions
\chi_{V_{ik}}
for mutually disjoint compact open subsets of
Y
. Upon taking intersections of these subsets, it follows that
F
can be written in the form
F=\sum_{k}c_{k}\chi_{U_{k}}\otimes\chi_{V_{k}}
, where
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
and
V_{1},\dots,V_{n}
are mutually disjoint compact open subsets of
X
and
Y
, respectively. Without loss of generality we may assume that these sets are all nonempty. For every index
k
pick
(x_{k},y_{k})\in U_{k}\times V_{k}
. Then the relation

0=\gamma(F)(x_{k},y_{k})=c_{k}\chi_{U_{k}}(x_{k})\chi_{V_{k}}(y_{k})=c_{k}

gives
c_{k}=0
. Hence
F=0
, and it follows that
\gamma
is injective.

To show surjectivity, it suffices to verify that the characteristic function
\chi_{W}
of an arbitrary compact open subset
W\subseteq X\times Y
is contained in the image of
\gamma
. For this it is enough to write
W
as a disjoint union of sets of the form
U\times V
where
U\subseteq X
and
V\subseteq Y
are compact open. In order to obtain such a decomposition of
W
, note first that since
X
and
Y
are totally disconnected and locally compact they both have a basis for their topology made up of compact open sets.
In particular, for every point
w=(x,y)\in W
we find compact open neighborhoods
U_{w}\subseteq X
of
x
and
V_{w}\subseteq Y
of
y
such that the rectangle
R_{w}=U_{w}\times V_{w}
is contained in
W
. Since
W
is compact we obtain a finite cover of
W
by rectangles
R_{w_{1}},\dots,R_{w_{n}}
for some
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\in W
. Upon taking intersections of the compact open sets
U_{w_{i}}
and
V_{w_{i}}
making up the rectangles
R_{w_{i}}
, we can refine this to a finite cover of
W
consisting of mutually disjoint compact open rectangles as required.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
X
be a totally disconnected locally compact space and let
K\subseteq X
be a closed subset. Then the canonical restriction map
C_{c}^{\infty}(X)\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(K)
, mapping
f
to
f_{\mid K}
, is surjective.

Proof.

For any given
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(K)
we have to construct a function
F\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X)
such that
F_{|K}=f
. Since every element of
C_{c}^{\infty}(K)
is a linear combination of characteristic functions it suffices to consider the case that
f=\chi_{U}
for some compact open set
U\subseteq K
. In this case, since
U
is open, there exists an open set
V\subseteq X
such that
V\cap K=U
. Using that
V
is open and
X
is totally disconnected we can write
V
as a union of compact open subsets of
X
. Since we have
U\subseteq V
, these sets are in particular an open cover of the compact set
U
. This means that we can find finitely many compact open subsets
W_{1},\dots,W_{n}\subseteq X
such that
W_{i}\subseteq V
for all
i
and the union
W
of the
W_{i}
satisfies
W\cap K=U
. It follows that the function
F=\chi_{W}
has the desired properties.
∎

Let
X,Y,Z
be totally disconnected locally compact spaces and let
p:X\rightarrow Z,q:Y\rightarrow Z
be continuous maps. Then
C_{c}^{\infty}(X)
and
C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)
become
C_{c}^{\infty}(Z)
-modules via the pullback homomorphisms
p^{*},q^{*}
, see Lemma
2.5
. The balanced tensor product of
C_{c}^{\infty}(X)
and
C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)
over
C_{c}^{\infty}(Z)
with respect to
p,q
is defined as the quotient

C_{c}^{\infty}(X)\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)=(C_{c}^{\infty}(X)\otimes C_{c}^{\infty}(Y))/R,

where
R
is the linear subspace spanned by all elements of the form
fp^{*}(h)\otimes g-f\otimes q^{*}(h)g
for
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X),g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)

and
h\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Z)
.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
X,Y,Z
be totally disconnected locally compact spaces and let
p:X\rightarrow Z,q:Y\rightarrow Z
be continuous maps. Then the canonical
C_{c}^{\infty}(Z)
-linear map

C_{c}^{\infty}(X)\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(X\times_{p,q}Y)

is an isomorphism.

Proof.

It is straightforward to check that the composition of the canonical homomorphism
\gamma:C_{c}^{\infty}(X)\otimes C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(X\times Y)
with the restriction homomorphism
C_{c}^{\infty}(X\times Y)\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(X\times_{p,q}Y)
factorises through
C_{c}^{\infty}(X)\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)
. We shall write
\gamma_{p,q}
for the resulting
C_{c}^{\infty}(Z)
-linear map
C_{c}^{\infty}(X)\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(X\times_{p,q}Y)
. Due to Proposition
2.6
and Lemma
2.7
the map
\gamma_{p,q}
is surjective,
and it remains only to show that
\gamma_{p,q}
is injective.

Assume that
F\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X)\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)
satisfies
\gamma_{p,q}(F)=0
. As explained in the proof of Proposition
2.6
, we can represent
F
as a linear combination
F=\sum_{k}c_{k}\chi_{U_{k}}\otimes\chi_{V_{k}}
where
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
and
V_{1},\dots,V_{n}
are mutually disjoint compact open subsets of
X
and
Y
, respectively. If there exists an index
k
and points
x\in U_{k},y\in V_{k}
such that
p(x)=q(y)
then
(x,y)\in X\mathrel{{\mathop{\times}\limits}}Y
and
c_{k}=c_{k}\chi_{U_{k}}(x)\chi_{V_{k}}(y)=\gamma_{p,q}(F)(x,y)=0
.
Therefore we may assume without loss of generality that
p(U_{k})\cap q(V_{k})=\emptyset
for all
k
. Using that
Z
is locally compact and hence regular we can then find compact open sets
E_{k}\subseteq Z
such that
p(U_{k})\subseteq E_{k}
and
E_{k}\cap q(V_{k})=\emptyset
for all
k
.
It follows that
e_{k}=\chi_{E_{k}}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Z)
satisfies
\chi_{U_{k}}\cdot e_{k}=\chi_{U_{k}}
and
e_{k}\cdot\chi_{V_{k}}=0
for all
k
. Hence we conclude

F=\sum_{k}c_{k}\chi_{U_{k}}\otimes\chi_{V_{k}}=\sum_{k}c_{k}\chi_{U_{k}}\cdot e_{k}\otimes\chi_{V_{k}}-c_{k}\chi_{U_{k}}\otimes e_{k}\cdot\chi_{V_{k}}=0

as required.
∎

2.4.
Proper groupoids

In this subsection we review some basic facts about proper groupoids, compare
[
21
]
,
[
24
]
.

Let us first recall that a (left) Haar system on an étale groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is a family
(\lambda^{x})_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}
of positive regular Borel measures
\lambda^{x}
on
\mathcal{G}
such that

(1)

the support of
\lambda^{x}
is
\mathcal{G}^{x}
for all
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
,

(2)

for every
f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
the function
\lambda(f):\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
given by

\lambda(f)(x)=\int_{\mathcal{G}^{x}}f(\beta)d\lambda^{x}(\beta)

is contained in
C_{c}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
,

(3)

we have

\int_{\mathcal{G}^{s(\alpha)}}f(\alpha\beta)d\lambda^{s(\alpha)}(\beta)=\int_{\mathcal{G}^{r(\alpha)}}f(\beta)d\lambda^{r(\alpha)}(\beta)

for all
f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
and
\alpha\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
.

Every étale groupoid
\mathcal{G}
admits a canonical Haar system
(\lambda^{x})_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}
given by the counting measures on the range fibers, so that

\int_{\mathcal{G}^{x}}f(\beta)\lambda^{x}(\beta)=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{G}^{x}}f(\beta)

for
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and
f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{G})
.
This Haar system behaves well with respect to integration of locally constant functions in the following sense.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an ample groupoid and let
f:\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a locally constant function such that
\operatorname{supp}(f)\cap r^{-1}(K)
is compact for all compact
sets
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. Then the function
\lambda(f):\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
defined by

\lambda(f)(x)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}^{x}}f(\alpha)

is locally constant.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and let
V
be a compact open neighbourhood of
x
. By assumption the set
\operatorname{supp}(f)\cap r^{-1}(V)\subseteq\mathcal{G}
is compact. Hence
g=f\chi_{r^{-1}(V)}
is contained in
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
. Writing
g
as a linear combination of characteristic functions of compact open bisections of
\mathcal{G}
it is straightforward to check that
\lambda(g)
is locally constant.
Since by construction the functions
\lambda(g)
and
\lambda(f)
agree on
V
it follows that
\lambda(f)
is locally constant in a neighborhood of
x
, and since
x
was arbitrary this yields the claim.
∎

Let us now come to the definition of properness for groupoids. Recall that we write
s,r
for the source and range maps, respectively.

Definition 2.10
.

An étale groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is called proper if
(s,r):\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\times\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is a proper map.

If
\mathcal{G}
is a proper étale groupoid then the quotient space
\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is Hausdorff,
and if
\mathcal{G}
is ample then
\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is a totally disconnected locally compact space.
The canonical quotient map
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
induces an embedding
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)})\rightarrow C^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
in this case. As a consequence, every essential
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-module becomes an essential
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-module in a canonical way.

Next we review the concept of a cut-off function for étale groupoids, compare
[
24
, Definition 6.7]
.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an étale groupoid. A cut-off function for
\mathcal{G}
is a continuous function
c:\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
such that

(1)

for every
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
we have
\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}^{x}}cs(\alpha)=1
,

(2)

the map
r:\operatorname{supp}(cs)\rightarrow\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is proper.

It is shown in
[
24
, Proposition 6.10 and Proposition 6.11]
that the existence of cut-off functions is closely related to properness.
The following result is a variant of
[
24
, Proposition 6.11]
.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a proper ample groupoid with
\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
paracompact. Then
\mathcal{G}
admits a locally constant cut-off function. If
\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is compact
then
\mathcal{G}
admits a locally constant cut-off function with compact support.

Proof.

The quotient
\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is a totally disconnected locally compact Hausdorff space. By assumption it is also paracompact, and hence can be written as a disjoint union of a family of open
\sigma
-compact totally disconnected locally compact Hausdorff spaces, see
[
4
, Section 9.10]
.
Every
\sigma
-compact totally disconnected locally compact space, in turn, can be written as a disjoint union of a countable family of compact open subsets.
As a consequence, there is a cover
(V_{i})_{i\in I}
of
\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
consisting of mutually disjoint compact open subsets.

Since the projection map
\pi:\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is open we can find
n_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
and compact open subsets
U_{i,1}\dots,U_{i,n_{i}}

of
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
for each
i\in I
such that
\pi(U_{ij})\subseteq V_{i}
for all
j
and
\pi(U_{i,1})\cup\cdots\cup\pi(U_{i,n_{i}})=V_{i}
.
Without loss of generality we can arrange the sets
U_{i,j}
to be mutually disjoint.
We then define
d:\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
by

d=\sum_{i\in I}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\chi_{U_{i,j}}.

By construction
d
is well-defined and locally constant. In fact,
d
is the characteristic function of the union of the sets
U_{i,j}
.

If
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is compact then
\pi(K)\cap V_{i}
is nonempty only for finitely many
i\in I
, and hence

\operatorname{supp}(d)\cap\pi^{-1}(\pi(K))
is compact. As a consequence,

\mathrm{supp}(ds)\cap r^{-1}(K)=(s\times r)^{-1}(\operatorname{supp}(d)\times K)=(s\times r)^{-1}(\operatorname{supp}(d)\cap\pi^{-1}(\pi(K))\times K)

is compact by properness of
\mathcal{G}
. According to Lemma
2.9
it follows that the function
\lambda(ds)
is locally constant.

Note that for every
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
there exists an index
i\in I
such that
\pi(x)\in V_{i}
. This implies that there exists some
1\leq j\leq n_{i}

and an element
\alpha\in\mathcal{G}^{x}
such that
s(\alpha)\in U_{i,j}
,
and we conclude that
\lambda(ds)(x)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}^{x}}d(s(\alpha))>0
.
It is then straightforward to check that
c(x)=d(x)/\lambda(ds)(x)
is a locally constant cut-off function for
\mathcal{G}
.

Finally, if
\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is compact then the index set
I
in the above construction can be taken to be a singleton, and then both
d
and
c
have compact support.
∎

3.
The category of
\mathcal{G}
-modules

In this section we discuss
\mathcal{G}
-modules for an ample groupoid
\mathcal{G}
and show that the category of
\mathcal{G}
-modules is monoidal. This allows us to introduce
\mathcal{G}
-algebras as algebra objects in this category. We also discuss the notion of a
\mathcal{G}
-anti-Yetter-Drinfeld module.

3.1.
\mathcal{G}
-modules

Let us write
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
for the Steinberg algebra of
\mathcal{G}
, that is, the vector space
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
equipped with the convolution product given by

(f*g)(\alpha)=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{G}^{r(\alpha)}}f(\beta)g(\beta^{-1}\alpha)=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}_{s(\alpha)}}f(\alpha\gamma^{-1})g(\gamma).

The algebra
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
has local units, given by the characteristic functions of compact open subsets of the unit space
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, and it is unital iff
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is compact, compare
[
22
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

A left
\mathcal{G}
-module is an essential left module over
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
. A linear map
f:M\rightarrow N
between left
\mathcal{G}
-modules is called
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant if it is
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
-linear.

One defines right
\mathcal{G}
-modules in the same way, namely as essential right modules over
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
.
We denote by
\mathcal{G}\operatorname{-Mod}
the category of essential left
\mathcal{G}
-modules and
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant linear maps. Since we will be mostly working with left modules, we refer to the objects of
\mathcal{G}\operatorname{-Mod}
simply as
\mathcal{G}
-modules.

Our first aim is to provide an alternative description of
\mathcal{G}
-modules, inspired by the discussion in
[
5
]
.
Consider the maps
d_{0},d_{1},d_{2}:\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}^{(1)}=\mathcal{G}
given by

d_{0}(\alpha,\beta)=\beta,\quad d_{1}(\alpha,\beta)=\alpha\beta,\quad d_{2}(\alpha,\beta)=\alpha

for
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{G}^{(2)}=\mathcal{G}\times_{s,r}\mathcal{G}
.
Each of these maps can be used to turn
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(2)})
into a
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-module with the action by pointwise multiplication, that is,

(f\cdot_{i}g)(\alpha,\beta)=f(\alpha,\beta)g(d_{i}(\alpha,\beta)).

Let us write
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(2)},d_{i})
for the resulting
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-module.
Then if
P,Q
are
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-modules and
T:P\rightarrow Q
is a
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-linear map we get induced linear maps

\id\otimes T:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(2)},d_{i})\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})}P\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(2)},d_{i})\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})}Q

for
i=0,1,2
. We will denote these maps by
d_{i}^{*}(T)
in the sequel, in order to distinguish the different module structures on
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(2)})
used in the construction.
Consider the special case
P=C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}M,\;Q=C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}M
for a
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-module
M
, where both
P
and
Q
are viewed as
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-modules with the action by pointwise multiplication in the first tensor factor. If we write

v_{0}=rd_{1}=rd_{2},\quad v_{1}=rd_{0}=sd_{2},\quad v_{2}=sd_{0}=sd_{1},

then we can view the tensor product maps
\id\otimes T
for
i=0,1,2
in this case as maps

\displaystyle d_{0}^{*}(T)

\displaystyle:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(2)})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}M\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(2)})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}M,

\displaystyle d_{1}^{*}(T)

\displaystyle:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(2)})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}M\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(2)})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}M,

\displaystyle d_{2}^{*}(T)

\displaystyle:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(2)})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}M\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(2)})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}M,

taking into account the canonical identifications of the respective tensor products. These identifications will be tacitly used in the following definition.

Definition 3.2
.

A
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-comodule is an essential
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-module
M
together with a
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-linear isomorphism

T_{M}:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}M\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}M

satisfying the coaction identity

d_{0}^{*}(T_{M})d_{2}^{*}(T_{M})=d_{1}^{*}(T_{M}).

A morphism of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-comodules is a
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-linear map
f:M\rightarrow N
such that
(\id\otimes f)T_{M}=T_{N}(\id\otimes f)
.

The terminology used here is inspired by the duality theory for actions of groups and quantum groups. We will write
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\operatorname{-Comod}
for the category of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-comodules.

Our aim is to show that
\mathcal{G}
-modules and
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-comodules are essentially the same thing. Let us first explain how to pass from
\mathcal{G}
-modules to
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-comodules. Consider
M=C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})=\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
as a left module over itself. The homeomorphism

t:\mathcal{G}\times_{s,r}\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}\times_{r,r}\mathcal{G},\qquad t(\alpha,\beta)=(\alpha,\alpha\beta)

induces a linear isomorphism
T:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
, given by

T(f)(\alpha,\beta)=f(\alpha,\alpha\beta)

for
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})=C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\times_{r,r}\mathcal{G})
. Note that
T
is right
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
-linear with respect to the multiplication action on the second tensor factor because
t
is right
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant. Moreover,
T
is
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-linear with respect to the pointwise multiplication action on the first tensor factor on both sides. We will also refer to
T
as the canonical map of
M=C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
.

Let us show that the canonical map turns
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})=\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
into a
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-comodule. To this end, note that for each
i=0,1,2
we have homeomorphisms

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\times_{d_{0},\pi}(\mathcal{G}\times_{s,r}\mathcal{G})\cong\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\times_{v_{2},r}\mathcal{G},

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\times_{d_{0},\pi}(\mathcal{G}\times_{r,r}\mathcal{G})\cong\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\times_{v_{1},r}\mathcal{G},

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\times_{d_{1},\pi}(\mathcal{G}\times_{s,r}\mathcal{G})\cong\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\times_{v_{2},r}\mathcal{G},

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\times_{d_{1},\pi}(\mathcal{G}\times_{r,r}\mathcal{G})\cong\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\times_{v_{0},r}\mathcal{G},

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\times_{d_{2},\pi}(\mathcal{G}\times_{s,r}\mathcal{G})\cong\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\times_{v_{1},r}\mathcal{G},

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\times_{d_{2},\pi}(\mathcal{G}\times_{r,r}\mathcal{G})\cong\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\times_{v_{0},r}\mathcal{G},

where
\pi
denotes the projection to the first copy of
\mathcal{G}
in either case.
Using these homeomorphisms we can identify the maps induced by
t
on these fibre products as

\displaystyle(\id\times_{d_{0},\pi}t)

\displaystyle:\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\times_{v_{2},r}\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\times_{v_{1},r}\mathcal{G},\quad(\id\times_{d_{0},\pi}t)(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(\alpha,\beta,\beta\gamma),

\displaystyle(\id\times_{d_{1},\pi}t)

\displaystyle:\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\times_{v_{2},r}\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\times_{v_{0},r}\mathcal{G},\quad(\id\times_{d_{1},\pi}t)(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(\alpha,\beta,\alpha\beta\gamma),

\displaystyle(\id\times_{d_{2},\pi}t)

\displaystyle:\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\times_{v_{1},r}\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\times_{v_{0},r}\mathcal{G},\quad(\id\times_{d_{2},\pi}t)(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(\alpha,\beta,\alpha\gamma).

From this description it is obvious that
(\id\times_{d_{2},\pi}t)(\id\times_{d_{0},\pi}t)=(\id\times_{d_{1},\pi}t)
. Since
d_{i}^{*}(T)
is the transpose of
\id\times_{d_{i},\pi}t
this yields the coaction identity
d_{0}^{*}(T)d_{2}^{*}(T)=d_{1}^{*}(T)
for
T
.

Now let
M
be an arbitrary
\mathcal{G}
-module. Then
M
is a non-degenerate
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-module with the restricted action along the inclusion
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
. Moreover, we obtain a
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-linear isomorphism
T_{M}:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}M\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}M

as the unique map fitting into the commutative diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}))\otimes_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})}M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}))\otimes_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})}M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}M}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T\otimes\id}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cong}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cong}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{M}}

Here we use the identification
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\otimes_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})}M\cong M
and right
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
-linearity of
T
.
From the construction, it is immediate that we obtain analogous commutative diagrams linking
d_{i}^{*}(T_{M})
and
d_{i}^{*}(T)\otimes\id
for
i=0,1,2
and hence the coaction identity

d_{0}^{*}(T_{M})d_{2}^{*}(T_{M})=d_{1}^{*}(T_{M})
holds. We will refer to
T_{M}
as the canonical map of
M
in the sequel.

These constructions are clearly compatible with
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant linear maps. That is, if
f:M\rightarrow N
is
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant, then it is a morphism of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-comodules with respect to the canonical maps
T_{M},T_{N}
.
In summary, we obtain the following result.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
M
be a
\mathcal{G}
-module. Then
M
becomes a
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-comodule via the canonical map
T_{M}
defined above. This assignment defines a functor
A:\mathcal{G}\operatorname{-Mod}\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\operatorname{-Comod}
.

Let us now discuss how to pass from
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-comodules to
\mathcal{G}
-modules. To this end consider the integration map
\lambda:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
defined by

\lambda(f)(x)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}^{x}}f(\alpha).

This map is
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-linear with respect to the action of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
on
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
induced by the range map
r
and the obvious multiplication action on
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
. Indeed, for
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}),h\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
we have

\lambda(h\cdot f)(x)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}^{x}}h(r(\alpha))f(\alpha)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}^{x}}h(x)f(\alpha)=h(x)\lambda(f)(x)=(h\cdot\lambda(f))(x)

as required. In fact, we have the following equivariance property.

Lemma 3.4
.

The integration map
\lambda:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
is
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant with respect to the left multiplication action on
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})=\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
.

Proof.

Let
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
and let
U\subseteq\mathcal{G}
be a compact open bisection. We calculate

\displaystyle\lambda(\chi_{U}*f)(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{G}^{x}}(\chi_{U}*f)(\beta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{G}^{x}}\chi_{U}(\alpha)f(\alpha^{-1}\beta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}^{x}}\chi_{U}(\alpha)\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}^{s(\alpha)}}f(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}^{x}}\chi_{U}(\alpha)\lambda(f)(s(\alpha))

\displaystyle=\chi_{U}\cdot\lambda(f)(x),

and this yields the claim.
∎

Now assume that
M
is a
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-comodule with canonical map
T_{M}
and define
\mu_{M}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\otimes M\rightarrow M
by the formula

\mu_{M}=(\lambda\otimes\id)T^{-1}_{M}q_{M},

where
q_{M}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\otimes M=C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\otimes M\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}M
is the quotient map.
Consider the diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(2)})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(2)})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(2)})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}M}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(T^{-1}_{M})_{13}}

Here

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T^{-1}\otimes\id}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d_{1}^{*}(T^{-1}_{M})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T^{-1}\otimes\id}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda\otimes\id\otimes\id}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\id\otimes T^{-1}_{M}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda\otimes\id\otimes\id}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T^{-1}_{M}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda\otimes\id}

where
\pi
is the projection onto the second factor. Observing that
(\lambda\otimes\id)T^{-1}(f\otimes g)=f*g=\mu(f\otimes g)
is the convolution product,
we obtain

(\lambda\otimes\id)(\id\otimes\lambda\otimes\id)d_{1}^{*}(T^{-1}_{M})(f\otimes g\otimes m)=\mu_{M}(\mu\otimes\id)(f\otimes g\otimes m)

for all
f,g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
and
m\in M
. Applying the coaction identity
d_{2}^{*}(T^{-1}_{M})d_{0}^{*}(T^{-1}_{M})=d_{1}^{*}(T^{-1}_{M})
we conclude that
\mu_{M}
turns
M
into a
left
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
-module. Since both
\lambda
and
q_{M}
are surjective we see that
\mu_{M}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\otimes M)=M
, and it follows that the resulting module structure is essential.

The construction of
\mu_{M}
is compatible with morphisms, that is, if
f:M\rightarrow N
is a morphism of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-comodules then
f
is
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant for the corresponding
\mathcal{G}
-module structures. This completes the proof of the following result.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
M
be a
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-comodule. Then
M
becomes a
\mathcal{G}
-module via the action
\mu_{M}
defined above. This assignment defines a functor
B:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\operatorname{-Comod}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}\operatorname{-Mod}
.

Upon combining Lemma
3.3
and Lemma
3.5
we are now ready to establish the correspondence between
\mathcal{G}
-modules and
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-comodules.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an ample groupoid. The constructions described above implement an isomorphism of categories between the category
\mathcal{G}\operatorname{-Mod}
of
\mathcal{G}
-modules and the category
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\operatorname{-Comod}
of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-comodules.

Proof.

We have already constructed functors
A:\mathcal{G}\operatorname{-Mod}\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\operatorname{-Comod}
and
B:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\operatorname{-Comod}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}\operatorname{-Mod}
, and it suffices to show that the compositions
BA
and
AB
equal the identity on
\mathcal{G}\operatorname{-Mod}
and
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\operatorname{-Comod}
, respectively.

For the
\mathcal{G}
-module
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
, the
\mathcal{G}
-module
BA(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G}))
is obtained by passing from the canonical map
T:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
to the
\mathcal{G}
-module structure
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\times\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
given by

(\lambda\otimes\id)T^{-1}q(f\otimes g)(\alpha)=(\lambda\otimes\id)T^{-1}(f\otimes g)(\alpha)=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{G}^{r(\alpha)}}f(\beta)g(\beta^{-1}\alpha)

for
f,g\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
and
\alpha\in\mathcal{G}
.
This coincides with the left multiplication action of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
on itself. It follows that
BA(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G}))=\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
as
\mathcal{G}
-modules. Using this, combined with the canonical identification
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\otimes_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})}M\cong M
, we see that we have in fact
BA(M)=M
as
\mathcal{G}
-modules for every
M\in\mathcal{G}\operatorname{-Mod}
.

Now let
N
be a
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-comodule with canonical map
T_{N}:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}N\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}N
. In order to describe the canonical map
T_{AB(N)}
we start from the module structure
\mu_{B(N)}=(\lambda\otimes\id)T^{-1}_{N}q_{N}
on
B(N)
. Then, by construction,

\displaystyle T^{-1}_{AB(N)}(\chi_{U}\otimes n)

\displaystyle=\chi_{U}\otimes\mu_{B(N)}(\chi_{U}\otimes n)

\displaystyle=\chi_{U}\otimes(\lambda\otimes\id)T^{-1}_{N}q_{N}(\chi_{U}\otimes n)

\displaystyle=\chi_{U}\otimes(\lambda\otimes\id)T^{-1}_{N}(\chi_{U}\otimes n)

for any compact open bisection
U\subseteq\mathcal{G}
and
n\in N
.
Let us write
T^{-1}_{N}(\chi_{U}\otimes n)=\sum_{i}\chi_{U_{i}}\otimes n_{i}
for compact open bisections
U_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
and elements
n_{i}\in N
. Since
T^{-1}_{N}
is
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-linear we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\chi_{U_{i}}\otimes n_{i}

\displaystyle=T^{-1}_{N}(\chi_{U}\otimes n)

\displaystyle=T^{-1}_{N}(\chi_{U}\chi_{U}\otimes n)

\displaystyle=\chi_{U}\cdot(\sum_{i}\chi_{U_{i}}\otimes n_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\chi_{U}\chi_{U_{i}}\otimes n_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\chi_{U\cap U_{i}}\otimes n_{i}.

Hence we can assume without loss of generality that
U_{i}\subseteq U
for all
i
.
With this understood, we calculate

\displaystyle T^{-1}_{AB(N)}(\chi_{U}\otimes n)

\displaystyle=\chi_{U}\otimes(\lambda\otimes\id)(\sum_{i}\chi_{U_{i}}\otimes n_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\chi_{U}\otimes\lambda(\chi_{U_{i}})n_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\chi_{U}\cdot\lambda(\chi_{U_{i}})\otimes n_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\chi_{U}\cdot\chi_{r(U_{i})}\otimes n_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\chi_{U\cap U_{i}}\otimes n_{i}

\displaystyle=T^{-1}_{N}(\chi_{U}\otimes n),

using that the action of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
on the left tensor factor is given by the range map in the penultimate step. Since
U
and
n
were arbitrary we conclude
T_{AB(N)}=T_{N}
as required.
∎

Theorem
3.6
can be phrased as saying that a
\mathcal{G}
-module
M
is the same thing as the data of an essential
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-module structure on
M
together with a
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-linear map
T_{M}:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}M\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}M
satisfying the coaction identity. This is how this result will be used in the sequel.

3.2.
Tensor products

The category
\mathcal{G}\operatorname{-Mod}
admits a natural tensor product operation. More precisely, if
M,N
are
\mathcal{G}
-modules then they are in particular essential
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-modules by restriction of the action, and we obtain a natural
\mathcal{G}
-module structure on the tensor product
M\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}N
.

We shall describe this action using the comodule picture developed in the previous section. According to Theorem
3.6
,
it suffices to define a
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-linear isomorphism

T_{M\otimes N}:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}(M\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}N)\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}(M\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}N),

satisfying the coaction identity.
We start with the case
M=N=\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
and consider the homeomorphism

t_{\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}}:\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{s,r}}\limits}}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{r,r}}\limits}}\mathcal{G})\rightarrow\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{r,r}}\limits}}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{r,r}}\limits}}\mathcal{G}),\quad t_{\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}}(\alpha,(\beta,\gamma))=(\alpha,(\alpha\beta,\alpha\gamma)).

With the notation used in the proof of Lemma
3.3
we obtain natural identifications

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\times_{d_{0},\pi}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{s,r}}\limits}}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{r,r}}\limits}}\mathcal{G}))\cong\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{v_{2},r}}\limits}}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{r,r}}\limits}}\mathcal{G}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\times_{d_{0},\pi}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{r,r}}\limits}}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{r,r}}\limits}}\mathcal{G}))\cong\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{v_{1},r}}\limits}}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{r,r}}\limits}}\mathcal{G}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\times_{d_{1},\pi}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{s,r}}\limits}}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{r,r}}\limits}}\mathcal{G}))\cong\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{v_{2},r}}\limits}}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{r,r}}\limits}}\mathcal{G}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\times_{d_{1},\pi}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{r,r}}\limits}}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{r,r}}\limits}}\mathcal{G}))\cong\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{v_{0},r}}\limits}}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{r,r}}\limits}}\mathcal{G}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\times_{d_{2},\pi}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{s,r}}\limits}}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{r,r}}\limits}}\mathcal{G}))\cong\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{v_{1},r}}\limits}}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{r,r}}\limits}}\mathcal{G}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\times_{d_{2},\pi}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{r,r}}\limits}}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{r,r}}\limits}}\mathcal{G}))\cong\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{v_{0},r}}\limits}}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{r,r}}\limits}}\mathcal{G}),

and one checks that the induced maps

\displaystyle\id\times_{d_{0},\pi}t_{\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}}

\displaystyle:\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{v_{2},r}}\limits}}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{r,r}}\limits}}\mathcal{G})\rightarrow\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{v_{1},r}}\limits}}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{r,r}}\limits}}\mathcal{G}),

\displaystyle\id\times_{d_{1},\pi}t_{\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}}

\displaystyle:\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{v_{2},r}}\limits}}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{r,r}}\limits}}\mathcal{G})\rightarrow\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{v_{0},r}}\limits}}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{r,r}}\limits}}\mathcal{G}),

\displaystyle\id\times_{d_{2},\pi}t_{\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}}

\displaystyle:\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{v_{1},r}}\limits}}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{r,r}}\limits}}\mathcal{G})\rightarrow\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{v_{0},r}}\limits}}(\mathcal{G}\mathrel{{\mathop{\times_{r,r}}\limits}}\mathcal{G}),

are given by

\displaystyle(\id\times_{d_{0},\pi}t_{\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}})(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)

\displaystyle=(\alpha,\beta,(\beta\gamma,\beta\delta)),

\displaystyle(\id\times_{d_{1},\pi}t_{\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}})(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)

\displaystyle=(\alpha,\beta,(\alpha\beta\gamma,\alpha\beta\delta)),

\displaystyle(\id\times_{d_{2},\pi}t_{\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}})(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)

\displaystyle=(\alpha,\beta,(\alpha\gamma,\alpha\delta)),

respectively. It follows that the linear isomorphism

\displaystyle T_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\otimes\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})}:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}(C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}))

\displaystyle\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}(C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})),

\displaystyle T_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\otimes\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})}(f)(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)

\displaystyle=f(\alpha,\alpha\beta,\alpha\gamma)

induced by transposition of
t_{\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}}
satisfies

d_{0}^{*}(T_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\otimes\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})})d_{2}^{*}(T_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\otimes\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})})=d_{1}^{*}(T_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\otimes\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})})

as required. We also note that
T_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\otimes\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})}
is right
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\otimes\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
-linear with respect to the action on the second and third tensor factors.

Now let
M,N
be arbitrary
\mathcal{G}
-modules. Then the canonical isomorphism

(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\otimes\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G}))\otimes_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\otimes\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})}(M\otimes N)\rightarrow M\otimes N

induces a linear isomorphism

(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G}))\otimes_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\otimes\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})}(M\otimes N)\rightarrow M\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}N.

Using the left
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
-action on
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})=\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
corresponding to the
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-comodule structure constructed above we obtain the desired
\mathcal{G}
-module structure on
M\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}N
through this identification.

For calculations it is useful to know how the tensor product
\mathcal{G}
-module structure looks in terms of compact open bisections.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
M,N
be
\mathcal{G}
-modules. Then the
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
-module structure on the tensor product
M\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}N
satisfies

\chi_{U}\cdot(m\otimes n)=(\chi_{U}\cdot m)\otimes(\chi_{U}\cdot n),

for any compact open bisection
U
of
\mathcal{G}
.

Proof.

In view of the construction of the tensor product action it suffices to consider the case
M=N=\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
. Given compact open bisections
U,V,W
of
\mathcal{G}
and
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
such that
r(\alpha)=r(\beta)=r(\gamma)
we compute

\displaystyle T^{-1}_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\otimes\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})}(\chi_{U}\otimes\chi_{V}\otimes\chi_{W})(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)

\displaystyle=(\chi_{U}\otimes\chi_{V}\otimes\chi_{W})(\alpha,\alpha^{-1}\beta,\alpha^{-1}\gamma)

\displaystyle=\chi_{U}(\alpha)\chi_{V}(\alpha^{-1}\beta)\chi_{W}(\alpha^{-1}\gamma).

It follows that
T^{-1}_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\otimes\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})}(\chi_{U}\otimes\chi_{V}\otimes\chi_{W})
is the characteristic function of the set
U\times UV\times UW
.
Applying the integration map
\lambda
to the first tensor factor therefore gives

\chi_{U}\cdot(\chi_{V}\otimes\chi_{W})=\chi_{UV}\otimes\chi_{UW}=(\chi_{U}\cdot\chi_{V})\otimes(\chi_{U}\cdot\chi_{W}),

and since characteristic functions of compact open bisections span
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
this yields the claim.
∎

We will always view the tensor product
M\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{0})}N
of
\mathcal{G}
-modules
M,N
as a
\mathcal{G}
-module with the action defined above. Let us summarise our discussion as follows.

Proposition 3.8
.

The category
\mathcal{G}\mbox{-}\operatorname{Mod}
with the tensor product operation defined above is a monoidal category.

Proof.

Let
M,N
and
P
be
\mathcal{G}
-modules. Then we have a canonical isomorphism

(M\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}N)\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}P\cong M\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}N\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}P\cong M\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}(N\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}P)

of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-modules, and using Lemma
3.7
we see that the action on either side is given by

\chi_{U}\cdot(m\otimes n\otimes p)=(\chi_{U}\cdot m)\otimes(\chi_{U}\cdot n)\otimes(\chi_{U}\otimes p)

for
m\in M,n\in N,p\in P
. We conclude that the above isomorphism is
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant, thus giving the required associativity constraint.

The tensor unit is given by the trivial
\mathcal{G}
-module
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
, with the action
f\cdot h=\lambda(f)h
for
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G}),h\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
. Note here that the maps
\lambda\otimes\id,\id\otimes\lambda:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
satisfy

(\id\otimes\lambda)(\chi_{U}\otimes\chi_{U})=(\lambda\otimes\id)(\chi_{U}\otimes\chi_{U})=\lambda(\chi_{U})*\chi_{U}=\chi_{U}

for any compact open bisection
U
of
\mathcal{G}
.
Due to Lemma
3.7
it follows that the canonical identifications
M\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})\cong M\cong C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{0})}M
are
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant for every
\mathcal{G}
-module
M
.

With these structures in place, the axioms for a monoidal category are readily verified.
∎

3.3.
\mathcal{G}
-algebras

Our main object of study in this paper are
\mathcal{G}
-algebras over an ample groupoid
\mathcal{G}
in the following sense.

Definition 3.9
.

A
\mathcal{G}
-algebra is a
\mathcal{G}
-module
A
together with a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant linear map
m:A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}A\rightarrow A
, written
m(a\otimes b)=ab
, such that
(ab)c=a(bc)
for all
a,b,c\in A
.

This can be phrased equivalently as saying that a
\mathcal{G}
-algebra is a non-unital algebra object in the monoidal category
\mathcal{G}\mbox{-}\operatorname{Mod}
.

By definition, if
A,B
are
\mathcal{G}
-algebras then a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant algebra homomorphism
\phi:A\rightarrow B
is a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant linear map of the underlying
\mathcal{G}
-modules such that
\phi(ab)=\phi(a)\phi(b)
for all
a,b\in A
.

Let us list some examples and constructions with
\mathcal{G}
-algebras.

3.3.1.
Algebras of functions

We obtain examples of commutative
\mathcal{G}
-algebras from actions of
\mathcal{G}
on totally disconnected locally compact spaces.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
X
be a totally disconnected locally compact
\mathcal{G}
-space. Then
C_{c}^{\infty}(X)
with pointwise multiplication is a
\mathcal{G}
-algebra in a natural way.

Proof.

Let us denote the anchor map of
X
by
\pi
. Then we obtain a
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-linear map

T_{C_{c}^{\infty}(X)}:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}C_{c}^{\infty}(X)\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}C_{c}^{\infty}(X)

by setting

T_{C_{c}^{\infty}(X)}(f)(\alpha,x)=f(\alpha,\alpha\cdot x).

An argument analogous to the discussion before Lemma
3.3
shows that this map satisfies the coaction identity, thus turning
C_{c}^{\infty}(X)
into a
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-comodule. Hence the claim follows from Proposition
3.6
.
∎

The construction in Proposition
3.10
is compatible with tensor products. More precisely, if
X,Y
are totally disconnected locally compact
\mathcal{G}
-spaces, with anchor maps
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
, respectively, then
the canonical map

C_{c}^{\infty}(X)\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(X\times_{\pi_{1},\pi_{2}}Y)

is an isomorphism of
\mathcal{G}
-algebras, compare Proposition
2.8
.

3.3.2.
Algebras associated with pairings

Another class of examples of
\mathcal{G}
-algebras comes from
\mathcal{G}
-modules equipped with
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant pairings in the following sense.

Definition 3.11
.

Let
E
be a
\mathcal{G}
-module. A
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant pairing on
E
is a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant linear map
h:E\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}E\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
.

We may equivalently view a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant pairing as in Definition
3.11
as a
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-bilinear map
h:E\times E\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
such that

h(\chi_{U}\cdot e,\chi_{U}\cdot f)=\chi_{U}\cdot h(e,f)

for all
e,f\in E
and all compact open bisections
U\subseteq\mathcal{G}
. Given such a pairing, consider the tensor product

\mathcal{K}(E)=E\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}E

as a
\mathcal{G}
-module with the diagonal action. Then
\mathcal{K}(E)
becomes a
\mathcal{G}
-algebra with the multiplication defined by

\displaystyle(e_{1}\otimes f_{1})(e_{2}\otimes f_{2})=e_{1}\otimes h(f_{1},e_{2})f_{2}=e_{1}h(f_{1},e_{2})\otimes f_{2}

for
e_{1},e_{2},f_{1},f_{2}\in E
. Note that the multiplication in
\mathcal{K}(E)
depends on the pairing
h
, so it would be more accurate to write
\mathcal{K}(E,h)
for the resulting
\mathcal{G}
-algebra. However, in the sequel the pairings we use will always be clear from the context.

The most important example of a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant pairing is the
regular pairing

\lambda:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\times\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
defined by

\displaystyle\lambda(f,g)(x)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}^{x}}f(\alpha)g(\alpha),

for
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. We will simply write
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{G}}=\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G}))
for the associated
\mathcal{G}
-algebra.

3.3.3.
C_{c}^{\infty}(X)
-algebras

Given an algebra
A
we write
ZM(A)
for the center of the multiplier algebra of
A
.

Definition 3.12
.

Let
X
be a totally disconnected locally compact space. A
C_{c}^{\infty}(X)
-algebra is an algebra
A
together with an essential homomorphism
C_{c}^{\infty}(X)\rightarrow M(A)
which takes values in
ZM(A)
.

Let us record the following obseration, in analogy to the study of groupoid actions in the
C^{*}
-algebra setting.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an ample groupoid and let
A
be a
\mathcal{G}
-algebra. If the multiplication in
A
is nondegenerate then
A
is canonically a
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-algebra.

Proof.

The underlying
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-module structure of
A
determines an essential homomorphism
\iota:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})\rightarrow M(A)
such that
\iota(f)a=f\cdot a
for
a\in A
. If
m\in M(A)
is an arbitrary multiplier and
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
we
get
am\iota(f)b=amf\cdot b=f\cdot(amb)=af\cdot(mb)=a\iota(f)mb
for all
a,b\in A
, and using nondegeneracy of the multiplication we conclude
that
\iota(f)
is contained in
ZM(A)
.
∎

We also note that if the ample groupoid
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}^{(0)}=X
is obtained by viewing a totally disconnected locally compact space
X
as a groupoid then every
C_{c}^{\infty}(X)
-algebra is canonically a
\mathcal{G}
-algebra.

3.3.4.
Unitarisation

By a unital
\mathcal{G}
-algebra object we shall mean a unital algebra object in the category of
\mathcal{G}
-modules, that is, a
\mathcal{G}
-algebra
A
in the sense of Definition
3.9
together with a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant homomorphism
u:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})\rightarrow A
such that
u(f)a=au(f)=f\cdot a
for
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)}),a\in A
.
A
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant algebra homomorphism between unital
\mathcal{G}
algebra objects is called unital if it commutes with the unit maps in the obvious way.

A basic example of a unital
\mathcal{G}
-algebra object is
A=C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
with the trivial
\mathcal{G}
-action and
u=\id
. This example shows already that a unital
\mathcal{G}
-algebra object does not need to have a unit element in general. For this reason, we speak of unital
\mathcal{G}
-algebra objects and not of unital
\mathcal{G}
-algebras.

Definition 3.14
.

Let
A
be a
\mathcal{G}
-algebra. The
\mathcal{G}
-unitarisation of
A
is defined as

A^{+}=A\oplus C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})

viewed as
\mathcal{G}
-module with the given action on
A
and the trivial action on
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
, and the multiplication given by

(a,f)\cdot(b,g)=(ab+g\cdot a+f\cdot b,fg)

for
a,b\in A
and
f,g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
. Here the dot product denotes the
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-structure on
A
induced from its
\mathcal{G}
-module structure.

Let us write
\Alg_{\mathcal{G}}(A,B)
for the set of all
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant algebra homomorphisms between
\mathcal{G}
-algebras
A,B
. If
A,B
are unital
\mathcal{G}
-algebra objects then we denote by
\Alg^{u}_{\mathcal{G}}(A,B)
the set of all unital
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant algebra homomorphisms.

With this notation in place, let us show that the
\mathcal{G}
-unitarisation of a
\mathcal{G}
-algebra satisfies the following universal property.

Lemma 3.15
.

Let
A
be a
\mathcal{G}
-algebra. Then
A^{+}
is a unital
\mathcal{G}
-algebra object, and there is a natural bijection

\Alg^{u}_{\mathcal{G}}(A^{+},B)\cong\Alg_{\mathcal{G}}(A,B)

for every unital
\mathcal{G}
-algebra object
B
.

Proof.

By construction, the embedding into the first summand is a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant homomorphism
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})\rightarrow A^{+}
which turns
A^{+}
into a unital
\mathcal{G}
-algebra object.

If
\phi:A^{+}\rightarrow B
is a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant unital algebra homomorphism then the restriction of
\phi
to
A\subset A^{+}
defines a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant algebra homomorphism
\phi:A\rightarrow B
. Conversely, if
\psi:A\rightarrow B
is a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant algebra homomorphism then we obtain a unital
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant algebra homomorphism
\psi^{+}:A^{+}\rightarrow B
by setting

\psi^{+}(a,f)=\psi(a)+u(f)

where
u:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})\rightarrow B
is the unit morphism.

It is straightforward to check that these assignments determine mutually inverse bijections as claimed.
∎

3.3.5.
Crossed products

The algebraic crossed product
A\rtimes\mathcal{G}
of a
\mathcal{G}
-algebra
A
can be defined in a similar way as for discrete groups.

More precisely, we define
A\rtimes\mathcal{G}=A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
as a vector space, with the left action of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
on
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
given via the range map. The multiplication in
A\rtimes\mathcal{G}
is determined by

(a\otimes\chi_{U})(b\otimes g)=a\chi_{U}\cdot b\otimes\chi_{U}*g

for
a,b\in A
, compact open bisections
U\subseteq\mathcal{G}
and
g\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
. It is straightforward to check that this turns
A\rtimes\mathcal{G}
into an algebra.

The crossed product admits algebra homomorphisms
i_{A}:A\rightarrow M(A\rtimes\mathcal{G})
and
i_{\mathcal{G}}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\rightarrow M(A\rtimes\mathcal{G})
such that
i_{A}(a)i_{\mathcal{G}}(f)=a\otimes f

for all
a\in A,f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
.
By definition, a covariant representation of
(A,\mathcal{G})
on an algebra
B
is a pair of essential homomorphisms
\phi:A\rightarrow M(B),\pi:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\rightarrow M(B)
such that
\phi(f\cdot a)\pi(g)=\phi(a)\pi(f*g)
for all
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)}),a\in A,g\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
and

\phi(\chi_{U}\cdot a)\pi(\chi_{U})=\pi(\chi_{U})\phi(a)

for all compact open bisections
U\subseteq\mathcal{G}
and
a\in A
. Clearly the maps
i_{A},i_{\mathcal{G}}
define a covariant representation of
(A,\mathcal{G})
on
A\rtimes\mathcal{G}
.

Proposition 3.16
.

Let
A
be a
\mathcal{G}
-algebra. The crossed product
A\rtimes\mathcal{G}
is universal for covariant representations of
(A,\mathcal{G})
, that is, for every algebra
B
and every covariant representation
(\phi,\pi)
of
(A,\mathcal{G})
on
B
there exists a unique essential algebra homomorphism
\psi:A\rtimes\mathcal{G}\rightarrow M(B)
such that
\phi=\psi i_{A}

and
\pi=\psi i_{\mathcal{G}}
.

Proof.

We define
\psi(a\otimes f)=\phi(a)\pi(f)
. This gives a well-defined linear map
A\rtimes\mathcal{G}\rightarrow M(B)
by the first part of the covariance condition. Using the second part of the covariance condition we calculate

\displaystyle\psi(a\otimes\chi_{U})\psi(b\otimes\chi_{V})

\displaystyle=\phi(a)\pi(\chi_{U})\phi(b)\pi(\chi_{V})

\displaystyle=\phi(a)\phi(\chi_{U}\cdot b)\pi(\chi_{U})\pi(\chi_{V})=\psi((a\otimes\chi_{U})(b\otimes\chi_{V}))

for
a,b\in A
and all compact open bisections
U,V\subseteq\mathcal{G}
, and it follows that
\psi
is a homomorphism. It is straightforward to check that
\psi
is essential, satisfying
\phi=\psi i_{A},\pi=\psi i_{\mathcal{G}}
, and since
A\rtimes\mathcal{G}=i_{A}(A)i_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G}))
it is uniquely determined.
∎

3.4.
Anti-Yetter-Drinfeld modules

Consider the set of loops

\mathcal{G}_{ad}=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}\mid r(\alpha)=s(\alpha)\}

in
\mathcal{G}
. This is a subgroupoid of
\mathcal{G}
with the same objects, often called the isotropy subgroupoid or inertia groupoid.
Noting that
\mathcal{G}_{ad}=(s,r)^{-1}(\Delta)
, where

\Delta\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\times\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is the diagonal, we see that
\mathcal{G}_{ad}
is a closed subset of
\mathcal{G}
, and thus a totally disconnected locally compact space with the subspace topology.

Due to Lemma
2.7
, a function
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}_{ad})
can be represented as a linear combination of restrictions to
\mathcal{G}_{ad}
of characteristic functions of compact open bisections of
\mathcal{G}
.
We will often view the characteristic function
\chi_{U}
of a compact open bisection
U\subseteq\mathcal{G}
as an element of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}_{ad})
in this way.

It is straightforward to check that
\mathcal{G}_{ad}
is a
\mathcal{G}
-space with the adjoint action

\alpha\cdot\beta=\alpha\beta\alpha^{-1}

for
\alpha\in\mathcal{G},\beta\in\mathcal{G}_{ad}
, with anchor map
\pi=r=s:\mathcal{G}_{ad}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. According to Lemma
3.10
we therefore obtain a natural
\mathcal{G}
-algebra structure on
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}_{ad})
. We will write
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}}
for this
\mathcal{G}
-algebra in the sequel.

Definition 3.17
.

A
\mathcal{G}
-anti-Yetter-Drinfeld module is a
\mathcal{G}
-module
M
which is also an essential
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}}
-module such that the module action induces a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant linear map
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}}\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}M\rightarrow M
. A morphism of
\mathcal{G}
-anti-Yetter-Drinfeld modules is a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant linear map which is also
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}}
-linear.

The terminology used here is motivated from the theory of quantum groups, compare
[
29
]
.
A basic example of a
\mathcal{G}
-anti-Yetter-Drinfeld module is obtained by considering
M=\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}}\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}E
for a
\mathcal{G}
-module
E
, with the diagonal action of
\mathcal{G}
and the action of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}}
by multiplication on the first tensor factor.

One can view
\mathcal{G}
-anti-Yetter-Drinfeld modules equivalently as essential modules over the crossed product
A(\mathcal{G})=\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}}\rtimes\mathcal{G}
. This observation is a special case of Proposition
3.16
, note that both
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}}
and
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
are subalgebras of the multiplier algebra
M(A(\mathcal{G}))
, and composition with the inclusion maps gives the asserted equivalence. We will frequently use this identification between
\mathcal{G}
-anti-Yetter-Drinfeld modules and
A(\mathcal{G})
-modules in the sequel.

Given a
\mathcal{G}
-anti-Yetter-Drinfeld module
M
our goal is to define a certain canonical automorphism
T=T_{M}:M\rightarrow M
. We start with
M=A(\mathcal{G})=\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}}\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})=C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}_{ad}\times_{\pi,r}\mathcal{G})
, in which case we define
T
by the formula

T(f)(\alpha,\beta)=f(\alpha,\alpha\beta)

for
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}_{ad}\times_{\pi,r}\mathcal{G})
, in a similar way as in the discussion of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-comodules.

Lemma 3.18
.

The map
T:A(\mathcal{G})\rightarrow A(\mathcal{G})
defined above is an isomorphism of
A(\mathcal{G})
-bimodules.

Proof.

It is clear that
T
is bijective with inverse given by
T^{-1}(f)(\alpha,\beta)=f(\alpha,\alpha^{-1}\beta)
. The left and right
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}}
-module structures on
A(\mathcal{G})
are given by

(h\cdot f)(\alpha,\beta)=h(\alpha)f(\alpha,\beta),\qquad(f\cdot h)(\alpha,\beta)=h(\beta^{-1}\alpha\beta)f(\alpha,\beta)

for
h\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}},f\in A(\mathcal{G})
. We thus obtain

\displaystyle(h\cdot T(f))(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=h(\alpha)f(\alpha,\alpha\beta)=(h\cdot f)(\alpha,\alpha\beta)=T(h\cdot f)(\alpha,\beta)

and

\displaystyle(T(f)\cdot h)(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=h(\beta^{-1}\alpha\beta)f(\alpha,\alpha\beta)=(f\cdot h)(\alpha,\alpha\beta)=T(f\cdot h)(\alpha,\beta),

which shows that
T
is both left and right
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}}
-linear.
For
g\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
we have

\displaystyle(g\cdot T(f))(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}^{r(\alpha)}}g(\gamma)f(\gamma^{-1}\alpha\gamma,\gamma^{-1}\alpha\gamma\gamma^{-1}\beta)=T(g\cdot f)(\alpha,\beta)

and

\displaystyle(T(f)\cdot g)(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}_{s(\beta)}}f(\alpha,\alpha\beta\gamma^{-1})g(\gamma)=T(f\cdot g)(\alpha,\beta),

and it follows that
T
is left and right
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
-linear. Combining these observations yields the claim.
∎

In view of Lemma
3.18
we can define
T_{M}:M\rightarrow M
for a
\mathcal{G}
-anti-Yetter-Drinfeld module
M
by

T_{M}=m_{M}(T\otimes\id)m_{M}^{-1},

where
m_{M}:A(\mathcal{G})\otimes_{A(\mathcal{G})}M\rightarrow M
is the canonical isomorphism. This defines an automorphism of the
\mathcal{G}
-anti-Yetter-Drinfeld module
M
.

Lemma 3.19
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an ample groupoid and let
\phi:M\rightarrow N
be a morphism of
\mathcal{G}
-anti-Yetter-Drinfeld modules. Then
T_{N}\phi=\phi T_{M}
.

Proof.

Using the canonical isomorphisms and
m_{N}(\id\otimes\phi)=\phi m_{M}
we compute

\displaystyle T_{N}\phi

\displaystyle=m_{N}(T\otimes\id)(\id\otimes\phi)m_{M}^{-1}=m_{N}(\id\otimes\phi)(T\otimes\id)m_{M}^{-1}=\phi T_{M}

as required.
∎

In calculations it is useful to have an explicit formula for the action of the canonical automorphism. We will only need this for
\mathcal{G}
-anti-Yetter-Drinfeld modules of the form
M=\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}}\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}E
for a
\mathcal{G}
-module
E
, in which case we get

T_{M}(\chi_{U}\otimes e)=\chi_{U}\otimes\chi_{U^{-1}}\cdot e

for any compact open bisection
U\subseteq\mathcal{G}
and
e\in E
.

4.
Equivariant periodic cyclic homology

In this section we define bivariant equivariant periodic cyclic homology with respect to an ample groupoid
\mathcal{G}
which is fixed throughout.

4.1.
Projective systems

Following Cuntz and Quillen
[
13
]
, in the sequel we will not only consider
\mathcal{G}
-algebras but work in greater generality with pro-
\mathcal{G}
-algebras. This s convenient when it comes to discussing quasifreeness, even if one is only interested in
\mathcal{G}
-algebras. Accordingly, we shall consider projective systems of
\mathcal{G}
-modules
and
\mathcal{G}
-anti-Yetter-Drinfeld modules. We briefly discuss these concepts here and fix our notation, and refer
to
[
13
]
,
[
27
]
,
[
28
]
for more details.

To an additive category
\mathcal{C}
one associates the pro-category
\mathrm{pro}(\mathcal{C})
of projective systems over
\mathcal{C}
as follows. A projective system
over
\mathcal{C}
consists of a directed index set
I
, objects
V_{i}
for all
i\in I
, and morphisms
p_{ij}:V_{j}\rightarrow V_{i}
for all
j\geq i
. These
morphisms are assumed to satisfy
p_{ij}p_{jk}=p_{ik}
if
k\geq j\geq i
. By definition, the objects of
\mathrm{pro}(\mathcal{C})
are projective systems over
\mathcal{C}
.
The space of morphisms in
\mathrm{pro}(\mathcal{C})
between
(V_{i})_{i\in I}
and
(W_{j})_{j\in J}
is defined by

\Mor((V_{i}),(W_{j}))=\varprojlim_{j}\varinjlim_{i}\Mor_{\mathcal{C}}(V_{i},W_{j}),

where the limits are taken in the category of abelian groups. Any object of
\mathcal{C}
can be viewed as a constant pro-object, and this gives rise to a fully faithful embedding
of
\mathcal{C}
into
\mathrm{pro}(\mathcal{C})
. Moreover the category
\mathrm{pro}(\mathcal{C})
is again additive in a canonical way.

If we apply these general constructions to the category of
\mathcal{G}
-modules we obtain the category
\mathrm{pro}(\mathcal{G}\operatorname{-Mod})
of pro-
\mathcal{G}
-modules.
A morphism in
\mathrm{pro}(\mathcal{G}\operatorname{-Mod})
will be called a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant pro-linear
map. Similarly, we have the category of pro-
\mathcal{G}
-anti-Yetter-Drinfeld modules.

If
\mathcal{C}
is an additive monoidal category with tensor product functor
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
one defines the tensor
product
V\otimes W
of pro-objects
V=(V_{i})_{i\in I}
and
W=(W_{j})_{j\in J}
by

(V_{i})_{i\in I}\otimes(W_{j})_{j\in J}=(V_{i}\otimes W_{j})_{(i,j)\in I\times J},

equipped with the obvious structure maps. Here
I\times J
is ordered using the product ordering.
With this tensor product the category
\mathrm{pro}(\mathcal{C})
becomes additive monoidal and the embedding functor
\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathrm{pro}(\mathcal{C})
is monoidal. The existence of a tensor product in
\mathrm{pro}(\mathcal{C})
yields a natural notion of algebra objects and algebra homomorphisms in this category, which we refer to as pro-algebras and their homomorphisms.

According to Proposition
3.8
the category
\mathcal{G}\operatorname{-Mod}
is additive monoidal.
By definition, a pro-
\mathcal{G}
-algebra is an algebra object in
\mathrm{pro}(\mathcal{G}\operatorname{-Mod})
, in the same way as
\mathcal{G}
-algebras are algebra objects in
G\operatorname{-Mod}
, compare section
3
. An algebra homomorphism
f:A\rightarrow B
in
\mathrm{pro}(\mathcal{G}\operatorname{-Mod})
will simply be called a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant homomorphism. Clearly, every
\mathcal{G}
-algebra is a pro-
\mathcal{G}
-algebra in a canonical way.

Occasionally we will encounter unital pro-
\mathcal{G}
-algebras. The
\mathcal{G}
-unitarisation
A^{+}
of a pro-
\mathcal{G}
-algebra
A
is defined in the same way as for
\mathcal{G}
-algebras. Similarly, the construction of crossed products for
\mathcal{G}
-algebras carries over to pro-
\mathcal{G}
-algebras.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be any additive category. An admissible extension in
\mathrm{pro}(\mathcal{C})
consists of objects
K,E,Q\in\mathrm{pro}(\mathcal{C})
and morphisms
\iota:K\rightarrow E,\pi:E\rightarrow Q
in
\mathrm{pro}(\mathcal{C})
, such that there exist morphisms
\sigma:Q\rightarrow E,\rho:E\rightarrow K
in
\mathrm{pro}(\mathcal{C})
satisfying
\rho\iota=\id,\pi\sigma=\id
and
\iota\rho+\sigma\pi=\id
. In other words, we require that
E
decomposes into a direct sum of

K
and
Q
in
\mathrm{pro}(\mathcal{C})
. We will write

\textstyle{K\;\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\iota}
\textstyle{E\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi}
\textstyle{Q}

or
0\rightarrow K\rightarrow E\rightarrow Q\rightarrow 0
for an admissible extension.

Let
K,E
and
Q
be pro-
\mathcal{G}
-algebras. An admissible extension of pro-
\mathcal{G}
-algebras is an admissible extension

0\rightarrow K\rightarrow E\rightarrow Q\rightarrow 0
in
\mathrm{pro}(\mathcal{G}\operatorname{-Mod})
such that
\iota
and
\pi
are
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant algebra homomorphisms. In connection with excision we will only need that the underlying objects of
K,E,Q
in
\mathrm{pro}(C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})\operatorname{-Mod})
form an admissible extension, or equivalently, the splitting
\sigma
for the homomorphism
\pi
is a morphism of pro-
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-modules but not necessarily
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant.

We will frequently describe morphisms between pro-objects by writing down explicit formulas involving “elements” in subsequent sections. This can be justified by observing that these formulas encode identities between abstractly defined morphisms.

4.2.
Paracomplexes

Let us next review the notion of a paracomplex in a para-additive category, compare
[
28
]
.

Definition 4.1
.

A para-additive category is an additive category
\mathcal{C}

together with a natural isomorphism
T
of the identity functor
\id:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
.

In other words, we are given invertible morphisms
T(M):M\rightarrow M
for all
objects
M\in\mathcal{C}
such that
\phi T(M)=T(N)\phi
for all morphisms
\phi:M\rightarrow N
. In the sequel we will
simply write
T
instead of
T(M)
.

Any additive category is para-additive by setting
T=\id
. The para-additive categories that are relevant for us are the category of
\mathcal{G}
-anti-Yetter-Drinfeld modules and its pro-category, compare Lemma
3.19
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a para-additive category.
A paracomplex
C=C_{0}\oplus C_{1}
in
\mathcal{C}
is a given by objects
C_{0}
and
C_{1}
in
\mathcal{C}
together with morphisms
\partial_{0}:C_{0}\rightarrow C_{1}
and
\partial_{1}:C_{1}\rightarrow C_{0}
such that

\partial^{2}=\id-T,

where the differential
\partial:C\rightarrow C_{1}\oplus C_{0}\cong C
is the composition of
\partial_{0}\oplus\partial_{1}

with the canonical flip map. A chain map
\phi:C\rightarrow D
between two paracomplexes is a morphism from
C
to
D
that commutes with
the differentials.

In general it does not make sense to speak about the homology of a paracomplex, but one can give meaning to the statement that two paracomplexes are homotopy equivalent, by using the standard formulas for chain homotopies.

The paracomplexes we are interested in arise from paramixed complexes in the following sense.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a para-additive category. A paramixed complex
M
in
\mathcal{C}
is a sequence of objects
M_{n}
together with differentials
b
of degree
-1
and
B
of degree
+1
satisfying

b^{2}=0
,
B^{2}=0
and

[b,B]=bB+Bb=\id-T.

One can define Hochschild homology of a paramixed complex in the usual way since the
Hochschild operator
b
satisfies
b^{2}=0
. However, we will not study Hochschild homology in this paper and focus entirely on periodic cyclic homology.

4.3.
Equivariant differential forms

For a pro-
\mathcal{G}
-algebra
A
we define the space of differential forms over
A
by
\Omega^{0}_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}(A)=A
and the iterated tensor products over
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
given by

\Omega^{n}_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}(A)=A^{+}\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}A^{\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}n}\cong A^{\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}n+1}\oplus A^{\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}n}

for all
n>0
, where we recall that
A^{+}
denotes the
\mathcal{G}
-unitarisation defined in section
3
.
We always view
\Omega^{n}_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}(A)
as a pro-
\mathcal{G}
-module with the diagonal action. Elements of
\Omega^{n}_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}(A)
contained in the first summand of the above decomposition, that is, tensors of the form
a^{0}\otimes a^{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a^{n}
with
a^{0},a^{1},\dots,a^{n}\in A
, will usually be written
a^{0}da^{1}\cdots da^{n}
. Similarly, elements in the second summand will be denoted
da^{1}\cdots da^{n}
. If we want to cover both cases at the same time we shall write
\langle a^{0}\rangle da^{1}\cdots da^{n}
, following
[
18
]
.

The pro-
\mathcal{G}
-module
\Omega^{n}_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}(A)
becomes an
A
-
A
-bimodule object in
\mathrm{pro}(\mathcal{G}\operatorname{-Mod})
with the left
A
-module structure

a\cdot(\langle a^{0}\rangle da^{1}\cdots da^{n})=a\langle a^{0}\rangle da^{1}\cdots da^{n},

and the right
A
-module determined by the Leibniz rule, that is,

\displaystyle(\langle a^{0}\rangle da^{1}\cdots\,da^{n})\cdot a

\displaystyle=\langle a^{0}\rangle da^{1}\cdots d(a^{n}a)+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(-1)^{n-j}\langle a^{0}\rangle da^{1}\cdots d(a^{j}a^{j+1})\cdots da^{n}da

\displaystyle\quad+(-1)^{n}\langle a^{0}\rangle a^{1}da^{2}\cdots da^{n}da.

We note that the
A
-bimodule
\Omega^{n}_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}(A)
can be identified with the
n
-fold tensor product of
\Omega^{1}_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}(A)
over
A
in the category of pro-

We are now ready to define bivariant equivariant periodic cyclic homology.

\mathcal{G}

In this case, given any

one obtains an equivalence between the category of
\mathcal{G}
-anti-Yetter-Drinfeld modules and the category of
\mathcal{G}^{x}_{x}
-anti-Yetter-Drinfeld modules by sending a
\mathcal{G}
-anti-Yetter-Drinfeld module
M
to
\chi\cdot M
, where
\chi\in M(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}}))=C^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}_{ad})
denotes the characteristic function of
\mathcal{G}_{x}^{x}
.
Applying this functor, or rather its extension to the corresponding pro-categories, to the Hom-complex defining
HP^{\mathcal{G}}_{*}(A,B)

yields the desired isomorphism.
∎

5.
Homotopy invariance, stability and excision

In this section we show that
HP^{\mathcal{G}}_{*}
satisfies similar homological properties as equivariant
KK
-theory, compare
[
3
]
.

5.1.
Homotopy invariance

We establish first that
HP^{\mathcal{G}}_{*}
is homotopy invariant with respect to smooth homotopies in both variables.

Let
A,B
be pro-
\mathcal{G}
-algebras. By definition, a smooth
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant homotopy between
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant algebra homomorphisms
\phi_{0},\phi_{1}:A\rightarrow B
is a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant algebra homomorphism
\Phi:A\rightarrow B[0,1]
such that
\Phi_{i}=ev_{i}\,\Phi
equals
\phi_{i}
for
i=0,1
. Here
B[0,1]=B\otimes C^{\infty}[0,1]
is the algebra of smooth functions on the unit interval with values in
B
, equipped with the
\mathcal{G}
-action which treats
C^{\infty}[0,1]
as a dummy term, and
ev_{i}:B[0,1]\rightarrow B
is evaluation at
i
.

Recall from Section
4
that
\theta^{n}\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}(A)
denotes the
n
-th level of the Hodge tower, and that
\theta^{1}\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}(A)=X_{\mathcal{G}}(A)
is the
equivariant
X
-complex. We have canonical projection maps
\xi_{n}:\theta^{n}\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}(A)\rightarrow\theta^{n-1}\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}(A)
for all
n\geq 1
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
A
be a quasifree pro-
\mathcal{G}
-algebra. Then the map
\xi_{2}:\theta^{2}\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}(A)\rightarrow X_{\mathcal{G}}(A)
is a homotopy equivalence of pro-paracomplexes of
\mathcal{G}
-anti-Yetter-Drinfeld modules.

Proof.

Since
A
is quasifree there exists by Theorem
4.7
a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant pro-linear
map
\nabla:\Omega^{1}_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}(A)\rightarrow\Omega^{2}_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}(A)
such that

\nabla(a\omega)=a\nabla(\omega)\text{ and }\nabla(\omega a)=\nabla(\omega)a-\omega da

for all
a\in A
and
\omega\in\Omega^{1}_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}(A)
. We extend
\nabla
to forms of higher degree by setting

\nabla\left(\langle a^{0}\rangle da^{1}\cdots da^{n}\right)=\nabla\left(\langle a^{0}\rangle da^{1}\right)da^{2}\cdots da^{n}.

Then we have

\nabla(a\omega)=a\nabla(\omega),\quad\nabla(\omega\eta)=\nabla(\omega)\eta+(-1)^{|\omega|}\omega d\eta

for
a\in A
and
\omega,\eta\in\Omega_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}(A)
. Moreover we set
\nabla(a)=0
for
a\in\Omega^{0}_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}(A)=A
.

One then obtains a map
\nabla_{\mathcal{G}}:\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}^{n}(A)\rightarrow\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}^{n+1}(A)
of pro-
\mathcal{G}
-anti-Yetter-Drinfeld modules by setting

\nabla_{\mathcal{G}}(f\otimes\omega)=f\otimes\nabla(\omega).

We will use
\nabla_{\mathcal{G}}
to construct an inverse of
\xi_{2}
up to homotopy.
Firstly, an explicit computation gives
\left[b_{\mathcal{G}},\nabla_{\mathcal{G}}\right]=\id
on
\Omega^{n}_{\mathcal{G}}(A)
for
n\geq 2
. Since
\left[b_{\mathcal{G}},\nabla_{\mathcal{G}}\right]
commutes
with
b_{\mathcal{G}}
this equality holds on
b_{\mathcal{G}}(\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}(A))\subseteq\Omega^{1}_{\mathcal{G}}(A)
as well. As a consequence, we obtain a well-defined
map
\nu:X_{\mathcal{G}}(A)\rightarrow\theta^{2}\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}(A)
by setting
\nu=\id-[\nabla_{\mathcal{G}},B_{\mathcal{G}}+b_{\mathcal{G}}]
, noting that
[\nabla_{\mathcal{G}},B_{\mathcal{G}}]
increases the degree of
differential forms by
2
.

Using Lemma
3.19
with the fact that
\nabla_{\mathcal{G}}
is a map of pro-
\mathcal{G}
-anti-Yetter-Drinfeld modules one checks that
\nu
is a chain map with
respect to
\partial=B_{\mathcal{G}}+b_{\mathcal{G}}
. Explicitly, we have

\begin{array}[]{ll}\nu=\id-\nabla_{\mathcal{G}}d&\text{ on }\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}^{0}(A)\\
\nu=\id-\left[\nabla_{\mathcal{G}},b_{\mathcal{G}}\right]=\id-b_{\mathcal{G}}\nabla_{\mathcal{G}}&\text{ on }\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}^{1}(A)/b_{\mathcal{G}}\left(\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}(A)\right),\end{array}

and this implies
\xi_{2}\nu=\id
. Moreover, by construction

\nu\xi_{2}=\id-\left[\nabla_{\mathcal{G}},B_{\mathcal{G}}+b_{\mathcal{G}}\right]
is homotopic to the identity.
∎

Let again
A,B
be pro-
\mathcal{G}
-algebras and let
\Phi:A\rightarrow B[0,1]
be a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant homotopy. The derivative
\Phi^{\prime}:A\rightarrow B[0,1]
is a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant pro-linear map satisying
\Phi^{\prime}(ab)=\Phi^{\prime}(a)\Phi(b)+\Phi(a)\Phi^{\prime}(b)
for all
a,b\in A
.

We define
\eta:\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}^{n}(A)\rightarrow\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}^{n-1}(B)
by

\eta(f\otimes a^{0}da^{1}\cdots da^{n})=\int_{0}^{1}f\otimes\Phi_{t}(a^{0})\Phi^{\prime}_{t}(a^{1})d\Phi_{t}(a^{2})\cdots d\Phi_{t}(a^{n})dt

for
n>0
and
\eta=0
on
\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}^{0}(A)
. Using the fact that
\Phi^{\prime}
is a derivation with respect to
\Phi
one computes
\eta b_{\mathcal{G}}+b_{\mathcal{G}}\eta=0

on
\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}^{n}(A)
for all
n\geq 0
.
In particular, we have
\eta b_{\mathcal{G}}(\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}^{3}(A))\subseteq b_{\mathcal{G}}(\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}(B))
, and hence we obtain a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant pro-linear
map
\eta:\theta^{2}\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}(A)\rightarrow X_{\mathcal{G}}(B)
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\Phi:A\rightarrow B[0,1]
be a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant homotopy between pro-
\mathcal{G}
-algebras
A,B
. Then we have
X_{\mathcal{G}}(\Phi_{1})\xi_{2}-X_{\mathcal{G}}(\Phi_{0})\xi_{2}=\partial\eta+\eta\partial
, where
\eta:\theta^{2}\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}(A)\rightarrow X_{\mathcal{G}}(B)
is the map defined above. Hence the chain maps
X_{\mathcal{G}}(\Phi_{t})\xi_{2}:\theta^{2}\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}(A)\rightarrow X_{\mathcal{G}}(B)
for
t=0,1
are homotopic.

Proof.

Recall that the boundary operator is
\partial=B_{\mathcal{G}}+b_{\mathcal{G}}
. For
j=0
we have

[\partial,\eta](f\otimes a)=\eta(f\otimes da)=\int_{0}^{1}f\otimes\Phi_{t}^{\prime}(a)dt=f\otimes\Phi_{1}(a)-f\otimes\Phi_{0}(a).

For
j=1
we get

\displaystyle[\partial,\eta]

\displaystyle(\chi_{U}\otimes a^{0}da^{1})=d_{\mathcal{G}}\eta(\chi_{U}\otimes a^{0}da^{1})+\eta B_{\mathcal{G}}(\chi_{U}\otimes a^{0}da^{1})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}(\chi_{U}\otimes d(\Phi_{t}(a^{0})\Phi_{t}^{\prime}(a^{1}))+\chi_{U}\otimes\Phi_{t}^{\prime}(a^{0})d\Phi_{t}(a^{1})

\displaystyle\quad-\chi_{U}\otimes\Phi_{t}^{\prime}(\chi_{U^{-1}}\cdot a^{1})d\Phi_{t}(a^{0}))dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}b_{\mathcal{G}}(\chi_{U}\otimes d\Phi_{t}(a^{0})d\Phi_{t}^{\prime}(a^{1}))+\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\chi_{U}\otimes\Phi_{t}(a^{0})d\Phi_{t}(a^{1}))dt

for any compact open bisection
U\subseteq\mathcal{G}
.
Since the first term vanishes in
X_{\mathcal{G}}(B)
we conclude

[\partial,\eta](\chi_{U}\otimes a^{0}da^{1})=\chi_{U}\otimes\Phi_{1}(a^{0})d\Phi_{1}(a^{1})-\chi_{U}\otimes\Phi_{0}(a^{0})d\Phi_{0}(a^{1}).

Finally, on
\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}^{3}(A)/b_{\mathcal{G}}(\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}(A))
we have
\partial\eta+\eta\partial=\eta b_{\mathcal{G}}+b_{\mathcal{G}}\eta=0
, with the last equality due to the calculation just before this Lemma.
∎

We are now ready to state and prove the following result.

Theorem 5.3

(Homotopy invariance)
.

Let
A
and
B
be pro-
\mathcal{G}
-algebras and let
\Phi:A\rightarrow B[0,1]
be a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant homotopy. Then the elements
\left[\Phi_{0}\right]
and
\left[\Phi_{1}\right]
in
HP_{0}^{\mathcal{G}}(A,B)
are equal. More generally, if
A
is a quasifree pro-
\mathcal{G}
-algebra then the elements
\left[\Phi_{0}\right]
and
\left[\Phi_{1}\right]

in
H_{0}(\Hom_{A(\mathcal{G})}(X_{\mathcal{G}}(A),X_{\mathcal{G}}(B)))
are equal.

Proof.

The second part of the Theorem follows directly by combining Lemma
5.1
and
Lemma
5.2
.

In order to show that the first part of the Theorem can be viewed as a special case of the second, assume that
\Phi:A\rightarrow B[0,1]
is a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant homotopy.
We tensor
A
and
B
with
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{G}}
to obtain a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant
homotopy
\Phi\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{G}}:A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{G}}\rightarrow(B\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{G}})[0,1]
.
Passing to the periodic tensor algebras we obtain a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant algebra homomorphism
\mathcal{T}(\Phi\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{G}}):\mathcal{T}(A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{G}})\rightarrow\mathcal{T}((B\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{G}})[0,1])
.

Consider the
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant pro-linear map

\displaystyle l:B\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{G}}\otimes C^{\infty}[0,1]

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathcal{T}(B\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{G}})\otimes C^{\infty}[0,1]

\displaystyle l(b\otimes T\otimes f)

\displaystyle=\sigma(b\otimes T)\otimes f,

where
\sigma:B\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{G}}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}(B\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{G}})
is the standard
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant pro-linear splitting.
Then
l
is a lonilcur, and we get an associated
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant homomorphism

[[l]]:\mathcal{T}((B\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{G}})[0,1])\rightarrow\mathcal{T}(B\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{G}})[0,1]

by the universal property of the periodic tensor algebra from Proposition
4.5
. Consider the
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant homotopy

\Psi=[[l]]\mathcal{T}(\Phi\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{G}}):\mathcal{T}(A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{G}})\rightarrow\mathcal{T}(B\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{G}})[0,1]

and note that
\Psi_{t}=\mathcal{T}(\Phi_{t}\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{G}})
for all
t\in[0,1]
.
Since
\mathcal{T}(A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{G}})
is quasifree we are now in the setting of the second part of the Theorem, and this concludes the proof.
∎

We note that as an application of homotopy invariance one can show that
X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}A)
is homotopy equivalent to
X_{\mathcal{G}}(A)
if
A
is a quasifree pro-
\mathcal{G}
-algebra, compare
[
28
, Proposition 10.5]
.

5.2.
Stability

Let us next show that
HP^{\mathcal{G}}_{*}
is stable in both variables with respect to tensoring with the algebra
\mathcal{K}(E)
of smoothing operators associated to a
\mathcal{G}
-module
E
together with a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant pairing, compare section
3
.

We denote by
h:E\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}E\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
the given
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant pairing on
E
and define the twisted trace map
ttr:\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}}\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(E)\rightarrow\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}}
by setting

ttr(f\otimes e_{1}\otimes e_{2})=(\id\otimes h)(T(f\otimes e_{2})\otimes e_{1})

for
f\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}}
and
e_{1},e_{2}\in E
. Explicitly, we have

ttr(\chi_{U}\otimes e_{1}\otimes e_{2})=\chi_{U}\otimes h(\chi_{U^{-1}}\cdot e_{2}\otimes e_{1})\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}}\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})\cong\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}}

for any compact open bisection
U\subseteq\mathcal{G}
.

Lemma 5.4
.

The twisted trace map introduced above satisfies

ttr(\chi_{U}\otimes L_{0}L_{1})=ttr(\chi_{U}\otimes(\chi_{U^{-1}}\cdot L_{1})L_{0})

for any compact open bisection
U\subseteq\mathcal{G}
and
L_{0},L_{1}\in\mathcal{K}(E)
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the claim for
L_{0}=e_{1}\otimes e_{2},L_{1}=e_{3}\otimes e_{4}
for any
e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},e_{4}\in E
. In this case we obtain

\displaystyle ttr(\chi_{U}\otimes L_{0}L_{1})=h(\chi_{U^{-1}}\cdot e_{4}\otimes e_{1})h(e_{2}\otimes e_{3})

and

\displaystyle ttr(\chi_{U}\otimes(\chi_{U^{-1}}\cdot L_{1})L_{0})=h(\chi_{U^{-1}}\cdot e_{2}\otimes\chi_{U^{-1}}\cdot e_{3})h(\chi_{U^{-1}}\cdot e_{4}\otimes e_{1}).

Moreover, note that
b(\chi_{U^{-1}}\cdot e_{2}\otimes\chi_{U^{-1}}\cdot e_{3})=\chi_{U^{-1}}\cdot b(e_{2}\otimes e_{3})
by
\mathcal{G}
-equivariance of the pairing.

It is therefore enough to observe that
\chi_{U}\otimes f=\chi_{U}\otimes\chi_{U^{-1}}\cdot f
for all
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
, or equivalently, that the canonical map
T
of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}}\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
equals the identity.
∎

We say that a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant pairing
h
on a
\mathcal{G}
-module
E
is admissible if there exists a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant linear embedding
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})\rightarrow E
such that the restriction of
h
to
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})\subseteq E
agrees with the canonical isomorphism
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})\cong C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
. Using the same identification one checks that such an embedding induces a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant algebra homomorphism
\iota:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(E)
.
More generally, if
A
is a
\mathcal{G}
-algebra then we obtain a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant algebra homomorphism
\iota_{A}:A\cong A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})\rightarrow A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(E)
by tensoring the identity on
A
with
\iota:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(E)
.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
A
be a pro-
\mathcal{G}
-algebra, and let
E
be a
\mathcal{G}
-module equipped with an admissible
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant bilinear pairing. Then the class

[\iota_{A}]\in H_{0}\Hom_{A(\mathcal{G})}(X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}A),X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}(A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(E))))

is invertible.

Proof.

We have to find an inverse for
[\iota_{A}]
. First observe that the canonical
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant linear map
A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(E)\rightarrow\mathcal{T}A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(E)
is a lonilcur and hence induces
a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant homomorphism
\lambda_{A}:\mathcal{T}(A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(E))\rightarrow\mathcal{T}A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(E)
.
Define
tr_{A}:X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(E))\rightarrow X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}A)
by

tr_{A}(f\otimes x\otimes L)=ttr(f\otimes L)\otimes x

and

\displaystyle tr_{A}(f\otimes(x_{0}\otimes L_{0})d(x_{1}\otimes L_{1}))

\displaystyle=ttr(f\otimes L_{0}L_{1})\otimes x_{0}dx_{1}

\displaystyle tr_{A}(f\otimes d(x_{1}\otimes L_{1}))

\displaystyle=ttr(f\otimes L_{1})\otimes dx_{1}.

Here, we use the twisted trace
ttr
, see Lemma
5.4
. By construction,
tr_{A}
is a map of
\mathcal{G}
-anti-Yetter-Drinfeld modules.
We have

\displaystyle tr_{A}d_{\mathcal{G}}(f\otimes x\otimes L)

\displaystyle=tr_{A}(f\otimes d(x\otimes L))

\displaystyle=ttr(f\otimes L)\otimes dx

\displaystyle=d_{\mathcal{G}}(ttr(f\otimes L)\otimes x)

\displaystyle=d_{\mathcal{G}}tr_{A}(f\otimes x\otimes L),

and for a compact open bisection
U\subseteq\mathcal{G}
we calculate

\displaystyle b_{\mathcal{G}}tr_{A}

\displaystyle(\chi_{U}\otimes(x_{0}\otimes L_{0})d(x_{1}\otimes L_{1}))=b_{\mathcal{G}}(ttr(\chi_{U}\otimes L_{0}L_{1})\otimes x_{0}dx_{1})

\displaystyle=ttr(\chi_{U}\otimes L_{0}L_{1})\otimes(x_{0}x_{1}-(\chi_{U^{-1}}\cdot x_{1})x_{0})

\displaystyle=ttr(\chi_{U}\otimes L_{0}L_{1})\otimes x_{0}x_{1}-ttr(\chi_{U}\otimes(\chi_{U^{-1}}\cdot L_{1})L_{0})\otimes(\chi_{U^{-1}}\cdot x_{1})x_{0}

\displaystyle=tr_{A}(\chi_{U}\otimes(x_{0}x_{1}\otimes L_{0}L_{1})-\chi_{U}\otimes(\chi_{U^{-1}}\cdot x_{1})x_{0}\otimes(\chi_{U^{-1}}\cdot L_{1})L_{0})

\displaystyle=tr_{A}b_{\mathcal{G}}(\chi_{U}\otimes(x_{0}\otimes L_{0})d(x_{1}\otimes L_{1})),

using the twisted trace property from Lemma
5.4
. Similarly one checks

b_{\mathcal{G}}tr_{A}(\chi_{U}\otimes d(x_{1}\otimes L_{1}))=tr_{A}b_{\mathcal{G}}(\chi_{U}\otimes d(x_{1}\otimes L_{1})).

It follows that
tr_{A}
is a chain map of paracomplexes.

We define
\tau_{A}=tr_{A}X_{\mathcal{G}}(\lambda_{A})
and claim that
[\tau_{A}]
is an inverse for
[\iota_{A}]
. From the definitions one immediately computes

[\iota_{A}]\cdot[\tau_{A}]=\id
.
It thus remains to show that
[\tau_{A}]\cdot[\iota_{A}]=\id
. Consider the
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant homomorphisms
i_{j}:A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(E)\rightarrow A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(E)\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(E)
for
j=0,1
given by

\displaystyle i_{0}=\id\otimes\iota,\quad i_{1}=(\id\otimes\sigma)i_{0}.

Here we use the canonical identification

A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(E)\cong A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(E)\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})

in the definition of
i_{0}
, and the tensor flip automorphism
\sigma
of
\mathcal{K}(E)\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(E)
given by
\sigma(T_{1}\otimes T_{2})=T_{2}\otimes T_{1}
in the definition of
i_{1}
.

We calculate
[i_{0}]\cdot[\tau_{A\otimes\mathcal{K}(E)}]=\id

and
[i_{1}]\cdot[\tau_{A\otimes\mathcal{K}(E)}]=[\tau_{A}]\cdot[\iota_{A}]
. Let us show that the maps
i_{0}
and
i_{1}
are
\mathcal{G}
-equivariantly homotopic. To this end observe that
\mathcal{K}(E)\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(E)\cong\mathcal{K}(E\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}E)
as
\mathcal{G}
-algebras and denote by
\Sigma
the flip automorphism of
E\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}E
given by
\Sigma(e\otimes f)=f\otimes e
. For
t\in[0,1]
we then obtain a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant linear endomorphism
\Sigma_{t}
of
E\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}E
given by

\Sigma_{t}=\cos(\pi t/2)\id+\sin(\pi t/2)\Sigma,

and we note that
\Sigma_{t}
is invertible with inverse
\Sigma_{t}^{-1}=\cos(\pi t/2)\id-\sin(\pi t/2)\Sigma
. Since
\Sigma
is isometric with respect to the tensor product pairing on
E\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}E
the same is true for
\Sigma_{t}
, and it follows that
\sigma_{t}=\Sigma_{t}\otimes\Sigma_{t}
defines
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant algebra automorphism of

\mathcal{K}(E\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}E)=(E\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}E)\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}(E\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}E).

The family
(\sigma_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
depends smoothly on
t
, and by construction we have

\sigma_{0}=\id
and
\sigma_{1}=\sigma
. Now define
h_{t}:A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(E)\rightarrow A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(E)\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(E)
by
h_{t}=(\id\otimes\sigma_{t})i_{0}
for
t\in[0,1]
. Then each
h_{t}
is a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant algebra homomorphism, and by construction

h_{j}=i_{j}
for
j=0,1
. Since the family
(h_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
depends again smoothly on
t
we have thus constructed a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant smooth homotopy between
i_{0}
and
i_{1}
. According to Theorem
5.3
we obtain
[i_{0}]=[i_{1}]
, and hence
[\tau_{A}]\cdot[\iota_{A}]=\id
as required.
∎

In order to discuss the implications of Theorem
5.5
for the stability properties of the functor
HP^{\mathcal{G}}_{*}
we need some preparation.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
E,F
be
\mathcal{G}
-modules and assume that
E\cong F
as
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-modules. Then
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}E\cong C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}F
as
\mathcal{G}
-modules.

Proof.

Assume that
\phi:X\rightarrow Y
is a
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-linear isomorphism. Then
T_{F}^{-1}\circ(\id\otimes\phi)\circ T_{E}
is a
\mathcal{G}
-linear isomorphism from
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}E
to
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}F
.
∎

For a transformation groupoid
\mathcal{G}=\Gamma\ltimes X
, associated to a discrete group
\Gamma
acting on a totally disconnected locally compact space
X
, we
have
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\cong\bigoplus_{\gamma\in\Gamma}C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
as left
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-modules.
Hence Lemma
5.6
shows that there is a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant isomorphism

C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\cong\bigoplus_{\gamma\in\Gamma}C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})

in this case. The same is true for ample groupoids
\mathcal{G}
which can be covered by a family of disjoint global range sections.

However, not every ample groupoid admits such a covering. This fails already in the case of finite groupoids, as the example of a disjoint union of finite groups of different orders shows. In this case we cannot write the underlying
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-module of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
as a direct sum of copies of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
.

The following lemma allows one to circumvent this by passing to infinite direct sums of copies of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
.
In the sequel we write
V^{\oplus\kappa}
for a direct sum of copies of
V
indexed by a set of cardinality
\kappa
.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
E
be a
\mathcal{G}
-module equipped with a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant bilinear pairing
h:E\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}E\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
. If
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})

and
E
are projective as essential
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-modules and the map
h
is surjective then there exists an isomorphism

E^{\oplus\kappa}\cong C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})^{\oplus\kappa}

of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-modules for any infinite cardinal
\kappa
such that
E
admits a generating set of cardinality at most
\kappa
.

Proof.

We fix
I
with
|I|=\kappa
and a family
(e_{i})_{i\in I}
of elements of
E
which generate
E
as a
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-module.
Then we obtain a surjection
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})^{\oplus\kappa}\rightarrow E
of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-modules by mapping
(f_{i})_{i\in I}
to
\sum_{i\in I}f_{i}\cdot e_{i}
. Since
E
is projective this surjection splits, so that
E
can be written as a direct summand of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})^{\oplus\kappa}
. By our assumption that
\kappa
is infinite it follows that
E^{\oplus\kappa}
is a direct summand of
(C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})^{\oplus\kappa})^{\oplus\kappa}\cong C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})^{\oplus\kappa}
.
Explicitly, let us choose a direct complement
P
, so that

P\oplus E^{\oplus\kappa}\cong E^{\oplus\kappa}\oplus P\cong C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})^{\oplus\kappa}.

By writing again
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})^{\oplus\kappa}\cong(C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})^{\oplus\kappa})^{\oplus\kappa}
we then get

E^{\oplus\kappa}\oplus C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})^{\oplus\kappa}\cong E^{\oplus\kappa}\oplus(P\oplus E^{\oplus\kappa})^{\oplus\kappa}\cong C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})^{\oplus\kappa}.

Similarly, using that the pairing
h
is surjective we obtain a surjection
E^{\oplus\kappa}\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-modules mapping
(x_{i})_{i\in I}
to
\sum_{i\in I}h(x_{i}\otimes e_{i})
. Since
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
is projective it follows that
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
is a direct summand in
E^{\oplus\kappa}
. In the same way as above we can then write
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})^{\oplus\kappa}
as a direct summand of
E^{\oplus\kappa}
, and construct an isomorphism

E^{\oplus\kappa}\oplus C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})^{\oplus\kappa}\cong E^{\oplus\kappa}.

Combining these considerations, we therefore obtain an isomorphism

E^{\oplus\kappa}\cong E^{\oplus\kappa}\oplus C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})^{\oplus\kappa}\cong C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})^{\oplus\kappa}

of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-modules as required.
∎

Let us make some comments on Lemma
5.7
. Projectivity of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
, viewed as an essential module over itself, is a mild assumption which is satisfied whenever
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is paracompact. This follows easily from the fact that one can write
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
as a disjoint union of compact open subsets in this case, compare the discussion in the proof of Proposition
2.12
.
If
\mathcal{G}
is paracompact, admitting a covering by mutually disjoint compact open range sections indexed by a set of cardinality
\mu
, then the same argument as
in
[
6
, Lemma 2.13]
shows that
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
, viewed as
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-module, is projective and admits a
generating set of cardinality
\mu
. Since the standard pairing on
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
is always surjective, Lemma
5.7
yields an isomorphism

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})^{\oplus\kappa}\cong C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})^{\oplus\kappa}

of left
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-modules for any infinite cardinal
\kappa\geq\mu
in this case.
If
\mathcal{G}
is
\sigma
-compact there exists a countable such covering family, so that the countable direct sum of copies of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
is isomorphic to a countable direct sum of
copies of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
.

We also note that if
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is discrete then every
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-module is projective. In contrast, for a general totally disconnected base space
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, a module of the form
E=\mathbb{C}
with the action of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
given by point evaluation at some point
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
will typically fail to be projective.

Theorem 5.8

(Stability)
.

Let
E
be a
\mathcal{G}
-module equipped with a surjective
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant bilinear pairing. If
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
and
E
are projective as essential
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-modules then there exists an invertible element in

HP^{\mathcal{G}}_{0}(A,A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(E))

for any pro-
\mathcal{G}
-algebra
A
. It follows that we have natural isomorphisms

HP^{\mathcal{G}}_{*}(A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(E),B)\cong HP^{\mathcal{G}}_{*}(A,B)\cong HP^{\mathcal{G}}_{*}(A,B\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(E))

for all pro-
\mathcal{G}
-algebras
A,B
.

Proof.

According to Lemma
5.7
we obtain an isomorphism
E^{\oplus\kappa}\cong C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})^{\oplus\kappa}
of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-modules for some infinite cardinal
\kappa
. Now let us view
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})^{\oplus\kappa}
as a
\mathcal{G}
-module via the trivial action.
Using Lemma
5.6
we obtain an isomorphism

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}E^{\oplus\kappa}\cong\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})^{\oplus\kappa}\cong\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})^{\oplus\kappa}

of
\mathcal{G}
-modules. Moreover, the pairing on the trivial
\mathcal{G}
-module
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})^{\oplus\kappa}

induced from
E^{\oplus\kappa}
is admissible.
Hence the proof of Theorem
5.5
gives homotopy equivalences

\displaystyle X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}(A

\displaystyle\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{G}}))\simeq X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}(A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{G}}\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})^{\oplus\kappa})))

\displaystyle\cong X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}(A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})^{\oplus\kappa})))

\displaystyle\cong X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}(A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}E^{\oplus\kappa})))

\displaystyle\simeq X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}(A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}E))

\displaystyle\cong X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}(A\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{G}}\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}\mathcal{K}(E)).

This yields the assertion.
∎

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a proper ample groupoid such that
\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is paracompact. According to Proposition
2.12
there exists a locally constant cut-off function
c
for
\mathcal{G}
. It follows that for any
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
the function
s^{*}(c)r^{*}(f):\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
given by

s^{*}(c)r^{*}(f)(\alpha)=c(s(\alpha))f(r(\alpha))

has compact support and is thus contained in
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
.

Now let
E,F
be
\mathcal{G}
-modules and let
\phi:E\rightarrow F
be a
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-linear map. As in the proof of Lemma
5.6
we obtain a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant linear map
\phi^{T}=T_{F}^{-1}(\id\otimes\phi)T_{E}:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}E\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}}F
.
Recall moreover from Lemma
3.4
that the integration map
\lambda:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
is
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant with respect to the left multiplication action on
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})=\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
.
Hence we obtain a linear map
\phi^{\mathcal{G}}:E\rightarrow F
by defining

\phi^{\mathcal{G}}(f\otimes e)=(\lambda\otimes\id)\phi^{T}(s^{*}(c)r^{*}(f)\otimes e),

using the canonical identification
X\cong C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}X
for
X=E,F
.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a proper ample groupoid with
\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
paracompact and let
E,F
be
\mathcal{G}
-modules. If

\phi:E\rightarrow F
is a
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-linear map then
\phi^{\mathcal{G}}:E\rightarrow F
is a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant linear map.

Proof.

For a compact open bisection
U\subseteq\mathcal{G}
and a compact open set
V\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
we have
\chi_{U}\cdot\chi_{V}=\chi_{r(U\cap s^{-1}(V))}=\chi_{U\cdot V}
.
Using this we calculate

\displaystyle\lambda(\chi_{U}*(s^{*}(c)r^{*}(\chi_{V})))(x)

\displaystyle=\chi_{U}\cdot\lambda(s^{*}(c)r^{*}(\chi_{V}))(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}^{x}}\chi_{U}(\gamma)\lambda(s^{*}(c)r^{*}(\chi_{V}))(\gamma^{-1}\cdot x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{G}^{\gamma^{-1}\cdot x}}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}^{x}}\chi_{U}(\gamma)c(s(\beta))\chi_{V}(r(\beta))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{G}^{x}}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}^{x}}\chi_{U}(\gamma)c(s(\gamma^{-1}\beta))\chi_{V}(r(\gamma^{-1}\beta))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}^{x}}\chi_{U}(\gamma)\chi_{V}(r(\gamma^{-1}))

\displaystyle=\chi_{r(U\cap s^{-1}(V))}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{G}^{x}}cs(\beta)\chi_{r(U\cap s^{-1}(V))}(x)

\displaystyle=\lambda(s^{*}(c)r^{*}(\chi_{U\cdot V}))(x)

for all
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. For
e\in E
we then compute

\displaystyle\phi^{\mathcal{G}}(\chi_{U}\cdot(\chi_{V}\otimes e))

\displaystyle=\phi^{\mathcal{G}}(\chi_{U\cdot V}\otimes\chi_{U}\cdot e)

\displaystyle=(\lambda\otimes\id)\phi^{T}(s^{*}(c)r^{*}(\chi_{U\cdot V})\otimes\chi_{U}\cdot e)

\displaystyle=(\lambda\otimes\id)\phi^{T}(\chi_{U}*(s^{*}(c)r^{*}(\chi_{V}))\otimes\chi_{U}\cdot e)

\displaystyle=\chi_{U}\cdot(\lambda\otimes\id)\phi^{T}(s^{*}(c)r^{*}(\chi_{V})\otimes e)

\displaystyle=\chi_{U}\cdot\phi^{\mathcal{G}}(\chi_{V}\otimes e)

as required, noting that
\phi^{T}
is
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
-linear.
∎

We remark that if the map
\phi
in Lemma
5.9
is already
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant then
\phi^{\mathcal{G}}=\phi
. Indeed, in this case we have
\phi^{\mathcal{G}}(f\otimes e)=\lambda(s^{*}(c))f\otimes\phi(e)=f\otimes\phi(e)
for all
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
and
e\in E
. In a similar way we get
(\phi\psi)^{\mathcal{G}}=\phi^{\mathcal{G}}\psi
and
(\theta\phi)^{\mathcal{G}}=\theta\phi^{\mathcal{G}}
if
\psi,\theta
are
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant linear maps.

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a proper ample groupoid with
\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
paracompact. Then we have a natural isomorphism

HP_{*}^{\mathcal{G}}(A,B)\cong H_{*}(\Hom_{A(\mathcal{G})}(X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}A),X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}B)))

for all
\mathcal{G}
-algebras
A,B
.

Proof.

The integration map defines a
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant surjection
\lambda:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
, compare Lemma
3.4
.
Moreover, the extension of functions by zero induces a
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-linear inclusion map
\iota:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
. Since

\lambda\iota(f)(x)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{G}^{x}}\iota(f)(\alpha)=f(x)

we see that
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
is a direct summand of the
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-module
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
. According to Lemma
5.9
it follows that the
trivial
\mathcal{G}
-module
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
is a direct summand of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
in the category of
\mathcal{G}
-modules as well. We conclude that the regular pairing on
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})

is admissible, so that the claim follows from Theorem
5.5
.
∎

5.3.
Excision

In the final part of this section we show that equivariant periodic cyclic homology satisfies excision in both variables. The key ideas go back to
[
18
]
, and the argument follows closely the proof in the group equivariant case
[
28
]
. For this reason we will be rather brief and only sketch the main strategy.

We consider an admissible extension

\textstyle{K\;\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\iota}
\textstyle{E\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi}
\textstyle{Q}

of pro-
\mathcal{G}
-algebras, with a fixed
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant pro-linear splitting
\sigma:Q\rightarrow E
for the quotient homomorphism
\pi:E\rightarrow Q
.

Let
X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}E:\mathcal{T}Q)
be the kernel of the map
X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}\pi):X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}E)\rightarrow X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}Q)
induced by
\pi
. The splitting
\sigma
yields a direct sum decomposition
X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}E)=X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}E:\mathcal{T}Q)\oplus X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}Q)
of pro-
\mathcal{G}
-anti-Yetter-Drinfeld modules. The resulting extension

\textstyle{X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}E:\mathcal{T}Q)\;\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}E)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

of paracomplexes induces long exact sequences in homology in both variables. Moreover, there is a canonical map
\rho:X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}K)\rightarrow X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}E:\mathcal{T}Q)
of paracomplexes of pro-
\mathcal{G}
-anti-Yetter-Drinfeld modules. The key step in the proof of the excision theorem is the following result.

Theorem 5.11
.

The map
\rho:X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}K)\rightarrow X_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{T}E:\mathcal{T}Q)
is a homotopy equivalence.

As a consequence of Theorem
5.11
one obtains excision in both variables for
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant periodic cyclic homology.

(Excision)
.

Let

6.
The Green-Julg Theorem

In this section we establish a homological analogue of the Green-Julg theorem for the equivariant
K
-theory of proper groupoids due to Tu
[
24
, Proposition 6.25]
. More precisely, we shall prove the following result.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a proper ample groupoid such that
\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is paracompact. Then there exists a natural isomorphism

HP_{*}^{\mathcal{G}}(C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)}),A)\cong HP_{*}^{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}(C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}),A\rtimes\mathcal{G})

for all pro-
\mathcal{G}
-algebras
A
.

Here
HP^{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}_{*}
is our notation for the equivariant periodic cyclic theory associated to the totally disconnected locally compact
space
\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, viewed as an ample groupoid. The crossed product
A\rtimes\mathcal{G}
becomes an essential
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-module via
pointwise multiplication on the copy of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
. That is, we have

(h\cdot f)(\alpha)=h(r(\alpha))f(\alpha)=f(\alpha)h(s(\alpha))

for all
h\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}),f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
.

For the proof of Theorem
6.1
we need some preparations. The basic strategy is to use localisation at the
\mathcal{G}
-orbits in
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
on both sides in order to reduce the claim to the Green-Julg theorem for finite groups established in
[
27
]
,
[
28
]
.

Let
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. By slight abuse of notation, we shall write
[x]
both for the
\mathcal{G}
-orbit
\mathcal{G}\cdot x\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and the class of
x
under the quotient
map
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. The subset
[x]\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is closed in
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
,
and since
\mathcal{G}
is proper it is discrete in the subspace topology.

For
[x]=\mathcal{G}\cdot x\in\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
consider the maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}_{[x]}\subseteq C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
of all functions vanishing at
[x]
. Given an essential
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-module
M
we write

M_{[x]}=M/\mathfrak{m}_{[x]}\cdot M

for the localisation of
M
at
[x]
. Since functions in
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
are locally constant this identifies indeed canonically with the usual ring-theoretic localisation at the maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}_{[x]}
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
0\rightarrow K\rightarrow E\rightarrow Q\rightarrow 0
be a short exact sequence of essential
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-modules. Then the localised sequence
0\rightarrow K_{[x]}\rightarrow E_{[x]}\rightarrow Q_{[x]}\rightarrow 0
is exact for all
[x]\in\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
.

Proof.

This is a well-known property of localisations; we shall give a direct argument in our special situation here for the convenience of the reader. Note that if
M
is an essential
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-module and
m=\sum f_{i}\cdot m_{i}\in\mathfrak{m}_{[[x]]}\cdot V
with
f_{i}\in\mathfrak{m}_{[[x]]},m_{i}\in M
then there exists
f\in\mathfrak{m}_{[[x]]}
such that
f\cdot m=m
. For this it suffices to take
f=\chi_{U}
where
U
is the union of the supports of the
f_{i}
.

Let us write
\iota:K\rightarrow E
and
\pi:E\rightarrow Q
for the maps in the short exact sequence. It is evident that
\pi_{[x]}
is surjective. If
e\in E
represents an element of
\ker(\pi_{[x]})
, then
\pi(e)\in\mathfrak{m}_{[x]}\cdot Q
,
and we can find
f\in\mathfrak{m}_{[x]}
such that
\pi(e-f\cdot e)=0
.
By exactness we thus have
e=f\cdot e+\iota(k)
for some
k\in K
, and hence
\iota(k)
and
e
represent the same element in
E_{[x]}
. It follows that
\ker(\pi_{[x]})=\im(\iota_{[x]})
. Finally, assume that
k\in K
represents an element of
\ker(\iota_{[x]})
. Then we have
\iota(k)\in\mathfrak{m}_{[x]}\cdot E
, and we find
f\in\mathfrak{m}_{[x]}
such that
\iota(k)=f\cdot\iota(k)=\iota(f\cdot k)
. Since
\iota
is injective this means that
k=f\cdot k
is contained in
\mathfrak{m}_{[x]}\cdot K
, so that
k
represents
0\in K_{[x]}
.
∎

Let us also state the following version of the well-known local-to-global principle of commutative algebra, compare
[
2
, Chapter 3]
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\phi:M\rightarrow N
be a homomorphism of essential
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-modules. The
\phi
is an isomorphism iff
\phi_{[x]}:M_{[x]}\rightarrow N_{[x]}
is an isomorphism for all
[x]\in\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
.

Proof.

Again we briefly sketch the proof for the convenience of the reader.
If
\phi
is an isomorphism then clearly all localised maps
\phi_{[x]}
are isomorphisms.

For the converse, let us first show that if an essential
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-module
V
satisfies
V_{[x]}=0
for all
[x]\in\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
then
V=0
.
For this let
v\in V
and choose a compact open set
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
such that
\chi_{K}\cdot v=v
. Let
[x]\in K
and note that
v=0
in
V_{[x]}
means that
v=f\cdot v
for some
f\in\mathfrak{m}_{x}
. Since the function
f
is locally constant we can thus find an open neighborhood
U
of
[x]
such that
\chi_{U}\cdot v=0
. Since this works for all
[x]\in K
we get
v=\chi_{K}\cdot v=0
.

With this in place, we can apply Lemma
6.2
to the short exact sequences
0\rightarrow\ker(\phi)\rightarrow M\rightarrow\im(\phi)\rightarrow 0
and
0\rightarrow\im(\phi)\rightarrow N\rightarrow N/\im(\phi)\rightarrow 0
to obtain canonical isomorphisms
\ker(\phi_{[x]})\cong\ker(\phi)_{[x]}
and
\im(\phi_{[x]})\cong\im(\phi)_{[x]}
, as well as
N_{[x]}/\im(\phi_{[x]})\cong(N/\im(\phi))_{[x]}
. This finishes the proof.
∎

\displaystyle=\sum_{\delta\in\mathcal{G}^{r(\alpha)}}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}^{s(\delta)}}\chi_{U}(\delta)f(\delta^{-1}\alpha\delta)F(\gamma^{-1}\delta^{-1}\alpha\delta\gamma,\gamma^{-1}\delta^{-1}\beta)

for a compact open bisection

\mathcal{G}

\mathcal{G}

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
-linear map

from

into

If

\mathcal{G}

\kappa^{\mathcal{G}}\otimes\id:\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}}\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}M\cong A(\mathcal{G})\otimes_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})}M\rightarrow\Hom_{A(\mathcal{G})}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}},A(\mathcal{G})\otimes_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})}M)\cong\Hom_{A(\mathcal{G})}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}},\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}}\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}M)

We can summarise our discussion as follows.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
M
be a
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
-module. Then the above construction yields a
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-linear
map
\kappa^{\mathcal{G}}:\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}}\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}M\rightarrow\Hom_{A(\mathcal{G})}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}},\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}}\otimes_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}M)

The
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-equivariant periodic cyclic homology groups in Theorem
6.1
are modules over
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
, and the
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant groups are modules over
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}_{ad})

is
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant, and hence induces an injective essential algebra homomorphism
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)})\rightarrow M(C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}_{ad}))

\mathcal{G}
-equivariant periodic cyclic homology groups as essential
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
-modules as well.

Let
U\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
be a
\mathcal{G}
-compact open and closed subset, that is,
U
is an open and closed set with
\mathcal{G}\cdot U\subseteq U
such that
\mathcal{G}\backslash U
is compact. We write
\mathcal{G}^{U}_{U}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
for the subgroupoid consisting of all arrows with source and target in
U
.
Multiplication by the characteristic function
\chi_{\mathcal{G}\backslash U}\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
yields a chain map

\Hom_{A(\mathcal{G})}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}}[0],\theta\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}(A))\rightarrow\Hom_{A(\mathcal{G}^{U}_{U})}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}^{U}_{U}}[0],\theta\Omega_{\mathcal{G}^{U}_{U}}(A_{U})),

where
A_{U}=\chi_{U}\cdot A
is the
\mathcal{G}^{U}_{U}
-algebra obtained from
A
by restriction from
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})
to
\chi_{U}\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G})\chi_{U}=\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G}^{U}_{U})
.
Since
\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is assumed to be paracompact it can be written as a disjoint union of compact open subsets. It follows that we obtain an isomorphism

\displaystyle\Hom_{A(\mathcal{G})}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}}[0],\theta\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}(A))

\displaystyle\cong\varprojlim_{U}\Hom_{A(\mathcal{G}^{U}_{U})}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}^{U}_{U}}[0],\theta\Omega_{\mathcal{G}^{U}_{U}}(A_{U}))

\displaystyle\cong\varprojlim_{U}\varprojlim_{m}\Hom_{A(\mathcal{G}^{U}_{U})}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{G}^{U}_{U}}[0],\theta^{m}\Omega_{\mathcal{G}^{U}_{U}}(A_{U}))

by taking the inverse limit of the above chain maps over all
\mathcal{G}
-compact open
sets
U\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. In a similar way we obtain an isomorphism of chain complexes

\displaystyle\Hom_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}(

\displaystyle C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)})[0],\theta\Omega_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}(A\rtimes\mathcal{G}))

\displaystyle\cong\varprojlim_{U}\Hom_{C^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash U)}(C^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash U)[0],\theta\Omega_{C^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash U)}(A_{U}\rtimes\mathcal{G}_{U}^{U}))

\mathcal{G}
-compact open subsets

\displaystyle\gamma_{U}(

\displaystyle\langle a_{0}\rtimes\chi_{U_{0}}\rangle d(a_{1}\rtimes\chi_{U_{1}})\cdots d(a_{n}\rtimes\chi_{U_{n}}))

\displaystyle=\chi_{U_{0}\cdots U_{n}}\otimes\langle a_{0}\rangle d(\chi_{U_{0}}\cdot a_{1})d(\chi_{U_{0}}\chi_{U_{1}}\cdot a_{2})\cdots d(\chi_{U_{0}}\cdots\chi_{U_{n-1}}\cdot a_{n})

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varprojlim_{m}\theta^{m}\Omega_{C^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash U)}(A_{U}\rtimes\mathcal{G}_{U}^{U})}

\displaystyle b_{\mathcal{G}_{U}^{U}}\gamma_{U}(\langle a_{0}\rtimes\chi_{U_{0}}\rangle d(a_{1}\rtimes\chi_{U_{1}})\cdots d(a_{n}\rtimes\chi_{U_{n}}))

Using Lemma
6.5
and Lemma

6.1
is a consequence of the following localised version of the Green-Julg theorem.

Theorem 6.6
.

Under the above assumptions the chain map
\gamma^{\mathcal{G}}
induces an isomorphism

HP_{*}^{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}(C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}),A\rtimes\mathcal{G})_{[x]}\cong HP_{*}^{\mathcal{G}}(C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)}),A)_{[x]}

[x]\in\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}

Proof.

Let us fix
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
.
The restricted groupoid
\mathcal{G}^{[x]}_{[x]}
is discrete, and the localisation
A_{[x]}
is naturally a
\mathcal{G}^{[x]}_{[x]}
-algebra. Similarly, the localisation
A_{x}

of
A
at the maximal ideal of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})
consisting of all functions vanishing at

is naturally a
\mathcal{G}^{x}_{x}
-algebra, and we note that
the isotropy group
\mathcal{G}^{x}_{x}
is finite.

Consider first the left hand side of the formula in Theorem
6.6
. A direct inspection shows that we have a canonical isomorphism

\Omega_{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}(A\rtimes\mathcal{G})_{[x]}\cong\Omega(A_{[x]}\rtimes\mathcal{G}_{[x]}^{[x]})

-algebra and localisation is exact by

\displaystyle HP_{*}^{C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)})}(C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}\backslash\mathcal{G}^{(0)}),A\rtimes\mathcal{G})_{[x]}

\displaystyle=H_{*}(\Hom(\mathbb{C}[0],\theta\Omega(A_{[x]}\rtimes\mathcal{G}^{[x]}_{[x]})))

\displaystyle\cong HP_{*}(A_{[x]}\rtimes\mathcal{G}^{[x]}_{[x]}).

Moreover we have a Morita equivalence

A_{[x]}\rtimes\mathcal{G}_{[x]}^{[x]}\sim_{M}A_{x}\rtimes\mathcal{G}^{x}_{x},

and according to
[
10
]
this induces an isomorphism

HP_{*}(A_{[x]}\rtimes\mathcal{G}_{[x]}^{[x]})\cong HP_{*}(A_{x}\rtimes\mathcal{G}^{x}_{x}).

It follows that the left hand side of the formula in Theorem
6.6
identifies with the ordinary periodic cyclic homology of the group crossed product

In a similar way we can describe the right hand side. Using that

\displaystyle HP_{*}^{\mathcal{G}}(C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)}),A)_{[x]}

This reduces the assertion to the Green-Julg theorem in group equivariant cyclic homology. Indeed, following through the above isomorphisms one checks that the map
