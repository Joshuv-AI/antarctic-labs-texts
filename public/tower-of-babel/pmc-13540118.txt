Immunogenicity and safety of prophylactic HPV vaccines in people living with HIV: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Bahar Senel, Gokhan Senel, Ceren Elma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHuman papillomavirus (HPV) is a major global public health concern, causing genital warts and cancers of the cervix, anus, oropharynx, vulva, and penis. People living with HIV (PLWH) face a disproportionately elevated burden of HPV infection and HPV-related malignancies due to chronic immunosuppression. We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis searching six databases from January 2006 to June 2026 without language restrictions. Twenty-five studies were included in the systematic review; 12 independent studies (N = 1,493 for HPV16) were included in the quantitative meta-analysis. Using a DerSimonian–Laird random-effects model with logit transformation, pooled seroconversion rates were: HPV16 97.8% (95% CI: 94.9–99.1%; I2 = 89.6%; 15 datasets), HPV18 94.2% (95% CI: 86.1–97.7%; I2 = 95.8%; 13 datasets), HPV6 97.0% (95% CI: 93.7–98.6%; I2 = 66.1%; 10 studies), and HPV11 97.1% (95% CI: 90.5–99.1%; I2 = 95.5%; 10 studies). CD4 count was the most consistently reported modifier of immunogenic response: HPV16 seroconversion was 98.5% in PLWH with CD4 > 350 cells/μL vs. 71.1% in those with CD4 ≤ 200 cells/μL (ACTG A5240). All three vaccine generations demonstrated high immunogenicity. Two doses of the nonavalent vaccine were non-inferior to three doses in virologically suppressed women (Papillon RCT). No vaccine-related serious adverse events were reported. GRADE certainty of evidence was moderate for HPV16, HPV6, and HPV11, and low for HPV18. Prophylactic HPV vaccination achieves high seroconversion rates across all vaccine generations in PLWH. CD4 count significantly modifies vaccine response, underscoring the importance of vaccination before severe immunosuppression develops. These findings support current international recommendations advocating HPV vaccination for all PLWH.KEYWORDS: HIV, HPV vaccine, immunogenicity, seroconversion, systematic review, meta-analysis, CD4 count

Introduction

Every year, hundreds of thousands of people die from cancers caused almost entirely by human papillomavirus (HPV) infection. Globally, HPV is responsible for approximately 690,000 new cancers annually, making it the most prevalent oncogenic sexually transmitted infection worldwide.1

PLWH face a disproportionately elevated burden of HPV infection. Immunosuppression impairs HPV clearance, promotes viral persistence, and accelerates progression to high-grade intraepithelial lesions and malignancy.2,3 The incidence of HPV-associated cancers – including cervical, anal, oropharyngeal, vulvar, and penile cancers – remains markedly elevated in PLWH even in the antiretroviral therapy (ART) era.4,5 Anal cancer occurs at rates 28- to 80-fold higher in people living with HIV (PLWH) than in the general population.6

The immunological consequences of HIV infection create a complex environment for vaccine responses. Chronic immune activation, T-cell dysregulation, and progressive CD4 lymphocyte depletion impair both humoral and cellular immunity, potentially limiting the magnitude and durability of vaccine-induced antibody responses.2,3 Antiretroviral therapy partially restores immune function, yet even virologically suppressed PLWH may mount attenuated responses to certain vaccines compared with HIV-negative individuals. Understanding these dynamics is critical for optimizing HPV vaccination strategies in this population.

Three generations of prophylactic HPV vaccines are currently available: the bivalent vaccine (HPV-2, Cervarix®, GlaxoSmithKline, Rixensart, Belgium; targeting HPV16 and HPV18), the quadrivalent vaccine (HPV-4, Gardasil®, Merck & Co., Rahway, NJ, USA; targeting HPV6, 11, 16, and 18), and the nonavalent vaccine (HPV-9, Gardasil-9®, Merck & Co., Rahway, NJ, USA; targeting nine HPV types). These vaccines demonstrate near-complete efficacy in HPV-naive immunocompetent populations.7,8 However, pivotal trials excluded people living with HIV (PLWH), leaving a critical evidence gap.9

Subsequent studies in PLWH demonstrated generally safe and immunogenic responses, though lower and more variable than in HIV-negative controls, with CD4 count, HIV viral load, and ART status emerging as key determinants of vaccine response.10–12 Prior meta-analyses are either outdated, restricted to specific vaccine types, or lack formal GRADE assessment.13,14 Critically, no prior meta-analysis has incorporated the ACTG A5298 trial15 and the Papillon randomized trial16 published through 2026.

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to: (1) pool HPV type-specific seroconversion rates in PLWH; (2) provide a narrative synthesis of geometric mean titers (GMTs) where quantitative pooling was not feasible due to assay heterogeneity; (3) investigate effects of CD4 count, ART status, vaccine generation, sex, and age group on immunogenicity; (4) assess clinical efficacy data; and (5) evaluate vaccine safety.

Patients/materials and methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines.17 The protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO on 6 June 2026 (CRD420261416881; last updated 19 June 2026), prior to formal screening and data extraction, which commenced on 8 June 2026. No deviations from the registered protocol were made. Ethical approval was not required as this is a systematic review of previously published data with no individual participant data.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they included PLWH of any age, sex, or CD4 count; reported administration of any prophylactic HPV vaccine; and reported seroconversion rates, GMTs, or clinical outcomes (HPV infection incidence, CIN 2/3, HGAIN/HSIL, or adverse events). For the quantitative meta-analysis, seroconversion was defined as development of detectable type-specific HPV antibodies above the assay-specific threshold (cLIA, IgG-LIA, or ELISA) at peak post-vaccination assessment (4–7 weeks after the final vaccine dose), among participants seronegative at baseline. One study (Denny 2013) reported post-vaccination seropositivity irrespective of baseline serostatus and was retained with a study-level note. Where studies reported multiple time points, the earliest peak post-vaccination measurement was used. RCTs, prospective and retrospective cohort studies, and controlled clinical trials published from January 2006 to June 2026 were included. Studies were excluded if they included exclusively HIV-negative populations, reported only modeled efficacy without primary data, or were protocol-only publications. Therapeutic HPV vaccine studies18,19 were retained in the systematic review for narrative synthesis only, as they address a fundamentally different clinical question from prophylactic vaccination; they were clearly distinguished from prophylactic vaccine studies and excluded from all quantitative meta-analyses. Deshmukh20 a Markov modeling study, was retained for its long-term outcome projections but was not included in the meta-analysis, consistent with the eligibility criterion excluding studies without primary outcome data. Murahwa21 was included as a cross-sectional post-vaccination surveillance study providing long-term follow-up data; it was retained for narrative synthesis only and excluded from the quantitative meta-analysis, as it did not report type-specific seroconversion rates.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Cochrane CENTRAL, CINAHL, Scopus, SCIE, and ClinicalTrials.gov on June 8, 2026, without language restrictions.22 The PubMed search combined MeSH terms and free-text keywords: (“HIV Infections”[MeSH] OR “HIV positive”[tiab] OR “people living with HIV”[tiab]) AND (“Papillomavirus Vaccines”[MeSH] OR “HPV vaccine”[tiab] OR “Gardasil”[tiab] OR “Cervarix”[tiab]) AND (“immunogenicity”[tiab] OR “seroconversion”[tiab] OR “efficacy”[tiab]).

Equivalent strategies were adapted for all databases. Reference lists of included studies were hand-searched. The complete search strategies for each database are provided in Supplementary Table S2. The search yielded 228 records in total (Supplementary Table S1). After removing 119 duplicate records using reference management software, 109 records were available for title and abstract screening. ClinicalTrials.gov was searched as a source of registered trial protocols and results; no unpublished eligible studies were identified.

The search was deliberately broad to maximize sensitivity. Grey literature was not formally searched; however, reference lists of all included studies and relevant systematic reviews were manually screened to identify additional eligible publications. The search was not date-restricted at inception but was limited to studies published from January 2006 onwards, corresponding to the period following initial regulatory approval of the quadrivalent HPV vaccine.

Study selection and data extraction

Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers using ASReview LAB (version 1.0), an active learning-based screening tool. Full texts of potentially eligible studies were assessed independently. Disagreements were resolved by consensus. Data were extracted independently by the primary reviewer using a standardized form and verified by the second reviewer. Extracted variables included study characteristics, population demographics, intervention details, and outcomes (HPV type-specific seroconversion rates with 95% CIs, GMTs, clinical outcomes, adverse events).

Risk of bias assessment

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs; n = 8) were assessed using the Cochrane RoB-2 tool.23 Observational and single-arm studies (n = 14) were assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS; scores ≥ 8 excellent, 7 very good, 6 good, ≤5 moderate).24 Complete domain-level risk-of-bias assessments are provided in Supplementary Tables S4 (RoB-2) and S5 (NOS).

Statistical analysis

Seroconversion rates were pooled using a logit transformation with a DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model. For studies reporting 100% seroconversion, a continuity correction of 0.5 was applied to the numerator and denominator prior to logit transformation to avoid undefined values. Study-specific 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the Wilson score method. Between-study variance (τ2) was estimated using the DerSimonian-Laird method. Where CD4 strata from the same study10 were entered as separate data points, they were treated as correlated subgroups and not counted as independent studies in the total study count. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran’s Q test and the I2 statistic (<25%: low; 25–50%: moderate; 51–75%: substantial; >75%: considerable). Pre-specified subgroup analyses explored heterogeneity by CD4 count category (>350, 201–350, ≤200 cells/μL), vaccine generation, sex/gender, and age group. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test where ≥ 10 studies were available.25 Certainty of evidence was assessed using GRADE.26 All analyses were conducted using Python 3 (scipy, numpy; Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA). To assess robustness, sensitivity analyses were performed using: (1) a reduced continuity correction (0.1 vs. primary 0.5); (2) the Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) estimator for τ2; and (3) the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation. Results were consistent across all methods (Supplementary Table S7).

Where fewer than ten studies were available for a given outcome, meta-analysis was conducted but publication bias assessment was not performed, as funnel plot asymmetry cannot be reliably assessed with small numbers of studies. Planned sensitivity analyses excluding high-risk studies and restricting to RCTs only were not conducted: no included studies were rated high risk overall, with the exception of Denny 2013 (high risk due to substantial protocol deviations), and only eight RCTs were available for HPV16 seroconversion, which was insufficient for a meaningful standalone meta-analysis.

Results

Study selection

Searches across six databases identified 228 records in total (PubMed/MEDLINE n = 152, ClinicalTrials.gov n = 40, Cochrane CENTRAL n = 18, CINAHL n = 8, Scopus n = 6, SCIE n = 4). After removing 119 duplicate records using reference management software, 109 records were screened by title and abstract; 66 were excluded, leaving 43 articles for full-text assessment. Of 43 full texts assessed, 8 were excluded (protocol-only publications n = 3; modeling study n = 1; no immunogenicity outcome n = 4). Of 35 eligible publications, 25 unique studies were identified after consolidating multiple publications arising from the same underlying cohorts (Supplementary Table S3). Twenty-five studies were included in the systematic review; of these, 13 provided type-specific seroconversion data and were included in the quantitative meta-analysis (Figure 1).

Study characteristics

The 25 included studies were published between 2009 and 2026, spanning 13 countries. Study designs comprised RCTs (n = 8; including placebo-controlled, head-to-head, and dose-comparison trials), single-arm open-label trials (n = 9), prospective cohort studies (n = 7), and other designs including a modeling study and a cross-sectional surveillance study (n = 2); some studies incorporated elements of more than one design category (see Table 1).42,43 Of these, 13 studies provided type-specific HPV seroconversion data and were included in the quantitative meta-analysis. The remaining 12 studies were included in the systematic review only, for the following reasons: reporting of geometric mean titers without type-specific seroconversion proportions30,31; clinical efficacy endpoints without peak immunogenicity data15,27; antibody persistence at late time points only37; cross-sectional post-vaccination surveillance design21; therapeutic HPV vaccine18,19; modeling study20; and overlapping cohort with a primary publication already included in the meta-analysis35,36,39. The quadrivalent vaccine (Gardasil, Merck & Co., Rahway, NJ, USA) was used in 10 meta-analysis studies, the nonavalent vaccine (Gardasil-9, Merck & Co., Rahway, NJ, USA) in 2, and the bivalent vaccine (Cervarix, GlaxoSmithKline, Rixensart, Belgium) in 1. Risk of bias

Among eight RCTs assessed with RoB-2, two were rated low risk27,30, five had some concerns12,15,16,18,35, and one was rated high risk,29 primarily due to substantial protocol deviations resulting in large data loss in the immunogenicity cohort. Kojic10 (ACTG A5240), a prospective cohort study, was assessed using the NOS and rated excellent (score 9/9). Of 14 observational studies assessed with NOS, scores ranged from 5 to 9. Complete domain-level assessments are provided in Supplementary Table S5. The most common source of concern among RCTs was open-label design and missing outcome data, particularly in studies terminated early due to pre-set futility rules (Wilkin ACTG A5298). Among observational studies, the primary limitation was the lack of a concurrent control group and potential confounding by ART status, CD4 count, and prior HPV exposure. Domain-level risk-of-bias assessments are provided in Supplementary Tables S4 (RoB-2) and S5 (NOS).

Immunogenicity outcomes

Fifteen datasets (N = 1,493; 12 independent studies) provided HPV16 seroconversion data for meta-analysis. The pooled random-effects rate was 97.8% (95% CI: 94.9%–99.1%; I2 = 89.6%) (Figure 2). Individual rates ranged from 71.1% (Kojic,10 CD4 ≤ 200 stratum) to 100% (Wilkin 2010,11 Levin 2010,12 Boey 202144). GMT data were available in four studies10,16,28,45; however, due to heterogeneity in serological platforms (cLIA, IgG-LIA, competitive Luminex immunoassay) and differing positivity thresholds, quantitative pooling of GMTs was not appropriate. A narrative synthesis is provided. Kahn28 reported GMTs of 3,234 mMU/mL for HPV16 and 613 mMU/mL for HPV18 at four weeks post-dose 3. Kojic10 reported higher GMTs in the CD4 > 350 stratum (HPV16: 1,200 mMU/mL) compared to the CD4 ≤ 200 stratum (571 mMU/mL), demonstrating the CD4 effect on antibody magnitude. Money 2016 reported 1.74- to 3.05-fold higher GMTs in virologically suppressed vs. non-suppressed women. Konopnicki 2026 reported comparable GMTs between two-dose and three-dose schedules across all nine vaccine types.

Subgroup analyses

CD4 count was the most consistently reported modifier of immunogenicity across included studies. In the ACTG A5240 cohort (Kojic 2014; N = 315), HPV16 seroconversion was 98.5% (CD4 > 350 cells/μL), 98.2% (CD4 201–350 cells/μL), and 71.1% (CD4 ≤ 200 cells/μL). Pooled HPV16 seroconversion rates by vaccine generation were: quadrivalent 97.8%, nonavalent 98.0%, and bivalent 100.0% (single study). The Papillon RCT (Konopnicki 2026) demonstrated non-inferiority of two doses (months 0 and 6) vs. three doses (months 0, 2, and 6) of the 9-valent HPV vaccine in virologically suppressed women with HIV (HIV RNA < 400 copies/mL, median CD4 649 cells/μL). In the modified intention-to-treat analysis (n = 90), seroconversion against all nine vaccine genotypes was 97.7% (42/43) with two doses vs. 97.9% (47/48) with three doses; the risk difference was 0.0024 (90% CI: −0.0886 to 0.0934), meeting the prespecified non-inferiority margin of 0.15 (Z = 2.7549, p = .0029). These results should not be generalized beyond virologically suppressed women.

The effect of ART-associated viral suppression on vaccine response was consistently observed across studies. Money et al.45 reported 1.74- to 3.05-fold higher peak antibody responses in women with suppressed HIV viral load compared to those with detectable viremia.45 Similarly, Brophy et al.13 demonstrated significantly higher GMTs in girls with suppressed viral load at the time of initial vaccination for HPV types 11, 16, and 18 at month 7, and for HPV types 6, 11, and 18 at month 24.13

Clinical efficacy

The ACTG A5298 RCT15 (N = 575, adults ≥ 27 y) demonstrated no statistically significant efficacy against persistent anal HPV infection, defined as detection of the same HPV type at two visits at least six months apart (vaccine efficacy 35%, 95.1% CI: −5% to 60%, p = .08), but 88% efficacy against persistent oral HPV infection using the same definition (95.1% CI: 2%–98%, p = .02). Median follow-up was 2.0 y. Post-treatment adjuvant vaccination (Gosens 2021, VACCAIN-P) did not prevent HGAIN recurrence (68.8% vaccine vs. 61.3% placebo; p = .38).

Safety

Safety data from 12 studies (N > 2,000) confirmed that HPV vaccination is safe in PLWH. Injection-site reactions (pain, erythema, swelling, induration) occurred in 20–62% of participants and were generally mild to moderate in severity. Systemic adverse events including fever, headache, malaise, and fatigue were reported in up to 64% of participants but were similarly mild and self-limiting. No vaccine-related serious adverse events were reported across any included study. No clinically significant changes in CD4 count or HIV viral load were observed following vaccination.

Publication bias and GRADE

Funnel plots were approximately symmetrical for HPV16 and HPV18; Egger’s test did not demonstrate significant asymmetry (p > .05).25

Certainty of evidence was assessed using the GRADE framework (Supplementary Table S6). The initial certainty for all seroconversion outcomes was moderate, reflecting the predominance of single-arm uncontrolled designs. For HPV16, HPV6, and HPV11, certainty was maintained at moderate (⊕⊕⊕◯), supported by the large effect size and observed dose-response gradient (CD4 effect), which offset downgrading for heterogeneity. For HPV18, certainty was downgraded to low (⊕⊕◯◯) due to considerable heterogeneity (I2 = 95.8%). Certainty for clinical efficacy outcomes was rated as very low (⊕◯◯◯) due to indirectness of the seroconversion surrogate endpoint and inconsistency between available RCTs. Immunogenicity, clinical efficacy, and safety were graded separately.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis represents the most comprehensive synthesis to date of HPV vaccine immunogenicity and efficacy in PLWH, building on prior meta-analyses that were either limited in scope or lacked formal GRADE assessment.46 Encompassing 25 studies in the systematic review and 13 studies in the quantitative meta-analysis, covering 1,493 participants across all three prophylactic HPV vaccine generations through June 2026. HPV vaccination achieves high pooled seroconversion rates across all four HPV types: HPV16 97.8%, HPV18 94.2%, HPV6 97%, and HPV11 97.1%

CD4 count emerged as the most consistently reported modifier of vaccine response. Seroconversion in the CD4 ≤ 200 stratum fell to 71.1% for HPV16, compared to 98.5% with CD4 > 350 cells/μL. This gradient supports current ACIP and WHO guidelines recommending vaccination for all PLWH with emphasis on earlier vaccination in the context of effective ART.47,48

HPV18 showed the lowest seroconversion and highest heterogeneity (I2 = 95.8%). The outlier result of 45% in the Kenyan adolescent cohort49 may reflect high background HPV18 seroprevalence in sub-Saharan Africa and perinatally acquired immunosuppression. The considerable heterogeneity for HPV16 (I2 = 89.6%) reflects diversity in study populations including vaccine generation, age group, and CD4 distribution, while the heterogeneity for HPV18 (I2 = 95.8%) additionally reflects methodological differences in serological assay methods and background seroprevalence. These sources of heterogeneity were explored in pre-specified subgroup analyses, with CD4 count being the most consistently reported effect modifier, though formal pooled subgroup analyses were not feasible given limited available data. The wide confidence intervals observed in several studies – most notably the Kojic 2014 CD4 ≤ 200 stratum (HPV16: 56.6%–82.3%) and Brophy 2018 (HPV16: 91.5%–99.0%; HPV11: 49.7%–78.0%) – reflect small sample sizes within CD4-stratified and pediatric subgroups, where individual variability in immune reconstitution contributes substantially to imprecision. Clinicians should interpret point estimates from these subgroups with caution.

The differential immunogenicity observed across vaccine generations warrants consideration. Quadrivalent vaccine studies consistently demonstrated high seroconversion rates across diverse populations, while the two nonavalent vaccine studies included in this meta-analysis (Konopnicki 2026, Boey 2021) similarly achieved near-complete seroconversion in well-controlled HIV disease.16,44 The single bivalent vaccine study (Denny 2013) demonstrated 100% HPV16 and 100% HPV18 seroconversion in South African women – consistent with the high immunogenicity of the AS04 adjuvant system.29 These findings suggest that all three vaccine generations are effective in PLWH when administered under optimal conditions of viral suppression and adequate CD4 counts.

Formal pooled subgroup analyses by age group and sex were not feasible due to inconsistent reporting across included studies. Narratively, age and sex did not appear to substantially modify vaccine immunogenicity. Studies in children living with HIV consistently demonstrated high seroconversion rates comparable to those in adults, supporting current guideline recommendations for early vaccination before sexual debut.12,13,49 Women living with HIV demonstrated robust responses across multiple studies conducted in diverse geographic settings, including sub-Saharan Africa, North America, and South Asia.29,45,50 These findings support the rationale for broad prophylactic HPV vaccination programs in PLWH.

The non-inferiority of the two-dose nonavalent schedule in the Papillon RCT16 has important public health implications, potentially improving vaccine uptake in resource-limited settings. The null result for anal HPV endpoints in ACTG A529815 requires contextualization: adults aged ≥ 27 y with high prior HPV exposure represent a challenging population, while vaccine-type-naive individuals clearly benefit from prophylactic vaccination.

Limitations of this review should be acknowledged. First, observational studies predominated across the systematic review (n = 17), with only eight RCTs, limiting causal inference. Second, the absence of a defined serological correlate of protection for HPV vaccines in immunocompromised populations precludes definitive conclusions about clinical protection from immunogenicity data alone. Third, serological assay heterogeneity across studies – encompassing cLIA, LIA, and ELISA platforms with different cutoffs – limits direct comparability of seroconversion rates. Fourth, duration of follow-up varied considerably across studies, and long-term antibody persistence data in PLWH remain sparse. Fifth, nonavalent vaccine data were available from only two studies, limiting conclusions about the newer HPV types,18,36,40 (52, 58). Sixth, publication bias cannot be entirely excluded; although funnel plots were approximately symmetrical, because only a limited number of studies were available for several outcomes, publication bias analyses should be interpreted cautiously.

Strengths of this review include prospective PROSPERO registration, a comprehensive search without language restrictions, formal RoB-2 and NOS risk-of-bias assessments, GRADE evaluation of certainty of evidence, and inclusion of the most recent evidence through June 2026, including the ACTG A5298 RCT and the Papillon trial. To our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis to include all three currently available HPV vaccine generations and the most recent randomized controlled trial data through 2026.

It is also important to acknowledge the inherent limitations of using seroconversion as a surrogate endpoint for clinical protection. No validated immunological correlate of protection has been established for HPV vaccines in immunocompromised populations, and the relationship between antibody titers and long-term protection against HPV infection and HPV-associated disease remains incompletely characterized in PLWH. The clinical efficacy data available from this review – including the null result for anal HPV endpoints in ACTG A529815 and the absence of HGAIN prevention in VACCAIN-P27 — highlight that immunogenicity does not automatically translate to clinical efficacy, particularly in populations with high preexisting HPV exposure. Future efficacy trials specifically designed for PLWH, with HPV-related disease endpoints rather than immunogenicity surrogates, remain an important research priority.

Conclusions

HPV vaccination is safe and achieves high seroconversion rates across diverse PLWH populations and all three vaccine generations. CD4 count is the most consistently reported modifier of humoral response, emphasizing the importance of vaccination before severe immunosuppression develops. Two-dose nonavalent vaccination was non-inferior to three doses in virologically suppressed women, with potential implications for program delivery in resource-limited settings. Certainty of evidence was moderate for HPV16, HPV6, and HPV11, and low for HPV18, highlighting the need for further high-quality studies in this population. These findings support current international recommendations advocating prophylactic HPV vaccination for all PLWH as early as possible.

Future research should prioritize randomized trials in diverse PLWH populations, standardization of serological assay methods to enable cross-study comparisons, and long-term follow-up studies to define the durability of vaccine-induced immunity. Integration of HPV vaccination into routine HIV care programs has the potential to reduce the disproportionate burden of HPV-related malignancies in this population.
