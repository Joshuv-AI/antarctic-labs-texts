Cerebral Fat Embolism After Traumatic Long‐Bone Fracture: A Case Report
Authors: Mariachiara Ippolito, Vincenzo Benenati, Valentina Gambino, Maria Lapi, Dario Nicosia, Alessandro Pace, Gabriele Presti, Fiammetta Ronga, Noemi Spina, Marco Tutone, Rosaria Vanella, Antonino Federico, Antonino Giarratano, Andrea Cortegiani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe here describe a rare case of a 26‐year‐old male patient, presenting to our hospital after a motorcycle–car accident for the surgical management of long‐bone fractures to the tibia and femur and shoulder dislocation and undergoing orthopedic surgery, who postoperatively developed seizure, coma, and respiratory failure due to a cerebral fat embolism. After some hours after the surgery, a loss of consciousness was described by caregivers, and the ICU team was alerted, and the patient was admitted to ICU. Intracranial hemorrhage and stroke were subsequently excluded and, finally, a whole‐body computed tomography angiography scan and head magnetic resonance imaging showed lesions consistent with cerebral fat embolism. Patent foramen ovale was then suspected by contrasted echocardiography. An early diagnosis helped to choose the correct management, with early tracheostomy, and to correctly interpret and manage hemodynamic dysregulations. Overall, the care provided lead to positive long‐term outcomes, as the patient was discharged alive and successfully completed neurological rehabilitation, regaining many functional abilities. The initial management, diagnostic pathway, and ICU management are here described.Keywords: case report, fat embolism syndrome, intensive care unit, trauma

Introduction

Traumatic long‐bone fractures may determine fat embolization syndrome, a rare condition occurring up to 72 h after the trauma and usually presenting with petechial rash, deterioration of consciousness, and respiratory failure [1]. In cases with a predominant central nervous system impairment, the syndrome has been defined as cerebral fat embolism [1]. The pathophysiology of the syndrome has not been fully explained but seems based on the microembolization of fat particles, able to reach the blood and systemic diffusion despite lung filter, with main final localization to the brain, and determining both mechanical and inflammatory/biochemical damages to the involved tissues and vessels. Patients affected may present clinical severity with acute respiratory distress syndrome, coma, and hemodynamic impairment, thus needing urgent intensive care unit (ICU) admission. However, the differential diagnosis may be challenging, and magnetic resonance imaging of the brain is necessary, despite no formal criteria have been adopted to diagnose the syndrome [2, 3]. Magnetic resonance imaging characteristically shows multiple hyperintense lesions with the typical “starfield pattern,” with lesions distributed in deep border zones and in deep white and gray matter, basal ganglia, thalamus, and cerebellum [1, 3]. The syndrome lacks a specific therapy, and the management is based on the supportive and intensive management of organ failure and hemodynamic impairment and the prevention of neurological worsening. A prompt diagnosis and correct management are pivotal for the survival of such patients, who may lately obtain neurological recovery, even in cases with an initial presentation with coma [1]. It is here presented a rare case of cerebral fat embolism after road trauma with positive long‐term outcomes, adhering to the CARE Guidelines [4].

Case History/Examination

We here describe the case of a 26‐year‐old male patient, with a silent past medical history, referred to our hospital after a motorcycle–car accident for the surgical management of long‐bone fractures to the tibia and femur and shoulder dislocation.

The patient was rescued out‐of‐hospital, and first assessed at a primary‐level hospital where head, spine, thoracic, pelvic, and abdominal traumas were excluded, prior to being transferred to our tertiary center to receive the orthopedic surgery. The patient was described as fully awake, spontaneously breathing, and hemodynamically stable, with negative objective examination except for long‐bone fractures. Due to the need for road transfer, several hours passed between the trauma and the urgent surgery.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

A computed tomography (CT) angiography was performed upon arrival at our center. Traumatic fractures were confirmed, and vascular involvement and pulmonary thromboembolism were excluded. At this point, the patient had developed clinical and hemodynamic instability due to active bleeding at the fracture sites, and thus the team decided to proceed with urgent surgery with the placement of an external fixator for the left femur and tibia exposed fractures and shoulder reduction. The operation was completed uneventfully under general anesthesia, and the patient was discharged stable from the operating room to the orthopedic ward.

About 3 h after being discharged from the operating room, alterations in consciousness were observed, with a neurological examination revealing aphasia, upward gaze deviation to the left, an inability to execute simple orders, and grimacing in response to painful stimulation. The patient was spontaneously breathing with SpO2 96% and was still hemodynamically stable. Intensivists consultation was asked, and the patient was admitted to intensive care unit (ICU) after CT imaging excluding brain or other sources of hemorrhages. During the first hours of ICU stay, the patient further deteriorated developing respiratory failure, and a Glasgow Coma Scale of 8 (E4, V2, M3) with occasional myoclonus in the right limbs, and thus underwent endotracheal intubation and mechanical controlled ventilation. Electroencephalogram was unremarkable for acute events. A CT angiography was thus repeated, showing unclear areas of brain lesions, clearly excluding hemorrhage, and not classically suggesting ischemic nature. The suspicion of cerebral fat embolism was posed according to the presence of Gurd and Wilson's criteria (Major: respiratory insufficiency and cerebral involvement; minor: anemia, thrombocytopenia, fever, renal signs). Urgent head magnetic resonance imaging was performed under neurological consultation, revealing numerous punctiform unstable lesions, with ubiquitous distribution, more represented in border areas, and finally suggesting the diagnosis of cerebral fat embolism (Figure 1). Cardiological consultation was performed to investigate the presence of cardiac shunting. Transcranial ultrasound, transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiogram was performed with contrast saline bubble testing and allowed for the identification of thinning and signs suggesting patency of foramen ovale, with few microbubbles reaching the left atrium after saline injection in the jugular internal vein (Figure 2). Of note, contrast‐enhanced thorax CT scan excluded significant pulmonary artero‐venous malformation.

The main differential diagnosis reasoning regarded the exclusion of intracranial hemorrhage, which was ruled out at the first post‐operative CT scan, and stroke with cerebral ischemia, lately excluded at the subsequent CT scans and magnetic resonance imaging showing typical localization and characteristics of lesions. In addition, clinical worsening occurred during in‐hospital postoperative care, and post‐traumatic brain injury or axonal damage had already been excluded at emergency department (ED) presentation. The hypothesis of central nervous system infection was not clinically sound as not supported by clinical history, symptoms, signs, or microbiological results.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

After confirmation of cerebral fat embolism, antiplatelet therapy was started and venous thromboprophylaxis continued as per ICU protocol. Anti‐edema therapy was also administered daily with dexamethasone 8 mg daily, and seizure prophylaxis was started with levetiracetam 500 mg bid.

During the ICU stay, to ease ventilator weaning and reduce the risk of ventilator‐acquired pneumonia, the patient underwent an early percutaneous tracheostomy on Day 5 and was then weaned off the ventilator on Day 8, remaining under spontaneous breathing with additional low O2 flow through a tracheal T‐piece. Hemodynamic management included the management of frequent paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity events, causing acute episodes of tachycardia, hypertension, hyperhidrosis, and fever, sometimes presenting with shaking tremors or abnormal postures of upper limbs. Clinical presentation of such episodes, the worst of which presented with a maximum external temperature of 40°C and invasive blood pressure of 220/100 mmHg, often triggered differential diagnosis discussions among the caring physicians, considering ICU‐acquired infections and seizures that were, respectively, ruled out by microbiological testing and electroencephalogram registrations. A definitive interpretation of such episodes was pivotal for correct management, including administering effective analgesia, beta‐blockers, and clonidine, and for the avoidance of further prescriptions of anti‐convulsant drugs and antibiotics. Of note, an open visit policy was adopted for family visits to the patient.

The remaining ICU stay was characterized by supportive measures, such as the management of anemia and electrolyte disbalances, early and continuous artificial enteral nutrition, and physiotherapy. The patient underwent periodic magnetic resonance imaging and subsequent neurological assessment, witnessing a progressive worsening of the lesions, becoming larger and confluent (Figure 1b) as per natural evolution, but without clinical worsening or new lesions at imaging. Neurological status remained stable after the first 2 weeks of ICU stay, with a FOUR (Full Outline of UnResponsiveness) 5 Score of 10 (E3, M2, B4, R1) [5]. Of note, on Day 22, the patient was transferred to the hemodynamic lab for foramen ovale closure. However, no significant atrial or septal defect was identified during the procedure. Then, a vena cava filter was placed to prevent further episodes of embolism and on ICU Day 25, definitive long‐bone surgical osteosynthesis, removal of external fixators was performed. At Day 45, the patient was discharged to a neurological rehabilitation center. At the latest follow‐up, 6 months from discharge, the patient has regained consciousness, ability to speak and swallow, and is able to walk with assistance.

Discussion

We described a rare case of cerebral fat embolism in a patient who underwent orthopedic surgery after long‐bone road trauma, with no significant atrial or septal defect and without pulmonary localization of emboli. The most relevant issues regarding this case management were ICU admission, early diagnosis, and the importance of multidisciplinary team effort in diagnosis, management, and rehabilitation. These points guaranteed correct subsequent management, avoiding useless intervention (e.g., thrombolysis for suspected stroke). Indeed, intracranial hemorrhage, stroke with cerebral ischemia, post‐traumatic brain injury or axonal damage, and central nervous system infections [6] were excluded at differential diagnosis stage. Imaging was pivotal to confirming the diagnosis, in line with available literature. Indeed, it has been described that the presence of numerous cerebral hyperintensities in trauma patients with hospital‐acquired neurologic deterioration and the presence of a skeletal fracture is consistent with the diagnosis of cerebral fat syndrome [7]. Furthermore, in‐hospital neurological deterioration with no history of head trauma is also in line with the diagnosis. ICU management reduced the onset of complications (e.g., severe anemia and disseminated intravascular coagulation, acute respiratory distress syndrome, ventilator‐acquired pneumonia) described in other cases [1], and probably contributed to the favorable outcomes. Despite severe neurological impairments present at ICU discharge, improvements on consciousness, awareness, and activities of daily living were registered at 6 months. Despite the rarity of the syndrome and the lack of causative and specific therapies, there is evidence in the literature that late neurological recovery is possible, mostly in patients with lesions regression at magnetic resonance imaging within the first 2 months [1, 2]. Experts agree that ICU admission and early diagnosis, together with advancements in orthopedic management of long‐bone fractures, have over time reduced both the mortality and the incidence of this syndrome. A peculiarity of the case is given also by the silent medical history of the patient, rendering the syndrome rarer. It has been indeed described in patients with paraplegia [8], when diagnosis and treatment of fractures may be delayed due to the principal medical condition, or in patients with muscular dystrophy, in whom spontaneous unnoticed fractures are frequent [9]. The lack of definitive atrial or septal defect in case of cerebral embolism following traumatic bone fracture, although rare, has been previously described. In these cases, the biochemical process at the base of cerebral embolism seems to be more likely [10, 11]. This theory involves the release of inflammatory molecules causing the release of fatty acid and the alteration of plasmatic fat transport. This may lead to aggregation of fat droplet and the sequestration in the microcirculation leading to tissue damage. The human and emotional management of such case remained challenging for the entire ICU stay, due to the expectation of relatives on neurological outcomes, once life‐threatening conditions come to resolution. The adoption of an open policy for family visits to the patient was decided by the team of physicians, in order to meet the emotional and psychological needs of parents and relatives and provide support. The adoption of this policy was rendered particularly challenging due to the occurrence of paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity episodes [12] during family visits, which were interpreted by the parents as the patient's emotional responses to their presence and regain of consciousness. On the other side, the prolonged presence of parents inside the ICU favored their gradual acceptance of the patient's neurological early outcomes, and also promoted the humanizing of caregiving provided by both physicians and allied professionals to this patient who remained unable to communicate for his entire ICU stay.

Our report has the strength of presenting a rare case that has important clinical learning points for physicians working with critically ill patients that should keep in mind this potential diagnosis in similar scenarios. It also provided a good diagnostic pathway that is easy to implement in other centers. Finally, it showed a severe complication of road trauma long‐bone fractures, witnessing the need for immediate referral of patients to hub centers without delays. Limitations also exist, as the case report has a descriptive nature, and no definitive or generalizable conclusions can be drawn from our data. Furthermore, we did not collect samples to investigate the presence of fat droplets in bronchoalveolar lavage, blood, or urine, which are however not needed for a formal diagnosis and were judged not clinically relevant in this patient.

Delays in long‐bone fractures immobilization is a risk factor for complications. When caring for patients with delayed surgical treatment of long‐bone fractures, fat embolism syndrome, and cerebral fat embolism should be considered as potential complication. Preoperative CT angiography may help identify eventually visible fat embolus and may trigger further diagnostic or preventive procedures (e.g., exclusion of patent foramen ovale, cava filter). Early ICU admission and identification of cerebral fat embolism as a cause of coma should be considered, as the syndrome can become progressively severe and cause organ failures. In case of suspected cerebral fat embolism, patent foramen ovale, intracardiac defect or pulmonary arterial‐ venous malformation should be suspected until proven otherwise with contrasted (transthoracic and/or transesophageal) echocardiography and transcranial doppler and contrast‐enhanced chest CT scan. The absence of these alterations does not exclude the occurrence of cerebral fat embolism. Among survivors of fat embolism syndrome, neurological recovery can occur at late follow‐up or never occur; thus, humanizing the care and providing support to family and caregivers should be a part of the ICU care of such patients.
