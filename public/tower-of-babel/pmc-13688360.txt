Hope Tempered by Caution: Public Beliefs in the USA about the Benefits and Drawbacks of Using Epigenetic Technologies in Clinical Care
Authors: Erika A Waters, Caitlin Yuen, Clarissa Gaona Romero, Julia Maki, Bryce Puesta Takenaka, Aantaki Raisa, Erin Linnenbringer, Jada G Hamilton, Jessica Mozersky, George P Souroullas, Chelsey R Carter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionClinical applications of epigenetic technologies could revolutionize precision medicine. However, successful uptake in clinical settings may be influenced by public opinion. We investigated public beliefs about possible benefits and drawbacks of epigenetic-based medical technologies.MethodsWe recruited 153 adults from across the USA to participate in 1 of 27 virtual focus groups, each lasting 45–75 min. Groups were stratified by education (less than bachelor’s degree vs. bachelor’s degree or more) and race and ethnicity (black or African American, Asian, Indigenous [Native Hawaiian, Pacific Islander, American Indian, Alaska Native], Hispanic, and white). Participants viewed an educational video about epigenetics prior to discussion. We used inductive and deductive qualitative content analysis.ResultsParticipants indicated that epigenetic technologies could be used to guide risk-stratified prevention (i.e., engage in behaviors to reduce the risk of developing a health condition; engage in screening and early-detection behaviors to enable early treatment; facilitate clinical history-taking; inform reproductive planning decisions) and to develop novel therapeutics and editing approaches (i.e., new medications; epigenetic editing). However, participants also mentioned concerns about the ethical, legal, and social implications (ELSIs) of epigenetics, including exacerbating health disparities; the high cost of treatment and health insurance; promoting eugenics; and epigenetic-based discrimination by healthcare providers. Each of the race, education, and ethnicity strata included content related to each of the three major subthemes of risk-stratified prevention, novel therapeutics, and ELSI considerations.ConclusionEfforts to translate basic epigenetics research to clinical practice hold promise, but it is also critical to create solutions that address people’s concerns.Keywords: Ethical, legal, and social implications; Epigenetics; Qualitative research

Introduction

Chemical alterations to the parts of DNA that regulate gene expression can affect human health in profound ways without changing the underlying DNA sequence [1]. Many types of such epigenetic modifications can occur (e.g., DNA methylation, histone modification), and they can be temporary, permanent, or (in limited cases) [2] transmissible to future generations. The effect of epigenetic modifications on health depends upon the gene whose expression is being altered. The downregulation of a tumor suppressor gene or the upregulation of an oncogene could both promote the development of cancer; in contrast, the upregulation of a tumor suppressor gene or the downregulation of an oncogene could inhibit cancer development [3].

An increasing number of medical technologies that rely on epigenetic mechanisms of action are being developed for a variety of health conditions, including metabolic conditions, neurological conditions, and different types of cancers [4]. These technologies hold promise for treating health conditions on a more personalized basis than many current therapeutics. For example, there are several FDA-approved drugs that target different epigenetic mechanisms of cancer (e.g., decitabine).

Due to linguistic and superficial conceptual similarities between the words epigenetics and genetics, it is possible that beliefs about the benefits and drawbacks of epigenetic technologies could echo beliefs about other genetics-related technologies. For example, although members of the public have recognized the potential for gene therapy to treat serious health conditions such as sickle cell anemia [5], they have also expressed concerns about potential side effects, the cost of these technologies, and potential misuse of genetic information, such as discrimination, “playing god,” and eugenics [5–7]. On the other hand, beliefs about the benefits and drawbacks of epigenetics might differ from those of genetics due to the increased emphasis in epigenetics on the role of the environment and lifestyle behavior in promoting epigenetic modifications. Skepticism about epigenetics research may have particular resonance for populations who have experienced mistreatment, exclusion, and under-representation in biomedical research and clinical settings due to their race, ethnicity [8], or level of formal education [9]. For example, experiences of medical mistreatment and broken trust could promote skepticism about the intended use of epigenetics technologies or information, which could limit the reach of clinical therapeutics in these groups, further exacerbating health disparities [10].

The work described in this manuscript is part of a program of research whose purpose is to facilitate the translation of epigenetics research into clinical and public health practice. Future steps involve nationally representative surveys and experiments to identify specific communication strategies. This study had two objectives: (1) to identify public beliefs about the benefits and drawbacks of using epigenetics technologies in clinical care and (2) to explore whether public beliefs varied between people with more vs. less formal education and among people from different racial and ethnic backgrounds.

Methods

Design and Participants

We conducted virtual focus groups of 45–75 min in duration. Inclusion criteria were age 18 years or older, spoke English, resided in the USA, used video conferencing technologies, and were at least minimally familiar with the concept of genetics (e.g., when asked to describe genetics, responded with a simple explanation including such terms as heredity, DNA, traits, or inheritance). The groups were stratified by self-reported education (bachelor’s degree or higher vs. less than a bachelor’s degree) and self-reported race and ethnicity (Asian, black or African American, Indigenous [which included American Indian, Native Hawaiian, Native Alaskan, Pacific Islander], white, and Hispanic or Latino/Latina). Individuals who identified as more than one race or ethnicity were asked which group they preferred to join.

Procedure

We recruited participants by placing paid Facebook and Craigslist advertisements in cities with larger Asian, Indigenous, and Hispanic populations based on US Census data,1 distributing flyers throughout the St. Louis area with a focus on neighborhoods with larger Asian, black, or Hispanic populations, promoting the study via partners with ties to local communities, word of mouth [11, 12], and posting on Researchmatch.org, a national opt-in panel of potential research participants. Interested individuals completed a telephone or online eligibility screener. A team member confirmed eligibility, checked for possible misrepresentation (see online suppl. Section A; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/phg/aenag003), and emailed participants a videoconference link and an appointment reminder the day before the focus group.

Each focus group included a facilitator and notetaker. The facilitator began by reviewing study logistics (including elements of consent), making introductions, offering the chance to use a pseudonym, and asking an ice-breaker question. Discussion began by eliciting participant perspectives on the meaning of genetics, prior exposure to epigenetics concepts, and presumed meaning of epigenetics. The facilitator then played an educational video about epigenetics twice (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=81rFpRsF80c; see transcript in online suppl. Section B). The video introduced viewers to the concepts of genetics and epigenetics broadly, spent considerable time on concepts related to epigenetics inheritance, and closed with a brief discussion of stress, lifestyle, and the environment. The remaining discussion was about reactions to the video, positive and negative implications of epigenetics for clinical care, personal or family health considerations, and wider social implications (e.g., environmental considerations, possible misuse). After discussion, participants received a resource guide for optional continued learning about epigenetics, completed a survey assessing demographic characteristics, and selected a gift card (USD 75 for a physical Walmart card or a virtual Amazon card). The full final facilitator’s guide, fieldnote template, demographic survey, and participant resource guide can be located at https://osf.io/q4j5t/.

Facilitator’s Guide and Stimuli

The goal of the facilitator’s guide was to understand people’s beliefs about a variety of possible uses or implications of epigenetics research and clinical technologies and therapies, while avoiding asking leading questions that might produce spurious results. Therefore, our questioning strategy always began with broad, open-ended questions (e.g., “What is your initial reaction to…”; “How could this information be used or misused?”; “What are the benefits and drawbacks to…?”). More directive follow-up questions were asked only after discussion related to these questions was exhausted.

We made several changes to the facilitator’s guide (see online suppl. Section C). In the first version of the guide, we included several questions designed to elicit beliefs about the benefits and drawbacks of epigenetics generally and clinical applications of epigenetics specifically. However, after the first eight focus groups, we noted little variability in responses. We therefore revised the guide to reduce the time allocated to questions related to clinical applications to ensure coverage of additional topics. In these first eight groups (4 white, 4 black or African American, equally divided by education), we also noticed that participants might need broader information about epigenetics to converse comfortably about different topics. We searched for a different video but did not find an acceptable substitute (e.g., <5 min in duration, intended for lay audiences, did not include childish-seeming animation, did not use a condescending or scornful tone toward any already-stigmatized groups). Therefore, before we discussed a topic in the focus groups, we referred back to the relevant content in the video and provided an example from research (e.g., before discussing environmental effects on epigenetics, we mentioned the video’s discussion of the environment and provided a one-sentence description of a study examining epigenetic effects of air pollution). Finally, a team member proposed that news headlines are another way that people might be exposed to epigenetics information. Consequently, we presented several real news headlines to participants and solicited their responses (e.g., “Will Your Lifestyle Hurt Your Grandchildren?” [13]).

Research Team and Reflexivity

The facilitators and notetakers included undergraduate and graduate students, a postdoctoral fellow, a research coordinator, and an assistant professor. Co-authors C.Y., C.G.R, J.M., B.P.T., A.R., and C.R.C. were facilitators or notetakers, and all facilitators were women except B.P.T. and one other male student. J.M. and C.R.C. each had more than 5 years experience conducting qualitative research; the trainees had 0–1 year of experience. The research team and participants had no relationship prior to the focus group. The only thing the participants knew was the goal of the research study and that they would be grouped by race and ethnicity (this was disclosed to explain why we asked for their race and ethnicity during eligibility screening). All facilitators and notetakers had a strong interest in conducting research that alleviates health disparities in the USA.

Analysis Plan

Video recordings were transcribed by Landmark Associates, Inc. The research team evaluated the transcripts for accuracy and to ensure that they contained no personal identifiers.

Guided by thematic analysis that included inductive and deductive elements, members of the research team began developing the codebook by generating a preliminary set of themes based on concepts in the literature on the ethical, legal, and social implications of genomics research (e.g., clinical applications, discrimination, determinism). Eight trained coders then reviewed 1–2 transcripts each, noting possible additional themes. All education, race, and ethnicity strata were represented in this initial round of review. The coders met to review and refine theme ideas into an initial codebook. Each coder reviewed 1–2 additional transcripts using this initial codebook and further reviewed and refined themes. After several iterations of this process, a final codebook was identified containing 27 themes that were interpreted into six broad categories. The full codebook can be viewed at https://osf.io/q4j5t/.

Four teams of two coders each used NVivo version 14 to independently code the contents of each transcript. Coders were permitted to assign multiple themes to a single segment of text, when appropriate. Any disagreements were discussed and resolved between coders. If necessary, the project manager served as tiebreaker. To prevent dyad-specific interpretations and applications of the codebook, coding assignments were rotated for each transcript, so each coder worked with all other coders.

Once all transcripts were coded, separate summaries were created for each theme. Theme summaries were created in stages. First, all the content coded to a particular theme was exported from NVivo to a dedicated document that was organized by strata. One coder reviewed all the content in the document and noted recurring sub-themes within each theme. Then, the coder wrote a 1–3 paragraph summary of the theme and each sub-theme, including representative quotes. Finally, the summary was reviewed by other coders to confirm and clarify interpretations. The study community advisory board provided feedback on the findings.

To address this study’s objective, we focus on the theme Epigenetics in Clinical Care. It was applied to transcript text “that suggest the ways that epigenetics can be utilized in biomedical settings. Concerns could be about data sharing, privacy, physician bias, insurance.” To identify potential patterns by race, ethnicity, or education within the Epigenetics in Clinical Care theme, a coder created a table [14] based on the theme summary and the text exported from NVivo related to this theme. Each row represented a sub-theme, and each column represented a race or ethnicity stratum. Each cell contained quotes related to the subtheme for the respective race or ethnicity stratum. The quotes within each cell were highlighted with different colors to signify different education strata. The coder then wrote 1–2 sentences describing the findings for each race, ethnicity, and education stratum.

We held 2–4 groups per stratum and each stratum included 11–20 participants (Table 1). The sample was composed of mostly people who reported being middle aged and female with moderate subjective socio-economic status and “good” self-reported health status (Table 2). Novel Epigenetic Therapeutics and Targeted Epigenome Editing Approaches

In our data, participants posited that epigenetics research might lead to new ways to treat existing health conditions. However, it is important to note that discussions related to this sub-theme occurred less frequently than discussions of other sub-themes, and conversations were less elaborate in groups of participants with less formal education. Participants focused mainly on the development of pharmaceuticals that could “really make people live longer and more productive lives” (I/LE). Some statements focused on manipulating the epigenetic mechanisms themselves: “they can learn how to alter these flags, remove the flags, somehow, through chemical effect or medication or something” (W/LE). Participants also identified similarities between epigenetics and other novel genomics technologies: “I was just thinking about CRISPR and the gene editing that they’re doing now. If this epigenetic thing comes to fruition…they might start editing those things too” (W/HE). That said, as is described below in more depth, epigenetics was also discussed in the context of existing health disparities: “Is this [drug] gonna be just for general patients, or is it gonna be for African American patients? Because actually, our race is the most high-risk of everything” (BAA/LE).

Risk-Stratified Prevention

In our study, there was extensive discussion across all education, race, and ethnicity strata of the ways in which epigenetics could be used to identify people at elevated risk of disease, which could then guide recommendations for taking risk-reducing actions. Both primary and secondary prevention activities were discussed. For example, one participant noted, “If we know that someone is more susceptible to these certain things, we can start doing these behaviors early or be testing more often or whatever needs to be done to help us prepare to avoid these diseases or things that potentially could happen later on” (BAA/HE). Other participants mentioned “early detection, especially for cancer” (I/LE). However, there were also participants who preferred to not know about being at elevated risk of developing future disease, particularly if there was an assumption that the disease is not preventable; instead, they preferred to focus on diagnosis. One participant responded, “Will I have Parkinson’s? I don’t wanna know if I’m going to. Yeah, when I have my actual problem, I wanna know, but don’t tell me that, in 10 years, I’m gonna have it. That’s not gonna help me” (W/HE).

In a small number of groups, participants noted that widespread implementation of epigenetics might result in expanded health history questionnaires. For example, one participant suggested, “if doctors…have the knowledge that…maybe this is because of something [that] happened in your ancestry, it could build a more detailed history…It could help us use our identity to provide us with more…specialized, individualized support” (A/LE). On the other hand, some participants emphasized the importance of history-taking in general: “…What happened to your parents, your grandparents, your great-grandparents? Where did you come from? What sorta things could they have lived through? It’s affecting you….I think that it needs to go outside of just a genetics research topic and into real life—these are what doctors are being trained to talk to their patients about” (I/HE).

There was also discussion of using epigenetics for primary and secondary prevention in the context of reproduction. One strategy was “taking supplements for certain things” (W/LE) or taking other action to improve one’s own health prior to becoming pregnant, with the ultimate goal of protecting children and grandchildren from hardship “so they… can live a healthy, happy life” (BAA/LE). Another strategy was to use epigenetics to inform selection of reproductive partners. One participant considered, “This is almost like epigenetic tests for people who are considering having kids because, if you have two traumatized adults thinking about having a kid, is that a good idea, or are you just putting your kid on—in a negative position as they start out in life?” (A/HE). Participants also wondered if it was possible to edit out harmful markers in utero by “inject[ing] something into the embryo, while it’s in the mother’s womb, that will literally reverse all health ailments from ever affecting the child” (H/HE). Rarely mentioned concepts included using epigenetics to select eggs and sperm to be used for in vitro fertilization (BAA/LE) and the possibility of terminating a pregnancy based on the result of epigenetics (BAA/HE).

Ethical, Legal, and Social Consequences

In our data, concerns about potential misuse of epigenetics information were widespread and covered many topics. However, such conversations were less elaborate in groups of participants with less formal education and among groups of Hispanic participants. Nevertheless, cost-related concerns were among the most prominent issues raised by all groups. This manifested in two ways. First, participants discussed the possibility of the cost of novel epigenetics technologies further exacerbating existing health disparities. One participant questioned, “Does that mean that only they [people with higher incomes] get to live the longer lives, they get to have a more healthier, more productive life, while the lower socioeconomic—all the challenges they’re already dealing with that they don’t have these advantages because they can’t afford it?” (I/HE). Another participant noted the possibility for such effects to have generational effects: “… those who can pay a premium, they will get extremely individualized healthcare that will help them so much in their life and their kids as well [emphasis added], but the regular folk will have a disadvantage when it comes to that” (A/HE). One participant emphasized, “Black and brown [people] need to have these same options offered to them” (BAA/HE).

The second category of cost concerns in our study was related to health insurance companies. For example, participants expressed concerns related to denial of coverage for people with epigenetic markers: “Are you worth even insuring for health insurance, or do we just think there’s just too many factors in your past DNA line that are too much of a liability?” (H/HE). Relatedly, another participant expressed, “How insurance pulls things…if they can determine these things, is it gonna make you a risk for other things? Is it gonna throw some flag up, financially, that way?” (W/LE). Other participants noted that, even if people are technically eligible for health insurance, costs might be prohibitive: “We’re gonna give you a tier membership, but know that your premium is going to be through the roof because we’re labeling you a higher risk” (H/HE). Another participant worried about the possibility of “someone’s genetic makeup and their epigenetics, maybe, being leaked to the biopharmaceutical sector where…the health companies would make someone’s premium so much higher” (H/HE).

Participants also raised the possibility of epigenetics information being used to promote or perpetuate eugenic policies and discriminatory beliefs. For example, if someone was “prone to be dyslexic…they could turn it off. It’s like eugenics, unfortunately” (I/HE). There were also concerns expressed that misuse of epigenetics could perpetuate population-level harm: “I would actually worry about it being weaponized against communities…about them using this information to cause genocide or things that are knocking us out of our own future” (BAA/HE). Similarly, another participant worried, “Maybe someone wants to control birth rates, and they say, ‘You can’t have this baby because epigenetics shows that it’s gonna have this, this, and that,’ and it could be used for reasons in a bad way” (H/LE). Another participant said epigenetics technologies “can be controversial of playin’ God,” (W/LE) and another wondered, “who says what’s the right or the wrong and what should be fixed and what shouldn’t?” (W/HE).

There were also concerns that healthcare providers might use epigenetic information in a way that harmed patients. Whereas some participants hoped that epigenetic information would help providers be less dismissive of health concerns, “not just saying, ‘Well, if you lose weight, everything’s gonna be better’” (W/HE), other participants noted that epigenetics may prompt physicians to “jump the gun based on what you say about your past history” (I/HE) and draw premature conclusions about a diagnosis or the treatability of a health problem. Participants also expressed concerns about racial discrimination in the healthcare setting: “sometimes, [epigenetics] may keep them from treating you properly because they figured something is in your past, and [the physician] may make a judgment on it…especially…if it’s a white doctor” (BAA/HE). There were also concerns about epigenetic therapeutics and epigenome editing approaches being “a money grab…a reason to put people on more medications or do more tests that may not be necessary” (H/HE). Finally, concerns about privacy and data sharing were mentioned, albeit rarely: “Doctors share all that [information]. It’s not just kept private…all that information gets shared around if you give permission for it” (I/LE). Concerns about how healthcare providers might misuse epigenetic technologies were not mentioned in groups of Asian participants.

Discussion

We explored public beliefs about the benefits and drawbacks of using epigenetic technologies in clinical care. We found that, although participants identified several possible beneficial uses of epigenetic technologies, these were tempered by concerns related to possible ethical, legal, and social consequences of the use – and misuse – of such technologies. Participants indicated that epigenetic technologies could be used in beneficial ways by identifying individuals at elevated risk of developing various health conditions, guiding behavioral and pharmaceutical approaches to reduce risk, screening individuals for pre-clinical signs of disease, and providing curative targeted epigenetic therapeutics and editing approaches. Participants also mentioned that epigenetic technologies could be used to inform reproductive planning decisions and to provide healthcare providers with more detailed personal history information. But the potential benefits contrasted with concerns about exacerbating health disparities due to race and socio-economic status, the anticipated high cost of treatment, the potential for insurance companies to use epigenetics as an excuse to raise premiums or deny coverage, a risk of promoting eugenics, and potential interpersonal discrimination and data sharing breaches.

Participants’ expressions of their perceptions of the benefits and drawbacks of using epigenetics for clinical care are generally consistent with what is reported about genomic technologies more broadly, including gene therapy [6, 7] and genetic testing, to identify individuals at elevated risk of developing health conditions [15]. Critically, however, participants in this study who expressed concerns about interpersonal discrimination resulting from disclosure of epigenetic risk information, particularly within the healthcare setting, resonate with academic writings related to the potential for epigenetic information to produce “biocentric narratives” that pathologize entire communities [16] and perpetuate medical stereotyping and substandard care, particularly for black individuals [17]. As information about epigenetics is disseminated by information sources outside the medical community, such as social media content or celebrities [18, 19], it will become increasingly important for health communicators to develop effective strategies for conveying scientifically accurate information about epigenetics technologies and their capabilities.

It is important to note that some participants may have conflated genetic and epigenetic concepts. Several concerns raised by participants in our study are similar to concerns raised by participants in studies of genetics more generally (e.g., access to care, insurance coverage, discrimination, and data privacy and protection) [20–22]. In many cases, when the participant did not elaborate on their comments (e.g., the participant who mentioned dyslexia), it was unclear whether they were straightforwardly generalizing their concerns about genetics broadly to the epigenetics context, or whether they were expressing concerns unique to epigenetics. In other cases, such as the participant who mentioned multi-generational benefits of epigenetics therapeutics, it was clearer that participants were focused more on epigenetics. Future research could investigate the extent to which several of the more unique concerns raised in these data, such as those involving personal blame or responsibility, experiences of trauma, and perceived reversibility, are specifically informed by individuals’ distinct understanding of epigenetics as opposed to conflation with principles of genetics. Future research should also determine the extent to which confusing these two concepts influences attitudes related to translating epigenetic technologies to the clinical setting and communication strategies for alleviating this confusion.

Few studies have investigated laypeople’s cognitive, affective, or behavioral responses to epigenetics information, and the scope of those studies is much narrower than the broadly exploratory approach we took for the study presented here. For example, in three studies, the purpose was to understand people’s openness to using epigenetics information for risk-stratified prevention activities, such as smoking cessation [23], epigenetic-based cancer risk assessment [24], or cancer screening [25]. However, only the cancer screening article discussed epigenetic-based discrimination, stigma, reproductive rights, or data protection [25]. Our findings are therefore both complementary to and extensions of this previous research; the studies of specific conditions support our findings that people are open to using epigenetics research for prevention purposes, and our research highlights ethical, legal, and social implication considerations that may not have been explored in those studies. Our study’s purposive recruitment of a highly demographically-diverse study sample is novel among these studies and provides critical data needed to understand the perspectives of people from racial and ethnic groups who have been historically underrepresented in genetics research in the USA.

The broad sub-themes described above – risk-stratified prevention, novel epigenetic therapeutics and targeted epigenome editing approaches, and ethical, legal, and social consequences – were mentioned by participants in each of the education, race, and ethnicity strata. Nevertheless, there are suggestions of differences among strata that future research should investigate more fully, perhaps using individual interviews of 20–30 participants per stratum. Specifically, conversation in groups of participants with less (vs. more) formal education tended to include fewer concrete examples for the novel therapeutics and editing approaches and ethical, legal, and social consequences sub-themes. To speculate, this difference might be due to less formal education, which could offer less opportunity to learn about genetic concepts and how to frame them for discussion [9, 26, 27]. That said, apart from discussions of cost and health insurance, we observed relatively few mentions of ethical, legal, and social consequences specifically related to clinical applications of epigenetics among participants in the Asian and Hispanic strata. We do not know if this absence is an artifact of running too few focus groups, or if these issues simply were not the most prominent concerns related to clinical care among these groups. On the other hand, a relatively strong focus on ethical issues raised in the strata of black and Indigenous participants in our study is likely the result of a long history of these groups being the targets of medical mistreatment by eugenicists in the USA. [8, 28].

Strengths, Limitations, and Future Directions

Our study included a large, socio-demographically diverse sample from across the USA that was recruited using in-person and online strategies. This increases the likelihood that our findings are applicable to multiple segments of the USA population. Our use of inductive and deductive analytic approaches ensures that, while our investigation was grounded in the literature, it also left space for sub-themes to be identified that have not been previously reported.

We lacked the resources needed to employ a full community-engaged research approach. This might limit the relevance of findings to individuals who have limited trust in the biomedical research enterprise. Future research dedicated specifically to understanding the perspectives of these individuals would further enrich scientific understanding of public beliefs about the benefits and drawbacks of epigenetic technologies in clinical settings. It is also possible that the beliefs expressed by our general population sample would be different from the beliefs of people who are engaged in making decisions directly related to epigenetic technologies. Therefore, this research should be replicated with participants recruited in clinical settings to ensure perceptions of benefits and drawbacks that are directly relevant to medical decision making are considered.

Other limitations include the following: we did not collect information about the specific geographic location of participants; this limits our ability to draw conclusions about specific US locations. Our strategy for identifying indications of misrepresentation may not have been completely effective, but there is error in any study that uses self-reported data for eligibility screening. The differences in relative frequency of codes could be partially related to group dynamics, question timing, moderator prompts, or limited number of groups. Therefore, any assertions of relative frequency should be interpreted with this in mind; other studies with different sampling or stratification strategies might report different relative frequencies of ideas. The educational video and many of the news headlines shown to participants were focused on epigenetic inheritance; this might have artificially increased the salience of the topic in participants’ minds and their beliefs about the risks and benefits of epigenetics technologies. However, epigenetic inheritance is also a strong focus of popular media about epigenetics [19], and so it is likely that our findings are reflective of beliefs held by other members of the public. Nevertheless, it is important to investigate the effects of videos that address a broader variety of epigenetics-related topics. This study was not designed to test specific strategies for communicating epigenetic information in clinical settings; however, our future research will explore some of those important considerations.

Conclusion: Implications for the Future

Several findings from our study that are relevant to clinical practice were mentioned by participants across educational, racial, and ethnic backgrounds. First, in general, participants from across demographic backgrounds identified potential benefits of clinical epigenetic technologies, suggesting that some members of the public may be open to their use if concerns about cost, trust, privacy, discrimination, and insurance are addressed. Overall, these findings show that future translation efforts hold promise and that the key for success is to also create solutions to address people’s concerns.
