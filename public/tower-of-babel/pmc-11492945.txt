Association between prophylactic closure of mucosal defect and delayed adverse events after endoscopic resection: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Shaoxiong Yi, Qinbo Cai, Lele Zhang, Huafeng Fu, Jie Zhang, Mingxuan Shen, Rongman Xie, Jian Zhang, Xun Hou, Dongjie Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the potential of prophylactic closure of mucosal defects to prevent adverse events following endoscopic resection of superficial layers of the gastrointestinal (GI) wall.DesignSystematic review and meta-analysis.Data sourcesWe searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science and the Cochrane Library for studies eligible for inclusion in our meta-analysis from inception to February 2022.Data extraction and synthesisWe compared the effects of closure versus non-closure of mucosal defects with respect to adverse events including delayed bleeding, delayed perforation and postpolypectomy coagulation syndrome (PPCS). We used a random-effects model for all analyses. Subgroup analyses were performed based on gastrointestinal sites, surgical procedures and study designs.ResultsIn total, this study includes 11 383 patients from 28 studies. For delayed bleeding, closure group was associated with a lower incidence (Risk Ratio [RR]: 0.40, 95% Confidence interval [CI]: 0.30 to 0.53, p<0.001; I2=25%) and consistent results were observed in the subgroups. Also, for delayed perforation, a combined analysis of all sites and surgical methods showed a protective effect of prophylactic closure of mucosal defects (RR: 0.42, 95% CI: 0.22 to 0.82, p=0.01; I2=0%). Similar results were observed in the subgroup analyses, despite the wide CIs. Regarding the PPCS, neither the pooled RRs nor the subgroup analyses showed significant differences.ConclusionProphylactic closure of mucosal defects is beneficial in reducing the incidence of delayed bleeding and delayed perforation after endoscopic resection, but there is no significant difference in reducing the incidence of PPCS.

Introduction

Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) and endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) are recently developed techniques for gastrointestinal neoplasms with no risk of lymph node metastasis. The development of endoscopic technology has enabled the en bloc resection of superficial lesions in the GI tract even with submucosal invasion.1 Endoscopic resection by EMR or ESD is frequently performed instead of highly invasive surgical procedures.

Compared with conventional surgery, endoscopic resection is minimally invasive, less costly, has a lower mortality and less need for hospitalisation.26 However, endoscopic resection is linked to the development of some adverse events, such as intraoperative perforation, delayed bleeding, delayed perforation and postpolypectomy coagulation syndrome (PPCS) due to its technical difficulty.7 Intraoperative adverse events like perforation and bleeding might be ameliorated by rigorous training and technical development. On the contrary, it is difficult to prevent the occurrence of delayed adverse events by only technical development.8 According to previous studies, the rates of delayed bleeding, delayed perforation and PPCS after endoscopic resection have been reported as 1.0%–24%, 0.3%–10.4% and 4.8%–40.8%, respectively.933

It is believed that the occurrence of adverse events after endoscopic surgery is related to the mucosal defects, which may be subjected to various stimuli, such as food, digestive juices, GI peristalsis, etc. These stimuli may hinder the healing of the wound, and eventually lead to the rupture of the blood vessels on the mucosal defect or even perforation.34 According to these theories, prophylactic closure of the mucosal defects was supposed to be an effective procedure to prevent adverse events for an artificial ulcer wound after endoscopic resection.3538 However, some studies suggested that preventive mucosal defect closure had no significant effect on the prevention of complications.73941 One meta-analysis summarising eight randomised controlled trials (RCTs) studies concluded that prophylactic closure did not reduce the incidence of delayed bleeding after colorectal ESD,42 but another meta-analysis43 identified statistical significance in reducing delayed bleeding and delayed perforation. And a meta-analysis44 of prophylactic closure of mucosal defects in duodenal endoscopic resection concluded that it was protective in preventing delayed bleeding but not in delayed perforation. Recently, another meta-analysis45 which pooled four RCTs studies concluded that clip closure prevents delayed bleeding after EMR of proximal large non-pedunculated colorectal polyps, but has no effect on delayed perforation and PPCS. It is still controversial whether preventive closure of mucosal defects after endoscopic resection is beneficial to patients. We therefore conducted a comprehensive meta-analysis of all available randomised controlled trials and observational studies to clarify the effect of prophylactic closure in preventing delayed adverse events after endoscopic resection at various GI sites.

Materials and methods

Search strategy

We conducted a systematic review of articles published in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science and the Cochrane Library from the inception to February 2022. All the search strategies were modified according to the following combination of keywords: (‘Endoscopic Submucosal Dissection’ OR ‘Endoscopic Mucosal Resection’ OR ESD OR EMR) AND (closure) AND (bleeding OR perforation OR ‘postpolypectomy coagulation syndrome’ OR PPCS). The detailed search strategies were provided in online supplemental table 1. Reference lists of included studies were scrutinised in order to identify potential additional studies missed through the original search strategy. Further, we restricted the search to studies written in English.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We considered studies eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: 1. Population: patients undergoing endoscopic resection for GI lesions (gastric, duodenum and colorectal); 2. Intervention: prophylactic closure; 3. Comparator: non-closure; 4. Outcome: incidence of delayed bleeding, delayed perforation or PPCS (also known as postpolypectomy syndrome and transmural burn syndrome46 after endoscopic resection; 5. Study design: RCT or observational studies (including retrospective and prospective studies). The following exclusion criteria were included: 1. Reviews, case reports/series, letters and conference abstracts, single-arm study and 2. studies not published in English.

Quality assessment

We assessed the risk of bias of RCTs using the revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomised trials (RoB-2).47 The risk of bias of observational studies was assessed using the risk of bias in non-randomised studies of interventions (ROBINS-I) tool.48 The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE)49 Approach was performed to assess the overall quality of evidence on outcome level for the analyses. The quality may be rated as high, moderate, low or very low. GRADE scoring was performed using the software GRADEpro, V.3.6.1 for Windows. RoB-2, ROBINS-1 and GRADE scoring were performed individually by two reviewers. Discrepancies were settled by a third reviewer.

Study selection

Two independent reviewers screened the titles and abstracts of all candidate studies for inclusion in the articles. Studies were obtained for a full-text review if they could not be identified by the title and abstract. Disagreements were solved by discussion.

Data extraction

The following data were independently extracted from each eligible study by two investigators: first author, year of publication, study period, region, study design, intervention, lesion location, patient number, gender, lesion size and incidence of adverse events. Data extraction was carried out independently by two reviewers. Disagreements were resolved by consultation with a third reviewer.

Statistical analysis

A random-effects model was used to calculate the pooled RRs and corresponding 95% CIs. The DerSimonian and Laird method (D-L method) were used for weighting. Heterogeneity analysis was performed using both χ2-based Q statistics and the I2 test. A p value<0.10 for the Q statistics or an I2 value≥50% indicated significant heterogeneity. Publication bias was measured by funnel plots and Egger’s test. Two methods were used for sensitivity analysis. First, we used fixed-effects models instead of random-effects models to reanalyse the data to assess the pooled RRs. Second, the ‘leave-one-out’ procedure was carried out to assess the robustness of the pooled-effect estimates by excluding each study one at a time, and estimating the pooled RRs with the remaining studies. If the removal of a study results in a significant change in the results, this means that the study is significantly heterogeneous from other studies. A significant change in the results was defined as an alteration in the effect estimate that was statistically significant at the p<0.05 level, indicating a bad robustness of the findings. Continuity correction was used to correct for zero events. The specific approach is to add 0.5 to each case, both to achieve the correction effect, but also to ensure a small correction bias. The subgroup analyses were performed according to the study designs (RCT, retrospective study), lesion locations (gastric, duodenum and colorectal) and surgical procedures (ESD and EMR). Statistical analyses were performed using software including Revman (V.5.4.1, The Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen) and Stata (V.18.0, Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA). All statistical tests were considered statistically significant with a two-sided p value<0.05.

Patient and public involvement

There was no patient or public involvement in this study.

Results

Study selection

A flow diagram for the study selection process is shown in figure 1. A total of 3241 references were identified using the search strategy described previously, of which 1207 were duplicates. In addition, 10 references were identified by manual searching of the reference lists of related studies. Of the remaining 2044 references, 1973 studies were removed from consideration after reviewing the titles and abstracts based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Of the remaining candidate studies, 8 review or meta-analysis, 14 abstract-only publication, 6 conference articles, 10 single-arm study and 2 case series. Finally, 28 studies that met the inclusion criteria were included in the final meta-analysis.

Delayed bleeding

Twenty-eight studies evaluated the rate of delayed bleeding after endoscopic resection. The pooled rate of delayed bleeding was 2.4% in the closure group and 6.3% in the non-closure group. Generally, prophylactic closure was significantly associated with reduced risk of delayed bleeding (RR: 0.40, 95% CI: 0.30 to 0.53, p<0.001) without significant heterogeneity (p=0.11, I2=25%) (figure 2). No evidence of a significant publication bias was found using funnel plots (online supplemental figure 1a) and Egger’s test (p=0.26).

Forest plot evaluating the rate of delayed bleeding. RR, Risk Ratio; CI, Confidence interval.

Further subgroup analyses based on surgical site and procedure also showed a statistically significant protective effect of prophylactic closure of mucosal defects against delayed bleeding (online supplemental figures 2–4). With the available data, we performed additional subgroup analyses in the colorectal ESD and EMR group by study designs (online supplemental figures 5 and 6). It is worth noting that the observed association was no longer statistically significant in the pooled analysis of the combined three RCTs of colorectal ESD (RR: 0.40, 95% CI: 0.08 to 2.14, p=0.28).

Delayed perforation

Twelve studies had evaluated the rate of delayed perforation after endoscopic resection. The overall pooled rate of delayed perforation in the closure group and the non-closure group were 0.60% and 1.6%, respectively. Prophylactic closure showed significant protective effect on preventing delayed perforation compared with non-closure group (RR: 0.42, 95% CI: 0.22 to 0.82, p=0.01) (figure 3). There was no significant heterogeneity among the included studies (p=0.75, I2=0%) and no evidence of a significant publication bias was found using funnel plots (online supplemental figure 1 b) and Egger’s test (p=0.66).

Forest plot evaluating the rate of delayed perforation. RR, Risk Ratio; CI, Confidence interval.

It is worth noting that the subgroup analyses showed results that contradicted the combined analysis. No protective effect of prophylactic closure of mucosal defects on delayed perforation was found after colorectal ESD (RR: 0.43, 95% CI: 0.15 to 1.25, p=0.12; I2=0%), and colorectal EMR (RR: 0.62, 95% CI: 0.20 to 1.92, p=0.41; I2=0%), and the observed association was only significant in the pooled analysis of the combined two RCTs studies of colorectal ESD (RR: 0.17, 95% CI: 0.03 to 0.97, I2=0%) (online supplemental figures 7–9). Unfortunately, no relevant studies on the stomach were retrieved. The protective effect of this prophylactic closure of mucosal defects was significant in the combined results of two studies in the duodenal site (RR: 0.18, 95% CI: 0.04 to 0.74, p=0.02) (online supplemental figure 10), but the surgical procedures were not entirely homogeneous.

Postpolypectomy coagulation syndrome

Eight studies have evaluated the rate of PPCS after endoscopic resection. The pooled rate of PPCS was 2.3% in the closure group and 4.4% in the non-closure group. There was no statistically significant association between PPCS and prophylactic closure (RR: 0.71, 95% CI: 0.35 to 1.44, p=0.34) (figure 4). There were no significant changes in subgroup analysis (online supplemental figures 11–13). There was no significant heterogeneity among the included studies (p=0.08, I2=45%) and no evidence of a significant publication bias was found using funnel plots (online supplemental figure 1c) and Egger’s test (p=0.99).

Forest plot evaluating the rate of postpolypectomy coagulation syndrome. RR, Risk Ratio; CI, Confidence interval.

Quality assessment

The risk bias of the included studies was shown in online supplemental tables 4 and 5. Fourteen RCTs were assessed according to the RoB-2 tool, of which seven were assessed as ‘low risk of bias’, six as ‘some concerns’ and one as ‘high risk of bias’. Fourteen observational studies were assessed according to the ROBINS-I tool, of which three were assessed as ‘high risk of bias’. The remaining studies were rated as having moderate risk of bias. The GRADE approach was adopted to assess the quality of evidence on outcome level for the analyses (online supplemental table 6). Due to inconsistent definitions of PPCS, the GRADE score of the outcome PPCS in RCTs was graded as moderate.

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the robustness of the association results, we further performed sensitivity analysis using two methods. There was no significant difference between the results of the fixed-effects and random-effects models (online supplemental figures 14–16). Also, the ‘leave-one-out’ procedure was shown in online supplemental figures 17–19. Particularly, when Motohiko Kato’s study was excluded, the effect of the combined outcome changed from RR: 0.42, 95% CI: 0.22 to 0.82, p=0.01 to RR: 0.52, 95% CI: 0.25 to 1.07, p=0.077, with no change in heterogeneity (I2=0%, p=0.94). We realise that with or without the study of Motohiko Kato studies show a similar effect, and the 95% CI of pooled RR is marginal. Therefore, we believe that this result remains robust. There were no significant changes in the pooled RRs for delayed bleeding and PPCS, indicating that the results were stable and reliable.

Discussion

Endoscopic resection has replaced surgery as the primary approach for the resection of early GI lesions. However, the incidence of delayed adverse events has remained a major concern. Prophylactic closure has had mixed results in preventing delayed adverse events, and there are currently no uniform guidelines supporting its routine application for this purpose. In general, the current meta-analysis showed that prophylactic closure was beneficial in reducing the incidence of delayed bleeding after endoscopic resection of superficial layers of the GI wall, but there was no significant difference in reducing the incidence of delayed perforation and PPCS. To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest meta-analysis to evaluate the effect of prophylactic closure on reducing the incidence of delayed adverse events after endoscopic resection of GI lesions.

Delayed bleeding is the most frequent complication, with reported rates between 2% and 24%.917 After endoscopic resection, especially ESD, abundant blood vessels in the submucosal layer and exposure of mucosal defects to gastric acid, pancreatic juice and intestinal fluid could result in delayed bleeding.50 Although it rarely requires surgery, this might lead to care monitoring, transfusions and emergency endoscopic haemostasis, with significantly prolong hospitalisation.51 Proton pump inhibitors and preventive haemostasis on the wound surface may reduce delayed bleeding, but also lead to new complications. For example, excessive coagulation with haemostatic forceps might induce incurable damage to the thin intestinal wall and significantly increase the risk of delayed perforation or PPCS.43 Therefore, preventive endoscopic closure of mucosal defects may be a worthwhile option to prevent mucosal defects from exposure to chemical and mechanical stimulation, to accelerate wound healing of mucosal defects and thereby to reduce the risk of delayed bleeding.37 It is also important to mention that the risk of delayed bleeding due to different surgical procedures and sites in the GI tract may vary. For example, compared with EMR, bleeding occurs more frequently when cutting submucosal vessels during ESD.35 The thin walls, redundancy and flexure of the colon make the technical difficulty and the risk of intraoperative damage to the intestinal wall is higher.36 The duodenal wall is extremely thin and the wound is exposed to bile and pancreatic juice, especially if the lesion is located distal to the duodenum.51 Considering all of this, we did a comprehensive combined analysis and subgroup analysis of each GI site and its surgical approach. In addition to this, in colorectal ESD and colorectal EMR, we further performed subgroup analyses based on different study designs (online supplemental figures 5 and 6). It was found that the observed association was no longer statistically significant in the pooled analysis of the combined three RCTs of colorectal ESD (RR: 0.40, 95% CI: 0.08 to 2.14; I2=0%). A possible reason for this may be the small number of researches leading to increased uncertainty and reduced precision, as evidenced by the wider CI. Other than that the results of all other subgroup analyses were the same as the combined analyses. Therefore, we believe that prophylactic closure of mucosal defects is beneficial in reducing the incidence of delayed bleeding, which was consistent with previous studies.434552 It is worth noting that most of the studies were in the colorectal site, with only three studies each in the stomach and duodenal sites. We get only one study on the protective effect of prophylactic closure of mucosal defects after gastric ESD on delayed bleeding, and we found no relevant literature of duodenal EMR. Therefore, the effect of prophylactic closure of mucosal defects in the gastric and duodenum on the prevention of delayed bleeding needs to be supported by more studies.

Delayed perforation is also a common adverse event after endoscopic resection and it requires emergency surgical treatment. The overall effect size of the combined analysis of all sites and surgical methods showed that endoscopic closure was shown to reduce the incidence of delayed perforation (RR: 0.42, 95% CI: 0.22 to 0.82, p=0.01; I2=0%). In the subgroup analysis, although the effect size was similar, the CI crossed the value of 1, suggesting a marginally significant effect. For example, the RR in the closure group is 0.43 (95% CI: 0.15 to 1.25, p=0.12; I2=0%) after colorectal ESD, and 0.62 (95% CI: 0.20 to 1.92, p=0.41; I2=0%) after colorectal EMR. Although not statistically significant, the effect size is of practical importance. Theoretically, prophylactic closure of mucosal defects to reduce delayed perforation after endoscopic resection seems feasible and safe. The results of the combined analysis also supported this theory. The mechanism of delayed perforation is still not clear, but another important risk factor is generally believed to be electrical or thermal damage caused by electrocoagulation. To prevent delayed bleeding, visible thick vessels or even any remaining vessels must be prophylactically clotted with endoscope haemostatic forceps. Preventive coagulation may lead to excessive coagulation of the muscularis propria, which may increase the risk of delayed perforation.51 It is worth noting that most of the included studies had wide CIs for the effect sizes. Due to the wide CI, there is considerable uncertainty regarding the size of the effect. Given the marginal crossing of the CI, further studies are warranted to confirm these findings.

Furthermore, this meta-analysis revealed no protective effect of prophylactic closure for PPCS, which is in concordance with prior studies.43 PPCS is a rare adverse event after endoscopic resection. The mechanism of PPCS is still unclear. Some studies have suggested that PPCS may be caused by transmural thermal injury caused by electrocoagulation and bacterial invasion from the mucosal defects.33 53 Liu et al43 speculate that the reason why endoscope closure has no effect on reducing the incidence of PPCS is that endoscope closure may help reduce the occurrence of local bacterial infections, but not alleviate transmural thermal damage.

Specifically, PPCS is manifested with localised abdominal pain, fever, leucocytosis and peritoneal inflammation without perforation.53 However, the definition of PPCS was not consistent among the studies.6 8 15 38 41 54 The ambiguous definition of PPCS may lead to inaccurate clinical diagnosis, which is an important reason for statistical bias in various reports. Additionally, the presence and seriousness of abdominal pain may not be always recorded with precision in some of these studies due to their retrospective nature.7 15 38 54 The recorded frequency of PPCS may differ from the real incidence of PPCS. Interestingly, prophylactic closure of mucosal defects had not shown protective effect on PPCS in either combined or subgroup analysis. Although more studies about the gastric and duodenal are needed to further support this conclusion, we believe that this conclusion is robust at least in colorectal site.

There are some limitations in this study. First, the effect of prophylactic closure mucosal defects is not only related to study design, lesion site and surgical procedure, but also to lesion size. However, due to limited data acquisition, this study cannot conduct further subgroup analysis based on lesion size. Second, most of the included studies were conducted in the East Asian countries, which may limit the generalisability of other races. Third, some of the closure devices and methods used in the included studies were different. These techniques may have different effects on endoscope closure. Fourth, there was some variation in the definition of adverse events of the included studies, especially in the follow-up time. Future studies are needed to be more rigorous about the duration of follow-up. Interpretation of our results should take these limitations and future advancements into consideration.

In conclusion, this meta-analysis suggests that prophylactic endoscopic closure may decrease the occurrence of delayed bleeding after endoscopic resection, but there is no significant difference in decreasing the incidence of delayed perforation and PPCS. Further cost analysis should be conducted to implement the most cost-effective adverse events prevention strategy in the future.
