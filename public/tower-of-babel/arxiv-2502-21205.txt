Abstract
. Given an axially-symmetric, ( n + 1 ) (n+1) -dimensional convex cone Ω ⊂ ℝ n + 1 \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1} , we study the stability of the free-boundary minimal surface Σ \Sigma obtained by intersecting Ω \Omega with a n n -plane that contains the axis of Ω \Omega . In the case n = 2 n=2 , Σ \Sigma is always unstable, as a special case of the vertex-skipping property recently proved in [ 16 ] . Conversely, as soon as n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 and Ω \Omega has a sufficiently large aperture (depending on the dimension n n ), we show that Σ \Sigma is strictly stable. For our stability analysis, we introduce a Lipschitz flow Σ t ​ [ f ] \Sigma_{t}[f] of deformations of Σ \Sigma associated with a compactly-supported, scalar deformation field f f , which satisfies the key property ∂ Σ t ​ [ f ] ⊂ ∂ Ω \partial\Sigma_{t}[f]\subset\partial\Omega for all t ∈ ℝ t\in\mathbb{R} . Then, we compute the lower-right second variation of the area of Σ \Sigma along the flow, and ultimately show that it is positive by exploiting its connection with a functional inequality studied in the context of reaction-diffusion problems.

Introduction

One of the most intriguing aspects of the theory of free-boundary minimal surfaces, and more generally of capillary surfaces, is their behaviour near the boundary of their container. A well-established regularity theory is known to hold when the free boundary of a minimal surface
\Sigma
meets a regular point of the boundary of its container
\Omega
(see
[
4
,
8
]
); in this case the
90
-degree contact-angle law (or, more generally, Young’s law for capillary surfaces
[
19
]
) holds true in a classical sense. Conversely, the behaviour of
\Sigma
near a generic point of
\partial\Sigma\cap\partial^{s}\Omega
, where
\partial^{s}\Omega
denotes the set of singular points of
\partial\Omega
, is much less known. Few exceptions are represented by the studies on free-boundary minimal surfaces in
3
-dimensional wedge domains, mainly due to Hildebrandt-Sauvigny
[
13
,
12
,
11
,
10
]
, and by the analogous studies pursued in the context of capillary surfaces, see e.g.
[
7
,
14
]
and the references therein).

A few years ago, Edelen-Li
[
5
]
extended the existing regularity theory by proving a very general
\varepsilon
-regularity theorem for free-boundary integral varifolds with bounded mean curvature in locally-almost-polyhedral domains. Subsequently, with the aim of pushing the regularity theory forward and beyond the locally almost-polyhedral class, in a couple of recent works we have shown some sort of complementary results in the context of sets of finite perimeter. The first result is a free-boundary monotonicity formula for perimeter almost-minimizers in a non-smooth domain which satisfies a so-called
visibility condition

[
15
]
. The second is a

for almost-minimal boundaries in convex sets
[
16
]
, which can be coupled with
[
5
]
and lead to a full, free-boundary regularity result for
2
-dimensional integral varifolds with bounded mean curvature in locally almost-polyhedral
3d
-domains.

Blow-up techniques are among the main tools used to retrieve information on the (boundary) properties of
\Sigma
at various levels (local structure, density properties, and ultimately regularity). Relying on boundary monotonicity formulas (see
[
15
]
) one can blow-up an almost-minimal (almost-minimizing) surface at one of its free-boundary points, obtaining free-boundary minimal (area-minimizing) cones inside conical domains (like, e.g., wedges). This is the first step of the procedure we have followed in
[
16
]
to show via a contradiction argument the vertex-skipping property, i.e., that the internal boundary
\Sigma
of a perimeter almost-minimizer in a convex set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
avoids all vertices of
\partial\Omega

It is then natural to ask whether the vertex-skipping property is a special feature of the
3
-dimensional setting, or if instead it holds true in any dimension. To answer this question we consider here a

\omega_{\lambda}(x^{\prime},x_{n+1})=\lambda\sqrt{|x^{\prime}|^{2}+x_{n+1}^{2}}

Then, we define

In Section
3

[
16
]
. The different (and, at first sight, quite unexpected) stability properties of
\Sigma
have a correspondence in some literature on minimal surfaces within cones. In particular, a result of Morgan
[
17

implies that the free-boundary
\partial\Sigma
is an area-minimizing
(n-1)
-surface in
\partial\Omega_{\lambda}

\lambda

[
1

, where the dimension

Going back to
[
17

1.1.
Organization of the paper.

In Section
2
we introduce some essential notation and construct a one-parameter family
\Sigma_{t}
of free-boundary perturbations of the plane
\Sigma
generated by a scalar field
f
defined on
\Sigma
. Instead of assuming that the support of
f
does not contain the origin (i.e., the tip of
\Sigma
) and then couple a standard one-parameter normal flow construction with suitable capacitary considerations, we consider for possible later uses an alternative construction of a local flow of bi-Lipschitz omomorphisms in a generic domain
\Omega
with Lipschitz boundary, which sends
\partial\Omega
into itself. In particular, if applied to the circular cone
\Omega_{\lambda}
, this flow is allowed to move the tip of
\Sigma
. Consequently, first and second lower-right variations of the area of
\Sigma
are introduced for being used in the next section.

Section
3
contains the main computations and results of the paper. In Lemma
3.1
we expand the Jacobian appearing in the definition of the area of
\Sigma_{t}
, in terms of an explicit expression depending on
f
and its (tangential) gradient
\nabla f
, up to a remainder term that we need to control with suitably uniform estimates. Then, after observing the nullity of the first right-lower variation of the area, we compute the second lower-right variation and observe that its positivity depends on a functional relation called
Kato inequality
, that is known in the context of reaction-diffusion problems and allows us to complete the proof of our main result, Theorem
3.4
.

Finally, in the Appendix we prove the technical lemma used in the computation of the Jacobian expansion of Lemma
3.1
.

2.
Notations and basic constructions

Let
n\geq 2
be a given integer. Given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we define
\tilde{x}=(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
such that
x=(x^{\prime},x_{n})
, with a slight abuse of notation. We identify
x
with
(x,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
x^{\prime}
with
(x^{\prime},0,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, whenever this does not create confusion. We denote by
\tilde{B}_{r}(\tilde{x})
the open ball of radius
r>0
centered at
\tilde{x}
, and we set
\tilde{B}_{r}:=\tilde{B}_{r}(0)
. For any
1\leq i\leq n+1
, we denote by
p_{i}
the hyperplane of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
of equation
x_{i}=0
, and use the same notation for the orthogonal projection of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
onto this hyperplane.
Given a differentiable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{R}
, we denote the partial derivative of
f
with respect to
x_{i}
as
\partial_{i}f
.

We now construct one-parameter families of compact deformations of a fixed,
n
-dimensional hyperplane
\Sigma
restricted to a Lipschitz epigraph
\Omega
. The deformations are defined on
\overline{\Omega}
and are “tangential”, in the sense that they send
\partial\Omega
into itself.

Specifically, given a Lipschitz function
\omega=\omega(x^{\prime},t):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, we let

(2.1)

\Omega=\{\tilde{x}=(x^{\prime},x_{n},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\,:\,x_{n}>\omega(x^{\prime},t)\}\,.

We also let
\Sigma:=\Omega\cap p_{n+1}
and set
\partial\Sigma=\overline{\Sigma}\cap\partial\Omega
.
Given
x=(x^{\prime},x_{n})\in\overline{\Sigma}
, we consider the parametric curve
\Gamma_{x}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
defined as

(2.2)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{x}(t)

\displaystyle:=(x^{\prime},x_{n}+\omega(x^{\prime},t)-\omega(x^{\prime},0),t)

and, with a slight abuse of notation, we identify
\Gamma_{x}
with
\Gamma_{x}(\mathbb{R})
.

The next lemma collects some key properties of the family
\{\Gamma_{x}:\ x\in\overline{\Sigma}\}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

The family of parametric curves
\{\Gamma_{x}:\ x\in\overline{\Sigma}\}
defines a foliation of
\overline{\Omega}
. More precisely, the following properties hold:

(i)

\Gamma_{x}(0)=x
and
\Gamma_{x}\subset\overline{\Omega}
, for all
x\in\overline{\Sigma}
;

(ii)

if
x\neq y\in\overline{\Sigma}
then
\Gamma_{x}\cap\Gamma_{y}=\emptyset
;

(iii)

\Gamma_{x}(t)\in\partial\Omega
for some
t\in\mathbb{R}
if and only if
\Gamma_{x}\subset\partial\Omega
.

(iv)

the map
(x,t)\mapsto\Gamma_{x}(t)
is Lipschitz.

Proof.

Property (i) directly follows from the definition of
\Gamma_{x}
and by observing that
x\in\overline{\Sigma}
implies
x_{n}\geq\omega(x^{\prime},0)
, hence

x_{n}+\omega(x^{\prime},t)-\omega(x^{\prime},0)\geq\omega(x^{\prime},t)\,.

Then, concerning property (ii), if
\Gamma_{x}(t)=\Gamma_{y}(u)
for some
x,y\in\overline{\Sigma}
and some
t,u\in\mathbb{R}
, by definition we must have
t=u
,
x^{\prime}=y^{\prime}
, and

x_{n}+(\omega(x^{\prime},t)-\omega(x^{\prime},0))=y_{n}+(\omega(y^{\prime},u)-\omega(y^{\prime},0))\,,

which implies
x_{n}=y_{n}
and hence
x=y
, which proves (ii). The less obvious implication of (iii) is the only if part: if
\Gamma_{x}(t)\in\partial\Omega
for some
t\in\mathbb{R}
, then

x_{n}+\omega(x^{\prime},t))-\omega(x^{\prime},0))=\omega(x^{\prime},t)\,,

i.e.,
x_{n}=\omega(x^{\prime},0)
. Consequently,

\Gamma_{x}(u)=(x^{\prime},\omega(x^{\prime},0)+\omega(x^{\prime},u)-\omega(x^{\prime},0),u)=(x^{\prime},\omega(x^{\prime},u),u)\in\partial\Omega

for all
u\in\mathbb{R}
. Finally, the proof of (iv) easily follows from the estimate

\displaystyle|\Gamma_{x}(t)-\Gamma_{y}(u)|

\displaystyle\leq|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|+|x_{n}-y_{n}|+|\omega(x^{\prime},t)-\omega(y^{\prime},u)|+|\omega(x^{\prime},0)+\omega(y^{\prime},0)|+|t-u|

\displaystyle\leq(1+2\mathrm{Lip}(\omega))(|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|+|x_{n}-y_{n}|+|t-u|).

∎

Let now
f:\overline{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{R}
be a Lipschitz function with compact support in
\overline{\Sigma}
. We define the
flow associated to
f
as

(2.3)

\Phi[f](x,t):=\Gamma_{x}(tf(x))

for all
(x,t)\in\overline{\Sigma}\times\mathbb{R}
. The map
\Phi[f]
satisfies
\Phi[f](x,t)=x
and, thanks to Lemma
2.1
, it is Lipschitz and one-to-one. When
\Phi[f](\cdot,t)
is differentiable at
x
(which is true for almost all
x\in\Sigma
), we denote by

D\Phi[f](x,t)=\left(\partial_{1}\Phi[f](x,t),\dots,\partial_{n}\Phi[f](x,t)\right)

the Jacobian matrix of
\Phi[f](\cdot,t)
at
x
, and by
D\Phi[f](x,t)^{T}
its transpose. We also define

S[f](x,t):=D\Phi[f](x,t)^{T}\cdot D\Phi[f](x,t)

and

J[f](x,t):=\sqrt{\det(S[f](x,t))}\,,

and note for future reference that

(2.4)

J[f](x,0)=1\quad\forall\,x\in\Sigma\,.

Then, we define

\Sigma_{t}[f]=\Phi[f](\Sigma,t)

and

A[f](t):={\mathcal{H}}^{n}(\Phi[f](\Sigma\cap\mathop{\mathrm{spt}}f,t))=\int_{\mathop{\mathrm{spt}}f}J[f](x,t)\,dx\,,

where the second identity follows from the Area Formula (see, e.g.,
[
2
, Theorem 2.91]
). The function
A[f](t)
represents the area of the compact portion of the hyperplane
\Sigma
deformed via the flow map
\Phi[f](\cdot,t)
. The minimality/stability of
\Sigma
is related to the asymptotic properties of
A[f](t)
when
t\to 0
. In general, we cannot expect that
A[f](t)
is (twice) differentiable at
t=0
, hence it will not be possible to compute the first and second variations of the area in a classical sense. However, we can test the local minimality of
\Sigma
by taking lower right variations, i.e.,

\underline{\partial_{t}}A[f](0^{+}):=\liminf_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{A[f](t)-A[f](0)}{t}

and, assuming
\underline{\partial_{t}}A[f](0^{+})=0
,

\underline{\partial_{t}^{2}}A[f](0^{+}):=2\liminf_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{A[f](t)-A[f](0)}{t^{2}}\,.

Therefore, a first-order necessary condition for local minimality is

(2.5)

\underline{\partial_{t}}A[f](0^{+})\geq 0\,,\qquad\forall\,f\in C^{0,1}_{c}(\overline{\Sigma})\,,

with strict inequality only if the local minimality is strict.
Then, a second-order necessary condition is

(2.6)

\underline{\partial_{t}}A[f](0^{+})=0\ \implies\ \underline{\partial_{t}^{2}}A[f](0^{+})\geq 0\,,

for all
f\in C^{0,1}_{c}(\overline{\Sigma})
.

3.
Stability of
\Sigma
in
\Omega_{\lambda}

We recall that
\Omega_{\lambda}
is the
(n+1)
-dimensional circular cone defined as the epigraph of the function

\omega_{\lambda}(x^{\prime},x_{n+1}):=\lambda\,\sqrt{|x^{\prime}|^{2}+x_{n+1}^{2}}\,,

with
\lambda>0
a fixed parameter defining the aperture of the cone. In this case, for all
f\in C^{0,1}_{c}(\overline{\Sigma})
, the one-parameter flow associated with
f
is given by

\displaystyle\Phi[f](x,t)

\displaystyle:=\left(x^{\prime}\,,\,\omega_{\lambda}(x^{\prime},tf(x))+x_{n}-\omega_{\lambda}(x^{\prime},0)\,,\,t\,f(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(x^{\prime}\,,\,\lambda\,\sqrt{|x^{\prime}|^{2}+t^{2}f(x)^{2}}+x_{n}-\lambda|x^{\prime}|\,,\,t\,f(x)\right)\,.

It is immediate to check that
\Phi[f](x,t)
is differentiable on the set

\Delta_{f}:=\{x\in\mathop{\mathrm{spt}}f\,:\,x\neq 0\,,\,f\text{ is differentiable at $x$}\}\cup\left(\Sigma\setminus\mathop{\mathrm{spt}}f\right)\,.

In particular, since
f
is Lipschitz, we infer that

{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(\Sigma\setminus\Delta_{f})=0\,.

For almost all
x\in\Delta_{f}
and for
1\leq i\leq n-1
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{i}\Phi[f](x,t)

\displaystyle=e_{i}+\left[t\,\partial_{i}f(x)\,\partial_{n+1}\omega_{\lambda}(x^{\prime},tf(x))+\partial_{i}\omega_{\lambda}(x^{\prime},tf(x))-\partial_{i}\omega_{\lambda}(x^{\prime},0)\right]e_{n}+t\,\partial_{i}f(x)e_{n+1}

(3.1)

\displaystyle=e_{i}+\lambda\left(\dfrac{t^{2}f(x)\,\partial_{i}f(x)}{\sqrt{|x^{\prime}|^{2}+t^{2}f(x)^{2}}}+\dfrac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{|x^{\prime}|^{2}+t^{2}f(x)^{2}}}-\dfrac{x_{i}}{|x|}\right)e_{n}+t\,\partial_{i}f(x)e_{n+1}

while, if
i=n
,

\displaystyle\partial_{n}\Phi[f](x,t)

\displaystyle=\left(t\,\partial_{n}f(x)\,\partial_{n+1}\omega_{\lambda}(x^{\prime},tf(x))+1\right)e_{n}+t\,\partial_{n}f(x)e_{n+1}

(3.2)

\displaystyle=\left(\lambda\dfrac{t^{2}f(x)\,\partial_{n}f(x)}{\sqrt{|x^{\prime}|^{2}+t^{2}f(x)^{2}}}+1\right)e_{n}+t\,\partial_{n}f(x)e_{n+1}\,.

We introduce the following notations:

(3.3)

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}(x,t):=\begin{cases}\lambda\left(\dfrac{t^{2}f(x)\,\partial_{i}f(x)}{\sqrt{|x^{\prime}|^{2}+t^{2}f(x)^{2}}}+\dfrac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{|x^{\prime}|^{2}+t^{2}f(x)^{2}}}-\dfrac{x_{i}}{|x|}\right)&\text{if $i=1,...,n-1$}\\[10.0pt]
\lambda\,\dfrac{t^{2}f(x)\partial_{n}f(x)}{\sqrt{|x^{\prime}|^{2}+t^{2}f(x)^{2}}}&\text{if $i=n\,,$}\end{cases}

(3.4)

\displaystyle\beta_{i}(x,t):=t\,\partial_{i}f(x)\,,\qquad\forall i=1,...,n\,.

We thus have

(3.5)

\partial_{i}\Phi[f](x,t)=e_{i}+\alpha_{i}(x,t)\,e_{n}+\beta_{i}(x,t)\,e_{n+1}\,,\qquad\forall i=1,...,n\,.

Lemma 3.1
.

Given
f\in{\mathcal{C}}^{0,1}_{c}(\overline{\Sigma})
, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

(3.6)

J[f](x,t)^{2}=1+t^{2}\left(|\nabla f(x)|^{2}+\dfrac{2\,\lambda\,f(x)\,\partial_{n}f(x)}{\sqrt{|x^{\prime}|^{2}+t^{2}f(x)^{2}}}\right)+R[f](x,t)\,,

where
R[f](x,t)
is such that

(3.7)

\mathop{\mathrm{spt}}R[f](t,\cdot)\subset\mathop{\mathrm{spt}}f\,,\qquad\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\dfrac{R[f](x,t)}{t^{2}}=0\,,\,\,\text{a.e. in $\Sigma\,,$}\qquad\left|\dfrac{R[f](x,t)}{t^{2}}\right|\leq C\,,

where
C\geq 0
is a constant depending on
\mathrm{Lip}f
only.

Proof.

The partial derivatives of
\Phi[f]
satisfy the assumptions of Lemma
A.1
, hence

J[f]^{2}=\left(1+\alpha_{n}\right)^{2}\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\beta_{i}^{2}\right)+\beta_{n}^{2}\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\alpha_{i}^{2}\right)-2\,(1+\alpha_{n})\beta_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}\,.

where
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}
are defined in (
3.3
) and (
3.4
). We now observe that, for every
1\leq i\leq n-1
,

(3.8)

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\alpha_{i}(t)=0\qquad\text{for $x\neq 0$.}

In addition,

(3.9)

\lim_{t\to 0}\dfrac{\alpha_{n}[f](t)}{t}=0\quad\text{for $x\neq 0$,}\qquad\lim_{t\to 0}\beta_{i}[f](t)=0\quad\text{for all $x$,}

and

(3.10)

\dfrac{\|\alpha_{n}[f](t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Sigma)}}{t}\leq\lambda\|\partial_{n}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Sigma)}\,,\qquad\dfrac{\|\beta_{i}[f](t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Sigma)}}{t}\leq\|\partial_{i}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Sigma)}\,.

Let us define

R[f]:=\alpha_{n}^{2}\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\beta_{i}^{2}\right)+2\alpha_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\beta_{i}^{2}+\beta_{n}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\alpha_{i}^{2}-2\,(1+\alpha_{n})\beta_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}\,.

By the definition of
\alpha_{i}
,
\beta_{i}
provided in (
3.3
), it is evident that
\alpha_{i}
and

\mathop{\mathrm{spt}}R[f](t,\cdot)\subset\mathop{\mathrm{spt}}f
. Owing to (

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\dfrac{R[f](x,t)}{t^{2}}=0\,,\,\,\text{a.e. in $\Sigma\,,$}

while (
3.10

\left|\dfrac{R[f](x,t)}{t^{2}}\right|\leq C\,,\qquad\text{$C$ constant depending on $\mathrm{Lip}f$.}

This proves the validity of (
3.7

\displaystyle J[f]^{2}-R[f]\ =\ 1+2\alpha_{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}^{2}\ =\ 1+t^{2}\left(|\nabla f(x)|^{2}+\dfrac{2\,\lambda\,f(x)\,\partial_{n}f(x)}{\sqrt{|x^{\prime}|^{2}+t^{2}f(x)^{2}}}\right)\,,

that is precisely what we claimed.

We observe that (

) explicitly depends on

Theorem 3.2
.

The following identity holds:

(3.11)

\underline{\partial_{s}}\mathcal{A}[f](0^{+})\,=\,\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla f(x)|^{2}\,dx-\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\dfrac{f(x^{\prime},\lambda|x^{\prime}|)^{2}}{|x^{\prime}|}\,dx^{\prime}\right)\,,\qquad\forall f\in C^{0,1}_{c}(\overline{\Sigma})\,.

Proof.

Owing to (
2.4
), we have
\mathcal{J}[f](0)\equiv 1
, hence

\displaystyle\underline{\partial_{s}}\mathcal{A}[f](0^{+})

\displaystyle=\liminf_{s\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathop{\mathrm{spt}}f}s^{-1}\big(\mathcal{J}[f](x,s)-\mathcal{J}[f](x,0)\big)dx

(3.12)

\displaystyle=\liminf_{s\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathop{\mathrm{spt}}f}s^{-1}\big(\mathcal{J}[f](x,s)-1\big)\,dx\,.

Now, (
3.6

\mathcal{J}[f](x,s)^{2}=1+s\,G\,,\qquad\text{where}\quad G:=\left(|\nabla f(x)|^{2}+\dfrac{2\,\lambda\,f(x)\,\partial_{n}f(x)}{\sqrt{|x^{\prime}|^{2}+sf(x)^{2}}}+\dfrac{R[f](x,\sqrt{s})}{s}\right)\,.

Thus by (

\int_{\mathop{\mathrm{spt}}f}s^{-1}(\mathcal{J}[f](x,s)-1)\,dx=\mathcal{I}^{1}_{s}+\mathcal{I}^{2}_{s}\,,

where

\mathcal{I}^{1}_{s}:=\dfrac{1}{s}\int_{\mathop{\mathrm{spt}}f}\sqrt{1+sG}-\left(1+s\,\dfrac{G}{2}\right)dx\,,\qquad\mathcal{I}^{2}_{s}:=\int_{\mathop{\mathrm{spt}}f}\dfrac{G}{2}\,dx\,.

An elementary computation yields

\sqrt{1+sG}-\left(1+s\,\dfrac{G}{2}\right)=\dfrac{-s^{2}G^{2}/4}{\sqrt{1+sG}+\left(1+s\dfrac{G}{2}\right)}\,,

consequently

|\mathcal{I}^{1}_{s}|\leq\dfrac{1}{4}\int_{\mathop{\mathrm{spt}}f}\dfrac{sG^{2}}{\left|\sqrt{1+sG}+\left(1+s\dfrac{G}{2}\right)\right|}dx\,.

Now it is immediate to observe that
G=0
whenever
x\notin\mathop{\mathrm{spt}}f
, and, as
s\to 0^{+}
,

\sqrt{s}\,G\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{a.e. in $\Sigma$,}\qquad|\sqrt{s}\,G|\leq C\quad\text{for some $C>0$ depending on $\mathrm{Lip}f$ only.}

By Dominated Convergence, we get

\lim_{s\rightarrow 0^{+}}\mathcal{I}^{1}_{s}=0\,.

Let us now study the limit as
s\rightarrow 0^{+}
of

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{2}_{s}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\,|\nabla f(x)|^{2}+\dfrac{\lambda\,f(x)\,\partial_{n}f(x)}{\sqrt{|x^{\prime}|^{2}+sf(x)^{2}}}+\dfrac{1}{2}\,\dfrac{R[f](x,\sqrt{s})}{s}\right)\,dx

By (
3.7
) and Dominated Convergence, we have

\int_{\Sigma}\dfrac{R[f](x,\sqrt{s})}{s}\,dx\longrightarrow 0\,,\qquad\text{as $s\rightarrow 0^{+}\,.$}

On the other hand, denoting by
g(x^{\prime}):=f(x^{\prime},\lambda|x^{\prime}|)^{2}
, an integration by parts yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\dfrac{\lambda\,f(x)\,\partial_{n}f(x)}{\sqrt{|x^{\prime}|^{2}+sf(x)^{2}}}\,dx

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\lambda}{s}\int_{\Sigma}\dfrac{s\,f(s)\,\partial_{n}f(x)}{\sqrt{|x^{\prime}|^{2}+sf(x)^{2}}}\,dx

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\lambda}{s}\int_{\Sigma}\partial_{n}\sqrt{|x^{\prime}|^{2}+sf(x)^{2}}\,dx

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\lambda}{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\left(\int_{\lambda|x^{\prime}|}^{+\infty}\partial_{n}\sqrt{|x^{\prime}|^{2}+sf(x)^{2}}dx_{n}\right)dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\lambda}{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}|x^{\prime}|-\sqrt{|x^{\prime}|^{2}+sg(x^{\prime})}\,dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\dfrac{g(x^{\prime})}{|x^{\prime}|+\sqrt{|x^{\prime}|^{2}+sg(x^{\prime})}}\,dx^{\prime}

Now, the integrand

\dfrac{g(x^{\prime})}{|x^{\prime}|+\sqrt{|x^{\prime}|^{2}+sg(x^{\prime})}}

is positive and monotonically non-increasing in
s
, for any
x^{\prime}\neq 0
. Then, by Beppo Levi’s Theorem, we obtain

\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\dfrac{g(x^{\prime})}{|x^{\prime}|+\sqrt{|x^{\prime}|^{2}+sg(x^{\prime})}}\,dx^{\prime}=\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\dfrac{g(x^{\prime})}{|x^{\prime}|}\,dx^{\prime}\,.

This implies that

\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\mathcal{I}^{2}_{s}=\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla f(x)|^{2}\,dx-\dfrac{\lambda}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\dfrac{g(x^{\prime})}{|x^{\prime}|}\,dx^{\prime}\,,

and the proof is concluded.
∎

Theorem
3.2
allows us to test in which cases
\Sigma
is stable. Indeed, by (
3.11
), the first variation of the area vanishes, hence the stability condition can be stated in terms of the lower-right second variation:

\underline{\partial_{t}^{2}}A[f](0^{+})=2\underline{\partial_{s}}\mathcal{A}[f](0^{+})\geq 0\,,

which is equivalent to the functional inequality

(3.13)

\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla f(x)|^{2}\,dx\geq\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\dfrac{f(x^{\prime},\lambda|x^{\prime}|)^{2}}{|x^{\prime}|}\,dx^{\prime}\,.

3.1.
The case
\mathop{\rm dim}\Sigma=2

In this case, we see that
\Sigma
is always unstable (we proved a more general version of this result in
[
16
]
).

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
n=2
. Then, for all
f\in C^{0,1}_{c}(\overline{\Sigma})
with the property that
f(0)\neq 0
, we have

\underline{\partial_{s}}\mathcal{A}[f](0^{+})<0\,.

In particular, this shows that
\Sigma
is unstable.

Proof.

When
n=2
the inequality (
3.13
) becomes

(3.14)

\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla f(x)|^{2}\,dx\geq\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}}\dfrac{f(x_{1},\lambda|x_{1}|)^{2}}{|x_{1}|}\,dx_{1}\,.

We now observe that, for all
f\in C^{0,1}_{c}(\overline{\Sigma})
, the left-hand side of (
3.14
) is always finite while the right-hand side is finite only if
f(0)=0
. Thus (
3.14
) fails for all
f\in C^{0,1}_{c}(\overline{\Sigma})
such that
f(0)\neq 0
.
∎

3.2.
The case
\mathop{\rm dim}\Sigma\geq 3

In this case, the aperture of the cone
\Omega_{\lambda}
plays a role. Indeed, we can prove that there exists a threshold aperture parameter
\lambda^{*}>0
, such that for larger apertures (i.e., for
0<\lambda\leq\lambda^{*}
), the hyperplane
\Sigma
becomes strictly stable.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
n\geq 3
. Then there exists
\lambda^{\ast}=\lambda^{\ast}(n)>0
such that, if
0<\lambda\leq\lambda^{\ast}
, we have

(3.15)

\underline{\partial_{s}}\mathcal{A}[f](0^{+})\geq 0\qquad\text{for all $f\in\,C^{0,1}_{c}(\overline{\Sigma})$}

and the inequality is strict whenever
f
is not identically zero, which means that
\Sigma
is strictly stable.

To prove Theorem
3.4
, we need the following result.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
n\geq 3
. Then

(3.16)

\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla f(x)|^{2}\,dx\geq\dfrac{K_{n}}{(1+\lambda)^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\dfrac{f(x^{\prime},\lambda|x^{\prime}|)^{2}}{|x^{\prime}|}\,dx^{\prime}\,,\qquad\text{for all $f\in H^{1}(\Sigma)$,}

where
K_{n}=2\Gamma^{2}(n/4)\Gamma^{-2}((n-2)/4)
and
\Gamma(u)
is Euler’s Gamma function.

Inequality (
3.16
) is known as
Kato’s Inequality
. A proof of a stronger version of (
3.16
), valid when
\Sigma
is a half-space (i.e. for
\lambda=0
), is provided in
[
3
, Theorem 1.4]
. Although our domain
\Sigma
is not a half-space, our version directly follows from inequality (9) in the same work. We also mention that a former proof of Kato’s Inequality was given by Herbst in
[
9
]
.

Proof of Lemma
3.5
.

Let us denote by
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
the half-space of points
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
x_{n}>0
. Inequality (9) of
[
3
]
states that

(3.17)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}|\nabla g(x)|^{2}\,dx\geq K_{n}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\dfrac{g(x^{\prime},0)^{2}}{|x^{\prime}|}\,dx^{\prime}\,,\qquad\text{for all $g\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})$.}

Given
f\in H^{1}(\Sigma)
, let us set

T(x):=(x^{\prime},x_{n}+\lambda|x^{\prime}|),\qquad g(x):=f(T(x))\,.

We have
g\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})
. Since
g(x^{\prime},0)=f(x^{\prime},\lambda|x^{\prime}|)
, we get

(3.18)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\dfrac{g(x^{\prime},0)^{2}}{|x^{\prime}|}\,dx^{\prime}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\dfrac{f(x^{\prime},\lambda|x^{\prime}|)^{2}}{|x^{\prime}|}\,dx^{\prime}\,.

We observe that

\nabla g(x)=\nabla f(T(x))+\lambda\partial_{n}f(T(x))\left(\dfrac{x^{\prime}}{|x^{\prime}|},0\right)\,,

and thus

(3.19)

|\nabla g(x)|^{2}\leq(1+\lambda)^{2}|\nabla f(T(x))|^{2}\,.

Combining (
3.17
), (
3.18
) and (
3.19
), and noting that
\det DT\equiv 1
, by the change of variable formula we get

\displaystyle(1+\lambda)^{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla f(x)|^{2}dx

\displaystyle=(1+\lambda)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}|\nabla f(T(x))|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}|\nabla g(x)|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\geq K_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\dfrac{f(x^{\prime},\lambda|x^{\prime}|)^{2}}{|x^{\prime}|}\,dx^{\prime}

and conclude the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

By Lemma
3.5
, the validity of (
3.13
), for all
f\in C^{0,1}_{c}(\overline{\Sigma})
, is guaranteed by the condition

(3.20)

0<\lambda\leq\dfrac{K_{n}}{(1+\lambda)^{2}}\,.

Since the function
\lambda\mapsto\lambda(1+\lambda)^{2}
is monotonically increasing from
0
to
+\infty
, there exists a unique
\lambda^{\ast}>0
such that

\lambda^{\ast}(1+\lambda^{\ast})^{2}=K_{n}

and, consequently, (
3.20
) is satisfied if and only if
0<\lambda\leq\lambda^{*}
, as wanted.
∎

Appendix A

Let
v_{1},...,v_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and denote by
S[v_{1},...,v_{k}]
the
k\times k
symmetric matrix which elements are the scalar products
\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle
, for
1\leq i,j\leq k
. By applying the definition of determinant and the Cauchy-Binet Formula (see, e.g.,
[
18
, Chapter 2]
) it is immediate to show that

(A.1)

\det(S[v_{1},...,v_{k}])=\left|v_{1}\wedge...\wedge v_{k}\right|^{2}\,,

where
\wedge
denotes the wedge-product of
v_{1},...,v_{k}
(we refer to
[
6
]
for a formal definition of
\wedge
and its main properties). We limit to mention that
\wedge
is alternating, i.e.
v_{1}\wedge...\wedge v_{k}
vanishes whenever
v_{i}=v_{j}
, for some
i\neq j
. Moreover, the wedge products
v_{1}\wedge...\wedge v_{k}
form a linear vector space, usually denoted by
\bigwedge^{k}\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. If
e_{1},...,e_{n+1}
denote the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, then the wedge products
e_{i_{1}}\wedge...\wedge e_{i_{k}}
, for all
1\leq i_{1}<...<i_{k}\leq n+1
, form a basis of
\bigwedge^{k}\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. We assume that
\bigwedge^{k}\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is endowed with the unique scalar product such that
e_{i_{1}}\wedge...\wedge e_{i_{k}}
, for
1\leq i_{1}<...<i_{k}\leq n+1
, are orthonormal. The norm associated with this scalar product is denoted by
|\cdot|
.

Let us introduce some convenient notation. We let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})
be a multi-index with
\alpha_{\ell}\in\{1,\dots,n+1\}
. Then, we set
e_{\alpha}=e_{\alpha_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{\alpha_{k}}
and, given
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
and
j\in\{1,\dots,n+1\}
, we define

e_{\alpha,i}^{j}=e_{\alpha_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{\alpha_{i-1}}\wedge e_{j}\wedge e_{\alpha_{i+1}}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{\alpha_{k}}

Lemma A.1
.

For
i=1,...,n
, let
v_{i}=e_{i}+A_{i}\,e_{n}+B_{i}\,e_{n+1}
, where
A_{i}
,
B_{i}
are real numbers. Then

(A.2)

\det(S[v_{1},...,v_{n}])=\left(1+A_{n}\right)^{2}\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}B_{i}^{2}\right)+B_{n}^{2}\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}A_{i}^{2}\right)-2\,(1+A_{n})B_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}A_{i}B_{i}\,.

Proof.

Let us set
\alpha=(1,2,\dots,n-1)
.
By definition of
v_{n}
, using the alternating property of the wedge product and the fact that
e^{n}_{\alpha,i}\wedge e_{n+1}=-e^{n+1}_{\alpha,i}\wedge e_{n}
, we get

(A.3)

\displaystyle v_{1}\wedge...\wedge v_{n}

\displaystyle=\left(1+A_{n}\right)e_{\alpha}\wedge e_{n}+B_{n}\,e_{\alpha}\wedge e_{n+1}\,+

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+B_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}A_{i}\,e^{n}_{\alpha,i}\wedge e_{n+1}+\left(1+A_{n}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}B_{i}\,e^{n+1}_{\alpha,i}\wedge e_{n}

\displaystyle=\left(1+A_{n}\right)e_{\alpha}\wedge e_{n}+B_{n}\,e_{\alpha}\wedge e_{n+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left[B_{n}A_{i}-\left(1+A_{n}\right)B_{i}\right]\,e^{n}_{\alpha,i}\wedge e_{n+1}\,.

By the orthonormality of the wedge products
e_{\alpha}\wedge e_{n},e_{\alpha}\wedge e_{n+1}
, and
e^{n}_{\alpha,i}\wedge e_{n+1}
for
i=1,\dots,n-1
, we obtain

|v_{1}\wedge...\wedge v_{n}|^{2}=\left(1+A_{n}\right)^{2}\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}B_{i}^{2}\right)+B_{n}^{2}\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}A_{i}^{2}\right)-2\,(1+A_{n})B_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}A_{i}B_{i}\,.

Then (
A.2
) immediately follows from (
A.1
).
∎
