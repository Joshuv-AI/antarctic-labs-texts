Evolutionary stasis in synapsid encephalization during the end-permian mass extinction
Authors: Julien Benoit, Lucas J. Legendre, Ricardo Araújo, Vincent Fernandez, Adam Midzuk, Claire Browning, Fernando Abdala, Jennifer Botha, Kenneth D. Angielczyk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Brain size and encephalization are correlated with extinction risks, making them central to understanding evolutionary outcomes in the face of the climatic and ecological challenges posed by mass extinction events. Yet, the end-Permian mass extinction—the most significant extinction event in the history of Life—has received little attention from a paleoneurological perspective. Here we use Synchrotron Radiation and CT scanning to study the evolution of brain endocasts of mammalian forerunners, the Permo-Triassic Synapsida, across this extinction event. During mass extinction events, it is expected that the brain would adapt either by growing larger to enhance behavioral response to the challenges, or shrinking to save energy. To test these predictions, we conducted a broad phylogenetic survey of synapsids across the end of the Permian and, unexpectedly, found stasis of the encephalization quotient. Furthermore, analysis of evolutionary rates indicates that stabilizing selection does not account for the observed stasis. We hypothesize that a lack of resources may have impeded neuroplasticity and prolonged a period of stasis that began in the late Permian. Brain size resumed varying after the crisis, marking the first steps toward the evolution of the mammalian brain.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53133-y.

Introduction

As the center of cognition and behavior, the brain is key to understanding how vertebrates adapted to major events in the history of life1,2. Prominent among these is the End-Permian Mass Extinction (EPME), during which an estimated 95% of marine and 70% to 80% of terrestrial animal species became extinct over an interval of one million years or less3–6. Some 252 million years ago, major volcanic activity responsible for the formation of the Siberian Traps emitted vast amounts of greenhouse gases into the atmosphere, leading to the climatic and environmental disruptions that eventually tipped the biosphere into a global ecological catastrophe3,5,7–12. As the amount of carbon dioxide built up dramatically in the atmosphere3,13, global temperatures are estimated to have increased by 10 to 14 °C, bringing land surface temperatures to a global average of 35–40 °C and causing unpredictable drought/flood regimes and ecosystem collapse3,7,10,11,14–19.

Brain size and encephalization are correlated with the extinction risk of a tetrapod species20–25. The Expensive Brain Hypothesis posits that the size of the brain decreases to save energy during biological crises, whereas the Cognitive Buffer Hypothesis suggests brain size evolves in the opposite direction to enhance behavioral response to the challenges23. Body mass evolves during biological crises too, leading to combinations of evolutionary responses always involving changes in relative brain size and, accordingly, paleoneurologists have scrutinized mass extinction events and found that encephalization in tetrapods responded in a variety of ways consistent with the above hypotheses2,25–28. The Great American Biotic Interchange and Holocene extinctions selected for mammals with higher encephalization quotients, whereas climatic shifts at the end of the Oligocene and Miocene drove two distinct pulses of brain expansion in proboscideans2,25,26,29. More strikingly, in the immediate aftermath of the end-Cretaceous mass extinction, the mammalian encephalization quotient decreased, while that in birds increased26–28,30. Similarly, encephalization might have responded to the profound ecological upheavals of the EPME; however, no studies have systematically examined changes in brain size across this interval. Here, we address this gap by quantifying patterns of relative brain size evolution in synapsids spanning the EPME.

In Permo-Triassic synapsids, the evolution of the central nervous system is studied using the endocranial cast of the braincase (hereafter referred to as the endocast), which is the space occupied by the brain and surrounding tissues (e.g., blood vessels, nerves, and meninges) in a fossil skull. Here, we use synchrotron and tube-based Computed Tomography (CT) scanning to reconstruct the endocasts of Permo-Triassic synapsid skulls, mostly from South Africa. To reconstruct the evolution of encephalization spanning the EPME, we applied a phylogenetic comparative approach. The Synapsid Encephalization Quotient (SEQ) is used here as a proxy for relative endocast size (53).

Results

Variations in SEQ across stratigraphy and phylogeny (Fig. 1) suggest that relative brain size was static on average across synapsids during the EPME. Our ancestral character state reconstructions indicate that SEQ values show minimal variance around 1.00. Only a few minor, yet statistically significant increases in SEQ (i.e., positive shifts compared to a Brownian Motion model) are detected prior to the EPME at the nodes Neotherapsida, Theriodontia, Eutheriodontia, and Cynodontia, and then after the EPME, at the nodes Eucynodontia, Probainognathia and Mammaliamorpha (Fig. 1). One significant shift in evolutionary rate (coincident with a SEQ increase) occurred in probainognathian cynodonts (Fig. 1; SI1). A second shift occurred just stemward of Eucynodontia (Platycraniellus + Eucynodontia), but only when including olfactory bulbs in the endocast volume. These negative shifts, suggest stabilizing selection on brain size evolution in these clades31,32. No Permo-Triassic synapsid clade outside eucynodonts shows any significant trend in SEQ value or evolutionary rates past the EPME. The presence or absence of olfactory bulbs in estimates of SEQ does not affect recovered trends and shifts in phenotypic mean or evolutionary rate. Body mass is found to have no significant effect on SEQ evolution. Separate analyses of evolutionary rates in an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) framework identified one shift towards an optimum for SEQ near the base of Mammalia in the Late Triassic, long after the EPME (SI1).

Discussion

Our results highlight a phase of SEQ stasis during the Lopingian Series and EPME (Fig. 1). Strikingly, they support that encephalization increased steadily during the Guadalupian (Fig. 1) but stalled after the end-Guadalupian extinction event33,34. Increases then resumed in the eucynodont lineage only, at the phylogenetic split between the Cynognathia and Probainognathia, the earliest representatives of which are found in the late Olenekian35. The well-documented increase in encephalization in mid-Lopingian cistecephalid dicynodonts36,37 is not statistically significant, but taking it into account, the evolution of encephalization among all synapsids essentially seems to have stalled between 255 and 250 million years ago, bracketing the EPME interval by two to three million years (Fig. 1). Although a comparatively slow pace of evolution is expected during ecologically and climatically stable times such as the Lopingian18,38,39, the apparent absence of evolutionary response of the brain during the environmental upheavals of the EPME requires further scrutiny.

According to the Cognitive Buffer Hypothesis, a large brain would support behavioral adaptations, flexible foraging strategies, and novel ways to find food and explore new ecological niches23,27. Hence, a larger brain would reduce extinction risks and encephalization would be expected to increase during extinction events21,24,28,29; but here we find no significant increase in encephalization across the EPME (Fig. 1).

The prevalence of low encephalization across Permo-Triassic tetrapods indicates that cognition was not a main factor of competitive interaction2,37,40–42. In the absence of strong selective pressure to act against it, maintaining such an energy expansive organ without access to stable and abundant food resources would result in smaller brained individuals being favored20,22,23. Accordingly, the Expensive Brain Hypothesis43,44 posits that, unless advantageous, a larger brain would be associated with higher risks of extinction21–23. Given the low cognitive competition highlighted above, and the generally arid conditions and scarcity of vegetation associated with the extinction in many areas globally11,13,16, it is expected that encephalization would have decreased during the EPME in synapsids. Furthermore, encephalization positively correlates with longevity, gestation length, age at sexual maturity, ambient temperature, and metabolic rate1,23,29,45. Consequently, the adaptations of the surviving synapsid lineages to use burrows for aestivation or torpor, their inferred elevated reproductive rate, and the elevated temperatures during the EPME would be more consistent with a decrease in encephalization8,11,46–49. Yet, such a decrease is also not observed here (Fig. 1).

Moreover, because SEQ measures brain size relative to body mass, any reduction in body mass would automatically inflate the calculated quotient2,30. Yet, the “lilliput effect”, which at least some synapsid lineages seemingly underwent during the EPME50–52, had no effect on encephalization (Fig. 1; SI1). In fact, body mass is found to have had no significant effect on SEQ evolution in Permo-Triassic synapsids (SI1).

To our knowledge, this is the first time that an extinction event is associated with stasis of the encephalization quotient in tetrapods2,25–30. Dicynodonts, cynodonts, and therocephalians across the EPME have SEQs that lie well within the synapsid range, away from both extremes (SI2). Onsets of excessively high and low SEQ values over the Permian demonstrate that encephalization was capable of marked variations in synapsids before they entered the EPME interval36,37,53,54. This observation indicates ample scope for variation; thus, the observed stasis is unlikely to reflect edge effects and does not align with trajectories predicted by existing hypotheses.

Because the brains of many non-mammaliaform synapsids likely did not fully occupy the endocranial cavity53,55, it may be argued that the size of the soft-tissue brain could vary without a proportional change in endocast volume. Nevertheless, comparative data from extant reptiles, in which the brain similarly incompletely fills the braincase, show that endocast volume scales tightly with brain volume and that brain volume is a good predictor of endocast volume56,57. Although some error arises from variation in non-neural endocranial tissues, evolutionary changes in brain size are expected to be reflected by corresponding changes in endocast size across amniotes. Therefore, poor fidelity between brain size and endocast volume is unlikely to explain the SEQ stasis.

Stabilizing selection may have led to the observed stasis, but our results show that it could have played a significant role in cynodonts more derived than Platycraniellus only, which excludes notable post-EPME taxa such as Galesaurus and Thrinaxodon (Fig. 1). Our analyses using an OU framework identify only one shift towards an optimal value for SEQ near the base of Mammalia in the Late Triassic (clade grouping Mammalia, Cifelliodon wahkarmoosuch, and Hadrocodium wui). Considered as a whole, our results indicate a series of shifts towards lower evolutionary rates throughout successive nodes in Probainognathia and less inclusive clades, coupled with shifts towards higher SEQ values in Probainognathia and Mammaliamorpha (Fig. 1). These trends eventually culminate in a shift towards a SEQ optimum in crown mammals. This finding strongly suggests the persistence of stabilizing selection upon SEQ values throughout the Middle and Late Triassic31,58,59. Conversely, significant increases in SEQ observed in the early history of therapsids (Neotherapsida, Theriodontia, Eutheriodontia, Cynodontia) are not linked with any rate shifts or optima. Therefore, if associated with any selective pressure, these shifts in SEQ did not result in phenotypic specialization that influenced the later evolutionary history of the clade, as shown by the stasis in both value and rate of SEQ throughout the Lopingian until the Early Triassic (Fig. 1, SI1).

In the seeming absence of stabilizing selection during the EPME, other, unconventional hypotheses are needed to account for this overall lack of evolutionary response. One potential hypothesis is that the challenges posed by the food shortages and harsh climatic conditions during the EPME imposed such high levels of energetic stress upon the synapsid brain that it strongly restricted the group’s ability to restart brain evolution after the Lopingian pause. Behavior often precedes morphological adaptations60–62, and it follows that neuroplasticity is a crucial precursor to brain size evolution63. In non-mammalian tetrapods, brain plasticity involves the reorganization of neural pathways and mobilization of young neurons (adult neurogenesis) that enable in-depth modifications of the central nervous system of a given individual. This is currently understood as a cornerstone mechanism of long-term brain adaptations, ultimately shaping the trajectory of brain macroevolution in tetrapods64,65. Unlike in other tetrapods, adult neurogenesis is limited mostly to the olfactory bulbs and hippocampus in modern mammals, but non-mammaliaform synapsids, which had much slower growth rates and likely reached sexual maturity before their body mass plateaued, might have plesiomorphically retained a form of adult neurogenesis more similar to that of non-mammalian tetrapods47,66,67. As a result, adult neurogenesis would have played an important role in the evolution of their brain. Given the cost of maintaining a population of young neurons for adult neurogenesis68,69, it is possible that the uniquely challenging conditions of the EPME, more particularly the absence of reliable food sources, water loss, low levels of atmospheric oxygen, and high temperatures and concentration of atmospheric carbon dioxide3,7,11,13,19,70 impeded neurogenesis altogether in therapsids. This would have crippled the long term ability of the synapsid brain to respond to the crisis71.

Furthermore, truncated developmental periods, best documented in Lystrosaurus47, correlate negatively with brain plasticity65, and could have contributed to the lack of SEQ responsivity to the EPME. If survivors reproduced early, their brains may not have reached full maturity before reproduction and death, limiting the extent to which neural plasticity could be expressed.

Our results are also consistent with the possibility that the initial cause of this stasis may find its roots deeper in time, at the end of the Guadalupian, as encephalization and stabilizing selection are found to be non-significant across the subsequent Lopingian series (Fig. 1). However, the relative stability of the environment in the Wuchiapingian18,39 may account for this stasis, the reality of which needs to be cautiously considered given that the cistecephalids notably contradict it36,37.

Ultimately, brain size resumed changing in the probainognathians—in which the SEQ increases from 0.94 (at the Eucynodontia node) to 1.05, and from 0.87 to 0.96 when excluding the olfactory bulbs (SI1)—despite significant stabilizing selection that is manifested in our results by multiple negative shifts of SEQ evolutionary rates (Fig. 1). Contrary to the EPME interval, this conforms well to the predictions of evolution under the Expensive Brain Hypothesis, because ecosystems and climate conditions remained unstable for most of the Triassic12,19,72,73. Accordingly, though probainognathian cynodonts would go through major changes in their anatomy, sensory organs and behavior35,37,54, the lack of resources to grow a larger brain likely imposed a hard ceiling on their encephalization quotient across the Triassic, not departing significantly from 1.00 until the origin of the Mammaliaformes and crown Mammalia (SI1). Most notably, the final major SEQ increase in non-mammaliaform cynodonts occurs within Mammaliamorpha, coinciding with the Carnian Pluvial Episode and supporting a growing body of evidence that this event was pivotal in shaping key mammalian traits, including hair and endothermy37,74,75.

Conclusion

This contribution highlights how encephalization unexpectedly displayed stasis despite the apparent absence of stabilizing selection during the EPME in the synapsid lineage, thus challenging current models of brain evolution. We hypothesize that the shortage of food, loss of water, deprivation of energy and atmospheric oxygen, and concentration of carbon dioxide were so severe that they may have impeded brain plasticity, ultimately crippling any possibility of the brain to change and adapt to the harsh conditions of the EPME. The patterns found in synapsids across the EPME show one of the most unexpected outcomes of a biotic crisis on a biological system and demonstrate that much remains to be learned about the pervasive effects of mass extinctions on the evolution of species.

Materials and methods

The data used in our phylogenetic comparative analyses is primarily taken from Bolton et al37.(SI2), which is the most comprehensive review of synapsid endocranial volumes, body masses, and SEQ values to date. Measurements are provided both including and excluding olfactory bulbs (OB). This dataset was supplemented by new measurements made on the specimens listed below. Digital 3D endocasts were reconstructed using manual segmentation in Avizo 2021 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Hillsborough, OR, USA).

The reconstruction of digital endocasts in Permo-Triassic synapsids often poses a challenge as the ventral aspect of the braincase is usually poorly ossified. To avoid this issue, as much as possible, the selected samples include specimens preserving an in-situ orbitosphenoid, complete pilae antoticae, and the subtle ridges on the braincase walls that mark the ventral limits of the endocast so that it could be reconstructed with reasonable accuracy. A previous analysis supports that deviations due to intraspecific variability, and inter-observer and methodological errors are low in this dataset37.

Body mass

For the newly CT scanned specimens, body mass was estimated using the method by Benoit et al76. based on skull length. For the Lystrosaurus specimens, however, the strong flexure of the snout that characterizes the genus results in a skull that is anteroposteriorly shortened. This precludes realistic body mass estimates based on skull length. To better estimate body mass in Lystrosaurus, we used the method by Benoit and Midzuk53 based on 3D sculptures of the complete body and a density of 1 g/L. Two models were created in order to reflect the different body shapes of Lystrosaurus: one with short legs and snout to reflect the morphology of the species L. murrayi, and another with longer legs and a long snout to more adequately reflect the body proportions of L. declivis and L. maccaigi. The models were then visually fitted on the skulls of the specimens according to their taxonomic affiliation (SI3). The long-snouted model was used for specimens NMQR 3641, NMQR 815, BP/1/2241, and BP/1/8496. The short-snouted model was used for specimens NMQR 3592 and BP/1/3900.

These new data were directly used to calculate the synapsid encephalization quotient (SEQ), a metric of relative brain size designed to compare Paleozoic and Mesozoic synapsids54.

It is commonly believed, though substantiated mostly by anecdotal evidence, that the brain occupies about 50% of the volume of the braincase in non-mammalian and non-avian tetrapods2. Consequently, paleoneurologists working on non-mammalian amniotes have often halved endocast volume values in order to obtain a measurement that is closer to the presumed “actual” brain size2,55. This was not done in the present work because recent data have demonstrated that brain volume can unpredictably occupy anything between 30% and 99% of the volume of the braincase in lepidosaurians, chelonians, and crocodilians56,57.

Phylogenetic comparative analyses

All statistical analyses were performed in R 4.5.2 (77 R Core Team, 2025—SI1). The data were log-transformed (natural logarithm) prior to analysis78,79. Missing data were imputed using Phylopars80 as implemented in package ‘Rphylopars’81,82; a lambda model (see below) was selected as the best fit for imputation using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). The phylogenetic tree was calibrated using first and last appearance data collected from Abdala35 and Smith83 using the cal3 time-scaling method as implemented in ‘paleotree’84–86. Likelihood models for observed frequency of taxa time ranges were built using make_durationFreqCont to estimate the sampling, branching, and extinction rates used to generate a sample of 1000 calibrated trees (SI1). A least-squares consensus based on mean patristic distances was generated from this sample using consensus.edges in ‘phytools’87, with polytomies solved using multi2di in ‘ape’88.

Phylogenetic signal of all three variables in our dataset (body mass, SEQ including olfactory bulbs, and SEQ excluding olfactory bulbs) was estimated using Pagel’s lambda89,90 in ‘phytools’. Lambda was found to be high and highly significant (λ > 0.6; p < 0.001) for all traits. To further check how close our traits follow a Brownian Motion (BM) model91, we tested the fit of each trait using the Akaike Information Criterion corrected for finite sample sizes (AICc)93,94 for a variety of evolutionary models29 with fitContinuous in ‘geiger’92. A Lambda model was selected as the best fit for both types of SEQ, thus justifying the use of a Brownian Motion model to reconstruct ancestral states for this trait95,96. Ancestral reconstructions for SEQ were performed with contMap97 in ‘phytools’.

The correlation between SEQ and body mass was tested using phylogenetic generalized least squares (PGLS) regressions98,99. We ranked the fit of different correlation structures in the variance-covariance matrix of our regressions, corresponding to five distinct evolutionary models—BM, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU), Early Burst, Lambda, and White noise, i.e. non-phylogenetic regression29, using AICc. For both types of SEQ (with and without olfactory bulbs), the White noise model was selected as the best fit. The regression of SEQ on body mass was recovered as significant (p < 0.05) but with an extremely low coefficient of determination (R2 < 0.1), suggesting that body mass does not have an impact on the variation of SEQ in synapsids. Therefore, we did not include body mass as a covariate in subsequent analyses of shifts in phenotypic mean and evolutionary rates of SEQ.

To infer shifts for SEQ in both value (i.e. phenotypic mean) and evolutionary rates along the branches of our tree, we used phylogenetic ridge regressions as implemented in ‘RRphylo’100,101. We chose ‘RRphylo’ as it is specifically designed for analyses on non-ultrametric trees, and therefore is particularly adapted to datasets that incorporate fossil data101 and it tends to overperform similar macroevolutionary approaches that can estimate evolutionary rates for each individual clade in the tree102. We tested for shifts in evolutionary rates using search.shift101, both on the whole tree and for the following specific clades: Biarmosuchia, Dinocephalia, Neotherapsida, Dicynodontia, Theriodontia, Gorgonopsia, Eutheriodontia, Therocephalia, Cynodontia, Eucynodontia, Cynognathia, Probainognathia, Mammaliamorpha, Mammaliaformes, and Mammalia. Variations in the phenotypic mean of SEQ were explored using search.trend103, both for the whole tree and for the same specific clades investigated with search.shift. For each clade of interest, significance of phenotypic trends was assessed using comparison of ridge regression slopes with BM simulations (1000 replicates—103). To account for phylogenetic uncertainty and sampling error, we tested the robustness of our significant shifts and trends by replicating analyses with random permutations of terminal taxa in our tree (1000 replicates per analysis) using overfitRR104–106; a threshold of 0.75 (i.e. ¾ of generated replicates) was used to assess significance (SI1). In addition to the ridge regression framework, we also performed analyses of SEQ in an OU framework in order to identify potential optima in values of our trait of interest, which together with lower evolutionary rates would indicate stabilizing selection31,58,59,107,108. These were performed using function OUshifts in ‘phylolm’ (Ho and Ané, 2014), a procedure able to process non-ultrametric trees, with shifts estimated using the modified BIC (mBIC—109,110. Plots of evolutionary rates and shifts on the calibrated tree were produced with ‘phytools’ and ‘strap’111.

Phylogenetic signal is very high for body mass (λ = 0.998) and relatively high for SEQ with and without olfactory bulbs (λ = 0.731 and 0.689, respectively), and highly significant for all three traits (p < 0.0001). The best evolutionary model recovered in ‘geiger’ for body mass is an Early Burst model, while a Lambda model is recovered as the best fit for both versions of SEQ (AICc weight of 0.667, 0.842, and 0.975, respectively). Neither type of SEQ is highly correlated to body mass: for both AICc-based model selection procedures, a White noise model (no phylogenetic signal) is selected as the best fit; both models are significant (p < 0.01), but the coefficient of determination is extremely low (without olfactory bulbs: R2 = 0.051; with olfactory bulbs: R2 = 0.076), suggesting a very low correlation between these traits.

Detailed results of significant shifts in phenotypic means and evolutionary rates are illustrated in Fig. 1 and are available in the Supplementary Code (SI1, in Markdown format, including p-values for assessing significance of shifts and additional visualizations of these shifts on the tree for both versions of SEQ).

Limitations on inferring stabilizing selection: The incompleteness of the fossil record has long been known to result in a ‘pull of the present’, i.e. accelerated rates of evolution in recent evolutionary history, in macroevolutionary analyses112. Although many approaches have been developed to correct biases over the past two decades, they do not always allow for an unambiguous explanation of the origin of a particular instance of stasis that could be attributed to either fossil record incompleteness or model inadequacy while still reflecting an actual evolutionary pattern112,113. Stasis could be attributed to other factors such as bounded variation in environmental components or the effects of pleiotropy, which can result in localized time-bounded variation that would be recovered as stasis and would not be testable as alternative explanations, nor being differentiable from stabilizing selection113,114. Further sampling would allow for the inclusion of additional predictors and consideration of associated uncertainties in the resulting model.

Date of the EPME

The Main Karoo Basin records the EPME in the terrestrial realm, which occurs at the boundary between the Daptocephalus Assemblage Zone (DAZ) and Lystrosaurus Assemblage Zone (LAZ)5,10,11; but whether this event is synchronous with the marine extinction -currently dated to 251.9 Ma and coincides with the Permian-Triassic boundary- is still debated115. This ongoing debate does not affect our results.
