AutoSynth: Automated Workflow Optimization for High-Quality Synthetic Dataset Generation via Monte Carlo Tree Search
Shuzhen Bi, Chang Song, Siyu Song, Jinze Lv, Jian Chen, Xinyun Wang, Aimin Zhou, Hao Hao

Abstract
Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) of large language models (LLMs) for specialized tasks requires high-quality training datasets, yet manual curation remains prohibitively expensive and time-consuming. While synthetic data generation offers a scalable alternative, its effectiveness depends on carefully designed generation workflows—complex, multi-stage processes integrating prompt engineering, programmatic logic, and model orchestration. Existing automated workflow optimization methods fail to address the cold start problem, as they require labeled datasets for reward modeling, particularly problematic for subjective, open-ended tasks where objective ground truth is unavailable. We introduce AutoSynth, a framework that automates workflow discovery and optimization without reference datasets by reframing the problem as a Monte Carlo Tree Search guided by a novel dataset-free hybrid reward signal. This signal enables meta-learning through two components: an LLM-as-judge evaluating sample quality against dynamically regenerated task-specific metrics, and another LLM-as-judge assessing workflow code and prompt quality. Experiments on subjective educational tasks reveal that while expert-designed workflows achieve higher human preference rates (96-99% win rates compared to AutoSynth’s 40-51%), models trained on AutoSynth-generated data dramatically outperform baseline models (40-51% vs. 2-5% win rates) and surpass expert-designed workflows on certain metrics, suggesting the discovery of quality dimensions not fully captured by human evaluators. These results are achieved while reducing human effort from 5-7 hours to just 30 minutes (>90% reduction). AutoSynth addresses the critical cold start challenge in data-centric AI, providing a scalable, cost-effective method for developing specialized LLMs for subjective tasks lacking objective ground truth. The code is available at https://github.com/bisz9918-maker/AutoSynth .

While effective for well-defined tasks, they share a fundamental limitation: their optimization presupposes a pre-existing labeled dataset to provide a reward signal (e.g., measuring correctness against a held-out answer key). This dependency renders them inapplicable to the most common and critical scenario: creating datasets for novel tasks where no reference data or ground truth exists.

This cold start problem is particularly severe for subjective, open-ended tasks (e.g., educational content, creative writing, personalized tutoring), where: (1) “correct" answers are ill-defined, (2) quality is multi-dimensional and context-dependent, and (3) no validation benchmarks exist. We address this critical gap by introducing

without requiring any reference datasets
. Our key insight is to replace dataset-dependent rewards with a novel
dataset-free hybrid reward signal
computed entirely by LLMs. This signal guides a Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) and uniquely enables a meta-learning process by combining two LLM-as-judge components:

1.

Sample Quality Assessment
: An evaluator LLM assesses generated samples against

at each iteration, allowing the system’s understanding of “quality" to co-evolve with its generation capabilities.

2.

Workflow Quality Assessment
: An optimizer LLM evaluates the workflow’s own code structure, prompt design, and logical coherence, preventing optimization from converging on brittle or unmaintainable implementations.

This process requires only a brief task description and minimal human alignment (approx. 30 minutes) to establish the initial baseline, after which the MCTS optimization proceeds fully autonomously.

We validate AutoSynth on two challenging subjective tasks in the educational domain:

and

1.

While expert-designed workflows still achieve near-perfect human preference rates (96–99%), models trained on AutoSynth data (40–51% win rates) dramatically outperform the baseline model (2–5% win rates).

2.

More importantly, AutoSynth-trained models consistently surpassed expert-designed workflows on metrics, suggesting the discovery and optimization of quality dimensions not fully captured by static human evaluation.

3.

This high-quality result is achieved while reducing human effort by over 90% (from 5–7 hours for expert design to 30 minutes for AutoSynth initialization).

4.

Ablation studies confirm that all components of our design—the hybrid reward, human-in-the-loop initialization, and iterative metric regeneration—are critical to system performance.

Our contributions are as follows:

1.

We propose the workflow optimization framework for synthetic data that operates without reference datasets, addressing the cold start problem for subjective tasks.

2.

We introduce a dataset-free hybrid reward signal that synergistically combines sample-level and workflow-level quality assessment.

3.

We develop a dynamic metric regeneration strategy that enables a meta-learning process, where evaluation criteria co-evolve with generation capabilities.

4.

We provide strong empirical evidence that this automated framework, while reducing human effort by over 90%, generates high-quality data that dramatically outperforms baselines and achieves superior performance metrics.

This work represents a significant step toward fully automated, data-centric AI development, particularly for the vast landscape of subjective tasks where traditional supervised learning fails due to the absence of objective ground truth.

Related Work

Our work bridges two previously disparate research streams: automated workflow optimization for LLM-based systems and synthetic data generation for supervised fine-tuning. We review both domains and articulate how AutoSynth addresses their fundamental incompatibility.

2.1
Automated Workflow Optimization for LLMs

Recent advances in agentic AI have motivated frameworks that automatically construct and optimize multi-step LLM-based workflows. We focus on systems that treat workflow design as a search or optimization problem.

Search-Based Workflow Optimization.
AFlow
[
25
]
pioneered the formulation of workflow optimization as a Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) problem over code-represented execution graphs. Workflows are modeled as directed acyclic graphs where nodes encapsulate LLM calls or computational operations, and edges encode control flow and data dependencies. AFlow iteratively refines these structures through tree-structured experience accumulation and execution-based feedback, demonstrating substantial performance gains across six benchmark datasets. AutoFlow
[
13
]
extends this paradigm by incorporating reinforcement learning to guide the search process, while FlowMind
[
24
]
explores automated prompt composition within workflow contexts. These approaches share a common architectural principle: they decompose complex tasks into modular, executable components and leverage search algorithms to discover optimal configurations.

The Evaluation Bottleneck.
Despite their sophistication, all existing workflow optimization frameworks share a critical limitation: they require
pre-existing labeled validation datasets
to compute reward signals. AFlow, for instance, evaluates candidate workflows by executing them on held-out test cases and measuring task-specific success metrics (e.g., correctness rates for mathematical problem-solving). This dependency renders these systems inapplicable to the most prevalent real-world scenario—creating datasets for novel tasks where no ground-truth evaluation data exists. Our work directly addresses this fundamental constraint.

Connections to AutoML.
These agentic workflow optimization methods represent a natural evolution of automated machine learning (AutoML)
[
6
]
. While classical AutoML focuses on low-level decisions such as hyperparameter tuning and architecture search, LLM-driven workflow automation
[
10
]
operates at a higher level of abstraction, automating prompt engineering, tool orchestration, and multi-step reasoning chains. AutoSynth pushes this frontier further by automating not just the workflow itself, but also the evaluation criteria that guide its optimization.

2.2
Synthetic Data Generation for Supervised Fine-Tuning

The success of supervised fine-tuning (SFT) for domain adaptation hinges critically on training data quality
[
26
]
. Synthetic data generation has emerged as a scalable alternative to human annotation, yet ensuring quality remains a persistent challenge.

Generation Paradigms.
Self-instruct
[
19
]
established the foundational paradigm of bootstrapping instruction-following datasets by prompting LLMs with seed examples. Distillation approaches
[
5
]
leverage powerful teacher models to generate training data for smaller student models, effectively transferring capabilities across model scales. While these methods reduce annotation costs, they inherit biases from the generating models and often produce data with limited diversity or factual inaccuracies
[
8
]
.

Quality Enhancement Techniques.
More sophisticated approaches incorporate iterative refinement mechanisms. Self-critique methods
[
17
]
enable models to improve their outputs through self-generated feedback loops, while verification-based techniques
[
4
]
employ external validators (e.g., unit tests for code generation) to filter low-quality samples. Preference-based approaches
[
18
]
use reward models to score and select high-quality generations. However, these techniques operate within
static, manually designed generation pipelines
, limiting their adaptability to diverse task requirements.

Persistent Quality Challenges.
The literature identifies four fundamental challenges in synthetic data generation that our framework explicitly targets: (1)
Faithfulness
—–mitigating hallucinations and factual errors
[
8
]
; (2)
Diversity
—–preventing mode collapse in generation
[
2
]
; (3)
Subjectivity
—–handling tasks with ill-defined correctness criteria
[
23
]
; and (4)
Controllability
—–precisely steering generation to meet nuanced specifications
[
14
]
. Existing methods address these challenges in isolation through task-specific heuristics, lacking a unified optimization framework.

2.3
Bridging the Gap: From Parallel Development to Convergence

The domains of workflow optimization and synthetic data generation have evolved largely independently, creating a critical gap. Workflow optimization systems (e.g., AFlow) excel at
process optimization
—given a fixed evaluation metric, they find efficient execution strategies. However, they cannot define or refine the metric itself. Conversely, synthetic data research focuses on
output quality
—–developing techniques like self-critique and preference learning to improve individual samples—–but relies on static generation pipelines designed through manual trial-and-error.

AutoSynth represents the first framework to bridge this gap. By introducing a dataset-free hybrid reward signal that evaluates both workflow structure and sample quality, we enable simultaneous optimization of the generation process and the quality criteria themselves. This creates a meta-learning system where improved workflows produce better data, which in turn informs more sophisticated evaluation metrics, which then guide further workflow refinement. This synergistic approach fundamentally addresses the cold start problem: in the absence of ground-truth data, the system bootstraps both its generation capabilities and its understanding of quality from minimal human guidance. In the absence of ground-truth data, AutoSynth bootstraps both its generation capabilities and its understanding of quality from minimal human guidance, enabling automated dataset creation in subjective domains where it was previously infeasible.

3.1
Problem Formulation: Synthesis as Dataset-Free Search

We formalize the automated dataset generation problem as follows: Given a natural language description
\mathcal{T}
of a desired dataset, our objective is to discover an optimal workflow
W^{*}
that generates a dataset
D
maximizing a quality function
Q(D)
:

W^{*}=\arg\max_{W\in\mathcal{W}}Q(D_{W})

where
\mathcal{W}
denotes the space of all possible workflows and
D_{W}
is the dataset produced by workflow
W
.

The fundamental challenge is that
Q
is neither predefined nor computable via comparison against reference data, as no ground-truth dataset exists. We address this by reformulating dataset synthesis as a search problem over the space of executable workflows, where the quality function
Q
is itself dynamically constructed through LLM-based evaluation.

We employ Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) to construct a search tree
\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
where each node
v\in\mathcal{V}
represents a complete workflow implementation, and each directed edge
e\in\mathcal{E}
represents a modification (mutation) applied to transform a parent workflow into a child workflow. The search is guided by a novel dataset-free hybrid reward signal, detailed in Section
3.4
.

3.2
Workflow Representation and Initialization

Workflow Structure.
A workflow
W
is formally defined as a composite entity
W=\langle P,C\rangle
, where:

1.

P
represents structured prompts, including system instructions, few-shot exemplars, and generation templates that guide LLM behavior.

2.

C
represents executable Python code implementing control logic, data transformations, error handling, and orchestration of multiple LLM API calls.

This dual representation combines the flexibility of natural language steering (via prompts) with the precision of programmatic control (via code).

Human-in-the-Loop Initialization.
AutoSynth begins with a brief human-guided phase to align the search with user intent and establish a functional baseline.

1.

An
Optimizer LLM
(Claude Sonnet 3.7) receives the task description
\mathcal{T}
and generates an initial workflow
W_{\text{init}}
.

2.

W_{\text{init}}
is executed to produce a small batch of sample data (5-10 instances).

3.

A human expert reviews the workflow (code and prompts) and the samples, providing concise feedback on correctness, alignment, and potential improvements.

4.

The Optimizer LLM incorporates this feedback to produce a revised workflow
W^{\prime}_{\text{init}}
.

5.

Steps 2-4 repeat for 1-3 iterations until a functional baseline
W_{0}
is obtained that successfully executes and produces reasonable data.

This process ensures the search begins in a promising region of the workflow space and captures nuanced user intentions. The resulting
W_{0}
becomes the root node of the MCTS search tree.

3.3
MCTS-Driven Workflow Optimization

The MCTS optimization loop (Figure
1
, middle) iteratively refines the baseline workflow by balancing exploration and exploitation.

3.3.1
Selection

The search begins by selecting a promising node. We identify the top-3 workflows in the tree ranked by their hybrid reward scores
R(W)
(defined in Section
3.4
). We then compute a probability distribution over these candidates:

p_{i}=\frac{R(W_{i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{3}R(W_{j})}

and sample one workflow
W_{\text{selected}}
according to this distribution. This probabilistic selection balances exploitation of high-scoring workflows with exploration of other promising candidates.

3.3.2
Refinement

The selected workflow
W_{\text{selected}}
is provided to the Optimizer LLM (Claude Sonnet 3.7), which acts as an expert programmer and prompt engineer. The LLM receives:

1.

The original task description
\mathcal{T}
.

2.

The complete code and prompts of
W_{\text{selected}}
.

3.

All accumulated experiences from child workflows (modification descriptions, reward scores, and qualitative feedback).

4.

Actionable improvement suggestions for
W_{\text{selected}}
.

The Optimizer proposes a targeted modification (e.g., code changes, prompt enhancements, structural adjustments) to produce a new child node
W_{\text{new}}
. The description of this modification (e.g., Added self-verification step for mathematical correctness) is recorded as an
experience
annotation on the edge connecting
W_{\text{selected}}
to
W_{\text{new}}
.

3.3.3
Evaluation

The newly generated workflow
W_{\text{new}}
is executed to produce a small evaluation batch of
n=5\text{-}10
data samples. This batch serves as the input for our Dataset-Free Hybrid Reward Signal computation (detailed in Section
3.4
), which concurrently assesses both the
quality of the generated samples
and the
structural integrity of the workflow
.

3.3.4
Backpropagation

The hybrid reward
R(W_{\text{new}})
computed for the new workflow is recorded and associated with the
W_{\text{new}}
node. Unlike traditional MCTS, which propagates averaged scores, AutoSynth preserves the precise score for each workflow.

What is propagated to ancestor nodes is the
experience record
: the modification description, the resulting reward
R(W_{\text{new}})
, and the qualitative feedback. This experience is appended to
W_{\text{selected}}
and its ancestors, enriching the context for future refinement decisions and allowing the Optimizer LLM to learn from both successful and unsuccessful modifications.

Termination.
The MCTS loop continues until convergence (scores stabilize) or a maximum of 30 iterations is reached. The highest-scoring workflow
W^{*}
is then selected.

3.4
Dataset-Free Hybrid Reward Signal

The core innovation enabling AutoSynth to operate without reference datasets is our hybrid reward mechanism. It combines assessments of both the generated data (the product) and the workflow itself (the process). The final reward is an unweighted average of these two components:

R(W)=0.5\cdot Score_{sample}(W)+0.5\cdot Score_{workflow}(W)

This balanced formulation prevents pathological solutions, such as a brittle workflow that produces good samples but is unmaintainable, or an elegant workflow that produces mediocre data.

3.4.1
Component 1: Dynamic Sample Quality Score (
Score_{sample}
)

This component assesses the quality of the workflow’s output
D_{W}
. Its calculation is founded on a dynamic metric generation process that enables the system to co-evolve its generation and evaluation capabilities.

Dynamic Metric Generation and Co-evolution.

Instead of relying on a fixed, predefined metric,
Score_{sample}
is computed against criteria that are dynamically generated at each MCTS iteration. This mechanism transforms the optimization into a meta-learning process, addressing the “Goodhart’s Law" problem: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

This co-evolutionary dynamic proceeds as follows:

1.

Metric Proposal:
Given the batch of samples
D_{W_{t}}
from the current workflow
W_{t}
, an
Evaluator LLM
(GPT-5) is prompted to act as a domain expert. It proposes a comprehensive set of evaluation metrics
M_{t}=\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\}
tailored to the task description
\mathcal{T}
.

2.

Metric Structure:
Each metric
m_{i}
includes a name, a detailed definition, a positive exemplar, and a negative exemplar to anchor scoring decisions.

3.

Self-Consistency Ensemble:
To ensure robustness, the Evaluator LLM independently generates three candidate metric sets. The set with the highest average semantic overlap with the other two is selected, filtering out idiosyncratic metrics.

Critically, this process is re-executed at every MCTS iteration. This dynamic regeneration compels the system to continuously refine its own conception of quality. As workflows improve (e.g., from generating factually correct text to generating text with clear analogies), the metrics evolve to capture these new dimensions of quality (e.g., adding an “Analogy Clarity" metric). The system does not merely optimize toward a static goal; it refines the goal itself as it learns.

Sample-by-Sample Evaluation.

Given the newly generated dynamic metrics
M_{t}
, the Evaluator LLM (GPT-5) scores each sample
d_{i}\in D_{W_{\text{new}}}
against each metric
m_{j}
. For each pair, the LLM provides:

1.

A numerical score
s_{ij}\in[1,5]
based on the metric’s exemplars.

2.

A brief qualitative justification for the score.

Aggregation.

The sample quality score is computed as the average across all samples and all metrics:

Score_{sample}(W)=\frac{1}{|D_{W}|\cdot|M_{t}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|D_{W}|}\sum_{j=1}^{|M_{t}|}s_{ij}

The qualitative justifications are concatenated and stored as improvement suggestions for the next MCTS refinement step.

3.4.2
Component 2: Workflow Quality Score (
Score_{workflow}
)

In parallel, the Optimizer LLM that performed the refinement step also conducts an introspective evaluation of the new workflow
W_{\text{new}}
itself, assessing the
process
rather than the
product
. This score is based on:

1.

Code Quality
(
Q_{\text{code}}
): Assessed on clarity (readability, documentation), efficiency (complexity, API optimization), and robustness (error handling, edge cases).

2.

Prompt Quality
(
Q_{\text{prompt}}
): Assessed on clarity (unambiguous instructions), specificity (detailed requirements), and effectiveness (likelihood of eliciting desired behavior).

This component acts as a regularizer, ensuring that optimized workflows are not only effective but also generalizable, maintainable, and human-interpretable.

4
Experiments

4.1
Experimental Setup

Tasks and Domains.
We evaluate AutoSynth on two challenging educational tasks where quality is inherently subjective and no ground-truth answers exist:

1.

Math Concept Explanation
: Generating pedagogically sound explanations of mathematical concepts tailored to students at specific learning stages.

2.

Interdisciplinary Lesson Plan Generation
: Creating comprehensive lesson plans that integrate multiple subject areas (e.g., mathematics and history, science and literature) with authentic contexts and coherent learning objectives.

These domains exemplify scenarios where AutoSynth’s dataset-free approach provides maximum value: traditional supervised learning struggles due to the absence of objective correctness criteria, and manually designing effective generation workflows demands substantial pedagogical expertise.

Implementation Details.
We employ a division of labor between two state-of-the-art LLMs to leverage their respective strengths:

1.

Optimizer LLM (Claude Sonnet 3.7)
: Responsible for workflow generation and refinement during MCTS expansion, as well as introspective evaluation of workflow code and prompt quality (
Score_{workflow}
). Claude’s strong capabilities in code generation and structured reasoning make it well-suited for programmatic workflow optimization.

2.

Evaluator LLM (GPT-5)
: Handles all data-centric operations including (1) executing workflows to generate training samples, (2) dynamically generating task-specific evaluation metrics from generated samples, and (3) scoring individual samples against these metrics (
Score_{sample}
). GPT-5’s advanced reasoning and nuanced assessment capabilities enable reliable quality evaluation in subjective educational domains.

The MCTS optimization runs for a maximum of 30 iterations or until convergence, defined as the top-3 workflow scores stabilizing within threshold
\epsilon=0.05
. In practice, most tasks converge within 15-25 iterations.

Baselines and Comparisons.
We compare AutoSynth against two critical baselines:

1.

Expert-Designed Workflow
: A senior educational technology researcher manually designs and iteratively refines a data generation workflow for each task. This process involves: (a) analyzing task requirements, (b) designing initial prompt templates and code structure, (c) generating sample data and manually evaluating quality, (d) iteratively refining the workflow based on observed weaknesses. This represents the current best practice for synthetic dataset creation and requires 5-7 hours of expert effort per task.

2.

Base Model (Zero-Shot)
: The unmodified Qwen-Instruct-32B model evaluated directly on test queries without any task-specific fine-tuning.

Dataset Generation and Fine-Tuning.
For each task and method (AutoSynth, Expert-Designed, Base Model), we generate 1,000 training examples using the respective workflows (no data generation for Base Model). We then perform supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on Qwen-Instruct-32B using standard hyperparameters: learning rate
2\times 10^{-5}
, batch size 16, 3 epochs with linear warmup and cosine decay. The Base Model serves as the pre-training checkpoint.

Evaluation Framework.
We employ the ELMES (Evaluating Large Language Models in Educational Scenarios) framework, an open-source automated evaluation system specifically designed for assessing LLMs in educational settings that incorporates a hybrid evaluation engine that objectively quantifies traditionally subjective pedagogical metrics using an LLM-as-a-Judge methodology
[
20
]
.
For all evaluations, we construct a held-out test set of 10 queries per task, ensuring no overlap with training data.

Key Findings:

1.

Substantial Improvement Over Base Model with Minimal Human Effort
: AutoSynth achieves 40-51% win rates in human pairwise evaluations across all methods, dramatically outperforming the base model (2-5% win rates) and demonstrating the critical value of task-specific dataset generation. Remarkably, this performance is obtained with only 30 minutes of human involvement for initialization and metric validation, compared to 5-7 hours required for expert workflow engineering—representing over 90% reduction in human effort. While expert-designed workflows achieve the highest win rates (96-99%), AutoSynth provides a compelling cost-quality trade-off for rapid deployment in resource-constrained scenarios.

2.

Superior Metric Performance with Divergent Human Preferences
: AutoSynth achieves higher metric scores than expert-designed workflows across both tasks (4.25 vs 4.18 for Math Concepts, 4.82 vs 4.75 for Lesson Plans), revealing an intriguing divergence between metrical and human evaluation. While human evaluators strongly prefer expert-designed outputs in pairwise comparisons (96-99% win rates), AutoSynth-trained models demonstrate superior performance on the formal evaluation metrics. This discrepancy suggests that expert-designed data may incorporate subtle stylistic preferences, rhetorical conventions, or pedagogical nuances that resonate strongly with human judges but are not explicitly captured by the metric definitions. Conversely, AutoSynth’s optimization process may more directly target the quantifiable dimensions specified in the evaluation rubrics. Both fine-tuned approaches demonstrate substantial improvements over the base model (0.51-1.07 point gains), validating that specialized dataset creation, whether automated or manual, is essential for educational applications.

3.

Bridging the Automation Gap
: The performance gap between AutoSynth (40-51% win rate) and expert-designed workflows (96-99% win rate) reveals both progress and remaining challenges in fully automated dataset generation. AutoSynth successfully automates the workflow design process that previously required hours of expert effort, but does not yet match the nuanced pedagogical sophistication of human-designed approaches in human evaluations. This gap represents an opportunity for future research in incorporating domain-specific knowledge and pedagogical frameworks into the optimization process. Notably, the consistency of AutoSynth’s relative position across both tasks (approximately 8-10x improvement over base model, achieving 41-53% of expert-level win rates) suggests stable generalization across diverse educational scenarios.

5
Conclusion

This work introduces AutoSynth, the first framework to automate synthetic dataset generation for subjective tasks without requiring reference data. By formulating workflow optimization as dataset-free search guided by dynamically evolving quality metrics, AutoSynth reduces human effort by over 90% (from 5-7 hours to 30 minutes) while maintaining competitive performance.

Our experiments on educational tasks reveal both promise and limitations. AutoSynth substantially outperforms baseline models (8-10× improvement in win rates) and achieves superior automated metric scores compared to expert-designed workflows. However, a performance gap remains in human preference evaluations (40-51% vs 96-99% win rates), indicating that automated optimization does not yet fully capture the pedagogical sophistication of human experts. We view this gap as a frontier for future research rather than a fundamental limitation.

Ablation studies validate our core design choices: the hybrid reward signal prevents pathological optimization, human initialization accelerates convergence, and iterative metric regeneration enables meta-learning that discovers latent quality dimensions. Each component contributes meaningfully to the framework’s effectiveness.

Future Directions.
Key opportunities include: (1) enhanced search strategies incorporating pedagogical principles through curriculum learning, (2) integration of domain-specific knowledge frameworks without extensive annotation, (3) transfer learning across related tasks, and (4) hybrid human-AI collaboration modes.

Beyond education, AutoSynth’s paradigm has broad implications for data-centric AI in domains with subjective quality criteria——from creative writing to personalized healthcare. Our work demonstrates that the cold-start problem in dataset creation can be meaningfully addressed through meta-learning that bootstraps quality standards from minimal human guidance, opening new pathways for democratizing specialized AI development.
