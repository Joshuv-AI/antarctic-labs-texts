Genome-wide characterization of the bZIP gene family in Rattus norvegicus and expression profiling analysis during brain development
Authors: Pan Lu, Zongjun Zhu, Yuanyuan Guan, Ya Wang, Ruiquan Chen, Hongbo Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe brown rat (Rattus norvegicus) serves as a cornerstone model organism in biomedical research, particularly for understanding physiological homeostasis and stress responses. The basic leucine zipper (bZIP) transcription factor family is a pivotal regulatory network involved in growth, organogenesis, and neurodevelopment. Despite its importance, a systematic characterization of the bZIP gene family in rats has remained elusive.ResultsIn this study, we performed a genome-wide identification of 61 RnbZIP genes, which were categorized into 10 distinct subfamilies based on phylogenetic relationships and chromosomal localization. Structural analysis revealed conserved motif arrangements within subfamilies, while collinearity analysis identified significant gene duplication events—predominantly tandem and segmental duplications—that have driven the evolutionary expansion of the RnbZIP family. Quantitative analysis showed that members within the same subfamily shared 45%–92% sequence similarity (calculated using the BLOSUM62 scoring matrix), and all duplicated gene pairs underwent strong purifying selection (Ka/Ks < 1). Comparative genomics across seven rodent species further underscored the evolutionary conservation and divergence of these factors. Expression profiling across diverse organs and brain developmental stages indicated that RnbZIP genes exhibit high tissue specificity. Notably, 10 candidate genes, including RnbZIP01, RnbZIP02, and RnbZIP08, demonstrated dynamic expression patterns during brain maturation, suggesting their essential roles in neurodevelopmental processes.ConclusionsOur findings provide a comprehensive structural and evolutionary framework for the RnbZIP gene family, highlighting their potential regulatory functions in rat organogenesis and brain development. This study establishes a valuable resource for further functional characterization of specific bZIP members in mammalian neurological systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13180-1.Keywords: Rattus norvegicus, The basic leucine zipper, Gene family, Gene expression

Introduction

The basic leucine zipper (bZIP) transcription factor family is a significant group of transcription factors found in mammals [1], recognized for their crucial role in regulating various physiological processes [2–5]. These processes encompass responses to oxidative stress, hormone signaling, metabolic regulation, cancer diagnosis, and the regulation of liver diseases [6–10]. bZIP proteins facilitate DNA binding and dimerization through their basic region and leucine zipper structure, allowing them to recognize and bind to cis-regulatory elements of target genes, thereby exerting regulatory functions [11, 12].

Recent studies have demonstrated that bZIP transcription factors are vital in mammalian physiological and pathological stresses. For instance, nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 1 (NFE2L1, also known as NRF1), is a member of the CNC-bZIP family. Deficiency of NFE2L1 results in abnormal adipogenesis and impaired adipose tissue (AT) [13], abnormal upregulation of NRF2 can promote cellular transformation and facilitate the growth, metastasis, and treatment resistance of cancer cells [14], NRF2 activation regulates numerous processes related to cell growth, differentiation, and apoptosis, and is closely associated with oxidative stress-mediated cancer. Furthermore, activation of NRF2 is implicated in the regulation of anti-inflammatory responses, metabolic disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, and multidrug resistance [15–17]. As bZIP domains are highly conserved across eukaryotes (including humans and rodents), cross-species comparative analysis can effectively reveal the evolutionary and functional characteristics of this family.

While significant developments have occurred in understanding the functions and regulatory mechanisms of bZIP genes across various organisms [18–26], their specific roles in rat certain remain poorly understood. Notably, research on the bZIP gene family in rat (Rattus norvegicus) is limited, and its specific roles in tissue differentiation and neurodevelopment warrant further investigation.

We hypothesized that gene duplication events drive the evolutionary expansion of the rat bZIP family, and tissue-specific expression patterns reflect the functional differentiation of bZIP genes in brain development. This study aimed to systematically analyze the bZIP gene family in rats through comprehensive bioinformatics analysis of its genome, focusing on gene structures, evolutionary relationships, and expression patterns. We explored the expression profiles of these bZIP genes under different stress and hormonal treatments and evaluated their potential roles in animal growth and development. Through this research, we provided new insights into the mechanisms of bZIP gene involvement in brain growth.

Results

Identification of bZIP genes in rats

In this study, we accurately identified all potential bZIP genes using the NCBI rat reference genome (GRCr8, GenBank accession: GCA_036323735.1), ensembl release 110 proteome, and UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot reviewed database (Table S1) [27]. The designated genes were labeled according to their chromosomal locations, ranging from RnbZIP01 to RnbZIP61. A comprehensive analysis was performed to identify the key characteristics, including coding sequence (CDS), isoelectric point (pI) and molecular weight (MW).

Among the 61 RnbZIP proteins analyzed, the smallest, RnbZIP50, consists of 116 amino acids, while the largest, RnbZIP19, contains 1,194 amino acids. The molecular weights of these proteins range from 13.48 kDa for RnbZIP50 to 128.99 kDa for RnbZIP19. Their pI values vary from 4.28 for RnbZIP39 to 10.67 for RnbZIP48, with an average value of 6.99. Sixteen RnbZIP proteins contain a bZIP domain, while an additional 45 possess a BRLZ domain, which might bestow supplementary roles or regulatory mechanisms. Regarding predicted subcellular localization, 56 RnbZIP proteins were found in the nucleus, one in the cell membrane, three in the cytoplasm, and one in the mitochondria (Table S1). The proportion of RnbZIP genes in the rat genome is approximately 0.13%. This discovery offers understanding into the development and variation of the bZIP gene family among various rodent species.

Phylogenetic analysis and classification of RnbZIP genes

To classify the subfamilies of bZIP proteins in rats, we developed a phylogenetic tree utilizing Maximum Likelihood (ML) method incorporating RnbZIPs from rats (61 members), MubZIPs from mice (Mus, 64 members), and DobZIPs from Ord’s kangaroo rat (Dipodomys ordii, 57 members) (Fig. 1, Table S1). Based on the classification methods, we categorized 182 bZIP proteins into 10 major topological groups (subfamilies A–J). These 10 major subfamilies contain 61 RnbZIP protein members, which is in agreement with the organization of bZIP proteins in mice, demonstrating that these branches of bZIP proteins have been preserved throughout the long-term evolution of rats. The clustering tree reveals that the RnbZIP20 protein establishes a specific topological configuration in conjunction with MubZIP29 and DobZIP35, suggesting new characteristics of evolutionary diversity in rats (Fig. 1, Table S1). Conserved motifs and gene structure analysis of RnbZIP genes

All protein, CDS, and genomic sequences used for alignment, gene structure, and motif analysis were directly extracted from the NCBI GRCr8 rat genome, with manual curation to remove redundant and incomplete isoforms. Through the comparison of DNA sequences of RnbZIP genes within the rat genome, we examined the quantity and organization of exons and introns in these genes. (Fig. 2, Table S2). The findings indicated that the 61 RnbZIP genes displayed a diverse range of intron counts, varying between 1 and 16 (Table S1). We have supplemented detailed introns information of all RnbZIP genes in Table S1 for clear and accurate presentation. Further statistical analysis revealed significant differences in intron number distribution among different subfamilies of the RnbZIP family. Specifically, genes in subfamily B possess a stable intron number of 2–3; subfamily D contains relatively more introns, ranging from 6 to 16; while subfamily A shows highly variable intron numbers (1–12), reflecting obvious evolutionary divergence among these subfamilies.

In these RnbZIP proteins, ten conserved motifs were identified using the MEME online program, subsequent analysis was conducted on the distinctive amino acid regions of these proteins (Fig. 3). Motifs 1 and 2 are extensively found in the majority of bZIP proteins, with motif 1 being absent only in RnbZIP02, RnbZIP01, RnbZIP30, RnbZIP07, RnbZIP39, RnbZIP19, and RnbZIP52, as these genes retain only motif 2, which is uniquely absent in RnbZIP40. For the majority of members, the locations of these two motifs are in close proximity to each other. Overall, RnbZIP proteins within the same subfamily display similar motif arrangements. For instance, group D, E, and F include motifs 1 and 2, subfamily D consists of motifs 1, 2, and 7, group C and J contain motifs 1, 2, and 8, while group A incorporates motifs 1, 2, 5, and 9, and group E and F comprise motifs 1, 2, and 4. group D and F also contain motifs 1, 2, 3, 6, and 10. Furthermore, certain motifs are exclusive to specific groups. motif 7 is unique to group D, motifs 5 and 9 are specific to group A, and motifs 6 and 10 are unique to group F. Additional analysis reveals that specific motifs are reliably positioned at particular sites within the structures of these RnbZIPs. In groups A, C, D, E, F, and J, motifs 1 and 2 are always positioned in the latter half of the pattern. Motifs 5 and 9 are typically situated at the beginning of group A, whereas motifs 6 and 10 predominantly appear at the start of group F. The layout of these motifs suggests that the motif structures within the same subgroup are analogous, demonstrating a potential conservation of protein structure within the same subgroup, which substantiated the arrangement of the phylogenetic trees.

Chromosomal distribution and gene duplication of RnbZIP genes

A total of 61 RnbZIP genes were identified, exhibiting an uneven distribution across 20 chromosomes (Fig. 4, Tables S3, S4), with each gene corresponding to a specific physical location on different chromosomes in rats. Chromosome 7 contained the highest number of RnbZIP members (10 bZIP genes, approximately 16.39%), followed by chromosome 01 (9 genes, approximately 14.75%). Chromosomes 2, 9, 12, 16, and 20 contained only one RnbZIP member each, while no RnbZIP genes were found on chromosomes 18, X, or Y. Gene members belonging to the same family that are positioned within a 200 kb region of a chromosome are defined as tandem repeats (genes arranged in tandem). Several tandem repeats were identified within the bZIP gene family of rats (Fig. 4, Tables S3, S4). A total of 4 tandem repeats encompassing 7 RnbZIP genes were detected on chromosomes 1, 7, 13, and 15. All RnbZIP genes participating in these tandem repeats are members of the same subgroup. Individuals within subfamily B exhibited a higher number of tandem repeats, which may explain why this subfamily exhibits the greatest number of members. Two pairs of segmental repetitions (expansion of extensive chromosomal segments) were identified among the RnbZIP genes (Fig. 4, Tables S3, S4), specifically RnbZIP51 on chromosome 13 and RnbZIP25 on chromosome 6, both belonging to subfamily B, and RnbZIP37 on chromosome 7 and RnbZIP17 on chromosome 4, both belonging to subfamily E. Quantitative synteny analysis identified 4 tandem duplication events and 2 segmental duplication events (Fig. 4), which were the main drivers for the expansion of subfamilies B, D, and F. All duplicated tandem and segmental RnbZIP gene pairs were under purifying selection with valid Ka/Ks values ranging from 0.166 to 0.438, directly supporting the conclusion of Ka/Ks < 1 and reflecting strong functional constraints during rodent evolution; two gene pairs were excluded from this calculation owing to saturated synonymous substitutions (pS ≥ 0.75, Ks = NaN) that precluded reliable Ka/Ks estimation, and all valid Ka/Ks datasets have been deposited in Table S5.

Comparative evolutionary analysis of RnbZIP and bZIP proteins in different organisms

To assess the genetic connections of bZIP proteins in rats and six selected rodent species (Dipodomys ordii, Ictidomys tridecemlineatus, Sciurus vulgaris, Urocitellus parryii, and Meriones unguiculatus) [28–33], an unrooted phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method. Ten conserved motifs were identified in the bZIP protein sequences of these rodent species using the MEME online tool (Fig. 5 and Figure S1, Table S6). Detailed information regarding the conserved motifs is provided in Tables S1 and S2. RnbZIP proteins tend to cluster with bZIP members from Dipodomys ordii and Meriones unguiculatus. With the exception of a few RnbZIP proteins, such as RnbZIP19, RnbZIP07, RnbZIP52, RnbZIP46, RnbZIP41, and RnbZIP39, all other RnbZIP proteins contain both motif 1 and motif 2. Additionally, some motifs are found exclusively within specific topological forms, such as motifs 4, 5, 8, and 10. Overall, the bZIP genes of these rodent species exhibit similar motif arrangements to those of rats within the same topological branches. Certain subfamilies of bZIP proteins across these species typically contain similar motifs, highlighting their evolutionary relationships.

Comparative genomic analysis of bZIP Genes between R. norvegicus and R. rattus

To further explore the evolutionary conservation and divergence of bZIP genes within the Rattus genus, we performed a comparative genomic analysis of bZIP family members between R. norvegicus and R. rattus (Figure S2). In total, 42 RnbZIP genes were found to have orthologous counterparts in the R. rattus genome, corresponding to 47 orthologous copies, indicating widespread gene conservation and slight copy number variation between the two species. Most orthologous genes (24 members) maintained a one-to-one correspondence, while 14 genes possessed two orthologs in R. rattus. Additionally, RnbZIP18 exhibited three orthologous copies, and both RnbZIP17 and RnbZIP37 had up to four orthologs, suggesting differential gene retention or local gene duplication in R. rattus. Subfamily distribution analysis revealed uneven conservation patterns: subfamilies A, D, and F possessed the most orthologous gene pairs (10 pairs each), followed by B and E (9 pairs each), C (8 pairs), J (4 pairs), I (3 pairs), G (2 pairs), and H (1 pair). Intriguingly, multiple structurally conserved gene clusters were identified between the two species, including the homologous pairs RnbZIP05/RnbZIP06 (XM_032893725.1/XM_032893615.1) and RnbZIP08/RnbZIP09 (XM_032891169.1/XM_032895295.1), as well as the clustered genes RnbZIP25/RnbZIP26/RnbZIP27 corresponding to XM_032908166.1/XM_032908167.1/XM_032908785.1. Notably, these highly conserved gene clusters do not belong to identical subfamilies, implying that they represent stably inherited and functionally important genomic blocks that have been conserved during the evolutionary divergence of the two Rattus species. All detailed orthologous information is summarized in Table S9.

Expression patterns of RnbZIPs in various rat organs

To assess the functional activities of 61 RnbZIP genes in rats, we utilized qRT-PCR to assess the expression levels of 61 RnbZIP members. The results revealed notable differences in amino acid compositions and disparate clustering connections among members of different subfamilies. We analyzed the transcriptional accumulation of these RnbZIP genes in four organs: brain, liver, testis, and muscle (Fig. 6). The tissue expression specificity index indicated that many RnbZIP genes exhibit preferential expression in specific organs of rats (Table S7). Ten genes (RnbZIP01, RnbZIP02, RnbZIP06, RnbZIP08, RnbZIP18, RnbZIP19, RnbZIP26, RnbZIP41, RnbZIP42, and RnbZIP55) demonstrated a tendency to be expressed in brain tissue; seven genes (RnbZIP04, RnbZIP05, RnbZIP15, RnbZIP28, RnbZIP37, RnbZIP53, and RnbZIP54) were predominantly expressed in the liver, while four genes (RnbZIP27, RnbZIP29, RnbZIP46, and RnbZIP52) showed a preference for expression in the testis. RnbZIP31, in contrast, was mainly expressed in muscle.

Validation of key RnbZIP genes expression in brain tissue

To further investigate the transcription patterns of ten key bZIP members in the rat brain, we then measured expression levels in various tissues and developmental stages. Derived from qRT-PCR analysis, we examined the transcriptional patterns of these ten RnbZIP genes in the brain, liver, testis, and muscle (Fig. 7A). These RnbZIP genes exhibited significant differences in expression patterns across various tissues, suggesting their potential roles in distinct physiological processes and tissue-specific functions. Notably, some genes exhibited pronounced expression in brain tissue, indicating their involvement in neurological functions, while low expression levels of other RnbZIP genes may suggest limitations or functional diversity in specific tissues. Among these ten genes, all displayed the highest expression levels in the brain; however, RnbZIP02 also presented significantly increased expression in the liver and muscle, while it was primarily expressed in the brain and almost not expressed in other tissues. Furthermore, the stage-specific expression profiles of these key RnbZIP genes closely parallel major neurodevelopmental milestones in the rat brain, providing vital clues about their physiological roles (Fig. 7B). At postnatal day 1 (1-day-old), the brain undergoes extensive neurogenesis, cell migration, and early axonal pathfinding. The relatively high expression of RnbZIP01 during this neonatal window suggests its crucial involvement in these early cytoarchitectural events. Notably, the 3–4 weeks period marks a critical transition into adolescence, characterized by the explosive peak of synaptogenesis, synaptic pruning, and massive oligodendroglial myelination. RnbZIP02 exhibits a dramatic, stage-specific expression surge precisely during this 3–4 weeks window, strongly implying that it serves as a key transcriptional regulator for these high-energy neurodevelopmental events, such as the rapid formation of myelin sheaths or the stabilization of synaptic networks. By 6 months of age, the brain has reached full functional maturity, focused on cyto-homeostasis and synaptic plasticity. At this adult stage, RnbZIP01 expression rebounds, while RnbZIP08 maintains consistently high transcription across all stages, indicating that RnbZIP08 likely functions as a core homeostatic factor required for the continuous maintenance of mature neural circuitry.

Discussion

This study revealed a total of 61 RnbZIP genes, which exhibited significant structural differences among the proteins they encode, thereby illustrating the complexity of the rat (Figs. 1 and 2, Table S1). The N-terminal basic region of bZIP transcription factors is known to contain conserved amino acid sequences that determine their DNA-binding ability [34–36]. Structurally similar transcription factors generally bind to specific cis-regulatory elements. Our analysis revealed that 16 RnbZIP genes (26.22%) were classified as bZIP proteins, while 45 RnbZIP genes (73.77%) were categorized as BRLZ proteins. This distribution may be consistent across rodent species, particularly within the Mus genus. Given the maintained amino acid configuration of bZIP proteins, they may operate through the formation of homodimeric and heterodimeric complexes; nevertheless, the particular molecular interactions remain to be fully elucidated. Previous research has indicated that the bZIP domain facilitates dimer formation, with the residues in the leucine zipper dimerization region being critical for this process [37].

Following the construction of the phylogenetic tree, the ten subfamily members are preserved similarly in Dipodomys ordii and mice. While some proteins may have experienced enhanced diversification or proliferation, genetic distances among various families tend to converge, suggesting that bZIP proteins within the same family may exhibit closer genetic relationships and likely derived from shared ancestral proteins. Topological tree analysis indicates that the largest observed rat subfamilies are D (n = 11, 18.03%), F (n = 11, 18.03%), B (n = 9, 14.75%), and C (n = 8, 13.11%). The similarity in member counts suggests that the bZIP genes of these specific subfamilies have undergone extensive expansion during rodent diversification.

Most RnbZIP genes consist of multiple exons, and various subfamilies exhibit notable variation in intron numbers, potentially leading to the expansion of the bZIP family and the emergence of novel functions. Tandem repeats can rapidly adapt to physiological demands and developmental requirements by expanding and contracting, thus preserving a stable number of genes involved in related functions without adding molecular complexity throughout evolution. Additionally, large-scale syntenic blocks are prevalent in mammalian genomes. While we describe these events as segmental duplications based on their current structural manifestations in the modern rat genome, it is important to note that these broad duplicated blocks are likely the evolutionary remnants of ancestral Whole Genome Duplications (WGDs) that occurred early in vertebrate history. Thus, both historical WGD events and subsequent local tandem repeats have played significant roles in the expansion of this gene family [38]. However, as observed in larger-scale genomic databases, distinguishing whether these specific duplication events are uniquely Rattus-specific, genus-specific, or remnants of more ancient mammalian expansions requires broader, multi-taxa comparative phylogenomic investigations in the future. Nevertheless, the identified duplication instances have led to the proliferation of many RnbZIP genes, which may provide a genetic basis for the functional diversification of the bZIP family. Furthermore, it is important to note that our current genomic characterization relies entirely on a single reference genome assembly (GRCr8). Consequently, individual-specific Copy Number Variations (CNVs), which are critical drivers of intra-specific genetic diversity and short-term adaptation, could not be captured in this study. Future population-level whole-genome re-sequencing across multiple individual rats will be essential to fully elucidate the role of CNVs in the ongoing evolution of the rat bZIP gene family. Furthermore, the structural and functional diversification of the RnbZIP gene family appears to be profoundly driven by the evolutionary mechanism of domain shuffling. As evidenced by our conserved motif analysis, while the core bZIP domain (comprising Motifs 1 and 2) remains strictly conserved across the family to ensure fundamental DNA-binding and dimerization capabilities, the structural architecture outside this central region exhibits remarkable variation. Distinct subfamilies have acquired specific combinations of auxiliary motifs (such as Motifs 3 through 10). This dynamic gain, loss, and rearrangement of functional modules are classical hallmarks of domain shuffling. By continuously recombining these auxiliary domains during evolution, ancestral bZIP genes were able to generate novel protein–protein interaction interfaces and regulatory networks. Such structural plasticity likely facilitated the functional divergence of RnbZIP proteins, ultimately enabling the rodent lineage to adapt to highly specialized and complex physiological requirements, particularly during brain development.

In this research, we analyzed the transcription patterns of the 61 RnbZIP genes, which displayed significant differences in the phylogenetic tree, and assessed their expression across various organs and developmental stages of the brain (Fig. 6). Almost all RnbZIP genes exhibited significant differential expression. As a model organism, the rat plays an indispensable role in scientific research, advancing biomedical fields and enhancing our understanding of complex biological processes [39, 40]. However, a comprehensive understanding of the regulatory mechanisms of genes associated with brain development remains insufficient. Sequencing results indicated that these ten RnbZIP genes exhibit distinct expression patterns in different tissues, reflecting their functional diversity. The pronounced expression of certain genes at specific stages suggests their significant roles in rat brain development, whereas low expression of other genes may indicate functional limitations. Further research into the expression and functions of these genes will elucidate their potential roles in the physiological adaptations of rats. The expression profiles of these ten RnbZIP genes at various developmental stages in the rat brain illustrate their diversity in growth and physiological processes. The notable expression of some genes at specific stages may indicate their importance in rat brain development or function, and further studies could clarify these genes’ specific biological functions and their dynamic regulation during different developmental stages.

Conclusion

We performed a comprehensive genome-wide analysis of the bZIP gene family in R. norvegicus. A total of 61 RnbZIP genes were identified and classified into 10 subfamilies. Tandem and segmental duplications drove the family expansion, and all duplicated genes were under strong purifying selection. Cross-rodent comparative analysis revealed high evolutionary conservation of the bZIP family. Tissue-specific and stage-dependent expression patterns indicated that key RnbZIP genes participate in brain development. This study provides a fundamental evolutionary and expression framework for future functional studies of bZIP transcription factors in mammals. However, further experimental validations, including transcriptional activity assays, downstream target gene identification, and in vivo functional studies, are still required to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms of RnbZIP genes in brain development.

Materials and methods

Gene identification

The latest high-quality reference genome of the rat (GRCr8) was obtained from the NCBI GenBank database, with accession number GCA_036323735.1 [27]. Initially, we employed the BLASTp program to search for all bZIP proteins within the rat genome, identifying a total of 61 RnbZIP candidates. The candidate genes were queried using BLASTP, with a score threshold set to ≥ 100 and an e-value of ≤ e − 10 [41]. Subsequently, the Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profile of the bZIP domain (PF00170) was directly downloaded from Pfam 35.0 (not via InterPro), and HMMER 3.0 was used for screening with an E-value cutoff of 0.01. Conserved domains were verified using Pfam and SMART [42], we detected conserved motifs within the rat bZIP proteins. Ultimately, we confirmed a total of 61 RnbZIP proteins (To facilitate intra-family structural and evolutionary comparisons, the identified genes were sequentially named RnbZIP01 to RnbZIP61 based on their chromosomal coordinates. The corresponding official gene symbols are detailed in Table S1 to avoid any nomenclature confusion). These proteins were then used as initial queries for validation in the NCBI protein database. Finally, we utilized the ExPasy online tool to delineate the basic characteristics of the bZIP genes in the rat, including sequence length, protein molecular weight, isoelectric point, and subcellular localization.

bZIP gene structure and conserved motif analysis

Using the default parameters of the ClusterW program, we performed multiple sequence alignments on 61 rat bZIP proteins to investigate their characteristic features further. MEGA 7.0 and GeneDoc 2.7 were used to manually refine conserved domains in the amino acid sequences of these bZIP proteins [43]. The GSDS online tool was used to analyze the exon–intron structure of these RnbZIP genes [44]. Subsequently, the MEME online program (https://meme-suite.org/meme/doc/mast.html) was employed to examine the conserved motifs and structural differences among these bZIP proteins [45]. The optimization parameters for the conserved motifs included a maximum of 10 motifs, with an optimal width of residues ranging from 6 to 200.

Chromosome distribution and gene duplication

Initially, based on the physical locations of these genes in the annotation files, all RnbZIP genes were assigned specific chromosome details. Chromosome location information for the RnbZIP genes was analyzed using Circos software. Gene duplication events were assessed with the Multiple Collinearity Scan toolkit (MCScanX) using default parameters [46]. A comparative analysis of the bZIP gene homologs among rats and six other rodent species (Dipodomys ordii, Ictidomys tridecemlineatus, Meriones unguiculatus, Sciurus vulgaris, Urocitellus parryii, and Marmota marmota) was conducted using the dual homology diagramming feature of TBtools software (v1.120) [47]. Duplicated gene pairs were defined as tandem (within 200 kb) or segmental duplications.

Phylogenetic analysis and classification of the RnbZIP family

Six rodent species were selected based on close phylogenetic proximity to R. norvegicus and high-quality genome availability. Full-length bZIP protein sequences were aligned using ClustalW. Full-length protein sequences were aligned using ClustalW. A Maximum Likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA-X. The reliability of the tree topology was evaluated by 1,000 bootstrap replicates. The Jones-Taylor-Thornton with gamma-distributed rates (JTT + G) model was selected as the best-fit amino acid substitution model based on the Bayesian Information Criterion implemented in MEGA-X. This model was selected for its robustness and wide application in conserved transcription factor family analysis. Only bootstrap values ≥ 70% were displayed. Multi-species phylogenetic trees were generated using Geneious R11 [48]. Additionally, this study developed a phylogenetic tree encompassing multiple species that includes bZIP protein sequences from rats and six rodent species (Dipodomys ordii, Ictidomys tridecemlineatus, Meriones unguiculatus, Sciurus vulgaris, Urocitellus parryii, and Marmota marmota), with all sequences obtained from the UniProt database.

Rat strains, cultivation conditions and qRT-PCR analysis

In this study, Healthy Male Wistar rats were obtained from Hefei Qingyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. For the developmental expression profiling, brain tissues were collected at postnatal days (1 day to 6 months). For tissue-specific profiling, 8-week-old adult rats were used. All animals were housed in a temperature-controlled environment with a 12-h light/dark cycle, then collected to extract total RNA for quantitative analysis. Primers for all genes were designed using Primer 5.0 software (Table S8). GAPDH was used as an internal control, experiments were conducted in triplicate. The quantitative primers for all genes were validated through melt curve analysis. The expression levels of these bZIP genes were analyzed using the 2−(ΔΔCt) method [49]. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui University of Chinese Medicine (Approval No. AZYFY20241011). Following the spirit of the "Guidelines for the Ethical Treatment of Experimental Animals" and the "AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals," all animals were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of pentobarbital sodium (100 mg/kg) at the end of the experiment.

Statistical analysis

One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test with a significance level established at 0.05. Histograms were created using Origin 2016 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, Massachusetts, USA).
