TIDieR-WASH: A Guideline for Reporting Implementation of Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene Interventions
Authors: Jonny Crocker, Emily Ogutu, Jedidiah S Snyder, Antoinette Kome, Ben Tidwell, Jan Willem Rosenboom, Jesse Shapiro, Jessy Samuyachi Mahongo, Kelly T Alexander, Michael E Gnilo, Om Prasad Gautam, Sara Hoffman, Sovattha Neou, Matthew C Freeman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction:Accurate information on context and implementation of public health interventions is necessary to replicate, adapt, and scale effective interventions and to interpret evaluations. Reporting the context and implementation of water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) interventions has been inconsistent and incomplete.Methods:To improve the reporting of WASH interventions, we developed the Template for Intervention Description and Replication for WASH (TIDieR-WASH) checklist and guide, by adapting and expanding the original TIDieR guide. We performed a scoping review of existing implementation reporting guidelines, and of current implementation reporting in WASH evaluations, to develop a list of candidate items to include in this checklist. We then used a 4-stages of review by international experts to reach consensus.Results:The resulting 14-item checklist comprises: a) name, b) theory of change, c) prior evidence, d) location and setting, e) context, f) suitability, g) implementers, h) recipients, i) targeting, j) activities, k) intervention dose, l) fidelity, m) costs, and n) materials. Definitions, explanations, examples, and instructions for use are reported in this article.Discussion:The checklist can be applied to all types of evaluations of WASH interventions, including direct service provision, behavior change, and systems strengthening interventions. We deliberately developed this guidance document to be accessible and relevant to practitioners and researchers. Adoption of this checklist will support transparent reporting of WASH implementation for research and practice. As with other sectors that have used TIDieR checklists, clear implementation reporting can assist with evidence synthesis, adaptation, and policy guidance. https://doi.org/10.1289/EHP14780

Background

Evaluation is the application of systematic methods to quantify and explore the link between a program or intervention and its intended outcomes.1 The results of evaluations can confirm and strengthen the evidence of intervention efficacy or effectiveness and can reveal heterogeneity of outcomes. Evaluations, including program evaluations and research studies, are one of the primary sources of evidence used to influence policy, practice, and funding decisions for foundations, nongovernmental organizations, governments, and multilateral agencies. High-profile impact evaluations can dramatically impact the funding and program landscape.2 Internal validity, external validity, and quality of reporting determine the value of evaluations. With inconsistent reporting of context and implementation, evaluations are not able to provide sufficient guidance for stakeholders to replicate, adapt, and scale successful interventions, and evidence synthesis across heterogeneous contexts and programs is a considerable challenge.

Variation in context and implementation drives heterogeneity in effects of water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) interventions, and reporting context and implementation is critical for evidence synthesis and effective adaptation.3,4 WASH interventions focus on increasing access to and use of safe WASH services.5,6 External validity—the applicability of evaluation results to a new population—is difficult to assess and infrequently addressed in the authors’ experience. Reporting the implementation outcomes of WASH interventions in particular presents several challenges. WASH interventions are complex and multifaceted, which has led to challenges in adaptation and scale.7 Measuring implementation fidelity of multicomponent WASH interventions is complex but critical to understanding what was done (e.g., activities or inputs), what was delivered (outputs), and effectiveness (outcomes and impacts).8 Contextual considerations required for interventions must be reported alongside implementation outcomes to allow accurate interpretation of findings and ensure programmatic and policy relevance, because context can drive variation in outcomes between studies.9–11

We believe that accurate and consistent reporting of context and implementation would facilitate comparisons of interventions’ effectiveness and impact, and enable cost–benefit analyses of public health investment strategies. Failure to report these details can lead to an inaccurate conclusion that an intervention is ineffective, when it may have been inadequately implemented or unsuitable for a given context (known as Type III error—e.g., a school handwashing intervention where soap was never delivered).12 In addition, adaptation and scale-up of promising interventions are impossible without sufficient implementation details, such as implementer qualifications, and intervention activities and dose (the quantity of an intervention delivered and amount of participation).

Despite extensive literature on the importance and application of process evaluation and implementation science,8,13,14 robust reporting of implementation details remains inconsistent in WASH and other environmental health interventions. As part of formative research, we found that even the most-cited WASH evaluations insufficiently reported the relevant implementation details needed to replicate, adapt, or scale up the evaluated interventions—as few as 5% reported all the implementation details required.15 This inadequate reporting presents a critical evidence gap because it limits accurate interpretation of evaluation findings and the subsequent use of those findings for evidence-based decision-making.

The field of Implementation Science has emerged over the past dozen years with a suite of methods to develop standard language, definitions, and guidance on implementation processes and evaluation methods, among other things.16 Many health research reporting guidelines have been developed and widely adopted, aided in part by the EQUATOR Network repository for these guidelines (www.equator-network.org, accessed February 2022). For example, the CONSORT checklist for reporting clinical trials has been endorsed by 500+ journals and has demonstrated impact on the quality of reporting in peer-reviewed articles.17 Researchers with a focus primarily on clinical practice have called for standardization of reporting implementation strategies.18 The original Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) checklist—designed to improve the completeness of reporting and the replicability of health care interventions within clinical trials—is also mandated by many journals.14 However, we lack guidance on how to report WASH and other environmental health interventions. Developing WASH-specific TIDieR guidance would facilitate transparent and consistent reporting of WASH interventions and could facilitate developing guidance for reporting implementation of environmental health interventions at large.

We developed an easily adoptable checklist approach to report context and implementation of WASH interventions that can be used by researchers, policymakers, and practitioners to support comparisons across studies, programs, and contexts. Our goal is to provide clear and consistent guidance that improves how WASH intervention context and implementation is measured and reported by those conducting WASH evaluations. Ideally, this checklist should be consulted before an intervention begins, be it part of a research study, project, or program to inform measurement and monitoring and evaluation. At a minimum, the checklist should be used during the write-up stage to ensure that all the recommended items are clearly reported and document the location of that information.

Methods for Developing the Checklist

We developed the checklist using an iterative process comprising a) a review of existing reporting checklists; b) a scoping literature review; and c) four stages of expert review and consensus.

To develop an initial list of reporting items for the checklist, we conducted an exploratory search to identify best practice guidelines for reporting details of interventions, projects, and implementation strategies in the fields of medicine and public health. We searched Google Scholar for articles that contained [(reporting OR checklist) AND (guidance OR guideline) AND (implementation OR intervention)]. We also reviewed the full list of 554 reporting guidelines stored on the EQUATOR Network, which compiles health research reporting guidelines (www.equator-network.org, accessed February 2022). We initially identified 14 distinct reporting items recommended by at least one of the four relevant reporting guidelines we identified, which included: a) the “Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) Checklist and Guide”14; b) the “Specifying and Reporting Complex Behaviour Change Interventions: the Need for a Scientific Method” editorial19; c) the “Action, Actor, Context, Target, Time (AACTT)” framework for specifying behavior20; and d) “Implementation Strategies: Recommendations for Specifying and Reporting.”13

To refine this initial list, we conducted a scoping review to assess and categorize the level of reporting of context and implementation in WASH evaluations. The scoping review, which was conducted as a step in the development of this checklist, has been published elsewhere.15 We reviewed the 40 most-cited evaluations of WASH interventions published in the last 10 y (2012–2022) identified by searching Web of Science (www.webofscience.com) using the following search terms: [(water OR sanitation OR hygiene OR handwashing OR hand-washing OR hand hygiene OR WASH) AND (intervention OR implementation OR project OR program OR case study OR trial OR “evaluat*” OR “sustain*” OR “assess*” OR “impact*” OR “outcome*” OR protocol”)]. We then screened articles for eligibility and extracted data on context and implementation reporting. We tested the preliminary list of 14 reporting items on a subset of the articles that met our systematic search criteria and expanded the list to better represent the complexity of WASH interventions. We expanded the list by identifying details of context and implementation that were reported in the articles included in our scoping review that were not captured by the preliminary list of 14 items, then creating a new item to capture that detail. Using this process, we expanded the 14 items to a total of 25 items during the scoping review and used the 25-item list for the first stage of our expert review. A mapping of how these lists correspond to each other is in Table S1.

Our expert review process had four rounds of feedback and consensus.

In the first round, we presented the list of 25 items at an academic conference (to ∼35 session attendees representing all major geographic regions of the world) to solicit feedback on the clarity and comprehensiveness of the items (i.e., whether they covered all relevant context and implementation items for WASH interventions). The main feedback was on the perceived value of the checklist and a concern that a reporting guideline did not already exist specifically for WASH.

This established method has been previously used for development of related guidelines.14,22,23 After each round of interaction with experts, the core team met to review feedback and revise the checklist and guideline for presentation and feedback at the subsequent expert review.

The checklist can be considered a table of contents for the intervention, pointing to the location where additional detail can be found, because the information recommended in the checklist will (in most cases) be located in journal articles, project reports, implementation protocols and plans, and other gray-literature resources. As such, the information does not need to be reported within the checklist template itself, unless it is not located elsewhere. Instead, the checklist template should be used to direct readers to where the information is located. All information should be publicly available, and URLs should be provided for online sources. For information that is located behind a paywall, that information should be replicated in an online supplement or other free-to-access location if permitted. See Table S2 for an example of how the checklist should be completed. The checklist should be used even if not all items can be full reported (“Not available” can be entered in these cases). We recommend that the checklist be consulted prior to starting an intervention so that monitoring tools can be designed to capture the required information. Although this checklist was designed to be easily adoptable, organizations may find that new skills and data collection tools are needed to properly document and report all the items recommended in this guidance.

For each of the 14 items in the checklist, we have provided additional details of what should be reported, the justification for including that item in the checklist, and multiple examples for each item in the Supplemental Material under “Reporting Examples,” some of which are reproduced from empirical studies.24–27 These examples are illustrative of the type of information that should be reported but are not necessarily sufficient reporting for a full program. Review of these examples is essential for those applying this checklist to their own evaluation. Not all checklist items require an equal level of detail—activities generally require the most text to adequately report, whereas intervention name requires one sentence.

A ToC describes how the intervention is expected to lead to the target outcomes. It shows the steps through which intervention components or activities translate into outcomes using if/then causal relationships that are evidence-based or hypothesized (e.g., provision of handwashing facilities and soap will increase pupil hand hygiene following toileting) and identifies mediating and moderating factors that affect outcomes.29 Related items that can be reported in place of a ToC if they meet the same criteria are impact pathway, causal pathway, logic model, conceptual framework, mechanism, or theory.

Each intervention component should be included in the ToC to show how it contributes directly or indirectly to target outcomes. The ToC should include mediators, moderators, target outcomes, and their definitions. “Mediators” (or intermediate outcomes) are the immediate changes or results that connect the intervention to the target outcomes. Mediators are considered necessary to achieve target outcomes, and listing and defining them can aid interpretation of effectiveness results.30 “Moderators” (or external factors) modify or influence causal relationships between intervention components and target outcomes and are often considered part of the context.30 “Target outcomes” communicate what the program was designed to impact. Reporting specific target outcomes is important for identifying the primary objective of the intervention, particularly when there is no impact evaluation. Reporting target outcomes can aid in identifying potential interventions to consider to address a gap or problem.

Reporting the ToC, as well as measuring and reporting the mediators and moderators, can be useful for diagnosing what went wrong in instances where there was no impact by identifying which pathways were not successfully achieved. Specifically, a ToC can be used to identify if there was intervention failure through process measures (i.e., an intervention component was not completed per protocol) or theory failure (i.e., the results of the activities and outputs did not result in the anticipated change).7 Reporting a ToC is particularly important for WASH, in which there are many potential mediators (e.g., individual behaviors) and moderators (e.g., pathogen exposure via other routes) that can interrupt the causal pathway.2 Organizational and theoretical frameworks are rarely applied to design and adapt WASH interventions, but relevant frameworks that could improve WASH intervention design do exist.7 The F-diagram, which describes the contamination pathways, behavior change interventions frameworks like the Capability, Opportunity, and Motivation Behavior (COM-B) model; the Integrated Behavioral Model for Water, Sanitation and Hygiene (IBM-WASH); and others have supported the understanding of behavior and habit formation, among others.31–34

Contextual factors are important drivers of implementation outcomes and intervention effectiveness,2,7,18,25,36 and are thus critical to report for accurate interpretation of results. They also help others decide whether a program can be effectively replicated in a different context, or whether it should be modified and adapted.

Context is often measured as part of a preimplementation needs assessment, situational assessment, or baseline assessment. Measurement and reporting of social context factors should explicitly address gender and social inclusion. For example, gender norms, roles, and power dynamics at a household, community, and institutional level that may affect WASH implementation and outcomes; tribal or caste dynamics that may influence the intervention; or any specific morays associated with target groups (e.g., widows, persons with disabilities) should be reported.

Reporting of governmental and institutional context factors should include some discussion and reflection on how these factors enable and hamper implementation, scale-up, and sustainability of the WASH intervention being reported. This set of factors is often referred to as the “enabling environment,” which has been defined as the set of interrelated conditions that impact the capacity of development actors in a sustained and effective manner and as the policy, institutional, and financial framework that is necessary for sustaining, scaling up, and replicating programs.35,37 Useful context domains for complex public health interventions have also been proposed.38 Describing potential and anticipated risks to successful implementation can also be part of describing the enabling environment. Enabling environment varies with the kind of intervention to be implemented. For instance, conditions for implementing a community-led total sanitation (CLTS) intervention may differ from conditions for implementing other sanitation interventions. Enabling environment can vary across locations for a single intervention.

Implementation capacity is a recognized priority in the WASH sector and is critical for effective implementation.24,39,40 Implementation often requires community and stakeholders engagement to ensure maintenance and sustainability of intervention outcomes. Diversity of service providers may create more opportunity for intervention failures where interventions are not delivered as intended and decisions not based on evidence.7 Therefore, identification of different actors that implement each intervention component is critical. Implementation of WASH interventions often involves contributions by local actors and volunteers (e.g., community health workers, teachers, community leaders, mothers), so these actor types and their roles should be specified. Women in particular are often targeted to carry out community- and household-level tasks such as hygiene promotion and water treatment.3,41,42 When women or men are specifically targeted to carry out intervention activities, this should be specified. In addition, during intervention design, gender mainstreaming can be incorporated to address gender inequality in implementation roles and intervention outcomes.43

Intervention recipients are also referred to as the population reached, beneficiaries, and the target population. Where the size and characteristics of the population reached differ from the population targeted, these differences should be reported. In addition, where different intervention components are targeted to different subpopulations (such as education related to menstrual health being targeted to school-age girls), the characteristics of each subpopulation and the intervention components they received should be reported. Providing information about the recipients and outlining their categories is necessary for targeting interventions. A clear description of the recipients is critical in assessing whether interventions were delivered to intended persons/communities and whether there were any inequities. Providing specific numbers of recipients can guide other implementers when planning to deliver specific interventions. If an intervention is successfully implemented among specific numbers of recipients with cost implications, this information can inform the cost analysis for future interventions. Recipient information is generally provided as demographic characteristics in a table, which should include a gender breakdown.

Targeting refers to any tailoring of the intervention to specific groups or subpopulations. In WASH, vulnerable and underserved populations are often targeted to receive subsidies, tiered pricing structures for services, or tailored behavior change messaging.44 Access to WASH services is often inequitable; underserved populations often include those living in poverty, people living with disabilities, ethnic and cultural minorities, women, children, and the elderly.39,45–48 Several distinct populations may be targeted for direct action (e.g., creating a policy or enacting a program), behavior change (e.g., the key outcomes or mediators of interest), or to produce the resulting outcome or impact (e.g., health change). Reporting these distinct populations and the respective components of an intervention delivered separately or differently to subgroups allows those considering adapting or replicating an intervention to understand how to reach various segments of the target population. Targeting can also help with controlling spillovers, especially with trials.

Activities and their details are the most critical component of implementation to report for others to be able to accurately interpret results, replicate, or adapt interventions. Details of activities can help the implementers make decisions on the replicating activities in other settings. Activity details can be used to measure intervention fidelity by comparing them to implementation plans. Activity details can help in identifying gaps with implementation and therefore with addressing those gaps.

Item 11. Intervention dose: Quantify the frequency and number of contacts between implementers and recipients, and the duration of those contacts.

For each activity that involves interactions between intervention implementers and recipients, the following should be reported:

dose quantity (frequency or number of interactions), and

dose quantity (frequency or number of interactions), and

dose duration (how long interactions with training or intervention recipients lasted).

dose duration (how long interactions with training or intervention recipients lasted).

Dose can have many other dimensions beyond these minimum recommended components (quantity and duration) that merit reporting when relevant, such as intervention quality and level of engagement.49,50 Accurate reporting of dose may require prospective tracking of implementation activities, particularly for behavior change interventions, as recall-based interviews may not accurately capture the quantity and duration of interactions with recipients. Dose is a determinant of intervention resource requirements (human and financial) and of effectiveness. Thus, it is critical for interpreting outcomes, intervention adaptation and scale-up, and cost analysis. Although dose will generally be reported together with activities, it merits a separate item because of its importance and because it is underreported for WASH interventions. Dose can also convey time required by recipients, and it should be reported by subgroup (e.g., gender) when it differs between subgroups.

Item 12. Fidelity: Report fidelity monitoring and actual fidelity. Include any planned or unplanned modifications to the intervention.

Reporting of fidelity should cover the following:

how fidelity was monitored and by who (i.e., oversight, implementation monitoring, quality control, or other efforts to ensure implementation proceeded as planned), and

how fidelity was monitored and by who (i.e., oversight, implementation monitoring, quality control, or other efforts to ensure implementation proceeded as planned), and

actual or measured fidelity to the intervention protocol or plan, including any planned or unplanned modifications to the intervention.

actual or measured fidelity to the intervention protocol or plan, including any planned or unplanned modifications to the intervention.

Monitoring of fidelity can be tracking of activities, modifications, or adaptations. It can also be efforts to ensure implementation goes according to plan, such as quality control and implementation oversight mechanisms, and should occur with any implementation with or without an impact evaluation. For reporting of measured or actual fidelity, the following details should be reported: what the modifications were, when and why they occurred, and what portion of the target population they applied to. Reporting of actual fidelity often refers to the intervention plan or protocol to highlight differences between plans and reality.

Fidelity refers to the extent to which an intervention is implemented as planned, including the timing of implementation.51 The terms used to describe this concept vary among disciplines and include treatment integrity and implementation fidelity. Fidelity should not be confused with adherence, which for WASH refers to intervention recipients’ behaviors and would generally be considered an outcome. Fidelity is critical in assessing the effectiveness of an intervention.52 It shows the extent to which the intervention delivered matches the intervention plan. If all components of an intervention are implemented, it is easier to link the intervention to the outcomes and to understand the existing variations with interventions as planned and as implemented, the feasibility of intervention implementation, or even an intervention’s effectiveness.52–54

Reporting of costs should follow a payer (or program) perspective in most cases. A payer perspective reports the costs that would be required to replicate the implementation of the intervention. The program funder should also be named. For economic evaluations, societal costs should be reported. Societal costs include the program costs and costs (financial and economic) incurred by organizations and individuals outside the main implementing organizations. These are particularly relevant for WASH interventions, in which local government, volunteers (teachers, community health workers), and community members often contribute time and resources.55

Payer-perspective costing is generally straightforward because most WASH interventions are funded by one organization or a few organizations, sometimes with government cost-sharing. Accurately costing WASH interventions (particularly with a societal perspective) can be challenging because of the complex institutional arrangements, cross-subsidies, flexible implementation, and local investments by volunteers and community members (financial or non).55 Activity-based costing and microcosting methods will provide more accurate estimates of costs due to these factors (as in comparison with top-down costing or gross costing). Although no comprehensive guides for costing WASH programs exist, there are relevant resources for costing nutrition and health programs56,57 and for reporting them.58

Cost data are used for budgeting future programs, assessing the scale of a program, assessing the intensity of the program (when costs are compared to the number of recipients) and are used for analyzing additional economic outcomes, such as cost efficiency, cost effectiveness, and cost–benefit ratios.

Reporting of materials should include details about hardware, including how hardware was constructed and installed. Even for predominantly demand-side interventions, there may be details of the available hardware through local markets that are worth reporting, even if not directly provided by the intervention. This detail can help the implementers in planning, costing, and decision-making.

Many WASH interventions involve software components. This material could include manuals, posters, incentive mechanisms, guidelines, policy documents, technology standards, or protocols that were used to guide implementation. When describing software, it is important to include copies of the materials or links to open-access, publicly available sources. This information can be placed as supplemental materials or added in an appendix to facilitate access. Sharing this information can reduce the instances of duplication of efforts and increase the possibilities of replication and therefore avoid misuse of resources. This information can be relevant for assessing fidelity as well.

Discussion

Who Should Use TIDieR-WASH?

We recommend that the TIDieR-WASH checklist and guidance be used by anyone reporting a WASH intervention, including researchers, policymakers, and practitioners. The types of interventions could include those related to behavior change; provision of WASH services, infrastructure development, capacity building, systems strengthening; and interventions across different scales of interventions (e.g., pilots, community-based, district- and national-level programs) and different settings (e.g., household, community, school, health care facilities). The TIDieR-WASH guidance is most applicable to low- and middle-income settings, where the majority of inadequate access to WASH and thus the majority of WASH interventions occur.59

This checklist was jointly developed by experts from academic institutions, implementing organizations, policy organizations, and foundations to maximize relevance to audiences working for these different types of institutions. It is relevant for reporting on the delivery of an intervention, including any evaluation that is disseminated publicly in any format or outlet (peer-reviewed journals, project reports, organizational websites), and is also relevant for internal monitoring, evaluation, and learning efforts by WASH organizations (often referred to as “M&E” or “MEL”).

Those reporting environmental health interventions other than WASH (e.g., clean cooking, food hygiene, bed nets, waste management) may also find this checklist useful. This version of the TIDieR checklist contains items and language well suited to WASH and environmental health interventions that are context-dependent and often involve externalities that affect intervention delivery and uptake.

Rationale for TIDieR-WASH

Our scoping review found that underreporting of implementation and context in WASH evaluations is a problem that exists even among the most-cited studies of the last 10 y.15 There is extensive evidence from the medical field that poor reporting of intervention details creates wasteful research that cannot be accurately interpreted14,60 and that reporting guidelines improve the completeness of reporting of clinical trials.61

The original TIDieR checklist published in 2014 has been cited more than 4,000 times according to PubMed (https://www.pmid2cite.com/) and more than 7,000 times according to Google Scholar (https://scholar.google.com/), demonstrating the scale of its impact. The widespread uptake of the TIDieR checklist has enabled systematic reviews that assess outcomes by intervention type or characteristic, such as finding that delivery mechanism (group vs. individual) and dose (number of sessions) may improve health outcomes for workplace lifestyle interventions62 and that postpartum weight management interventions delivered by health professionals yielded better outcomes than those delivered by nonhealth professionals.63 Although there are no systematic reviews of the original TIDieR checklist that investigate its impact on reporting quality (perhaps because it is only 10 y old), the CONSORT checklist first published in 1996 has had a demonstrated impact on the quality of reporting of randomized controlled trials,17 indicating that reporting guidelines can be beneficial.

The original TIDieR checklist is designed for reporting of medical trials. There exist TIDieR extensions for population health and vaccine trials23,64 as well as nontrials.65 However, for all these existing guidelines, the checklist items, terminology, and examples were not developed for WASH or other environmental health interventions. Thus, we set out to develop a context and implementation reporting checklist and guideline optimized for WASH interventions.

Although TIDieR-WASH is a reporting guideline, there are potential benefits to using this checklist to inform intervention design. Mainstreaming this checklist as a requirement for journal publication and as a funding requirement would support better adaptation and scale-up of WASH interventions. Certain checklist items (ToC, prior evidence, context, enabling environment, suitability, and targeting) all relate to how the intervention was designed and how it is appropriate for the context in which it was implemented. Considering these items during intervention design could ensure that the intervention is appropriately designed to yield target outcomes in a given context. Consideration of implementer roles, qualifications, and membership in vulnerable groups may also aid in addressing inequities and empowering vulnerable groups. Gender mainstreaming offers one such approach.43 Based on our experience and on feedback received at the 2022 Water & Health Conference,66 this checklist is ideally used prospectively to inform process monitoring. Implementation tracking, in which basic details of activities are recorded daily, can be used to collect data on recipients, activities, dose, fidelity, and costs in sufficient detail. Future extensions of this checklist could include definitions and examples specific to other types of environmental health interventions.

WASH Interventions, Gender, and Social Inclusion

The implementation of WASH interventions is affected by gender roles and norms and often relies on women for successful implementation. A recent systematic review of 133 WASH interventions found that 37% were gender-unequal and 63% were gender-blind (both of which should be avoided according to the WHO).43,48 None were classified as gender-sensitive, specific, or transformative, which are considered desirable. We recommend that gender influence on the intervention being reported, and gender-specific roles and burden be reported under context, implementers, recipients, targeting, and activities, ideally using the WHO Gender Responsiveness Assessment Scale.43 In addition, structural racism and colonialism impact global health67,68 and warrant deliberate consideration. Involving local communities in the design and implementation of interventions can be one method for doing so.67 The O’Neill–Lancet Commission on Racism, Structural Discrimination, and Global Health offers further resources on these important issues.68

Conclusion

A lack of reporting standards for WASH interventions has resulted in critical challenges in adapting, adopting, and scaling interventions. It has also limited the ability of researchers and practitioners to synthesize evidence across programs to understand a fundamental question of which interventions work, where, and under what conditions. Our TIDieR-WASH checklist, if widely adapted by the WASH sector, would improve knowledge across the sector by creating normative methods for reporting intervention design and implementation outcomes.
