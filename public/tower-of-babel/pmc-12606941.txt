Machine learning for screening and predicting the risk of developing uveitis in juvenile idiopathic arthritis
Authors: Li Li, Junmei Zhang, Jianghong Deng, Weiying Kuang, Xiaohua Tan, Chao Li, Shipeng Li, Caifeng Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveEstablishing a predictive model using clinical indicators for the early identification of JIA-U.MethodA cross-sectional study was conducted with 255 patients admitted at Beijing Children's Hospital between 2018 and 2023. The model was fitted using stepwise logistic regression as well as least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. Calibration and decision curve analysis were used for validation.ResultsThe final predictive model included four clinical variables (patient's gender, age at onset, arthritis subtype, and ANA status). A nomogram for risk prediction was developed, which demonstrated good discrimination in both the training cohort (AUC = 0.8417; 95% CI = 0.775-0.9085) and the testing cohort (AUC = 0.782; 95% CI = 0.6752-0.8884). Calibration curves showed that, through bootstrap resampling, the nomogram performed well in predicting the occurrence of uveitis in JIA.ConclusionThis study established a predictive model using routine clinical indicators to assess the risk of uveitis in JIA patients.Keywords: Juvenile idiopathic arthritis, Uveitis, Machine learning, Risk factors

Introduction

Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) is the most common pediatric rheumatic disease, defined by the onset of arthritis before the age of 16, lasting for 6 weeks or more, involving either a single joint or multiple joints, and excluding other known causes [1]. Chronic synovitis is the main symptom of JIA, and certain subtypes may be linked to dysfunction in multiple organs, such as the respiratory, digestive, neurological, and ophthalmic systems [2]. Among its various extra-articular manifestations, uveitis is one of the most serious complications [3]. JIA-associated uveitis (JIA-U) includes both acute and chronic forms. Epidemiological data indicate that approximately 21–25% of patients with JIA develop uveitis, with the incidence rising to as high as 30% among those who are ANA-positive [4]. Chronic uveitis is more commonly observed in ANA-positive patients and is typically asymptomatic, insidious in onset, prolonged in course, and prone to recurrence. Due to the young age of onset and limited ability of patients to articulate visual symptoms, delayed diagnosis is common [5]. A smaller subset of patients presents with acute anterior uveitis, characterized by sudden-onset unilateral eye pain, redness, photophobia, and visual changes, and is more frequently associated with enthesitis-related arthritis and HLA-B27 positivity [6]. Long-term follow-up studies have shown that nearly 20%−40% of affected patients develop at least one complication, such as band keratopathy, cataract, posterior synechiae, glaucoma, or cystoid macular edema, all of which may result in severe and irreversible visual impairment [7]. This study aims to develop a JIA with uveitis (JIA-U) predictive model using routine clinical variables through LASSO regression analysis, for clinical evaluation in JIA patients.

Method

The study involved 255 individuals who were diagnosed with juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) at Beijing Children’s Hospital between 2016 and 2023. Of these, 182 individuals had JIA without uveitis and 73 patients had JIA-U. The patients were divided into a training set (178 cases) and a testing set (77 cases) at a ratio of 7:3. Inclusion criteria were: [1] diagnosis of JIA based on the 2001 International League of Associations for Rheumatology (ILAR) classification criteria; and [2] completion of ophthalmologic screening. Exclusion criteria were: [1] uveitis attributed to infections such as viral, bacterial, parasitic, or mycoplasma etiologies; [2] ocular inflammation secondary to metabolic disorders; and [3] presence of other rheumatic diseases associated with uveitis. The time of patient enrollment was defined as the date when both JIA and uveitis were diagnosed, and relevant clinical data at diagnosis were collected through the electronic medical record system. General information included age, gender, and family history of rheumatic disease; clinical data included arthritis subtype, joint symptoms, and ocular symptoms; laboratory indicators included white blood cell count(WBC), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein(CRP), antinuclear antibody (ANA, A positive result was defined as a titer ≥ 1:80 by indirect immunofluorescence assay), rheumatoid factor (RF), anti-CCP antibodies, human leukocyte antigen B27 (HLA-B27), and joint ultrasound and MRI; ophthalmological screening indicators (included visual acuity, intraocular pressure, slit-lamp examination, and fundus examination); and treatment medications. The studies involving human participants were reviewed and approved by the medical ethics committee of Beijing Children’s Hospital, Capital Medical University (IEC-C-006-A04-V.07.02) on June 6, 2025. Ethics approval and informed patient consent from patients and their guardians had been obtained and collected.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are represented by means, medians, or interquartile ranges. For normally distributed continuous variables, a two-sample t-test is used for comparison; otherwise, the Mann-Whitney U test is applied. Categorical variables are analyzed using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. Factors associated with the occurrence of uveitis in JIA patients are determined through LASSO regression. The variables selected from the LASSO regression model are then used to construct a nomogram prediction model using the R (version 4.1) software package. The model’s discriminative ability is evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and bias calibration is performed through 1000 bootstrap resampling. A calibration curve is drawn using the Hosmer-Leme show test to assess the consistency between the nomogram model and the actual model. Decision curve analysis (DCA) is used to evaluate the clinical utility of the constructed prediction model. A p-value of < 0.05 is considered statistically significant.

Result

Characteristics of the included children with JIA

A total of 255 JIA patients were included in this study, among which 73 patients had a history of uveitis (Table 1). Compared to the two groups, there were statistically significant differences in the age of arthritis onset, arthritis subtype, hemoglobin levels, and the positive rate of ANA (p < 0.05). Compared with JIA patients without uveitis, those who developed uveitis had a younger age at arthritis onset. Oligoarticular JIA (OJIA) was the most common subtype (46.58%), followed by enthesitis-related arthritis (ERA) (27.40%). ANA positivity showed a statistically significant difference between the two groups. Additionally, the use of adalimumab was higher in JIA-U than in those without uveitis.

Model development

LASSO regression was used to select four variables from twelve candidate variables to improve model accuracy and reduce overfitting (Fig. 1). Stepwise logistic regression further confirmed that the selected variables exhibited significant differences (Table 2). Using R software, the outcome variable, the risk of uveitis in JIA, was employed to construct a nomogram prediction model (Fig. 2). Each individual factor in the nomogram corresponds to a specific score, and the sum of the scores of all risk factors gives the total score. The higher the total score, the higher the risk of developing uveitis.

Model Evaluation

The area under the ROC curve for the training set was 0.8417, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 0.775–0.9085, sensitivity of 0.635, specificity of 0.929, and Youden’s index of 0.564 (Fig. 3A). For the testing set, the area under the ROC curve was 0.782, with a 95% CI of 0.6752–0.8884, sensitivity of 0.571, specificity of 0.909, and Youden’s index of 0.48 (Fig. 3B). These data suggest that the model demonstrates good discriminative ability in predicting the occurrence of uveitis in JIA. The calibration curve, shown after Bootstrap resampling, indicates that in both the training and testing sets, the predicted probabilities of the nomogram for uveitis occurrence in JIA closely match the actual observed probabilities, with good predictive performance (Fig. 4). Decision curve analysis was used to compare the efficiency of different models; as shown in Fig. 5, the prediction model outperforms other metrics in terms of net benefit.

Discussion

Uveitis is a common extra-articular manifestation in JIA patients, with subtle clinical symptoms that make early diagnosis challenging. If not treated promptly, it may lead to severe vision impairment, affecting the patient’s quality of life and prognosis [8]. Therefore, early identification and prediction of the risk of uveitis in JIA patients are of significant clinical importance. This study conducted a retrospective analysis of clinical data from 255 JIA patients and developed a model to predict the risk of uveitis in these patients using machine learning methods. The model was internally validated, and the results indicated that it possesses good predictive ability and clinical utility.

In this study, we found that the age of onset in JIA-U was earlier than in those without uveitis (7.27 ± 3.27 years vs. 8.26 ± 4.06 years, p = 0.048). Regarding the arthritis subtypes, oligoarticular juvenile idiopathic arthritis (OJIA) accounted for 46.58% of the JIA-U group, which was significantly higher than in the non-uveitis group. Studies have shown that in patients with JIA, particularly those with OJIA, the synovial tissue is predominantly infiltrated by CD4+ T cells, with a marked accumulation of Th17 cells and a reduction or functional impairment of regulatory T cells (Tregs), resulting in uncontrolled inflammation. Immunohistochemical analysis of iris biopsy samples from patients with JIA-U has also demonstrated a predominance of CD4+ T cells within ocular tissues. In addition, a significant increase in γδ T cells has been observed in both the synovial fluid and peripheral blood of OJIA patients with uveitis; these cells can produce IL-17 and IFN-γ, which may further exacerbate intraocular inflammation. Compared to other JIA subtypes, the immune microenvironment in OJIA appears more prone to sustaining chronic ocular inflammation, which may underlie the higher incidence of uveitis in this subgroup [9, 10]. The ANA positivity rate in JIA-U patients was significantly higher than in the non-uveitis group (61.64% vs. 8.84%, p < 0.001). Histopathological and immunohistochemical analysis of ocular tissue from JIA-U revealed chronic non-granulomatous inflammation primarily composed of plasma cells, Russell bodies, and plasmacytoid lymphocytes in the iris and ciliary body. Immunohistochemistry showed an accumulation of CD20-positive B cells, along with some CD3 and CD8-positive T cell subgroups, indicating that B cell infiltration plays a crucial role in the development of the disease [11], which may lead to the production of abnormal autoantibodies against self-antigens. However, ANA positivity and titers showed no significant correlation with the activity or duration of JIA-U [12]. Additionally, inflammatory markers such as WBC count, CRP, and ESR did not show significant elevation in JIA-U patients, suggesting that the occurrence of uveitis may not completely parallel the activity of arthritis.

According to reports, the prevalence of JIA-U ranges from 4.74 to 20.5%, with regional and ethnic differences, being more common in Europe and North America, and relatively less frequent in Asia [13, 14]. Due to the low prevalence of this disease, this study employed LASSO regression analysis, which uses L1 regularization to shrink the coefficients of less important variables to zero, automatically selecting the key risk factors [15]. Ultimately, four routine clinical parameters were selected from the candidate variables: patient sex, age of onset, arthritis subtype, and ANA status. These variables also showed significant differences in stepwise logistic regression, further validating their importance in predicting the risk of uveitis. Based on these variables, we constructed a nomogram prediction model and evaluated it using ROC curves and calibration curves. Decision curve analysis (DCA) also indicated that the model provides higher net benefit in different clinical decision-making scenarios, outperforming other traditional indicators. This model has several advantages. First, this model incorporates basic clinical variables into a practical and generalizable risk stratification tool for JIA-associated uveitis. It demonstrates the ability to identify high-risk patients (particularly those with early-onset disease, oligoarticular JIA, and ANA positivity which facilitates timely referral to ophthalmology and closer monitoring, playing a critical role in preventing irreversible vision loss. Second, with the help of the nomogram, patients or their families can intuitively understand the probability of uveitis occurring in JIA, which helps improve the awareness and cooperation of patients and their families regarding the disease. Third, the model demonstrated good clinical stability and satisfactory predictive performance during internal validation, laying a reference foundation for external validation. Moreover, the observed association between clinical phenotypes and uveitis susceptibility underscores the need to further investigate immunopathological differences across JIA subtypes. Currently, ophthalmologic screening recommendations for JIA-U in China are largely adapted from guidelines developed in Western countries [16–18]. While these guidelines provide valuable reference, they are primarily based on data from Western populations, and their applicability to Asian children remains uncertain. Differences in genetic background, disease phenotypes, ANA distribution, and healthcare system structures highlight the need for region-specific screening protocols. This study provides preliminary data to support such efforts. By identifying key clinical predictors—age at onset, JIA subtype, ANA status, and sex—and validating their relevance in a Chinese pediatric cohort, we offer a potential foundation for developing tailored screening strategies based on local patient characteristics. In resource-limited settings, incorporating our nomogram into clinical practice may help optimize screening intervals and prioritize ophthalmologic referral for high-risk children. These findings support the development of personalized, evidence-based screening guidelines for Asian children with JIA and warrant further multicenter validation across diverse populations in the region. Despite the strong predictive ability demonstrated by the model constructed in this study, there are still some limitations. First, this is a single-center, retrospective study with a limited sample size, and the number of JIA-U cases is relatively small, all of which are from an Asian population. Additionally, although internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling, external validation with independent datasets from different populations is still needed to confirm the model’s generalizability. Finally, the model is primarily based on routine clinical variables and has not incorporated more biomarkers or genomic data. Future studies could explore integrating techniques like optical coherence tomography (OCT) to further improve the model’s predictive accuracy. In conclusion, we have established an internally validated JIA-U screening model using readily available clinical data in a single-center cohort. This model can inform JIA patients and their families about the risk of uveitis, which holds significant clinical implications.
