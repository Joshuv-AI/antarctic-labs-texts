Ultrasound assessment of diaphragm thickness and related determinants in healthy female adolescents: a retrospective study
Authors: Zeliha Celik, Nevin A Guzel, Halit Nahit Sendur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe diaphragm plays a crucial role in respiration and is influenced by changes in body composition. However, data on the relationship between diaphragm thickness, respiratory muscle strength, and body composition in healthy adolescents are limited. This study aimed to investigate the clinical indicators of diaphragm thickness and their relationships with respiratory muscle performance and body composition in a healthy female adolescent population.MethodsThis study included 87 healthy female adolescents (mean age 17.5 ± 2.5 years) within the 15–24 age range, aligning with the broad definition of adolescence. Pulmonary function and respiratory muscle strength (MIP, MEP) were measured using a spirometer. Diaphragm thickness was evaluated using a single ultrasound device with a linear transducer, and measurements were taken three times during breath-holding at both inspiration and expiration. No significant associations were found between diaphragm thickness and MIP, MEP, or other spirometric measures. Regression analyses demonstrated FFM was the strongest predictor of diaphragm thickness (p < 0.05).ConclusionsDiaphragm thickness in healthy female adolescents is significantly associated with FFM and anthropometric measures. These findings may indicate that diaphragm thickness could provide structural information about general muscle status during growth.Keywords: Body Composition, Diaphragm, Lung Volume Measurements, Maximal Respiratory Pressures

Background

The diaphragm is the primary muscle of respiration and has important roles beyond breathing. Connections between the diaphragm and various body systems influence posture, organ function, and even aspects of emotional regulation [1, 2]. Diaphragm structure and function can be assessed non-invasively by ultrasonography, which allows measurements of diaphragm thickness and thickening fraction (DTF) during the respiratory cycle [3]. Ultrasound assessment of the diaphragm is an inexpensive, safe, and rapid method that provides valuable information for real-time evaluation of respiratory function [4].

Recent findings indicate that diaphragm thickness is influenced by body composition [5, 6]. However, its ability to reflect respiratory muscle performance remains debated [7, 8]. While diaphragmatic excursion appears to relate more directly to inspiratory and expiratory pressures, static thickness may provide limited information on muscle strength [8]. Fat-free mass (FFM) plays a crucial role in respiratory health, and it significantly influences pulmonary function and diaphragm structure [5, 6]. Nutritional status and body composition have also been shown to affect diaphragm structure and pulmonary function in pediatric populations, and it is important to consider these factors when evaluating respiratory muscles [5]. Taken together, these findings highlight the complex interaction between anthropometry, body composition, and respiratory mechanics. On the other hand, most studies on diaphragm thickness and its relationship have primarily focused on children, adults, and elderly individuals [4, 5, 7, 9].

Adolescence is a period of rapid growth and hormonal changes, leading to significant changes in body composition and pulmonary functions [10, 11]. Given the rapid changes in anthropometry, FFM, and pulmonary functions during adolescence, understanding the structural determinants of the diaphragm may provide valuable information on respiratory health and early detection of lung problems. Data on adolescents are particularly limited regarding how diaphragm thickness relates to body composition and respiratory muscle performance, including pulmonary functions and respiratory muscle strength during this critical developmental period. In addition, substantial sex-related differences in body composition, particularly in fat-free and skeletal muscle mass, emerge during pubertal development [12]. As sex and body composition may influence diaphragm thickness, focusing on a single-sex population may reduce sex-related heterogeneity and allow the determinants of diaphragm thickness to be evaluated in a more homogeneous group [13]. On the other hand, although a positive association between FFM and diaphragm thickness may be expected, the determinants of diaphragm thickness during adolescence are still not fully understood. It is particularly unclear whether diaphragm thickness primarily reflects body composition and anthropometric characteristics or is also associated with functional measures such as respiratory muscle strength and pulmonary function. Investigating these relationships may provide further insight into the factors associated with diaphragm morphology during this critical period of growth and development. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the clinical indicators of diaphragm thickness and its associations with respiratory muscle performance and body composition in healthy female adolescents.

Methods

Study design and participants

The present retrospective study was approved (No: 2025/94) by the Amasya University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent for the assessments had been obtained from all participants at the time of the original evaluations, with parental consent obtained for participants under 18 years of age. The present study analyzed previously recorded data from a pre-existing data pool of the Department of Physiotherapy and Rehabilitation in Sports, Gazi University. Between July 2023 and July 2024, respiratory assessments and standardized diaphragm ultrasound measurements of individuals evaluated were recorded. No participants were newly recruited, and no additional assessments were performed specifically for the present study.

In our study, participants were included according to Sawyer’s broad age-range (10–24 years) definition of adolescence [14]. The inclusion criteria were: (1) female sex, (2) age between 15 and 24 years, (3) willingness to participate in the study, and (4) no active infection at the time of assessment.

The exclusion criteria were: (1) a history of neuromuscular disease, (2) scoliosis or chest wall deformity, (3) severe orthopedic problems that could affect the assessments, (4) upper or lower respiratory tract infection within the previous three months, and (5) diagnosis of any cardiopulmonary or other chronic disease.

Data collection

Pulmonary function and respiratory muscle strength were assessed with a spirometer (Cosmed® Pony Fx, Italy) in accordance with the American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society standards. The pulmonary function test parameters recorded included forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV₁), forced vital capacity (FVC), the FEV₁/FVC, and peak expiratory flow (PEF). Each measurement was repeated until acceptable and reproducible results were obtained. For the analysis, the highest values were used and expressed as percentages of the predicted values [15].

Respiratory muscle strength assessment, including maximal inspiratory and expiratory pressure (MIP-MEP) was performed with participants completing at least five trials, and the highest value recorded for analysis. For MIP measurement, participants were instructed to exhale completely to residual volume (RV) and then perform a maximal inspiratory effort against an occluded airway. MEP was measured following a maximal inspiration to total lung capacity (TLC), after which participants performed a maximal expiratory effort. Percentages of MIP-MEP values were calculated according to reference values [16].

Diaphragm thickness was evaluated using a single ultrasound device (RS 85, Samsung) with a linear transducer. The right hemidiaphragm of each participant was examined in the supine position, with the right arm placed above the head. Measurements were taken at the midaxillary line, between the ninth and tenth intercostal spaces, with the ultrasound probe held perpendicular to the chest wall (Fig. 1a). Diaphragm thickness was measured three times during breath-holding at peak inspiration and end expiration during the normal respiratory cycle (Fig. 1b). The median of the three measurements for each phase was used for analysis [17]. The assessments were performed by a physiotherapist (ZC) with expertise in cardiopulmonary rehabilitation and a radiologist (HNS) with extensive experience in ultrasonography. The physiotherapist conducted pulmonary function and respiratory muscle strength assessments, while all ultrasound examinations were performed by the radiologist. Both examiners had more than 10 years of clinical experience in their respective fields.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were performed using the Windows-based Statistical Package for Social Sciences version 20 (SPSS-20) statistical analysis program. The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Descriptive data were presented as percentages, means, and standard deviations.

Since the data were normally distributed, two-tailed Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationships between diaphragm thickness and other measured parameters. The correlation is expressed with the Pearson correlation coefficient (strong relationship; 0.5 ≤ r ≤ 1.0, moderate relationship; 0.3 ≤ r < 0.5, weak relationship; r < 0.3). The level of significance was determined as p < 0.05 [18].

After residual and goodness of fit statistics, the multiple linear regression forward model was applied for significant values to show how the MIP, MEP, and FFM influenced diaphragm thickness. The post-hoc power (1-β = 0.98) analysis was performed according to the correlation between diaphragm thickness and FFM using the G-Power program 3.0.10 system (Franz Faul, Universität Kiel, Germany) [19].

Discussion

The findings of this study revealed the association between diaphragm thickness and anthropometric and body composition measures in healthy female adolescents. In addition, in the multivariable regression model including FFM, MIP, and MEP, these variables together explained a significant proportion of the variance in diaphragm thickness during inspiration (18.9%, p = 0.023) and expiration (16.1%, p = 0.047), but only FFM demonstrated a significant independent contribution. Although the application of diaphragmatic ultrasound in the pediatric population is relatively recent, age-specific reference values are gradually becoming available. Reference values for diaphragmatic thickness and thickening fraction have been published for preschool and childhood ages, and some studies have also described correlations with anthropometric characteristics [20, 21]. However, there is insufficient data regarding reference values in adolescents.

The lack of a significant correlation between diaphragm thickness and respiratory muscle strength measures in our study may be related to the different characteristics represented by these parameters. Diaphragm thickness assessed by ultrasound provides information about diaphragm morphology, whereas MIP and MEP reflect maximal respiratory pressure generation, which may be influenced by multiple factors related to respiratory muscle performance. Therefore, the relationship between structural diaphragm measurements and functional respiratory muscle outcomes may not always be directly observed [8, 22]. Previous studies have shown inconsistent associations between diaphragm thickness, respiratory functions, and MIP-MEP, depending on population, measurement methods, and analysis [7, 8]. The lack of a significant relationship between diaphragm thickness and dynamic lung volumes in our study may indicate that diaphragm thickness, a structural measure, may not directly reflect functional spirometric parameters. This is consistent with the fact that ventilatory capacity is influenced by multiple mechanical and physiological factors, such as chest wall/elastance and airway diameter [23]. Furthermore, the inclusion of healthy female adolescents with pulmonary function and respiratory muscle strength values largely within the normal range may have resulted in limited variability of these parameters, which may have reduced the ability to detect significant associations. Yamada et al. found that while diaphragm excursion showed partial associations with inspiratory pressures, static diaphragm thickness was not correlated with MIP and MEP. They emphasized that diaphragm thickness may not be a reliable indicator of respiratory muscle strength [8].

The FFM plays a significant role in respiratory health. Recent studies suggest that FFM has a stronger correlation with pulmonary function and clinical outcomes than body mass index (BMI). FFM has previously been reported to have a significant impact on ventilatory function in children and adolescents. A study of 1,174 healthy children aged 10–18 years found positive and significant associations between FFM and FVC, FEV₁, PEF, and other flow parameters [24]. Enright et al. [6] found that loss of FFM is associated with loss of diaphragm muscle mass in patients with CF. Our findings indicate that FFM is associated with dynamic lung volumes except for FEV1%, and adolescents with greater FFM demonstrated increased diaphragm thickness and higher MEP values. These results indicate that diaphragm thickness may reflect an individual’s overall skeletal muscle status during growth. Furthermore, nutritional status and body composition have been shown to influence diaphragm morphology and pulmonary function [5, 6]. Güngör et al. [5] evaluated diaphragm thickness in pediatric patients with primary malnutrition and reported a significant positive correlation between weight, height, BMI, and diaphragm thickness. Similarly, Júnior et al. found that children with obesity have decreased lung volumes and highlighted that anthropometric measures and body composition may be predictors of impaired pulmonary function [25]. Considering these results, which are consistent with the results of our study, it is important to take anthropometric and body composition measurements into account when interpreting diaphragm ultrasound results in adolescents.

In our study, diaphragm thickness was evaluated at the peak inspiration and end expiration phases during the normal respiratory cycle. The diaphragm thicknesses in our study (inspiration: 1.89 ± 0.37 mm; expiration: 1.46 ± 0.29 mm) are consistent with the ranges reported in pediatric reference studies. Duyndam et al. [26] using a similar measurement technique, reported end-inspiration ranges of 1.69–2.09 mm and end-expiration ranges of 1.38–1.67 mm in pediatric patients aged 0–8 years. However, our DTF (29.5%) value was higher than the results of Duyndam et al. [26]. The differences in age, anthropometric measurements, measurement technique/measurement site, and tidal volume still make direct comparison with our adolescent sample challenging. Further studies are needed to establish reference values for diaphragm thickness and DTF in adolescent populations. Clinically, the observed association between diaphragm thickness and FFM suggests that body composition may be relevant when interpreting diaphragm thickness in healthy female adolescents. Ultrasound assessment of diaphragm thickness may provide complementary information on diaphragm morphology. However, diaphragm thickness should not be considered a direct measure of diaphragm function, particularly given the lack of significant associations with MIP and MEP in the present study. Future studies combining diaphragm thickness with functional ultrasonographic parameters, such as diaphragm excursion and thickening fraction, may provide a more comprehensive assessment of diaphragm function.

This study provides novel data on diaphragm thickness and its relationships with FFM, respiratory muscle strength (MIP and MEP), and anthropometric measures in a healthy female adolescent population. In addition, the study contributes to understanding the factors associated with diaphragm thickness during adolescence. However, the study has several limitations. The limited reference values for diaphragm thickness and DTF in adolescents make direct comparisons difficult. The study included only healthy female adolescents, limiting the generalizability of the findings to other age groups or clinical populations. The clustering of spirometric parameters near the upper end of the normal range may have limited the ability to detect correlations with diaphragm thickness. Another limitation of the study is that intra-observer reliability could not be evaluated. The individual repeated ultrasound measurements were not retained for subsequent reliability assessment, precluding retrospective calculation of the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Inter-observer reliability could also not be assessed, as all ultrasound examinations were performed by a single radiologist. Finally, physical activity level and sports participation were not assessed, although these factors may influence diaphragm morphology. This should be considered in future studies.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that diaphragm thickness is significantly associated with body composition and anthropometric characteristics in healthy female adolescents. This may show that diaphragm thickness could provide structural information about general muscle status during growth. Future studies should also include larger and more diverse adolescent populations, including both sexes and individuals with a broader range of respiratory function. Longitudinal designs incorporating advanced ultrasonographic parameters, such as diaphragm excursion, may provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between diaphragm morphology, respiratory muscle strength, and pulmonary function throughout growth and development.
