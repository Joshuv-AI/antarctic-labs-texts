A lightweight deep learning model with channel attention for kidney cell classification from microscopy images
Authors: Mithila Arman, Md. Mahid Arfan Rahat, Mahabuba Akter Thithi, Johir Uddin Khan, Shahriar Mahmud Kabir, Md. Imamul Islam, Mehedi Hasan, Mohammed Nazmus Shakib, Heng Siong Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate identification of renal cell types within tissue architecture is fundamental for understanding normal kidney physiology and detecting early pathological changes. While traditional histological examination is time-intensive and dependent on expert interpretation, deep learning based computational methods offer a scalable and reproducible alternative for large-scale cell classification. Existing general-purpose models are often over-parameterized and computationally inefficient when applied to resource-constrained settings. To address these limitations, this study introduces CytoECA-Net, a task-specific convolutional neural network optimized for classifying kidney cell types from fluorescence microscopy images. The proposed architecture follows a hierarchical five-stage design that employs depthwise separable convolutions to reduce redundant spatial filtering, combined with efficient channel attention and residual connections to capture discriminative intra-nuclear patterns. Evaluated on the TissueMNIST benchmark comprising approximately 236,000 images, CytoECA-Net achieves a classification accuracy of 75.56% and an AUC of 0.9564, with performance comparable to existing baseline architectures while using only 1.7 million parameters. It further demonstrates efficient computation with an inference time of 2.43 ms per image and low memory requirements. Additional evaluation on the KMC-RENAL histopathology dataset shows that the model achieves 97.76% accuracy and attains the highest performance among the evaluated models. Visual interpretability analysis using Grad-CAM confirms that the model focuses on biologically relevant nuclear structures. These results demonstrate that CytoECA-Net offers an effective balance of accuracy, efficiency, and interpretability for kidney cell classification, making it well suited for resource-limited biomedical and diagnostic environments.

Introduction

The human kidney maintains homeostasis through specialized cell types organized within the nephron, where each cellular population contributes distinct physiological functions essential for blood filtration and fluid balance1. Within the renal cortex, multiple specialized structures, including proximal tubules, distal convoluted tubules, collecting ducts, and thick ascending limbs, operate in coordination to facilitate blood filtration and reabsorption2. Accurate identification of these cell types within tissue architecture is essential for understanding normal renal physiology and disease pathogenesis3. Changes in cellular composition or spatial organization may reflect early pathological alterations, making reliable cell-type classification important for biological research and clinical diagnostics4.

Traditional assessment relies on histological examination under light microscopy, where trained pathologists identify renal cell types based on morphological features and spatial context5. Although effective, this process requires extensive expertise and time-intensive manual inspection6. These factors introduce limitations in reproducibility and scalability when analyzing large tissue volumes or high cell counts7.

Fluorescence microscopy extends traditional histological examination by enabling deeper visualization of cellular organization using nuclear staining with DNA-binding fluorophores8. This approach reveals chromatin organization patterns associated with cellular identity and functional state9. Despite these benefits, the interpretation of fluorescence images largely depends on manual analysis. As a result, the systematic evaluation of nuclear features across thousands of cells continues to pose challenges in scalability and consistency10. Computational analysis offers a promising alternative by enabling the extraction of subtle patterns that are difficult to discern visually, thereby supporting large-scale and reproducible cell classification11.

Deep learning has emerged as a central component of computer-aided diagnosis systems and performs well across a broad variety of medical imaging tasks12. With the advent of convolutional neural networks, it has become possible to automatically extract features without relying on handcrafted descriptors, as required in earlier machine learning methods13. Established CNN architectures have achieved high performance in medical image classification tasks, including radiological, histopathological, and cellular imaging applications12. More recently, transformer-based models, originally developed for natural language processing, have been studied alongside convolutional models14. Such attention-based models are capable of learning long-range dependencies but usually require larger datasets and greater computational resources14. Simultaneously, task-specific architectures have been proposed to address domain constraints by designing networks tailored to particular imaging modalities and classification purposes, enabling improved efficiency at lower computational cost15,16.

Progress in medical image analysis has been facilitated by standardized benchmark datasets, among which MedMNIST provides a comprehensive resource for evaluating classification performance across diverse medical imaging tasks17,18. In this collection, TissueMNIST is a kidney cell classification dataset comprising about 236,000 fluorescent nuclear images obtained from human kidney tissue18. TissueMNIST presents a difficult classification task because of complex inter-class morphological variations and variability commonly found in fluorescence microscopic images19. Baseline architectures encounter notable performance challenges on this dataset20. Despite the difficulty of this task, limited evidence exists of architectures optimized specifically for this problem. State-of-the-art methods tested on TissueMNIST are mostly based on general-purpose designs intended for broader vision tasks; thus, they are often computationally costly and achieve relatively low classification accuracy18.

To overcome these drawbacks, this paper presents a specialized convolutional neural network, named CytoECA-Net, that is tailored to the problem of classifying kidney cell types from fluorescence nuclear images. Unlike generalized models that suffer from over-parameterization on grayscale nuclear data, CytoECA-Net leverages depthwise separable convolutions to minimize redundant spatial filters. Through the systematic integration of Efficient Channel Attention (ECA) within a residual framework, the network captures intricate intra-nuclear dependencies potentially corresponding to biological markers such as chromatin distribution and nucleolar organization. This is achieved while maintaining a compact footprint of only 1.7 million parameters. This balance of efficiency and representational power allows for high-fidelity classification in resource-constrained diagnostic environments. Related work

Deep learning and machine learning methods have been applied to classify kidney cell types from fluorescence nuclear images, with varying emphasis on biological analysis, dataset construction, and model design. The following review summarizes representative contributions that have shaped current approaches to kidney cell classification.

To begin with, the foundational work of Woloshuk et al.21 proposed an imaging-based framework for in situ classification of human kidney cell types using nuclear morphology. Their method combined tissue cytometry with supervised deep learning to generate large-scale labeled datasets from 3D DAPI-stained nuclear images. Two end-to-end convolutional models, NephNet3D and NephNet2D, were evaluated using three-dimensional nuclear volumes and two-dimensional slices or maximum projections. Experimental results showed that NephNet3D achieved an accuracy of approximately 80.26%, whereas NephNet2D achieved 66.55%. The primary emphasis of this work was biological validation, dataset construction, and three-dimensional analysis rather than the development of compact or efficiency-oriented deep learning architectures. Yang et al.18 introduced MedMNIST v2 as a standardized benchmark for lightweight biomedical image classification, comprising multiple 2D and 3D datasets, including TissueMNIST derived from kidney nucleus images by Woloshuk et al.21 The benchmark emphasizes uniform dataset curation and evaluation to enable fair comparison and reproducible research. A later extension, MedMNIST+, further broadened its scope22. In the TissueMNIST, baseline and AutoML methods were compared; AutoKeras obtained the highest accuracy of 70.3%, but there were no task-specific architecture designs or interpretability studies. Based on the initial MedMNISTv2 benchmark, Doerrich et al.20 expanded the evaluative procedure by utilizing the MedMNIST+ dataset, in where they evaluated various input resolutions and training regimes of established CNN and transformer models. The benchmark provides useful information on the behavior of models in diverse prototyping and aids in making informed architectural decisions. On TissueMNIST, DenseNet 121 reported the highest accuracy of 74.25%. In line with the initial benchmark, the work also approached comparative benchmarking without suggesting task-specific architectures or examining model explainability.

Alongside large-scale benchmarking efforts, other works investigate architectural design choices through task-specific models. Manzari et al.23 presented MedViT, a hybrid CNN-Transformer model, which can classify medical images against various standard datasets. The model was tested on the MedMNIST v2 benchmark and scored around 73.1% classification accuracy on the TissueMNIST data, which competes with the state-of-the-art architecture. MedViT was designed deliberately to provide an efficient-representative balance, with the smallest variant having approximately 10 million parameters, which remains lightweight for Transformer-based models but is still larger than compact CNN designs. However, the study does not provide tissue-specific interpretability analysis for TissueMNIST, limiting insights into cell-type–specific nuclear morphology and biological relevance. Building upon their earlier MedViT framework, Manzari et al.24 introduced MedViT V2, which extends the original design by integrating Kolmogorov–Arnold Network layers and enhanced dilated neighborhood attention to improve scalability and robustness in medical image classification. The model was evaluated on the MedMNIST v2 benchmark and achieved an accuracy of approximately 71.6% on the TissueMNIST dataset. However, for TissueMNIST, the classification performance decreases relative to the previous version despite an increase in model parameters, indicating limited benefit from architectural scaling for this task.

In addition to supervised model design, other works have examined alternative learning paradigms such as few-shot, self-supervised, and complex-valued learning. Liu et al.25 studied few-shot medical image classification using a subspace-based framework built on features extracted from a pre-trained ResNet-18. The method replaces SVD-based subspaces with discriminant analysis and non-negative matrix factorization to maintain class separation under limited data. Experiments on fourteen medical datasets, including TissueMNIST, used an extreme few-shot setup with 65 training and 20 testing images per class. On TissueMNIST, discriminant analysis reached 21.86 ± 2.15% accuracy. A limitation of this study is that performance remains low for multi-class nuclear images, and no visual explainability is provided to assess biological relevance. To assess the effectiveness of self-supervised learning for medical image classification, Bundele et al.26 evaluated several SSL methods, including SimCLR, MoCo v3, BYOL, and DINO, using ResNet-50 and ViT-Small backbones. Representation quality was tested on eleven MedMNIST datasets under in-domain, low-label, cross-dataset transfer, and out-of-distribution settings with frozen encoders and linear classifiers. On TissueMNIST, the models reached accuracy in the low 70% range, showing consistent benchmark performance. From a methodological perspective, a limitation is the reliance on large backbone models and the absence of interpretability at the cellular or nuclear scale. Adopting a complex-valued learning paradigm for medical image classification, Sun et al.27 proposed Complex Mixer, which extends the MLP-Mixer into the complex domain using incentive learning and self-supervised pretraining with random masking. The model was evaluated on the MedMNIST v2 benchmark and achieved 73.9% accuracy on the TissueMNIST dataset, placing it among competitive deep learning approaches. Despite competitive performance, these complex-valued representations and incentive learning increase architectural and mathematical complexity. The study also omits visual explainability and analysis of discriminative nuclear regions.

In contrast to studies relying on the TissueMNIST benchmark, other works have explored fluorescence image classification using different datasets and imaging settings. Chanchal et al.28 proposed RenalNet, a lightweight CNN for classifying renal cell carcinoma subtypes from hematoxylin and eosin–stained kidney histopathology images. The study used a class-balanced dataset derived from whole-slide images from The Cancer Genome Atlas, with expert-guided region-of-interest patch extraction. RenalNet integrates channel residual transformations and group convolution–based localization to capture nuclear and spatial features, achieving a peak accuracy of 97.24% with low computational complexity. Despite these gains, the framework relies on manual ROI extraction and lacks visual explainability to support diagnostic interpretation. Winfree et al.29 introduced an integrated tissue cytometry framework that combines machine learning with high-content fluorescence imaging for in situ cell classification and spatial neighborhood analysis in human kidney tissue. The Volumetric Tissue Exploration and Analysis (VTEA) pipeline unifies nucleus-based segmentation, feature extraction, clustering, and interactive visualization within a single environment, enabling biologically interpretable analysis of two-dimensional and three-dimensional kidney imaging data. A notable strength of this work is its emphasis on interpretability and biological insight through bidirectional mapping between image space and analytical features. A limitation is that the semi-automated workflow requires expert involvement and remains sensitive to segmentation quality in densely packed or low-contrast regions. Carnevali et al.30 developed Artificial Intelligence of the Nucleus (AINU), a deep learning framework based on a DenseNet-121 architecture to classify cellular states using nanoscale nuclear organization derived from super-resolution microscopy. The model was trained on stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy (STORM) images capturing the spatial distribution of histone H3, RNA polymerase II (Pol II), and DNA. Using relatively small but highly specialized datasets, AINU successfully distinguished human somatic cells, induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs), HSV-1-infected cells, and cancer (HeLa) cells, achieving strong performance with AUC values typically around 0.95–0.98 and reaching up to 0.99 in optimal settings. Interpretability analyses, including occlusion-based attribution and class activation mapping (CAM), revealed that nucleolar Pol II localization serves as a key discriminative feature for identifying stem cells. Despite this, the approach relies on specialized super-resolution imaging techniques and limited, domain-specific datasets, which may restrict scalability and generalizability to conventional fluorescence microscopy workflows. Mamalakis et al.31 developed a multi-spectral pipeline using high-content fluorescence microscopy to distinguish normal human cells from metastasizing counterparts. The architecture employs a deep multi-attention channel network that forces global spatial correlation across image channels representing actin, vimentin, and DNA. Their MHL-Den model achieved a recall of 95.79% and a macro AUC-ROC of 87.86%. While the method provides enhanced interpretability via a weighted geometric mean of Grad-CAM scores, it relies on a relatively small, specialized dataset of 220 cells, which may limit the assessment of its generalization to the broader morphological diversity found in large-scale benchmarks. An et al.32 proposed ProteinFormer, a framework that integrates ResNet for local feature extraction with a modified transformer for global information fusion to address the spatial dynamics of protein translocation. For scenarios with limited data, the authors developed GL-ProteinFormer, which incorporates residual attention and a ConvFFN module to reintroduce spatial inductive biases. Evaluated on the fluorescence microscopy-based Cyto_2017 dataset, ProteinFormer achieved an F1 score of 91% for single-label tasks and 81% for multi-label tasks. A limitation of this approach is the reliance on a complex hybrid architecture, alongside a lack of detailed computational performance metrics, as the study does not report the specific parameter count, inference time, or memory usage required for the model. Liu et al.33 introduced ILViT, an Inception-Linear Attention-based Lightweight Vision Transformer, for microscopic cell classification. The model integrates Dynamic Inception Convolution (DIC) to maintain a wide receptive field with reduced parameters and a Contrastive Omni-Kolmogorov Attention (COKA) module to capture global contextual information. Evaluated on the ICPR-HEp-2 dataset, which utilizes indirect immunofluorescence (IIF) images, ILViT achieved a classification accuracy of 97.436%. However, the architecture maintains a relatively high parameter count of 8.98 million. Furthermore, ILViT was designed for 224 × 224 images; its patch-based tokenization would discard fine-grained chromatin texture in the smaller native resolution of fluorescence nuclear patches. Yee et al.34 introduced an automated deep learning framework for breast cancer cell line classification using high-resolution fluorescence microscopy. The methodology utilizes a channel-wise intermediate data-fusion strategy to independently extract and integrate organelle-specific features from mitochondria, early endosomes, and recycling endosomes. Evaluated on six breast cancer cell lines, the framework achieved a classification accuracy of 97.1 ± 1.1%. Despite this high accuracy, the approach faces limitations regarding architectural complexity, with the fusion networks requiring approximately 72 million parameters. Furthermore, the pipeline depends on manual sparsity filtering, where patches are rejected if the calculated foreground ratio is below a threshold of 0.1.

Several recent studies have explored CNN architectures with integrated attention mechanisms for medical image classification. Ferdous et al.35 introduced MedNet, a lightweight convolutional architecture that integrates depthwise separable convolutions with the CBAM attention mechanism within a residual framework to enhance feature representation in medical image classification. The model was evaluated across multiple benchmarks, including DermaMNIST, BloodMNIST, OCTMNIST, and Fitzpatrick17k, demonstrating competitive performance while maintaining low computational complexity. This work highlights the growing trend toward efficiency-oriented CNN designs that combine channel–spatial attention with residual learning. However, the evaluation is primarily conducted on general medical imaging datasets and does not specifically address fluorescence nuclear imaging or fine-grained cellular morphology. In addition, limited analysis is provided regarding interpretability at the nuclear level, which is critical for microscopy-based tasks such as kidney cell classification. These limitations indicate that further task-specific architectural adaptation is required for TissueMNIST-like datasets. Gomaa and Saad36 proposed SCAR-Net, a residual channel attention network that integrates Squeeze-and-Excitation blocks within residual pathways to recalibrate channel-wise feature importance. The architecture demonstrates effective feature discrimination with only 2.1 million parameters across multiple benchmark datasets. SCAR-Net employs SE blocks after each residual unit for channel recalibration through global average pooling and fully connected layers. While this design effectively combines residual learning with channel attention, the architecture relies solely on SE-based channel attention without exploring alternative attention mechanisms such as ECA, which uses adaptive kernel-sized 1D convolution to model local cross-channel interaction without dimensionality reduction. Arnob et al.37 proposed a lightweight attention-augmented convolutional neural network for multiclass retinal disease classification using color fundus images. The architecture utilizes depthwise separable convolutions alongside dual squeeze-and-excitation and global-context attention modules to optimize feature representation. While the model achieves 87.9% accuracy with integrated explainability, several architectural limitations can be identified. Although smaller than traditional deep backbones, the 16.6M parameter size may still be relatively high for capturing localized retinal features. Residual connections appear only in later stages, which may weaken the preservation of low-level texture information. The attention modules are applied sequentially rather than in parallel, which may limit effective multi-scale feature integration. Liu et al.38 proposed a deep learning model for medical image classification using a multi-scale channel attention mechanism. The architecture utilizes an inverted residual bottleneck structure to minimize information loss and integrates an improved Pyramid Segmentation Attention (PSA) module. This module replaces the standard Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) component with an Efficient Channel Attention (ECA) block to achieve non-dimensionality reducing cross-channel interaction. While validated on skin lesion datasets, achieving accuracies of 77.6% and 88.2% on ISIC2019 and HAM10000 respectively, the design offers a relevant approach for capturing complex morphological features in medical imaging. An identified limitation is that the attention mechanism is applied as a plug-in within the high-dimensional feature maps of the bottleneck, which may fail to enforce spatial-semantic consistency across all residual pathways.

Existing studies on kidney cell classification from fluorescence nuclear images have addressed dataset construction, benchmarking, and selected task-specific model designs, often emphasizing biological analysis or general-purpose evaluation. Benchmark-driven works prioritize comparative performance across architectures, while task-aware model design for TissueMNIST remains limited. Recent approaches often rely on large backbones or generalized architectures, with less attention to compact CNNs tailored to nuclear morphology. In addition, most studies report classification performance without providing visual explanations of discriminative nuclear regions. These observations indicate a need for lightweight, task-specific models that balance accuracy, efficiency, and interpretability for in situ kidney cell classification. To bridge these gaps, this study proposes CytoECA-Net, a compact architecture designed to enhance feature extraction. The proposed design prioritizes computational efficiency and robust feature representation while maintaining competitive classification performance. Data set

For kidney cell classification, the TissueMNIST dataset was obtained from MedMNIST+18,22. This is a popular and widely used benchmark in medical image analysis research. MedMNIST+ provides standardized datasets that support fair and consistent evaluation of machine learning models. The TissueMNIST dataset is derived from the BBBC05121 dataset provided by the Broad Bioimage Benchmark Collection39. This dataset contains 236,386 individual cell nuclei segmented from three deceased donor kidney nephrectomy specimens, organized into eleven cell type classes (condensed to eight for classification tasks). These images were captured from the cortex of deceased donor kidneys using confocal fluorescence microscopy to investigate the specialized architecture of the nephron. The samples were prepared as 50 μm thick sections from frozen cortical specimens and labeled with DAPI, a common DNA stain, to visualize nuclear morphology. Ground truth labels for each cell type were established through a semi-automated tissue cytometry workflow using the Volumetric Tissue Exploration and Analysis (VTEA) tool, which identifies cell types by clustering the fluorescence intensity of specific markers. This study follows the standard training, validation, and test split defined by MedMNIST+. Preprocessing

To maintain a fair and unbiased comparison across different network architectures, only minimal and widely adopted preprocessing operations were applied. The augmentation strategy was kept conservative to ensure that performance differences could be attributed primarily to architectural design rather than dataset-specific augmentation effects. All images were used at the 64x64 resolution that was provided with the selected configuration of MedMNIST v2 in order to ensure the same input dimensions for all experiments. Since the original data are single-channel images, each of the samples was converted to a three-channel representation with grayscale channel replication to fulfill the input requirement of standard convolutional architectures. Pixel intensity normalization was subsequently applied to improve numerical stability during training and to reduce inter-sample variability. During training, random horizontal flip, random vertical flip (50% probability) and random rotations of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pm 10^{\circ }$$\end{document} were included in the data augmentation process. These transformations preserve the original structural properties of the images while introducing some spatial variation to the images to improve generalization.

Overview of the proposed CytoECA-Net

The CytoECA-Net is a specialized convolutional neural network architecture engineered for the classification of in situ cytometric images within the TissueMNIST dataset. The design philosophy emphasizes a balance between high-dimensional feature extraction and parameter efficiency, which is a critical requirement for automated cell classification systems in biomedical research and potential clinical applications. As shown in Figure 2, the architecture is a hierarchical structure with five main computational stages followed by an optimized classification head.Fig. 2Detailed architecture of CytoECA-Net, showing the hierarchical arrangement of convolutional and depthwise separable convolution blocks enhanced with residual connections and Efficient Channel Attention (ECA). The network combines sophisticated structural elements that incorporate Depthwise Separable Convolutions (DSC), Efficient Channel Attention (ECA) and Residual Connections. These elements are arranged in an effective way to capture multi-scale morphological patterns characteristic of tissue cytology while keeping a small number of parameters. These components and their integration into the CytoECA-Net framework are structurally and mathematically dissected in the following sections.

Structural design principles

The efficacy of CytoECA-Net is based on the combination of three fundamental building blocks. First, depthwise separable convolutions are used to replace the dense convolutions in the deep layers of the network40. A standard convolution performs spatial and channel-wise filtering simultaneously, which often leads to high computational redundancy. In contrast, the depthwise separable convolution mechanism factorizes this operation into a depthwise convolution for spatial filtering and a pointwise 1 × 1 convolution for channel fusion, which is particularly effective for histopathological images where discriminative local textures and stain-dependent tissue patterns must be captured across multiple feature channels. The depthwise separable convolution operation is defined as1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{Y} = \textbf{X} * \textbf{K}_{\textrm{dw}} * \textbf{K}_{\textrm{pw}}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where the depthwise convolution stage is given by2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{Z}_{k}(i,j) = \sum _{u,v} \textbf{X}_{k}(i+u, j+v)\,\textbf{K}_{\textrm{dw}}^{k}(u,v), \quad k = 1, \ldots , M, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}followed by the pointwise convolution stage3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{Y}_{n}(i,j) = \sum _{k=1}^{M} \textbf{Z}_{k}(i,j)\,\textbf{K}_{\textrm{pw}}^{k,n}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{K}_{\textrm{dw}}$$\end{document} denotes the depthwise spatial kernels applied independently to each input channel, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{K}_{\textrm{pw}}$$\end{document} represents the 1 × 1 pointwise kernels used for channel-wise feature fusion.

The second core component is the Efficient Channel Attention block41. In fluorescence microscopy analysis, certain feature maps may carry greater discriminative power than others, such as those encoding nuclear morphology and DAPI intensity patterns compared to those representing background regions. While traditional squeeze and excitation networks rely on dimensionality reduction to compute channel importance, this strategy often introduces unnecessary complexity and weakens the direct correspondence between channels. The Efficient Channel Attention block addresses this limitation by modeling local cross channel interactions using a one dimensional convolution without dimensionality reduction. Given an input feature map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{X} \in \mathbb {R}^{H \times W \times C}$$\end{document}, global average pooling is first applied to generate a channel descriptor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{z} \in \mathbb {R}^{C}$$\end{document}, expressed as4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} z_c = \frac{1}{H W} \sum _{i=1}^{H} \sum _{j=1}^{W} X_c(i,j). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Channel attention weights are then computed through a one dimensional convolution5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{s} = \sigma \left( \textrm{Conv1D}(\textbf{z}, k) \right) , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma (\cdot )$$\end{document} denotes the sigmoid activation function and k is an adaptively determined kernel size dependent on the channel dimension. The final recalibrated feature map is obtained by channel wise multiplication6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{Y}_c = s_c \cdot \textbf{X}_c. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This mechanism enables selective emphasis of informative biological markers while maintaining negligible parameter overhead, making it well suited for deep feature extraction.

The third component is the Residual Block with identity mapping42. Deep neural networks often suffer from the vanishing gradient problem, where the signal used to update weights diminishes as it propagates through many layers. By introducing skip-connections, the CytoECA-Net allows for the direct flow of information from earlier stages to later ones. In the architecture, these residual paths work in parallel with the main convolutional stream. This dual-path approach ensures that low-level textural details are preserved and fused with high-level semantic abstractions, a combination that is essential for distinguishing between subtle tissue classes in the TissueMNIST dataset.

Architectural stages and layer configuration

The CytoECA-Net processes input images through five distinct stages designed to progressively abstract visual information. Stage 1 serves as the initial feature extractor, employing a standard 3 × 3 convolution with 64 filters to capture basic edge and color distributions. This is immediately followed by Batch Normalization and an Efficient Channel Attention (ECA) block to recalibrate the initial feature set. A Max-Pooling operation then reduces the spatial resolution to prepare the data for more complex operations. Stages 2 and 3 represent the core of the feature extraction process and are characterized by a parallel fusion strategy. In these stages, the input is branched into two paths: one path utilizes a Depthwise Separable Convolution (DS-Conv) block followed by ECA, while the other employs a Residual Block. This parallel configuration is preferred over a single deeper depthwise separable path because the two branches are expected to capture complementary representations: the DS-Conv branch, owing to its channel-wise spatial decomposition, tends to emphasize fine-grained local textural patterns, whereas the residual branch, through its identity mapping, is better positioned to preserve broader structural and shape-level information across the stage. The outputs of these parallel paths are fused via element-wise addition, enabling the model to jointly learn transformed separable features and preserved residual representations, thereby enhancing feature diversity. Notably, the residual branch itself incorporates an internal identity mapping, resulting in a hierarchical residual design that combines both intra-block and inter-branch skip connections. This two-level residual approach is a deliberate strategy that encourages stable gradient propagation and at the same time tends to retain low-level representations and stimulate stage-level feature refinement. Stages 4 and 5 focus on high-level semantic extraction by moving to a more profound configuration of 512 filters, using DS-Conv and ECA blocks to coalesce the features into a high-dimensional feature space. The frequent use of max pooling across stages ensures robustness to spatial variations and orientation changes commonly observed in tissue samples. The last convolutional step is then followed by Global Average Pooling (GAP) rather than the conventional flattening to reduce the spatial dimensions of each feature map to one value, reducing the chances of overfitting due to the low number of learnable parameters in the classification head. The classifier is a three-layer multilayer perceptron comprising 256, 128 and 8 neurons, respectively, with a Sigmoid Linear Unit (SiLU) activation function that provides more efficient gradient propagation and convergence. In order to improve further generalization, dropout layers of 0.3 and 0.2 are interspersed in the classification head to prevent co-adaptation of neurons. Experimental setup

For comparative evaluation, several baseline architectures were selected, including VGG1643, DenseNet12144, EfficientNet-B445, MobileNetV346, MobileViT-v247, ConvNeXt-Tiny48, RepVGG-A049, and MambaOut-S50. VGG16 provides a reference based on sequential convolutional blocks with a large number of parameters. DenseNet121 employs dense inter-layer connectivity that promotes feature reuse, which is relevant for fluorescence nuclear images where subtle texture and intensity cues support cell-type discrimination. EfficientNet-B4 follows compound scaling of network depth, width, and input resolution to achieve balanced performance. MobileNetV3 utilizes depthwise separable convolutions with lightweight attention components to reduce computational cost. MobileViT-v2 integrates convolutional operations with transformer-based representations to capture both local and global features. ConvNeXt-Tiny is a modern convolutional architecture that incorporates updated design strategies while maintaining a convolutional structure. RepVGG-A0 adopts a re-parameterization strategy that enables a multi-branch training structure and a simplified inference-time architecture. MambaOut-S is based on state space modeling to capture long-range dependencies efficiently. Together, these architectures reflect differences in design philosophy, feature representation, and computational efficiency. In addition, two recent medical image classification models were incorporated to provide a more domain-specific comparison aligned with current research trends. RenalNet28 is designed for kidney histopathology classification using multi-scale feature extraction with channel attention and residual transformations to capture morphological variations. MedNet35 is a lightweight convolutional model that combines depthwise separable convolutions with attention mechanisms to enhance feature representation under limited computational resources. Together, these architectures provide a comprehensive evaluation framework covering diverse connectivity patterns, attention mechanisms, and efficiency-focused designs.

All baseline networks were implemented in two configurations, initialized with ImageNet-pretrained weights and trained from scratch, to assess both transfer learning benefits and intrinsic architectural performance.The PyTorch framework with the TIMM library was used to implement and train models. Python 3.11.13, PyTorch 2.6.0, TIMM 1.0.15 were used as the experimental environment. The data balancing techniques were not applied, and the standard cross-entropy loss was applied without class weighting, resampling, and synthetic data generation to isolate the effect of architectural design. This methodological choice ensures that performance differences between architectures can be attributed to their intrinsic feature learning capabilities rather than dataset-specific optimization strategies. The AdamW optimizer was used in the optimization because it is effective in stabilizing the training and regulating overfitting with decoupled weight decay. The maximum epochs were 150 and early stopping occurred at a patience of 15 epochs. The model that had the lowest loss during the validation was saved and evaluated. Per-class Brier scores were computed using a one-versus-rest formulation, where the mean squared difference between the predicted probability and the binary indicator was calculated across all samples for each class independently. The overall Brier score was derived following the standard multi-class formulation, computed as the mean of the summed squared differences between the predicted probability vector and the one-hot encoded label vector across all samples and all eight classes. The per-class scores range from 0.0192 for GEC to 0.0770 for CCD, with intermediate values of 0.0246 for POD, 0.0297 for DCT, 0.0333 for LEC, 0.0361 for IEC, 0.0551 for TAL, and 0.0675 for PTS. The elevated score for CCD is consistent with the inter-class confusion observed in the confusion matrix. The overall multi-class Brier score is 0.3427, which reflects the cumulative squared error summed across all eight class dimensions per sample and is expected to be substantially larger than individual per-class scores given the additive nature of this formulation. The calibration curves for most classes track closely along the diagonal reference line, indicating that predicted confidence levels are broadly consistent with observed outcomes. The top row (Fig. 9a–e) displays the original fluorescence nuclear images, while the bottom row (Fig. 9f–j) shows the corresponding Grad-CAM heatmaps. For correctly classified samples (Fig. 9f–i), the model generally prioritizes nuclear regions. Specifically, for the DCT, IEC, and PTS classes (Figures 9f–h), high activation intensities are well-localized and concentrated primarily on nuclear boundaries and internal structural textures. In contrast, the correctly classified CCD sample (Figure 9i) exhibits a notably more diffuse activation pattern. This variation suggests that while the model typically relies on localized nuclear details, it may utilize a broader spatial context to distinguish the specific morphological characteristics of the CCD cell type.

The misclassified case (Fig. 9e and j) provides further insight into the model’s behavior. A DCT sample incorrectly predicted as CCD shows a dispersed activation pattern (Fig. 9j) that qualitatively mirrors the diffuse signature observed in the true CCD class (Fig. 9i). This observation suggests that the misclassification might not be due to the model focusing on irrelevant background noise, but could potentially be attributed to overlapping or similar feature representations between the DCT and CCD classes. This interpretation is consistent with our confusion matrix analysis, which identifies these two classes as having the highest rate of mutual interference. Overall, the Grad-CAM visualizations indicate that CytoECA-Net prioritizes nuclear morphological features over background artifacts, reinforcing the potential biological relevance of its learned representations.

For each architecture, the best-performing variant (pretrained or trained from scratch) was selected for statistical comparison. The results are presented in Table 9. The first baseline is a standard convolutional neural network with no attention mechanism or residual pathways, requiring 4.1 million parameters to achieve an accuracy of 74.37%. A change to depthwise separable convolutions reduces the number of parameters to 0.6 million, but accuracy drops to 73.28% and AUC to 94.98%. This performance trade-off indicates that while depthwise separable layers improve efficiency, they limit the capacity to represent complex features in fluorescence nuclear images when used in isolation. Integrating ECA blocks into the lightweight framework restores accuracy to 74.37% at 0.6 million parameters. Channel-wise attention mechanisms therefore enable the network to selectively emphasize relevant nuclear features without enlarging the model.

To assess whether a simple integrated skip connection is sufficient or whether a dedicated parallel residual block is necessary, an additional variant is evaluated in which the parallel ResidualBlock is replaced by a direct 1×1 projection shortcut added to the output of the depthwise separable path. This variant achieves an accuracy of 74.33% and an AUC of 0.9525, matching the ECA-only configuration but failing to reach the performance of the full model. The result demonstrates that while an integrated skip connection preserves gradient flow, it lacks the complementary representational capacity provided by the parallel standard convolutional residual branch. The full parallel residual design introduces additional cross-channel feature interactions through its two standard 3×3 convolutions, which are absent in a lightweight projection shortcut, and this capacity is reflected in the 1.23% accuracy gain observed in the complete architecture.

The full CytoECA-Net configuration achieves the best performance across all metrics, with an accuracy of 75.56% and an AUC of 0.9564. The combination of depthwise separable layers and parallel residual connections provides a balanced trade-off between feature extraction depth and computational cost. To address the selection of a three-layer MLP classification head over a simpler single fully-connected layer, an additional experiment is conducted in which the MLP head is replaced with a single linear layer directly following global average pooling. This variant achieves an accuracy of 75.47%, an F1-score of 75.17%, and an AUC of 0.9563, with a parameter count of 1.6 million. Although performance differences between the two configurations are marginal, the three-layer MLP head yields consistent improvements across all reported metrics. The additional non-linear capacity introduced by the MLP head allows the model to learn a more discriminative mapping from the pooled feature representation to the class output, which is particularly relevant given the morphological similarity among kidney cell subtypes in fluorescence nuclear images. The parameter overhead associated with this design choice is 0.1 million, representing a negligible increase relative to the overall model size. On this basis, the three-layer MLP classification head is retained in the final architecture.

Limitation and future work

Although the proposed architecture achieves competitive performance relative to established baseline models while maintaining lower computational requirements, additional improvement remains necessary. The model has a classification accuracy that is 75.56%; this value denotes that additional optimization is necessary to use this model in high-stakes medical applications. Current experimental results are based on a training regime that excludes data balancing loss functions, sophisticated augmentation strategies, and extensive preprocessing, which likely limits the final predictive performance. Future research will be aimed at combining class-weighting methods and more accurate preprocessing procedures to enhance the overall accuracy and stability of the models. Moreover, even with the incorporated visual explanations of Gradient-weighted class activation mapping, the interpretability is limited due to the absence of expert-level pathological validation. Future studies need to focus on the implication of medical experts to compare the described discriminative nuclear areas with clinical biomarkers to guarantee biological importance and diagnostic value.

Conclusion

This study introduced CytoECA-Net, a lightweight convolutional neural network tailored for kidney cell classification from fluorescence nuclear images. By combining depthwise separable convolutions with efficient channel attention within a residual structure, the proposed model effectively captures discriminative nuclear morphology while maintaining a compact parameter footprint. Evaluation on the TissueMNIST benchmark shows that CytoECA-Net reaches a classification accuracy of 75.56%, maintaining performance comparable to commonly used CNN baselines with significantly fewer parameters at 1.7 million and lower inference time. To further examine its performance across a different imaging modality, the model was also evaluated on the KMC-RENAL histopathology dataset, achieving 97.76% accuracy and attaining the highest performance among the baseline models. Grad-CAM analysis indicates that the model primarily attends to biologically meaningful nuclear regions during correct predictions, whereas misclassifications are associated with more diffuse and ambiguous activation patterns. Although the achieved performance reflects the known difficulty of the TissueMNIST task, where subtle inter-class nuclear differences limit separability, the results highlight the benefit of task-specific, efficiency-oriented architectural design. Despite these improvements, the overall classification accuracy remains modest, indicating scope for further enhancement. Future work will explore improved preprocessing strategies and more discriminative feature representations to further enhance performance, while maintaining the model’s efficiency advantages.
