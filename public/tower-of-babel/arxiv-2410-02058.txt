On the Hausdorff dimension and attracting laminations for fully irreducible automorphisms of free groups
Ilya Kapovich

Abstract
Motivated by a classic theorem of Birman and Series about the set of complete simple geodesics on a hyperbolic surface, we study the Hausdorff dimension of the set of endpoints in ∂ F r \partial F_{r} of some abstract algebraic laminations associated with free group automorphisms. For an exponentially growing outer automorphism φ ∈ Out ⁡ ( F r ) \varphi\in{\rm Out}(F_{r}) we show that the set of endpoints ℰ L ⊆ ∂ F r \mathcal{E}_{L}\subseteq\partial F_{r} of any of the attracting laminations L L of φ \varphi has Hausdorff dimension 0 0 and packing dimension 0 0 , for any visual metric on the boundary ∂ F r \partial F_{r} , and similarly that L ⊆ ∂ 2 F r L\subseteq\partial^{2}F_{r} (where ∂ 2 F r \partial^{2}F_{r} is equipped with the product metric of a visual metric) has Hausdorff dimension 0 0 and packing dimension 0 0 . If φ ∈ Out ⁡ ( F r ) \varphi\in{\rm Out}(F_{r}) is an atoroidal and fully irreducible, we deduce the same conclusions for the ending lamination Λ φ \Lambda_{\varphi} of φ \varphi that gets collapsed by the Cannon-Thurston map ∂ F r → ∂ G φ \partial F_{r}\to\partial G_{\varphi} for the associated free-by-cyclic group G φ = F r ⋊ φ ℤ G_{\varphi}=F_{r}\rtimes_{\varphi}\mathbb{Z} . By contrast, the set of endpoints of any of these laminations has upper box dimension > 0 >0 for any visual metric on ∂ F r \partial F_{r} .

Introduction

A classic 1985 result of Birman and Series
[
1
]
shows that if
S
is a closed hyperbolic surface then the union of all complete simple geodesics on
S
is nowhere dense set of Hausdorff dimension
1
. Their result implies, in particular, that if
\lambda
is a geodesic lamination on
S
and
\tilde{\lambda}
is the lift of
\lambda
to
\mathbb{H}^{2}=\tilde{S}
, then the set
L
of all
(p,q)
in
\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}-diag=\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}-diag
such that the geodesic from
p
to
q
in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
belongs to
\tilde{\lambda}
, is nowhere dense in
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}-diag
and has Hausdorff dimension
0
. Moreover, since the projection from
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}-diag
to
\mathbb{S}^{1}
is Lipschitz, and Lipschitz maps do not increase the Hausdorff dimension, it follows that
\{p\in\mathbb{S}^{1}:(p,q)\in L\text{ for some }q\}
has Hausdorff dimension
0
as a subset of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

There have been many generalizations and extensions of the Birman-Series theorem; in particular see the work of Lenzhen and Souto
[
45
]
and of Sapir
[
49
]
. It is interesting to understand what kind of analogs of the Birman-Series theorem hold for arbitrary word-hyperbolic groups. The notion of a simple geodesic usually no longer has a reasonable interpretation there, but for a non-elementary word-hyperbolic group
G
one can still study ”abstract algebraic laminations” on
G
, see Section
3
below. Namely, an
abstract algebraic lamination

L
on
G
is a subset
L\subseteq\partial^{2}G=\partial G\times\partial G-diag
such that
L
is closed,
G
-invariant and flip-invariant. Abstract algebraic laminations on word-hyperbolic groups naturally arise as supports of geodesic currents, as non-injectivity sets of Cannon-Thurston maps, and also come from some constructions associated with iterating automorphisms and with
\mathbb{R}
-tree actions. In this paper we consider the case of
G=F_{r}
, the free group of finite rank
r\geq 2
, which already provides a wide variety of interesting ”non-classical” examples to consider.

For an arbitrary metric space
(X,d)
and a subset
A\subseteq X
, one has
\dim_{H}(A)\leq\dim_{p}(A)
, where
\dim_{H}(A)
is the Hausdorff dimension of
A
and where
\dim_{p}(A)
is the
packing dimension
of
A
. The packing dimension behaves better than the Hausdorff dimension with respect to direct products, and we obtain our Hausdorff dimension results in this paper as consequences of proving that for the sets under consideration the packing dimension is equal to
0
.

Even in the free group case, in terms of looking for possible analogs of the Birman-Series theorem, it is necessary to limit the class of abstract algebraic laminations on
F_{r}
under consideration to examples that have some similarities to ”nice” geodesic laminations on hyperbolic surfaces. Otherwise one can easily get various (non-interesting) examples with positive Hausdorff dimension. E.g. the boundary
\partial CV_{r}
of the projectivized Outer space
CV_{r}
contains the (projective class of) the Bass-Serre tree
T
corresponding to a proper free product decomposition
F_{r}=U\ast V
. If the free factor
U
is non-cyclic, then the dual algebraic lamination
L(T)
of
T
(as defined in
[
13
]
) contains a copy of
\partial^{2}U
, which leads to positive Hausdorff dimension for
L(T)\subseteq\partial^{2}F_{r}
and for its set of endpoints in
\partial F_{r}
.

Our additional motivation comes from trying to understand how large, e.g. in terms its Hausdorff dimension, the set of non-conical limit points is for various convergence group actions of non-elementary word-hyperbolic groups on boundaries of Gromov-hyperbolic spaces, particularly those actions that come together with a Cannon-Thurston map (see more on this topic below). This question is only partially understood even in the classical Kleinian groups context. A recent result of M. Kapovich and Liu
[
40
]
shows that if
\Gamma\leq Isom(\mathbb{H}^{3})
is a finitely generated, non-free, torsion-free geometrically infinite Kleinian group such that the injectivity radius of
\mathbb{H}^{3}/\Gamma
is bounded away from
0
, then the Hausdorff dimension of the non-conical limit set of
\Gamma
is positive. This result applies, in particular, to the fiber surface subgroups of closed fibered hyperbolic 3-manifolds. However, the case of free geometrically infinite groups is not yet understood even in the classical Kleinian groups setting. Note, however, that for a f.g. geometrically infinite
\Gamma\leq Isom(\mathbb{H}^{3})
, such that the injectivity radius of
\mathbb{H}^{3}/\Gamma
is bounded away from
0
and the geometrically infinite ends are bounded by incompressible surfaces, the Hausdorff dimension of the non-horospherical limit set is known to be
0
(Mahan Mj, private communication, via M. Kapovich). See the recent paper of Lecure and Mj
[
43
]
for related results on non-horospherical limit points. The non-injectivity set of the Cannon-Thurston map, that is, the set of endpoints of the
Cannon-Thurston algebraic lamination
, provides a nice canonical subset of the set of non-conical limit points (and of non-horospherical limit points). Thus it becomes interesting to try to prove that the set of endpoints of the Cannon-Thurston lamination has Hausdorff dimension
0
, and the case of the free group
F_{r}
is particularly intriguing in light of the above discussion.

Recall that an element
\varphi\in Out(F_{r})
is
exponentially growing
if for some (equivalently, any) free basis
A
of
F_{r}
there exists
1\neq w\in F_{r}
such that

\lambda_{A}(\varphi,w)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{||\varphi^{n}(w)||_{A}}>1.

Here
||u||_{A}
is the cyclically reduced length of
u\in F_{r}
with respect to
A
. It is known
[
41
]
that in the above formula for an arbitrary
\varphi\in Out(F_{r})
the actual limit
\lambda(\varphi,w)\geq 1
as
n\to\infty
always exists and is independent of
A
. Moreover, for
\varphi\in Out(F_{r})
there are only finitely many possible values for
\lambda(\varphi,w)
as
w
varies over
F_{r}-\{1\}
, see
[
41
]
. If
\varphi\in Out(F_{r})
is fully irreducible then
\lambda(\varphi)=\lambda(\varphi,w)>1
, for all
w\neq 1
in
F_{r}
such that
[w]
is a non-periodic conjugacy class for
\varphi
.

In
[
4
]
Bestvina, Feighn and Handel, given any exponentially growing
\varphi\in{\rm Out}(F_{r})
define a finite nonempty set of
\mathcal{L}(\varphi)
attracting laminations
for
\varphi
. If
\varphi
is fully irreducible, the set
\mathcal{L}(\varphi)
consists of a single element, namely
\mathcal{L}(\varphi)=\{L_{\varphi}\}
, and
L_{\varphi}
is called
the attracting lamination
of
\varphi
. Thus for an exponentially growing
\varphi\in{\rm Out}(F_{r})
, we have
\mathcal{L}(\varphi)=\{L_{1},\dots,L_{m}\}
and each
L_{k}=L_{k}(\varphi)\subseteq\partial^{2}F_{r}
is an abstract algebraic lamination on
F_{r}
.

The technical definition of elements of
\mathcal{L}(\varphi)
in
[
4
]
in terms of relative train track representatives of
\varphi
(which is ultimately shown to be independent to be of the choice of such representatives) is rather complicated and we omit its details here. We explain how this definition works for fully irreducible
\varphi\in{\rm Out}(F_{r})
in Section
8.2
below and note that for an arbitrary exponentially growing
\varphi\in{\rm Out}(F_{r})
elements of
\mathcal{L}(\varphi)
can be recovered from understanding fixed points in
\partial F_{r}
of representatives of
\varphi
in
{\rm Aut}(F_{r})
. The latter fact turns out to be sufficient for our purposes using a recent result of Hilion and Levitt
[
31
]
.

Attracting laminations of fully irreducibles play an important role in the study of the dynamics and geometry of free group automorphisms and appear naturally in many contexts, see for example
[
3
,
4
,
37
,
39
,
16
]
, etc. More generally, attracting laminations of exponentially growing elements of
Out(F_{r})
proved to be a useful tool in the structure theory of
Out(F_{r})
and its subgroups, see
[
4
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
.

Recall that the (unprojectivized)
Outer space

\mbox{cv}_{r}
consists of all
\mathbb{R}
-trees
T
equipped with a minimal free discrete isometric action of
F_{r}
, where two such trees are considered equal if there exists an
F_{r}
-equivariant isometry between them. See Section
4
below for further discussion and background references.

When working with products of metric spaces (and their subsets), we take the product metric to be the supremum metric. That is, if
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
are two metric spaces, we equip
X\times Y
with the
product metric

\widehat{d}
defined as
\widehat{d}((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))=\max\{d_{X}(x,x^{\prime}),d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})\}
, where
x,x^{\prime}\in X
and
y,y^{\prime}\in Y
. Most other natural choices of a metric on
X\times Y
are bi-Lipschitz equivalent to
\widehat{d}
, and hence they don’t change the Hausdorff and packing dimensions of subsets of
(X\times Y.\widehat{d})
.

If
(X,d)
is a Gromov-hyperbolic metric space with a basepoint
x_{0}\in X
and if
a>1
, a metric
d
on
\partial X
is called a
visual metric
with the
visual parameter
a
if there exists
C\geq 1
such that for any
p,q\in\partial X

\frac{1}{C}a^{-(p,q)_{x_{0}}}\leq d(p,q)\leq Ca^{-(p,q)_{x_{0}}}

where
(p,q)_{x_{0}}
is the Gromov prpduct of
p
and
q
with respect to
x_{0}
, see
[
35
]
for precise definitions. If
X
is an
\mathbb{R}
-tree
(that is a
0
-hyperbolic geodesic metric space) then
(p,q)_{x_{0}}=d_{T}(x_{0},\tau)
where
\tau
is the bi-infinite geodesic from
p
to
q
in
T
; moreover, in this case
d_{a}=a^{-(p,q)_{x_{0}}}
is a visual metric on
\partial X
with the visual parameter
a
, for any
a>1
. This is the only explicit construction of a visual metric that we will need in this paper. For an arbitrary Gromov-hyperbolic geodesic metric space
(X,d)
it is known that is some
a_{0}>1
such that for every
1<a\leq a_{0}
there exists a visual metric on
\partial X
with visual parameter
a
. Again, see
[
35
]
for details. As noted above, when working with
\partial G\times\partial G
, we will usually equip it with the product metric
\widehat{d}
corresponding to some visual metric
d
on
\partial G
. For subsets of
\partial G\times\partial G
(including
\partial^{2}G=\partial G\times\partial G-diag
) we will use the restriction of the product metric
\widehat{d}
.

Our main result is (see Theorem
8.3
below):

{thm}

Let
\varphi\in{\rm Out}(F_{r})
(wher
r\geq 2
) be exponentially growing and let
L\in\mathcal{L}(\varphi)
,
L\subseteq\partial^{2}F_{r}
be an attracting lamination of
\varphi
. Let
\mathcal{E}_{L}\subseteq\partial F_{r}
be the set of endpoints of
L
.
Let
a>1
be an arbitrary visual parameter and let
d
be a visual metric on
\partial F_{r}
with visual parameter
a
, and let
\widehat{d}
be the corresponding product metric on
\partial F_{r}\times\partial F_{r}
.

Then the metric spaces
(\partial F_{r},d)
and
(\partial^{2}F_{r},\widehat{d})
we have

\dim_{H}(\mathcal{E}_{L})=\dim_{p}(\mathcal{E}_{L})=0,\quad\text{ and }\dim_{H}(L)=\dim_{p}(L)=0

where
\dim_{H}
is the Hausdorff dimension and
\dim_{p}
is the packing dimension.

To prove Theorem
1
, we establish a more general result, Theorem
7.2
below (see also Corollary
7.2
below for a more precise and general statement), which shows that the conclusion of Theorem
1
holds for any abstract algebraic lamination
L
on
F_{r}
whose laminary language
L_{\Gamma}
, with respect to some (equivalently, every) marked graph
\Gamma
in
\mbox{cv}_{r}
, is subexponentially growing in terms of its subword complexity (the latter condition is equivalent to the laminary language
L_{\Gamma}
of
L
having topological entropy
h(L_{\Gamma})=0
, see Remark
2.3
and Corollary
7.2
below).

{rem}

For a bounded metric space
S
one has the general inequalities
\dim_{H}(S)\leq\dim_{p}(S)\leq\overline{\dim}_{B}(S)
where
\overline{\dim}_{B}(S)
is the
upper box dimension
of
S
, see Remark
7.2
for the definition. As noted in Remark
7.2
below, our proof of Theorem
7.2
actually shows that standard ”cylindrical slices” of
\mathcal{E}_{L}
for a subexponentially growing algebraic lamination
L
on
F_{r}
have upper box dimension
0
. However, the upper box dimension is not well-behaved under countable unions. As again observed in Remark
7.2
, since for a nonempty algebraic lamination
L
the set of its endpoints
\mathcal{E}_{L}
is dense in
\partial F_{r}
, it follows that
\overline{\dim}_{B}(\mathcal{E}_{L})=\overline{\dim}_{B}(\partial F_{r})
, while
\overline{\dim}_{B}(\partial F_{r})>0
for all visual metrics. Thus the conclusions of Theorem
7.2
and Theorem
1
cannot be extended to the upper box dimension.

We also show, in Corollary
5
, that the growth function of the language of an algebraic lamination on
L
is an invariant (for a natural notion of equivalence) depending only on
L
and not on the choice of a marked metric graph in
\mbox{cv}_{r}
.
A result of Lustig
[
42
]
implies this fact for free bases of
F_{r}
, that is for the case where the marked graphs are
r
-roses, and we provide an additional argument to cover the general case.

We first prove Theorem
1
for the case where
\varphi\in{\rm Out}(F_{r})
is fully irreducible (see Theorem
8.2
) using a more direct argument, using the fact that in this case the laminary language
(L_{\varphi})_{\Gamma}
can be described via a primitive substitution (up to some technicalities associated with distinguishing between the cases where the attracting lamination
L_{\varphi}
is orientable or non-orientable, in the sense of
[
16
]
). The languages associated with primitive substitutions are known to be subexponentially growing by classic results from symbolic dynamics
[
44
,
50
]
. We also show, see Corollary
5
below, that, for an abstract algebraic lamination
L
on
F_{r}
, the equivalence class of the growth function of the laminary language
L_{\Gamma}
does not depend on the choice of a marked graph
\Gamma
.

For the case of an arbitrary exponentially growing
\varphi\in{\rm Out}(F_{r})
, we use a recent result of Hilion and Levitt
[
31
]
mentioned above, who proved that in this case for every attracting lamination
L\in\mathcal{L}(\varphi)
the laminary language
L_{\Gamma}
of
L
is subexponentially growing (and in fact its complexity function
p_{L_{\Gamma}}(n)
is at most quadratic). Using our Theorem
7.2
this implies Theorem
1
.

If
H\leq G
is a non-elementary word-hyperbolic subgroup of word-hyperbolic group
G
and the inclusion
i:H\to G
extends to a continuous map
\partial i:\partial H\to\partial G
, this extension
\partial i
is called the
Cannon-Thurston map
. The first nontrivial example of the Cannon-Thurston map was constructed by Cannon and Thurston
[
9
]
in 1984 for fiber surface subgroups of closed hyperbolic
3
-manifold groups fibering over the circle. More generally, if
H
is a non-elementary word-hyperbolic group
H
with a convergence action on a metrizable compactum
Z
and if this action extends to a continuous
H
-equivariant map
\partial i:\partial H\to Z
, this extension is also called the
Cannon-Thurston map
. (In the previous setting one takes
Z=\partial G
.) Associated to
\partial i
one can define
\Lambda=\{(p,q)\in\partial^{2}H:\partial i(p)=\partial i(q)\}
. Then
\Lambda
is an abstract algebraic lamination on
H
called the
Cannon-Thurston lamination
.

For
\varphi\in{\rm Out}(F_{r})
put
G_{\varphi}=F_{r}\rtimes_{\varphi}\mathbb{Z}
be the corresponding free-by-cyclic group. By
[
8
]
,
G_{\varphi}
is word-hyperbolic if and only if
\varphi
is
atoroidal
, that is
\varphi
has no nontrivial periodic conjugacy classes in
F_{r}
. If
G_{\varphi}
is word-hyperbolic, then a general result of Mitra
[
47
]
implies that the Cannon-Thurston map
\partial i:\partial F_{r}\to\partial G_{\varphi}
exists and is onto. Moreover, another general result of Mitra
[
46
]
describes the fibers of
\partial i
in this case. The quotient group
G_{\varphi}/F_{r}=\mathbb{Z}
has
\partial\mathbb{Z}=\{\pm\infty\}
. Associated to the two points comprising
\partial\mathbb{Z}
, there are
ending algebraic laminations

\Lambda_{\varphi},\Lambda_{\varphi^{-1}}\subseteq\partial^{2}F_{r}
on
F_{r}
; see Section
9
below for their definition. The results of
[
46
]
imply that if
G_{\varphi}
is word-hyperbolic then for two distinct point
p,q\in\partial F_{r}
one has
\partial i(p)=\partial i(q)
if and only if
(p,q)\in\Lambda_{\varphi}\cup\Lambda_{\varphi^{-1}}
. Thus the Cannon-Thurston lamination here is
\Lambda=\Lambda_{\varphi}\cup\Lambda_{\varphi^{-1}}
. In the case where
\varphi\in{\rm Out}(F_{r})
is atoroidal and fully irreducible, Kapovich and Lustig
[
39
]
described the precise relationship between
L_{\varphi}
and
\Lambda_{\varphi}
and proved, in particular, that
\Lambda_{\varphi}
is equal to the transitive closure of
L_{\varphi}
. Using this result and Theorem
1
above, we obtain (see Corollary
9
below):

{cor}

Let
\varphi\in{\rm Out}(F_{r})
is atoroidal and fully irreducible and let
G_{\varphi}=F_{r}\rtimes_{\varphi}\mathbb{Z}
be the corresponding free-by-cyclic group.

Let
\Lambda_{\varphi}\subseteq\partial^{2}F_{r}
be the ending algebraic lamination of
\varphi
. Let
\Lambda\subseteq\partial^{2}F_{r}
be the Cannon-Thurston lamination for the Cannon-Thurston map corresponding to the inclusion
F_{r}\leq G_{\varphi}
. Let
a>1
be an arbitrary visual parameter and let
d
be a visual metric on
\partial F_{r}
with visual parameter
a
, and let
\widehat{d}
be the corresponding product metric on
\partial F_{r}\times\partial F_{r}
.

Then for the metric spaces
(\partial F_{r},d)
and
(\partial F_{r}\times\partial F_{r},\widehat{d})
we have

\dim_{H}(\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda_{\varphi}})=\dim_{p}(\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda_{\varphi}})=0\text{ and }\dim_{H}(\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda})=\dim_{p}(\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda})=0

and

\dim_{H}(\Lambda_{\varphi})=\dim_{p}(\Lambda_{\varphi})=0\text{ and }\dim_{H}(\Lambda)=\dim_{p}(\Lambda)=0

In Section
10
we discuss several open problems raised by Theorem
1
and Corollary
1
and by the earlier related results.

Symbolic dynamical systems

We only give a brief overview of some basic facts and terminology from symbolic dynamics here and refer the reader to
[
44
,
50
]
for more detailed background information.

Throughout this paper we will assume that
\Sigma
is a nonempty finite alphabet consisting of at least two elements.

2.1
Shifts and subshifts

The free monoid
\Sigma^{\ast}
is the set of all finite words (including the empty word) over the alphabet
\Sigma
. For a finite word
v\in\Sigma^{\ast}
we denote by
|v|
the length of
v
. The alphabet
\Sigma
is endowed with the discrete topology, and there are two natural associated
full shift
spaces, the
one-sided full shift

\Sigma^{\mathbb{N}}
(where
\mathbb{N}=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}=\{0,1,2,3,\dots\}
) and the
two-sided full shift

\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
. Both
\Sigma^{\mathbb{N}}
and
\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
are equipped with the product topology. These spaces also come equipped with continuous
shift maps

s:\Sigma^{\mathbb{N}}\to\Sigma^{\mathbb{N}}
and
s:\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}\to\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
, defined by the same formula,
\big(s\left((x_{i})_{i}\right)\big)_{j}=x_{j+1}
. Closed
s
-invariant subsets of
\Sigma^{\mathbb{N}}
and
\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
are called
subshifts
. A finite nonempty subword of a finite, semi-infinite or bi-infinite word
w
over
\Sigma
is called a
factor
; the set of all factors of
w
is denote
\mathcal{F}[w]
.

For a subshift
X
of
\Sigma^{\mathbb{N}}
or of
\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
, the
language

\mathcal{F}[X]\subseteq\Sigma^{\ast}
is
\mathcal{F}[X]:=\cup_{w\in X}\mathcal{F}[w]
.

2.2
Substitutions

For a finite nonempty alphabet
\Sigma
, a
substitution
is a free monoid homomorphism
\theta:\Sigma^{\ast}\to\Sigma^{\ast}
such that for every letter
a\in\Sigma
we have
|\sigma(a)|>0
. Since
\Sigma^{\ast}
is a free monoid,
\theta
is uniquely determined by specifying a family of nonempty words
(\sigma(a))_{a\in\Sigma}
in
\Sigma^{\ast}
. A substitution
\theta:\Sigma^{\ast}\to\Sigma^{\ast}
is called
primitive
if there exists an integer
k\geq 1
such that for all letters
a,b\in\Sigma
the word
\theta^{k}(a)
contains
b
. For a substitution
\theta:\Sigma\to\Sigma
we denote by
\mathcal{F}[\theta]
the set of all words
v\in\Sigma^{\ast}
such that there exist
m\geq 1
and
a\in\Sigma
such that
v
is a factor of
\theta^{m}(a)
; that is

\mathcal{F}[\theta]=\cup_{m=1}^{\infty}\cup_{a\in\Sigma}\mathcal{F}[\theta^{m}(a)].

Let
\theta:\Sigma^{\ast}\to\Sigma^{\ast}
be a primitive substitution. There are two subshifts
X_{\theta}\subseteq\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
and
X_{\theta}^{+}\subseteq\Sigma^{\mathbb{N}}
naturally associated with
\theta
. The subshift
X_{\theta}\subseteq\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
is defined as the set of all
w\in\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
such that every factor of
w
belongs to
\mathcal{F}[\theta]
. Similarly,
X_{\theta}^{+}\subseteq\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
is defined as the set of all
w\in\Sigma^{\mathbb{N}}
such that every factor of
w
belongs to
\mathcal{F}[\theta]
. In both cases one has
\mathcal{F}[X_{\theta}]=\mathcal{F}[X_{\theta}^{+}]=\mathcal{F}[\theta]
. The one-sided subshift
X_{\theta}^{+}
also has a useful description in terms of periodic points of
\theta
. We say that
a\in\Sigma
is a
periodic direction
if there exists
k\geq 1
such that
\theta^{k}(a)
begins with
a
. The subset
\Sigma_{0}
of
\Sigma
of all periodic directions is nonempty. Choose
k\geq 1
such that for every
a\in\Sigma_{0}
for
\theta^{\prime}=\theta^{k}
we have
\theta^{\prime}(a)
starts with
a
.
Then, the word
a
is an initial segment of
\theta^{\prime}(a)
, the word
\theta^{\prime}(a)
is an initial segment of
(\theta^{\prime})^{2}(a)
, and so on. For each
a\in\Sigma_{0}
we can define an
eigenray

\rho_{a}\in\Sigma^{\mathbb{N}}
such that for all
m\geq 1
the word
(\theta^{\prime})^{m}(a)
is an initial segment of
\rho_{a}
. By construction one has
\theta^{\prime}(\rho_{a})=\rho_{a}
for all
a\in A
. It is then true (see
[
50
]
) that for every
a\in\Sigma
we have
\mathcal{F}[\theta]=\mathcal{F}[\rho_{a}]
and, moreover, the closure of the
s
-orbit of
\rho_{a}
in
\Sigma^{\mathbb{N}}
equals
X_{\theta}^{+}
. We will see further below that the attracting lamination for a fully irreducible outer automorphism
\varphi\in{\rm Out}(F_{r})
is defined via a rather similar procedure to
X_{\theta}
and
X_{\theta}^{+}
, using a train track map
f:\Gamma\to\Gamma
representing
\varphi
.

2.3
Complexity functions

By a
language
over a nonempty finite alphabet
\Sigma
we mean a subset
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\Sigma^{\ast}
.

For a language
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\Sigma^{\ast}
and an integer
n\geq 1
we denote by
p_{\mathcal{L}}(n)
the number of words
v\in\mathcal{L}
with
|v|=n
and we denote by
\beta_{\mathcal{L}}(n)
the the number of nonempty words
v\in\mathcal{L}
with
|v|\leq n
. By convention we also set
p_{\mathcal{L}}(0)=\beta_{\mathcal{L}}(0)=0
.
We define the
topological entropy

h(\mathcal{L})
of
\mathcal{L}
as

h(\mathcal{L})=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log p_{\mathcal{L}}(n)}{n}.

{rem}

Note that if
X
is a subshift of
\Sigma^{\mathbb{N}}
or of
\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
, the language
\mathcal{F}[X]
is closed under taking subwords and hence the complexity function
p_{\mathcal{F}[X]}(n)
satisfies
p_{\mathcal{F}[X]}(n)\leq p_{\mathcal{F}[X]}(n+1)
for all
n\geq 1
. Moreover, it is known
[
44
]
that in this case
p_{\mathcal{F}[X]}(n)
is submultiplicative, that is satisfies
p_{\mathcal{F}[X]}(n+m)\leq p_{\mathcal{F}[X]}(n)p_{\mathcal{F}[X]}(m)
, where
m,n\geq 1
.

For a subshift
X
of
\Sigma^{\mathbb{N}}
or of
\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
the
topological entropy

h(X)
is defined as

h(X)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log p_{\mathcal{F}[X]}(n)}{n}.

(It is known, in view of submultiplicativity of
p_{\mathcal{F}[X]}(n)
, that the above limit actually exists.) Note that by definition
h(X)=h(\mathcal{F}[X])
.

A key basic result from symbolic dynamics (see
[
50
, Proposition 5.12]
) says:

{prop}

Let
\theta:\Sigma^{\ast}\to\Sigma^{\ast}
be a primitive substitution. Then there exists
C>0
such that for all
n\geq 1

p_{\mathcal{F}[\theta]}(n)\leq Cn.

By summing up, the previous proposition directly implies:

{cor}

Let
\theta:\Sigma^{\ast}\to\Sigma^{\ast}
be a primitive substitution. Then there exists
C^{\prime}>0
such that for all
n\geq 1

\beta_{\mathcal{F}[\theta]}(n)\leq C^{\prime}n^{2}.

Note that Proposition
2.3
implies that if
\theta:\Sigma^{\ast}\to\Sigma^{\ast}
is a primitive substitution then
h(X_{\theta})=h(X_{\theta}^{+})=0.

Definition
\thethm

(Subexponential function)
.

We will say that a monotone nondecreasing function
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
is
subexponential
or
subexponentially growing
if for every real number
a>1
we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(n)}{a^{n}}=0
.

This condition has a useful equivalent reformulation:

{lem}

Let
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be a monotone nondecreasing function. Then
f
is subexponential if and only if

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log f(n)}{n}=0.

(Here we interpret
\log 0
as
\log 0=0
.)

Proof.

(1) Suppose
f(n)
is subexponential. If
f(n)=0
for all
n\in N
, there is nothing to prove. Thus we assume that
f(n)\geq 1
for all
n\geq n_{0}
for some
n_{0}\geq 1
.

Then
f(n)\geq f(n_{0})\geq 1
for
n\geq n_{0}
, and hence for
n\geq n_{0}
we have

\frac{1}{n}\log f(n)\geq\frac{1}{n}\log n_{0}\to_{n\to\infty}0,

so that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log f(n)\geq 0.

Now fix any
\varepsilon>0
and put
a=e^{\varepsilon}>1
.
By assumption

\frac{f(n)}{e^{\varepsilon n}}=\frac{f(n)}{a^{n}}\to_{n\to\infty}0

and therefore
f(n)\leq e^{\varepsilon n}
for all large enough
n
.
Hence

\frac{1}{n}\log f(n)\leq\varepsilon

for all large enough
n
, and so

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log f(n)\leq\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, we have

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log f(n)\leq 0.

It follows that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log f(n)}{n}=0,

as required.

(2) Suppose now that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log f(n)}{n}=0.

Choose
a>1
. Thus
\log a>0
. The above limit implies that for all large enough
n
we have

\frac{\log f(n)}{n}<\frac{1}{2}\log a.

Therefore for
n\to\infty
we have

\log f(n)<\frac{1}{2}n\log a

and

0\leq f(n)<a^{n/2}.

Then for
n\to\infty

0\leq\frac{f(n)}{a^{n}}<a^{-n/2}\to_{n\to\infty}0,

which implies

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(n)}{a^{n}}=0.

Since
a>1
was arbitrary, the function
f(n)
is subexponential, as required.
∎

Definition
\thethm
.

We say that a language
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\Sigma^{\ast}
is
subexponentially growing
if the function
\beta_{\mathcal{L}}(n)
is subexponential. Similarly, we say that a subshift
X
is
subexponentially growing
if the language
\mathcal{F}[X]
is subexponentially growing.

{rem}

Note that, for a subshift
X
,
p_{\mathcal{F}[X]}(n)
is monotone non-decreasing and
\beta_{\mathcal{F}[X]}(n)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{\mathcal{F}[X]}(n)\leq np_{\mathcal{F}[X]}(n)
for
n\geq 1
. Thus we have
p_{\mathcal{F}[X]}(n)\leq\beta_{\mathcal{F}[X]}(n)\leq np_{\mathcal{F}[X]}(n)
. Hence, in view of Lemma
2.3
, we have
X
is subexponentially growing if and only if
p_{\mathcal{F}[X]}(n)
is subexponential if and only if
h(X)=0
if and only if
h(\mathcal{F}[X])=0
.

Corollary
2.3
implies that for a primitive substitution
\theta:\Sigma^{\ast}\to\Sigma^{\ast}
the language
\mathcal{F}[\theta]
is subexponentially growing, as are the subshifts
X_{\theta}
and
X_{\theta}^{+}
.

Abstract algebraic laminations

We refer the reader to
[
35
]
for background info on the boundaries of word-hyperbolic groups. For the purposes of this paper the main cases of relevance are where
G
is a free group or a surface group.

Let
G
be a non-elementary word-hyperbolic group and let
\partial G
be its hyperbolic boundary. We put
\partial^{2}G=\partial G\times\partial G-diag=\{(p,q)\in\partial G\times\partial G:p\neq q\}
. We endow
\partial^{2}G\subseteq\partial G\times\partial G
with the subspace topology from
\partial G\times\partial G
. Note that
G
has a natural translation action by homeomorphisms on
\partial G\times\partial G
which leaves
\partial^{2}G
invariant and thus also gives an action of
G
by homeomorphisms on
\partial^{2}G
: for
g\in G
and
(p,q)\in\partial^{2}G
we put
g(p,q)=(gp,gq)
. The space
\partial^{2}G
also comes with a natural ”flip map”
\mathfrak{f}:\partial^{2}G\to\partial G
interchanging the coordinates, where
\mathfrak{f}:(p,q)\mapsto(q,p)
for
(p,q)\in\partial^{2}G
.

Definition
\thethm

(Abstract algebraic lamination)
.

Let
G
be a non-elementary word-hyperbolic group. An
abstract algebraic lamination
on
G
is a subset
L\subseteq\partial^{2}G
such that
L
is closed in
\partial^{2}G
, is
G
-invariant and
\mathfrak{f}
-invariant.
For an abstract algebraic lamination
L
on
G
a pair
(p,q)\in L
(where
p,q\in\partial G
and
p\neq q
) is called a
leaf
of
L
or an
abstract leaf
of
L
.

We also denote
\mathcal{E}_{L}=\{p\in\partial G:(p,q)\in L\text{ for some }q\in\partial G\}
and call
\mathcal{E}_{L}\subseteq\partial G

the set of endpoints of
L
.

Suppose that a non-elementary word-hyperbolic group
G
and that
G
acts properly discontinuously and cocompactly by isometries on a proper Gromov-hyperbolic geodesic metric space
(X,d)
.
By the Milnor-Svarc lemma, the orbit map
G\to X,g\mapsto gx_{0}
(where
x_{0}\in X
is some base-point) defines a
G
-equivariant quiasi-isometry, which then extends to a
G
-equivariant homeomorphism
\partial G\to\partial X
. In this situation we will identify
\partial X
with
\partial X
via this homeomorphism and will usually suppress this identification.

Then any two distinct points in
\partial G
can be connected by a (generally non-unique) bi-infinite geodesic in
X
. In this setting, if
L
is an abstract geodesic lamination on
L
and
\gamma
is a bi-infinite geodesic in
X
from
p\in\partial X
to
q\in\partial X
such that
(p,q)\in L
, we will say that
\gamma
is a
geometric leaf
of
L
with respect to
X
.

Note that if
\Lambda
is a geodesic lamination on a closed hyperbolic surface
S
then
\Lambda
can be viewed as an abstract geodesic lamination on
G=\pi_{1}(S)
in the above sense. Lifts of the leaves of
\Lambda
to
\tilde{S}=\mathbb{H}^{2}
are geometric leaves with respect to
\mathbb{H}^{2}
for this abstract geodesic lamination.

Outer space

For the remainder of this paper let
F_{r}
be the free group of finite rank
r\geq 2
. By that we mean that
F_{r}=F(a_{1},\dots,a_{r})
is a free group with a particular preferred free basis
A=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{r}\}
, although we usually will suppress the reference to this basis
A
. We will briefly recall some of the Outer space terminology and notations here and refer the reader to
[
2
,
15
,
26
,
51
]
for a more detailed background info.

4.1
Graphs

We will use here the same conventions and notations regarding graphs and metric graphs that are carefully set up in
[
17
]
. We briefly recall some of the relevant terminology here and refer the reader to
[
17
]
for more details. By a
graph
we mean a 1-dimensional CW-complex. For a graph
\Delta
the set of
0
-cells of
\Delta
is denoted by
V\Delta
and its elements are called the
vertices
of
\Delta
. The set of non-oriented open topological 1-cells of
\Delta
is denoted
E_{top}\Delta
and its elements are called
topological edges
. Every topological edge of
\Delta
is a copy of the open unit interval and thus admits two exactly orientations (e.g. as a 1-manifold). An
oriented edge
of
\Delta
is a topological edge of
\Delta
with a choice of an orientation on it. The set of all oriented edges of
\Delta
is denoted
E\Delta
. There is a natural fixed-point-free involution
{}^{-1}:E\Delta\to E\Delta,e\mapsto e^{-1}
corresponding to reversing the orientation on an oriented edge. Additionally, the attaching maps for
\Delta
naturally define the
enpoint maps

o:E\Delta\to V
and
t:E\Delta\to V
, where for
e\in E\Delta
, the vertex
o(e)
is the
origin
of
e
and
t(e)
is the
terminus
of
e
. [Note that we allow
o(e)=t(e)
.] By construction, for every
e\in E\Delta
we have
o(e^{-1})=t(e)
. For a vertex
v\in V\Delta
the
degree

\deg_{\Delta}(v)
is the number of
e\in E\Delta
with
o(e)=v
. A
metric graph
structure
\mathfrak{J}
on
\Delta
identifies every topological edge
e
of
\Delta
via a homeomorphism with a nonempty finite open interval
J\subseteq\mathbb{R}
in such a way that this homeomorphism can be continuously extended to a map from the closure
\bar{J}
of
J
to the closure of
e
in
\Delta
. In particular, this identification assigns every topological edge
e
of
\Delta
(and hence every oriented edge of
\Delta
) some new positive
length

\mathfrak{J}(e)
, namely the length of the interval
J
. For the most part, when working with metric graph structures, one only needs to use the information about these new edge-lengths. A
metric graph
is a graph
\Delta
together with a metric graph structure on it.

An
orientation
on a graph
\Delta
is a partition
E\Delta=E^{+}\Delta\sqcup E^{-}\Delta
such that for every
e\in E\Delta
we have
e\in E^{+}\Delta\iff e^{-1}\in E^{-}\Delta
.

4.2
Paths

For a graph
\Delta
, a
path
or
edge-path
in
\Delta
is a sequence
\gamma=e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
of elements of
E\Delta
such that for all
1\leq i<n
we have
t(e_{i})=o(e_{i+1})
. In this case we say that
n=|\gamma|
is the
combinatorial length
of
\gamma
and we put
o(\gamma):=o(e_{1})
and
t(\gamma):=t(e_{n})
. We also define the
inverse path

\gamma^{-1}:=e_{n}^{-1},\dots,e_{1}^{-1}
. If
\Delta
is a metric graph with a metric graph structure
\mathfrak{L}
, then for an edge-path
\gamma
as above, the
metric length
of
\gamma
is
\mathfrak{J}(\gamma)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathfrak{J}(e_{i})
. For any vertex
v\in V\Delta
we also view
\gamma=v
as a path with
o(\gamma)=t(\gamma)=v
and
|\gamma|=\mathfrak{J}(\gamma)=0
, and we put
\gamma^{-1}=\gamma
. A path
\gamma
in
\Delta
is
reduced
or
immersed
if
\gamma
contains no subpaths of the form
e,e^{-1}
where
e\in E\Delta
. The notions of an edge-path and of being reduced naturally extend to semi-infinite and bi-infinite edge-paths in
\Delta
. We denote by
\Omega(\Delta)
the set of all finite edge-paths in
\Delta
. For a subset
P\subseteq\Omega(\Delta)
, denote
\overline{P}=\{\gamma^{-1}:\gamma\in P\}
.

4.3
Outer space

The
unprojectivized Outer space

\mbox{cv}_{r}
is the space of all
\mathbb{R}
-trees
T
with a minimal nontrivial free discrete isometric action of
F_{r}
, considered up to
F_{r}
-equivariant isometry.
Here ”minimal” means that
T
has no
F_{r}
-invariant subtrees
T^{\prime}\subsetneq T
, and ”nontrivial” means that
F_{r}
does not have a global fixed point in
T
.
There is a natural topology as well as an action of
{\rm Out}(F_{r})
on
\mbox{cv}_{r}
, but their definitions are not important for the purposes of this paper. By abuse of notation, in the
\mbox{cv}_{r}
setting, we will not distinguish between an
\mathbb{R}
-tree
T
as above an its
F_{r}
-equivariant isometry class and will write
T\in\mbox{cv}_{r}
.

Note that if
T\in\mbox{cv}_{r}
then the action of
F_{r}
on
T
is properly discontinuous and cocompact, and the quotient space
T/F_{r}
is a finite topological graph
\Gamma
with
\pi_{1}(\Gamma)=F_{r}
where all vertices of
\Gamma
have degrees
\geq 3
. Moreover,
\Gamma
inherits the
metric graph structure
from
T
where we give each edge of
\Gamma
the same length as any of its preimages in
T
.
Also, starting with a a finite connected metric graph with all vertices of degree
\geq 3
and with
\pi_{1}(\Gamma)=F_{r}
, we get a point
T\in\mbox{cv}_{r}
by using the universal cover
\tilde{\Gamma}
with the lifted metric graph structure. In the case where such a graph
\Gamma
has a single vertex,
\Gamma
is an
r
-rose. If every petal of this rose is given length
1
, then
T
is isometric to the Cayley tree of
F_{r}
with respect to a free basis given by the petals of the
r
-rose.

In the above discussion, when we write
\pi_{1}(\Gamma)=F_{r}
, we mean that there is a particular isomorphism
\alpha:F_{r}\to\pi_{1}(\Gamma)
identifying our fixed free group
F_{r}=F(a_{1},\dots,a_{r})
with
\pi_{1}(\Gamma)
. This isomorphism, sometimes called a ”marking”, is an essential component of describing points of
\mbox{cv}_{r}
when they are represented by metric graphs
\Gamma
. In such a description of points of
\mbox{cv}_{r}
we always need a pair
(\Gamma,\alpha)
where
\Gamma
is a finite connected metric graph with all vertices of degree
\geq 3
and with
b_{1}(\Gamma)=r
and where
\alpha:F_{r}\to\pi_{1}(\Gamma)
is a marking. We refer to such a pair
(\Gamma,\alpha)
as a
marked metric graph
.

Recall that by convention from Section
3
, every
T\in\mbox{cv}_{r}
determines an identification
\partial F_{r}=\partial T
.
We also need to recall the construction of visual metrics on
\partial T
. If
X_{0}\in T
and
p,q\in\partial T
, we denote by
(p,q)_{x_{0}}
the length of the common overlap in
T
of the geodesic rays
[x_{0},p)
and
[x_{0},q)
. Note that
(p,q)_{x_{0}}=\infty
iff
p=q
for
p,q\in\partial T
. For an arbitrary
visual parameter

a>1
and a base-point
x_{0}\in T
, define the
standard visual metric

d_{a}
on
\partial T=\partial G
as

d_{a}(p,q)=a^{-(p,q)_{x_{0}}},

where
p,q\in\partial T
. It is well-known and easy to check that, since
T
is an
\mathbb{R}
-tree, this
d_{a}
is indeed a metric.

Laminary languages

Definition
\thethm

(Laminary language)
.

Let

be an abstract algebraic lamination on

. Let

and let
\Gamma=T/F_{r}
be the quotient marked metric graph with

. For the finite alphabet

consider the language
L_{\Gamma}\subseteq(E\Gamma)^{\ast}
consisting of all finite edge-paths
\gamma
in
\Gamma
with
|\gamma|>0
such that there exists a bi-infinite geodesic
\tau
in

from

to

\tilde{\gamma}
of

. We call

the

.

Laminary languages for abstract algebraic laminations on free groups were formally introduced and studied in
[
12

. All the definitions and results stated there deal with the case where
\Gamma
is an
r
-rose (that is
\Gamma
corresponds to some free basis of
F_{r}
). However, all of the discussion regarding laminary languages in
[
12
]
works essentially verbatim, or with very minor changes, for arbitrary marked metric graphs
\Gamma
as above.

Definition
\thethm

(Laminary subshifts)
.

Let
L
be an abstract algebraic lamination on
F_{r}
, let
T\in\mbox{cv}_{r}
and let
\Gamma=T/F_{r}
be be the quotient marked metric graph with
\pi_{1}(\Gamma)=F_{r}
. Denote by
X_{L,\Gamma}
the set of all bi-infinite reduced paths
w=\dots e_{-2},e_{-1},e_{0},e_{1},e_{2},\dots
in
\Gamma
such that
w
lifts to some bi-infinite geodesic in
T
connecting
p\in\partial T
to
q\in\partial T
with
(p,q)\in L
. Denote by and by
X_{L,\Gamma}^{+}
the set of all semi-infinite reduced edge-paths
w_{+}
in
\Gamma
(with edges in
w_{+}
indexed by
\mathbb{N}
) that appear as subpaths of bi-infinite paths from
X_{L,\Gamma}
. Then
X_{L,\Gamma}\subseteq(E\Gamma)^{\mathbb{Z}}
is a subshift with
\mathcal{F}[X_{L,\Gamma}]=L_{\Gamma}
. Similarly,
X_{L,\Gamma}^{+}\subseteq(E\Gamma)^{\mathbb{N}}
is a subshift with
\mathcal{F}[X_{L,\Gamma}^{+}]=L_{\Gamma}
.
We call
X_{L,\Gamma}
the
laminary subshift
for
L
with respect to the marked graph
\Gamma
. We call
X_{L,\Gamma}^{+}
the
one-sided laminary subshift
for
L
with respect to
\Gamma
.

Note that since
\mathcal{F}[X_{L,\Gamma}]=L_{\Gamma}
we have
p_{L_{\Gamma}}(n)=p_{X_{L,\Gamma}}(n)
for all
n\geq 1
. Since
X_{L,\Gamma}
, Remark
2.3
applies to
p_{L_{\Gamma}}(n)
. In particular, the function
p_{L_{\Gamma}}(n)
is monotone non-decreasing and submultiplicative.

By convention, we denote
\mathbb{N}=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}=\{m\in\mathbb{Z}:m\geq 0\}
.

Definition
\thethm
.

For monotone non-decreasing functions
f,g:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
, we say that
f\sim g
if there exists
C\geq 1
such that for all
n\geq 1
we have

f(n)\leq Cg(Cn),\quad\text{ and }g(n)\leq Cf(Cn).

Note that
\sim
is an equivalence relation on the set of all monotone non-decreasing functions
\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
.

Recall that in Section
2.3
, for a language
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\Sigma^{\ast}
we defined the functions
p_{\mathcal{L}}(n)
and
\beta_{\mathcal{L}}(n)
for
n\geq 1
. In the context of the laminary languages, by definition, for an integer
n\geq 1
we have
p_{L_{\Gamma}}(n)=\#\{\gamma\in L_{\Gamma}:|\gamma|=n\}
and
\beta_{L_{\Gamma}}(n)=\#\{\gamma\in L_{\Gamma}:|\gamma|\leq n\}
. If
\Gamma
is a metric graph with a metric structure
\mathfrak{J}
, we will extend the above definition of
\beta_{L_{\Gamma}}(n)
to this metric graph setting. Namely, for an integer
n\geq 1
put

\beta_{L_{\Gamma},\mathfrak{J}}(n)=\#\{\gamma\in L_{\Gamma}:\mathfrak{J}(\gamma)\leq n\}.

{lem}

Let
L
be an abstract algebraic lamination on
F_{r}
. Let
\Gamma=T/F_{r}
be the quotient marked metric graph with the metric structure
\mathfrak{J}
. Then there exists
C\geq 1
such that for all
n\geq 1
we have

\beta_{L_{\Gamma},\mathfrak{J}}(n)\sim\beta_{L_{\Gamma}}(n).

Proof.

Since
\Gamma
is a finite graph, there exists
C\geq 1
such that for every edge-path
\gamma
in
\Gamma
we have

\frac{1}{C}|\gamma|\leq\mathfrak{J}(\gamma)\leq C|\gamma|.

The conclusion of the lemma now follows.
∎

{prop}

Let
L
be an abstract algebraic lamination on
F_{r}
. Let
T\in\mbox{cv}_{r}
and let

be the quotient marked graph. Let

We will assume that

and there is nothing to prove.

Put

.

We give each edge of
\Gamma^{\prime}

1

,

Hausdorff and packing dimensions

We briefly recall the basic definitions related to Hausdorff and packing dimensions. We refer the reader to
[
7
]
,
[
21
, Section 6]
,
[
22
, Ch. 2]
and
[
32
]
for a more detailed background info.

Definition
\thethm

(Hausdorff measure)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and let
S\subseteq X
be a subset of
X
. Let
\delta\geq 0
be arbitrary

For
\varepsilon>0
put

H_{\delta,\varepsilon}(S)=\inf\{\sum_{i\in I}({\rm diam}(U_{i}))^{\delta}:S\subseteq\cup_{i\in I}U_{i},\,{\rm diam}(U_{i})<\varepsilon,\,I\text{ countable}\}

Thus the infimum is taken over all countable covers of
S
by sets of diameter
<\varepsilon
.
For
S
and fixed
\delta\geq 0
, the function
H_{\delta,\varepsilon}(S)
is monotone non-increasing in
\varepsilon>0
. Therefore the following (possibly infinite) limit exists:

H_{\delta}(S):=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}H_{\delta,\varepsilon}(S)=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}H_{\delta,\varepsilon}(S).

The number
H_{\delta}(S)
is called the
\delta
-dimensional Hausdorff measure
of
S
or just
\delta
-Hausdorff measure
of
S
.

It is known that
H_{\delta}
is a metric outer measure on
(X,d)
and that Borel subsets of
X
are
H_{\delta}
-measurable.

For a fixed
S
, the definition above directly implies that
H_{\delta}(S)
is a monotone non-increasing function of
\delta
. Moreover, it turns out that if
S\subseteq X
, there exists a unique number
c\in[0,\infty]
such that for all
0\leq\delta<c
we have
H_{\delta}(S)=\infty
and for all
\delta>c
we have
H_{\delta}(S)=0
. This number
c
is called the
Hausdorff dimension
of
S
. More precisely:

Definition
\thethm

(Hausdorff dimension)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and let
S\subseteq X
be a subset of
X
.

The
Hausdorff dimension

\dim_{H}(S)
is defined as

\dim_{H}(S):=\inf\{\delta\geq 0:H_{\delta}(S)=0\}

where this infimum is interpreted as
\infty
if
H_{\delta}(S)>0
for all
\delta\geq 0
.

Definition
\thethm

(Packing measures)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and let
S\subseteq X
be a subset of
X
.

For
s\geq 0
the
s
-packing pre-measure
of
S
is

P_{0,s}(S)=\limsup_{\delta\to 0+}\{\sum_{i\in I}diam(B_{i})^{s}\}

where
(B_{i})_{i\in I}
are all countable covers of
S
by disjoint closed balls of diameter
\leq\delta
and centers in
S
.

For
s\geq 0
the
s
-packing measure of
S
is

P_{s}(S)=\inf\{\sum_{j\in J}P_{0,s}(S_{j}):S\subseteq\cup_{j\in J}S_{j},\text{ where $J$ is countable}\}.

It turns out that, for
s\geq 0
,
P_{0,s}
is in general only a pre-measure on
X
, but
P_{s}
is an actual metric outer measure on
(X,d)
.

For a given subset
S\subseteq X
, the family
(P_{s}(S))_{s\geq 0}
exhibits a similar behavior to Hausdorff measures, with a unique critical value:

Definition
\thethm

(Packing dimension)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and let
S\subseteq X
be a subset of
X
.
The
packing dimension
of
S
is

\dim_{p}(S):=\inf\{s\geq 0:P_{s}(S)=0\}

where again this infimum is taken to by
+\infty
if
P_{s}(S)>0
for all
s\geq 0
.

Recall that for metric spaces
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
and a subset
S\subseteq Y
, a function
f:S\to Y
is
\alpha
-Hölder
(where
\alpha>0
) if there exists
C>0
such that for all
x,x^{\prime}\in S

d_{Y}(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))\leq Cd_{X}(x,x^{\prime})^{\alpha}

A
1
-Hölder function is called
Lipschitz
.

We note several basic properties of Hausdorff and packing dimensions:

{prop}

[
7
,
21
]

Let
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
be metric spaces. Let
S\subseteq X
and let
f:S\to Y
be a function.

1.

[
7
, (2.7.3)]
We have
\dim_{H}(S)\leq dim_{p}(S)
.

2.

If
Q\subseteq S
then
\dim_{H}(Q)\leq\dim_{H}(S)
and
\dim_{p}(Q)\leq\dim_{p}(S)
.

3.

If
f
is a a Lipschitz function then

\dim_{H}(f(S))\leq\dim_{H}(S)

and

\dim_{p}(f(S))\leq\dim_{p}(S)

In particular, if
\dim_{H}(S)=0
then
\dim_{H}(f(S))=0
, and similarly, if
\dim_{p}(S)=0
then
\dim_{p}(f(S))=0
.

4.

[
21
, Proposition 6.8.9]
If
f:S\to Y
is
\alpha
-Hölder then

\dim_{H}(f(S))\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\dim_{H}(S)

and

\dim_{p}(f(S))\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\dim_{p}(S).

5.

If
f:S\to Y
is a Hölder function and
\dim_{H}(S)=0
then
\dim_{H}(f(S))=0
.

6.

If
f:S\to Y
is a Hölder function and
\dim_{p}(S)=0
then
\dim_{p}(f(S))=0
.

7.

[
23
, Section 2.10]
If
A=\cup_{j\in J}A_{j}
for subsets of a metric space
(X,d)
, where
J
is countable, then

\dim_{H}(A)=\sup_{j\in J}\dim_{H}A_{j}.

8.

[
7
, Ch 2.7]
If
A=\cup_{j\in J}A_{j}
for subsets of a metric space
(X,d)
, where
J
is countable, then

\dim_{p}(A)=\sup_{j\in J}\dim_{p}A_{j}.

We also need the following fact regarding the behavior of the above dimensions under products:

{prop}

[
32
]

Let
(A,d_{A})
and
(B,d_{B})
be metric spaces and let
A\times B
be equipped with the product metric
d
.
Then

\displaystyle\dim_{H}(A)+\dim_{H}(B)

\displaystyle\leq\dim_{H}(A\times B)\leq\dim_{H}(A)+\dim_{p}(B)

\displaystyle\leq\dim_{p}(A\times B)\leq\dim_{p}(A)+\dim_{p}(B).

We will not explicitly use the above definition of the packing dimension, but we need the following estimate for it.

For a subset
S
of a metric space
(X,d)
and a number
\delta>0
, denote by
N(S,\delta)
the smallest number of closed balls of diameter
\leq\delta
needed to cover
S
. (And we put
N(S,\delta)=\infty
if no such finite cover exists.)

{prop}

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and let
S\subseteq X
be a subset of
X
.

Then

\dim_{p}(S)\leq\limsup_{\delta\to 0+}\frac{\log N(S,\delta)}{\log(1/\delta)}.

Proof.

We have
\dim_{p}(S)\leq\overline{\dim}_{B}(S)
(see (2.7.3) in
[
7
]
), where
\overline{\dim}_{B}(S)
is the
upper box dimension
of
S
. In turn,
\overline{\dim}_{B}(S)
is defined as

\overline{\dim}_{B}(S)=\limsup_{\delta\to 0+}\frac{\log N(S,\delta)}{\log(1/\delta)}.

The statement of the proposition now follows.
∎

7.1

{conv}

In this subsection we fix a free basis

which is a rose of

We also fix an algebraic lamination

on
F_{r}
and consider the one-sided laminary subshift

(A\cup A^{-1})^{\mathbb{N}}
. However,

\partial_{A}F_{r}
of

\partial_{A}F_{r}

Recall that an element

9
Ending laminations and the Cannon-Thurston map

Given
\varphi\in{\rm Out}(F_{r})
and an automorphism
\Phi\in{\rm Aut}(F_{r})
in the outer automorphism class
\varphi
, one can consider the free-by-cyclic group

G=F_{r}\rtimes_{\Phi}\mathbb{Z}=F_{r}\rtimes_{\Phi}\langle t\rangle=\langle F_{r},t|tht^{-1}=\Phi(h),h\in F_{r}\rangle.

Replacing the stable letter
t
in the above HNN-extension by
t_{1}=ut
, where
u\in F_{r}
, replaces
\Phi
by its composition with the inner automorphism of
F_{r}
corresponding to the conjugation by
u
. Therefore
G
only depends on
\varphi
rather than on
\Phi
and we denote
G_{\varphi}=G
. The group
G_{\varphi}
naturally fits into a short exact sequence

1\to F_{r}\to G_{\varphi}\to\mathbb{Z}\to 1.

Brinkmann
[
8
]
proved that
G_{\varphi}
is word-hyperbolic if and only if
\varphi
is atoroidal. A general result of Mitra
[
47
]
about short exact sequences of word-hyperbolic groups implies that if
G_{\varphi}
is word-hyperbolic then the inclusion
i:F_{r}\to G_{\varphi}
extends to a continuous
F_{r}
-equivariant map
\partial i:\partial F_{r}\to\partial G_{\varphi}
(which in this case is easily seen to be surjective). The map
\partial i
is called the
Cannon-Thurston map
.

In
[
46
]
Mitra also describes the fibers of
\partial i
for the case of short exact sequences of three word-hyperbolic groups. We will recall how Mitra’s result works for the case of a short exact sequence
(\clubsuit)
above, assuming that
\varphi\in{\rm Out}(F_{r})
is atoroidal (so that
G_{\varphi}
is word-hyperbolic).

Definition
\thethm

(Ending lamination)
.

Let
\varphi\in{\rm Out}(F_{r})
be an atoroidal element. Let
f:\Gamma\to\Gamma
be a topological representative of
\varphi
and let
T_{f}=\tilde{\Gamma}\in\mbox{cv}_{r}
, where every edge of
T_{f}
is given length
1
.

For
1\neq h\in F_{r}
put
\Lambda_{\varphi,h}\subseteq\partial^{2}F_{r}
to be the set of all
(p,q)\in\partial^{2}F_{r}
such that for every finite edge subpath
\beta
of positive length in the bi-infinite geodesic
\tau
from
p
to
q
in
T_{f}
, the projection
\gamma
of
\beta
to
\Gamma
has the property that for some
n\geq 1

\gamma
is a subpath of a cyclic permutation of the cyclicallay tightened form of the path
f^{n}(h)
in
\Gamma
. (The assumption that
\varphi
is atoroidal implies that
n\to\infty
as
|\beta|\to\infty
.)
Put

\Lambda_{\varphi}=\bigcup_{1\neq h\in F_{r}}\Lambda_{\varphi,h}.

It is known
[
39
,
18
]
that
\Lambda_{\varphi}
is an abstract algebraic lamination on
F_{r}
and that its definition above does not depend on the choice of a topological representative of
\varphi
. See
[
18
]
for an equivalent description of
\Lambda_{\varphi}
directly in terms of
\partial F_{r}
, without the reference to choosing a topological representative of
\varphi
or even a generating set for
F_{r}
.

The subset
\Lambda_{\varphi}\subseteq\partial^{2}F_{r}
is called the
ending lamination corresponding to
\varphi
.

A key result of Mitra
[
46
]
about the fibers of the Cannon-Thurston map for short exact sequences of word-hyperbolic groups, when applied to
G_{\varphi}
, implies:

{prop}

[
46
]

Let
\varphi\in{\rm Out}(F_{r})
be an atoroidal element and let
\partial i:\partial F_{r}\to\partial G_{\varphi}
be the Cannon-Thurston map.
Then for distinct
p,q\in\partial F_{r}
we have
\partial i(p)=\partial i(q)
if and only if
(p,q)\in\Lambda_{\varphi}\cup\Lambda_{\varphi^{-1}}
.

For a subset
S\subseteq\partial^{2}F_{r}
, denote by
Tran(S)\subseteq\partial^{2}F_{r}
the transitive closure of
S
in
\partial^{2}F_{r}
. That is,
Tran(S)
consists of all
(p,q)\in\partial^{2}F_{r}
such that for some
n\geq 1
there exist
(p_{0},p_{1}),(p_{1},p_{2}),\dots,(p_{n-1},p_{n})\in S
with
p_{0}=p
and
p_{n}=q
.

In
[
39
]
Kapovich and Lustig showed how
\Lambda_{\varphi}
is related to
L_{\varphi}
in the case where
\varphi\in{\rm Out}(F_{r})
is atoroidal and fully irreducible:

{prop}

[
39
]

Let
\varphi\in{\rm Out}(F_{r})
be atoroidal and fully irreducible.

Then
\Lambda_{\varphi}=Tran(L_{\varphi})=L(T_{-}(\varphi))
, where
T_{-}(\varphi)\in\overline{\mbox{cv}_{r}}
is the attracting tree for
\varphi^{-1}
and where
L(T_{-}(\varphi))
is the dual lamination of
T_{-}(\varphi)
, in the sense of
[
13
]
. Moreover,
\Lambda_{\varphi}\setminus L_{\varphi}
consists of
F_{N}
-orbits of finitely many points of
\partial^{2}F_{r}
.

{cor}

Let
\varphi\in{\rm Out}(F_{r})
is atoroidal and fully irreducible and let
G_{\varphi}=F_{r}\rtimes_{\varphi}\mathbb{Z}
be the corresponding free-by-cyclic group. Let
\Lambda_{\varphi}\subseteq\partial^{2}F_{r}
be the ending algebraic lamination of
\varphi
. Let
\Lambda\subseteq\partial^{2}F_{r}
be the Cannon-Thurston lamination for the Cannon-Thurston map corresponding to the inclusion
F_{r}\leq G_{\varphi}
.

Let
a>1
be an arbitrary visual parameter and let
d
be a visual metric on
\partial F_{r}
with the visual parameter
a
. Let
\widehat{d}
be the corresponding product metric on
\partial F_{r}\times\partial F_{r}
.
Then:

1.

For the metric spaces
(\partial F_{r},d)
and
(\partial F_{r}\times\partial F_{r},\widehat{d})
we have

\dim_{H}(\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda_{\varphi}})=\dim_{p}(\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda_{\varphi}})=0\text{ and }\dim_{H}(\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda})=\dim_{p}(\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda})=0

and

\dim_{H}(\Lambda_{\varphi})=\dim_{p}(\Lambda_{\varphi})=0\text{ and }\dim_{H}(\Lambda)=\dim_{p}(\Lambda)=0.

2.

For the metric space
(\partial F_{r},d)
and
(\partial F_{r}\times\partial F_{r},\widehat{d})
we have

\dim_{H}\left(\bigcup_{\psi\in{\rm Out}_{f.i.a.}(F_{r})}\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda_{\psi}}\right)=\dim_{p}\left(\bigcup_{\psi\in{\rm Out}_{f.i.a.}(F_{r})}\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda_{\psi}}\right)=0.

and

\dim_{H}\left(\bigcup_{\psi\in{\rm Out}_{f.i.a.}(F_{r})}\Lambda_{\psi}\right)=\dim_{p}\left(\bigcup_{\psi\in{\rm Out}_{f.i.a.}(F_{r})}\Lambda_{\psi}\right)=0.

Proof.

For (1), note that, for an atoroidal fully irreducible
\varphi
, by Proposition
9
we have
\Lambda_{\varphi}=Tran(L_{\varphi})
which directly implies
\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda_{\varphi}}=\mathcal{E}_{L_{\varphi}}
by the definition of transitive closure.
Then Theorem
8.2
implies that
\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda_{\varphi}}
has Hausdorff dimension
\dim_{H}(\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda_{\varphi}})=0
. Hence, by equivalences established in Corollary
7.2
. we have

\dim_{H}(\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda_{\varphi}})=\dim_{p}(\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda_{\varphi}})=\dim_{H}(\Lambda_{\varphi})=\dim_{p}(\Lambda_{\varphi})=0.

The same conclusion applies to
\varphi^{-1}
. Recall that by Mitra
[
46
]
, we have
\Lambda=\Lambda_{\varphi}\cup\Lambda_{\varphi^{-1}}
.
Therefore

\dim_{H}(\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda})=\dim_{p}(\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda})=\dim_{H}(\Lambda)=\dim_{p}(\Lambda)=0,

and (1) holds, as required.

Now part (1) implies part (2) since the index set
{\rm Out}_{f.i.a.}(F_{r})
is countable.

∎

Open problems

Theorem
1
and Corollary
1
raise several further natural questions.

{prob}

Let
\varphi\in{\rm Out}(F_{r})
is fully irreducible and atoroidal, and let
d
be a visual metric on
\partial F_{r}
for some
a>1
.

Let
\partial i:\partial F_{r}\to\partial G_{\varphi}
be the Cannon-Thurston map.
Let
\mathcal{N}\subseteq\partial G_{\varphi}
be the set of all is non-conical limit points for the action of
F_{r}
on
\partial G_{\varphi}
, and let
\mathcal{B}=(\partial i)^{-1}(\mathcal{N})
.

Is it true that
\dim_{H}(\mathcal{B})=0
?

A result of
[
33
]
provides a characterization of non-conical limit points for a Cannon-Thurston map
j:\partial H\to Z
for the convergence action of a non-elementary word-hyperbolic metric group
H
on a metrizable compactum
Z
. Applied to the setting of Problem
10
, this result says that
p\in\partial F_{r}
belongs to
\mathcal{B}
if and only if
p
is ”asymptotic” to the Cannon-Thurston lamination
\Lambda=\Lambda_{\varphi}\cup\Lambda_{\varphi^{-1}}
. There are both dynamical and geometric characterizations of being ”asymptotic” to
\Lambda
obtained in
[
33
]
. Using these results,
[
33
]
also proves that if the Cannon-Thurston map
\partial i:\partial H\to\partial G
is non-injective, where
H
is a non-elementary word-hyperbolic subgroup of a word-hyperbolic group
G
, then there always exists a non-conical limit point
z\in\partial G
for the action of
H
on
\partial G
such that
\#((\partial i)^{-1}(z)=1
. In the context of Problem
10
it follows that
\Lambda=\Lambda_{\varphi}\cup\Lambda_{\varphi^{-1}}
is a proper subset of
\mathcal{B}
. However, precisely how much bigger than
\Lambda_{\varphi}\cup\Lambda_{\varphi^{-1}}
the set
\mathcal{B}
is remains unclear, particularly in terms of the Hausdorff dimension of
\mathcal{B}
.

As noted above, the group
G_{\varphi}=F_{r}\rtimes_{\varphi}\mathbb{Z}
is word-hyperbolic if and only if
\varphi
is atoroidal, which includes a wide variety of situations where
\varphi
is atoroidal but not fully irreducible. In light of Corollary
1
it is natural to ask:

{prob}

Let
\varphi\in{\rm Out}(F_{r})
be an arbitrary atoroidal element. Does the conclusion of Corollary
1
hold for the set of endpoints
\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda_{\varphi}}\subseteq\partial F_{r}
of the ending lamination
\Lambda_{\varphi}
? Is it true that the abstract algebraic lamination
\Lambda_{\varphi}
is subexponentially growing?

Kapovich and Lustig
[
39
]
proved that if
\varphi\in{\rm Out}(F_{r})
is atoroidal and fully irreducible then
\Lambda_{\varphi}=L(T_{\varphi^{-1}})
where
L(T_{\varphi^{-1}})
is the dual algebraic lamination (in the sense of
[
13
]
) of the attracting tree
T_{\varphi^{-1}}\in\overline{cvr}
of the fully irreducible
\varphi^{-1}
. Here
\overline{\mbox{cv}_{r}}
is the closure of
\mbox{cv}_{r}
in the equivariant Gromov-Hausdorff convergence topology, or, equivalently, in the length function topology
[
48
]
.

Attracting trees of fully irreducibles provide basic examples of ”arational” trees in
\overline{cvr}
. Recall that an
\mathbb{R}
-tree
T\in\overline{\mbox{cv}_{r}}
is called
arational
if for its dual lamination
L(T)
no leaf of
L(T)
is carried by a proper free factor of
F_{r}
. Bestvina and Reynolds proved
[
6
]
that the hyperbolic boundary
\partial\mathcal{FF}_{r}
of the free factor complex
\mathcal{FF}_{r}
is equal to
\mathcal{AT}_{r}/\sim
where
\mathcal{AT}_{r}\subseteq\overline{\mbox{cv}_{r}}
is the set of all arational trees and where for
T,S\in\mathcal{AT}_{r}
we have
T\sim S
whenever
L(T)=L(S)
. This naturally raises the following question:

{prob}

Let
T\in\mathcal{AT}_{r}
be arbitrary. Does the conclusion of Theorem
1
hold for
L(T)
? Is it true that the abstract algebraic lamination
L(T)
is subexponentially growing?

Note that the work of Coulbois and Hilion
[
11
]
, for
T\in\overline{\mbox{cv}_{r}}
, provides a description of
L(T)
, in terms of a certain dynamical system given by a finite collection of partial isometries of a compact subtree
K
of
T
. It may be possible to use this description for
T\in\mathcal{AT}_{r}
, perhaps under some additional ergodicity assumptions, to gain information about the growth rate of the laminary language of
L(T)
.

Another situation where the structure of the Cannon-Turson laminations on
F_{r}
is well understood concerns extensions of
F_{r}
by purely atoroidal convex-cocompact subgroups of of
Out(F_{r})
. Every such subgroup
Q\leq{\rm Out}(F_{r})
is itself word-hyperbolic and defines an extension
1\to F_{r}\to G_{Q}\to Q\to 1
where
G_{Q}
is known to be again word-hyperbolic by a result of Dowdall and Taylor
[
20
]
. A general result of Mitra for short exact sequences of hyperbolic groups then implies that the Cannon-Thurston lamination
\Lambda
has the form
\Lambda=\cup_{z\in\partial Q}\Lambda_{z}
where
\Lambda_{z}\subseteq\partial^{2}F_{r}
are ending algebraic laminations on
F_{r}
. In
[
18
]
Dowdall, Kapovich and Taylor showed that for each

there is an
F_{r}
-free arational tree
T_{z}\in\overline{\mbox{cv}_{r}}
with

. Thus
\Lambda=\cup_{z\in\partial Q}L(T_{z})
. This fact is used in
[
18
]
to show that in this situation the Cannon-Thurston map
\partial i:\partial F_{r}\to\partial G_{Q}
is at most
2r
-to-one.

{prob}

Let
Q\leq{\rm Out}(F_{r})
be a purely atoroidal convex-cocompact subgroup defining the extension
G_{Q}
of
F_{r}
by
Q
. Let
\Lambda=\cup_{z\in\partial Q}\Lambda_{z}=\cup_{z\in\partial Q}L(T_{z})
be the corresponding Cannon-Thurston lamination on
F_{r}
.

(a) If

is a visual metric on
\partial F_{r}
, is it true that
\dim_{H}(\Lambda)=0
?

(b) Let
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\partial F_{r}
be the full
\partial i
-preimage of the set of non-conical limit points in
\partial G_{Q}
(for the action of
F_{r}
on
\partial G_{Q}
). Is it true that
\dim_{H}(\mathcal{B})=0
?

The most general versions of the problems discussed above concern the context of Cannon-Thurston maps for arbitrary word-hyperbolic groups:

{prob}

Let
H
be a non-elementary word-hyperbolic group with a convergence action on a metrizable compactum
Z
such that the Cannon-Thurston map
\partial i:\partial H\to Z
exists and let
\Lambda\subseteq\partial^{2}H
be the associated Cannon-Thurston algebraic lamination. Let

be a visual metric on
\partial H=\partial X
coming from a properly discontinuous and cocompact isometric action of

on some Gromov-hyperbolic space

.

(a) For the metric space
(\partial H,d)
, is it true that
\dim_{H}(\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda})=0
and
\dim_{H}(\Lambda)=0
?

(b) More generally, if
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\partial H
is the full
\partial i
-preimage of the set of all non-conical limit points in

, is it true that
\dim_{H}(\mathcal{B})=0

Note that since all visual metrics on

are Hölder-equivalent, the answer to the above problem does not depend on the choice of

.
