VA-ECMO–Facilitated Clot Debulking and PFO Closure in a Patient With Recurrent Pulmonary Thromboses
Authors: Kathryn Harris, Javier Farinas, Jamie Lesnock, Candice Lee, Barinder Hansra
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
A 27-year-old female with stage IIIc cervical cancer presented with dyspnea and stroke symptoms. Work-up revealed bilateral pulmonary embolisms, acute/subacute strokes, and a patent foramen ovale. After multidisciplinary team discussion, the patient underwent patent foramen ovale closure, complicated by cardiogenic shock requiring venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation. She successfully underwent pulmonary thromboendarterectomy, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation decannulation, and hospital discharge.

History of Presentation

A 27-year-old woman with stage IIIc1 human papilloma virus–related cervical adenocarcinoma treated previously with chemotherapy and radiation, recently diagnosed pulmonary embolism (PE), right common femoral deep vein thrombus, and renal vein thrombosis presented with progressive shortness of breath, pleuritic chest pain, and migraines. On presentation, she was tachycardic, normotensive, and tachypneic, requiring 6 L of supplemental oxygen to maintain normal saturations. Past Medical History

She was diagnosed with advanced stage cervical adenocarcinoma 1 year prior to presentation. She underwent chemoradiation therapy and cervical biopsies 8 months later that were negative for disease. Positron emission tomography/computed tomography (CT) with fluorodeoxyglucose showed increased uptake in the right pelvis with repeat biopsies that were negative for disease recurrence.

Two months later, she was diagnosed with extensive venous thrombosis including inferior vena cava, bilateral common iliac veins, left renal vein, and bilateral ovarian veins. She was found to have a submassive saddle PE and was treated with systemic and catheter-directed thrombolysis. She was discharged on apixaban and required 3 L of supplemental oxygen at rest.

One month later, she presented with recurrent exertional dyspnea. Her hospitalization was complicated by recurrent vaginal bleeding due to heparin infusion requiring multiple blood transfusions and type II non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction with high-sensitivity troponin elevated to 2,400 μg/L.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential for acute shortness of breath in this patient includes but is not limited to recurrent PE, acute coronary syndrome, heart failure with pulmonary effusions related to chemotherapy, pulmonary infarction, pneumonia, pulmonary hypertension (PH), chronic thromboembolic PH, and alveolar hemorrhage related to catheter-directed lysis.

Investigations

On admission, CT chest (Figure 1) showed chronic, nonocclusive bilateral PEs with evidence of multifocal consolidation consistent with pulmonary infarcts and small bilateral pulmonary effusions. A transthoracic echocardiogram showed normal systolic function with a flattened intraventricular septum, moderate right ventricular (RV) dilation with severely decreased function, severe tricuspid regurgitation, pulmonary artery dilation, and a pulmonary artery systolic pressure of 77 mm Hg. Color Doppler and agitated saline demonstrated a patent foramen ovale (PFO) with early right-to-left shunting. CT abdomen showed extensive clot burden extending into bilateral iliac veins. Management

Given ongoing anticoagulation, systemic and catheter-directed lysis was contraindicated for management of intermediate-high risk submassive PE. Her persistent PFO placed her at prohibitive risk of embolization with any catheter-directed intervention. Therefore, she was medically managed with systemic therapeutic anticoagulation. During her hospitalization, she developed an acute right facial droop and aphasia. CT of her head was negative for intracranial hemorrhage. She was treated conservatively with permissive hypertension for presumed acute stroke.

There was a multidisciplinary discussion involving cardiology, interventional cardiology, advanced heart failure, neurology, CT surgery, vascular surgery, gynecologic oncology, and palliative care as well as the patient and her family to address thrombus burden and potential PFO closure. Ultimately, because she had multiple thromboembolic events, it was decided to pursue PFO closure.

Left internal jugular venous, right radial arterial, and right femoral arterial access were obtained in anticipation of needing extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support. With anesthesia induction, she became hypoxic due to extensive shunting. She was emergently placed on venoarterial-extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) with inflow catheter seated in the right internal jugular vein and outflow catheter in the right femoral artery. Left subclavian venous access was obtained for PFO closure, a nontraditional approach because of patient’s clot burden. Under transesophageal echocardiogram guidance, a 25-mm Amplatzer PFO closure device (Abbott Cardiovascular) was successfully deployed across the interatrial septum with significant reduction in right to left shunting with agitated saline. Six passes with the ClotTriever device (Inari Medical Inc) were performed under direct transesophageal echocardiogram visualization via retrograde approach from the left subclavian vein for debulking of the inferior vena cava thrombus with removal of firm, well-organized thrombus. This was complicated by right iliofemoral occlusion that persisted despite balloon angioplasty. Because of the chronicity of residual pulmonary artery thrombus, definitive surgical therapy was required. The patient remained on mechanical support with VA-ECMO for 14 days post procedure. Additionally, milrinone infusion was started for management of RV failure. Pathology from the inferior vena cava thrombus was positive for multiple molecular biomarkers consistent with endocervical adenocarcinoma.

After numerous multidisciplinary team discussions with cardiac critical care, advanced heart failure, cardiothoracic surgery, gynecologic oncology, radiology, and the patient, various treatment options were presented: palliative chemotherapy with the intent to treat the active malignancy noted on pathology of the clot; weaning VA-ECMO and no longer pursuing cancer-directed therapy because of disease recurrence and anticipated significant morbidity and mortality; surgical intervention with pulmonary thromboendarterectomy (PTE) procedure. The patient was consistent in her wishes to pursue therapy that would allow her the opportunity to spend as much time as she could with her husband and child. The risks and benefits of the surgical approach were discussed, and she opted for surgical intervention. She was therefore transferred to an outside hospital for PTE. Prior to transfer, a right heart catheterization was performed that confirmed severe PH with bilateral pulmonary angiograms showing extensive occlusive disease.

Discussion

The presence of a PFO in patients who present with PE is a poor prognostic indicator. In this subgroup of patients, PFOs can not only cause severe hypoxemia but also paradoxical embolization. Paradoxical embolism can lead to acute ischemic strokes related to right-to-left shunting in the setting of RV dysfunction. Traditionally, patients with PFO and contraindications to fibrinolysis have undergone surgical embolectomy. However, the current guidelines do not address recommendations regarding management of PFO in patients with PE.

PFO closure in the setting of severe PH is high risk due to the possibility of acute RV failure with closure, leading to severe hypotension during anesthesia induction. Current management of acute RV failure includes volume optimization, ionotropic support, and, if necessary, mechanical circulatory support with VA-ECMO.1 In this patient, monitoring hemodynamics with right heart catheterization prior to induction on anesthesia may have been helpful to mitigate risks. For patients with severe PH, intracardiac echocardiogram for guidance during PFO closure as opposed to transesophageal echocardiogram could be considered to avoid risks associated with general anesthesia. However, in this case, the patient’s bilateral iliac vein thrombus precluded the use of intracardiac echocardiogram due to the high risk for embolization of thrombus with a femoral approach.

Currently, PTE is the only treatment for chronic thromboembolic PH, a rare long-term complication of recurrent PE, which improves hemodynamics and survival. Medical management with PH therapy reduces the degree of pulmonary vascular resistance.2 Treatment with PH therapy was not initiated in this case due to lack of efficacy in patients with recent PE. Given her recurrent headaches, she was unable to tolerate initiation of prostacyclin therapy. However, long-term riociguat therapy is indicated.3

Follow-up

The patient underwent successful PTE (Figure 2) bridged with VA-ECMO and subsequently transitioned to RV assist device support. She was decannulated from the RV assist device 1 week post procedure. Surgical pathology from bilateral pulmonary thrombus was consistent with metastatic cervical adenocarcinoma. She was initiated on riociguat therapy for persistent PH and discharged to inpatient rehabilitation after 4 weeks. She was then quickly able to improve her strength and mobility and returned home without oxygen. Conclusions

Multidisciplinary teams are needed to manage complex medical cases. In patients with PH secondary to recurrent PEs in the setting of underlying malignancy who cannot tolerate or fail anticoagulation, PTE should be considered.
