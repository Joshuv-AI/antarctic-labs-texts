Lifted Heston Model: Efficient Monte Carlo Simulation with Large Time Steps
NICOLA F. ZAUGG, LECH A. GRZELAK

Abstract
The lifted Heston model [ 1 ] is a stochastic volatility model emerging as a Markovian lift of the rough Heston model [ 13 ] and the class of rough volatility processes. The model encodes the path dependency of volatility on a set of N N square-root state processes driven by a common stochastic factor. While the system is Markovian, simulation schemes such as the Euler scheme exist, but require a small-step, multidimensional simulation of the state processes and are therefore numerically challenging. We propose a novel simulation scheme of the class of implicit integrated variance schemes [ 2 , 3 ] . The method exploits the near-linear nature between the stochastic driver and the conditional integrated variance process, which allows for a consistent and efficient sampling of the integrated variance process using an inverse Gaussian distribution. Since we establish the linear relation using a linear projection in the L 2 L^{2} space, the method is optimal in an L 2 L^{2} sense and offers a significant efficiency gain over similar methods. We demonstrate that our scheme achieves near-exact accuracy even for coarse discretizations and allows for efficient pricing of volatility options with large time steps.

Introduction

Stochastic volatility models are a core class of models in mathematical finance to price exotic derivatives
[
20
,
9
]
. Their flexibility enables robust calibration to most market regimes, and the models are able to price exotic derivatives consistently. The rise of rough volatility models, i.e., models whose volatility process resembles a fractional Brownian motion, promised to further advance the capabilities of the models by incorporating the inherent mean-reverting nature of volatility
[
7
,
8
]
. These models can capture certain features of volatility which regular stochastic volatility models cannot capture, for instance, the sharp term structure of the at-the-money implied volatility
[
13
]
. Such rough volatility models emerged in various shapes, all based on the idea of a fractional driver for volatility. A prominent rough model is the so-called
“rough Heston model"

[
13
]
, based on the classical Heston model
[
16
]
with a CIR-type mean-reversion process for volatility. The drift and diffusion terms are then adjusted using a fractional kernel to add a rough structure to the paths of the volatility. The volatility process
\tilde{V}_{t}
is defined as the equation

\tilde{V}_{t}=\tilde{V}_{0}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\frac{(t-s)^{H-0.5}}{\Gamma(H+0.5)}\lambda(\theta-\tilde{V}_{s})\,{\rm d}s+\nu\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\frac{(t-s)^{H-0.5}}{\Gamma(H+0.5)}\sqrt{\tilde{V}_{s}}\,{\rm d}W_{s},

(1.1)

where
\Gamma
is the gamma function,
\lambda,\theta,\nu,V_{0}
are the usual Heston parameters, and
H\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
the so-called
“Hurst"
coefficient, determining the roughness of the volatility paths.
While theoretically compelling, rough models face significant computational challenges due to the non-Markovian structure of rough volatility models and the lack of established numerical frameworks to address those challenges. The standard method is therefore to approximate the rough models with a Markovian system of differential equations. The fractional kernel
\frac{(t-s)^{H-0.5}}{\Gamma(H+0.5)}
, which causes the non-Markovian property, can be expressed as an infinite sum of exponentials and then truncated to finite terms
[
6
]
. This approximation naturally leads to a Markovian multidimensional factor model, where each factor is an auxiliary process (or state processes) representing one summation term of the truncated exponential sum. For the rough Heston model, the multidimensional model was then established as the
lifted Heston model

[
1
]
, representing a
Markovian lift
of the non-Markovian rough Heston model.

Article Aim

The numerical challenges inherent in rough volatility models motivate the development of new and innovative algorithms, both for rough volatility itself and for Markovian counterparts such as the lifted Heston model
[
19
,
6
,
3
,
21
]
. In this paper, we introduce a novel efficient sampling scheme for the lifted Heston model based on the integrated variance implicit (IVI) schemes
[
2
,
3
]
. The novel method obtains the optimal implicit equation between integrated variance and diffusion process of the volatility by utilizing a linear projection method. It is capable of simulating much larger time steps than a classical Euler method, provides robust (positive) samples for the variance process at all times, and outperforms other IVI schemes significantly in terms of convergence. A Python implementation of the algorithm can be found on
Github
. In this section, we introduced the stochastic setup of the lifted Heston model.
Section

2
covers a brief literature review for (lifted) Heston model schemes, and discusses the theoretical aspects of the novel scheme. It contains the main theorems to derive the required quantities for the method. In
Section

3
, we then present numerical examples to illustrate the effectiveness of the new method.

2
A Monte Carlo Scheme of Lifted Heston Model

The classical method to simulate a system of SDEs, such as the lifted Heston model, is the Euler-Maruyama (EM) scheme. The EM scheme iterates forward in time to obtain the paths and converges strongly in
L^{2}
with an increasing number of steps, as
\Delta t
goes to 0. Since the convergence depends on the size of the time steps, the scheme is not capable of efficiently simulating
larger time steps
, where fewer simulation grid points are required to simulate the paths. As in the standard Heston model, larger time steps in the EM scheme can cause the variance process
V_{t}
in (
1.3
) to become negative due to a large negative observation of
{\rm d}W^{2}_{t}
, leading to defective paths. This issue can be solved by introducing a volatility-path reflection or absorption at
0
, at the cost of introducing a bias in the simulation
[
11
,
4
,
18
]
.

The connection of the lifted Heston model to square-root processes offers the possibility of applying the well-established research on one-dimensional square-root processes, such as the regular Heston model, to the lifted Heston model. The Heston model, whose volatility process is a CIR process, has various numerical approaches and insights available in the literature, which are relevant to the lifted Heston model. The exact simulation scheme of Broadie and Kaya
[
10
]
introduced a new approach, where the sampling is focused on the
integrated variance
directly, rather than increments of the Gaussian noise process, as it would be usual for Euler-type schemes. Although this sampling proved to be computationally expensive, as it requires path-wise sampling from an expensive distribution, their research was improved using a cheaper stochastic collocation sampler
[
15
]
, and inspired simpler, non-exact, yet highly efficient schemes based on similar principles
[
5
,
2
]
. The derivations of Broadie and Kaya showed that large time step sampling is possible for stochastic volatility models when the integrated variance is sampled directly.
Abi Jaber and Attal
[
3
]
then suggested an integrated variance framework in a general framework for rough volatility models, utilizing an inverse Gaussian sampling scheme for faster convergence compared to a Gaussian sampling scheme. The core idea is to establish an implicit relation between the integrated variance process
X_{s,t}:=\int_{s}^{t}V_{u}{\rm d}u
and the diffusion term
Z_{s,t}:=\int_{s}^{t}\sqrt{V_{u}}{\rm d}W^{2}_{u}
for two fixed times
s<t
, from which one can derive an explicit distribution of
X_{s,t}
. This type of algorithm is called integrated variance implicit (IVI), and has been shown to be viable for the regular Heston model
[
2
]
. However, as the method relies on an endpoint discretization of a time integral, the linear approximation that governs the implicit relation becomes increasingly inaccurate for larger time steps, bounding its viability as a large time step scheme.

In this section, we introduce a novel IVI scheme called the
constrained linear projection (C-LP) scheme
for the lifted Heston model that is capable of taking larger time steps. The scheme derives the implicit relation through a linear projection onto a subspace in
L^{2}
, yielding an optimal linear approximation. The method avoids the trivial integral approximation, and we prove it to be the best linear approximation between
X_{s,t}
and
Z_{s,t}
in an
L^{2}
sense. The linear projection is derived by calculating conditional moments of
X_{s,t}
and
Z_{s,t}
, which we obtain directly from the stochastic processes.

2.1
Explicit solution with integrated variance

The starting point for the linear projection scheme is the explicit representation of the log-price process
\log S_{t}
. We apply a log transformation to the stochastic differential equation of (
1.2
), obtaining the SDE

{\rm d}\log S_{t}=\big(r-\frac{1}{2}V_{t}\big){\rm d}t+\sqrt{V_{t}}{\rm d}W^{1}_{t}.

(2.1)

In its explicit form, this SDE is equivalent to

\log\frac{S_{t}}{S_{s}}=\int_{s}^{t}\big(r-\frac{1}{2}V_{u}\big){\rm d}u+\int_{s}^{t}\sqrt{V_{u}}{\rm d}W^{1}_{u},\quad t_{0}<s<t.

(2.2)

Since the Brownian motions
W_{t}^{1},W_{t}^{2}
are correlated with a coefficient
\rho
, we can find orthogonal Brownian motions
\tilde{W}_{t}^{1},\tilde{W}_{t}^{2}
, such that
W_{t}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\tilde{W}_{t}^{2}
and
W_{t}^{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\rho\tilde{W}_{t}^{2}+\sqrt{(1-\rho^{2})}\tilde{W}_{t}^{1}
, such that we can write the equation as

\log\frac{S_{t}}{S_{s}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(t-s)r-\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{t}V_{u}{\rm d}u+\rho\int_{s}^{t}\sqrt{V_{u}}{\rm d}\tilde{W}^{2}_{u}+\sqrt{(1-\rho^{2})}\int_{s}^{t}\sqrt{V_{u}}{\rm d}\tilde{W}^{1}_{u}.

(2.3)

This shows that the log price
S_{t}
given
S_{s}
is given by the deterministic interest rate drift and three stochastic quantities. The second term
\int_{s}^{t}V_{u}{\rm d}u
is the integrated variance between
s
and
t
. The third and fourth terms are stochastic integrals with quadratic variation

\left\langle\int_{s}^{t}\sqrt{V_{u}}{\rm d}\tilde{W}^{2}_{u}\right\rangle=\int_{s}^{t}V_{u}{\rm d}u,\quad\left\langle\int_{s}^{t}\sqrt{V_{u}}{\rm d}\tilde{W}^{1}_{u}\right\rangle=\int_{s}^{t}V_{u}{\rm d}u.

(2.4)

Since the integrand
\sqrt{V_{u}}
is independent of
\tilde{W}^{1}_{u}
, we can sample the third term from a normal distribution once we have computed the integrated variance
\int_{s}^{t}V_{u}{\rm d}u
. The forward simulation
S_{t}
therefore reduces to obtaining a simulation of the integrated variance
X_{s,t}:=\int_{s}^{t}V_{u}{\rm d}u
and the stochastic integral
Z_{s,t}:=\int_{s}^{t}\sqrt{V_{u}}{\rm d}\tilde{W}^{2}_{u}
. We can then write the log price process as

\log\frac{S_{t}}{S_{s}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(t-s)r-\frac{1}{2}X_{s,t}+\rho Z_{s,t}+\sqrt{(1-\rho^{2})X_{s,t}}\,N_{s,t},\quad N_{s,t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).

(2.5)

For a defined integrated state process
X^{n}_{s,t}:=\int_{s}^{t}U^{n}_{u}{\rm d}u,n\leq N
, we express the variance increment in terms of these variables:

V_{t}=V_{s}+g_{0}(t)-g_{0}(s)+\sum_{n=1}^{N}-x_{n}X^{n}_{s,t}-\lambda X_{s,t}+\nu Z_{s,t}.

(2.6)

The simulation scheme therefore requires sampling of the quantities
X_{s,t},Z_{s,t}
and
X^{n}_{s,t},n\leq N
. The core idea is to sample
X_{s,t}
through an inverse Gaussian distribution with given parameters, and then derive the values for
Z_{s,t}
and
X^{n}_{s,t},n\leq N
from these samples. We derive the distribution parameter for the inverse Gaussian distribution. The main observation is that the quadratic variation of the stochastic integral
Z_{s,t}
is exactly equal to
X_{s,t}
. The objective of the scheme is then to obtain a
linear equation
between the two random variables
X_{s,t}
and
Z_{s,t}
, which enables a stopping time interpretation of the two quantities. Suppose we assume two processes
t\mapsto\hat{X}_{s,t},t\mapsto\hat{Z}_{s,t}
, such that the quadratic variation of
\hat{Z}_{s,t}
is equal to
\hat{X}_{s,t}
. Furthermore, assume a linear transformation for the random variables
\hat{X}_{s,t}
and
\hat{Z}_{s,t}
, i.e. coefficients
\alpha,\beta
, such that

\hat{X}_{s,t}=\alpha+\beta\hat{Z}_{s,t},\quad a.s.

(2.7)

In this case, since the quadratic variation
\langle\hat{Z}\rangle_{s,t}
is almost surely equal to
\hat{X}_{s,t}
, the process
t\mapsto\hat{Z}_{s,t},t\geq s
is a local martingale, as it is a stochastic integral, and has a quadratic variation process
t\mapsto\hat{X}_{s,t}
. We can then leverage the Dubins-Schwarz theorem
[
12
]
, which states the following:

Lemma 2.1

(Dubins-Schwarz
[
12
]
)
.

Let
t\mapsto\hat{Z}_{s,t}
be a local martingale with quadratic variation process
\langle\hat{Z}\rangle_{s,t}=\hat{X}_{s,t}
. Let
T_{t},t\geq s
be a sequence of random times
T_{t}=\inf\{u:\langle\hat{Z}\rangle_{s,u}>t\}
. Then, the process

\hat{W}_{t}:=\hat{Z}_{s,T_{t}},

is a Brownian motion and we have that
\hat{Z}_{s,t}=\hat{W}_{\langle\hat{Z}_{s,t}\rangle}=\hat{W}_{\hat{X}_{s,t}}
.

After application of the lemma to
Z_{s,t}
, the
Equation

2.7
becomes

\hat{X}_{s,t}=\alpha+\beta\hat{W}_{\hat{X}_{s,t}},\quad a.s.

(2.8)

The equation implies that if random variable takes value
\hat{X}_{s,t}=t
, then we have
\hat{W}_{t}=\frac{t-\alpha}{\beta}
. Therefore, we can write
\hat{X}_{s,t}
as the first passage time of the Brownian motion
\hat{W}_{t}
:

\hat{X}_{s,t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\inf\left\{t\geq 0:t-\beta\hat{W}_{t}=\alpha\right\}.

(2.9)

The first passage time of a Brownian motion with positive drift of a level
\alpha
is well-known to follow an inverse Gaussian distribution
[
14
]

IG(\mu,\nu)
with parameters
\mu=\alpha
, and
\gamma=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\beta^{2}}
. The IG distribution has a probability density function
f_{IG(\mu,\gamma)}
given by

f_{\hat{X}_{s,t}}(x)=f_{IG(\mu,\gamma)}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2\pi x^{3}}}\exp\left(\frac{\gamma(x-\mu)^{2}}{2\mu^{2}x}\right),\quad x>0.

(2.10)

We can now define a simulation scheme: We define approximation variables
\hat{X}_{s,t},\hat{Z}_{s,t}
, such that
\hat{X}_{s,t}=\alpha+\beta\hat{Z}_{s,t}
almost surely, and then conclude that
\hat{X}_{s,t}\sim IG(\mu,\gamma)
. We can subsequently obtain samples of
\hat{X}_{s,t}
by sampling from an IG distribution with given parameters, and derive
\hat{Z}_{s,t}
from the given equation. These samples are sufficient to forward-propagate the quantities
S_{t}
through
Equation

2.5
, using
X_{s,t}\approx\hat{X}_{s,t}
and
Z_{s,t}\approx\hat{Z}_{s,t}
. The samples for random variables
\hat{X}^{n}_{s,t},n\leq N
are then derived from
\hat{Z}_{s,t}
, also from a set of linear equations of the same form, a step which we will elaborate in
Section

2.3
. First, we consider how to obtain a linear equation
Equation

2.7
, such that the random variables
\hat{X}_{s,t}
and
\hat{Z}_{s,t}
can be sampled.

2.2
A linear approximation between
X_{s,t}
and
Z_{s,t}

Both
X_{s,t}
and
Z_{s,t}
are stochastic variables which share a dependency through the dynamics of the individual variance processes
U^{n}_{t}
, as seen from (
1.4
). For our scheme, we require an approximate linear dependency between the variables, which we derive using a
linear projection approach
. We obtain the linear equation by projecting the random variable
Z_{s,t}
onto the linear subspace spanned by
X_{s,t}
in
L^{2}
. The projection is derived from the conditional moments of
(X_{s,t},Z_{s,t})
on
\mathcal{F}_{s}
, which we obtain from the dynamics of the processes of the model, and we show how to obtain them in
Theorem

2.3
and
Theorem

2.5
. By definition the linear projection minimizes the
L^{2}
error
\mathbb{E}[(\hat{X}_{s,t}-X_{s,t})^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]
between the approximation
\hat{X}_{s,t}=\alpha+\beta\hat{Z}_{s,t}
and the true variable
X_{s,t}
. The approach yields the
best linear approximation
in a mean-squared sense (see
2.2
), and avoids the approximation of the integral altogether.

Remark

(Conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{s}
)
.

In the following results, we derive the conditional moments of the stochastic quantities required for the incremental changes as in (
2.5
),(
2.6
), where the conditioning is always on the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{s}
at the beginning of the increment. When it is clear, we will use the shorter notation
\mathbb{E}_{s}[X_{s,t}]:=\mathbb{E}[X_{s,t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]
to denote the
conditional expectation
on
\mathcal{F}_{s}
for increased readability.

The lemma below shows how to derive the linear projection given the moments of
(X_{s,t},Z_{s,t})
.

Lemma 2.2

(Optimal Linear Projection)
.

Let
s<t
be fixed and let
\hat{X}_{s,t}(\alpha,\beta)=\alpha+\beta Z_{s,t}
be a linear predictor for the random variable
X_{s,t}
. The expression
\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[(X_{s,t}-\hat{X}_{s,t}(\alpha,\beta))^{2}\right]
has a global minimum at
\alpha=\mathbb{E}_{s}[X_{s,t}]
and
\beta=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{s}[Z_{s,t}X_{s,t}]}{\mathbb{E}_{s}[Z_{s,t}^{2}]}
.

Proof.

The partial derivatives of
(a,b)\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[(X_{s,t}-\hat{X}_{s,t}(\alpha,\beta))^{2}\right]
are given by

\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[(X_{s,t}-\hat{X}_{s,t}(\alpha,\beta))^{2}\right]=2\mathbb{E}_{s}[\alpha+\beta Z_{s,t}-X_{s,t}],

and

\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[(X_{s,t}-\hat{X}_{s,t}(\alpha,\beta))^{2}\right]=2\mathbb{E}_{s}[(\alpha+\beta Z_{s,t}-X_{s,t})Z_{s,t}].

Setting the derivatives equal to
0
we obtain from the first equation that
\alpha=\mathbb{E}_{s}[X_{s,t}]
since
\mathbb{E}_{s}[Z_{s,t}]=0
, and from the second equation that

\mathbb{E}_{s}[Z_{s,t}X_{s,t}]=\beta\mathbb{E}_{s}[Z_{s,t}^{2}]\iff\beta=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{s}[Z_{s,t}X_{s,t}]}{\mathbb{E}_{s}[Z_{s,t}^{2}]}.

From the second-order derivatives, we confirm that the extreme point is a local minimum, and since the function is convex, the local minimum is a global minimum.
∎

We obtain the optimal linear predictor for
X_{s,t}
given
Z_{s,t}
by evaluating the expectation of
\mathbb{E}_{s}[X_{s,t}],\mathbb{E}_{s}[Z^{2}_{s,t}]
and the covariance
\mathbb{E}_{s}[Z_{s,t}X_{s,t}]
. Since the quadratic variation
\langle Z\rangle_{s,t}
of the diffusion term
Z_{s,t}
is
\langle Z\rangle_{s,t}=X_{s,t}
, we also have that
\mathbb{E}_{s}[X_{s,t}]=\mathbb{E}_{s}[Z^{2}_{s,t}]
by Itô’s isometry, which leaves two quantities to be determined for the linear predictor. We will now show how to derive these quantities directly from the dynamics of the processes given by (
1.2
-
1.4
) in the subsequent two theorems. As a byproduct, we also derive the quantities
\mathbb{E}_{s}[X^{n}_{s,t}]
and
\mathbb{E}_{s}[X^{n}_{s,t}Z_{s,t}]
for all
n\leq N
, which will be use subsequently as well. The derivation involves computing the respective quantities on the state processes
U_{t}^{n}
themselves, by solving
N
-dimensional linear systems, and then aggregating the processes to the dynamics of
V_{t}
.

Theorem 2.3

(Expectation of Integrated Variance)
.

Consider the lifted Heston model given by equations (
1.2
-
1.4
) and consider a pair of times
s,t>0
such that
s<t
. Let
\boldsymbol{\omega}=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\dots,\omega_{N})
,
\boldsymbol{U}_{s}=(U_{s}^{1},U_{s}^{2},\dots,U_{s}^{N})^{T}
and the matrix
A
be

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=-\lambda\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega}-\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{x})=-\lambda\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{1}&\omega_{2}&\cdots&\omega_{N}\\
\omega_{1}&\omega_{2}&\cdots&\omega_{N}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\omega_{1}&\omega_{2}&\cdots&\omega_{N}\\
\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&x_{2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&x_{N}\\
\end{pmatrix},

with
\boldsymbol{1}_{N}=(1,1,\dots,1)^{N}
the one-vector.
We can express the expectation of the integrated variance and state processes
X_{s,t}=\int_{s}^{t}V_{u}{\rm d}u
and
X^{n}_{s,t}=\int_{s}^{t}U^{n}_{u}{\rm d}u
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{s}[X_{s,t}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{n}\mathbb{E}_{s}[X^{n}_{s,t}]+\int_{s}^{t}g_{0}(u){\rm d}u

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\omega}\left(A^{-1}\left({\rm e}^{A(t-s)}-I_{N}\right)\boldsymbol{U}_{s}+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\right)+\int_{s}^{t}g_{0}(u){\rm d}u,

(2.11)

where
I_{N}
the identity matrix of size
N
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
a solution to the initial value problem

\frac{{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{u}}{{\rm d}u}=A\boldsymbol{\xi}_{u}-\lambda G_{0}(s,u)\boldsymbol{1}_{N},

(2.12)

between
[s,t]
with initial value
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s}=\boldsymbol{0}_{N}:=(0,0,\dots,0)^{T}
.

Proof.

The exception
\mathbb{E}_{s}[X_{s,t}]
is derived as the solution of a multidimensional initial value problem for the integrated state processes
X_{s,t}^{n}:=\int_{s}^{t}U^{n}_{u}{\rm d}u
. The first step is to express the expectation of
X_{s,t}
as a linear combination of
X^{n}_{s,t}
. From
Equation

1.3
, we find

\mathbb{E}_{s}[X_{s,t}]=G_{0}(s,t)+\sum_{n=1}\omega_{n}\mathbb{E}_{s}[X_{s,t}^{n}],

where
G_{0}(s,t):=\int_{s}^{t}g_{0}(u){\rm d}u
.
We will now derive a system of equations for
X^{n}_{s,t}
for all
n\leq N
. Using the state processes dynamics of
Equation

1.4
, we derive that for
n\leq N
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{s}[X^{n}_{s,t}]

\displaystyle=(t-s)U_{s}^{n}+\int_{s}^{t}-x_{n}\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[\int_{s}^{u}U^{n}_{l}{\rm d}l\right]-\lambda\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[\int_{s}^{u}V_{l}{\rm d}l\right]{\rm d}u

\displaystyle=(t-s)U_{s}^{n}+\int_{s}^{t}-x_{n}\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[X_{s,u}^{n}\right]-\lambda\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[X_{s,u}\right]{\rm d}u

\displaystyle=(t-s)U_{s}^{n}+\int_{s}^{t}-x_{n}\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[X_{s,u}^{n}\right]-\lambda\left[G_{0}(s,u)+\sum_{m=1}\omega_{m}\mathbb{E}_{s}[X_{s,u}^{m}]\right]{\rm d}u.

Defining
\mu_{t}^{n}=\mathbb{E}_{s}[X_{s,t}^{n}]
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}=(\mu_{t}^{1},\mu_{t}^{2},\dots,\mu_{t}^{N})^{T}
, the array of expectations can be expressed as a linear system

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}=\int_{s}^{t}\boldsymbol{U}_{s}+A\boldsymbol{\mu}_{u}-\lambda G_{0}(s,u)\boldsymbol{1}_{N}{\rm d}u,

or equivalently

\frac{{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}}{{\rm d}t}=\boldsymbol{U}_{s}+A\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}-\lambda G_{0}(s,t)\boldsymbol{1}_{N}.

We now split the system into two subproblems
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{u}=\boldsymbol{\eta}_{u}+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{u},u\in[s,t]
, where
\boldsymbol{\eta}
is stochastic containing
\boldsymbol{U}_{s}
, and
\boldsymbol{\xi}
a deterministic term. Let
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
be such that

\frac{{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}}{{\rm d}t}=\boldsymbol{U}_{s}+A\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t},\quad\frac{{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}}{{\rm d}t}=A\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-\lambda G_{0}(s,t)\boldsymbol{1}_{N},\quad\boldsymbol{\eta}_{s}=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s}=0.

In this case, we find that
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
solves the original system, since

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}+\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{U}_{s}+A\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}+A\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-\lambda G_{0}(s,t)\boldsymbol{1}_{N}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{U}_{s}+A(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t})-\lambda G_{0}(s,t)\boldsymbol{1}_{N}.

We can therefore solve the subsystems separately and add their solutions. We begin with
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}
, which is an inhomogeneous system and its solution is

\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}=\int_{s}^{t}{\rm e}^{A(t-u)}{\rm d}u\,\boldsymbol{U}_{s}=A^{-1}\left({\rm e}^{A(t-s)}-I_{N}\right)\boldsymbol{U}_{s},

where
{\rm e}^{A}
is the matrix exponential, i.e. the power series expansion
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{A^{n}}{n!}
. Similarly, the second subproblem is also inhomogeneous, but since
G_{0}
depends on
t
as well, we have

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}=\int_{s}^{t}-{\rm e}^{A(t-u)}\lambda G_{0}(s,u)\boldsymbol{1}_{N}{\rm d}u.

Since
\mu^{n}_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{s}[X_{s,t}^{n}]
, we find

\mathbb{E}_{s}[X_{s,t}]=\boldsymbol{\omega}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}+G_{0}(s,t)=\boldsymbol{\omega}\left(A^{-1}\left({\rm e}^{A(t-s)}-I_{N}\right)\boldsymbol{U}_{s}+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\right)+G_{0}(s,t).

∎

Remark

(Numerically solving
\boldsymbol{\xi}
)
.

To calculate the expectation of
X_{s,t}
, we require, in most cases
2
2

2

depends on the exact choice of
g_{0}
, a numerical solver for the initial value problem of
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
. These can be solved using standard libraries, such as
scipy.integrate.solve_ivp
. Note that we could have also solved the differential equation
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}
directly using such a numerical solver. This turns out to be infeasible, since the value then depends on
\boldsymbol{U}_{s}
, which differs path-wise, meaning that we need to solve an equation per path. By splitting the equation
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}
, we obtain a part
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
which requires a numerical technique, but which is independent of
\boldsymbol{U}_{s}
, and therefore needs to be calculated only once. The term
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}
, on the other hand, can be calculated analytically by a simple matrix multiplication.

Note that the expectation of the integrated variance could be expressed as an integration of
V_{t}
in
t
through
Equation

1.6
by interchanging the integration and expectation. However, as the expression is a Volterra equation, it is available analytically and therefore cannot be integrated in a straightforward way. For this reason, we derive the expectation as shown in the proof. From the theorem we can derive well-known quantities of the Heston model of
Equation

1.10
, such as the unconditional mean of variance.

Corollary 2.1

(Expectation of the Heston Model Variance)
.

Let
V_{t}
be the variance process in the Heston model and let
s<t
be fixed times and let
t_{0}=0
. We have
A=-\lambda
and
G_{0}(s,t)=V_{0}(t-s)+\frac{1}{2}\theta\lambda(t^{2}-s^{2})
. The conditional mean of the integrated variance is then given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{s}[X_{s,t}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\lambda}({\rm e}^{-\lambda(t-s)})U^{1}_{s}+\int_{s}^{t}-\lambda{\rm e}^{-\lambda(t-u)}\left[V_{0}(u-s)+\frac{1}{2}\theta\lambda(u^{2}-s^{2})\right]{\rm d}u

\displaystyle+V_{0}(t-s)+\frac{1}{2}\theta\lambda(t^{2}-s^{2}).

(2.13)

In particular, when
s=t_{0}=0
, we get the unconditional expectation
\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]
as

\mathbb{E}[X_{0,t}]=-\frac{1}{\lambda}(V_{0}-\theta){\rm e}^{-\lambda t}+\theta t+\frac{(V_{0}-\theta)}{\lambda}.

(2.14)

Taking the derivative of
\mathbb{E}[X_{0,t}]
we obtain the unconditional expectation of
V_{t}
as

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\mathbb{E}[X_{0,t}]=\mathbb{E}[V_{t}]=(V_{0}-\theta){\rm e}^{-\lambda t}+\theta.

(2.15)

The second term for the linear projection we need to derive is the covariance term
\mathbb{E}_{s}[X_{s,t}Z_{s,t}]
between the integrated variance and the diffusion term. We again reduce the problem first into its state-processes by computing
\mathbb{E}_{s}[X^{n}_{s,t}Z_{s,t}]
, from which we then aggregate the total covariance.
Theorem

2.5
shows how to derive the expression. For its derivation, we require a supporting lemma regarding the integration of a specific term.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let a matrix
A=-\lambda\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega}-\text{diag}(X)
be a full-rank matrix of size
N
and let
V,\Lambda
be the eigen-decompositions of
A
, such that
A=V\Lambda V^{-1}
. We have that the integral
\int_{s}^{t}{\rm e}^{A(t-u)}\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega}{\rm e}^{A(u-s)}{\rm d}u
with
\boldsymbol{1}_{N}=(1,1,\dots,1)^{T}
can be written as

\int_{s}^{t}{\rm e}^{A(t-u)}\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega}{\rm e}^{A(u-s)}{\rm d}u=VEV^{-1},

with
E\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
given by

\displaystyle E_{i,j}

\displaystyle=u_{i}v_{j}\frac{{\rm e}^{(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i})(t-s)}-1}{(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i})},\quad\quad i,j\leq N\quad\text{ if }i\neq j,

\displaystyle E_{i,i}

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{\lambda_{i}(t-s)}(t-s),\quad\quad\quad\quad i\leq N,

where
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in R^{N}
are row vectors such that
V(\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega})V^{-1}=\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{v}^{T}
, and
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{N}
are the distinct eigenvalues of
A
.

Proof.

First, we note that since
\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega}
is of rank one, the matrix in the eigenbasis of
A
is also of rank one, meaning that we can find vectors
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}
using a singular value decomposition of

Q:=V\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot V^{-1}.

We find matrices
L,M
and a vector
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},0,\dots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
with positive constant
s_{1}
, such that
Q=L\cdot\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{s})\cdot M^{T}
. Then, we find
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}
as the first row vectors of
L,M
, respectively, scaled by
\sqrt{s_{1}}
. We can then write the integral as

\int_{s}^{t}{\rm e}^{A(t-u)}\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega}{\rm e}^{A(u-s)}{\rm d}u=V\left[\int_{s}^{t}{\rm e}^{\Lambda(t-u)}\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{v}^{T}{\rm e}^{\Lambda(u-s)}{\rm d}u\right]V^{-1}.

Since
\Lambda
is diagonal, so is
{\rm e}^{\Lambda}
, and the integrant reduces element-wise to the expression

I_{i,j}=u_{i}v_{j}{\rm e}^{\lambda_{i}(t-u)-\lambda_{j}(u-s)}.

After element-wise integration from
s
to
t
, we see that the integral of
I
is
E
, and therefore conclude the proof of the lemma.
∎

The lemma will be used in the following result.

Theorem 2.5

(Covariance of Integrated Variance and Diffusion)
.

Consider the lifted Heston model given by equations (
1.2
-
1.4
) and let
A
be a matrix as in
Theorem

2.3
. Furthermore, let
A=V\Lambda V^{-1}
be its eigen-decomposition. For a pair of times
s,t>0
such that
s<t
, we have that the covariance between
X_{s,t}
and
Z_{s,t}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{s}[X_{s,t}Z_{s,t}]=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{n}\mathbb{E}_{s}[X^{n}_{s,t}Z_{s,t}]

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\omega}\bigg[\nu\left[VEV^{-1}-A^{-1}({\rm e}^{A(t-s)}-I)\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega}\right]A^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}_{s}

\displaystyle+\nu\int_{s}^{t}{\rm e}^{A(t-u)}\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{u}+G_{0}(s,u)\right){\rm d}u\bigg],

(2.16)

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{u}
solves the differential equation

\frac{{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{u}}{{\rm d}u}=A\boldsymbol{\xi}_{u}-\lambda G_{0}(s,u)\boldsymbol{1}_{N},

(2.17)

with
\xi_{s}=0
, and
E\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
a matrix given by

\displaystyle E_{i,j}

\displaystyle=u_{i}v_{j}\frac{{\rm e}^{(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i})(t-s)}-1}{(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i})},\quad\quad i,j\leq N\text{ if }i\neq j,

\displaystyle E_{i,i}

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{\lambda_{i}(t-s)}(t-s),\quad\quad\quad\quad i\leq N,

where
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in R^{N}
are such that
V(\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega})V^{-1}=\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{v}^{T}
, and
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{N}
are the distinct eigenvalues of
A
.

Proof.

For the covariance, we will again utilize the integral representation of the state processes. We derive the covariance
\mathbb{E}_{s}[X^{n}_{s,t}Z_{s,t}]
for any
n
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{s}[X^{n}_{s,t}Z_{s,t}]

\displaystyle=\int_{s}^{t}\mathbb{E}_{s}[U^{n}_{s}Z_{s,t}]{\rm d}u-\int_{s}^{t}x_{n}\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[\int_{s}^{u}U_{l}^{n}{\rm d}lZ_{s,t}\right]{\rm d}u

\displaystyle-\int_{s}^{t}\lambda\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[\int_{s}^{u}V_{l}{\rm d}lZ_{s,t}\right]{\rm d}u+\nu\int_{s}^{t}\mathbb{E}_{s}[Z_{s,u}Z_{s,t}]{\rm d}u

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s}[U^{n}_{s}Z_{s,t}](t-s)-\int_{s}^{t}x_{n}\mathbb{E}_{s}[X^{n}_{u}Z_{s,t}]{\rm d}u-\int_{s}^{t}\lambda\mathbb{E}_{s}[X_{s,u}Z_{s,t}]{\rm d}u+\nu\int_{s}^{t}\mathbb{E}_{s}[Z_{s,u}Z_{s,t}]{\rm d}u.

Since
Z_{s,t}
is a martingale and independent of
\mathcal{F}_{s}
, the first term is equal to zero. Similarly, we can split each remaining integral into two by writing
Z_{s,t}=Z_{s,u}+Z_{u,t}
. Now the term
Z_{u,t}
is centered and independent of
U_{u}^{n}
and
Z_{s,u}
, meaning that we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{s}[X^{n}_{s,t}Z_{s,t}]=-\int_{s}^{t}x_{n}\mathbb{E}_{s}[X^{n}_{s,u}Z_{s,u}]{\rm d}u-\int_{s}^{t}\lambda\mathbb{E}_{s}[X_{s,u}Z_{s,u}]{\rm d}u+\nu\int_{s}^{t}\mathbb{E}_{s}[X_{s,u}]{\rm d}u

\displaystyle=-\int_{s}^{t}x_{n}\mathbb{E}_{s}[X^{n}_{s,u}Z_{s,u}]{\rm d}u-\int_{s}^{t}\lambda\sum_{m=1}\omega_{m}\mathbb{E}_{s}[X^{m}_{s,u}Z_{s,u}]{\rm d}u+\nu\int_{s}^{t}\mathbb{E}_{s}[X_{s,u}]{\rm d}u,

(2.18)

where we also used that
\mathbb{E}_{s}[Z^{2}_{s,u}]=\mathbb{E}_{s}[X_{s,u}]
. This is again a system of differential equations in integral form. Let
\kappa^{n}_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{s}[X^{n}_{s,t}Z_{s,t}]
and
\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{t}=(\kappa^{1}_{t},\kappa^{2}_{t},\dots,\kappa^{N}_{t})^{T}
, and let
\eta_{t},\xi_{t}
be as in the notation of
Theorem

2.3
, such that
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}
, with
\mu_{t}^{n}=\mathbb{E}_{s}[X^{n}_{s,t}]
. We can write the entire system after multiplication by
\boldsymbol{\omega}
as the one-dimensional system

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{s}[X_{s,t}Z_{s,t}]

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\omega}\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{n}\left[-\int_{s}^{t}x_{n}\kappa^{n}_{u}{\rm d}u-\int_{s}^{t}\lambda\sum_{m=1}\omega_{m}\kappa^{m}_{u}{\rm d}u+\nu\int_{s}^{t}\sum_{m=1}\omega_{m}\mu_{u}^{n}+G_{0}(s,u){\rm d}u\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{n}\left[-\int_{s}^{t}x_{n}\kappa^{n}_{u}{\rm d}u-\int_{s}^{t}\lambda\sum_{m=1}\omega_{m}\kappa^{m}_{u}{\rm d}u+\nu\int_{s}^{t}\sum_{m=1}\omega_{m}(\xi_{u}^{m}+\eta_{u}^{m})+G_{0}(s,u){\rm d}u\right]

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\omega}\int_{s}^{t}A\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{u}+\nu\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\left[\boldsymbol{\omega}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{u}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{u}+G_{0}(s,u)\right]{\rm d}u.

The system is again split into two separate equations
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t},\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}
such that
\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t}
. The term
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t}
contains the deterministic part
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{u}
and
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}
contains the stochastic term
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{u}
. We begin with the differential equations for
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}
:

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}=A\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}+\nu\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}.

Since
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}=A^{-1}\left[{\rm e}^{A(t-s)}-I_{N}\right]\boldsymbol{U}_{s}
and the system is again inhomogeneous, we write

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}

\displaystyle=\nu\int_{s}^{t}{\rm e}^{A(t-u)}\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega}\left[{\rm e}^{A(u-s)}-I_{N}\right]A^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}_{s}{\rm d}u,

\displaystyle=\nu\left[\int_{s}^{t}{\rm e}^{A(t-u)}\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega}{\rm e}^{A(u-s)}{\rm d}u-\int_{s}^{t}{\rm e}^{A(t-u)}\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega}{\rm d}u\right]A^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}_{s},

since
A^{-1}
commutes with
A
and therefore with its exponential power series. While the second integral is easily analytically solvable, the first integral is more difficult. The matrix
\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega}
does not commute with
A
or its power series, and we cannot simply move it outside of the expression. We use the relation of
A=\lambda\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega}-\text{diag}(X)
to solve the integral, which we derive in
2.4
. The second integral is equal to
A^{-1}({\rm e}^{A(t-s)}-I)\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega}
. We conclude that

\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}=\nu\left[VEV^{-1}-A^{-1}({\rm e}^{A(t-s)}-I)\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega}\right]A^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}_{s}.

The second equation
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t}
is deterministic and solves the differential equation

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t}=A\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t}+\nu(\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}+G_{0}(s,t)),

which has a solution

\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t}=\nu\int_{s}^{t}{\rm e}^{A(t-u)}\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{u}+G_{0}(s,u)\right){\rm d}u.

We conclude that since
\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}
, we have therefore that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{s}[X_{s,t}Z_{s,t}]

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\omega}\kappa_{t}=\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\omega}\bigg[\nu\left[VEV^{-1}-A^{-1}({\rm e}^{A(t-s)}-I)\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega}\right]A^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}_{s}+\nu\int_{s}^{t}{\rm e}^{A(t-u)}\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{u}+G_{0}(s,u)\right){\rm d}u\bigg].

∎

2.3
A Linear Projection Algorithm

We combine the theoretical results of
Theorem

2.3
and
Theorem

2.5
and the insights from
Section

2.1
, in particular
Equation

2.6
, to derive the (constrained) linear projection (C-LP) algorithm to simulate paths of the lifted Heston model. We will first lay the basis of the algorithm, which is purely based on the linear projection. In a second step, we then introduce a constraint of the algorithm to ensure that it is well-defined at all times.

Suppose we define a time discretization
t_{0}<t_{1}<t_{2}\dots<t_{M}
and are currently at a time
t_{i}
with
0\leq i\leq M
. Given the current state of
\boldsymbol{U}_{t_{i}}
, we compute the (conditional) linear projection of
Z_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}
onto the linear subspace of
X_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}
. We define:

\alpha_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}],\quad\beta_{i}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}{\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}Z_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}]},

(2.19)

using the theorems from the previous section. Note that
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
are conditional expectations, and hence path-wise distinct. The linear approximation defines the relation
\hat{X}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}=\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}\hat{Z}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}
, where
\hat{X}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}
then follows an inverse Gaussian distribution
\hat{X}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}\sim IG(\mu,\gamma)
with coefficients
\mu=\alpha
,
\gamma=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\beta^{2}}
. We sample (path-wise) from such a distribution to obtain samples of
\hat{X}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}
, and samples for
\hat{Z}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}
are then calculated as
\hat{Z}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}=\frac{\hat{X}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}-\alpha_{i}}{\beta_{i}}
. To update the state processes
\boldsymbol{U}_{t_{i}}
to
\boldsymbol{U}_{t_{t+1}}
, we also require
\hat{X}^{n}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}
for all
n\leq N
. Theorems
2.3
and
2.5
also provide linear projection coefficients for
\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X^{n}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}]
and
\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X^{n}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}Z_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}]
, from which we obtain

\alpha^{n}_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X^{n}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}],\quad\beta^{n}_{i}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}{\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X^{n}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}Z_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}]},\quad n\leq N.

(2.20)

This means that we obtain the samples

\hat{X}^{n}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}=\alpha^{n}_{i}+\beta^{n}_{i}\hat{Z}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}.

(2.21)

The samples for
\hat{X}^{n}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}
are consistent with
\hat{X}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}
, as we have that

\hat{X}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{n}\hat{X}^{n}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}+\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}g_{0}(u){\rm d}u.

(2.22)

We can now update the state processes as

\hat{U}^{n}_{t_{i+1}}=\hat{U}^{n}_{t_{i}}-x_{n}\hat{X}^{n}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}-\lambda\hat{X}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}+\nu\hat{Z}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}},

(2.23)

and derive
\hat{V}_{i+1}
for the new states from
Equation

2.6
(or directly as the weighted sum of
U_{t_{i+1}}^{n}
), as well as the new values for the asset price from
Equation

2.5
.

2.4
Constraining the Slope

While the linear projection method yields the
L^{2}
-optimal relation between the variables, it is nevertheless an approximation of a nonlinear relation. For this reason we have to ensure that a potential approximation scheme
\left\{(\hat{X}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}},\hat{Z}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}):0\leq i\leq M-1\right\}
of the process is well-defined at all times. While deriving the coefficients
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}
is not problematic, we have to ensure that the following conditions are fulfilled:

\alpha_{i}>0\text{ and }\beta_{i}\neq 0,\quad 0\leq i\leq M-1.

The conditions ensure that the inverse Gaussian distribution is well-defined at all time steps, and that
\hat{Z}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}
can be computed. To ensure these properties, we will systematically restrict the parameter choice for
\beta_{i}
on a
feasibility domain
, where it is ensured that
\beta_{i}\neq 0
and the subsequent step leads to a parameter
\alpha_{i+1}>0
. Since
\alpha_{i+1}
is the expectation of
\int_{t_{i+1}}^{t_{i+2}}V_{u}{\rm d}u
, conditioned on
\hat{V}_{t_{i+1}}
, the restriction is obtained by ensuring that
\hat{V}_{t_{i+1}}=\boldsymbol{\omega}\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{t+1}+g_{0}(t_{i+1})
is positive at all times. To ensure that this quantity remains positive, we define a set of constraints with the current state for
\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{t}
for the parameter choice of
\beta_{i}
, and the updated values. We express the value for the updated variance using (
2.23
), i.e.
\hat{V}_{t_{i+1}}=\boldsymbol{\omega}\boldsymbol{\hat{U}}_{t_{i+1}}+g_{0}(t_{i+1})
, as a function of the value of
x=\hat{X}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}
, and the coefficient
\beta
:

C_{i}(x,\beta)=\boldsymbol{\omega}\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{t_{i}}+\boldsymbol{\omega}(-\text{diag}({\boldsymbol{x}})\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}(x)-\lambda x+\nu\frac{x-\alpha_{i}}{\beta})+g_{0}(t_{i+1}),

(2.24)

with
\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}(x)=\left(\hat{X}^{1}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}(x),\hat{X}^{2}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}(x),\dots,\hat{X}^{N}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}(x)\right)^{T}
and
\hat{X}^{n}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}(x)=\alpha^{n}_{i}+\frac{\beta^{n}_{i}(x-\alpha_{i})}{\beta_{i}}
.
Note that the projection for
\hat{X}^{n}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}
remains unchanged, since the state processes
\hat{U}^{n}_{t_{i+1}}
can take negative values while still leading to positive
\hat{V}_{t_{i+1}}
, and hence require no constraining. The function
C(x,\beta)
defines a set of open constraints
\left\{C_{i}(x,\beta)>0:x\in(0,\infty)\right\}
. Since the set contains uncountably many constraints, we aim to replace the set with an equivalent set of constraints that is much smaller. It turns out that the set can be reduced to a single closed constraint
C_{i}(0,\beta)\geq 0
if we restrict
\beta
to a smaller domain.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\left\{C_{i}(x,\beta)>0:x\in[0,\infty)\right\}
be a collection of constraints given by
Equation

2.24
. Denote the scalar
\bar{\omega}:=\boldsymbol{\omega}\boldsymbol{1}_{N}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{n}
. The coefficient
\beta\in(0,\infty)
satisfies

\beta\leq\beta^{L_{i}}:=\frac{\nu\bar{\omega}}{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{n}\left(x_{n}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X^{n}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}Z_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}]}{\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}Z_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}]}+\lambda\right)},

(2.25)

and
C_{i}(0,\beta)\geq 0
if and only if all constraints in
\left\{C_{i}(x,\beta)>0:x\in[0,\infty)\right\}
are satisfied. Furthermore, the linear projection estimate for
\beta_{i}
is smaller than
\beta^{L_{i}}
.

Proof.

We note that
x\mapsto C_{i}(x,\beta)
is a linear function for fixed
\beta
. This means that the constraint at
0
fulfills all constraints for a
\beta
if and only if the slope is positive, meaning that
\frac{{\rm d}C_{i}(x,\beta)}{{\rm d}x}\geq 0
. If the slope is negative, there is an
x>0
such that
C(x,\beta)<0
. The derivative can be found by differentiating
Equation

2.24
:

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}C_{i}(x,\beta)}{{\rm d}x}=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{n}\left(-x_{n}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X^{n}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}Z_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}]}{\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}Z_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}]}-\lambda+\frac{\nu}{\beta}\right).

Since
\nu>0
, the derivative is non-negative for all
\beta\in(0,\beta^{L_{i}}]
if and only if it is the case for
\beta=\beta^{L_{i}}
. The upper boundary
\beta^{L_{i}}
is chosen such that the derivative is
0
.

To prove that
\beta_{i}\leq\beta^{L_{i}}
, we show that the derivative is positive at
\beta_{i}
. We have

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}C_{i}(x,\beta_{i})}{{\rm d}x}=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{n}\left(-x_{n}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X^{n}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}Z_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}]}{\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}Z_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}]}-\lambda+\frac{\nu\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}]}{\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}Z_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}]}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}Z_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}]}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{n}\left(-x_{n}\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X^{n}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}Z_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}]-\lambda\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}Z_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}]+\nu\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}]\right).

The last expression is similar to the calculations of
Theorem

2.5
. We can show that

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X^{n}_{t_{i},t}Z_{t_{i},t}]\bigg|_{t=t_{i+1}}=-x_{n}\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X^{n}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}Z_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}]-\lambda\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}Z_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}]+\nu\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}],

by differentiation the
Theorem

2.5
. We will hence show that

\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}Z_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}],\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X_{t_{i},t}Z_{t_{i},t}]\bigg|_{t=t_{i+1}}=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{n}\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X^{n}_{t_{i},t}Z_{t_{i},t}]\bigg|_{t=t_{i+1}},

are both positive, in which case the slope is indeed positive. We prove this from the differential equation of
\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}Z_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}]
. Again, from the proof of
Theorem

2.5
, we know that

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{t}=A\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{t}+\nu\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\mu_{t},

where
\kappa^{n}_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X^{n}_{t_{i},t}Z_{t_{i},t}]
and
\mu_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X_{t_{i},t}]
for
t\geq t_{i}
. The system has a solution

\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{t}=\nu\int_{t_{i}}^{t}{\rm e}^{A(t-u)}\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\mu_{u}{\rm d}u.

To obtain
\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X_{t_{i},t}Z_{t_{i},t}]
we multiply with
\boldsymbol{\omega}
:

\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X_{t_{i},t}Z_{t_{i},t}]=\nu\boldsymbol{\omega}\int_{t_{i}}^{t}{\rm e}^{A(t-u)}\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\mu_{u}{\rm d}u=\nu\int_{t_{i}}^{t}\boldsymbol{\omega}{\rm e}^{A(t-u)}\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\mu_{u}{\rm d}u.

We will now prove that the integrant is non-negative for all
u\in[t_{i},t]
. This implies that
\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X_{t_{i},t}Z_{t_{i},t}]
is non-negative and increasing, hence has a non-negative derivative. Since
\mu_{t}
is non-negative, as
V_{t}
is almost surely non-negative, it suffices to show that
\boldsymbol{\omega}{\rm e}^{A(t-u)}\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\geq 0
for all
u\in[t_{i},t].

The exponential of the matrix
A=-\lambda\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega}-\text{diag}(x)
can be written as

{\rm e}^{A(t-u)}={\rm e}^{-\lambda\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\rm e}^{-\text{diag}(x)(t-u)},

since the terms commute. The term
{\rm e}^{-\text{diag}(x)(t-u)}
is the exponential of a diagonal matrix, and hence has only positive entries on the diagonals. Multiplying with
\boldsymbol{1}_{N}
then yields

{\rm e}^{-\text{diag}(x)(t-u)}\boldsymbol{1}_{N}=({\rm e}^{-x_{1}(t-u)},{\rm e}^{-x_{2}(t-u)},\dots,{\rm e}^{-x_{N}(t-u)})^{T},\quad u\in[t_{i},t],

which is a vector of only non-negative entries.

We now consider the other term
\boldsymbol{\omega}{\rm e}^{-\lambda\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega}}
. We calculate the
m
power
(\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega})^{m}
for any integer
m\geq 0
. Then,
(\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega})^{m}=\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\bar{\omega}^{m-1}\boldsymbol{\omega}
. This means that we can write the exponential as

\boldsymbol{\omega}{\rm e}^{-\lambda\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega}}=\boldsymbol{\omega}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{m}(\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\boldsymbol{\omega})^{m}}{m!}=\boldsymbol{\omega}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{m}\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\bar{\omega}^{m-1}\boldsymbol{\omega}}{m!}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{m}\bar{\omega}^{m}\boldsymbol{\omega}}{m!},

which is exactly equal to
{\rm e}^{-\lambda\bar{\omega}}\boldsymbol{\omega}
. This is again a (row) vector with only positive entries, since
\omega_{n}\geq 0,n\leq N
. The inner product of the two non-negative vectors is therefore a non-negative scalar, which concludes the proof that
\boldsymbol{\omega}{\rm e}^{A(t-u)}\boldsymbol{1}_{N}\mu_{u}
is non-negative for all
u\in[t_{i},t]
, and therefore proves the claim.
∎

The conclusion from the lemma is that it suffices to consider a single closed constraint to ensure that
\hat{V}_{t_{i+1}}
stays positive. This constraint helps us define a definition of feasibility.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\boldsymbol{U}_{t_{i}}
be a given state of the lifted Heston model at time
t_{i}
, and
t_{i+1}>t_{i}
a second time for a time step
t_{i+1}-t_{i}
. We call the coefficient
\beta

feasible
if
\beta\in(0,\beta^{L_{i}}]
and
C_{i}(0,\beta)\geq 0
.

With the definition of feasibility, we can define the constrained algorithm by obtaining the parameters
\alpha_{i},\beta^{C}_{i}
as the
L^{2}
minimizer, under the constraint
C_{i}(0,\beta)
. The coefficient
\beta^{C}_{i}
is then found as

\beta^{C}_{i}\;:=\;\arginf_{\beta\in(0,\beta^{L_{i}}]}\quad\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}\!\left[\left(X_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}-(\alpha_{i}+\beta Z_{t_{i},t_{i+1}})\right)^{2}\right]\quad\text{s.t.}\quad C_{i}(0,\beta)\geq 0.

(2.26)

The minimization problem is well-defined if the constrained set for
\beta
is non-empty.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\boldsymbol{U}_{t_{i}}
be a given state of the lifted Heston model at time
t_{i}
, and
t_{i+1}>t_{i}
a second time for a time step
t_{i+1}-t_{i}
. Furthermore, let
\alpha_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}]
and
\beta_{i}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}Z_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}]}{\alpha_{i}}
. The set
\mathcal{B}
given by

\mathcal{B}=\{\beta\in(0,\infty):\beta\text{ is feasible }\},

(2.27)

is non-empty.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
\beta^{L_{i}}
is feasible, and therefore
C_{i}(0,\beta^{L_{i}})\geq 0
. We find

\displaystyle C_{i}(0,\beta^{L_{i}})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\omega}\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{t_{i}}+\boldsymbol{\omega}(-\text{diag}({\boldsymbol{x}})\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}(0)-\nu\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\beta^{L_{i}}})+g_{0}(t_{i+1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{n}\hat{U}^{n}_{t_{i}}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{n}\left[-x_{n}\left(\alpha^{n}_{i}-\frac{\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X^{n}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}Z_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}]}{\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}Z_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}]}\alpha_{i}\right)\right]

\displaystyle-\nu\frac{\bar{\omega}\alpha_{i}{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{n}\left(x_{n}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X^{n}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}Z_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}]}{\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[X_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}Z_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}]}+\lambda\right)}}{\nu\bar{\omega}}+g_{0}(t_{i+1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{n}\hat{U}^{n}_{t_{i}}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{n}(-x_{n}\alpha_{i}^{n}+-\lambda\alpha_{i})+g_{0}(t_{i+1}).

(2.28)

The last expression is exactly equal to the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[V_{t_{i+1}}]
, conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}}
, and is therefore positive.
∎

The proof shows that there exists a suitable
\beta^{C}_{i}
for the optimization. The next result shows how to obtain it analytically

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
c:=\boldsymbol{\omega}\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{t_{i}}-\boldsymbol{\omega}(\text{diag}({\boldsymbol{x}})\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}(0))+g_{0}(t_{i+1})

The optimal slope
\beta^{C}_{i}
is given by

\beta^{C}_{i}=\begin{cases}\beta_{i}\quad&\text{if }{\beta_{i}}\text{ is feasible},\\
\frac{\nu\alpha_{i}\bar{\omega}}{c}\quad&\text{if }{\beta_{i}}\text{ is not feasible.}\end{cases}

(2.29)

Proof.

Firstly, note that
c
is strictly larger than
0
, since it is greater than or equal to
section

2.4
. If the unconstrained minimum is feasible, it is the constrained minimum. Hence,
\beta_{i}=\beta_{i}^{C}
in this case. Since
\alpha_{i}\geq 0
, the constraint is convex, and we can solve the optimization problem using the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions. The conditions state that the minimum is on the boundary
C_{i}(0,\beta^{C}_{i})=0
, if
\beta_{i}
is not feasible. Solving for
C_{i}(0,\beta^{C}_{i})=0
for
\beta^{C}_{i}
, we obtain the solutions for the cases. Lastly, by shuffling the terms we see that
\frac{\nu\alpha_{i}\bar{\omega}}{c}\leq\beta^{L_{i}}
, so that
C_{i}(0,\beta^{C}_{i})=0
is attainable.
∎

2.5
Weak Consistency

In this section, we analyze the properties of convergence of the numerical scheme. Since the sampled quantity is not a Gaussian noise, showing a strong convergence property is challenging, as the discretization process must be reformulated in a time-continuous Itô process. As Kloeden and Eckhard
[
17
]
point out, an easier property to verify is
weak consistency
, which says that the simulation increments in the limit match the moments of the true increments.

Lemma 2.10

(Weak Consistency)
.

Let
\hat{V}^{h}_{t}
denote a C-LP simulation as described in
Algorithm

1
of the lifted Heston model with step size
h>0
. Then, for any
t\geq t_{0}
, the following limits are fulfilled:

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\hat{V}^{h}_{t+h}-\hat{V}^{h}_{t}\right]}{h}=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[{V}^{h}_{t+h}-{V}^{h}_{t}\right]}{h}=g_{0}^{\prime}(t)+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{n}\left(-x_{n}U_{t}^{n}-\lambda V_{t}\right),

(2.30)

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\mathbb{V}\text{ar}_{t}\left[\hat{V}^{h}_{t+h}-\hat{V}^{h}_{t}\right]}{h}=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\mathbb{V}\text{ar}_{t}\left[{V}^{h}_{t+h}-{V}^{h}_{t}\right]}{h}=\nu^{2}V_{t}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{n}^{2}.

(2.31)

Proof.

Let
\alpha,\beta
be the mean and, possibly constrained, slope parameter at time
t
for stepsize
h
. The increment
\Delta^{h}\hat{V}_{t}:=\hat{V}^{h}_{t+h}-\hat{V}^{h}_{t}
is the one step update, and therefore given by

\Delta^{h}\hat{V}_{t}=g_{0}(t+h)-g_{0}(t)+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{n}\left(-x_{n}\hat{X}^{n}_{t,t_{h}}-\lambda\hat{X}_{t,t+h}+\nu\frac{\hat{X}_{t,t+h}-\alpha}{\beta}\right),

where
\hat{X}_{t,t+h}
is an inverse Gaussian random variables with
\mu=\alpha,\gamma=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\beta^{2}}
, with
\alpha=\mathbb{E}_{t}[X_{t,t+h}]
.
Since the expectation of an IG random variable is
\mu
, we see that the diffusion term
\frac{\hat{X}_{t,t+h}-\alpha}{\beta}
falls off in expectation. The random variable
\hat{X}^{n}_{t,t_{h}}
is given by

\hat{X}^{n}_{t,{t+h}}=\mathbb{E}_{t}[X^{n}_{t,{t+h}}]+\frac{\mathbb{E}_{t}[X^{n}_{t,{t+h}}Z_{t,{t+h}}]}{\mathbb{E}_{t}[X_{t,{t+h}}Z_{t,{t+h}}]}\left(\hat{X}_{t,{t+h}}-\mathbb{E}_{t}[X_{t,{t+h}}]\right),\quad n\leq N.

Again, in expectation, the second term falls off as
\mathbb{E}_{t}\left(\hat{X}_{t,{t+h}}-\mathbb{E}_{t}[X_{t,{t+h}}]\right)=0
. We conclude that

\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta^{h}\hat{V}_{t}]=\Delta g_{0}(t)+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{n}\left(-x_{n}\alpha^{n}-\lambda\alpha\right),

with
\alpha^{n}:=\mathbb{E}_{t}[X^{n}_{t,{t+h}}]
. Since
X_{t,t_{h}}
is the integral of
V_{t}
, we see that
\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\alpha}{h}=V_{t}
, and equivalently,
\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\alpha^{n}}{h}=U^{n}_{t}
. This proves the first limit.

For the variance, we note that
\mathbb{V}\text{ar}_{t}(\hat{X}_{t,t+h})=\frac{\mu^{3}}{\gamma}=\alpha\beta^{2}
, and therefore

\mathbb{V}\text{ar}_{t}(\lambda\hat{X}_{t,t+h})=\lambda^{2}\alpha\beta^{2},\quad\mathbb{V}\text{ar}_{t}(\nu\frac{\hat{X}_{t,t+h}-\alpha}{\beta})=\nu^{2}\alpha,

and similarly,

\mathbb{V}\text{ar}_{t}(\hat{X}^{n}_{t,t+h})=\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}_{t}[X^{n}_{t,{t+h}}Z_{t,{t+h}}]}{\mathbb{E}_{t}[X_{t,{t+h}}Z_{t,{t+h}}]}\right)^{2}\alpha\beta^{2}.

We will now show that
|\beta|\to 0
, as
h\to 0
. Assume first that
\beta
is not constrained. Using Cauchy-Schwartz, we have

|\beta|=\frac{\left|\mathbb{E}_{t}[X_{t,t+h}Z_{t,t+h}]\right|}{\mathbb{E}_{t}[X_{t,t+h}]}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{t}[X^{2}_{t,t+h}]\mathbb{E}_{t}[Z^{2}_{t,t+h}]}{\mathbb{E}_{t}[X_{t,t+h}]}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{t}[X^{2}_{t,t+h}]\mathbb{E}_{t}[X_{t,t+h}]}{\mathbb{E}_{t}[X_{t,t+h}]},

where the last equality follows from the fact that
X_{t,t+h}
is the quadratic variation of
Z
. Since
X_{t,t+h}
is an integral with almost surely finite integrant
V_{t}
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{t}[X^{2}_{t,t+h}]\to 0
, as
h\to 0
. In the constrained case, we have that

\lim_{h\to 0}c=\boldsymbol{\omega}\boldsymbol{U}_{t}-\lim_{h\to 0}\boldsymbol{\omega}(\text{diag}({\boldsymbol{x}})\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t,t_{h}}(0)+\lim_{h\to 0}g_{0}(t+h)=\boldsymbol{\omega}\boldsymbol{U}_{t}+g_{0}(t)=V_{t},

and since
\alpha\to 0
, we have
\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\nu\alpha\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{n}}{c}=0
, if
V_{t}>0
. The constraint algorithm cannot attain
V_{t}=0
due to the constraint, which proves the assertion.
∎

3.4
Alternative Integrated Variance Schemes

Abi Jaber and Attal
[
3
]
derived a general method for simulating rough Heston-type models, which can be applied to the lifted Heston model using a specific kernel choice to obtain a similar scheme. In this section, we briefly compare the numerical properties of the two schemes and highlight their differences. While the general framework of Abi and Attal offers flexibility in modeling (possible for other kernels than the rough Heston model), and also significantly improves convergence for larger steps compared to the Euler scheme, the formulation of the C-LP as the
best
possible approximation suggests a faster convergence and a possibility for even larger time steps.

While both methods rely on sampling of the integrated variance through an inverse Gaussian distribution, the main difference lies in how to obtain the linear approximation. In the Abi/Attal scheme, the linear relation is derived from the dynamics of the process. The authors use a right-point approximation of certain integrals that integrate over the entire time step. This implies that the approximation error is proportional to the time step size. In the C-LP scheme, we avoid such an approximation and obtain the relation as a least-square optimization, meaning that the approximation error only depends on the inherent non-linearity between the two random variables.
Figure

displays the impact of the right-point approximation in examples of computed linear estimators versus a true distribution obtained from the EM scheme.

4
Conclusions

The lifted Heston model is a stochastic volatility model, where the volatility is driven by a set of state processes with a common driver. Although Markovian, the multidimensional nature of the process is challenging, as traditional methods require a small step approximation of the multidimensional process. Incorporating the insights from the well-studied simulation of the regular Heston model, we develop a novel scheme capable of taking larger time steps. The scheme establishes a linear relation between the integrated variance process
\int_{s}^{t}V_{u}{\rm d}u
and the stochastic driver
\int_{s}^{t}\sqrt{V_{u}}{\rm d}W_{u}
using an
L^{2}
projection. To ensure that the scheme remains well-defined, this projection is then constrained to a feasible parameter space, and is hence called the
constrained linear projection method
(C-LP). This method provides the best possible scheme in the class of integrated variance implicit schemes, as it optimizes the linear approximation, which is the only source of approximation.

In the numerical experiments in
Section

3
, we then show that the scheme is capable of simulating the lifted Heston model for much larger steps than the Euler scheme, providing robust simulations for any valid parameter set. Additionally, we demonstrate that this method outperforms similar schemes, such as the Abi/Attal method, as it employs a suboptimal linear approximation of the integrated variance and stochastic driver.

Proof of
Equation

1.6

Unlike the regular Heston model, the unconditional expectation of the variance process is not straightforward in the lifted Heston Model. While the value depends on the exact implementation of
g_{0}(t)
, we can derive an integral equation to obtain a general form of the solution. To obtain it, we first remove the
U_{t}^{n}
term in (
1.4
) using Itô’s lemma. We obtain for any
n\leq N

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\rm e}^{x_{n}t}U_{t}^{n}

\displaystyle=x_{n}{\rm e}^{x_{n}t}U_{t}^{n}+{\rm e}^{x_{n}t}{\rm d}U_{t}^{n}

\displaystyle=-x_{n}{\rm e}^{x_{n}t}U_{t}^{n}{\rm d}t+x_{n}{\rm e}^{x_{n}t}U_{t}^{n}{\rm d}t-{\rm e}^{x_{n}t}\lambda V_{t}{\rm d}t+{\rm e}^{x_{n}t}\nu\sqrt{V_{t}}{\rm d}W_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle=-{\rm e}^{x_{n}t}\lambda V_{t}{\rm d}t+{\rm e}^{x_{n}t}\nu\sqrt{V_{t}}{\rm d}W_{t}^{2},

and therefore for any
T\geq t_{0}
,

U_{T}^{n}=\int_{t_{0}}^{T}-\lambda{\rm e}^{-x_{n}(T-t)}V_{t}{\rm d}t+\int_{t_{0}}^{T}{\rm e}^{-x_{n}(T-t)}\sqrt{V_{t}}{\rm d}W_{t}^{2}.

Taking the expectation in
Equation

1.3
, we obtain the Volterra equation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[V_{T}]

\displaystyle=g_{0}(T)+\boldsymbol{\omega}\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[\boldsymbol{U}_{T}]

\displaystyle=g_{0}(T)-\lambda\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{n}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}{\rm e}^{-x_{n}(T-t)}\mathbb{E}[V_{t}]{\rm d}t.

Plugging in the choice of
g_{0}
as in
Equation

1.5
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V_{T}]

\displaystyle=V_{0}+\lambda\theta\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\omega_{n}}{x_{n}}\left[1-{\rm e}^{-x_{n}(T-t_{0})}\right]-\lambda\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{n}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}{\rm e}^{-x_{n}(T-t)}\mathbb{E}[V_{t}]{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=V_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega_{n}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\lambda(\theta-\mathbb{E}[V_{t}]){\rm e}^{-x_{n}(T-t)}{\rm d}t,

which shows the importance of
\theta
in controlling the long-term mean of
V_{t}
.
