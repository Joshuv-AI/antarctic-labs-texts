Principled and Tractable RL for Reasoning with Diffusion Language Models
Anthony Zhan

Abstract
Diffusion large language models (dLLMs) are a new paradigm of non-autoregressive language models that are trained to predict multiple tokens in parallel and generate text via iterative unmasking. Recent works have successfully pretrained dLLMs to parity with autoregressive LLMs at the 8B scale, but dLLMs have yet to benefit from modern post-training techniques, e.g. reinforcement learning (RL), that have proven effective for autoregressive models. Crucially, algorithms designed for traditional LLMs aren’t directly compatible with diffusion frameworks due to inherent differences in modeling assumptions. Moreover, existing attempts at dLLM post-training with RL rely on heuristic-based objectives with no theoretical grounding. In this work, we present Amortized Group Relative Policy Optimization (AGRPO), a principled on-policy RL algorithm designed specifically for dLLMs. AGRPO uses Monte Carlo sampling to compute an unbiased policy gradient estimate, making it the first tractable, faithful adaptation of policy gradient methods for dLLMs. We demonstrate AGRPO’s effectiveness on different math/reasoning tasks, a common setting for RL with LLMs, achieving up to +7.6% absolute gain on GSM8K and 3.8x performance on the Countdown task over the baseline LLaDA-8B-Instruct model and 1.3x performance gains over comparable RL methods such as diffu-GRPO. Furthermore, these gains persist across different numbers of sampling steps at inference time, achieving better tradeoffs between compute and performance. Our results demonstrate that online RL algorithms can be extended to diffusion LLMs in principled ways, maintaining both theoretical soundness and practical effectiveness.



1
Introduction

Many recent efforts in LLM research have centered around reinforcement learning, specifically in the verifiable reward (RLVR) setting. In a typical setup, base models are trained on math or coding problems and incentivized to reason through the solution step-by-step, getting a reward if the final answer is correct. The main goal of RLVR is to elicit mathematical thinking/reasoning capabilities, allowing models to solve complex real-world tasks.

This wave of interest in RL and reasoning, initially spurred by models like OpenAI’s o1
(
OpenAI et al. 2024
)
and DeepSeek’s R1
(
DeepSeek-AI et al. 2025
)
, has led to the development of numerous RL algorithms designed specifically for transformer-based autoregressive (AR) LLMs. With the success of these algorithms, among them Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
, AR LLMs have grown incredibly strong on problem-solving benchmarks, often matching or exceeding human expert level. Closed frontier models have even achieved gold medal performance at international math and coding competitions, a remarkable feat
(
Luong & Lockhart 2025
;
Lin & Cheng 2025
)
.

In a parallel line of research, dLLMs have recently emerged as a more versatile alternative to the traditional autoregressive paradigm. Continuous diffusion models have long been established as the dominant framework for image and video generation, relying on a denoising/score matching objective. Works such as D3PM
(
Austin et al. 2021
)
and SEDD
(
Lou et al. 2024
)
successfully transferred this diffusion framework to discrete settings, including language. Successive efforts such as MDLM
(
Sahoo et al. 2024
)
and RADD
(
Ou et al. 2025
)
have simplified and merged different formulations, with consensus settling on a masked token prediction objective.

While autoregressive models are trained to predict the next token in left-to-right order, dLLMs are trained to predict an
arbitrary
number of tokens in
arbitrary
positions. Current state-of-the-art dLLMs, such as LLaDA-8B
(
Nie et al. 2025
)
and MMaDA
(
Yang et al. 2025
)
, are usually almost on par with open-source AR models such as LLaMA3-8B and Qwen2.5-7B on common NLP benchmarks.

Once trained, these models can generate text with greater flexibility than standard left-to-right prompting (e.g. infilling) by starting with a completely or partially masked sequence and iteratively unmasking tokens. In addition, dLLMs have the benefit of being able to trade off compute and quality at inference time: one can decrease the number of steps/forward passes to save compute at the cost of slightly worse generations
(
Lou et al. 2024
)
. However, these models still struggle to match AR models in downstream tasks that require long-form thinking and reasoning. This discrepancy stems from fundamental challenges in applying existing post-training techniques to dLLMs: autoregressive models can easily compute exact probabilities for all tokens with a single forward pass, whereas diffusion models only compute probabilities one denoising step at a time, making them unamenable to current RL formulations. Unlocking true reasoning capabilities would be a giant leap forward for dLLMs, solidifying them as a true rival of AR LLMs.

Our work helps dLLMs close this gap by proposing a principled, unbiased form of GRPO designed especially for dLLMs: Amortized GRPO (AGRPO). Through a simple modification of the original GPRO objective — by viewing the inner sum over all tokens as an expectation over timesteps — we make dLLMs compatible with the well-established realm of online RL with LLMs and pave the way for future dLLM-inspired works.

Our main contributions are as follows:

•

Soundness.
We derived a modified form of GRPO’s objective (which is itself derived from well-known policy gradient methods) that is both tractable for dLLMs and theoretically sound thanks to its unbiased nature. We also explain why comparable methods, based on heuristic approximations of token probabilities, fail to be theoretically sound.

•

Efficiency.
We explain how to practically implement our proposed algorithm in a way that is both memory- and compute-efficient, and discuss various tradeoffs surrounding implementation.

•

Efficacy.
We train models using AGRPO on three mathematical reasoning tasks: GSM8K, MATH, and Countdown, achieving (absolute) accuracy gains of 7.6%, 5.2%, and 29.6%, respectively. In addition, we show that AGRPO consistently retains high accuracy when evaluated with different numbers of sampling steps, completely redefining the inference compute/quality frontier.

Understanding diffusion language models

2.1
Pretraining

Figure 2:
Models under the autoregressive and diffusion paradigms are trained on different objectives. Next-token prediction (left) is a narrower, easily parallelizable task, whereas masked token prediction (right) is harder and less conducive to parallelism since it involves predicting multiple tokens. Diffusion models must also optimize for a lower bound rather than the exact likelihood.

The most common form of discrete diffusion for language is the masked (or “absorbing”) approach, where models are trained to reverse data corrupted by randomly masking tokens
(
Lou et al. 2024
;
Sahoo et al. 2024
;
Arriola et al. 2025
)
. Concretely, given a distribution
p
on sequences of discrete tokens
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
, models are trained to maximize the following evidence-based lower bound (ELBO) on the likelihood
(
Nie et al. 2025
;
Ou et al. 2025
)
:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\sim U[0,1]\\
x,x^{t}\sim p^{t}\end{subarray}}\left[\frac{1}{t}\sum_{x^{t}_{i}=\blacksquare}\log p_{\theta}(x_{i}\mid x^{t})\right]

(1)

where
x,x^{t}\sim p^{t}
means that
x
is sampled from
p
and
x^{t}
is obtained from
x
by independently setting each token
x_{i}
to the mask token
\blacksquare
with probability
t
.
1
1

1

One can derive an equivalent discrete ELBO that samples over
x^{t}
with exactly
j

\blacksquare
’s, summing from
j=1
to
n
, which is empirically more stable
(
Ou et al. 2025
;
Zheng et al. 2025
)
.
This masking process is also known as the “forward process.” For example,
x^{t}
might have 3
\blacksquare
’s after the forward process, so the objective would be a sum of 3 cross-entropy terms.

One can view equation
1
as a weighted sum of BERT-like cross-entropy objectives, where the models learn to “denoise” data at varying levels of noise, from clean data to pure noise
Zheng et al. 2025
. In other words, the model learns to predict marginal distributions of tokens given arbitrary context. Note that transformer-based diffusion models typically use full self-attention
(
Peebles & Xie 2022
)
, allowing them to use context from both the left and right.

The crucial difference compared to the classic AR cross-entropy objective is that this is a harder/more general task (Figure
2
): in each training example, many tokens in different positions can be masked, and the model must learn to perform a single denoising step over all masked tokens. (This is a strict
superset
of the next-token prediction task.)

By contrast, AR models constrain themselves to only consider the single next token in left-to-right order. The benefits of imposing this causal constraint are twofold: it lets training be parallelized so that transformer models can learn to generate the whole example with a single forward pass, and it also lets AR models maximize the exact likelihood (via the chain rule) rather than a lower bound.

2.2
Inference

Despite a more complicated training setup, dLLMs enjoy several benefits at inference time: they can generate text in arbitrary order, are naturally self-speculative (i.e. one can see the model’s best guess for the entire sequence at every step), and can trade off compute and generation quality by choosing to unmask more or less tokens per step.

To generate text, dLLMs start with an all- or partially-masked sequence, obtain marginal distributions for each masked token, and then unmask some of these by sampling from their marginals. The tokens to be unmasked can be chosen either randomly, adhering to the theoretical “backward pass,” or by keeping the tokens with highest probability, as proposed by
Nie et al. 2025
. (We refer to these as “random” and “low-confidence” unmasking, respectively.) The rest of the tokens are kept the same, and this new sequence is fed back into the model; this process is repeated until all tokens are unmasked.

Throughout this paper, we use
m
to refer to the number of sampling steps/forward passes, and
n
to refer to the sequence length. Typically, the number of tokens unmasked at each step
n/m
is chosen to be relatively small (
\leq 8
) — unmasking higher tokens at each step severely degrades quality as measured by perplexity/accuracy
(
Lou et al. 2024
;
Nie et al. 2025
)
. (Note that
n=m
for autoregressive models.)

Remark
.

One drawback of dLLMs is that the context length must be fixed ahead of time, instead of being dynamically grown as with AR LLMs. Works such as BlockDiff address this issue by introducing a hybrid autoregressive/diffusion framework
(
Arriola et al. 2025
)
; we stay within the normal diffusion framework for simplicity.

3
From PPO to AGRPO

3.1
The RL setup

By viewing tokens as the action space, context as the state, and language models as neural network-parameterized policies
\pi_{\theta}
, one can cast the problem of post-training LLMs as an online reinforcement learning (RL) problem. Diffusion models naturally fit into this RL framework thanks to their multi-step denoising nature
Black et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2025a
.

Early attempts at RL with LLMs
(
Luo et al. 2025
)
borrowed standard policy gradient methods such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), showing that PPO could improve reasoning abilities beyond SFT. Notably, models trained with RL picked up emergent capabilities such as backtracking and self-correction
(
Xiong et al. 2025
)
.

A necessary step in PPO is computing the reward
r_{t}
and value
V_{t}
for each step
t
, which are combined with Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) to produce advantage estimates
A_{t}

(
Schulman et al. 2017
)
. However, as LLMs have grown in size, the intermediate step of training separate reward and value models has become extremely cumbersome.

In RLVR environments, including many math and coding datasets, one can easily obtain ground truth rewards without an external model, which gets rid of the reward model. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), a widely used algorithm proposed by
Shao et al. 2024
, gets rid of the value model by computing advantages using the mean reward across a group of rollouts, further reducing memory and compute costs. Base models trained with GRPO showed much stronger performance across math benchmarks and even exhibited “A-ha moments” indicative of genuine mathematical reasoning
(
DeepSeek-AI et al. 2025
)
.

3.2
The GRPO objective

To introduce notation, let
D
be a distribution over questions
q
,
\{o^{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}
a group of
G
outputs (or rollouts) generated from policy
\pi_{old}
(which can optionally be offline),
r_{i}
the respective rewards, and
A_{i}=\frac{r_{i}-\operatorname{mean}\{r_{i}\}}{\operatorname{std}\{r_{i}\}}
the (normalized) advantage estimates.

Note that rewards in the RLVR setting are outcome-based, so we receive a single reward per rollout. This means the advantage estimate
A_{i}
applies equally to all tokens in
o^{i}
.

GRPO’s objective function,

\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\sim D\\
\{o^{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{old}(\cdot\mid q)\end{subarray}}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|o^{i}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o^{i}|}\operatorname{clip}\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o^{i}_{t}\mid q,o^{i}_{<t})}{\pi_{old}(o^{i}_{t}\mid q,o^{i}_{<t})},1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\right)A_{i}\right],

(2)

naturally lends itself to AR LLMs, where the action probabilities for all steps can be computed in a single forward pass. (For clarity, the
\min
and
D_{\mathrm{KL}}
terms have been omitted from the inner sum.)

However, trying to apply this algorithm to dLLMs out-of-the-box would be infeasible, as dLLMs would require a separate forward pass for
each
term in the inner sum. This discrepancy between AR and diffusion models is the same as the discrepancy in pretraining discussed in Section
2.1
— autoregressive models can obtain exact token probabilities for every step in parallel, while dLLMs must obtain them sequentially. A naive implementation would require
O(|o^{i}|)
forward passes per gradient step, which is simply intractable for online RL.

3.3
Previous attempts at dLLM post-training

Although dLLM post-training progress remains relatively quiet, there have been several attempts to adapt AR post-training techniques such as GRPO. Here we summarize two recent proposals for dLLM-specific RL algorithms and explain why they fail to hold up under theoretical analysis.

diffu-GRPO

Zhao et al. 2025
propose a two-stage dLLM post-training process consisting of SFT on reasoning traces followed by RLVR. We focus on their proposed RL algorithm, diffu-GRPO. Instead of spending
m
forward passes to compute the GRPO objective
2
, diffu-GRPO estimates all token probabilities under a
single
denoising step, i.e. they fully mask the output and approximate
\pi_{\theta}(o^{i}_{t}\mid q,o^{i}_{<t})
by
\pi_{\theta}(o^{i}_{t}\mid q)
for all
t
. They also introduce random masking to the prompt (Figure
1
) in a manner similar to dLLM pretraining, which they claim helps stabilize training. However, there is an evident mismatch with respect to RL assumptions: the rollouts are not generated in a single step, and the context/state clearly matters when the model is unmasking tokens (i.e. choosing actions).

UniGRPO
MMaDA is a multimodal dLLM developed by
Yang et al. 2025
which is post-trained by UniGRPO, an algorithm that unifies RL training across different tasks and modalities. Identifying the lack of output masking in diffu-GRPO as a potential flaw, they propose randomly masking the entire sequence multiple times and approximating token probabilities by averaging across samples where that token is masked. More formally, given rollout
o
and
\alpha\in[0,1]
, let
o^{\alpha}
denote a randomly noised version of
o
where each token is independently masked with probability
\alpha
(as in dLLM pretraining). UniGRPO assumes

\pi_{\theta}(o_{t}\mid q,o_{<t})\approx\mathbb{E}_{\alpha\sim U[0,1]}\left[\bm{1}_{\{o^{\alpha}_{t}=\blacksquare\}}\pi_{\theta}(o^{\alpha}_{t}\mid q,o^{\alpha})\right]

and approximates the RHS by sampling
\alpha
.
2
2

2

To ensure every token is masked at least once, the algorithm enforces
\alpha=1
for the last sample. This breaks theoretical soundness since samples are no longer distributed
\sim U[0,1]
.
This follows the heuristic from pretraining where the model learns to recover the true output from randomly added noise and context.

Remark
.

The GRPO objective also includes a KL penalty
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}||\pi_{ref})
term which diffu-GRPO and MMaDA both interpret as a sequence-level KL divergence. Since diffusion models don’t allow for exact likelihood calculation, both works further assume
\pi_{\theta}(o^{i}\mid q)\approx\sum_{t=1}^{m}\pi_{\theta}(o_{t}\mid q,o_{<t})
, which doesn’t strictly hold under the diffusion framework.

Although both methods proposed above obtained positive empirical results, they both suffer from a reliance on biased, one-step approximations of token probabilities. This approach makes RL tractable for dLLMs, but comes at the cost of inconsistency with the underlying RL framework.

The root cause of this inconsistency is that unlike dLLM pretraining, RL policies must take into account their multi-step nature and assign action probabilities in a context-dependent way. With this consideration in mind, we propose a new algorithm called Amortized Group Relative Policy Optimization (AGRPO). AGRPO is the first
principled
adaptation of policy gradient methods to dLLMs that computes an
unbiased
policy gradient estimate by dropping unnecessary approximations in favor of exact token probabilities.

4
Amortized GRPO

4.1
Deriving the AGRPO objective

In this section, we show how to reinterpret the GRPO objective as an expectation across timesteps with respect to the uniform measure. Consider the inner sum in equation
2
, with clipping removed for clarity:

\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o^{i}_{t}\mid q,o^{i}_{<t})}{\pi_{old}(o^{i}_{t}\mid q,o^{i}_{<t})}A_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\{1,\dots,m\}}\left[\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o^{i}_{t}\mid q,o^{i}_{<t})}{\pi_{old}(o^{i}_{t}\mid q,o^{i}_{<t})}A_{i}\right].

(3)

Note that
|o^{i}|
has been replaced by
m
, the number of sampling steps (which may be
<|o^{i}|
if multiple tokens are unmasked per step).
3
3

3

Liu et al. 2025b
show that even with AR models, replacing the
\frac{1}{|o^{i}|}
term by a constant
\frac{1}{C}
helps mitigate response length bias.
The RHS can now be estimated via Monte Carlo (MC) sampling by drawing
k\ll m
timesteps from the uniform distribution on
\{1,\dots,m\}
, computing the
exact
inner terms, and averaging (Figure
1
).

Following subsequent improvements to GRPO, such as Dr. GRPO
(
Liu et al. 2025b
)
, we also use unnormalized advantages
A_{i}=r_{i}-\operatorname{mean}\{r_{i}\}
. This helps avoid bias from particularly easy or hard problems where advantages have low variance.

The full AGRPO objective is

\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\sim D\\
\{o^{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{old}(\cdot\mid q)\end{subarray}}\\
\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\{1,\dots,m\}}\left[\min\left(\rho^{i}_{t}A_{i},\operatorname{clip}\left(\rho^{i}_{t},1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\right)A_{i}\right)-\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\,||\,\pi_{ref})\right]\right]

(4)

where

\rho^{i}_{t}=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o^{i}_{t}\mid q,o^{i}_{<t})}{\pi_{old}(o^{i}_{t}\mid q,o^{i}_{<t})}

and the KL term represents the divergence at timestep
t
, i.e.
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q,o^{i}_{<t})\,||\,\pi_{ref}(\cdot\mid q,o^{i}_{<t}))
. By keeping the KL term inside the expectation, we can approximate it via MC sampling as well. We use
Schulman 2020
’s unbiased KL estimator, which is also used by
Shao et al. 2024

4.2
Low-discrepancy sampling

Once
k
is fixed, a naive MC sampling algorithm would draw
k
i.i.d. samples and average them. However, we can actually reduce the variance further by introducing correlation across samples so that they collectively “cover” a wide range of timesteps while ensuring that the marginal distribution for each sample is still uniform on
\{1,\dots,m\}
. This is known as low-discrepancy sampling, and is used in practice to lower training variance for both continuous and discrete diffusion models
(
Kingma et al. 2021
;
Sahoo et al. 2024
;
Zheng et al. 2025
)
.

We follow
Zheng et al. 2025
’s discrete low-discrepancy sampler, which works as follows:

1.

Sample
u_{j}
i.i.d. from
U([0,1])
for
j=0
to
k-1
.

2.

“Bin” them into
k
disjoint bins by defining
u_{j}^{\prime}=(u_{j}+j)/k
.

3.

Define the final samples
t_{j}\in\{1,\dots,m\}
as
t_{j}=\lfloor mu_{j}^{\prime}\rfloor+1
.

Low-discrepancy sampling induces the desirable property that as
k
approaches
m
, we fully recreate the original GRPO objective. In other words, one can achieve higher fidelity by scaling the amount of compute.

4.3
Practical considerations

In this section, we discuss various decisions and tradeoffs made regarding the actual implementation of AGRPO. As with all online RL algorithms, the goal of any implementation is to run as efficiently as possible while maximizing efficacy. A more detailed discussion follows in Appendix
A.4
.

4.3.1
Caching partially masked states

In order to obtain exact token probabilities, we must recreate the exact state/context at the step where that token was unmasked. To do this efficiently, we cache the unmasking order during generation so that each token is associated with a timestep
t
. Then, to get the partially masked state at timestep
t
, we simply mask out all tokens with timestep
\geq t
.

4.3.2
Choosing
k

When choosing the number of Monte Carlo samples
k
to use, the immediate tradeoff is clear: increasing
k
lowers variance but costs more compute. However, another important piece to consider here is the generation time: in online RL, the cost of generating rollouts often dwarfs the cost of computing the loss (
O(m)
forward passes vs.
O(1)
forward and backward pass per iteration). dLLMs especially suffer from long inference times due to full self-attention and the lack of KV caching. Thus we can choose moderately large
k
without significantly increasing wall time, as long as
k\ll m
.

Note that one can accumulate the gradient immediately after each MC sample is computed by calling
loss.backward()
(without taking an optimizer step)
inside
the for loop. This frees the computational graph and avoids excess memory usage.

Empirically, we do indeed observe more stable training and faster convergence for larger values of
k
. Therefore, the matter of choosing
k
is mostly up to one’s discretion; see Appendix
A.1.1
for specific values of
k
chosen in our experiments.

5
Experiments

To empirically validate our proposed algorithm, we start from the state-of-the-art open source dLLM LLaDA-8B-Instruct
(
Nie et al. 2025
)
and train three separate models using AGRPO on three different reasoning tasks: GSM8K, MATH, and Countdown. GSM8K and MATH are standard problem-solving benchmarks consisting of 8.5k and 12.5k math problems at the grade school and high school level, respectively
(
Cobbe et al. 2021
;
Hendrycks et al. 2021
)
. Countdown is a popular math reasoning task where the model is given a list of 3-4 numbers and a target number; the goal is to combine the numbers using arithmetic operations (
+,-,\times,/
) and parentheses to get the target number
(
Zhao et al. 2025
)
.

Due to compute constraints, we use Low-Rank Adaptation
(
Hu et al. 2021
)
and fix the response length to be
n=384
for GSM8K + MATH and
n=256
for Countdown. We set
m=192
for GSM8K and
m=128
for MATH + Countdown, so that 2-3 tokens are unmasked per step. This balances inference wall time and stability, both of which are critical for online RL.

During training, we generate rollouts with random remasking to inject more stochasticity into the environment and incentivize exploration. We switch to low confidence remasking (which can be thought of as a form of annealing
(
Nie et al. 2025
)
) for evaluation. Accuracies are reported with
n=512,m=256
for MATH and
n=256,m=128
for GSM8K + Countdown.

A big advantage of using dLLMs is the ability to trade off compute and quality at inference time. We investigate how AGRPO affects such tradeoffs on GSM8K by fixing the response length at

Our results firmly establish AGRPO as a viable way to transfer RL techniques to the dLLM setting, demonstrating that proper RLVR with unbiased policy gradients can instill robust reasoning skills regardless of model type. 6
Conclusion

This work presents AGRPO, an online RL algorithm designed for dLLMs that computes unbiased policy gradient estimates via Monte Carlo sampling, making it the first such method that is both principled and tractable. After theoretically grounding AGRPO, we show how to effectively post-train dLLMs using our proposed algorithm, beating comparable methods across multiple tasks and redefining the inference compute/quality frontier. We hope future works can build on our methods, either theoretically or empirically, and further close the gap between dLLM and AR LLM post-training.

A.3
Limitations

Since AGRPO involves multiple forward passes per gradient step, it is inherently quite compute-hungry, and training can be quite slow. Furthermore, there are many potential ways to estimate the GRPO objective with Monte Carlo sampling; sampling along the time axis is a natural way, but there could other ways, e.g. importance sampling, that optimally reduce variance.

Empirically, we observe that some models trained with AGRPO with high reward can exhibit signs of entropy collapse, namely reduced token diversity and formulaic, “template”-y answers.

A.4
Further practical considerations

float64 Gumbel-based categorical sampling
dLLMs typically use the Gumbel-max trick to sample from output logits. However,
Zheng et al. 2025
point out that naively using
float32
causes an inconsistency between theoretical and actual behavior due to numerical instability. We follow their recommendation of using
float64
for the sampling stage.

Handling EOS tokens
Since dLLMs generate with a fixed number of sampling steps, in the later stages, the model can spend many “garbage” steps producing
EOS
tokens at the end of a sequence (while other sequences in the batch are still generating useful tokens). Gradient updates on these steps don’t provide meaningful information to the model, so we set the max timestep in our low-discrepancy sampler (Section
4.2
) to be the last timestep a non-
EOS
token was generated.

A.5
Remarks on dLLM inference

A.5.1
Instruct-tuned models

dLLMs such as LLaDA-8B-Instruct, which have undergone supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on instruction-following traces, tend to place higher probabilities on
EOS
tokens. When combined with low-confidence unmasking, this leads to an unnaturally high proportion of
EOS
tokens in later positions and terse, stilted responses
(
Nie et al. 2025
)
.

Thus, to generate text with standard left-to-right prompting, we divide the response into smaller blocks, unmask tokens within the leftmost block, and continue to the next block once all tokens in the current block have been unmasked. This is known as
semi-autoregressive
sampling
(
Yang et al. 2025
)
. In our experiments, we use semi-autoregressive sampling with a block size of 24 or 32.

A.5.2
Bidirectional prompting

In this paper, we work with traditional left-to-right prompting, which is the native format for the reasoning datasets we use. This leaves a big dLLM advantage on the table — namely their ability to generate text from arbitrary context.

Future works could consider designing reasoning tasks that involve using context from both the left and right; for example, giving the model a problem and the numerical answer, and forcing it to deduce the intermediate steps.

A.5.3
KV caching

Since dLLMs use full self-attention instead of causal self-attention, embeddings for the same token position can change depending on the sampling step. This fundamentally prevents dLLMs from using the same KV caching mechanism as AR LLMs. As a result, generating same-quality text with dLLMs is significantly slower than same-scale AR models, which is especially painful for online RL. However, there has been some recent interest in KV caching alternatives for dLLMs
(
Wu et al. 2025
;
Ma et al. 2025
)
.

Since dLLMs already have the ability to unmask multiple tokens at once, we believe a successful implementation of KV caching will be the key to realizing dLLMs’ potential as a faster, more flexible alternative to AR models.
