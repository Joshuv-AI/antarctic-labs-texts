Synergistic effects of tDCS combined with aerobic exercise on pulmonary function and heart rate in sedentary young people
Authors: Xuemei Ni, Huimin Zhou, Yun Lu, Haohao Zhu, Ying Shen, Yingying Ji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo investigate the synergistic effects of transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) combined with aerobic exercise (AE) on pulmonary function- and heart rate-related indices in sedentary young people and to explore their correlation. Forty-eight subjects were randomly divided into the AE + tDCS group and the AE + sham tDCS group. The AE + sham tDCS group underwent 40 min of moderate-intensity AE and 30 min of sham tDCS intervention, whereas the AE + tDCS group received 30 min of tDCS intervention along with 40 min of moderate-intensity AE, 5 times per week for 4 weeks. In these subjects, the forced vital capacity (FVC), peak expiratory flow (PEF), forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV1), FEV1/FVC, resting heart rate (RHR), aerobic peak heart rate (HRpeak), heart rate recovery at 1 min (HRR1), and heart rate recovery at 3 min (HRR3) were assessed before and after the intervention. Compared with the preintervention period, the AE + tDCS group showed significant improvements in pulmonary function (FVC, PEF, FEV1, and FEV1/FVC) and heart rate (RHR, HRR1, and HRR3) (p < 0.05). Additionally, compared with the AE + sham tDCS group, the AE + tDCS group showed significant improvements in lung function (FVC, PEF, FEV1, and FEV1/FVC) (p < 0.05), and heart rate (RHR and HRR3) (p < 0.05). Moreover, the difference between the pre- and postintervention FVC, FEV1, and RHR values in the AE + tDCS group showed significant improvement (p < 0.05). This study also revealed a significant correlation between differences in the pulmonary function- and heart rate-related indicators pre- and postintervention. tDCS combined with AE can effectively improve indices related to pulmonary function and heart rate in sedentary young people, and correlations between the FEV1/FVC ratio and the HRpeak, HRR1 and HRR3 values were detected.Keywords: TDCS, Aerobic exercise, Sedentary young people, Pulmonary function, Heart rateSubject terms: Health care, Translational research

Introduction

With the development of society and changes in work and lifestyle, sedentary behavior and a lack of exercise have become common problems among young people. Sedentary young people are physically inactive or in a chronically low metabolic equivalent state when they are active1; these conditions may negatively affect individual health status in the long term. There is increasing evidence that sedentary behavior is closely associated with a variety of diseases, such as cardiorespiratory dysfunction, metabolic dysfunction, and cognitive inhibition; additionally, sedentary behavior has become a public health problem that affects the physical health of people nationwide2 and is an important factor that contributes to the increase of all-cause mortality3. Cardiorespiratory endurance is a key indicator of an individual’s exercise capacity4, and cardiorespiratory activity is jointly innervated by the sympathetic and parasympathetic nerves of the autonomic nervous system (ANS)5,6; sedentariness can lead to a decrease in cardiorespiratory endurance. The prefrontal cortex (PFC) can synergize with the ANS through relevant neurotransmitters to maintain physiological homeostasis in individuals7. Several recent studies have proposed the concept of a brain‒heart pathway8,9. The identification of dynamic and continuous neural communication pathways between the heart and brain sheds light on the effect of the brain on cardiovascular responses under different conditions10. Central commands that originate from the cortex or subcortical region and descend to the medulla oblongata via the spinal cord and exogenous cardiac ganglia manage autonomic outflow to regulate the cardiac response11,12. The regulation of the heart is known to involve efferent pathways in the autonomic nervous system12,13. Moreover, high-frequency stimulation of the L-DLPFC by repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) may regulate cortisol levels and subsequent activity of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis through brain–heart communication and may indirectly affect the cardiovascular system.

tDCS is a noninvasive, safe, and effective technique for regulating the central nervous system that is commonly used in the clinic; tDCS can affect the cerebral cortex through the output of a continuous low-intensity current to the target area through two electrode sheets (anode and cathode) to regulate the polarity of the axon resting membrane, exerting immediate or long-duration effects. tDCS alters neuronal excitability and neural activity through subthreshold stimulation of neuronal membranes and improves the brain network connections between the target brain area and adjacent brain areas. The safety of tDCS has been confirmed previously14. In previous studies, we reported that subliminal stimulation of the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) by tDCS for 30 min at 2.0 mA over a total of 20 sessions could improve individual executive function and motor ability15. Studies have also shown that a single tDCS of 2.0 mA for 20 min can reduce HR, regulate heart rate variability, and improve cardiovascular health16. Moreover, tDCS can improve the brain network connections between the DLPFC and neighboring brain regions; increase synaptic plasticity and neuroplasticity; enhance the efficiency of collaboration between brain regions; and improve cognitive, emotional, and motor functions, and tDCS is a commonly used central neuromodulation technique in clinical practice16–18. Most importantly, tDCS technology is convenient and flexible, and it can be evaluated in the “online” state or combined with other treatments in the “online” state, which is the relative advantage of other neuroregulatory technologies.

AE is a kind of continuous, dynamic, rhythmic oxygen metabolism activity that involves the use of large muscle groups of the whole body; it is a common peripheral regulation technique and activity, and its common forms include swimming, running, power cycling, Baduanjin, taijiquan, etc. Various types of people can improve their physical fitness and reduce the incidence of diseases (e.g., respiratory diseases, cardiovascular and cerebral vascular diseases, etc.) through AE19. Moderate-intensity AE has been shown to activate the brain reticular activation system and enhance the Oxy-Hb signal in the right DLPFC and the right prefrontal pole (FPA)20. Further studies have shown that neural activation in the prefrontal cortex significantly increases after acute moderate AE21,22. Some studies have confirmed that individuals with different levels of cardiorespiratory fitness can benefit from moderate-intensity AE and that better cardiorespiratory fitness improves the oxygenation of the left prefrontal cortex, enhances the level of functional connectivity in the brain, and protects the health of the brain23,24. Increased excitability of the human PFC enhances limbic connectivity and controls the limbic system through frontal lobe tonic inhibition, which reduces HR25. Some studies have shown that the PFC inhibits the overactivity of limbic structures and that overexcitation can lead to autonomic imbalances and increased HR26,27. As a technique for regulating the central nervous system, tDCS can improve human cardiopulmonary function by stimulating the DLPFC centrally. AE, as a peripheral regulation technique, can also activate the PFC region and has a regulatory effect on cardiopulmonary function. The combined application of these two techniques to improve cardiopulmonary function with a multimodal intervention mode (more than one intervention modality) that combines the central and peripheral modalities to optimize therapeutic efficacy and exert a synergistic or superimposed effect is still a topic worth exploring. The aim of this study was to observe the improvement in pulmonary function and heart rate in sedentary youths by multimodal intervention. That is, sedentary young people received 2-mA, 30-min a-tDCS from the left side of the brain DLPFC during 40 min of moderate-intensity AE, and this was centrally combined with the periphery for 20 sessions; then, improvements in lung function- and heart rate-related indices due to the combined effect of these two interventions were observed. Concurrently, the effects of tDCS and AE on the correlation of cardiopulmonary function in sedentary young people were further explored to determine correlations of cardiopulmonary function in young people and to provide more valuable treatment options for patients (e.g., lung transplantation patients, Parkinson’s disease patients, stroke patients, etc.) in the clinic.

Materials and methods

Study design and settings

This study was a randomized controlled trial (RCT). The experimental protocol was thoroughly explained to the participants on the day of the experiment. Before they participated, they were asked to complete a tDCS questionnaire assessing their eligibility for the study and any potential use of psychostimulants prior to the experiment. Upon completion of the questionnaire, the participants were asked to sign an informed consent form. The participants were then randomly assigned to the AE + tDCS or AE + sham tDCS groups. All the experiments were conducted either in the early morning (8–10 am) or early afternoon (1–3 pm). Following the experiment, the participants were asked to complete a tDCS experience questionnaire, which aimed to collect information about any side effects of the stimulation. These potential side effects included minor discomfort, mild headache, neck pain, tingling, itching, a burning sensation, sleepiness, a metallic/iron taste, fatigue, trouble concentrating, acute mood change, investigator, skin redness, and skin irritation.

Participants

On the basis of the results of previous studies, G*Power software was used to calculate an effect size of −0.7328. A two-sided test was typically used, with the expectation that the target effect size had 90% efficacy and a Type I error of 5% (α = 0.05); on this basis, the sample size needed for each group was calculated to be at least 16 participants. The sample size was increased to 20 participants per group to account for a 20% dropout rate. The study was a randomized controlled study in which 48 university students were recruited and randomly assigned to two groups according to the random number table method. College students were recruited to participate in this trial from November 2021 to December 2023, and all the subjects were informed of the possible risks before the trial. A randomized numerical table method was used to divide the subjects into a test group and a control group, with 24 participants in each group. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuxi Mental Health Center (Ethics WXMHCIRB2021LLky145) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All the participants signed informed consent forms before being randomly assigned to their respective groups.

The inclusion criteria were (1) age between 18 and 25 years; (2) a metabolic equivalent less than 1.5 meto in the active state; (3) a body mass index (BMI) less than 25; (4) no exercise habits (the standard is 30 min or more moderate intensity exercise at least 3 times/week for more than one year) and no contraindications to exercise; (5) no contraindications to tDCS; and (6) voluntary participation in the trial and signed the informed consent form.

The exclusion criteria included (1) a history of cerebrovascular accidents, hypertension, diabetes and other chronic diseases; (2) a history of respiratory diseases; and (3) poor physical condition, poor motor function, and inability to complete the test.

The criteria for withdrawal from the trial included (1) unwilling to continue or the desire to withdraw from the test; (2) inability to complete the test for various reasons.

Randomization

In accordance with the random number table method, the 48 subjects were numbered in the order of entry into the group (01 to 48), and starting from the first column of numbers in the fourth row of the number table, all numbers less than or equal to 48 were found and labeled one by one from the top to the bottom (removing the 00). Then, the duplicates were removed until the 48 numbers were identified. Of the numbers found, sets 01 to 24 were used as the test group and 25 to 48 were used as the control group.

Treatment methods

This study used a randomized design in which participants participated in two experimental sessions, each 20 days apart (5 sessions per week for 4 weeks): (1) AE + tDCS - AE with anodal tDCS and (2) AE + sham tDCS - AE with sham tDCS (Fig. 1).

The protocols of the study. Illustration of the intervention protocol for AE and tDCS (sham and active) included 30-s periods of RU/RD. A stimulation current of 2 mA was applied for 30 min in the active tDSC sessions and for 10 s in the sham-tDCS sessions. Abbreviations: HR, heart rate; RD, ramp down; RU, ramp up; tDCS, transcranial direct current stimulation.

AE + tDCS

The subjects in the AE + tDCS group received tDCS intervention along with AE. ① AE: AE was performed on a fixed-power bicycle (model motion cycle 600 mmed, Germany). The intensity of moderate-intensity AE was individualized according to the maximum heart rate (HRmax) (HRmax = 220-age), which ranged from 60% to 70% of the HRmax for 40 min, and consisted of three phases: (1) a 5-min warm-up exercise in which the heart rate was maintained at 55%−60% of the HRmax; (2) a 30-min peak exercise in which the heart rate was maintained at 60%−70% of the HRmax; and (3) a 5-min end period in which the heart rate was maintained below 65% of the HRmax. HR was recorded using a heart rate monitor (model DB18, Philips, Suzhou Erda Medical Equipment Co., Ltd.) during exercise, 5 times/week for 4 weeks. ② tDCS was applied at 30 min of peak exercise. tDCS was supplied by a battery-operated constant-current electrical stimulator (YZB/Chuan 0185–2014, Sichuan Equipment Note 20142210040, Sichuan Intelligent Electronic Industry Co., Ltd.), which outputs current through a pair of electrode pads (7 cm × 5 cm) sheathed in a saline-soaked rectangular liner, and the electrode positions were located on the basis of the 10–20 International Electroencephalogram System. The L-DLPFC (F3) was designated as the target area for stimulation by the anodic electrode, and the cathodic electrode was placed in the contralateral supraorbital area. The current intensity was 2 mA, and the duration was 30 min (including 30 s of current rise and fall at the beginning and end to avoid itching, tingling and other uncomfortable symptoms caused by increases and decreases in the current), 5 times/week for 4 weeks (Fig. 1).

AE + sham tDCS

The subjects in the AE group received AE along with a sham tDCS intervention. The electrode position of the sham (placebo) stimulation was the same as that of the real stimulation, and to simulate the slight tingling sensation caused by the real stimulation and to ensure that participants were blinded to whether they received electrical stimulation, the sham stimulation was set at the beginning and end of the session with a slowly increasing and slowly decreasing current that lasted for 30 s. However, there was no current output when the subjects performed AE. Subjects were unaware of the type of tDCS during the intervention.

Assessment criteria

The subjects in both groups were assessed before and after the intervention by a professional therapist who was unaware of the grouping of the subjects.

Pulmonary function assessment

In both groups of subjects, pulmonary function was assessed by a professional cardiopulmonary therapist with a portable lung function tester (Model: X1, Serial No.: X1180201000007, SECCO Xiamen Medical Devices Co., Ltd.), with the subjects in a sitting position. The assessed indices consisted mainly of the exertion lung volume (FVC), maximum expiratory flow (PEF), the exertion expiratory volume in the first second (FEV1) and the FEV1/exertion lung volume (FEV1/FVC).

Heart rate assessment

The heart rates of the subjects were assessed during the first and last power cycling AE. Before the test, all the subjects were told not to perform strenuous exercise 2 h before the test, and they were allowed to rest for 10 min before the test. Heart rate (HR) was recorded by a portable finger pulse oximeter (model DB18; Philips, Suzhou Erda Medical Equipment Co., Ltd.), including the resting heart rate (RHR), peak heart rate (HRpeak), and end-of-period heart rate (HRpeak). Heart rate (HR) values, including the resting heart rate (RHR), aerobic peak heart rate (HRpeak) and 1 min after the end of exercise (Heart Rate Recovery, HRR1), and 3 min Heart Rate (HRR3) were recorded.

Statistical methods

In this study, SPSS 26.0 software was used to statistically analyze the raw data. For the analysis of the subjects’ basic information, one-way ANOVA was used to compare the age, height and weight of the two groups of subjects. First, we used a 2 × 2 two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) for FVC, PEF, FEV1/FVC, RHR, HRpeak, HRR1, and HRR3, in which the between-group factor was the 2 intervention modalities (AE + tDCS vs. AE + sham tDCS), and the within-group factor was 2 times (preintervention vs. postintervention). Differences in lung function and heart rate between the two groups before and after the intervention were also calculated and compared among the three groups using one-way ANOVA; if comparisons of measures did not conform to the chi-square test, Welch’s test was used. To understand the relationship between changes in lung function and changes in heart rate, Spearman correlation analysis was used to analyze the differences in FVC, PEF, FEV1, FEV1/FVC, RHR, HRpeak, HRR1 and HRR3 before and after the intervention using two-sided tests. The test level was set to α = 0.05, the significance level of the two-sided test was p < 0.05, and the F test effect size was expressed using η².

Results

Basic information

All 48 subjects in the 2 groups completed the trial, and there were no lost cases. Twenty-four subjects were included in each of the AE + tDCS and AE + sham tDCS groups, and the difference between the baseline data of the two groups was not statistically significant (p > 0.05) (Table 1). In terms of adverse effects, the main types of feedback from the questionnaire were mild tingling sensation (100%), itching (91.67%), burning sensation (58.33%), drowsiness (4.17%), redness of the skin (91.67%), and skin irritation (83.33%) in the AE + tDCS group; however, no subjects requested to stop the intervention because of adverse effects, and none of the participants reported serious side effects (Table 2).

PEF

When the PEF flow rate before and after intervention was used as the dependent variable, the results revealed no interaction effect between treatment (AE + tDCS vs. AE + sham tDCS) × time (pre vs. post) (F1,46 = 3.704, p = 0.060, η²p = 0.075). There was no main effect for treatments (AE + tDCS vs. AE + sham tDCS) (F1,46 = 1.415, p = 0.240, η²p = 0.030), but there was a main effect for time (pre vs. post) (F1,46 = 29.161, p = 0.001, η²p = 0.388).

There was a significant increase in the PEF flow rate from preintervention (4.88 ± 1.42 L/s) to postintervention (5.98 ± 1.16 L/s) in the AE + tDCS group (F1,46 =26.826, p = 0.001, η²p = 0.368) and from preintervention (4.75 ± 1.46 L/s) to postintervention (5.27 ± 1.29 L/s) in the AE + sham tDCS group (F1,46 = 6.039, p = 0.018, η²p = 0.116) (Table 3).

Before the intervention, there was no significant difference in the PEF flow rate between the two groups (F1,46 = 0.107, p = 0.745; η²p = 0.002). After the intervention, the PEF flow rate in the AE + tDCS group was not significantly different from that in the AE + sham tDCS group (F1,46 = 4.031, p = 0.051, η²p = 0.081) (Table 3; Fig. 2).

FEV1

When the FEV1 volumes before and after intervention were used as the dependent variable, the results revealed a significant interaction effect of treatment (AE + tDCS vs. AE + sham tDCS) × time (pre vs. post) (F1,46 = 13.845, p = 0.001, η²p = 0.231). There was a main effect for treatments (AE + tDCS vs. AE + sham tDCS) (F1,46 = 6.537, p = 0.014, η²p = 0.124) and a main effect for time (pre vs. post) (F1,46 = 90.432, p = 0.001, η²p = 0.663).

There was a significant increase in the FEV1 volume from preintervention (3.07 ± 0.58 L) to postintervention (3.84 ± 0.71 L) in the AE + tDCS group (F1,46 = 87.523, p = 0.001, η²p = 0.655) and from preintervention (2.85 ± 0.56 L) to postintervention (3.19 ± 0.62 L) in the AE + sham tDCS group (F1,46 = 16.754, p = 0.001, η²p = 0.267) (Table 3).

Before the intervention, there was no significant difference in the FEV1 volume between the two groups (F1,46 = 1.742, p = 0.193, η²p = 0.036). After the intervention, the FEV1 volume in the AE + tDCS group was significantly greater than that in the AE + sham tDCS group (F1,46 = 11.345, p = 0.002, η²p = 0.198) (Table 3; Fig. 2).

FEV1/FVC

When the FEV1/FVC ratio before and after intervention was used as the dependent variable, the results revealed no interaction effect between treatment (AE + tDCS vs. AE + sham tDCS) × time (pre vs. post) (F1,46 = 1.260, p = 0.268, η²p = 0.027). There was a main effect for treatments (AE + tDCS vs. AE + sham tDCS) (F1,46 = 7.935, p = 0.007, η²p = 0.147) and a main effect for time (pre vs. post) (F1,46 = 54.279, p = 0.001, η²p = 0.541).

There was a significant increase in the FEV1/FVC ratio from preintervention (0.80 ± 0.06%) to postintervention (0.87 ± 0.05%) in the AE + tDCS group (F1,46 = 36.038, p = 0.001, η²p = 0.439) and from preintervention (0.77 ± 0.06%) to postintervention (0.82 ± 0.05%) in the AE + sham tDCS group (F1,46 =19.500, p = 0.001, η²p = 0.298) (Table 3).

Before the intervention, there was no significant difference in the FEV1 volume between the two groups (F1,46 = 3.018, p = 0.089, η²p = 0.062). After the intervention, the FEV1 volume in the AE + tDCS group was significantly greater than that in the AE + sham tDCS group (F1,46 = 10.468, p = 0.002, η²p = 0.185) (Table 3; Fig. 2).

RHR

There was no main effect for treatments (AE + tDCS vs. AE + sham tDCS) (F1,46 = 1.299, p = 0.260, η²p = 0.027), but there was a main effect for time (pre vs. post) (F1,46 = 16.217, p = 0.001, η²p = 0.261).

HRR3

When HRR3 before and after intervention was used as the dependent variable, the results revealed no interaction effect between treatment (AE + tDCS vs. AE + sham tDCS) × time (pre vs. post) (F1,46 = 0.923, p = 0.342, η²p = 0.020). There was a main effect for treatments (AE + tDCS vs. AE + sham tDCS) (F1,46 = 4.627, p = 0.037, η²p = 0.091) and a main effect for time (pre vs. post) (F1,46 = 11.916, p = 0.001, η²p = 0.206).

There was a significant increase in the HRR3 from preintervention (93.79 ± 14.69 bpm) to postintervention (87.67 ± 13.57 bpm) in the AE + tDCS group (F1,46 = 9.735, p = 0.003, η²p = 0.175), and there was no significant increase from preintervention (100.63 ± 14.72 bpm) to postintervention (97.17 ± 12.96 bpm) in the AE + sham tDCS group (F1,46 = 3.103, p = 0.085, η²p = 0.063) (Table 3).

Before the intervention, there was no significant difference in the HRR3 between the two groups (F1,46 = 2.591, p = 0.114; η²p = 0.053). After the intervention, the HRR3 in the AE + tDCS group was significantly greater than that in the AE + sham tDCS group (F1,46 = 6.154, p = 0.017, η²p = 0.118) (Table 3; Fig. 3).

Discussion

Sedentary behavior can cause individuals to suffer from decreased exercise tolerance and autonomic nervous system imbalance29. The DLPFC, which is an important component of the PFC, is a cortical structure that is unique to primates, and it has long been recognized by scholars to play an important role in the modulation of higher-order cognitive (executive) functions30; the DLPFC is also considered to be a high-level center of locomotion that is related to the top-down adjustment of motor control and contributes greatly to the motor performance of the body31. The DLPFC is preferentially connected to the human locomotor system32, which is closely related to psychological and physiological functions during locomotion. It is important for motor endurance, executive function, attention distribution, emotion regulation, reward mechanisms and memory formation33 and provides multidimensional regulation of cognition–emotion–behavior. In addition, the DLPFC is involved in regulating autonomic function, and as executive control continues to improve, the greater the autonomic reactivity is, and the greater the need is for the involvement of the DLPFC cortex34. Hu et al.‘s observation of the executive control network in lung cancer patients through functional magnetic resonance imaging revealed altered functional connectivity of the DLPFC bilaterally35. An investigative study revealed that diseases of the lungs are determinants of the development of cognitive decline and dementia and that impairments in attention, memory, and motor skills are common in patients with lung disease36. A randomized controlled trial by Etemadi et al.37 revealed that anodic tDCS to the left DLPFC, but not to the M1 region, increased muscular activity under normobaric hypoxic conditions, improved subjects’ cardio-physiological responses, executive function and strength performance, and had positive effects on emotional responses and sensory arousal. Therefore, anodal tDCS in the left DLPFC may increase muscle neural drive, decrease perceived exertion, and increase perceptual responses through top-down neuromodulation, in turn improving executive function and exercise endurance performance and enhancing pulmonary function.

An individual’s sedentary lifestyle and reduced physical activity can cause impaired health, an imbalance in the ANS and poor lung function38. The ANS regulates pulmonary function through sympathetic/parasympathetic nerves5, and animal studies have confirmed that inhibition of sympathetic overactivity along with enhancement of parasympathetic activity can achieve ANS homeostasis and thereby mediate improvement in lung function in lung-injured rabbits39. AE can better improve pulmonary function; improve FVC, maximal expiratory volume and diffusion volume40; and increase power output; moreover, a higher level of controlled exercise requires more attention from the brain for the maintenance of exercise performance41. AE is dynamically adjusted by feedforward and feedback loops through central regulation and exercise pressor reflexes to improve pulmonary function, and central and peripheral circulation and interaction are associated with each other; this positive influence is associated with sympathetic/parasympathetic adjustments42. Wang et al.43 reported that asthma is associated with impaired neuropsychological functioning in individuals, that abnormalities in the regulation of ANS or neuroendocrine functioning may lead to the development of asthma-related symptoms, and that sites such as the left anterior cingulate cortex and the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex were correlated with pulmonary function (FVC and FEV1/FVC) according to correlation analysis. Roghayeh assessed methamphetamine-addicted male subjects after 8 weeks of Pilates combined with anodal tDCS of the prefrontal lobe, and they reported significant increases in FVC volume, PEF flow rate, and the FEV1/FVC ratio as well as significant improvements in aerobic capacity17. In this study, we found that the FVC volume, FEV1 volume and FEV1/FVC ratio in the tDCS + AE group significantly improved in the AE group, and the difference between the FVC and FEV1 significantly improved compared with that in the control group. This study demonstrated that central intervention and peripheral intervention with AE on the DLPFC in sedentary people who receive tDCS can stimulate the DLPFC area to regulate the ANS system from the top to the bottom and that peripheral intervention strengthens the movement control mode and generates positive feedback to the DLPFC. Peripheral intervention strengthens the motor control mode and produces positive feedback to the center, which proves that combined multimodal intervention can better enhance the activity of the cerebral cortex and coordinate the functional coupling between the center and the periphery, thus improving the pulmonary function of sedentary people.

The neurovisceral integration model proposes the hypothesis that autonomic, attentional, and emotional integration form a functional network, which is referred to as the central autonomic network, and that the brain regulates visceral cardiovascular activity, endocrinology, and behavioral performance through the central autonomic network, with the PFC region being an important neuromodulatory region of the network44. Additionally, the DLPFC has also been shown to play an important role in this circuit34. The neurovisceral integration model also proposes that the central autonomic network system is connected to the heart via the vagus nerve and the stellate ganglion, that vagus-mediated heart rate variability (HRV) is correlated with PFC-regulated executive function, and that the HRV can reflect brain junctions and functional states related to executive function6. HR-HRV is dependent on cycle length, and individuals with lower HR have relatively higher HRV; HRV is an important measure of neurocardiac function that can better reflect the interactions of neural pathways and ANS dynamics between the heart and brain systems45. It has been suggested that the PFC is a core region that regulates HRV, that HRV is associated with functional connectivity between the PFC and the amygdala, and that HRV can mediate PFC activation46,47. The results of a meta-analysis48 revealed that tDCS, which is a noninvasive brain stimulation technique that stimulates the PFC region, effectively reduced blood pressure and heart rate and increased HRV, demonstrating the major importance of this higher center in cardiovascular regulation. Studies have shown that a single anodic tDCS stimulation of the left DLPFC can alleviate stress-induced heart rate acceleration and sympathoexcitation/parasympathoinhibition, effectively modulating autonomic and endocrine levels and improving their effects on psychological stress in healthy individuals49. Shen et al.16 reported that transcranial DCS stimulation of the DLPFC utilizing anodic HD can modulate, via the brain-center pathway, cortical or subcortical regions to increase cortical excitability in the left DLPFC, decrease heart rate and modulate HRV in healthy young people, suggesting that tDCS could be used an intervention to improve cardiovascular and autonomic function. Moreira et al.50 performed anodic tDCS at 2 mA for 20 min on the left side of the DLPFC in soccer players and reported that their heart rate recovery was reduced, their heart rate recovery index was increased, and their sense of well-being was improved; these results suggested that tDCS of the DLPFC significantly improved well-being and parasympathetic function.

A transient acceleration of heart rate occurs after acute AE when cardiac vagal regulation decreases and sympathetic regulation increases; however, regular AE increases vagal regulation of heart rate and decreases sympathetic activity, and parasympathetic dominance improves autonomic nervous system homeostasis and decreases heart rate51. The positive effects of exercise on executive function have been well documented, with significant improvements observed even in individuals under hypoxic conditions52; additionally, studies have confirmed that the level of cardiac fitness is closely related to executive function, with individuals with better executive function demonstrating better cardiac fitness28. AE increased vagal-mediated HRV in sedentary older adults after they performed AE 3 times per week for 12 weeks, and these subjects showed a corresponding improvement in executive function29. The results of the present study also verified that compared with AE alone, tDCS combined with AE improved the resting heart rate in sedentary healthy young people, with 1-min and 3-min heart rate recovery at the end of the session, and improved the heart rate recovery index, suggesting that compared with AE alone, AE combined with central and peripheral modulation by tDCS can modulate brain networks and the ANS to improve cardiac function.

The cardiopulmonary system is often considered a complex nonlinear dynamical system that consists of two weakly coupled oscillators (cardiac activity and respiratory motion) as the basic components, simultaneously coupled to each other through several structural and functional types of cardiopulmonary interactions53, and the cardiopulmonary coupling is regulated bidirectionally to achieve the desired state of physiological function. Cardiopulmonary coupling is influenced by individual fitness, with trained athletes demonstrating greater cardiopulmonary coupling than sedentary people54. Previous studies have also confirmed a significant correlation between autonomic and pulmonary function, suggesting a synchronization of cardiopulmonary activity55. A study of impaired lung function in young individuals and cardiac phenotype in middle age revealed that a decreased FEV 1/FVC ratio was associated with impaired cardiac function and suggested that cardiopulmonary interactions can occur simultaneously in patients with cardiopulmonary system diseases56. In the present study, correlation analysis revealed that the differences between pre- and postintervention lung function and heart rate were significantly positively correlated with each other and that the parameters of lung function and heart rate were significantly negatively correlated with each other. These findings are consistent with the results of previous studies57, indicating that an increase in pulmonary function-related indices is associated with a decrease in heart rate and that an improvement in pulmonary function may mediate a decrease in heart rate, resulting in greater executive function and cardiopulmonary coupling capacity.

Limitations and perspectives

Our study provides a novel treatment for patients with clinical cardiorespiratory dysfunction and a novel direction for competitive sports training programs. Moreover, there were no serious adverse reactions in either group of subjects, indicating that the combined application of the two interventions has a good safety profile. The method that was used in this study is simple and relatively easy to perform. This method combines tDCS with AE in the form of cycling, providing a more straightforward and convenient rehabilitation treatment for patients with pulmonary dysfunction, such as those with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, those with Parkinson’s disease combined with pulmonary dysfunction, and those with stroke combined with pulmonary dysfunction.

However, the intervention time of this study was short, the sample size was small, and the subject population was a healthy population, which do not represent the characteristics of the overall population; thus, the effectiveness of this intervention in other populations needs to be verified, and further research will be performed in the future to identify more valuable programs.

Conclusions

In summary, after four weeks of intervention, the results of this study revealed that anodic tDCS with left-sided DLPFC combined with AE can effectively improve pulmonary function and heart rate in healthy young people, and correlations between the FEV1/FVC ratio and HRpeak, HRR1 and HRR3 were detected. These findings indicate the potential advantages of tDCS combined with AE in improving lung function and heart rate, and there is a certain correlation between pulmonary function and heart rate.
