miRNA: The Next Frontier in Dermatology Research and Therapeutics
Authors: Abheek Sil, Disha Chakraborty
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Engagement of microribonucleic acids (miRNA) in the regulation of cutaneous cellular health and diseases is a rapidly advancing niche in dermatology basic research. miRNAs have been identified to play a key role in the pathogenesis of various cutaneous inflammatory, autoimmune and neoplastic conditions, among others. In addition, their purported role as therapeutic targets and biomarkers in diseased conditions harbours exciting news for the approaching years in clinical research. The current review outlines the possible translational role of miRNA in skin health and diseases (encompassing pathogenesis, diagnosis, biomarkers and therapy) from bench to bedside.

Introduction

Microribonucleic acids (miRNA) represent a family of noncoding RNAs, which consists of 19–25 nucleotides and regulates the expression of approximately 30% of human protein-coding mRNAs.[1] Although not coding for any protein by themselves, miRNAs can regulate the expression of other protein-coding gene at the posttranscriptional level. MiRNAs can bind to complementary sequences of the three prime untranslated regions of target mRNAs, leading to the modulation of gene expression. By altering target expression, miRNAs can affect various cellular activities including cell proliferation and cell development in vitro or carcinogenesis and immune response in vivo. A lot of researches have paid attention to the possibility that miRNAs play a role in the pathogenesis of various human disorders. However, miRNA research in dermatology is relatively in its nascent stage. In human skin, miRNAs are involved in the development, morphogenesis and maintenance by influencing cell proliferation, differentiation, immune regulation and wound healing.[2] In this comprehensive review, we summarize the role of miRNAs in the pathogenesis, diagnosis, disease activity evaluation and therapeutic applications in various dermatological conditions.

Search strategy

A comprehensive full strategic electronic search of PubMed, Scopus, MEDLINE, Google Scholar and EBSCO databases was carried out using the MeSH and non-MeSH keywords like “miRNA” OR “microRNA” AND “Dermatology” OR “inflammatory skin diseases” OR “cutaneous malignancies” OR “wound healing” OR “therapy” over the period 2001–2022. Two reviewing authors analysed and extracted the data from the searches. The references of extracted articles were also scanned for additional information to compile this narrative review.

Origin and maturation of miRNA

Lee et al.[3] first discovered miRNA in Caenorhabditis elegans as derivatives from larger primary RNA transcripts in the human genome.[4] Individual miRNA genes are transcribed by polymerase II or III into primary miRNA (pri-miRNA) transcript and subsequently processed into a precursor miRNA (pre-miRNA) by Drosha (RNASEN) and DGCR8 (DiGeorge syndrome critical region 8) enzymes.[5] After nuclear processing, a pre-miRNA is exported into the cytoplasm by XPO5 (Exportin 5) for final processing by Dicer and loading into the RNA-induced silencing complex. The loaded miRNA-eRNA–induced silencing complex is then guided to the 30 untranslated region of target mRNA genes where it binds and disrupts translation or triggers mRNA degradation[678] [Figure 1].

Psoriasis

Psoriasis is an immune-mediated, common, chronic inflammatory skin disorder whose pathogenesis is still unclear. It is evident that immune dysregulation plays a key role in its pathogenesis with a complex interaction between genetic, environmental factors, keratinocytes, T helper cells and their cytokines.[9] The first psoriasis-specific miRNA found in skin was miR-203 in the year 2007 by Sonkoly et al.[10] Others like miR146a, miR-21 and miR-125b subsequently followed, and until date, more than 250 miRNAs have been found to be expressed in psoriatic skin. These have differential expression in skin and various organs. While miR-203 is highly skin specific, others like miR146a are for leucocyte-rich organs, miR-125b for ectodermal organs, and miR-21 for bladder, colon, prostrate and lung.

IL-6 is a well-known proinflammatory cytokine in psoriasis which functions via a transcription factor STAT3; miR-203 activates this pathway via inhibition of SOCS3 (signal of cytokine suppression).[11] Studies by Meisgen et al.[12] and O’Connel et al.[13] found that miR-146a directly targets TNF receptor-associated factor 6 and IL-1 receptor-associated kinase 1 (IRAK1) which negatively regulates nuclear factor kappa-light-chain enhancer of activated B cells (NF-kB) and thereby acts as a negative inflammatory regulator in psoriasis. It is also highly associated with IL-17, another established cytokine in psoriasis. Zhao M et al.[14] found evidence suggesting that miR-210 is similarly expressed by CD4 helper T cells and leads to overexpression of proinflammatory cytokines IL-17, IFN-γ. According to Zibert et al.,[15] miR-221 and 222 promote epidermal proliferation in psoriatic skin by activating a key enzyme involved in angiogenesis, inflammation, proliferation, i.e., matrix metalloprotease. However, in contrast to this observation, fibroblast growth factor receptor 2 (FGFR2) was identified as one of the direct targets for suppression by miR-125b by luciferase reporter assay. The expression of miR-125b and FGFR2 was inversely correlated in both transfected keratinocytes and psoriatic skin.[16] Its low levels are associated with the expression of TNFα on keratinocytes which in turn is the basis of anti-TNFα therapy in psoriasis.[17]

It is unclear as to whether a single or a group of miRNAs act as potential biomarkers of psoriasis. miR-33, miR-143, miR-1266 and miR-223 all correlate with the disease severity.[18] A major drawback is insufficient data regarding miRNA expression in subtypes other than psoriasis vulgaris. In addition, further research is needed to determine the specific molecular mechanism of miRNAs to facilitate its use as effective therapeutic agents [Table 1].

Atopic dermatitis

Atopic dermatitis (AD), also known as atopic eczema, is a chronic inflammatory, disabling skin disorder making it a therapeutic challenge for dermatologists. Skin barrier dysfunction in atopy is largely influenced by innate and acquired immunity which in turn is under the control of genetic expression of miRNAs.[19] The overall predominant effect of miRNA in atopic dermatitis is anti-inflammatory. This was studied by Hener P et al.,[20] in a mouse model by artificially provoking atopic dermatitis. Dicer is a double-stranded RNA ribonuclease needed for miRNA maturation, and its artificial ablation lead to exacerbation of atopic dermatitis in the mouse model. This was through elevation of thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), a well-known proinflammatory mediator in AD.

MiRNAs in serum, urine, as well as lesional skin of AD patients were studied by Lv Y et al.,[21] and all have shown significance. According to genome wide analysis, high levels of miRNA 203 in serum are associated with greater soluble tumour necrosis factor receptor (TNFR I/II) linked inflammation, while its low urinary levels cause dysregulation of serum IgE levels of AD patients. A study by Vaher H et al.[22] found that MiR-10a-5p directly targets a damage-associated positive regulator of keratinocyte proliferation and migration known as HAS3. Hence, its abnormal upregulation results in skin barrier dysfunction and is observed in both lesional and nonlesional skin of AD patients. Jeong D et al.[23] provided evidence that miR-124 is a strong negative regulator of the chronic inflammation in AD via the NF-κB pathway. It decreases the levels of circulating IL-8, chemokine motifs CCL5, 8 enhancing the possibility for development of a new drug in management of AD. Th2 pathway mediated by the cytokine IL-13 plays a major role in AD. A study by Ingram JL[24] stated that miR-143 decreases IL-13 levels, more specifically by IL-13Ra1, thereby downregulating genes coding for proteins of AD (loricrin, involucrin and filaggrin). MiR-155 helps in differentiation of Th17 cells which explains why it is an important positive regulator in AD. Its expression causes decreased circulating cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4) and increased helper T cells which contributes to the immune dysfunction in AD.[25] Microarray helps analysing the miRNA expression in skin. Alopecia

Alopecia areata (AA) is an autoimmune T-cell–mediated inflammatory disorder, its main target being the anagen hair bulb. Identification of miRNA profile in such patients can give us more clue about its cellular mechanism. This was studied by Sheng et al.[26] by microarray on total 2549 human miRNAs and identified 36 miRNAs in blood to be specific for AA and correlating with severity of AA. Functional analysis revealed that they targeted several pathways involving, transforming growth factor-β, p53, several phases of cell cycle and insulin signalling. They were further quantified using real-time PCR. Five out of them, miR-185-5p, miR-125b-5p, miR-186-5p, miR-210 and miR-1246 were found to correlate with the active phase of AA. A similar analysis was done by Mohammadi P et al.[27] in patients of androgenetic alopecia (AGA) using their follicular stem cells. Preliminary analysis found a total of 748 miRNAs to be related, but further quantification by PCR revealed miR-324-3p to be most specific for keratinocyte proliferation and differentiation. Similar to AA, the pathways targeted in AGA were mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and transforming growth factor (TGF)-β.[28] These findings create avenue for drug development targeting specific pathways, in future.

Vitiligo

There are three major factors involved in the pathogenesis of vitiligo-genetic, immunity and oxidative stress. MiRNAs control the biological processes of T lymphocytes and contribute to immune dysregulation in vitiligo. MiRNA profiling by Mansuri et al.[29] determined that miR-133b e is highly expressed in epidermis of vitiligo patients and increases secretion of IL-17A/F and IL-22 from Th17 cells. Other upregulated miRNAs are miR-224-3p and miR-4712-3p as evidenced by Wang Y et al.[30] T cell is stimulated by the inhibition of miR-3940-5p through IL-2R.[31]

Melanogenesis is under the regulation of MITF transcription factor and tyrosinase enzymes. Inhibition of MITF by either downregulation or upregulation of miRNAs; MiR-720 and miR-1308 are downregulated, while miR-1225-3p, miR634, miR-197, miR-766, miR-574-5p and miR-328 are upregulated.[32]

Xu et al.[33] provided evidence that oxidative stress enhances expression of miR-1, miR-135a, miR-9 and miR-183 by targeting heat shock protein 60 (HSP60) and HSP70. Stress–responsive, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory factor (SIRT1) is a target that prevents oxidative stress by interacting with p53. It is targeted by miR-135a and miR-9. Both serum and skin of vitiligo patients have elevated levels of miR-25 and causes melanocyte destruction by the inhibition of MITF.[3435]

There have been novel approaches to treat vitiligo by using miRNAs.[36] One is to use oligonucleotides with sequences complimentary to endogenous miRNAs and are therefore miRNA antagonists. In addition, small molecule inhibitors that specifically inhibit miRNAs have been used. The second strategy is to use miRNA mimics that replace the original ones and modulate gene expression.

Other pigmentary alterations

The pigmentation of our skin depends on melanin pigment of melanosomes within melanocytes. The miRNA expression of melanocytes is different in Caucasian and darker skin type. A study by Lo Cicero et al.[37] stated that miR-3196 is exclusive for Caucasian skin type and is upregulated by UVB. Another miRNA specific for Caucasian skin is miR-330-5p and its function is inhibition of tyrosinase enzyme.[38] Oxidative stress significantly contributes to skin damage and pigmentation. According to Serre C et al.[39] miR-25 has specific targets in both melanocytes and keratinocytes. In melanocytes, it increases oxidative stress by upregulating MITF (microphthalmia-associated transcription factor), inhibiting tyrosinase of melanocytes thereby leading to decreased skin pigmentation. In keratinocytes, miR-25 decreases SCF (stem cell factor) and bFGF (basic fibroblast growth factor) secretion. MITF is targeted by other miRNAs like miR218, miR-508 and miR-137. Melanogenesis is inhibited by miR-27-3p, miR125b and miR-145 through different signalling pathways and genetic regulation (Wnt3a and TYR, TYRP1, DCT, SH3BP4, MYO5A, RAB27A).[404142] MiR-203 promotes melanogenesis by inhibiting the CREB1/MITF/Rab27a pathway as ascertained by Noguchi S et al.[43] MiR-211 has a unique function of maintaining melanocyte stem cells by controlling the targeting TGF-β receptor pathway.[44] Jian Q et al.[45] found that Lansium Domesticum is a natural leaf extract that expresses miR-490-3p resulting in reduced hyperpigmentation probably by inhibiting tyrosinase activity. Hence, miRNAs qualify as promising treatment avenues warranting further clinical trials.

Acne

Acne of the pilosebaceous unit is due to multifactorial etiology. Chronic inflammation by Cutibacterium acnes, high sebum production, follicular hyperkeratinisation, UV radiation, oxidative stress, hormonal imbalance are few implicated stressors. As per Xia et al.,[46] miR-143 has an anti-inflammatory role by inhibition of Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2). Staphylococcus epidermidis reduces C. acnes mediated inflammation by this mechanism. Inhibition of TLR2 has been further explored for its therapeutic potential via Syringa vulgaris (Lilac) extract through stimulation of miR-105. Schneider et al.[47] identified miR-203 and miR-574-3p and inhibited by miR-338-3p and miR-7 as promoters of sebaceous lipogenesis.

In acne scars, miR-21 is unique as it promotes cell proliferation, migration and fibrogenesis. However, it operates at a critical level since both upregulation or downregulation impairs wound re-epithelisation.[4849] MiR-203 is also a multiplayer by decreasing cell proliferation, invasion and ECM production of keloid fibroblasts by repressing epidermal growth factor receptor1 and fibroblast growth factor2.[50]

Urticaria

A study by Qu et al.[51] provided evidence that miR-194 negatively modulates thrombospondin 1 (THBS1) and inhibits the activation of TGF-β/SMAD pathway, thereby alleviating the inflammatory response and human dermal microvascular endothelial cells (HDMECs) permeability of mast cells in chronic idiopathic urticarial (CIU). Among the differential expression of 16 miRNAs in CIU patients, five miRNAs (29c-5p, 361-3p, 2355-3p, 2355-5p and 4264) were found to be elevated, making them potential biomarkers for diagnosing autoimmune urticaria.[52]

Maintaining skin health

Barrier function

The skin barrier is composed of a cornified envelop, microbiome and tight junctions. It is not only a mechanical barrier but has biological and immunoregulatory properties. Since miRNAs are well known to influence keratinocyte proliferation, migration and differentiation, it is implied that they help maintain the skin barrier by regulating the involved genes. Though several miRNAs are implicated, miR 203 is most important. A comprehensive analysis of miRNA expression during keratinocyte differentiation by Hildebrand J et al.[53] concluded that miR 203 regulates extracellular calcium levels through protein kinase C and also fatty acid oleic acid levels in serum.[54] The role of miRNA in atopic dermatitis has been previously discussed. To fully explore the therapeutic and cosmeceutical properties of miRNA, large-scale studies are needed.

Skin aging

There are two types of skin aging: chronological and photoaging. Intrinsic chronological aging or senescence is a natural process that manifests with visible signs such as hyperpigmentation, wrinkling, dull and dry skin. Photoaging has similar clinical features, but it is UV mediated and not age dependant. MiRNA expression is different in them.

Chronological: A mouse model study by Li T et al.[55] revealed that miR-23a-3p is highly expressed in fibroblasts of aged skin due to downregulation of hyaluronan synthase 2 (HAS2) leading to reduced ground substance of dermis hyaluronic acid and thereby contributing to premature aging. Other miRNAs specific for aged human dermal fibroblasts are miR-34 family, miR-29 family and miR-424. They target downregulation of COL1A1 and elastin and upregulate MMP-1 through p16, ErbB signalling pathway resulting in the age-related changes in skin. MiRNA expression is different in aged keratinocytes.[56] Here, the targets are LOX (lysyl oxidase), IDH1 (isocitrate dehydrogenase) and AVEN (apoptosis and caspase activation inhibitor which cause keratinocyte apoptosis. The miRNAs targeted by these pathways are miR-200a and miR30a. The enzyme β-galactosidase and p16INK4A and p53 are also upregulated in senescence with increased expression of miR-137 or miR-668. Plantago lanceolata extract was used to reverse aging as it was known to increase collagen and elastin synthesis.[57]

Photoaging: MiR-34a, miR145 and miR-383 are common for both photoaging and chronological aging. Syed et al.[58] conducted a study on cultured keratinocytes that showed that miR-124 expression was increased by UVB in a dose-dependent manner. The specific enzyme here also was β-galactosidase, and the higher number of SA-β-gal positive keratinocytes was linked with photoaging.

UVA and UVB irradiation have different miRNA profiling, miR-23a being common to both. MiR-23b, on the other hand, was found to protect human keratinocytes from DNA damage by targeting topoisomerase-1 enzyme. Another proinflammatory miRNAs specific for keratinocytes and implicated in photoaging is miR-21-3p. Among human fibroblasts, miRNA-mRNA interaction had been extensively studied by Song J et al.,[59] for miR-101 identifying Ezh2 as its direct target inducing UV-induced senescence. Two natural extracts Entella asiatica, a culinary vegetable, and troxerutin, a flavonoid, have been found to reverse UV-induced damage by modulating expression of various miRNAs.[60]

Wound healing

Wound repair is a complex sequence of events encompassing haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation and remodelling. Proliferation phase is further divided into three stages: re-epithelisation, angiogenesis and formation of granulation tissue. During re-epithelisation, keratinocytes and stem cells migrate to the site and proliferate and then return back and attach to the dermis and basement membrane. Angiogenesis is via TGF-b (transforming growth factor), FGF (fibroblast growth factor) and the PDGF (platelet-derived growth factor), which help in the formation of new blood vessels from previous ones. Formation of granulation tissue involves replacement of the haemostatic plug by a matrix composed of hyaluronic acid, glycosaminoglycans, proteoglycans and fibronectin mediated by proliferation and migration of fibroblasts.[61]

Oncogenesis

In order to understand the role of miRNA in oncogenesis, we must first have an idea about cancer genomics at a molecular level. Every tumour has to function through its microenvironment consisting of 2 components, i.e., cells like macrophages, endothelial cells, immune regulatory cells, fibroblasts and an extracellular matrix. This microenvironment helps the tumour cells to expand by several signalling mechanisms, secretion of adhesion factors, cytokines, changes in pH levels and oxidative stress.[62] MiRNAs are noncoding, bind to the 3’UTR region of mRNAs and are capable of causing transcriptional repression of several tumour suppressor genes. Hence, miRNA of the tumour cells as well as those of its microenvironment act as modulators of signalling pathways that influence tumour angiogenesis, proliferation and differentiation.[6364] This is universally true for all tumours (melanoma and nonmelanoma skin cancers).

Basal cell carcinoma

BCC is an ultraviolet radiation-induced, locally invasive skin cancer mediated by e thsonic hedgehog signalling pathway. MiRNA expression is different for nodular and infiltrative forms. A study by Heffelfinger C et al.[65] showed evidence suggesting that let-7g and miR-183 are over expressed in nodular forms, while miR-150, miR-31, miR-146a and miR-886-5p are specific for infiltrating lesions. This is of significance since BCC rarely metastasises but carries poor prognosis when it does, so identification of type-specific miRNA enhances the scope of targeted drug development. Sand et al.[66] conducted extensive research on miRNA expression in BCC and identified total 16 miRNAs that are upregulated and 10 miRNAs that are downregulated in BCC. However, miR203 was of greater significance as it directly downregulates hedgehog pathway by delaying transition of cell cycle from G1 to S phase.

Squamous cell cancer

Ultraviolet radiation and immunosuppression are two important risk factors for squamous cell cancer (SCC). MiRNAs have a significant role in all aspects of SCC development including its oncogenesis, differentiation and spread. Another study by Sand et al.[67] found miR-31 to be highly positively regulated and specifically expressed in SCC lesions in comparison to normal skin. It directly targets tumour suppressor RhoTBT1 and cells expressing miR-31 displayed unique properties like higher cell motility and colony forming capacity. Other positively regulated miRNAs in SCC are miR-186, miR-221, miR-506, miR-142- 5p and miR-365. Various other research endeavours have identified direct targets of these miRNAs such as PTEN for miR-221, RETREG1 and APAF1 for miR-186 and LAMC1 and p65 for miR-506.[697071] MiR-199a is of importance, but there is conflicting evidence regarding its role. Studies by Wang SH et al.[72] suggested that it promoted the invasive characteristics of SCC by inhibiting E-cadherin. On the other hand, it also directly targeted BCAM, FZD6, WNT7a, DDR1, PODXL and CD44 and thereby decreased proliferation, differentiation and migration of tumour cells.[737475] MiRNAs that negatively regulate SCC are miR-497, miR-34a and miR-181a. In prognostic terms, MiR 203 is associated with a positive outcome, while miR 205 serves as a negative prognostic factor.[76]

Melanoma

Among all cutaneous malignancies, miRNAs in melanoma are most extensively studied. Although MiR21 is expressed in normal melanocytes, nevi as well as melanoma, its levels are significantly raised in the latter. It acts by the ERK/NF-κB pathway specifically through SPRY1, PDCD4 and PTEN. It not only influences proliferation of cancer cells but is also associated with higher tumour burden and invasive melanoma. Increased expression of miR-21 enhanced the invasive potential of melanoma cell lines through TIMP3 (tissue inhibitor of matrix metalloproteinases-3) inhibition. Therefore, inhibition of miR-21 in melanoma may reduce melanoma invasiveness.[76] MiR-211 has reverse function as that of miR21, as its levels are lower in melanoma cells than normal melanocytes. However, it increases invasiveness of melanoma and contributes to resistance against BRAF inhibitors as well as cisplatin.[7778]

We have previously discussed the role of miR203 in psoriasis and SCC. Early research found it to be a negative prognostic factor in melanoma as well as contributing to reduced invasiveness and proliferation of melanoma cells, acting via g BIM1 and SLUG. However, newer studies by Chang X have established miR203 as a biomarker of early metastatic melanoma.[7980]

Autoimmune diseases

Systemic lupus erythematosus

In SLE patients and MRL/lpr mice, Huang et al.[81] found a correlation between the increase in Th17 cells and the decrease in miR-590-3p. Direct targeting of autophagy-related 7 (Atg7) by miR-590-3p agomir transfection promoted the apoptosis of Th17 cells (autophagy suppression). As a therapeutic application, using agomir lessened lupus nephritis and the size of cutaneous lesions in lupus mice in vivo.

Scleroderma

Systemic sclerosis is an autoimmune skin disorder with fibrosis in skin and internal organs together with vasculopathy and immune dysregulation. MiRNAs by causing transcriptional repression control the genes responsible for the manifestations. MiRNAs with high expression are miR-21, miR-29, miR-130b, miR-21-5p, miR-92a-3p, miR-155-5p and miR-16-5p.[82] Gene profiling in patients with lung fibrosis and breast carcinoma shows high expression of miR-92a and miR-155. The IGF2 gene is encoded by miR-483-5p, and it is found in serum of scleroderma patients with significant lung fibrosis.[8384] Makino et al.[85] demonstrated that let-7a expression was downregulated in SSc and LSc skin both in vivo and in vitro, compared with normal or keloid skin. Intraperitoneal injections of miRNA injection improved the skin fibrosis induced by bleomycin in mice through intermittent overexpression of let-7a in the skin.

Dermatomyositis

MiRNA expression in serum, skin as well as muscle influences the disease. Parkes et al.[86] found that miR-223-3p is related to prognosis with lower serum levels resulting in shorter disease duration and better recovery. Other serum miRNAs downregulated are miRNA-223 and 7, while miR-21 is upregulated.

Therapeutic application

To treat each disease by miRNAs, miRNAs downregulated in the disease should be supplemented using miRNA mimics, while abnormally upregulated miRNAs should be decreased by miRNA inhibitors. One of the advantages of miRNA treatment is that multiple factors causing each disease can be normalized at the same time because miRNAs can regulate multiple targets. Certain chemicals possess the ability to target and modulate miRNAs to facilitate inflammatory suppression. Such bioactive compounds that target pri-miRNAs, pre-miRNAs, miRNA processing and loading into the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) structure have potential for further drug design and development. Hype, hope and apprehensions associated with the use of miRNA

MiRNAs have garnered significant attention in dermatology due to their potential as novel therapeutic targets. The hype surrounding miRNAs stems from their ability to regulate multiple genes and pathways, making them attractive candidates for developing targeted therapies. Additionally, miRNAs can be detected in body fluids, such as blood and saliva, offering a noninvasive method for diagnosing and monitoring skin diseases. The ability of miRNAs to modulate gene expression and their accessibility for detection makes them a promising area of research in dermatology.

One of the major hopes associated with miRNAs in dermatology is their potential to revolutionize the treatment of skin diseases. MiRNAs can regulate key pathways involved in skin inflammation, proliferation and differentiation, making them valuable targets for developing new therapeutic strategies. By targeting specific miRNAs, it may be possible to modulate disease processes and achieve more effective and personalized treatments for dermatological conditions. Additionally, the use of miRNAs as biomarkers holds promise for early diagnosis, prognosis and monitoring of skin diseases, enabling timely interventions and improved patient outcomes. The hope is that miRNA-based therapies will offer safer, more effective and targeted treatments for a range of dermatological conditions.

Despite the promising potential of miRNAs, there are several apprehensions associated with their use in dermatology. One concern is the off-target effects of miRNA-based therapies, where unintended genes may be affected, leading to unforeseen consequences. Additionally, the delivery of miRNAs to target tissues can be challenging, as they are susceptible to degradation and may require specialized delivery systems. Another concern is the complexity of miRNA networks, as a single miRNA can target multiple genes, and multiple miRNAs can regulate a single gene, making it challenging to predict the outcomes of miRNA-based interventions. There are also concerns regarding the safety and long-term effects of manipulating miRNA expression, as miRNAs play essential roles in normal physiological processes. Addressing these apprehensions will be crucial for the successful translation of miRNA-based therapies into clinical practice in dermatology.

Conclusion

In conclusion, miRNAs can be used for various clinical applications including the clarification of pathogenesis, diagnosis, evaluation of disease activity and treatment. To further the clinical use, most important miRNAs in the pathogenesis of each disease should be identified, and mechanisms how miRNAs regulate target genes should be clarified in more detail. Further studies are needed to evaluate the appearance of any adverse effects, to determine appropriate dose of miRNAs administration in each disease and to develop nonviral delivery system for stable transfer of miRNAs to the target tissues.
