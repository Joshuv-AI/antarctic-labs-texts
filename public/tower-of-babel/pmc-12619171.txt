Influence of COVID-19 on endocrine and metabolic profiles in women with pathological ovarian aging
Authors: Hui Jiang, Rongrong Zhang, Shutong Meng, Tingting Meng, Changjing Wang, Wei Luo, Jiangtao Zhang, Jing Zhang, Runxin Yao, Zi-Jiang Chen, Peihao Liu, Xue Jiao, Yingying Qin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNumerous studies have explored the impact of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) on endocrine and metabolic functions, yet research targeting the population with pathological ovarian aging (POA) remains scarce. The aim of our study was therefore to explore the influence of COVID-19 on ovarian function, thyroid function, liver function, and lipid profiles in women with POA.DesignNested, self-controlled study.ResultsThis is an observational self-controlled study. 141 women diagnosed with COVID-19 during the study period were recruited from the prospective Chinese Ovarian Aging Cohort (COACH). After being infected with COVID-19, patients with POA presented further reproductive hormone dysregulation as evaluated by significantly decreased estradiol (E2) (22.4 [IQR 7.8–62.1] pg/mL vs. 16.9 [IQR 6.2–51.9] pg/mL, P = 0.034) and inhibin B (INHB) (16.3 [IQR 10.0-23.6] pg/mL vs. 12.3 [IQR 10.0–21.0] pg/mL, P = 0.029) concentrations. In addition, patients with POA may experience thyroid dysfunction, liver dysfunction, and lipid metabolism disorders after SARS-CoV-2 infection, as indicated by significantly decreased triiodothyronine (FT3) levels, and significantly increased aspartate transaminase (AST) and total cholesterol (TC) levels (all P < 0.05). We performed subgroup analyses stratified by age to determine whether POA patients of different age groups exhibit different endocrine and metabolic responses to SARS-CoV-2 infection. The levels of E2 (25.5 [IQR 9.3–77.6] pg/mL vs. 14.6 [IQR 5.1–37.9] pg/mL, P = 0.006) and INHB (16.9 [IQR 10.0-25.1] pg/mL vs. 10.7 [IQR 10.0–22.0] pg/mL, P = 0.012) were significantly decreased after SARS-CoV-2 infection in women younger than 35 years. In both subgroups, the level of FT3 was significantly decreased after SARS-CoV-2 infection, whereas the level of TC was significantly increased after SARS-CoV-2 infection, independent of age (all P < 0.05).ConclusionsThis study provides preliminary clinical evidence that patients with POA and confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection may experience further reproductive hormone dysregulation, along with thyroid dysfunction, liver dysfunction, and lipid disorders, facilitating improved clinical management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-025-01854-5.

Introduction

In March 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) declared the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), which initiated a severe global health and economic crisis [1]. At present, SARS-CoV-2 continues to infect people worldwide, affecting all aspects of human life. Beyond affecting the respiratory system, COVID-19 has been demonstrated to impair the immune, cardiovascular, and reproductive systems [2–4]. Of particular concern is the escalating global reproductive health crisis, exacerbated by the synergistic impacts of SARS-CoV-2 infection, which has intensified focus on emerging reproductive health challenges. Some evidence suggests that SARS-CoV-2 is present in the ovaries, liver, thyroid, and blood vessels, as detected by quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)-based viral nucleic acid testing, electron microscopy, or immunohistochemical staining [5]. As such, COVID-19 has the potential to affect ovarian function. For women of reproductive age, several studies have explored the impact of COVID-19 on ovarian function, although the findings remain inconclusive. Some studies have reported that COVID-19 can cause impairment to the ovaries, as indicated by irregular menstruation [6], changes in reproductive hormone levels [7], and clinical cases of premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) secondary to COVID-19 [8, 9]. In contrast, other studies have reported no statistically significant changes in reproductive hormones [10, 11]. Notably, the impact of COVID-19 on ovarian function in women with pathological ovarian aging (POA) remains largely unexplored. Given the critical importance of ovarian function, particularly ovarian reserve, in this population, further research is essential to elucidate whether COVID-19 influences ovarian function.

Patients with POA are women who experience premature ovarian aging before the age of 40, characterized by elevated follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) levels. As a typical example of POA, POI represents a more advanced stage of premature ovarian aging, with elevated FSH levels >25 IU/L [12]. Ovaries serve both reproductive and endocrine functions. Ovarian aging, which leads to ovarian dysfunction, can have adverse effects on a woman’s fertility and quality of life. Therefore, factors affecting ovarian function are of great concern for patients with POA, especially those who desire to have children. Uncertainty about the effect of SARS-CoV-2 infection on ovarian function leads to further anxiety in these patients [13]. Therefore, it is urgent and necessary to explore the impact of SARS-CoV-2 infection on ovarian function in patients with POA. In addition, we paid attention to other important endocrine and metabolic markers in patients with POA, such as thyroid function, liver function, and lipid profiles.

Based on a prospective cohort of women with POA, we analyzed the characteristics and values for biomarkers of ovarian functions as well as thyroid function, liver function, and lipid profiles before and after SARS-CoV-2 infection, to provide better guidance and management for patients with POA affected by COVID-19.

Materials and methods

Participants

The study was based on a prospective cohort study - Chinese Ovarian Aging Cohort (COACH) (ChiCTR2000039987) established in 2018. The criteria for inclusion in the cohort were as follows: age < 40 years, and elevated FSH level ≥ 15 IU/L (on two occasions > 4 weeks apart). Women with endocrine abnormalities such as polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), hyperprolactinemia, and other endocrine disorders were excluded. Questionnaires were administered to participants in this cohort during the COVID-19 outbreak, which began at the end of 2022. A total of 179 questionnaires were collected after the infection, and 141 individuals were ultimately included in the analysis (Fig. 1). Demographic characteristics such as age, body mass index (BMI), and etiology of POA were obtained from the patients and saved before the infection. Biomarkers of reproductive function, thyroid function, liver function, and lipid profiles, such as INHB, FT3, and AST, were measured for 6 months (IQR 3–12 months) before the infection, and for 2 months (IQR 2–3 months) after the infection.Fig. 1Flow chart of study population selection. Biochemical measures and ultrasonography

Reproductive endocrine hormones, including FSH, estradiol (E2), luteinizing hormone (LH), prolactin (PRL), and testosterone (T) were measured through chemiluminescence immunoassays (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) on day 2–4 of the menstrual cycle. Anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) and inhibin B (INHB) levels were detected by an ultrasensitive enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (Kangrun Biotech, Guangzhou, China). Transvaginal ultrasonography was routinely conducted. The AFC was defined as the number of bilateral follicles (2–9 mm in diameter) in the early follicular phase.

The levels of thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPO-Ab) and thyroglobulin antibody (TG-Ab) were determined using an automated chemiluminescence immunoassay analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). The concentrations of serum thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), free triiodothyronine (FT3), free thyroxine (FT4), total protein (TP), globulin (GLO), albumin (ALB), alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), gamma glutamyl transferase (GGT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), apolipoprotein AI (ApoAI) and apolipoprotein B (ApoB) were examined with an automatic biochemistry analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland).

Statistical analysis

SPSS 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) was used for statistical analysis. The one-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test was used for evaluating the normality of the distribution. Continuous data with a normal distribution are presented as the means ± standard deviations and were compared via paired Student’s t tests. Nonnormally distributed continuous data are presented as medians (interquartile ranges) and were compared via the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Categorical variables are expressed as percentages and were compared via the paired chi-square test. The P value < 0.05 on a two-tailed test was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline and clinical characteristics

A total of 141 patients with POA who had been infected with SARS-CoV-2 completed the questionnaires and the study continued with these patients. The median age was 33 years (IQR 30–36 years), and the median BMI was 22.8 kg/m2 (IQR 20.8–25.2 kg/m2). The etiologies of these patients with POA included genetic (n = 14, 9.9%), iatrogenic (n = 11, 7.8%), autoimmune (n = 32, 22.7%) and idiopathic (n = 84, 59.6%). All participants were diagnosed with COVID-19 through SARS-CoV-2 antigen rapid detection tests (RDTs). The severity of COVID-19 was classified according to the “Chinese Clinical Guidance for COVID-19 Pneumonia Diagnosis and Treatment (7th edition)” published by the Chinese National Health Commission (NHC) [14]. Mild cases exhibited slight clinical symptoms without any pneumonia indications on imaging. Moderate cases were characterized by fever and respiratory symptoms, along with pneumonia detected by imaging. Severe cases were determined based on fulfilling at least one of the following conditions: a respiratory rate reaching or exceeding 30 breaths per minute, an SpO2 level of 93% or lower at rest, or over 50% progression of the disease within 48 h on imaging. Critical cases were identified by respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation, the occurrence of shock, or the need for admission to an intensive care unit. Among the patients, 86.5% had mild infection (n = 122), and only 4.2% had infection for more than 15 days. The most common symptoms reported by these women included fatigue, dizziness, or headache (n = 70, 49.7%), myalgia (n = 67, 47.5%), and fever (≥ 38.5℃, n = 62, 44.0%). Additionally, the majority of patients had been vaccinated (n = 121, 85.8%). Ovarian function and hypoestrogenic symptoms before and after SARS-CoV-2 infection

To investigate whether COVID-19 affects the ovarian function of patients with POA, analyses were conducted in a self-controlled and longitudinal study design, where each participant was compared only to themselves before and after SARS-CoV-2 infection (Table 2). The biomarkers of ovarian function deteriorated after SARS-CoV-2 infection, with significantly decreased levels of E2 (22.4 [IQR 7.8–62.1] pg/mL vs. 16.9 [IQR 6.2–51.9] pg/mL, P = 0.034) and INHB (16.3 [IQR 10.0–23.6.0.6] pg/mL vs. 12.3 [IQR 10.0–21.0] pg/mL, P = 0.029), and significantly increased levels of T (16.1 [IQR 8.2–22.9] ng/dL vs. 17.5 [IQR 10.0–24. 9] ng/dL, P = 0.034). However, no differences were observed in FSH levels, LH levels, FSH/LH ratio, AMH levels, or the AFC before and after SARS-CoV-2 infection (all P > 0.05). Furthermore, hypoestrogenic symptoms, such as vaginal irritation, hot flashes, and night sweats, were reported by some participants. Thyroid function, liver function, and lipid profiles before and after SARS-CoV-2 infection

We also paid attention to other important endocrine and metabolic markers in patients with POA, such as thyroid function, liver function, and lipid profiles (Table 3). Among the biomarkers of thyroid function, the FT3 level significantly decreased after SARS-CoV-2 infection (4.7 [IQR 4.4–5.1] U/L vs. 4.6 [IQR 4.1–4.9] U/L, P < 0.001). In addition, the AST and ALB levels were significantly increased (16.0 [IQR 13.0–19.5.0.5] U/L vs. 17.0 [IQR 15.0–20.0] U/L, P = 0.001; 45.6 ± 2.4 g/L vs. 46.1 ± 2.3 g/L, P = 0.001), whereas the GLO level was significantly decreased (26.7 ± 3.3 g/L vs. 26.2 ± 4.8 g/L, P = 0.003). For the lipid profiles, the TG, LDL-C, and TC levels were significantly increased after SARS-CoV-2 infection (1.1 [IQR 0.8–1.5] mmol/L vs. 1.2 [IQR 0.9–1.7] mmol/L, P = 0.014; 2.8 ± 0.7 mmol/L vs. 2.9 ± 0.8 mmol/L, P = 0.016; and 4.6 [IQR 4.1–5.2] mmol/L vs. 5.0 [IQR 4.2–5.5] mmol/L, P < 0.001). Subgroup analysis stratified by age

Given that symptom severity and clinical outcomes vary across age groups, age stratification is essential for identifying whether patients with POA of different age groups exhibit different endocrine and metabolic responses to SARS-CoV-2 infection. Additional subgroup analysis was made for two age groups: <35 years (n = 93) and ≥ 35 years (n = 48) (Table 4). In both subgroups, the level of FT3 significantly decreased, whereas the level of TC significantly increased after SARS-CoV-2 infection. There were no significant differences in the changes in FSH levels, LH levels, AMH levels, the AFC, or the FSH/LH ratio before and after infection in both subgroups (all P > 0.05). In addition, patients in the group aged < 35 years, similar to those in the entire cohort, presented significantly decreased levels of E2 (25.5 [IQR 9.3–77.6] pg/mL vs. 14.6 [IQR 5.1–37.9] pg/mL, P = 0.006) and INHB (16.9 [IQR 10.0–25.1.0.1] pg/mL vs. 10.7 [IQR 10.0–22.0] pg/mL, P = 0.012) but increased levels of T (18.5 [IQR 10.2–22.9] ng/dL vs. 20.6 [IQR 12.2–25.6] ng/dL, P = 0.005), indicating reproductive hormone dysregulation. In terms of liver function, AST and ALB levels increased (P = 0.003, P < 0.001), whereas GLO levels decreased (P = 0.025) after SARS-CoV-2 infection. Discussion

In this study, we revealed that reproductive hormone dysregulation in patients with POA was further exacerbated after SARS-CoV-2 infection, as manifested by a significant reduction in INHB and E2 levels. In addition, the indicators of thyroid function, liver function, and serum lipids were also affected. Subgroup analysis by age revealed that COVID-19 tended to affect ovarian reserve and metabolism in younger patients with POA.

To explore the effects of SARS-CoV-2 infection on ovarian function, patients were measured for reproductive hormones and AFC. The INHB concentration in COVID-19 patients with POA significantly decreased after SARS-CoV-2 infection, suggesting further dysregulation of reproductive hormones. INHB is mainly produced by the granulosa cells of small antral follicles, and its levels decline as the number of antral follicles decreases with advancing reproductive age [15]. Compared with the FSH level, the INHB level provides more direct and earlier feedback on the ovarian reserve [16]. A diminished ovarian reserve begins with a decrease in the INHB level. When the INHB level fails to maintain the FSH level within the normal range, the FSH level increases and the ovarian response becomes poor [17]. In addition, there was an increase in T levels and a decrease in E2 levels after SARS-CoV-2 infection. A previous study revealed a significant increase in T levels after SARS-CoV-2 infection in women of reproductive age, which is consistent with the results of our study [7]. We found that the level of LH tended to increase after infection, although the difference was not significant. An elevated LH level may stimulate follicular membrane cells to secrete more T, which in turn affects follicular development and maturation [18]. Increased T levels and decreased E2 levels indicate adverse changes in reproductive hormones in patients with POA after SARS-CoV-2 infection. In our study, there were no significant differences in FSH, AMH, or AFC, in line with the reports on women of reproductive age infected with COVID-19 [10, 11, 19]. For FSH, the lack of sufficient follow-up time, combined with limited sensitivity, may have prevented it from changing in time [16, 17]. For AMH and AFC, the pre-infection condition was already poor, leaving little room for further deterioration. Furthermore, the biological variability and the insensitivity of short-term follow-up may also contribute to this observation.

Decreased INHB, elevated T, and reduced E2 levels may potentially have adverse effects on fertility. It is advisable to monitor reproductive hormone levels such as INHB, AMH, and FSH, along with performing ovarian ultrasonography, to facilitate early detection of changes in ovarian reserve and develop personalized treatment strategies. In addition, for patients desiring fertility, early consultation with a reproductive medicine specialist is recommended to explore the feasibility of assisted reproductive technologies such as in vitro fertilization.

To date, several SARS-CoV-2 receptors/coreceptors, such as angiotensin converting enzyme 2 (ACE2), Kringle Containing Transmembrane Protein 1 (KREMEN1), and neuropilin-1 (NRP1), which mediate the invasion of SARS-CoV-2, have been identified [20–25]. These findings can help us understand the mechanism of adverse effects on the ovaries caused by SARS-CoV-2 invasion. Among the SARS-CoV-2 receptors, ACE2 is the most important receptor known to date. ACE2 and transmembrane serine protease 2 (TMPRSS2) are co-expressed in oocytes and granulosa cells [26, 27]. SARS-CoV-2 binds specifically to ACE2 through the receptor-binding domain (RBD) of its spike protein. Cleavage of the viral spike protein via the host cell’s TMPRSS2 mediates viral entry into host cells [28, 29]. Considering these findings, SARS-CoV-2 may invade oocytes and granulosa cells by interacting with receptors/coreceptors via its spike protein, which further mediates fusion between viral and cellular membranes. Of note, ACE2 is widely distributed in human vascular endothelial cells and induces endothelial lesions in blood vessels [30]. Therefore, reproductive hormone dysregulation can be caused by viral invasion into the ovarian vasculature. In addition to the viral invasion via receptors/coreceptors, dysregulated immune responses or inflammation triggered by the SARS-CoV-2 may also impair ovarian function. The release of cytokines, such as interleukin (IL)−6, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), IL-1β, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12, IL-17, IL-23, and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), may lead to deterioration of ovarian function through different pathways, containing inducing apoptosis, oxidative stress, inflammatory responses and impairing the supply of oxygen and nutrients to ovarian tissue [31–36]. Further experiments are warranted to validate the possible mechanism underlying SARS-CoV-2 infection on ovarian function.

To account for possible age-specific changes in the above markers, we performed a subgroup analysis based on age and found that the INHB level was significantly decreased only in the younger group. This suggests that ovarian reserve, as assessed by INHB levels, is more susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 infection in younger patients. Generally, young women of reproductive age exhibit more active immune systems than elderly women, leading to more intense inflammatory responses following infections. If such inflammatory responses become excessive, they may result in more severe immunopathological damage, which could subsequently affect ovarian tissue. Further research is needed to elucidate the precise mechanisms underlying these processes. Notably, the majority (85.82%) of our cohort population had been vaccinated. Although the effects of COVID-19 vaccination are considered minor based on previous research, we cannot exclude its potential confounding influence completely. Available data show that COVID-19 vaccines may have a slight effect on menstruation but do not affect potential fertility [37–39]. Yang et al. enrolled 1655 women in a study, of whom 974 participated in the AMH analysis and 1222 in the AFC analysis. They confirmed that COVID-19 vaccination is not associated with changes in ovarian reserve by multiple biomarker assays of AMH and AFC [38]. A prospective study involving 129 women who received COVID-19 vaccination found no significant differences in AMH levels before and after the vaccination [39]. Additionally, studies have shown that COVID-19 vaccination does not cause thyroid dysfunction, may rarely induce liver dysfunction, and can lead to isolated abnormalities in a few lipid markers [40–42].

Since the pathogenesis of POA and SARS-CoV-2 infection correlate with autoimmune damage, changes in thyroid and liver function markers are also within the scope of our study. Previous studies have reported isolated low FT3 levels in the general population after SARS-CoV-2 infection [43]. Consistent with these results, we also observed a decrease in FT3 levels in the POA population. T3, the active form of thyroid hormones, is involved in metabolism. A decrease in FT3 levels can lead to reduced catabolism and decreased consumption, and prevent excessive metabolism caused by thyroid hormones during severe illness [44]. This is a self-protection mechanism of the body under disease or stress conditions. Since the SARS-CoV-2 receptor, ACE2, is highly expressed in thyroid tissues, the virus can invade thyroid tissues directly through the ACE2 receptor, which then initiates the immune response, exacerbates the systemic inflammatory response, and causes thyroid damage [45, 46]. Many proinflammatory cytokines act on the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axis, resulting in a decrease in the efficiency of converting T4 to T3, after which low-T3 syndrome occurs [47].

In addition to affecting the thyroid, COVID-19 can also affect the liver. The initial report on liver damage published in The Lancet revealed an elevation in AST levels, which is consistent with our results [48]. The AST level can predict the severity of infection, and an abnormal AST level is an independent predictor of COVID-19-related mortality [49]. Since ACE2 is widely distributed in human vascular endothelial cells and the liver has an extremely rich vascular distribution, the virus may cause liver damage by infecting the epithelial cells of hepatic blood vessels [30]. Moreover, the membrane protein CLEC4G, which is highly expressed in liver cells, can also mediate SARS-CoV-2 invasion and infection in a way independent of ACE2 [50]. Undeniably, other causes, such as medication, cannot be ruled out regarding liver damage.

By analyzing lipid changes before and after SARS-CoV-2 infection, we found that TG, LDL-C, and TC levels in patients with POA were significantly higher after infection than before infection. Consistent with our results, a study reported that SARS-CoV-2 infection increased the risk of dyslipidemia in the general population, which can be reflected in increased TG, LDL-C, and TC levels [51]. These findings suggest that SARS-CoV-2 infection further increased the unfavorable impact on serum lipids. To prevent cardiovascular disease (CVD) secondary to SARS-CoV-2 infection, we need to be cognizant of the effects of SARS-CoV-2 infection on serum lipid levels, especially in women with POA, who are susceptible to dyslipidemia [52]. Based on the above results, we found that although some biomarkers of thyroid function, liver function, and lipid profile showed statistically significant differences before and after infection, the absolute changes were small (e.g., FT3). Whether these changes have detrimental pathophysiological effects on patients requires further investigation. Still, it might be necessary for women with POA who underwent a SARS-CoV-2 infection to pay attention to these biomarkers when undergoing laboratory examinations, to prevent related diseases.

Considering the specificity of the POA population, we evaluated the impact of SARS-CoV-2 infection on them, based on the prospective Chinese Ovarian Aging Cohort (COACH). Notably, we used a self-controlled design to minimize the influence of individual differences on the observations, which could accurately determine the impact of COVID-19 on women with POA. Our study also has limitations. The absence of a control group constitutes a limitation of this study. As a nested analysis within a prospective cohort, our design inherently lacked sufficient non-POA participants to serve as controls. In addition, although several pre- and post-infection comparisons reached statistical significance, the absolute changes observed were minimal. Whether such differences hold true clinical significance and pathological impact warrants further investigation. Finally, the follow-up period was not long enough to determine the long-term impact of COVID-19 on the POA population, suggesting that its impact on ovarian function may be temporary. This study analyzed changes in endocrine and metabolic profiles in women with POA approximately 2–3 months after SARS-CoV-2 infection. It could be argued that the endocrine and metabolic alterations induced by SARS-CoV-2 infection may recover over a longer period. In future research, we aim to follow up with participants and collect longitudinal clinical data to explore the long-term effects of SARS-CoV-2 infection, including whether the observed adverse changes are reversible.

Conclusions

This study presents initial clinical evidence suggesting that POA patients with SARS-CoV-2 infection may experience further reproductive hormone dysregulation, along with thyroid dysfunction, liver dysfunction, and lipid disorders, supporting clinicians in optimizing patient care.
