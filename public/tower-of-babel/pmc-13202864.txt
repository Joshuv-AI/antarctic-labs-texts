Multidrug-resistant organism carriage in children admitted to a pediatric intensive care unit of a Greek tertiary hospital: prevalence and risk factors
Authors: Olympia P Rozakea, Panagiota Psallida, Anastasios Doudoulakakis, Aristoula Patsoura, Alexandra Soldatou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntimicrobial resistance is a global public health threat. Colonization with multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs) often precedes infection and promotes transmission. However, data in pediatric populations remain limited. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of MDRO carriage among children admitted from the community to a Greek Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) and to explore associated risk factors.MethodsA cross-sectional study of children aged 0–16 years admitted to the PICU was conducted from January 1st to June 30th, 2024, excluding those hospitalized > 48 h or residing in care facilities. MDROs were identified from nasal, rectal, or bronchial secretion cultures collected within 48 h of admission. Epidemiological and clinical data were retrieved from medical records, and statistical analysis was performed to explore potential associations.ResultsOf 86 included children (median age 4.5 years, IQR 1–11; 53.5% male), 80 were screened within 48 h. MDRO colonization was identified in 18/80 patients (22.5%). ESBL-producing Escherichia coli (40.0%) and MRSA (20.0%) predominated. In exploratory multivariable analysis, lower absolute body weight (kg) was associated with MDRO carriage, with each 1 kg increase corresponding to approximately 18% lower colonization risk (OR = 0.818, 95% CI 0.680–0.984, P = 0.033), whereas no significant association was observed for weight-for-age.ConclusionsHigh rates of MDRO carriage in children admitted from the community to a PICU highlight an important public health concern and the need for enhanced preventive measures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13756-026-01738-3.Keywords: Antimicrobial resistance, Colonization, Resistant microbial flora, Community pediatric population

Background

Antimicrobial resistance is among the most critical challenges to global public health, with significant health consequences. Annually, 1.27 million deaths are directly attributed to multidrug-resistant organism (MDRO) infections, with total related deaths estimated at 4.95 million. Projections indicate that by 2050, mortality due to MDROs could reach approximately 10 million, a figure comparable to cancer-related deaths [1].

Antimicrobial resistance also imposes significant economic burdens on healthcare systems worldwide. Within the European Economic Area, the estimated annual expenditure reaches about €11.7 billion, including €6.6 billion in additional healthcare costs and €5.1 billion in economic losses from reduced productivity, such as premature mortality and extended sick leave [2]. In Greece, the annual expenditure for MDRO infections is estimated to surpass €42.6 million [3]. On a global scale, antimicrobial resistance could cause yearly economic losses of up to $3.4 trillion in gross domestic product (GDP) by 2030 if no urgent action is taken [4]. Because of these far-reaching impacts, multidrug resistance has been recognized as one of the top ten threats to global public health [5].

Children are particularly susceptible to colonization by MDROs due to immature immune systems, frequent close contact with peers in settings such as daycare centers and schools, and underdeveloped personal hygiene practices. Consequently, the pediatric population serves as a significant reservoir for the community spread of multidrug-resistant microorganisms [6]. Furthermore, MDRO carriage can disrupt normal microbial flora, leading to long-term immune and metabolic health disorders [7]. Carriers are also at increased risk of developing difficult-to-treat infections that may require prolonged hospitalization [8]. This increased risk results in greater exposure to hospital-associated hazards, additional psychological stress for both the child and family, and potential long-term physical and psychological sequelae, ultimately affecting the child’s development and quality of life [9].

Internationally, multiple studies have examined the carriage of multidrug-resistant organisms among children at hospital admission. For example, a German study with 3,964 children found a 4.31% MDRO carriage rate [10]. In higher-burden settings such as Pakistan, admission carriage was much higher, reaching 68.3% among children [11]. Studies from France and Belgium in daycare centers reported extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacteriaceae prevalence in healthy children of 7.6–15.8%, highlighting the community as a reservoir [12, 13].

In Greece, data specifically concerning the pediatric population are limited. Studies have shown a significant presence of Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus Aureus (MRSA) in patients in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) and in outpatient adults (5-30.7%) [14, 15]. Moreover, a 2024 study recorded hospital-acquired MDROs in adult patients, with Klebsiella pneumoniae carbapenemase-producing (KPC), Acinetobacter baumannii, and Vancomycin-resistant Enterococci (VRE) being the dominant strains [16]. Although most of the data pertain to adults, they reflect the substantial ecological burden posed by multidrug-resistant microorganisms in the country.

Given that most existing literature focuses on adult populations and that pediatric MDRO carriage in Greece remains understudied, this research addresses a critical knowledge gap with important public health implications. Documenting microbial carriage in children admitted from the community may offer indirect insights into the epidemiology of these pathogens outside hospital settings. Especially among severely ill children, identifying multidrug-resistant strains upon admission to a Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) can elucidate dissemination patterns and inform targeted prevention, control, and therapeutic strategies. Therefore, the objectives of this study were: (i) to determine the prevalence of MDRO carriage in pediatric patients admitted to the PICU of “Panagiotis & Aglaia Kyriakou” Children’s Hospital from the community, and (ii) to explore potential risk factors for carriage.

Methods

A cross-sectional study of all children aged 0–16 years who were admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit of “Panagiotis & Aglaia Kyriakou” Children’s Hospital from January 1, 2024, to June 30, 2024, was conducted. Exclusion criteria were hospitalization for more than 48 h in another ward or living in care facilities. Within 48 h of admission to the PICU, targeted nasopharyngeal and rectal swabs were obtained from all patients, and, where applicable, bronchial secretions were collected via transtracheal aspiration to identify MDROs. Both exclusion criteria and collection time frame were selected to differentiate between carriage at the time of admission and hospital-acquired colonization. Six eligible admissions were not screened within the predefined 48-hour timeframe and were therefore excluded from the analysis.

All specimens were subjected to microbiological culture and assessed for growth on routine or selective media (chocolate, sheep blood, Chapman, and MacConkey agar, plus ceftazidime 10 µg and cefotaxime 5 µg discs). Pathogen identification was performed via the VITEK system (Biomerieux, Marcy-L’Etoile, France) at the hospital’s Microbiology Laboratory. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted utilizing the classic disk diffusion (Kirby-Bauer) method or minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) determination via VITEK or Εtest® (Biomerieux), in accordance with EUCAST guidelines. Isolated MDROs included ESBL-producing E. coli, Carbapenemase-producing Enterobacteriaceae (CRE), Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), Vancomycin-resistant Enterococci (VRE), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CRPA), and Acinetobacter baumanii resistant to carbapenems (CRAB). ESBL production was assessed using discs of cefotaxime, ceftazidime, and cefepime with or without clavulanic acid. Phenotypic detection of AmpC hyperproduction was performed based on resistance to cefotaxime, cefoxitin, ceftazidime, and susceptibility to cefepime disc, and confirmation using the bioMérieux Etest CN/CNI (cefotetan/cefotetan-cloxacillin). E. coli ATCC 25922 (negative control) and Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 13883 (AmpC positive control) were used for quality control. When carbapenem resistance was identified, carbapenemase production was further characterized using the rapid phenotypic O.K.V.I Resist-5 assay (Coris BioConcept, Belgium).

Demographic data (gender, age, absolute body weight, etc.), perinatal factors (gestational age, NICU hospitalization, breastfeeding, etc.), and previous medical history (recent antibiotic use, prior hospitalizations, medical devices, etc.) were collected from medical records. Gestational age was categorized according to standard neonatal definitions as term (≥ 37 weeks), late preterm (33–36 weeks), very preterm (28–32 weeks), and extremely preterm (< 28 weeks). To improve clarity, categories with no observations were omitted from the display tables. Gestational age data were collected for the entire study population wherever available and were not limited to neonates. Chronic illness was defined as any pre-existing condition associated with long-term morbidity and regular healthcare utilization, such as chronic kidney disease, craniosynostosis, or other congenital or long-term conditions. Parental occupation was recorded and categorized as home-based, office-based, manual labor, healthcare-related, or teaching. Due to very small numbers, some categories, including healthcare-related occupations, were grouped under “other” for presentation purposes. Due to the absence of systematically recorded height data, weight-for-age z-scores were calculated as an alternative standardized measure based on WHO child growth standards [17]. All data were entered into an electronic database, following coding procedures, to ensure patient anonymity and confidentiality. Each participant was assigned a unique identification code (ID), with no potential linkage to their name or other personal information.

Statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio (version 4.3.1) software. Descriptive statistics were generated for all eligible PICU admissions to assess the prevalence of MDRO carriage, hereafter referred to as the initial analysis. For regression analyses, only cases with complete data across the variables of interest were included, hereafter referred to as the final analysis dataset, to enhance completeness and validity. A flow diagram illustrates the patient selection process (Fig. 1). The level of statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Two multivariable models were developed to explore potential associations between selected clinical variables and MDRO carriage, applying a slightly more liberal P-value threshold of 0.12 in univariate analyses to determine candidate variables for inclusion. This criterion was applied to avoid excluding potentially important predictors, given the limited sample size, and to enhance model stability [18].

Of 155 PICU admissions during the study period, 86 met the inclusion criteria. Six eligible admissions were not screened within the predefined 48-hour timeframe. 80 patients underwent MDRO screening and were included in the initial descriptive analysis of MDRO carriage. For univariate and multivariable regression analyses, 73 patients with complete data across variables of interest were included.

Results

Initial analysis

Demographic, perinatal, and clinical characteristics of the sample ( N = 86)

The median age was 4.5 years [interquartile range (IQR): 1–11], and the median body weight was 16.5 kg (IQR: 9–42). 53.5% were boys. According to the WHO weight-for-age criteria, 59.3% had normal weight, 14.0% were underweight, and 26.8% were overweight or obese. Regarding medical history, 45.0% had been admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit, and 56.1% had been hospitalized at least once in the previous 2 years. 43.5% had received antibiotics within the previous six months. 52.3% had a chronic illness or congenital anomaly. Medical devices, such as tracheostomy or gastrostomy, were present in 15.1% of the cohort. 90% of children were fully vaccinated according to age, and 72.5% were delivered by cesarean section. Breastfeeding information was missing in 41.9% of cases, and parental occupational data were incomplete in up to 24.0% of cases. The primary reasons for hospital admission included postoperative monitoring (39.5%), infections (18.6%), and trauma (17.4%) (Supplementary Table 1, Additional file).

MDRO carriage (N = 80)

Of 86 patients, six underwent carriage testing more than 48 h after admission. Therefore, microbiological data were analyzed for 80 patients. The prevalence of MDRO carriage was 22.5% (18/80). The most frequently identified MDROs were ESBL-producing E. coli (40%) and MRSA (20%). Two patients were colonized with the CRPA of the Verona integron-encoded metallo-β-lactamases (VIM) type. Two patients had overlapping colonization, specifically MRSA with ESBL-producing E. coli and MRSA with AmpC (beta-lactamase)-hyperproducer Enterobacter cloacae. Final analysis

Demographic, perinatal, and clinical characteristics of the sample (N = 73)

The median age of the study population was 4 years (IQR: 0–11), and 60.3% were male. According to the WHO weight-for-age criteria, 60.3% had normal weight, 12.3% were underweight, and 27.4% were overweight or obese. Most patients were born via cesarean section (75.3%) and at term (72.6%). A history of NICU admission was present in 46.6%, and 56.2% had not received antibiotics in the previous six months. 89% were fully vaccinated according to age, and 52.1% had chronic or congenital conditions. Medical devices were used by 16.4% of patients, and one patient (1.4%) had a history of prior MDRO carriage. The primary reason for admission was postoperative monitoring (43.8%), followed by infections (19.2%), trauma (16.4%), and other causes (20.5%) (Supplementary Table 2, Additional file).

Univariate analysis indicated that weight-for-age (P = 0.035), weight (P < 0.001), and gestational age (P = 0.016) were significantly correlated with MDRO carriage. Other variables, including gender, medical devices, recent antibiotic use, and prior hospitalization, did not demonstrate significant associations (Supplementary Table 3, Additional file).

Variables with P < 0.12, specifically age, body weight, surgery prior to current PICU admission, and reason for admission, were subsequently subjected to multivariate analysis.

Multivariable analysis

To investigate factors potentially associated with the carriage of multidrug-resistant organisms upon admission to the intensive care unit, a multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted. The dependent variable was MDRO carriage (0 = No, 1 = Yes), and the explanatory variables included age (years), body weight (kg), surgery prior to current PICU admission, and reason for admission (Table 3). Additionally, a separate multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed using MDRO carriage as the dependent variable (0 = No, 1 = Yes) and weight-for-age, surgery prior to current PICU admission, and reason for admission as explanatory variables (Supplementary Table 4, Additional file). Given the limited number of outcome events, these analyses are exploratory, and findings should be interpreted cautiously. It is important to note that gestational age and history of MDRO carriage were excluded from the model due to wide confidence intervals.

The results of the multivariate logistic regression indicated that absolute body weight was negatively and statistically significantly associated with MDRO carriage (OR = 0.818, 95% CI 0.680–0.984, P = 0.033). Specifically, for each additional kilogram of body weight, the likelihood of MDRO carriage decreased by approximately 18%, suggesting that children with lower body weight were at increased risk of MDRO carriage. In contrast, age (OR = 1.274, 95% CI 0.834–1.945, P = 0.263), surgery prior to current PICU admission (OR = 1.226, 95% CI 0.258–5.819, P = 0.797), and reason for admission (surgery: OR = 1.559, 95% CI 0.192–12.624; infection: OR = 0.339, 95% CI 0.051–2.250; other: OR = 0.436, 95% CI 0.040–4.809; overall P = 0.401) were not significantly associated with MDRO carriage in the multivariate analysis.

In the alternative multivariate logistic regression, in which weight-for-age was used instead of absolute body weight, no statistically significant association with MDRO carriage was observed (OR = 1.062, 95% CI 0.658–1.714, P = 0.807). Neither surgery prior to current PICU admission (OR = 1.190, 95% CI 0.00–4.717, P = 0.804) nor the reason for admission (surgery: OR = 2.007, 95% CI 0.345–11.673; infection: OR = 0.977, 95% CI 0.197–4.847; other: OR = 1.111, 95% CI 0.181–6.816; overall P = 0.820) was significantly associated with the outcome.

Discussion

This study demonstrated a 22.5% prevalence of MDRO carriage among new PICU admissions at the “Panagiotis & Aglaia Kyriakou” Children’s Hospital during the first half of 2024. This frequency exceeds the corresponding rates reported in developed countries (4.31–15.8%) [10, 13], but remains lower compared to developing countries, where carriage can exceed 60% [11]. Although there are no comparable pediatric data from Greece, a high incidence (12.2%) of hospital-acquired MDROs in adults has been extensively documented. Therefore, the intermediate prevalence observed in this study appears to reflect the country’s epidemiological reality, where resistance rates in adults are among the highest in Europe [16].

Regarding the types of microorganisms, the most prevalent isolates were ESBL-producing Escherichia coli (40.0%) and MRSA (20.0%). Two patients were colonized with multiple MDRO species. The increased presence of ESBL-producing strains is consistent with international data. Specifically, a meta-analysis involving 7,374 children with urinary tract infections reported a prevalence of 14% for ESBL-producing strains, with significant geographic variation ranging from 2% in America to 76% in Africa, and lower rates observed in Eastern Mediterranean countries (5%) [19]. Similarly, MRSA carriage has been documented to vary geographically, ranging from 0% to 22.5%, with the lowest rates observed in developed nations of Europe and the USA [20]. Lastly, data from Greek adults showed that the most frequently encountered multidrug-resistant pathogens were Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae [16], indicating that Gram-negative bacteria are a primary concern of multidrug resistance in the country.

Additionally, the study identified an association between lower absolute body weight and MDRO carriage in multivariable analysis, with each 1 kg decrement corresponding to approximately an 18% increase in the likelihood of carriage. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. In pediatric populations, body weight is strongly correlated with age, and multicollinearity may affect the stability and interpretation of regression estimates, particularly given the limited number of MDRO-positive cases in our cohort. Notably, MDRO carriers were substantially younger than non-carriers, suggesting that younger age may represent an important underlying characteristic of colonized patients. Therefore, the association observed with lower body weight likely reflects, at least in part, the close relationship between age and body size rather than a direct independent effect of body mass.

International literature recognizes low weight as a significant risk factor for the development of multidrug-resistant carriage in neonates [21]. In older infants and children, chronic nutritional status, as reflected by indices such as BMI and weight-for-age, has been more extensively studied, indicating that underweight status is a risk factor for MDRO carriage [22]. The absence of an association in the present study may relate to the limited number of outcome events and reduced statistical power, rather than to the absence of a true relationship. Overall, these findings should be considered exploratory and warrant confirmation in larger, adequately powered studies.

Furthermore, risk factors mentioned in the literature, including recent hospitalization, antibiotic use, and presence of medical devices [23], were not significantly associated with MDRO carriage in this study, potentially due to the limited sample size and population heterogeneity. Despite the lack of statistical significance for some variables, observed trends may become significant in larger cohorts.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference, allowing only the identification of associations between risk factors and MDRO carriage. Six eligible admissions were not screened, and were excluded from analysis, which may have introduced selection bias. The limited number of MDRO-positive cases reduced the statistical power of multivariable analyses and may have led to unstable effect estimates; therefore, these analyses should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis generating. In pediatric populations, potential multicollinearity between age and body weight may further influence regression estimates, underscoring the need for cautious interpretation. Reliance on medical records for data collection is contingent upon the accuracy and completeness of existing documentation. The six-month study duration may not capture potential seasonal variations in prevalence. Lastly, the results may not fully reflect MDRO carriage in the broader pediatric community, as children admitted from the community to a single PICU may differ from the general pediatric population in terms of health status and healthcare exposure. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution and serve as a foundation for future research involving larger sample sizes, more comprehensive data, and methodologies capable of establishing stronger evidence of causal relationships.

Conclusions

The high prevalence of MDRO colonization among children admitted from the community to the PICU may indicate a significant public health issue in Greece, underscoring the need for preventive measures and enhanced microbial surveillance. Exploratory analyses suggested an association between lower body weight and MDRO carriage, but this relationship should be interpreted cautiously due to the limited number of outcome events and potential model instability. Further studies are required to validate these findings and clarify their clinical implications.
