Assessment of Promoter Hypermethylation in Tumor Suppressor Genes in Oral Leukoplakia and Oral Submucous Fibrosis
Authors: Nishath S. Abdul, Shubhangi Mhaske, Syed Afroz, Satyaprakash Nigam, Bhuvan Jyoti, Sasidhar Singaraju, Sahana Shivakumar, Shivakumar G. Channaiah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Promoter hypermethylation of tumor suppressor genes is a hallmark of early carcinogenesis in oral lesions.Aim:To assess the methylation status of p16, DAPK, and MGMT in oral leukoplakia and OSMF.Methods:DNA extracted from tissue samples of 30 leukoplakia, 30 OSMF, and 20 healthy controls was analyzed using methylation-specific PCR.Results:Promoter hypermethylation was significantly higher in both patient groups compared to controls (P < 0.05), with p16 most frequently affected.Conclusion:Aberrant promoter methylation may serve as an early molecular marker in oral precancerous conditions, aiding early intervention and risk stratification.

INTRODUCTION

Oral leukoplakia and oral submucous fibrosis (OSMF) are potentially malignant disorders with a significant risk of transformation into oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC). While traditional risk factors, such as tobacco and areca nut consumption, are well established, recent research has emphasized the importance of molecular and epigenetic alterations in their pathogenesis. Among these, promoter hypermethylation—a form of epigenetic silencing of tumor suppressor genes—has emerged as a critical mechanism in early carcinogenesis.

Promoter hypermethylation involves the addition of methyl groups to cytosine residues within CpG islands located in gene promoter regions. This leads to transcriptional repression of tumor suppressor genes involved in cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, and DNA repair. Genes, such as p16, DAPK, MGMT, and RASSF1A, are frequently hypermethylated in oral potentially malignant lesions and OSCC, suggesting their early involvement in malignant transformation.[1] Importantly, these epigenetic changes are reversible, making them promising biomarkers for early detection and therapeutic intervention.

In OSMF and leukoplakia, studies have demonstrated a progressive increase in promoter methylation levels correlating with histopathological severity, supporting the hypothesis that epigenetic events precede and promote malignant transformation.[2] Furthermore, hypermethylation patterns vary with patient habits and lesion type, highlighting the interplay between environmental exposure and epigenetic modulation.[3]

This study aims to evaluate the frequency and pattern of promoter hypermethylation in key tumor suppressor genes in patients with oral leukoplakia and OSMF. By understanding these epigenetic alterations, clinicians and researchers can identify molecular markers for risk stratification, early diagnosis, and the development of demethylating therapeutic strategies.

METHODOLOGY

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted in a tertiary dental care institution over a period of 12 months. The study population included patients diagnosed clinically and histopathologically with oral leukoplakia or oral submucous fibrosis (OSMF), as well as age- and sex-matched healthy controls without any oral lesions. A total of 90 participants were recruited and categorized into three groups: Group I (oral leukoplakia, n = 30), Group II (OSMF, n = 30), and Group III (healthy controls, n = 30).

After obtaining informed consent, incisional biopsy samples were collected from lesional sites in Groups I and II, and normal oral mucosa from controls. Samples were immediately preserved in RNAlater solution and stored at −80°C until analysis. Genomic DNA was extracted using the phenol–chloroform method, followed by sodium bisulfite treatment using a commercially available kit to convert unmethylated cytosines to uracil, leaving methylated cytosines unchanged.

Methylation-specific polymerase chain reaction (MSP) was then performed to assess promoter methylation status of selected tumor suppressor genes, including p16, DAPK, and MGMT. Primer sequences specific for methylated and unmethylated alleles were used, and PCR products were analyzed on 2% agarose gel electrophoresis stained with ethidium bromide. The presence or absence of methylated bands indicated the methylation status of each gene.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26. Chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were used to compare methylation frequencies between groups. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

The study analyzed promoter methylation status of three tumor suppressor genes—p16, DAPK, and MGMT—in patients with oral leukoplakia, oral submucous fibrosis (OSMF), and healthy controls. A total of 90 participants were included (30 in each group).

Promoter hypermethylation of p16 was observed in 70% of leukoplakia samples and 60% of OSMF cases, indicating a strong epigenetic silencing of this tumor suppressor gene in potentially malignant oral disorders. In contrast, only 13.3% of healthy controls showed p16 methylation. This difference was statistically significant (P < 0.001), suggesting a strong association between p16 hypermethylation and oral premalignancy [Table 1].

DAPK, a gene associated with apoptosis regulation, showed methylation in 56.7% of leukoplakia and 50% of OSMF samples, again with a significantly lower frequency (10%) in the control group (P < 0.001). The data imply that epigenetic inactivation of DAPK may facilitate the survival of aberrant oral epithelial cells [Table 1].

MGMT, a DNA repair gene, was methylated in 46.7% of leukoplakia and 40% of OSMF patients, compared to only 6.7% of controls. The significant P value indicates that downregulation of MGMT via methylation might play a role in the accumulation of genetic mutations in the pathogenesis of these lesions [Table 1].

Overall, promoter hypermethylation was significantly more frequent in both oral leukoplakia and OSMF groups compared to healthy controls for all three genes studied. These findings highlight the potential utility of epigenetic biomarkers for early diagnosis and risk stratification of oral premalignant disorders.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates a significant prevalence of promoter hypermethylation in tumor suppressor genes p16, DAPK, and MGMT among patients with oral leukoplakia and oral submucous fibrosis (OSMF), compared to healthy individuals. The findings suggest that epigenetic alterations are early events in the pathogenesis of potentially malignant oral disorders and may serve as biomarkers for early detection and disease progression monitoring.

The p16 gene, known to regulate the G1 checkpoint in the cell cycle, was hypermethylated in 70% of leukoplakia and 60% of OSMF cases. This aligns with earlier studies that reported high rates of p16 methylation in dysplastic oral lesions, indicating its role in the loss of cell cycle control during malignant transformation.[45]

DAPK, which facilitates apoptosis in response to oncogenic stress, also showed significantly higher methylation levels in both patient groups. Its inactivation by promoter methylation has been previously reported in head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (HNSCC), implicating its contribution to cell survival and tumor progression.[6]

Similarly, MGMT, a DNA repair gene that protects against G-to-A transitions by removing mutagenic adducts, was silenced in a substantial proportion of premalignant lesions. Loss of MGMT function through methylation may result in genomic instability and increase susceptibility to carcinogenesis, as supported by previous molecular pathology studies.[78]

Overall, this study supports the hypothesis that promoter hypermethylation of critical tumor suppressor genes is a key epigenetic mechanism in the development of oral precancerous conditions. Longitudinal studies with larger cohorts are needed to determine if these methylation markers can predict malignant transformation.

CONCLUSION

This study highlights the significant role of promoter hypermethylation in tumor suppressor genes such as p16, DAPK, and MGMT in the early stages of oral leukoplakia and OSMF. The findings underscore the potential of these epigenetic markers as diagnostic and prognostic tools for oral potentially malignant disorders. Incorporating methylation profiling into routine screening may improve early detection and risk assessment for malignant transformation.
