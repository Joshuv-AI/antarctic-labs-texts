The algorithm of dissatisfaction: social media and body image crisis among university students
Authors: Daniel García-Gutiérrez, Cristina García-Salido, Estel·la Ramírez-Baraldes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTo map and critically synthesize evidence (2020–2025) on the relationship between social media use/exposure and body image among university students, we conducted a scoping review guided by Arksey and O’Malley’s methodological framework and reported according to PRISMA-ScR. A descriptive numerical summary and qualitative thematic synthesis were produced; meta-analysis was not conducted due to heterogeneity in study designs and outcome measures.MethodologySeven databases (PubMed/MEDLINE, PsycINFO, Embase, CINAHL, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar) were searched using controlled vocabulary and free-text terms related to body image constructs, social media platforms/content, and higher education populations. Eligible peer-reviewed studies (quantitative, qualitative, mixed-methods, and systematic/scoping reviews) included samples with ≥ 70% university students and assessed a body image outcome in relation to social media exposure.ResultsA total of 81 studies (N = 19,947; ages predominantly 18–25; ~70% women) met inclusion criteria: 55.6% were cross-sectional, 18.5% experimental, 14.8% mixed-methods, and 11.1% longitudinal or EMA-based.DiscussionSix non-exclusive thematic groups were identified: [1] body dissatisfaction/shame/low physical self-esteem (63.0%); [2] eating disorders and weight control behaviours (32.1%); [3] cosmetic procedure seeking and probable body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) (11.1%); [4] protective/positive content (body positivity/neutrality, self-compassion), including brief “social media breaks” (16.0%); [5] psychological mediators/moderators (including self-objectification) (33.3%); and (6) methodological contributions (e.g., EMA designs, longitudinal and experimental approaches, and social media–specific measurement advances) (3.7%). Across all designs, the emotional the emotional valence, degree of idealization, and appearance-focused or stigmatizing nature of contentconsistently predicted outcomes more strongly than time-based exposure metrics. Brief, theory-based, digitally delivered interventions showed modest short-term improvements, although questions remain about their scalability and long-term efficacy.ConclusionDespite methodological advances, the evidence base is still constrained by cross-sectional self-report data, varied measurement tools, and underrepresentation of male and gender-diverse populations. Universities are encouraged to incorporate media literacy, comparison reappraisal, and self-compassion into student wellbeing programs, while future research should prioritize harmonized effect size reporting, mechanism-focused trials, and ethically governed digital trace methodologies.

Introduction

Body image has emerged as a salient public-health and mental-health concern on university campuses worldwide. Recent studies report that between one-third and two-thirds of undergraduates experience clinically meaningful body dissatisfaction, with particularly high rates among women, students with higher body-mass indices, and sexual- and gender-minority groups [1–3]. These disturbances co-occur with anxiety, depressive symptoms, disordered eating, and reduced academic engagement, thereby compromising students’ wellbeing and educational attainment.

University students warrant specific attention because this developmental period is marked by identity formation, increased autonomy, changing peer networks, and heightened sensitivity to appearance-based social comparison. At the same time, university life is typically accompanied by intense social media immersion, making this population particularly relevant for examining how digital appearance pressures may affect body image and related psychological outcomes.

Digital ecosystems, especially image- and video-centric platforms, have intensified appearance scrutiny during a developmental stage already characterized by identity exploration and social comparison. A growing body of work indicates that what students see on social media (e.g., “thinspiration,” filtered selfies, weight-stigmatizing discourse) is a stronger predictor of harm than total screen time [4–6]. Experimental and ecological-momentary-assessment (EMA) studies demonstrate immediate, within-day drops in body satisfaction and spikes in negative affect following exposure to idealized imagery [7–9], lending causal weight to sociocultural, objectification, and social-comparison frameworks. Importantly, the effects of social media on body image are shaped not only by the characteristics of the content encountered but also by how individuals use social media platforms. Research has increasingly distinguished between passive consumption of appearance-related content, such as scrolling through curated images of peers or influencers, and more active forms of engagement, such as posting selfies, editing photographs, monitoring feedback, or seeking reassurance about appearance [4–6, 10–12]. These behaviors may intensify upward social comparison, reinforce self-objectification, and increase reliance on external validation [13–16]. Algorithmically curated feeds may further amplify these processes by repeatedly exposing users to appearance-salient content. Incorporating these distinctions provides a more comprehensive framework for understanding how social media engagement influences body image among university students [17].

At the same time, social media also hosts counter-discourses: exposure to body-positivity (#BoPo) or body-neutrality content, self-compassion messaging, and brief “digital breaks” can yield modest improvements in body satisfaction and mood [18, 19]. Pilot interventions delivered via apps or platforms, such as media-literacy modules, values-affirmation tasks, and mindful self-compassion programs, have begun to attenuate risk indicators [20–23], yet questions remain regarding their durability, scalability, and equity of access.

Methodological innovation since 2020 is noteworthy: investigators have adopted longitudinal cohorts, laboratory experiments, mixed-methods designs, and EMA to probe within-day dynamics [13, 24, 25]. Mediation and moderation models now clarify how (e.g., upward comparison, perfectionism, self-objectification, alexithymia) and for whom (e.g., by media literacy, self-compassion, BMI, age, photo-editing habits) social media exerts its effects [13, 14, 26, 27]. Measurement work has also advanced, with validated instruments tailored to social-media contexts [28].

An additional reason for restricting the review to studies published from 2020 onward relates to major transformations in the social media ecosystem during this period. In recent years, image- and video-centered platforms have expanded rapidly, accompanied by algorithmically curated feeds, appearance filters, and intensified engagement metrics such as likes, shares, and follower counts. These platform affordances differ substantially from earlier social networking environments and may amplify appearance-based comparison and self-presentation pressures among emerging adults. Moreover, recent studies have increasingly adopted methodological innovations such as ecological momentary assessment, longitudinal modeling, and platform-specific measures of social media engagement. For these reasons, the present review focuses on the 2020–2025 period to map the most recent empirical evidence reflecting these technological and methodological developments, while earlier foundational literature informs the conceptual background of the review.

Although systematic reviews and meta-analyses excel at synthesizing homogeneous intervention trials, they often exclude emerging qualitative work and grey literature and cannot accommodate the heterogeneity of designs and outcomes in this field [29]. A scoping review, by contrast, permits comprehensive mapping of quantitative, qualitative, and grey sources to chart the breadth, nature, and thematic patterns of research on body image and social media use among university students [30]. Existing reviews in this field have provided valuable evidence on social networking site use, body dissatisfaction, and related symptoms, but many have focused on adolescents or broader young-adult populations, on specific outcomes such as body dissatisfaction or eating pathology, or on earlier platform environments [5, 17, 31]. The present review extends prior syntheses by focusing specifically on university students, by integrating adverse and protective outcomes together with mediators, moderators, interventions, and methodological developments, and by mapping evidence from the contemporary social media ecosystem between 2020 and 2025.

Despite this progress, critical gaps persist: cross-sectional self-report surveys still predominate, hampering causal inference; definitions of “fitspiration,” “body positivity,” and “negative body talk” vary widely, complicating synthesis; men and gender-diverse students remain under-represented; cultural moderators are rarely modelled; and almost all studies rely on self-reported exposure rather than passive digital-trace data, limiting ecological validity.

Accordingly, this scoping review aims to map the existing empirical work, quantitative, qualitative, and grey literature, on the interplay between body image and social media use in university student populations. We will identify prevailing themes and methodological approaches, chart gaps in longitudinal, mixed-methods, and gender-inclusive research, and propose directions for future inquiry to inform more robust, evidence-based interventions.

Study design

Drawing on the methodological scaffold introduced by the authors [29] and later refined by [30], this scoping review was conducted to map the recent empirical landscape on body-image research in higher-education settings with a specific focus on social media use and exposure. The approach, recommended for topics where evidence is diverse and rapidly expanding, offers a structured route to describe what is known, where consensus or inconsistency exists, and where substantive gaps remain. Reporting will follow the PRISMA extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR) to ensure methodological transparency [32].

Rapid evidence mapping is especially pertinent to body-image scholarship because prevalence estimates, psychosocial and digital correlates (e.g., social comparison, internalization of ideals), and intervention strategies vary widely across regions, disciplines, platforms and study designs. Scoping reviews, by cataloguing the breadth and nature of available work, clarify the size and characteristics of the literature, help determine whether a full systematic review is warranted, highlight methodological shortcomings and disseminate aggregated findings to researchers, clinicians and policy makers [33].

The review considered peer-reviewed primary studies published from January 2020 through July 2025 that examined body image, body dissatisfaction, or closely related constructs in relation to social media use, exposure, or content among university populations. Although database searches were conducted broadly, eligibility was limited to studies published in English or Spanish. Both quantitative and qualitative designs were eligible for inclusion. Two reviewers independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts, and extracted and charted data, resolving disagreements through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer when necessary. Descriptive numerical summaries of study characteristics were accompanied by qualitative thematic synthesis to identify recurring determinants, intervention approaches, and equity considerations.

In line with the objectives of scoping reviews, both primary empirical studies and review papers were considered eligible to comprehensively map the breadth of evidence, conceptual developments, and methodological approaches in the field.

Research question

The primary research question guiding this review is: What is the scope, characteristics, and methodological quality of studies (2020–2025) examining links between social media use/exposure and body image among university students, and what do they report regarding adverse-outcome prevalence, determinants (risk/protective), psychological mechanisms, and intervention effects in higher-education settings?

Search methods

This section details the procedures used to retrieve empirical research on body-image concerns among university students in relation to social media use. The description is intended to provide sufficient transparency for replication or subsequent updating of the review.

Search strategy

A transparent and reproducible search strategy was designed by combining controlled vocabulary, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) in PubMed/MEDLINE, CINAHL Headings in the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), Entree in Embase, and the American Psychological Association Thesaurus (APA Thesaurus) in PsycINFO, with free-text keywords drawn from titles and abstracts. Truncation with the asterisk (*) was used to retrieve spelling variants and different word endings (e.g., universit* retrieves university and universities). Where available, proximity operators (e.g., NEAR/3, W/3) were applied to increase precision.

The strategy targeted three concept blocks: (1) body-image constructs, (2) social media exposure/content, and (3) university student populations, with an additional filter restricting the publication window to 1 January 2020–23 July 2025. Iterative pilot runs in PubMed were used to refine synonyms, field tags, and operator choices until additional relevant records ceased to appear. The final PubMed syntax served as the template for all other databases, with platform-specific adjustments limited to field tags and Boolean/proximity operators. Sampling process flowchart

All records retrieved from PubMed/MEDLINE, PsycINFO, Embase, the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), Scopus, the Web of Science Core Collection (WOS) and Google Scholar were exported to Mendeley. Duplicate citations were first removed automatically and then checked manually to eliminate variant entries. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts and excluded clearly irrelevant papers. The same reviewers assessed the full texts of potentially eligible studies against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria; disagreements were resolved by discussion or, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer. Ethical considerations

This study is a scoping review that synthesizes and analyses data from previously published peer-reviewed articles. No primary data was collected, and no direct interaction with human participants occurred. Therefore, institutional ethics committee approval and informed consent were not required. All studies included were conducted in accordance with their respective ethical standards as reported by the original authors. The authors declare that they do not have any conflicts of interest.

Discussion

This scoping review synthesized 57 empirical studies and 24 review papers examining body image among university students in relation to social media use/exposure. The empirical studies provided the participant-level evidence, whereas the review papers helped contextualize broader conceptual and methodological trends. The evidence converges on three core assertions. First, what students see, i.e., the valence, idealization, and stigma embedded in content, is a stronger predictor of harm than time spent online [4–6]. Second, increasingly sophisticated designs (experiments, ecological momentary assessment [EMA], longitudinal models) corroborate theoretically posited mechanisms, especially upward social comparison, self-objectification, perfectionism, and negative affect [2, 7, 9, 14]. Third, brief, digital, theory-driven interventions (media literacy, self-compassion, values affirmation, curated positive/neutral content, short abstinence periods) yield modest but consistent benefits, though durability and scalability remain unclear [19, 20, 22, 23, 38]. Beyond content exposure, the present synthesis also highlights the importance of social media use processes. Passive consumption of appearance-focused content may increase opportunities for upward comparison, whereas active engagement behaviors such as posting images, editing photos, monitoring feedback, or seeking reassurance may intensify self-objectification and externalized self-evaluation [13–16]. In addition, algorithmic recommendation systems may repeatedly expose users to appearance-related material, reinforcing these comparison processes over time. Future research should therefore consider not only the valence or idealization of content but also patterns of engagement and platform affordances when examining body image outcomes among university students.

Having outlined the overall pattern, we interpret each thematic cluster in turn. Across domains, the synthesis not only identifies thematic groupings but also highlights the consistency of findings across study designs (e.g., cross-sectional, experimental, and EMA), as well as variability in outcome measurement and methodological approaches. This approach allows for a more nuanced interpretation of the strength and limitations of the evidence within each domain, as summarized in Tables 2 and 3, and informs implications for higher-education settings. Body dissatisfaction, shame, and low physical self-esteem.

Across cross-sectional, experimental, and EMA designs, exposure to “fit,” “thin,” and “slim-thick” ideals reliably predicted decreases in body satisfaction and increases in shame [2, 10, 38–42]. EMA studies showed within-day drops in satisfaction following fit/thinspiration exposure [7–9], strengthening causal claims beyond cross-sectional inference. Qualitative work further illustrates how students narrate cumulative exposure as corrosive to self-worth [13, 24, 37]. These findings are further supported by additional studies linking exposure to curated and idealized content on platforms like TikTok and Instagram with increased dissatisfaction and diminished physical self-esteem [1, 43, 44].

Disordered eating behaviors and eating-disorder (ED) risk.

Disordered eating behaviors and eating-disorder (ED) risk.

Disordered eating behaviors and eating-disorder (ED) risk.

Roughly one third of studies linked social media engagement to restriction, binge symptoms, “drunkorexia” [45], and subclinical ED presentations [4, 46, 47]. Instagram feeds rich in low-calorie-density foods were tied to higher disordered eating symptoms [21, 48], and pandemic-related distress amplified vulnerability[49] EMA data again captured same-day covariation between exposure and ED behaviors [8, 36]. Despite these convergences, outcome measures varied widely (e.g., different ED screeners, single-item proxies), complicating meta-analytic synthesis and underscoring the need for harmonized measurement. Other studies have also documented the role of thin ideal internalization and appearance-driven engagement in predicting disordered eating symptoms [20, 43, 45, 46].

Cosmetic procedure seeking and probable body dysmorphic disorder.

Cosmetic procedure seeking and probable body dysmorphic disorder.

Cosmetic procedure seeking and probable body dysmorphic disorder.

Filtered selfies, #beauty engagement, and targeted gym advertisements were associated with stronger cosmetic-surgery attitudes [42, 50–53]. Cross-sectional evidence from medical/dental students in Bangladesh and Egypt revealed notable rates of probable or subthreshold BDD [50, 54].Self-objectification emerged consistently as a key mechanism linking social media exposure to adverse mental and behavioral outcomes [15, 16]. In line with objectification theory, it is more appropriately conceptualized as a mediating psychological process rather than an outcome per se, through which repeated exposure to idealized and appearance-focused content may be internalized and translated into body-related distress and behavioral responses. This distinction is important, as it clarifies how upstream exposure processes may progressively contribute to downstream outcomes such as cosmetic procedure seeking or probable body dysmorphic disorder.

These trajectories suggest a potential pathway from body dissatisfaction toward more active forms of body modification, which campuses, and particularly health profession faculties, should address proactively. Supporting this interpretation, Alruwayshid et al. [55] found that appearance-based comparisons on social media significantly predicted intentions to pursue cosmetic surgery among young adults.

Internalized weight bias and negative body talk.

Internalized weight bias and negative body talk.

Peer discourse matters. Interaction with stigmatizing or appearance-focused talk predicted internalized weight bias and intensified shame [13, 56, 57]. Earlier foundational work has similarly underscored how appearance-based peer commentary reinforces internalization of weight stigma [33]. Previous research has also highlighted how social norms around weight and appearance in university settings can sustain cycles of negative body talk. Further evidence shows that appearance-based comparisons and peer discourse on social media platforms amplify weight bias internalization and self-directed body criticism [33, 58]. Interventions must therefore extend beyond visual literacy to address the language of body disparagement.

Negative affect and appearance-related anxiety.

Negative affect and appearance-related anxiety.

Idealized content exposure increased negative affect, social appearance anxiety, and broader emotional distress [9, 27, 59, 60]. Notably, negative affect functions both as an outcome and a mediator linking exposure to disordered eating and dissatisfaction, suggesting reciprocal feedback loops that may reinforce maladaptive engagement [23, 61]. Additional work has shown that appearance anxiety can be intensified by frequent photo editing and concerns about self-presentation on social platforms [8, 18, 36].

Across experimental and quasi-experimental designs, exposure to body positive (#BoPo), self-compassion, or body-neutrality content improved body satisfaction and mood showed that a one-week social media break enhanced self-esteem and body image [6, 18, 19, 38, 62]. These are low-cost, student-friendly strategies, yet effects were typically modest and short-term. Comparative studies of #BoPo versus neutrality indicate both confer benefits, perhaps via different pathways (e.g., reducing comparison vs. enhancing acceptance). Future research should adopt multi-arm, longer-term trials to confirm durability and explore hybrid approaches (e.g., curating feeds plus periodic abstinence).

Media literacy and micro-interventions.

Media literacy and micro-interventions.

Media literacy and micro-interventions.

Feasibility trials and small RCTs reported promising outcomes for media-literacy modules [20], values-affirmation exercises [23], and mindful self-compassion via Facebook [21, 22] directly targeted the social media–ED-risk link, attenuating risk indicators. However, most interventions lacked long-term follow-up, diverse samples, and active comparison arms. Implementation-science frameworks could guide the next phase, assessing fidelity, cost-effectiveness, and integration into campus services.

Mechanisms and moderators.

Mechanisms and moderators.

Mechanisms and moderators.

Explicit mechanism testing is a notable strength of literature. Upward social comparison, appearance perfectionism, self-objectification, internalized beauty ideals, and negative affect most often operated as risk-enhancing mediators linking social media exposure to poorer body image and eating-related outcomes [9, 13–16, 25, 27]. Evidence for mediating mechanisms was relatively consistent across studies, although variability in model specification and measurement approaches limits direct comparison of effect sizes. By contrast, media literacy, self-compassion, and emotion regulation tended to function as protective moderators or buffering factors, attenuating the harmful effects of appearance-focused engagement. BMI, age, weight perception, and photo-editing habits also emerged as important moderators in some studies [26, 63], age and weight perception [57], and photo-editing habits [14]. These findings also highlight culturally contingent vulnerability profiles. In particular, studies conducted in Iranian, East Asian, and Middle Eastern university student populations suggest that collectivist values, modesty norms, and stronger expectations regarding social conformity may shape how appearance pressures are experienced on social media. In some contexts, such norms may intensify pressure to conform to culturally sanctioned ideals and discourage open resistance to appearance-based expectations; in others, they may partially constrain overt self-presentation while still reinforcing internalized appearance monitoring. These dynamics suggest that the influence of social media on body image is not culturally uniform. These findings justify precision prevention, tailoring campus interventions to psychological profiles (e.g., high perfectionism) and situational triggers (e.g., heavy filter use).

Measurement advances and syntheses.

Measurement advances and syntheses.

Measurement advances and syntheses.

Maheux et al. validated the Appearance-Related Social Media Consciousness Scale, improving construct specificity. Such tools facilitate nuanced assessments of appearance-related processes in real time, providing the granularity needed for micro-intervention design [28]. Reviews reinforce the consensus that content type and process variables trump crude exposure duration [5, 6, 64]. The field is moving toward more nuanced, psychometrically robust measurement, an essential prerequisite for cumulative science.

Across studies, social media use/exposure was operationalized in multiple ways, including time-based measures, platform-specific use, passive versus active engagement, appearance-focused interaction, exposure to idealized or stigmatizing content, and, in a smaller number of studies, process-level variables such as comparison, internalization, feedback monitoring, and appearance-related consciousness. This heterogeneity helps explain why content and process variables often appear more informative than crude duration-based metrics.

Differential patterns: gender, culture, platform, and context.

Differential patterns: gender, culture, platform, and context.

Differential patterns: gender, culture, platform, and context.

Women comprised ~ 70% of participants and showed greater vulnerability to dissatisfaction, comparison, and anxiety [1, 3, 65, 66]. Men, under-represented, reported muscularity-focused concerns and responded differently to fitspiration/self-compassion content [63, 67]. Non-binary and gender-diverse students were largely underrepresented. Only a very small number of studies reported these participants separately, which limited interpretation of their specific experiences and outcomes. This remains an important gap, especially given evidence from broader body image literature suggesting potentially distinct vulnerability profiles in gender-diverse populations.

Culturally, qualitative and cross-national studies suggest that body ideals and appearance pressures vary meaningfully across contexts [13, 31, 37, 55, 68–71]. For example, some studies described tensions between “thin” ideals and “slim-thick” ideals, differences in the acceptability of cosmetic modification, and variation in the extent to which stigma around weight and appearance is normalized in peer discourse. Studies from Middle Eastern and Asian contexts also suggested that social conformity, modesty norms, and family or community expectations may shape how appearance ideals are internalized and negotiated. However, few studies directly compare cultural contexts using equivalent measures, limiting firm conclusions [13, 31, 37, 55, 68–71]. For instance, Iranian university students reported complex tensions between religious modesty norms and emergent beauty pressures on social platforms [72].

Platform-specific risks emerged: TikTok filters and short-form videos [1, 43, 44]. Instagram’s curated imagery [10, 12], WeChat Moments [36], Snapchat use linked to “drunkorexia” [45], and dating apps[73] which promote rapid appearance-based judgment and often lack protective features such as content filters. However, direct comparative studies of platform affordances (video vs. static images, degree of filter augmentation, algorithmic recommendation patterns) remain scarce.

COVID-19 contextualized several studies, both as a stressor increasing maladaptive social media use [43, 49] and as a backdrop for technology-mediated fitness practices [43, 74]. The long-term post-pandemic trajectory of these patterns is yet to be charted.

Methodological strengths and limitations of the evidence base.

Methodological strengths and limitations of the evidence base.

Methodological strengths and limitations of the evidence base.

The 81 studies show a welcome broadening of methodology beyond traditional cross-sectional surveys. EMA captured within-day fluctuations between exposure and body-image states [7, 18, 36], while randomized or quasi-experimental designs strengthened causal inference [40, 41, 67]. Longitudinal models traced temporal trajectories [25], and mixed-methods work contextualized quantitative effects [13, 24]. Several teams used validated or newly validated instruments[28, 75] and applied robust statistical approaches (mediation, moderation, multilevel modelling), aligning design choices with theoretical mechanisms.

Nevertheless, cross-sectional, self-report surveys still predominate, restricting causal inference. Convenience samples, largely female and drawn from psychology/health programs, limit generalizability [76, 77]. Operational definitions of key constructs (“fitspiration,” “body positivity,” “negative body talk”) varied widely, hindering synthesis and meta-analytic aggregation. Intervention studies were typically short, small, and lacked active control groups [20, 22, 23], making effect durability uncertain. Reliance on self-reported exposure (time online, type of content) invites recall and social desirability biases; ethically governed passive digital-trace data could enhance ecological validity [7, 36].

Our appraisal indicates most studies fall within a moderate risk-of-bias range, primarily due to sampling and measurement constraints. Accordingly, while protective effects (e.g., media literacy, self-compassion, #BoPo exposure) appear promising, the certainty surrounding their magnitude and persistence is best described as low to moderate.

Further limitations concern the temporal restriction of the review to studies published between 2020 and 2025. Although this decision allowed the review to focus on the most recent social media environments and methodological approaches, it necessarily excludes earlier foundational research that contributed to the development of current theoretical models of social comparison, objectification, and body image. Therefore, the conclusions of this review should be interpreted as reflecting the contemporary empirical landscape rather than the full historical development of the field.

Implications for higher-education practice, policy, and research.

Implications for higher-education practice, policy, and research.

Implications for higher-education practice, policy, and research.

Findings justify embedding media literacy, self-compassion, and comparison-reappraisal modules in university health curricula [20, 23, 26]. Counselling services should screen for platform-specific triggers (filters, dieting content) and incorporate mechanism-targeted therapies (e.g., addressing perfectionism, emotion regulation). Peer-led initiatives could challenge negative body talk and weight stigma [13, 57].

Universities can partner with student unions to promote feed-curation campaigns (e.g., follow #BoPo, unfollow comparison triggers) and trial structured “digital sabbaticals” [38]. Collaboration with platform stakeholders could advance filter transparency and algorithmic accountability [53, 61].

Given cultural and gender gaps, interventions should be culturally sensitive and inclusive of men, non-binary students, and diverse academic disciplines [37, 50, 71, 72]. Tailored messaging may be necessary for muscularity concerns among men [63] and cosmetic-modification pressures among health-profession students [54].

Conclusion

This scoping review delineates a coherent picture of how social media intersects with body image within higher-education contexts. Across 81 studies, three messages stand out. First, the quality of exposure, specifically, idealized, appearance-centric, or stigmatizing content, emerges as the principal driver of harm, overshadowing crude metrics such as time spent online. Second, the field has moved beyond simple associations: theoretically grounded mechanisms (e.g., social comparison, self-objectification, perfectionism, negative affect) are now routinely modelled, and buffering factors (media literacy, self-compassion, emotion regulation) are beginning to be specified. Third, small, digitally delivered, theory-driven interventions and strategic content curation demonstrate consistent, if modest, improvements in body image and related affective outcomes; however, questions remain about sustainability, reach, and scalability.

Methodologically, the evidence base is diversifying, incorporating EMA, experiments, longitudinal cohorts, and mixed methods, but still leans heavily on cross-sectional self-report data from predominantly female convenience samples. Heterogeneous operational definitions and outcome measures hinder cumulative synthesis, and most intervention trials lack active comparators and long-term follow-up. Consequently, the overall certainty of evidence, particularly for protective strategies, is best judged as low to moderate.

For universities, the implications are actionable. Health promotion curricula can integrate media literacy, comparison-reappraisal, and self-compassion components; counselling services can screen for platform-specific triggers; and campus-wide campaigns can encourage feed curation and periodic digital breaks. Equity demands gender-inclusive and culturally attuned approaches, and partnerships with platform stakeholders could improve transparency around filters and algorithmic recommendations.

Future research should prioritize preregistered longitudinal and experimental designs, harmonized effect-size reporting, intersectional sampling, platform-comparative analyses, and hybrid methodologies that blend EMA with ethically governed passive digital traces. Implementation science frameworks will be pivotal for translating promising micro-interventions into sustainable campus programs.

In short, social media functions as a double-edged sword in university life: it can erode body image through pervasive ideals and stigmas, yet it also offers scalable channels for brief, targeted, preventive interventions. Leveraging the latter while mitigating the former is the next imperative for researchers, practitioners, and policy makers in higher education.
