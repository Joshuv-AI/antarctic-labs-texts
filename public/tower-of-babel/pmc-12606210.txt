Targeting the p38/MAPK pathway to induce apoptosis: a multidimensional mechanistic exploration of Mentha and its active compound diosmetin against liver cancer
Authors: Yang Song, Ying Du, Weitong Li, Yuting Fan, Yuxin Zhang, Haotian Shen, Li Cheng, Jinhui Wang, Fengmei Deng, Bin Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiver cancer, a malignancy with high global incidence and mortality, currently relies on surgical resection, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, all of which face significant limitations, necessitating novel therapeutic strategies. Mentha (ME), a medicinal and edible herb, has demonstrated antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and broad-spectrum anticancer activities, yet its molecular mechanisms against liver cancer remain unclear. This study will comprehensively explore the anti-liver cancer mechanisms of ME and its key bioactive constituent, diosmetin (Dio). A multi-disciplinary approach, which incorporates network pharmacology, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulations, was adopted in this study to thoroughly explore the bioactive components of ME and the mechanisms through which they exert anti-liver cancer effects. Functional validation was conducted through CCK-8 viability assays, clonogenic survival assays, scratch wound healing, Transwell migration assays, Western blotting, immunofluorescence, and TUNEL apoptosis assays in human liver cancer cell lines (HepG2 and HuH-7). ME exhibited potent anti-Liver cancer activity, significantly suppressing cell viability, proliferation (CCK-8/clonogenic assays), and migration (scratch/Transwell assays, P < 0.01), while downregulating metastasis-related proteins MMP2/MMP9 (Western blot/immunofluorescence, P < 0.01). Network pharmacology identified TP53 (p53), TNF, CASP3 (caspase3), IL6, and IL1B as core targets. Based on the results of molecular docking (ΔG < − 4 kcal/mol) and molecular dynamics simulations (maximum ΔTotal), Dio was prioritized for subsequent experimental validation. Further validation demonstrated Dio’s multi-modal efficacy: GO/KEGG analysis revealed its dual action via p38/MAPK signaling and apoptosis pathways, corroborated by upregulated pro-apoptotic markers (p53, caspase3, Bax, p38) and downregulated Bcl2 (P < 0.01), alongside TUNEL-confirmed apoptosis induction (P < 0.01). This study is the first to demonstrate that ME and its active compound Dio inhibit liver cancer progression via multi-target regulation of the p38/MAPK pathway, providing a theoretical foundation for developing ME-based natural therapeutics against liver cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23009-8.Keywords: Liver cancer, Mentha, Diosmetin, Apoptosis, p38/MAPK pathwaySubject terms: Cancer prevention, Organelles

Introduction

Making up 90% of primary liver cancer cases, Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is globally recognized as the sixth most widespread malignancy. With a dismal 5-year survival rate that is lower than 18%, it ranks as the third major cause of cancer-related fatalities1–6. The etiology of HCC has a strong connection with the progression of chronic liver disorders. Among these, hepatic fibrosis, cirrhosis, and viral hepatitis (HBV and HCV infections) are key contributing factors to the pathogenesis7–11. Current clinical strategies predominantly rely on surgical resection, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy12; however, these approaches face significant limitations: surgery often leaves residual micrometastases leading to high recurrence rates13, while radiotherapy and chemotherapy are hampered by systemic toxicity that compromises efficacy-safety balance14–16. Thus, developing novel therapies with high efficacy and low toxicity remains an urgent unmet need in HCC management.

Over the past few years, natural products have attracted significant interest within the field of oncology, primarily because of their multi-target characteristics and desirable safety features17–21. These agents not only alleviate cancer-related symptoms but also mitigate adverse effects and complications induced by chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or targeted therapies22,23. Studies demonstrate that herbal medicines can synergize with conventional treatments to enhance efficacy, reverse drug resistance, and reduce toxicity, leading to their inclusion in international HCC clinical guidelines24–28. For instance, Astragalus polysaccharides potentiate PD-1 inhibitor-mediated antitumor immunity29, while curcumin suppresses HCC metastasis via modulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway30. Nevertheless, the bioactive components and precise mechanisms of most herbal agents remain incompletely characterized, hindering their clinical translation.

Mentha (ME), a plant with both medicinal and culinary uses, demonstrates a wide range of biological activities. These include antioxidant, antimicrobial, anticancer, antiviral, antiallergic, anti - inflammatory, and antihypertensive properties31,32. Its volatile components (e.g., menthol) inhibit cancer cell proliferation via apoptosis induction33. Meanwhile, flavonoid compounds, with Dio being a prime example, impede the growth and multiplication of tumors. This is accomplished through the modulation of cell cycle advancement and lipid metabolic processes34. Notably, despite ME’s established efficacy in managing gastrointestinal35 and cardiovascular disorders36, its molecular mechanisms against HCC remain unexplored.

Against this backdrop, the current study systematically explores the mechanisms by which ME and its active component Dio inhibit HCC through a combination of network pharmacology prediction, molecular docking screening, molecular dynamics simulations, and in vitro experimental validation, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The key findings include: 1.Affinity targeting: Dio, a core derivative component of ME, exhibits better binding affinity to HCC-related targets compared to other components. 2.Functional inhibition: Both ME and Dio are capable of suppressing the proliferation and migration of HepG2/HuH-7 cells and reducing the expression of MMP2/MMP9(P < 0.01). Their role in promoting apoptosis is linked to the increased levels of p53, Bax, and caspase3, along with the decreased level of Bcl2. 3.Central Hub Validation: The p38/MAPK signaling axis—a well-documented therapeutic target in HCC37–39—was further confirmed as the central hub mediating ME-dependent apoptosis. These discoveries not only provide mechanistic evidence for ME’s anti-HCC efficacy but also establish a theoretical foundation for developing multi-target natural product-based therapies against this lethal malignancy.

Research workflow for the anti-liver cancer effect of ME and its active component diosmetin (Dio). First, network pharmacology analysis was conducted to screen the core targets of ME acting on liver cancer, followed by in vitro experiments to verify the effect of ME on human hepatocellular carcinoma cells HepG2/HuH-7. Subsequently, molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation were used to screen the active component Dio from ME; network pharmacology analysis was then performed for Dio and liver cancer, and in vitro experiments were further applied to verify the effect and potential mechanism of Dio on the aforementioned liver cancer cells.

The active component Dio from Mentha (ME) upregulates the p38/MAPK signaling pathway in hepatocellular carcinoma HepG2 cells. p38/MAPK exerts pro-apoptotic effects by directly phosphorylating members of the Bcl2 family: phosphorylation of Bax (Bcl2-associated X protein) activates its pro-apoptotic function, while phosphorylation of Bcl2 suppresses its anti-apoptotic activity. Concurrently, p38/MAPK enhances the transcriptional activity of p53, promoting the expression of downstream target genes and thereby driving apoptosis.

Materials and methods

Network pharmacology

Acquisition of ME, active component dio, liver cancer targets, and common target screening

The possible targets of ME and its bioactive constituent Dio were sourced from multiple databases, namely the Herb Database (http://herb.ac.cn/), ETCM (http://www.tcmip.cn/ETCM/), PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and the TCMSP Database (https://tcmspw.com/tcmsp.php). Conversely, targets associated with liver cancer were retrieved from GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/), CTD (https://ctdbase.org/), and DisGeNET (https://www.disgenet.org/). Using the SRplot platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/login/), the common targets between ME and liver cancer, as well as those between Dio and liver cancer, were recognized through Venn diagrams.

Building the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network of crucial targets

The shared targets were uploaded to STRING 12.0 (https://cn.string-db.org/) for the purpose of building a protein - protein interaction (PPI) network. Subsequently, this network was rendered visible and examined with the aid of Cytoscape 3.9.1 in order to pinpoint the key hub targets.

Enrichment analysis based on gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG)

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed on the key targets. These analyses were conducted using the SRplot platform. This process emphasized the top 10 KEGG pathways that showed significant enrichment. Subsequently, a network linking Dio, targets, KEGG pathways, and liver cancer was additionally established by leveraging Cytoscape version 3.9.1.

Molecular docking

Employing the TCMSP platform, the active components of ME were screened according to the standards of oral bioavailability (OB ≥ 30%) and drug - likeness (DL ≥ 0.18). This screening process resulted in the identification of five prominent candidates: diosmetin (Dio), naringenin, linarin, genkwanin, and sitosterol. Molecular docking experiments between these selected compounds and key targets were carried out using PyMol 2.5 and AutoDock 4.2. The docking results were then presented as 2D and 3D interaction diagrams through LigPlot + V2, while SRplot was employed to create binding energy heatmaps.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Gromacs2022.3 software was utilized to conduct molecular dynamics simulations. In terms of small molecule preprocessing, AmberTools22 was applied to assign the GAFF force field to small molecules, whereas Gaussian 16 W was used for hydrogenating small molecules and computing RESP charges. These charge data were incorporated into the topology file of the molecular dynamics system. Simulations were conducted under conditions of 300 K constant temperature and 1 Bar atmospheric pressure. The Amber99sb-ildn force field was utilized, with water molecules (Tip3p water model) as the solvent, and the overall charge of the simulation system was neutralized by introducing a suitable number of Na + ions. The simulation system was first subjected to energy minimization via the steepest descent method. This was followed by 100,000 steps of isothermal-isochoric (NVT) equilibrium and isothermal-isobaric (NPT) equilibrium in sequence, with each equilibrium stage having a coupling constant of 0.1 ps and a duration of 100 ps. Finally, free molecular dynamics simulations were run, consisting of 5,000,000 steps with a step size of 2 fs, totaling 100ns in duration. After the simulation was completed, the software’s built-in tools were used to analyze the trajectories, calculating parameters such as the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) of each amino acid trajectory, and the protein radius of gyration, in combination with data including free energy (MMGBSA) and free energy landscape.

Cells, drugs and reagents

The HCC lines HepG2 (TCH-C196) and HuH-7 (TCH-C217) used in this experiment were purchased from the official website of Hycyte Biotechnology (https://www.hycyte.com/sys-col-115/). The STR profiling report, mycoplasma detection results, and other relevant documents (Attachment 1,2) provided by the supplier showed that the matching rate of these cell lines with the standard sequences in the database was ≥ 0.8, with no cross-contamination. The mycoplasma detection result was verified as negative, confirming that the identity of the cells is authentic and reliable. ME with a purity of 98% was procured from Shaanxi Sinotro Bio-Tech Co., Ltd., while Dio, which has a purity of 99.63% and a CAS number of 520 − 34–3, was sourced from MedChemExpress. ME was dissolved in distilled water, then filtered using a 0.22 μm membrane (Millipore SLGPR33RB) and kept at a temperature of − 20 °C. A variety of antibodies were obtained from Proteintech. These antibodies included MMP2 (10373–2 - AP), MMP9 (10375–2 - AP), Tubulin (66200–1 - Ig), GAPDH (60004–1 - Ig), Bcl − 2 (26593–1 - AP), Bax (50599–2 - Ig), Caspase3 (19677–1 - AP), p53 (60283–2 - Ig), p38 (14064–1 - AP).

CCK-8 cell viability assay

In 96-well plates, HepG2 cells (1 × 10⁴ cells per well) were exposed to ME at concentrations of 2, 4, 8, 16, and 32 mg/mL or Dio ranging from 10 to 70 µM for a period of 24 h. Once the incubation was complete, the CCK − 8 working solution (prepared with a ratio of DMEM: CCK-8 = 1:10) was introduced. Following that, a microplate reader was employed to measure the absorbance at 450 nm. The same treatment protocol was applied to HuH-7 cells.

Clonogenic assay

In 24-well plates, with 3 × 10³ HepG2 cells seeded per well, they were incubated with ME at concentrations of 3.5, 7, and 14 mg/mL or Dio at 10, 20, and 40 µM for a duration of three days. Next, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and then stained using crystal violet. Regarding HuH-7 cells, 6 × 10³ cells were seeded into each well of 6-well plates and were treated in a similar way with ME at concentrations of 2, 3, and 4 mg/mL.

Scratch wound healing assay

HepG2 cells (2.7 × 106 well in 6-well plates) were scratched perpendicularly and treated with ME (3.5, 7, 14 mg/mL) or Dio (10, 20, 40 µM) in serum-free medium. Wound closure was imaged at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h. HuH-7 cells were treated analogously with ME (2, 3, 4 mg/mL).

Transwell invasion assay

Serum - starved HepG2 cells (3 × 10⁵ cells per chamber in a medium supplemented with 5% FBS) were subjected to ME at concentrations of 3.5, 7, and 14 mg/mL or Dio at 10, 20, and 40 µM in the upper chamber. In the meantime, the lower chamber was filled with a 20% FBS - containing medium. After 72 h, invaded cells were fixed, stained, and imaged. HuH-7 cells (1.5 × 105 chamber) were treated with ME (2, 3, 4 mg/mL) under identical conditions. After 24 h, invaded cells were fixed, stained, and imaged.

Western blot

After a 24 - hour incubation period with ME or Dio, the cells were disrupted in a buffer chilled on ice, which included 1 mM PMSF. The obtained cell lysates were then spun down at 14,000 ×g for 10 min. Next, the BCA assay was employed to measure the protein concentrations in the lysates. Subsequently, the samples were separated by SDS - PAGE. Finally, with the help of Image Lab software, the protein bands were made visible using ECL reagents.

Immunofluorescence

In 24 - well plates, in which 2 × 10⁴ cells per well were seeded on coverslips, the cells experienced a sequential series of processes. Initially, the cells were fixed. Then, they were made permeable with 0.3% Triton X − 100. Next, the cells were blocked with 5% goat serum. After that, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Following a 1 - hour incubation at 37 °C with secondary antibodies, the cell nuclei were stained with DAPI. Finally, cell images were obtained using a Thermo EVOS5000 fluorescence microscope.

TUNEL assay

Cells on coverslips were fixed, permeabilized, and processed using the Beyotime TUNEL Kit (C1086). Apoptotic nuclei were stained and imaged as above.

Statistical analysis

The findings were reported as the mean plus/minus the standard deviation, obtained from three independent experimental attempts. In the aspect of statistical treatment, the Student’s t - test was employed to make contrasts between the two groups. All these analyses were performed by means of GraphPad Prism 9.5. Statistical significance was determined when the p - value was lower than 0.05.

Results

ME-liver cancer common target network and PPI analysis

A total of 296 ME-related targets were retrieved from the Herb and ETCM databases, while 1,601 Liver cancer-associated targets were identified via DisGeNET. Intersection analysis using a Venn diagram revealed 78 common targets between ME and Liver cancer (Fig. 3A). In order to clarify how these common targets function in relation to each other, a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was built with the help of the STRING database. This network was then rendered visible using Cytoscape 3.9.1, as illustrated in Fig. 3B. Network topology analysis using the CentiScaPe plugin in Cytoscape evaluated three centrality parameters: degree (> 28), betweenness centrality (> 126), and closeness centrality (> 0.0063). Fourteen hub targets meeting these thresholds were identified (Table 1), from which the top five core targets—TP53 (p53), TNF, CASP3 (caspase3), IL6, and IL1B—were prioritized (Fig. 3C).

ME suppresses HCC cell viability and proliferation

To assess the cytotoxic impact of ME on HCC cells, HepG2 and HuH-7 cell lines were exposed to escalating ME concentrations (2, 4, 8, 16, 32 mg/mL) for 24 h. The results of CCK − 8 assays revealed a dose - dependent reduction in cell viability. The calculated IC50 values were 15.52 mg/mL for HepG2 cells and 4.42 mg/mL for HuH-7 cells, as shown in Fig. 4A. To assess ME’s impact on cell proliferation, HepG2 cells were treated with ME at 3.5, 7, 14 mg/mL (based on IC50), while HuH-7 cells received 1, 2, 4 mg/mL (based on IC50). Clonogenic assays demonstrated a dose-dependent decrease in colony formation, with reduced colony number compared to untreated controls (Fig. 4B).

The ramifications of ME on the viability and proliferative prowess of HCC cells were explored. (A) The CCK − 8 assay was harnessed to elucidate the impact of ME on the viability of HepG2 and HuH-7 cells. (B) The clonogenic assay was implemented to assess the inhibitory effect of ME on the propagation of HepG2 and HuH-7 cells, thereby determining its suppressive influence on cellular proliferation.

ME inhibits HCC cell migration

To elucidate the impact of ME on the migratory and invasive capabilities of HCC cells, scratch wound healing and Transwell invasion assays were employed. In the scratch assays, HepG2 and HuH-7 cells were administered ME, and the kinetics of wound closure were quantified at 24, 48, and 72 h post-treatment. ME significantly reduced horizontal migration distance in a dose-dependent manner, with higher concentrations leading to lower migration rates (Fig. 5A). For vertical migration assessment, Transwell assays demonstrated that ME treatment markedly decreased the number of migrated cells through the chamber membrane compared to untreated controls. This suppression was also dose-dependent, with fewer cells penetrating the membrane at higher ME concentrations (Fig. 5B).

ME inhibits MMP2 and MMP9 expression

The matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) family is not an insignificant group of enzymes that participate in the hydrolysis of the basement membrane and extracellular matrix. MMP2 and MMP9 are highly expressed in HCC. To assess ME’s ability to inhibit liver cancer progression, HepG2 and Huh-7 cells were treated with three different doses of ME for 24 h. After this incubation, Western blot analysis was performed to measure the protein expression levels of MMP2 and MMP9. The results demonstrated that, contrary to an increase, the expression levels of MMP2 and MMP9 in the groups treated with ME were markedly decreased compared to those in the control group (Fig. 6A). To further verify the impact of ME on liver cancer, immunofluorescence staining was also conducted. These findings aligned with the Western blot analysis outcomes. More precisely, a progressive decline in MMP2 protein expression was observed as the concentrations of ME escalated (Fig. 6B).

Analysis of molecular dynamics simulation results

To confirm the stability of ligand-receptor binding, we conducted a detailed molecular dynamics simulation (MD) evaluation on four protein-small molecule complex systems: IL1B-Genkwanin, IL1B-Naringenin, IL1B-Sitosterol, and TNF-Diosmetin. The simulation results were quantified using multiple key metrics, including the radius of gyration of the protein (gyrate-pro), number of hydrogen bonds (hbond-pro-lig), root mean square deviation of the complex (rmsd-complex), root mean square fluctuation of the protein (rmsf-pro), and solvent-accessible surface area of the protein (sasa-pro). All these metrics indicated high stability of the systems: small standard deviations reflected minimal fluctuations during the simulation, the integrity of the complex structure was maintained, and the ligand-protein affinity was favorable. Additionally, the binding free energy calculated via the MM/GBSA method further confirmed the strong binding affinity of these systems. The negative total binding energy (ΔTotal) indicated a spontaneous binding process.

MD simulations of IL1B-genkwanin, IL1B-naringenin and IL1B-sitosterol

In the IL1B-Genkwanin system: The average radius of gyration of the protein was 1.50 nm with a standard deviation of only 0.01 nm, indicating that the protein structure was highly compact and stable, with no significant unfolding or contraction (Fig. 8A). The average number of hydrogen bonds was 1 (derived from the integerization of 1.05 ± 0.85), which reflected the formation of stable hydrogen bond interactions between the Genkwanin ligand and IL1B protein. Despite relatively large fluctuations, hydrogen bond formation was maintained overall (Fig. 8B). The average RMSD of the complex was 0.19 nm with a standard deviation of 0.02 nm, demonstrating that the complex maintained extremely high structural stability during the simulation, with almost no obvious drift (Fig. 8C). The average RMSF of the protein was 0.13 nm with a standard deviation of 0.08 nm, indicating moderate flexibility of amino acid residues, no regions with excessive fluctuations, and maintained rigidity of the protein backbone (Fig. 8D). The average solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) was 89.53 nm² with a standard deviation of 1.75 nm², suggesting stable surface exposure without drastic changes caused by ligand binding (Fig. 8E).

Collectively, these results indicated that the IL1B-Genkwanin complex reached an equilibrium state during the simulation, with favorable affinity.

For the IL1B-Naringenin system: The average radius of gyration of the protein was 1.51 nm with a standard deviation of 0.01 nm. Similar to the IL1B-Genkwanin system, this indicated a highly consistent and stable protein conformation (Fig. 8A). The average number of hydrogen bonds was 2 (derived from 1.89 ± 0.98), showing that the Naringenin ligand formed more hydrogen bonds with the IL1B protein, which enhanced the strength and stability of the interaction (Fig. 8B). The average RMSD of the complex was 0.20 nm with a standard deviation of 0.02 nm, indicating minimal overall structural fluctuations in the system and the achievement of a stable equilibrium state (Fig. 8C). The average RMSF of the protein was 0.13 nm with a standard deviation of 0.07 nm, showing uniform distribution of residue flexibility and no locally unstable regions (Fig. 8D). The average solvent-accessible surface area was 89.44 nm² with a standard deviation of 1.64 nm², indicating stable surface properties (Fig. 8E). These metrics highlighted the excellent stability of the IL1B-Naringenin system, and the increased number of hydrogen bonds further improved its affinity.

In the IL1B-Sitosterol system: The average radius of gyration of the protein was 1.51 nm with a standard deviation of 0.01 nm, which also indicated the compactness and stability of the protein structure (Fig. 8A). The average number of hydrogen bonds was 1 (derived from 1.05 ± 0.75), suggesting that the Sitosterol ligand formed a reliable hydrogen bond network with the protein. Although the number of hydrogen bonds was small, it was sufficient to maintain binding (Fig. 8B). The average RMSD of the complex was 0.22 nm with a standard deviation of 0.02 nm. The system was overall stable; although the RMSD value was slightly higher, it remained within an acceptable range, indicating that equilibrium was achieved after slight dynamic adjustments (Fig. 8C). The average RMSF of the protein was 0.13 nm with a standard deviation of 0.07 nm, showing uniform residue fluctuations (Fig. 8D). The average solvent-accessible surface area was 90.38 nm² with a standard deviation of 2.15 nm², indicating a slight increase in surface exposure but controllable fluctuations (Fig. 8E). These results demonstrated that the IL1B-Sitosterol complex had good stability and moderate affinity, making it suitable for optimization to enhance hydrogen bond interactions.

The average RMSF of the protein was 0.11 nm with a standard deviation of 0.06 nm, representing the lowest residue flexibility and indicating high protein rigidity (Fig. 9D). These metrics showed that the TNF-Diosmetin system had the best stability and extremely strong affinity.

MM/GBSA binding free energy analysis

The MM/GBSA calculation further quantified the binding affinity of these systems. The van der Waals energy contribution (ΔEvdW) was negative in all systems: -26.82 ± 0.46 kcal/mol for IL1B-Genkwanin, -26.86 ± 0.37 kcal/mol for IL1B-Naringenin, -30.23 ± 0.85 kcal/mol for IL1B-Sitosterol, and − 37.28 ± 0.27 kcal/mol for TNF-Diosmetin. This indicated that hydrophobic interactions were the main source of the driving force, with the most significant contribution observed in the TNF-Diosmetin system. The electrostatic contribution (ΔEelec) was also negative, with IL1B-Naringenin exhibiting the strongest electrostatic effect (-48.66 ± 1.05 kcal/mol), reflecting intense charge attraction. The polar solvation energy (ΔGPB) was positive, but partially offset by the negative contribution of the nonpolar solvation energy (ΔGSA). Although the total solvation energy (ΔGsolv) was positive, it did not dominate the binding process. The gas-phase free energy (ΔGgas) was significantly negative in all systems, with TNF-Diosmetin showing the lowest value (-66.12 ± 1.48 kcal/mol). The total binding free energy (ΔTotal) values were as follows: IL1B-Genkwanin: -18.92 ± 1.15 kcal/mol, IL1B-Naringenin: -26.75 ± 1.14 kcal/mol, IL1B-Sitosterol: -22.06 ± 2.67 kcal/mol, TNF-Diosmetin: -32.35 ± 1.51 kcal/mol. All ΔTotal values were negative with small standard deviations, confirming high binding affinity and spontaneous binding(Table 3). These data indicated that the TNF-Diosmetin system had the strongest affinity, followed by IL1B-Naringenin, and all systems exhibited favorable pharmaceutical potential.

Summary of MD simulation

The molecular dynamics simulation results showed that the four protein-small molecule complex systems (IL1B-Genkwanin, IL1B-Naringenin, IL1B-Sitosterol, and TNF-Diosmetin) all exhibited high structural stability and favorable binding affinity. MM/GBSA analysis revealed that the total binding free energy (ΔTotal) ranged from − 18.92 to -32.35 kcal/mol. Among the energy components, the van der Waals energy and electrostatic contribution were the main drivers of the negative ΔTotal. Although the solvation effect was positive, it was offset by the overall gas-phase free energy. The TNF-Diosmetin system exhibited the strongest affinity (-32.35 kcal/mol), followed by the IL1B-Naringenin system (-26.75 kcal/mol). Based on the above conclusions, the monomer Diosmetin was selected for subsequent experiments to verify its biological activity.

Dio-liver cancer target interaction network and key target enrichment analysis

From the Herb and ETCM databases, 34 potential targets of diosmetin (Dio) were retrieved. By creating a Venn diagram, an analysis was conducted to find the common targets shared by Dio and liver cancer, with 25 overlapping targets being identified (Fig. 10A). In order to understand the way these common targets interact functionally, a PPI network was created with the help of the STRING database and displayed by means of Cytoscape 3.9.1 (Fig. 10B). Within this network, the nodes represent the target genes. Nodes with greater size and darker hues correspond to higher degree values, which signify the centrality of the interactions. In contrast, edges illustrate the relationships among genes. To gain deeper insights into the potential mechanisms of Dio in liver cancer, we performed a Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis using the SRplot platform. The results showed that within the realm of biological processes (BP), they were predominantly associated with the reaction to lipopolysaccharide; for cellular components (CC), they were associated with the regulation of DNA - binding transcription factor activity; and regarding molecular functions (MF), they were linked to aromatase activity (Fig. 10C). The KEGG pathway analysis for the shared targets spotlighted the top 10 pathways that exhibited substantial enrichment: lipid metabolism and atherosclerosis, chemical carcinogenesis-reactive oxygen species, pathogenic Escherichia coli invasion, Yersinia invasion, pertussis infection, toxoplasmosis, C-type lectin receptor signal transduction pathway, Chagas disease, MAPK signal transduction pathway, and programmed cell death (Fig. 10D). Additionally, a Dio-target-KEGG-Liver Cancer network was constructed (Fig. 10E), where triangles denote liver cancer and Dio, dark green V-shapes represent KEGG pathways, and light green circles indicate target genes.

Dio suppresses HCC cell viability, proliferation, and migration

In order to assess the impact of Dio on the viability of HCC cells, specifically HepG2 and Huh-7 cells, these cells were cultivated with escalating concentrations of Dio for a period of 24 h. The CCK − 8 assay was utilized to assess cell viability (as illustrated in Fig. 11A). Treatment with Dio led to a concentration - dependent decline in the viability of both cell lines, with the calculated IC50 values being 46.49 µM for HepG2 cells and 554.7 µM for Huh-7 cells. The IC50 value of Dio for HuH-7 cells is extremely high, which may be related to the specific molecular characteristics of this cell line. Existing studies have confirmed that HuH-7 cells can induce the expression of the CYP3A4 drug-metabolizing enzyme (this enzyme is not expressed in HepG2 cells)43, and CYP3A4 may accelerate the metabolic inactivation of Dio, leading to a decrease in the intracellular concentration of active drug. Therefore, a higher dose of Dio is required to achieve a 50% cell inhibitory effect. Based on this, subsequent studies were focused on HepG2 cells. To investigate Dio’s anti-proliferative effects, HepG2 cells were exposed to either 0.1% DMSO (functioning as the solvent control) or Dio at concentrations of 10, 20, and 40 µM (determined based on the IC50 values). Colony formation assays demonstrated that, relative to the control group, there was a dose-dependent reduction in the number of colonies formed (Fig. 11B). Wound healing and Transwell chamber assays were employed to explore the effect of Dio on cell migration. Wound closure rates were measured at 24, 48, and 72 h after treatment. Dio significantly suppressed horizontal cell migration in a concentration - dependent fashion, while the vehicle (DMSO) had no noticeable effect (Fig. 11C). Likewise, Transwell assays indicated that Dio diminished the vertical migration ability of cells, as fewer cells were able to pass through the membrane at higher Dio concentrations, whereas DMSO alone did not have a significant impact (Fig. 11D).

Dio inhibits MMP2 and MMP9 expression

To explore whether Dio, a key component of ME, can suppress liver cancer, HepG2 cells were cultured with 0.1% DMSO (functioning as the solvent control) or three progressively increasing concentrations of Dio for 24 h. Following this, Western blot analysis was carried out to assess the protein expression levels of MMP2/MMP9. The findings showed that DMSO exerted no substantial impact on MMP2/MMP9 expression. In contrast, treatment with Dio brought about a dose - related decrease in the levels of both proteins (Fig. 12A). To further validate the antitumor effects of Dio, immunofluorescence staining was performed. Consistent with the Western blot findings, DMSO minimally influenced MMP2 expression, but increasing Dio concentrations progressively suppressed MMP2 protein levels (Fig. 12B).

Discussion

This investigation thoroughly clarified the multi-target and multi-mechanistic routes through which ME and its bioactive component Dio inhibit liver cancer. The study integrated network pharmacology forecasting, molecular docking techniques, and in vitro experimental verification. It has been documented that ME demonstrates multiple biological functions, such as anti-cancer, antibacterial, antidiabetic, and antioxidant capabilities36,44–46. Notably, its antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects (e.g., suppressing gastric pathogens and modulating the gut microbiome35 may indirectly influence HCC progression by regulating the tumor microenvironment. Furthermore, ME’s protective effects on the cardiovascular system36 suggest its potential to indirectly suppress HCC development by ameliorating metabolic disorders (e.g., insulin resistance), aligning with the therapeutic needs of HCC patients with metabolic syndrome. However, research on the direct anti-Liver Cancer effects of ME remains limited. This study fills this gap by establishing a novel “component-target-pathway” multidimensional analytical framework.

Venn analysis of drug and disease targets identified 78 overlapping targets between ME and Liver Cancer. Cell experiments demonstrated that ME significantly inhibited HCC cell viability, proliferation, and migration. ME remarkably diminished the expression of MMP2 and MMP9 (P < 0.01), echoing the outcomes of prior studies. These proteins, MMP2 and MMP9, act as key regulators in degrading the tumor basement membrane and extracellular matrix. Since they promote cell migration and invasion, suppressing them can efficiently prevent the invasion and metastasis of HCC47–49. After screening the active components in ME using TCMSP, the selected components were subjected to molecular docking with the previously identified core targets. Among these components, Dio showed consistent docking scores with all core targets, with ΔG consistently below − 4 kcal/mol. According to the conventional evaluation criteria for molecular docking studies, this ΔG range indicates that Dio has moderate binding affinity with the core targets. However, compared with the docking results of other active components in ME with the core targets (most of which have ΔG values between − 2 and − 4 kcal/mol), Dio has certain advantages in binding ability. In addition, molecular dynamics simulation results showed that all four protein-small molecule complex systems (IL1B-Genkwanin, IL1B-Naringenin, IL1B-Sitosterol, and TNF-Dio) exhibited high structural stability and good binding affinity. MM/GBSA analysis indicated that the TNF-Dio system had the strongest affinity (-32.35 kcal/mol). Considering the specificity of molecular docking and the cross-comparison results among ME components, Dio was finally selected as the core research object for subsequent in vitro validation to ensure the targeting and effectiveness of subsequent experiments. Prior studies reported Dio’s antitumor effects in prostate50, ovarian51, gastric52, and skin cancers53, consistent with our findings. Venn analysis of Dio and Liver Cancer targets revealed 25 shared targets. The results of GO and KEGG enrichment analyses demonstrated that Dio may regulate the apoptotic process of HCC cells via the p38/MAPK signaling pathway. Notably, the p38/MAPK signaling pathway exhibits a crucial functional duality—existing studies have confirmed that its activation can either exert a pro-apoptotic effect or display an anti-apoptotic effect. This functional difference is closely associated with the kinetic characteristics of pathway activation and substrate-binding properties: On one hand, p38/MAPK possesses hypersensitive properties and has an activation threshold. When this pathway is strongly and continuously activated, it is more likely to induce cell apoptosis; conversely, if the activation intensity is weak and transient, the pathway tends to exert an anti-apoptotic effect54. On the other hand, different substrates have varying affinities for p38/MAPK, and this binding specificity further regulates the transmission direction of downstream signals in the pathway, ultimately collectively mediating the dual functions of p38/MAPK in the regulation of cell apoptosis55. Additionally, existing studies have indicated that effectors of the MAPK pathway are potential therapeutic targets for HCC37–39, and this conclusion further validates the network pharmacology analysis results of the present study. Experiments conducted in vitro demonstrated that Dio strongly repressed the viability, growth, and migratory ability of HepG2 cells, along with reducing the levels of MMP2/MMP9 (P < 0.01). Further studies showed increased apoptosis rates in Dio-treated cells via TUNEL staining. Western blot revealed that Dio significantly upregulated p53, caspase3, Bax (pro-apoptotic proteins), and p38 (MAPK pathway protein), while downregulating Bcl2 (anti-apoptotic protein) (P < 0.01). Immunofluorescence confirmed enhanced Bax and p38 expression and reduced Bcl2 levels (P < 0.01). These findings align with network pharmacology predictions, elucidating the potential mechanisms of ME and Dio against Liver Cancer.

Although this study emphasizes the core role of Dio in the ME action system, as a typical complex natural product, ME contains other bioactive components such as Naringenin, Linarin, Genkwanin, and Sitosterol. These components are not merely capable of independently expanding the anticancer potential of ME— the potential synergistic or additive effects between these components and Dio also constitute a crucial dimension for interpreting the overall activity of ME, which warrants further in-depth investigation. As reported in existing studies, Naringenin can induce cell apoptosis via the MAPK pathway56. This mechanism is similar to that of Dio, suggesting that the two may enhance the overall activity through “effect superposition” during the anti-hepatocellular carcinoma process of ME. This also provides a key clue for the subsequent exploration of the multi-component synergistic mechanism of ME in this study. In future work, we plan to adopt an orthogonal experimental design to evaluate the additive/synergistic effects among the components of ME. Meanwhile, considering the lack of in vivo validation in the current study and to further enhance the translational relevance of the research, we will establish a patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model—a specific in vivo model that can better mimic the complexity of the tumor microenvironment—to systematically assess the in vivo efficacy and toxicity of Dio. In subsequent studies, we may also explore other in vivo models such as orthotopic liver models to improve the validation system.

Conclusions

This study not only validates the scientific hypothesis that ME and Dio inhibit liver cancer progression through the p38/MAPK pathway but also provides a methodological paradigm for “multi-component, multi-target” research in natural medicine. Subsequent investigations are expected to offer a theoretical foundation for developing ME-based combination therapies for liver cancer.
