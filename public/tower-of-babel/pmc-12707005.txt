Incremental vs. standard hemodialysis for preserving residual kidney function: a case-control study
Authors: Bianca Vitória dos Santos Barbosa, Vanessa Piacitelli Cassimiro Sobrinho, Ana Paula Rovani Cardoso, Moisés Teixeira Sobrinho, Daniela Ponce
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeChronic kidney disease (CKD) is a global health issue, and hemodialysis (HD) is the most commonly used dialysis method. Incremental HD has been proposed as an alternative, but evidence on its safety and benefits is limited. This study compared residual kidney function (RKF) preservation in patients undergoing incremental versus standard HD, also evaluating quality of life, hospitalizations and mortality.MethodsThis retrospective, longitudinal, observational case-control study included stage 5 CKD patients who started either incremental or standard HD between January 2021 and January 2023. The case group included patients who began incremental HD (2 weekly sessions lasting 4 h or 3 weekly sessions lasting < 3.5 h), and the control group included patients who started standard HD schedule (thrice-weekly, minimum 4-hour sessions). The groups were matched by age, sex, underlying disease, comorbidities, and baseline lab values.Results80 patients were included (50% male; mean age 63.8 ± 11.9 years). Diabetes (32.5%) and hypertension (22.5%) were the main underlying conditions. Over two years, 3.8% had kidney transplants, 6.2% died, and 2.5% recovered kidney function. 45% of incremental HD patients retained RKF; none did in the standard HD schedule group. Incremental group had fewer infections, longer time free from loss of RKF than control group and better quality of life. No differences were found in cardiovascular events, mortality, or transplantation. RKF preservation was associated with incremental HD, higher albumin, and absence of bloodstream infections.ConclusionStarting HD with an incremental may better preserve RKF without compromising survival, while improving quality of life. Further clinical trials are needed to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of incremental HD and support its broader, patient-centered use.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-025-04550-0.Keywords: Hemodialysis, Incremental dialysis, Chronic kidney disease, Residual kidney function

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a major global public health issue due to its rising incidence and prevalence, as well as its impact on morbidity and mortality. Upon reaching CKD stage 5, patients often require kidney replacement therapy (KRT) [1–4].

The number of patients with stage 5 CKD on dialysis is growing fast, with an estimated annual increase of nearly 7%. The most common causes are diabetic kidney disease and hypertension [5–8].

Hemodialysis (HD) is the most widely used dialysis method worldwide for treating CKD. Incremental HD refers to a treatment approach that adjusts the frequency and/or duration of HD sessions based on the patient’s residual kidney function (RKF). First introduced in the late 1990s using urea kinetic models, incremental HD follows a gradual approach to dialysis therapy similar to peritoneal dialysis (PD), in which incremental strategies have long been used. While research on incremental HD is still limited, it is increasingly recognized as a smoother transition from pre-dialysis to full dialysis therapy. In contrast, HD—typically performed three times per week for four hours—has been the standard for over three decades and, therefore, should be considered in the context of current practices [9–11].

Incremental HD, typically done twice a week, is recommended for patients with an RKF-defined urea clearance of at least 3 mL/min [12, 13]. This regimen can continue as long as urine output remains at least 500 mL/day and urea clearance is at least 1 mL/min. RKF should be assessed at least every three months [14]. When urine output falls below 500 mL/day or urea clearance drops below 1 mL/min, HD should be increased from two to three weekly sessions to compensate for RKF decline. The treatment can still be incremental by reducing the duration of HD sessions [14, 15].

The important role of RKF in HD adequacy is increasingly recognized as a key factor influencing dialysis duration and frequency and likely improves quality of life. Furthermore, maintaining RKF in both HD and PD lowers the risk of death by clearing small and medium-sized solutes, preserving kidney metabolic, hemodynamic, and endocrine functions, and reducing damage to the cardiovascular system, nutritional imbalances, and mineral metabolism disorders while enhancing the overall well-being of KRT patients [16].

Most patients begin KRT with some level of RKF, and its preservation is highly valued in PD due to its strong association with improved patient survival and treatment success. However, RKF preservation in HD has been less studied and often overlooked. The HD procedure itself, which involves extracorporeal circulation, may cause ischemic damage to the kidneys, and the cumulative effect of repetitive ischemic events can accelerate RKF decline. Therefore, decisions regarding the initiation of incremental HD should consider both RKF and urine volume as well [17].

Recent studies suggest that incremental HD is a safe treatment option for newly diagnosed dialysis patients that may help preserve RKF, delaying the need for full-dose dialysis by about a year [10, 18, 19]. Beyond RKF preservation, other key outcomes in incremental HD patients should be evaluated, including nutritional status markers (albumin and creatinine), quality of life, cardiovascular events, hospitalizations, and mortality [20, 21]. However, research on this topic remains scarce and there are no studies carried out in Latin America. Further studies are needed to better understand the safety and effectiveness of incremental HD and establish clearer guidelines for its use [22].

This study aimed to compare RKF preservation in patients undergoing incremental versus standard HD one year after starting dialysis. Secondary goals included comparing quality of life, nutritional status, fluid balance, dialysis adequacy, cardiovascular events, hospitalization, and mortality between patients on incremental and standard HD after 6 and 12 months of therapy initiation.

Methods

This retrospective longitudinal observational case-control study included incident HD patients who started either standard or incremental dialysis treatment between January 1, 2021, and January 31, 2023. Data were collected from the hospital’s electronic system after ethics committee approval.

Patients

This study included patients with stage 5 CKD, aged between 18 and 85 years, who initiated HD—either in a planned or unplanned manner—at the Botucatu Medical School Hospital, Brazil. All eligible patients (from both groups) had a urea clearance greater than 3 mL/min and urine output of at least 800 mL/day. Pregnant women, patients with acute kidney injury, and kidney transplant recipients were excluded.

Patients were divided into two groups:

Case group (Incremental HD schedule): Patients who started dialysis with lower frequency, 2 weekly sessions (3–4 h per session) or 3 weekly sessions (3–3.5 h per session), depending on their standard Kt/V. To achieve a standard Kt/V (Krt/V + single pool HD Kt/V) of at least 2.1 weekly, some patients were treated with 2 sessions of 3.5–4 h and others with 3 sessions lasting 3–3.5 h. RKF was assessed every three months aimed to achieve standard Kt/V >2.1 per week.

Case group (Incremental HD schedule): Patients who started dialysis with lower frequency, 2 weekly sessions (3–4 h per session) or 3 weekly sessions (3–3.5 h per session), depending on their standard Kt/V. To achieve a standard Kt/V (Krt/V + single pool HD Kt/V) of at least 2.1 weekly, some patients were treated with 2 sessions of 3.5–4 h and others with 3 sessions lasting 3–3.5 h. RKF was assessed every three months aimed to achieve standard Kt/V >2.1 per week.

Control group (Standard HD schedule): All patients were treated with thrice-weekly hemodialysis régimen lasting 4 h, without considering KrT/V.

Control group (Standard HD schedule): All patients were treated with thrice-weekly hemodialysis régimen lasting 4 h, without considering KrT/V.

On line calculators to calculate RKF and Kt/V (single pool and standard) were used [23, 24].

Control patients were individually matched to case patients based on age range, sex, underlying disease, comorbidities, use of drugs to preserve residual renal function and baseline laboratory values, including hemoglobin, albumin, phosphorus, PTH (parathyroid hormone), and creatinine clearance (within a maximum variation of 3%).

Incremental HD was continued as long as urine output remained at least 500 mL/day and urea clearance was at least 1 mL/min. RKF was assessed every three months If urine output dropped below 500 mL/day or urea clearance was lower than 1 ml/min, the patient was switched to standard HD schedule [13–15].

The HD prescription was also changed from twice weekly to thrice weekly based on clinical evaluation that takes into consideration the presence of fluid overload, urine volume, uremic symptoms (such as fatigue, loss of appetite, attention deficit, restless leg syndrome), high interdialytic gains (> 5%) and poor blood pressure control and on biochemical data (potassium > 6.0 meq/l or bicarbonate lower than 18 meq/L). Biochemical exams were repeated within a month before the decision.

This study followed the ethical principles of Resolution 466/12, which governs research involving human subjects in Brazil. The institutional Human Research Ethics Committee approved the study (CAAE: 66693322.9.0000.5411), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study protocol

The study protocol included clinical and laboratory data, and information on dialysis procedures and follow-up of patients undergoing incremental and standard HD schedule. Patients were monitored from the start of therapy and assessed for the following outcomes: RKF preservation, nutritional status, quality of life, hospitalization, cardiovascular events, and mortality.

The following tests were collected:

Monthly: Urea, creatinine, bicarbonate, potassium, complete blood count, calcium, and phosphorus (routine tests according to CKD 154 guidelines).

Monthly: Urea, creatinine, bicarbonate, potassium, complete blood count, calcium, and phosphorus (routine tests according to CKD 154 guidelines).

Quarterly: Creatinine and urea clearance to assess RKF preservation, PTH, ferritin, serum iron, total cholesterol and fractions. Additionally, nutritional assessment (anthropometry and bioimpedance) was conducted.

Quarterly: Creatinine and urea clearance to assess RKF preservation, PTH, ferritin, serum iron, total cholesterol and fractions. Additionally, nutritional assessment (anthropometry and bioimpedance) was conducted.

Samples for laboratory testing were collected during the second HD session of the week (Wednesdays, Thursdays, Fridays or Saturdays), and the nutritional assessment was performed following HD sessions by the center’s dietitian. All patients were treated with high flux membrane.

Within six months of therapy initiation, the Kidney Disease Quality of Life Short Form (KDQOL-SF™ 1.3) questionnaire was completed.

The study protocol was discontinued if any of the following occurred: death, kidney function recovery, transplantation, or need for full-dose HD (urine output below 500 mL/day, urea clearance < 1 ml/min or based on clinical evaluation).

Data analysis

Study data were entered into a spreadsheet where typographical errors were verified. Analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS 29 statistical software (version 9.2: SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA, 2012).

The sample size was calculated based on an alpha error of 5%, beta error of 20%, statistical power of 80%, and an expected difference in RKF preservation between groups of 30%. The required sample size was estimated as 39 patients per group.

Descriptive analysis was performed for all patients included during the study period, calculating measures of central tendency and dispersion for continuous variables and frequency distributions for categorical variables. For group comparisons, the Chi-Square test was used for categorical variables, while continuous variables were analyzed using a t-Test when normally distributed or Mann-Whitney test when non-normally distributed.

The dependent variables were defined as RKF preservation and death. Kaplan-Meier curves with log-rank test were used to compare kidney function survival between groups, and binary logistic regression was performed to identify variables associated with RKF preservation. This multivariate analysis included variables with p < 0.1 from the univariate analysis.

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

This study included 80 patients with CKD 5D who started either standard HD (n = 40) or incremental HD (n = 40) between January 2021 and January 2023 (Fig. 1). Of these, 50% were male, with a mean age of 63.8 ± 11.9 years. Diabetes was the most common underlying disease (32.5%), followed by hypertension (22.5%).

Over the study period, 3.8% of patients underwent transplantation, 6.2% died and one patient recovered kidney function. Among the 40 patients treated with incremental HD, 77.5% had a reduced number of weekly sessions, while 22.5% (9 patients) continued with three sessions per week but with shorter session durations.

During follow-up, four patients (10%) were switched to standard HD schedule after 11.1 ± 7.4 months of therapy (Fig. 2). Additionally, for three patients who started on incremental HD with a reduced number of weekly sessions, the frequency was later increased to three sessions per week, but with shorter session durations, thereby maintaining an incremental HD approach while accounting for RKF in standard Kt/V calculations. HD prescription schedules have been changed based on urea clearance, urine output and clinical evaluation (Fig. 2).

Comparisons between the incremental HD and standard HD groups showed no differences in age, sex, underlying disease, or comorbidities. Most laboratory values at baseline and after 6, 12, and 24 months were also similar, including creatinine and urea clearance. The only difference observed in initial lab or clinical parameters was parathyroid hormone levels. There was difference between the two groups in creatinine clearance and albumin levels after 6 months. At 12 and 24 months, differences were noted in albumin levels and urea clearance, as shown in Table 2. Infections were more frequent in the control (standard HD schedule) group. Cardiovascular events, mortality, transplantation rates, or the type of vascular access used, showed no significant differences (Table 2). The two groups had similar survival curve (Fig. 3). After 24 months of therapy, none of the patients in the standard HD group had 24-h urine output exceeding 500 mL or urea clearance above 1 mL/min, whereas in the incremental HD group, 45% still retained RKF and remained on the incremental regimen. Discussion

This study aimed to assess the preservation of residual kidney function (RKF) in incident patients undergoing incremental versus standard hemodialysis schedule after initiating therapy. Additional objectives included comparing their clinical course in terms of nutritional parameters, dialysis adequacy, survival, and quality of life.

According to recent studies, RKF preservation contributes to fluid balance and the elimination of uremic toxins even at lower levels. It also facilitates the removal of middle molecules and protein-bound solutes, and is associated with better nutritional status and lower mortality [25].

Jaques et al. observed that the proportion of patients initiating dialysis therapy with incremental HD has been increasing over the years, indicating a progressive shift in clinical practice. Although a significant number of patients were switched to standard HD schedule over the course of their study, they reported a mean duration of 11.1 ± 7.4 months for the incremental approach [26].

This study evaluated 80 patients with stage 5 CKD who initiated hemodialysis between January 2021 and January 2023, with half receiving standard HD and half receiving incremental HD. Fifty per cent of the overall population were male, with a mean age of 63.82 ± 11.9 years. Diabetes was the most common underlying condition, followed by hypertension. More than half of the patients experienced bloodstream infections (BSI) and exit-site infections (ESI) during follow-up. The overall mortality rate was 6.2%.

The incremental and standard HD schedule groups were similar in terms of age, sex, underlying disease, comorbidities, and most laboratory parameters at baseline and after 6, 12, and 24 months of therapy. After 24 months, none of the patients in the standard group had urine output greater than 500 mL in 24 h, whereas 33 patients (82.5%) of those in the incremental group still had residual urine output above 500 mL per day.

No significant differences were found between the standard and incremental groups in most of the clinical and laboratory parameters evaluated at the moment of dialysis start. Only levels PTH was higher in the standard group, and only albumin was significantly different after 6 months (higher in incremental patients). These findings differ from those reported by Ahmed et al., who observed slightly lower serum phosphorus levels in patients undergoing incremental HD, likely due to increased urinary excretion in individuals with greater RKF [27].

There were no significant differences in clinical outcomes such as infection, hospitalization, and death. However, the prevalence of BSI was clinically relevant, being 20% higher in the standard HD group (p = 0.07). The lack of statistically significant differences is likely due to the small number of patients included in the study. Possible explanations for this can be attributed to a combination of factors, including increased access manipulation, greater reliance on intravascular catheters, and heightened exposure to potential sources of infection within healthcare facilities [28].

A recent systematic review by Caton et al. included 26 studies comparing clinical outcomes between patients undergoing incremental versus standard hemodialysis schedule. In seven studies, no difference was found in hospitalization rates, while two studies reported that incremental HD was associated with reduced hospitalization rates. One study, however, indicated that incremental HD was linked to an increased risk of hospitalization [29].

In this study, logistic regression identified albumin levels, phase angle, bloodstream infection, and incremental hemodialysis as predictors of RKF preservation. The kidney survival curve illustrates the impact of incremental HD on maintaining RKF.

Traditionally, 4-h hemodialysis sessions, three times a week, are prescribed from the start of treatment for CKD patients requiring kidney replacement therapy. This empirical prescription is based on previous studies that primarily included prevalent patients on hemodialysis.

According to recent studies, incremental hemodialysis can be a safe treatment approach for incident patients and is associated with greater RKF preservation, delaying the need for full-dose dialysis by approximately one year [10, 17, 18].

Incremental hemodialysis involves starting with fewer than three weekly sessions or shorter sessions of less than four hours, based on the patient’s RKF. This approach allows for a more gradual transition to full-dose dialysis and better reflects the progressive nature of chronic kidney disease. While accounting for RKF is already standard practice in peritoneal dialysis, it has not been routinely implemented in hemodialysis.

Although the National Kidney Foundation (NKF) KDOQI 2015 guidelines allow the reduction in the weekly HD dose for patients with a residual kidney urea clearance (KrU) higher than 3 ml/min/1.73m2, surprisingly enough, few centers follow this recommendation. Our center did not follow this until 2022. In 2022, we started to indicate incremental HD based on criteria described above (case groups) and control group patients were individually matched to case patients based on age range, sex, underlying disease, comorbidities, use of drugs to preserve residual renal function and baseline laboratory values, including hemoglobin, albumin, phosphorus, parathyroid hormone, and creatinine clearance (within a maximum variation of 3%).

In this study, all patients (case and control) had urea clearance greater than 3 ml/min and urine volume higher than 800 ml/day when HD was started. In case group, to achieve a standard Kt/V (Krt/V + Kt/V hd) of at least 2.1 weekly, some patients were treated with 2 sessions of 3.5–4 h and others with 3 sessions lasting 3–3.5 h. RKF was assessed every three months aimed to achieve standard Kt/V > 2.1 per week. In control group, all patients were treated with thrice-weekly hemodialysis régimen lasting 4 h, without considering KrT/V. It was named standard HD schedule [23, 24].

Given the absence of clear consensus in the literature regarding the optimal prescription of incremental HD, this study established predefined criteria guided by RKF. Patients with urea clearance > 2 mL/min or urine output > 800 mL/day were prescribed two sessions per week. Conversely, when urea clearance was between 1 and 2 mL/min or when urine output was within the range of 500–800 mL/day, patients remained on three sessions per week, but with shorter duration (3–3.5 h). In addition, clinical evaluation, including fluid overload, urine volume, uremic symptoms (fatigue, anorexia, attention deficit, restless leg syndrome), interdialytic weight gain > 5%, and poor blood pressure control—together with biochemical parameters (serum potassium > 6.0 mEq/L or bicarbonate < 18 mEq/L) also guided treatment adjustments. Laboratory tests were repeated within one month before making prescription changes.

In this study, at the beginning of treatment, of the 40 patients who received incremental hemodialysis, 75.5% had their weekly session frequency reduced. In the remaining 22.5% (9 patients), session duration was shortened while maintaining three sessions per week.

Incremental HD was continued as long as urine output remained at least 500 mL/day and urea clearance was at least 1 mL/min. RKF was assessed every three months. If urine output dropped below 500 mL/day or urea clearance was lower than 1 ml/min, the patient was switched to thrice-weekly hemodialysis régimen lasting 4 h.

During the first year of follow-up, 4 patients (10%) were switched to standard hemodialysis. Another 3 patients who initially started incremental hemodialysis with a reduced weekly frequency had their treatment adjusted to three sessions per week, but with shorter durations. In these cases, incremental hemodialysis was maintained, with RKF accounted for in the calculation of standard Kt/V. In the incremental HD group, RKF preservation was greater. At 24 months, almost half of these patients still had preserved RKF, compared to none in the standard HD group.

Our data support previous studies showing that incremental dialysis is safe and associated with RKF preservation, in addition to improving patients’ quality of life. Our patients undergoing incremental HD had better scores for sexual function, sleep, pain, and general health compared to those in the standard group.

Health-related quality of life is defined as an individual’s perceived physical and mental health status over time and includes conceptual domains related to physical, mental, and disease-related functioning. Individuals with chronic illnesses often report lower quality of life, and patients on dialysis consistently score lower than the general population. Improving both prognosis and quality of life has become a key objective in kidney replacement therapy [30].

Challenges to implementing incremental dialysis include concerns about patient acceptance when adjustments to session frequency or duration become necessary, as well as the potential for increased cardiovascular events and a decline in nutritional status. In the present study, no differences were found between groups regarding cardiovascular events or episodes of hypervolemia. However, albumin levels were higher in the incremental group. Albumin is a well-established marker that reflects both nutritional status and chronic inflammation and is closely associated with outcomes in hemodialysis patients. Previous studies have shown that hypoalbuminemia is linked to increased morbidity and mortality in this population [31].

In this study, albumin levels at 6 months of therapy were an independent predictor of RKF preservation, as were higher phase angle values, which also reflect better nutritional and volume status, as demonstrated in the study by Eun-Jung et al. [32].

In addition to albumin and phase angle values at 6 months, BSI was also identified in this study as a risk factor for RKF loss in hemodialysis patients. Infections remain a major cause of morbidity and mortality in patients undergoing hemodialysis, despite advances in preventive care and the development of new antimicrobial agents. The treatment of BSI itself—particularly the use of antibiotics—may also contribute to RKF decline [33].

Some studies suggest that preparing patients for incremental dialysis should start before switching dialysis therapy. Early preparation can help improve acceptance of gradual increases in session length or frequency as RKF declines [34]. In this study, 10% of patients in the incremental group transitioned to standard hemodialysis after an average of 11.1 ± 7.4 months of therapy, and 55% after 24 months. Only two patients resisted transitioning to the standard regimen by increasing session frequency while maintaining shorter durations. A study by Yoshitsugu et al. [35], which included 85 incident patients who started dialysis incrementally, found that the majority (over 80%) preserved residual kidney function (RKF) during the first year. In contrast, those undergoing frequent nocturnal HD experienced a faster decline in RKF, supporting the idea that incremental HD may be an appropriate initial treatment approach.

Previous reports have shown that over 80% of patients initiating kidney replacement therapy with an incremental schedule did not suffer any relevant complications as the dose of dialysis was progressively adjusted as their residual kidney function declined, similar to the approach taken in this study [36].

Growing evidence suggests that starting dialysis with a fixed regimen—without considering RKF, may be potentially harmful. However, regular monitoring of kidney function is essential to ensure that patients do not become undertreated as RKF declines [37].

Our study has some limitations, including its single-center design, observational nature, and the small sample size. Despite these limitations, our findings suggest that initiating hemodialysis incrementally may better preserve RKF compared to standard therapy, while offering similar survival and improved quality of life.

In brief, patients treated with standard versus incremental hemodialysis schedule differed only in baseline levels of parathyroid hormone, as well as in albumin at 6 months and creatinine and urea clearance at 6, 12 and 24 months. Differences were also observed in the number of infections, which was higher in the standard HD group, and in quality-of-life parameters related to sexual function, sleep, pain, and general health—all of which were better in the incremental HD group. The two groups were similar in terms of cardiovascular events, mortality, and kidney transplantation. RKF preservation was associated with albumin levels, phase angle, bloodstream infection, and the use of incremental hemodialysis. Thus, starting HD with an incremental approach may better preserve RKF compared to standard therapy schedule, while offering similar survival and improved quality of life. Clinical trials are needed to further evaluate the safety and effectiveness of incremental HD and to promote broader acceptance of this patient-centered, rather than disease-centered, approach.
