Acute Pulmonary Embolism With Multiple ST‐Segment Elevations in Anterior and Inferior Leads: A Case Report
Authors: Xiaorui Zhao, Xiaoqing Fu, Wenqiang Li, Jie Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute pulmonary embolism (APE) is a common and potentially life‐threatening cardiovascular emergency. Although APE typically presents with nonspecific electrocardiogram (ECG) abnormalities, a rare but critical presentation may mimic ST‐segment elevation myocardial infarction. We report a case of APE with multiple ST‐segment elevations in the anterior and inferior leads.Case PresentationA 74‐year‐old man presented with chest tightness and dyspnea. Awareness of the ECG changes associated with APE and early diagnostic intervention can help prevent misdiagnosis and ensure timely treatment.

1. Introduction

Acute pulmonary embolism (APE) is a major cardiovascular emergency with a highly variable clinical presentation [1]. Because its symptoms are often nonspecific and may overlap with those of acute coronary syndromes and other cardiopulmonary disorders, timely diagnosis can be difficult. Electrocardiogram (ECG) is one of the first supplementary examinations to be performed in cases of suspected APE, and ECG abnormalities can be seen in 70%–80% of patients [2], ranging from the traditional S1Q3T3 pattern to nonspecific ST‐segment depression or T‐wave changes. By contrast, ST‐segment elevation is an unusual manifestation of APE and may lead to an initial misdiagnosis of ST‐segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) [3]. Here, we describe an elderly man with APE whose ECG showed simultaneous ST‐segment elevation in the anterior and inferior leads, highlighting an uncommon but clinically important diagnostic pitfall.

2. Case Presentation

A 74‐year‐old man with a 3‐year history of hypertension was admitted to our hospital because of exertional chest tightness and dyspnea, each lasting approximately 10 min at a time over 1 day. He did not report typical ischemic chest pain. His highest previously recorded blood pressure was 150/96 mmHg. He had been taking candesartan 8 mg once daily regularly, and his usual blood pressure was well controlled at approximately 120/80 mmHg. The patient had no history of coronary artery disease but had a history of alcohol consumption. There was no recent surgery, prolonged travel, or trauma, and he had no family history of unprovoked APE. On initial evaluation, his pulse rate was regular (116/min), blood pressure 118/60 mmHg, respiratory rate 23 breaths/min, and oxygen (O2) saturation 90% on room air. Lung auscultation revealed clear breath sounds bilaterally. Cardiac examination showed normal S1 and S2, with a mildly accentuated P2. No new murmur or pericardial rub was heard. Physical examination also revealed increased girth of the left calf compared with the right.

The initial ECG showed sinus tachycardia (116 bpm) with ST‐segment elevation in Leads III, aVF, and V1–V4; T‐wave inversion in Leads II, III, aVF, and V1–V6; a QR pattern in Lead V1; Q waves in Leads V2–V3; and an S1Q3T3 pattern (Figure 1). His cardiac troponin I level was 0.43 ng/mL (normal, < 0.3 ng/mL) and peaked at 0.84 ng/mL over the next 2 days; his NT‐proBNP was 2183.2 ng/mL; and his plasma D‐dimer level showed a marked elevation (4.59 mg/L). Emergency bedside echocardiography showed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 58% without regional wall motion abnormalities. It also demonstrated mild pulmonary hypertension, with an estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure of 43 mmHg, severe tricuspid regurgitation, a tricuspid regurgitation jet velocity of 3.1 m/s, and a right ventricular transverse diameter of 38 mm. Lower extremity venous Doppler revealed thrombosis in the left posterior tibial vein. Subsequently, APE was suspected, and emergency CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) was performed, revealing multiple thrombi in the left and right main pulmonary arteries, bilateral lobes, and several segmental branches (Figure 2). Emergency coronary angiography was not performed because the patient had no typical persistent ischemic chest pain, only mild troponin elevation, and multiple concomitant findings strongly suggesting APE, including hypoxemia, elevated D‐dimer, deep vein thrombosis (DVT), and echocardiographic evidence of pulmonary hypertension. The patient was treated with intravenous heparin and started on oral anticoagulants. He was discharged 9 days later with significant symptom relief. A 12‐lead ECG recorded 7 days later showed regression of diffuse ST‐segment abnormalities, and the Q wave disappeared in Leads V2 and V3 (Figure 3).

3. Discussion

APE can present with a broad range of symptoms and electrocardiographic abnormalities, which may obscure the diagnosis at first contact. In routine practice, sinus tachycardia, T‐wave inversion, right bundle branch block, and the S1Q3T3 pattern are more familiar ECG findings in this setting [4]. ST‐segment elevation, however, is far less common and may be particularly misleading because it immediately raises concern for acute coronary occlusion. The present case is noteworthy because ST‐segment elevation was present in both the anterior and inferior leads, a combination that can strongly suggest STEMI at first glance.

The mechanism of ST‐segment elevation in APE has not been fully established and is likely multifactorial. Previous reports have suggested that abrupt obstruction of the pulmonary arterial bed causes a sudden increase in right ventricular afterload, leading to acute right ventricular wall stress, increased oxygen demand, and reduced myocardial perfusion [2, 5]. Under these conditions, the right ventricle may develop ischemic electrical changes that are recorded in the anterior precordial leads, especially V1–V4, and in some cases in the inferior leads as well. Other noncoronary mechanisms proposed in the literature include hypoxemia‐related myocardial injury, catecholamine excess, and secondary repolarization abnormalities associated with acute right ventricular dysfunction [2, 6, 7]. In our patient, the absence of left ventricular regional wall motion abnormalities and the confirmation of pulmonary embolism by CTPA make these explanations more plausible than primary coronary occlusion.

A review of the literature suggests that, although this ECG pattern may initially point toward STEMI, especially in an elderly hypertensive patient, the overall interpretation should depend on the full clinical context rather than the ECG alone. In our case, the ECG certainly raised concern for STEMI because of the ST‐segment elevation in the anterior and inferior leads. However, the overall findings were more suggestive of APE than of primary coronary occlusion. The patient did not report typical persistent ischemic chest pain, troponin elevation was only mild, and echocardiography showed no left ventricular regional wall motion abnormalities. In contrast, he presented with dyspnea, hypoxemia, markedly elevated D‐dimer, documented DVT, and echocardiographic evidence of pulmonary hypertension and right‐sided pressure overload. Taken together, these findings favored APE over STEMI, and the diagnosis was subsequently confirmed by emergency CTPA.

This case emphasizes the importance of interpreting ST‐segment elevation within the broader clinical setting. Although acute myocardial infarction was an important early consideration, acute pericarditis was also less likely because the ECG lacked diffuse ST‐segment elevation and PR‐segment depression, the patient had no typical pleuritic or positional chest pain, and no pericardial rub was detected. In contrast, dyspnea, hypoxemia, venous thrombosis, and echocardiographic evidence of right‐sided pressure overload all pointed toward pulmonary embolism. In such situations, early bedside imaging and prompt CTPA are critical to avoid diagnostic delay and inappropriate management [8].

The DVT in the left posterior tibial vein was another clue suggesting the diagnosis of APE. The presence of DVT is a significant risk factor for pulmonary embolism, with studies showing that DVT is present in up to 60% of patients with APE [9]. This finding should always prompt clinicians to consider venous thromboembolism and initiate appropriate imaging studies for confirmation.

Early initiation of anticoagulation therapy remains the cornerstone of APE management. In this case, intravenous heparin followed by oral anticoagulants effectively reduced thrombus formation and minimized the risk of recurrent embolism [10]. The patient’s clinical improvement, including the regression of ST‐segment abnormalities and the resolution of Q waves on follow‐up ECG, emphasizes the importance of timely anticoagulation therapy in improving outcomes. The overall prognosis of APE is influenced by the extent of thromboembolic burden, the degree of right ventricular dysfunction, and the timing of intervention. Prompt treatment, including anticoagulation or, in severe cases, thrombolysis or embolectomy, significantly improves survival.

4. Conclusions

This case illustrates the rare but significant presentation of ST‐segment elevation in APE, which can mimic acute myocardial infarction. Recognizing this ECG pattern is crucial for early diagnosis, which may prevent misdiagnosis and unnecessary delays in anticoagulant therapy. Clinicians should include APE in the differential diagnosis when a patient presents with chest tightness or chest pain, dyspnea, and ST‐segment elevation, especially if there is a history of DVT. CTPA remains the gold standard for confirming the diagnosis, allowing for rapid intervention and improving patient outcomes.
