SIRT1-mediated deacetylation of DRP1 suppresses excessive mitophagy and ameliorates pediatric functional dyspepsia
Authors: Shuan Yin, Dongdong Dai, Gaoyan Wang, Shaomei Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFunctional dyspepsia (FD) is a prevalent gastrointestinal disorder in children, but its underlying molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood. Mitochondrial dysfunction and excessive mitophagy have been implicated in FD, but the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. This study aimed to investigate the role of SIRT1 in mitochondrial quality control and its potential therapeutic value in pediatric FD. Serum samples from pediatric FD patients and healthy controls were analyzed for SIRT1 expression and inflammatory cytokines. Human gastric smooth muscle cells (HGSMCs) were treated with carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP) to induce mitochondrial stress. Mitochondrial function, mitophagy, oxidative stress, and protein interactions were assessed using qPCR, Western blot, co-immunopcipitation, and functional assays including ATP levels, mitochondrial membrane potential, ROS, MDA, SOD, and GSH-Px. The results showed that SIRT1 was significantly downregulated in both pediatric FD patient sera and CCCP-stimulated HGSMCs, accompanied by elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β. Overexpression of SIRT1 improved cell viability, ATP production, mitochondrial membrane potential, and suppressed excessive mitophagy and oxidative stress. Conversely, DRP1 knockdown phenocopied the protective effects of SIRT1, reducing mitophagy and oxidative stress in CCCP-stimulated cells. SIRT1 directly interacted with DRP1 and promoted its deacetylation at lysine 283, leading to accelerated DRP1 degradation. Rescue experiments confirmed that DRP1 overexpression reversed the protective effects of SIRT1 on mitochondrial function and redox homeostasis. In conclusion, SIRT1 exerts protective effects in pediatric FD by deacetylating DRP1 at K283, thereby inhibiting excessive mitophagy and ameliorating mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress. Introduction

Functional dyspepsia (FD) ranks among the most prevalent gastrointestinal disorders in pediatric populations, affecting approximately 10–15% of school-aged children globally [1]. Characterized by chronic or recurrent pain centered in the upper abdomen, along with symptoms like early satiety and postprandial fullness, this condition significantly impacts children’s nutritional intake, academic performance, and overall quality of life [2]. Current treatment strategies, predominantly focusing on acid suppression and prokinetic agents, often provide only partial symptom relief [3], highlighting the urgent need to elucidate the molecular underpinnings of this disorder.

Mitochondrial dysfunction may play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of FD [4]. Mitochondria serve as cellular power plants, generating ATP through oxidative phosphorylation while simultaneously regulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) production [5]. In gastrointestinal smooth muscle cells, mitochondrial integrity is particularly crucial for maintaining normal contractile function and motility [6]. Emerging evidence suggests that disturbances in mitochondrial quality control mechanisms, especially excessive mitophagy (selective autophagy of damaged mitochondria), may disrupt gastrointestinal motility patterns characteristic of FD [7].

The sirtuin family member SIRT1 has emerged as a central regulator of mitochondrial homeostasis [8]. This NAD+-dependent deacetylase orchestrates diverse cellular processes including energy metabolism, stress responses, and autophagy through post-translational modification of target proteins [9, 10]. In the context of gastrointestinal physiology, SIRT1 maintains smooth muscle contractility by preserving mitochondrial function and mitigating oxidative damage [11]. Its protective roles extend to various metabolic and age-related disorders, where SIRT1 activation has shown promise in ameliorating mitochondrial dysfunction.

Dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1) is the master regulator of mitochondrial fission [12]. DRP1 activity is finely tuned by post-translational modifications, with acetylation status serving as a critical molecular switch. Hyperacetylation of DRP1 at specific lysine residues promotes mitochondrial fragmentation and excessive mitophagy, phenomena observed in neurodegenerative diseases and metabolic disorders [13, 14]. Given the deacetylase activity of SIRT1, we hypothesized that SIRT1 might regulate mitochondrial stability in FD through deacetylation of DRP1.

Despite these advances, the role of SIRT1-mediated DRP1 deacetylation in pediatric FD remains unknown. Therefore, this study aimed to determine whether SIRT1 exerts a protective effect in FD by modulating DRP1 acetylation and mitochondrial quality control, thereby providing new insights into the molecular basis of FD and identifying potential therapeutic targets.

Methods

Clinical sample collection

Serum samples were collected from pediatric FD patients and age-matched healthy controls. Whole blood was centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min to isolate serum, which was stored at − 80 °C until further analysis. The study protocol was approved by The Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University, and written informed consent was obtained from their guardians.

Pediatric patients diagnosed with FD according to the Rome IV criteria were included in the study. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age between 6 and 14 years; (2) presence of FD symptoms for at least 3 months, with symptom onset at least 6 months prior to diagnosis; (3) no history of organic, metabolic, or systemic diseases that could explain the symptoms. Exclusion criteria included: (1) presence of gastrointestinal organic diseases (e.g., peptic ulcer, inflammatory bowel disease, celiac disease); (2) history of abdominal surgery; (3) use of antibiotics, probiotics, or drugs affecting gastrointestinal motility (including proton pump inhibitors and prokinetics) within 4 weeks prior to enrollment; (4) presence of psychiatric disorders, including diagnosed anxiety or depressive disorders; (5) acute or chronic infections; (6) refusal to provide informed consent; (7) evidence of current Helicobacter pylori infection, as determined by ¹³C-urea breath test or stool antigen test; (8) refusal to undergo upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, or endoscopic findings revealing organic lesions such as peptic ulcer, erosive gastritis, or malignancy. Age- and sex-matched healthy controls with no gastrointestinal symptoms or chronic diseases were recruited from the same community during routine health examinations.

Cell culture and CCCP stimulation

Human gastric smooth muscle cells (HGSMCs) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. To induce mitochondrial stress, HGSMCs were treated with 10 µM carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP, C2759, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 24 h before further experiments.

Cell transfection

HGSMCs were transfected with an SIRT1 overexpression plasmid, an DRP1 overexpression plasmid, Short hairpin (sh) RNA targeting DRP1 (shDRP1), shRNA negative control (shNC), or empty vector (Genepharma, Shanghai, China) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Transfection efficiency was confirmed by qPCR after 48 h.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from serum and HGSMCs using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), followed by cDNA synthesis using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, Shiga, Japan). RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with A260/A280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.0 considered acceptable. RNA integrity was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, displaying distinct 28 S and 18 S ribosomal RNA bands. Hemolysis in serum samples was evaluated visually and by measuring absorbance at 414 nm; samples with significant hemolysis were excluded from analysis. The stability of GAPDH as a reference gene was confirmed by consistent cycle threshold (Ct) values across all serum samples (Ct range: 22–24), indicating its suitability for normalization in this study. qPCR was performed on a StepOnePlus system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) with SYBR Green Master Mix (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The relative mRNA expression of gene was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as the internal control. The primer sequences are as follows:

SIRT1: Forward Primer 5’-TAGCCTTGTCAGATAAGGAAGGA-3’; Rerverse Primer 5’-ACAGCTTCACAGTCAACTTTGT-3’;

DRP1: Forward Primer 5’-AAGGAGCCAGTCAAATTATTG-3’; Rerverse Primer 5’-AGTCAACAAAGTCTCAGTATT-3’;

GAPDH: Forward Primer 5’-ATGTTCGTCATGGGTGTGAA-3’; Rerverse Primer 5’-GGTGCTAAGCAGTTGGTGGT-3’

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Serum levels of IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, and C-reactive protein (CRP) were quantified using ELISA kits. Blood samples were collected from pediatric FD patients and healthy controls (n = 26 per group), and sera were isolated and stored at -80 °C until analysis. The assays were performed using the Human IL-6 ELISA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), Human TNF-α ELISA Kit (BosterBio, Pleasanton, CA, USA), Human IL-1β ELISA Kit (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and Human CRP ELISA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), strictly following the manufacturers’ instructions.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan). HGSMCs were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well and cultured overnight. Following transfection and CCCP treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA).

ATP level measurement

Intracellular ATP levels were measured using the ATP assay kit (ab83355, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA). Briefly, HGSMCs were washed with PBS and lysed with the provided ATP assay buffer. The lysates were centrifuged and the supernatant was collected. Subsequently, the reaction mix, which contained ATP probe, converter and developer, was added to each sample. After incubation in the dark at room temperature for 30 min, luminescence was measured using a GloMax® Discover Luminometer (Promega). A standard curve generated with known concentrations of ATP was used to calculate the absolute ATP levels in each sample.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) assay

MMP was evaluated using the JC-1 mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA). After treatment, HGSMCs were incubated with JC-1 staining solution at 37 °C for 20 min. Cells were then washed twice with PBS, and fluorescence intensity was measured at 530 nm (for JC-1 monomers, indicating mitochondrial depolarization) and 590 nm (for JC-1 aggregates, indicating healthy mitochondria) using a fluorescence microplate reader (BioTek).

Measurement of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels

Intracellular ROS levels were detected using the fluorescent probe 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA, Sigma-Aldrich). HGSMCs were seeded in 6-well plates and treated as indicated. Cells were then incubated with 10 µM DCFH-DA in serum-free medium at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. After washing twice with PBS, fluorescence intensity was measured at excitation and emission wavelengths of 488 nm and 525 nm, respectively, using a fluorescence microplate reader (BioTek).

Detection of malondialdehyde (MDA), Superoxide dismutase (SOD), and Glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px)

The levels of MDA (A003-1-2), SOD (A001-3-2), and GSH-Px (A005-1-2) in HGSMCs were determined using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All procedures were performed strictly following the provided protocols.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from HGSMCs using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-SIRT1 (1:1000, 9475, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-LC3I/II (1:1000, 4180, CST), anti-PINK1 (1:1000, 6946, CST), anti-Parkin (1:1000, 2132, CST), and anti-P62 (1:1000, 5114, CST), anti-DRP1 (1:1000, 8570, CST), and anti-GAPDH (1:5000, 2118, CST). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, 7074, CST) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Immunoprecipitation (IP)

To evaluate the acetylation levels of DRP1, IP was performed followed by immunoblot analysis with an acetyl-lysine-specific antibody. Briefly, cell lysates were prepared using IP lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors and deacetylase inhibitors to preserve acetylated modifications. The lysates were incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-DRP1 antibody conjugated to protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After extensive washing, the immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted and subjected to Western blot analysis using an anti-acetyl-lysine antibody.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

HGSMCs lysates were prepared using IP lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The lysates were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation using specific primary antibodies against SIRT1 (1:100, 9475, CST) or DRP1 (1:100, 8570, CST). Normal rabbit IgG was used as a negative control to assess non-specific binding. Following primary antibody incubation, protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were added and incubated for 2 h at 4 °C to capture the antibody–protein complexes. The beads were subsequently washed with cold PBS, and bound proteins were eluted by boiling sample buffer. The immunoprecipitated proteins were then separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes for Western blot analysis.

Site-directed mutagenesis and acetylation assay

The DRP1 K283A and K679A mutant was generated using the QuikChange II XL site-directed mutagenesis kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). DRP1 and its acetylation levels were detected by IP and Western blot analysis.

Cycloheximide chase assay

HGSMCs were treated with 100 µg/mL cycloheximide (CHX, Sigma-Aldrich) for 0, 2, 4, and 8 h. DRP1 protein levels were analyzed by Western blot using anti-DRP1.

Bioinformatics analysis

Download GSE169304 dataset from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds/?term=GSE169304) to analyze differentially expressed genes in FD patients compared to healthy controls and show them in the form of heat maps. The raw data of GSE169304, generated on the GPL20301 Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform (Homo sapiens), were downloaded and processed using R software (version 4.2.1). The expression data were preprocessed using the limma package. Specifically, Robust Multi-array Average (RMA) algorithm was applied for background correction, quantile normalization, and probe summarization. The normalized expression data were then log2-transformed. Differential expression analysis between FD patients and healthy controls was performed using linear model fitting combined with empirical Bayes moderation (eBayes) in the limma package. Genes with an absolute fold change (|FC|) > 1.5 and a Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted P-value < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed.

Potential acetylation sites on DRP1 were predicted using the PhosphoSitePlus database (https://www.phosphosite.org/).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and presented as mean ± SD. Statistical significance was determined by Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. The diagnostic value of SIRT1 in distinguishing pediatric FD patients from healthy controls was analyzed by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The correlation analysis was performed using pearson correlation.

Results

SIRT1 is decreased in pediatric FD

As shown in Fig. 1A, hierarchical clustering heatmap revealed differentially expressed genes in pediatric FD, with SIRT1 identified as a candidate gene due to its significant downregulation. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the 26 pediatric FD patients and 26 age- and sex-matched healthy controls are summarized in Table 1. There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of age, gender distribution, or prevalence of chronic comorbidities. Among the FD patients, 15 cases were diagnosed with epigastric pain syndrome (EPS) and 11 with postprandial distress syndrome (PDS). The mean dyspepsia symptom score of pediatric FD patients was 5.12 ± 1.00. Compared to healthy controls, pediatric FD patients exhibited reduced serum SIRT1 mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 1B and C). This was accompanied by a significant upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, whereas CRP levels showed no significant difference between patients and healthy controls (Fig. 1D-G). Pearson correlation analysis further verified a significant negative correlation between the expression of SIRT1 and the levels of IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β as well as CRP in pediatric FD patients (Fig. 1H-K). ROC curve analysis demonstrated that serum SIRT1 had high diagnostic value for distinguishing pediatric FD patients from healthy controls (AUC = 0.9504) (Fig. 1L). Furthermore, SIRT1 expression was markedly decreased in CCCP-stimulated HGSMCs (Fig. 1M).

Overexpression of SIRT1 enhances mitochondrial function and suppresses mitophagy in HGSMCs

To investigate the biological role of SIRT1, we transfected HGSMCs with an SIRT1 overexpression plasmid to elevate its expression (Fig. 2A). Cell viability assays demonstrated that SIRT1 overexpression enhanced the survival of CCCP-stimulated HGSMCs (Fig. 2B). CCCP treatment significantly reduced ATP levels, which were restored by SIRT1 upregulation (Fig. 2C). JC-1 staining revealed diminished mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) in CCCP-exposed cells, while SIRT1 overexpression reversed this decline (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, CCCP stimulation increased LC3II/LC3I, PINK1, and Parkin protein expression while decreasing P62, effects that were attenuated upon SIRT1 overexpression (Fig. 2E).

Overexpression of SIRT1 alleviates oxidative stress in HGSMCs

CCCP stimulation in HGSMCs significantly elevated ROS and MDA levels while reducing SOD and GSH-Px activity. Notably, SIRT1 upregulation effectively counteracted these CCCP-induced alterations, restoring redox homeostasis (Fig. 3A-D).

SIRT1 promotes DRP1 degradation through deacetylation at K283

To identify potential target genes regulated by SIRT1, we performed volcano plot analysis of SIRT1-associated genes, which revealed 1687 positively correlated genes and 1518 negatively correlated genes (Fig. 4A). KEGG enrichment analysis of the SIRT1 negatively correlated genes demonstrated a significant enrichment in the mitophagy pathway (Fig. 4B). Subsequent bubble plot analysis of mitophagy-related genes identified DRP1 as a candidate gene (Fig. 4C). Compared to healthy controls, pediatric FD patients exhibited elevated serum DRP1 mRNA levels (Fig. 4D). Pearson correlation analysis demonstrated a significant negative correlation between DRP1 and SIRT1 expression (Fig. 4E). CCCP-stimulated HGSMCs exhibited elevated DRP1 levels, which were reversed by SIRT1 overexpression (Fig. 4F). Co-IP confirmed direct SIRT1-DRP1 interaction (Fig. 4G). The acetylation sites of DRP1 were predicted using the PhosphoSitePlus database, and the sites with the highest modification scores were K283 and K679 (Fig. 4H). Subsequently, site-directed mutagenesis was performed at these two residues. Functional analysis revealed that mutation of the K283 site specifically reduced both DRP1 expression and its acetylation level, whereas mutation at the K679 site had no significant effect (Fig. 4I). Furthermore, cycloheximide chase assays revealed that SIRT1 overexpression accelerated DRP1 protein degradation (Fig. 4J). These results demonstrate that SIRT1 directly deacetylates DRP1 at K283, thereby promoting its proteasomal degradation and reducing its protein stability.

Knockdown of DRP1 suppresses mitophagy and oxidative stress in CCCP-stimulated cells

To investigate the biological role of DRP1, we transfected cells with shDRP1 to knock down its expression, using a non-targeting shNC as a control (Fig. 5A). DRP1 knockdown significantly enhanced the survival of CCCP-stimulated cells compared to the shNC group (Fig. 5B). CCCP treatment markedly reduced intracellular ATP levels, which were substantially restored by DRP1 downregulation (Fig. 5C). JC-1 staining revealed a diminished MMP in CCCP-exposed cells; however, DRP1 knockdown reversed this decline (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, CCCP stimulation increased the LC3-II/LC3-I ratio, upregulated PINK1 and Parkin protein expression, and decreased P62 levels—effects that were markedly attenuated upon DRP1 knockdown (Fig. 5E), suggesting that DRP1 silencing suppresses excessive mitophagy. CCCP stimulation in cells significantly elevated oxidative stress markers, as evidenced by increased ROS and MDA levels (Fig. 5F and G) and reduced activity of antioxidant enzymes, including SOD (Fig. 5H) and GSH-Px (Fig. 5I). Notably, DRP1 knockdown effectively counteracted these CCCP-induced alterations. These data demonstrate that DRP1 knockdown, similar to SIRT1 overexpression, preserves mitochondrial function, suppresses excessive mitophagy, and alleviates oxidative stress under CCCP-induced pathological conditions.

DRP1 mediates the protective effects of SIRT1 in CCCP-stimulated HGSMCs

To determine whether SIRT1’s biological functions are DRP1-dependent, we conducted rescue experiments by transfecting HGSMCs with a DRP1 overexpression plasmid (Fig. 6A). Under CCCP stimulation, DRP1 upregulation reversed the SIRT1-induced enhancements in cell viability, ATP levels, and MMP (Fig. 6B-D). Similarly, the suppressive effects of SIRT1 overexpression on LC3II/LC3I, PINK1, and Parkin protein expression, as well as its promotion of P62 expression, were abolished by DRP1 restoration (Fig. 6E). Furthermore, DRP1 overexpression negated the SIRT1-mediated reductions in ROS and MDA levels and the increases in SOD and GSH-Px activities in CCCP-treated HGSMCs (Fig. 6F-I).

Discussion

Pediatric FD remains a clinical challenge due to its multifactorial pathogenesis and inconsistent treatment outcomes [15]. SIRT1, a key NAD⁺-dependent deacetylase, plays a central role in regulating mitochondrial homeostasis, cellular stress adaptation, and post-translational modifications [16]. In our study, The observed reduction in SIRT1 levels in children with FD provides clinically relevant insights. In addition to the reduced SIRT1 levels, pediatric FD patients exhibited significantly elevated serum pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, which were inversely correlated with SIRT1 expression. This negative association suggests that SIRT1 deficiency may contribute to the inflammatory milieu characteristic of FD, further supporting its role as a key modulator of the disease process. The strong diagnostic value of serum SIRT1, as indicated by an AUC of 0.9504, underscores its potential as a biomarker. This decrease in SIRT1 was reliably recapitulated in CCCP-treated human gastric smooth muscle cells, establishing an experimentally useful model that mirrors the clinical condition.

Emerging evidence demonstrates that SIRT1 signaling orchestrates mitophagy across multiple pathological contexts. In pulmonary endothelial cells (ECs), SIRT1 promotes endosome-mediated mitophagy, thereby suppressing STING/NLRP3 inflammasome activation [17]. Similarly, SIRT1-dependent deacetylation of FOXO3 enhances mitophagy in endometrial cancer, conferring hormonal therapy resistance [18]. Notably, pharmacological targeting of SIRT1-induced mitophagy improves cerebrovascular homeostasis and cognitive function in Alzheimer’s disease models [19]. Furthermore, activation of the SIRT1/PGC-1α axis triggers mitophagy to mitigate oxidative damage in intestinal epithelial cells [20]. In this study, overexpression of SIRT1 in stressed gastric smooth muscle cells resulted in a marked improvement in mitochondrial function. Cells exhibiting severe ATP depletion demonstrated substantial recovery in energy production following SIRT1 upregulation. Consistent with these observations, JC-1 staining indicated that SIRT1 overexpression effectively preserved mitochondrial membrane potential, which would otherwise be severely compromised under stress conditions. These findings suggest that SIRT1 plays a critical role in maintaining mitochondrial integrity and cellular energy homeostasis, thereby supporting normal digestive function at the cellular level.

The mitophagy findings yielded notable insights. Consistent with prior reports [21], CCCP treatment induced pronounced mitophagy, as evidenced by a marked increase in the LC3II/LC3I ratio. However, SIRT1 overexpression substantially modulated this process. The concomitant normalization of p62 levels further indicates that SIRT1 not only suppresses excessive mitophagy but also contributes to the restoration of regulated mitophagic flux. Additionally, assessments of oxidative stress underscored another facet of SIRT1-mediated protection. Mitochondrial stress typically elicits accumulation of reactive oxygen species and elevated lipid peroxidation, as reflected by increased MDA. SIRT1 overexpression effectively attenuated these oxidative insults and enhanced the activity of antioxidant enzymes, including SOD and GSH-Px. Notably, the interplay among SIRT1, ROS, and mitophagy is intricate and bidirectional. Under physiological conditions, basal mitophagy eliminates damaged mitochondria, thereby limiting ROS overproduction. However, excessive or dysregulated mitophagy, as observed in CCCP-stimulated cells, can disrupt mitochondrial homeostasis and exacerbate oxidative stress [22–24]. SIRT1, by deacetylating DRP1 and reducing excessive mitophagy, preserves mitochondrial integrity and function, leading to attenuated ROS generation. Conversely, ROS accumulation can activate stress-sensitive pathways that further promote mitophagy, forming a potential vicious cycle [25–27]. Our findings demonstrate that SIRT1 overexpression breaks this cycle by suppressing excessive mitophagy and enhancing antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, GSH-Px), thereby lowering ROS and MDA levels. These results position SIRT1 as a central hub integrating mitochondrial quality control and redox balance in gastric smooth muscle cells.

SIRT1 plays a crucial role in maintaining metabolic homeostasis, stress response, and genomic stability by deacetylating transcription factors, co-regulators, and metabolic enzymes. To identify potential targets regulated by SIRT1, we performed bioinformatic analyses of SIRT1-associated genes. KEGG enrichment revealed a significant negative correlation between SIRT1 and the mitophagy pathway. Notably, DRP1, a key regulator of mitochondrial fission, was identified within this pathway. Additionally, a significant inverse relationship between SIRT1 and DRP1 expression, suggesting that DRP1 may be negatively modulated by SIRT1 in the context of mitophagy regulation. Emerging evidence further underscores the pivotal role of SIRT1 in modulating mitochondrial dynamics via the PGC-1α/DRP1 pathway across various pathological contexts. In non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), SIRT1 mitigates hepatic steatosis and mitochondrial impairment by suppressing Drp1 acetylation and expression [14]. Similarly, in spinal cord injury, caffeic acid phenethyl ester (CAPE) reduces neuroinflammation and oxidative stress via SIRT1-dependent activation of the PGC-1α/DRP1 axis, leading to improved mitochondrial function [28]. Furthermore, SIRT1 has been shown to preserve cardiac function under high-altitude hypoxia by regulating the PGC-1α–DRP1 pathway to restrain excessive mitochondrial fission [29]. Our findings reveal that SIRT1, as a NAD⁺-dependent deacetylase, directly deacetylates DRP1 at K283. This deacetylation promotes DRP1 degradation, thereby suppressing excessive mitophagy. In the context of pediatric functional dyspepsia, reduced SIRT1 expression leads to DRP1 hyperacetylation, increased protein stability, and subsequent mitophagy activation. Notably, DRP1 knockdown in CCCP-stimulated HGSMCs phenocopied the protective effects of SIRT1 overexpression, suppressing excessive mitophagy and oxidative stress while preserving mitochondrial function. These loss-of-function experiments further validate DRP1 as a critical downstream effector through which SIRT1 exerts its cytoprotective actions in gastric smooth muscle cells. Rescue experiments further confirmed the functional significance of this interaction, as DRP1 overexpression abolished the protective effects conferred by SIRT1.

While SIRT1 shows promise as a diagnostic marker, larger patient studies must confirm its reliability. Animal studies should test whether enhancing SIRT1 activity actually improves digestive symptoms. These future directions could transform our mechanistic understanding into tangible patient benefits.

In summary, this study establishes SIRT1 deficiency as a critical mechanistic factor in pediatric functional dyspepsia and elucidates how its loss disrupts mitochondrial homeostasis through DRP1 hyperacetylation. By integrating clinical data with molecular evidence, we provide a mechanistic framework that not only clarifies the cellular pathogenesis of FD but also identifies potential therapeutic targets. These findings may inform the development of novel mechanism-based therapies for affected pediatric patients.
