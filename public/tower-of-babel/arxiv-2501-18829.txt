On the unexpected geometrical origin of the algebra of symmetries
O. Ramírez, Y. Bonder

Abstract
The fundamental symmetries in gravity and gauge theories, formulated using differential forms, are gauge transformations and diffeomorphisms. These symmetries act in distinct ways on different dynamical fields. Yet, the commutator of these symmetries forms a closed, field-independent algebra. This work uncovers a natural correspondence between this algebra and the Lie bracket of some vector fields on the principal fiber bundle associated with the physical theory, providing a geometric interpretation of the symmetry algebra. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the symmetry algebra is independent of the connection. Finally, we analyze an example illustrating how a specific connection, associated with Lorentz-Lie transformations, simplifies the symmetry algebra in the presence of spacetime Killing vector fields.

2.1
Action

Our starting point is the construction of an action functional for generic theories. We work in
N
spacetime dimensions. In addition, we consider a collection of
p
-forms with a algebra index
\Psi^{\mu}
, which represent general matter fields and/or vielbeins. We employ vielbeins to describe gravity, which are more commonly known as tetrads in the specific case of four-dimensional spacetime

(1)

Observe that we do not explicitly consider higher-derivative terms in our analysis. Naively, one might argue that such terms are unnecessary since
\text{D}^{2}\Psi^{\mu}=F^{\mu}_{\ \nu}\Psi^{\nu}
and
\text{D}F^{\mu}_{\ \nu}=0
(see equation (
62

[
15
]
, as argued in the context of the Lovelock theorem
[
16
]
. The Hodge star map, in turn, necessitates the introduction of a (background or dynamical) spacetime metric. In this work, we do not explicitly include such higher-order theories. Nonetheless, their incorporation into our framework is straightforward.

We can express the variation of

\displaystyle\delta S

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\left[\delta\Psi^{\mu}\wedge\frac{\partial L}{\partial\Psi^{\mu}}+\delta\text{D}\Psi^{\mu}\wedge\frac{\partial L}{\partial\text{D}\Psi^{\mu}}+\delta F^{\mu}_{\ \nu}\wedge\frac{\partial L}{\partial F^{\mu}_{\ \nu}}\right]

(2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\left\{\delta\Psi^{\mu}\wedge\left[\frac{\partial L}{\partial\Psi^{\mu}}-(-1)^{p}\text{D}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\text{D}\Psi^{\mu}}\right)\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\delta\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \nu}\wedge\left[\Psi^{\nu}\wedge\frac{\partial L}{\partial\text{D}\Psi^{\mu}}+\text{D}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial F^{\mu}_{\ \nu}}\right)\right]\right\}

An infinitesimal transformation of the fields,

On the other hand, infinitesimal diffeomorphisms act on the fields by the Lie derivative along the vector field

\displaystyle\delta_{\text{Diff}}(\xi)\Psi^{\mu}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\Psi^{\mu}=\text{i}_{\xi}\text{d}\Psi^{\mu}+\text{d}\text{i}_{\xi}\Psi^{\mu},

\displaystyle\delta_{\text{Diff}}(\xi)\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \nu}=\text{i}_{\xi}\text{d}\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \nu}+\text{d}\text{i}_{\xi}\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \nu},

(7)

where we use Cartan’s magic formula (
59

Note that, when acting on objects with algebra indexes, diffeomorphisms are not necessarily covariant, namely,
\delta_{\text{Diff}}(\xi)\Psi^{\mu}
does not transform according to equation (
4

\displaystyle\delta_{\text{LT}}(\xi)\Psi^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{i}_{\xi}\text{D}\Psi^{\mu}+\text{D}\text{i}_{\xi}\Psi^{\mu},

(8)

\displaystyle\delta_{\text{LT}}(\xi)\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{i}_{\xi}F^{\mu}_{\ \nu}.

(9)

We can readily show that any LT satisfies

14

\delta_{\text{LT}}(\xi)=\delta_{\text{Diff}}(\xi)-\delta_{\text{GT}}(\text{i}_{\xi}\mathcal{A}).

(10)

In other words, any LT is a combination of a diffeomorphism and a gauge transformation with a field-dependent gauge parameter

\eta_{1}

The commutators can be calculated using two approaches: the conventional method, which involves acting with the symmetries on fields, or the geometrical method we propose below. We begin this section by presenting the conventional calculation.

3.1
Conventional calculation

In this part of the paper, we compute the commutators of gauge transformations and diffeomorphisms by acting on the fields. We begin by computing the commutators when acting on a connection field. To obtain the commutator of two gauge transformations, we note that successive gauge transformations produce

\displaystyle\delta_{\text{GT}}(\lambda_{1})\delta_{\text{GT}}(\lambda_{2})\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{\text{GT}}(\lambda_{1})\{\text{d}(\lambda_{2})^{\mu}_{\ \nu}+\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \rho}(\lambda_{2})^{\rho}_{\ \nu}-\mathcal{A}^{\rho}_{\ \nu}(\lambda_{2})^{\mu}_{\ \rho}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(\text{D}[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]_{\mathfrak{g}})^{\mu}_{\ \nu}+(\text{D}\lambda_{1})^{\mu}_{\ \rho}(\lambda_{2})^{\rho}_{\ \nu}-(\text{D}\lambda_{1})^{\rho}_{\ \nu}(\lambda_{2})^{\mu}_{\ \rho},

\text{D}\lambda^{\mu}_{\ \nu}=\text{d}\lambda^{\mu}_{\ \nu}+\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \rho}\lambda^{\rho}_{\ \nu}-\mathcal{A}^{\rho}_{\ \nu}\lambda^{\mu}_{\ \rho}

[\delta_{\text{GT}}(\lambda_{1}),\delta_{\text{GT}}(\lambda_{2})]\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \nu}=-(\text{D}[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]_{\mathfrak{g}})^{\mu}_{\ \nu}=\delta_{\text{GT}}(-[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]_{\mathfrak{g}})\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \nu},

(13)

where we use equation (

Our next task is to compute the commutator of a gauge transformation and an LT. We first note that

\displaystyle\delta_{\text{LT}}(\xi)\delta_{\text{GT}}(\lambda)\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{D}\text{i}_{\xi}\text{D}\lambda^{\mu}_{\ \nu}+\text{i}_{\xi}\text{D}\text{D}\lambda^{\mu}_{\ \nu}

\displaystyle\text{D}(\text{i}_{\xi}\text{D}\lambda^{\mu}_{\ \nu})+\text{i}_{\xi}F^{\mu}_{\ \rho}\lambda^{\rho}_{\ \nu}-\text{i}_{\xi}F^{\rho}_{\ \nu}\lambda^{\mu}_{\ \rho},

\delta_{\text{GT}}(\lambda)\delta_{\text{LT}}(\xi)\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \nu}=\delta_{\text{GT}}(\lambda)\text{i}_{\xi}F^{\mu}_{\ \nu}=-\text{i}_{\xi}F^{\rho}_{\ \nu}\lambda^{\mu}_{\ \rho}+\text{i}_{\xi}F^{\mu}_{\ \rho}\lambda^{\rho}_{\ \nu}.

Thus,

[\delta_{\text{LT}}(\xi),\delta_{\text{GT}}(\lambda)]\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \nu}=\text{D}(\text{i}_{\xi}\text{D}\lambda^{\mu}_{\ \nu})=\delta_{\text{GT}}(\text{i}_{\xi}\text{D}\lambda)\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \nu}.

In other words, the commutator of a gauge transformation and an LT is a gauge transformation with parameter

Now we study the commutator of two LTs. We observe that

\displaystyle\delta_{\text{LT}}(\xi_{1})\delta_{\text{LT}}(\xi_{2})\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{\text{LT}}(\xi_{1})\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}F^{\mu}_{\ \nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{D}\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}F^{\mu}_{\ \nu}+\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}\text{D}\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}F^{\mu}_{\ \nu}.

From equation (

\displaystyle[\delta_{\text{LT}}(\xi_{1}),\delta_{\text{LT}}(\xi_{2})]\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \nu}

3.2
Geometric method

In this subsection, we explore the connection between the symmetry space of a given physical theory and a principal fiber bundle. This approach is motivated by fact that in a principal fiber bundle all fields have identical transformation properties. In contrast, the diverse transformations observed in spacetime arise as a consequence of the projection onto the base manifold (see
C

Our method is begins by establishing a homomorphism between two distinct vector spaces. We now proceed to construct a vector space associated with the symmetries of the theories.

3.2.3
Homomorphism

We proceed to use the associations of symmetries and vector fields on

4
Connection independence

In the preceding section, we employ LTs to define the notion of horizontality. A natural question arises: do these results remain valid under alternative definitions of horizontality? A generic combination of the relevant transformations can be written as

5
Conclusions

In this work, we use the first-order formalism to study gauge and gravity theories. We analyze the structure of symmetries and demonstrate that their commutators form a closed algebra. Remarkably, we also show that these commutators are independent of whether they are computed by acting on regular fields or on connection fields, despite the distinct nature of these calculations. This observation points to an underlying geometric structure.

The geometric structure we identify is associated with principal fiber bundles, where all the fields have the same transformation properties. Specifically, we construct vector fields on the fiber bundle that encode the relevant symmetries. The key structure is provided by the connection on the principal fiber bundle, which selects vertical and horizontal directions. Vertical directions correspond to vectors associated with the fibers, which are isomorphic to the Lie group, and thus represent gauge transformations, while horizontal directions are associated with, say, local translations (LTs). Importantly, the Lie bracket of the vector fields representing the symmetries reproduces the algebra originally derived by acting on the dynamical fields, uncovering the geometric structure behind the symmetry algebra.

The formalism we develop is fundamentally kinematical, with the action only playing the role of defining the theories’ symmetries: gauge and diffeomorphism invariance. In fact, these symmetries are present simply because there are no nondynamical fields in the actions
[
14
,
34
]
. Extending our framework to include nondynamical fields, which would “break” the symmetries, represents a promising avenue for future research. Such generalizations could offer new mathematical insights and broaden the applicability of our approach.

Another intriguing avenue of research involves studying principal fiber bundles with nontrivial topology. These arise when the base manifold,
M
(which in our case represents spacetime), exhibits a nontrivial topology, leading to fiber bundles that are not globally
M\times G
. We anticipate that these scenarios could unlock new applications of our formalism, particularly in gravitational theories. For example, these topological features could facilitate investigations of torsion effects. A compelling direction is the potential generalization of the Aharonov-Bohm effect to gravity theories, framed within principal fiber bundles. Such a generalization could pave the way for experimental proposals that reduce noise, improving upon earlier methods, such as the one described in Ref.
[
35
]
.

Additionally, the study of symmetries of the theories in asymptotically flat spacetimes, where one can define the ADM mass and angular momentum
[
36
]
, is particularly interesting. For spacetimes that are also stationary, our formalism could offer new methods to compute Noether charges and produce alternative derivations of the first law of black hole mechanics
[
31
]
. Such tools could provide a deeper geometric interpretation of these laws through the lens of principal fiber bundle geometry, offering fresh perspectives on this topic.

\wedge
, which is essentially an antisymmetrized tensor product. This operation takes a
q
-form and an
r
-form as input and produces a
(q+r)
-form as output.

The exterior derivative,
d
, is a map from
p
-forms to
p+1
-forms defined by acting with any torsionless derivative on the
p
-form and antisymmetrizing all the spacetime indexes. Another operator we use is the inner derivative along the vector field
\xi
,
\text{i}_{\xi}
, which acts on a
p
-form and yields a
p-1
-form. This
p-1
-form is obtained by saturating the original form with
\xi
. Both operators follow a graded Leibniz rule, namelt, if
\omega
is a
q
-form, then

\displaystyle\text{d}(\omega\wedge\eta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{d}\omega\wedge\eta+(-1)^{q}\omega\wedge\text{d}\eta,

(57)

\displaystyle\text{i}_{\xi}(\omega\wedge\eta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{i}_{\xi}\omega\wedge\eta+(-1)^{q}\omega\wedge\text{i}_{\xi}\eta.

(58)

Remarkably, the Lie derivative of a
q
-form
\omega
along
\xi
can be written as

\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\omega=(\text{d}\text{i}_{\xi}+\text{i}_{\xi}\text{d})\omega.

(59)

This is the celebrated Cartan’s magic formula.

We can also define a covariant exterior derivative,
D
, which extends the concept of the exterior derivative to
\mathfrak{g}
-valued
p
-forms, such as
T^{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{l}}{}_{\nu_{1}\dots\nu_{k}}
. Unlike the standard exterior derivative,
D
ensures that the
p
-form transforms covariantly under gauge transformations. It is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\text{D}{}T^{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{l}}{}_{{\nu_{1}\dots\nu_{k}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{d}{}T^{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{l}}{}_{{\nu_{1}\dots\nu_{k}}}+\mathcal{A}^{\mu_{1}}_{\ \rho}T^{\rho\dots\mu_{l}}{}_{{\nu_{1}\dots\nu_{k}}}+\dots+\mathcal{A}^{\mu_{l}}_{\ \rho}T^{\mu_{1}\dots\rho}{}_{{\nu_{1}\dots\nu_{k}}}

(60)

\displaystyle-\mathcal{A}^{\rho}_{\ \nu_{1}}T^{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{l}}{}_{{\rho\dots\nu_{k}}}-\dots-\mathcal{A}^{\rho}_{\ \nu_{k}}T^{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{l}}{}_{{\nu_{1}\dots\rho}}.

We can show that
D
satisfies a graded Leibniz rule analogous to (
57
).

Moreover, any connection
1
-form
\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \nu}
defines the following curvature
2
-form:

F^{\mu}_{\ \nu}=\text{d}\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \nu}+\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \rho}\wedge\mathcal{A}^{\rho}_{\ \nu}.

(61)

We can easily show that this curvature
2
-form satisfies the Bianchi identity:

\text{D}{}F^{\mu}_{\ \nu}=0.

(62)

In addition, we can verify that

\text{D}{}\text{D}{}\Psi^{\mu}=F^{\mu}_{\ \nu}\wedge\Psi^{\nu}.

(63)

This generalizes in a straightforward manner to forms with additional algebra indexes: for each index, a corresponding term is generated, with the condition that indexes in the lower position have a minus sign.

Finally, we note that any two vector fields in a manifold define a third vector field via its Lie bracket. If we consider spacetime
M
as the manifold and an arbitrary function
f
on it, this bracket, for the
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}
vector fields, takes the form

[\xi_{1},\xi_{2}]_{M}(f)=\xi_{1}[\xi_{2}(f)]-\xi_{2}[\xi_{1}(f)].

(64)

Appendix B
Identities

In this appendix, we deduce some identities that are relevant throughout the paper. The starting point is
[\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{1}},\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}]=\text{i}_{[\xi_{1},\xi_{2}]_{M}}

[
38
]
. Acting on an algebra-valued
p
-form,
\phi^{\mu}
, we get

\displaystyle\text{i}_{[\xi_{1},\xi_{2}]_{M}}\phi^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{1}}\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\phi^{\mu}-\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\mathcal{L}_{\xi_{1}}\phi^{\mu}

(65)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{d}\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\phi^{\mu}+\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}\text{d}\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\phi^{\mu}-\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\text{d}\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}\phi^{\mu}-\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}\text{d}\phi^{\mu}

\displaystyle\text{D}\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\phi^{\mu}-\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \nu}\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\phi^{\nu}+\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}(\text{D}\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\phi^{\mu}-\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \nu}\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\phi^{\nu})

\displaystyle-\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}(\text{D}\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}\phi^{\mu}-\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \nu}\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}\phi^{\nu})-\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}(\text{D}\phi^{\mu}-\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \nu}\phi^{\nu})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{D}\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\phi^{\mu}+\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}\text{D}\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\phi^{\mu}-\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\text{D}\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}\phi^{\mu}-\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}\text{D}\phi^{\mu}.

Furthermore, we can easily verify that the identity (
65
) holds when we apply it to tensors with additional algebra indexes. In particular, for a
p
-forms of the form
T^{\mu}_{\ \nu}
, it becomes

\text{i}_{[\xi_{1},\xi_{2}]_{M}}T^{\mu}_{\ \nu}=\text{D}\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}T^{\mu}_{\ \nu}+\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}\text{D}\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}T^{\mu}_{\ \nu}-\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\text{D}\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}T^{\mu}_{\ \nu}-\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}\text{D}T^{\mu}_{\ \nu}.

(66)

Using equation (
65
), we can manipulate
\text{i}_{[\xi_{1},\xi_{2}]_{M}}\text{D}\Psi^{\mu}
and
\text{D}\text{i}_{[\xi_{1},\xi_{2}]_{M}}\Psi^{\mu}
, obtaining,

\displaystyle\text{i}_{[\xi_{1},\xi_{2}]_{M}}\text{D}\Psi^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{D}\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\text{D}\Psi^{\mu}+\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}\text{D}\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\text{D}\Psi^{\mu}-\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\text{D}\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}\text{D}\Psi^{\mu}-\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}\text{D}\text{D}\Psi^{\mu}

(67)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{D}\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\text{D}\Psi^{\mu}+\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}\text{D}\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\text{D}\Psi^{\mu}-\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\text{D}\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}\text{D}\Psi^{\mu}-\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\text{D}\text{D}\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}\Psi^{\mu}

\displaystyle-\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}F^{\mu}_{\ \nu}\Psi^{\nu}+\text{i}_{\xi_{1}}F^{\mu}_{\ \nu}\wedge\text{i}_{\xi_{2}}\Psi^{\nu},

\displaystyle\text{D}\text{i}_{[\xi_{1},\xi_{2}]_{M}}\Psi^{\mu}

Appendix C
Principal fiber bundles

A principal fiber bundle over
M
with fiber
G
is a mathematical structure consisting of a total space,
P
, a base space,
M
, a Lie group
G
(the “fiber”), and a projection map
\pi:P\to M
, such that
P
is a smooth manifold that locally looks like
M\times G
. We can define a connection
1
-form on
P
,
\omega
, that has the information on how
T_{u}P
, the tangent space over
u\in P
, splits into the vertical and horizontal parts, which we denote, respectively, by
V_{u}P
and
H_{u}P
. Importantly, the manifolds associated with the vertical directions are isomorphic to
G
.

We say that
X
is a vertical vector field if
\pi_{*}X=0
. To construct elements in
V_{u}P
, we can take
\lambda\in\mathfrak{g}
and define a curve in
P
, by acting with the group right multiplication,
R_{g}
, as

R_{\exp(t\lambda)}u=u\exp(t\lambda),

(69)

where
t
is the parameter of the curve. Note that
\pi(u)=\pi(u\exp(t\lambda))
, and therefore (
69
) is on a single fiber for all
t
. We define
\lambda^{\#}\in V_{u}P
as the tangent vector to the curve (
69
) at
t=0
. Concretely,
\lambda^{\#}
acts on
f:P\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

\lambda^{\#}f(u)=\frac{d}{dt}f(u\exp(t\lambda))\bigg|_{t=0}.

(70)

If we repeat the previous construction on each fiber, we obtain the vector field
\lambda^{\#}
, which is known as the fundamental vector field generated by
\lambda
. In fact, the map
\#
is an isomorphism between
\mathfrak{g}
and
V_{u}P

[
15
]
.

We define the connection 1-form,
\omega
, also known as the Ehresmann connection or simply the connection in
P
, by

\omega(\lambda^{\#})=\lambda,

(71)

and

R_{g}^{*}\omega=g^{-1}\omega g,

(72)

for all
\lambda\in\mathfrak{g}
and
g\in G
. Importantly,
\omega
enables us to characterize
H_{u}P
by the condition that any horizontal vector
X
satisfies

\omega(X)=0.

(73)

Consider a collection of open sets
\{U_{i}\}
covering
M
. A local section is a smooth map
\sigma_{i}:U_{i}\to P
such that
\pi\circ\sigma_{i}
is the identity map on
U_{i}
. This means that
\sigma_{i}(x)
is a point in
P
“on top of”
x\in M
. We define the
1
-form valued at
\mathfrak{g}
,
\mathcal{A}_{i}=\sigma_{i}^{*}\omega
in
U_{i}
, which we call the local connection and which corresponds, in the context of gauge theories, to a gauge field (the algebra indexes are omitted for simplicity). On the other hand, given a section
\sigma
and a local connection
\mathcal{A}=\sigma^{*}\omega
in
M
, there exists
\omega
, a
1
-form connection on
P
, such that
\sigma^{*}\omega=\mathcal{A}
; in fact, we can express
\omega
as

\omega=g^{-1}\pi^{*}\mathcal{A}g+g^{-1}\text{d}_{P}g,

(74)

where
\text{d}_{P}
is the exterior derivative in
P
. We can show that this
\omega
satisfies (
71
) and (
72
)
[
15
]
. Another relevant property of
\omega
is that, by construction, it is global in
P

[
15
]
, that is, given two open sets
U_{i}
and
U_{j}
,
\omega|_{U_{i}}=\omega|_{U_{j}}
in
U_{i}\cap U_{j}
.
This relation generates the transformation property of the local connection.

Consider a curve,
\gamma
, in
M
. We say that a curve
\tilde{\gamma}\subset P
is the horizontal lift of
\gamma
if
\pi\circ\tilde{\gamma}=\gamma
and if the tangent to
\tilde{\gamma}
, denoted
\tilde{X}
, is a horizontal vector. hence,
\omega(\tilde{X})=0
, which is a differential equation, ensuring that
\tilde{\gamma}
exists and it is unique. We can express
\tilde{X}
as a function of
X
, the tangent vector to
\gamma
. To do this, we consider a local section
\sigma
that allows us to write
\tilde{\gamma}(t)=\sigma(\gamma(t))g(t)
where
g(t)\in G
. Then, the tangent vector can be expressed as
\tilde{X}=\tilde{\gamma}_{*}X
, which we can, in turn, rewrite as
[
15
]

\tilde{X}=R_{g(t)*}(\sigma_{*}X)+[g(t)^{-1}\text{d}g(X)]^{\#}.

(75)

Notice that, at each
u\in P
,
\pi_{*}\tilde{\xi}=\xi
. Also, from equation (
75
) and
\omega(\tilde{X})=0
, we can derive a differential equation for the element
g
that generates the horizontal lifting of the curve. This equation takes the form

\text{d}g(X)=\frac{dg(t)}{dt}=-\mathcal{A}(X)g(t).

(76)

We can apply this construction to relate vector fields on
M
and horizontal lifts. This relationship is unique and the resulting vectors satisfy
\pi_{*}\tilde{X}=X

[
25
]
.

We can also define a curvature,
\Omega
, associated with the connection
1
-form
\omega
using Cartan’s structure equation. The definition is

\Omega=\text{d}_{P}\omega+\omega\wedge\omega.

(77)

The geometric interpretation of
\Omega
arises when we apply it to
X,Y\in H_{u}P
:

\Omega(X,Y)=\text{d}_{P}\omega(X,Y)=X[\omega(Y)]-Y[\omega(X)]-\omega([X,Y]_{P})=-\omega([X,Y]_{P}),

(78)

where
[\cdot,\cdot]_{P}
is the Lie bracket in
P
. Thus, the horizontal lifting of a closed infinitesimal parallelogram in
M
, associated with two vectors whose lifts correspond to the fields
X
and
Y
, describes the failure of the lifted circuit to close. This failure is vertical and is captured by
\Omega(X,Y)
.

Using equation (
74
), we can calculate
\Omega
. The first term of
\Omega
produces

\displaystyle\text{d}_{P}\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{d}_{P}g^{-1}\pi^{*}\mathcal{A}g+g^{-1}\text{d}_{p}\pi^{*}\mathcal{A}g-g^{-1}\pi^{*}\mathcal{A}\wedge\text{d}_{p}g+\text{d}_{p}g^{-1}\wedge\text{d }_{P}g

(79)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(g^{-1}\text{d}_{p}gg^{-1})\wedge\pi^{*}\mathcal{A}g+g^{-1}\pi^{*}\ of\mathcal{A}g

\displaystyle-g^{-1}\pi^{*}\mathcal{A}\wedge\text{d}_{p}g-(g^{-1}\text{d}_{p}g)\wedge g^{-1}\text{d}_{P}g.

On the other hand, for the second term of
\Omega
we have

\displaystyle\omega\wedge\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(g^{-1}\pi^{*}\mathcal{A}g+g^{-1}\text{d}_{p}g)\wedge(g^{-1}\pi^{*}\mathcal{A}g+g^{-1}\text{d}_{p}g)

(80)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g^{-1}\pi^{*}(\mathcal{A}\wedge\mathcal{A})g+g^{-1}\pi^{*}\mathcal{A}\wedge\text{d}_{P}g

\displaystyle+(g^{-1}\text{d}_{p}gg^{-1})\wedge\pi^{*}\mathcal{A}g+(g^{-1}\text{d}_{p}g)\wedge(g^{-1}\text{d}_{p}g).

Adding (
79
) and (
80
), we find

\Omega=g^{-1}\pi^{*}(\text{d}\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}\wedge\mathcal{A})g=g^{-1}\pi^{*}Fg,

(81)

where
F^{\mu}_{\ \nu}=\text{d}\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \nu}+\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \rho}\wedge\mathcal{A}^{\rho}_{\ \nu}
is called the field strength of
\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{\ \nu}
.

It is clear that, when evaluating
\Omega
on two arbitrary horizontal lifts,
X
and
Y
, we get

\Omega(X,Y)=g^{-1}\pi^{*}F(X,Y)g=g^{-1}F(\pi_{*}X,\pi_{*}Y)g=g^{-1}F(\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})g,

(82)

where
\tilde{X}
and
\tilde{Y}
are the projections of
X
and
Y
to
M
, respectively, and we use that
\text{i}_{Y}\text{i}_{X}F=F(X,Y)
. Finally, from equation (
74
), we also have

0=\omega(\tilde{X})=g^{-1}\mathcal{A}(X)g+g^{-1}\text{d}_{P}g(\tilde{X}),

(83)

and

\text{d}_{P}g(\tilde{X})=\text{d}g(X)=-\mathcal{A}(X)g.

(84)

Equation (
84
) corresponds to a differential equation in terms of the horizontally lifted vector field, which plays an important role in the main body of the paper.
