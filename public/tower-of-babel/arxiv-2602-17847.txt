Stabilization of Nonlinear Systems by Gain-Limited Feedback Laws
Bryce Christopherson, Farhad Jafari

Abstract
We study local stabilization of nonlinear control systems under explicit gain constraints on the feedback law. Using a quantitative refinement of Brockett’s openness condition, we introduce the notion of a maximal continuous openness rate for the system vector field near equilibrium. Combining this with a local-section characterization of stabilizability, we derive a general necessary condition for the existence of gain-limited stabilizing feedback. This condition yields sharp no-go results for broad classes of nonlinear systems, including systems that are stabilizable only by nonsmooth feedback. Several examples illustrate how openness rates impose fundamental lower bounds on stabilizing feedback growth near an equilibrium point.

Introduction

In many real-world situations, one is often interested not only in stabilizing a system via feedback, but in stabilizing a system by a
realistically-implementable feedback
. For instance, if a system is theoretically stabilizable but all real-world implementations of a stabilizing control require one to produce, say, a rotor moving at
10^{70}
km/s, then the system is effectively impossible to stabilize. Here, we will focus on the general constraint of a gain limitation on the feedback achieving the desired stability (i.e. a requirement that the control
u
stabilizing the system near equilibrium point
x_{e}
satisfy some kind of a growth condition near
x_{e}
). This problem is quite natural in classical textbook examples of stability: For instance, one might ask whether it is possible to stabilize a pendulum on a cart upright if the cart can only move back and forth slowly. Likewise, a host of real-world examples of this problem can be found: for example, one may wish to know whether it is possible to design a thermostat controller capable of stabilizing the temperature in residential setting to a comfortable one using a furnace system with a fixed maximum heat output (e.g., 100K BTU/h).

More precisely, we will address the following general question:

Question 1

(Gain-Limited Stabilization Problem)
.

Given a control system of the form

\displaystyle\overset{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{0.45}{$\bullet$}}}{x}=f(x,u),

\displaystyle\enskip t\geq 0.

(1)

that is locally asymptotically stabilizable at the equilibrium point
x_{e}
and a nonnegative function
d
defined on a neighborhood of
x_{e}
, when does a feedback controller
u(x)
exist such that
u(x)
locally asymptotically stabilizes (
1
) at
x_{e}
and satisfies

\|u(x)\|\leq d(x)

(2)

for all
\|x_{e}-x\|
sufficiently small?

Notice,
1
is simple in the case of
d(x)
a constant function and
u
continuous. That is, it is always possible: If we assume
u(x)
is continuous and stationary and that (
1
) can be stabilized at
x_{e}
, then we can stabilize the system with a control of arbitrarily small norm on a sufficiently small ball around
x_{e}
. In this case, what is usually studied is the trade off between the size of this ball and the norm bound on the control. While interesting in its own right, this formulation of the problem fails to capture the fine-grained details regarding the growth rate of the control near the equilibrium, which is often still of practical interest. Additionally, the problem in this tradeoff is ‘non-local’ in the sense that it relies on characterizations of the system’s trajectories behavior away from the equilibrium point. Non-constant limiting functions
d
, however, are the interesting case and are at the heart of many real-world problems.

In this paper, we introduce a quantitative refinement of Brockett’s openness condition via a maximal continuous openness rate. We derive necessary inequalities linking openness rates to admissible feedback growth, and obtain sharp lower bounds on gain decay for systems stabilizable only by non-smooth feedback. We also identify exponent thresholds linking system nonlinearity and control gain decay. For clarity, we restrict ourselves here to feedbacks yielding a continuous closed-loop vector field with unique forward solutions; this aligns with; e.g., Coron and Zabczyk
7
;
23
, but avoids Filippov or Krasovskii notions of solutions. Our obstructions therefore apply to continuous stabilizers in the classical sense.

3
The Approach

The key idea of our approach is to quantify the ‘rate of openness’ near the equilibrium point of a stabilizable system and use this in conjunction with a characterization of local asymptotic stabilizability in terms of local sections
6
. To be more specific, the idea goes back to a necessary condition of Brockett
3
, which says that the vector field inducing the system must satisfy a certain ‘local openness’ property at the equilibrium if such a controller exists, the definition of which we give now:

Definition 3

(Locally Open)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be topological spaces. A mapping
f\colon X\rightarrow Y
is said to be
open at
x\in X
if we have
f(x)\in\textrm{int}f(\mathcal{O})
for any neighborhood
\mathcal{O}
of
x
.

As it is well known, the origin of this property goes back to the classical Banach-Schauder open mapping theorem of functional analysis, though it should be noted that the class of
open mappings
is a more restrictive one than those that are simply open at a point. Using this property, we can state Brockett’s theorem, as refined by Coron
7
and Zabczyk
23
, in the following form:

Theorem 4

(Brockett’s Theorem - Continuous Extension)
.

If the system (
1
) is locally asymptotically stabilizable by means of continuous feedback laws (
Definition

1
) satisfying
2
, then it is necessary that the mapping
f
is open at the equilibrium point (
Definition

3
).

While a mapping may have the openness property at a given point, this property alone does not admit any measuring device to quantify the rate at which the mapping is open about that point. Some effort has been done to use the variational-analytic property of
linear openness
(known also as

and as

, see, e.g.,
17

20

6
;
5
. The idea of linear openness is to get some rough measurement tool for ‘how open’ a mapping is at a point.

Definition 4
.

A mapping
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be linearly open around
\bar{z}
with modulus
\kappa>0
if there exists a neighborhood
\mathcal{O}
of
\bar{z}
such that

\mathbb{B}_{\kappa r}\big(f(z)\big)\subseteq f\big(\mathbb{B}_{r}(z)\big)\quad\textrm{for any}\enskip z\in\mathcal{O}\enskip\textrm{and}\enskip r>0\enskip\textrm{with}\enskip\mathbb{B}_{r}(z)\subseteq\mathcal{O}.

Furthermore, we define the supremum over all the moduli
\kappa
for which
f
is linearly open around
\bar{z}
to be the linear openness/covering bound of

\bar{z}
and denote it by

is not linearly open around
\bar{z}

Linear openness first appeared in

arose later on

;
20
;
13

Here, we will try to make this device more refined. Specifically, to quantify the rate of local openness that Brockett’s theorem prescribes must occur, we introduce the following definition:

Definition 5

Let
X
,
Y
be metric spaces and let

is continuously open at

This definition allows us to measure how rapidly the image of a shrinking ball must expand, which we will show directly determines ‘how much’ control is required to stabilize the system.

Norm Estimates via Openness Rates

To begin, we start by proving a few facts about continuous openness (
Definition

5
), which will be one of the main tools we use to analyze
1
. As suggested by
Example

1
, we adopt the following notation:

Definition 6
.

Let
X
be a normed space,
x^{*}\in X
, and suppose
K
is a compact neighborhood of
x^{*}
. We say the
inscribed radius
(or
inradius
)
of
K
at
x^{*}
is the quantity

\Gamma_{x^{*}}(K)=\sup\{r\geq 0:\mathbb{B}_{r}(x^{*})\subseteq K\}.

As a side note, observe that, via F. Riesz’s theorem, if
K
is a compact neighborhood of
x^{*}
and
X
is a Hausdorff topological vector space, then necessarily
X
must be finite dimensional. So, the notion of
Definition

6
implicitly assumes a finite dimensional context.

Now, we will use this notion to show that continuous openness at a point and openness at a point are, in fact, equivalent for continuous functions. The proof of this introduces a maximal rate function that will play a heavy role later.

Theorem 5
.

Let
X
,
Y
be finite dimensional normed vector spaces and suppose
f:X\rightarrow Y
is continuous. Then,
f
is open at
x^{*}\in X
if and only if
f
is continuously open at
x^{*}
. Moreover,
f
is open at a rate of

g(r)=\Gamma_{f(x^{*})}\big(f\big(\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{r}(x^{*})\big)\big)

(3)

and this rate is maximal among all rates at which
f
is continuously open at
x^{*}
; i.e. if
h
is any other function such that
f
is open at
x^{*}
at a rate of
h
, then
h(r)\leq g(r)
near
x^{*}
.

Proof.

The reverse direction is immediate, so let us assume that
f
is open at
x^{*}\in X
. Define
g(r)
as above. Since
f
is continuous and
\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{r}(x^{*})
is compact when
X
is finite dimensional,
K_{r}:=f\big(\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{r}(x^{*})\big)
is compact as well and
g(r)
is well defined (since
g(r)=\sup\{\rho\geq 0:\mathbb{B}_{\rho}(x^{*})\subseteq K_{r}\}=\inf\{\|y-f(x^{*})\|:y\in Y\setminus K_{r}\}
; i.e., the infimum of the distance from a point to the closed subset
Y\setminus K_{r}
of the finite-dimensional normed space
Y
is attained by some
y_{0}\in Y\setminus K_{r}
). As
f
is open at
x^{*}
,
K_{r}
is a neighborhood of
f(x^{*})
for
r>0
. Hence, it contains some ball, so
g(r)>0
for
r>0
. So,
g(r)
is positive definite (that
g(0)=0
is apparent from
Definition

6
).

For monotonicity, observe that for
0<r_{1}<r_{2}
sufficiently small,
\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{r_{1}}(x^{*})\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{r_{2}}(x^{*})
, so
K_{r_{1}}=f\big(\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{r_{1}}(x^{*})\big)\subseteq f\big(\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{r_{2}}(x^{*})\big)=K_{r_{2}}
. So, any ball about
f(x^{*})
contained in
K_{r_{1}}
must also be contained in
K_{r_{2}}
and

g(r_{1})=\Gamma_{f(x^{*})}(K_{r_{1}})\leq\Gamma_{f(x^{*})}(K_{r_{2}})=g(r_{2}).

That is,
g
is monotonically increasing.

Finally, for the continuity of
g
, observe that
f
is uniformly continuous on some fixed ball
\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{R}(x^{*})
. As
\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{r}
varies continuously with respect to
r
in the Hausdorff metric, so do their images under
f
; i.e.
r\mapsto K_{r}
is continuous. As
K\mapsto\Gamma_{f(x^{*})}(K)=\inf\{\|y-f(x^{*})\|:y\in Y\setminus K\}
is continuous with respect to the Hausdorff metric on compact sets
K
,
r\mapsto\Gamma_{f(x^{*})}(K_{r})=g(r)
is continuous as well.

Now, if
h
is any other function such that
f
is continuously open at
x^{*}
at a rate of
h
, then
\mathbb{B}_{h(r)}\big(f(x^{*})\big)\subseteq f\big(\mathbb{B}_{r}(x^{*})\big)
. So,
h(r)\leq\sup\{\rho>0\ |\ \mathbb{B}_{\rho}\subseteq f\big(\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{r}(x^{*}))\}
; i.e.,
h(r)\leq\Gamma_{f(x^{*})}(f(\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{r}(x^{*})))=g(r)
for all
r
sufficiently small. Hence,
g
is indeed maximal among all rate functions for which
f
is continuously open at
x^{*}
.
∎

Using our tool to quantify the rate of openness in Brockett’s theorem (i.e.
Theorem

4
and

), we can estimate the norm of
f
on compact neighborhoods of equilibrium points.

Lemma 6
.

Let
X
,
Y
be normed vector spaces and suppose
f:X\rightarrow Y
is continuously open at
x^{*}\in X
at a rate of
g
. Then, if
K
is a compact neighborhood of
x^{*}
,
\|\left.\kern-1.2ptf\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{K}\|\geq\|f(x^{*})\|+g(\Gamma_{x^{*}}(K))
.

Proof.

By definition,
\mathbb{B}_{g(r)}\big(f(x^{*})\big)\subseteq f(\mathbb{B}_{r}(x^{*}))
. Then,
\mathbb{B}_{\Gamma_{x^{*}}(K)}(x^{*})\subseteq K
and

\mathbb{B}_{g(\Gamma_{x^{*}}(K))}\big(f(x^{*})\big)\subseteq f(\mathbb{B}_{\Gamma_{x^{*}}(K)}(x^{*}))\subseteq f(K).

So,
\|\left.\kern-1.2ptf\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{K}\|=\sup_{x\in K}\|f(x)\|\geq\|f(x^{*})\|+g(\Gamma_{x^{*}}(K))
.
∎

Corollary 7

Let

. Then,

\|\left.\kern-1.2ptf\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{K}\|\geq\|f(x^{*})\|+\Gamma_{f(x^{*})}\big(f\big(\mathbb{B}_{\Gamma_{x^{*}}(K)}(x^{*})\big)\big)

While

looks somewhat opaque and the reader might suspect that it may not offer practically accessible information, it turns out to actually be quite usable. Here are some examples.

Example 2
.

Consider
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by
f(x)=2x+3
. Set
x^{*}=1
and take

. Then,

is a compact neighborhood of

is continuously open at

\|\left.\kern-1.2ptf\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{K}\|\geq\|f(x^{*})\|+\Gamma_{f(x^{*})}\big(f\big(\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{\Gamma_{x^{*}}(K)}(x^{*})\big)\big)

by

.

To verify this, observe that
\|\left.\kern-1.2ptf\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{K}\|=\sup_{x\in[0,2]}|2x+3|=7
. Likewise, since
f(x^{*})=f(1)=2(1)+3=5

Thus,

\|\left.\kern-1.2ptf\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{K}\|=7\geq 5+2=\|f(x^{*})\|+\Gamma_{f(x^{*})}\big(f\big(\mathbb{B}_{\Gamma_{x^{*}}(K)}(x^{*})\big)\big)

Example 3

Consider

,

and take

\Gamma_{x^{*}}(K)=\Gamma_{0}\big(\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{1}(0)\big)=1
. So,

\Gamma_{f(x^{*})}\big(f\big(\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{\Gamma_{x^{*}}(K)}(x^{*})\big)\big)=\Gamma_{b}\big(f(\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{1}(0))=\Gamma_{b}\big(A\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{1}(0)+b\big)=\Gamma_{0}\big(A\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{1}(0)\big)=\sigma_{\min}(A)

where
\sigma_{\min}(A)=1
is the smallest singular value of
A
.

Now, since
f
is a homeomorphism, it is certainly open at
x^{*}
. So, by
Corollary

7
, we have

\|\left.\kern-1.2ptf\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{K}\|\geq\|f(x^{*})\|+\Gamma_{f(x^{*})}\big(f\big(\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{\Gamma_{x^{*}}(K)}(x^{*})\big)\big)=5+1=6.

To see that this is indeed true, observe that
z=\begin{bmatrix}3/10\\
4/5\end{bmatrix}\in\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{1}(0)=K

\|f(z)\|=\left\|\begin{bmatrix}18/5\\

So,
\|\left.\kern-1.2ptf\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{K}\|\geq 6
is certainly true.

As a final corollary, we get the following estimate in the other direction as well. For this, we need a generalized inverse of the maximal rate function which, we note, may not be locally invertible near zero even though it is monotonically increasing and positive definite (consider a Cantor-type plateau set around zero).

Corollary 8
.

Let
X,Y
be finite-dimensional normed spaces and suppose
f:X\to Y
is continuously open at
x^{*}\in X
with maximal rate
g(r)
as in (
3
). Define the right-continuous generalized inverse

g^{\leftarrow}(s):=\inf\{r>0:\ g(r)\geq s\}.

Then for any
S\subset X

\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{s}(f(x^{*}))\subset f(S)
, one has

\sup_{x\in S}\|x-x^{*}\|\;\geq\;g^{\leftarrow}(s).

Proof.

Let
X,Y
be normed vector spaces, let
x^{*}\in X
, and set
y^{*}:=f(x^{*})
. Assume
f
is continuously open at
x^{*}
. Let
S\subset X
satisfy
\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{s}(y^{*})\subset f(S)
. Then,

s\leq\Gamma_{y^{*}}\big(f(S)\big)\leq\Gamma_{y^{*}}\big(f(\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{\sup_{x\in S}\|x-x^{*}\|}(y^{*}))\big)=g\left(\sup_{x\in S}\|x-x^{*}\|\right).

So,
s\leq g\big(\sup_{x\in S}\|x-x^{*}\|\big)
and, by the definition of
g^{\leftarrow}
, we get
g^{\leftarrow}(s)\leq\sup_{x\in S}\|x-x^{*}\|
as desired.
∎

Local Section Domain Conditions

Using
Theorem

3
with
Corollary

8
, we get an easy necessary condition for
1
to be answered in the affirmative.

Theorem 9
.

Suppose the control system (
1
) is locally asymptotically stabilizable and
u
is a stabilizing controller satisfying (
2
). Then, writing
g(r):=\Gamma_{0}\big(f(\mathbb{B}_{r}(0,0))\big)
and
h(r):=\|f\big(\cdot,u(\cdot)\big)^{-1}\|_{\mathbb{B}_{r}(0)}
, it is necessary that

r\leq g\left(\sqrt{\|d\|_{\mathbb{B}_{h(r)}(0)}^{2}+\big(h(r)\big)^{2}}\right)\textrm{ for all sufficiently small }r.

(4)

Proof.

Suppose
u(x)
is a stabilizing control. Then, by
Theorem

3
,
we know it is of the form
u(x)=(\alpha_{2}\circ\alpha_{1}^{-1})(x)
for some local right inverse
\alpha
of
f
such that
\overset{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{0.45}{$\bullet$}}}{x}=\alpha_{1}^{-1}(x)
has the origin as a locally asymptotically stable equilibrium. That is, for some compact neighborhood
K
of
(0,0)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}
and continuous function
\alpha:f(K)\rightarrow K
satisfying
f(\alpha(x))=x
for all
x\in f(K)
and such that
\overset{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{0.45}{$\bullet$}}}{x}=\alpha_{1}^{-1}(x)
has the origin as a locally asymptotically stable equilibrium, where
\alpha(x)=\big(\alpha_{1}(x),\alpha_{2}(x)\big)
with
\alpha_{1}:f(K)\rightarrow\textrm{proj}_{1}(K)
and
\alpha_{2}:f(K)\rightarrow\textrm{proj}_{2}(K)
. So,
\alpha_{2}\circ\alpha_{1}^{-1}:\textrm{proj}_{1}(K)\rightarrow\textrm{proj}_{2}(K)
.

Now, take
r>0
sufficiently small so that
\mathbb{B}_{r}(0)\subseteq f(K)
and let
g(r)=\Gamma_{0}\big(f\big(\mathbb{B}_{r}(0,0)\big)\big)
denote the maximal rate of openness of
f
at
(0,0)
. Using
Corollary

8
with
S=\alpha\big(\mathbb{B}_{r}(0)\big)
for some sufficiently small
r
, since
f\big(\alpha\big(\mathbb{B}_{r}(0)\big)\big)=\mathbb{B}_{r}(0)
, we have

\sup_{z\in\alpha\big(\mathbb{B}_{r}(0)\big)}\|z\|\geq g^{\leftarrow}(r).

That is,

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{B}_{r}(0)}\sqrt{\|\alpha_{1}(x)\|^{2}+\|\alpha_{2}(x)\|^{2}}\geq g^{\leftarrow}(r).

Writing
h(r)=\|\alpha_{1}\|_{\mathbb{B}_{r}(0)}
, we get the following:

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{B}_{r}(0)}\sqrt{\|\alpha_{1}(x)\|^{2}+\|(u\circ\alpha_{1})(x)\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq g^{\leftarrow}(r)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathbb{B}_{h(r)}(0)}^{2}\geq\sup_{x\in\alpha_{1}(\mathbb{B}_{r}(0))}\|u(x)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\big(g^{\leftarrow}(r)\big)^{2}-\big(h(r)\big)^{2}

Hence, via our assumption that (
2
) holds, it is necessary that

\sqrt{\big(g^{\leftarrow}(r)\big)^{2}-\big(h(r)\big)^{2}}\leq\|d\|_{\mathbb{B}_{h(r)}(0)}

for all sufficiently small
r
. The desired result comes from then noting that
\alpha_{1}(x)=F_{u}^{-1}(x)
for
F_{u}(x):=f(x,u(x))
, isolating
g^{\leftarrow}(r)
to produce

s:=\sqrt{\|d\|_{\mathbb{B}_{h(r)}(0)}^{2}+h(r)^{2}}\geq g^{\leftarrow}(r)

and noting that if
s\geq g^{\leftarrow}(r)
, then
g(s)\geq r
.
∎

Example 4
.

Consider the unicycle
\overset{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{0.45}{$\bullet$}}}{x}=u_{1}\cos(\theta)
,
\overset{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{0.45}{$\bullet$}}}{y}=u_{1}\sin(\theta)
,
\overset{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{0.45}{$\bullet$}}}{\theta}=u_{2}
. This is a classic example of a nonholonomic system that fails to have a continuous stabilizing feedback. To see this fact using the machinery of
Theorem

9
, observe that
g(r)=0
(which is precisely the obstruction presented by Brockett’s theorem), so it is impossible to have
r\leq g\left(\sqrt{\|d\|_{\overline{\mathbb{B}}{h(r)}(0)}^{2}+\big(h(r)\big)^{2}}\right)
for any
d
. That is, when
g=0
,
Theorem

9
recovers Brockett’s condition (albeit in an obvious way); while in the case where
g>0
,
Theorem

9
yields a quantitative refinement on it.

To contrast
Example

4
, if
g
is positive, the inequality (
4
) cannot fail for all admissible bounds
d
without restricting the class of
d
; indeed, choosing
d
sufficiently large makes the condition of
Theorem

9
vacuous. The useful content comes from prescribing a gain class (e.g. Hölder/Lipschitz/saturation).

Example 5
.

Let
f(x,u)=x^{3}+u^{3}
. If
d(x)=\kappa\|x\|
for some
\kappa>0
, then there is no locally stabilizing feedback
u(x)
such that
F_{u}(x):=f(x,u(x))
is locally bi-Lipschitz and satisfies
1
for any neighborhood
D
of the origin. That is, all stabilizing controllers
u(x)
such that
F_{u}
is bi-Lipschitz (for instance, linear feedbacks,
C^{1}
exponentially stabilizing feedbacks, etc.) grow faster than
\kappa\|x\|
near the origin for any
\kappa>0
.

To see this, suppose
u(x)
is a stabilizing feedback such that
F_{u}
is locally bi-Lipschitz. Then, for all sufficiently small
r
,
h(r)=\|F_{u}^{-1}\|_{\mathbb{B}_{r}(0)}\leq Lr
for
L
the reciprocal of the lower Lipschitz constant of
F_{u}
. Likewise,
\|d\|_{\mathbb{B}_{h(r)}(0)}=\kappa h(r)\leq\kappa Lr
. So, noting that
g(r):=\Gamma_{0}(f(\mathbb{B}_{r}(0,0)))\leq 2r^{3}
, the necessary condition of
Theorem

9
becomes

r\leq g\left(\sqrt{\|d\|_{\mathbb{B}_{h(r)}(0)}^{2}+\big(h(r)\big)^{2}}\right)\leq 2L^{3}r^{3}(\kappa^{2}+1)^{3/2}.

But this says that
\frac{1}{2L^{3}(\kappa^{2}+1)^{3/2}}\leq r^{2}
, which is false for all
r<\sqrt{\frac{1}{2L^{3}(\kappa^{2}+1)^{3/2}}}
.

Example 6
.

Consider the system
\overset{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{0.45}{$\bullet$}}}{x}=f(x,u)
where
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
is defined by

f(x,u):=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{2}\\
x_{1}x_{2}+x_{2}^{2}+u^{3}\end{bmatrix}.

This system is
not
exponentially stabilizable by
C^{1}
feedback laws, but it
is
exponentially stabilizable by merely continuous feedback laws. For example, the (continuous) feedback

u(x)=\sqrt[3]{-2x_{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}x_{1}-x_{1}x_{2}-x_{2}^{2}}

yields

\overset{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{0.45}{$\bullet$}}}{x}_{2}=x_{1}x_{2}+x_{2}^{2}+u(x)^{3}=-\tfrac{1}{2}x_{1}-2x_{2},

and hence the closed-loop linearization at
0
is

\left.\kern-1.2ptJ_{F_{u}}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-\tfrac{1}{2}&-2\end{bmatrix},

whose eigenvalues are
-1\pm\sqrt{1/2}
, both strictly negative. In particular the origin is locally exponentially stable for this (non-
C^{1}
) feedback.

Now suppose we ask whether there exists a stabilizing feedback satisfying
1
for

d(x)=\kappa\|x\|^{\alpha},\qquad\kappa>0,\ \alpha>0,

within the class of exponentially stabilizing feedbacks (therefore rending the closed-loop map
F_{u}
continuously differentiable and, in particular, bi-Lipschitz, since its linearization must be Hurwitz).
Write

h(r):=\|F_{u}^{-1}\|_{\mathbb{B}_{r}(0)}.

If
F_{u}
is locally bi-Lipschitz with lower Lipschitz constant
m>0
, then
F_{u}^{-1}
is Lipschitz with
constant
1/m
, and hence for all sufficiently small
r
,

h(r)\leq Lr,\qquad L:=\frac{1}{m}.

(5)

Moreover,

\|d\|_{\mathbb{B}_{h(r)}(0)}=\kappa\,h(r)^{\alpha}.

Next, note that near the origin the second component of
f
is limited by
|u|^{3}
, and therefore
the maximal openness rate of
f
at
(0,0)
satisfies, for all sufficiently small
\rho
,

g(\rho)=\Gamma_{0}\big(f(\mathbb{B}_{\rho}(0,0))\big)\ \leq\ C\,\rho^{3}

(6)

for some constant
C>0
depending only on the chosen norm on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}

(e.g.
C=1
for the max norm and
C=2
for a simple Euclidean estimate).

Hence the necessary condition of
Theorem

9
implies, for all sufficiently small
r
with

h(r)\leq\Gamma_{0}(D)
,

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\sqrt{\kappa^{2}h(r)^{2\alpha}+h(r)^{2}}\right)^{3}=C\left(\kappa^{2}h(r)^{2\alpha}+h(r)^{2}\right)^{3/2}.

Using (
5
) gives

r\leq C\left(\kappa^{2}(Lr)^{2\alpha}+(Lr)^{2}\right)^{3/2}.

For small
r
, the dominant term inside the parentheses is
(Lr)^{2\min\{\alpha,1\}}
, so the right-hand side
is
O\!\left(r^{3\min\{\alpha,1\}}\right)
. In particular, if
\alpha>1/3
, then

3\min\{\alpha,1\}>1
and the inequality forces

r\leq C^{\prime}r^{p}\qquad\text{with }p>1,

which is impossible for all sufficiently small
r>0
. Thus, if
\alpha>1/3
, then there is no locally exponentially stabilizing feedback
u
such that
F_{u}
is locally bi-Lipschitz
and
\|u(x)\|\leq\kappa\|x\|^{\alpha}
near the origin (for any
\kappa>0
).

At the threshold
\alpha=1/3
, the necessary condition reduces (for small
r
) to a lower bound on
\kappa

depending on
C
and
L
; and for
\alpha<1/3
the estimate does not yield an obstruction.

In practice, the inradius may be challenging to compute, but bounding
\|f\|
may be more straightforward. To this end, we get the following corollary by applying
Lemma

6
.

Corollary 10
.

Under the assumptions of
Theorem

9
, the maximal openness rate satisfies

g(r)\leq\|\left.\kern-1.2ptf\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{r}(0,0)}\|

for all
r>0
. Consequently, a necessary condition for the existence of a stabilizing feedback
u(x)
satisfying
\|u(x)\|\leq d(x)
near the origin is

r\leq\|\left.\kern-1.2ptf\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{{\sqrt{\|d\|_{\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{h(r)}(0)}^{2}+h(r)^{2}}}}(0,0)}\|

for all sufficiently small
r>0
.

Proof.

By
Corollary

7
applied to
K_{r}=\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{r}(0,0)
, we have for each small
r
that

\|\left.\kern-1.2ptf\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{K_{r}}\|\geq\Gamma_{0}\big(f\big(\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{\Gamma_{(0,0)}(K_{r})}(0,0)\big)\big)=\Gamma_{0}\big(f\big(\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{r}(0,0)\big)\big)=g(r),

yielding the upper bound on

. Evaluating both at the argument of

in the necessary condition (
4
) of
Theorem

gives the result.
∎

Remark 1
.

This condition may be weaker than (
4
) since
g(r)\leq\|\left.\kern-1.2ptf\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{r}(0,0)}\|
(with equality only when

is a ball centered at

of radius
\|\left.\kern-1.2ptf\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{r}(0,0)}\|
), but it is useful when the supremum norm of

.

Example 7
.

Consider
f(x,u)=x^{3}+u^{3}
as before, where
g(r)=2r^{3}
. Here,
\|\left.\kern-1.2ptf\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{r}(0,0)}\|=2r^{3}
, so the bound is tight. If
d(x)=\kappa\|x\|
and
F_{u}
is bi-Lipschitz with lower constant

, then

and the condition becomes
r\leq 2\left(\sqrt{\kappa^{2}(Lr)^{2}+(Lr)^{2}}\right)^{3}=2L^{3}r^{3}(\kappa^{2}+1)^{3/2}
, leading to the same contradiction for small

as in the original example.

Theorem 11
.

Under the assumptions of
Theorem

9
, suppose
\|f(x,u)\|\leq\phi(\|(x,u)\|)
near the origin for a continuous, non-decreasing
\phi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
with
\phi(0)=0
. Then necessarily

r\leq\phi\!\left(\sqrt{\|d\|_{\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{h(r)}(0)}^{2}+h(r)^{2}}\right)

for small
r>0
.

Moreover, if
\phi(s)\leq Ms^{\alpha}
near
0
for
M>0
,
\alpha>1
; if
F_{u}
is bi-Lipschitz with constant
L>0
(e.g., exponential stabilizers); and if
d(x)\leq k\|x\|^{\beta}
for
k>0
,
\beta>0
; then
\beta\leq 1/\alpha
is required.

Proof.

Since
f(\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{r}(0,0))\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{\phi(r)}(0)
, we have
g(r)\leq\phi(r)
(by the definition of
g
and the inradius therein). Substituting into (
4
) yields the first claim.

For the second,
h(r)\leq Lr
implies
\|d\|_{\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{h(r)}(0)}\leq k(Lr)^{\beta}
. Then
\sqrt{\|d\|^{2}+h^{2}}\leq Lr\sqrt{k^{2}(Lr)^{2(\beta-1)}+1}\sim kL^{\beta}r^{\beta}
for small
r
if
\beta<1
(if
\beta\geq 1
, then
H(r)\sim r
and we reach the obstruction
r\leq M(Lr)^{\alpha}
, which is already a contradiction for small
r
when
\alpha>1
). Thus
r\leq M(kL^{\beta}r^{\beta})^{\alpha}=Mk^{\alpha}L^{\alpha\beta}r^{\alpha\beta}
, or
1\leq Mk^{\alpha}L^{\alpha\beta}r^{\alpha\beta-1}
. For
\beta>1/\alpha
,
\alpha\beta-1>0
and the right side approaches
0
as
r\to 0^{+}
, yielding a contradiction for small
r
.
∎

Remark 2
.

This threshold
\beta\leq 1/\alpha
reveals a fundamental trade-off: steeper nonlinearities (increases in
\alpha
) demand gentler gain decay (decreases in
\beta
), amplifying the control near the equilibrium. This is tight for homogeneous systems where images approximate balls.

Example 8
.

In
Example

6
, the cubic
u
-term gives
\phi(s)\leq Cs^{3}
(i.e.
\alpha=3
), mandating
\beta\leq 1/3
. Compactly, gains vanishing too swiftly doom stabilization.

It is worth noting that the requirement (
4
) of
Theorem

9
can, in fact, be strengthened. This requires that we relate the maximal rate of openness of
F_{u}(x):=f\big(x,u(x)\big)
to
f(x,u)
. Unfortunately, this means we must estimate the maximal rate of openness of
F_{u}
as well, which may be difficult in practice.

Theorem 12
.

Suppose the control system (
1
) is locally asymptotically stabilizable and
u
is a stabilizing controller satisfying (
2
). Then, writing
F_{u}(x):=f(x,u(x))
,
g_{f}(r):=\Gamma_{0}\big(f(\mathbb{B}_{r}(0,0))\big)
,
g_{F_{u}}(r):=\Gamma_{0}\big(F_{u}(\mathbb{B}_{r}(0))\big)
, and
h(r):=\|f\big(\cdot,u(\cdot)\big)^{-1}\|_{\mathbb{B}_{r}(0)}
, it is necessary that

g_{F_{u}}\big(h(r)\big)\leq g_{f}\left(\sqrt{\|d\|_{\mathbb{B}_{h(r)}}^{2}+\big(h(r)\big)^{2}}\right)\textrm{ for all sufficiently small }r

(7)

Moreover,
g_{F_{u}}\big(h(r)\big)\geq r
, so this condition is at least as strong as the condition (
4
) of
Theorem

9
.

Proof.

Write
G_{u}(x):=\big(x,u(x)\big)
and
F_{u}(x):=(f\circ G_{u})(x)=f\big(x,u(x)\big)
. Then,
\|G_{u}\|_{\mathbb{B}_{r}(0)}=\sup_{\|x\|\leq r}\sqrt{\|x\|^{2}+\|u(x)\|^{2}}
, so if
\|u(x)\|\leq d(x)
for all
x
sufficiently small, this yields
\|G_{u}\|_{\mathbb{B}_{r}(0)}\leq\sqrt{r^{2}+\|d\|_{\mathbb{B}_{r}}^{2}}
. Let us write
\rho(r)=\sqrt{r^{2}+\|d\|_{\mathbb{B}_{r}}^{2}}
so the above becomes
\|G_{u}\|_{\mathbb{B}_{r}(0)}\leq\rho(r)
.

Now, write
g_{f}
and
g_{F_{u}}
for the maximal openness rates of
f
and
F_{u}
, respectively; i.e.,

g_{f}(r)=\Gamma_{0}\big(f(\mathbb{B}_{r}(0,0))\big),\quad g_{F_{u}}(r)=\Gamma_{0}\big(F_{u}(\mathbb{B}_{r}(0))\big).

Then, the inclusion
G_{u}(\mathbb{B}_{r}(0))\subseteq\mathbb{B}_{\rho(r)}(0,0)
yields
F_{u}\big(\mathbb{B}_{r}(0)\big)=f\big(G_{u}(\mathbb{B}_{r}(0))\big)\subseteq f(\mathbb{B}_{\rho(r)}(0,0))
. Taking inradii, this yields the basic inequality
g_{F_{u}}(r)\leq g_{f}\big(\rho(r)\big)
. Note that the necessary condition (
4
) can be stated, in the language used here, as
g_{f}\big(\rho\big(h(r)\big)\big)\geq r
. Hence, replacing
r
with
h(r)
in the inequality
g_{F_{u}}(r)\leq g_{f}\big(\rho(r)\big)
, we obtain the necessary condition (
7
).

Now, we show that this condition is properly stronger. Since
F_{u}
is open at the origin whenever
u
is a stabilizing control, and since
u=\alpha_{2}\circ\alpha_{1}^{-1}
for some locally defined continuous right inverse
\alpha
of
f
such that
\alpha_{1}
is a homeomorphism, we have
F_{u}(\alpha_{1}(x))=f(\alpha(x))=x
for all sufficiently small
x
. Accordingly,
F_{u}\big(\alpha_{1}(\mathbb{B}_{r}(0))\big)=\mathbb{B}_{r}(0)
, so
Corollary

8
yields

h(r)=\|F_{u}^{-1}\|_{\mathbb{B}_{r}(0)}=\|\alpha_{1}\|_{\mathbb{B}_{r}(0)}\geq g_{F_{u}}^{\leftarrow}(r).

That is,
g_{F_{u}}\big(h(r)\big)\geq r
. So, the condition (
7
) implies the condition (
4
).
∎

As a final note, one may be unenthused by the requirement that
h(r)
be known (or easily estimable) to apply
Theorem

9
or
Theorem

12
. Unfortunately, there is no real hope of fixing this, as any weakening of the requirements (
4
) or (
7
) would require an upper bound on
h
, which can be arbitrarily large. That is, certainly the closed-loop map
f(x,u(x))
must be open at the origin, but it may collapse the radii of balls extremely quickly, leading to an extremely fast-growing inverse in norm.

One may hope that this could be remedied by placing some
lower bound
condition on the growth rate of the stabilizing control
u
(i.e. one of the form
d_{1}(x)\leq\|u(x)\|\leq d_{2}(x)
for all sufficiently small
\|x\|
, a direct counterpart to (
2
)). This even seems desirable in practice, since one may not be able to produce a controller capable of arbitrarily fine and precise adjustments. However, it is still not enough. That is, even under a seemingly practical two-sided gain restriction, the inverse-growth function
h(r)
can still be made arbitrarily large, so no control-independent upper bound on
h(r)
can exist without additional hypotheses (e.g. a quantitative lower openness bound for
F_{u}
independent of
u
, or a bi-Hölder/bi-Lipschitz condition). This is formalized in Theorem
13
.

Theorem 13
.

There is, in general, no continuous function
H:[0,\varepsilon)\to[0,\infty)
satisfying
h(0)=0
and depending only on the open-loop system
f
and on two-sided magnitude bounds
d_{1},d_{2}
such that every stabilizing feedback
u
satisfying

d_{1}(x)\leq\|u(x)\|\leq d_{2}(x)\qquad\text{for all sufficiently small }\|x\|

must also satisfy

h(r):=\|F_{u}^{-1}\|_{\mathbb{B}_{r}(0)}\leq H(r)\qquad\text{for all sufficiently small }r.

Proof.

To show this, we consider the simple one-dimensional control-affine system

\overset{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{0.45}{$\bullet$}}}{x}=f(x,u)=x+u.

(8)

We will demonstrate that there exist constants
c,C>0
such that for every prescribed rate
H
with
H(r)\to 0
as
r\downarrow 0
, one can find a locally asymptotically stabilizing feedback
u
for (
8
) satisfying
c\|x\|\leq\|u(x)\|\leq C\|x\|
for all
\|x\|
sufficiently small, but with
h(r)>H(r)
for some
r
in any interval
(0,\epsilon)
(where the upper bound of this interval depends on the control
u
).

For each odd integer
p\geq 3
, define the feedback
u_{p}(x):=-x-x^{p}
. Then the closed-loop map is
F_{u_{p}}(x)=f(x,u_{p}(x))=x+u_{p}(x)=-x^{p}
, and the closed-loop dynamics are
\overset{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{0.45}{$\bullet$}}}{x}=-x^{p}
, which has the origin as a (locally, indeed globally) asymptotically stable equilibrium with unique forward solutions.

For
\|x\|\leq\frac{1}{2}
and any
p\geq 3
we have
\|x\|^{p}\leq\|x\|^{3}\leq\frac{1}{4}\|x\|
. Hence

\|u_{p}(x)\|=\|x+x^{p}\|\leq\|x\|+\|x\|^{p}\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{4}\right)\|x\|=\frac{5}{4}\|x\|

and also

\|u_{p}(x)\|=\|x+x^{p}\|\geq\|x\|-\|x\|^{p}\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{4}\right)\|x\|=\frac{3}{4}\|x\|.

Thus for all
\|x\|\leq\frac{1}{2}
,

d_{1}(x)=\frac{3}{4}\|x\|\ \leq\|u_{p}(x)\|\leq\frac{5}{4}\|x\|=d_{2}(x),

with constants independent of
p
.

Finally, we compute
h
. Since
F_{u_{p}}(x)=-x^{p}
, its inverse is
F_{u_{p}}^{-1}(y)=-(|y|)^{1/p}\,\mathrm{sgn}(y)
, and therefore

h_{p}(r):=\|F_{u_{p}}^{-1}\|_{\mathbb{B}_{r}(0)}=\sup_{\|y\|\leq r}\|F_{u_{p}}^{-1}(y)\|=r^{1/p}.

Now, let
H:[0,\varepsilon)\to[0,\infty)
be any continuous function with
H(0)=0
. Since
H(r)\to 0
as
r\downarrow 0
, there exists
\delta\in(0,\varepsilon)
such that
0<r<\delta
implies
H(r)<e^{-1}
. So, choose an odd
p\geq 3
large enough so that
r_{p}:=e^{-p}<\min\{\delta,\varepsilon\}
. Then
r_{p}\in(0,\varepsilon)
and, crucially,

h_{p}(r_{p})=r_{p}^{1/p}=(e^{-p})^{1/p}=e^{-1}.

Therefore,
h_{p}(r_{p})=e^{-1}>H(r_{p})
.

Finally, note that
r_{p}^{1/p}=e^{-1}<\frac{1}{2}
, so the relevant inverse-image scale

\|x\|\leq r_{p}^{1/p}
lies inside the neighborhood
\|x\|\leq\frac{1}{2}
where the two-sided bounds
d_{1}(x)\leq\|u_{p}(x)\|\leq d_{2}(x)
hold. Hence each
u_{p}
is an admissible stabilizing feedback obeying the prescribed two-sided magnitude bounds near
0
, yet it violates the inequality
h(r)\leq H(r)
at the (arbitrarily small) radius
r=r_{p}
. Because
r_{p}=e^{-p}\to 0
as
p\to\infty
, this shows that for every

Conclusion

In this work we introduced a quantitative refinement of Brockett’s classical openness condition and used it to analyze stabilization under explicit gain constraints on feedback laws. The key conceptual contribution is the notion of a
maximal continuous openness rate
, which measures how rapidly the image of shrinking neighborhoods under the system vector field must expand. Unlike Brockett’s condition, which is purely topological and binary in nature (open vs. not open), the openness rate provides a metric obstruction: it quantifies the minimum “expansive capability” required of the dynamics in order for stabilization with bounded or slowly growing feedback to be possible. By combining this quantitative openness framework with the local-section characterization of stabilizability, we derived general necessary inequalities linking openness rates to admissible feedback growth. These results show that stabilization is not merely a matter of whether a system is stabilizable, but how strongly the feedback must grow near equilibrium. In particular, we established lower bounds on gain decay and identified exponent thresholds that depend only on the nonlinear structure of the vector field. The examples demonstrate that these bounds are sharp for broad classes of homogeneous and polynomial systems, and that even when stabilization is possible (including via nonsmooth controllers), the openness geometry may force the feedback to grow faster than any prescribed rate. Our results represent a departure from classical bounded-control stabilization results such as those of Sussmann, Sontag and Yang
22
and related work on stabilization with saturated or bounded inputs. Those results address whether stabilization can be achieved under amplitude constraints, often relying on constructive Lyapunov or feedback design techniques. In contrast, the present work provides intrinsic geometric obstructions that apply independently of the feedback construction method. Rather than producing controllers, we characterize when no controller within a given gain class can exist. In this sense, the openness rate plays a role analogous to metric regularity bounds in variational analysis: it determines the “invertibility strength” of the dynamics and thereby sets fundamental limits on achievable feedback regularity and growth.

From a practical perspective, the results formalize an engineering intuition: systems whose vector fields contract neighborhoods too rapidly near equilibrium require increasingly aggressive feedback, which may exceed actuator capabilities or violate smoothness constraints. The openness rate thus provides a tool for predicting when actuator saturation or gain limits will fundamentally preclude stabilization by smooth or slowly growing feedback.

Several directions remain open. A natural next step is to develop sufficient conditions or constructive procedures that attain the lower bounds identified here. Another important avenue is extending the framework to nonsmooth dynamics and differential inclusions, where Filippov solutions and relaxed controls play a role. Finally, exploring connections between openness rates, control Lyapunov functions, and optimal control formulations may yield a unified geometric picture of stabilization limits under resource constraints.
