Leveraging natural language processing artificial intelligence for automated data extraction of ejection fraction and strain from cardiovascular imaging reports
Authors: Sherry-Ann Brown, Mehri BagheriMohamadiPour, Rodney Sparapani, Kristen Osinski, Jun Zhang, Gift Echefu, Ifeoluwa Stowe, Abdulaziz Hamid, Anai Kothari, for the Cardio-Oncology Artificial Intelligence Informatics Precision & Equity (CAIPE) Research Team Investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Study objectiveTo determine the feasibility of using natural language processing (NLP) to extract ejection fraction (EF) and related cardiac imaging parameters for cancer survivors, across multiple imaging modalities, over a 20-year period.DesignRetrospective observational study applying NLP to multi-modality cardiovascular imaging reports.SettingLarge academic medical center with electronic health records spanning 2000–2020.ParticipantsMore than 4000 patients with multi-modality cardiac imaging reports, including echocardiography, nuclear cardiology, and cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR).InterventionsApplication of NLP algorithms to extract EF and related measurements from free-text cardiac imaging reports. Manual review was performed to assess and resolve discrepancies.Main outcome measuresApplicability of NLP for extracting EF across echocardiography, nuclear cardiology, and CMR reports.ResultsNLP successfully extracted EF values across all imaging modalities. The method demonstrated comparable performance in echocardiography, nuclear cardiology, and CMR reports. Discrepancies identified through manual review highlighted the importance of algorithm training to accommodate modality-specific terminology and improve sensitivity.ConclusionNLP offers a scalable approach for extracting EF and related parameters from large volumes of unstructured imaging reports across multiple modalities. While NLP is modality-agnostic, algorithm training and manual review are essential. This approach can facilitate longitudinal analyses of cardiac function in cancer survivors and other patient populations.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease is prevalent in adults at rates of 50% (including hypertension) and 10% (excluding hypertension) [1], resulting in annual direct costs of $220 billion [1]. With over 655,000 deaths per year, heart disease remains the major cause of death in the United States, closely followed by cancer [2], [3]. Indeed, cardiovascular disease is the second leading cause of death among cancer survivors, trailing only cancer recurrence or secondary cancer development [4], [5], [6], [7]. Each year, nearly 2 million new cancer diagnoses and over 600,000 new cancer-related deaths are estimated [8]. Approximately 17 million cancer survivors are in the United States [9], and this number is expected to reach more than 22 million by 2030 [10]. African Americans cancer survivors have higher rates of cardiovascular disease than Caucasians, an inequity related to structural and socioeconomic disparities [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18].

The most frequent cardiovascular imaging modality used in clinical care and research involving cancer survivors is echocardiography. The prevalent echocardiographic parameter compromised in cancer survivors is the ejection fraction (EF), which is a measure of cardiac function. Although echocardiography is the most widely used cardiovascular imaging modality used to assess the EF, myocardial perfusion scans used in nuclear cardiology along with cardiac magnetic resonance imaging can also yield the EF to assess cardiac function. For studies with small sample sizes, manual extraction of data from incompletely structured multi-modality cardiovascular imaging reports for may be feasible. However, for studies with large sample sizes, manual data extraction from multiple types of cardiovascular imaging studies may not be feasible, efficient, or precise. The application of artificial intelligence (AI) for automated data extraction from incompletely structured cardiovascular imaging reports for large datasets has the potential to substantially improve data gathering and management for cardiovascular research, including studies of cardiovascular disease in cancer survivors.

AI is a broad term referring to tasks normally performed by human intelligence that are instead performed by computers. In healthcare, there is considerable excitement about the potential for AI to improve data management and clinical decision-making. While AI applications in medicine have impacted a variety of fields, these applications may have the greatest impact in cutting-edge research fields such as cardiology. Common applications of AI in cardiology involve cardiovascular imaging. Most AI articles in cardiovascular imaging have so far focused on measurement of imaging parameters [19], [20], yet not on the use of AI to automate data extraction from incompletely structured cardiovascular imaging reports. Indeed, there is literature describing the state of the art, the promises, and the challenges of AI in cardiology [21], [22], [23], [24], but their scope does not include automated data extraction from incompletely cardiovascular imaging reports.

We hypothesized in this study that common text elements and regular expressions (regex) in natural language processing (NLP) could be leveraged to automate data extraction from incompletely structured transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE), transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE), multi-gated acquisition (MUGA), single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) myocardial perfusion imaging (MPI), and cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging reports. Earlier studies have examined EF extraction using NLP within single imaging modalities. Our approach has expanded in the course of the study to accommodate multiple modalities and timepoints, ultimately demonstrating the feasibility of integrating and extracting data from heterogeneous cardiovascular imaging reports within a longitudinal clinical framework.

Methods

Study population

The team identified data for >4000 adult male and female cancer survivors with ethnic and racial diversity over a follow-up period spanning 2000 to 2020 [25] (Fig. 1). The Informatics for Integrating Biology and the Bedside (I2B2) cohort discovery tool was applied to the Clinical Research Data Warehouse (CRDW) to identify cancer survivors to build the study database. The I2B2 clinical research tool enables investigators to identify patients with specific attributes. The CRDW is a clinical data store that aggregates data from various sources including electronic health records, billing systems, and other clinical systems to provide a more comprehensive view of patient records spanning 20 years. Due to the longitudinal nature of the cardiovascular imaging data, and the variation in cardiac diagnostic data capture across the Froedtert Health System over time, the CRDW team queried multiple cardiovascular imaging documentation sources to find the analytic features. CRDW partnered with the Froedtert Information Technology team to build a secure data pipeline to ingest discrete cardiovascular imaging data from third party systems. CRDW also built custom extracts to find measurement features within various imaging reports for service dates that preceded the implementation of the current cardiovascular imaging reporting system in 2015. The imaging reports were deidentified by the CRDW team and shared with our data science team members who used NLP to extract the cardiovascular imaging features.

Cardiovascular imaging reports

Beyond TTE and TEE, we pursued various methods of collaboration across the institution to achieve alternative methods of EF ascertainment. To do this, we also obtained data from cardiovascular imaging platforms that were not TTE or TEE. These included cardiac nuclear studies (i.e., MUGA, SPECT, and PET if available) and CMR, to determine whether we were able to accurately extract EF from these alternative cardiovascular imaging reports. To ensure robust extraction of echocardiographic variables, we mapped heterogeneous text expressions into standardized terms and applied approximate physiologic constraints (e.g., EF 0‐100%, ESV ≤ EDV). Supplemental Table 1 and Supplemental Table 2 list the full set of medical expressions identified for each echocardiographic parameter, illustrating the heterogeneity in clinical reporting that the NLP algorithms were designed to handle. Supplemental Table 2 outlines a standardized framework for mapping commonly reported imaging parameters to structured formats suitable for NLP in this context. For each clinical term, representative expression formats, physiological constraints or logical relationships, and associated units are specified. Key NLP considerations include variability in terminology, capitalization, spacing, unit placement, and value ordering, as well as the presence of ranges, modality-specific tags, or missing units. Together, these mappings provide a foundation for harmonizing heterogeneous free-text reports into analyzable cardiovascular data.

Natural language processing framework

Natural language processing was implemented using a rule-based, regex–driven framework in both Python and R to extract structured cardiovascular imaging variables from unstructured clinical reports (Fig. 3 and Fig. 4). Clinically relevant expressions were encoded to identify target variables within free-text reports. Two independent implementations, each developed by a separate data scientist team using distinct programming environments, were iteratively compared and reconciled to determine an optimal extraction strategy. Dataset and preprocessing

The study dataset consisted of cardiovascular imaging reports stored in comma-separated value format, with each row corresponding to a unique patient-report-date instance. Multiple reports per patient across longitudinal time points were included. The dataset contained columns for patient-specific deidentification hashes, full report text, and extracted cardiovascular parameters.

Variables of interest included ejection fraction, global longitudinal strain, ventricular volumes (Vd and Vs), and volumetric indices including end-diastolic volume and end-systolic volume. These parameters were primarily derived from transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography reports, although additional imaging modalities were subsequently evaluated. Extraction of these variables supported downstream machine learning applications for prediction of cardiovascular outcomes in a cohort of cancer survivors (Brown et al., PrevCardioOncAI, JAHA 2025).

Python-based extraction algorithm

In Python, the built-in “re” package was used to identify clinical expressions and extract associated numerical values based on spatial proximity within report text. Following data import, report content was parsed to align patient identifiers with corresponding study dates. For each report, the algorithm searched for predefined sets of expressions corresponding to each clinical variable (Supplemental Table 1).

Upon identification of a target expression, such as EF or ESV, the algorithm searched within a defined proximity window to locate the nearest associated numerical value. Extracted values were recorded and stored in structured format. This process was applied iteratively across all reports in the dataset, producing a comprehensive output file containing extracted variables.

To enhance detection accuracy, expression sets incorporated both abbreviated forms (e.g., “EF”) and extended clinical phrases (e.g., “the ejection fraction of the left ventricle is”) (Supplemental Table 2). In cases where multiple values were identified for a single parameter within a report, such as reported ranges, minimum and maximum values were extracted and averaged to generate a single representative value.

In instances where ventricular volumes (Vd and Vs) were not explicitly reported, left ventricular internal dimensions in diastole and systole (LVIDd and LVIDs) were extracted and used to calculate volumes using the Teicholz formulae, with final values rounded to two decimal places.

Teicholz formulae Vd=7×LVIDd32.4+LVIDd, Vs=7×LVIDs32.4+LVIDs

Physiologic constraints and numerical boundaries were applied to guide extraction accuracy, including limiting ejection fraction values to clinically plausible ranges (e.g., 〈100%) and enforcing consistency among related parameters (Table 2 and Supplemental Table 2). These constraints, along with defined clinical relationships, supported algorithmic validation and reduction of erroneous value capture. To address variability in report structure, expression definitions, positional ordering, and proximity thresholds were carefully specified. Global longitudinal strain extraction

Extraction of global longitudinal strain values was performed using a defined set of anchor expressions reflecting common reporting conventions in our dataset (Supplemental Table 1). These expressions enabled identification of strain values across diverse report formats and vendor-specific nomenclature. Manual review was conducted by three unblinded reviewers to assess concordance between extracted values and source report content and to identify systematic extraction patterns and failure modes. This process focused on descriptive validation of algorithm performance rather than creation of a formally annotated gold standard dataset; interrater reliability statistics were not calculated. Additional validation safeguards included dual platform comparison between Python and R implementations, application of physiologic constraints, and iterative refinement of extraction rules. Non-target values, such as right ventricular strain, were excluded through context-aware filtering. Extracted data were exported in structured comma-separated value format with standardized rounding.

Methodological positioning

This work represents a deterministic, rule-based NLP approach optimized for interpretability, transparency, and clinical alignment. By combining dual platform implementation, structured validation, and physiologic constraint enforcement, the framework enables accurate and reproducible extraction of cardiovascular imaging variables from unstructured clinical text, supporting downstream analytics and predictive modeling in cardio-oncology populations.

Results

Epidemiological cohort

Among the >4000 cancer survivors in our longitudinal cohort (n = 4626) spanning 2000–2020, the cohort was organically (not intentionally) approximately evenly split between men and women (n = 2343 men (51%), n = 2283 women (49%)) (Fig. 1). In the cohort, 78% (n = 3600) were Caucasians, 18% (n = 834) were African Americans, 2% (n = 77) were Hispanics, 1% (n = 66) were of other races/ethnicities, and 1% (n = 51) were unknown. Overall age range was 18–90 years old. Approximately half (n = 1876 (52%)) of the Caucasian individuals in the cohort were males, with 48% (n = 1724) females. Similarly, approximately half (n = 376 (45%)) of the African American individuals in the cohort were males, with 55% (n = 456) females. This trend was similar among Hispanics, with 1% (n = 36) males and 1% (n = 41) females; as well as among other races/ethnicities, with 42% (n = 28) males and 58% (n = 38) females; and also among those of unknown race/ethnicity, with 53% (n = 27) males and 47% (n = 24) females.

In the cohort, the most prevalent cancers were breast (n = 502 (11%)), lung (n = 711 (16%)), hematologic (n = 744 (16%)), and prostate (n = 401 (9%)). The vast majority of individuals with breast cancer were females (n = 494 (98%)), with 2% (n = 8) males. Approximately half (n = 329 (46%)) of the individuals with lung cancer were men, with 54% (n = 382) women. Approximately half (n = 418 (56%)) of those with hematologic cancers were men, with (n = 334 (44%)) women.

Approximately 70% (3133) of the cohort had formally diagnosed cardiomyopathy, and more than 60% (n = 2826) had overall heart failure, at any time over 20 years of follow-up in the epidemiological cohort (n = 4626). At baseline 56% (n = 2598) of the cohort had already been diagnosed with cardiomyopathy and 49% (n = 2253) with overall heart failure.

Ascertainment of ejection fraction

The cohort dataset contained >26,000 imaging reports representing 4626 patients (Fig. 2). This included cardiovascular imaging data from TTE and TEEs, in addition to nuclear cardiac imaging (NM; identified in reports as myocardial pharmacologic, myocardial perf, gated heart MUGA, myocardial stress rest) and CMR (identified in reports as cardiac MR or cardiac morph), as well as non-cardiac imaging reports from unrelated CTs and MRIs.

Using the regex-based approach independently then collaboratively with Python and R, values for EF were successfully extracted from all cardiovascular imaging reports containing EF values, including TTE and TEEs, as well as NM and CMR, for 4626 patients in our cohort (Fig. 2).

Alternative methods of EF ascertainment

From among the alternative methods of EF ascertainment, such as NM (i.e., MUGA, SPECT, and PET if available) and CMR, a combined total of 2157 reports were obtained (Fig. 2).

Among the algorithmically extracted EF data from these >2000 reports, 1065 reports yielded an EF value (denoted “non-missing”), while 1092 did not (denoted “missing”). Manual review of 213/1065 (20%) of the non-missing reports indicated 100% accuracy of the EF value extracted from each of the 213 reports (i.e., algorithmically extracted EF = actual EF observed manually on report). Manual review of 218/1092 (20%) of the missing EF values demonstrated a common pattern of non-informative rows, such as: “THIS EXAM OR SERIES OF FILMS IS REPORTED IN COMBINATION WITH REPORT OF NUC MED MYOCARDIAL PERFUSION SPECT IMAGING…”. This particular pattern was noted in reports that did not actually provide a manually observed EF value. Therefore, using the common text pattern “THIS EXAM OR SERIES OF FILMS IS REPORTED”, we eliminated corresponding matching rows/reports absent of EF values. This decreased the missing number to 33, for reports absent of manually observed EF values but without an obvious codable pattern to guide removal. These 33 were manually reviewed and removed, and we subsequently proceeded with the 1065 non-missing data values from among the alternative methods of EF ascertainment, subsequently combined with the EF data values from TTE and TEEs.

Extraction of strain values

Of particular note was the extraction of strain values. We initially ran the regular expression R algorithm against the 908 TTE reports that included the term “strain”; 148 patient reports did not result in an actual extracted value for strain (Fig. 2).

The lead team cardiologist manually reviewed these 148 patient reports to ascertain whether strain was indeed reported, and to determine potential additional specific phrases to discover them, since our queries identified the term “strain” in the reports but the algorithms did not extract a corresponding value. The cardiologist manually extracted strain values from 15 of the 148 reports; the remaining 133 reports did not contain actual strain values.

We then investigated nuances in the report expressions to explain the discrepancies. The most common findings among the 148 patient reports with missing strain values was the statement, “The peak systolic strain cannot be assessed due to inadequate endocardial definition”, or that strain could not be assessed due to abnormal heart rhythm; these were found in a total of 75 reports. The next 10 reports with missing strain values indicated that strain was obtained (“Strain Analysis Performed”), yet on reviewing the report no strain was actually reported, which may indicate an unsuccessful attempt was made to measure strain, or the statement was erroneously retained in the final report, from a default report template. Terms for LV global longitudinal strain were mentioned in different ways in 6 reports, although the reports did not state or explain why they did not actually measure or report a value for strain.

Importantly, numerous other decoys were also discovered in echocardiographic reports including the term “strain” but without actual global longitudinal strain values. A common reason was due to the imaging study being performed at the time of patient care to specifically assess for any evidence of right ventricular strain (“RV strain”; listed only in the Indication or Reason for Study section of the report), particularly in patients with pulmonary emboli; this was noted in 38 imaging reports. Two reports actually mentioned RV strain described in the Interpretation, Summary, or detailed study findings, not only in the Indication. The least common reason for missing strain values on echocardiographic reports that included the word “strain” was that the word “strain” was found contained in larger words such as “constrained” or “restrained”, in two reports.

Some echocardiography reports which actually included strain values included atypical ways of reporting strain; two of these atypical ways were repeated, suggesting this may have previously been a common way of reporting strain, particularly since these phrases were noted in the external/outreach sites that were previously referral sites to our institution, many of which actually had then been purchased by the core tertiary hospital at our institution over the two decades of longitudinal data in our study. These atypical phrasings had not been included in the initial regex used in the algorithm codes, but were later incorporated for completeness. We therefore overcame these hurdles by adding new corresponding expressions to the algorithms used for identifying the relevant strain values.

From a representative echocardiography report, the NLP system accurately extracted clinically relevant parameters, including LVEF (55% by Simpson's method), global longitudinal strain (−19.2%), LV internal dimensions (LVIDd 5.4 cm, LVIDs 3.2 cm), and volumetric measures (EDV 135 ml, ESV 70 ml). Extracted values were cross validated against physiologic constraints (e.g., LVIDs < LVIDd, ESV < EDV) to ensure plausibility. Context-aware regular expression patterns enabled precise capture of EF and strain values despite variability in reporting syntax, avoiding spurious numeric extractions. Across simulated reports, the system achieved complete adherence to rule-based criteria, with edge cases flagged for review. A visual interface facilitated manual correction when needed and supported structured export (JSON/FHIR), demonstrating both the accuracy and interoperability of the pipeline for downstream clinical and research applications.

Discussion

NLP can be used to process large amounts of medical data in shorter timeframes than manual human processing. Accurate results can be obtained by coding and applying the right algorithms and manually reviewing results through a clinical lens. More complex data may require more nuanced algorithms. Accordingly, we used NLP to efficiently and accurately extract cardiovascular imaging parameters (in the form of medical expressions, or regex) from patient imaging reports. We used this method to ascertain baseline EF assessment on cardiovascular imaging within 36 months prior to cancer diagnosis for all 4626 individuals. We applied a regex method to automate data extraction of EF, strain, LVIDd, LVIDs, EDV, and ESV from the cardiovascular imaging reports. Specific medical regex was implemented using Python and R. Several patterns for expressing these medical expressions in patient reports were examined and applied, capturing variation in how the results were expressed. We also performed neighborhood searches and matched the related measurement values for each patient. Several individuals did not have EF specifically obtained on echocardiography in the 3-year timeframe prior to their cancer diagnosis. For these individuals, EF was gleaned from other cardiovascular imaging modalities, such as prior/baseline MUGA, myocardial perfusion, or less frequently CMR. Regex NLP coding was also effective for these alternative forms of cardiovascular imaging. We performed manual review to determine reasons for discrepancies in the reports or between the results of the R and Python algorithms. Such manual review can be a useful and important step in potential best practices for using regex to extract values from clinical reports in feasibility studies (Table 3 and Fig. 5).

Within cardiovascular informatics, as would be expected, echocardiography has emerged as the cardiovascular imaging modality most frequently assessed for natural language processing (NLP)–based extraction of left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) albeit so far in limited studies, representing a potential foundational capability for scalable, computable cardiovascular phenotyping (Supplemental Table 3). LVEF values in echocardiographic reports are embedded within semi-structured and free-text narratives where variability in syntax, numeric representation, and contextual qualifiers—including qualitative descriptors, value ranges, and temporally referenced measurements—limits reliable structured capture and necessitates increasingly sophisticated extraction approaches. Early rule-based systems establish feasibility and clinical relevance, with near-perfect performance for identifying reduced EF using regex within [26]. Large-scale rule-based and hybrid pipelines have demonstrated both scalability and robustness, including high fidelity extraction across more than 600,000 reports through contextual filtering of negation and historical values [27], as well as efficient cohort construction for heart failure with reduced ejection fraction using distributed regex-based frameworks [28]. In parallel, broader phenotyping efforts have expanded beyond single parameter extraction, with multi-element systems enabling high accuracy recovery of LVEF alongside dozens of echocardiographic variables [29], and hybrid dictionary- and rule-based approaches demonstrating consistently high precision for LVEF across diverse clinical document types [30].

Recent work has also focused on improving generalizability and reducing dependence on rigid report structure, including corpus-independent text mining approaches leveraging fuzzy matching to achieve high performance across multiple echocardiographic parameters [31], and large-scale knowledge-guided pipelines enabling structured curation of over 130,000 TTE reports with strong agreement to clinician-derived data [32]. The field has now entered a transformer and large language model (LLM) era, in which zero-shot and lightweight open-source models can accurately extract nuanced LVEF representations with high precision, recall, and correlation to reference standards [33], and consensus-based LLM ensembles can achieve near-perfect accuracy in structured extraction from intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography reports [34]. Collectively, these studies illustrate a clear methodological progression from rule-based systems to hybrid and deep learning approaches, culminating in scalable, high-throughput pipelines capable of extracting complex and context-dependent LVEF representations with strong concordance to expert annotation, thereby establishing echocardiography as a benchmark modality for NLP-driven cardiac function phenotyping, clinical decision support, and real-world evidence generation.

Extending beyond echocardiography, LVEF may be reported across cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR), nuclear cardiology studies, and coronary computed tomography angiography (CTA). Yet, in these reports, LVEF remains embedded within heterogeneous narrative reports characterized by substantial variability in structure, terminology, and contextual framing, posing distinct challenges for reliable NLP-based extraction (Supplemental Table 3). Compared with echocardiography, these modalities often interleave functional and non-functional findings, inconsistently report LVEF depending on acquisition protocols, and incorporate modality-specific conventions that complicate normalization of numeric and qualitative EF representations. Nevertheless, early cross-domain investigations have demonstrated the feasibility of generalizing LVEF extraction beyond echocardiography, with sequence-tagging approaches successfully adapting prior modules across echocardiography, radiology, and clinical notes [35]. Recent work has therefore highlighted this increased complexity of less structured report types [35]. Similarly, hybrid dictionary- and rule-based frameworks have shown that radiology reports could support high-precision extraction of cardiac function metrics, including LVEF, though with modestly lower overall performance compared with echocardiography due to linguistic heterogeneity [30].

Among non-echocardiographic modalities, CMR has emerged as the most extensively studied domain, with transformer-based deep learning workflows enabling large-scale extraction of LVEF and related volumetric parameters from free-text reports and facilitating linkage to downstream clinical outcomes [36], and two-tier NLP systems achieving near-human accuracy in extracting LVEF within disease-specific phenotyping pipelines such as hypertrophic cardiomyopathy [37]. More recently, zero-shot large language model approaches have demonstrated the ability to identify and normalize multiple cardiac functional indicators, including LVEF, from cardiac MRI reports with near-human annotation accuracy and high named entity recognition performance [38]. Despite these advances, dedicated NLP pipelines optimized specifically for nuclear cardiology studies (e.g., MUGA, SPECT, PET) and coronary CTA remain comparatively limited, with most existing approaches embedded within broader radiology or multimodal frameworks rather than tailored to modality-specific linguistic patterns. Consequently, while modern transformer-based and LLM-driven methods have improved generalizability across heterogeneous imaging narratives, a critical gap persists in achieving the level of standardization, validation, and high-throughput scalability observed in echocardiography. Addressing this gap will be essential to enable comprehensive multimodality LVEF phenotyping across contemporary imaging ecosystems, thereby supporting longitudinal disease surveillance, integrated cardiovascular risk stratification, and robust real-world evidence generation.

There is inherently also a need to adapt the algorithms for multi-modality use. Medical regex used to extract data from a specific report type (such as MUGA) may not be effectively blindly applied to other types of cardiovascular imaging reports. Expressions in the algorithms need to be rewritten or expanded to capture the variety of phrases used to report measurement values, and their units, in multi-modality imaging reports, such as TTE, TEE, NM studies, and CMR. Each health system would need to assess and collate their regex, which would need to be used to modify the algorithms to match their population reports. Yet, the core of the algorithm would be similar across studies.

Several challenges should be recognized, related to inconsistencies and variability in how data are reported (Supplemental Table 4). Common issues include irregular spacing, punctuation, and unit placement, as well as variability in capitalization, missing data, and inconsistent unit formats such as millimeters versus centimeters. Data may also be presented in different forms, such as ranges, means, or single values, with expression order varying between placing the value before or after the descriptive phrase. Measurement formatting is inconsistent, with some reports using integers while others use decimals, and units such as percentages, centimeters, or milliliters may appear before, after, or be entirely omitted. Additionally, formatting inconsistencies in capitalization, lowercase, and the use of underscores complicate standardization. Some reports present multiple values for the same measurement, requiring de-duplication or prioritization, while punctuation and syntax issues, such as run-on expressions or missing terminal punctuation, further complicate interpretation. These challenges underscore the need for clear rules to manage ranges, handle missing or mixed reporting, and standardize formatting across reports. We addressed each of these challenges by systematically modifying, improving, and applying the algorithms, with great output accuracy.

While regex-based extraction itself does not represent a novel methodological contribution within the broader field of clinical NLP, the primary contribution of the present work lies in demonstrating the feasibility of large-scale automated extraction of ejection fraction and strain parameters across multiple cardiovascular imaging modalities over two decades of real-world clinical reporting. The study therefore emphasizes longitudinal infrastructure development and practical implementation within a cardio-oncology research context rather than methodological innovation in NLP algorithm design.

In cardio-oncology, NLP strategies can be applied to extract clinically meaningful information from electronic health records. For example, deterministic approaches can identify LVEF values from echocardiography reports using structured pattern recognition (Supplemental Table 5). Hybrid methods extend this capability by detecting mentions of anthracycline agents such as doxorubicin and applying machine learning to validate the temporal sequence of treatment events. At the probabilistic end of the spectrum, transformer-based deep learning models enable detection and linkage of cardiotoxicity progression within unstructured clinical narratives, providing a comprehensive view of patient trajectories. The table depicts this spectrum from deterministic to probabilistic NLP approaches. Deterministic methods (left) rely on regex and heuristics for precise but rigid pattern recognition. Hybrid approaches (middle) combine regex, rule-based logic, and machine learning to enable context-aware parsing, negation detection, and weak supervision. Probabilistic deep learning models (right), such as BioBERT and ClinicalBERT, leverage contextual embeddings for advanced tasks including entity/relation extraction, temporal reasoning, and sentiment analysis. Across methodological eras, approaches to LVEF extraction from clinical text have progressed from highly precise but locally constrained rule-based and dictionary-driven systems to more adaptive machine learning and transformer-based models, culminating in emerging large language model frameworks that offer broad generalizability across heterogeneous report types while introducing new requirements for rigorous validation and oversight.

In cardio-oncology, extracting cardiovascular imaging data via NLP faces challenges from method complexity, data heterogeneity, and clinical demands. Regex provides interpretable first-pass extraction of variables such as LVEF and chemotherapy exposures but struggle with synonyms, format variation, and site-specific report styles [39]. Machine learning NLP models, including BERT-based systems (BioBERT, ClinicalBERT), offer semantic flexibility for nuanced statements and temporal relations, though they require annotated data and computational resources [40] (Supplemental Table 5). Hybrid pipelines combining regex, machine learning, and targeted manual review balance speed, interpretability, and coverage but demand careful attention to privacy, reproducibility, and regulatory compliance [41]. Transparent reporting of performance metrics, domain tuning, and limitations remains essential for scientific rigor [42]. Together, these considerations highlight that successful NLP deployment requires an integrated, context-sensitive approach that aligns methodological capability with clinical relevance.

Table 3 summarizes recommended strategies for extracting structured cardiovascular variables from echocardiography and other imaging reports using NLP. Potential best practices include algorithm design considerations (e.g., regex-based detection in Python and R), handling variability in expression formats, expanding data sources across modalities, integrating manual review, and eliminating decoys. Additional steps address neighborhood logic for accurate value capture, normalization of units and formats, reconciliation of multi-value reports, and management of anomalies in text structure. Iterative refinement, cross-platform concordance, and institution-specific optimization further enhance algorithm accuracy and generalizability.

An important methodological consideration arose from the marked temporal evolution in data structure across the study period. Although more contemporary cardiovascular imaging data were readily retrievable in structured, discrete formats within the CRDW, earlier records were predominantly embedded within unstructured reports and disparate modality-specific systems. To achieve longitudinal completeness, it was therefore necessary to extend data acquisition beyond conventional warehouse queries and systematically interrogate heterogeneous archival sources spanning two decades. This experience highlights a fundamental challenge in real-world cardiovascular informatics: durable phenotyping across time requires deliberate integration of legacy data environments that predate modern structured reporting infrastructures.

This study has several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, this work was originally designed as a feasibility and implementation study embedded within a longitudinal cardio-oncology research infrastructure rather than as a formal algorithm benchmarking or NLP methods comparison study. Accordingly, standard NLP performance metrics including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, NPV, F1 score, confidence intervals, and confusion matrices were not originally generated. Manual review was conducted to assess concordance between extracted values and report content and to identify systematic extraction patterns and failure modes, but these reviews were not intended to establish generalizable estimates of algorithmic performance in all circumstances.

Second, in this feasibility study, manual review was performed by three unblinded reviewers rather than through construction of a formally annotated gold standard reference dataset with blinded independent adjudication and interrater reliability assessment. While this approach was sufficient for feasibility assessment and descriptive validation within the context of this study, it limits the ability to quantify reproducibility, agreement statistics, or error rates in a manner comparable to supervised NLP evaluation frameworks. This methodological limitation should be considered when assessing the interpretability of performance claims. Third, the NLP algorithms were developed, refined, and applied within a single academic health system using institution-specific cardiovascular imaging reports spanning multiple decades, imaging vendors, and reporting templates. Expressions, neighborhood logic, and decoy patterns were derived empirically from these reports. As a result, the findings should not be interpreted as evidence of out-of-the-box generalizability to other institutions. External validation and site-specific adaptation would be required prior to deployment in different health systems. Notwithstanding, the heterogeneity of imaging report structure across various outreach sites of the health system help increase the likelihood of generalizability to an extent.

Fourth, while the manuscript and Supplementary Tables describe regex expression classes used to identify numeric values and associated terminology, we do not provide exhaustive pattern specifications for all expression variants encountered. Similarly, although neighborhood-based extraction is described conceptually, fully parameterized definitions of neighborhood window sizes are not exhaustive. This is particularly relevant, because cardiovascular imaging reports frequently contain multiple numeric values in close textual proximity, including references to prior studies, alternative measurements, ranges, or non-target parameters. This may result in multiple candidate values being identified within a given context window. While general prioritization logic is described, formalized conflict resolution rules governing selection among multiple candidate values are not fully specified. These components were developed empirically and iteratively based on observed report structure across the heterogenous health system, rather than predefined or optimized against an annotated homogenous reference standard. As a result, full reproducibility and external implementation may require site-specific adaptation (even across heterogenous sites) and refinement of these algorithmic parameters. Thus, we present our method for pursuing such adaptation, which would also need to be customized in other settings. Across our heterogenous health system, cardiovascular imaging reports spanned a 20-year period during which substantial changes occurred in imaging technology, reporting systems, vendor software, and documentation templates. These temporal shifts introduce heterogeneity in report structure, terminology, and formatting that complicates both extraction and evaluation. While the algorithms were adapted to accommodate this variability, the impact of temporal evolution on extraction behavior could not be formally quantified.

Fifth, no empirical comparison was performed between the regex-based approach used in this study and alternative NLP strategies, including rule-based clinical NLP frameworks, hybrid systems, or machine learning–based models. Although such comparisons are valuable for contextualizing performance, they would require additional annotated data, model development, and validation that were beyond the scope of the current work. However, we have now included a section of literature review discussing comparisons among NLP methods, including modern ML-based NLP models.

Sixth, derivation of volumetric parameters using the Teichholz formula was performed only when directly reported volumetric measurements were unavailable. Derived values were retained for completeness in analyses. This approach introduces known modeling assumptions and limitations and should not be interpreted as equivalent to direct measurement. Seventh, strain extraction posed unique challenges due to frequent decoy usage, conditional statements indicating failed measurement, modality-specific phrasing, and historical variability in reporting syntax. While these challenges were characterized descriptively and addressed through iterative refinement, formal quantitative evaluation of strain extraction performance was not performed. This study focused on feasibility and practical implementation rather than formal benchmarking. In addition, the frequency of specific reporting formats such as numeric ranges, decimal values, or inconsistent unit expressions was not formally quantified, as the study focused on feasibility of rule-based extraction rather than detailed characterization of report formatting patterns.

Finally, because this study prioritized transparency of algorithm logic, expression mappings, and extraction behavior over formal benchmarking, readers should interpret the findings as evidence of feasibility and practical implementation rather than definitive validation of NLP performance for EF or strain extraction. This study leveraged a multiracial cohort of cancer survivors, with comprehensive longitudinal clinical, laboratory, and echocardiographic data. The multi-site heterogenous health system design enabled feasibility assessment of model transferability across different healthcare settings and a diverse patient population.

Conclusion

We showed that NLP can be used to effectively extract measurement values from multi-modality cardiovascular imaging, with a particular focus on EF and related parameters. This helped us collate “baseline” EF for >4000 cancer survivors, capturing data from before they started treatment, in a longitudinal cohort spanning the years 2000–2020. Using NLP provided a tremendous level of efficiency. The regular expression method worked very well for our dataset and may be considered by other health systems as well. Standardization of reporting terminology in multi-modality cardiovascular imaging reports may be helpful to facilitate regex NLP studies across cardiology. Regular expression can be implemented using Python or R. Various patterns must be ascertained and applied, followed by manual review. Neighborhood searches can be helpful to overcome some of the challenges with how data are presented in imaging reports. Decoys and missing data can be assessed and addressed to produce a consistent and robust dataset. This study and the best practice pearls we have gleaned provide a foundation for our current and future work using NLP for data extraction in feasibility studies. The extracted information facilitates large data input for machine learning, including deep learning, algorithms that other groups and ours use to predict cardiovascular outcomes in cancer survivors [25].
