Early childhood asthma and adiposity until adolescence: A prospective birth cohort study using body fat percentage
Authors: Mónica Rodrigues, Margarida Henriques, Susana Santos, Pedro Moreira, André Moreira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAsthma and obesity frequently coexist in childhood, yet whether asthma predisposes to later adiposity remains underexplored, particularly using measures beyond body mass index (BMI). Considering this, we aimed to examine the association between asthma in early childhood and subsequent adiposity through adolescence, assessed using BMI and body fat percentage, and to evaluate sex‐specific effects.MethodsThis prospective study included 4717 participants from the Portuguese population‐based Generation XXI birth cohort. Physician‐diagnosed asthma at age 4 years was the exposure. Incident obesity and overweight/obesity were assessed at ages 7‐, 10‐, and 13‐years using BMI and body fat percentage estimated by bioelectrical impedance analysis and classified according to age‐ and sex‐specific percentiles. Children who were already overweight or obese at age 4 were excluded from the respective analysis. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI), adjusting for confounders. Effect modification by sex was tested, and sex‐stratified analyses were performed.ResultsAsthma was associated with a higher risk of incident obesity defined by body fat percentage in adjusted analyses (aHR = 1.91, 95% CI: 1.16–3.16), whereas associations with BMI‐defined obesity were not statistically significant. Sex‐stratified analyses showed that these associations were confined to girls, who had increased risk of obesity (aHR = 1.97, 95% CI: 1.19–3.26) and overweight/obesity (aHR = 1.75, 95% CI: 1.02–3.01) defined by body fat percentage. No significant associations were observed among boys.ConclusionsAsthma in early childhood was associated with increased adiposity in adolescence assessed by body fat percentage, particularly among girls. These findings suggest that children with asthma may develop excess fat accumulation not fully captured by BMI and support the integration of body composition and metabolic risk assessment into pediatric asthma care.

Key messageAsthma in early childhood seems to be associated with increased body fat in adolescence, particularly among girls. Integrating obesity risk awareness into pediatric asthma care may help reduce cardiometabolic burden in the long‐term.

Key message

Asthma in early childhood seems to be associated with increased body fat in adolescence, particularly among girls. Integrating obesity risk awareness into pediatric asthma care may help reduce cardiometabolic burden in the long‐term.

INTRODUCTION

Asthma and obesity are among the most prevalent chronic conditions in childhood and adolescence, both showing rising incidence and substantial long‐term health consequences. 1 , 2 Their frequent coexistence has driven increasing interest in shared biological and behavioral pathways originating early in life. 1 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 While obesity is a well‐established risk factor for asthma, the reverse temporal relationship, whether asthma in early childhood predisposes to later obesity, remains comparatively underexplored.

Several mechanisms may explain how asthma could influence subsequent excessive bodyweight or adiposity. Respiratory symptoms, exercise‐induced bronchoconstriction, and fear of exacerbations may limit physical activity, particularly when asthma is poorly controlled, promoting sedentary behaviors. 7 Dietary patterns may also contribute, as energy‐dense diets have been associated with both asthma and obesity. 1 , 8 Dietary patterns may mediate part of the association if asthma leads to lifestyle changes that influence diet. In addition, asthma treatment has been implicated in weight gain trajectories, although evidence is inconsistent and may reflect disease severity rather than treatment effects. 3 , 9

Beyond behavioral factors, biological pathways linking asthma to adiposity have been proposed. Asthma is increasingly recognized as a condition with systemic inflammatory features, and chronic airway inflammation may contribute to metabolic dysregulation. 10 , 11 Adipokines such as leptin have been implicated in both asthma pathophysiology and fat accumulation. Leptin may increase airway inflammation and influence T‐cell function, with one study suggesting higher leptin levels in children with asthma independent of body mass index (BMI). 12 Moreover, shared early‐life determinants—including maternal adiposity, gestational weight gain, smoking during pregnancy, and other environmental exposures may influence the development of both asthma and excess adiposity. 1 , 3 , 4

Longitudinal evidence studying asthma as a determinant of later obesity remains limited. 3 , 5 , 6 , 13 Existing cohort studies are relatively few, often have short follow‐up periods, 4 , 5 and rely predominantly on BMI to define obesity. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 13 BMI does not distinguish fat from lean mass and may misclassify adiposity during growth and pubertal development. 14 Moreover, potential sex‐specific associations have not been consistently examined, despite known differences in asthma expression, physical activity patterns, and hormonal influences between boys and girls.

In this context, we investigated the prospective association between physician‐diagnosed asthma at 4 years of age and subsequent adiposity up to 13 years in a large population‐based birth cohort. We assessed incident obesity and combined overweight/obesity using both BMI‐ and body fat percentage‐based definitions. We also explored sex‐specific associations to better characterize adiposity risk in children with early‐life asthma.

METHODS

Study design and participants

This prospective study was conducted within the Generation XXI population‐based birth cohort from Portugal. 15 , 16 Between 2005 and 2006, all mothers delivering live‐born infants in five public maternity units in the metropolitan area of Porto were invited to participate. A total of 8495 mothers (92%) and 8647 newborns were enrolled at baseline. Detailed maternal, obstetric, demographic, and lifestyle information was collected through structured interviews, and neonatal data were obtained from clinical records.

The entire cohort was invited to the follow‐up study waves at four different time‐points considered for the present study: when the children were 4 years old, 2009–2011 (n = 7459, 86%); 7 years old, 2012–2014 (n = 6889, 80%); 10 years old, 2015–2016 (n = 6397, 74%); and at 13 years old, 2018–2020 (n = 4640, 54%). Participation was lower at the 13‐year visit because follow‐up was stopped early due to the COVID‐19 pandemic. Children without information on asthma status or adiposity measures (BMI or body fat percentage) at age 4 were excluded. To ensure the assessment of incident outcomes over follow‐up, children who already presented obesity or overweight/obesity at age 4 were excluded from the respective analyses. Specifically, when obesity was the outcome, children with obesity (BMI z‐score >2 or ≥95 percentile for body fat percentage for age and sex) at age 4 were excluded from the respective analysis; when overweight/obesity was the outcome, children with overweight (BMI z‐score >1 and ≤2 or ≥85th and <95th percentile for body fat) or obesity at age 4 were excluded. Only children with at least one subsequent follow‐up assessment with available adiposity data were included.

Of the 8647 children enrolled at birth, 4717 (54.6%) met the inclusion criteria and were retained in the analyses (Figure 1).

Exposure: Asthma at age 4 years

The exposure of interest was physician‐diagnosed asthma, which was obtained based on the International Study of Asthma and Allergies in Childhood (ISAAC) standardized questionnaire, considering a positive answer to the following question “Has your child ever been diagnosed with asthma by a physician?” as reported at the 4‐year‐old visit.

Outcomes: Adiposity measures

Body mass index (BMI)

Height and weight were measured at 4, 7, 10, and 13 years of age by trained researchers using standardized procedures. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2) and converted into age‐ and sex‐specific BMI z‐scores. 17 , 18 At the 4‐year follow‐up, when children's ages ranged from 44 to 71 months, BMI z‐scores were calculated using the World Health Organization (WHO) growth standards 18 or the WHO growth reference for school‐aged children, 17 as appropriate for each child's age. For the subsequent visits (7, 10, and 13 years), BMI z‐scores were calculated according to the WHO reference for school‐aged children and adolescents. 17 BMI z‐scores were then classified as underweight (z‐score <−2), normal weight (z‐score ≥−2 and ≤+1), overweight (z‐score >+1 and ≤+2), or obesity (z‐score >+2), according to WHO classification. 17

Body fat percentage

Body fat percentage was assessed using tetrapolar bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) with the Akern BIA Anniversary 101 device. The device was calibrated according to manufacturer specifications. Measurements were performed with participants wearing light clothing and no metal accessories, after fasting and bladder emptying.

Fat‐free mass was estimated using the equation developed by Schaefer et al. 19 which incorporates bioelectrical impedance, height, and age and was validated in pediatric populations. Fat mass was calculated as the difference between body weight and fat‐free mass. Body fat percentage categories (underfat defined as <2nd percentile, normal fat as 2nd‐85th percentile, overfat as 85th‐95th percentile, and obese as ≥95th percentile) were defined using age‐ and sex‐specific reference percentiles proposed by McCarthy et al. 20 Due to the lack of body fat reference curves for the entire age period, for children younger than 5 years, the lowest age category available in the reference was applied. Sensitivity analyses were conducted restricting the sample to children aged ≥5 years at the 4‐year follow‐up for body fat outcomes to ensure robustness of findings (analysis on Table S7).

Definition of events and time‐to‐event outcomes

Four time‐to‐event outcomes were defined using repeated assessments at ages 7, 10, and 13 years, with age as the underlying time scale and entry at age 4 years. Outcomes included: (1) incident obesity defined by BMI; (2) incident obesity defined by body fat percentage; (3) incident overweight or obesity defined by BMI; and (4) incident overweight or obesity defined by body fat percentage. Event time was defined as the age at which the outcome was first observed, and children who did not develop the outcome were censored at their last available assessment.

Covariates

Covariates were selected according to previous literature if they were related to asthma diagnosis exposure and obesity outcome. This included: child's sex (Female/Male), birthweight (<2500 g/≥2500 g), parity (primiparous/multiparous), maternal smoking status during pregnancy (ever vs. no‐smoking), maternal age (years) at child's birth, GWG categories (insufficient, adequate, and excessive), 21 maternal BMI (underweight, normal weight, overweight, and obese) 22 and breastfeeding (ever vs. never). Maternal level of education was assessed through the number of completed schooling years and categorized into ≤9, 10–12, or >12 years. 23

Children's physical activity status at 4‐, 7‐, 10‐ and 13‐year‐old follow‐up visits were also considered, based on dichotomous questions. At 4 years of age, parents answered the question: “Does the child practice any type of scheduled or regular sport?” at 7, 10, and 13 years, the question was: “Does the child/adolescent practice any type of scheduled or regular sport, outside of school?”, categorized as yes vs. no. 24

Children's dietary intake at 4 years of age was assessed using a 35‐item qualitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) that captured usual consumption over the previous 6 months. 25 Dietary patterns at age 4 were previously derived through latent class analysis. Three patterns were identified based on response profiles and mean daily consumption frequencies. The first pattern (“Energy dense foods” – “EDF”) reflected higher intakes of energy‐dense, micronutrient‐poor foods and beverages (e.g., sweets, sugar‐sweetened drinks, processed and high‐fat foods). The second pattern (“Snacking”) was characterized by lower consumption of foods typically eaten at main meals and relatively higher intake of snack‐type foods. The third (“Healthier”) pattern showed greater consumption of fruits, vegetables, and fish and lower intake of energy dense food items. 26

A directed acyclic graph (DAG) was used to confirm the minimal sufficient adjustment set for confounding control (Figure S1).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as means and standard deviations, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using independent sample t‐tests for continuous variables and chi‐square tests for categorical variables.

Associations between asthma at age 4 and subsequent adiposity outcomes were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models, reporting hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Both crude and adjusted models were fitted. The proportional hazards assumption was verified by visual inspection of log‐minus‐log plots, with no violations detected.

Effect modification by sex was evaluated by including an interaction term between asthma and sex, and, as p for interaction was <.05, sex‐stratified analyses were also performed. Missing data on covariates were handled using multiple imputation by fully conditional specification (FCS) in SPSS, assuming missing at random, using 10 imputed data sets. Complete‐case analyses were performed and presented on Table S1. Kaplan–Meier curves and corresponding log‐rank test results comparing cumulative incidence of outcomes by asthma status are presented in Figures S2–S13 and Tables S2–S5. Sensitivity analyses additionally adjusting for dietary patterns and physical activity variables at age 4 are presented in Table S6. Descriptive analyses in the full cohort and by sex regarding differences in body fat percentage, BMI and asthma are presented in Table S8. Descriptive analysis of children with asthma by sex are also presented in Table S9.

All phases of the study complied with the Ethical Principles for Medical Research Involving Human Subjects expressed in the Declaration of Helsinki*. The baseline and follow‐up evaluations were approved by the University of Porto Medical School/S. João Hospital Centre Ethics Committee, except the 13‐year follow‐up that was approved by the ISPUP Ethics Committee. At baseline and follow‐up evaluations, all procedures were explained to participants, and an informed consent was signed by one of the parents or legal guardians (at 13 years, it was also signed by the participants).

The baseline evaluation was additionally approved by the Data Protection National Commission, and the study follows the present EU General Data Protection Regulation under close supervision of the Data Protection Office of ISPUP. 27

RESULTS

At 4 years of age, 181 children (3.8%) had a physician diagnosis of asthma. Among these children, asthma persistence was high across follow‐up, with 76.8% reporting asthma at 7 years, 71.9% at 10 years, and 74.0% at 13 years, among those with available data at each time point. Baseline maternal and child characteristics according to asthma status are shown in Table 2. Children with asthma were more frequently boys (63.0% vs. 51.0%, p = .002) and had slightly younger mothers (28.8 vs. 29.8 years, p = .002). Differences were also observed in maternal pre‐pregnancy BMI (p = .003) with a lower proportion of mothers of children with asthma having normal weight (61.6% vs. 66.6%). Maternal gestational weight gain categories also differed (p = .038), with excessive GWG more common among mothers of children with asthma (45.2% vs. 36.0%). Maternal education, smoking during pregnancy, breastfeeding, parity, and birth weight did not differ significantly between groups.

Asthma at age 4 was positively associated with subsequent obesity defined by body fat percentage (Table 3). In the overall sample, children with asthma had a higher risk of incident obesity based on body fat percentage in adjusted analyses (aHR = 1.91, 95% CI: 1.16–3.16), whereas the crude estimate did not reach statistical significance (HR = 1.30, 95% CI: 0.96–1.75). Sex‐stratified analyses revealed marked differences. Among girls, asthma was associated with a significantly higher risk of incident obesity defined by body fat percentage in both crude (HR = 1.91, 95% CI: 1.16–3.15) and adjusted models (aHR = 1.97, 95% CI: 1.19–3.26). In contrast, no significant associations were observed among boys (aHR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.68–1.47).

When overweight or obesity was defined using body fat percentage, asthma was associated with a significantly increased risk in adjusted analyses (aHR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.03–3.00). This association was driven by girls, among whom asthma was associated with a higher hazard of developing excess adiposity (aHR = 1.75, 95% CI: 1.02–3.01). No significant associations were observed among boys.

In line with these findings, when examining differences within children with asthma (Table S9), girls were less likely than boys to report practicing sports outside of school at 13 years of age (33.9% vs. 61.4%, χ 2, p < .001).

DISCUSSION

In this large prospective birth cohort, asthma diagnosed at 4 years of age was associated with an increased risk of developing adiposity until adolescence when adiposity was assessed using body fat percentage, but not consistently when defined by BMI. These associations were largely confined to girls, highlighting important sex‐specific differences in the asthma‐adiposity relationship.

Our findings suggest that early childhood asthma may predispose to increased fat accumulation rather than generalized weight gain. This distinction is clinically relevant, as BMI does not differentiate between fat and lean mass and may inadequately capture excess adiposity during periods of rapid growth and pubertal development. Several studies have demonstrated that BMI may misclassifies a substantial proportion of children with excess body fat, thereby underestimating obesity prevalence and related health risks. 14 , 17 , 20 , 28

Although most previous research has focused on obesity as a risk factor for asthma, a growing body of longitudinal evidence supports the reverse pathway. Multi‐cohort studies have reported an increased risk of later obesity among children with asthma, with a 23% increased risk of developing obesity (95% CI: 4%–44%) reported in the study by Stratakis et al. 6 and an adjusted hazard ratio of 1.66 (95% CI: 1.18–2.33) reported in the study by Contreras et al. 7

Evidence from individual cohort studies further supports this association. In the Southern California Children's Health Study (CHS), it was found that children with asthma had a 51% increased risk of developing obesity during childhood and adolescence (HR = 1.51; 95% CI: 1.08–2.10) compared with children without asthma at baseline. 4 Additional analyses from the CHS using joint transition modeling with cross‐lagged structures showed that non‐obese children with asthma had 37% higher odds of becoming obese at the next annual visit (OR = 1.38; 95% CI: 1.12–1.71), while obesity at baseline did not predict subsequent asthma in the short term (OR = 1.25; 95% CI: 0.94–1.62). 13

Our results are consistent with these findings but extend existing knowledge by demonstrating that associations are stronger and more evident when adiposity is defined by body composition rather than BMI alone. This may partly explain inconsistencies across cohorts relying exclusively on BMI‐based outcomes.

Several mechanisms may underlie the observed association. Children with asthma may limit physical activity due to respiratory symptoms, fear of exacerbations, or reduced exercise tolerance, leading to lower energy expenditure. 4 , 8 Unhealthy dietary patterns 3 and asthma treatments 9 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 have also been proposed as contributing factors, although evidence remains inconsistent. In addition, asthma is increasingly recognized as a condition with systemic inflammatory features. 10 , 11 Pro‐inflammatory mediators and adipokines, such as leptin, may link airway inflammation with metabolic dysregulation and fat accumulation, even in the absence of overt obesity. 12

A key finding of our study is the clear sex‐specific pattern, with associations observed almost exclusively among girls. Importantly, our analyses compared children with and without asthma within each sex. Therefore, although girls may have a higher body fat percentage overall, this is unlikely to fully explain the observed association, which reflects differences between children with and without asthma within the same sex. Behavioral factors may partly explain this difference. In our cohort, girls with asthma were substantially less likely than boys to report participation in sports during adolescence, consistent with literature showing greater perceived barriers to physical activity among girls. 33 Reduced physical activity may therefore act as an important mediator linking asthma to later adiposity in girls.

Biological mechanisms may further contribute to sex differences. Pubertal hormonal changes influence both immune responses and metabolic regulation. Estrogens and progesterone have been shown to enhance Th2‐mediated inflammation and airway hyperresponsiveness, while androgens exert anti‐inflammatory and bronchodilatory effects. 34 Around puberty, asthma prevalence and severity tend to increase among girls, coinciding with hormonal changes that may also favor fat deposition. 35

From a public health and clinical perspective, our findings underscore the importance of early, integrated prevention strategies of excessive weight gain in children with asthma. Physical activity improves cardiopulmonary fitness, lung capacity, muscular strength, and endurance, 36 while also reducing airway eosinophilic inflammation. 37 In children and adolescents with asthma, regular physical activity lowers hospitalization rates, reduces school absenteeism, and decreases the use of bronchodilators. 38 It also improves daytime and nocturnal asthma control, enhances sleep quality, and supports psychological well‐being. 37 , 38 Besides these effects, physical activity also contributes to obesity prevention by promoting energy balance, thereby addressing these two interrelated conditions, asthma and obesity. 3 , 15 Given the considerable inter‐individual variability among patients with asthma, personalized exercise prescriptions may lead to better clinical outcomes and quality of life. 36 , 38 Beyond respiratory health control, attention should be given to growth patterns, body composition, and lifestyle counseling, particularly among girls. Early identification of children at higher metabolic risk may help mitigate long‐term cardiometabolic consequences.

Our study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. Asthma diagnosis was based on parental report of a physician diagnosis and did not capture disease severity, persistence, or treatment factors that may influence adiposity trajectories. However, the questionnaires used were based on the standardized International Study of Asthma and Allergies in Childhood (ISAAC), 41 a widely used and validated approach in epidemiological studies. 42 Loss to follow‐up may have introduced selection bias; however, differences between included and excluded participants had small effect sizes, suggesting limited impact on the observed associations. Moreover, body fat percentage categories were defined using UK reference curves, which may not perfectly reflect the distribution of adiposity in Portuguese children. However, these reference curves have the advantage of being age‐ and sex‐specific and being widely used in epidemiological research. In addition, as the study was conducted within a single Portuguese birth cohort, the generalisability of the findings to other populations may be limited. Additionally, transient wheezing and current asthma were not analyzed separately. This may influence the interpretation of findings, potentially attenuating the observed associations.

Despite these limitations, the study has several important strengths. Its prospective design, large population‐based sample, and long follow‐up into adolescence allowed assessment of temporal associations between early‐life asthma and subsequent adiposity. By focusing on incident outcomes and excluding prevalent cases, we reduced the likelihood of reverse causation. Adiposity was assessed using repeated, objective measurements, rather than self‐reported. The use of body fat percentage derived from validated bioelectrical impedance equations, subsequently classified according to the age‐ and sex‐specific body fat reference curves, represents a major methodological advance over BMI‐only approaches, allowing for a standardized assessment of adiposity across developmental stages. Additionally, although body fat percentiles were applied using reference curves available from 5 years onwards, sensitivity analyses restricted to children aged ≥5 years at 4‐year‐old follow‐up yielded consistent results (Table S7). Furthermore, we conducted both complete‐case analyses and analyses using multiple imputations. Results were consistent between both analyses, with comparable effect estimates, direction, and statistical significance. Finally, confounder selection guided by a directed acyclic graph strengthens the robustness of causal inference.

In conclusion, asthma in early childhood was associated with an increased risk of subsequent adiposity when assessed using body fat percentage, particularly among girls. These findings indicate that children with asthma may be prone to excess fat accumulation that is not fully captured by BMI alone. From a clinical perspective, routine follow‐up of children with asthma should extend beyond respiratory control to include careful monitoring of growth patterns and body composition, alongside early counseling on physical activity and healthy lifestyle behaviors. Integrating metabolic risk awareness into pediatric asthma care may help reduce long‐term cardiometabolic burden, especially in girls.
