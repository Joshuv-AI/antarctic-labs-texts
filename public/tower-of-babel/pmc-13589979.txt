Current Perspectives on the Use of Adjuvant Pembrolizumab for Hepatocellular Carcinoma
Authors: Anna Mingazzini, Daniel Riado Minguez, Ylenia Babini, Elia Gigante, Mohamed Bouattour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite implementation of surveillance in at-risk populations and the availability of potentially curative treatment such as resection or ablation, recurrence of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains a frequent clinical challenge driven by both occult residual disease and de novo carcinogenesis, and can have a major impact on patients’ quality of life. Current evidence does not support adjuvant therapy after curative treatment in early-stage HCC, and active surveillance remains the standard of care. Following the historical failure of adjuvant tyrosine kinase inhibitors, the integration of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in advanced HCC treatment has renewed interest in their application in the curative-intent setting, particularly for their potential to eradicate microscopic residual disease. Preliminary evidence on adjuvant PD-1-based combinations supported the plausibility of immune modulation, although most of today’s evidence derives from retrospective heterogeneous cohorts including multiple anti-PD-1 agents. Pembrolizumab, a monoclonal anti-PD-1 antibody that restores antitumor T-cell activity, has recently attracted growing interest on the postoperative management of early- and intermediate-stage HCC. KEYNOTE-937 was a Phase III trial comparing pembrolizumab to placebo after curative treatment whose results failed to demonstrate improvement in recurrence-free survival. This outcome suggested that postoperative PD-1 blockade alone may be insufficient to prevent tumor relapse, especially without proper selection on residual disease or treatment sensitivity. Conversely, pembrolizumab-based strategies administered in the neoadjuvant or perioperative setting remain underexplored. As persistence of tumor antigens with intact tumor burden may offer a more favorable immunological context, this setting may be better suited for enhancing antitumor immune priming. In conclusion, the negative findings of KEYNOTE-937 provide a useful foundation for refining future PD-1-based strategies in HCC. Progress will require integration of molecular residual disease assessment with pathological risk stratification and liver disease etiology, in order to identify patients with immunologically targetable residual disease who may derive meaningful benefit from adjuvant pembrolizumab.

Introduction

As the sixth most common tumor by incidence worldwide and the third leading cause of cancer mortality,1 hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) plays a significant role in clinical oncology and continues to have a strong impact on global cancer mortality.

Advances in modern imaging techniques, along with a stronger emphasis on prevention and surveillance in high-risk individuals, have increased the detection of HCC at an earlier stage, expanding the population eligible for potentially curative interventions such as surgical resection or local ablation.

Following current guidelines, after curative-intent resection or ablation, patients who have achieved a complete response are generally scheduled for a personalized post-treatment surveillance.

However, tumor relapses remain frequent in HCC, usually as local recurrence, with cumulative rates of 35% at 1 year and 70% at 5 years. Recurrence is strongly associated with unfavorable prognosis, reaching a median overall survival of 21 months from relapse.2

The most well-known risk factors for cancer recurrence include tumor size and vascular invasion, although even single lesions under 2 cm have been demonstrated to hide microscopic intrahepatic metastases and microvascular invasion (MVI) that could promote local relapse.3

Therefore, while surgery or ablation can eradicate a dominant visible lesion, these treatments fail to neutralize cancer recurrence by themselves.

The underlying reasons for failure of the current curative treatments to prevent HCC recurrence are related to the etiological heterogeneity of HCC recurrence itself. It is clinically useful to distinguish early recurrence, generally occurring within the first two years after treatment, from late recurrence.4

Early recurrence is considered a tumor-driven process related to unrecognized pre-existing intrahepatic metastases, microscopic residual disease (MRD) or aggressive pathological features of the primary lesion such as microvascular invasion (MVI) and satellite nodules.

Late recurrence, on the other hand, is mostly related to long-term carcinogenic pressure arising from chronically injured liver, where cirrhosis, viral hepatitis, metabolic dysfunction, alcohol-related inflammation, oxidative stress, and persistent immune tolerance uninterruptedly promote hepatocarcinogenesis even after complete resection of a primary tumor.5

This insufficient biological control provided an initial impetus for exploring post-operative systemic treatments whose mechanism could target the biological determinants of recurrence.

One of the earliest efforts to introduce adjuvant therapy in HCC was the phase III STORM trial, in which patients were randomized to sorafenib or placebo after surgery or ablation. Results failed to show a statistically significant improvement in the primary endpoint of recurrence-free survival, providing an early indication that post-curative HCC relapse was determined by biologically heterogeneous factors that could not be managed by an unselected anti-angiogenic therapy.6

The later transition to the immunotherapy era in advanced HCC brought about a paradigm shift in the natural history of disease, not only due to improved survival, but also to increased objective response up to 30% and more durable response when immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)-based combinations were employed.7,8

This rekindled the interest in whether these novel mechanisms of action could improve postoperative outcomes after curative treatment of localized disease. This hypothesis was primarily supported by how the chronically inflamed liver microenvironment is dominated by immunosuppressive cell populations, whose composition varies depending on cancer etiology. Its modulation could potentially have a strong impact on carcinogenesis prevention and development: more specifically, chronic viral hepatitis seems to favor a tolerogenic microenvironment by exhausting effector CD8+ T cells and attracting immunosuppressive cells, while chronic dyslipidemia activates dysfunctional immune cells as natural killer T (NKT) cells, T helper 17 (TH 17) cells, IgA+ plasma cells and CD8+PD1+ T cells that disrupt tumor immune surveillance and favor hepatocarcinogenesis. In addition, alcohol exposure promotes the translocation of bacterial pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) from the gut, which suppresses hepatic immune responses, increases tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) infiltration and promotes infiltration of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSC).9 These converging immunosuppressive mechanisms provided a compelling biological rationale for testing ICI-based strategies in the adjuvant setting.

IMbrave050 phase III trial was the first adjuvant ICI-based study on HCC reported,8 although its initial encouraging trend on recurrence-free survival (HR 0.72) was not sustained at longer follow-up, with a median RFS of 33.2 versus 36.0 months and HR 0.90.10 This trend of earlier separation and later convergence of RFS curves could suggest that adjuvant immunotherapy may have a transient impact on preventing very early recurrences arising from occult micrometastases, although the actual biological mechanisms have not yet been determined.

Pembrolizumab, an anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) antibody, was first tested in monotherapy in advanced HCC as second-line therapy in Phase II KEYNOTE-224, where it showed durable antitumoral responses after sorafenib.11 Later studies include phase III KEYNOTE-240 and KEYNOTE-394 trials, the latter testing an exclusively Asian population and reporting significant results in both overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS), hinting at a possible antitumor activity of pembrolizumab on HCC biology, although without a uniform benefit across all populations.12,13

Against this inconclusive background, phase III KEYNOTE-937 was the first large-scale randomized, double-blind trial to evaluate pembrolizumab as a single-agent adjuvant therapy in HCC patients after curative resection or ablation. Results at a median follow-up of 50.7 months did not show a significant improvement in recurrence-free survival, with median RFS of 46.7 months versus 45.5 for placebo. Similarly, overall survival also did not show a significant improvement (HR 1.08), indicating that clinical response to immunotherapy in advanced disease does not necessarily predict an equivalent activity on MRD eradication in the post-curative setting.14

Current guidelines still frame adjuvant pembrolizumab as purely investigational in clinical trials, while active surveillance remains the standard of care for patients after complete resection or ablation.15

The negative results reflect the complexity of HCC recurrence, supporting the need for a more biologically informed approach to risk stratification and patient selection, in particular with focus on tumor microenvironment modulation before and around curative treatment. Perioperative strategies with pembrolizumab in early-stage HCC have been explored in phase II trials, reporting encouraging survival outcomes.16,17

Overall, the biological rationale for single-agent postoperative PD-1 blockade in the adjuvant setting is supported by its activity on HCC.

However, KEYNOTE-937 results clearly challenge the clinical benefit of non-biomarker-enriched adjuvant pembrolizumab, urging a shift towards biomarker-enriched populations and perioperative approaches, where PD-1 blockade is delivered in a setting where tumor antigen is still present.

This review aims to critically examine clinical evidence, mechanistic rationale, current guideline-based implications, and future biomarker- and timing-driven strategies for adjuvant or peri-curative pembrolizumab in HCC.

Main Body

Biological and Clinical Complexity of HCC Recurrence

When discussing potential adjuvant therapy in HCC, it is fundamental to highlight the heterogeneity of underlying mechanisms still active after curative treatment and leading to local recurrence, frequently the final clinical manifestation of a variety of biological processes.

The most commonly used classification for HCC recurrence makes a temporal and biological distinction between early tumor-driven relapse, related to microscopic residual disease and aggressive pathological features, and late recurrence arising from chronically diseased liver.

Hypothetical preventive immune-modulating strategies cannot be evaluated independently from this distinction between tumor-related and extra-tumoral mechanisms.

For instance, microvascular invasion and satellitosis have been observed in up to 39% of patients with recurrent HCC, with their presence being established as an independent predictor of aggressive disease and mortality.18

A PD-1 inhibitor could contribute to preventing recurrence from an immune-sensitive microscopic residual disease, but it would likely have less of an impact on late multifactorial carcinogenesis.

Recurrence patterns are also strongly related to HCC etiology, particularly in the context of late recurrence.

HCC can either arise from a chronic viral infection, as hepatitis B virus (HBV) promotes carcinogenesis by direct oncogenic mechanisms while hepatitis C virus (HCV) indirectly favors oxidative stress, steatosis and cirrhosis, or chronic inflammation related to alcohol consumption or metabolic liver disease like metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) or metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), which generate immune dysfunction and metabolic stress. Occasional mixed or even cryptogenic etiologies further complicate the patient stratification.19

These distinct recurrence pathways are indeed challenging for the efficacy of an unselected adjuvant immunotherapy in preventing tumor relapse without a deeper investigation into etiological and microenvironmental substrate.

This is especially relevant considering how recurrence-free survival, the most common endpoint in trials in this setting, may combine the results on an immune-sensitive micro metastatic recurrence, an immune-resistant MASH-related carcinogenesis or even de novo HCC with different characteristics and outcomes.

This endpoint heterogeneity may inherently dilute the detectable signal of any adjuvant intervention that acts selectively on one biological pathway, and future trials should consider event-specific analyses or biomarker-enriched stratification to better capture a potential treatment effect.

The Historical and Mechanistic Lessons of STORM and IMbrave050

One of the first efforts to introduce adjuvant therapy in HCC was the phase III STORM trial, in which patients were randomized to sorafenib 400 mg twice a day or placebo for a maximum of 4 years after surgery or ablation. Results failed to show a statistically significant improvement in the primary endpoint of recurrence-free survival, with a median RFS of 33.3 months on the treatment arm versus 33.7 months with placebo (HR 0.94). These results provided early evidence that post-curative HCC relapse is determined by biologically heterogeneous factors unlikely to be managed by an unselected anti‑angiogenic therapy.6

A similar trial emerged when later immunotherapy redefined the advanced HCC standard of care. IMbrave050 was the first phase III study testing the potential efficacy of combined immune checkpoint inhibitors and anti-angiogenic therapy on resected or ablated early HCC. Atezolizumab and bevacizumab were administered for up to 17 cycles after curative treatment and compared to active surveillance.

At 17.4 months, interim results showed a significantly improved recurrence-free survival with HR 0.72.9

However, at a longer follow-up of 35.1 months, this advantage was not sustained, with a median RFS of 33.2 versus 36.0 months for active surveillance, with an HR 0.90.10

In an updated analysis, an exploratory longitudinal assessment of hazard rates showed recurrence occurring later in the experimental arm, supporting that combined programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) blockade may postpone early recurrences in some patients, possibly by targeting occult micrometastases, but fails to generate a durable immune control on the liver. Alternative explanations include bevacizumab-induced changes in tumor vascularity that influence radiological detection of recurrence during treatment, with a hypothetical rebound effect after treatment discontinuation. A further hypothesis focuses on the later convergence of RFS curves as a reflection of the changing biological composition of recurrence events, with progressively more relevance of de novo tumors arising from diseased liver.

These speculative explanations, however, cannot be sufficiently explored by the RFS pattern alone.10

From Pembrolizumab Activity in Advanced-HCC to the Adjuvant Hypothesis

The absence of durable benefit observed in previous trials did not invalidate the potential rationale of postoperative treatment, and more recently KEYNOTE 240/394 trials tested PD-1 blocker pembrolizumab on advanced HCC,12,13 with promising results that informed the adjuvant hypothesis.

The phase II KEYNOTE-224 phase II trial reported an objective response rate (ORR) of 18.3%, with duration of response of 21.0 months, and estimated 77.0% of patients maintaining response at 12 months.11

A second cohort of treatment-naïve advanced HCC patients, in the same trial showed similar results with an ORR 16%, a median OS of 17 months, and 58% of patients alive at 12 months.20

However, the phase III KEYNOTE-240 did not reach the prespecified statistical thresholds and was subsequently declared negative.

Evolution of the therapeutic landscape during trial conduct likely contributed to the discrepancy between clinical activity and statistical failure: advanced-setting therapies such as regorafenib had concurrently become available after sorafenib treatment, likely improving post-progression survival in the placebo arm and subsequently diluting PD-1 blocker impact on overall survival.12

A more favorable outcome was reported by KEYNOTE-394, which compared pembrolizumab as second-line monotherapy to placebo in an exclusively Asian population.

Results showed a significant improvement in both median overall survival (HR 0.79) and progression-free survival (2.6 versus 2.3 months). Objective response rate was higher with pembrolizumab than with placebo (12.7% versus 1.3%), and the median duration of response was 23.9 versus 5.6 months, suggesting that a minority of responders may derive highly durable benefit.

The formally positive outcome of KEYNOTE-394, in contrast to the formally negative KEYNOTE-240 trial, could potentially be interpreted in the light of a more selected patient population and more favorable statistical design, although no direct cross-trial comparison has been published.13

Biological Rationale for Adjuvant Pembrolizumab

Immune surveillance plays a fundamental role in preventing carcinogenesis by recognizing and inhibiting tumor cell growth. However, the liver microenvironment is chronically exposed to numerous exogenous antigens due to its physiological function of blood-borne toxins metabolism, and therefore is an intrinsically tolerogenic organ. This antigen-tolerant state in a chronically inflamed liver, which itself determines immunosuppression, can create a favorable microenvironment for cancer progression.

Several cellular and soluble mediators sustain liver tolerance, including cytokines (interleukin 10 and transforming growth factor-beta, or TGF-β), Kupffer cells and liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSEC). Hepatocytes and other intrahepatic cells also show an overexpression of immune checkpoints including programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and its ligand PD-L1.21

These observations support a well-founded rationale for the application of a PD-1 blockade to restore exhausted cytotoxic T-cell activity and enhance antitumor surveillance inside the liver.

HCC-specific T-cell responses have shown higher activity in earlier disease stages, with SALL4- and CTA-specific immunity reflecting a potential protective antitumor response.22 Therefore, restoring cytotoxic T-cell activation and proliferation after curative treatment could enhance immune control on residual disease.

Outside of a strictly adjuvant program, the phase II trial by Qiao et al studied the effect of anti-PD-1 blockade after transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) and ablation on early-to-intermediate HCC patients at high risk of recurrence. Results were encouraging, with a 1-year RFS of 73.3% versus 46.7% in the group that underwent only locoregional treatment. Local tumor progression (LTP) rate was also significantly reduced in the immunotherapy-treated group.23

The single-arm phase Ib PETAL study also explored the combination of up to two TACE sessions followed by pembrolizumab for up to 1 year in patients with liver-confined HCC. Results demonstrated encouraging efficacy signals, with an objective response rate of 53% at 12 weeks after the last TACE, a median PFS of 8.9 months and a median OS of 33.5 months, without evidence of synergistic toxicity.24

The specific benefit of adjuvant anti-PD-1 therapy in this setting may derive from the reactivation of exhausted antitumor T cells in a more active setting (early HCC) after abrupt tumor antigen release induced by locoregional treatment, amplifying immunosurveillance in a low-burden disease setting, and could be particularly relevant against early recurrence, whose driving factor is residual microscopic disease.

Even after complete radiologic response following resection or ablation, there may still persist occult tumor clones or micrometastases that later emerge as early recurrence. During this interval before macroscopic recurrence, residual malignant cells may still be susceptible to reinvigorated cytotoxic T-cell surveillance.

However, it is still challenging to identify, among curatively treated patients at high risk of recurrence, those truly immunotherapy-sensitive that most likely would benefit from adjuvant treatment.

Clinical evidence for adjuvant PD-1 blockade in HCC before KEYNOTE-937 (Table 1).

Available literature specifically on pembrolizumab in this setting remains limited, with most available evidence derived from heterogeneous postoperative cohorts treated with various PD-1 inhibitors in monotherapy or combined with other systemic or locoregional strategies.

Most available studies were retrospective or propensity-score matched cohorts, with RFS as the primary endpoint at the time of publication, as OS data were still immature or confounded by subsequent treatments at recurrence.

It should also be stressed that available studies were largely conducted in Asian cohorts and, when reported, the underlying etiology was predominantly viral hepatitis, particularly HBV infection. This represents a major limitation to the external validity of these preliminary data in Western populations, whose fastest-growing HCC etiology is related to MASLD/MASH, characterized by a distinct immune and inflammatory microenvironment that may not share the same sensitivity to treatment.

The most relevant evidence for adjuvant pembrolizumab in HCC before KEYNOTE-937 derived from postoperative cohorts, with pembrolizumab included among other anti-PD-1 agents.

In a multicenter propensity score-matched analysis by Xu et al, adjuvant immunotherapy, including pembrolizumab among other agents, was evaluated in patients with intermediate or advanced resected HCC. After matching, adjuvant immunotherapy was associated with improved survival, with a median RFS of 29.6 vs 19.3 months in the control group and a median OS of 35.1 vs 27.8 months.29

Pembrolizumab or tislelizumab were also studied as adjuvant treatment in a retrospective study by Shen et al including high-risk HCC patients after curative hepatectomy. Results showed similar improved outcomes in both RFS and OS, with a 2-year RFS of 74.2% vs 37.8% in the control group and 2-year OS of 95.7% vs 77.0%.26

Zhao et al further suggested a biomarker-oriented perspective for the potential benefit of postoperative anti-PD-1 therapy, including pembrolizumab, in a study that included adjuvant tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) therapy in both cohorts. In particular, the final analysis showed improved outcomes in vessels encapsulating tumor clusters (VETC)-positive HCC, while VETC-negative patients did not show a statistically significant benefit.27

This last interpretation is consistent with recent evidence from advanced HCC, where complete VETC-HCC (defined by VETC in at least 55% of tumoral area) has been associated with a higher objective response rate to atezolizumab plus bevacizumab, in particular 66% compared to 37% of non- or incomplete-VETC-HCC.35

Although relevant, these studies could not demonstrate a pembrolizumab-specific effect in preventing HCC recurrence.

Stronger evidence on adjuvant PD-1 blockade has been sustained by combination studies as the retrospective analysis by Ouyang et al, where high-risk patients with resected HCC received TKI associated with a PD-1 antibody, including pembrolizumab. RFS within the first 6 months after surgery was significantly improved in comparison to hepatectomy alone, but with no significant differences in OS or even RFS beyond the 6 months landmark, suggesting a stronger effect on early recurrence rather than on the more complicated long-term recurrence processes.30

Danzeng et al further examined adjuvant therapy in an MVI-enriched population. After propensity score matching, postoperative lenvatinib plus PD-1 blockade was associated with higher RFS than surgery alone, but still with no significant difference in OS or recurrence type.31

Huang et al extended the combination strategy to locoregional therapy in huge HCC, reporting that in a retrospective propensity score-matched cohort study postoperative adjuvant transarterial chemoembolization (PA-TACE) associated with PD-1 inhibitors, including pembrolizumab, improved both RFS and OS compared with PA-TACE alone.32 Despite these earlier RFS signals, randomized evidence specific to pembrolizumab after resected HCC remained missing.

The strongest signal for adjuvant immune checkpoint blockade in resected HCC actually came from a multicenter, open-label, randomized phase II trial by Wang et al, which included HCC patients with histological microvascular invasion after hepatectomy. The trial specifically compared adjuvant sintilimab, a different anti-PD-1 blocker, to active surveillance, meeting its primary endpoint with a median RFS of 27.7 versus 15.5 months.

These improved outcomes persisted after adjustment for baseline differences in cirrhosis and MVI grade. With currently immature data on the OS curve, however, the clinical durability of this early RFS advantage remains uncertain.33 The sintilimab trial was conducted in a predominantly HBV-positive Chinese population. The generalizability to Western or MASH-related HCC is limited.

A small single-arm phase II trial also investigated a potential role for adjuvant nivolumab, reporting a 1-year RFS of 78.6% and median RFS of 26.3 months after resection or ablation.34

Although most of these studies reported favorable RFS signals with postoperative PD-1 blockade, evidence remained fragmented and largely indirect. In particular, pembrolizumab was generally explored together with other PD-1 inhibitors (Table 1), whose efficacy and pharmacological mechanisms cannot be considered fully interchangeable and support at most a general evaluation of postoperative PD-1 blockade. Interpretation is further limited by the retrospective design and single-center nature of most cohorts, and concomitant treatments such as TKIs or TACE make it difficult to determine the independent contribution of immunotherapy to the observed effects.

Moreover, the available data suggested a stronger effect on early recurrence than on durable survival, consistent with a potential impact on microscopic residual disease but not necessarily on late de novo carcinogenesis.

The phase III KEYNOTE-937 trial was the first large-scale study to directly evaluate pembrolizumab as adjuvant therapy after curative resection or ablation in HCC. This double-blind trial included 959 patients randomized to receive either pembrolizumab 200 mg every 3 weeks up to 17 cycles or placebo (Figure 1). Patient enrollment required complete radiological response after curative treatment, good clinical status and liver function. Randomization was subsequently stratified following geographic region, prior local therapy, recurrence risk (based on tumor number, MVI and histological grade) and AFP level.

At a median follow-up of over four years, there was no statistically significant difference in recurrence-free survival (RFS) between the two groups, with a median RFS of 46.7 vs 45.5 months (HR 1.06, p=0.719). 48-month RFS was 50% for both groups. Overall survival was also similar (HR 1.08, p=0.704), as for 48-month distant metastasis free survival (DMFS), 71% vs 70%, HR 0.98.14

Moving to the safety profile, treatment-related adverse events occurred in 60% of patients who received at least one cycle of pembrolizumab, most frequently pruritus, rash and hypothyroidism. Grade ≥3 adverse events occurred in 32% of patients treated with pembrolizumab versus 22% treated with the placebo arm, and grade ≥3 drug-related adverse events occurred in 14% and 5% of patients, respectively. Immune-mediated adverse events and infusion reactions were also more frequent with pembrolizumab, occurring in 26% versus 7% of treated patients, with corticosteroids being required in 48 patients versus only 1 patient in the control group.14

These data did not reveal any new safety signals, but together with the negative RFS results, it is important to highlight their clinical meaning in a formally disease-free setting.

Immune-mediated hepatitis and corticosteroid exposure are a particularly relevant issue in HCC, where underlying disease and limited liver function may increase the clinical impact of treatment toxicity.

It should also be noted that higher HBV reactivation rates were reported while undergoing immunotherapy (1.9% in patients with HCC versus 0.5% in other cancer types),36 although a post‑hoc analysis on KEYNOTE-224 and KEYNOTE-240 showed no pembrolizumab-attributed viral-induced hepatitis flare in patients with HBV infection or isolated anti-HBc positivity and the only HCV-related flare while undergoing pembrolizumab was not attributed to treatment.37

The negative efficacy results represent an even greater burden when considering that treatment-related adverse effects are experienced by a clinically disease-free population. Together with monitoring requirements and healthcare costs, this further questions the acceptability of routine adjuvant pembrolizumab, although there are still no formal cost-effectiveness analyses in this setting.

Biomarker Strategies After KEYNOTE-937

The negative results of KEYNOTE-937 are best explained in the context of biological recurrence heterogeneity.

Although a variety of high-risk patient characteristics were considered in the trial design, KEYNOTE-937 did not select patients using MRD markers such as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) positivity, PD-L1 expression, immune-inflamed tumors, activated T-cell signatures, or any other biomarker suggesting susceptibility to PD-1 blockade.

The postoperative liver state after HCC treatment is biologically heterogeneous: after curative treatment, some patients may no longer harbor clinically relevant residual disease (MRD-negative). With no remaining malignant cell population to be targeted, pembrolizumab is unlikely to generate a measurable gain in recurrence-free survival.

Current clinicopathological risk factors, including microvascular invasion, tumor size, multifocality, satellitosis, poor differentiation and elevated AFP, enrich recurrence risk but remain imperfect surrogates for biologically active minimal residual disease or immune sensitivity.

Future postoperative trials in HCC could give a stronger focus on MRD assessment as a stratification or selection criterion.

Currently available KEYNOTE-937 data show a stratification by recurrence risk, treatment modality, geographic region and AFP at diagnosis, but did not define a biomarker-positive subgroup with proven benefit from adjuvant pembrolizumab.

Pathological variables such as MVI, satellitosis, VETC pattern, poor differentiation and multifocality may help identify patients with occult dissemination, whereas viral, metabolic and alcohol-related liver disease may shape immune tone and the probability of later de novo carcinogenesis. Therefore, pathological and etiological variables should be integrated in recurrence-risk stratification when selecting patients for adjuvant trials. However, selection or stratification based on MVI should also consider how its recognition can be affected by interobserver variability and differences in tissue sampling.38 Standardized pathological assessment and possibly a central pathology review should be integrated in future trials in order to reduce patient misclassification and between-center heterogeneity.

Patient selection based on postoperative ctDNA positivity, persistent or rising AFP, and other dynamic markers could further help identify patients with ongoing subclinical disease before evident radiological recurrence. However, HCC heterogeneity, low tumor shedding, background cirrhosis, and the absence of standardized assays make ctDNA evaluation challenging and its use is currently limited to trial stratification without implications in routine clinical practice.

Tumor-immune profiling could also be integrated into risk stratification, as translational data from advanced HCC suggest that disease control via pembrolizumab may be associated with a combined positive score (CPS) of PD-L1 expression, low neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, low AFP or protein induced by vitamin K absence or antagonist-II (PIVKA-II). On the other hand, CTNNB1/beta-catenin activation and MET amplification have been observed among non-responders.39

The many interactions between tumor microenvironment and metabolic pathways also add a further layer of complexity, with emerging evidence of a potential role for intratumoral microbial patterns as prognostic and therapeutic biomarkers.40

The key challenge is therefore to determine whether MRD status, tumor immune phenotype and clinicopathological risk can be integrated to identify patients who are most likely to benefit from postoperative PD-1 blockade. Preliminary Evidence of Peri-Curative Immunotherapy (Table 3)

As previously stated, HCC recurrence is determined by different mechanisms varying from residual disease to early intrahepatic dissemination to de novo carcinogenesis of the cirrhotic liver.

One strong limitation of purely postoperative treatment is that most or all tumor antigenic sources might have already been removed by resection or ablation, leaving no microscopic residual disease for immunomodulation to work on.

Therefore, selecting a different timing of perioperative treatment, where immunotherapy can operate with tumor in situ, could open a different therapeutic window.

This different timing hypothesis for pembrolizumab has been preliminarily explored in the NeoLEAP-HCC trial, evaluating perioperative pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib in patients with resectable high-risk HCC. Three preoperative cycles were administered, followed by resection and later continuation of both agents for up to 12 months after surgery.

Although a single-arm phase II study cannot establish a survival benefit, it reported initial evidence of feasibility and biological activity, with a major pathological response in almost 39% of resected patients, pathological complete response in 8%, and significant tumor necrosis in almost 30% of cases. Toxicity was manageable, with grade 3 adverse events documented in 14.0% of patients and no grade 4–5 events, although one of the 43 enrolled patients experienced delayed surgery because of immune-related hyperglycemia with ketoacidosis.16 While the small sample size does not allow a realistic estimate of the true risk, delay in surgery can have a strong clinical relevance in a curative-intent population and should be weighed carefully against an unproven oncological benefit.

As far as the local ablation setting is concerned, the single-arm phase II IKF-IMMULAB trial evaluated pembrolizumab for two cycles before imaging and local therapy (including radiofrequency ablation (RFA), microwave ablation (MWA), brachytherapy or combination with TACE), then continued for up to 12 months. The trial did not meet its expected 30% objective response rate before local therapy, reaching 13.3%. However, median time to recurrence was 16.43 months, with 2-year and 3-year OS rates of 76% and 66%, respectively.17 These preliminary results still cannot give an effective validation for peri-interventional pembrolizumab, but nonetheless they illustrate initial evidence of immunotherapy tested around local treatment rather than after local treatment has been completed.

A similar concept has been further explored with nivolumab in the NIVOLEP trial,42 where two neoadjuvant doses of immunotherapy were administered before electroporation, followed by adjuvant nivolumab. Radiological and pathological responses were reported in 24.2% and 26.3% of cases, with a 1-year local recurrence-free survival of 70.6%, representing a broader evidence base of the potential benefit of immunotherapy in the peri-interventional setting.

The PLENTY41 study is also worth mentioning in this context. In this pilot study, 22 patients with HCC beyond Milan criteria were randomized to receive neoadjuvant pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib before liver transplantation. The 12-month tumor-specific RFS after transplantation was 87.5% with perioperative treatment versus 37.5% in the control group, while 30-month tumor-specific RFS was 37.5% versus 12.5%. Radiological ORR in the treatment group was 30% by RECIST 1.1 and 60% by mRECIST, and 60% of patients had significant tumor necrosis, with complete tumor necrosis of 30%. No acute allograft rejection occurred in either group. The PLENTY study is an important proof-of-concept for the pre-transplant setting, but interpretation is limited by its small sample size and it warrants a caveat about the washout period between pembrolizumab and transplantation, which is still one of the main concerns in liver transplantation in order to minimize rejection risk.43

Additional support for perioperative strategies comes from other studies evaluating survival benefits of perioperative PD-1 blockade treatments.

For instance, CARES-00944 is a multicenter randomized Phase 2/3 trial evaluating perioperative camrelizumab plus rivoceranib versus surgery alone. Results showed a median event-free survival of 42.1 versus 19.4 months (HR 0.59), with a major pathological response in 35% versus 8% with surgery alone, with a 3% rate of complete response. Although not pembrolizumab-specific, CARES-009 provides strong evidence that perioperative combination of PD-1 blockade and anti-angiogenic therapy can improve event-free survival, thus giving more context to the biological and clinical plausibility of perioperative ICI-based combinations in HCC.

Taken together, these studies support how delivering PD-1 blockade while tumor antigen is still present may enhance antitumor immune priming. At the same time, pathological necrosis, major pathological response, and recurrence-related endpoints may offer more informative measures of biological activity than adjuvant recurrence-free survival alone. However, neither endpoint has been validated to this day as a surrogate for long-term outcomes. Their prognostic value therefore remains exploratory and still requires further prospective correlation with HCC survival in larger studies.

Discussion

As for today’s knowledge, pembrolizumab monotherapy does not improve recurrence-free survival or overall survival after complete resection or ablation.

KEYNOTE-937 evaluated a clinicopathologically selected population, although patients were not enriched by either molecular residual disease or predictive tumor-immune biomarkers, which may have limited the ability to identify a subgroup more sensitive to postoperative PD-1 blockade.

There are several biological mechanisms that may explain why unselected adjuvant immunomodulation may fail in HCC. Curative intent treatment such as surgical resection or local ablation can already determine an absence of immunologically targetable residual disease, and at the same time the ongoing oncogenic pressure of the diseased liver that cannot be easily reversed.

RFS as the main endpoint itself may capture biologically distinct events, limiting the ability to demonstrate whether a specific subgroup could benefit from adjuvant treatment.

Classical pathological factors including MVI, tumor dimensions, multifocality or satellitosis, poor differentiation, elevated AFP and margins have a defined prognostic value on recurrence risk, but at the same time they could be insufficient to predict pembrolizumab sensitivity.

Further studies could focus more on identifying biological high-risk features rather than clinical high‑risk ones, prioritizing the identification of biologically active residual disease with immune-sensitive biology, more likely to benefit from immune modulation even in a macroscopically disease-free setting.

Immune-inflamed phenotypes such as high CD8+ T-cell infiltration, interferon-gamma signaling, or other markers of pre-existing antitumor immunity may represent more plausible candidates for PD-1-based strategies, in opposition to immune-excluded tumors, which are generally characterized by WNT/beta-catenin activation, suppressive myeloid infiltration, Treg enrichment and poor antigen presentation.45

More in detail, postoperative ctDNA positivity could be integrated with radiological or molecular marker evaluation, although currently no single biomarker provides a validated predictor of adjuvant therapy benefit in HCC, and ctDNA evaluation in HCC remains challenging given tumor heterogeneity, background cirrhosis and the absence of standardized assays.

A more complete representation of liver disease etiologies should also be considered in patient stratification, in particular evaluating treatment sensitivity in MASLD/MASH-associated HCC together with viral etiologies.

Beyond patient selection, future studies could define the optimal timing for immune checkpoint blockade.

Postoperative therapy is administered after removal or destruction of the dominant antigen source, limiting clonal expansion of tumor-reactive T-cell clones and tissue-based immune monitoring.

Neoadjuvant or perioperative strategies, on the other hand, may deliver treatment in a setting of intact tumor antigen and measurable tumor microenvironment. In addition, local therapies such as ablation may release tumor antigens and inflammatory signals, potentially creating a more favorable window for immune activation.

Pre-existing studies mostly focused on pathological response (MPR), whose value as a surrogate for long-term benefit remains insufficient, and a more structural investigation of long-term oncological outcomes in larger studies is needed.

The perioperative setting also carries specific risks, in particular surgical delay or immune-related adverse events like hepatitis on an already impaired liver reserve, both able to offset the potential treatment benefit if not carefully anticipated and managed, and therefore should be carefully reported in future perioperative studies.

In conclusion, future trials should be biomarker-enriched and MRD-guided, prioritizing an integration of clinic-pathological high-risk features with actionable residual disease traits such as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) positivity, immune-inflamed signatures or VETC pattern.

Recurrence should also be characterized more precisely, as a clearer distinction between early metastatic recurrence and late de novo carcinogenesis could enable a more tailored approach to both adjuvant treatment and surveillance.

Endpoint analysis should also evolve beyond RFS, focusing on other surrogates that could better capture the potential benefit of either active treatment or surveillance, like extrahepatic recurrence, treatment-free survival and eligibility of recurrent disease to local treatment.

Finally, trials evaluating pembrolizumab in perioperative strategies may become of broader relevance, as they could help determine whether tumor antigen persistence can impact the efficacy of immune activation, defining with more precision the potential rationale of PD-1 blockade in early-stage HCC.

Conclusion

Active surveillance remains the standard approach for HCC patients treated with surgical resection or local ablation. However, clinical trial enrollment remains a valid option for individuals with very high-risk pathological features or suspected biologically active residual disease, although multidisciplinary evaluation is fundamental to weigh the potential benefits of adjuvant treatment on the overall history of disease against treatment toxicity.

KEYNOTE-937 provides the latest evidence on adjuvant pembrolizumab monotherapy in HCC, and its negative results do not support empirical postoperative pembrolizumab as standard of care.

This probably suggests that a postoperative PD-1 strategy alone is insufficient to address the different mechanisms underlying HCC recurrence, which represents the final result of heterogeneous processes from residual micro metastatic disease to de novo carcinogenesis.

The potential role of pembrolizumab in early-stage HCC will depend on better patient selection and on matching treatment timing to recurrence biology.

Future progress will likely depend on integrating MRD-guided stratification, ctDNA and AFP dynamics with more conventional clinical and etiological variables.

Neoadjuvant or perioperative strategies may also deserve further investigation given their possibility to exploit in situ tumor antigens.
