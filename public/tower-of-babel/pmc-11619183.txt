The association between systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) and early nosocomial infections after cardiopulmonary bypass surgery in children with congenital heart disease
Authors: Mei Li, Yijun Nie, Zhiyong Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfections occurring postoperatively in pediatric patients with congenital heart disease (CHD) following cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) surgery pose a considerable challenge, affecting the duration of hospitalization, financial costs, and patient outcomes. Studies investigating the association between systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) and early infections after CHD surgery are very rare. This study seeks to delineate the link between SII and the occurrence of early nosocomial infections in pediatric patients undergoing CPB surgery for CHD.MethodsA cross-sectional analysis was performed on 325 pediatric patients who underwent CPB surgery for CHD between July 2020 and June 2023. The primary exposure was the SII value on the first postoperative day. The outcome was the occurrence of nosocomial infections within the first week following CPB surgery. Multivariable logistic regression models and subgroup analyses were employed to evaluate the association between SII and the risk of early nosocomial infections.ResultsThe median age of the study cohort was 4.4 years, with a male preponderance of 51.7%. The median SII value was recorded at 0.6 × 10^12/L. The rate of nosocomial infections within the first week post-CPB surgery was 53.5%. An inverse association was observed between SII and the incidence of early nosocomial infections. After controlling for multiple confounders, an increment of 1 × 10^12/L in SII corresponded to a 25% reduction in the likelihood of nosocomial infections (OR = 0.75; 95%CI: 0.57, 0.99; P = 0.044). Subgroup analyses substantiated the consistency of these findings.ConclusionsThe study demonstrated that an elevated SII corresponded to a reduced likelihood of early nosocomial infections after CPB surgery in children with CHD, a finding that merits additional investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-024-04378-w.Keywords: Systemic immune-inflammation index, Nosocomial infection, Cardiopulmonary bypass, Congenital heart disease

Introduction

Congenital heart disease (CHD) encompasses a range of structural cardiac and intrathoracic vascular anomalies that carry functional implications, representing the most prevalent class of congenital anomalies globally [1]. Varied definitions, demographic factors, and methodological approaches lead to a broad spectrum of reported CHD prevalence, ranging from 4 to 50 cases per 1,000 live births, with approximately 1.5 severe cases and an equivalent number of moderate to severe instances [1]. Surgical intervention is often essential in the management of CHD, with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) playing a pivotal role in these operations. During pediatric open-heart surgery with CPB, factors such as hypothermia, aortic clamping, surgical stress, myocardial and pulmonary ischemia/reperfusion, and other elements trigger neutrophil activation and complement system engagement, leading to the release of substantial endotoxins and inflammatory mediators. This cascade can result in a dysregulation of inflammatory and anti-inflammatory responses, culminating in an inflammatory storm that may impair immune function [2]. Additionally, postoperative interventions like ventilator support, central venous catheters, and pericardial and thoracic drainage tubes introduce further risks for early nosocomial infections [3]. Despite improvements in surgical and perioperative management, infections postoperatively continue to be a major contributor to morbidity and mortality among these patients [4]. Early nosocomial infections, in particular, can lead to extended hospitalizations, heightened healthcare expenditures, and adverse outcomes [5]. Thus, the timely identification of nosocomial infection risk in children with CHD following open-heart surgery with CPB is a critical issue for clinical practitioners.

The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), derived from the counts of neutrophils, platelets, and lymphocytes [Platelet count × Neutrophil count/Lymphocyte count], has gained recognition as a novel biomarker for inflammatory and neoplastic conditions. Some studies have shown that SII has predictive value in the prognosis of patients with heart failure or atrial fibrillation [6, 7]. Prior research has indicated that SII serves as an independent hematological predictor of adverse outcomes following corrective cardiac surgery in patients with acyanotic heart disease and pulmonary hypertension [8]. However, the correlation between SII and the susceptibility to early nosocomial infections has not been thoroughly investigated. This study endeavors to elucidate the association between SII and the occurrence of early nosocomial infections in pediatric patients with CHD following CPB surgery.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

This cross-sectional investigation encompassed 325 pediatric patients who underwent cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) surgery for congenital heart disease (CHD) at the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University from July 2020 to June 2023. Inclusion Criteria: (1) 28 days < age < 18 years; (2) The children were diagnosed with congenital heart disease by preoperative cardiac physical examination, echocardiography, cardiac CT, or thoracic catheterization; (3) Children who underwent open heart surgery under cardiopulmonary bypass in our hospital. Participants were categorized into infection and non-infection groups based on the occurrence of infection within one week post-surgery. Exclusion criteria included patients with active infections within 1 week prior to surgery (manifested by fever, elevated white blood cell count, procalcitonin, C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, or infectious lesions detected on chest X-ray), as well as those lacking data on neutrophil, lymphocyte, or platelet counts on the first postoperative day (Fig. 1). This study was approved by the Scientific Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (2023-E630-01). Clinical trial number: not applicable.

The flow chart of case inclusion study

Preoperative medication and extracorporeal circulation

Prophylactic antibiotics were administered to all pediatric patients preoperatively to mitigate the risk of infection. The antibiotics of choice were cefovecin or cefazolin, with clindamycin as an alternative for patients with cephalosporin allergies. A single intravenous dose was given 30 min before the surgical procedure. Hypothermic CPB was employed during surgery, with the priming solution primarily consisting of colloids, and red blood cell suspension was added sparingly to prevent excessive hemodilution. Myocardial protection was achieved through antegrade delivery at the aortic root, utilizing either St. Thomas’ Hospital cardioplegia solution or histidine-tryptophan-ketoglutarate (HTK) solution.

Postoperative infection diagnostic criteria

Diagnostic criteria for hospital-acquired pulmonary infections were consistent with those outlined in the International ERS/ESICM/ESCMID/ALAT guidelines for the management of hospital-acquired pneumonia and ventilator-associated pneumonia [9].

Diagnostic criteria for sepsis were based on the ‘Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis and Treatment of Pediatric Septic Shock (Infectious Shock) (version 2015)’ [10].

Urinary tract infections: Diagnosis of urinary tract infection is based on either the presence of ≥ 5 white blood cells per high-power field (WBC/HPF) in a clean-catch midstream urine sediment examination or a bacterial colony count of ≥ 1 × 10^5 CFU/mL in urine culture.

Collection of clinical data

Data extracted from the medical records were as follows: age, weight, gender, classification of anesthesia, surgical risk classification, preoperative ejection fraction (EF), aortic cross clamping (ACC) and cardiopulmonary bypass (CBP) time during operation. Laboratory examination data included blood routine examination, procalcitonin (PCT), Pro brain natriuretic peptide (Pro-BNP), Serum lactate levels, liver and renal functions on the first postoperative day. Early nosocomial infections post-CPB surgery included hospital-acquired pulmonary infections, sepsis, bloodstream infections, urinary tract infections and mediastinitis or sternal wound infections. Clinical characteristics were compared among patients classified according to tertiles of SII values [Platelet count × Neutrophil count/Lymphocyte count /1000].

American society of anesthesiologists (ASA) classification

Anesthetic grading was determined based on the preoperative health status of pediatric patients and classified according to the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Physical Status Classification System [11], with grades ranging from 1 to 5.

Risk adjustment for congenital heart surgery 1 (RACHS-1)

Based on the complexity and risk of congenital heart disease surgery in pediatric patients, the surgeries were classified according to the Risk Adjustment for Congenital Heart Surgery 1 (RACHS-1) scale [12], which categorizes procedures into six levels, ranging from 1 to 6.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Categorical variables were presented as percentages and compared using the chi-square test. The Kruskal-Wallis’ test and chi-square tests were applied to compare continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to assess the independent association between SII and the incidence of early nosocomial infections, controlling for potential confounders. The missing values of all collected clinical data indicators were less than 10%, and the missing values were removed for statistical analysis. SII was analyzed as both a continuous and categorical variable in different adjusted models, in accordance with the “Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology” (STROBE) guidelines [13]. Subgroup analyses were conducted using stratified linear regression models, and modifications and interactions of subgroups were evaluated using likelihood-ratio tests.

All analyses were conducted using the statistical software package R 3.3.2 (http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation) and Free Statistics (version 1.9.2). A two-tailed test was performed, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the participants

Data from 325 consecutive patients were analyzed. The baseline characteristics, stratified by tertiles of SII, are detailed in Table 1. The median age of the participants was 4.4 years (IQR, 2.8 to 7.3), with 51.7% being male. The SII values were distributed with a median of 0.6 × 10^12/L (IQR, 0.3 to 1.2). Patients in the highest SII tertile were older and exhibited higher weights, white blood cell counts, albumin, and creatinine levels. The incidence of early nosocomial infections within the first week post-CPB surgery was 53.5% (174 out of 325 patients). Statistically significant differences in the incidence of early nosocomial infections were observed across SII tertiles (P < 0.001). The median time to infection onset in the infection group was 18.5 h (IQR, 12.5 to 21.0). The distribution of infection types included 75% (130 cases) pulmonary infections, 22% (39 cases) pulmonary infections with sepsis, 2% (4 cases) sepsis alone, and 1% (1 case) pulmonary infection with urinary tract infection (Supplementary Figure_1).

Discussion

In this study, we explored the relationship between SII and the incidence of early nosocomial infections in children with CHD following CPB surgery. Across all adjusted models, we observed an inverse relationship between SII and the incidence of early nosocomial infections. Upon conducting a sensitivity analysis by treating the SII as a categorical parameter, we observed a consistent trend. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses confirmed the robustness and stability of our findings.

The Systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), first delineated by Hu et al. [14] in 2014, was a prognostic and readily accessible indicator calculated using the formula ‘SII = platelet count × neutrophil count/lymphocyte count’. This index underscores the critical interplay among neutrophils, lymphocytes, and platelets in inflammatory responses, initially developed to assess the prognosis of hepatocellular carcinoma post-surgical resection. Abundant literature supported the exploration of the SII in relation to cancer prognosis, with meta-analyses highlighting its utility as a prognostic marker for overall survival, progression-free intervals, and responsiveness to immunotherapies across a spectrum of cancer types [15–18]. SII had also been extensively studied for its diagnostic potential in immune and cardiovascular conditions [19–21], likely due to the heightened neutrophil counts in endothelial dysfunction and their synergistic role with platelets in atherosclerosis [22, 23]. Moreover, specific lymphocyte subsets were recognized for their inflammatory modulation and negative impact on atherosclerotic development [24]. A meta-analysis had also revealed that an elevated SII was significantly correlated with an increased risk of postoperative atrial fibrillation following cardiac surgery, underscoring its value as a predictive biomarker [25]. However, the number of studies concentrating on infectious outcomes was comparatively limited in relation to other fields [26]. Elevated SII levels had been correlated with higher mortality in sepsis patients [27], while a distinct study within a sepsis cohort revealed an inverted J-shaped correlation between SII levels and the 28-day mortality risk, identifying the nadir of this risk at SII level of 0.774 × 10^12/L [28]. The relationship between the SII and the mortality rate in sepsis remains unclear at present. Research on SII in the field of pediatric infections is exceedingly scarce. Although Walian et al. [8] have indicated that SII is a potent predictor of poor outcomes in children undergoing CPB surgery for acyanotic CHD, the association between SII and the risk of early postoperative infections in these patients has not yet been explored.

It is noteworthy that the majority of current literature reports a positive correlation between SII and poor outcomes in various diseases. In contrast, our study reveals a negative correlation between SII and the risk of early postoperative infections following CPB surgery for CHD. This discrepancy may be attributed to the fact that our research focused on SII levels on the first postoperative day, whereas most literature examines SII levels at admission or preoperatively. In pediatric cardiopulmonary bypass procedures for open-heart surgery, a cascade of inflammatory responses is triggered by hypothermia, aortic clamping, and surgically induced trauma, alongside ischemia/reperfusion injury to the myocardium and lungs [2]. This inflammatory milieu and the concomitant immunosuppression can significantly alter the levels of platelets, neutrophils, and lymphocytes, which are integral components of the SII calculation. Platelets, traditionally viewed as hemostasis agents, are now recognized as multifaceted players in the inflammatory and immune responses [29–31]. The interaction between platelets and neutrophils is particularly noteworthy, as it can induce the release of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) in response to bacterial or viral infections; platelets also internalize pathogens and may directly kill infected cells, highlighting their crucial role in both initiating and resolving inflammation in response to infection [32, 33]. In summary, the recruitment of neutrophils by platelets in response to pathogen detection may result in a reduced count of these cells in circulation, leading to a lower SII. This inverse relationship between SII and the incidence of postoperative infections suggests its utility as a prognostic biomarker in clinical practice.

The subgroup and sensitivity analyses showed that our results were stable. SII may provide auxiliary utility for the early prediction of nosocomial infections in pediatric patients following CPB surgery for CHD. However, the study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causality. Second, the study was conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future prospective, multicenter studies are needed to validate these results.

Conclusion

Our study provides evidence that SII is negatively correlated with early nosocomial infections after CPB surgery in children with CHD. Higher SII values may be associated with a lower risk of early nosocomial infections. This correlation deserves further investigation.
