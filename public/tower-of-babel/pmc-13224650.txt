Not just a calling: the psychological logic of medical specialty preferences among second-year medical students in Poland
Authors: Maciej Walkiewicz, Mateusz Guziak, Katarzyna Nowicka-Sauer, Matylda Czarnecka, Magdalena Błażek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe choice of medical specialty does not arise solely from a sense of calling, but is a complex psychological and social process. The goal of the study was to examine whether selected psychological characteristics: emotional intelligence (EI; overall score and the dimensions of recognizing and using emotions) and sense of coherence (SOC; overall score and the dimensions of comprehensibility, manageability, and meaningfulness), together with indicators of student functioning (study-related satisfaction, study-related stress, peer relationships, financial status), are associated with medical students’ declared specialty choice (procedural vs. non-procedural) as well as with their preferences for commonly chosen specialties.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 300 s-year medical students at Medical University in Gdańsk, Poland. Standardized tools were used to measure EI (Emotional Intelligence Questionnaire - INTE - Polish version of Schutte Self-Report Emotional Intelligence Test), SOC (Sense of Coherence Scale - SOC-29) and self-developed survey to assess academic experiences (study-related satisfaction and stress, peer relationships, financial status). Data were analysed using multinomial logistic regression.ResultsHigher overall EI was associated with a preference for non-procedural specialties. The ability to recognize emotions was negatively associated with choosing paediatrics and non-procedural specialties. Higher study satisfaction favoured a preference for paediatrics and non-procedural specialties. A preference for plastic surgery was associated with higher overall EI and lower overall SOC, whereas a preference for endocrinology was associated with better self-reported peer relationships.ConclusionsMedical students’ specialty preferences are associated with psychological factors and academic experiences. The results may support career counselling and mentoring programs, helping students make more informed career decisions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09195-7.Keywords: Students, Medical; Career Choice; Specialty Preference; Emotional Intelligence; Sense of Coherence; Cross-Sectional Studies

Background

The choice of medical specialty is one of the key milestones in shaping the professional career of future doctors. These decisions are influenced by multiple factors, including clinical interests, work-life considerations, availability of residency positions, and financial prospects [1, 2]. Attention has also been paid to the psychological determinants, such as personality, motivation, and emotional intelligence (EI) [3, 4]. One of the most frequently discussed distinctions in the literature is between procedural and non-procedural specialties. Some studies report that students inclined toward procedural medicine tend to score higher on attributes such as action orientation, decisiveness, self-confidence, and risk tolerance, while group differences in empathy appear across specialty interests rather than uniformly by an procedural/non-procedural divide [5–7]. In contrast, preferences for non-procedural specialties are often associated with communication skills, interpersonal sensitivity, and an emphasis on long-term relationships with patients [1, 8]. External reasons (e.g., status, prestige, wealth) and internal motivations (e.g., feeling of mission, value congruence) have been identified as factors influencing speciality choice, with variations in patterns observed across different contexts and research [2]. Beyond this procedural/non-procedural division, specialty choice is also shaped by psychological characteristics. For example, psychiatry attracts students with higher empathy and openness to experience and an interest in relational aspects of care [3, 9]. Internal medicine, which emphasizes broad clinical knowledge and analytical reasoning, is selected by students with a strong cognitive orientation [1]. Family medicine’s focus on continuity of care aligns with a relationship-oriented profile [10]. Plastic surgery is linked to strong achievement needs, perfectionism, and aesthetic interests [3, 11]. Neurology attracts students who are comfortable with diagnostic complexity and clinical uncertainty [12]. Paediatrics commonly appeals to students with a prosocial orientation and high empathy [9, 13].

From a psychological perspective, the role of emotional intelligence (EI) in medical career decision-making is interesting. A systematic review found associations between EI and doctor-patient communication and aspects of clinical performance [4]. Individuals with higher EI choose specialties requiring intensive interpersonal interaction [13]. But because EI is made up of several parts, these parts may not always fit the needs of different specialities. For instance, interpersonal empathy is often highlighted in e.g., paediatrics, psychiatry, and family medicine. Emotion regulation is relevant in fields that involve high emotional load, such as neurology, oncology, and palliative care. A systematic review found associations between EI and doctor-patient communication and aspects of clinical performance, while noting methodological limitations in the evidence base [4]. Individuals with higher EI have been reported to choose specialties requiring intensive interpersonal interaction [13]. However, due to the multidimensional nature of EI, its components may align differently with the demands of various specialties. For instance, interpersonal empathy is often highlighted in pediatrics, psychiatry, and family medicine. Emotion regulation is relevant in fields that involve high emotional load, such as neurology, oncology, and palliative care. Assertiveness and clear emotional expression are frequently discussed in relation to surgery and emergency medicine. Relationship-building skills are emphasized in longitudinal care settings, such as internal medicine and geriatrics [4, 14].

Another potential predictor of professional adaptation in medicine is the sense of coherence (SOC) [15, 16]. Evidence on the association between SOC and specialty choice is mixed and currently insufficient for firm conclusions. Some single-centre reports suggest that selected SOC components (particularly meaningfulness) may relate to preferences for certain specialties, but robust multicentre replications and focused systematic reviews are lacking [17].

Moreover, greater academic workload and time pressure during medical school have been associated with higher decision-related stress, while higher study satisfaction may mitigate this stress [18–20]. Peer influences have also been described as shaping specialty preferences [21, 22]. Finally, financial considerations are frequently cited; however, the empirical association between debt and specialty choice is inconsistent across studies [20, 23].

To date, EI and SOC have rarely been examined jointly in relation to specialty preferences, despite representing complementary dimensions of psychological functioning. The former captures emotional processing in interpersonal contexts, the latter reflects a global dispositional orientation toward stress, resources, and meaning. An integrated assessment of both constructs is therefore needed. An integrated assessment of whether EI and SOC are related to specialty preferences is therefore needed. A better understanding of these associations may support career counselling and the design of mentoring programs for medical students. In this study, we attempt to empirically examine these relationships by analysing the links between, among others, EI and SOC and declared specialty preferences.

The goal of the study was to examine whether selected two key psychological characteristics: emotional intelligence (EI; overall score and the dimensions of recognizing and using emotions) and sense of coherence (SOC; overall score and the dimensions of comprehensibility, manageability, and meaningfulness), together with indicators of student functioning (study-related satisfaction, study-related stress, peer relationships, financial status), are associated with medical students’ declared specialty choice (procedural vs. non-procedural) as well as with their preferences for commonly chosen specialties. Specifically, this study addresses two questions. First, we ask whether EI, SOC, and indicators of student functioning are associated with students’ preference for procedural versus non-procedural specialties. Second, we examine whether these same factors are associated with preferences for specific commonly chosen specialties.

Methods

Participants

A total of 300 second-year medical students from the Medical University of Gdańsk (Poland) participated. Of 445 invited students, 300 completed the survey (response rate = 67.4%). Recruitment was conducted via student group representatives and announcements during scheduled psychology classes. Participation was voluntary and anonymous; data were collected by an individual independent of the research team, and all records were handled confidentially. Data collection took place in April-May 2024. The survey was administered online using the Webankieta platform (https://www.webankieta.pl/). Participants were introduced to the study during the ‘Psychology in Medicine’ course. At the conclusion of the lectures, a QR code was provided, enabling students to access the survey at any time. Additionally, the link to the questionnaires was distributed to students via official university mailing lists dedicated to the second-year cohort.”

Students were included in the study if they were actively enrolled in their second year at the time of the survey and possessed a fluent command of Polish. Students who were not actively enrolled in the second year or who had insufficient Polish proficiency were excluded.

Sample composition

Modelling was conducted on 240 observations from the dataset. Sixty observations were excluded due to missing data on variables included in the modelling. The dataset comprised n = 157 women (65.4% of the sample) and n = 79 men (32.9%). Participants were aged 19 to 38 years (M = 21.4, SD = 1.9, Med = 21). Their places of residence were as follows: rural areas (n = 57, 23.75%), towns up to 100,000 inhabitants (n = 60, 25%), cities with 100,000–500,000 inhabitants (n = 73, 30.42%), and cities with more than 500,000 inhabitants (n = 50, 20.83%).

Methods

Emotional Intelligence Questionnaire - INTE (Polish version of Schutte Self-Report Emotional Intelligence Test - SSEIT). This instrument measures general emotional intelligence and two subscales: (a) the ability to recognize, understand, and regulate emotions - both one’s own and others’, and (b) the ability to effectively use emotions to guide personal behaviour or influence others [24, 25].

Sense of Coherence Scale -SOC-29. This standardized questionnaire assesses the global sense of coherence and its three components: comprehensibility, manageability, and meaningfulness. Sense of coherence is defined as a global orientation that expresses the extent to which one has a pervasive, enduring, though dynamic feeling of confidence that stimuli from the internal and external environment are structured, predictable, and explicable; that resources are available to meet the demands posed by these stimuli; and that these demands are challenges worthy of investment and engagement [26]. Respondents rate 29 statements on a 7-point scale reflecting various reactions.

Preliminary Interview Questionnaire. A self-developed survey created in accordance with the study’s objectives and conceptual framework (see Supplementary Material 1). It included the following sections:

A)Preferred medical specialty. Participants indicated up to three preferred specialties (rank-ordered) from a list of nationally recognized specialties. For analytical purposes, specialty selections were additionally collapsed into two broad categories: procedural and non-procedural. The classification was established a priori by consensus of the research team: procedural specialties were defined as those involving frequent operative interventions and manual procedures as a core component of routine practice (e.g., general surgery, plastic surgery, orthopaedics, ophthalmology, gynaecology), whereas non-procedural specialties were defined as those relying predominantly on diagnostic assessment, prolonged patient interaction, and pharmacological management (e.g., internal medicine, endocrinology, psychiatry, neurology, paediatrics). Statistical analysis

A series of analyses were conducted using logistic regression modelling. The dependent variable in all tested models was the participant’s declared intention to choose a specialization classified as belonging to either procedural or non-procedural medicine (the “non-procedural” category was coded as a “success,” i.e., 1, while “procedural” was coded as 0). This variable was constructed by assigning each of the possible preferences to the appropriate category.

Independent variables included the scores from the SOC questionnaire, analysed both as subscales (comprehensibility, manageability, and meaningfulness) and as a general SOC score, as well as the scores from the Emotional Intelligence questionnaire, analysed both as subscales (1 - the ability to recognize, understand, and regulate emotions - both one’s own and those of others; 2 - the ability to effectively use emotions to guide personal behaviour or influence others) and as a general INTE score. In addition, Likert-scale measures were included: satisfaction with studying medicine, study-related stress, peer relationships, and financial status.

Subsequently, we attempted to fit logistic regression models in which the dependent variable was the choice of a specific specialization. From the pool of all specializations listed in the questionnaire, only those selected by at least 10% of respondents were included in the modelling process (i.e., chosen by a minimum of 10% of participants). It should be noted that the maximum percentage of responses for any single specialization was 15%. Independent variables for these models were selected in the same way as for the models with procedural/non-procedural specialization as the dependent variable. First, multiple logistic regression models were fitted, followed by simple logistic regression models.

All analyses were performed using the R statistical environment [27] and RStudio [28]. Several additional packages were also used: psych [29] and tidyverse [30].

Reflexivity statement

The author team consists of psychologists and medical educators who have worked closely with medical students over the past two decades, witnessing substantial shifts in Polish higher medical education firsthand. We acknowledge that our strong commitment to student well-being may inherently influence our interpretation of the findings. To mitigate this potential bias, we relied on rigorous statistical analyses and triangulated our results with the broader international literature on the subject. We also recognise that, as faculty members at a single institution, our observations may not fully capture the diversity of medical student experiences across Poland.

Taken together, significant predictors appeared selectively and explained a modest share of variance (pseudo-R² typically 0.05–0.10), suggesting that students’ specialization preferences reflect a multifactorial configuration of psychological and contextual characteristics. Cross-sectionally, the clearest patterns involved higher General EI (typically a positive association) and satisfaction with studying (positive), with specialization-specific differences.

Participants selected from a pool of 68 nationally recognized medical specialties. The distribution of preferences was highly dispersed: four specialties exceeded the 10% endorsement threshold and were included in the regression models (plastic surgery, endocrinology, neurology, and paediatrics), while the remaining 64 specialties, including family medicine/general practice, psychiatry, dermatology, and anaesthesiology, each fell below this threshold. The maximum endorsement rate for any single specialty was 15%. Specialties below the 10% threshold were excluded from regression modelling due to insufficient statistical power and the risk of unstable parameter estimates; however, they were retained in the binary procedural/non-procedural classification analysis.

Discussion

The findings of our study indicate that the two key psychological constructs examined EI and SOC showed distinct patterns of association with specialty preferences. EI emerged as a consistent predictor at the broad category level (procedural vs. non-procedural) and across several individual specialties, while SOC revealed a specialty-specific pattern, with a significant negative association with plastic surgery. Contextual indicators: peer relationships, academic satisfaction, and financial status contributed selectively and are discussed below as secondary findings. In line with previous literature [2, 4], these results suggest that psychological determinants should be considered on an equal footing with traditional predictors of a medical career.

Direct comparisons with earlier studies are constrained, as a small number of investigations have concurrently analysed EI SOC concerning speciality preferences inside a singular analytical framework. Most previous studies have concentrated on personality traits, empathy, or motivational factors (13; 19). The limited availability of integrated psychological treatments highlights the exploratory character of our findings and the necessity for replication in larger and more heterogeneous groups.

In this study, a significant association was found between the overall level of EI and the preference for procedural versus non-procedural specialties. Students with higher EI more often reported an interest in non-procedural fields. This trend most likely reflects the nature of such specialties, which largely rely on empathy in communication and long-term, more intensive relationships with patients [4, 13]. However, it should be emphasized that lower EI in this context should not be interpreted as a competence deficit of those preferring procedural specialties. It may instead indicate different psychological functioning styles, such as task orientation, quick decision-making, higher tolerance for pressure and uncertainty, and less reliance on the interpersonal dimensions of care.

It is also intriguing that a higher score on the recognizing emotions (INTE subscale) was associated with a lower probability of choosing a non-procedural specialty. A similar pattern also appeared in the analysis of paediatrics, which seems to require above-average communication skills, patience, and the ability to work with children and their families in emotionally intense situations [9, 13]. This may suggest that perceptual sensitivity to emotional signals (recognize, understand, and regulate emotions) alone, if not supported by an equally high ability to use emotions to guide behaviour and influence others (ability to effectively use emotions to guide personal behaviour or influence others), potentially increases the cost of emotional labour and favours avoidance of fields with high and prolonged relational burden. In this sense, individuals highly sensitive to emotions may prefer a work environment where contacts are shorter, more episodic, and task-focused (e.g., the operating room), rather than long-term relationships with the patients and their family. In paediatrics, an additional burden is triadic communication (child-parent-doctor), which increases emotional and regulatory effort. It is possible that the lack of competence in using and managing emotions in regulating behaviour in social interactions may discourage some students from preferring this field. The result concerning paediatrics also revealed a significant association with academic satisfaction. This may suggest that students who are more engaged and satisfied with their studies more often choose specialties with a strong prosocial component. Academic satisfaction therefore seems to serve as an indicator of adaptive functioning, coping with stress, constructive relationships with teaching staff, and a positive attitude toward the future profession, all qualities particularly desirable in caring roles. Alternatively, this relationship may be bidirectional. Students who identify early with the mission of helping children may approach their studies with greater intrinsic motivation and a sense of purpose, which translates into higher educational satisfaction. In this sense, the preferences of paediatrics may be not only a result but also a predictor of the educational experience, reflecting deeper links between professional identity and the perception and internalization of medical education. However, we did not obtain confirmation of this speculation, for example in the associations between the choice of paediatrics and the SOC variable.

Another exception from the general trend was plastic surgery, more often chosen by individuals with a higher level of overall EI. This may suggest that this specialty sets requirements that go beyond technical proficiency and surgical precision. It often involves intimate conversations about appearance and body image, complexes, or experienced trauma resulting from damage to the face or other body parts. This requires physicians to navigate the complex emotions of patients with sensitivity and empathy [11]. Especially the aesthetic segment is heavily burdened by the process of negotiating expectations and dealing with possible patient disappointment. In this field, especially in private practice, a patient “client”-oriented model occurs more often than elsewhere. Higher EI may facilitate clear communication of possibilities and limitations and de-escalation of post-procedural tension. This pattern illustrates the added value of examining SOC alongside EI: the SOC finding for plastic surgery would have been missed entirely in a study focused solely on EI and it opens a distinct line of inquiry into how global dispositional orientations, beyond emotion-specific competences, shape specialty preferences.

The analysis of SOC at the macro level did not show significant differences between the preferences of procedural and non-procedural specialties. The only exception was plastic surgery. A higher overall SOC level was associated with a lower probability of choosing this field, while the “manageability” component showed a positive trend. This may reflect the divergent action of SOC components. “Manageability” (a sense of having resources) supports functioning in an environment requiring precision, advanced communication, and management of expectations. On the other hand, elements related to the need for comprehensibility and meaningfulness may discourage engagement in a field where subjective patient satisfaction is more difficult to control. The observed effects were of small magnitude and borderline statistical significance; therefore, we interpret them with caution and recommend replication in larger, independent samples.

Another interesting finding was the positive correlation between preference for endocrinology and the quality of peer relationships. This may highlight the importance of relational competences in shaping interest in this specialty. Endocrinology, focused on long-term treatment of chronic diseases (e.g., thyroid disorders, diabetes, or hormonal conditions), likely requires patience, building trust, and effective interdisciplinary cooperation. Students reporting strong peer ties may have greater social competence and thus be better prepared for environments that require adjustment to team-based styles of work.

The relationship between preference for neurology and students’ subjectively perceived financial status can be interpreted in various ways. One possible explanation is that individuals in a better financial situation are more willing to choose specialties that are intellectually demanding, time-consuming, and burdened with greater diagnostic uncertainty, such as neurology. This field is perceived as cognitively rigorous and less immediately rewarding, which may discourage students experiencing pressure for quick financial independence. At the same time, neurology combines high prestige with the need for broad clinical and cognitive competences and resilience to ambiguity and complexity in decision-making [12]. In academic centres, it is also often associated with the additional burden of scientific activity. Students who feel financially secure may therefore be more likely to decide on this demanding path. The influence of third variables (e.g., academic achievements, personality traits, or socioeconomic background) and the limitations of self-report cannot be excluded. Due to the lack of consistent theoretical foundations in the existing literature, we also treat this result as preliminary and in need of replication and further analysis. It should be noted that the Polish healthcare system operates on a dual model. Physicians frequently combine lower-paid employment in the public sector (National Health Fund) with more lucrative private practice. Substantial income variation across specialties arises primarily from opportunities for private outpatient care, on-call supplements, and regional demand rather than from fee-for-service differentials as in some other healthcare systems. Specialities such as endocrinology, dermatology, and plastic surgery, which facilitate a smoother transition to private practice, are frequently regarded as providing a more expedient path to financial stability. This backdrop is pertinent as the correlation between perceived financial position and speciality preference identified in our data may indicate students’ recognition of these systemic disparities, rather than decisions influenced by debt, as noted in several US research [23].

The observed relationships generally highlight the importance of non-cognitive and psychosocial variables in the process of specialty preferences. Although these decisions are sometimes presented as rational or driven by the labour market, they also reflect the psychological characteristics of the individual and the socio-environmental contexts in which future doctors function. These results therefore broaden the understanding of how psychological variables interact in shaping specialty preferences. This is consistent with other publications suggesting that specialty preferences is not purely pragmatic but deeply rooted in personal experiences and social representations of the physician’s role [2, 3]. These conclusions confirm that medical students’ career decisions are multidimensional and shaped at the intersection of internal resources and external influences.

Implications for practice

The findings of this study carry practical implications both for those responsible for medical education and for students planning their careers. They highlight the importance of psychosocial variables in guiding specialty preferences. The results may also be used to develop individualized career counselling models, for example, assigning students to mentoring programs based on psychological profile. This may reduce the risk of burnout and increase long-term job satisfaction, and consequently lead to better patient care. In view of the links between EI and specialty preferences, including EI training in curricula may increase students’ adaptive flexibility and better prepare them for the challenges of different specialties. From the perspective of the health care system, taking psychosocial predictors of specialty choice into account may support strategic workforce planning, especially in shortage areas, which is what we are currently experiencing in Poland. Medical graduates are motivated by higher remuneration to undertake shortage specialties. A better understanding of students’ motivations can help design more targeted interventions that attract candidates to less popular fields.

The low endorsement of family medicine among second-year students deserves particular attention. This pattern aligns with established patterns in Central and Eastern Europe, where family medicine is often regarded as having less prestige, autonomy, and compensation compared to hospital-based and procedural specialities [31, 32]. In Poland, this perception is compounded by the structural underinvestment in primary care within the public healthcare system. Our finding, while not amenable to regression modelling due to the low endorsement rate, serves as an indirect indicator of the attitudinal challenges facing primary care recruitment and reinforces the importance of early career counselling interventions targeting shortage specialties. Future studies with larger samples or oversampling designs could enable formal modelling of predictors for less frequently preferred specialties, including family medicine.

Implications for research

The presented results open several new directions for research on the psychological determinants of medical specialty preferences. In particular, it would be important to follow students across subsequent stages of education and professional development, which would make it possible to assess the stability of these preferences. Larger and more diverse samples (different years, universities, countries) will increase the possibility of generalization and allow cross-cultural comparisons, revealing the role of educational and systemic contexts. Future studies could include, among other factors, tolerance of ambivalence, decisional consistency, attachment style, psychological resilience, and specific clinical experiences during studies and internship as potential mediators or moderators of the decision-making process. Additionally, qualitative studies may provide deeper, contextual insight into students’ motivations and decision strategies. Further work could also examine whether the match between the chosen specialty and the individual emotional-personality profile predicts long-term indicators such as job satisfaction, level of burnout, quality of relationships with patients, and the risk of problematic use of substances (e.g., alcohol, prescription medications, psychoactive substances) and other addictive behaviours.

Strengths and limitations

Several important limitations should be taken into account that affect the scope of interpretation and the possibility of generalization. Participants in our study reported preferred specialties rather than actual choices. Preferences may change with clinical experience, exam results, or labour market dynamics. All data came from self-reports, which carry the risk of cognitive distortions. The study was conducted on a limited sample at a single university among second-year students, which narrows the possibility of generalizing the conclusions to the broader population of medical students. The study was cross-sectional, which prevents causal inferences about the relationships among the analysed variables. A crucial methodological limitation concerns the sample size in our specialty-specific logistic regression models. According to the events-per-variable (EPV) criterion, logistic regression models generally require 10 to 15 events (i.e., students choosing a given specialty) per predictor variable to avoid the risk of overfitting. Given the relatively small absolute number of students opting for specific fields—such as endocrinology or plastic surgery—our models for these individual specialties are statistically underpowered. Consequently, these specific findings, including the association between a preference for endocrinology and better peer relationships, should be interpreted with caution as purely exploratory and hypothesis-generating. They require validation in larger, multi-centre cohorts.

The binary classification (procedural vs. non-procedural specialisations) represents an analytical simplification, and some specialties may encompass both procedural and non-procedural elements. Future studies may benefit from alternative or multi-dimensional classification frameworks.

The decision to study second-year medical students was made for several reasons. At this stage, students have had limited clinical exposure and have not yet entered clinical clerkships, which allows the identification of baseline specialty preferences that are relatively uninfluenced by clinical rotations, role models, or institutional socialization [1, 33]. It enables examination of psychological factors that precede, rather than follow from, clinical experience. Second, this approach aligns with the Professional Identity Formation (PIF) literature, which emphasises that career-relevant attitudes and identities begin forming early in medical training, well before students enter the clinical environment [34]. Third, comparable designs have been employed in published studies examining psychological predictors of specialty interest among pre-clinical students [13]. Preferences at this stage are preliminary and may shift substantially over the course of training. However, understanding the early psychological architecture of these declarations is valuable because it identifies pre-clinical determinants that later interact with clinical exposure and systemic factors.

Despite these limitations, in our view this work makes a valuable contribution to knowledge about the psychological determinants of medical specialty choice and may provide a solid basis for further, more comprehensive studies.

Conclusions

The study results indicate that the choice of medical specialty is not “only a calling,” but a complex process determined not only by academic and systemic factors, but also by individual psychological resources. The links between the key psychological constructs EI and SOC and specialty preferences proved particularly informative, with EI showing broad associations across the procedural/non-procedural divide and SOC revealing a specialty-specific pattern for plastic surgery. In addition, significant correlations were noted between selected specialties (such as paediatrics, plastic surgery, endocrinology, and neurology) and psychosocial variables, including the quality of peer relationships, financial status, and academic satisfaction.

These findings suggest that specialty decisions are strongly embedded in students’ individual experiences and socio-emotional functioning. In short, it is not only a calling, but also a psychological profile that can be identified, supported, and shaped on the way to a better match of the doctor-specialisation.
