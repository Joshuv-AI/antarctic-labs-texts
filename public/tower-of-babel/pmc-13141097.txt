Beyond protocols: what are nurses’ lived experiences and perceived systemic barriers in central venous catheter management in a resource-limited tertiary hospital in Ghana? A qualitative study
Authors: Mohammed Rafik Rajah, Yakubu H. Yakubu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore nurses’ experiences of central venous catheter (CVC) management in a resource-limited tertiary hospital.DesignA qualitative exploratory-descriptive study using in-depth, semistructured interviews.SettingA low-resource tertiary care facility in Ghana, West Africa.Participants13 registered nurses working in intensive and critical care units.MethodsIndividual semistructured interviews were conducted and analysed using reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsFour (4) main themes and 14 related subthemes were identified. The main themes included (1) system constraints shaping CVC care (supplies, infrastructure and workload); (2) practice and protocol feasibility (infection prevention and workarounds); (3) capability building and support (knowledge/skills, practical training, supervision and mentoring and nurses’ voice) and (4) psychosocial impact (burnout, fear of complications and limited emotional support). Nurses described confidence in routine tasks but reported limited support for managing complications and sustaining protocol adherence.ConclusionsIn resource-limited settings, effective CVC management extends beyond individual clinical competence and requires robust institutional support, adequate staffing, ongoing professional development and responsive organisational systems. Addressing these structural and psychosocial factors is essential to improving nursing practice, enhancing patient safety and supporting global infection prevention priorities.

Introduction

Central venous access devices (CVADs) are vascular access devices whose catheter tip terminates in a central venous location, specifically at the cavoatrial junction or in the lower third of the superior vena cava or, when inserted via the femoral route, in the inferior vena cava above the renal veins.1 According to the World Congress on Vascular Access (WoCoVA) and NAVIGATE (NomenclAture Via Integrated Global Advancements in Terminology Efficiencies) classification, this group includes devices such as centrally inserted central catheters, peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs), implanted ports and femorally inserted central catheters.1 It is noteworthy that, in Ghana, the term ‘central venous catheters’ (CVCs) is widely used and is also referred to as ‘central venous access devices’, which are essential tools in the management of patients who are critically ill.

They enable the administration of vasoactive medications, parenteral nutrition, blood products and long-term intravenous therapy, as well as haemodynamic monitoring and renal replacement therapy in intensive care settings. Contemporary clinical standards emphasise that CVCs play a critical role in modern infusion therapy and critical care delivery across healthcare systems worldwide.2 3

Despite their clinical importance, CVCs are invasive devices associated with a range of potential complications, including mechanical injury during insertion, catheter thrombosis and catheter-related bloodstream infections (CRBSIs).4 5 These complications may occur during or after catheter placement and can significantly increase patient morbidity, hospital length of stay and healthcare costs.6 CRBSIs remain a major source of healthcare-associated infection globally and contribute substantially to hospital morbidity and mortality.7

To minimise these risks, several international organisations have developed evidence-based recommendations for the indication, insertion and maintenance of CVADs. Recent consensus statements and clinical standards emphasise strict aseptic technique, appropriate device selection, ultrasound-guided insertion, routine assessment of catheter necessity and adherence to infection-prevention strategies as key components of safe CVC management.6 These guidelines also highlight the importance of clinician competence, multidisciplinary collaboration and ongoing monitoring of vascular access outcomes to ensure patient safety.3

However, translating evidence-based guidelines into routine clinical practice remains challenging, particularly in resource-constrained health systems. Hospitals in low- and middle-income countries frequently face structural limitations, such as shortages of sterile supplies, high patient workloads and limited specialised training in vascular access management. These contextual factors may influence adherence to recommended practices and contribute to variations in the quality and safety of CVC care.

Nurses play a central role in the maintenance and monitoring of CVCs, including infection prevention, catheter site care and early detection of complications. Their daily experiences at the bedside provide critical insights into the organisational, clinical and resource-related factors that shape CVC management practices. However, while quantitative research has largely focused on infection rates, complication incidence and procedural outcomes, relatively little is known about the lived experiences of nurses responsible for CVC care in resource-limited critical care environments.

Understanding these frontline experiences is essential for identifying barriers to safe CVC management and for developing context-appropriate interventions that strengthen infection prevention and vascular access practices. Exploring these perspectives may also inform targeted training programmes, institutional policies and quality improvement initiatives aimed at improving patient safety.

Therefore, this study aims to explore the lived experiences of nurses involved in CVC management in a tertiary hospital in Northern Ghana. Using an exploratory-descriptive qualitative approach, the study seeks to provide insights into the clinical challenges, systemic constraints and emotional demands associated with CVC care in a resource-limited critical care setting.

Methods

Study design

This study used an exploratory-descriptive qualitative design to gain an in-depth understanding of nurses’ lived experiences regarding the management of CVCs within a resource-limited tertiary healthcare setting.8 An exploratory-descriptive qualitative approach was chosen to capture the subjective meanings, perceptions and challenges encountered by nurses in their daily CVC-related practices.9

Study setting

The study was conducted in the critical care areas of a tertiary referral hospital in Northern Ghana, including the General Intensive Care Unit (ICU), Maternal ICU, Neonatal ICU and the emergency department, where CVCs are inserted and managed. These units provide care for patients who are critically ill requiring advanced monitoring and invasive interventions such as vasoactive therapy, haemodynamic monitoring and renal replacement therapy.

Registered nurses working in these units are primarily responsible for the routine care and maintenance of CVCs, including infection prevention practices, site care and patient monitoring. The units operate within a resource-constrained environment characterised by variable availability of sterile supplies, high patient acuity and limited nurse-to-patient ratios (1:9), all of which directly influence CVC management practices.

This setting was considered appropriate for exploring nurses’ experiences of CVC management, as it reflects the clinical realities of critical care delivery in a low-resource tertiary hospital and captures the organisational and systemic factors shaping frontline nursing practice.

Participants and recruitment

Registered nurses with at least 1 year of clinical experience in wards where CVC management is routinely performed were eligible to participate. A purposive sampling technique, a non-probability method commonly used in qualitative research, was employed to recruit participants based on their direct experience with CVC care.10 11 This approach ensured the inclusion of information-rich participants capable of providing in-depth perspectives relevant to the study objectives.

Nurses were recruited from intensive care, surgical, medical and nephrology wards of Tamale Teaching Hospital to capture a broad and diverse range of experiences. A total of 13 participants were recruited.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research. However, nurses, as key healthcare providers, were involved as participants, sharing their experiences with CVC management. The findings will be disseminated to hospital management and the Ministry of Health, Ghana, to inform policy and practice improvements.

Data collection

In this study, we collected demographic data to characterise the study participants and provide context for the qualitative findings. Information obtained included gender, age group, marital status, educational level, religion and years of professional work experience. These characteristics describe the background profile of the nurses interviewed and help contextualise their experiences with CVC management. In addition, individual face-to-face semistructured interviews were conducted in a quiet, private location within the hospital premises to ensure confidentiality and participant comfort. Qualitative research interviews are a crucial method for gathering in-depth information about individuals’ experiences, perspectives and understandings.12 They involve a conversational approach where a researcher asks open-ended questions to explore a specific topic in detail. An interview guide was developed based on existing literature and expert input, covering topics such as knowledge of CVC protocols, challenges encountered in CVC care, training experiences, emotional responses to CVC-related complications and perceived institutional support.

Interviews were conducted in English, lasted approximately 30–45 min each and were audio-recorded with participant consent. Field notes were taken to capture non-verbal cues and contextual information.13 Data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached.14 15 Data saturation was operationally defined as the stage in data collection when no new themes, codes or insights emerged from consecutive interviews. Interviews and analysis were conducted concurrently, allowing the research team to monitor the emergence of new concepts during the coding process. In this study, data were considered to have reached saturation when two consecutive interviews yielded no additional codes or categories, indicating that the thematic structure of nurses’ experiences had been sufficiently captured. Researcher reflexivity

Researcher reflexivity was considered throughout the study. Members of the research team have clinical backgrounds in nursing and critical care, which supported contextual understanding while also requiring deliberate attention to minimise potential bias during data collection and interpretation. To enhance rigour, transcripts and preliminary interpretations were reviewed collaboratively and were kept grounded in participants’ accounts.

Data management and storage

All interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ consent, transcribed verbatim and de-identified. Digital audio files, transcripts and field notes were stored on password-protected devices, with access limited to the research team. Identifying details were removed from transcripts prior to analysis.

Pilot testing of the interview guide

A pilot interview was conducted with one nurse working in a similar clinical setting to assess the clarity, relevance and flow of the interview guide. Based on the pilot, minor revisions were made to improve wording, reduce ambiguity and adjust question order for smoother sequencing.

Language and cultural considerations

Interviews were conducted in English, which is the professional language routinely used in clinical settings, reducing the need for translation. Cultural and contextual considerations relevant to the Ghanaian healthcare environment were taken into account during interviewing and analysis, for instance, local workflow norms, hierarchical communication patterns and family involvement in obtaining supplies, to support accurate representation of participants’ intended meanings.

Data analysis

Participants’ demographic characteristics were summarised using descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) and analysed using SPSS V.25, while qualitative interview data were analysed using thematic analysis with the support of manual data management and coding. These qualitative data were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s13 thematic analysis, following the six-phase framework outlined in their work. This structured yet flexible approach included (1) familiarisation with the data, (2) generating initial codes, (3) searching for themes, (4) reviewing themes, (5) defining and naming themes and (6) producing the report.

This method enabled a systematic examination of patterns within the interview transcripts, ensuring that the analysis was grounded in the lived experiences of participants. Coding and theme development were conducted inductively, allowing themes to emerge from the data rather than imposing preconceived categories.14

Thematic analysis proved particularly well-suited to exploring multifaceted challenges, knowledge gaps and systemic factors influencing CVC management in the study setting. Its adaptability to complex real-world clinical contexts strengthened the credibility and relevance of the findings.

Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and cross-checked for accuracy. Thematic analysis was conducted following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach as indicated above. A reflexive, iterative process was employed throughout the analysis to ensure that the themes accurately reflected participants’ experiences. Data were managed manually. To enhance rigour, two independent researchers coded the data, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.

Techniques to enhance trustworthiness

To enhance the trustworthiness of this qualitative study on CVC management among the nurses of the surveyed wards, the researchers followed Guba and Lincoln’s15 four criteria: credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability. These strategies were applied to ensure that the findings authentically reflect nurses’ lived experiences and could inform practice in similar healthcare settings. The study’s design, data collection and analytical processes were executed to uphold methodological integrity throughout the study.

Credibility was strengthened through multiple measures. Member checking allowed participants to validate the accuracy of their interview summaries, ensuring alignment with their intended meaning. A pilot test of the interview guide at the Northern Regional Hospital refined the questions for clarity and cultural appropriateness. The 3-week data collection period supported prolonged engagement, which built trust and yielded deeper insights. Additionally, triangulation of semistructured interviews with non-participant observations enhanced the richness and reliability of the findings by corroborating evidence across different data sources.

To support transferability, the researchers provided thick descriptions of the study setting, participant demographics and data collection procedures. Dependability was ensured through a well-documented audit trail, outlining each phase of the research process for external verification. Peer debriefing and intercoder agreement further enhanced consistency and analytical rigour. Finally, confirmability was addressed through reflexive journaling and a transparent analytical process grounded in verbatim participant narratives. Together, these techniques ensured that the study findings were both contextually grounded and methodologically sound.

Findings

A total of 13 nurses participated in the study, comprising 8 males and 5 females. Most participants (n=7) were aged between 28 and 37 years. Most participants had between 1 and 13 years of clinical experience in various inpatient settings where CVCs are managed. Male participants accounted for 61.5% of the sample. Thematic analysis of the interview data revealed four major themes and associated subthemes that captured the lived experiences of nurses managing CVCs at Tamale Teaching Hospital. These themes highlight the interplay between system constraints, feasibility of recommended practices, capability-building needs and the psychosocial demands associated with CVC care in a resource-limited setting. System constraints shaping CVC care

This theme describes the system-level conditions that shaped nurses’ ability to deliver CVC care. Participants highlighted how shortages of essential supplies and interruptions in infrastructure, combined with high workload and staff pressures, influence what care can be delivered consistently.

Supply and equipment shortages

Nurses described limited access to basic items needed for safe CVC care, such as sterile gloves, dressings, antiseptics and sterile dressing sets. Shortages were reported to contribute to delays in routine site care and to the use of whatever materials were available to avoid halting patient care.

Eer… we don’t have enough gloves or dressing kits, and that makes it hard to maintain proper care (IDI, P1).

Another nurse reinforced this concern, highlighting how inconsistent access to supplies compromises safe practice:

Sometimes we run out of essential items like antiseptics or sterile gloves, and that makes our work difficult (IDI, P11).

Infrastructure interruptions affecting aseptic care

Infrastructure interruptions were also reported to affect aseptic practice. Participants noted that interruptions in water supply could delay or complicate hand hygiene, and that lack of water or electricity could disrupt Central Sterile Supply Department (CSSD) sterilisation processes, limiting access to sterilised items like forceps and kidney dishes needed for dressing procedures. One participant explained how maintaining sterility becomes challenging under such conditions:

One of the biggest challenges is maintaining sterility during catheter insertion and care, especially in a busy hospital environment. Sometimes, there is a shortage of essential supplies like sterile gloves, disinfectants and specialised dressings (IDI, P12).

Workload and staffing pressures affecting care delivery

Understaffing and high workload were described as reducing time for assessment, dressing changes, documentation and patient education, contributing to missed or delayed elements of care during busy shifts.

One participant described the impact of workload on their ability to provide adequate care:

When you have too many patients to care for at the same time, you can’t always give each one the attention they need, and that’s frustrating (IDI, P5).

Another nurse similarly noted the difficulty of allocating sufficient time to patients requiring CVC care:

When you have many patients to care for, it’s not always easy to give enough time to every CVC patient (IDI, P6).

Practice and protocol feasibility

This theme describes infections and exit-site care challenges, the gap between protocols and feasible practice, workarounds and staffing pressures affecting care and patient and family factors influencing practice. Details are explained in the subthemes below.

Infection prevention and exit-site care challenges

Participants described infection prevention as difficult to sustain because it depended on both nursing practice and conditions on the ward. Nurses linked infection risk to intermittent availability of sterile supplies, like gloves, dressings, antiseptics; limited time for patient education and episodes of high-acuity care where competing priorities reduced attention to exit-site care and routine maintenance. Patient non-compliance, like touching or pulling the line with unclean hands, was also described as contributing to contamination risk.

One big challenge is infection control… Another issue is the availability of sterile equipment sometimes… Even sometimes the sutures used to suture the CVC in place are expired (IDI, P1).

Gap between protocols and feasible practice

Participants described a gap between protocol requirements and what could be delivered consistently under ward conditions. They noted that when supplies were intermittent and workload was high, recommended steps could be delayed or modified and documentation and patient education could be deprioritised during busy shifts.

Workarounds and coping strategies

In response to these constraints, nurses described practical coping strategies. Some reported delaying non-urgent dressing changes or other aspects of line care when patient acuity was high or when required items were unavailable. Participants also noted that patients’ relatives were sometimes asked to purchase supplies from nearby medical shops, including affordable antiseptic solutions (eg, povidone-iodine or spirit/alcohol) for dressing and, at times, even the CVC itself when the hospital had no stock (to enable insertion by the medical doctor). In addition, participants described disruptions in CSSD functioning due to a lack of water or electricity, which could limit access to sterilised dressing items (eg, forceps and kidney dishes) and further constrain aseptic practice.

Sometimes the relatives have to go and buy what they can afford—povidone iodine or spirit—so we can do the dressing (IDI, P12).

Patient and family factors influencing care

Participants described patient and family-related factors as influencing CVC care. Examples included patients touching or pulling at the line or dressing, which nurses perceived as increasing contamination risk. Family involvement was also described as central to enabling care during stock-outs, as relatives were sometimes asked to purchase required items to support dressing changes or catheter insertion.

Capability building and support

Another major theme that emanated from this study is ‘capability building and support’. It included subthemes like knowledge and skills in routine CVC care. Need for practical, hands-on training and refresher sessions, supervision and mentorship support and the need to hear nurses’ voices.

Knowledge and skills in routine CVC care

Participants generally demonstrated a practical understanding of the purpose of CVCs and of routine catheter care. At the same time, they acknowledged limits in their preparation for managing more complex situations, particularly complications.

Participants described CVCs as essential devices for patients who are critically ill, particularly for administering medications, fluids and blood products and for ongoing monitoring. Their accounts reflected recognition of both the clinical value of CVCs and the risks associated with their use.

For me, a central venous catheter is a high-risk, high-importance device… but needs to be handled carefully because of the infection risks and complications (IDI, P5).

Participants also linked effective routine CVC care to improved patient outcomes, including fewer infections and complications and uninterrupted delivery of medications and fluids.

Participants described routine CVC care practices, such as hand hygiene, site inspection, dressing care, flushing and patient education, indicating familiarity with standard maintenance activities. Confidence was reported mainly for routine tasks, while several participants described limited preparation for complication management (eg, suspected infection, occlusion and dislodgement). Nurses suggested that skills-based refresher sessions (including simulation) and structured supervision could support competency development over time.

Need for practical, hands-on training and refresher sessions

Many participants described confidence in routine aspects of CVC care, such as hand hygiene, preparing a clean field, inspecting the insertion site, changing dressings when materials were available, flushing/locking and monitoring early signs of infection or blockage. Confidence was often described as strong for tasks performed frequently during shifts, particularly in the ICU environment.

I’m confident with the basic things—hand hygiene, checking the site, doing the dressing when we have the items and flushing appropriately. Those ones we do every day (IDI, P3).

Most of the time, the routine care is not a problem for us. The issue comes when there is a complication or when the materials are not there (IDI, P7).

Although participants felt able to perform routine care, many emphasised the need for more practical, skills-based training focused on complication management, for example, suspected infection, occlusion, dislodgement, bleeding and standardising dressing techniques. Participants described that refreshers were infrequent and often theoretical, and they requested supervised practice, simulations and bedside demonstrations.

We need more hands-on training, not only talking. Especially on what to do when you suspect infection or when the line is blocked (IDI, P6).

Sometimes you are doing the dressing, but you’re not sure if you are following the correct steps because no one has done a practical refresher with us for a long time (IDI, P10).

Supervision, mentorship and feedback to support practice

Participants described supervision and mentorship as central to building competence, often occurring informally through observing senior colleagues, receiving bedside guidance and being corrected during practice. Nurses noted that when mentoring and feedback were inconsistent, techniques could vary across staff, particularly when materials were limited, and staff relied on experience to decide how to proceed.

Most of what I know about the central line care, I learnt on the ward. You watch the seniors, then you start doing it yourself (IDI, P2).

Another buttresses the essence of the mentorship and feedback and indicates that

Experience teaches you, especially when you meet complications. But it would be better if someone guides you step-by-step, not only learning the hard way (IDI, P8).

Nurses’ voices should be heard in protocol development and review

Participants described protocols as useful in principle but difficult to implement consistently when supplies were intermittent and workloads were high. They also reported limited access to regular refresher training and variable supervision, and some felt that frontline nursing perspectives were not routinely considered during protocol development or revision. Nurses suggested that structured mentorship and practical refresher training could improve implementation.

Participants described organisational support as insufficient for sustained CVC safety. They reported intermittent availability of basic supplies needed for dressing changes and aseptic technique, limited opportunities for training and inconsistent supervision and feedback. Mentorship from experienced staff was described as practical support, especially for less experienced nurses.

Psychosocial impact of CVC care

This theme captured participants’ descriptions of the emotional and psychological demands associated with CVC care in a high-acuity, resource-constrained environment. Nurses described feeling stressed by the responsibility of maintaining a high-risk device, particularly when supplies were limited and workloads were high. Three related subthemes were identified: burnout, fear of patient complications and limited emotional support.

Burnout

Participants described exhaustion and feelings consistent with burnout, which they linked to heavy workload, staffing constraints and repeated exposure to patients who are critically ill, requiring continuous monitoring and interventions. Some noted that persistent system constraints could make it difficult to sustain motivation and attention to detailed line care over time.

The workload is too much, and you get tired… sometimes you feel burned out, but you still have to continue because the patients depend on you (IDI, P13).

Fear of patient complications

Nurses described worry about adverse outcomes, such as bloodstream infection, bleeding, catheter dislodgement or occlusion. This fear was heightened by the perception that complications can occur suddenly and that nurses might be held responsible, even when contributing factors were outside their control (eg, supply shortages, delayed procedures or limited monitoring time during busy shifts).

Anytime you are managing the central line, you are always thinking about what can go wrong—sepsis, bleeding, or the line coming out—so you become anxious (IDI, P4).

Limited emotional support

Participants described limited emotional support following stressful events or complications. Some reported that debriefing, reassurance or supportive supervision was inconsistent, and that communication could become blame-focused rather than problem-solving. Nurses suggested that mentoring, supportive leadership and opportunities to discuss difficult cases could reduce emotional strain.

…sometimes we are blamed when complications arise, but the real issue is often the system we work in—lack of resources, high patient loads, and inadequate staffing… this affects us emotionally (IDI, P9).

Discussion

This qualitative study examined nurses’ experiences of CVC management in a tertiary hospital in Ghana. Across five themes, nurses described how CVC safety is shaped by the interaction of knowledge and skills with workload, supply chains, infrastructure (including water for hand hygiene), supervision and workplace culture. These findings broadly align with evidence from West Africa and other low-resource settings showing that infection prevention depends on both individual practice and the systems that make recommended care feasible.1618

Nurses attributed infection prevention difficulties to constrained supplies (sterile gloves, antiseptics and dressings), high patient volumes and limited time for line care and patient education. Similar constraints are reported in West African hospitals, including Nigeria, where the availability of infection prevention and control (IPC) materials and workload pressures are associated with inconsistent adherence to recommended practices.19 20 Interpreted through WHO IPC guidance, the findings point to gaps in core components related to the IPC programme and guidelines, availability of infrastructure and supplies (including Water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH)), workforce/staffing and monitoring with feedback factors required to deliver reliable catheter maintenance and reduce device-associated infections.21 22

Training and competency development also influenced CVC management practices. Although participants reported familiarity with routine catheter care procedures, several described limited opportunities for continuing professional development related to vascular access management. Similar observations have been reported in studies examining infection prevention capacity in African healthcare settings, where training opportunities are often sporadic and unevenly distributed across clinical units.19 Global practice standards emphasise the importance of ongoing competency assessment, structured training and multidisciplinary approaches to vascular access management to minimise complications such as CRBSIs.21 22 The experiences reported by participants suggest that gaps between recommended standards and routine practice may partly reflect limited access to sustained training opportunities. It is worth noting that nurses in this research setting do not insert CVC; it is done by a medical doctor, unlike in some developed countries, where specialised and trained nurses insert PICC, mainly due to cost-benefit reasons.23 24

Participants also described difficulties implementing existing protocols due to high patient workloads and limited supervisory support. Previous research has shown that the presence of written guidelines alone does not necessarily lead to changes in clinical practice unless accompanied by supportive institutional structures, including leadership engagement, mentorship and integrated workflow systems.25 26 In some cases, participants indicated that protocol implementation was influenced by contextual pressures such as staffing shortages and competing clinical priorities, which may affect the feasibility of maintaining strict adherence to infection prevention procedures.

The findings further revealed the influence of organisational and sociocultural factors on clinical practice. In the Ghanaian context, hierarchical decision-making structures and communication patterns within hospital settings may shape how nurses implement or escalate concerns regarding catheter management. Additionally, patient and family expectations, as well as varying levels of understanding about infection prevention measures, may influence patient cooperation with catheter care procedures. Similar dynamics have been reported in other African healthcare settings, where sociocultural norms and communication patterns can influence adherence to infection prevention practices and patient engagement with treatment processes.

Participants also described the emotional strain associated with managing high-risk procedures such as CVC care. Concerns about potential complications, combined with staffing shortages and heavy workloads, contributed to feelings of stress and anxiety among nurses. Previous studies in nursing workforce research have linked such conditions to burnout, reduced job satisfaction and challenges in staff retention.2729 These findings suggest that interventions aimed at improving CVC management may need to address both clinical competencies and broader workforce well-being.

From a policy perspective, the findings suggest several potential strategies that may support improved CVC management in similar settings. Cost-effective training approaches, such as peer-led workshops, simulation-based skill sessions and mentorship programmes within hospital units, may strengthen practical competencies in catheter care without requiring extensive financial resources. In addition, strengthening infection prevention committees and integrating routine CVC care audits into quality improvement activities may support more consistent adherence to recommended practices. These approaches are consistent with global infection prevention strategies promoted by international health organisations, including the WHO guidelines on healthcare-associated infection prevention.

A low-cost, practical option to support protocol adherence is the use of a standardised CVC dressing checklist (eg, indication review, hand hygiene method used, antiseptic used, dry time observed, dressing type, securement, documentation). Where staffing permits, a second nurse can act as a checker during dressing changes, which may also operationalise the mentorship and supervision needs described by participants.

Environmental conditions may also affect exit-site care. In hot climates, perspiration and humidity can reduce dressing integrity and increase the need for reassessment and timely dressing changes. Future work could examine how temperature and sweating influence dressing performance and infection prevention in similar settings.

Researcher reflexivity was considered throughout the research process. Members of the research team have professional backgrounds in intensive care nursing, which may have influenced how participants’ accounts were interpreted, particularly regarding clinical procedures and infection prevention practices. To minimise potential bias, the analysis involved repeated review of transcripts, collaborative discussion among researchers and attention to participants’ own descriptions of their experiences. Participants described recurring interruptions in water and electricity supply and intermittent availability of sterile materials, which they reported as prompting improvisation to avoid halting care. These contextual realities were considered during interpretation, while maintaining a focus on what participants reported rather than assumptions about practice.

The findings contribute to understanding how structural, organisational and sociocultural factors shape CVC management practices in a resource-constrained tertiary hospital setting. Addressing these factors may support the development of context-appropriate interventions to strengthen infection prevention practices and improve patient safety in similar healthcare environments. Participants reported variability in the antiseptic agents used during CVC care, and povidone-iodine and spirit/alcohol were commonly mentioned for skin disinfection. However, the dataset did not allow detailed comparison of antiseptic choice (eg, chlorhexidine-based products vs povidone-iodine vs alcohol) across wards or situations, including whether substitutions were made during stockouts. Future studies could examine this in more detail. Participants also indicated that sutures were frequently used to secure CVCs and that some supplies (eg, sutures) were at times expired due to limited availability. The continued use of sutures for catheter stabilisation may reflect resource constraints, as sutureless securement devices require regular replacement and may be more costly. However, evidence suggests that suturing may increase infection risk and does not necessarily prevent accidental catheter dislodgement.3032 These findings support structured approaches to vascular access management. Establishing a dedicated vascular access team may improve catheter insertion and maintenance practices, provide training and mentorship for nurses and strengthen surveillance of central line-associated bloodstream infections in resource-constrained settings.3336 Hand hygiene should align with WHO recommendations, using either alcohol-based hand rub or soap-and-water depending on availability and contamination risk, supported by periodic monitoring and feedback.37

Strengths

This study used a qualitative approach to describe nurses’ accounts of CVC management in a resource-limited tertiary hospital. Purposive sampling was used to recruit nurses with direct experience of CVC care. We used member checking, triangulation (interviews and observations), peer review of codes/themes and reflexive journaling during analysis.

Limitations

This study was conducted at a single site with a small sample (n=13), so findings may not reflect all resource-limited hospitals. Data were self-reported and may be affected by recall or social desirability. The study focused on nurses; future work could include other professionals involved in CVC care (eg, physicians and infection prevention staff) and/or use multi-site or mixed-methods designs.

Conclusions

This study explored nurses’ experiences of managing CVCs in a resource-limited tertiary hospital in Ghana. The findings indicate that CVC care is shaped by multiple interconnected factors, including shortages of essential supplies, high workload and staffing constraints, inconsistent access to training and limited organisational support. Although nurses demonstrated some level of awareness of recommended infection prevention practices, structural and contextual challenges often affected their ability to implement these practices consistently. These findings highlight the need for system-level interventions to strengthen vascular access management in similar settings. Practical measures may include improving the availability of essential materials, implementing regular hands-on training and mentorship programmes, strengthening infection prevention monitoring and establishing dedicated vascular access teams to support catheter insertion, maintenance and surveillance of catheter-related infections. Addressing these organisational and capacity-building needs may contribute to safer CVC management and improved patient outcomes in resource-constrained healthcare environments.
