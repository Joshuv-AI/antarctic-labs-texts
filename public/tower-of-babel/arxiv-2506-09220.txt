Abstract
. This paper examines the gap between the promises and real-world performance of emerging AI personal assistants. Drawing on interviews with early adopters of devices like Rabbit R1 and Humane AI Pin, as well as services like Ohai and Docus, we map user experiences through the lens of Uses and Gratifications and Uncertainty Reduction Theory. We identify three core types of user uncertainty—functional, interactional, and social—and explore how each disrupts different user gratifications. We show that while marketing hype fuels initial adoption, unmet expectations often result in frustration or abandonment. Our findings highlight the importance of transparency, task-specific design, and user control over contextual memory and personalization. We provide design and policy recommendations, including user-facing explainability tools and calls for regulatory benchmarks such as CI-Bench, to guide ethical and interpretable AI integration. Our study offers actionable insights for creating more usable, trustworthy, and socially aligned AI assistants.



1.
Introduction

The allure of artificial intelligence (AI) has captivated the public imagination, promising a future where intelligent machines seamlessly integrate into our lives, anticipating our needs, and effortlessly completing tasks. Upon first glance, the vision of ubiquitous computing presented by Mark Wieser, a future where technology seamlessly blends into the background of our lives, is here in a way we have not experienced before
(
Weiser 1994
)
. However, Wieser also cautioned against the allure of "magic," where the inner workings of technology become opaque and user understanding fades into mere acceptance
(
Weiser 1994
)
. The current wave of AI personal assistants, with their sleek designs and promises of effortless efficiency, embodies this tension between integration and transparency. The promise is particularly potent in the realm of personal assistants, where devices like the Rabbit R1 and Humane AI Pin are fueled by compelling marketing campaigns, have emerged as harbingers of this AI-powered future.

In a YouTube video that had more than four million views (see Figure
1
) reviewing one AI-powered personal assistant product - Rabbit R1, the video producer,
‘Coffeezilla’, refers to the device as a scam because it does not actually accomplish promised features that would allow the device to know the details of one’s daily routine and help them while accomplishing those tasks. Promotional claims for Rabbit R1 directly quoted in the YouTube video included the ability to
“speak or type with [Rabbit] R1. I can also use R1 to call a ride I can also use R1 to order food I can also make R1 perform a set of more complex actions. For example, if I want to plan a trip, I can just describe what I want in full.”
The YouTube influencer then details his negative experiences with the product, saying that it does not perform any of these advertised tasks. Specifically, he tries to order an Uber, and the R1 device stated
“ride share status is currently unavailable…apologies the Uber ride request could not be processed.”
The conclusion of the product review ultimately points to the failure of the central AI functionality of the Rabbit R1 called a Large Action Model
(
Zitron [n. d.]
)
. The LAM is supposed to learn the actions that users want to do as well as bypassing different APIs to take action on platforms like Uber, Spotify, and DoorDash
(
Shepherd [n. d.]
)
. The reviews argue that the LAM cannot learn from user actions, nor can it bridge digital platform ecosystems
(
Van Dijck et al. 2018
)
. Although many responses expressed deep confusion about how the tasks marketed for the device differed from those already available through existing technologies, such as cell phones or voice assistants like Amazon Alexa, there was also notable interest in the "artificial intelligence" component of the device.

As AI personal assistants proliferate, understanding their real-world usage and value becomes paramount. Observing how people integrate devices like the Rabbit R1 and Humane AI Pin, alongside subscription services like Ohai.ai and Docus AI, reveals not only the practical benefits but also the discrepancies between marketing promises and lived experiences. Our study delves into this rapidly evolving landscape, examining the persistent gap between the aspirational claims of these technologies and their actual day-to-day use.

We build on Ackerman’s notion of the sociotechnical gap—the disconnect between what is socially desirable and what is technically feasible—and analyze how this divide manifests as uncertainties in AI personal assistants
(
Ackerman 2000
)
. Drawing on Uses and Gratifications Theory
(
Katz et al. 1973
;
Wei et al. 2024
)
, we examine how users adopt these systems to fulfill four distinct needs: technological, utilitarian, hedonic, and social gratification. Simultaneously, we incorporate Uncertainty Reduction Theory
(
Zhang et al. 2022b
;
Kramer 1999
)
to explain how uncertainties surrounding system functionality, interaction, and social role disrupt those gratifications.

Specifically, we introduce three categories of uncertainty: functional, interactional, and social. Each of these maps onto one or more user gratifications and offer a lens for understanding user frustrations and eventual adaptations. We then offer design recommendations aimed at closing the sociotechnical gap and building more transparent and context-aware AI systems.

2.
Related Work

This section examines the evolving landscape of AI-powered conversational assistants, tracing their technological development, user motivations, and design implications. In §
2.1
, we begin by highlighting the shift from early text-based tools like Clippy to today’s large language model-powered assistants, and showing how these systems have become integrated into everyday routines and domestic settings. We then turn to two theoretical lenses to interpret adoption and usage: Uses and Gratifications Theory (§
2.1.1
) identifies four types of user motivations—technological, hedonic, utilitarian, and social—that shape how people engage with AI assistants, while Uncertainty Reduction Theory (§
2.1.2
) explains how users manage uncertainty when adopting new AI systems. In §
2.2
, we explore emerging design challenges around user autonomy, system transparency, and the role of embodiment in AI design. Finally, §
2.3
expands the scope to emphasize that conversational assistants are rarely used in isolation. Instead, they operate within broader constellations of tools and services that require thoughtful integration to support user control and collaboration.

2.1.
Evolution of Conversational Assistants

A foundational example of a digital assistant was Microsoft’s Clippy, which demonstrated the potential of text-based natural language feedback despite its unpopularity
(
Mutchler 2018
;
Baym et al. 2019
)
. It offered early insights into virtual assistant design, including the importance of responding only when summoned
(
Satyanarayanan 2018
)
. More advanced “legacy assistants” like Siri (launched in 2011), Alexa (launched in 2014), and Google Assistant (launched in 2016) introduced voice interactions for tasks such as setting reminders and controlling smart home devices. Analyses of Google Home usage show that these assistants are primarily used for music, hands-free search, and IoT automation
(
Ammari et al. 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2018
)
, including integration with devices such as smart lights (e.g., Philips Hue
(
Philips Hue 2024
)
), thermostats (e.g., Nest
(
Google Nest 2024b
)
), stereo systems (e.g., Sonos
(
Sonos 2024
)
), and cameras (e.g., Nest Cam
(
Google Nest 2024a
)
). These assistants became embedded into household routines through automation rules and were shaped by the complexity of domestic environments
(
Porcheron et al. 2018
;
Rode et al. 2005
;
Tolmie et al. 2010
)
. Voice interaction, as a natural modality, simulates human dialogue to complete tasks
(
Nass et al. 1995
)
, with task completion influencing user satisfaction
(
Shechtman and Horowitz 2003
;
Kiseleva et al. 2016
)
. The newest generation of AI assistants, powered by large language models like GPT and Claude, leverages NLP and machine learning to deliver more integrated and versatile user experiences
(
Roslan and Ahmad 2023
)
.

However, when assistants fail to complete tasks, users engage in repair strategies. Group-based interactions with Siri show users treating it as a humanlike agent
(
Porcheron et al. 2017
;
Luger and Sellen 2016
)
. Amazon reviews reveal emotional attachments to Alexa, framing it as a companion
(
Purington et al. 2017
)
. These assistants now play social as well as functional roles, raising design challenges around agency, privacy, and user expectations.

2.1.1.
Uses and Gratifications Theory

The Uses and Gratifications Theory (UGT) offers a user-centered perspective on technology adoption by emphasizing the personal needs and motivations that drive engagement
(
Katz et al. 1973
;
Rubin 2009
;
Lin and Ng 2024
)
. It distinguishes between anticipated and actual gratifications experienced through technology use
(
McLean and Osei-Frimpong 2019
)
. UGT has been widely applied to media and technology studies
(
Wei et al. 2024
)
, including research on AI technologies such as chatbots and smart assistants
(
Xie et al. 2023
)
. In the context of voice assistants like Amazon Alexa, studies show that both utilitarian (e.g., efficiency) and symbolic (e.g., identity and status) benefits shape adoption
(
Ammari et al. 2019
;
McLean and Osei-Frimpong 2019
;
Yadav et al. 2024
)
. These insights have led to the identification of four key gratification types that guide user interaction with AI systems
(
Gan and Li 2018
)
.

(1)

Technological Gratification
: satisfaction derived from engaging with advanced or novel technologies. For example, McLean et al.
(
McLean and Osei-Frimpong 2019
)
echo earlier studies about the early adoption of voice assistants like Amazon Alexa
(
Ammari et al. 2019
)
and showing that users might adopt these technologies to enhance their image and social status, much like people purchase luxury items for status.

(2)

Hedonic Gratifiction
: pleasure-driven gratification where the enjoyment users gain from using the technology or a specific use-case of the technology. For example, users who find pleasure in engaging with voice assistants for tasks like playing music or casual conversations
(
Ammari et al. 2019
)
would have a stronger intention to use them in the future
(
Jo 2022
)
and a player who enjoys the immersive and elements of a game is more likely to continue playing due to the pleasurable experiences it offers
(
Li et al. 2015
)
.

(3)

Utilitarian Gratification
: practical benefits of a technology use such as efficiency in everyday routines
(
Ammari et al. 2019
)
, and accomplishing daily tasks. For example, Brachten et al.
(
Brachten et al. 2021
)
describe how employees in an organization appreciated chatbots that could efficiently handle routine inquiries, thereby allowing them to focus on more complex tasks.

(4)

Social Gratification
: the satisfaction individuals obtain from using media or technology to fulfill their social needs, such as connecting with others, building relationships, and enhancing social interactions
(
Xie et al. 2023
)
. For example, students experience social gratification by engaging with SNSs to stay connected with peers and expand their social networks
(
Ifinedo 2016
)
.

2.1.2.
Uncertainty Reduction Theory

Users have specific ways of coping and maintaining their uncertainties in their experiences with the use or presence of AI. Chang et al. introduced two key uncertainty reduction strategies for generative AI systems like ChatGPT: “interactive" uncertainty reduction strategies, which involves direct engagement with the system or other users, and “passive" uncertainty reduction strategies which involves users reducing uncertainty by observing others’ interactions with the AI systems
(
Chang et al. 2024
)
.

Consistent with earlier findings on AI adoption, we found Uncertainty Reduction Theory useful for understanding how users seek to reduce uncertainty in their decision-making processes when adopting new technologies
(
Kramer 1999
)
. Combining UGT and URT provides a robust framework for understanding technology use and adoption
(
Chang et al. 2024
)
. We further examine this interplay between uncertainties and gratifications in our subsequent sections.

2.2.
From Affordances to Experience: Design Challenges in Human-AI Interaction

As conversational agents become more intelligent by incorporating AI, emerging design challenges must be addressed to ensure their usability. A recurring issue is the gap in understanding AI capabilities—designers often struggle to grasp what AI can and cannot do
(
Dove et al. 2017
;
Yang et al. 2018a
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
, a problem compounded by the general public’s limited knowledge of machine learning. Bridging technical affordances (such as conversational interaction or multi-modal feedback)
(
Holmquist 2017
;
Yang et al. 2018b
)
with user experience requires rethinking how AI systems are designed and deployed
(
Luger and Sellen 2016
;
Kuniavsky et al. 2017
;
Lancaster et al. 2024
)
.

Some examples of these gaps include AI assistants’ frequent misinterpretation of user intent, where the system overlooks contextual cues or fails to infer goals accurately, resulting in outputs that do not align with user expectations
(
Rao et al. 2023
;
Kim et al. 2024
)
. This misalignment can be made worse if users also see the tone of the response as out of step with their expectations (e.g., formal or flat tone)
(
Ray 2023
)
. Finally, the system sometimes refuses to engage with controversial or sensitive topics—especially when paired with generic disclaimers that do not provide explanations for refusal— which can also be perceived as unhelpful or obstructive
(
Borji 2023
)
.

Foundational HCI principles, as proposed by Shneiderman and others, remain central to AI design
(
Shneiderman and Plaisant 2010
;
Shneiderman 2000
;
Shneiderman and Maes 1997
;
MacKenzie 2013
;
North et al. 1996
)
. Core features such as intuitive search, direct manipulation
(
Buschek 2024
)
, and immediate visual feedback continue to guide good design
(
Lee et al. 2024
;
North et al. 1996
)
. User autonomy is especially critical
(
Dove et al. 2017
)
, aligning with long-standing debates between user-controlled systems and agent-based automation
(
Nass et al. 1994
;
Beun et al. 2003
;
Luger and Sellen 2016
)
. Yet, intelligent systems often lead to unrealistic user expectations, particularly in complex or sensitive contexts
(
Cranshaw et al. 2017
;
Yang et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2022a
)
. When users lack control or struggle to understand system behavior, frustration and abandonment may result
(
Bisante et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2021
)
. Educating users about a system’s limitations can align expectations and improve satisfaction
(
Stone et al. 2022
;
Hinderks et al. 2019
)
.

Embodiment also plays a critical role in AI design. While human-like agents can support social interaction, excessive anthropomorphism can mislead users
(
Dautenhahn 2007
)
. Research emphasizes that embodiment should focus on functional and social cues, not mimicry
(
De Angeli et al. 2006
;
Beun et al. 2003
;
Nass et al. 1994
)
. Designing effective embodied agents requires understanding both conversational roles and how users adapt within broader sociotechnical contexts, thereby prioritizing design approaches that center on user experience
(
Hinderks et al. 2019
)
.

2.3.
Considering Multi-Device Ecosystems

The success or failure of new technologies often depends on how well they integrate into users’ existing ecosystems. Forlizzi’s concept of product ecologies highlights that a product’s meaning and value emerge from a dynamic network of relationships—among users, technologies, social contexts, and environments—rather than from functional features alone
(
Forlizzi 2008
;
Suchman 1987
;
Suchman 2007
)
. This approach emphasizes the interconnected nature of these factors, showing how changes in one part of the ecology can impact user behavior, technology adoption, and future design directions.

Extending this framework, Rossitto et al. examined how teamwork is supported through constellations of tools distributed across people and spaces
(
Rossitto et al. 2014
)
. They found that such configurations are unique to each group and shaped by shared values, routines, and work divisions. Ignoring these multi-device interactions risks creating niche technologies with limited interoperability, reducing fluid collaboration
(
Rossitto et al. 2014
)
. While personal assistants appear tailored to individual use, they exist within broader ecosystems of devices and services that shift with context and task. As such, seamless integration and communication across platforms are critical to supporting user agency and control.

3.
Context and Methods

The first wave of AI devices, including the Humane AI Pin and the Rabbit R1, arrived on the consumer market in early 2024. The Humane AI Pin, after much anticipation, officially launched on November 9, 2023, with pre-orders opening that same week. The Rabbit R1, while lacking the same pre-launch fanfare, also hit the market in early 2024. At the same time subscription-based AI services like Ohai.ai and Docus AI began carving out their niches. Ohai debuted in mid-2023, while Docus followed suit in early 2024. These services, unlike their device counterparts, are powered by generative AI models like OpenAI’s GPT, suggesting a potential technological divide for how artificial intelligence is used between wearables and subscription services.
Devices like the Humane AI Pin and the Rabbit R1 represent the tangible manifestation of AI assistants, offering hands-free interaction through voice commands and even gestures in the case of the Humane Pin. These devices aim to integrate seamlessly into daily life, with the Humane Pin aspiring to replace smartphones altogether by projecting information and interactions onto the physical world. Conversely, the Rabbit R1 positions itself as a complementary tool, a "pocket companion" augmenting smartphone capabilities with its conversational interface and task-oriented approach.
Despite their shared goal of offering hands-free assistance, the devices diverge in their marketed roles. The Humane AI Pin, with its ambition to be a "second brain" and "personal team," promises a holistic AI experience encompassing scheduling, communication, and even wellness coaching
(
Humane Inc. 2024
)
. This positions the Humane Pin as a comprehensive life manager, anticipating needs and providing timely information through its various personality profiles. In contrast, the Rabbit R1 emphasizes task execution, envisioning a world where users command an "army of digital rabbits" to carry out specific actions
(
Rabbit Inc. 2024a
)
. Rabbit’s technology is built on the premise of a Large Action Model that is supposed to learn how to use any software it runs into, takes actions, and gets better over time by studying how people use online interfaces
(
Rabbit Inc. 2024b
)
. These distinctions highlight a key difference in their envisioned user relationships: the Humane Pin as a constant companion and the Rabbit R1 as a tool for delegated efficiency.
While devices cast a wide net as general-purpose assistants, subscription services like Ohai and Docus adopt a tailored approach focusing on specific contexts and user needs. Ohai specializes in household assistance, particularly in managing schedules and tracking to-dos through text and email interactions. The expressed purpose of Ohai is to
‘lighten the mental load for everyone’
because managing a household and caring for loved ones is a significant contributor to burnout among Chief Household Officers."

Devices offer the allure of constant, hands-free AI interaction, with ambitions ranging from task management to comprehensive life organization. 3.1.
Data Collection and Analysis

After receiving Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval, over the course of 4 months, we conducted in-depth interviews with participants who purchased emerging AI devices and subscription services including Rabbit R1, Humane AI Pin, Docus and Ohai.All participants were required to show proof of purchase for devices and active subscription service status. Our semi-structured interviews lasted approximately 60 minutes and were conducted remotely by the first author. During our interviews we asked questions surrounding device expectations, motivations behind purchase, usage, and data privacy to understand if the promises purported by companies were realized in everyday use. Participants received compensation for their time in the amount of $100 for successfully completing the interview. Participant backgrounds were diverse but gendered norms of early technology adoption were evident as shown in Table
1
below. We used a grounded approach to analyze all of our interview transcripts (n=9)
(
Charmaz 2006
)
. Applying practices of open coding and constant comparison to identify patterns and relationships in our data set. Initially a set of themes including minimizing distractions, personalization, distribution of labor, and mistake proofing emerged from our findings. Refining themes continued to reveal disjunctions between use cases and privacy. These perspectives portrayed differing expectations of technology and assumptions about machine learning and artificial intelligence. The findings presented in this paper have the most insight to offer given the research question(s) focusing on understanding user motivations for adoption of emerging AI devices.

4.
Findings

This section examines how participants made sense of and adapted to the capabilities and limitations of next-generation AI assistants. In §
4.1
, we explore how marketing-driven hype shaped user expectations and how those expectations clashed with the functional realities of devices like the Rabbit R1 and Humane AI Pin. In §
4.1.1
, we show how participants drew comparisons between legacy voice assistants and newer AI devices across functionality, content generation, and interaction modality. Building on this, §
4.2
highlights how users carved out value by integrating AI assistants into task-specific workflows or adopting domain-specific services like Ohai. Finally, §
4.3
explores contradictions in user expectations—particularly the tension between wanting proactive, emotionally responsive interactions and accepting the limited contextual memory and functional focus of current systems.

4.1.
Driven by Hype: Navigating Expectations versus Reality

Excitement and anticipation surrounding AI assistants like the Rabbit R1 and Humane AI Pin were intertwined with the aspirational promise of creating a "second version of the user" through technological advances like Large Action Models (LAM). By comparing marketing claims with the actual experiences of our participants, we highlight the gap between the proposed capabilities of these devices and their real-world performance. Understanding the perception and role of these technologies in our everyday lives also provides insight into how the public views AI which is necessary to develop trustworthy devices and tools.

Participants in our study were drawn to purchase these devices and services due to their compelling advertising promising to streamline the mundane activities of a busy modern life. Best expressed by P2, a Rabbit R1 owner,
"man, the advertisement, the YouTube videos, the Ted Talks, all that stuff was just like, whoa, really? That’s crazy for $199; you can’t lose, you know what I’m saying?”
The utility of the device to record, memo, generate images and text summaries as well as offer seamless integration with services like Uber and Spotify, were all selling points. P4 thought the Rabbit would understand and execute commands with ease:
"When I originally saw it, I thought it could have a very clear sense and just do it—like, have the ‘action’ part to it….like order me a pizza…and carry out the task…based on my past order(s).”
Similarly, P3 purchased the Humane AI Pin based on
“Ambient computing for the real world [was advertised]…and it looks sophisticated. You can also use it as it was shown (in the promos) and when I wear it I honestly feel it shows professionalism.”

However, the discrepancy between the advertised ‘AI’ functionalities and the actual capabilities of these devices and services was particularly pronounced when they failed to execute tasks that were promoted as key capabilities. P2 expressed disappointment with Rabbit, stating,
“When it was advertised, it indicated that it was a learning LAM, some type of learning module in which it would get the information and do all these tasks for you. It’s not able to do that. That technology doesn’t even exist."

One of the key features of Rabbit, as P2 explained, was
"you’re supposed to say, hey, get me an Uber to da da da da. Well, they can’t do that because it depends on scripts, and if that website changes, those scripts no longer work."
P6 argued that these devices could not engage in dynamic interactions because they relied on rule-based static responses:
"I will mess with [the Rabbit] logic, and it’ll do the same thing…that’s a pre-programmed response. I’ll even say things like play me anything, excluding the Beatles, play me anything minus, Hey Jude, or play me anything that sounds kind of classic rock, and it’ll still play the Beatles."
Despite the Rabbit R1’s claims of
intuitively learning
, P6 concluded that the device was only capable of so much:
"I knew it was all marketing. I knew it was never going to be what they promised. I’m not fully disappointed.”

While some participants were able to discern the lack of artificial intelligence, many conflated AI capabilities with more basic voice-activated commands. For example, P7 shared how awesome it was that the device would
“connect me to the world without distracting me….I can just pick it up, push the button and say, what’s the weather? Hey, when’s the next basketball game?”
Limiting distractions through curated responses were common goals amongst participants. This mistaken understanding of AI leads to unrealistic expectations, diminished trust, and a lack of critical thinking about AI’s capabilities. This user mindset was described by P4, despite acknowledging its contributions where it did function well, concluded that the Rabbit is
"a clicky [Mcdonalds] happy-meal toy [or] a Tamagotchi that speaks."
In fact, P4 thought there was so much of a gap between the promised functionality and the actual product that he was looking forward to joining
“a class action lawsuit.”

4.1.1.
Comparisons between Legacy Voice Assistants and Next-gen AI Assistants

This section explores how participants compared their experiences with traditional voice assistants (e.g., Amazon Alexa, Google Assistant) to next-generation AI assistants like the Rabbit R1 and Humane AI Pin. These comparisons centered around three key areas: (1) Functionality, (2) Content Creation, and (3) Modality.

Functionality:

Participants used legacy assistants for routine tasks such as smart home control (P5), reminders (P2), scheduling (P8), and shopping lists (P9). However, they noted limitations of legacy assistants in flexibility and conversational depth. P5 and P6 emphasized that AI assistants provided more elaborate responses, while P3 and P2 encountered inconsistent performance—such as failure to make a call, clunky media controls, and raw system messages instead of interpretable error messages—highlighting a lack of polish in newer systems compared to the error-handling finesse of Siri or Alexa.

Content Creation:

Legacy assistants were constrained to executing predefined commands and retrieving information from fixed or web-based sources. In contrast, participants like P1, P3, and P6 praised the Rabbit R1 for its generative capabilities, including the creation of novel content and handling of nuanced prompts. P2, for instance, used the Rabbit R1 to generate visual content via Midjourney
(
Midjourney 2024
)
, aiding in his social media campaign for mental health awareness.

Modality:

Participants also noted major differences in interaction design. The Rabbit R1, co-designed with Teenage Engineering
(
Teenage Engineering 2024
)
, was described by P4 as “playful,” but its scroll wheel and non-touch interface frustrated users like P4 and P6, who expected touchscreen functionality. The AI Pin, with its gesture-based and screen-less interface, introduced a novel projection display. While P2 and P3 found it innovative—“beaming text onto your palm”—they also found it impractical in bright or uneven environments. P2 even returned the device, citing usability concerns.

In contrast, legacy assistants were embedded in familiar interfaces that required little adaptation. While some participants (P8, P9) appreciated the novelty of newer devices, others (P2, P4) preferred the intuitive and frictionless interactions offered by traditional systems.

4.2.
Beyond the Generalist: Adapting AI to Fit the User’s Ecosystem

Rather than expecting next-generation assistants to serve as general-purpose AI tools, participants who derived the most value identified specific use cases where these technologies integrated meaningfully into their broader technological ecosystems. This value was realized in two primary ways. First, participants adapted general-purpose devices like the Rabbit R1 to serve focused roles within customized workflows. By leveraging features such as voice recording or rapid information retrieval, they were able to offset the device’s broader limitations. Often, this involved integrating the assistant with other tools, positioning it as one part of a larger multi-device setup. Second, participants preferred services that were explicitly designed for specialized tasks, such as Ohai for calendar management or Docus for medical support. These purpose-built systems, typically powered by large language models trained on domain-specific datasets, offered tailored functionality that general-purpose assistants struggled to replicate. These examples of task-specific adoption show how users carved out productive roles for AI by aligning their strengths with real, contextual needs.

P6, a nurse with ADHD, created an intricate workflow that combined Rabbit and ChatGPT to reduce both time and cognitive effort during medical charting. As he explained,
“The joke is that I have 30 seconds to [see the patient] and 30 minutes to chart it… Now I can do 12 [patients] within an hour… easily, easily!”
By uploading recordings from the Rabbit R1 into ChatGPT Plus, he generated summaries and detailed synopses for medical records, significantly streamlining his documentation process. He emphasized the cognitive relief this brought, particularly in light of his ADHD:
“Even just the mental energy alone… is incredible.”
Though the Rabbit was marketed as a general-purpose device, it proved most useful when adapted to a specific, personalized workflow.

In contrast, services like Ohai were built with targeted functionality from the start. Ohai specializes in calendar management and was quickly embedded into participants’ daily routines. P1 described his typical use:
“I just text: ‘I have a sales meeting tomorrow from 7 to 8:30 a.m….’ because I know Ohai can understand it and execute what I’m trying to get it to do.”
Participants appreciated how Ohai used natural language processing to interpret and act on input, distinguishing it from conventional digital calendars.

Both P1 and P2 preferred Ohai to general-purpose assistants like Gemini due to its task-specific precision and seamless integration across platforms. As P1 put it,
“Ohai kind of bridges the gap between one of the purely generative AI models… and the integrations you would get with a workspace on Gemini.”
Ohai’s ability to connect both Google and Apple calendars also solved a long-standing issue for P1.

Importantly, P1 emphasized how Ohai recognized the complexity of his social roles—father, manager, son, caregiver—and adapted accordingly. Ohai helped him offload the cognitive burden of assigning tasks to appropriate social identities (e.g., caregiver vs. coworker) and prioritizing them based on context. P5, who lives with bipolar disorder, used Docus to reduce the cognitive strain of searching for medical information. Trained on a medical-specific corpus, Docus provided what he called a
“50/50 success rate.”
He appreciated the system’s differential diagnosis feature, which mimicked doctor-patient conversations by adjusting responses as more symptoms were provided. However, he was frustrated when the system failed to answer a drug interaction query, instead recommending he consult a doctor. As he put it:
“It’s not very useful if you ask the AI a question and end up getting recommended to consult your doctor again.”
Still, P5 remained optimistic, wishing the system were more proactive—even if it required disclaimers or additional data:
“I’m not disappointed. But [I wish it were] more proactive.”

While these task-specific applications offered notable cognitive relief, P5’s experience underscores a key tension in user expectations: the desire for AI to be both conservative (e.g., not to give incorrect medical information) and proactive (e.g., provide medical answers based on user history). These contradictions in what users want from AI assistants are explored further in the next section.

4.3.
Designing for Utility and Empathy: What Users Want from AI

Providing functional support relies on a different set of capabilities than offering emotional or social support. As discussed in §
4.1
, marketing for next-gen AI assistants often promised functional benefits—efficiency, automation, and anticipatory task support. While our participants found value in these task-specific functions, many also hoped for emotionally intelligent interactions. They wanted assistants that reflected human-like mannerisms and could engage in socially appropriate, context-aware conversations. Yet, this desire for emotionally resonant, human-like behavior often conflicted with the current limitations in contextual memory and precision.

For example, although Docus could ask medically relevant questions, P5 was frustrated that it repeatedly requested information he had already provided. The assistant failed to retain prior context, making interactions feel disjointed. Similarly, Rabbit struggled with continuity. P4 noted,
“If I say something in a conversation… and end it, the conversation is then gone.”
P6 echoed this sentiment:
“I can’t tell it to remember things… Not really. Does it remember my preferences? No.”
As a result, P6 adapted his own communication style—
“I convey my needs [to Rabbit] so much that now I do it with people too.”
The burden of remembering and repeating preferences shifted from the system to the user.

Despite these limitations, participants occasionally experienced moments of more engaging, human-like interaction. P4 described the Rabbit’s responses as conversational and responsive, stating it
“feels like a conversation… it’ll ask you a question in the direction you’re thinking.”
P8 also praised the Rabbit’s ability to understand complex queries and return rich, narrated answers, distinguishing it from earlier assistants like Siri and Alexa.

P9 described using Rabbit as a personal “biographer” during walks. They appreciated its conversational nature and the RabbitHole feature

“If I want to go back again, I have the whole script of the conversation.”
P5 similarly hoped Docus would evolve from a task-based assistant into something more emotionally attuned. He imagined a future version that would feel like it cared about him:
“It’ll know me so well that it’ll say, ‘Hey, you haven’t spoken to your uncle in Cuba in a week. Want me to mark him on your calendar?’ I’d pay for that.”

5.
Discussion

Our findings highlight a persistent gap between user expectations and the actual capabilities of AI personal assistants, revealing ongoing challenges in the adoption of emerging technologies. Users often encounter uncertainty about what these assistants can realistically do and how they fit into daily life, leading to mismatches between anticipated and experienced gratifications. These uncertainties—functional, interactional, and social—can erode trust, diminish user satisfaction, and limit long-term engagement. By mapping these uncertainties to distinct user gratifications, we identify strategies for better aligning expectations with system performance, grounded in the framework of Uses and Gratifications Theory.

As introduced in §
2.1.1
, UGT helps explain why users adopt new technologies and the extent to which these technologies fulfill specific needs—technological, utilitarian, hedonic, and social
(
Katz et al. 1973
;
Rubin 2009
;
Lin and Ng 2024
)
. While participants reported gratification in some interactions with AI assistants, others resulted in disappointment, revealing a disconnect between device functionality and user motivations. These mismatches generate uncertainty and interfere with the satisfaction of intended gratifications
(
Dalsgaard and Dindler 2014
;
Ackerman 2000
)
. As noted in §
2.1.2
, such uncertainties are central to understanding barriers in the adoption and continued use of AI systems.

While earlier work, including Lin and Ng
(
Lin and Ng 2024
)
, examines gratifications and technology acceptance, it often overlooks how marketing hype shapes user expectations and how the failure to meet these promises fosters uncertainty. Our findings indicate that users approach AI assistants with complex, context-sensitive expectations—anticipating not just efficient task performance but also socially responsive, conversational experiences. When these expectations are unmet, particularly in areas like contextual memory and natural dialogue, users experience disappointment. Ackerman frames this disconnect as a “sociotechnical gap”: the divide between users’ socially situated needs (“social requirements”) and the limitations of technical implementation (“technical feasibility”)
(
Ackerman 2000
)
. To explore how this gap plays out in everyday use, we introduce three forms of uncertainty—functional, interactional, and social—through the combined lenses of user gratification and mediated uncertainty
(
Zhang et al. 2022b
;
Chang et al. 2024
)
:

(1)

Functional uncertainty:
Users are unclear about the AI assistant’s actual capabilities and limitations, leading to confusion or hesitation in use. This uncertainty stems from ambiguous system boundaries, leaving users unsure of which tasks the assistant can reliably complete. This is detailed in §
5.1
.

(2)

Interactional uncertainty:
This refers to users’ uncertainty about how to effectively communicate with or issue commands to the assistant, often resulting in miscommunication, inefficient or frustrating interactions. This uncertainty is covered in §
5.2
.

(3)

Social uncertainty:
Users express uncertainty about the assistant’s appropriate role in their social lives—whether it should act as a helpful tool, a social companion, or something in between. We explore this uncertainty in §
5.3
.

In this section, we map user gratifications and uncertainties to clarify the relationship between the types of gratification driving user adoption and different dimensions of technology uncertainties. For each of the uncertainties, we also provide design and policy recommendations that can alleviate the uncertainty. We summarize the relationships between these uncertainties and gratifications in Table
2
.

Our work further contextualizes recent literature on AI assistant particularly regarding how intent misalignment, tone mismatches, and perceived obstruction contribute to user uncertainty. As discussed in the new related work section, AI systems like ChatGPT frequently misinterpret user goals or overlook contextual cues, resulting in responses that feel irrelevant or out of step with user intent
(
Rao et al. 2023
;
Kim et al. 2024
)
. These failures exacerbate interactional uncertainty, especially when compounded by tone mismatches, which can diminish users’ sense of being understood or respected during interactions
(
Ray 2023
)
. Additionally, refusal to engage—particularly when prompted by controversial or sensitive topics—has been perceived as obstructive, even when framed in safety language
(
Borji 2023
)
. These patterns further illustrate how AI assistants fall short of social and hedonic gratifications, and they reinforce our framing of the sociotechnical gap: users expect contextually responsive, socially competent agents, yet current implementations often default to rigid, risk-averse responses that violate those expectations. These insights expand our categorization of functional, interactional, and social uncertainty by tying them directly to design failures in tone, intent recognition, and refusal logic, offering additional leverage points for both interface design and policy intervention which we present in the following sections.

5.1.
When Promises Fail: Functional Uncertainty in AI Assistant Use

Functional uncertainty arises when users are unsure about what their AI assistant can or cannot do. This includes confusion around whether the assistant can reliably complete specific tasks or deliver consistent performance. As illustrated in Figure
1
, this uncertainty was especially pronounced in cases where marketing hype significantly misrepresented actual capabilities—leading some users, like P4, to wonder if the Rabbit R1 was a scam and consider joining a class action lawsuit due to the stark mismatch between promised and real functionality.

Such uncertainty was most common among users seeking technological or utilitarian gratification. Participants like P2 and P3 were initially attracted by the promise of advanced functionality but grew frustrated when core features—such as ordering food or rideshares—did not work. P4 expected the Rabbit R1 to recall prior pizza orders and manage routine tasks but found that even basic advertised features like Uber integration failed. These unfulfilled expectations undermined trust and often led early adopters to abandon the devices altogether.

Utilitarian gratification also suffered. P5, using Docus for medical support, was repeatedly told to “see a doctor,” even when seeking straightforward guidance. P6 found that the Rabbit R1 lacked sufficient support for journaling and note-taking, leading him to supplement it with ChatGPT. This workaround reflects how users created multi-tool ecosystems to compensate for the limited functionality of their AI assistants.

Even hedonic gratification was disrupted. P4, who used Rabbit for entertainment and casual storytelling, reported that the assistant often responded with generic or irrelevant content, which undermined the intended enjoyment and playfulness of the interaction.

5.1.1.
Design Implications: Bridging the Gap Between Design Promises and AI Assistant Capabilities

The Rabbit R1 and AI Pin exemplify how inflated marketing claims can create unrealistic user expectations. Our findings suggest that AI product development often prioritizes aesthetic design and promotional narratives over actual user needs. Devices marketed as intelligent, autonomous "second selves" ultimately fell short in practice. As a result, many users based their purchasing decisions on aspirational marketing rather than an informed understanding of the technology, underscoring the need for widespread AI literacy
(
Stolpe and Hallström 2024
)
.

To build more trustworthy and usable systems, AI development must be better aligned with real-world user needs
(
Lancaster et al. 2024
;
Rakova et al. 2021
)
. A core challenge for designers is setting and communicating realistic expectations about what AI assistants can do—both in their functionality and in how they are positioned through marketing
(
Manzini et al. 2024
)
.

Task-specific AI agents like Ohai and Docus proved more effective, in part because their design and marketing were aligned with clear, well-scoped use cases. As users like P1 and P6 demonstrated, assistants built around domain-specific language models—such as calendar management or medical support—were more likely to meet expectations
(
Ackerman 2000
)
. Participants selectively delegated tasks based on their comfort and perceived reliability of the assistant. These tools enabled targeted task offloading, which participants found more manageable and trustworthy
(
Zubatiy et al. 2023
;
Weis and Wiese 2022
;
Gilbert et al. 2020
;
Wahn et al. 2023
)
. In contrast, general-purpose assistants often failed to integrate seamlessly into user routines and offered limited value in return.

Pairing Task-Specific Design with Transparent Communication

To mitigate functional uncertainty, designers should focus on transparency in both the design and communication of AI systems. Broad, general-purpose marketing claims—like those made for the Rabbit R1 and AI Pin—tend to over-promise and ultimately erode user trust. Instead, clearly defining task-specific capabilities and limitations can improve alignment between user expectations and system performance. As the success of Ohai and Docus illustrates, users respond more positively to assistants that support targeted, contextually relevant use cases. Marketing should reflect the assistant’s true scope rather than promote aspirational narratives of autonomy. While marketing AI assistants more realistically is central, it is also important for users to have AI literacy which we discuss in the next section.

5.1.2.
Policy Recommendation: Advancing AI Literacy for Informed Adoption

The difficulty of defining AI—even among experts
(
Schank 1987
;
Stone et al. 2022
)
—is compounded for consumers and product designers
(
Weitz et al. 2024
;
Stone et al. 2022
)
. As shown in §
4.1
, participants like P3 and P7 misunderstood simple rule-based functions as sophisticated AI features, which contributed to inflated expectations and subsequent disappointment.

This underscores the urgent need for improving public understanding of what AI is, what it can (and cannot) do, and how it operates in real-world applications. Recent work highlights that AI literacy—distinct from traditional computer science education—is critical for promoting more informed and successful AI adoption
(
Jin et al. 2024
)
. We recommend integrating AI literacy into early education curricula to ensure future users are better equipped to critically evaluate AI technologies. This would not only improve user satisfaction and reduce functional uncertainty but also support the development of more transparent, ethical, and user-aligned AI systems
(
Funa and Gabay 2025
)
.

5.2.
Designing for Fluency: Supporting User-Curated AI Ecosystems

Interactional uncertainty arises when users are unclear about how to effectively communicate with or operate their AI assistant. This type of uncertainty influences how users phrase inputs, interpret system responses, and build interaction fluency over time.

For those seeking hedonic gratification, such as P4, this uncertainty manifested in difficulty crafting prompts that elicited engaging or entertaining outputs. Attempts to prompt the Rabbit for jokes or storytelling often resulted in irrelevant or incoherent responses, diminishing the entertainment value and hedonic satisfaction.

Social gratification was similarly disrupted. P9, who used the Rabbit as a journaling companion during walks, discovered that conversations lacked continuity across sessions. Accessing previous entries required logging into a separate platform, breaking the conversational flow and weakening the device’s perceived role as a relational partner.

Interactional uncertainty also posed challenges for users pursuing technical and hedonic goals. P3 found that the Humane AI’s sophisticated form factor was undermined by its unintuitive interface and limited interaction modalities. Even users with strong technical skills struggled to figure out how to issue commands or retrieve information effectively. P6 was unable to get the Rabbit R1 to perform note-taking tasks directly. To resolve this, he integrated the Rabbit’s voice-recording function with ChatGPT’s summarization capabilities, thereby creating a customized workflow. This adaptation provided utilitarian gratification not only by achieving a functional outcome but also by reinforcing P6’s sense of technological competence and agency in managing his tools.

5.2.1.
User-Curated Constellations: AI Assistants in Everyday Technological Bricolage

Our findings reveal a key insight: users achieved meaningful utilitarian gratification not by relying on AI assistants as standalone solutions, but by actively integrating them into existing technological ecosystems. Although participants were initially attracted by marketing claims that positioned devices like the Rabbit R1 and Humane AI Pin as comprehensive, all-in-one AI companions, these expectations quickly gave way to more pragmatic adaptations. As the limitations of these assistants became apparent, users began to identify specific use cases where the technology added genuine value—often by incorporating it into a broader, personalized configuration of tools.

For example, P6 used the Rabbit R1’s voice recording feature in tandem with ChatGPT’s summarization capabilities to create an efficient workflow for note-taking and task management. Similarly, P1 found Ohai especially useful for managing schedules across multiple calendar platforms (Google, Apple, Outlook), leveraging its task-specific strengths. These practices reflect Forlizzi’s concept of product ecologies, where the value of a product is shaped through its interaction with users and their surrounding environments
(
Forlizzi 2008
)
.

We observed individuals engaging in a form of technological bricolage—crafting their own constellations of services and devices to suit personal needs. This builds on earlier work on constellations of technologies
(
Rossitto et al. 2014
;
Hutchins 2020
;
Stone et al. 2022
)
, but in our study, users were often the sole architects of these ecologies, navigating compatibility and integration on their own terms. The most effective uses of AI emerged when systems supported modularity—that is, when assistants were not locked into rigid ecosystems, but could flexibly integrate with other tools
(
Kabudi et al. 2021
)
. This flexibility enabled users to tailor assistants like Ohai and ChatGPT to address specific tasks, enhancing satisfaction and utility.

Still, constructing these personalized ecosystems often required a level of technical fluency that not all participants possessed. While the hype surrounding AI assistants suggested they would function as comprehensive, plug-and-play solutions, our study shows that real value emerged when users exercised agency—assembling interoperable technologies that worked together to meet their needs. This interoperability not only reinforced utilitarian gratification but also helped users mitigate performance-related uncertainties associated with relying on a single assistant.

5.2.2.
Design Implications: Designing for System Transparency and Modularity

Prior research underscores the challenges users face in detecting and understanding AI failures
(
Bisante et al. 2023
)
. To build trust and improve user satisfaction, AI systems must prioritize transparency by offering clear, interpretable explanations of how they function and why they may fail
(
Zubatiy et al. 2023
;
Mathur et al. 2023
;
Weitz et al. 2024
)
. This approach, often referred to as “explainable AI design,” plays a critical role in bridging the gap between system behavior and user expectations. When users are informed about how decisions are made, they are more likely to accept outputs—even if those outputs fall short of their immediate goals.

System transparency should not be treated as a secondary feature but as a foundational element of AI system design. Explanations must be embedded from the outset—not only to support model inspection but to enhance user comprehension and interaction. Jin et al. argue for tailoring explanations to different user needs through a human-centered approach to AI explainability
(
Jin et al. 2021
)
. As illustrated in their framework, three core explanation forms—rules, examples, and features—can serve as intuitive scaffolds for user understanding. Rules offer logical reasoning patterns behind a system’s behavior (“if-then” statements), examples help ground explanations in concrete, relatable instances, and features highlight which input variables most influenced a decision. By integrating such explanation types into AI interfaces, designers can create diagnostic feedback that is both interpretable and actionable, supporting user trust and more informed interaction with AI assistants. Earlier work on explanatory interactive learning
(
Teso et al. 2023
;
Schramowski et al. 2020
)
view explanations as a way for the system to communicate with the user, who then in turn, now that they understand how the model works, can provide feedback that leads to successful model refinement
(
Stammer et al. 2023
;
Teso and Kersting 2019
)
.

In healthcare-specific tools like Docus, for example, LLM-generated explanations could help clarify why the system cannot answer certain queries. P5, who reported frustration with incomplete responses from Docus, may have benefited from a simple explanation indicating that the system lacked access to personalized health history due to privacy restrictions. Because AI systems in healthcare often do not have access to complete electronic medical records—due to privacy laws like HIPAA—they may default to general, conservative advice
(
Alowais et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2021
;
Jeyaraman et al. 2023b
;
Jeyaraman et al. 2023a
)
. Without this context, users may interpret a system’s caution as incompetence or failure, rather than a safety measure. Relevant design recommendations are presented next.

5.2.3.
Interpretable Explanations Through Embedded System Feedback

We recommend that AI assistants—especially in sensitive domains like healthcare—integrate interpretable feedback mechanisms that explain what the system can and cannot do, and why certain failures occur. For example, when a user asks a medical question that cannot be answered due to lack of access to medical history, the system could display or vocalize a message such as:

“To provide a more specific recommendation, I would need access to your medical history, which I do not have for privacy reasons. I can offer general guidance or recommend that you speak with your healthcare provider.”

This could be implemented through an explainer overlay in the user interface, a fallback message, or a dedicated “Why can’t I do [task]?” button linked to contextual explanations. These transparent, human-readable messages demystify the assistant’s limitations and prevent users from assuming the system is simply broken or unhelpful. This approach aligns with broader calls for more design at the intersection of interactive AI and explainable AI
(
Turchi et al. 2025
;
Raees et al. 2024
;
Bertrand et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2021
)
, thus making the inner workings of the assistant interpretable, context-aware, and aligned with users’ informational and emotional needs.

5.2.4.
Policy Recommendation: Protecting Users through Interpretable AI Interfaces

Recent work has advocated for the importance of regulating explainability in AI, while also identifying challenges arising from the need to draw on a multitude of disciplines (e.g., engineers, designers, and legal scholars) as well as users
(
Nahar et al. 2024
)
. Panigutti et al. call attention to the importance of integrating explainability into the legal governance of AI, particularly in high-risk domains (e.g., accessing financial or medical resources), where transparency must be accompanied by clarity on accountability and recourse
(
Panigutti et al. 2023
)
. We argue for the need for robust legal frameworks that require AI systems—not only in high-stakes environments but also in everyday tools like personal assistants—to embed interpretable feedback directly into their interfaces. Such frameworks would help users understand not just what an AI assistant can do, but why it fails or succeeds in particular contexts, fostering trust and informed use.

5.3.
Between Friend and Tool: Controlling Memory and Context Fidelity

Social uncertainty refers to users’ confusion or ambivalence about the social role an AI assistant should fulfill—whether it should function purely as a tool, a companion, or something in between. This form of uncertainty was particularly evident among participants seeking social gratification. P9 described using Rabbit as a conversational partner and biographer during walks, while P5 wished for Docus to serve as a caregiving companion that would, for instance, remind him to contact his uncle. P6 appreciated the Rabbit’s humorous tone but, like P9, wanted a more persistent and relational interaction—expectations that the device could not meet. Across these cases, the lack of memory and contextual continuity diminished the sense of companionship, leaving participants questioning what kind of relationship they were meant to have with the assistant. For P9, the loss of journaling continuity after each session significantly weakened Rabbit’s perceived social role.

Even as participants desired more companion-like, contextually aware AI assistants, many did not fully grasp the implications of such personalization
(
Zubatiy et al. 2023
)
. This disconnect raises concerns about contextual integrity—the extent to which information is shared and used appropriately within specific social contexts. Highly personalized AI requires significant access to user data to deliver relevant responses, potentially at the cost of user autonomy or privacy. Conversely, restricting data access can undermine the assistant’s utility. This tension underscores the importance of transparency and user control in AI design.

Future AI systems should empower users to define these boundaries—determining what should be remembered, when, and under what circumstances. Nissenbaum’s theory of contextual integrity emphasizes that privacy is not simply about controlling access to information, but about ensuring that information flows in socially appropriate ways
(
Nissenbaum 2004
)
. Trust in AI systems depends on clearly defining responsibility across multiple actors: the user, other individuals in their social networks, designers, and policy makers
(
Milosevic 2021
;
Jeyaraman et al. 2023b
;
Jeyaraman et al. 2023a
;
Procter et al. 2023
)
.

5.3.1.
Design Recommendation: Controlling Context Fidelity

Participants held varied views on how context-aware their assistants should be. P4, P5, and P6 wanted precise, context-sensitive responses and were frustrated when the assistant forgot prior interactions. Others, like P2 and P8, preferred more fluid, humanlike conversation. P9 envisioned a persistent, biographer-like companion—someone to talk to during walks, who could preserve and build on those conversations across time.

To reduce social uncertainty and support more meaningful interactions, AI assistants should enable users to define how context is retained and applied. This includes giving users fine-grained control over what information is remembered, how it is used, and when it should be forgotten.

Enabling Context Fidelity and Relational Personalization

To accommodate the wide range of user expectations around memory and social interaction, AI assistants should incorporate user-customizable memory settings, supporting the emerging design principle of context fidelity
(
Diederich et al. 2022
;
Ghalebikesabi et al. 2024
;
Talukdar and Biswas 2024
)
. Users should have clear agency over what the assistant remembers, when it forgets, and how contextual data is applied across interactions
(
Im et al. 2021
)
. Following models like ChatGPT’s memory controls—where users can disable memory, manage stored content, or initiate “temporary chat” sessions without data retention
(
Lunden 2025
)
—AI assistants should provide transparent, accessible options for managing memory preferences.

In addition, assistants should allow users to define persistent interaction sessions (e.g., “morning journaling”) and select relational modes (e.g., “friend,” “biographer,” “assistant”). These personalized settings would help clarify the assistant’s intended social function, reduce interactional ambiguity, and strengthen its capacity to fulfill users’ social and emotional needs. Supporting such fine-grained personalization is critical for building trust, supporting autonomy, and enabling AI systems to function as meaningful social partners.

5.3.2.
Policy Recommendation: Benchmarking Contextual Integrity in AI Systems

Research has shown that users’ willingness to engage with AI often hinges on their perception of risk and privacy trade-offs
(
Skirpan et al. 2018
;
Ackerman and Mainwaring 2005
)
. Not having a contextually appropriate understanding of privacy boundaries might lead to
older adults sharing sensitive information including personal medical and financial information with voice assistants that they perceived as companions
(
Oewel et al. 2023
)
. Similarly, in our study, several participants expressed privacy apathy. P5, for instance, showed little concern about why Docus did not recall his medical history, while P4 was unconcerned about surveillance, jokingly reducing it to the cost of receiving “more Facebook advertisements.”

The responsibility for setting context fidelity parameters does not lie solely with individual users. As assistants increasingly operate in shared or relational contexts, the question of who controls contextual integrity—especially in relation to others’ data or overlapping social dynamics—requires broader input from designers, users, and policy frameworks
(
Nissenbaum 2004
)
. In this regard, future regulations should mandate the adoption of auditing tools like CI-Bench, which evaluates AI assistants based on their adherence to principles of contextual integrity
(
Cheng et al. 2024
)
. Cheng et al.
(
Cheng et al. 2024
)
argue that violations of contextual norms often arise from inappropriate information flows—such as sharing information with unintended recipients or inferring sensitive attributes in the wrong context (e.g., is it appropriate to share a user’s personal data?). These diagnostic benchmarks can help system designers and regulators pinpoint where assistants breach social expectations and offer a standard for what “appropriate” behavior looks like. Embedding frameworks like CI-Bench into regulatory compliance pipelines would not only foster alignment with social privacy norms but also enable enforceable accountability for AI assistant behaviors.

5.4.
Limitations and Future Work

Recruiting early adopters of emerging AI technologies presents inherent challenges due to the limited user base during initial development stages. To address this, future research should explore online discourse surrounding these technologies on platforms such as
r/RabbitR1
and
r/AiPin
. These forums offer rich, user-generated content that can provide insights into user experiences, expectations, and the evolving perceptions of AI assistants. Prior work has demonstrated the value of analyzing online communities to understand emerging technologies (e.g.,
(
Katta 2025
;
Qi et al. 2023
;
Ali et al. 2025
)
).

In addition to analyzing online conversations, future studies should examine the performance of AI assistants in specialized domains where accuracy and contextual understanding are paramount. These include healthcare diagnosis and treatment planning, legal and financial advisory tools, and educational applications. Evaluating AI in these settings is essential for ensuring reliability, fairness, and user trust, particularly among vulnerable populations.

6.
Conclusion

Our study reveals the widening gap between the expectations set by AI assistant marketing and the functional realities users experience. By mapping different forms of uncertainty—functional, interactional, and social—against user gratifications, we show how current systems often fall short of delivering the fluid, intelligent, and socially responsive assistance they promise. Rather than comprehensive “second selves,” the most successful AI assistants were those that offered clearly scoped, task-specific value within a broader user-curated ecosystem. Users who could creatively integrate multiple tools achieved the most satisfaction. To close the sociotechnical gap, future AI systems must prioritize interpretability, modularity, and contextual control. Designers must embed explanations into the core of the interface, not as an afterthought, and policies must require tools like CI-Bench to evaluate whether systems uphold contextual integrity. By aligning system design and regulation with human expectations and real-life use, we can foster more trustworthy and empowering AI experiences.
