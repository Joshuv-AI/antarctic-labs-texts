CBCT evaluation of bone thickness in nasomaxillary and zygomaticomaxillary buttresses for Le Fort I osteotomy in UCLP patients
Authors: Yunus Çetiner, Duygu Çelik Özen, İrfan Üstündağ, Elif Begüm Yiğit Çetiner, Şuayip Burak Duman, Bahadır Sancar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCleft lip and palate (CLP) is one of the most common congenital anomalies affecting the craniofacial skeleton and is frequently associated with maxillary hypoplasia and midface deficiency. In these patients, orthognathic surgery—particularly Le Fort I osteotomy—constitutes a critical step in treatment. However, postoperative stability may be influenced by anatomical variations in the maxillary buttress regions. This study aimed to evaluate total bone thickness in the nasomaxillary and zygomaticomaxillary buttresses using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) and to compare the findings between Unilateral Cleft Lip and Palate (UCLP) patients and age- and sex-matched non-cleft individuals.MethodsThis retrospective study included CBCT images of 51 patients with UCLP and 51 age- and sex-matched control individuals. Bone thickness measurements were performed at predefined anatomical reference points representing the nasomaxillary and zygomaticomaxillary buttresses. Measurements were analyzed to evaluate regional thickness distribution patterns and intergroup differences. Statistical analyses were conducted to compare measurements between groups and between cleft and non-cleft sides in UCLP patients.ResultsIn the nasomaxillary and zygomaticomaxillary buttress regions, significant variations in total bone thickness were observed. Compared with controls, the UCLP group demonstrated lower values at several measurement sites, while marked differences were also identified between the cleft and non-cleft sides. The most pronounced differences were observed in the anterior regions, where the cleft side consistently exhibited reduced bone thickness. No significant right–left differences were detected in the control group.ConclusionsRegional variations in total bone thickness were identified in UCLP patients. These findings provide clinically relevant anatomical information and should be considered during preoperative assessment and surgical planning for Le Fort I osteotomy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09073-0.

Background

CLP is one of the most common congenital craniofacial anomalies affecting the facial and oral regions, occurring in approximately one in every 700 live births [1].

Its etiology is multifactorial and includes genetic predisposition as well as environmental factors such as exposure to chemicals and/or radiation, maternal health status, and inadequate nutrition [2]. CLP is frequently associated with maxillary hypoplasia; the severity of hypoplasia is variable, and due to the presence of the cleft, normal growth rates cannot be maintained, becoming more pronounced with the patient’s development. This condition results in midfacial deficiency, skeletal Class III relationships, and dental malocclusions, often requiring multiple surgical interventions to achieve satisfactory aesthetic and functional outcomes [3, 4]. Orthognathic surgery generally constitutes the final stage of treatment in patients with CLP. More than 25% of these patients develop significant maxillary hypoplasia requiring surgical intervention, and the most commonly performed procedure is maxillary osteotomy [5]. For the correction of maxillary retrusion, the conventional surgical approach is considered to be Le Fort I osteotomy [6]. The standard Le Fort I osteotomy, first described by Obwegeser in 1962 for patients with cleft lip and palate, remains one of the most commonly performed surgical techniques today [7, 8]. The need for orthognathic surgery in patients with UCLP ranges from 15.6% to 48.3% [9]. Orthognathic surgery in cleft patients is generally challenging, as there is a risk of relapse following maxillary advancement due to increased skeletal instability. Scar tissue resulting from previous surgical interventions and the inherent nature of the cleft may also predispose these patients to ischemic complications. It is well known that larger maxillary advancements are associated with higher levels of instability and relapse. Although fixation with titanium plates and screws provides greater stability in anterior and inferior movements, relapse may still occur [10]. Conventional maxillary osteotomy may be unstable, and soft tissue tension due to scar contracture can increase surgical relapse, thereby complicating surgical outcomes and distancing the patient from functionally and aesthetically acceptable results [11].The craniofacial morphology of patients with CLP differs from that of non-cleft patients. Maxillary deficiencies are generally corrected with Le Fort I osteotomy. In patients with cleft palate, previous scar tissue or pharyngoplasty, the severity of the deformity, and the presence of pharyngeal flaps or grafts may lead to skeletal instability and postoperative relapse. Instability and relapse make surgical mobilization, orthodontic treatment, and particularly the fixation technique critically important [12]. Surgery involves sectioning and repositioning of the maxilla and mandible, followed by stabilization using plates and screws; this process contributes to achieving facial symmetry, correcting occlusion, and improving aesthetics [13]. The craniofacial skeleton is supported by vertical and horizontal buttresses that form the more robust regions of the face. These structures protect important anatomical components such as the teeth, nasal cavities, sinuses, and orbit [14]. The transmission or absorption and balancing of external forces and masticatory loads to the cranial base are provided by the buttresses [15]. Vertical buttresses include the nasomaxillary, zygomaticomaxillary, pterygomaxillary, and vertical mandibular regions of the facial skeleton [16]. Due to their relative thickness and strength, buttress systems are preferred sites for the placement of plates and screws in craniofacial surgeries. Nasomaxillary and zygomaticomaxillary buttresses are frequently used for skeletal anchorage in orthodontics because of their thicker cortical bone structure [17].

CBCT is widely used in dentistry due to its low radiation dose, three-dimensional imaging capability, and high resolution, and it plays an important role in clinical, radiographic, and finite element analysis studies. The SEDENTEXCT Consortium has emphasized that, in terms of radiation dose, CBCT is the most appropriate method for supporting three-dimensional imaging in patients with cleft lip and palate, making CBCT a valuable tool for both preoperative planning and postoperative evaluation [18–25].

Maxillary bone quality is a critical factor influencing the success and postoperative stability of orthognathic surgery. Although bone thickness in Le Fort I osteotomy fixation regions has been investigated in non-cleft populations, evidence regarding patients with UCLP remains limited. Therefore, the aim of this study was to compare bone thickness in the nasomaxillary and zygomaticomaxillary buttresses between UCLP patients and matched non-cleft controls using CBCT. We hypothesized that significant differences in bone thickness exist between the groups in these clinically relevant fixation regions.

Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of İnönü University Faculty of Health Sciences(approval no:2026/9062). Due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized CBCT records, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of İnönü University Faculty of Health Sciences. A power analysis was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7; Franz Faul, Universität Kiel, Germany). The effect size was set at Cohen’s d = 0.50 (moderate effect), with a type I error level of α = 0.05 and a type II error level of β = 0.20 (power = 80%). Based on these parameters, a minimum of 51 participants was required for each group. Therefore, 102 individuals (51 UCLP and 51 controls) were included in the study. Although some analyses were performed using side-based measurements, the sample size calculation was based on the number of participants rather than the number of measurement sites, as bilateral measurements obtained from the same individual are not statistically independent observations.

Patient selection

In this study, CBCT images of 51 patients with unilateral cleft lip and palate (UCLP) (20 females and 31 males; mean age: 22.14 ± 4.8 years) and 51 non-cleft control individuals (20 females and 31 males; mean age: 22.14 ± 4.8 years) were evaluated. The UCLP group consisted of patients diagnosed with unilateral cleft lip and palate who underwent CBCT imaging at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology, İnönü University, between January 2018 and December 2025. Of the 51 UCLP patients, 18 presented with right-sided clefts and 33 with left-sided clefts. Inclusion criteria for the UCLP group were: (1) confirmed diagnosis of unilateral cleft lip and palate, (2) availability of CBCT images with adequate image quality for assessment of the study region, and (3) complete demographic records.

The control group was randomly selected from the same institutional CBCT archive and consisted of individuals without cleft lip and palate, craniofacial anomalies, or maxillofacial pathologies affecting the measurement region. These CBCT examinations had originally been acquired for various diagnostic and treatment-planning purposes, including impacted tooth assessment, implant planning, orthodontic evaluation, and other routine dentomaxillofacial indications. To minimize potential confounding factors, a one-to-one matching procedure was performed based on age and sex. Each control subject was individually matched to a UCLP patient of the same sex and a similar age. Consequently, both groups had identical sex distributions and comparable age characteristics.

Exclusion criteria for both groups included: (1) craniofacial syndromes other than cleft lip and palate, (2) history of orthognathic surgery or craniofacial reconstructive procedures, (3) bone defects, pathological lesions, or supernumerary teeth in the measurement area, (4) CBCT scans in which the region of interest was outside the field of view, and (5) severe metal or motion artifacts compromising image interpretation.

Image acquisition

All imaging procedures were performed using the same CBCT device(NewTom 5G, QR, Verona, Italy). The scanning parameters were set at 110 kVp, 1–20 mA, a field of view (FOV) of 15 × 12 cm², and a voxel size of 0.3 mm. Scans were obtained in the supine position using a fixed-angle device with a vertical gantry, and the images were analyzed using NNT software.

Evaluation of bone thickness in the nasomaxillary and zygomaticomaxillary regions

To determine the planned screw insertion sites for Le Fort I osteotomy fixation, CBCT axial sections, multiplanar reconstruction (MPR) images, and three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction images were jointly evaluated by an oral and maxillofacial radiology specialist (DCO) and an oral and maxillofacial surgery specialist (YC). The measurement points were determined by consensus under the guidance of the oral and maxillofacial radiologist and were selected to represent the conventional screw positions used for fixation at the nasomaxillary and zygomaticomaxillary buttresses following Le Fort I osteotomy [17, 26]. As all measurement locations were established through joint consensus, no inter-observer agreement analysis was performed for point selection. In the nasomaxillary buttress region, the reference level was defined on axial sections at the simulated osteotomy line, corresponding to 5 mm superior to the canine root apex. Three fixation points (A-C) were identified superior to this reference level, while five fixation points (D-H) were established inferior to the reference level in the anteroposterior direction. In the zygomaticomaxillary buttress region, six measurement points (I-N) were identified along the zygomaticomaxillary buttress, representing the conventional superior and inferior screw insertion sites used for miniplate fixation following Le Fort I osteotomy. Three points (I-K) were located at the superior aspect of the buttress, whereas three points (L-N) were positioned inferiorly, corresponding to the typical configuration of fixation screws placed in the zygomaticomaxillary buttress region. Bone thickness measurements were then performed by DCO using NNT software on axial sections at each designated point (Figs. 1 and 2), similar to previously described CBCT-based assessments of screw insertion sites in the nasomaxillary and zygomaticomaxillary buttresses.

Standardization and reliability analysis

To assess measurement reproducibility, 20% of the sample was randomly selected and re-evaluated by the same examiner (DCO) two weeks after the initial measurements using the identical measurement protocol. The examiner was blinded to the initial measurements during the second assessment. Intraobserver reliability was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement. The analysis demonstrated excellent intraobserver reliability (ICC = 0.922; 95% CI: 0.893–0.943), indicating high reproducibility and consistency of the measurement procedure.

Statistical methods

The normality of the variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of data distributions. Variables that did not conform to a normal distribution were presented as median (minimum-maximum) values. Differences between two independent groups were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test, while differences among three independent groups were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. Bonferroni correction was applied for post hoc pairwise comparisons following significant Kruskal-Wallis H test results. Effect sizes (r) were calculated for Mann-Whitney U test comparisons using the formula r = Z/√N to evaluate the magnitude of observed differences. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and analyzed using the Pearson chi-square test. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 51 UCLP patients and 51 control subjects were included in the study. No significant differences were observed between the groups regarding age or sex distribution (p > 0.05) (Table 1). Comparison of the UCLP and control groups demonstrated significant differences at six measurement sites. Bone thickness values were significantly lower in the UCLP group at points C, D, J, and K, whereas significantly greater values were observed at points E and F. No significant differences were identified at the remaining measurement sites Effect size analysis demonstrated large effects at points E (r = 0.761), F (r = 0.675), and J (r = 0.593), whereas the remaining significant comparisons showed only small effects. (Table 2). When the non-cleft side, cleft side, and control group were analyzed separately, significant intergroup differences were observed at points A–K, whereas no significant differences were detected at points L–N (Table 3). The most pronounced differences were observed at the anterior measurement sites (A–D) and at points G–H, where the cleft side consistently demonstrated markedly reduced bone thickness compared with both the non-cleft side and the control group. In contrast, the non-cleft side generally exhibited the greatest bone thickness values at points A–I. At points J and K, the control group demonstrated significantly greater bone thickness values than both cleft and non-cleft sides. No significant differences were observed between the right and left sides in the control group (Table 4). Similarly, comparison of the cleft and non-cleft sides revealed significant differences at points A–I, whereas measurements at points J–N were comparable between the two sides (Table 5).

Discussion

Plate–screw fixation used in orthognathic surgery depends on the structural characteristics of the bone in the maxillary buttress regions. Therefore, the present study investigated bone thickness in the nasomaxillary and zygomaticomaxillary buttress regions, which are frequently used for fixation following Le Fort I osteotomy. To reveal possible anatomical differences in these regions in patients with UCLP, groups with and without UCLP were compared. The obtained data provide a clinical basis that may assist in determining appropriate screw positions during surgical fixation planning.

Previous CBCT-based investigations have consistently demonstrated that unilateral cleft lip and palate is associated with substantial alterations in craniofacial and maxillary morphology. Haylaz et al. [26] reported significantly reduced midfacial width, midfacial depth, maxillary width, and maxillary length in UCLP patients compared with non-cleft controls, indicating generalized maxillary developmental deficiency. Similarly, Abuhijleh et al. [27] demonstrated maxillary retrusion and altered craniofacial growth patterns in UCLP individuals across different developmental stages. These findings suggest that the effects of UCLP extend beyond the alveolar cleft itself and influence the morphology of the entire maxillary complex. In addition to generalized maxillary deficiency, previous studies have demonstrated significant regional asymmetry in UCLP patients. Parveen et al. [28] reported reduced maxillary dimensions and volume on the cleft side, whereas Starbuck et al. [29] identified pronounced morphological differences concentrated within the oronasal and midfacial regions. Collectively, these studies indicate that cleft-related anatomical alterations are not uniformly distributed throughout the craniofacial skeleton and may vary according to anatomical location. Therefore, regional differences in bone thickness within fixation-related areas of the maxilla may be expected in UCLP patients. Although most existing studies have examined bone thickness in the nasomaxillary and zygomaticomaxillary regions, they generally focus on miniplate applications and do not provide clinical guidance applicable to Le Fort I osteotomy [30–32]. Furthermore, these investigations were conducted predominantly in non-cleft populations and therefore do not account for the anatomical alterations associated with unilateral cleft lip and palate. In contrast, the present study specifically evaluated bone thickness in fixation-related regions of the nasomaxillary and zygomaticomaxillary buttresses in UCLP patients. The findings demonstrated that bone thickness was not uniformly distributed across the buttress regions and that significant side-related and regional differences were present in UCLP patients. These observations suggest that fixation sites routinely used during Le Fort I osteotomy may exhibit substantial anatomical variability in cleft patients compared with non-cleft individuals. To the best of our knowledge, no previous CBCT-based study has specifically compared bone thickness in the nasomaxillary and zygomaticomaxillary buttresses between UCLP patients and matched non-cleft controls in the context of Le Fort I osteotomy fixation planning. Achieving optimal functional and aesthetic outcomes after orthognathic surgery in patients with CLP is considerably more challenging compared with the normal population. Scarring changes resulting from numerous soft tissue surgeries and the necessity for extensive maxillary advancement are the main factors that negatively affect surgical planning and postoperative stability [33]. The application of multiple surgical interventions in CLP patients is quite common and usually begins in early childhood. Although these procedures are necessary, they often lead to scar formation and limitation of the maxillary growth potential. After completion of the adolescent growth period, approximately 10–20% of these patients require orthognathic surgery to achieve aesthetically and functionally satisfactory outcomes [10].

Previous studies have demonstrated that UCLP patients exhibit higher relapse rates and lower postoperative stability following Le Fort I osteotomy compared with non-cleft individuals. Factors such as extensive maxillary advancement, scar tissue formation, and altered craniofacial morphology have been suggested as contributors to this increased instability [10, 11, 33–35].

Previous studies evaluating miniplate and miniscrew stability have consistently emphasized the importance of regional bone characteristics, particularly bone thickness and density, in achieving primary fixation stability [31, 36–40].

Fayed et al. [41] evaluated bone dimensions for orthodontic mini-implant placement using CBCT and reported regional variations in bone thickness throughout the maxilla. Similarly, Lee et al. [42] demonstrated that the infrazygomatic crest region exhibited greater bone thickness than surrounding anatomical areas, supporting its suitability for skeletal anchorage. Rossi et al. [31] further reported that bone thickness varies according to anatomical location and tends to increase toward posterior and basal regions of the maxilla. Collectively, these studies highlight the heterogeneous distribution of bone thickness within the maxilla and support the importance of site-specific anatomical assessment prior to fixation procedures. Medeiros et al. [32] assessed bone thickness suitable for miniplate placement in the nasomaxillary and zygomaticomaxillary regions using tomography-based evaluation. The study reported that the most appropriate placement site in the nasomaxillary region was approximately 3 mm away from the apex of the maxillary canine root. In the zygomaticomaxillary region, the suitable miniplate placement area was reported to be located distal to the distobuccal root of the first molar and approximately 3.5 mm from the border of the infraorbital foramen. The authors emphasized the importance of evaluations based on regional anatomical reference points to ensure miniplate stability. Chuang et al. [17] evaluated cortical bone thickness in the nasomaxillary and zygomatic–maxillary buttress regions using CBCT-based simulation in 128 patients scheduled for Le Fort I osteotomy. The study reported the presence of thicker cortical bone in the upper and lower vertical planes of the nasomaxillary buttress region. Additionally, bone thickness was found to be greater in male patients compared to female patients. In the zygomatic–maxillary region, cortical bone thickness was reported to increase particularly near the zygomatic apex and adjacent to the distal segment.

The present findings are generally consistent with the regional distribution patterns reported by Chuang et al. [17] and Medeiros et al. [32], who demonstrated considerable variation in bone thickness across the nasomaxillary and zygomaticomaxillary buttresses. However, unlike previous studies performed in non-cleft populations, the present study demonstrated pronounced side-related differences and marked reductions in bone thickness in several cleft-affected regions. In addition to statistical significance, effect size analysis demonstrated large differences between the UCLP and control groups at points E, F, and J. Notably, the median bone thickness difference exceeded 2 mm at points E and F. Given that local bone thickness is an important determinant of screw selection and fixation stability, these findings may be clinically relevant during Le Fort I osteotomy planning. In contrast, although statistically significant differences were observed at some other measurement points, their effect sizes were small, suggesting more limited clinical relevance. The regional variability identified in the present study suggests that fixation-related anatomy may differ considerably among UCLP patients. Consequently, preoperative CBCT assessment may provide valuable patient-specific anatomical information during fixation planning. The marked reduction in bone thickness observed in several cleft-affected regions highlights the importance of careful evaluation of potential fixation sites during surgical planning. However, because fixation stability, screw performance, and postoperative outcomes were not evaluated, the clinical implications of these anatomical differences should be interpreted with caution.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective single-center design may have introduced selection bias and may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, only bone thickness was evaluated, whereas other factors potentially influencing fixation stability, such as bone density, screw-related characteristics, and surgical technique, were not assessed. In addition, potential confounding factors, including cleft severity, previous cleft-related treatment history (e.g., lip and palate repair), and skeletal pattern, were not analyzed because of the retrospective nature of the study. Although alveolar bone grafting had not been performed in the included patients, previous cleft-related treatment procedures may have influenced maxillary morphology. Furthermore, the CBCT voxel size of 0.3 mm may have limited the precision of very small thickness measurements, particularly in regions where bone thickness approached the voxel resolution. Finally, postoperative outcomes, fixation stability, and relapse rates were not evaluated; therefore, direct clinical implications regarding fixation performance cannot be established from the present findings. Future prospective clinical and biomechanical studies are warranted to further clarify the relationship between regional bone thickness and fixation outcomes following Le Fort I osteotomy.

Conclusions

Significant regional and side-related differences in bone thickness were identified within the nasomaxillary and zygomaticomaxillary buttresses of patients with unilateral cleft lip and palate. Bone thickness was generally reduced in several cleft-affected regions compared with matched non-cleft controls, indicating substantial anatomical variability in fixation-related areas. These findings highlight the importance of individualized preoperative assessment of maxillary buttresses in UCLP patients undergoing Le Fort I osteotomy
