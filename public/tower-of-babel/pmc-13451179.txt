Metabolic fatty acid substrates enhance the structural and mechanical performance of human-induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes
Authors: Mahmoud Gaballah, Kirsi Penttinen, Chandra Prajapati, Katriina Aalto-Setälä
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Human-induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs) offer a powerful platform for disease modeling, drug discovery, and regenerative therapies. However, their clinical and research utility remains limited by their immature, fetal-like phenotype. In the human heart, postnatal metabolic maturation involves a critical switch from glycolysis to fatty acid β-oxidation, enabling efficient ATP production via oxidative phosphorylation. In this study, we investigated whether mimicking this metabolic shift in vitro by culturing hiPSC-CMs in a fatty acid-based maturation medium (FAM) could enhance their structural and functional development compared to a conventional glucose-based medium (GLM). hiPSC-CMs cultured in FAM for two weeks exhibited significant improvements in morphological, metabolic, and mechanical maturation markers. Morphologically, FAM-cultured CMs exhibited enhanced sarcomeric organization, increased cellular alignment, and a more elongated and rod-like shape, which are characteristics typically associated with mature CMs. Ultrastructural analysis further confirmed improved maturation, revealing more organized sarcomeres and densely packed mitochondria compared to GLM-cultured CMs. Metabolically, FAM-cultured CMs demonstrated a clear shift from glycolytic to oxidative metabolism, as evidenced by higher mitochondrial membrane potential, increased oxidative phosphorylation capacity, elevated ATP production, and reduced glycolytic activity. These metabolic adaptations indicate a more adult-like energy profile, consistent with enhanced fatty acid β-oxidation. Mechanically, FAM-cultured cardiomyocytes exhibited enhanced functional maturity, as evidenced by faster calcium transients and greater contraction amplitude, indicating improvements in specific electrophysiological properties. In conclusion, fatty acid supplementation effectively promotes the structural, metabolic, and mechanical maturation of hiPSC-CMs, resulting in a more adult-like phenotype. This strategy provides a robust and straightforward approach to enhance the physiological relevance of hiPSC-CMs for preclinical applications in disease modeling, drug testing, and regenerative medicine.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-026-06375-y.

Introduction

Despite advances in medical technology and treatments, heart diseases remain the leading cause of death worldwide. The proliferation capacity of adult cardiomyocytes (CMs) is limited, resulting in a scarcity of viable human CM sources for drug discovery and, potentially in the future, for repairing injured hearts [1]. The diseased heart may progressively decline, leading to severe long-term consequences such as arrhythmia, congestive heart failure, or even death. Despite a broad range of available treatment options, conventional management often fails to restore damaged CMs with improved contractility and reduced arrhythmic behavior [2]. CMs contain the highest number of mitochondria among all cell types, producing large amounts of ATP, which is essential for critical cellular functions such as contraction, calcium homeostasis, and signaling [3]. Consequently, any dysregulation in mitochondrial function can lead to various cardiomyopathies and heart failure [4].

Before birth, prenatal CMs exhibit predominantly fragmented mitochondrial networks [5] and primarily depend on glucose as their main energy substrate [6]. However, after birth, there is a metabolic shift towards utilizing fatty acid β-oxidation as the predominant energy substrate [7]. The complete oxidation of a single fatty acid molecule can generate approximately 3–4 times more ATP per molecule compared to glucose oxidation [8]. Therefore, mature CMs rely on oxidative phosphorylation as a robust and efficient pathway for ATP synthesis, crucial for sustaining the heart’s continuous mechanical function. Thus, the transition to oxidative energy production is a hallmark of cardiac metabolic maturation [9–11].

Human-induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs) present a valuable platform for in vitro human heart modeling. hiPSC-CMs provide an unlimited supply of highly purified contracting human CMs, facilitated by rapid advancements in differentiation and enrichment techniques. These cells are valuable for studying cellular pathophysiological processes, conducting drug screening, and modeling diseases [12, 13]. However, hiPSC-CMs often exhibit immature fetal-like characteristics, both metabolically and functionally, which differ from adult CMs in various aspects such as cellular structure and metabolism, thus limiting their applications [14, 15]. Consequently, promoting the maturation of hiPSC-CMs has emerged as a primary research focus to enhance their potential applications in myocardial regeneration, disease modeling, and drug screening [16].

In cultured hiPSC-CMs, standard cell culture media primarily provide glucose as the main energy source for cells, promoting de novo lipogenesis while suppressing fatty acid oxidation [17, 18]. Consequently, ATP production primarily occurs through aerobic glycolysis rather than oxidative phosphorylation, maintaining an immature metabolic phenotype [7]. This imbalance may restrict cellular functions that are crucial for maturation. Immature hiPSC-CMs may not fully replicate the physiological and pathological responses of the patient’s heart to stress and drugs. They typically exhibit low sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) calcium (Ca2+) content and, in many instances, action potentials (APs) that rely on calcium (Ca2+) rather than sodium (Na+) currents [16]. Therefore, the transition towards utilizing oxidative phosphorylation as the primary energy substrate is critical for the proper maturation of cultured CMs [7]. Supplementing hiPSC-CMs with fatty acids in the media could potentially induce a more physiologically relevant postnatal state and enhance the developmental maturation of CMs [19].

We hypothesized that adjusting the culture medium composition of hiPSC-CMs to reflect the metabolic substrate utilization of adult cardiomyocytes in vivo would promote a shift from glycolytic to oxidative metabolism, thereby enhancing their maturation. This study investigates how different metabolic substrates influence the molecular, structural, and functional maturation of hiPSC-CMs in vitro. To investigate this, we cultured hiPSC-CMs for two weeks in two distinct media: a glucose-based medium (GLM) and a fatty acid-based medium (FAM) and assessed various maturation markers. Our findings reveal that hiPSC-CMs can adapt to using fatty acids as their primary energy source. Understanding how metabolic substrates influence CM development could contribute to generating more physiologically mature hiPSC-CMs, enhancing their applications in disease modeling, drug screening, and regenerative medicine.

Materials and methods

Human induced pluripotent stem cells culture and expansion

This study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Pirkanmaa Hospital District for the culture and differentiation of human induced pluripotent stem cell lines (permit number: Aalto-Setälä R08070). Informed written consent was obtained from all donors prior to the derivation of the hiPSC lines. hiPSC line UTA.04602.WT used in this study was generated from dermal fibroblasts of a healthy 55-year-old female [20]. The derivation of the undifferentiated hiPSCs line was generated by retroviral reprogramming human fibroblasts using OCT4, SOX2, KLF4, and c-MYC and checked for pluripotency and normality of karyotype [21]. Before CMs differentiation, the hiPSCs were maintained and expanded on mouse embryonic fibroblast feeder cells (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) in KSR medium (KnockOut DMEM) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) containing 10% KnockOut Serum Replacement (Gibco), 1% MEM NEAA (Gibco), 1% GlutaMAX (Gibco), 0.2% β-mercaptoethanol (Gibco), and 0.5% penicillin/streptomycin (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland).

Cardiac-directed cell differentiation and hiPSC-CM purification

Once confluent, the hiPSCs were differentiated into human CMs utilizing the embryoid body differentiation protocol, modified from Karakikes et al. [22] and Lian et al. [23] (full protocol in Supplementary Information). Following differentiation, the resulting cell population contained cardiomyocytes as well as other cell types, including endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and undifferentiated cells. To enrich for cardiomyocytes, cells were dissociated and purified by magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) between days 20–30 of differentiation [24, 25]. Purification was performed using a two-step procedure. First, cultures were dissociated into a single-cell suspension using the Multi Tissue Dissection Kit 3 (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Second, cardiomyocytes were isolated using the PSC-Derived Cardiomyocyte Isolation Kit, human (Miltenyi Biotec), in which non-cardiomyocytes were magnetically labeled with a depletion cocktail and anti-biotin microbeads and removed using a MACS Column in a MACS Separator (full protocol in Supplementary Information). After cell sorting, the cells were resuspended in glucose or fatty acid medium conditions conducted in a humidified incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2. hiPSC-CMs were plated on cell plates or coverslips coated with 0.1% gelatin at high density suited to form a confluent synchronously beating monolayer.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cultured hiPSC-CMs were washed twice with 1× Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS) and then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Sigma-Aldrich) for 20 min. After fixation, the cells were blocked with a blocking solution consisting of 10% normal donkey serum (NDS), 0.1% Triton X-100, and 1% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) in PBS for 45 min. Following blocking, the cells were incubated with different groups of primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The first group was incubated with mouse monoclonal Anti-α-Actinin (Sigma-Aldrich; A7811) and rabbit anti-HIF1α (Invitrogen; 700505). The second group was incubated with mouse monoclonal Tom20 antibody (Santa Cruz; sc-17764) and rabbit anti-sarcomeric α-Actinin polyclonal antibody (Thermo Scientific; BS-10367R). The third group was incubated with rabbit polyclonal calsequestrin antibody (Thermo Scientific; PA1-913) and mouse monoclonal Anti-Actinin (Sigma-Aldrich; A7811). The fourth group was incubated with mouse monoclonal Anti-α-Actinin (Sigma-Aldrich; A7811). The fifth group was incubated with rabbit polyclonal cardiac troponin T antibody (Abcam; ab92546). The sixth group was incubated with mouse monoclonal myosin-binding protein C (Santa Cruz Biotechnology; sc-166081). After primary antibody incubation, cells were washed three times with 1% BSA in PBS and then incubated with secondary antibodies, including donkey anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor™ 488 (Thermo Scientific; A-21202) and donkey anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor™ 568 (Thermo Scientific; A10042), for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Finally, cell nuclei were stained using Vectashield mounting medium with DAPI (Vector Laboratories). Cells were observed under the Olympus IX51 fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Shinjuku, Tokyo, Japan). Fluorescent image analysis was performed with ImageJ software (U. S. National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA).

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) measurements

We assessed the differences in the mitochondrial membrane potential using MitoTracker Red CMXRos (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) and JC-1 dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). MitoTracker Red CMXRos labels mitochondria based on membrane potential which passively diffuses across the plasma membrane and accumulates in active mitochondria. However, it is primarily used for labeling and tracking mitochondria rather than for precise ΔΨm measurements [27]. In contrast, JC-1 is a ratiometric dye that quantitatively measures mitochondrial membrane potential. It accumulates in mitochondria in a potential-dependent manner, resulting in a fluorescence emission shift from green to red. In mitochondria with high membrane potential, JC-1 forms red fluorescent aggregates, whereas, in depolarized mitochondria, it remains as green fluorescent monomers [28]. Cultured hiPSC-CMs were cultured in 24-well plates until they reached 70–80% confluency in glucose or fatty acid medium conditions for 10 days before starting the experiment.

A.MitoTracker Red CMXRos measurements

MitoTracker Red CMXRos measurements

Cultured hiPSC-CMs were stained by adding MitoTracker Red CMXRos dye (150 nM) to the culture medium, ensuring even distribution, followed by incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 20 min, keeping the dye protected from light throughout the process. After incubation, hiPSC-CMs were washed 2–3 times with PBS to remove excess dye and then replaced with fresh PBS or culture medium for imaging. High-resolution images of the labeled mitochondria were captured using a Nikon A1R confocal microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) inside a control chamber equipped with appropriate filters for MitoTracker Red CMXRos detection. ImageJ was used to analyze the fluorescence intensity of MitoTracker Red CMXRos dye. The level of fluorescence of randomly selected cells was analyzed by measuring the grey value of stained and unstained areas (background). Fluorescence intensity was calculated by taking the mean grey values of selected cells minus the mean fluorescence of the background.

B.JC-1

JC-1

Cultured hiPSC-CMs were stained by adding JC-1 dye (10 µM) to the culture medium, ensuring even distribution, followed by incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 10 min, keeping the dye shielded from light to preserve its integrity. After incubation, the dye solution was removed, and the cells were washed 2–3 times with PBS to eliminate excess dye. Imaging was conducted using a Nikon A1R confocal microscope inside a climate control chamber, where green fluorescence (indicating depolarized mitochondria) was excited at ~ 488 nm, and red fluorescence (indicating polarized mitochondria) was excited at ~ 550 nm. Images were captured for analysis, with the red-to-green fluorescence intensity ratio used to measure mitochondrial membrane potential, where a reduction in this ratio reflects mitochondrial depolarization.

Morphological analysis

Following immunostaining, hiPSC-CMs morphology parameters, including cell area, circularity index, and perimeter, were assessed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). For each condition, a minimum of 40 cells was analyzed. To measure sarcomere length, myofibrils containing at least ten consecutive and well-organized α-Actinin-positive bands were selected. The total length of these bands was measured and divided by their count to calculate the sarcomere length average.

Transmission electron microscope

Cultured hiPSC-CMs grown on glass coverslips were washed with PBS and then fixed in a solution of 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 2% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer for 30 min. After fixation, the buffer was washed away using 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer. Subsequently, hiPSC-CMs could be stored in 2% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer until further processing, ensuring that samples remain hydrated to preserve cellular structures. hiPSC-CMs were then post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide for 1 h to enhance membrane contrast and stabilize lipids. After post-fixation, the cells were dehydrated in a graded ethanol series. Subsequently, the coverslip was dipped in acetone and placed onto an aluminum planchette. Epson was immediately added to cover the cells, providing the stability necessary for ultrathin sectioning. A beem capsule filled with Epson was then placed upside down over the cells and incubated for 2 h to allow the Epson to infiltrate into the cells. After incubation, samples were baked at 60 °C overnight. The following day, samples were transferred directly from the oven to a hot plate, where the coverslip was carefully removed, ensuring that no glass fragments remained attached. Ultrathin sections (approximately 60 nm thick) were cut using an ultramicrotome with diamond knives (Leica EM Ultracut UC6i or UC7, Leica Mikrosysteme GmbH, Austria) and collected onto copper grids. The sections were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate to enhance contrast, particularly for organelle membranes and other ultrastructural details. Finally, the samples were examined, and images were captured on a JEM-1400PLUS transmission electron microscope (JEOL Ltd, Japan) equipped with an Orius SC 1000B bottom-mounted CCD camera (Gatan, California, USA). Images were taken at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV, with magnifications ranging from 1,000× to 10,000×.

Western blot

Cultured hiPSC-CMs were collected by directly lysing monolayer cell attachment with 2x Laemmli lysis buffer (Bio-Rad) containing 5% β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma). The lysates were run on a 4–20% Mini-PROTEAN TGX Precast Protein Gel (Bio-Rad). Proteins were transferred from the gel onto a PVDF membrane using the Trans-Blot Turbo Blotting System with the Trans-Blot Turbo RTA Mini PVDF Transfer Kit (Bio-Rad). Following the transfer, the membranes were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Sigma-Aldrich) in 0.1% Tween-20 (Sigma) in tris-buffered saline (TBS) for 3 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with various primary antibodies diluted in 3% BSA in 0.1% TBS-Tween. These primary antibodies include mouse anti-β-actin (Santa Cruz; sc-47778), rabbit anti-HIF1α (Invitrogen; 700505), rabbit anti-Calsequestrin (Thermo; PA1-913), mouse anti-SERCA2 (Santa Cruz; sc-376235), rabbit anti-Phospholamban (Santa Cruz; sc-530650), and mouse anti-MYH7 (Sigma-Aldrich; MAB1552). Following the primary antibody incubation, the membranes were washed sequentially with 0.5%, 0.1%, and 0.05% TBS-Tween. Next, the membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with secondary antibodies diluted in 3% BSA in 0.1% TBS-Tween. The secondary antibodies used were anti-mouse goat IgG (Santa Cruz; sc-516102) and anti-rabbit swine IgG (Dako; P0217). Then, the membranes were washed again sequentially with 0.5%, 0.1%, and 0.05% TBS-Tween. The protein-antibody complexes were detected using Amersham ECL Prime Western Blotting Detection Reagent (GE Healthcare Life Sciences), and imaging was performed using the ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, California, USA). The antibodies were stripped using stripping buffer (0.705% β-mercaptoethanol, 2% SDS, and 0.03125 M Tris in Milli-Q water) for 30 min at 56 °C. The images were analyzed using Image Lab Software (Bio-Rad). Following this, the membranes were washed, and a new blocking step was initiated.

Mitochondrial bioenergetic analysis

Mitochondrial oxidation and glycolysis were evaluated by analysis of oxygen consumption rate (OCR, pmol/min) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR, mpH/min), respectively. Additionally, total ATP production was quantified by assessing the ATP production rates from both glycolytic and mitochondrial pathways using Seahorse Wave Desktop Software (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). hiPSC-CMs were maintained for 7–10 days in a glucose or fatty acid medium. Following this, hiPSC-CMs were dissociated into single cells and seeded onto Seahorse XF-24 cell plates at a density of 100,000 cells per well.

A.Mitochondrial Oxidation Assessment

A.Mitochondrial Oxidation Assessment

Mitochondrial Oxidation Assessment

Mitochondrial oxidation was conducted using XF Cell Mitochondrial Stress Kit (Agilent Technologies, USA) to measure the oxygen consumption rate (OCR, pmol/min). One hour before the assay, cells were incubated in a non-CO2 incubator at 37 °C in Seahorse XF basal medium supplemented with 1 M glucose, 100 mM pyruvate, and 200 mM L-glutamine. OCR measurements were obtained following sequential automatic injections of specific substrates and inhibitors: oligomycin (1.5 µM, ATP synthase inhibitor, Sigma-Aldrich), FCCP (0.5 µM, oxidative phosphorylation uncoupler, Sigma-Aldrich), and a combination of rotenone and antimycin A (0.5 µM each, electron transport chain complex III inhibitors, Sigma-Aldrich). OCRs were obtained from the slope of change in oxygen over time. Key OCR parameters were calculated as follows: (a) Basal respiration: The last rate measurement before oligomycin injection, minus the non-mitochondrial respiration rate (minimum rate after antimycin A injection). (b) ATP production: The last rate measurement before oligomycin injection, minus the minimum rate after oligomycin injection. (c) Maximal respiration: The maximum rate measurement after FCCP injection, minus the non-mitochondrial respiration rate. (d) Spare respiratory capacity: Maximal respiration, minus basal respiration.

B.Glycolysis Assessment

B.Glycolysis Assessment

Glycolysis Assessment

The glycolysis assay was conducted using the XF Glycolysis Stress Kit (Agilent Technologies, USA) to measure the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR, mpH/min), an indicator of glycolytic activity. One hour before the assay, cells were incubated in a non-CO2 incubator at 37 °C in Seahorse XF basal medium supplemented with 200 mM L-glutamine. ECAR measurements were performed with automated sequential injections of specific substrates and inhibitors: glucose (10 mM, Sigma-Aldrich), oligomycin (1 µM, an ATP synthase inhibitor, Sigma-Aldrich), and 2-deoxy-glucose (2-DG) (50 mM, a glucose analog that inhibits glycolysis by competitively binding to hexokinase, the first enzyme in the glycolytic pathway, Sigma-Aldrich). ECARs were obtained from the slope of change in H+ concentration over time. Key ECAR parameters were calculated as: (a) Glycolysis: The maximum ECAR measurement before oligomycin injection minus the last measurement before glucose injection. (b) Glycolytic capacity: The maximum ECAR measurement after oligomycin injection minus the last measurement before glucose injection. (c) Glycolytic reserve: The glycolytic capacity minus the glycolysis rate.

C.Total ATP production rate quantification

C.Total ATP production rate quantification

Total ATP production rate quantification

The total ATP production rate in living cells was evaluated utilizing the Agilent Seahorse XFp Real-Time ATP Rate Assay (Agilent Technologies, USA). Importantly, this assay can differentiate between ATP generated via mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis—the two primary metabolic pathways for ATP synthesis in CMs. Using metabolic modulators, such as oligomycin and a combination of rotenone and antimycin A, injected sequentially, the assay enables precise calculation of ATP production rates from each pathway. Before the assay, cells were incubated for one hour in a non-CO2 incubator at 37 °C with Seahorse XF basal medium containing 1 M glucose, 100 mM pyruvate, and 200 mM L-glutamine. Total ATP production was measured following the sequential automated injection of (a) Oligomycin (1.5 µM): An ATP synthase inhibitor (Sigma-Aldrich) and (b) Rotenone (0.5 µM) and Antimycin A (0.5 µM): Inhibitors of electron transport chain complex III (Sigma-Aldrich). This setup allowed for the assessment of ATP production contributions from both glycolysis and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

The total RNA of cultured hiPSC-CMs was extracted using Monarch® Total RNA Miniprep Kit (New England Biolabs, Massachusetts, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The quantity and purity of RNA were validated using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies) before further analysis. All RNA samples were treated with DNAase (ThermoFisher Technologies) to remove potential DNA contamination. Reverse transcription of the total RNA to complementary DNA (cDNA) was performed using a High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription kit (Applied Biosystems) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative PCR for cDNA samples was performed using TaqMan Gene Expression Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) according to standard protocols on the ABI7300 thermal cycler (Applied Biosystems) for 10 min at 95◦C followed by 40 cycles (95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 1 min). All PCR reactions were performed in triplicate. The mean cycle threshold (Ct) values of triplicate wells for each sample were collected and the expression data was normalized to the control housekeeping gene glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) and assessed using the comparative ΔCt method. The fold expression changes were calculated by the 2 − ΔΔCT method, as previously described [29]. We analyzed 26 genes involved in calcium handling, sarcomere structure, ion channels, fatty acid metabolism, mitochondrial function, and genes enriched in fetal tissue (full gene list available in Supplementary Information, Table S1). Our goal was to include key genes associated with CM maturation to investigate whether alterations in gene expression are related to changes in the medium used for CM culture.

Calcium imaging

Cultured hiPSC-CMs were maintained on glass coverslips in either glucose or fatty acid medium conditions for 7–10 days prior to experimentation. hiPSC-CMs were loaded with the calcium indicator Cal-520®, AM (AAT Bioquest) for 90 min at 37 °C. Afterward, the Cal-520®, AM was washed out, and hiPSC-CMs were allowed a 30-minute de-esterification in an extracellular solution (137mM NaCl, 5mM KCl, 0.44mM KH2PO4, 20mM HEPES, 4.2mM NaHCO3, 5mM D-glucose, 2mM CaCl2, 1.2mM MgCl2, and 1mM Na-pyruvate). hiPSC-CMs were continuously perfused at 37 °C with the extracellular solution and the intracellular calcium fluorescence signals were acquired at 20× magnification using an Axio Observer A1 microscope (Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Germany) at 37 °C. Intracellular calcium transients were analyzed with Clampfit 10.5 software (Molecular Devices, USA), with ΔF/F0 representing peak amplitude, in which F0 presented the fluorescence intensity at the onset of the experiment. The effects of doxorubicin (0.25 − 1µM) were evaluated by comparing calcium transients before and after treatment across different doses.

Membrane action potential imaging

The FluoVolt™ Membrane Potential probe (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was employed according to the manufacturer’s protocol to investigate sub-millisecond membrane potential changes. Cultured hiPSC-CMs were washed twice with the extracellular solution and incubated for 15–30 min at 37 °C in a loading solution of FluoVolt™ dye, PowerLoad™, and extracellular solution. After removing the dye, hiPSC-CMs were continuously perfused with an extracellular solution at 37 °C, followed by live-cell imaging. Sodium channel activity was assessed using tetrodotoxin (TTX) (2–4µM), a selective Na⁺ channel blocker (Abcam), with subsequent processing performed similarly to the calcium assay protocol.

Contractility imaging

Bright-field videos of spontaneously beating hiPSC-CMs were captured using a Nikon Eclipse TS100 microscope (Japan) equipped with an Optika DIGI-12 video camera (Italy). Recordings were made for 30 s at 60 frames per second, with the temperature controlled at 37 °C. Bright-field videos were analyzed for beating physiology using the open-source tool MUSCLEMOTION within ImageJ software to quantify contractions according to specified instructions [30]. The recorded beating rhythms were further analyzed using Clampfit software version 10.5 (Molecular Devices, USA).

Action potential measurement

Action potentials were recorded from spontaneously beating hiPSC-CMs in perforated patch configuration using amphotericin B at 36 °C ± 1 °C. Current-clamp recordings were digitally sampled at 20 kHz and filtered at 2 kHz using a low-pass Bessel filter on the recording amplifier. An Axon Series 200B patch-clamp amplifier connected to a Digidata 1440a AD/DA converter driven by the pCLAMP 10.2 software (Molecular Devices, California, USA) was used. The patch electrodes had tip resistance of 1.8–2.5 MΩ and contained the following intracellular solution: 132 mM potassium methanesulfonate (KMeSO4), 4 mM ethylene glycol-bis (β-aminoethyl ether)-N, N,N′,N′-tetraacetic acid (EGTA), 20 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl2, and 1 mM CaCl2 (pH was adjusted to 7.2 with KOH). The extracellular solution contained 143 mM NaCl, 4.8 mM KCl, 1.8 mM CaCl2, 1.2 mM MgCl2, 5 mM D-glucose, and 10 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)−1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) (pH was adjusted to 7.4 with KOH). Recorded spontaneous APs were analyzed for beat per minute (BPM), maximum diastolic potential (MDP), action potential amplitude (AP), and AP duration at 90% and 50% repolarization (APD90 and APD50). The APD90/APD50 ratio describes the shape of the repolarization curve and was used to classify the specific subtype of hiPSC-CMs. hiPSC-CMs were classified as ventricular-like or atrial-like hiPSC-CM for APD90/APD50 < 1.4 (indicative of pronounced plateau phase) and APD90/APD50 > 1.5 (indicative of triangular shape) respectively. All the drugs and chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) unless otherwise specified. KMeSO4 was ordered from MP Biomedicals (California, USA).

Statistical analysis

All experiments data were conducted at least three times using cells from three to four independent CM differentiation batches. Data is presented as mean ± standard errors of the mean (SEM). Statistical analysis was conducted with IBM SPSS Statistics 26 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Statistical differences were calculated using the two-tailed unpaired Student’s t tests. P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Standard glucose-based media (e.g., EB medium) support hiPSC-CM differentiation and maintenance but promote a predominantly glycolytic metabolism, suppress fatty acid oxidation, and result in an immature metabolic phenotype that limits physiological relevance [7]. To overcome this limitation, we developed a modified fatty acid-based medium that mimics the adult cardiac metabolic environment by incorporating fatty acids as the primary energy substrate, reducing glucose availability, and supplementing creatine, L-carnitine, and taurine to support cardiomyocyte energetics and oxidative phosphorylation, thereby promoting functional and metabolic maturation [31].

Glycolytic function was evaluated using the Seahorse Glycolysis Stress Test, following sequential addition of glucose, oligomycin (an ATP synthase inhibitor), and 2-deoxy-glucose (2-DG), a glucose analog that inhibits glycolysis. FAM-cultured CMs showed significantly higher glycolysis, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve compared to GLM-cultured CMs (Fig. 4B1–B3), reflecting improved metabolic flexibility and glycolytic efficiency.

Total cellular ATP production was quantified using the Seahorse XFp Real-Time ATP Rate Assay. This assay utilizes metabolic modulators such as oligomycin (an ATP synthase inhibitor) and a combination of rotenone and antimycin A (inhibitors of mitochondrial complexes I and III), which are sequentially injected to calculate ATP production rates from mitochondrial OXPHOS and glycolysis. FAM-cultured CMs primarily relied on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) for ATP generation, whereas GLM-cultured CMs depended predominantly on glycolysis (Fig. 4C), demonstrating a substrate-driven metabolic shift toward oxidative metabolism.

Given the role of HIF1α in promoting glycolytic metabolism, its expression and localization were examined. Immunofluorescence showed predominant nuclear localization of HIF1α in GLM-cultured CMs, while FAM-cultured CMs exhibited mainly cytoplasmic localization (Fig. 4E). Western blot analysis confirmed significantly higher HIF1α protein levels in GLM-cultured CMs compared to FAM-cultured cells (Fig. 4D). Consistently, quantitative RT-PCR revealed significant upregulation of fatty acid metabolism–related genes, including PPARα (Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha), and CPT1B (Carnitine Palmitoyltransferase 1B), in FAM-cultured CMs (Fig. 4F), indicating activation of fatty acid oxidation pathways.

Together, these data demonstrate that fatty acid–based culture conditions promote metabolic maturation of hiPSC-CMs by enhancing mitochondrial respiration, shifting ATP production toward OXPHOS, suppressing HIF1α-driven glycolytic dependence, and upregulating key regulators of fatty acid metabolism.

Discussion

hiPSC-CMs hold significant promise for disease modeling, drug screening, and regenerative medicine. However, a major challenge remains in their immature phenotype, which closely resembles fetal rather than adult CMs [32, 33]. This immaturity restricts their translational relevance, particularly for modeling adult cardiac diseases and pharmacological responses. A central contributor to this phenotype is their metabolic profile: hiPSC-CMs cultured in conventional glucose-based media rely predominantly on glycolysis, in contrast to adult cardiomyocytes, which depend mainly on oxidative metabolism and fatty acid oxidation [34]. To overcome this limitation and promote a more mature phenotype, various strategies have been explored. These include long-term culture [35, 36], genetic modifications [37, 38], metabolic and hormonal treatments [39], substrate stiffness adjustments [40, 41], microRNA-based approaches [42, 43], and 3D tissue engineering techniques [44, 45]. While these methods have improved the maturation of hiPSC-CMs in terms of their morphology, molecular markers, and functionality, fully mature CMs have not yet been consistently achieved [34].

In this context, metabolic conditioning represents a particularly promising strategy. In the present study, we sought to enhance hiPSC-CM maturation by shifting cellular energy substrate utilization from glucose to fatty acids, thereby promoting oxidative metabolism. By culturing hiPSC-CMs in glucose-based medium or fatty acid-based medium and performing a comprehensive comparative analysis, we demonstrate that fatty acid supplementation significantly enhances structural, metabolic, and functional maturation. Cardiomyocytes cultured in FAM exhibited improved mitochondrial function, enhanced contractile performance, and selective electrophysiological maturation, collectively resembling a more adult-like cardiomyocyte phenotype.

Fatty acid supplementation markedly promoted the morphological and structural maturation of hiPSC-CMs. FAM-cultured cells displayed enhanced sarcomeric organization, with well-aligned and mature striations, whereas GLM-cultured CMs exhibited less organized sarcomeric structures. These differences are functionally important, as proper sarcomere alignment is essential for effective contraction and electrical activity [19]. Ultrastructural analysis further supported these findings, revealing improved myofilament and Z-line alignment, increased mitochondrial density, and more defined sarcomeres in FAM-cultured CMs, consistent with a shift toward oxidative metabolism and mature cellular architecture [7]. Quantitative morphometric analysis confirmed these structural changes, showing increased cell perimeter, cell area, and sarcomere length, along with a reduced circularity index, consistent with a more elongated, adult-like cardiomyocyte morphology [19].

These structural changes were supported by molecular remodeling, including upregulation of key myocardial structural genes (TNNT2, MYH7, MYBPC3) and increased β-myosin heavy chain expression, a hallmark of adult human cardiomyocytes. Conversely, fetal-associated genes (MYH6, NPPB, ACTA1) were downregulated in FAM-cultured cells, indicating a metabolic-driven transition from a fetal to a more adult-like gene expression program [7].

Mitochondrial maturation emerged as a central feature of fatty acid-mediated conditioning [46]. FAM-cultured hiPSC-CMs exhibited structurally mature mitochondria with densely packed cristae, increased perinuclear localization, and enhanced mitochondrial abundance, reflecting elevated metabolic demand and biogenic activity [47]. Functional assays further demonstrated increased mitochondrial membrane potential and polarization [48, 49], while TOM20 staining revealed a more organized, interconnected mitochondrial network resembling adult cardiomyocyte mitochondrial architecture [50]. This spatial organization supports efficient energy distribution and excitation–contraction coupling [51, 52].

At the molecular level, fatty acid supplementation induced upregulation of mitochondrial regulatory genes (PPARGC1A, ESRRA, AKAP1), providing mechanistic insight into the observed mitochondrial remodeling. PPARGC1A is a master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative metabolism [8], ESRRA controls mitochondrial gene transcription and respiratory capacity [53], and AKAP1 regulates mitochondrial signaling and functional organization [54]. Together, these changes indicate that fatty acid-based metabolic conditioning actively drives mitochondrial biogenesis, oxidative metabolic programming, and metabolic maturation.

The metabolic transition from glycolysis to oxidative phosphorylation is a defining hallmark of cardiomyocyte maturation, reflecting the shift from a fetal to an adult-like metabolic phenotype [55]. In this study, hiPSC-CMs cultured in FAM exhibited enhanced mitochondrial respiration and a greater reliance on oxidative phosphorylation for ATP production, indicating advanced metabolic maturation. Seahorse XF bioenergetic profiling demonstrated significantly increased oxygen consumption rates (OCRs), including basal respiration, ATP production, maximal respiration, and spare respiratory capacity in FAM-cultured CMs, reflecting enhanced mitochondrial oxidative capacity, metabolic efficiency, and bioenergetic flexibility. These features are characteristic of adult cardiomyocytes, which depend predominantly on oxidative phosphorylation for energy production [7].

Interestingly, FAM-cultured CMs also exhibited elevated extracellular acidification rates (ECARs), including glycolysis, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve. This indicates preserved glycolytic competence and metabolic adaptability, suggesting that FAM-cultured cells develop a flexible metabolic phenotype capable of efficiently utilizing both fatty acids and glucose. Such dual metabolic flexibility is a hallmark of mature cardiomyocytes, which adapt substrate utilization according to physiological demands. Real-time ATP rate analysis further confirmed this metabolic shift, showing predominant reliance on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation in FAM-cultured CMs, whereas GLM-cultured cells remained largely glycolysis-dependent, consistent with an immature metabolic state [34]. These findings align with previous studies demonstrating that fatty acid supplementation promotes oxidative metabolism and energetic maturation in hiPSC-CMs [11].

At the molecular level, metabolic remodeling was associated with altered regulatory signaling pathways [56]. HIF-1α, a master regulator of glycolytic metabolism and hypoxic signaling [57, 58], plays a key role in cardiac metabolic development [59]. As CMs mature from fetal to postnatal life, there is a decrease in HIF-1α signaling, accompanied by an increase in peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α (PPARα), which serves as an activator of fatty acid oxidation [56, 60]. To explore these metabolic shifts, we focused on the role of HIF1α, a key transcription factor involved in promoting glycolytic metabolism. In GLM-cultured CMs, immunofluorescence analysis suggested a higher degree of nuclear localization of HIF1α compared to FAM conditions, in line with a glycolysis-favoring metabolic state [61]. In contrast, FAM-cultured CMs exhibited primarily cytoplasmic localization and reduced overall HIF1α expression, indicating a more oxygen-rich, metabolically mature state supportive of oxidative phosphorylation [62]. These differences in HIF1α expression and subcellular distribution are consistent with the distinct metabolic profiles observed between the two culture conditions. These findings align with previous evidence linking HIF1α downregulation to improved mitochondrial activity and CM maturation [11]. Additionally, gene expression analysis revealed the upregulation of key regulators of fatty acid metabolism in FAM-cultured CMs, including PPARα, CPT1B, and ACOX1. These genes are central to fatty acid uptake and β-oxidation. PPARα is a master regulator of lipid metabolism [63], CPT1B facilitates the transport of fatty acids into mitochondria [64], and ACOX1 catalyzes the first step in peroxisomal β-oxidation [65]. These molecular changes provide strong evidence that fatty acid-based culture actively drives metabolic reprogramming toward an adult-like cardiac energy metabolism. Besides metabolic and structural changes, electrophysiological characteristics are also key indicators of CM maturation [66]. Fatty acid conditioning also improved functional and electrophysiological properties. FAM-cultured hiPSC-CMs displayed faster calcium transient kinetics, higher beating rates, and greater contraction amplitudes, reflecting more efficient calcium cycling and excitation–contraction coupling [67]. These observations are consistent with previous reports showing that metabolic cues mimicking the in vivo cardiac environment support the development of adult-like calcium dynamics in hiPSC-CMs [68]. Under doxorubicin stress, FAM-cultured CMs exhibited enhanced sensitivity with tachycardia and arrhythmogenic activity, indicative of more developed calcium handling systems [69], whereas GLM-cultured cells showed weaker responses but earlier cessation of beating. Calsequestrin immunostaining further revealed more organized sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium storage in FAM-cultured cells, supporting improved intracellular calcium regulation. Despite similar transcriptional levels of SERCA2a, phospholamban, and calsequestrin, these functional differences suggest regulation at post-transcriptional, structural, or organizational levels rather than gene expression alone.

Direct measurements of contractility indicated better mechanical functionality [70] in FAM-cultured CMs, with higher contraction amplitude, increased beating rate, and more efficient calcium handling, as indicated by faster contraction and relaxation times. These results lead to a more organized and responsive contractile apparatus in cells cultured under fatty acid-enriched conditions. At the electrophysiological level, a trend toward upregulation of key ion channel genes was observed, indicating progressive electrical maturation [71]. Voltage-sensitive dye (FluoVolt™) analysis revealed differences in electrophysiological parameters; however, patch clamp analysis did not show any significant differences between FAM-cultured and GLM-cultured CMs. To evaluate sodium channel functionality, we treated the cells with tetrodotoxin (TTX), a selective sodium channel blocker. In FAM-cultured CMs, TTX induced asystole at a half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) of approximately 4 µM, which corresponds to the known sensitivity of the predominant cardiac sodium channel, Nav1.5. This suggests that these cells have undergone a maturation-related shift from expressing TTX-sensitive sodium channels, such as Nav1.2 and Nav1.3, to the more TTX-resistant Nav1.5 isoform. In contrast, GLM-cultured CMs exhibited greater sensitivity to TTX and stopped beating at lower concentrations, likely due to a higher proportion of immature sodium channels. This maturation-related shift toward Nav1.5 dominance is a recognized hallmark of hiPSC-CM electrophysiological maturation [68], further supporting the role of metabolic conditioning in driving functional cardiac maturation [71].

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that mimicking the postnatal metabolic shift by culturing hiPSC-derived CMs in fatty acid-based medium significantly enhances their structural organization, metabolic profile, and mechanical contractile maturation. FAM-cultured cells exhibited more organized sarcomeres, elongated morphology, and mitochondrial enrichment, reflecting ultrastructural maturity. Metabolically, they showed a clear transition toward oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid β-oxidation, with increased ATP production and reduced reliance on glycolysis. Notable enhancements were also observed in specific electrophysiological properties, particularly in calcium handling and contractile strength. These findings support the use of fatty acid supplementation as a straightforward and effective strategy to promote hiPSC-CM maturation. This approach enhances the physiological relevance of hiPSC-CMs, broadening their utility in cardiac disease modeling, drug screening, and regenerative applications.

Study limitations

This study provides important insights into the role of fatty acid-based media in promoting hiPSC-CM maturation; however, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, experiments were conducted over a relatively short culture period, and longer-term studies are needed to assess the durability of the observed maturation effects. Second, the study focused exclusively on cardiomyocytes, without incorporating other cardiac cell types such as fibroblasts and endothelial cells, which are essential for cardiac development, structural organization, and paracrine signaling. Future co-culture systems, organoids, or multicellular models may provide a more physiologically relevant assessment. Third, the use of a two-dimensional (2D) monolayer culture system does not recapitulate the complex three-dimensional (3D) architecture, mechanical cues, and cell–cell interactions of native cardiac tissue; therefore, 3D engineered heart tissues and organ-on-a-chip platforms may offer more advanced maturation insights.

In addition, the study was limited to healthy control hiPSC lines, and it remains unclear whether similar maturation benefits extend to disease-specific or patient-derived models. While transcriptional and selected protein-level analyses were performed, global proteomic and metabolomic profiling was not conducted and could further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the observed phenotypic changes. The study is also limited by the relatively small number of biological replicates in some experiments, reflecting the technical complexity and inherent variability of hiPSC differentiation; future studies with larger numbers of independent differentiation batches will strengthen statistical power and generalizability. Finally, the findings are based on a single hiPSC line, and validation across multiple genetically diverse lines will be essential to confirm reproducibility and robustness.
