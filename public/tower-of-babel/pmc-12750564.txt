Genome-wide identification of heat shock factors in Myricaria laxiflora and their response to abiotic stresses
Authors: Linbao Li, Bicheng Dun, Jiayi Gu, Wei Yin, Junchen Wang, Yang Su, Weibo Xiang, Di Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Myricaria laxiflora is a rare and endangered shrub in China, and its wild populations are threatened by diverse abiotic stresses. Heat shock factors (HSFs) are essential for coping with environmental stresses, however, information about HSFs in M. laxiflora is limited. Currently, a total of 21 MlHSFs were systematically detected in the latest released genome of M. laxiflora. The MlHSFs were assigned into three subgroups (i.e., A, B and C) according to their conserved protein domains. All the MlHSFs were predicted to localize in nucleus with the conserved HSF-type DNA-binding domain, suggesting their potential roles as transcription factors. MlHSF proteins from the same subgroup shared similar conserved protein domains, the variable C-terminal domains of HSF proteins implied their functional diversity. A total of six gene duplication events from the same subgroup were detected among the HSF gene members, and all the duplication events belonged to segmental duplication. Furthermore, plenty of cis-elements were detected upstream of HSF gene members, these cis-elements could be primarily categorized into light response, as well as stress and hormone response. Transcriptomic analysis showed that most MlHSFs were significantly inhibited by cadmium and waterlogging stresses in most cases, however, MlHSFC1 was significantly stimulated by both cadmium and waterlogging stresses in M. laxiflora. A comprehensive co-expression network of MlHSFs has been established to reveal the potential roles of MlHSFs in photosynthesis, redox homeostasis and protein folding under abiotic stresses. The results obtained suggested the crucial roles of MlHSFs in adapting to environmental stresses in M. laxiflora.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12359-2.

Introduction

Extreme environmental stresses might cause damage to membrane integrity, osmotic and ion homeostasis, which further lead to protein misfolding and the imbalance of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [1–3]. Heat shock factors (HSFs) are crucial to maintain protein homeostasis by the transcriptional regulation of genes that encode molecular chaperones, such as heat shock protein (HSP) [4]. The HSF proteins harbor five conserved structural domains, i.e., the N-terminal DNA-binding domain (DBD), the N-terminal oligomerization domain (OD) for oligomerization with two hydrophobic heptapeptide repeat regions (e.g., HR-A and HR-B), the nuclear export signal (NES), the variable C-terminal transcriptional activation domain (CTAD), and the nuclear localization signal domain (NLS) [5–7]. The DBD of HSF proteins recognizes the heat shock elements (5’-nGAAnnTTCn-3’) and transcriptionally regulates downstream target genes regulated by HSF [5]. The HSF proteins could be assigned into three subgroups (i.e. HSFA, HSFB and HSFC), according to the length of flexible linker between DBD and OD, as well as the inserted amino acids in the HR-A/B region [6, 8]. Only HSFA proteins contain the CTAD with hydrophobic amino acid residues (AHAs) motif for transcriptional activating [6, 8]. However, HSFB proteins harbor a highly conserved repressor domain named tetrapeptide (LFGV) motif [9]. The N-terminal DBDs are considered as the most conserved domains of HSF proteins, while the ODs and AHA motif are variable among all the HSF proteins [10]. The HSFC subgroup contains the least members compared to other subgroups, and HSFC2 members are only detected in monocots [5, 10, 11], suggesting the specific roles of HSFC2 genes in monocot plants.

Plant HSFs play crucial roles in the adaptation to diverse abiotic stresses [1]. For example, HSFB1 plays important roles in coordinating growth and drought tolerance by regulating mRNA levels of HSP101 in Arabidopsis thaliana [12]. The HSFA1, HSFA2, HSFA3, and HSF3 genes in Arabidopsis also confer their roles in thermotolerance [13–17]. The LpHSFA3 from Lolium perenne positively regulates the mRNA levels of LpHSFA2a, thereby strengthening the capacities of thermotolerance [18]. In maize seedlings, ZmHSF20 negatively regulates the tolerance of heat stress by repressing both ZmHSF4 and Cellulose synthase A2 (ZmCesA2), thereby maintaining relationship between growth and defense response [19]. Additionally, over-expressions of Triticum aestivum TaHSFA2h, Lilium longiflorum LlHSFA4, Allium sativum AsHSFB1, Panicum virgatum PvHSF03, Rhodomyrtus tomentosa RtHSFA2a, Amorphophallus albus AaHSFA1 and Sorbus pohuashanensis SpHSFA8a in Arabidopsis also improve the heat tolerance of transgenic plants [20–26]. In poplar, overexpression of PeHSFA2 significantly improve the thermotolerance by transactivating the expressing of several heat shock response genes [27]. In wheat, the alternative splicing isoforms of TaHSFA6e have different transcriptional activities of three downstream TaHSP70 genes, the TaHSFA6e-III with an additional C-terminal amphipathic helix shows higher transcriptional activity under heat stress [28]. A recent study showed that the tomato HSFA1a is critical for plant thermotolerance by regulating chromatin spatial reorganization of promoter-enhancer contacts [29].

Except for heat stress, HSFs also are involved in coping with diverse environmental stresses. The transgenic Arabidopsis plants harboring the wheat TaHsfA2d show higher yield and the accumulation of biomass under salinity and drought conditions [30]. Furthermore, Haloxylon ammodendron HaHSFA1 and wheat TaHsfA2-11 play roles in salt and drought stress, respectively [31, 32]. However, maize ZmHSF08 shows negative effects on plants under salt and drought stresses [33], and ZmHSF11 also negatively regulates thermotolerance [34]. The transgenic Arabidopsis plants harboring the Trifolium repens TrHSFB2a also show reduced growth under several stress conditions, including drought and salt [35]. Additionally, heterologous expression of Sedum alfredii SaHsfA4a/SaHsfA4c and P. virgatum PvHSF16 in yeast increased the cadmium tolerance [36, 37]. Additionally, the tomato HSFA1a improves the cadmium tolerance by elevating the mRNA levels of melatonin biosynthesis genes [38]. Therefore, HSF genes in plants are essential for coping with diverse environmental stresses [1]. However, little information on HSF is available in rare and endangered plants.

Myricaria laxiflora is a native endangered shrub, which is narrowly distributed in the Three Gorges Reservoir Region in China [39]. M. laxiflora plays crucial roles in maintaining the ecological safety of the hydro-fluctuation belts in this area, due to its remarkable capacity to adapt waterlogging stress [40, 41]. The physiological and omics methods have been conducted to uncover the gene regulatory network underlying the physiological alterations in M. laxiflora in response to flooding stress, and the potential key genes that regulate these processes have also been identified [39–41]. Additionally, the wild populations of M. laxiflora were threatened by the high contamination of cadmium in soil, due to the city expansion and industry development [42]. The HSFs in plants are crucial for adapting to diverse abiotic stresses, particularly heat stress, hypoxic conditions and heavy metal stress [1, 37]. However, the possible roles of HSFs in coping with waterlogging stress remain to be explored. As M. laxiflora might cope with both waterlogging and heavy metal stresses to complete its life cycle in the native habitat, the potential roles of MlHSFs in these stress conditions could be expected in the context of identifying molecular targets for improving the tolerance to these abiotic conditions.

Here, the genome-wide identification of MlHSF members was conducted with the latest released genome information of M. laxiflora [39, 43]. The gene and protein structures as well as gene duplication events were explored, the mRNA levels of MlHSFs in adapting abiotic stresses (waterlogging and heavy metal stresses) were evaluated. Finally, the gene co-expression transcriptional network centered by MlHSF gene members was constructed to uncover the potential roles of MlHSFs responding to stress conditions. The results will provide the fundamental insights into the roles of MlHSFs in regulating the tolerance to abiotic stresses and the candidates for genetic improvement.

Materials and methods

Identification of MlHSFs in the genome of M. laxiflora

The procedures of identifying MlHSF gene members in M. laxiflora were based on the previously published papers [44, 45] and the genome of M. laxiflora was downloaded from the Figshare website (https://figshare.com/articles/online_resource/_i_Myricaria_laxiflora_i_genome_data/25131164). Briefly, the protein sequences of known Arabidopsis AtHSFs were obtained from TAIR. Then the blastp was conducted using the protein sequences of AtHSF genes as queries against the genome of M. laxiflora with Blast Several Sequences to a Big Database tool in TBtools-Ⅱ software (v2.099) [46]. Sequences with the E-values lower than 10e− 5 were retained. Furthermore, the hidden Markov model (HMM) of HSF-type DNA-binding (PF00447) was downloaded from InterPro database. Then the PF00447.hmm was used as query against all the protein sequences of M. laxiflora by Simple HMM Search tool in TBtools-Ⅱ software (v2.099) with default parameters [46]. The results acquired from blastp and HMM search were combined to identify the HSF genes in M. laxiflora. Afterwards, protein sequences of candidate MlHSFs were submitted to the hmmscan tool to verify the presence of HSF-type DNA-binding domain in each MlHSF protein. Only the proteins containing the HSF-type DNA-binding domain were considered as HSF genes in M. laxiflora. All the identified MlHSF gene sequences were present in Table S1.

Construction of phylogenetic tree

To construct the phylogenetic tree of HSF genes, the HSF protein sequences from A. thaliana, rice, and Brachypodium distachyon (Table S2) were obtained from a published study [47]. The MEGA11 software [48] was applied to construct the phylogenetic tree using the neighbour-joining method. The iTOL website [49] was used for visualization.

Physicochemical analysis of MlHSF proteins

The physicochemical information of MlHSF was obtained using the Protein Paramter Calc in TBtools-Ⅱ software (v2.099) [46]. Gene location information was extracted using GXF Gene Position & Info Extract in TBtools-Ⅱ software (v2.099) [46] with the genome annotation (gff3) file. The subcellular localizations of MlHSF proteins were analyzed on WoLF PSORT website [50].

Analysis of the gene and protein structures

The conserved domains of MlHSF proteins were identified using MEME website [51]. The xml file from MEME website and the gff3 file were used together to visualize the gene and protein structures using the Gene Structure View (Advanced) tool in TBtools-Ⅱ software (v2.099) [46].

Gene duplication and synteny analysis

The One Step MscanX tool in TBtools-Ⅱ software (v2.099) [46] was used to analysis with the gene duplication events of MlHSFs with default parameters. The circos plot representing the MlHSF gene duplication events was generated using Advanced Circos tool in TBtools-Ⅱ software (v2.099) with default parameters [46]. The collinearity maps of HSFs in M. laxiflora, A. thaliana and rice were analyzed using the Multiple Synteny Plot tool in TBtools-Ⅱ software (v2.099) [46].

Cis-element analysis of MlHSF genes in M. laxiflora

The 2000 bp promoter sequences of MlHSFs were obtained using TBtools-Ⅱ software (v2.099) [46], subsequently, the potential cis-elements in the promoters of MlHSFs were predicted using PlantCARE [52].

Gene expression analysis of MlHSFs in different tissues and under different stresses

To analyze the gene-specific expression profiles in diverse tissues (including root, leaf, stem, flower and fruit), transcriptomes of different tissues were obtained from previous publication [43]. Since the construction of the Three Gorges Project, the plant protection team of the Three Gorges Corporation has implemented the ex-situ protection of M. laxiflora. The M. laxiflora plants used in this study were from the artificial propagation. For abiotic stress conditions, the transcriptomes were acquired from the NCBI database under the project identifiers of PRJNA1164124 and PRJNA840865, corresponding to time-course cadmium and waterlogging treatments, respectively. The experimental designs of these transcriptomes were described into the published papers [40, 42]. The transcriptomes were downloaded using iSeq [53], and then the low-quality reads were filtered using fastp [54]. Subsequently, the clean transcriptomes were mapped to the genome of M. laxiflora using bowtie2 [55]. Then the gene count quantifications across the samples were performed using featureCounts in the R software [56, 57]. The different expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the DEseq2 package [58] in R software. The genes with fold changes under the given comparison greater than 2 or less than −2, and the false discovery ratio (FDR) lower than 0.05, were considered as significantly differentialy expressed. The heatmaps representing the gene expression levels were generated in R software using the pheatmap package [59].

Co-expression and gene enrichment analysis

Co-expression analysis was conducted in R using the WGCNA package [60]. Subsequently, the MlHSFs-centered co-expression network was extracted from the entire gene network generated by WGCNA analysis with an in-house R script. The Cytoscape [61] was used to visualize the MlHSFs-centered gene network.

For GO and KEGG enrichment analysis, all protein sequences of M. laxiflora were annotated using the EggNOG database [62]. Afterwards, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed in R with the clusterProfiler package [63] as suggested by the previous publication [64]. The bubble diagram was drawn in R with ggplot2 packages as suggested by the previous publications [65–67].

Results

Identification and phylogenetic analysis of MlHSF in M. laxiflora

A total of 21 MlHSFs were detected in the genome of M. laxiflora, which were evenly distributed across 10 out of 12 chromosomes of M. laxiflora (Table 1). Five MlHSF members (e.g., MlHsfA1b, MlHsfB3, MlHsfA6, MlHsfA4b and MlHsfB2b) were located on linkage group 8 (LG8), whereas no members were detected on LG3 and LG10 (Table 1). The length of MlHSFs CDS regions varied from 705 to 1593 bp, and the corresponding protein length ranged from 235 to 531 aa (Table 1). The estimated molecular weights of all MlHSF proteins were from 27.14 to 58.44 kDa (Table 1). As transcription factor (TF), all the MlHSFs were predicted located in nucleus (Table 1), suggesting that they potential roles in transcriptional regulating gene expression. Furthermore, most MlHSF proteins were acidic proteins with the theoretical isoelectric points (pIs) of below to 7.0, while two MlHSF proteins (MlHsfB4a and MlHsfB3) were in alkaline (Table 1). Structures of gene and protein

Gene structure analysis of MlHSFs showed that most MlHSFs had both 5’ and 3’ untranslated region (UTR), expect for MlHSFB4a, MlHSFA5, MlHSFA6, MlHSFA1a and MlHSFA3b (Fig. 2A). All MlHSFs harbored one or two introns with two or three exons (Fig. 2A). Six MlHSFs contained a long intron (defined as longer than 2 kb) within the CDS region (Fig. 2A). Conserved protein domain analysis revealed that the MlHSFs from the same subgroups shared similar protein motifs (Fig. 2B). A total of 10 conserved protein motifs were detected among all MlHSF proteins (Table 2 and Fig. 2B). The motifs 2, 5, 1 and 4 were conserved in all MlHSF proteins, except for MlHSFA3a, which only contained motif 4 of these motifs (Fig. 2B). Motif 6 was detected in most MlHSF members, except for MlHSFC1 and MlHSFA9 (Fig. 2B). Motif 7 was only detected in subgroup B, whereas motif 3 was conserved among all members from subgroups A and C (Fig. 2B). Most MlHSF proteins from subgroups A and C contained motif 9 and/or 10, except for MlHSFC1, MlHSFA9 and MlHSFA2a (Fig. 2B). Notably, only MlHSFA4b contained two copies of motif 10 (Fig. 2B). Gene duplication analysis of MlHSF

Gene duplication events play important roles in expanding plant gene families, endowing new biological functions for gene family during the plant evolutionary history [68]. A total of six gene duplication pairs identified as segment duplication were detected for MlHSFs in the genome of M. laxiflora, including MlaHsfA9 and MlaHsfA2b, MlaHsfA3a and MlaHsfA3b, MlaHsfB1 and MlaHsfB4a, MlaHsfA1a and MlaHsfA1c, MlaHsfA4b and MlaHsfA4a, MlaHsfA1b and MlaHsfA1c (Table S3 and Fig. 3A). Interestingly, all duplication gene pairs belonged to the same gene subgroup, with five and one gene duplication events occurring within subgroups A and B, respectively (Table S3 and Fig. 3A). Only the MlaHsfB1 and MlaHsfB4a gene duplication pair was located on the same LG, while the remaining gene duplication events occurred across different LGs (Fig. 3A). Cis-elements analysis of MlHSF promoters

To uncover the potential regulatory mechanisms of MlHSF in M. laxiflora, the cis-elements in the promoters of MlHSFs were analyzed (Fig. 4). All the detected cis-elements were assigned into 3 categories, e.g. light response, plant growth and development, as well as stress and hormone response (Fig. 4). For light response, Box 4 was detected in all MlHSFs (except MlHSFA3b), ca. 5–8 Box 4 cis-elements were detected in the promoters of MlHSFA1a, MlHSFA1c, MlHSFA6, MlHSFA8, MlHSFB2b and MlHSFC1 (Fig. 4A). Additionally, plenty of G-box and CTT-motif associated with light response were found in the promoters of MlHSFs, notably, 5–6 G-box cis-elements were predicted in the promoters of MlHSFC1, MlHSFB4a and MlHSFB2b (Fig. 4A). In the categories of plant growth and development, A-box, CAT-box, circadian and GCN4 motifs were detected in the promoters of some MlHSFs (Fig. 4A). In the stress and hormone response categories, abscisic acid response elements (ABREs) and anaerobic induction essential element (ARE) cis-elements were detected in almost all promoters of MlHSFs (Fig. 4A), suggesting that MlHSFs might be regulated by the ABA signaling pathway. Additionally, TGACG-motifs were found in most promoters of MlHSFs (Fig. 4A). Consistent with their major roles of HSFs in adapting to environmental stresses, ca. 23–64% of cis-elements detected in the individual MlHSF promoter were associated with light response, while the proportions of stress and hormone response categories ranged from 29% to 68% for each gene (Fig. 4B). Significantly fewer cis-elements of plant growth and development were detected in the promoters of MlHSFs compared to the other two categories, for instance, no cis-elements related to plant growth and development were found in the promoters of MlHSFB2a, MlHSFA3a, MlHSFA4a, and MlHSFB4b (Fig. 4). Furthermore, the gene duplication pairs of MlHSFA4a and MlHSFA4b contained the similar motifs in promoters (Fig. 4). Transcriptional expression analysis of MlHSFs under abiotic stresses

Under cadmium exposure treatments, five MlHSFs (e.g., MlHSFA2b, MlHSFA7, MlHSFB2a, MlHSFB2b and MlHSFB4a) were significantly downregulated after 48 and 72 h of treatment compared to the control condition in the leaves of M. laxiflora (Fig. 6A). Compared to the control condition, the foliar expression levels of MlHSFA4b were significantly upregulated after 24 h of cadmium exposure, while the mRNA levels of MlHSFC1 showed significantly upregulated after cadmium treatment for 72 h (Fig. 6A). Similar to cadmium exposure, the mRNA levels of MlHSFC1 were also significantly upregulated in the roots of M. laxiflora after waterlogging treatment (Fig. 6B). MlHSFC1 was the only gene that was significantly induced throughout the time course of waterlogging treatment in the roots (Fig. 6B). Discussion

Since HSFs paly essential roles in adapting to diverse environmental stresses, the HSF gene members have been widely detected in plenty of plant species, such as asparagus [69], moso bamboo [70], Rhodomyrtus tomentosa [26], switchgrass [25, 37], pea [71], Sorbus pohuashanensis [72], rice [73, 74], Rhododendron simsii [75], Medicago sativa [76, 77], barley [78], cucumber [79], Betula platyphylla [7] and so on. Besides, a large-scale identifications of HSFs in hundreds of plants have also been conducted to construct an HSF gene database [80]. However, little information about HSF genes was available in rare and endanger plant species, such as M. laxiflora. With the recently released high-quality chromosome-level genome of M. laxiflora [39, 43], a total of 21 MlHSFs were identified, which belonged to three subgroups (e.g., HSFA, HSFB and HSFC) (Table 1; Fig. 1). The number of identified MlHSFs in M. laxiflora was comparable to those in Arabidopsis, tomato and B. distachyon [5, 11]. The HSFB subgroup could be divided into five clades (i.e., HSFB1-HSFB5) [11], similar to Arabidopsis, none of the HSFB5 were detected in M. laxiflora (Table 1). Consistent with other dicotyledons [11], only one MlHSF1C gene was identified, while HSFC2 member was absent in M. laxiflora (Table 1).

The typical HSF proteins contain the conserved DBD in the N-terminal region with DNA binding activities [5]. In line with this, the MEME analysis revealed that motifs 2, 5 and 1were conserved in most MlHSF proteins, except for MlHSFA3a (Fig. 1). The motifs 2, 5 and 1 corresponded to the DBD of HSF proteins (Table 2), the absence of motifs 2, 5 and 1 in MlHSFA3a suggested this truncated protein may have lost the DNA binding activities. The C-terminal of MlHSF proteins varied a lot among different subgroups (Fig. 1), which was consist with previous studies [5, 10], The variable C-terminal regions of MlHSF proteins among different subgroups implied functional differentiations of MlHSFs from different subgroups. For instance, studies showed that only HSFA contained the AHA motif for transactivating activities, while HSFB contained a repressor domain of LFGV motif [1]. The conserved domains of NLS and NES in plant HSF proteins ensure their cellular locations in nucleus and functions as TFs [6]. In line with this, all the MlHSF proteins were predicted to be located in the nucleus (Table 1), which was consist with their function as TFs. Additionally, the gene duplication events detected only within MlHSF subgroups also suggested whole genome duplication (WGD) plays crucial roles in expanding the number of MlHSF subgroup members. Taken together, the obtained data indicated that MlHSF members from different subgroups might play distinct roles in adapting to environmental stresses.

The cis-element analysis showed that considerable motifs associated with light response, as well as stress and hormone response, were detected in the promoters of most MlHSFs (Fig. 4). While almost all the MlHSFs were constitutively highly or moderately expressed in root, stem, leaf, flower and fruits of M. laxiflora (Fig. 5). These data suggested that the MlHSFs play essential roles in normal growth and development and in adapting to environmental stresses. To unveil the possible roles played by MlHSFs in M. laxiflora under abiotic stresses, the mRNA levels of MlHSFs responding to time-series cadmium and waterlogging stresses were evaluated (Fig. 6). The expression levels of MlHSFA2a, MlHSFA2b, MlHSFA7, MlHSFB2b, MlHSFA7, MlHSFA3b, MlHSFA2b, MlHSFA9, MlHSFA8 and MlHSFB2b were also significantly inhibited by short-time waterlogging treatments (Fig. 6). The downregulations of most MlHSFs might be ascribed to decreased protein synthesis process with low demands of protein folding due to the energy crisis caused by anaerobic respiration in M. laxiflora under waterlogging stress [40, 41]. Interestingly, aside from the fact that most MlHSFs were inhibited by both cadmium and waterlogging stress, the MlHSFC1, the only gene from MlHSFC subgroup, was significantly stimulated by both cadmium and waterlogging stress (Fig. 6), suggesting that MlHSFC1 might play essential roles in coping with abiotic stress. The precise function of plant HSFC is still poorly explored so far [1]. Therefore, it is of interest to characterize of MlHSFC1 and explore its biological functions in coping with abiotic stresses in future.

The photosynthesis capacities were significantly decreased in the leaves of M. laxiflora under cadmium and waterlogging stresses [39, 42]. Additionally, increased levels of ROS were also detected in M. laxiflora under both cadmium and waterlogging stresses [39, 41, 42]. In Arabidopsis, HSFA1a can be activated by several stresses (including heat stress, pH changes and H2O2) depending on the redox state [81]. Additionally, Arabidopsis HSFA4A is also regulated by oxidative stress in response to salt condition [82]. Consistent with this, GO and KEGG analysis of the genes in the co-expression network centered by MlHSFs showed that these genes participated in cellular oxidant detoxification, photosynthesis-related pathway, chaperone-mediated protein folding and redox homeostasis (Fig. 7). Taken together, the obtained results indicated that MlHSFs in M. laxiflora might be crucial in coping with environmental stresses by regulating photosynthesis, redox homeostasis and protein folding.

Conclusion

In summary, a total of 21 MlHSF gene members were identified in M. laxiflora, which could be further categorized into three subgroups. The MlHSF proteins from the same subgroup shared similar conserved protein domains. The MlHSF proteins harbored the conserved N-terminal DBD with variable C-terminal regions. The gene duplication events were only found within the same MlHSF subgroup, suggesting they play important roles in expanding the number of subgroup members of MlHSF. Molecular evolutionary rate analysis revealed that all the MlHSFs were subjected to purifying selection. Cis-elements analysis detected a large number of motifs assigned to light response, as well as stress and hormone response. Almost all the MlHSFs were expressed in all detected tissues, notably, MlHSFB1 and MlHSFC1 had higher expression levels in stem and root. Gene expression analysis showed that most MlHSFs were significantly inhibited by both cadmium and waterlogging stresses, except for MlHSFC1. A MlHSFs-centered co-expression network has been established with WGNCA method, and the enrichment analysis revealed the functions of MlHSFs in photosynthesis, redox homeostasis and protein folding under abiotic stresses. In the current study, we systematically identified MlHSFs and analyzed their expression patterns in response to diverse abiotic stresses. These findings may provide valuable genetic resources for breeding plant varieties with enhanced resistance to abiotic stresses. However, the lack of an efficient genetic transformation system for M. laxiflora could hinder the functional characterization of these MlHSFs.
