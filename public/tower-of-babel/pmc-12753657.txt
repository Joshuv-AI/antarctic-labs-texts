Potentially toxic metals, source apportionment, meteorological impacts and health risks assessment of fine particulate matter (PM2.5) over Ilorin, Nigeria
Authors: Samuel Nwabachili, Oluwasesan Falaiye, Muyiwa Orosun, Jamiu Adeniran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAir pollution is a growing global concern due to harmful constituents like potentially toxic metals (PTMs), which can attach to particles such as dust, soot, and secondary aerosols, increasing their toxicity. This study assessed the seasonal variation, source apportionment, meteorological patterns, and health risks associated with PTMs (V, Mn, Cd, Cr, Fe, Zn, Ni, As, Co, Cu, Pb) in PM₂.₅ over Ilorin, Nigeria. PM2.5 data for 2019 were obtained from the SPARTAN network at the University of Ilorin and processed for analysis. Results showed that PTM concentrations—particularly Fe, Zn, Cr, Pb, and Co, were significantly higher during the dry season. Cu and Cd also contributed to observed seasonal variations. PMF showed that the sources of pollutant were crustal, industrial sources, secondary inorganic, and biomass burning. EF showed that Cu, Pb, As had values that were between 10 and 100 indicating that they were from both crustal and anthropogenic sources. Cd and Zn had values of 953.27 and 217.87 respectively, which were greater than 100 indicating that they were from industrial sources. Finally, V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, had values of 6.05, 1.97, 2.47, 1.00, and 5.28 respectively, which indicates that they were majorly from crustal sources. The Health risk assessment (non-cancer risk) via inhalation revealed a high hazard index (HI = 99.12), mainly from Fe (66.48) and Zn (31.76). Monte Carlo simulation for cancer risk (CR) indicated Cr and As as the highest contributors via inhalation (7.06E-05 and 2.84E-06), while Ni posed the greatest risk via dermal exposure (3.20E-05). These findings highlight significant health concerns associated with airborne PTMs and the need for targeted air quality management, particularly during the dry season.Keywords: PTMs, Monte carlo simulation, Health risk assessment, PM2.5, Source apportionment, Positive matrix factorisationSubject terms: Diseases, Environmental sciences, Risk factors

Introduction

Air pollution is regarded as a major problem plaguing the globe, presently, and this is largely due to rapid urbanization1. Particulate Matter (PM), a constituent of air pollution, are clssified into PM2.5, PM2.5–10.5, PM10, total suspended particulate (TSP). They consist of pollutants that are either inorganic or organic, such as the potentially toxic metals (PTMs). These PTMs are classified into two groups: the cancer-causing (Ni, Cd, Cr, and As) and the non-cancer-causing (Pb, Hg, and As) according to the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC)1. They are of great concern because they can be adsorbed onto the surface of atmospheric particles that already exist, some of which are soot, dust, and secondary aerosols. This process of adsorption is dependent on the surface area, chemical composition, and environmental conditions2. PM2.5 can undergo gas-to-particle conversion and condensation in a high-temperature environment, such as combustion sources. This process leads to the formation of new particles or the growth of existing ones2. They exhibit high toxicity, persistence, and bioaccumulative potential once in the atmosphere, as they lack natural degradation pathways, thereby making them a significant concern in terms of long-term environmental and human exposure. The primary routes of human exposure to these toxic metal-bound particles are inhalation, ingestion of contaminated food (particularly from roadside vendors), and dermal contact. Among these, inhalation and dermal absorption are considered the most direct and prevalent pathways3. Numerous health risk studies have been conducted in Ilorin and other cities in Nigeria.

Ajayi et al.4 examined indoor samples from various tertiary institutions in the city of Ondo, Nigeria. A non-cancer health risk analysis was conducted for PTMs using their Samples. The HQ and HI values were below the threshold value of 1, suggesting no significant non-carcinogenic health risk associated with exposure to the measured elements. The study, however, could have included a cancer risk assessment, as some of the PTMs being studied were carcinogens according to the IARC. Yomi-Agbajor and Gbadebor5 assessed the levels of pollution, ecological risk, and health risks associated with chemical elements in the dust and soil of the city of Warri, Nigeria. They reported that the concentration of PTMs was relatively high, with some exceeding the permissible limit. HQs were greater than 1, indicating adverse health effects. The CRs exceeded the allowable limit. The use of a probabilistic approach, such as the Monte Carlo Simulation, would have produced a more accurate result for CRs, as this method would properly handle variabilities and uncertainties. In Owerri, Nigeria, Opara et al.6 analyzed the concentration of PM around dumpsites in the city, along with a non-cancerous health risk assessment associated with inhalation. They reported that most dumpsites exceeded the Nigerian National Ambient Air Quality Standard allowable limit for PM10, and the HQs for PM2.5 exceeded 1 for nearly all the dump sites. Although the study could have been strengthened by including a carcinogenic risk assessment for PM₂.₅, its absence does not diminish the overall scientific merit of the work, as other analytical components were sufficiently robust to support its publication. An assessment of temporal cancer risk associated with Cd and As, via the inhalation and dermal contact route, in and around the University of Ilorin, using a probabilistic approach, was carried out by Falaiye et al.7. They reported that some months recorded risks that were higher than set limits. Also, the most potent pathway was the inhalation pathway. The gap in their study was that non-cancer risks were not addressed. Also other PTMs regarded as carcinogens were not analysed for their health risks (Ni, and Cr).

Cancer risk (CR) assessment is often fraught with uncertainties, which can lead to either an underestimation or overestimation of the risk. This can affect the resources that is put into mitigating these problems8. An overestimation can lead to a waste of resources, and vice versa. Hence, the importance of the probabilistic approach lies in its ability to handle uncertainties and variabilities.

This study assesses the seasonal variation and comparative distribution of potentially toxic metals (PTMs) in Ilorin. It further examines the wind patterns and backward air mass trajectories over the study area to understand the influence of regional transport on pollutant dispersion. Source apportionment was conducted using a receptor modeling approach to identify and quantify possible emission sources contributing to ambient PM₂.₅ concentrations. Additionally, the study evaluates the non-carcinogenic health risks associated with exposure to selected PTMs; V, Mn, Cu, Fe, Zn, Pb, Co, Cr, Cd, As, and Ni—detected in PM₂.₅, as well as the carcinogenic health risks linked to As, Ni, Cd, and Cr, which are classified as Group 1 and 2 carcinogens by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC). Both inhalation and dermal exposure pathways were considered in the human health risk assessment to provide a comprehensive understanding of potential health implications associated with PM₂.₅-bound metals. The cancer health risk was assessed using a probabilistic approach. Furthermore, the variability of the PTMs over the study period, the levels of concentration, and source apportionment were also analyzed. Unlike previous studies that focused primarily on concentration levels, this research provides a comprehensive, multi-dimensional analysis.

Materials and methodology

Description of study area

The study was conducted in Ilorin, the capital city of Kwara State, Nigeria, located at latitude 8°29’47” N and longitude 4°32’30"E. The town is characterized by distinct wet and dry seasons, with temperatures ranging from 33 °C to 34 °C from November to January, and from 34 °C to 43 °C from February to April8,9. It also experiences monthly mean temperatures that are very high, with values ranging from 25.0°C to 28.9°C. The total annual rainfall in the area is approximately 1200 mm (1971–2000)10,11. The Map of Ilorin is shown in Fig. 1. According to the 2006 census, Ilorin had 777,677 inhabitants, with later estimates reaching 900,000. Ilorin’s road network includes a mixture of arterial roads, sub-arterials, and tertiary roads. Traffic flow studies in Ilorin report that market/commercial zones exert a disproportionate influence on traffic volumes, particularly along corridors such as Muritala/Amilegbe, Offa Garage, Tipper Garage, and Sawmill, where peak flows can exceed 4,000 vehicles per hour during the rush period. Ilorin is home to a range of industrial establishments, such as the Olam cashew processing industry and flour mill, Tuyil Pharmaceuticals, Rajrab Pharmaceuticals, Chellaram Motorcycle Assembly, KAMWIL, Golden Connectionaries, etc.

Data collection and analytical methods

The daily concentration of PM2.5 data for 2019 was collected from the Surface Particulate Matter Network (SPARTAN), available on the SPARTAN website (www.spartan-network.org). SPARTAN is installed at the top of the Physics Department (Block 4), University of Ilorin, Ilorin, Nigeria, with a coordinate of longitude 8.484 °N, latitude 4.674 °E, and an elevation of 400 m above sea level. The data was in comma-separated value (CSV) format. It was imported into the Excel sheet and converted into usable data for analysis. The dataset was reviewed, and missing values were replaced with NA. Those below the detectable limit were replaced with BD. Duplicates were then identified to ensure that the data used were complete, consistent, and plausible. The cleaned data was then aggregated into monthly intervals, as this would help facilitate trend analysis. A single sensor was deployed for this study.

The SPARTAN methodology enables robust assessment of PM₂.₅ mass, composition, and source influences, and supports epidemiological and air quality modeling studies by ensuring high precision, consistency, and international comparability12. SPARTAN is a network that monitors long-term PM2.5 concentrations globally by utilising a method that is both standardised and cost-effective. To collect ambient dust samples, each instrument utilizes dual filters that capture aerosol samples for analysis of mass and chemical composition. These samples are then collected daily for 160 min over a 9-day cycle (to avoid day-of-week sampling bias), equivalent to 24 h of total sampling time per filter. The filter cartridges are designed to run for up to 63 days across seven active slots, unattended. The filters are then returned to Dalhousie University for Laboratory analysis. Gravimetric determination of mass is then carried out using a Sartorius Ultramicro Balance with 0.1 µg detection limit, in a controlled cleanroom environment (21 ± 1.5 °C; 35 ± 5% RH), as well as following EPA protocols. The precision of replicate mass measurements averages 4%. Black carbon is estimated using a Smoke Stain Reflectometer (SSR), calibrated against thermal-optical elemental carbon measurements obtained from preferred quartz filters. each of these filters is then halved for parallel analysis. Half of the filter paper was then digested in 10% nitric acid and analyzed via inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, Thermo Scientific X-Series 2) to quantify PTMs. This includes key metals like V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Ni, Cu, Zn, As, Cd, and Pb, with detection limits typically < 5 ng/m³12. The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) Standard Reference Material (SRM 2783; Air Particulate on Filter Media) was employed to ensure quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC) of the elemental analyses performed by inductively coupled plasma–mass spectrometry (ICP–MS). NIST SRM 2783 provides certified mass fractions for multiple trace elements commonly detected in ambient particulate matter, serving as an internationally recognized benchmark for data reliability. For QA/QC validation, the SRM was analyzed under the same conditions as the field samples, and the measured elemental concentrations were compared with the certified values provided by NIST. The analytical accuracy was evaluated through recovery tests, with acceptable recoveries defined within ± 10% of the accredited concentrations. In addition, procedural blanks and replicate analyses were included to assess precision and potential contamination. The SPARTAN network adheres to standardized QA/QC protocols based on NIST guidelines, which include instrument calibration using multi-element standards, the use of SRMs for inter-laboratory comparability, and routine data validation to ensure consistency and traceability of results across all network sites. The use of NIST SRM 2783 therefore guarantees analytical reliability and comparability of elemental data within and across the SPARTAN monitoring framework., Gaithersburg, MD, USA. The SRM was analyzed alongside field samples at the Dalhousie University ICP-MS laboratory, which adheres to SPARTAN network QA/QC protocols.

Enrichment factor analysis and source apportionment

Enrichment factor analysis

To assess whether an element in particulate matter is predominantly from crustal or anthropogenic sources, the enrichment factor (EF), a quantitative tool, was used. The EF employs the normalization of measured elemental concentrations against a reference element with a predominant crustal origin, and then compares that ratio with crustal abundance benchmarks13. It is based on Eq. 113.

Where: Ci is the concentration of element i in the PM2.5 sample, Cref is the concentration of chosen reference element (commonly Fe, Al, Ti, or Sc) in the same sample. The denominator uses average crustal abundance ratios of standards published. For this study, Fe was selected as the reference element due to its high crustal abundance, minimal anthropogenic interference, and its use across many aerosol studies14–19.

When EF < 10, it is regarded as predominantly of crustal origin, when 10 ≤ EF ≤ 100, it is moderately enriched, indicating a mixed natural and anthropogenic origin, and when EF > 100, it means strong enrichment attributed primarily to anthropogenic activities20.

PMF analysis

Positive Matrix Factorisation (PMF) is regarded as a multivariate (involving more than one variable at a time) receptor model. It is used to identify and quantify the sources of pollutants, which is based on measured environmental data21. It is widely used in air quality research to identify and apportion particulate matter sources, such as traffic emissions, industrial activities, biomass burning, and natural dust, without requiring prior knowledge of the source profiles. PMF, under the constraint that all values remain non-negative, decomposes a matrix of observed species concentrations into two matrices: factor contributions and factor profiles, based on Eq. 222,23.

The input data comprised species concentration and uncertainty matrices. Before analysis, the data were pre-processed to address missing values and concentrations below detection limits in accordance with the EPA PMF guidelines. To screen the data after loading it into PMF before model run, signal-to-noise ratios as well as error fractions were employed. To estimate the uncertainties uij for each concentration value, Eq. 4 was used, which accounted for both analytical uncertainty and method detection limits (MDL).

The error fraction is the relative analytical error and is typically between 5-10%, as derived froµ the laboratory. For this study, we used an error fraction of 5%. For concentrations below the detection limit (BDL), the concentration was replaced with ½ MDL, and the uncertainty was set to 5/6 MDL, as recommended by the EPA PMF guidelines. Missing values were substituted with the median of the species, with their uncertainty set to 4 × the median. The signal-to-noise ratios were automatically computed by the software to assess data quality, and this was done after loading the concentration and uncertainty matrices into the PMF model 5.0 (EPA PMF 5.0 Download (Free) - EPA PMF 3.0.exe). Species that had SN > 2 were classified as strong; those with 1 < S/N < 2 were classified as weak, and those with SN that were less than 1 were classified as bad and therefore excluded from the analysis. For this study, no data were excluded.

To assess model stability and explore rational ambiguity, diagnostics such as displacement (DISP), bootstrap (BS), and Fpeak rotations were employed. To interpret factor profiles, a comparison was made between chemical signatures and known emission sources, aided by enrichment factors and previous literature.

Meteorological evaluation and air mass transport assessment

The wind rose is a visual representation of how often winds blow from each direction and within specific speed categories. It serves as a circular histogram that objectively summarises wind direction at a given location over a particular period24. The significance of the wind rose in atmospheric research lies in its role in linking dominant wind directions with pollutant sources, thereby aiding in emission source identification and dispersion interpretation25. It is also helpful in constructing pollution roses—diagrams similar to wind roses but employing pollutant concentrations instead of wind speed—to identify source sectors associated with elevated pollutant levels26.

To analyze and visualize the prevailing wind directions and speeds during the sampling period, wind rose analysis was conducted using Python software version 3 (Download Python | Python.org). Daily wind speed and direction were imported and processed using the pandas, matplotlib, and windrose libraries. Furthermore, the Wind Rose Axes module was used to generate wind rose plots, which displayed the frequency distribution of wind from different directions in discrete speed intervals. This provided a clear understanding of the dominant wind patterns, which are used to identify likely pollutant transport directions and source contributions. To ensure alignment with the sampling timeline, an evaluation of consistency with PM2.5 concentration peaks was conducted on each wind rose plot, which was used to support source identification, particularly in interpreting back trajectory and PMF results.

The Hybrid Single-Particle Lagrangian Integrated Trajectory (HYSPLIT) model (https://www.ready.noaa.gov/HYSPLIT.php), widely used in atmospheric transport and dispersion, was developed by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA). This model enables researchers to calculate the trajectories of simple air parcels. It also facilitates the execution of complex simulations involving pollutant dispersion, chemical transformation, and deposition. To simulate the movement of air masses and the fate of airborne pollutants, HYSPLIT combines Lagrangian and Eulerian approaches. HYSPLIT is an essential tool in source apportionment, transboundary pollution studies, volcanic ash transport, and emergency response to hazardous releases. This is because it is of great value in identifying potential source regions of air pollutants through back trajectory analysis. Back trajectory analysis involves tracing the path of an air parcel backward in time from a receptor point (e.g., a monitoring station)27,28.

For this study, 120-hour back trajectory simulations were conducted using the HYSPLIT model to assess the long-range transport pathways of air masses arriving at the study site. These simulations were conducted at three interval heights (100 m, 500 m, and 1000 m Above Ground Level (AGL)) to capture vertical variability. The meteorological data used for this model were obtained from the Global Data Assimilation System (GDAS1), which has a 1° × 1° spatial resolution. The model was run on the HYSPLIT web platform (https://www.ready.noaa.gov/HYSPLIT.php). The back trajectory that resulted were clustered, analysed, and used to determine potential geographic source regions of air masses that influence the study area. This supported the source apportionment findings and helped distinguish between local and regional sources of pollution.

For this study, the cancer risk assessment was conducted using a Monte Carlo Simulation (MCS). Results and discussion

It ranged from 6.91 µg/m³ in October to 51.14 µg/m³ in December, with an overall mean concentration of approximately 17.83 µg/m³. it was revealed by the data that there was a distinct seasonal variation, with relatively low concentrations (this was observed from May to October), which corresponded to the wet season. The sharp increase that was recorded in December indicated the onset of the dry season. Furthermore, it could be attributed to the rise in emission activities, such as biomass burning and harmattan dust transport, as well as reduced atmospheric dispersion. The contrast in the season suggested that there is a strong influence of meteorological conditions, such as rainfall, wind speed, and atmospheric stability, on particulate matter concentration.

Seasonal variations and comparative analysis of potentially toxic metals (PTMs) in Ilorin

Figure 2 illustrates the monthly variations of the studied PTMs over the study period. The PTMs showed a notable increase during the dry season (November–December), with particularly elevated levels of Fe, Zn, Cr, Pb, and Co. In contrast, concentrations of all the metals declined significantly during the wet season (June–August), likely due to dilution effects and wet scavenging through rainfall. Interestingly, mid-season anomalies were observed, with Cd peaking in August and Cu in September. These unusual spikes may be indicative of localized events or specific industrial activities such as. A similar study conducted by Iroegbulem et al.35 in the suburbs of Lagos reported that Fe and Zn exhibited substantial seasonal variation, with concentrations markedly higher during the dry season. Additionally, their cluster analysis revealed a clear separation between wet and dry seasons, identifying Cd and Cu as key contributors to the observed seasonal variability. The Cd and Cu peaks observed in this study during August and September may reflect a similar influence from indoor or outdoor industrial sources.

Seasonal wind pattern and trajectory analysis results

Figure 4 illustrates the wind rose, showing wind statistics over Ilorin during the study period. In March, winds blew predominantly from the southwest (SW, 225°–240°) sector, with frequencies of approximately 40–45% at speeds of 1–3 m/s, and minor occurrences (5–10%) at 3–5 m/s, marking the early inflow of moisture preceding the main rains. In April, the SW–WSW winds strengthened, with an increased frequency of approximately 50% at 1–3 m/s, signifying the onset of the rainy season. From May to August, the winds were mainly influenced by the S–SSW (180°–210°) sector. In May, the SSW–SW winds (190°–230°) accounted for approximately 60% of the total frequency at speeds of 1–3 m/s, while speeds exceeding 3 m/s represented 10–15%, indicating the advancement of rainfall. June and July recorded the peak of maritime influence, with S–SSW winds contributing approximately 65% and 60%, respectively, within 1–3 m/s. Moderate winds (3–5 m/s) accounted for 15–20%. In August, dominant SSE–S winds continued, although their frequency decreased slightly to around 50% for speeds of 1–3 m/s, possibly indicating the August break—sustained wet-season winds with reduced updraft intensity. By September, there was an eastward shift, with winds predominantly from the SE (135°–150°), and a reduced dominant speed frequency (1–3 m/s) of 40–45%, reflecting monsoon retreat. The transition continued in October toward the ESE (110°–125°), where the frequency of 1–3 m/s winds declined to about 35%, and calmer winds (0–1 m/s) increased to 10%, signifying the early onset of the dry season. In November, northeasterly (NE–ENE, 45°–60°) winds became dominant, accounting for approximately 50% of total wind frequency at 1–3 m/s. This pattern intensified in December, with NE winds reaching about 70%, confirming the full onset of the Harmattan season and the influx of Sahelian air masses.

Windrose diagram showing the distribution of wind direction and wind speed over the study area from March through to December. The lengths of the radial bars represent the frequency of wind blowing from each direction, while the colour scale indicates wind-speed classes (m·s⁻¹).

The wind rose analysis further elucidates the directional dependence of PM₂.₅ sources over Ilorin. During the wet season, the prevailing southwesterly maritime winds transport cleaner air masses from the Atlantic Ocean, resulting in the dilution and damp deposition of particulate matter. In contrast, during the dry season, the northeasterly Harmattan winds convey dust-laden and metal-enriched aerosols from the Sahel and northern Nigeria, which increase PM₂.₅ concentrations. Additionally, the southwestern quadrant, which aligns with Ilorin’s industrial corridor, vehicular traffic routes, and biomass burning areas, likely contributes to elevated PM₂.₅ levels observed during transitional months. Therefore, the directional distribution of winds provides crucial evidence linking the transport and accumulation of potentially toxic metals (PTMs) to both regional (Sahelian dust) and local (traffic and industrial) emission sources.

Figure 5 presents the 120-h backward HYSPLIT trajectories aggregated weekly for the study period, revealing a clear seasonal progression of air-mass origin over Ilorin. In March, trajectories were predominantly from the southwest (SW–SSW), indicating an early intrusion of maritime air masses; northeasterly flows were absent. This early maritime influence is reflected in increased humidity and the presence of maritime sulfate in the PMF results. April and May show a consistent SSW–SW inflow across weeks, with many paths crossing the Gulf of Guinea; these southerly trajectories coincide with rising humidity and an increase in secondary inorganic aerosol (sulfate, nitrate, ammonium) contributions identified by PMF. During June and July, trajectories remain largely from the SSE–SSW quadrant, consistent with the peak monsoon and frequent wet deposition; correspondingly, PM₂.₅ mass and crustal metal loadings are suppressed, while secondary aerosol signals remain pronounced. August exhibits southerly to southeast flows with a greater directional spread, consistent with the August break and somewhat more variable vertical structure. This variability may explain isolated mid-season metal spikes (e.g., Cd in August), when local sources or episodic events dominate. In September, trajectories lengthen and shift toward SE–ESE origins, suggesting the beginning of monsoon retreat and reduced uplift. In October, there is a marked shift to easterly and northeasterly flows as dry continental air increases. November and December are dominated by NE–ENE and northeasterly trajectories extending deep into the Sahel and Sahara, consistent with Harmattan conditions and long-range dust transport; these periods align with the PMF-identified crustal/soil factor and spikes in Fe, Zn and other crustal metals. Overall, the HYSPLIT trajectories support a mixed influence of (i) regional maritime transport that enhances secondary inorganic aerosols during the wet season, (ii) local and peri-urban sources that produce episodic trace-metal enrichments, and (iii) long-range Saharan/Harmattan dust intrusions that dominate the dry season crustal signature.

Enrichment factor analysis results

According to Table 7, the elements with EF < 10 include Cr, Mn, Co, V, and Ni. This implied that the elements exhibited minimal to low enrichment, suggesting that their dominant origin was from crustal sources, likely due to the resuspension of soil dust or natural mineral sources. Elements with EF values between 10 and 100 included Cu, As, and Pb, as shown in Table 5. They all fall under moderate to high enrichment, which implies that their origin was mixed, but predominantly anthropogenic. For instance, Cu is associated with brake wear from vehicles, As often arises from the combustion of coal and biomass, and finally, Pb suggests vehicular or battery-related emissions. Elements that had EF values greater than 100 were Zn and Cd. They exhibited very high to extreme enrichment, which strongly suggested that they originated from purely anthropogenic sources. Cd had an EF value of 953.27, which is an indicator of unusual activity and is commonly linked to industrial activities, burning of plastic waste, and metallurgical processes. This finding is consistent with those of Yang et al.37, who reported that industrial sectors are significant emitters of Cadmium in delicate particulate matter. Furthermore, the EF value for Cd indicates that it originates from industrial sources, such as galvanization, tire wear, and paint.

PMF analysis result

Figure 6 shows the profiles of the components identified by the PMF analysis. Four distinct source profiles were resolved from the Positive Matrix Factorisation (PMF) analysis; these source profiles contribute to the ambient particulate matter. Factor 1 was attributed to the crustal or natural origin, as Al, Si, Ca, Fe, and Ti are the significant markers for soil dust and resuspended road dust. They had specie percentages of approximately 85%, 50%, 85%, 75%, and 80% respectively38. While Fe is a well-known crustal element, its presence in secondary inorganic sources in this study implies atmospheric aging. This aligns with the findings of Lin et al.39, who reported that approximately 65–75% of Fe-containing particles were internally mixed with nitrate and sulfate, indicating chemical aging and the formation of secondary aerosols. It contributed, as shown in Table 8, ~ 36.87% to the total pollution source. Factor 2 was attributed the industrial emission origin, and this was because PTMs such as Zn, Pb, and Cu, Ni, and Cd were dominant, with specie percentage of approximately 50%, 55%, 50%, 60%, and 85% respectively. This source contributed ~ 15.47% of the total source contribution, as shown in Table 6. Significant loadings of Zn, Pb, Cu, Ni, Cd, As, Co, Se, V, and Rb are consistently attributed to industrial emissions in receptor-based source apportionment studies40–42. These industrial emissions could be metal processing, waste incineration, or manufacturing activities. Factor 3 was attributed to the formation of secondary inorganic aerosols through atmospheric chemical reactions. This factor was characterized by high loadings of SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻, and NH₄⁺, with species percentages of approximately 70%, 60%, and 85%, respectively. Secondary inorganic aerosols, typically ammonium sulfate and ammonium nitrate, are marked by SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻, and NH₄⁺ from the atmospheric transformation of SO₂, NOₓ, and NH₃43–45. From Table 6, this source contributed ~ 27.72% to the pollution sources. The presence of NO₃⁻ and other carbonaceous species also implied the contributions from vehicular emissions. K+, Cl−, and NO₃⁻ are markers for biomass burning46–48. Factor 4 was attributed to the biomass burning source, as it showed strong signals of Cl− and NO₃⁻, and K+ with specie percentages of 70%, 55%, and 50% respectively, which had the possibility of originating from open burning of vegetation, or agricultural residue combustion, contributing ~ 19.95% to the total source profile as shown in Table 6. These source profiles provide a comprehensive understanding of the major contributors to pollution by PM in the study area.

Conclusion

This study assessed the concentration, seasonal variability, and potential health implications of potentially toxic metals (PTMs) bound to PM₂.₅ over Ilorin, Nigeria. The results revealed that Fe (0.33 µg/m³), Zn (0.159 µg/m³), and Pb (0.0187 µg/m³) were among the most abundant elements, with elevated levels during the dry season due to the influence of Harmattan winds and local emissions. Enrichment factor analysis indicated that Cd (EF = 953.27) and Zn (EF = 217.87) were highly enriched, confirming dominant anthropogenic sources, while Cr, Mn, Co, V, and Ni (EF < 10) originated mainly from crustal materials. Positive Matrix Factorization (PMF) identified four principal sources of PM₂.₅: crustal dust (natural resuspension), industrial emissions, secondary inorganic aerosols, and biomass burning. Among these, industrial emissions contributed significantly to Cd (74.8%), Ni (44.5%), and Pb (46.8%), while crustal dust dominated Fe (40.4%) and Mn (55.8%). The health risk assessment revealed that Fe and Zn posed the highest non-carcinogenic risks via inhalation (HQ = 66.48 and 31.76, respectively), while As (HQ = 0.16) was the primary contributor via dermal contact. The total hazard index (HI) for inhalation (99.12) exceeded unity, implying potential cumulative non-carcinogenic health concerns. Monte Carlo probabilistic analysis indicated that Cr exhibited the highest mean cancer risk (7.06 × 10⁻⁵) via inhalation, followed by As (2.84 × 10⁻⁶) and Cd (2.63 × 10⁻⁶), all within or slightly above the acceptable risk threshold (10⁻⁶ – 10⁻⁴). This study establishes that airborne PTMs in Ilorin are influenced by both local and regional sources, with industrial and Harmattan-related emissions contributing substantially to dry-season pollution. The findings provide baseline data for policy formulation on air quality control and highlight the need for continuous monitoring and mitigation of toxic metal exposure in Nigerian urban environments.

In brief, the findings of this study highlight the impact of seasonal meteorology, long-range transport, and local anthropogenic activities on the composition and toxicity of PM₂.₅ in the city of Ilorin. Industrial emissions remain a significant concern, particularly in terms of both carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic risks. The following recommendations outline measures to mitigate air pollution and associated health risks in urban environments in Nigeria. There should be an implementation of localized air quality management plans, and these plans should be tailored to the emission profiles and seasonal patterns specific to each city. Stricter emission controls should be enforced on industrial facilities, particularly those emitting PTMs such as Cd, Pb, Cr, and Ni. Real-time ambient air quality monitoring networks should be established, including those for PM₂.₅ and its constituents, as this will facilitate continuous assessment and public health warnings. Cleaner fuel technologies and emission filters for vehicular and generator exhaust should be promoted. Dust control strategies, such as urban greening, should be implemented during the dry season to help reduce the resuspension of dust particles. Efforts should be made to increase public awareness and education about the various pollution sources, their health effects, and the target audience should be individuals and communities. Finally, there should be an integration of air quality data into public health policies, making sure that decisions made in environmental and urban planning are evidence-based.
