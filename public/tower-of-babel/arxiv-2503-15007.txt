Supervaluations, truth, and intutionistic logic
Pablo Dopico

Abstract
The supervaluationist approach to fixed-point semantics is, arguably, the most celebrated and studied competitor to the Strong Kleene approach within Kripkean truth. In this paper, we show how to obtain supervaluationist fixed-point theories of truth for intuitionistic logic. In particular, we show how to do supervaluations over Kripke structures for intuitionistic logic, and we obtain the corresponding theories of truth, both semantic and axiomatic. Furthermore, we technically compare our approach with previous attempts to formalize Kripkean supervaluational theories over possible-world semantics. Finally, we show that, unlike in the case of classical logic, a fixed-point theory over intuitionistic logic can be given that is both supervaluational and highly compositional in nature.

Introduction

In his
Kripke (1975)
, Saul Kripke devised a celebrated theory of truth, based on a fixed-point construction, that delivered a partial but fully transparent truth predicate. In other words, this theory meets the intuition that a sentence
\varphi
is true if and only if
\varphi
is the case. Mathematically, the construction on which the theory is based runs as follows: one starts with a set of sentences granted to be true, and then applies an operator that collects the consequences of those sentences on some partial logic. By employing a monotone operator, the well-known theory of positive inductive definitions guarantees the existence of fixed-points, including a minimal one, i.e., a fixed-point contained in any other fixed-point. If the partial logic is adequately defined, such fixed-points will meet the desired transparency condition.

Historically, the partial logic most often used has been Strong Kleene (SK) logic. Its resulting fixed-point theory has motivated the famous (classical) axiomatic theory of Kripke-Feferman, KF (
Feferman (1991)
;
Cantini (1989)
), as well as the (arguably) more faithful non-classical theory known as Partial Kripke-Feferman, PKF
(
Halbach and Horsten, 2006
)
. But Kripke immediately noticed that the theory based on SK logic would fail to declare true many truths of classical logic. For example, ‘
\lambda\rightarrow\lambda
’, for
\lambda
the Liar sentence, fails to be in the fixed-points of the SK construction.

It is as a remedy to this failure that supervaluational fixed-point theories appear. Thus, Kripke’s idea was to use as the partial logic a form of supervaluational logic in the style of
van Fraassen (1966)
. The original motivation behind supervaluationism is straightforward: to produce a logic that allows for truth-value gaps—and, more specifically, those engendered by non-denoting terms—while preserving all the truths of classical logic. Similarly, in the context of Kripke’s theory, supervaluational logics become a way to recover all those classical validities that are lost in SK truth. And while the SK approach remains the most popular one, supervaluational truth has by now been studied in detail (
Cantini (1990)
;
Kremer and Urquhart (2008)
;
Meadows (2013)
) and is attracting a good deal of interest in recent years (
Stern (2018)
;
Incurvati and Schlöder (2023)
;
Stern (2025)
). Consequently, we nowadays have not only a full picture of
semantic
supervaluational truth in the different conceptions proposed by Kripke, but also a fairly thorough understanding of
axiomatic
supervaluational truth as given by
Cantini (1990)
, and, more recently,
Dopico and Hayashi (2024)
.

There is a caveat, though. All these contributions—with the notable exception of
Stern (2025)
—concern truth achieved by supervaluating over classical models. That is, one supervaluates by looking at the set of sentences satisfied by all classical models meeting certain conditions. But supervaluation is, after all, a method, and nothing prevents us from applying such method to an alternative class of semantic structures. Therefore, the question which motivates this paper is: what does supervaluation over intuitionistic structures look like?

There are at least a couple of very relevant motivations for addressing this question. In the first place, and as mentioned, supervaluational truth emerged as a way to recover the classical validities that SK truth just takes as gappy. But classical truth is by no means universally accepted. A defender of constructive mathematics will be wary to accept all classical validities, and yet might be on board with accepting intuitionistic tautologies. Hence, supervaluational truth over intuitionistic logic might be more suitable as a formal theory of truth for the constructive mathematician.

In this sense, this paper follows somehow the spirit of previous work where intensional notions (such as truth, but also necessity) have been presented as a predicate in a setting based on possible-worlds—see, e.g.,

Finally, a further motivation stems from the limitations of supervaluationist truth over intuitionistic logic. In particular, its incompatibility with compositional properties for disjunctions and existentially quantified sentences. Given that intuitionistic arithmetic, unlike its classical counterpart, possesses the disjunction and existential properties—that is, a disjunction will be provable if and only if a disjunct is, and similarly for the existentially quantified statements—, it is reasonable to hope that supervaluationist theories over intuitionistic logic could retain compositionality for these connectives.

The paper is divided as follows. In the next section, we briefly introduce our notation for the remainder of the paper. In section
3

4

5
compares our approach with what we dubbed the
fixed-frame
approach, the one found in preceding work on Kripkean truth over possible-world semantics. Finally, in section
6
we show that obtaining a theory that is both supervaluational and highly compositional is indeed possible: by modifying the forcing relation for the Kripke structures, we produce a supervaluational theory where the truth predicate distributes over disjunction and the existential quantifier.

3.2
The semantic construction

The simplest approach for a semantic, supervaluational theory in classical logic based on the fixed-point approach is the following. Given an interpretation for the truth predicate,
X
, one considers all models of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tr}}
such
that their arithmetical part is just
\mathbb{N}
and whose interpretation of the truth predicate includes
X
. In other words: one considers all classical structures where the interpretation for the truth predicate
extends
the one given. (Sometimes, additional conditions are imposed; we will return to that later on.) Thus, writing
\langle\mathbb{N},S\rangle
for the classical model for
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tr}}
over the standard model
\mathbb{N}
where we let
S
be the interpretation of
\mathrm{Tr}
, the classical approach is formalized as:

\langle\mathbb{N},X\rangle\vDash_{\mathrm{sv}}A\text{ iff for all }X^{\prime}\supseteq X,\langle\mathbb{N},X^{\prime}\rangle\vDash A

(1)

Here,
\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\mathrm{sv}}A
is to be read as “
A
is sv-satisfied by
\mathcal{M}
”, and each model of the form
\langle\mathbb{N},X^{\prime}\rangle
is a way of extending
\langle\mathbb{N},X\rangle
.

However, since intuitionistic structures differ from classical ones, we need to translate the classical supervaluationist framework in the the most faithful way possible. In particular, there are two differences between the classical and the intuitionistic case, concerning what a extension of the interpretation for the truth predicate is, that we see as crucial. These rely on an understanding of the interpretation for the truth predicate which equates the latter with the relation
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}
, where
\mathcal{M}
is some first-order intuitionistic Kripke
\omega
-structure. First, the relation, and with it the interpretation for
\mathrm{Tr}
, can be extended by adding to the structure
more worlds
over which the function is defined. That is, by enlarging a given structure with more worlds (and, possibly, more elements to the accessibility relation), and defining the interpretation of truth at those worlds—in such a way that respects the requirements for intuitionistic Kripke
\omega
-structures—we are producing an extended truth interpretation. Formally, we start with a structure
\mathcal{M}
whose truth interpretation function is
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}
. Then, in considering another another structure
\mathcal{N}
which possesses all the worlds in
\mathcal{M}
and additional ones, and whose interpretation of truth for all worlds already in
\mathcal{M}
is that of
\mathcal{M}
,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}}
genuinely extends
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}}
. Second, the relation, and with it the interpretation for
\mathrm{Tr}
, might be extended by assigning
larger sets of sentences
to some of the worlds, and keeping the rest equal. That is, a truth interpretation
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}}
might extend another
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}
by having the same worlds, but assigning a larger interpretation of truth for some of those worlds—provided it respects the hereditary conditions. In that case, clearly, we also have
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}}
. In addition, and combining these two points, an interpretation
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}}
will also extend another when
\mathcal{N}
contains all the worlds in
\mathcal{M}
plus additional ones,
and
the interpretation of truth at every shared world is equal or greater in
\mathcal{N}
than in
\mathcal{M}
.

In sum, a structure
\mathcal{M}
extending the truth interpretation of another structure
\mathcal{N}
means that: i)
\mathcal{M}
includes at least all the worlds contained in
\mathcal{N}
, and; ii) in every world contained in both
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
, the interpretation of
\mathrm{Tr}
at
\mathcal{M}
extends the interpretation of
\mathrm{Tr}
at
\mathcal{N}
. This being said, there are different ways of conceiving an extended truth interpretation—we will touch on a previously explored such conception in section
5
. For now, we stick to the one just propounded. A simple formal rendering of it could be the following:

Definition 5

(Naive interpretation extension)

Given structures

w\Vdash^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{svi}}\varphi
is to be read as “
w
svi-forces
\varphi
”.
w\in W_{\mathcal{M}}
svi-forces
\varphi
when, for any structure into which
\mathcal{M}
is embeddable via some function
f
, the image of
w
under the embedding function forces
\varphi
. Thus, the notion of svi-forcing is tied to a world
w
. The antecedent in the definiendum (in this case,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}\leqq_{f}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
) is called the admissibility requirement. We refer to the forcing scheme defined by the svi-forcing relation as the scheme SVI.

Let us now give the informal picture. Forcing in Kripke models is a relation between a world and a formula, but determined by a structure, an accessibility relation, and a truth interpretation. Hence, in supervaluating, we range over alternative structures, accessibility relations, and truth interpretations, thus considering what could have been somewhat extended while respecting what is, so to say, ‘determinately true’—i.e., the original interpretation of the truth predicate at a given world.

Next, we define a Kripke-jump; the latter is no longer relative to a world:

Definition 9
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathrm{SENT}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tr}}}
. The Kripke-jump operator over the svi-forcing relation is a function
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi}}:\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{SENT}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tr}}})\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{SENT}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tr}}})
defined as follows::

\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi}}(X):=\{\#\varphi\;|\;\forall\mathcal{M},\forall w\in W_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}(w)=X\Rightarrow w\Vdash^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{svi}}\varphi)\}

By analogy with the classical case, we can abbreviate the set condition on the right-hand-side, i.e.
\forall\mathcal{M},\forall w\in W_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}(w)=X\Rightarrow w\Vdash^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{svi}}\varphi)
, by
X\vDash_{\mathrm{svi}}\varphi
. Roughly put, then,
X\vDash_{\mathrm{svi}}\varphi
amounts to what is supervaluationally forced at all worlds with truth interpretation equal to
X
, abstracting away from the particularities of a world in a structure.

As is to be expected:

Proposition 4
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathrm{SENT}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tr}}}
. Then
X\vDash_{\mathrm{svi}}\varphi
, for
\varphi
any intuitionistically valid formula of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tr}}
.

On the other hand,
X\vDash_{\mathrm{svi}}\varphi
will not always hold when
\varphi
is a classical but not intuitionistic validity. For example:

Example 1
.

Let
X\subsetneq\mathrm{SENT}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tr}}}
be such that there is a sentence
\varphi
of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tr}}
for which
\#\varphi\notin X
and
\#\neg\varphi\notin X
.
Then
X\nvDash_{\mathrm{svi}}\mathrm{Tr}\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner\vee\neg\mathrm{Tr}\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner
.

Proof.

Take any structure
\mathcal{M}
and worlds
w_{0},w_{1}\in W_{\mathcal{M}}
such that:

•

\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}(w_{0})=X
,

•

\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}(w_{1})=X\cup\{\#\varphi\}

•

w_{0}\leq_{\mathcal{M}}w_{1}

Consider the identity embedding. Since
w\nVdash_{\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{Tr}\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner\vee\neg\mathrm{Tr}\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner
, then
w\nVdash^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{svi}}\mathrm{Tr}\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner\vee\neg\mathrm{Tr}\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner
, whence
X\nvDash_{\mathrm{svi}}\mathrm{Tr}\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner\vee\neg\mathrm{Tr}\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner
follows.
∎

The result in Proposition
4
can in fact be refined in the following way:

Proposition 5
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathrm{SENT}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tr}}}
. Then
\forall\mathcal{M},w(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}(w)=X\Rightarrow w\Vdash_{\mathcal{M}}\varphi)
implies
\#\varphi\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi}}(X)
.

Proof.

Pick some arbitrary
\mathcal{M}
and
w
s.t.
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}(w)=X
. It suffices to show
w\Vdash^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{svi}}\varphi
. So take a structure
\mathcal{N}
and function
f
s.t.
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}\leqq_{f}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}}
, and show
f(w)\Vdash_{\mathcal{N}}\varphi
. Since
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}}(f(w))=X^{\prime}\supseteq X
, take the truncated structure
\mathcal{N}_{f(w)}
. Now, create a new structure
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
by adding a root
n^{\prime}
that accesses
f(w)
, and such that
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}(n^{\prime})=X
. It’s easy to see that this structure is well-defined and, by assumption,
n^{\prime}\Vdash_{\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}\varphi
. So, by hereditariness,
f(w)\Vdash_{\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}\varphi
, which entails
f(w)\Vdash_{\mathcal{N}_{f(w)}}\varphi
. Finally, the latter entails
f(w)\Vdash_{\mathcal{N}}\varphi
.
∎

That is, the SVI-jump over
X
really captures whatever is satisfied by all worlds with truth interpretation equal to
X
.

3.3
Fixed-points and fixed-point models

Let us now investigate the SVI-jump and, in particular, the fixed-points that it gives rise to. First, we show that said fixed-points exist.

Proposition 6
.

The following holds:

i)

\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi}}(\cdot)
is monotonic, i.e.,
X\subseteq Y\Rightarrow\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi}}(X)\subseteq\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi}}(Y)
. Therefore, there are fixed points of
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi}}(\cdot)
.

ii)

For all
\mathcal{M}
and worlds
w\in W_{\mathcal{M}}
, there exists a structure
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
satisfying the admissibility requirements. Therefore, there are non-degenerate fixed points of
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi}}(\cdot)
.

Proof.

For i): Let
\#\varphi\notin\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi}}(Y)
. Then fix
\mathcal{M}
and
w\in W_{\mathcal{M}}
such that
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}(w)=Y
and fix
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
such that
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}\leqq_{f}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
and
f(w)\nVdash_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}\varphi
. Now, let
\mathcal{M}_{0}
be the truncated structure
\mathcal{M}_{f(w)}
, and define a new structure
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{0}
which is exactly like
\mathcal{M}_{0}
but
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{0}}(f(w))=X
. Then
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{0}}\leqq_{\mathrm{id}}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}_{0}}
(where
\mathrm{id}
is the identity function) and
f(w)\nVdash_{\mathcal{M}_{0}}\varphi
, so
\#\varphi\notin\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi}}(X)
.

For ii): just take
\mathcal{M}
itself.
∎

Note that there is an alternative way of defining the Kripke jump:

Definition 10
.

\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi^{\prime}}}(X):=\{\#\varphi\;|\;\forall\mathcal{M},\forall w\in W_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}(w)\supseteq X\Rightarrow w\Vdash^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{svi}}\varphi)\}

Proposition 7
.

The following holds:

i)

\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi^{\prime}}}(\cdot)
is monotonic, i.e.,
X\subseteq Y\Rightarrow\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi^{\prime}}}(X)\subseteq\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi^{\prime}}}(Y)
. Therefore, there are fixed points of
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi^{\prime}}}(\cdot)
.

ii)

For all structures
\mathcal{M}
and worlds
w\in W_{\mathcal{M}}
, there exists a structure
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
satisfying the admissibility requirements. Therefore, there are non-degenerate fixed points of
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi^{\prime}}}(\cdot)
.

Proof.

For i): for any structure
\mathcal{M}
, world
w\in W_{\mathcal{M}}
,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}(w)\supseteq Y
implies
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}(w)\supseteq X
when
X\subseteq Y
. Therefore, if
\#\varphi\notin\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi^{\prime}}}(Y)
, the structure
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
and world
w^{\prime}
such that
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}(w^{\prime})\supseteq Y
and
w^{\prime}\nVdash^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{svi}}\varphi
is a counterexample for
\#\varphi\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi^{\prime}}}(X)
.
For ii): as before.
∎

Nonetheless, in what follows, we will stick to the jump
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi}}
, as it will make the proofs in section
6
more immediate. Once we know there are fixed points of the operator, we can define fixed-point structures in the following way:

Definition 11
.

A fixed-point structure of the scheme
\mathrm{SVI}
is a persistent first-order intuitionistic Kripke
\omega
-structure
\mathcal{M}:=\langle W_{\mathcal{M}},\leq_{\mathcal{M}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{M}},\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}\rangle
such that for all
w\in W_{\mathcal{M}}
, there is an
X\subseteq\mathrm{SENT}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tr}}}
with
X=\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi}}(X)
and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}(w)=X
.

In short, a fixed-point structure is a structure of the kind we have been considering where the extension of
\mathrm{Tr}
at any world is a fixed point of SVI.

The main advantage of the Kripkean approach to truth is that it yields models which are transparent with respect to the truth predicate. Thus, the fixed-point construction with the Strong Kleene schema gives us interpretations
(X^{+},X^{-})
(i.e., extension and antiextension) such that

\langle\mathbb{N},X^{+},X^{-}\rangle\vDash_{\mathrm{sk}}\varphi\Leftrightarrow\langle\mathbb{N},X^{+},X^{-}\rangle\vDash_{\mathrm{sk}}\mathrm{Tr}\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner.

Similarly, the fixed-point supervaluationist approach, as sketched in (
1
) above, gives an interpretation
X
such that

\langle\mathbb{N},X\rangle\vDash_{\mathrm{sv}}\varphi\Leftrightarrow\langle\mathbb{N},X\rangle\vDash_{\mathrm{sv}}\mathrm{Tr}\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner.

In our intuitionistic approach, transparent models can also be given on the basis of fixed-points of the operator
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi}}
. In particular, we can provide transparent models for every fixed-point of
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi}}
based on the one-world frame. This is to be contrasted with the possibility of providing transparent models over arbitrary frames, which we could not establish—cf. section
5
.

As a notational add-on, for
\mathcal{M}:=\langle W_{\mathcal{M}},\leq_{\mathcal{M}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{M}},\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}\rangle
a structure, we write
\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\mathrm{svi}}\varphi
when
w\Vdash^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{svi}}\varphi
holds for all
w\in W_{\mathcal{M}}
.

Proposition 8
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathrm{SENT}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tr}}}
be such that
X=\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi}}(X)
. Let
\mathcal{M}:=\langle W_{\mathcal{M}},\leq_{\mathcal{M}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{M}},\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}\rangle
be a persistent first-order intuitionistic Kripke
\omega
-structure given by:

•

W_{\mathcal{M}}=\{w\}
.

•

\leq_{\mathcal{M}}=\{\langle w,w\rangle\}
.

•

\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}=\{\langle w,\#\varphi\rangle\;|\;\#\varphi\in X\}
.

Then,
w\Vdash^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{svi}}\varphi\Leftrightarrow w\Vdash^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{svi}}\mathrm{Tr}\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner
. Therefore,
\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\mathrm{svi}}\varphi\Leftrightarrow\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\mathrm{svi}}\mathrm{Tr}\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner
.

Proof.

The second claim follows by definition from the first; we prove the latter.

Left-to-right: assume
w\Vdash^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{svi}}\varphi
. We want to show
\#\varphi\in X
, i.e.,

\forall\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\forall w^{\prime}(\mathcal{I}(w^{\prime})=X\Rightarrow w^{\prime}\Vdash^{\mathcal{M^{\prime}}}_{\mathrm{svi}}\varphi)

So take arbitrary
\mathcal{M^{\prime}}
and
w^{\prime}\in W_{\mathcal{M^{\prime}}}
with
\mathcal{I}(w^{\prime})=X
. In order to prove
w^{\prime}\Vdash_{\mathrm{svi}}\varphi
, we take
\mathcal{N}
with
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M^{\prime}}}\leqq_{f}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}}
, and we want to show
f(w^{\prime})\Vdash_{\mathcal{N}}\varphi
. But now, since
\mathcal{M}
is a one-world structure, the function that
g
that sends
w
to
w^{\prime}
is an EI function between
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M^{\prime}}
, and so
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}\leqq_{g}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M^{\prime}}}
. Composing
g
and
f
we get a function
h
such that
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}\leqq_{h}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}}
, with
h(w)=f(w^{\prime})
. From
w\Vdash^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{svi}}\varphi
, we obtain
h(w)\Vdash_{\mathcal{N}}\varphi
, i.e.,
f(w^{\prime})\Vdash_{\mathcal{N}}\varphi
, as desired.

Right-to-left:
w\Vdash^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{svi}}\mathrm{Tr}\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner
implies
\#\varphi\in X
. Hence, since
X=\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{sv}}(X)
,
\forall\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\forall w^{\prime}(\mathcal{I}(w^{\prime})=X\Rightarrow w^{\prime}\Vdash^{\mathcal{M^{\prime}}}_{\mathrm{svi}}\varphi)
. Let
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
be
\mathcal{M}
and
w^{\prime}
be
w
, it follows that
w\Vdash^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{svi}}\varphi
, as desired.
∎

Isolated, then, worlds can exhibit a transparent behaviour when coupled with a fixed-point as their interpretation of truth. Things change if we consider larger structures. It is easy to see where the proof for Proposition
8
will fail if we considered more complex, many-worlds frames. While the move from
w\Vdash^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{svi}}\mathrm{Tr}\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner
to
w\Vdash^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{svi}}\varphi
would still go through, the argument for the other direction would break: the Kripke models
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
over which the jump operator
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{sv}}
ranges might be structurally very different from the original model
\mathcal{M}
where
\varphi
is svi-forced, hence impeding that
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}
be embedded in
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}
. In other words: since our jump ranges over all Kripke models where some world’s interpretation of truth is a fixed-point, knowing that
w\Vdash^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{svi}}\varphi
does not suffice, as it does not guarantee that
\varphi
is supervaluationally forced in models based on different frames.

In spite of this, fixed-points themselves
are
transparent:

Proposition 9
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathrm{SENT}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tr}}}
be such that
X=\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi}}(X)
. Then,
\#\varphi\in X\Leftrightarrow\#\mathrm{Tr}\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner\in X
.

Proof.

Left-to-right: assume
\#\varphi\in X
and take
\mathcal{M}
,
w
such that
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}(w)=X
. Since
\#\varphi\in X
,
f(w)\Vdash_{\mathcal{N}}\mathrm{Tr}\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner
for any
\mathcal{N},f
such that
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}\leqq_{f}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}}
, hence
w\Vdash_{\mathrm{svi}}^{\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{Tr}\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner
. Since
\mathcal{M},w
were arbitrary,
\#\mathrm{Tr}\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi}}(X)=X
.

Right-to-left: assume
\#\mathrm{Tr}\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner\in X
. Take
\mathcal{M},w
such that
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}(w)=X
. Since
\#\mathrm{Tr}\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner\in X=\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi}}(X)
,
w\Vdash^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{svi}}\mathrm{Tr}\ulcorner\varphi\urcorner
, which is only possible if
\#\varphi\in X
.
∎

3.4
Alternative supervaluation schemes

The classical approach formulated in (
1
) above is, as we mentioned, the simplest supervaluational scheme, which is sometimes known as the SV-scheme. This basic approach considers all possible truth extensions. However, it is far from being the only approach possible. In the literature there are, in fact, three more popular schemes,

(

corresponding to additional conditions, or admissibility requirements, that we impose on the extensions:

\displaystyle X\vDash_{\mathrm{vb}}\varphi

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\forall X^{\prime}(X^{\prime}\supseteq X\,\&\,X^{\prime}\cap X^{-}=\varnothing\Rightarrow X^{\prime}\vDash\varphi)

(2)

\displaystyle X\vDash_{\mathrm{vc}}\varphi

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\forall X^{\prime}(X^{\prime}\supseteq X\,\&\,X^{\prime}\cap X^{\prime-}=\varnothing\Rightarrow X^{\prime}\vDash\varphi)

(3)

\displaystyle X\vDash_{\mathrm{mc}}\varphi

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\forall X^{\prime}(X^{\prime}\supseteq X\,\&\,\mathrm{MCX}(X^{\prime})\Rightarrow X^{\prime}\vDash\varphi)

(4)

Here,
\mathrm{MCX}(X)
stands for ‘

is a maximally consistent set of sentences of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tr}}
’, i.e., for any sentence
\varphi
of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tr}}
, either
\#\varphi\in X
or
\#\neg\varphi\in X
, but not both. Moreover, given a set of sentences
X
,
X^{-}
is the set of sentences
\{\#\varphi\;|\;\#\neg\varphi\in X\}
. In all the cases shown, the corresponding jump-operators are monotonic, hence they have fixed-points. Moreover, as long as one starts applying the operator to consistent sets of sentences, the fixed-points will be non-degenerate.

All of this is then reproducible in our intuitionistic setting. Given
\mathcal{M}

\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\mathcal{M}}(w)

Definition 12
.

Let
\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle w\Vdash^{\mathcal{M}}_{vbi}\varphi:\Leftrightarrow\forall\mathcal{M}^{\prime},f(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}\leqq_{f}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}\;\&\;\forall w^{\prime}\geq f(w)(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}(w^{\prime})\cap\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\mathcal{M}}(w)=\varnothing)\Rightarrow w^{\prime}\Vdash_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}\varphi)

\displaystyle w\Vdash^{\mathcal{M}}_{mci}\varphi:\Leftrightarrow\forall\mathcal{M}^{\prime},f(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}\leqq_{f}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}\;\&\;\forall w^{\prime}\geq f(w)(\mathrm{MCX}(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}(w^{\prime})))\Rightarrow w^{\prime}\Vdash_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}\varphi)

As with
\Vdash_{\mathrm{svi}}
, the antecedents of the conditionals in the definitions above are known as the admissibility requirements (or conditions) for the corresponding schemes. For the sake of brevity, we write
\mathrm{Ad}_{1}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{M}^{\prime},f)
,
\mathrm{Ad}_{2}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{M}^{\prime},f)
and
\mathrm{Ad}_{3}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{M}^{\prime},f)
for the admissibility requirements of vbi-, vci- and mci-forcing. Thus, for example,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}\leqq_{f}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}\;\&\;\forall w^{\prime}\geq f(w)(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}(w^{\prime})\cap\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\mathcal{M}}(w)=\varnothing)
abbreviates as
\mathrm{Ad}_{1}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{M}^{\prime},f)
. As before, the schemes defined by the vbi-, vci-, and mci-forcing relations will be known as, respectively, VBI, VCI, and MBI.

It is essential that the admissibility conditions of the schemes defined above quantify over all worlds accessible from the image of the world we consider—in the case above, over all worlds accessible from
f(w)
. The reason has to do with the purpose of these conditions. They can be seen as a way of setting requirements on the interpretations to be considered, with the aim of imposing features for the
internal
theory of truth, i.e., for the behaviour of the truth predicate
inside
the fixed-points. For example: the scheme VC, (
3
) above, imposes that the truth predicate be consistent
inside
the fixed-points. These features, in all the cases we consider, take the form of a negation or conditional when formalized. In the case of VC, for instance, the feature is formalized as the sentence
\#\neg(\mathrm{Tr}t\wedge\mathrm{Tr}\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\neg$\cr\hfil.\hfil\crcr}}t)
(for any arbitrary closed term
t
), which is present in all fixed-points of the scheme. But the forcing relation for the conditional and the negation in the intuitionistic structures we consider is global (or rather, upward-looking), hence, in order to preserve these features, the admissibility condition must also be upward-looking and not local. That is, precisely, what we achieve by quantifying over all worlds accessible from the image of the world we start with.

The corresponding jumps
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{vbi}},\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{vci}},\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{mci}}
are exactly like in Definition
9
, using the relevant supervaluational-forcing relations. These operators are all monotonic, and thus give rise to fixed-points. These arguments for monotonicity are all replicas of the proof in Proposition
6
.i. For the existence of non-degenerate fixed-points of
\mathcal{J}_{mci}

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tr}}

\varphi

\#\varphi
or
\#\neg\varphi
is not in the set), then the extra admissibility conditions on the interpretations can always be fulfilled—either by the initial interpretation itself, in the case of VBI and VCI; or by a maximally consistent extension of the initial interpretation in the case of MCI (see also footnote
5
). It is also easy to notice that that:

Finally, we can easily establish the relation between the four schemes:

Proposition 11

Let

The fixed-frame approach

As repeatedly stated, our supervaluational-forcing relation
w\Vdash^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{svi}}\varphi
ranges over structures and embedding functions that might contain more worlds and enlarged accessibility relations than the original Kripke model (
\mathcal{M}
) for whose world (
w
) the relation is defined. Likewise, the Kripke jump for a set
X\subseteq\mathrm{SENT}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tr}}}
ranges over all structures which possess a world with truth interpretation
X
, offering a global sense of what supervaluationally follows, in intuitionistic logic, from a given set of sentences. This strategy, however, is to be contrasted with the approach that some papers have adopted when building fixed-point constructions over possible-world semantics—e.g,
Halbach et al. (2003)
,
Halbach and Welch (2009)
,
Nicolai (2018)
,
Stern (2025)
. Although, with the exception of Stern, the rest of authors are primarily concerned with a fixed-point construction for a necessity predicate, the framework applies equally well for truth. The goal of this section is to characterize this approach, that we shall call the
fixed-frame
approach, and compare it to the one developed in section
3
.

Consider a persistent first-order intuitionistic Kripke
\omega
-structure
\mathcal{M}:=\langle W_{\mathcal{M}},\leq_{\mathcal{M}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{M}},\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}\rangle
. The fixed-frame approach, as its name makes clear, “fixes” the frame (i.e., the worlds and the accessibility relation), and considers only interpretation extensions that keep the same set of worlds as their domain. That is, the extensions of
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}
considered are interpretation relations
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq W_{\mathcal{M}}\times\mathrm{SENT}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tr}}}
with
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{M}}
. No additional worlds, and hence no enlarged accessibility relation, is allowed. There is also no reference to embeddings, so interpretations for isomorphic structures with different labels for the worlds are left aside. Let us write
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}\preceq\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{M}}
when
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{M}}
is an extension of
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}
thus understood. Since we are keeping the frame the same, let us write
w,\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}\Vdash\varphi
or
w,\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{M}}\Vdash\varphi
to indicate that the frame is fixed, and the interpretation relation in question is
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}
, or
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{M}}
, respectively—instead of providing a new structure for each new interpretation. We maintain this notation for the rest of the section, even when the frame varies. For the world
w\in W_{\mathcal{M}}
,
w
and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}

ff-forcing
the
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tr}}
-sentence
\varphi
is then defined as

w,\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}\Vdash_{\mathrm{ff}}\varphi:\Leftrightarrow\forall\,\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{M}}\succeq\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}(w,\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{M}}\Vdash\varphi)

Moreover, the Kripke jump is defined on interpretation relations, not on sets of sentences.
6
6

6

To be wholly faithful to the original approaches, authors tend to conceive of the interpretation for truth
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}
for a model
\mathcal{M}
as a

from
W_{\mathcal{M}}
to
\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{SENT}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tr}}})
, rather than a subset of
W_{\mathcal{M}}\times\mathrm{SENT}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tr}}}
.
The idea is that, when applied to an evaluation function
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}:W_{\mathcal{M}}\times\mathrm{SENT}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tr}}}
, the jump
\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{ff}}
yields another evaluation function
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{M}}:W_{\mathcal{M}}\times\mathrm{SENT}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tr}}}
such that, for each
w\in W_{\mathcal{M}}
:

\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{M}}(w)=\mathcal{J}^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{ff}}(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}})(w)=\{\#\varphi\;|\;w,\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}\Vdash_{\mathrm{ff}}\varphi\}

This jump is monotonic, and hence will yield fixed-points, including a minimal one (see, e.g.,
(
Nicolai, 2018
, §4.2, §5)
).

So how does the approach outlined in the previous sections compare to the fixed-frame approach? Two features of the fixed-frame approach stand out. On the one hand, as mentioned, this approach is tied to a particular structure, with a fixed frame. Additionally, the fixed-frame approach is based on interpretation relations: it yields, as fixed-points, an interpretation relation for the frame of the model
\mathcal{M}
over which the construction is carried out. By contrast, our fixed-points are not tied to a particular structure, but rather, have been obtained by supervaluating over every possible structure. This picture is, arguably, more faithful to the spirit of classical supervaluationism, where one really aims to range over
all
models possessing some features. Moreover, the construction renders sets of sentences as fixed-points: this is, on the one hand, more in line with the classical case; and, on the other, seems advantageous when it comes to discerning ‘what is true’ intuitionistically, irrespective of the underlying structure. On top of that, but related to this last point, the sentence-based jump (as opposed to the interpretation-based fixed-frame jump) might allow for a better and more homogeneous philosophical reading of the truth construction propounded by Kripke. For instance, it is often claimed that the minimal fixed-point of Kripke’s constructions enjoys a privileged position.
7
7

7

See
Kremer (1988)
for an in-depth discussion. Note that this is not a claim made by Kripke in
Kripke (1975)
.
Our approach can preserve this idea: in starting with the empty set,
\varnothing
, one obtains a minimal fixed-point of
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{svi}}
standing for the least set of sentences that is supervaluationally true under intuitionistic logic. The fixed-frame approach, on the contrary, has no ‘overall’ least fixed-point. Instead, it requires that a frame be given in advance—only then can the least fixed-point be obtained, which is nonetheless only a least fixed-point relative to the frame.

At the same time, the fixed-frame approach enjoys a great advantage with respect to the construction we have introduced: transparent models for the truth predicate can be given for any frame whatsoever. Indeed, it can be checked that if

and formulae
\varphi
.

6
Compositional supervaluations

One salient feature of intuitionistic logic with respect to classical logic is the greater degree of compositionality it displays at the proof-theoretic level. Thus, Gentzen’s classic
(
1935
)
already showed that intuitionistic sequent calculus possessed the disjunction property, whereby a disjunction is provable if and only if at least one disjunct is. Likewise, the existence property---i.e., an existentially quantified formula is provable if and only if there is a witness for it---is a well-known feature of even weak arithmetical theories over intuitionistic logic.
8
8

8

See
Friedman (1975)
.
The question is whether this can be translated into the theory of truth.

Evidently, compositionality is a very desirable feature of truth theories. Our usual logical connectives are truth functional, and this should intuitively be captured by our theory of truth. Supervaluational theories over classical logic, however, tend to be at odds with compositionality, particularly with the compositionality of disjunctions and existentially quantified statements. Unlike the highly compositional truth theory obtained with the SK schema, the semantic constructions obtained via supervaluations over classical logic fail to deliver compositionality for those logical constants. Moreover, the same occurs with the axiomatic supervaluational theories over classical logic. But, given the connection between intuitionistic logic, and the disjunction and existence properties, one would hope that supervaluational theories of truth over intuitionistic logic might be able to deliver compositionality for said logical constants.

That these hopes are not mere chimeras shows in the fact that Leigh and Rathjen
(
2012
)
already established the consistency of an axiomatic theory—their theory H
i
—that looks very much like
\mathrm{ISV}
but includes compositional axioms for disjunction and the existential quantifier. Nonetheless, Leigh and Rathjen provided no supervaluational construction that matched their theory. Hence, the goal of this section is to develop what we call
compositional supervaluations
. These will be the basis of a Kripkean construction that will deliver a supervaluational theory of truth with compositional properties for disjunction and the existential quantifier, along those for conjunction and the universal quantifier. These compositional supervaluations are, however, rather peculiar. Their main peculiarity lies in that this supervaluational forcing relation is determined compositionally for all connectives except for the conditional, making the latter the only one with a clear supervaluational behavior. Nevertheless, as we shall prove, the final theory is fully supervaluational: the fixed-points of the theory can be obtained with a supervaluational jump in the style of section
3
.

Another key difference of this approach the forcing relation employed for the underlying models, which is inspired by—but different from—the forcing relation introduced by Došen to deal with non-hereditary Kripke models in
(
Došen, 1991
)
. This forcing relation takes a “global” approach to disjunction and the existential quantifier. Such a global approach is what allows for compositionality for these connectives, which are precisely the one for which compositionality tends to be at odds with in supervaluational theories. Let us first introduce the models over which we want our supervaluations to range, together with this new forcing relation.

6.1
Global forcing

In defining the new forcing relation, that we shall call
global
forcing, we want to make sure that it is a satisfactory one. Thus, in this section we will provide a completeness theorem for the global forcing relation with respect to the intuitionistic predicate calculus (although the proof will be given in the appendix). For this purpose, we need to abstract momentarily from the language
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tr}}
, and consider the landscape of first-order, intuitionistic languages.

Each such first-order, intuitionistic language is denoted by
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IPC}}
. Models for these languages are of the form
\mathcal{M}:=\langle W_{\mathcal{M}},\leq_{\mathcal{M}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{M}},\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}\rangle
.
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{M}}
is now allowed to assign any non-empty set to the domain of a world. The union of the range of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{M}}
—in plain words, the domain of the whole structure
\mathcal{M}
—is denoted by

.
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}
is a triple of relations
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{P}}
(assigning an interpretation to predicates),
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{C}}
(assigning an interpretation to constants), and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{F}}
(assigning an interpretation to functions). Finally, given a closed term

, we write

for the value of the term
t
under the structure
\mathcal{M}
and the world

. Thorough definitions of these points are left for the appendix, so as not to overcrowd this subsection.

A persistent first-order Kripke model for such an intuitionistic language is one where the interpretation of predicates, constants, and functions for the worlds, as well as the sets assigned to the domain of each world, is non-strictly increasing. This is also properly defined in the appendix. For the sake of simplicity, and as is often done, if
\mathcal{M}:=\langle W_{\mathcal{M}},\leq_{\mathcal{M}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{M}},\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{M}}\rangle

One can see that the changes with respect to the standard forcing relation concern

Theorem 3

.

\vdash_{\mathrm{IPC}}\varphi\Leftrightarrow\vDash_{\mathrm{G}}\varphi.

That is, the intuitionistic predicate calculus is (weakly) complete with respect to the class of persistent first-order Kripke models endowed with the global forcing relation. We leave the proof to the appendix. We also have the following strong soundness result, whose proof is also given in the appendix:

Corollary 2

(Strong soundness of G)
.

Let
\varphi

\Gamma

\mathcal{L}

\Gamma\vdash_{\mathrm{IPC}}\varphi\Rightarrow\,\forall\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\mathrm{G}}\Gamma\Rightarrow\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\mathrm{G}}\varphi)

The right-to-left direction of Corollary
2
, what is often called strong completeness, is not immediate. We conjecture that it is provable, but we could not establish it. Our proof of Theorem
3
is based on a similar result that Došen proved for his forcing relation in

, which builds on a translation with the modal logic S4. In fact, Došen established a strong completeness result. However, Došen’s result only covered the case of intuitionistic propositional logic. Extending it to the predicate case becomes difficult, as the translation into the modal language is no longer suitable. Similarly, we could have established a strong completeness result for the propositional fragment of intuitionistic logic with respect to the G-forcing relation, but fail to deliver the same for the predicate case.

One connective is not considered above: the conditional. Although full compositionality for it cannot be expected, one direction thereof (namely, the possibility of distributing truth over the conditional) does obtain. Conclusion

In this paper, we have introduced supervaluationist theories of truth over intuitionistic logic. To that effect, we have shown how to do supervaluations for intuitionistic logic and, in particular, how to do supervaluations over Kripke structures. In addition, we have paired the semantic theory thus constructed with an axiomatic theory that mimicked Cantini’s celebrated theory VF. As we mentioned in the introduction, a project like ours yields a potential formal theory of truth for the constructive mathematician, one who does not accept classical logic but still thinks that all intuitionistic validities are true. We have also compared the particular method we employ, based on interpretation embeddings and ranging over possibly greater structures, with the one that has often been used in the literature to produce theories of truth and or necessity with possible-world semantics.

Furthermore, we aimed to fix a common problem of supervaluational truth theories over classical logic: the failure of compositionality for disjunctions and existentially quantified sentences. We addressed this issue by presenting a semantic scheme that was compositionally delivered, but which, has we eventually showed, corresponds to the basic format of supervaluations over a different forcing relation. The outcome is a theory of truth that not only delivers transparent fixed-points, but that is at the same time intuitionistically supervaluational (thus recovering all validities of intuitionistic logic) and enjoys a greater degree of compositionality than the supervaluational theories over classical logic.
