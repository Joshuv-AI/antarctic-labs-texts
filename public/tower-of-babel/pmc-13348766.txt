Effects of percussive massage vs. foam rolling on hamstring function: a comparative study using shear wave elastography, tensiomyography, and MyotonPro
Authors: Josef Fischer, Josefina Manieu Seguel, Kristina Drole, Andreas Konrad, Markus Tilp, Armin H. Paravlic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study investigated the comparative acute effects of 3 × 30 s of vibration foam rolling (VFR) or percussive massage (PM) on the hamstring muscles, specifically range of motion (ROM), maximum voluntary isometric contraction (MVIC), and muscle stiffness, using shear wave elastography (SWE), tensiomyography (TMG), and the MyotonPro. Further, the correlation between the three stiffness measures was examined. MethodsSeventeen participants (27.1 ± 3.1 years; 7 females) were randomized to VFR or PM on two occasions. Muscle stiffness of the biceps femoris (BF) was assessed at baseline and immediately post-intervention with SWE, TMG, and the MyotonPro. A sit-and-reach test was conducted and MVIC of the hamstring muscles was measured unilaterally.ResultsA significant time effect was observed for MVIC torque (p = 0.01, ƞ² = 0.319). ROM and BF muscle stiffness were unaffected by either intervention. No significant associations were found between the stiffness measurements from the three techniques.ConclusionThe acute application of 3 × 30 s of VFR or PM did not enhance ROM or alter BF muscle stiffness. However, both interventions reduced MVIC torque, with no significant difference between them. The lack of correlation between the SWE, TMG, and MyotonPro measurements suggests that these techniques cannot be used or interpreted interchangeably.

Background

Optimizing muscle temperature and joint range of motion (ROM) is a cornerstone of pre-activity preparation in both clinical and athletic settings. Increased intramuscular temperature is associated with improved muscle contractility and reduced tissue viscosity, while sufficient ROM is critical for achieving sport-specific movement patterns and potentially mitigating injury risk [1, 2]. Besides typical warm-ups such as running, cycling, or stretching, other techniques designed to increase body temperature and joint ROM have recently been explored in sports practice and the scientific literature. These include, for example, percussive massage (PM) [3] and foam rolling (FR) [4, 5], which are often favored for their ability to improve flexibility without the performance deficits sometimes associated with prolonged static stretching [6].

Meta-analytical evidence indicates that acute FR significantly improves joint range of motion (ROM), with females potentially experiencing greater gains [5], and may slightly enhance sprint performance (+ 0.7%, Hedges’ g = 0.28 [-0.01, 0.57]) without impairing jump or strength capacity [7]. These benefits are likely mediated by reductions in muscle stiffness [8], decreased pain perception and thixotropic effects [9, 10]. Furthermore, the addition of vibration (VFR) appears to elicit greater ROM improvements compared to conventional FR [11]. Similarly, PM has gained popularity as a modality that can acutely increase ROM without negatively affecting muscle performance [12]. Proposed mechanisms include neuronal responses (e.g., muscle relaxation via tonic vibration reflex), vascular effects such as increased blood flow, and mechanical changes including reduced tissue tension and alter the connective tissues viscoelasticity [12]. Supporting these mechanisms, recent findings demonstrated that 120 s of PM applied to the plantar flexors increased stretch tolerance while decreasing muscle–tendon unit stiffness [13].

Besides the estimation of muscle-tendon unit stiffness, as performed by Nakamura et al. [13], localized muscle stiffness can be assessed with more recent techniques such as shear wave elastography (SWE), tensiomyography (TMG), and myotonic devices (MyotonPro). Although several studies have published the results of various interventions using these three techniques (SWE, TMG, MyotonPro) [14, 15], the inter-device reliability and the differences in measurement accuracy/sensitivity have rarely been considered. To date, a few studies have investigated this issue by comparing the results obtained from two of the afore-mentioned techniques; however, the results have been conflicting [14, 15]. For example, Alfuraih and colleagues [14] aimed to investigate the association between muscle stiffness of the biceps femoris (BF) and vastus lateralis (VL) using SWE and TMG. The authors did not find any association between the SWE- and TMG-derived variables, regardless of the muscle assessed. Similarly, no significant associations were found between the TMG- and Myoton-derived parameters of the rectus femoris and VL in older adults, suggesting that each technique could be evaluating different biomechanical properties of skeletal muscle. In contrast, Lee and colleagues [16] found moderate to good correlation between the muscle stiffness measured by SWE and myotonometry, both at rest and during voluntary contraction in healthy young adults. Due to the high methodological differences in the afore-mentioned studies, considering the populations investigated and the techniques compared, it is difficult to draw a firm conclusion about their association. With the increased adoption of these techniques for clinical purposes, we believe that comparing all three techniques within the same research setting could enhance our understanding of their interrelationship.

Although the effects of VFR and PM seem to be similar, to the best knowledge of the authors, to date, no study has compared the acute effects on muscle function (i.e., ROM, muscle performance) as well as muscle stiffness (assessed with various devices) of both modalities. In professional sports where ‘every minute counts’ and warm-up windows are narrow, it is vital to identify which modality provides the superior outcome for a given time investment. If one method proves more effective than the other within a short duration, it should be prioritized in elite performance settings. Furthermore, most studies have applied intervention durations of ≥ 120 s [11, 12]. Hence, a more practical approach with < 120 s is missing in the literature.

Thus, the purpose of this study was to investigate the acute effects of 3 × 30 s VFR or PM on the hamstring muscles on ROM, maximum voluntary isometric contraction (MVIC), as well as muscle stiffness, assessed with SWE, TMG, and a MyotonPro device in a randomized cross-over design. A secondary aim of this study was to examine the interrelationship between muscle stiffness measurements obtained using these three techniques.

We hypothesized that both interventions would lead to an increase in ROM but have no effect on MVIC. Furthermore, we hypothesized that the increases in ROM would be related to the decreases in muscle stiffness, which would be in agreement between the three techniques (SWE, TMG, and MyotonPro).

Methods

Study design

Each participant completed two laboratory sessions, at least 48 h apart, for both interventions (PM or VFR), randomized by drawing cards. Muscle stiffness of the BF long head (BFlh) was assessed immediately before (pre) and within 5 min after the intervention (post) using the three techniques (SWE, MyotonPro, TMG) in a random order on the dominant leg. In addition, a sit-and-reach test to assess posterior chain flexibility (ROM) and MVIC measurements of the hamstring muscles were conducted to assess changes in muscle function. To prevent any transient alterations in muscle mechanical properties caused by maximal contractions, a strict testing order was maintained. Muscle stiffness of the BFlh was assessed first, followed by the sit-and-reach test for flexibility and finally the MVIC measurements of the hamstring muscles. The short form of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) was used to assess participants’ activity levels. To ensure maximum consistency and eliminate inter-rater variability, the pre- and post-intervention assessments for each specific measurement technique were always performed by the same investigator. Due to the nature of the interventions, participants and the primary investigator could not be blinded to the treatment allocation; however, the data analysis was performed on anonymized datasets to reduce bias.

Participants

We calculated the necessary group size based on previous data from our lab (i.e., effect of VFR on the hamstring muscles on ROM) [17] with a large effect size (f = 0.4), an alpha = 0.05, and a power = 0.8. The calculation for a two-way repeated measures ANOVA led to a necessary group size of 16 participants We recruited 17 physically active male (10) and female (7) participants (age: 27.1 ± 3.1 years; body mass: 71.6 ± 16.0 kg; height: 175 ± 8.5 cm). Participants had no injuries at the lower extremities and were informed about the test procedure, benefits, and risks before they signed a written informed consent form. Ethical approval was obtained by the Ethics Committee (approval code redacted for anonymity), and the study conformed to the standards of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Procedures

Muscle shear modulus

An ultrasound scanner (Aixplorer V12.3, Supersonic Imaging; Aix-en-Provence, France) with a linear transducer array (4–15 MHz, SuperLinear 10 − 2; Vermon, Tours, France) was used to measure muscle shear modulus of the BFlh in SWE mode (smoothing level 5, persistence off, scale 0–450 kPa). The participant lay prone on a physiotherapy bed at rest, with a knee angle set at 5° of flexion, secured by a foam pad. Probe placement was standardized using a tattoo marker. The probe was placed on the muscle at 50% on the line between the lateral epicondyle of the tibia and the ischial tuberosity [18]. The range of interest (ROI) was set on the muscle tissue at maximal size, excluding any aponeurosis, with the probe aligned with the muscle fascicles throughout the measurement. Three 10-s videos were recorded for each participant. First, a custom MATLAB script was used to extract the shear modulus values for all frames across the 10-s video. Subsequently, a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet was utilized to automatically identify the three consecutive frames with the lowest standard deviation (SD) of the shear modulus. The mean of these three frames was then used for final analysis. All ultrasound imaging procedures were performed by the same investigator (J.F.), who has two years of specialized experience in conducting musculoskeletal ultrasound and elastography assessments in a university laboratory setting.

MyotonPro

A MyotonPro device (Myoton Ltd., Estonia) was used to assess the muscle stiffness of the BFlh. Similarly, as for the SWE assessment, the participant was asked to remain in a resting position, lying prone, with a knee angle of 5° of flexion. The center of the ultrasound probe placement for the SWE was used to assess the BFlh muscle stiffness. For the stiffness assessment, the probe of the MyotonPro device was applied perpendicular to the tissue, as suggested by the manufacturer. Five consecutive mechanical impacts of 15 ms and a force of 0.3–0.4 N were applied from the MyotonPro device. This was then repeated three times. The average value for the 3 × 3 mechanical impacts was taken for the statistical analysis [19].

Tensiomyography

The BF contractile property assessment was also performed while prone at rest at a knee angle set at 5° knee flexion. A well-established methodology was used, as previously described in [20, 21]. The sensor was set perpendicular to the skin normal plane above the muscle belly at the same assessment spot as for the SWE and MyotonPro device. A rounded (5-cm diameter) self-adhesive cathode and anode (Axelgaard; Aarhus, Denmark) were set 5 cm distally and 5 cm proximally to the measuring point, on all muscles assessed. Rest periods of 30 s were given between each stimulus, in order to minimize the effects of fatigue and potentiation. An average value of the two maximal stimuli was used for further analysis.

Sit-and-reach performance (ROM)

The sit-and-reach range of motion of the treated leg was assessed using a back saver sit-and-reach test with a Sit-and-Reach Trunk Flexibility box (Fabrication Enterprises Baseline Model 12-1086; New York, USA). The test was conducted as described in previous work, and only for the treated leg [22]. The test was performed three times, with a 15-s break in between the trials for each leg. The average of the three trials for each leg was taken for further analysis. While this test is commonly used to evaluate hamstring extensibility, it is important to acknowledge that sit-and-reach performance is also inherently influenced by lumbar extensibility and pelvic mobility [23].

Maximum Voluntary Isometric Contraction (MVIC) peak torque

MVIC knee flexor peak torque measurements were conducted using a computerized dynamometer / testing device equipped with force sensors (Compass 540; Proxomed, Alzenau, Germany), with the hip and knee angles set at 80° and 60°, respectively [24]. The alignment of the knee joint axis and machine was visually inspected, and the foot position was recorded for consistency. The trunk and thigh were secured with straps and a pad to prevent movement. Each participant completed three 5-s MVIC trials with 1-min rest intervals, and the highest torque value was used for further analysis.

Warm-up

Both sessions began with a 5 min warm-up on a stationary bike at 60 rpm and 1 W/kg bodyweight to ensure a standardized relative intensity across all participants [25]. This standardized warm-up was implemented to ensure that all participants entered the testing phase in a similar physiological state. By elevating intramuscular temperature and reducing initial tissue viscosity (thixotropic effects), we aimed to minimize the influence of varying pre-laboratory physical activity on the baseline stiffness measurements [1, 2].

Foam rolling intervention

A foam roller with additional vibration (Blackroll Standard foam roller in combination with a Blackroll Booster Set; Bottighofen, Switzerland) was used with a vibration intensity of 32 Hz [26]. The rolling duration was 3 × 30 s, with a rolling frequency of 15 repetitions per minute (2 s from distal to proximal and 2 s from proximal to distal) [27], applied on the dominant leg only. A break of 30s was provided between each set. Rolling start was proximal to the knee, and the turning point was close to the ischial tuberosity. The participant rolled with their body weight and was asked to add as much pressure as possible (i.e., to the initial point of discomfort) on the middle part of the thigh while moving linearly forward and backward. A metronome provided auditory signals to pace the movement.

Percussive massage intervention

For the PM treatment, the subject was positioned prone on a therapy couch. The PM treatment was applied by the same investigator using a Hypervolt device (Hyperice; California, US). This device provides percussions at 30 Hz with a soft attachment head. PM was applied to the BF of the dominant leg for 1.5 min (three sets of 30 s, with 30-s breaks). The massage started at the lateral popliteal fossa and moved toward the ischial tuberosity, with a cadence of 2 s in each direction. The investigator aimed to maintain consistent pressure (7/10 on the visual analog scale, where 0 indicates no pain/pressure and 10 indicates the maximum tolerable pain/pressure, as reported by the participant.) This ‘strong but tolerable’ intensity was selected to ensure a sufficient mechanical stimulus to the deep tissues while avoiding involuntary muscle guarding, which would confound the subsequent stiffness assessments.

Statistical analyses

SPSS (version 26.0, SPSS Inc.; Chicago, Illinois) and JASP (version 0.18.3.0, JASP; University of Amsterdam) were used for the statistical analyses. All statistical analyses were conducted at the participant level (N = 17), utilizing only the data from the treated (dominant) limb for each individual.

Muscle stiffnesses of the BFlh, hip flexion ROM, and MVIC knee flexor peak torque were the tested variables. A Shapiro-Wilk test was used to verify the normal distribution of the data (muscle stiffnesses of the BFlh with the MyotonPro (p = 0.564–0.933), the SWE (p = 0.074–0.703), and the TMG (p = 0.048–0.888), the hip flexion ROM (p = 0.510–0.949), and the MVIC knee flexor peak torque (p = 0.243–0.549). Although the Shapiro-Wilk test indicated a marginal deviation from normality for one TMG measurement (p = 0.048), visual inspection of the histograms and Q-Q plots revealed no severe skewness or extreme outliers. Therefore, given the robustness of parametric tests to minor normality violations, we proceeded with a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA [factors: time (pre vs. post) and intervention (VFR vs. PM)]. When significant effects were observed, post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using paired t-tests.

To check the baseline conditions (pre-values) of all parameters in both interventions (VFR and PM) for similarity, paired t-tests were performed.

For the inter-day reliability, the relative reliability for all the dependent variables was estimated using the intra-class correlation coefficients (ICCs), two-way random model (consistency type for inter-day and intra-day assessments, whilst the absolute agreement type was adopted for inter-rater assessment). ICC values were considered as very high if > 0.90, high if between 0.70 and 0.89, and moderate if between 0.50 and 0.69 [28]. Furthermore, the standard error of measurement (SEM) and the coefficient of variation (CV) were calculated as measures of absolute reliability, which indicates within-subject variation, as previously suggested in the literature [29]. To further address the data reliability issues, the bias and random error were calculated, followed by the minimal detectable change (MDC) calculation, which is a measure of the minimal amount of change unrelated to variations of the assessment that can be considered as clinically relevant. Moreover, Bland-Altman plots with 95% limits of agreement were plotted to evaluate the agreement between the inter-day assessments [30].

Furthermore, to investigate the associations between three different methods of muscle stiffness measurements (SWE, MYO and TMG), a Pearson’s correlation (r) analysis was performed using baseline data. Additionally, to evaluate the relationships between the intervention effects, change scores (post-intervention – pre-intervention) were calculated for the stiffness measurements and a subsequent Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed on these values. The following thresholds of the correlation coefficient were used to assess the magnitude of the relationships analyzed: weak: ≤ 0.35; moderate: 0.36–0.67; and high: ≥ 0.68 [31]. In case data was not normally distributed, Spearman correlation was applied. The alpha level was set to 0.05 for all the analyses. No adjustments for multiple comparisons were applied to the correlation analyses. While this approach inherently increases the risk of Type I error, it did not impact the interpretation of the current data, as no significant correlations were observed.

Reliability analysis

The results of the inter-day reliability analysis are provided in Table 4. Between-day differences and Bland-Altman plots are presented in Fig. 1. The reliability analysis shows a high to very high average ICC (relative reliability score) for all the dependent variables, ranging from 0.792 to 0.979 for SWE and average ROM, respectively. Measures of absolute variability, presented as CV%, range from 4.02% (ROM max) to 18.0% (SWE). Except for SWE (p = 0.049), there is no significant difference between the pre-values of the two measurement days for muscle stiffness. In addition, the Bland-Altman plots show that the variations in the differences between the two trials fall within the limits of agreement for all the measures of muscle stiffness (Fig. 1. B, D and F; LOA 95% = −4.722 to 7.829, − 24.95 to 19.79, and − 2.699 to 2.385 for SWE, MyotonPro, and TMG, respectively).

Discussion

The study aimed to examine the acute effects of a 3 × 30 s of VFR or PM on hip flexion ROM, MVIC of the hamstrings, and muscle stiffness of the BF muscle, assessed using the SWE, TMG, and MyotonPro techniques in a randomized cross-over design. On the one hand, we found significant acute effects of VFR and PM on MVIC torque, which was reduced from pre- to post-values. On the other hand, we found no acute effects of either VFR or PM on ROM or muscle stiffness of the hamstring muscles. In addition, we found no significant associations between the BF muscle stiffness measurements taken with the three different techniques, both at baseline (pre) and for changes from pre- to post-intervention. Finally, except for muscle stiffness measured by SWE, all the other measures of interest were found to be reliable.

Our study yielded results that are similar to a previous study by Nakamura et al. [32], which examined the effects of different FR (with no vibration effect) durations on muscle stiffness measured by SWE. Similar to our results, Nakamura and colleagues found no changes in the stiffness measurements from pre- to post-testing, despite using intervention times totaling 300 s. Similarly, Reiner et al. [33] compared pre- and post-stiffness values of the pectoralis major muscle using SWE, with a FR intervention lasting two minutes, and also observed no significant differences in stiffness values. However, another study [34] showed a significant reduction in muscle stiffness from pre- to post-testing when measured with a MyotonPro device, although without any significant difference with TMG measurements [34].

In addition, another study [4] measuring the different muscles of the quadriceps femoris with SWE found a reduction in muscle stiffness from pre- to post-testing with both regular FR and VFR. This reduction was only observed in the rectus femoris, and not in the VL or vastus medialis. Furthermore, Skinner et al. [35] investigated the influence of PM on hamstring stiffness using a MyotonPro device and observed a reduction in stiffness from pre- to post-measurement. Notably, this study analyzed both limbs of the participants to increase their data pool, which may have potentially enhanced the magnitude of the observed effect, due to contralateral mechanisms. All the studies that reported a significant reduction in stiffness with FR or PM had longer treatment durations than applied in the present study, lasting between 120 and 360 s (Table 5). While this difference in intervention duration might partly explain why we did not observe a stiffness reduction, attributing the lack of effect solely to time is speculative. The absence of significant changes is likely multi-factorial. For instance, the mechanical load applied during the treatments may have been insufficient to substantially alter the deeper tissue properties. Additionally, potential measurement site standardization errors, especially given the anatomical complexity of the biceps femoris, could have masked localized treatment effects. While a longer intervention duration might increase local tissue perfusion and alter stiffness, as suggested by the dose-dependency reported by Schroeder et al. [34], also device sensitivity limitations must be acknowledged. It can be speculated that any observed difference, based on the findings of the aforementioned studies, would likely be small, as they failed to exceed the MDC threshold [34].

Our study also found no significant correlation between the stiffness measurements with MyotonPro, SWE, and TMG. This aligns with a study on the VL, which compared the inter- and intra-day reliability and correlation of the SWE, TMG, MyotonPro, and strain elastography techniques [37]. This study only found a significant correlation between the strain elastography and SWE values, but, as in the results of this study, no correlation was observed between the TMG, SWE, and MyotonPro measurements. Rather than mere measurement disagreement, this lack of correlation highlights that these devices are likely measuring fundamentally different biomechanical properties, despite all falling under the umbrella term of ‘muscle stiffness.’ Stiffness is a complex, multi-dimensional characteristic. Each of these devices operationalizes it through completely distinct physical mechanisms: SWE uses acoustic radiation force to generate shear waves and measures their propagation speed, from which tissue shear modulus is estimated, MyotonPro assesses transverse mechanical compliance through damped surface oscillation, and TMG evaluates radial displacement following electrically evoked contraction. Because each technique relies on a different physiological and mechanical premise, it is plausible that their metrics do not strongly correlate. Therefore, these devices should not be viewed as interchangeable tools measuring the exact same phenomenon, but rather as complementary instruments capturing different facets of the muscle’s mechanical profile.

The study of Bravo-Sánchez et al. [37] confirmed moderate to high ICC values for the three measurement techniques used in the current study. While the MyotonPro and TMG techniques show overall very high reliability, the SWE ICC values for the inter- and intra-day reliability in the afore-mentioned study ranged from ICC = 0.62 to 0.80. The fundamental technological aspect of SWE assessment enables the measurement of muscle stiffness that is less likely influenced by the subcutaneous fat layer, which might be the case with Myoton and TMG [38]. However, its complex procedure makes it more challenging to maintain reliability. This variability could be due to factors such as the experience of the examiner with the technique, changes in pressure applied by the tester, movement artifacts, and probe positioning.

Regarding MVIC values, our study demonstrated a significant time effect for torque values, with a slight decrease from pre- to post-testing. Mean torque values decreased by an average of 3.6 Nm, and peak torque values decreased by an average of 4.6 Nm. Similar results were reported in a literature review by Behm et al. [27], showing that FR durations of 5 s to 60 s had either a negative trivial or small effect. Regarding the PM treatment, one study showed no effect on MVIC pre- to post-testing, with a total treatment time of 5 min [3]. The reason for the torque decreases observed in our study might have been due to the relatively high pressure applied by the investigator during the PM treatment (7 out of 10 on the visual analog scale). This relatively high pressure might have felt uncomfortable, especially as participants did not apply the treatment themselves.

Based on Behm et al. [27], we expected that our protocol would increase ROM, especially in the VFR protocol, as their study predicted large effect sizes with three rolling sets and 30-s rolling durations. However, we did not find any significant changes in ROM for either the VFR or PM treatments. This was also reported by Reiner et al. [33], who foam rolled the pectoralis major muscle for 2 min, but did not see an increase in ROM. One reason for the lack of ROM increases after the PM and VFR treatments might have been the 5-min warm-up performed shortly before the ROM testing. As one proposed mechanism for acute ROM increases after dynamic stretching or FR is a thixotropic effect [9], this warm-up might have overshadowed the effects of both interventions.

During our study, we encountered some limitations that could play a role in explaining our results. Firstly, measuring muscle stiffness in men and women in the same group could be a factor that influenced the results. Lee et al. [16] reported significant differences in muscle stiffness of the BF measured with the SWE and MyotonPro techniques at resting position between men and women. Furthermore, we did not control for the menstrual cycle phase of our female participants across the separate testing days. Because hormonal fluctuations throughout the menstrual cycle potentially affect muscle stiffness, flexibility and force production [39–42], variations in hormonal status between the two laboratory visits could have introduced additional within-subject variability in these neuromuscular measurements. This variability might have influenced the reliability of the assessments or masked subtle intervention-induced changes within our female cohort.

A second limitation was the different anatomical variations that are present in the hamstring muscle complex, especially within the BF. Pennation angles in the BFlh are smaller on the distal part (knee insertion) and larger on the proximal and middle parts [43], which gives it a higher capacity to generate force. Hence, the local stiffness might not represent the overall stiffness of the muscle-tendon unit. We encountered difficulties assessing the BFlh with SWE because we standardized the test location for every participant, without considering the possibility of different attachment sites along the femur for the short head of the BF [43]. An additional limitation is the testing position utilized for the muscle stiffness assessments. Measurements were taken with the hamstring in a shortened, resting position to ensure a purely passive state and to provide a highly reproducible posture for immediate post-intervention testing. However, assessing the muscle in this near-slack state may have reduced the sensitivity of the SWE, TMG, and MyotonPro devices to detect subtle intervention-induced changes or correlations between the measurement techniques. Future studies should consider assessing muscle stiffness at longer muscle lengths (i.e., under passive stretch), where changes in tissue mechanical properties and inter-device correlations might be more pronounced. A further consideration is the difference in data processing and averaging strategies across the three stiffness measurement techniques. For each device, we followed specific protocols (e.g., frame selection based on SD for SWE [44], triple-pulse averaging for MyotonPro [45], and maximal response for TMG [28]) to optimize the accuracy of each individual measure. While this approach ensures high data quality, the use of different data processing procedures may have introduced methodological variance that could influence the observed correlations between the devices. Also, based on the reliability metrics (i.e., MDCs and CVs) observed in this study, detecting very small magnitude of changes can be challenging for SWE and TMG. Consequently, it is possible that subtle physiological alterations fell within the range of normal measurement variability. Therefore, our non-significant findings should be interpreted with this context in mind, and future comparative studies might benefit from larger intervention doses to elicit more pronounced and easily detectable changes. While we reported inter-day reliability to establish the general consistency of our baseline measurements, our study design did not include a separate control session to assess pure intra-day reliability. Because our primary aim was to detect acute, within-session changes, intra-day reliability metrics would have more precisely reflected the specific measurement sensitivity of the devices under these exact time constraints. Finally, ROM was assessed using the sit-and-reach test. Because performance in this test is influenced not only by hamstring extensibility but also by spinal and pelvic mobility, any potential localized changes in hamstring flexibility following the interventions might have been masked by the stiffness of these adjacent structures.

Conclusions

Our data suggest that the acute application of 3 × 30 s of VFR or PM is not a sufficient stimulus to increase hamstring ROM or alter BF muscle stiffness. Therefore, we believe that longer application periods may be necessary to improve ROM and BF muscle stiffness. Regarding muscle performance, a slight reduction in MVIC peak torque was observed from pre- to post-testing. However, this decline should be interpreted cautiously, as it emerged purely as a main effect of time and was not intervention-specific. This indicates that the reduction in force occurred irrespective of the specific treatment applied (PM or VFR), and may reflect general fatigue from the testing protocol itself rather than a negative consequence of the modalities. Furthermore, we observed non-significant associations between the SWE, TMG, and MyotonPro measurements of muscle stiffness. However, because our short intervention protocol induced only minimal changes in stiffness, the range of values may have been too narrow to robustly detect potential correlations. While our findings suggest that these techniques may assess different biomechanical muscle properties and should be used interchangeably with caution, future studies employing longer interventions are needed to definitively establish their interrelationships.
