Applications of artificial intelligence in mechanical engineering for the field of upper limb exoskeletons
Authors: Izabela Rojek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development, selection, and adaptation of assistive technologies such as exoskeletons to assist people with disabilities is associated with a complex decision-making process due to the uncertainty of evaluation criteria. Traditional decision-making methods in this area often fail to address these complex challenges, leading to inefficiencies in the preparation and implementation of the exoskeleton production process and, consequently, reduced product quality. To overcome these challenges, this article proposes an artificial intelligence (AI)-based decision support approach for the development of upper limb exoskeletons. This approach reduces costs, improves production quality, and accelerates exoskeleton design and production, with accuracy reaching 100 per cent using a multilayer perceptron, enabling more accurate and realistic results. The article presents new models supporting the classification of hand dysfunctions, exoskeleton design (indicating the position of exoskeleton actuators, the number of actuators required, and the maximum grip force of the exoskeleton), and estimating manufacturing costs. This shows how to optimize AI-assisted technologies in the form of exoskeletons in support systems for people with disabilities.

Introduction

An exoskeleton (external skeleton) is a device worn on the body or part of the body that assists the user in performing movements passively (facilitating movement by partially alleviating weight and supporting movement with rubber bands, springs, etc.) or actively (facilitating movement with servo mechanisms, hydraulic or pneumatic actuators, etc.). Attempts to develop exoskeletons have been made for a long time. The dynamic development of exoskeletons began in the late 20th and early 21 st centuries with projects funded by the US Department of Defense’s Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) aimed at developing exoskeletons for national security and defence purposes, and the development of medical exoskeletons1,2. 3D-printed exoskeletons constitute a distinct and easily customisable group of medical products3–6. 3D printing is the basis for mass customisation in various industries, and its particularly groundbreaking applications can be found in healthcare, among other areas. An additional value is the integration of technologies such as 3D scanning, reverse engineering and artificial intelligence (AI), which supports not only design, but also optimise material selection and waste minimisation5. Despite such extensive research, there is a significant gap between scientific research, engineering and use. It is therefore necessary to bridge this research gap while maintaining established methodological requirements and therapeutic flexibility appropriate for medical devices. In order to enable large-scale industrial production of personalised exoskeletons, it is necessary to develop new business models. The increasingly widespread use of environmentally friendly 3D printing in everyday medical practice also requires the preparation of interdisciplinary teams – involving medical and technical specialists – and organisational changes at the systemic level in order to respond as effectively as possible to the needs of users and clinicians2. This article is original research, and the description of previous studies provides a framework for expanding the research to include the subsequent models presented in the article. The novelty and contribution of this article, and the goal of this research, was to develop models that expand the possibilities of exoskeleton design: 1. personalizing the hand exoskeleton by classifying hand dysfunctions even before the exoskeleton is designed, 2. the creation of the hand exoskeleton in the control section, specifically the selection of the location of the actuators in the exoskeleton, the determination of the number of actuators, and the determination of the maximum gripping force of the exoskeleton, 3. estimating the costs of creating the elements of the hand exoskeleton. Methods and evaluation (data origin, preprocessing, data partitioning, model settings, and uncertainty) are described for each model.

Motivation and contribution

Motivation

Decision support in the area of designing and controlling personalised exoskeletons using artificial intelligence (AI) and mechanical engineering (ME), including additive manufacturing technology, has been growing rapidly in recent years. This is of great importance in the context of Industry 4.0 and 5.07. The combination of artificial intelligence and additive manufacturing opens up new possibilities in the field of production. This can be seen in many publications describing the application of various AI methods, including machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) methods8–11.

Contribution

The primary technical goal of AI models in a hand exoskeleton is to adaptively control movement assistance based on real-world user signals (e.g., kinematics, forces, EMG), so that the timing and level of assistance are precisely matched to the current intention and capabilities of the hand. The novelty of these models lies not in the use of AI alone, but in continuous learning at the individual user level, instead of applying rigid, predefined control profiles. AI models enable real-time differentiation between active, assisted, and passive movement, a feat difficult to achieve with classical algorithms. Furthermore, they integrate multiple uncertain data sources into a single decision model, increasing the stability and safety of human-exoskeleton interactions. This avoids general assumptions about the ”average user,” and allows the system to respond to the specific, measurable characteristics and needs of a given individual. This article discusses the role and means of AI-support and examines AI techniques for the developed problems of selecting exoskeleton parameters. In order to apply the presented approach, the article discussed an illustrative example of the upper limb exoskeleton developed using AI for the needs of support systems for people with disabilities. The article compared my research and publications with earlier existing concepts to examine the advantages of the proposed solution. In the summary section, The article discussed the developed solution in general terms to facilitate its replication on other data and future directions for the development of the presented approach. The novelty of AI in hand exoskeletons lies in the combination of embedding intelligence directly into the assistive device and embedding it in external decision support or recommendation systems. Unlike existing AI-based decision support systems, which analyze user data and suggest interventions offline or semi-automatically, AI-driven hand exoskeletons measure, interpret, and actuate in real time in a closed loop. This enables continuous adaptation to the user’s intentions, muscle activity, and task context at the millisecond level. Therefore, AI models are responsible not only for decision-making but also for translating intentions into precise, safe, and personalized mechanical assistance. Furthermore, learning occurs during daily use, allowing the exoskeleton to evolve as the user recovers or their functional abilities change. This tight integration of AI cognitive functions with embodied actuation represents a fundamental advancement that goes beyond advisory or supervisory AI systems in assistive technologies.

Literature review

Previous studies presented in the literature show a significant increase in solutions related to upper limb exoskeletons. The article focuses on analysing the literature in terms of this type of exoskeleton and the issues addressed by the authors of the publications. An important issue raised in article12 is the problem of learning correct upper limb movements. Participants learned the trajectory of elbow joint movement using an exoskeleton. After training, improvements were observed in both movement reproduction and proprioception precision. The results suggest that passive training promotes the creation of movement representation in the joint space and improves deep sensation through its intensive use. Another important study from the perspective of rehabilitation was the implementation of a mirror therapy system using virtual reality and an exoskeleton for the rehabilitation of hand motor disorders13. Hand motor disorders are one of the leading causes of disability worldwide. Rehabilitation therapies, such as mirror therapy, play a key role in the process of returning to fitness. In traditional therapy, the patient moves their healthy hand in front of a mirror, and the reflection of the movement makes the brain ‘think’ that the disabled hand is also moving, which stimulates its regeneration. In rehabilitation using exoskeletons, it is often necessary to develop individual machine learning models that recognise the patient’s movement intentions based on EEG (electroencephalogram) signals14. The main problem with this approach is the long calibration time required to train the model. Therefore, an approach based on transfer learning, which eliminates the need for a separate calibration session, has been proposed. This approach has been tested on healthy volunteers and is planned for future use in rehabilitation therapy, where the movement intentions of a patient’s paralysed limb will be read from EEG signals during bilateral arm movements mapped by an exoskeleton from the healthy arm to the affected arm. In recent years, upper limb exoskeletons (OEs) have been developed to reduce the risk of work-related musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) in industrial workers15. Although previous studies have shown the effectiveness of OEs in reducing shoulder muscle activity during static work and with raised arms, there has been a lack of research evaluating the impact of these devices on muscle co-activation during repetitive arm movements. Therefore, article16 analysed the effect of passive exoskeleton anti-gravity support on shoulder muscle co-activation during repetitive arm movements. The study showed that a passive upper limb exoskeleton can effectively reduce shoulder muscle co-activation, which could form the basis of a new methodology for evaluating and optimising such devices in the workplace. Another important study was one involving the classification of five typical human upper limb movement states based on surface electromyography (sEMG) signals to support the real-time control system of an upper limb assistive exoskeleton16. The authors proposed a deep learning model combining convolutional neural networks (CNN), bidirectional LSTM networks (BiLSTM) and an attention mechanism, which allows for increased accuracy in recognising movement states in complex conditions. The results highlight the model’s potential in motion state classification, supporting the development of intelligent and advanced control of upper limb exoskeletons. Another method of exoskeleton control was presented by the authors of article17. They presented a method of adaptive tracking control with a predefined time (PT) for non-linear pure-feedback systems, with a particular emphasis on its practical application in robotic exoskeleton technology. The simulation results confirmed the effectiveness of the proposed control method, demonstrating its potential in controlling robotic exoskeletons while taking into account state constraints, which proves its usefulness in real-world applications. Furthermore, a review of the literature on hand exoskeletons revealed that there are very few such solutions available and that they have limited functionality. The published information showed that all observed studies in the field of five-finger (so-called all-grip)) hand exoskeletons ended at the early stages of prototyping (technology readiness level [TRL] <7). Currently, there is no hand exoskeleton on the medical technology market or in use that is ready for commercialisation and production, even within the framework of mass customisation. This is now possible within the paradigms of Industry 4.0 and eHealth, including the use of 3D printing18. It is expected that the hand exoskeleton will improve the results of acute therapies (e.g. in the recovery process) or long-term deficits in hand motor function. In this area, the proposed hand exoskeletons are non-invasive human-machine interfaces, tailored not only to specific user populations (people that have suffered strokes, brain injuries or amputations or that have congenital defects), but also solutions that are easy to personalise. The therapeutic use of the above group of solutions 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, in the user’s natural environment (at home, at work, etc.) instead of traditional therapy and movement support sessions (as part of hospital or outpatient treatment) can not only improve movement learning and repetition generation, but also accelerate the adaptation of the exoskeleton to the user thanks to artificial intelligence algorithms combined with faster electronic systems and sets of calibrated sensors.

Development of a hand exoskeleton and elbow exoskeleton

A review of the literature also showed that one of the biggest challenges in developing exoskeletons is designing a functional, personalised exoskeleton for the hand that primarily supports the fingers and their manipulative capabilities. The human hand is considered to be one of the most precise and versatile tools available to humans. For the reasons mentioned above, any deficit in this area reduces health-related quality of life and is the subject of research in both rehabilitation and physiotherapy as well as biomechanics. The study of the hand exoskeleton highlights the uncertainty associated with decision-making, as user behavior, condition, and human-device interaction change over time and cannot be precisely predicted. Uncertainty pertains to functional criteria, such as improved range of motion, grip precision, or fatigue reduction, which may vary between users and therapy sessions. Biomechanical criteria encompass variability in forces, torques, and kinematic alignment, which impact the safety and effectiveness of the assistive device. Economic criteria, such as the cost of implementation, maintenance, and scalability of the solution, are also considered, which are subject to market and organizational uncertainty. Regulatory criteria introduce additional uncertainty related to certification requirements, legal liability, and compliance with medical standards. Therefore, the study clearly defines mathematical methods for modeling uncertainty, such as probabilistic approaches, fuzzy logic, Bayesian estimation, or adaptive control algorithms, to ensure that the system’s decisions are transparent and justifiable.

This study was intentionally designed as a pilot, proof-of-concept study of AI methods applied to upper limb exoskeletons. The use of a small sample size (n = 10) is consistent with the exploratory nature of early-stage engineering research, where the primary goal is to assess technical feasibility, algorithm performance, and system integration rather than establish statistically generalizable or clinically validated results. In the context of AI-based exoskeletons, pilot studies with limited samples are commonly used to:Validate sensor and control architectures;Evaluate initial model behavior under controlled conditions;Identify sources of variability that will form the basis for subsequent large-scale studies.

The current sample size is therefore sufficient to support initial hypothesis generation, parameter tuning, and identification of methodological limitations, while minimizing unnecessary exposure of participants early in system development. Due to the small sample size and controlled experimental conditions, the results of this study have limited external validity. The results should not be interpreted as representative of broader populations, including individuals with neurological or musculoskeletal disorders, nor should they be extrapolated to real-world clinical or rehabilitation settings. Inter-individual variability, long-term adaptation, and user-specific biomechanical differences were not fully accounted for in the current study design. Furthermore, this work does not evaluate clinical effectiveness, safety, or therapeutic outcomes. The AI models and system configurations evaluated in this paper remain experimental. Any observed performance gains reflect system-level feasibility under constraints, not readiness for clinical application. The results of this pilot study are intended to inform future studies involving larger, more diverse cohorts, longer evaluation periods, and collaboration with clinical experts. Such studies will be essential to assess generalizability, robustness, and practical utility before making any claims regarding clinical significance or rehabilitation effectiveness. Selection of participants

Exoskeleton testers were recruited using convenience sampling. Ten testers with hand dysfunction volunteered for the study. All met the inclusion criteria, ten were qualified for testing, and none discontinued testing. Participants were either of working or post-working age. The age range was 19 to 78. The study included n=10 participants who met the defined inclusion criteria, including a specific range of hand dexterity, the absence of medical contraindications, and consent to participate in the experiment. Exclusion criteria related to pain, acute injuries, or neurological conditions were also taken into account. Participants performed a set of standardized functional tasks, such as cylindrical and pincer grips, as well as finger flexion and extension sequences, reflecting everyday manual activities. Data acquisition and preprocessing

The full ML process (pipeline) began with a formal definition of the modeling objective and input and output functions, including sensory signals and variables describing the user’s state. During the feature definition stage, temporal and statistical representations of the signals were defined to preserve information relevant to the dynamics of hand movement. Data preprocessing included noise filtration, amplitude normalization, and synchronization of signals from different sensors. Additionally, data were segmented into time windows corresponding to the motor tasks being performed. Missing data were identified based on signal discontinuities or acquisition errors and then handled through statistical imputation or the rejection of fragments that did not meet quality criteria. To reduce the impact of inter-subject variability, feature standardization was performed within each participant. The dataset was divided into training, validation, and test subsets, with a separation by subject, ensuring that data from a single user did not appear in more than one set. This partitioning enabled a reliable assessment of the model’s generalization ability to new users. Given the exploratory, conceptual nature of this study and the limited sample size (n = 10), a data separation strategy was employed for each participant. Data from each participant were treated as independent units so that the training and evaluation sets did not contain samples from the same participant. This approach was chosen to prevent data leakage and provide a more conservative estimate of model performance, particularly in the presence of participant-specific biomechanical and neuromuscular patterns known to influence upper limb movement and exoskeleton interaction. Separation for each participant is particularly important in upper limb exoskeleton studies, where within-subject correlations can artificially inflate performance metrics if randomized sampling is used. Although this strategy reduces the amount of data available for training, it better reflects the model’s ability to generalize to individuals within the limited scope of the pilot study. Therefore, performance results should be interpreted as an indicator of feasibility rather than as evidence of robust generalization. Hyperparameters were selected using manual and/or restricted grid-based searches, con-ducted exclusively on the training data within each subject split. The selection process prioritized model stability, interpretability, and computational feasibility over exhaustive optimization. This decision was driven by the small size of the dataset and the goal of demonstrating methodological feasibility rather than achieving maximum predictive accuracy. To reduce the risk of overfitting, hyperparameter ranges were limited to values commonly re-ported in prior AI and biomechanical modeling literature. No hyperparameters were tuned using test data. Therefore, the resulting model configurations should be considered preliminary, serving to validate the suitability of the chosen AI approach in this application domain. Due to the limited sample size and hyperparameter search space, the reported performance metrics are subject to variability and may not scale to larger or more heterogeneous populations. More comprehensive hyperparameter optimization strategies (e.g., nested cross-validation or Bayesian optimization) were intentionally deferred to future studies with expanded datasets. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as optimized or ready-to-implement system performance. During empirical studies, a pain threshold limitation was introduced into the control. Theoretical maximum grip force refers to the force value calculated based on the actuator specifications and mechanical transmission model, assuming ideal operating conditions. It was derived from the nominal actuator torque or linear force, gear ratios, and effective moment arms, ignoring losses due to friction, compliance, and control constraints. Therefore, this value represents an upper limit of achievable grip force, not the force expected in practice. However, experimentally measured grip force was obtained under actual operating conditions and inherently accounts for the effects of mechanical losses, controller dynamics, and user-device interaction. Force measurements were performed using a calibrated force sensor placed at the finger tip or at the grip interface, depending on the grip configuration being tested. During testing, the exoskeleton performed a controlled closing movement while the user maintained a relaxed posture to minimize voluntary input. Multiple trials were recorded for each configuration, and the reported force values represent the average peak force across repetitions. This distinction provides a clear separation between model-based performance estimates and empirically validated device capabilities. Input parameter validity was defined as the degree to which the input variables used by the model were physically meaningful, reliably measured, and representative of the modeled phenomena. An input parameter was considered valid if it fell within predefined physiological or mechanical limits, came from calibrated sensors, and exhibited consistent behavior across repeated measurements and subjects. Validity was assessed by checking range limits, detecting outliers using statistical criteria (e.g., z-scores or interquartile ranges), and verifying signal stability over time. Only parameters that met all validity criteria were retained for further analysis and model training. The computational method consisted of first normalizing valid input data, then computing derived features according to defined feature functions, and finally aggregating them over task-specific time windows. Incorrect or missing parameter values were either excluded from feature calculations or handled using predefined imputation rules to avoid bias. For model development, the dataset was partitioned at the subject level into training, validation, and test sets. Specifically, 70 per cent of the subjects were assigned to the training set, 15 per cent to the validation set, and 15 per cent to the test set, to ensure that no data from the same subject appeared in more than one subset.

Models training and validation

The training process included selecting a model architecture and optimizing hyperparameters on a validation set. Final model quality assessment was performed on an independent test set using previously defined performance metrics. The training setup involved applying a loss function tailored to the task, such as mean squared error for regression or cross-entropy for classification. The optimization process was implemented using the Adam algorithm, which ensures stable and efficient model weight up-dates. Training was conducted for a fixed number of epochs, empirically selected based on the convergence of the loss function and validation results. Regularization techniques, such as the L2 penalty and early stopping mechanism, were used to reduce overfitting. Additionally, the training progress on the validation set was monitored, enabling the selection of the best model in terms of generalizationability. A detailed methodology for creating models is presented later in the article, taking into account the specificity of the developed models.

Supporting the creation of the control unit for the hand exoskeleton

The hand exoskeletons shown in Figure 1 are controlled using traditional methods without the use of artificial intelligence. The control section of the hand exoskeleton from Fig. 1a is made up of the following elements: linear actuators (linear servomechanisms), a linear actuator controller (servomechanisms) and a computer with control software. The actuators and actuator controller were selected through trial and error. The exoskeleton is controlled using software developed as part of a doctoral dissertation27. The exoskeleton control software uses hand movement support parameters, i.e.:the flexion angles of individual pairs of kinematic phalanges, from which the distance travelled is calculated,the mass of individual phalanges, needed to calculate the forces with which the fingers bend and straighten,measurement time, which is obtained from information about the data vector collected from the measuring device,other parameters, such as speed and acceleration.

The program code is compatible with all types of Arduino thanks to the ability to select Arduino (specifically the microcontroller) through the Arduino IDE environment using the board manager. Control software was created for the DF Romero V2 controller using the Atmega 32u4 microcontroller compatible with Arduino Leonardo in C++. The SerialCommands library, which enables command tokenisation and the creation of arguments for these tokens (commands) using a software serial port, including USB was used. The control section of the hand exoskeleton shown in Fig. 1b is responsible for controlling the pneumatics. Two separate valves determine what movement is to be performed, bending or straightening. Setting the relay on a given valve to low opens the valve, while setting it to high closes it. At the same time, the servo mechanisms are controlled via PWM and a duty cycle signal, which is used to determine the angle of rotation of the servo. The controller is responsible for the full functionality of the exoskeleton. The controller is based on the Atmega 32u4 microcontroller, and the software for it was written in C++ and includes all the necessary functions: servo movement, pneumatic control, demonstration and testing algorithms. The controller allows for power connection, which is also important due to the small size of the device and in order to ensure uninterrupted operation. The actuator rule was defined based on the available stroke length, with a 25 mm threshold chosen as a practical boundary between single- and multiple-actuator solutions. This threshold reflects mechanical and anatomical constraints, as strokes below 25 mm can typically be reliably achieved with a single compact actuator while maintaining sufficient force, bandwidth, and integration with the hand exoskeleton. For larger required displacements exceeding 25 mm, a single actuator would result in increased size, reduced controllability, or unfavorable gear ratios, justifying the use of multiple actuators or segmented control. From a design perspective, this rule simplifies early decision-making by directly linking kinematic requirements with control complexity. Quantitatively, the 25 mm threshold reduced the number of potential actuator configurations by eliminating oversized single-actuator designs for tasks requiring long strokes. As a result, recommendations have shifted toward single-actuator architectures for precision manipulation and rehabilitation tasks with limited joint movement, and toward multi-actuator architectures for tasks requiring a larger range of motion or combined finger movements. This principle has improved consistency in actuator selection and increased the robustness of design recommendations by aligning mechanical feasibility with functional requirements.

Discussion

The article draws on our experience in grants related to the design, construction, and testing of hand exoskeletons from 2019 to the present, as well as on publications and research results of other scientists analyzed during this time. AI offers real benefits in personalizing and optimizing exoskeletons—better intention prediction, adaptive movement support, and potentially greater comfort and efficiency. Large, long-term comparative studies that clearly evaluate the effectiveness of AI vs. traditional systems in real-world, or user applications are lacking. Research is currently dominated by short-term studies, prototypes, and re-views, which demonstrate potential but do not yet provide a complete answer to the question of which approach is better for long-term wear and user adaptation. The system was validated using a combination of real-world user datasets and controlled patient studies, allowing for evaluation of the algorithm’s performance in relevant conditions. The AI-generated results were further compared with expert assessments from therapists and engineers to ensure their accuracy and safety. Simulated biomechanical scenarios were also incorporated early in development, allowing for testing extreme or rare cases without risk to the user. This hybrid approach enhances the reliability of the validation and meets regulatory requirements for medical devices. We received positive comments from users after testing our exoskeletons with people with hand disabilities. Standard, long-term comparisons of patient satisfaction between AI-powered exoskeletons and traditional solutions are not yet common in the scientific literature; most studies examine either the robotic devices as a whole or their effectiveness without detailed comparisons of subjective user perceptions based on the AI application. Users typically report positive experiences and satisfaction with rehabilitation robots; they are often perceived as safe, helpful, and engaging. In some studies, users of rehabilitation robots (including walking exoskeletons) reported overall high satisfaction with the safety, effectiveness, and comfort of the devices—but also identified areas for improvement (e.g., ease of adjustment and weight)—although these studies did not specifically compare AI vs. traditional systems. Systematic reviews and qualitative studies show that patient experiences with rehabilitation robots are generally positive, particularly when the device appears helpful and is accepted as part of therapy, but users may have varying expectations and concerns about its use. In ML modelling based on biological patterns, such as ours, ensuring feature importance estimation and interpretability is crucial. Based on the biological structure of Dynastes Hercules, a bio-inspired robot foot structure was designed and optimized for movement on trusses of varying heights. The influence of various factors was analyzed theoretically and experimentally, and the feasibility of the scheme was verified through simulation. The principles of influence of various factors described in this paper can be applied to the design of robot feet. but also other elements, including exoskeletons, to improve their operational capabilities and stability, increase range and efficiency28. Surface electromyogram (sEMG) from handgrip can be used to detect sarcopenia using ML methods with rational features extracted from sEMG signals. Interpretability of the resulting ML models using a novel feature importance estimation method is achieved. Accuracy exceeds 70 per cent and sensitivity exceeds 75 per cent, indicating moderate classification performance29. Principles of neuroscience and biomechanical analysis can be used to optimize attachment points in cable-powered exoskeletons to achieve natural movement, energy efficiency, and controlled muscle coordination in the user. Extracted biomechanical features are used to generate trajectories, enabling enhanced natural movement under exoskeleton control. A genetic algorithm allows for minimizing energy consumption and optimizing attachment points in a cable-powered system30. A method for in-situ joint stiffness identification using an eye-in-hand camera was also proposed, minimizing the need for specialized external measurement devices—useful for both robots and exoskeletons. A robot stiffness model was developed based on the D-H method and the principle of virtual operation. Vision-based coordinate calibration and a two-stage method for selecting the optimal measurement position were then performed31. Human activity recognition can also utilize data from IMU, ambient, GPS, and smartphone audio sensors. IMU and ambient sensors are used for locomotion. CNNs were used for indoor/outdoor activity classification, and LSTM networks were used for locomotion recognition. K-fold cross-validation methods yielded accuracies of 89-87 per cent for locomotion activity and 96 per cent for indoor/outdoor activity32. Differences in individual motor characteristics have led to the need for unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) methods based on weight considerations (WMDD)33. ML models are increasingly being used for injury classification, which would simplify exoskeleton fitting in some cases34,35. In the next stages of exoskeleton research, multimodal agent-based artificial intelligence (MAA) may prove crucial, as it is more effective in parameterizing the transformation of human-machine interactions, including data from multiple modalities (e.g., language, image, sound), understanding complex environments, and performing complex tasks36. The development of exoskeletons as a super robotic technology requires organizational structures and determinants of a specialized medical device industry. Social and producer networks, their division into network subgroups, methods of information and resource diffusion, as well as the economic base, degree of openness, and technological innovation are strongly related to the need to strengthen interregional connections, foster innovation ecosystems, and improve the openness of talent and trade within individual regions37. Interdisciplinary AI-based decision support for the planning, design, and production of upper limb exoskeletons is currently being developed based on three pillars. The first is understanding intent based on human biomedical signals (especially sEMG). The second pillar involves adaptive/collaborative control, which personalises assistance in real time, and the third pillar involves adaptive exoskeleton design and data-driven therapy planning. In this paper, we present the key elements of the second and third pillars. Recent studies and reviews point to the development of motion state recognition, more precise control, and oriented personalisation using kinematic and demographic data. Analysis of the literature indicates numerous difficulties in designing upper limb exoskeletons, particularly hand exoskeletons, resulting from the high number of degrees of freedom, small component sizes, and the need for precise user interaction. In this area, in addition to the complexity of the mechatronic artifact itself, a significant challenge is the adaptation of the device by humans, which is strongly linked to hand dysfunctions, motor skills, and medical recommendations for rehabilitation and recovery. For this reason, two complementary design levels are distinguished, which allow for the streamlining of the exoskeleton development process and increase its clinical utility. The first stage involves developing a reference model of the hand exoskeleton, defining the overall kinematic architecture, ranges of motion, drive layout, sensor systems, and basic ergonomic assumptions. The reference model provides a design knowledge base enabling proof of concept, biomechanical simulations, and the assessment of compliance with safety standards and functional requirements. The second design level focuses on adapting the reference model to the individual hand. The device is designed to meet the user’s needs by parameterizing dimensions, adjusting support forces, personalizing movement trajectories, and integrating them with the rehabilitation process. This stage takes into account anthropometric data, the user’s condition, and feedback from therapists, allowing for iterative adaptation of the device to the user’s evolving needs. This two-level approach shortens the design cycle, increases the adaptability of the solution, and makes the entire process more cost-effective and attractive from a commercial production perspective. As part of future research, in addition to control, hybrid decision support structures (e.g., rule-based and case-based) are being tested to help engineers and clinicians select tasks and parameters for upper limb rehabilitation assisted by exoskeletons, signalling a shift from pure low-level control to higher-level decision support. Digital twins of exoskeletons and digital twins of users are emerging. These simulate the interaction between the patient and the device, accelerate parameter adjustment, and enable safer, personalised recommendations prior to implementation, although the transfer of this into routine practice is still in its infancy. Key gaps include small, isolated data sets; limited external validation and long-term results; opaque models that hinder trust-building among users and clinicians; and underdeveloped governance around accountability and safety in AI-assisted care. The lack of comparisons is primarily due to the fact that AI-powered exoskeletons are still rapidly developing prototypes, and their architecture and algorithms often change faster than reliable long-term studies can be conducted. Furthermore, high costs, regulatory complexity, and patient heterogeneity make it difficult to design studies that fairly compare AI devices with traditional solutions. Nevertheless, comparisons are essential, as only studies lasting at least medium term will allow for assessing the true clinical value, safety, and acceptability of AI in everyday healthcare. Cost-reduction benefits stem from the automation and personalization of support, which reduces staff time and the number of therapy sessions required to achieve comparable results. Improved therapy quality is confirmed by quantitative indicators such as increased range of motion, reduced task completion time, and reduced muscle activity measured by EMG com-pared to pre-intervention baseline data. Performance benchmarks show that adaptive systems can increase movement repeatability by several dozen percent compared to conventional therapy. Comparative data also indicate shorter rehabilitation times or faster achievement of functional milestones at a similar or lower unit cost. Cost-effectiveness indicators, such as the cost per point improvement on a functional scale, are also important from an economic perspective, as they tend to be lower for technology-assisted solutions. Together, these quantitative metrics provide an objective justification for investment in advanced systems delivering both tangible savings and measurable improvements in the quality of care. Priority research areas include standardised multimodal data streams (sEMG, kinematics, vision), interpretable and uncertainty-aware AI-based simulation models for testing exoskeletons and safe human-in-the-loop learning, integrated digital twin testing platforms (exoskeletons), and studies comparing the functional recovery and usability of soft and rigid upper limb exoskeleton systems in everyday situations. The regulatory constraints of the MDR and ISO 13485 are incorporated into the AI decision support pathway by designing the system using a ”safety-by-design” approach from the algorithm concept stage. AI algorithms are classified as risk-sensitive, and their role is clearly defined as decision support rather than autonomous clinical decision-making, as required by the MDR. Within ISO 13485, the model training, validation, and update processes are documented as part of the quality management system. Full traceability of input data, model versions, and results is ensured, enabling auditing and risk analysis. Additionally, human-in-the-loop mechanisms are implemented to ensure that the user can verify and approve AI recommendations. This ensures that the AI decision-making pathway remains transparent, auditable, and compliant with regulatory requirements for medical devices. User-specific factors such as anthropometry, range of motion, and disability level are entered into the system as input parameters during device setup and calibration. Decision-making algorithms use this data to personalize the level of assistance, movement trajectory, and safety limits. During use, the system dynamically adapts, updating its decisions based on current biomechanical signals and the user’s progress. This ensures that the assistance remains tailored to the user’s individual capabilities and changing functional status.

Conclusions

The emerging AI-assisted process for 3D printing of hand exoskeletons combines deep learning-based analysis of hand movements and grip with the anatomical data of a specific patient to automatically suggest kinematic structures and joint settings. The article demonstrates the personalisation of hand exoskeletons through the classification of hand dysfunctions, cost analysis, and selected areas of hand exoskeleton control using various neural network models. AI-based design and optimisation algorithms refine component geometry for lightness, strength and ergonomics, while AI-based topology optimisation and material selection improve printability and durability. AI-based additive manufacturing processes adjust support structures and parameters to ensure precision, reduce waste, and accelerate the production of personalised exoskeletons.
