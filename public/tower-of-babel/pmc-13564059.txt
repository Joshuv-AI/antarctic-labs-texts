Plexiform neurofibroma of the scrotum in a 16-year-old male: A case report
Authors: Junzhou Su, Ying Wang, Sijie Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plexiform neurofibroma is a benign peripheral nerve sheath tumor considered pathognomonic for neurofibromatosis type 1. Isolated scrotal occurrence without systemic manifestations of neurofibromatosis type 1 is exceedingly rare. We report a 16-year-old male with a progressively enlarging right scrotal mass measuring 8 × 4 cm. There were no café-au-lait spots, axillary freckling, or family history of neurofibromatosis type 1. Magnetic resonance imaging showed the characteristic target sign. Complete surgical excision via a scrotal approach with testicular preservation was performed. Histopathology confirmed plexiform neurofibroma, with positive staining for S-100, SOX10, and CD34. No recurrence was observed at 6-month follow-up.

Introduction

Plexiform neurofibroma is a distinct histological subtype of neurofibroma characterized by growth along nerve plexuses, frequently involving nerve trunks and their branches, resulting in a multinodular or “bag of worms” appearance.1 It is considered nearly pathognomonic for neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1), occurring in 27–44% of patients with NF1.2 While plexiform neurofibroma is typically congenital and exhibits progressive growth with age, its occurrence as an isolated lesion in the scrotum without systemic NF1 manifestations is exceptionally uncommon, especially in the adolescent population.3 To our knowledge, fewer than 20 cases of scrotal neurofibromas in children have been reported, with only a handful representing the plexiform subtype in the absence of NF1.3,4 We present a case of isolated right scrotal plexiform neurofibroma in a 16-year-old male with no other stigmata of NF1.

Case presentation

A 16-year-old male presented with a one-year history of a gradually enlarging right scrotal mass. He reported no pain, voiding symptoms, or trauma. His past medical history was unremarkable, and his family history was negative for neurofibromatosis.

Physical examination revealed a well-developed adolescent. No café-au-lait spots, axillary or inguinal freckling, or cutaneous neurofibromas were identified. The right hemiscrotum was enlarged, containing a firm, non-tender, approximately 8 × 4 cm solid mass with limited mobility. The mass was intimately associated with the right testis, epididymis, and spermatic cord, with indistinct margins. The left testis and epididymis were normal. The mass was non-transluminant.

Laboratory investigations, including alpha-fetoprotein, beta-human chorionic gonadotropin, and lactate dehydrogenase, were within normal limits.

Initial scrotal ultrasonography revealed a hypoechoic mass with ill-defined borders and a serpiginous “plexiform” appearance. Subsequent scrotal MRI with and without contrast demonstrated a mass that was isointense on T1-weighted images and heterogeneously hyperintense on T2-weighted images, with internal serpiginous hypointense septations consistent with the characteristic “target sign” of neurogenic tumors. On coronal and sagittal views, the mass showed similar features with clear delineation of its relationship to the surrounding structures (Fig. 1A and B). Post-contrast imaging showed heterogeneous progressive enhancement. The right testis, epididymis, and spermatic cord were displaced, with indistinct interfaces with the mass. MRI findings were highly suggestive of plexiform neurofibroma.Fig. 1Preoperative MRI. (A) Coronal view; (B) Sagittal view.

Preoperative MRI. (A) Coronal view; (B) Sagittal view.

The patient underwent surgical exploration through a right vertical scrotal incision. Intraoperatively, a gray-white, multinodular, unencapsulated tumor measuring approximately 8 × 4 cm was identified within the scrotum, adherent to the testis, epididymis, and spermatic cord (Fig. 2A). The tumor exhibited a plexiform growth pattern along nerve fascicles. Careful dissection was performed to preserve the testis, epididymis, vas deferens, and spermatic vessels, achieving complete macroscopic excision of the mass. Intraoperative frozen section analysis reported a benign spindle cell tumor of neural origin. Estimated blood loss was 30 mL.Fig. 2(A) Intraoperative view; (B) Gross specimen.

(A) Intraoperative view; (B) Gross specimen.

Gross examination of the resected specimen measured 8 × 4 × 3 cm, with a firm, gray-white, multinodular cut surface without hemorrhage or necrosis (Fig. 2B).

Microscopically, the tumor showed a plexiform growth pattern with expanded nerve fascicles embedded in a myxoid matrix. On routine hematoxylin and eosin staining, the characteristic plexiform arrangement of nerve fibers was clearly visible (Fig. 3, 10× magnification). Higher magnification revealed a mixture of spindle-shaped Schwann cells, fibroblasts, and perineurial-like cells with wavy, buckled nuclei. No nuclear atypia or mitotic figures were identified. Antoni A or B areas and Verocay bodies were absent. Immunohistochemistry showed diffuse positivity for S-100 and SOX10. CD34 staining highlighted the preserved intraneural fibroblast network. Neurofilament staining demonstrated scattered axons traversing the lesion. The Ki-67 proliferation index was <3%. Stains for desmin, cytokeratin, EMA, and STAT6 were negative. Pathological diagnosis was plexiform neurofibroma of the right scrotum.Fig. 3Routine histopathology.

Routine histopathology.

The postoperative course was uneventful. The drain was removed on postoperative day 3, and the patient was discharged on day 5. At 6-month follow-up, there was no evidence of local recurrence on physical examination or scrotal ultrasound. No café-au-lait spots or other NF1-related manifestations had developed. Annual follow-up was recommended.

Discussion

Plexiform neurofibroma is a benign peripheral nerve sheath tumor considered pathognomonic for NF1, with some studies suggesting that the presence of plexiform neurofibroma essentially confirms the diagnosis of NF1.5 However, rare cases of isolated plexiform neurofibroma without other NF1 stigmata have been reported, predominantly in the head and neck region and, less frequently, in the genitourinary tract.6

Genitourinary involvement by plexiform neurofibroma is exceptionally rare. A review of the literature identified fewer than 20 reported cases of scrotal neurofibromas in children, with only a handful representing the plexiform subtype in the absence of NF1.3,4,7 Gelikman et al. reported a 10-year-old male with isolated intrascrotal plexiform neurofibroma, and Jaber et al. described a similar case in a 6-year-old child.4,7 Our case adds to this limited body of literature and is noteworthy for the relatively large tumor size (8 × 4 cm) compared to previously reported cases.

The diagnostic challenge lies in distinguishing plexiform neurofibroma from other paratesticular masses, including adenomatoid tumors, leiomyomas, schwannomas, and malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors.8 Schwannomas require careful differentiation; they are typically encapsulated, exhibit Antoni A and B areas with Verocay bodies, and lack the plexiform growth pattern characteristic of plexiform neurofibroma.9 Immunohistochemistry is valuable in this distinction, with CD34 highlighting the distinctive intraneural fibroblast network in plexiform neurofibroma, a feature absent in schwannomas.10

Imaging plays a crucial role in preoperative diagnosis. Ultrasonography serves as an effective initial screening modality, typically revealing a heterogeneous hypoechoic mass with serpiginous tubular structures. MRI provides more characteristic findings; the “target sign” on T2-weighted images—hyperintense signal with central hypointensity—represents central fibrocollagenous tissue surrounded by myxoid matrix and is highly suggestive of plexiform neurofibroma.11 In our case, initial ultrasonography raised suspicion of a neurogenic tumor, and subsequent contrast-enhanced MRI confirmed the characteristic features of plexiform neurofibroma, providing valuable guidance for surgical planning.

For large intrascrotal plexiform neurofibroma, complete surgical excision with testicular preservation is the standard of care.12 The surgical approach should be tailored to tumor location and size. In our case, the scrotal location and intimate relationship with the testis and epididymis warranted a direct scrotal incision, which provided excellent exposure, minimized morbidity, and facilitated rapid recovery. Meticulous dissection is essential to preserve the testis, epididymis, vas deferens, and spermatic vessels.

The most critical aspect of postoperative management is long-term surveillance for NF1. Although our patient currently does not meet the diagnostic criteria for NF1, plexiform neurofibroma may represent the initial presenting feature, with other manifestations potentially developing later in life.13 Approximately 8–13% of plexiform neurofibromas may undergo malignant transformation to malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor, characterized by rapid growth, pain, and increasing tumor size.14 Therefore, annual clinical evaluation and imaging surveillance are recommended.

Conclusion

Isolated scrotal plexiform neurofibroma without NF1 is an exceedingly rare entity in adolescents. Ultrasonography is a useful initial screening tool, while contrast-enhanced MRI with its characteristic “target sign” can provide valuable preoperative diagnostic clues. Definitive diagnosis requires histopathological and immunohistochemical confirmation. Complete surgical excision with testicular preservation via a scrotal approach achieves excellent outcomes. Long-term follow-up is mandatory to monitor for local recurrence, malignant transformation, and the potential emergence of NF1-related manifestations.
