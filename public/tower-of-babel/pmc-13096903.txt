Understanding and addressing the health impacts of wildfires on Latines in California
Authors: Elizabeth M. Rojo, David J.X. González, Lara Schwarz, Paulina Castro Nava, Ana Mascareñas, Federico Castillo, Hayden B Bosworth, Jose G Perez-Ramos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Globally, and particularly in California, the scale, intensity, and frequency of wildfires are increasing as climate change worsens. The gravity of this issue was recently made clear in Los Angeles County, California, where 29 people were killed and over 16,000 structures were destroyed by a series of seven wildfires in January 2025. Researchers across scientific disciplines have responded to recent wildfire disasters in California and elsewhere to fill in key knowledge gaps relating to wildfire impacts on the environment and human health. However, there remains a lack of understanding regarding how these health impacts disproportionately affect marginalized communities, which is crucial for developing equitable health policies and interventions. California is an important setting to consider wildfire impacts on Latines, as community leaders and policymakers in the state have advocated for and implemented innovative climate justice policies, and much of the recent literature examining wildfire health impacts has leveraged California-based datasets. In this paper, we explore wildfire health equity issues among Latines in California, with particular attention to linguistically isolated people, outdoor workers, and immigrants. We explore socio-structural factors that drive disparities and propose a path forward to advance understanding and address underlying inequities.

Introduction

Globally, and particularly in California, the scale, intensity, and frequency of wildfires are increasing as climate change worsens [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. The gravity of climate change was recently made clear in Los Angeles County, California, where 29 people were killed and over 16,000 structures were destroyed by a series of seven wildfires in January 2025 [5]. Beyond immediate impacts, the full public health toll of this event remains to be seen, as it will take time to analyze the impacts of exposures to smoke, ash, hazardous waste materials, and other pollutants generated by this disaster in this densely populated region. Over the past two decades, the majority of wildfire-related deaths in California were attributed to smoke exposure due to its spread far beyond the wildfire burn zone [6]. Exposure to wildfire smoke is associated with higher risk of adverse cardiovascular, respiratory, reproductive, and mental health outcomes, which can have both short and long-term consequences for physical and socioeconomic well-being [[7], [8], [9]]. Indeed, researchers across scientific disciplines have responded to recent wildfire disasters in California and elsewhere to fill in key knowledge gaps relating to wildfire impacts on the environment and human health. Despite this increase in research, there remain many knowledge gaps about the resulting health inequities. Understanding whether and how these health impacts disproportionately affect marginalized communities is crucial for developing equitable health policies and interventions.

In the United States (US), racially marginalized communities experience disproportionate socio-structural burdens that can exacerbate the impacts of wildfires and other climate-driven disasters [10]. For Latines, socio-structural factors related to wildfires include barriers to accessing culturally appropriate information from public agencies, disproportionately high representation among outdoor workers, persistent housing segregation, and systemic barriers to accessing healthcare and other social services, often due to immigration status [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. Additionally, Latines and other racially/ethnically marginalized groups are more likely to live near existing hazardous sites such as fossil fuel infrastructure, including in areas with high wildfire risk [37]. Collectively, the impacts of these socio-structural factors can increase exposures and susceptibility to wildfire-related health hazards.

California is an important setting to consider wildfire impacts on Latine health. Community leaders and policymakers in California have advocated for and implemented innovative climate justice policies. There is political will to take more actions to address climate change that can influence policy-making in other states and at the federal level [17]. Additionally, 40% of the California population of 39 million identify as Latine, making this the largest racial/ethnic group in the state [18]. In 2010, most Latines in California (81.5%) were of Mexican descent, with 4.1% of Salvadoran descent, 2.4% of Guatemalan descent, and 12.0% with roots in other Latin American countries [19]. Across racial/ethnic groups in California, Latines are the most likely to support state-level action to address climate change [20].

Here, we explore wildfire health equity issues among Latines in California, with particular attention to linguistically isolated people, outdoor workers, and immigrants. In this paper, we discuss key findings and gaps in recent literature regarding socio-structural determinants of health. We emphasize the urgent need to improve data collection efforts and rigorously apply a health equity lens to better understand, and ultimately address, underlying inequities that drive wildfire-related health disparities among Latines. Note that many health equity studies examine Hispanic rather than Latine identity, based on typologies promulgated by the US Census Bureau and used widely in sociodemographic and biomedical databases. These identity groups are not completely interchangeable. Here, when discussing papers that use the term Hispanic, we also use that term for clarity, though our overall interest is in the Latine population.

By engaging in an in-depth discussion on the ways in which California Latines are marginalized with respect to wildfire-related risks, we illustrate the importance of a nuanced approach to environmental health equity work. Researchers, advocates, and policymakers working with other marginalized groups may find value in our in-depth discussion of California’s Latine communities. Other marginalized groups face disproportionate wildfire-related risks that warrant urgent attention, including gender and sexual minorities and people living with disabilities, each with distinct underlying drivers and intersectionalities [21,22]. Coordinated efforts are needed across affected communities, research institutions, and policymakers to fill critical knowledge gaps, mitigate wildfire health impacts, and address root causes of socio-structural oppression and climate change.

Wildfire health equity research

The rise in wildfire activity has offset decades of improvements in outdoor air quality, increasingly exposing people to air pollutants and other health hazards [2]. The principal component of wildfire smoke is fine particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter of less than 2.5 µm (PM2.5). Exposure to PM2.5 is one of the leading global causes of morbidity and mortality [23]. There are well-documented short- and long-term impacts of PM2.5 on health, and emerging evidence indicates that PM2.5 originating from wildfires is more harmful than from other sources [24]. Wildfire PM2.5 can be composed of various chemical compounds, and particulates derived from burning structures have higher concentrations of hazardous metals, including nickel, copper, zinc, and lead [25]. The health impacts of wildfire smoke are widespread, crossing state and international borders [26].

Disparities in wildfire smoke exposure

The evidence regarding disparities in exposures to wildfires in California is mixed. Across the United States, considering wildfire hazard and socioeconomic factors, census tracts with majority Black, Hispanic, or Native American populations have a 50% higher wildfire vulnerability [27]. In California, Casey et al. analyzed sociodemographic characteristics of people exposed to wildfire PM2.5 for each year from 2006 to 2020. Hispanic people were not more likely to be exposed to wildfire smoke than other racial/ethnic groups; American Indian/Alaska Native and non-Hispanic white people consistently had disproportionately high exposure [28]. In another study analyzing data from low-cost sensors in the Los Angeles metropolitan area, the census tracts with higher proportions of Hispanic residents also had higher wildfire PM2.5 concentrations, indicating that disparities may exist at local levels that are not seen when looking at the data statewide [29]. Additionally, Hispanic residents in California appear to be disproportionately represented among counties exposed to PM2.5 from both prescribed and agricultural burns, though these types of human-initiated landscape fires are outside the scope of the current manuscript [30].

Differential exposure to wildfire smoke for California Latine populations across studies is not consistent. This lack of consistency could be explained by the heterogeneity in wildfire smoke exposure across different Latine populations and regions, highlighting how health disparities can vary between places. Many studies that examine these disparities measure ambient wildfire smoke exposure at the zip code or census-tract of residence, which does not account workplace exposures, household characteristics, or actions taken by individuals to protect themselves. Wildfire smoke exposures among Latines who work outdoors or in settings with higher infiltration of outdoor air pollution may be more vulnerable. While some people may be able to stay indoors in a home or building with air filtration during a wildfire smoke event, others may have to continue working outdoors. These differences will have strong implications for individual exposure levels that would not be captured by residence-based census-level exposure.

Disparities in wildfire-related health outcomes

The literature on health impacts of wildfire-related exposures has grown rapidly over the past decade, with some studies examining racialized health disparities with smoke exposures. Again, there is some evidence of health disparities for Latine populations associated with wildfire smoke exposures, but these findings are inconsistent. Do et al. evaluated community-level vulnerability to the effects of wildfire smoke and found that zip codes in California with a higher percentage of Black and Pacific Islander residents were at higher risk, but zip codes with a higher proportion of Hispanic populations were not at increased risk [31]. When considering the joint effects of wildfire smoke and extreme heat on cardiorespiratory hospitalizations in California, zip codes with a higher percentage of Hispanic population showed an elevated risk [32].

Considering other health outcomes, exposure to wildfire PM2.5 is associated with elevated risk of emergency department visits for diabetes, with elevated risks for Hispanic people compared to non-Hispanic white people, and particularly elevated risks of pneumonia-related visits [33]. A study investigating hospital visits for acute respiratory infections associated with wildfire smoke found a greater magnitude of increase among California Hispanic people compared to other racial/ethnic groups [34]. However, hospital visits for any respiratory issue increased more significantly for non-Hispanic white people [34]. A study on the 2020 California wildfires and mental health found wildfire smoke to be associated with an increased risk of depression for Hispanic individuals as compared to non-Hispanic Black or non-Hispanic White individuals but not for other mood-affective disorders [35].

Wildfire hazards and the built environment

Wildfires can also affect communities already living near hazardous sites. For example, in California, Latines are disproportionately represented among people living near oil and gas wells [36]. González et al. found that wildfires are increasingly burning through areas with oil and gas wells, with over a million California residents living within 1 km of wells in areas with high wildfire risk [37]. Further work on these topics can help determine specific pathways through which wildfire in places with hazardous infrastructure impacts health, and what kinds of interventions would alleviate these harms (e.g., prioritizing sites for decommissioning, regular monitoring).

Identifying research limitations within the Latine community

From a health equity perspective, we should be mindful of limitations in the existing wildfire health literature and consider evidence from diverse disciplines across the social and natural sciences. Wildfire health research to date has largely examined health disparities through stratification on racial/ethnic identity, where for example wildfire-related exposures and health outcomes are examined separately for Latine people and compared to estimates for other racial/ethnic groups. The Latine identity group is itself large and heterogeneous [38]. There is comparatively little work specific to Latines who are linguistically isolated, immigrants, or low-income. Indeed, disparities among these groups may be masked when Latine and Hispanic people are considered as a whole.

Here, we complicate that discussion by examining underlying socio-structural determinants within the Latine population that could drive health disparities, but which may not be apparent when examining Latines as a whole. Linguistically isolated people, for example, do not have access to the same wildfire health information and alerts that English-speaking people do, and machine-translated public health information can fail to convey information in culturally relevant ways. Outdoor workers, many of whom are socioeconomically marginalized, may elect to prioritize economic concerns over health-related concerns during heavy wildfire smoke days. Concerns among immigrants regarding enforcement can prevent individuals from accessing resilience hubs or seeking medical care during wildfires [39]. Without proper data and theoretical framing, it is difficult for public health researchers to critically examine these socio-structural determinants of health, and for policymakers and affected communities to take effective action.

Wildfires and socio-structural determinants of health

Socio-structural determinants of health are defined as the root cause of disparities and inequities in society [40,41]. These roots typically perpetuate poor social and health outcomes among marginalized and under-resourced communities. These socio-structural determinants can undermine individual and collective capacities to improve their livelihoods, including healthcare availability, information access, housing quality, occupational conditions, and economic stability. These factors shape health outcomes and come into play during climate-driven extreme events. Socio-structural determinants can be important in exacerbating differences in wildfire smoke exposure, and even populations living and working in the same ambient exposure may have a varying internal dose of wildfire smoke due to protective measures that are not equally accessible. These determinants can change the vulnerability of populations and even with equal exposure, populations will not be affected equally due to vulnerability factors that modify risks. Also, actions and policies are often not accessible to all populations equally, further exacerbating the potential risk.

Barriers to healthcare access

Accessibility to quality health insurance is an important determinant of vulnerability to harmful exposures. Children with untreated respiratory issues, for example, could be more biologically susceptible to exacerbations when they breathe wildfire smoke. California’s Medicaid program, known as Medi-Cal, provides public health insurance services to low-income families and is one of a handful of state-based public health programs that provides coverage to children and adults regardless of immigration status. Limited access to affordable healthcare leaves many uninsured, with conditions, such as asthma, worsened or caused by wildfire smoke [42]. Despite recent changes to Medi-Cal eligibility requirements, the costs of healthcare are unaffordable for many, resulting in a large uninsured or underinsured population. Compounding this limited access, many Latines live in neighborhoods with older housing stock that is more permeable to smoke during wildfire events, as well as other pollutants throughout the year, which can increase the susceptibility of occupants to exposures.

Occupational risks

In California, 92% of farmworkers identify as Latine [43]. Without adequate protections, working outdoors during wildfire smoke events can impact workers’ health. Wildfire-related disruptions can threaten economic stability when people lose work during disasters. These patterns of risk mirror those seen during the COVID-19 pandemic, when Latine frontline workers in factories and agricultural jobs were disproportionately exposed to the virus due to a lack of workplace protections and their essential roles in the economy [44].

The intersections between outdoor work and immigration status can exacerbate wildfire-related health hazards, particularly in the current political climate with the federal administration accused of violating immigrants’ rights and protections. Farmworkers who are H-2A visa holders rely on their employers for housing and transportation. By law, H-2A farmworker housing must meet specific standards for safety, cleanliness, and space. However, recent research indicates such standards are commonly violated and that enforcement is inconsistent [45]. Consequently, farmworkers who hold H-2A visas often experience poor unsanitary conditions, excessive indoor heat, and poor indoor air quality, particularly during wildfire smoke events [45]. Farmworkers who hold H-2A visas can face barriers to mobility during wildfires, particularly when their work sites or housing is located in an evacuation zone. The immigration status can also impede farmworker’s agency for self-advocacy. These hazards are likely heightened for undocumented farmworkers.

Recently implemented wildfire-related policies, seeking to address economic impacts of wildfires, may exacerbate health risks for Latine farmworkers. For example, in 2017, Sonoma County implemented the Agricultural Pass (AgPass) Program, which allows farmworkers to enter active wildfire evacuation zones to carry out critical work [46]. This practice results in exposure to higher concentrations of wildfire PM2.5 among these workers, a majority of whom are undocumented immigrants from Latin America, compared to other outdoor workers [46]. The lack of workplace regulations compounds these kinds of risks, as farmworkers often lack access to N95 masks and in-home or workplace air filters. Additionally, among farmworkers, factors including linguistic isolation, fear of losing employment, and concerns about immigration status can prevent individuals from speaking out about unsafe working conditions [39].

These factors can also be a barrier to receiving timely medical care. In general, under the current federal administration, concerns regarding immigration status will likely be heightened among farmworkers, including those who are undocumented or have undocumented family members. Latines who are US-citizens or who are immigrants with work authorization can face harassment and experience stress from immigration raids, which can also impact health [47]. In a September 2025 emergency ruling for the case Noem v. Vasquez Perdomo, the Supreme Court stayed a lower court order, effectively allowing officials from the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) to stop individuals in Southern California based on their apparent race, their worksite, and whether they speak Spanish or accented English. In a dissenting opinion, Justice Sonia Sotomayor wrote, “We should not have to live in a country where the Government can seize anyone who looks Latino, speaks Spanish, and appears to work a low wage job.” The stress associated with encounters with DHS agents, including among California Latines who are citizens and immigrants, could exacerbate wildfire-related health risks and capacity to take protective actions during wildfire disasters. The intersections between these kinds of immigration-related social stressors and wildfire-related stressors to our knowledge have not yet been examined by biomedical researchers.

Linguistic isolation

Researchers and practitioners should consider linguistic isolation as a key socio-structural determinant of health in the context of wildfires, particularly given that many of the interventions involve sharing timely information and resources [15]. Wildfire-related guidance from state and local agencies sources is not consistently available in non-English languages. Mendez et al. investigated experiences of undocumented Latine and Indigenous Mesoamerican farmworkers in Santa Barbara and Ventura Counties during the 2017 Thomas Fire, finding that disaster relief funding was directed away from these communities towards more privileged individuals and communities [14]. At the time, the Thomas Fire was the largest in state history, yet no public information was available in any language other than English. While the wildfire smoke alert system eventually offered texts in Spanish, community organizers highlighted that this still excluded 1 in 3 of the county’s 40,000 farmworkers who spoke an Indigenous language, and that texts were not always accurately translated. Involving the most impacted populations in developing solutions to respond to such disasters is important to their effectiveness. In 2022, advocates and public officials in Ventura County filled this gap by developing a wildfire alert system in Spanish, Mixteco, and Zapoteco [48]. This pilot project in Ventura County could serve as a model for other jurisdictions, though to our knowledge the impact of the program has not yet been evaluated.

A path forward

The increasingly impactful wildfires in California underscore the coinciding difficulties of climate-driven disasters and persistent socio-structural oppression. Government responses to such crises are often reactive, but a shift to proactive action—mindful of climate projections, changing demographics, and ongoing developments in the built environment—can help in addressing persistent inequities. There is clear need for interventions, which can be more effective with leadership from the impacted communities themselves. State and local policymakers can take action to address the disproportionate wildfire impacts among Latines. Community leaders, advocates, and researchers can collaborate to develop interventions to mitigate wildfire-related harms for marginalized Latine and other at-risk groups. In doing so, principles of cultural humility such as challenging power imbalances and institutional accountability should be utilized to further address socio-structural determinants of health through community-led initiatives that begin with ensuring that the research teams are asking the right questions [49].

Improvements in data collection and disaggregation

The systemic lack of detailed reporting of socio-structural factors, such as language and immigration status, in biomedical datasets is a substantial barrier to examining wildfire-related health disparities. In addition, disaggregation of racial/ethnic data, particularly for groups as heterogeneous as Latines, is important to understanding differential underlying socio-structural factors that drive disparities [38,50]. To more carefully examine socio-structural factors, health researchers can make use of detailed electronic health records from hospital systems, such as new diagnosis codes on social risks and needs (Z-codes), which were added to the 10th revision of the International Classification of Disease (ICD-10) to improve data collection on social determinants of health [51]. These Z-codes remain under-utilized [51]. Additionally, community health workers and community clinics serving rural communities could employ similar approaches to collect data from patients, including those who do not utilize larger hospital systems. Similarly, when federal policies eliminate datasets that document economic, social, and health disparities, it becomes even more important for community-level data to be supported and collaboratively maintained by local governments and institutions.

Representation and inclusion in research and health service delivery teams

Teams conducting wildfire health equity work should ensure that relevant health equity expertise is properly represented and recognized. When conducting environmental justice research, Casey et al. recommend recognizing the constant evolution of theory and methods in the field, attend to nuanced social constructs such as ethnicity, and take a solution-oriented approach to addressing documented disparities [52]. Those without health equity experience should seek training and collaborations with established scholars to ensure proper contextualization of research questions and the quality of methods and interpretation [53]. Additionally, limited funding to conduct such research should be directed towards research teams with proper health equity training, community orientation, and a track record of publishing studies that interrogate socio-structural determinants of health.

It is important to ensure that Latine researchers and practitioners have equitable access to professional training and opportunities so they can study and advocate for their own communities. Paired with rigorous scientific and policy training, lived experience, and culturo-linguistic assets of Latine professionals, can improve the quality of scientific research and reach of scientific communication, as well as the relevance and practicality of policy recommendations. However, Latines are persistently underrepresented in professional disciplines related to wildfire response, including environmental non-profits and epidemiology research institutions [16,54]. In a context of federal disinvestment in representation and inclusion efforts, state institutions, industries, and policymakers can support policies that mitigate barriers in accessing educational and professional opportunities for minoritized Latine individuals. Additionally, Latine community leaders, community health workers, and advocates play essential roles in driving culturally-sustaining interventions [55].

Training improvements in healthcare and hospital preparedness

Healthcare providers and public health practitioners can be trained to identify wildfire-related health conditions, promote protective strategies for at-risk individuals and increase health risk communication for vulnerable populations. Hospital preparedness should include training health-care staff in management of smoke-related emergencies and community health workers for psychological preparedness in the context of wildfires [56]. Providing easy-to-understand risk communications that are adapted to the specific needs of vulnerable populations is important and should prioritize communications to communities that have greater exposure and risk [57]. For example, tools such as the Smoke Sense Urbanova, a free application that supports reducing risks from breathing wildfire smoke which is available in English and Spanish can be recommended by nurses and community health workers to their patient populations, particularly those that are at-risk [58]. Providing multilingual communication of health risks through healthcare services can be effective in reaching at-risk individuals that may not otherwise have access to this information.

Policy and accountability

Needed policy changes include providing critical information to linguistically isolated communities, including speakers of Spanish and Indigenous Mesoamerican languages. Some jurisdictions and organizations are already addressing this need as discussed in Section 3 in Ventura County. In the San Francisco Bay Area, El Tímpano is a community-based organization that facilitates participatory journalism, including providing text-message-based news in Spanish and Mam, an Indigenous Mesoamerican language [59]. The organization provides information and resources on a wide range of topics, including wildfires and other disasters. Access to information is particularly critical during wildfire emergencies, and evacuation orders, post-wildfire health warnings, and recovery resources should be linguistically accessible for all California residents. Researchers and practitioners should consider that adopting health protective behaviors—such as wearing masks, using air filters, and sharing air quality information—is a social process that involves social groups collectively processing, interpreting, and taking action [60]. Community-participatory strategies that align with cultural values, local knowledge, and lived experiences enhance the effectiveness of interventions. We also call for integrated evaluation methods alongside program execution to better contextualize the effectiveness of these initiatives.

Strengthening occupational safety measures for workers exposed to hazardous conditions can further mitigate health risks for farmworkers and others in outdoor industries. These measures include access to protective equipment such as N95 masks and real-time air quality monitoring during wildfires. With respect to AgPass, Chunga Pizarro et al. called for more rigorous oversight of employers, use of fine-scale and real-time air quality data, follow-up to monitor farmworkers’ health post-exposure, and offering hazard pay to participating farmworkers [46]. These recommendations should be considered by decision makers in other jurisdictions considering similar programs.

Public investments should be directed to addressing underlying socio-structural determinants of health disparities. For example, investments in public health infrastructure, including community clinics, public hospitals, and resilience hubs, can help Latines and other marginalized communities prepare for and respond to wildfires. Funds can also be directed towards communities with older housing stock that is more permeable to outdoor air pollutants, including wildfire PM2.5. An example of this is the Tulare County Weatherization Pilot Project, a pilot partnership between the California Department of Public Health and Tulare County working to reduce health risks associated with climate catastrophes [61]. Attention should also be paid to wildfire insurance and equitable delivery of post-disaster aid, so that existing socioeconomic disparities are not worsened, particularly for households and small businesses that often lack the resources to rebuild. As policy interventions are piloted and scaled, it will also be necessary to rigorously evaluate their effectiveness.

Conclusion

Addressing socio-structural determinants of health necessitates a broad-scale, integrated approach. Even when federal leadership on climate was lacking, Californians and their elected representatives have examined and implemented influential policies [17]. As climate change worsens wildfire-related risks, California can continue to be a laboratory for policy innovation and advancement of health equity research, centering the perspectives and needs of Latine and other marginalized populations.
