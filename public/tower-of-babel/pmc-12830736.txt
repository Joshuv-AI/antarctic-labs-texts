Impact of heavy rainfall on ambulance response time in Japan
Authors: Athicha Uttajug, Xerxes Seposo, Muhammad Abdul Basit Ahmad Tajudin, Kayo Ueda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ambulance response times (ART) are critical determinants of both acute and chronic patient outcomes. However, little is known about the impact of extreme weather events such as heavy rainfall on ART. The objective of this study was to examine the association between heavy rainfall and ART. ART was calculated as the interval between the receipt time of an emergency call and the arrival of the ambulance at the patient’s location. We analyzed 170,195 dispatch records collected during the rainy seasons (June–September) from 2012 to 2018. Each record was matched with hourly rainfall data corresponding to the call time. A linear threshold model was applied to identify rainfall thresholds and estimate delays in ART across 15 cities in the Kyushu region of Japan. The median ART was 8 min, with the majority of dispatches (59.3%) attributed to acute illness. Hourly rainfall was significantly associated with longer ART, increasing by 0.84 min on average (95%CI 0.48, 1.25) for each additional 1 mm of rainfall exceeding the threshold of 34 mm. The impact varied by case severity: severe cases (hospitalization ≥ 3 weeks) had a lower threshold of 10 mm, with an estimated delay of 0.33 min (95% CI 0.33, 0.64), while mild cases (no hospitalization) had a higher threshold of 35 mm and a longer delay of 1.63 min (95% CI 0.64, 2.32). In the Kyushu region of Japan, there was a significant delay in ART when rainfall intensity reached 30 mm/hour. Transports involving severe patients were more sensitive to heavy rainfall events. These results highlight that extreme weather such as heavy rainfall can reduce the performance of emergency medical services.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32864-4.

Introduction

Emergency medical services (EMS) are essential for providing timely pre-hospital care, with ambulance response times (ART) serving as a key indicator of their efficiency and effectiveness. In life-threatening situations, such as severe trauma, acute diseases (e.g., acute myocardial infarction and stroke), pandemics, disasters, or extreme weather events, the speed of ART directly affects the quality of care provided to patients. Delays in ART can result in a significant deterioration of a patient’s condition, emphasizing the need for a robust EMS system1,2. Previous studies have demonstrated that longer ART are associated with worse health outcomes, including reduced survival rate in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest3,4, cardiovascular complications5, neurological disorders6, and mortality7. In contrast, shorter ART has been linked to improved patient outcomes8–10. Understanding the factors that contribute to longer ART is therefore essential for optimizing EMS performance and enhancing patient care.

Previous studies have documented various factors that can impact ART, including traffic conditions11, ambulance services demand12, geographical location13,14, and weather conditions such as temperature and rainfall15,16. Additionally, extreme weather such as temperatures including both hot and cold days, have been demonstrated to increase ambulance callouts and lengthen ART significantly17.

As the frequency, duration, and intensity of extreme weather events are projected to increase due to climate change18, understanding their impact on EMS is crucial for developing effective interventions to mitigate adverse outcomes. This aligns with Sustainable Development Goal 13 (SDG 13), which emphasizes the need to strengthen resilience and adaptive capacity to climate-related hazards19. Healthcare facilities play a vital role in achieving this objective by ensuring continuity of care and supporting community health during extreme events.

Japan is particularly vulnerable to a range of extreme weather events, especially those caused by heavy rainfall, such as floods and landslides20. These events can affect public health both directly through injuries, illnesses, and exacerbation of chronic conditions and indirectly by delaying ART and straining healthcare services. During the heavy rain events of 2018 and 2020, an increased demand for medical consultations was observed in the first week21,22. However, no study has yet examined the impact of heavy rainfall on ART.

Heavy rainfall could be a crucial factor contributing to delays in ART. Previous study has found a linear association between rainfall events and delays in ART16. Another study also emphasized that heavy rainfall events significantly increase ART compared to calls attended during fine weather11. We hypothesized that this effect arises through multiple mechanisms, including reduced visibility23, worsening road conditions24, decreased traffic speed24,25, and potential changes in driver behavior. Prior research has shown that heavy rainfall, particularly when combined with poor visibility, can substantially increase travel time on the road compared to light or moderate rainfall23. One study also reported that traffic speed decreased by approximately 8% under such conditions25. Despite this evidence, the rainfall intensity at which these delays become critical for patient transport, especially in emergency contexts, has not been well investigated. Identifying this threshold and its impact on ART is therefore crucial for developing mitigation strategies and optimizing resource allocation.

Given this context, our study aimed to investigate the association between rainfall events and ART using a threshold model. Specifically, we identified the rainfall intensity at which ART delays become significant and analyzed the linear association between rainfall and ART. This model assumes that the impact of rainfall on ART remains relatively stable up to a certain point but increases linearly once the threshold is exceeded26.

Methods

Study area and period

The study areas were selected based on the information from the Japanese Meteorological Agency (JMA) report titled “Weather events that caused disasters (1989 to present)”20. This report includes information on various extreme weather events such as storm surges, typhoons, heavy snowfall, and heavy rainfall, providing crucial details on the date and time of occurrence, affected areas, meteorological parameters and their intensity, and subsequent impacts. According to the report, a total of 51 cities across the Kyushu region experienced heavy rainfall between 2012 and 2018 (Supplementary Figure S1). However, after integrating this data with ambulance response data based on fire department locations and meteorological stations, only 15 cities were selected for the analysis (Fig. 1). The study period was limited to rainy seasons from June to September.

Ambulance response time (ART)

We used individual records from emergency ambulance dispatch data to calculate ART. This data was obtained from the Fire and Disaster Management Agency and included information such as the time of the call, the time of arrival at the patient’s location, the patient’s gender and age, the severity of the situation, and the cause of the call. Severity was determined by medical doctors upon hospital arrival and classified as: dead, critical, serious (hospitalization ≥ 3 weeks), moderate (hospitalization < 3 weeks), mild (no hospitalization), and others. The ART was calculated in minutes, based on the time from the call to the arrival at the scene. We excluded records where the ART was longer than 60 min or less than or equal to 0 min, as well as those that did not contain records of either the emergency call arrival or the arrival at the site.

Hourly meteorological variables

Hourly rainfall and temperature data were obtained from the JMA. In this study, we used data from both observational and automatic weather stations (Automated Meteorological Data Acquisition Systems (AMeDAS)) which covered all the cities of interest. When multiple stations were presented within a city, the hourly rainfall and temperature data were averaged for that city. Hourly meteorological data was integrated with ART at the city level. Subsequently, we matched each ambulance record with hourly meteorological data for the time the call was made. For example, if a call was made at 10:30 a.m., the rainfall and temperature recorded for that hour (10:00–11:00 a.m.) were assigned to the ambulance record.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted to examine differences in median ART. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare gender groups, while the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied to analyze differences across other groups, including age, cause of the call, and severity. We employed a mixed-effect and threshold model with a gamma distribution to examine the ART-heavy rainfall association. Gamma distribution was utilized because response variable is highly skewed (Supplementary Figure S2). The threshold linear model was introduced in this study to examine whether rainfall intensity exhibits a threshold effect on ART, particularly in the context of extreme weather events. We hypothesized that light or moderate rainfall may have a minimal impact on ART (Supplementary Figure S3), whereas heavy rainfall beyond a certain intensity could disproportionately delay ART.

We identified rainfall thresholds by creating a grid of potential thresholds, using a range of rainfall intensities across the Kyushu region from 1 to 82 mm (Supplementary Table S1). For each threshold, we fitted the mixed-effect model using the glmer() function (lme4 package, R version 4.3.0)28 and applied a gamma distribution to obtain maximum likelihood estimates. The models were adjusted for temperature, years, days of the week, and random effect terms for the city. A previous study has found that demand for ambulance services (number of calls) affected ART delays11. However, we did not adjust for this variable in our model as the data indicated that most of the calls were made during non-rainfall events (Supplementary Figure S4), and this variable may not influence the heavy rainfall-ART association as we also observed no significant difference between the model with and without the hourly number of calls. We determined the rainfall threshold by selecting the model that minimized the profile likelihood, resulting in the best-fitting model with the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC).

After establishing the threshold, we estimated the linear association between rainfall and ART, expressed as the increase in ART for each 1 mm increase in rainfall above the identified threshold. Stratification analysis was performed based on the severity of calls. We observed that the average and median ART for mild and severe cases were higher during heavy rain compared to no/light rain hours (Supplementary Table S2). However, the ART for moderate cases was not different for no/light rain and heavy rainfall. Subsequently, we performed the analyses to identify the threshold rainfall for mild and severe cases similar to the principal model. Furthermore, we performed the sensitivity analysis by varying the rainfall threshold to ensure the linear association between rainfall and ART. These thresholds were selected based on the five lowest AIC values identified in the principal model.

Results

Descriptive summary of ambulance dispatches

A total of 170,195 ambulance dispatch records with a median ART of 8 min (ranging from 6 to 11 min) were included in this study (Table 1). Over half of the calls (59.0%) were made for older adults, but the median response times did not differ significantly between age groups. Similarly, there was no significant difference in the proportion of calls or ART between males and females. The majority of calls (59.3%) were due to acute illness. However, the ART was longer for calls related to drowning, with a median ART of 13 min. Most of the calls were made for mild (36.4%) and moderate cases (48.6%).

Descriptive summary of rainfall

The average hourly rainfall of the Kyushu region is 0.5 mm, with the maximum hourly rainfall ranging from 35.3 to 82.3 mm across 15 cities (Fig. 2). Miyazaki city experienced the highest maximum hourly rainfall, reaching 82.3 mm, along with the highest frequency of heavy rainfall events exceeding 20 mm and 50 mm per hour throughout the study periods. The average and range of rainfall intensity of each city is presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Findings from threshold model

Using a linear threshold model, we identified a potential rainfall threshold of 34 mm, representing the intensity that produced the lowest AIC compared to other rainfall intensities (Supplementary Figure S5 and Table S3). The analysis showed that ART remained stable at approximately 8 min but increased by 0.84 min (95% Confidence Interval (CI): 0.48, 1.25) for every additional 1 mm of rainfall above the threshold (Fig. 3).

Stratification analyses revealed threshold rainfall intensities of 35 mm for mild cases and 10 mm for severe cases (Fig. 4). Rainfall significantly lengthened ART by 1.63 min (95% CI 0.94, 2.32) when exceeding 35 mm for mild cases, while for severe cases, ART increased by 0.33 min (95% CI 0.03, 0.64) after surpassing 10 mm. Sensitivity analyses indicated that the estimated effects of rainfall on ART did not significantly differ when the potential threshold rainfall varied between 33 and 37 mm (Supplementary Figure S7).

Discussion

This study found a significant impact of heavy rainfall on ambulance response time (ART) from 2012 to 2018. Delays in ART were observed when hourly rainfall intensity exceeded a threshold of 34 mm, with an approximately 1-minute increase in ART beyond this threshold, across 15 cities in the Kyushu region. Previous studies have examined the association between rainfall and ART employing linear model16. Additionally, a study in Singapore analyzed the impact of rainfall on ART using categorical variables, reporting that heavy rain resulted in longer ART compared to fine weather conditions11. However, this study did not specify how the rainfall categories were defined or the precise intensities associated with delays.

Previous epidemiological studies have investigated the effects of heavy rainfall on health and healthcare facilities, with heavy rainfall events defined in different ways, such as by official thresholds set by government agencies29, categorical groupings11, percentile-based cutoffs30,31, or recurrence intervals derived from historical data (retuning period)32. In contrast, this study employed an approach that incorporated the entire range of rainfall intensities into the model to identify potential thresholds. This method enables the determination of thresholds based on the goodness of fit of the model. According to the JMA33, heavy and very heavy rainfall are defined as hourly rainfall of 20–30 mm and 30–50 mm, respectively. This study’s findings indicate that rainfall intensities exceeding 34 mm are associated with significant delay ART, suggesting that heavy rainfall events substantially influenced the time of ambulance transport.

The stratification analyses revealed the difference between threshold rainfall and risk functions among mild and severe cases. For the mild cases, we observed a threshold rainfall of 35 mm, above this, the ART was significantly extended by a 1.63-minute increase. On the other hand, the threshold was much lower among the severe cases (10 mm) and ART increased by 0.33 min above this threshold. The lower threshold for severe cases may indicate a more urgent need for emergency response, as even moderate rainfall (10 mm/hr.) could further hinder the medical emergency service performance to reach patients quickly.

The possible explanation is the inherent urgency of the cases. For mild cases, which are less critical and do not require hospitalization, ART tends to be longer overall and may include additional buffer time to accommodate unexpected delays. This buffer could make response time less sensitive to rainfall until it exceeds the threshold. In contrast, for severe cases, EMS teams aim to minimize ART as much as possible. With little or no buffer time, even moderate rainfall can cause noticeable delays, making severe cases more vulnerable to disruptions. This is consistent with a previous study reporting shorter ART for severe cases compared with less severe ones34. These findings suggest that case severity not only affects baselines ART but also modifies its sensitivity to extreme weather conditions.

This study has some limitations. First, traffic conditions, which could influence the association between heavy rainfall and ART, were not included in the analysis. Prior research has identified various factors affecting traffic flow, such as road networks and congestion35. Additionally, the distance between fire departments and incident location was not accounted for due to data unavailability. While heavy rainfall may contribute to road deterioration and closures, other events such as natural disasters can also result in road closures36. Future studies should consider incorporating these variables, as it may significantly delay ambulance response times. Second, the study was conducted in the Kyushu region where a climatic zone is characterized by intensive rainfall. As a result, the findings may have limited generalizability to other locations with differing climatic and sociodemographic characteristics. Last, exposure misclassification may occur, as rainfall could be absent or have stopped during the response time even if that hour is classified as heavy rainfall. This could potentially bias the effect estimates toward overestimation. Using rainfall data with a finer temporal resolution would improve the accuracy of the estimates. In addition, considering additional dimensions of rainfall (e.g., frequency and duration) in future studies would help provide a more comprehensive understanding of how rainfall events affect ART.

Conclusion

In summary, heavy rainfall can impair the performance of EMS, particularly emergency ambulance transport. We observed that ART was significantly delayed once rainfall intensity reached 30 mm/hour across the Kyushu region of Japan. Transports involving severe patients were more sensitive to rainfall, showing earlier delays in ART compared with other patient groups. The findings of this study provide important insights to guild EMS preparedness and management during extreme weather events.
