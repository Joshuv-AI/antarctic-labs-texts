Abstract
. In this paper, we first show that the collection of all subsets of ℝ \mathbb{R} having lower dimension γ ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] \gamma\in[0,1] is dense in Π ⁡ ( ℝ ) \Pi(\mathbb{R}) , the space of compact subsets of ℝ \mathbb{R} . Furthermore, we show that the set of Borel probability measures with lower dimension β ∈ [ 0 , m ] \beta\in[0,m] is dense in Ω ⁡ ( ℝ m ) \Omega(\mathbb{R}^{m}) , the space of Borel probability measures on ℝ m \mathbb{R}^{m} . We also prove that the quantization and the lower dimension of a measure ϑ \vartheta coincide with those of the convolution of ϑ \vartheta with a finite combination of Dirac measures. In the end, we compute the lower dimension of the invariant measure associated with the product IFS.

1.1.
Delineation

In this paper, we study the density properties of various subsets of
\Pi(X)
, the collection of all compact subsets of a complete metric space
X
. Let
\Omega(X)
denote the collection of all Borel probability measures on
X
, where
X
is a Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{m}
of dimension
m
with Euclidean metric. We show that for any
0\leq\beta\leq m
, the set of Borel probability measures with lower dimension
m
forms a dense subset of
\Omega(X)
. Simultaneously, we demonstrate that the set of Borel probability measures with quantization dimension
\beta
is also dense in
\Omega(X)
. Furthermore, in Subsection
3.2
, we prove that the lower dimension of the invariant measure associated with a product IFS is equal to the sum of the lower dimensions of the invariant measures corresponding to the individual IFSs involved in the product.

Remark 1.1
.

Sometimes, the study of complex measures can be reduced to the analysis of simpler ones, such as Dirac measures or smooth measures, which are often more tractable analytically. Approximating a given measure by such simpler measures plays a crucial role, particularly when the goal is to preserve important geometric features, such as dimensional characteristics. This motivates us to approximate a given measure
\vartheta
by convolving it with a finite combination of Dirac measures, while ensuring that its dimensional properties are retained.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall some definitions and basic results for our paper. For any
F\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m},
we denote
|F|=\sup_{x,y\in F}\|x-y\|_{2}
and
\text{Card}(F)
as the diameter and cardinality of
F
, respectively.

Let
\Pi(\mathbb{R}^{m})
denote the collection of all compact nonempty subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with the Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|_{2}
. The Hausdorff distance
\mathfrak{H}
between any
E,F\in\Pi(\mathbb{R}^{m})
is elaborated as

\mathfrak{H}(E,F):=\inf\{\epsilon>0:E\subset F_{\epsilon}\text{ and }F\subset E_{\epsilon}\},

where
E_{\epsilon}
denotes the
\epsilon
-neighbourhood of
E.

Definition 2.1
.

[
19
]

Let
E
be a nonempty subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}.
The lower dimension of
E
is defined as

\displaystyle\dim_{L}(E):=\sup

\displaystyle\Big\{\beta:\exists~M>0\text{ such that,}\forall~0<r<R\leq|E|\text{ and }x\in E,

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102ptN_{r}(\mathcal{B}_{R}(x)\cap E)\geq M\Big(\frac{R}{r}\Big)^{\beta}\Big\},

where
\mathcal{B}_{R}(x)=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\|y-x\|_{2}\leq R\}
and
N_{r}(\mathcal{B}_{R}(x)\cap E)
is the smallest number of open set required for a
r
-cover of
\mathcal{B}_{R}(x)\cap E.

The properties of lower dimension can be visited in the book
[
19
]
. It is noted that the lower dimension only satisfies stability under closure and bi-Lipschitz mapping.

Definition 2.2
.

[
19
]

Let
\vartheta
be a Borel probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\text{supp}(\vartheta)
denote the support of
\vartheta.
Then the lower dimension of
\vartheta
is defined by

\displaystyle\dim_{L}(\vartheta)=\sup\Bigg\{

\displaystyle\beta\geq 0:\exists~M>0\text{ such that,}\forall~0<r<R\leq|E|\text{ and }x\in\text{supp}(\vartheta),

\displaystyle\hskip 165.02606pt\frac{\vartheta(\mathcal{B}_{R}(x))}{\vartheta(\mathcal{B}_{r}(x))}\geq M\Big(\frac{R}{r}\Big)^{\beta}\Bigg\},

otherwise it is
0.

We would like to mention here that if a measure
\vartheta
is doubling and fully supported on a closed set
F,
then
\dim_{L}(\vartheta)\leq\dim_{L}(F),

for details, see
[
19
, Lemma 4.1.2]
.

Definition 2.3
.

[
22
]

Let
\vartheta
be a Borel probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{m},~r\in(0,+\infty)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}.
Then, the
n
th quantization error of order
r
of
\vartheta
is stated as:

V_{n,r}(\vartheta):=\text{inf}\Big\{\int d(x,A)^{r}d\vartheta(x):A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m},\ \text{Card}(A)\leq n\Big\},

where
d(x,A)
denotes the distance of point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
to the set
A
with respect to the Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|_{2}
on
\mathbb{R}^{m}.

The quantization dimension of order
r
of
\vartheta
is defined by:

D_{r}(\vartheta):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{r\log n}{-\log(V_{n,r}(\vartheta))},

if the limit exists, otherwise, we define the lower and upper quantization dimensions of order
r
of
\vartheta
by taking the limit inferior and limit superior of the sequence, respectively.

Definition 2.4
.

[
22
]

For a given
s>0,
the
s
-dimensional lower and upper quantization coefficient of order
r
for
\vartheta
is defined by:

\liminf_{n\to\infty}n^{\frac{r}{s}}V_{n,r}(\vartheta),~\text{and}~\limsup_{n\to\infty}n^{\frac{r}{s}}V_{n,r}(\vartheta),

respectively.

Let
\Omega(\mathbb{R}^{m})
denote the set of all Borel probability measures on the Euclidean space
(\mathbb{R}^{m},\|.\|_{2})
. Then,

\displaystyle d_{L}(\vartheta,\theta):=\sup_{h\in\text{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}\Big\{\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}hd\vartheta-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}hd\theta\Big|\Big\},\ \ \quad\vartheta,\theta\in\Omega(\mathbb{R}^{m}),

defines a metric on
\Omega(\mathbb{R}^{m})
, where
\text{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{m})=\{h:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}:h\text{ is Lipschitz function}\\
\text{with Lipschitz constant }\leq 1\}
(see
[
38
,
43
]
). If
(X,d)
is a compact metric space, then

(\Omega(X),d_{L})
is a compact metric space (see
[
6
, Theorem 5.1]
).

Definition 2.5
.

Let
\vartheta,\theta\in\Omega(\mathbb{R}^{m}).
The convolution of
\vartheta
and
\theta
is denoted by
\vartheta*\theta
and defined by the push-forward of the product measure
\vartheta\times\theta
under the mapping
(x,y)\to x+y.
That is, for any
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we have

\vartheta*\theta(E)=(\vartheta\times\theta)\big(\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}:x+y\in E\}\big).

Definition 2.6
.

Let
\vartheta\in\Omega(\mathbb{R}^{m})
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.
The translation of
\vartheta
by
x
is defined as

(\vartheta+x)(E)=\vartheta(E+x),~~~E\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Let us define

\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{m})=\Big\{\vartheta\in\Omega(\mathbb{R}^{m}):\vartheta=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\delta_{x_{i}},~x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},~a_{i}>0,\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}=1,~~k\in\mathbb{N}\Big\}.

Here
\delta_{x}
denotes the Dirac measure supported at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.
It is observed that
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
is dense in
\Omega(\mathbb{R}^{m})
w.r.t.
d_{L}
on
\Omega(\mathbb{R}^{m}),
see
[
9
]
. The existence of an invariant measure supported on the attractor of the associated iterated function system is shown in
[
26
]
. Feng et al.
[
17
]
studied the convolutions of equicontractive self-similar measures on
\mathbb{R}.

We define essential supremum norm
\|.\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}
of a measurable function
g
with respect to a measure
\vartheta
by

\|g\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}=\inf\{C\geq 0:|f(x)|\leq C~\text{for $\vartheta$-almost every x}\}.

Let
\vartheta,\lambda\in\Omega(\mathbb{R}^{m})
be such that
\vartheta
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\lambda.
Then by Radon-Nikodym theorem, there exists a measurable function
g:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to[0,\infty)
such that

\vartheta(A)=\int_{A}gd\lambda,~~~\text{for any Borel set}~A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Further, the function
g
is called the Radon-Nikodym derivative or density of
\vartheta
with respect to
\lambda
, and is denoted by
\frac{d\vartheta}{d\lambda}.

We call
\vartheta
has
\mathcal{L}^{\infty}
-density if it is absolutely continuous with respect to
\lambda
, and density function
g
satisfying

\|g\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}<\infty.

Now we are ready to prove the upcoming result. Here and throughout the paper, we assume that
D_{r}(\vartheta)
exists whenever it occurs.

3.
Main Results

In the subsequent theorems, for the reader’s better understanding, as well as for simplicity and clarity, we present the proof on
\mathbb{R}
, where the main ideas are more transparent. The results can be extended to
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with suitable modifications, following a similar technique.

Theorem 3.1
.

For each
\gamma\in[0,1],
the set
L^{\gamma}=\{A\in\Pi(\mathbb{R}):\dim_{L}(A)=\gamma\}
is dense in
\Pi(\mathbb{R}).

Proof.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a compact set. Then, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exist points
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\in A
and open balls
\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(a_{1}),\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(a_{2}),\ldots,\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(a_{n})
with radius
\epsilon
such that
A\subset\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(a_{j})
, and
\mathcal{B}_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(a_{i})\cap\mathcal{B}_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(a_{j})=\emptyset
for
i\neq j
. Define the finite set
A_{*}=\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\}\in\Pi(\mathbb{R})
, without any loss one may assume
a_{1}<a_{2}<\dots<a_{n}
. Since the lower dimension of a set with isolated points is zero (see
[
19
]
), then
\dim_{L}(A_{*})=0
. In view of the definition of the Hausdorff metric, we obtain

\mathfrak{H}(A,A_{*})<\epsilon\quad\text{and}\quad\dim_{L}(A_{*})=0.

This establishes the claim for the case
\gamma=0
.
Now, assume
\gamma\in(0,1]
. By
[
11
, Theorem 1]
, there exists a compact set
A_{1}\subset\mathcal{B}_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(a_{1})
such that
\dim_{L}(A_{1})=\gamma
. Since the lower dimension is translation invariant, we may assume without loss of generality that
a_{1}\in A_{1}
.
Choose
\delta=a_{2}-a_{1}
, and consider the
\delta
-translation of
A_{1}
, denoted by
A_{2}
. Then
a_{2}\in A_{2}
. Moreover, for any
y\in A_{2}
, there exists
x\in A_{1}
such that

y=x+\delta=x+(a_{2}-a_{1}).

It follows that

d(y,a_{2})=|x+(a_{2}-a_{1})-a_{2}|=|x-a_{1}|<\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}.

Thus,
A_{2}\subset\mathcal{B}_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(a_{2})
with
\dim_{L}(A_{2})=\gamma
. Repeating this procedure for each
j
, we construct sets
A_{j}\subset\mathcal{B}_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(a_{j})
satisfying
\dim_{L}(A_{j})=\gamma
and
A_{i}\cap A_{j}=\emptyset
for
i\neq j
.
Define
A^{\prime}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}A_{j}
. Since for
i\neq j
,
A_{i}
and
A_{j}
are disjoint, it follows that
\inf_{x\in A_{i},y\in A_{j}}d(x,y)>0.
Then, by
[
19
, Lemma 3.4.9]
, we have
\dim_{L}(A^{\prime})=\gamma,
and by the definition of the Hausdorff metric and construction of the set
A
, we obtained
\mathfrak{H}(A,A^{\prime})<\epsilon,
which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

The proof given in
[
52
, Theorem 3.8]
is not applicable in the case of the lower dimension, since, unlike the Assouad dimension, the lower dimension does not satisfy the open set and the finite stability property.

It is noted that in general lower dimension is not comparable to the Hausdorff dimension of set, as an example
\dim_{L}(\mathbb{Q})=1,~\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathbb{Q})=0
and
\dim_{L}([0,1]\cup\{2\})=0,~\dim_{\mathcal{H}}([0,1]\cup\{2\})=1
(see
[
19
]
). Larman
[
35
]
showed that if
F
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m},
then

\dim_{L}(F)\leq\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(F).
Further, if
F
is compact and Ahlfors regular, then
\dim_{L}(F)=\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(F)
(see
[
19
, Theorem 6.4.1]
). Thus, it is worth mentioning next few results on the approximation of a compact set preserving the lower dimension and the Hausdorff dimension.

Theorem 3.3
.

The set
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{H}L}=\{A\in\Pi(\mathbb{R}):\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(A)\neq\dim_{L}(A)\}
is dense in
\Pi(\mathbb{R}).

Proof.

Observe that the set
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{H}L}
is non-empty, as the Bedford–McMullen carpet with non-uniform fibers has the Hausdorff dimension strictly greater than its lower dimension (see
[
20
, Theorem 2.1]
). Also, for any bounded closed interval
I
and
a\in\mathbb{R}
such that
a\notin I
, the Hausdorff dimension of
I\cup\{a\}
is
1
, but the lower dimension is
0
. This concludes that the set
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{H}L}
is non-empty.
Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}
be a compact set. Then, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exist points
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}
and open balls
\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(a_{1}),\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(a_{2}),\ldots,\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(a_{n})
such that
A\subset\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(a_{j})
, with
\mathcal{B}_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(a_{i})\cap\mathcal{B}_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(a_{j})=\emptyset
for
i\neq j
. Define
A_{*}=\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(a_{1})\cup\{a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\}
. Since the Hausdorff dimension satisfies the open set property and
A_{*}
has isolated points, we have
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(A_{*})=1
and
\dim_{L}(A_{*})=0
. Furthermore, by the definition of
A_{*}
, we have
\mathfrak{H}(A,A_{*})<\epsilon
. This completes the assertion.

∎

Theorem 3.4
.

The set
\mathcal{D}=\{A\in\Pi(\mathbb{R}):\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(A)=\dim_{L}(A)\}
is dense in
\Pi(\mathbb{R}).

Proof.

Since the Hausdorff and lower dimensions of the singleton set
\{a\}
, for any
a\in\mathbb{R}
, are zero, the set
\mathcal{D}
is non-empty. Let
A\in\Pi(\mathbb{R})
be a compact set. Then, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exist points
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\in A
such that
A=\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(a_{j})
,
where
\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(a_{j})
denotes the open ball around
a_{j}
of radius
\epsilon
. We may assume that the balls
\mathcal{B}_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(a_{i})
and
\mathcal{B}_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(a_{j})
are disjoint for
i\neq j
. Define

A^{\prime}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{B}_{\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}}(a_{j}).

Since the open balls
\mathcal{B}_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(a_{i})
and
\mathcal{B}_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(a_{j})
are disjoint for
i\neq j
, it follows that

\inf_{x\in\mathcal{B}_{\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}}(a_{i}),\,y\in\mathcal{B}_{\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}}(a_{j})}d(x,y)>0\quad\text{for }i\neq j.

Then, by
[
19
, Lemma 3.4.9]
, we have
\dim_{L}(A^{\prime})=1
. Also, by the countable stability and the open set property of the Hausdorff dimension, we have
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(A^{\prime})=1
. In view of the definition of the Hausdorff metric and the construction of the set
A^{\prime}
, we obtain
\mathfrak{H}(A,A^{\prime})<\epsilon
, which completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 3.5
.

For each
\gamma\in[0,1].
The set
\mathcal{D}=\{A\in\Pi(\mathbb{R}):\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(A)=\dim_{L}(A)=\gamma\}
is dense in
\Pi(\mathbb{R}).

Proof.

In the case of
\gamma=0
, for any
\epsilon>0
, we construct the set
A_{*}
as in Theorem
3.1
. Then
\dim_{L}(A_{*})=\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(A_{*})=0
, and
\mathfrak{H}(A,A_{*})<\epsilon
. This shows that the assertion is true for
\gamma=0
.
Now, let
\gamma\in(0,1]
. Then there exists an Ahlfors-regular set
A
of dimension
\gamma
(see
[
4
]
). Following the same approach as in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, the result can be obtained. Hence, we omit the proof.

∎

3.1.
Applications in dimension-preserving approximation of measures

This subsection is dedicated for the approximation of a probability measure preserving lower dimension and quantization dimension. Furthermore, it addresses whether, if one probability measure is absolutely continuous with respect to another probability measure, one can establish a relationship between their quantization dimension. We begin by establishing some lemmas necessary for our main results.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R})=\big\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\delta_{x_{j}}:x_{j}\in\mathbb{R},a_{j}>0,\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}=1,n\in\mathbb{N}\big\}
and
\vartheta
be a probability measure with finite support. Then, for a fixed
\omega\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}),

\dim_{L}(\vartheta*\omega)=\dim_{L}(\vartheta),

where
\vartheta*\omega
is the convolution of
\vartheta
and
\omega.

Proof.

Let
\vartheta
be a probability measure with finite support. We define sets

\displaystyle A_{\vartheta}=\Bigg\{

\displaystyle\beta\geq 0:\exists~M>0\text{ such that},\forall~0<r<R<|\text{supp}(\vartheta)|\text{ and }x\in\text{supp}(\vartheta),

\displaystyle\hskip 199.16928pt\frac{\vartheta(\mathcal{B}_{R}(x))}{\vartheta(\mathcal{B}_{r}(x))}\geq M\Big(\frac{R}{r}\Big)^{\beta}\Bigg\},

\displaystyle A_{\vartheta*\omega}=\Bigg\{

\displaystyle\gamma\geq 0:\exists~M>0\text{ such that},\forall~0<r<R<|\text{supp}(\vartheta*\omega)|\text{ and }

\displaystyle\hskip 102.43008ptx\in\text{supp}(\vartheta*\omega),\frac{\vartheta*\omega(\mathcal{B}_{R}(x))}{\vartheta*\omega(\mathcal{B}_{r}(x))}\geq M\Big(\frac{R}{r}\Big)^{\gamma}\Bigg\}.

Then, by the definition of the lower dimension for any
s\in A_{\vartheta}
, there exists a constant
M
for any
x\in\text{supp}(\vartheta)
and
0<r<R<|\text{supp}(\vartheta)|,
we have

\frac{\vartheta(\mathcal{B}_{R}(x))}{\vartheta(\mathcal{B}_{r}(x))}\geq M\bigg(\frac{R}{r}\bigg)^{s}.

Note that, for the Dirac measure
\delta_{y}
supported on
y\in\mathbb{R}
the support of the convolution
\text{supp}(\vartheta*\delta_{y})=\text{supp}(\vartheta)+\{y\}
, for each
x\in\text{supp}(\vartheta*\delta_{y})
, we have
z=x-y\in\text{supp}(\vartheta)
.
Now, let
\omega=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\delta_{x_{j}}
be a probability measure in
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R})
; then for any
x\in\text{supp}(\vartheta*\omega)
and
0<r<R<|\text{supp}(\vartheta*\omega)|,
we have

\displaystyle\frac{(\vartheta*\omega)(\mathcal{B}_{R}(x))}{(\vartheta*\omega)(\mathcal{B}_{r}(x))}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\vartheta*\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\delta_{x_{j}})(\mathcal{B}_{R}(x))}{(\vartheta*\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\delta_{x_{j}})(\mathcal{B}_{r}(x))}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\vartheta(\mathcal{B}_{R}(x)-x_{j})}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\vartheta(\mathcal{B}_{r}(x)-x_{j})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\vartheta(\mathcal{B}_{R}(x-x_{j}))}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\vartheta(\mathcal{B}_{r}(x-x_{j},))}\geq\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}M\bigg(\frac{R}{r}\bigg)^{s}\vartheta(\mathcal{B}_{r}(x-x_{j}))}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\vartheta(\mathcal{B}_{r}(x-x_{j}))}

\displaystyle\geq M\bigg(\frac{R}{r}\bigg)^{s}.

In the above expression, the fourth inequality arrived due to the fact that
x-x_{j}\in\text{supp}(\vartheta).
Thus, it implies that
s\in A_{\vartheta*\omega}.

Again, let
s\in A_{\vartheta}^{C}
be any real number. Then, for some
x\in\text{supp}(\vartheta)
and
0<r<R<|\text{supp}(\vartheta)|
, we have

\frac{\vartheta(\mathcal{B}_{R}(x))}{\vartheta(\mathcal{B}_{r}(x))}<M\bigg(\frac{R}{r}\bigg)^{s}.

Notice that for
x\in\text{supp}(\vartheta),~x+x_{j}\in\text{supp}(\vartheta*\omega).
Consider

\displaystyle\frac{(\vartheta*\omega)(\mathcal{B}_{R}(x+x_{j}))}{(\vartheta*\omega)(\mathcal{B}_{r}(x+x_{j}))}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\vartheta(\mathcal{B}_{R}(x))}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\vartheta(\mathcal{B}_{r}(x))}

\displaystyle<\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}M\bigg(\frac{R}{r}\bigg)^{s}\vartheta(\mathcal{B}_{r}(x))}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\vartheta(\mathcal{B}_{r}(x))}

\displaystyle<M\bigg(\frac{R}{r}\bigg)^{s}.

This implies that
s\in A_{\vartheta*\omega}^{C},
concluding the assertion.
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\vartheta
be a Borel probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\beta
be a positive real number. Then
\dim_{L}(\vartheta)=\dim_{L}(\theta),
where
\theta(E)=\vartheta(\beta E),
for all Borel set
E.

Proof.

Let
A_{\vartheta}
and
A_{\theta}
be the sets defined similarly as in Lemma
3.6
with respect to the measures
\vartheta
and
\theta
, respectively. Observe that support of
\theta
is
\frac{1}{\beta}\text{supp}(\vartheta),
and
|\text{supp}(\theta)|=|\frac{1}{\beta}\text{supp}(\vartheta)|.
For any
s\in A_{\vartheta},~~x\in\text{supp}(\theta),
and
0<r<R<|\text{supp}(\theta)|,
we have

\frac{\theta(\mathcal{B}_{R}(x))}{\theta(\mathcal{B}_{r}(x))}=\frac{\theta(\mathcal{B}_{R}(\frac{y}{\beta}))}{\theta(\mathcal{B}_{r}(\frac{y}{\beta}))},

for some
y\in\text{supp}(\vartheta).
Also,
\beta\mathcal{B}_{r}(x)=\mathcal{B}_{\beta r}(\beta x),
and
0<\beta r<\beta R<|\text{supp}(\vartheta)|
whenever
0<r<R<|\text{supp}(\theta)|.
Now, since
\theta(D)=\vartheta(\beta D)
for any Borel set

, we obtain

\displaystyle\hskip 54.06006pt\frac{\theta(\mathcal{B}_{R}(\frac{y}{\beta}))}{\theta(\mathcal{B}_{r}(\frac{y}{\beta}))}=\frac{\vartheta(\beta\mathcal{B}_{R}(\frac{y}{\beta}))}{\vartheta(\beta\mathcal{B}_{r}(\frac{y}{\beta}))}=\frac{\vartheta(\mathcal{B}_{\beta R}(y))}{\vartheta(\mathcal{B}_{\beta r}(y))}\geq M\bigg(\frac{R}{r}\bigg)^{s}.

This implies that
s\in A_{\theta}.

Similarly for any
s\in A_{\vartheta}^{C},
we obtained

\frac{\theta(\mathcal{B}_{R}(x))}{\theta(\mathcal{B}_{r}(x))}=\frac{\vartheta(\mathcal{B}_{\beta R}(y))}{\vartheta(\mathcal{B}_{\beta r}(y))}<M\bigg(\frac{R}{r}\bigg)^{s},
for some

and
0<r<R<|\text{supp}(\vartheta)|.
this implies that

Thus, we get the required result.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
\vartheta,\theta
be finite Borel measures. Then we have that

D_{r}(\vartheta+\theta)=\max\{D_{r}(\theta),D_{r}(\vartheta)\}.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle V_{n,r}(\vartheta+\theta)=

\displaystyle\inf\Big\{\int d(x,A)^{r}d(\vartheta+\theta)(x):A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m},~\text{Card}(A)\leq n\Big\}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\inf\Big\{\int d(x,A)^{r}d\vartheta(x):A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m},~\text{Card}(A)\leq n\Big\}

\displaystyle+\inf\Big\{\int d(x,A)^{r}d\theta(x):A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m},~\text{Card}(A)\leq n\Big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V_{n,r}(\vartheta)+V_{n,r}(\theta).

By the definition of quantization dimension, it follows that

D_{r}(\vartheta+\theta)\geq\max\{D_{r}(\vartheta),D_{r}(\theta)\}.

Now, let
s>\max\{D_{r}(\vartheta),D_{r}(\theta)\}.
In view of
[
22
, Propostion
11.3
]
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{\frac{r}{s}}V_{n,r}(\vartheta)=0~~~\text{and}~~~~\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{\frac{r}{s}}V_{n,r}(\theta)=0.

Let
\epsilon>0.
Then there exist natural numbers
N_{1}(\vartheta,\epsilon)
and
N_{2}(\theta,\epsilon)
such that

n^{\frac{r}{s}}V_{n,r}(\vartheta)<\epsilon~~~\forall~~n\geq N_{1},~\text{and}~~n^{\frac{r}{s}}V_{n,r}(\theta)<\epsilon~~~\forall~~n\geq N_{2}.

Choose
N_{0}=\max\{N_{1},N_{2}\},
we immediately have

n^{\frac{r}{s}}V_{n,r}(\vartheta)<\epsilon~\text{and}~~n^{\frac{r}{s}}V_{n,r}(\theta)<\epsilon~~~\forall~~n\geq N_{0}.

Further, using the definition of
n
-th quantization error of order
r
of a measure, there exist sets
A(n,\vartheta,\epsilon)
and
B(n,\theta,\epsilon)
satisfying the following:

n^{\frac{r}{s}}\int d\Big(x,A(n,\vartheta,\epsilon)\Big)^{r}d\vartheta(x)<\epsilon~~\text{ and }~~n^{\frac{r}{s}}\int d\Big(x,B(n,\theta,\epsilon)\Big)^{r}d\theta(x)<\epsilon~~\forall~~n\geq N_{0}.

Since
\text{Card}\Big(A(n,\vartheta,\epsilon)\Big)\leq n
and
\text{Card}\Big(B(n,\theta,\epsilon)\Big)\leq n
, it follows that

\displaystyle\text{Card}\Big(A(n,\vartheta,\epsilon)\cup B(n,\theta,\epsilon)\Big)\leq 2n,\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle(2n)^{\frac{r}{s}}\int d\Big(x,A(n,\vartheta,\epsilon)\cup B(n,\theta,\epsilon)\Big)^{r}d\vartheta(x)<2^{\frac{r}{s}}\epsilon,

for every
n\geq N_{0}.

Using all the above estimates, we have

\displaystyle(2n)^{\frac{r}{s}}

\displaystyle V_{2n,r}(\vartheta+\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2n)^{\frac{r}{s}}~\inf\Big\{\int d(x,A)^{r}d(\vartheta+\theta)(x):A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m},~\text{Card}(A)\leq 2n\Big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2n)^{\frac{r}{s}}~\inf\Big\{\int d(x,A)^{r}d\vartheta(x)+\int d(x,A)^{r}d\theta(x):A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m},~\text{Card}(A)\leq 2n\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(2n)^{\frac{r}{s}}\int d\Big(x,A(n,\vartheta,\epsilon)\cup B(n,\theta,\epsilon)\Big)^{r}d\vartheta(x)+

\displaystyle(2n)^{\frac{r}{s}}\int d\Big(x,A(n,\vartheta,\epsilon)\cup B(n,\theta,\epsilon)\Big)^{r}d\theta(x)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 2^{\frac{r}{s}+1}\epsilon.

Since
\lim_{n\to\infty}(2n)^{\frac{r}{s}}V_{2n,r}(\vartheta+\theta)=0,
we have
D_{r}(\vartheta+\theta)<s.
Further, since
s>\max\{D_{r}(\vartheta),D_{r}(\theta)\}
is arbitrary, it follows that
D_{r}(\vartheta+\theta)\leq\max\{D_{r}(\vartheta),D_{r}(\theta)\}.
This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\vartheta
be a finite Borel measure. For a fixed
\theta\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
, we have that

D_{r}(\vartheta*\theta)=D_{r}(\vartheta).

Proof.

Let
\theta\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{m}).
By definition of
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{m}),
we write
\theta=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}
for some
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
a_{i}\geq 0
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}=1.
Now, note that

\vartheta*\theta=\vartheta*\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}\Big)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\vartheta*\delta_{x_{i}}.

Using Lemma
3.8
, we have

D_{r}(\vartheta*\theta)=D_{r}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\vartheta*\delta_{x_{i}}\Big)=\max_{1\leq i\leq k}\{D_{r}(a_{i}\vartheta*\delta_{x_{i}})\}=\max_{1\leq i\leq k}\{D_{r}(\vartheta*\delta_{x_{i}})\}.

From the above, it is enough to prove that
D_{r}(\vartheta*\delta_{x_{i}})=D_{r}(\vartheta).
We proceed as follows.

\displaystyle V_{n,r}(\vartheta*\delta_{x_{i}})

\displaystyle=\inf\Big\{\int d(x,A)^{r}d(\vartheta*\delta_{x_{i}})(x):A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m},\,\text{Card}(A)\leq n\Big\}

\displaystyle=\inf\Big\{\int d(x+x_{i},A)^{r}d\vartheta(x):A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m},\,\text{Card}(A)\leq n\Big\}

\displaystyle=\inf\Big\{\int d(x+x_{i},B+x_{i})^{r}d\vartheta(x):B=A-x_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m},\,\text{Card}(A)\leq n\Big\}

\displaystyle=\inf\Big\{\int d(x,B)^{r}d\vartheta(x):B\subset\mathbb{R}^{m},\,\text{Card}(B)\leq n\Big\}

\displaystyle=V_{n,r}(\vartheta).

This completes the proof of the lemma.

Remark 3.11

Note 3.12

It is worth noting that for any compactly supported Borel probability measure

Remark 3.15
.

It is known that for any closed set

, the lower dimension satisfies
\dim_{L}(E)=\sup\{\dim_{L}(\vartheta):\operatorname{supp}(\vartheta)=E\}
(see
[

is a closed set containing isolated points, then any Borel probability measure

In the following theorem, we prove the relationship between the quantization dimensions of two measures when one is absolutely continuous with respect to the other. A similar result was proved by Zhu in his thesis
[
57
]
; however, our approach is different.

Theorem 3.16
.

Let
\vartheta,\theta\in\Omega(\mathbb{R}^{m})
such that
\vartheta
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\theta
with
\mathcal{L}^{\infty}
-density. Then,
D_{r}(\vartheta)\leq D_{r}(\theta).

Proof.

By Radon-Nikodym theorem and hypothesis taken in the theorem, there exists a measurable
\mathcal{L}^{\infty}
-function
g:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to[0,\infty)
such that

\vartheta(A)=\int_{A}gd\theta,~~~\text{for any Borel set}~A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Now, we have

\displaystyle V_{n,r}(\vartheta)=

\displaystyle\inf\Big\{\int d(x,A)^{r}d\vartheta(x):A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m},\,\text{Card}(A)\leq n\Big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\Big\{\int d(x,A)^{r}g(x)d\theta(x):A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m},\,\text{Card}(A)\leq n\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|g\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}\inf\Big\{\int d(x,A)^{r}d\theta(x):A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m},~\text{Card}(A)\leq n\Big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|g\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}V_{n,r}(\theta),

where
\|g\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}
represents essential supremum norm of
g
with respect to measure
\theta.

This further yields

D_{r}(\vartheta)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{r\log n}{-\log\big(V_{n,r}(\vartheta)\big)}\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{r\log n}{-\log\big(\|g\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}V_{n,r}(\theta)\big)}=D_{r}(\theta).

Thus, the proof of the theorem is established.
∎

Let

.

In this way, for every measure belonging to

Example 3.20
.

Let

(see

Substituting the above values yields

Furthermore, if the IFSs

[
19
, Theorem 7.4.1]
, we have

\displaystyle\dim_{L}(\vartheta)

\displaystyle=\min_{i,j}\frac{\log p_{i}q_{j}}{\log c}=\min_{i,j}\left(\frac{\log p_{i}}{\log c}+\frac{\log q_{j}}{\log c}\right)

\displaystyle=\min_{i}\frac{\log p_{i}}{\log c}+\min_{i}\frac{\log q_{i}}{\log c}=s+t.

Thus, we have
\dim_{L}(\vartheta)=s+t.

∎

Note 3.22
.

In
[
47
, Theorem 6.6]
, it is shown that if
\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is an IFS satisfying the ESC, and
\vartheta
is a self-similar measure associated with a probability vector

Open 3.23

Open 3.24
