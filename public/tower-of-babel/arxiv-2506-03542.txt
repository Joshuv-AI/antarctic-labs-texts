Learning Monotonic Probabilities with a Generative Cost Model
Yongxiang Tang, Yanhua Cheng, Xiaocheng Liu, Chenchen Jiao, Yanxiang Zeng, Ning Luo, Pengjia Yuan, Xialong Liu, Peng Jiang

Abstract
In many machine learning tasks, it is often necessary for the relationship between input and output variables to be monotonic, including both strictly monotonic and implicitly monotonic relationships. Traditional methods for maintaining monotonicity mainly rely on construction or regularization techniques, whereas this paper shows that the issue of strict monotonic probability can be viewed as a partial order between an observable revenue variable and a latent cost variable. This perspective enables us to reformulate the monotonicity challenge into modeling the latent cost variable. To tackle this, we introduce a generative network for the latent cost variable, termed the Generative Cost Model ( GCM ), which inherently addresses the strict monotonic problem, and propose the Implicit Generative Cost Model ( IGCM ) to address the implicit monotonic problem. We further validate our approach with a numerical simulation of quantile regression and conduct multiple experiments on public datasets, showing that our method significantly outperforms existing monotonic modeling techniques. The code for our experiments can be found at https://github.com/tyxaaron/GCM .



1
Introduction

Many machine learning problems exhibit a monotonic relationship between inputs and outputs. These problems can be divided into two types: the first is strict monotonicity, where an increase in the input is assured to yield an increase in the output. For instance, the relationship between equipment usability and its age, or the relationship between auction winning rates and bidding prices. The second type is implicit monotonicity, often shown through correlations, such as those between an individual’s height and weight or between a company’s stock price and its yearly earnings. For both types of monotonic problems, it’s necessary to use a model that can predict the monotonic probability based on particular inputs. We call these input monotone variables as
revenue variables
, where higher revenue correlates with an increased probability of a more positive response.

Common deep learning methods to address the monotonicity problem can be broadly categorized into two types
(
Runje & Shankaranarayana 2023
)
: monotonic by
construction
and by
regularization
. The construction approach maintains strict monotonicity through customized structures in deep neural networks, such as nonconvex or nonconcave monotonic activation functions, positive weight matrices, and min-max structures
(
Sill 1997
)
. On the other hand, the regularization approach promotes monotonicity by designing specific loss functions
(
Sill & Abu-Mostafa 1996
)
; however, these approaches do not guarantee strict monotonicity.

Unlike conventional approaches, this paper introduces a
generative
framework to tackle the monotonicity problem. In our approach to estimating
p(\textnormal{y}|\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}})
, where
\textnormal{y}\in\mathbb{R}
is a response variable that maintains monotonicity with respect to the revenue variable
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
but not necessarily with
\bm{\mathbf{x}}
, we employ a two-step process. (i) The regression task is converted into a classification task via variable substitution, reducing
y
to a binary state (
0
or
1
). (ii) We proved that solving the monotonic binary classification problem is equivalent to identifying the latent
cost variable

\bm{\mathbf{c}}
, such that
\bm{\mathbf{c}}\perp\!\!\!\perp\bm{\mathbf{r}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{x}}
and
\textnormal{y}\mid\{\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}\}\overset{d}{=}\mathbb{I}(\bm{\mathbf{c}}\prec\bm{\mathbf{r}})\mid\{\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}\}
. Notably,
\overset{d}{=}
indicates that two variables share an identical distribution,
\prec
denotes the partial order in the vector space, and
\mathbb{I}
is the indicator function. Consequently, we shift focus to modeling
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
and eliminate the need to design a strictly monotonic function, granting us increased flexibility in modeling
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
.

This paper introduces a combined generative process for
\bm{\mathbf{x}}
,
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
,
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
and
y
. Although direct co-generation of
\bm{\mathbf{x}}
,
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
, and
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
via a single latent variable is not feasible since
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
must be conditionally independent of
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
given
\bm{\mathbf{x}}
, i.e.,
\bm{\mathbf{c}}\perp\!\!\!\perp\bm{\mathbf{r}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{x}}
. To address this, we independently draw two different latent variables
\bm{\mathbf{z}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{w}}
from Gaussian priors and independently sample
\bm{\mathbf{x}}
,
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
, and
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
from
p(\bm{\mathbf{x}}|\bm{\mathbf{z}})
,
p(\bm{\mathbf{c}}|\bm{\mathbf{z}})
, and
p(\bm{\mathbf{r}}|\bm{\mathbf{w}},\bm{\mathbf{x}})
, ensuring
\bm{\mathbf{c}}\perp\!\!\!\perp\bm{\mathbf{r}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{x}}
. Subsequently,
y
is generated as
\textnormal{y}=\mathbb{I}(\bm{\mathbf{c}}\prec\bm{\mathbf{r}})
. For estimating the posterior
p(\bm{\mathbf{z}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}},\textnormal{y})
, we employ a variational model
q_{\phi}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}})
, which is learned by optimizing the evidence lower bound (ELBO). This approach is termed the generative cost model (
GCM
).

Additionally, to capture implicit monotonicity, we alter the GCM generation procedure by integrating a latent kernel variable
\bm{\mathbf{k}}
. This guarantees that both
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
and
y
exhibit monotonic behavior concerning
\bm{\mathbf{k}}
. We refer to this refined method as the implicit generative cost model (
IGCM
). While this approach does not uphold strict monotonicity, it is better aligned with practical modeling applications.

In the last part, we conduct two types of experiments. First, we design a numerical simulation for quantile regression where the predicted
r
-th quantile increases monotonically with respect to the variable
\textnormal{r}\in(0,1)
. We evaluate the performance of traditional techniques versus our GCM approach, demonstrating that our method exhibits enhanced accuracy. To further evaluate the monotonic problem with a multivariate revenue variable
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
, we conduct experiments on six publicly available datasets: the Adult dataset
(
Becker & Kohavi 1996
)
, the COMPAS dataset
(
Larson et al. 2016
)
, the Diabetes dataset
(
Teboul
)
, the Blog Feedback dataset
(
Buza 2014
)
, the Loan Defaulter dataset and the Auto MPG dataset
(
Quinlan 1993
)
. In most tasks, our model outperforms existing approaches. Further ablation studies and time complexity analysis are available in the appendix.

The main contributions of our paper are summarized as follows:

•

We introduce a universal technique that reformulates the problem of monotonic probability into a modeling problem for latent cost variables, avoiding restrictions in conventional monotonic neural networks.

•

We address the modeling of the cost variable using generative approaches, including the generative cost model (GCM) for strict monotonic problems and the implicit generative cost model (IGCM) for implicit monotonic problems.

•

We evaluate our method through simulated quantile regression and machine learning tasks on multiple public datasets, demonstrating that our method consistently outperforms traditional monotonic models.

Definition 2.4

(Monotonic Conditional Probability)
.

A conditional probability

is defined as monotonic if and only if
\bm{\mathbf{y}}\mid\{\bm{\mathbf{r}}=\bm{r}_{1}\}\prec_{\rm r.v.}\bm{\mathbf{y}}\mid\{\bm{\mathbf{r}}=\bm{r}_{2}\}
for any pair of vectors
\bm{r}_{1}\prec\bm{r}_{2}
.

Alternatively,

\Pr(\bm{\mathbf{y}}\succ\bm{t}|\bm{\mathbf{r}}=\bm{r}_{1})<\Pr(\bm{\mathbf{y}}\succ\bm{t}|\bm{\mathbf{r}}=\bm{r}_{2})

for any pair of vectors
\bm{r}_{1}\prec\bm{r}_{2}
. Within this paper, we refer to this relationship between
\bm{\mathbf{y}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
as:
\bm{\mathbf{y}}
being (conditionally) monotonic (increasing) with respect to
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
. Throughout this paper, all discussions of monotonicity are assumed to be of the increasing type; for decreasing cases, the original variables can be substituted with their negatives.

3
Related Work

Monotonic Modeling.

In numerous machine learning tasks, we hold the prior knowledge that the output should be monotonic with respect to certain input variables. A straightforward idea is to identify a monotonic function and optimize its parameters to approximate the desired monotonic output. It can be formulated as follows:

\displaystyle{\rm minimize}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\textnormal{y},G_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}))

\displaystyle{\rm subject\ to}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial G_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}})}{\partial\bm{\mathbf{r}}}\succ\bm{\mathbf{0}}.

The Min-Max architecture
(
Sill 1997
)
represents a foundational advancement in monotonic neural networks, utilizing a piecewise linear model to approximate monotonic target functions. This architecture maintains monotonicity through (i) positive weight matrices, (ii) monotonic activation functions, and (iii) a Min-Max structure.

Continuing in the direction of monotonic by construction,
(
Nolte et al. 2022
)
introduced the Lipschitz monotonic network, enhancing robustness through weight constraint.
(
Igel 2024
)
proposed the smoothed min-max monotonic network by substituting the classic Min-Max structure with a smoothed log-sum-exp function, which prevents the neurons from becoming silent.
(
Runje & Shankaranarayana 2023
)
developed the constrained monotonic neural network, refining the approximation of nonconvex functions through adjusted activation functions. Furthermore,
(
Kim & Lee 2024
)
addressed the scalability limitations present in traditional monotonic models.

An alternative approach of monotonic modeling is through regularization strategies, which can be formulated as:

\displaystyle{\rm minimize}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\textnormal{y},G_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}))+\mathcal{R}(G_{\theta}).

Here, the regularization
\mathcal{R}(G_{\theta})>0
if
G_{\theta}
is not monotonic at some points, while
\mathcal{R}(G_{\theta})=0
if monotonicity is preserved. Crucially, the entire term
\mathcal{R}(G_{\theta})
is differentiable with respect to
\theta
, ensuring that the model adheres more closely to monotonicity. This direction includes monotonicity hints
(
Sill & Abu-Mostafa 1996
)
, which employ hint samples and pairwise loss to guide model learning. Certified monotonic neural networks
(
Liu et al. 2020
)
certify monotonicity by verifying the lower bound of the partial derivative of monotonic features.
(
Gupta et al. 2019
)
proposed a pointwise penalization method for negative gradients. Furthermore, counter example guided methods were introduced by
(
Sivaraman et al. 2020
)
.

Additionally, lattice networks
(
Garcia & Gupta 2009
)
address the monotonic problem via construction or regularization techniques; extensive works have been carried out in this area by
(
Milani Fard et al. 2016
;
You et al. 2017
;
Gupta et al. 2019
;
Yanagisawa et al. 2022
)
.

Monotonicity is a significant concept in various machine learning domains.
(
Ben-David 1995
;
Lee et al. 2003
;
van de Kamp et al. 2009
;
Chen & Guestrin 2016
)
integrate monotonicity into tree-based models. The QMIX method
(
Rashid et al. 2020
)
incorporating monotonic value functions in multi-agent reinforcement learning. Additionally,
(
Lam et al. 2023
)
introduce a multi-class loss function utilizing the monotonicity of gradients of convex functions. Moreover,
(
Haldar et al. 2020
)
and
(
Xu et al. 2024
)
explore the application of monotonicity in online business.

Generative Model via Variational Inference.

Variational inference (VI)
(
Peterson 1987
;
Saul & Jordan 1995
)
serves as a potent method for generative models, and has recently made considerable progress
(
Kingma 2013
;
Rezende et al. 2014
;
Burda et al. 2016
;
Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015
;
Ho et al. 2020
;
Song et al. 2021
)
. VI simplifies the challenge of Bayesian inference by transforming it into a more feasible optimization problem, where latent variables are approximated within a chosen family of distributions. This is done by maximizing the evidence lower bound (ELBO) rather than the actual evidence.

Recently, there has been an emerging interest in examining generative models under specific conditions. For instance, significant contributions have appeared in text-to-image generation
(
Ramesh et al. 2021
;
Ramesh et al. 2022
;
Saharia et al. 2022
;
Rombach et al. 2022
)
. Typically, these generative models initiate the generative process with predefined conditions (such as text, image, or video), generally represented as
p(\textnormal{x},\textnormal{y})=\mathbb{E}_{\textnormal{z}\sim p(\textnormal{z}|\textnormal{x})}p(\textnormal{x})p(\textnormal{y}|\textnormal{x},\textnormal{z})
, where
x
is the condition,
z
is the latent variable, and
y
is the target. Consequently, these models focus mainly on predicting the posterior
p(\textnormal{z}|\textnormal{x})
and the conditional density
p(\textnormal{y}|\textnormal{x},\textnormal{z})
. Since directly inferring the posterior
p(\textnormal{z}|\textnormal{x})
is computationally challenging, the posterior is often approximated by the variational model
q(\textnormal{z}|\textnormal{x})
, optimized through the ELBO. This paper adopts this framework to build our generative cost model.

4
Method

4.1
Problem Formulation

In the case of a general monotonic problem involving
(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}},\textnormal{y})
, where the output variable
\textnormal{y}\in\mathbb{R}
is continuous, the model is structured as follows:

\displaystyle\textnormal{y}\mid\{\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}\}\sim{\mathcal{F}}(\textnormal{y};G(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}})),

(1)

where
\mathcal{F}
denotes the probability family for
y
. The function
G
is monotonic with respect to
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
and produces a monotonic parameter for
\mathcal{F}
. As a result,
y
preserves monotonicity with respect to
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
. As an illustration, if
\mathcal{F}
is a Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}\left(\textnormal{y};\mu(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}),\sigma(\bm{\mathbf{x}})^{2}\right)
with
G=\mu(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}})
predicts its mean parameter, then
G
must be a monotonic function of
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
to ensure that
y
is monotonic with respect to
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
.

This general monotonic probability problem can be transformed into the binary case by the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any random variable
\textnormal{t}\in\mathbb{R}
defined over
\text{Dom}(\textnormal{y})
, the domain of
y
, and that
\textnormal{y}\perp\!\!\!\perp\textnormal{t}\mid\{\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}\}
. Define
\textnormal{y}^{*}=\mathbb{I}(\textnormal{y}>\textnormal{t})\in\{0,1\}
and
\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{*}=[-\textnormal{t},\bm{\mathbf{r}}]
. Then
\textnormal{y}\mid\{\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}\}
is a monotonic conditional probability with respect to
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
, if and only if
\textnormal{y}^{*}\mid\{\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{*}\}
is a monotonic conditional probability with respect to
\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{*}
.

The proof of this lemma is available in Appendix
B.1
. This lemma demonstrates the equivalence between the problems of
(\textnormal{y}^{*},\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{*})
and
(\textnormal{y},\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}})
. As a result, the monotonic modeling task for the triplet
(\textnormal{y},\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}})
, where
\textnormal{y}\in\mathbb{R}
, can be simplified to the binary problem
(\textnormal{y}^{*},\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{*})
. Here,
\textnormal{y}^{*}\mid\{\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}^{*}\}\sim{\mathcal{B}ernoulli}(\textnormal{y}^{*};G(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{[}}-\textnormal{t},\bm{\mathbf{r}}]))
. Given that
\Pr(\textnormal{y}\leq s|\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}})=1-\Pr(\textnormal{y}>s|\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}})=1-G(\bm{\mathbf{x}},[-s,\bm{\mathbf{r}}])
, the probability density function of
y
is given by:

p(\textnormal{y}=s|\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}})=-\frac{\partial G(\bm{\mathbf{x}},[-s,\bm{\mathbf{r}}])}{\partial s}.

(2)

This equivalence illustrates the transformation of a general monotonic probability problem into a binary monotonic one. Additional information regarding this transformation is provided in Appendix
D.3
. As a result, our investigation now centers on the binary classification problem for
(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}},\textnormal{y})
, where
\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
represents the nonmonotonic variables,
\bm{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
signifies the monotonic variable, and
\textnormal{y}\in\{0,1\}
is the binary outcome that retains monotonicity with respect to
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
. We refer to
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
as the
revenue variable
of
y
. The reasoning is that when
y
is considered a decision variable, a profit-maximizing decision will favor higher values of
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
, thereby ensuring the monotonicity between
y
and
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
.

The distribution of the Bernoulli variable
y
is defined by:

\displaystyle\textnormal{y}\mid\{\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}\}

\displaystyle\sim{\mathcal{B}ernoulli}\left(\textnormal{y};G(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}})\right).

(3)

According to Definition
2.4
, the function
G
is required to be monotonic with respect to
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
. Learning the function
G
poses significant difficulties, particularly when opting for a complex structure, such as a deep neural network. A considerable amount of prior research has concentrated on designing or learning an appropriate neural network for
G
. Our research, however, takes a different approach by introducing a latent cost variable, thereby eliminating the necessity of directly learning the function
G
.

4.2
The Cost Variable

Figure 3:
Within the density contour plot for the cost variable
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
, the shaded area represents the event
\bm{\mathbf{c}}\prec\bm{\mathbf{r}}
. This event indicates that the probability of the cost variable
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
falling within this shaded region is expressed as
\Pr(\bm{\mathbf{c}}\prec\bm{\mathbf{r}})
. Thus, for any vectors
\bm{r}_{1}\prec\bm{r}_{2}
, it follows that
\Pr(\bm{\mathbf{c}}\prec\bm{r}_{1})<\Pr(\bm{\mathbf{c}}\prec\bm{r}_{2})
.

In this section, we examine the binary problem. The conventional approach, as defined in Equation (
3
), involves identifying a strictly monotonic function
G(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}})
with respect to
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
. In this paper, instead of searching for a suitable function
G
, we introduce a random variable
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
to model
y
. This random variable
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
fulfills the following conditions:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{r}}\perp\!\!\!\perp\bm{\mathbf{c}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{x}},

\displaystyle\textnormal{y}\mid\{\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}\}\overset{d}{=}\mathbb{I}(\bm{\mathbf{c}}\prec\bm{\mathbf{r}})\mid\{\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}\},

where
\overset{d}{=}
denotes that the two variables share the same distribution. The existence of the cost variable
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
is guaranteed by the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
y
be a binary variable influenced by
\bm{\mathbf{x}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
, with the constraint that
\Pr(\textnormal{y}=1|\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}})
is continuous for
\bm{\mathbf{r}}\in D
, where
D
is bounded. Then
\Pr(\textnormal{y}=1|\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}})
is monotonically increasing with respect to
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
if and only if there exists a random variable
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
in
D
such that
\bm{\mathbf{r}}\perp\!\!\!\perp\bm{\mathbf{c}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{x}}
and
\textnormal{y}\mid\{\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}\}\overset{d}{=}\mathbb{I}(\bm{\mathbf{c}}\prec\bm{\mathbf{r}})\mid\{\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}\}
.

The proof of this lemma is available in Appendix
B.2
, and we also address the case of unbounded
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
in Appendix
C
. Here, we define
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
as the
cost variable
, with its intuitive interpretation akin to that of
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
: it represents the resistance of a profit-maximizing decision. As shown in Figure
3
, the likelihood of
\textnormal{y}=1
corresponds to the probability that the revenue
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
domains the cost
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
. This lemma implies that once the cost variable
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
is obtained, the initial probability
\Pr(\textnormal{y}=1|\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}})
varies monotonically with
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
. Consequently, the task of identifying the monotonic function
G
essentially transforms into determining the cost variable
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
. However, since
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
is not directly observable, we must infer it from the observable variables
\bm{\mathbf{x}}
,
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
, and
y
, posing a significant unresolved challenge.

4.3
Generative Cost Model

In addressing the challenge of modeling the cost variable
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
, the distribution of
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
can be complicated, making it challenging to select an appropriate distribution family. To bypass the necessity of picking a specific distribution family, we employ a generative approach capable of automatically approximating intricate distributions. A direct idea involves first sampling a latent variable
\bm{\mathbf{z}}
, and subsequently generating
\bm{\mathbf{x}}
,
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
, and
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
independently, conditional on
\bm{\mathbf{z}}
. However, this approach does not ensure
\bm{\mathbf{c}}\perp\!\!\!\perp\bm{\mathbf{r}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{x}}
, as
\bm{\mathbf{z}}
still serves as a shared factor influencing both
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
. To achieve
\bm{\mathbf{c}}\perp\!\!\!\perp\bm{\mathbf{r}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{x}}
, we design a generative model using an additional latent variable
\bm{\mathbf{w}}
with the following process:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{z}}\sim p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}),\bm{\mathbf{w}}\sim p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{w}}),

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{x}}\sim p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{x}}|\bm{\mathbf{z}}),\bm{\mathbf{c}}\sim p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{c}}|\bm{\mathbf{z}}),\bm{\mathbf{r}}\sim p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}|\bm{\mathbf{w}},\bm{\mathbf{x}}),

\displaystyle\textnormal{y}=\mathbb{I}(\bm{\mathbf{c}}\prec\bm{\mathbf{r}}).

p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{z}})
and
p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{w}})
are standard multivariate Gaussian distributions, while
p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{x}}|\bm{\mathbf{z}})
,
p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{c}}|\bm{\mathbf{z}})
, and
p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}|\bm{\mathbf{w}},\bm{\mathbf{x}})
are conditional Gaussian distributions. This entire framework is referred to as the generative cost model (
GCM

. It is evident from Figure

\bm{\mathbf{x}}
blocks the path between
\bm{\mathbf{r}}

\bm{\mathbf{c}}
, and that
y

\bm{\mathbf{r}}\perp\!\!\!\perp\bm{\mathbf{c}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{x}}
holds, ensuring the strict monotonicity of
y
with respect to
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
, as specified by Lemma

.

For learning this generative model, we employ the variational inference method, utilizing the variational model
q_{\phi}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}})
to approximate the posterior
p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}},\textnormal{y})
. As a result, the variational bound is formulated as:

\displaystyle\log p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}},\textnormal{y})

(5)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{z}}\sim q_{\phi}}\log\frac{\Pr_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{c}}\curlyvee_{\textnormal{y}}\bm{\mathbf{r}}|\bm{\mathbf{z}})p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{z}})}{q_{\phi}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}})}+\log p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}|\bm{\mathbf{z}})

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle ELBO}_{GCM}(\theta,\phi).

Here,
\curlyvee_{\textnormal{y}}
denotes
\prec
if
\textnormal{y}=1
, and
\nprec
if
\textnormal{y}=0
(note that
\nprec
is distinct from
\succeq
in the vector space). The right hand side of this inequality corresponds to the evidence lower bound (ELBO) of our model, with equality occurring only if
D_{KL}\left[q_{\phi}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}})\|p(\bm{\mathbf{z}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}},\textnormal{y})\right]=0
. The detailed derivation can be found in Appendix
D.1
. Comprehensive details on computing
{\rm Pr}_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{c}}\curlyvee_{\textnormal{y}}\bm{\mathbf{r}}|\bm{\mathbf{z}},\bm{\mathbf{r}})
can be found in Appendix
D.2
.

In the course of optimizing the ELBO, our model employs the reparameterization trick
(
Kingma 2013
)
as
\bm{z}^{(n)}=\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})+\bm{\mathbf{\sigma}}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})\odot\bm{\mathbf{\epsilon}}^{(n)}
, where
\bm{\mathbf{\epsilon}}^{(n)}
is sampled from the standard normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mathbf{0}},\bm{\mathbf{E}})
. Following the IWAE
(
Burda et al. 2016
)
framework, the final loss function of GCM is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{GCM}(\theta,\phi;\bm{x},\bm{r},y)

(6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\log\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{{\Pr}_{\theta}\left(\bm{\mathbf{c}}\curlyvee_{\textnormal{y}=y}\bm{r}|\bm{\mathbf{z}}=\bm{z}^{(n)}\right)p_{\theta}\left(\bm{\mathbf{z}}=\bm{z}^{(n)}\right)}{q_{\phi}\left(\bm{\mathbf{z}}=\bm{z}^{(n)}|\bm{\mathbf{x}}=\bm{x}\right)}.

Here, we drop the term
p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}|\bm{\mathbf{z}})
in Equation (
5
), since we do not need it for predicting
y
. The sampling number
N
influences the loss function, with a detailed ablation study on
N
provided in Appendix
F
. In scenarios where the output variable
y
is continuous, we additionally formulate the corresponding loss function, which is elaborated in Appendix
D.3
.

During inference, the aim is to estimate
y
while given
\bm{\mathbf{x}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
. We leverage the approximation
p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}},\textnormal{y})\simeq q_{\phi}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}})
to acquire:

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle Pr}_{\theta}(\textnormal{y}=1|\bm{\mathbf{x}}=\bm{x},\bm{\mathbf{r}}=\bm{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{z}}\sim p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}}=\bm{x},\bm{\mathbf{r}}=\bm{r},\textnormal{y}=1)}{\rm Pr}_{\theta}\left(\textnormal{y}=1|\bm{\mathbf{z}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}=\bm{r}\right)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\rm Pr}_{\theta}\left(\bm{\mathbf{c}}\prec\bm{r}|\bm{\mathbf{z}}=\bm{z}^{(n)}\right),

where
\bm{z}^{(n)}
is a random sample from
q_{\phi}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}}=\bm{x})
. This estimation is strictly monotonic with respect to
\bm{r}
, since
{\rm Pr}_{\theta}\left(\bm{\mathbf{c}}\prec\bm{r}|\bm{\mathbf{z}}=\bm{z}^{(n)}\right)
is a strict monotonic function of
\bm{r}
. Consequently, we have established strict monotonicity between
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
and
y
without the necessity of developing a monotonic function
G(\bm{x},\bm{r})
, thereby reducing the complexity involved in designing a strict monotonic neural network.

4.4
Implicit Generative Cost Model

While we have effectively established the generative cost model to address the strict monotonic problem, a challenge remains in that not all monotonic relationships indicate causation; some merely reflect correlation. For instance, both a child’s height and weight increase monotonically with age, which serves as the underlying monotonic factor. In considering the monotonic relationship between the output variable
y
and the revenue variable
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
, our goal is to identify the latent variable
\bm{\mathbf{k}}
, with which both
y
and
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
exhibit monotonicity. This model does not guarantee strict monotonicity of
p(\textnormal{y}|\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}})
; however, it introduces a monotonic correlation between
y
and
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
, owing to the strict monotonicity of
p(\textnormal{y}|\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{k}})
and
p(\bm{\mathbf{r}}|\bm{\mathbf{k}})
. We designate
\bm{\mathbf{k}}
as the kernel revenue variable.

The generative framework is specified as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{z}}\sim p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}),\bm{\mathbf{k}}\sim p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{k}}),

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{x}}\sim p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{x}}|\bm{\mathbf{z}},\bm{\mathbf{k}}),\bm{\mathbf{c}}\sim p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{c}}|\bm{\mathbf{z}}),\Delta\bm{\mathbf{r}}\sim p_{\theta}(\Delta\bm{\mathbf{r}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}}),

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{r}}=\Delta\bm{\mathbf{r}}+\bm{W}\bm{\mathbf{k}},\bm{W}\succ\bm{\mathbf{0}},

\displaystyle\textnormal{y}=\mathbb{I}(\bm{\mathbf{c}}\prec\bm{\mathbf{k}}).

The assurance of a monotonic relationship between
\bm{\mathbf{k}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
is given by the positive projection matrix
\bm{W}
, while the monotonic relationship between
y
and
\bm{\mathbf{k}}
is guaranteed by
\textnormal{y}=\mathbb{I}(\bm{\mathbf{c}}\prec\bm{\mathbf{k}})
and the independence condition
\bm{\mathbf{c}}\perp\!\!\!\perp\bm{\mathbf{k}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{x}}
. This model is referred to as the implicit generative cost model (
IGCM
), as shown in Figure
5
.

To learn the IGCM, we propose an additional variational model
q_{\psi}(\bm{\mathbf{k}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}})
to estimate the posterior distribution
p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{k}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}},\textnormal{y})
. Close to Equation (
5
), the ELBO of IGCM can be derived as:

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle ELBO}_{IGCM}(\theta,\phi,\psi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{z}}\sim q_{\phi},\bm{\mathbf{k}}\sim q_{\psi}}\log{\rm Pr}_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{c}}\curlyvee_{\textnormal{y}}\bm{\mathbf{k}}|\bm{\mathbf{z}})p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{x}}|\bm{\mathbf{z}})p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}|\bm{\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{z}}\sim q_{\phi}}\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{z}})}{q_{\phi}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}})}-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{k}}\sim q_{\psi}}\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{k}})}{q_{\psi}(\bm{\mathbf{k}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}})}.

Similarly to Equation (

p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{x}}|\bm{\mathbf{z}})
in

p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}|\bm{\mathbf{k}})
to capture the latent monotonicity between
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{k}}
. The corresponding loss function becomes:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{IGCM}(\theta,\phi,\psi;\bm{x},\bm{r},y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\log\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{k}}\sim q_{\psi}}{\Pr}_{\theta}\left(\bm{\mathbf{c}}\curlyvee_{\textnormal{y}=y}{\bm{\mathbf{k}}}|\bm{\mathbf{z}}=\bm{z}^{(n)}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\frac{p_{\theta}\left(\bm{\mathbf{z}}=\bm{z}^{(n)}\right)}{q_{\phi}\left(\bm{\mathbf{z}}=\bm{z}^{(n)}|\bm{\mathbf{x}}=\bm{x}\right)}

\displaystyle-\log\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{r}}=\bm{r}|\bm{\mathbf{k}}=\bm{k}^{(m)})

\displaystyle+D_{KL}\left[q_{\psi}(\bm{\mathbf{k}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}}=\bm{x},\bm{\mathbf{r}}=\bm{r})\|p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{k}})\right].

5.1
Experiment by Simulation

Quantile regression is a common problem in statistics, aiming to estimate the
r
-th quantile of
y
given
x
, based on observations of both
x
and
y
. The
r
-th quantile, represented as
Q_{\textnormal{y}|\textnormal{x}}(\textnormal{r})
, is defined by
Q_{\textnormal{y}|\textnormal{x}}(\textnormal{r})=F_{\textnormal{y}|\textnormal{x}}^{-1}(\textnormal{r})
, where
F_{\textnormal{y}|\textnormal{x}}
is the conditional cumulative distribution function of
y
given
x
. Due to the monotonic nature of
F
, its inverse,
Q_{\textnormal{y}|\textnormal{x}}(\textnormal{r})
, is also strictly monotonic with respect to
r
. The standard objective of linear quantile regression
(
Koenker 2005
)
is formulated as:

\displaystyle\hat{\beta_{\textnormal{r}}}=\underset{\beta_{\textnormal{r}}}{\mbox{arg min}}\sum_{i}\rho_{\textnormal{r}}\left(y^{(i)}-\hat{y}^{(i)}_{\textnormal{r}}\right),

\displaystyle{\rm where}\ \rho_{\textnormal{r}}(\Delta)={\textnormal{r}}\Delta_{+}+(1-{\textnormal{r}})(-\Delta)_{+}.

Here,
\Delta_{+}=\Delta
if
\Delta>0
, otherwise
\Delta_{+}=0
.
(x^{(i)},y^{(i)})
is the
i
-th example pair and
\hat{y}_{\textnormal{r}}^{(i)}=x^{(i)\top}\beta_{\textnormal{r}}
denotes the linear prediction of the quantile
Q_{\textnormal{y}|\textnormal{x}=x^{(i)}}(\textnormal{r})
, with
\beta_{\textnormal{r}}
as its parameter. For nonlinear relationships of
y
given
x
, neural networks can be leveraged to automatically capture these dynamics. Additionally, by integrating
r
into the network, it is possible to predict the
r
-th quantile of
y
given
x
using
\hat{y}_{\textnormal{r}}={\rm DNN}_{\theta}(x,\textnormal{r})
, for any
\textnormal{r}\in(0,1)
. This can also be represented in a generative format:

\displaystyle\textnormal{y}_{\textnormal{r}}\sim p_{\theta}(\textnormal{y}_{\textnormal{r}}|\textnormal{x},\textnormal{r}),

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{\textnormal{r}}=\mathbb{E}\textnormal{y}_{\textnormal{r}}.

However, this problem diverges from the original monotonic modeling, since the variable
r
is unobservable, preventing the direct implementation of the GCM method. To address this, we reformulate the quantile regression problem as follows:

\displaystyle{\rm sample}

\displaystyle r\sim\mathcal{U}([0,1]),

(7)

\displaystyle{\rm minimize}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\textnormal{y}_{\textnormal{r}}\sim p_{\theta}(\textnormal{y}_{\textnormal{r}}|\textnormal{x}=x,\textnormal{r}=r)}\rho_{r}(y-\textnormal{y}_{\textnormal{r}}),

which becomes an optimizable problem.

This paper evaluates various traditional methods alongside our generative cost model (GCM). The strictly monotonic characteristics of this context exclude the IGCM method from consideration. All methods share an underlying three-layer perceptron network architecture with
\rm tanh
activation functions. For training, network parameters are optimized using the stochastic gradient descent algorithm. The methods being compared include: (i) the positive weighted neural network (PosNN), which employs solely positive weight matrices (refer to Appendix
E
); (ii) the Min-Max network (MM)
(
Sill 1997
)
; (iii) the smooth Min-Max network (SMM)
(
Igel 2024
)
; (iv) the constrained monotonic network (CMNN)
(
Runje & Shankaranarayana 2023
)
; (v) the scalable monotonic network (SMNN)
(
Kim & Lee 2024
)
; (vi) the monotonicity hint model (Hint)
(
Sill & Abu-Mostafa 1996
)
; (vii) the pointwise loss method (PWL)
(
Gupta et al. 2019
)
. It should be noted that both the Hint and PWL techniques are weakly monotonic, aiming to enhance monotonicity without ensuring it. Training data are generated using a simulation configured as follows:

\displaystyle\textnormal{x}\sim\mathcal{U}(-1.5,1.5),

\displaystyle\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),

\displaystyle\textnormal{y}=0.3\sin(2(\textnormal{x}+0.8))+0.4\sin(3(\textnormal{x}-1.3))

\displaystyle+0.3\sin(5\textnormal{x})+0.2(0.8\textnormal{x}^{2}+0.6)\epsilon.

(8)

For each example
(\textnormal{x},\textnormal{y})=(x^{(i)},y^{(i)})
, we additionally sample
r^{(i)}\sim\mathcal{U}([0,1])
and optimize our models with Equation (
7
). It is important to note that the sampling of
r^{(i)}
operates independently of
\epsilon
. Our training procedure employs a batch size of
20
over
5,000
iterations, yielding a total of
100,000
training samples. The testing phase involves
1,000
samples per quantile, with
\textnormal{r}\in\{0.1,0.3,0.5,0.7,0.9\}
. Table
1
provides the mean absolute error (MAE) results for all methods involved in the quantile regression analysis. We conduct the experiment
10
times, varying the random seeds each time, and present the results with
95\%
confidence intervals. According to the results, GCM surpasses all competing methods.

We define the
r
-th quantile curve as
\Gamma_{r}=\{(x,y_{r})\}
; thus, for any
r_{2}>r_{1}
, the quantile’s monotonic property ensures that
\Gamma_{r_{2}}

illustrated as red curves, while the training data

denotes the CDF of the standard normal distribution. Conceptually, the predicted quantile curves ought to be distinct from each other, with the upper curve
\Gamma_{0.9}
and the lower curve

\Gamma_{0.1}
and
\Gamma_{0.9}
; and (iii) GCM demonstrates noticeable separations between the quantile curves and the fewest instances of outliers. Ultimately, GCM yields the most precise quantile curve predictions in contrast to traditional approaches.

The models we compare are identical to those presented in Section

, while the evaluation metrics are switched to root mean squared error (RMSE), classification accuracy (ACC) and area under the curve (AUC). For regression tasks, we apply the RMSE metric, while for classification tasks with a positive sample ratio between
[0.3,0.7]
, we utilize the ACC metric; otherwise, the AUC metric is employed. The datasets are divided into training and testing sets in a
4:1

)

. Each experiment is repeated

confidence intervals, as shown in Table
3
. To better understand the experimental results, we analyze the test results in three aspects: (i) comparison between GCM and IGCM, (ii) comparison between strict monotonic methods, and (iii) comparison between non-strict monotonic methods.

Comparison between GCM and IGCM.
Our analysis reveals that within public datasets, the IGCM outperforms the GCM. This improved performance is attributed to the fact that real-world scenarios do not always adhere to strict monotonic relationships; for instance, while BMI is correlated with diabetes, it is not necessarily causative. Typically, the selection of monotonic features is informed by experience or statistical analysis. Under these circumstances, implicit monotonic assumptions tend to be more applicable. Our experimental data support this assertion, as IGCM demonstrates superior performance to GCM in five of the six datasets.

Comparison between strict monotonic methods.
The strict monotonic methods evaluated include PosNN, MM, SMM, CMNN, SMNN, and the proposed GCM. Our evaluation indicates that the basic PosNN performs inadequately on most datasets, implying that simply implementing a positively weighted neural network is insufficient for handling monotonic problems. Notably, the traditional MM method, along with its successor SMM, demonstrates considerable effectiveness, indicating that the fundamental Min-Max architecture successfully optimizes the monotonic network. Recent advancements with CMNN and SMNN introduce novel monotonic network structures that surpass MM in several tests. Our proposed GCM method consistently achieves strong performance across all datasets, suggesting that reframing the monotonic problem as a generative one yields improved outcomes compared to merely modifying the network structure.

Comparison between non-strict monotonic methods.
Hint, PWL, and IGCM are non-strict monotonic approaches, with Hint and PWL serving as regularization techniques. In contrast, our newly developed IGCM tackles the non-strict issue via an innovative implicit monotonic modeling. This involves addressing two strict monotonic challenges:
p(\bm{\mathbf{r}}|\bm{\mathbf{k}})
and
p(\textnormal{y}|\bm{\mathbf{k}})
, thereby creating a non-strict monotonic relationship between
y
and
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
. This new technique offers an innovative probabilistic perspective for non-strict monotonic modeling. Experimental findings indicate enhancements when compared to conventional non-strict monotonic methods.

In conclusion, our GCM and IGCM methods deliver superior performance in most tasks, highlighting the efficacy of our generative approach. Further analysis of time complexity is provided in Appendix
G
and ablation studies are presented in Appendix
F
.

6
Conclusion

This paper introduces a novel generative approach to monotonic modeling. We reformulate the monotonicity problem by incorporating a latent cost variable
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
. Additionally, we propose two generative methods: GCM, which targets the strict monotonic problem, and IGCM, which focuses on the implicit monotonic problem. Our experimental findings indicate that GCM and IGCM successfully tackle the monotonicity challenge and substantially surpass traditional methods across multiple tasks.

Appendix C
Discussion on Bounded Revenue Variable

The bounded revenue variable issue can arise in practice, for instance, when our model is trained with
\|\bm{\mathbf{r}}\|<b
, but is used for inference with revenue
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
whose elements
\bm{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\gg b
. This discrepancy can cause an out-of-distribution (OOD) problem, resulting in
\Pr(\textnormal{y}=1|\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}})=\Pr(\bm{\mathbf{r}}\succ\bm{\mathbf{c}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}})\to 1
. We address this by hypothesizing a non-zero probability
\Pr(\bm{\mathbf{c}}=+\infty|\bm{\mathbf{x}})=p_{0}>0
, which yields a bounded estimate:

\Pr(\textnormal{y}=1|\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}})=\Pr(\bm{\mathbf{r}}\succ\bm{\mathbf{c}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}})<1-p_{0}.

This upper bound is independent of
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
. Alternatively, we can replace the revenue variable
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
with a bounded invertible monotonic function
h(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
, for instance, the sigmoid function. Consequently, the probability
\Pr(\textnormal{y}=1|\bm{\mathbf{x}},h(\bm{\mathbf{r}}))
will be monotonic with respect to
h(\bm{\mathbf{r}})
, effectively transforming the original unbounded problem into a bounded one.

D.1
Derivation of ELBO

The ELBO of GCM is expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\log p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}},\textnormal{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{z}}\sim p(\bm{\mathbf{z}})}p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}},\textnormal{y}|\bm{\mathbf{z}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{z}}\sim q_{\phi}}\frac{p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}},\textnormal{y}|\bm{\mathbf{z}})p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{z}})}{q_{\phi}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}})}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{z}}\sim q_{\phi}}\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}},\textnormal{y}|\bm{\mathbf{z}})p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{z}})}{q_{\phi}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{z}}\sim q_{\phi}}\log\frac{\Pr_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{c}}\curlyvee_{\textnormal{y}}\bm{\mathbf{r}}|\bm{\mathbf{z}},\bm{\mathbf{r}})p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}|\bm{\mathbf{z}})p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{z}})}{q_{\phi}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{z}}\sim q_{\phi}}\log\frac{\Pr_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{c}}\curlyvee_{\textnormal{y}}\bm{\mathbf{r}}|\bm{\mathbf{z}})p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{z}})}{q_{\phi}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}})}+\log p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}}|\bm{\mathbf{z}}).

D.2
Calculate the Likelihood

We assume that
\bm{\mathbf{c}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{z}}
is element-wise independent. Thus, we can decompose the conditional likelihood as follows:

{\rm Pr}_{\theta}(\bm{\mathbf{c}}\curlyvee_{\textnormal{y}}\bm{\mathbf{r}}|\bm{\mathbf{z}},\bm{\mathbf{r}})=1-\textnormal{y}-(-1)^{\textnormal{y}}\prod_{i}\int_{-\infty}^{\bm{\mathbf{r}}_{i}}p_{\theta}({\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{i}}|\bm{\mathbf{z}})d\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{i},

(10)

where
\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{i}
is the
i
-th element of
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{r}}_{i}
is the
i
-th element of
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
.

D.3
GCM for Continuous Regression

When
y
is a continuous variable, the regression problem can be transformed into a binary classification problem, as demonstrated in Lemma
4.1
. Consequently, this allows us to develop the generative model for the latent variables
t
and
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
, so that

\Pr(\textnormal{y}>\textnormal{t}|\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{z}})=\Pr(\bm{\mathbf{r}}\succ\bm{\mathbf{c}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{z}})=\Pr(\bm{\mathbf{r}}\succ\bm{\mathbf{c}}|\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{z}}).

(11)

We assume that
y
and
t
adhere to conditional Gaussian distributions: specifically,
p_{\theta}(\textnormal{y}|\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{z}})=\mathcal{N}\left(\textnormal{y};\mu_{\textnormal{y}}(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{z}}),\sigma_{\textnormal{y}}^{2}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})\right)
and
p_{\theta}(\textnormal{t}|\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{z}})=\mathcal{N}\left(\textnormal{t};\mu_{\textnormal{t}}(\bm{\mathbf{x}}),\sigma_{\textnormal{t}}^{2}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})\right)
. Here,
\sigma_{\textnormal{y}}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})
,
\mu_{\textnormal{t}}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})
, and
\sigma_{\textnormal{t}}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})
are functions we defined, while
\mu_{\textnormal{y}}(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{z}})
is determined via Equation (
11
). We recognize that

\Phi\left(\frac{\mu_{\textnormal{y}}(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{z}})-\mu_{\textnormal{t}}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\textnormal{y}}^{2}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})+\sigma_{\textnormal{t}}^{2}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})}}\right)=\Pr(\textnormal{y}>\textnormal{t}|\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{z}})=\Pr(\bm{\mathbf{r}}\succ\bm{\mathbf{c}}|\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{z}}),

enabling us to define
\mu_{\textnormal{y}}
as

\mu_{\textnormal{y}}(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{z}})=\sqrt{\sigma_{\textnormal{y}}^{2}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})+\sigma_{\textnormal{t}}^{2}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})}\left\{\Phi^{-1}\left(\Pr(\bm{\mathbf{r}}\succ\bm{\mathbf{c}}|\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{z}})\right)\right\}+\mu_{\textnormal{t}}(\bm{\mathbf{x}}).

The established definitions of
y
and
t
ensure that
t
is conditionally independent of
y
given
\bm{\mathbf{x}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
, as stipulated by Lemma
4.1
. By employing variational inference, the loss function corresponding to the continuous problem is expressed as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{z}}\sim q_{\phi}}\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\textnormal{y}|\bm{\mathbf{z}},\bm{\mathbf{r}})p_{\theta}\left(\bm{\mathbf{z}}\right)p_{\theta}\left(\bm{\mathbf{x}}|\bm{\mathbf{z}}\right)}{q_{\phi}\left(\bm{\mathbf{z}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{z}}\sim q_{\phi}}\left\{\frac{\left(\textnormal{y}-\mu_{\textnormal{y}}\right)^{2}}{2{\sigma_{\textnormal{y}}}^{2}}+\log\sigma_{\textnormal{y}}-\log\frac{p_{\theta}\left(\bm{\mathbf{z}}\right)p_{\theta}\left(\bm{\mathbf{x}}|\bm{\mathbf{z}}\right)}{q_{\phi}\left(\bm{\mathbf{z}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}}\right)}\right\}.

The estimator for
y
with respect to
\bm{\mathbf{x}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
is expressed as:

\displaystyle\hat{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mu_{\textnormal{y}}(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{r}},\bm{\mathbf{z}}=\bm{z}^{(n)}),

where
\bm{z}^{(n)}

q_{\phi}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}|\bm{\mathbf{x}})
