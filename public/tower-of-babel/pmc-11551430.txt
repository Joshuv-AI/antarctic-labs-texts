SPATEs promote the survival of Shigella to the plasma complement system upon local hemorrhage and bacteremia
Authors: Lorine Debande, Ahmad Sabbah, Lauriane Kuhn, Richard Patryk Ngondo, Julie Maucotel, Marina Valente-Barroso, Antonin C. André, Béatrice Roche, Matthieu Laborde, Maria-Victoria Cantalapiedra-Mateo, Tamou Thahouly, Ana Milinski, Laurent Bianchetti, Christine Allmang, Magali Frugier, Benoit S Marteyn
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceIn this study, we aimed to better understand the significance of the ability of Shigella to survive plasma exposure, as we observed that nonpathogenic Escherichia coli rapidly lysed upon exposure. Indeed, we reported that Shigella was exposed to plasma components first within the colonic mucosa, upon local hemorrhages, and then during bacteremia. The ability of Shigella to survive plasma exposure has not been previously studied. Here, we have shown, first, that all tested Shigella species were able to divide and grow in the presence of human plasma, and second, we found that SPATEs played a central role in this process by impairing the activation of the complement system.

Shigellosis, or bacillary dysentery, is caused by Shigella spp., a genus of pathogenic enterobacteria that specifically infect and colonize the human colon. Four species of Shigella have been identified: S. flexneri, S. sonnei, S. dysenteriae, and S. boydii. Millions of cases of shigellosis are still reported worldwide each year, resulting in hundreds of thousands of deaths in developing countries (1). Symptoms of shigellosis include severe hemorrhagic diarrhea, vomiting, fever, abdominal pain, and dehydration. Bloody and mucoid diarrhea in shigellosis patients is caused by local hemorrhage within the colonic mucosa, associated with endothelial damages. While clinical aspects of shigellosis have long been described, the impact of the presence of plasma components with strong antimicrobial activity within the colonic mucosa on Shigella survival has not been studied. In addition, Shigella are further exposed to plasma during bacteremia, which occurs in most severe cases of shigellosis (2), especially in malnourished children under the age of five (3–6) and in immunocompromised or cancer patients (7–9). All species of Shigella can cause bacteremia to the same extent (S. flexneri, S. sonnei, S. boydii, and S. dysenteriae) (10). The ability of Shigella to reach the bloodstream and cause bacteremia significantly increases the mortality rate in shigellosis patients (3, 11, 12).

Therefore, we hypothesized that the ability of Shigella to survive exposure to plasma components, both locally during hemorrhage and systemically, contributes to its virulence and may play a central role in shigellosis. The virulence of Shigella relies on the expression of two major secretion systems that are conserved among Shigella strains: the type III secretion system (T3SS), which is involved in host cell invasion, and the type V secretion system (T5SS). The major T5SS virulence factors secreted by Shigella strains belong to the serine protease autotransporter of Enterobacteriaceae (SPATE) family. SPATEs consist of two domains: a transmembrane β-barrel domain and a passenger domain, the latter being secreted upon autocleavage within the linker sequence. Two classes (I & II) of SPATEs have been defined according to their structure and properties (13). Like all pathogenic Escherichia coli strains, all virulent Shigella species secrete at least one member of the SPATE family; they are the most abundant virulence factors released by Shigella strains. S. flexneri 5a secretes SepA (class II), S. sonnei secretes SigA (class I), S. flexneri 2a secretes SepA, SigA, and Pic (class II). SepA was the first Shigella SPATE to be identified (14).

Local hemorrhage and bacteremia are induced at the late stage of Shigella infection, which could not be studied in available animal models of shigellosis until recently, since conventional mice are not susceptible to Shigella infection and young guinea pigs are only transiently infected by Shigella (15). Our team recently validated a new ascorbate-deficient guinea pig as a model for shigellosis which allows to follow prolonged Shigella infections (16). In this model, we reported that bacteremia was induced by S. flexneri 5a, 48 h postinfection (h.p.i.), whereas the molecular mechanisms remained elusive.

We have previously shown that Shigella progressed mainly under low-oxygen conditions in the late stages of infection when hypoxic foci of infection are formed within the colonic mucosa (17). This statement prevails also during bacteremia, as we reported that the blood plasma was poorly oxygenated (pO2= 8.4 mmHg) (18). We have previously shown that the T3SS was inactive in the absence of oxygen (17, 19), and we further hypothesized that the ability of Shigella to secrete virulence factors belonging to the T5SS, regardless of the oxygen availability, may be the “last weapon” of Shigella at later stages of the infectious process. The contribution of SPATEs to the virulence of Shigella is far from being fully understood, although some specific targets have been identified. It has been previously reported that Pic degraded O-glycosylated proteins on the surface of leukocytes (20), whereas SepA cleaved neutrophil alpha-1 antitrypsin, among many other proteins in the colon (21). Pic has previously been reported to cleave complement component C3 in vitro (22), suggesting that SPATEs may impair activation of the complement system and maintain the ability of Shigella to survive in plasma, although no direct evidence has yet been presented in vivo.

In this study, we confirmed that S. flexneri 5a and S. sonnei induced hemorrhage and reached blood vessels located in the colonic mucosa after prolonged infections. We demonstrated that all tested Shigella strains survived plasma exposure in vitro, in contrast to nonpathogenic E. coli. We have also shown that the survival of S. flexneri 5a and S. sonnei in the presence of plasma was promoted by SepA and SigA, respectively, which both cleaved the complement C3, as previously reported for Pic secreted by S. flexneri 2a, and various pathogenic E. coli species (EAEC, UPEC, or STEC) (22). Our results strongly suggest that SPATE-dependent cleavage of C3 impaired complement activation, avoiding the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC) (23). The SPATE-dependent capacity of Shigella to survive plasma exposure is anticipated to play an important role during late infectious steps.

Results

Shigella spp. Induce Hemorrhage, Reach Blood Vessels, and Survive the Plasma Exposure.

To evaluate the occurrence of the interaction between Shigella and plasma components during the infectious process, we analyzed the infected colonic mucosa of ascorbate-deficient guinea pigs (16) after prolonged infections. We first confirmed that hemorrhage was induced 48 h.p.i. in abscesses formed by S. flexneri 5a and S. sonnei, as revealed by the detection of red blood cells (RBCs), otherwise absent in noninfected tissues (Fig. 1A). These results are consistent with previous observations made in the human intestine of shigellosis patients in which the authors also reported that hemorrhage was caused by endothelial damage during shigellosis, possibly associated with peripheral vascular insufficiency (13). Here, we report that S. flexneri 5a and S. sonnei reached CD31+/CD34+ blood vessels in the guinea pig colonic mucosa (Fig. 1B). More specifically, most Shigella infectious foci detected in the colonic mucosa colocalized with blood vessels that appeared largely disorganized (Fig. 1B). It has been suggested that this ability of Shigella to reach blood vessels was related to bacteremia, as reported previously (16). Detection of hemorrhagic abscesses within the infected colonic mucosa of guinea pigs confirmed the local exposure of Shigella to plasma components.

We further investigated the capacity of Shigella to survive and grow in the presence of human plasma using M9 agar pad devices, that allowed bacterial growth to be monitored at a single-cell level (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). In this study, we decided to use citrated human plasma in vitro rather than serum to expose Shigella to the whole set of plasma proteins. We demonstrated that all tested Shigella strains (S. sonnei, S. flexneri 5a, and S. flexneri 2a) were able to survive and grow in the presence of human plasma, in contrast to nonpathogenic E. coli K12 which appeared to be lysed at early time points (Fig. 1 C and D). As a control, we confirmed that all strains grew at the same rate in the absence of plasma (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B and C). While all Shigella strains grew in the presence of plasma, we noticed a significant reduction in bacterial growth rate in the presence of plasma compared to phosphate buffer saline (PBS), particularly at later time points (18 h) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D), suggesting that the ability of Shigella to survive plasma exposure was associated with an active virulence mechanism; exposure to plasma represented a stress to which Shigella had to respond.

In addition, we aimed to identify the molecular mechanisms involved in the survival of Shigella upon plasma exposure. We had previously shown that plasma was poorly oxygenated (18) and that Shigella evolved mainly under low-oxygen conditions within foci of infection (17). Under these conditions, we have shown that Shigella T3SS was inactive (19). Alternatively, we hypothesized that Shigella SPATEs might support the ability of Shigella to survive exposure to plasma components, since Pic from Enteroaggregative E. coli (EAEC) was previously reported to target the plasma complement component 3 (C3) but also complement component 2 (C2) and complement component 4 (C4). Although no direct link with EAEC survival to plasma exposure was made (22).

Shigella SPATEs: Structure and Regulation of their Expression/Secretion.

SigA, SepA, and Pic are the only SPATEs secreted by Shigella species. We confirmed by mass spectrometry analysis of bacterial cultures that S. sonnei secreted only SigA, S. flexneri 5a secreted only SepA and S. flexneri 2a secreted SigA, SepA, and Pic (Fig. 2A). Since only partial information was available on the structure, secretion, and expression regulation of SPATEs, we provided additional missing data during this study.

The structure of the passenger domain of SepA was previously solved by X-ray crystallography (Q8VSL2 [Uniprot] or 5J44 [PDB]) (24) (Fig. 2B). The structure of the Pic passenger domain (Q54151 (Uniprot)) (Fig. 2B) was predicted using the structure prediction tool Alphafold (25, 26). Since the 3D model of the passenger domain of SigA was not available, we generated molecular models for SigA56-1008 (Uniprot-ID:Q3YXF8 from residue Met56 to Asn1008) using the structure prediction tool AlphaFold and ColabFold (27), a Web-interfaced implementation of AlphaFold 2. Five models were generated and ranked based on average pLDDT quality scores, AlphaFold’s standard metric. All five pLDDT scores were above 91 out of a maximum of 100. The top-ranked model with a score of 92.1 was selected for further analysis (Fig. 2B).

To check the validity of the model, we performed a BLASTp similarity search in the Protein Data Bank (PDB) using the SigA passenger domain as a query. The first hit we found was the structure of a homologous protein, with an identity of 46.4% and a similarity of 65%, representing the E. coli serine protease EspP (PDB-ID:3SZE and Swissprot-ID:O3259) (28). Overimposure of our 3D model to the EspP 3SZE structure showed high similarity between the structures and a RMSD of 3.6 angstrom (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). As expected, the pLDDT scores along the SigA passenger domain were very good, except for local loop regions, which are generally more flexible and/or present less structured elements, making them more difficult to model (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 B and C). Moreover, the predicted distance errors between protein residues were very low, which supports the quality of the SigA model (SI Appendix, Fig. S2D). The structure of the AlphaFold model of SigA reveals the presence of a globular N-terminal region that folds into both α-helices and β-strands and is connected to an elongated triangular prism of parallel β-strands. Interestingly, the Leu497–Arg560 region exits the prism and contributes to the folding of the globular region, while the Lys598 to Gly628 region folds to form an antiparallel β-sheet and a loop, both lying on the prism. The catalytic residues (Ser258, His126, and Asp154) are localized in the globular region of the passenger domain, as expected (SI Appendix, Fig. S2E).

We further investigated how the secretion of Shigella SPATEs was regulated by environmental cues. It was previously reported that the secretion of SPATEs was thermoregulated in EAEC (Pic) (29), S. flexneri 5a (SepA) (14), and S. flexneri 2a (SigA) (30), although we did not observe significant temperature-dependent regulation of SPATE secretion in S. sonnei, S. flexneri 5a, and S. flexneri 2a (grown at 30 °C, 37 °C, or 42 °C) (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). Shigella SPATEs were secreted by all Shigella strains tested regardless of temperature. In contrast, the oxygen-dependent regulation of SPATEs expression and secretion has not yet been studied, especially in poorly oxygenated environments such as plasma. We demonstrated that the secretion of SPATEs in S. flexneri 2a (SepA, Pic, SigA), S. flexneri 5a (SepA), and S. sonnei (SigA) was independent of the oxygen availability, as observed by Coomassie staining (Fig. 2C) and confirmed by western blot analysis using specific α-SPATE antibodies (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). Consistently, at a transcriptional level, no oxygen-dependent regulation of the expression of SPATE encoding genes was observed in the tested Shigella strains (Fig. 2D). In other words, we demonstrated here that SPATEs are produced and secreted by S. sonnei and S. flexneri 5a and 2a in the absence of oxygen, a condition encountered by Shigella within hypoxic foci of infection (17) and in the plasma (18), supporting their potential involvement during hemorrhage or bacteremia. To proceed, S. flexneri 5a and S. sonnei were used as models because they express and secrete only one member of the SPATE family (SepA and SigA, respectively).

SPATEs are Essential for Shigella Dissemination in the Blood In Vivo.

To define the importance of SPATEs during the S. flexneri 5a and S. sonnei virulence cycles in vivo, S. sonnei ΔsigA and S. flexneri 5a ΔsepA mutant strains were constructed, as well as complemented strains.

Upon intrarectal challenge of ascorbate-deficient guinea pigs (16), we demonstrated that S. sonnei ΔsigA and S. flexneri 5a ΔsepA mutants were attenuated upon prolonged infections (up to 48 h.p.i.) using complementary methods (Fig. 2E and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 C–E). First, we showed that the weight of animals infected with S. sonnei ∆sigA and S. flexneri 5a ∆sepA strains was significantly higher 48 h.p.i. compared to animals infected with the corresponding WT or complemented strains, with a marked weight gain between 24 h.p.i. and 48 h.p.i. (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C). As the severity of Shigella infection is correlated with weight loss in guinea pigs, these results were consistent with the reduced virulence of S. sonnei ΔsigA and S. flexneri 5a ΔsepA mutant strains. These results confirmed that SPATEs are essential for prolonged infections. To correlate these results with the bacterial load in the colon and in the bloodstream, colony-forming unit (CFU) counts were assessed in lumen, mucosa, and blood (Fig. 2E). S. sonnei ΔsigA and S. flexneri 5a ΔsepA mutant strains had a significantly reduced ability to colonize the colonic mucosa, and were less abundant in animal blood, unlike WT and complemented strains (Fig. 2E). We observed a low amount of RBCs (hemorrhage markers) in the colonic mucosa of guinea pigs infected with S. sonnei ΔsigA and S. flexneri 5a ΔsepA mutant strains (SI Appendix, Fig. S3D), contrary to what was observed with WT strains (Fig. 1A), suggesting that these mutant strains did not alter the colonic mucosa microvasculature. We confirmed that S. sonnei ΔsigA and S. flexneri 5a ΔsepA mutant strains were not detected in CD31+/CD34+ blood vessels (SI Appendix, Fig. S3E). Although it may be envisaged that SPATEs may be involved at earlier steps of the infectious process, such as the disruption of the epithelial barrier (21), these results strongly suggest that SPATEs contribute to the survival of Shigella strains to plasma exposure, during hemorrhage and bacteremia,

SPATEs Promote the Survival of Shigella to Plasma Exposure.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the contribution of Shigella secretion systems in the late phases of infection, especially when Shigella is exposed to plasma components during both local hemorrhage and bacteremia. We demonstrated that S. sonnei ∆sigA and S. flexneri 5a ∆sepA mutant strains exposed to plasma had a significant growth defect compared to WT strains (Fig. 3 A and B), and we confirmed both mutant strains had no growth defect in the absence of plasma (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A), validating the importance of SPATEs in the ability of Shigella strains to survive plasma exposure. We additionally observed by live microscopy the growth defect of both S. sonnei ΔsigA and S. flexneri 5a ∆sepA in the presence of plasma (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 B and C). The growth defect of the S. flexneri 5a ∆sepA mutant strain in the presence of plasma was weaker as compared to S. sonnei ΔsigA mutant strain, compared to corresponding WT strains (Fig. 3 A and B), we further aimed at confirming the importance of SepA in the capacity of S. flexneri 5a to survive and grow in the presence of plasma. In S. flexneri 5a, the sepA gene is located on the virulence plasmid. We demonstrated that the BS176 strain (virulence plasmid-cured S. flexneri 5a M90T) had a growth defect in the presence of plasma and had a rounded-cell phenotype (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D). We showed that the overexpression of SepA in BS176 (BS176 pSepA strain) suppressed the growth defect in the presence of plasma, but not the overexpression of the catalytically inactive SepAS267A (BS176 pSepAS267A strain) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D). These complementary results confirmed the contribution of SepA to the survival of S. flexneri 5a in plasma.

As Pic was previously reported to target C2, C3, and C4 (22), we hypothesized that the activation of the plasma complement system was responsible for the growth defect of S. sonnei ΔsigA and S. flexneri 5a ΔsepA mutant strains. Indeed, we demonstrated that these strains had no growth defect in decomplemented plasma (Fig. 3 C and D) and grew similarly to corresponding WT strains (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 E and F). The same result was obtained with E. coli K12 which survived and grew in the presence of decomplemented plasma (SI Appendix, Fig. S4G).

These results supported our hypothesis, and we set out to identify the complement component that could be targeted by SigA and SepA.

SigA and SepA Cleave Complement C3.

To identify SPATE targets in plasma, we used an unbiased approach consisting of incubating albumin-free plasma with purified SigA or SepA (Fig. 3E). Removing albumin, the major protein in plasma, was mandatory to visualize other plasma proteins of the same molecular weight (around 65 kDa). In the presence of SigA or SepA, we observed that the intensity of a band corresponding to 70 kDa decreased, suggesting that plasma proteins may be degraded by SigA and SepA (Fig. 3E). By mass spectrometry analysis, we identified complement C3 as the most abundant protein composing both bands 1 and 2 (Fig. 3F). It should be noted that fibrinogen alpha chain was also abundant in these bands (Fig. 3F); however, in this study, we decided to focus on the potential cleavage of C3 by SPATEs. The cleavage of C3 by SepA and SigA was confirmed with commercial purified C3 and Coomassie staining (Fig. 3 G and H). We further identified C3-degradation products by western blotting, and we showed that heat-inactivated SigA and SepA were no longer able to cleave C3 (Fig. 3I). As controls, we further demonstrated that SigA and SepA did not cleave complement C5 or various immunoglobulins such as IgG, IgM, IgA (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B). To identify SPATE cleavage sites on C3, the sequences of C3 α-chain, β-chain, and the cleaved α-chain formed in the presence of SigA were analyzed by mass spectrometry. We could only identify one potential cleavage site of SigA, located in the complement C3 α-chain between residues 680 and 740 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5C). Using a similar approach, SepA cleavage site on C3 could not be identified.

To further confirm the interaction between SPATEs and C3, we assessed their presence and abundance on bacteria cell surface upon plasma exposure. First, in controls (-plasma), we reported the presence of secreted SPATEs on the surface of S. flexneri 5a (SepA) and S. sonnei (SigA) WT strains, within dot-like structures (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). Second, we could demonstrate that C3 deposition was enhanced on the surface of S. sonnei ∆sigA and S. flexneri 5a ∆sepA mutant strains, compared to WT strains (Fig. 4A), similarly to E. coli K12 (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B) suggesting that SepA and SigA target C3 on bacteria surface.

Taken together, our results showed that SPATEs contribute to the impairment of the complement system by Shigella, which may promote its survival upon exposure to plasma. As we reported that Shigella induced hemorrhage, it was anticipated that plasma components were released within the infected colonic mucosa of animals. We further aimed to confirm the presence of C3 and other plasma components such as albumin, the most abundant plasma protein, in this microenvironment.

Shigella–C3 Interaction Occurs within Hemorrhagic Infected Tissues.

We demonstrated that hemorrhage was caused by Shigella infection by detecting RBCs in the colonic mucosa (Fig. 1A). We next evaluated the distribution of plasma components (C3 and albumin) to Shigella dissemination. In guinea pigs’ noninfected tissues, we were first able to immunodetect C3 and albumin strictly localized in blood vessels (Fig. 4 B and C). We observed that C3 and albumin were more abundant in the colonic mucosa infected with S. sonnei (Fig. 4B) and S. flexneri 5a (Fig. 4C) WT strains compared to the noninfected animals, and we demonstrated that Shigella foci colocalized with both C3 and albumin, confirming the interplay between Shigella and plasma components in hemorrhagic tissues. We observed no changes in the abundance and distribution of C3 and albumin in tissues infected with S. sonnei ∆sigA and S. flexneri 5a ∆sepA mutant strains compared with noninfected tissues (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B), confirming the absence of hemorrhage in this context, as previously reported (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 D and E).

Discussion

In this report, we could investigate the physiopathology of shigellosis at late stages of the infectious process thanks to a new guinea pig model of shigellosis recently validated by our team (16). We could show that Shigella was exposed to various plasma components within hemorrhagic colonic mucosa but also during bacteremia, which was observed in this model, as reported in humans (Fig. 1A). We demonstrated the virulence of Shigella is associated with its ability to survive exposure to plasma in vitro, unlike nonpathogenic E. coli K12, which has been observed with all tested Shigella strains (Fig. 1C). We demonstrated that SPATEs significantly contribute to the ability of Shigella to survive plasma exposure by cleaving the central component of the complement system, the complement C3 (Fig. 3 E–I). Our results shed light on the antimicrobial activity of the complement system, outside of the blood circulation, within hemorrhagic tissues infected by Shigella. Interestingly, we confirmed that the complement system was active in citrated plasma spotted onto M9 agar pads, either due to the presence of calcium in the M9 culture medium, or because the alternative pathway (calcium-independent) is supporting the plasma antimicrobial activity toward Shigella. The attenuation of S. sonnei ∆sigA and S. flexneri 5a ∆sepA mutant strains (Fig. 2E, SI Appendix, Figs. S3 C–E and S7 A and B) may be explained by their inability to resist the complement system within the colonic mucosa, hence impairing their dissemination within infected tissues. However, we cannot rule out the possibility that SPATEs may be involved in additional steps of the infectious process, since it was shown that SepA increased the invasion of epithelial cells by Shigella (21). In other words, the attenuation of S. sonnei ∆sigA and S. flexneri 5a ∆sepA mutant strains may be caused by other defects, beyond their inability to survive plasma exposure. Indeed, the full repertoire of SPATE-targets is far from being established and it will be interesting to evaluate their cytotoxicity or immunomodulatory activity on macrophages, dendritic cells, or neutrophils and to define whether or not they interfere with the coagulation cascade, since one of the additional targets identified in this study was the fibrinogen alpha-chain (Fig. 3F).

Despite a previous report suggesting that Shigella spp. are targeted by C3 in human serum and have at most intermediate resistance (31), it seems like complement resistance by Shigella spp. may be ensured by more than one virulence factor.

Conversely, further investigations will be required to identify additional virulence mechanisms involved in complement system subversion, which may be expressed by S. flexneri 5a (and potentially other Shigella species), since the susceptibility of the S. flexneri 5a ∆sepA mutant strains was less marked than the S. sonnei ∆sigA mutant strain in the presence of plasma (Fig. 3 A and B). One of the candidates would be the cell-surface exposed IcsP, an aspartyl-protease expressed by all Shigella species, which has previously been reported to target C3 on Shigella surface, limiting its accumulation during the intracellular life of the bacterium (32). It was previously reported that the O-antigen chain length, modulated by the polysaccharide copolymerase WzzBSF, contributed to complement-resistance by Shigella flexneri 2a (33). O-antigen chain length modulation is well conserved in Shigella serotypes except Shigella sonnei, with the latter possessing a g4 capsule, which was reported to also contribute to complement resistance (34).

Additionally, in this study, the use of an animal model of shigellosis which allowed to follow prolonged Shigella infection episodes (16), permitted to revisit molecular aspects of the hemorrhage induction during shigellosis. Further investigations will be required to evaluate its contribution to the maintenance of the hypoxic state of infected tissues, in association with Shigella aerobic respiration, which was previously shown to be essential to the formation of hypoxic foci of infection (17). It will also be important to further characterize the importance of Shigella-mediated endothelial damages, previously reported in shigellosis patients (13), on the development of the disease.

From the clinical perspective, our results obtained in vivo confirmed the ability of Shigella to reach blood vessels and to cause bacteremia. The importance of Shigella bacteremia has not yet been fully studied and appreciated through a global clinical study, but only reported in isolated case reports. This may potentially be due to the lack of specific diagnostic methods in areas where shigellosis remains endemic. Such a clinical investigation may potentially reveal the underestimated role of SPATEs in Shigella infection; it will be even more interesting to evaluate the distribution of SPATEs among Shigella isolates causing bacteremia, as previously defined in E. coli strains (35).

Materials and Methods

Bacterial Strains and Plasmids.

All strains and plasmids used in this study are listed in SI Appendix, Table S1. S. sonnei WT strain was obtained from the Pasteur Institute collection (CIP 106347). S. flexneri 2a and S. flexneri 5a WT strains belong to the laboratory collection. BS176 is S. flexneri 5a cured from its pINV virulence plasmid (36). E. coli K12 (E. coli str. K-12 substr. MG1655) was used as a control. S. sonnei ∆sigA and S. flexneri 5a ∆sepA mutant strains were constructed with the lambda-red recombination method (37). In brief, sigA and sepA genes were replaced by a chloramphenicol resistance cassette, which was amplified from the pKD3 plasmid using primers listed in SI Appendix, Table S2. Complementations of S. sonnei ∆sigA and BS176 were performed with plasmids pSigA3 (pSigA, AmpR) and pSepAS267A (AmpR) respectively, kindly provided by Mario Meza-Segura (21). S. flexneri 5a ∆sepA and BS176 complementations were performed with plasmid pZK15 (pSepA AmpR) (14) kindly provided by Pr. Claude Parsot. The strain E. coli HB101 pPic (TetR) (29) was used to overexpress and purify Pic. Shigella strains were grown in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) (Hach) liquid medium and on TSB petri dishes supplemented with 0,1% Congo Red and E. coli K12 strain was grown in lysogeny broth (LB) liquid medium and LB agar plates. Antibiotics were added when required at the following concentrations: Ampicillin 100 µg/mL, Chloramphenicol 25 µg/mL, and Tetracycline 10 µg/mL.

SigA, SepA, and Pic Purification.

SigA was purified from the S. sonnei culture supernatant. SepA and Pic were purified from BS176 pSepA and E. coli HB101 pPic strain, respectively grown overnight at 37 °C. Specific purification methods have been optimized for each SPATE, as described below.

SigA purification. S. sonnei WT strain was grown overnight at 37 °C and subcultured in a fresh medium for 5 h at 37 °C. Bacterial cultures were centrifuged for 15 min at 4,000 x g at 4 °C and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 µm filter and subsequently precipitated in ammonium sulfate (35% w/v) for 45 min at 4 °C under gentle agitation. After 30 min of centrifugation at 7,500 x g, the pellet was resuspended in 25 mM NaH2PO4/Na2HPO4 pH 7.5, 50 mM NaCl, and dialyzed overnight in the same buffer. The dialyzed sample was centrifuged for 10 min at 15,000 x g and the supernatant was loaded onto a DEAE column (TSK-gel DEAE-5PW - Tosoh). SigA was eluted by a NaCl gradient ranging from 50 mM to 1 M. Purified SigA was collected, dialyzed against the storage buffer (25 mM NaH2PO4/Na2HPO4 pH 7.5, 50 mM NaCl), quantified, and stored at 4 °C (up to 1 mo).

SepA and Pic purification. Overnight bacterial cultures were centrifuged for 15 min at 4,000 x g at 4 °C and the supernatant was filtered through 0.22 µm filters and subsequently precipitated in ammonium sulfate (30% w/v) for 45 min at 4 °C under gentle agitation. Precipitated samples were centrifuged for 30 min at 7,500 x g at 4 °C and pellets were resuspended in 1X PBS. Samples were concentrated on a 100 kDa cut-off Amicon filter (Merck) and loaded on a gel filtration column (Superdex 200 10/300 GL – GE Healthcare). Purified SepA and Pic were collected, quantified, and stored at −20 °C (for up to 1 mo).

Production of SPATEs Polyclonal Antibodies.

SigA, Pic, and SepA were purified by ammonium sulfate precipitation and quantified using the Bradford method. 4 mg of each protein was sent to the GeneCust® company to immunize New Zealand rabbits. seven immunizations were proceeded before blood collection. Immunoglobulins were purified on an antigen affinity chromatography. Antibodies were kept at -20 °C in 1X PBS supplemented with 0,02% NaN3. To further purify the antibodies specific to each SPATE, culture supernatants of S. flexneri 5a BS176 pSepA, S. sonnei ∆sigA pSigA and E. coli HB101 pPic were migrated on 1-well sodium dodecyl-sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gel and transferred on a nitrocellulose membrane. The latter was stained with Ponceau Red and the band corresponding to SepA, SigA, or Pic was cut, blocked with 1X PBS/0.1% Triton X-100/10% Fetal Bovine Serum and incubated overnight with anti-SepA serum, anti-SigA serum, or anti-Pic serum, respectively. After several washes with the blocking buffer, bound antibodies were collected using glycine 0.2 M pH 2.8 supplemented with 0.2% gelatin, then the pH was quickly neutralized with 25% v/v unbuffered Tris 1 M. Purified antibodies were tested on whole supernatants to assess their purity.

Human Plasma Collection and Treatments.

Human blood was collected from the antecubital vein of anonymous voluntary donors at the Etablissement Français du Sang de Strasbourg (authorization n°ALC/PIL/DIR/AJR/FO/606). ACD-containing blood bags were collected and rapidly transferred into an anoxic chamber (DG250, Don Whitley). Blood samples were centrifuged for 20 min at 1,800 rpm without break. The plasma was recovered and centrifuged for 20 min at 3,800 rpm to remove the remaining blood cells and platelets. Plasma samples were stored at −20 °C. Albumin-free plasma was obtained following the MinuteTM Albumin Depletion Reagent for Plasma and Serum kit instructions (Invent Biotechnologies), resuspended in 1X PBS, and stored at −20 °C. Decomplemented plasma was obtained by heating at 56 °C for 30 min and was stored at −20 °C.

Bacterial Growth Assay.

Bacteria were grown in M9 minimal medium (Merck) supplemented with 2 mM MgSO4, 0,1 mM CaCl2, and 0,4% glucose for one night at 37 °C (180 rpm). The next day, overnight culture was diluted to OD600nm 0.05 in fresh rich M9 media and grown for 2 h at 37 °C to reach OD600nm 0,22. 1 µL of bacterial culture and 5 µL of 1X PBS, plasma, or decomplemented plasma were deposited onto a 1% M9-agar pads. Pads were then incubated for 18 h at 37 °C in humid conditions to allow bacterial growth. Images were taken at 0, 4, and 18 h with an Axioskop two light microscope (Zeiss, Germany) equipped with an Optikam Pro6 digital Camera (Optika, Italy), using a 100x objective (Plan-NEOFLUAR 100X 1.3NA Ph3 oil) to monitor bacterial growth, in the presence or absence of human plasma. Bacterial growth quantification was assessed using Fiji v2.1.0 software.

SigA Prediction Structure.

To predict the structure of the passenger domain of S. sonnei SigA, the protein sequence Uniprot-ID:Q3YXF8 (NCBI protein sequence WP_052993189) from residue Met56 to Asn1008 was submitted to Colabfold v1.5.2 that implements AlphaFold2. Five models were requested with AMBER force field relaxation. The BLASTp similarity search with SigA passenger domain as a query was carried out with default parameters (Expect threshold 0.05, word size 5, BLOSUM62 scoring matrix, gap creation penalty 11, gap extension penalty 1) in the PDB. Structure manipulations were carried out in PyMOL 1.8.4. The align function which is based on primary sequence comparison was used to superimpose structures. Calculations of RMSD were run in Visual molecular dynamics 1.9.3. The predicted structure of SigA was deposited with ModelArchive and is publicly available (https://modelarchive.org/doi/10.5452/ma-s7yqq).

SPATEs Secretion Regulation.

To study the regulation of SPATE secretion, bacterial cultures of S. sonnei, S. flexneri 2a, and S. flexneri 5a were grown overnight at 37 °C (180 rpm) in liquid TSB. The following day, the cultures were diluted in fresh TSB to an initial OD600nm of 0.05 and cultured under various conditions. To study O2-dependent regulation of secretion, diluted cultures were incubated at 37 °C in the presence of O2 with agitation, or in the absence of O2 in an anoxic chamber without agitation (Don Whitley; DG250). To study temperature-dependent regulation, diluted cultures were incubated at 30, 37, or 42 °C for 6 h with agitation (180 rpm). Then supernatants were collected by spinning down the bacteria and filtered through 0,22 µm filters. 35% ammonium sulfate precipitation (w/v) was assessed to precipitate the secreted protein and after 30 min centrifugation at 7,500 x g at 4 °C, proteins were resuspended in 1X PBS and concentered using 100 kDa cut-off Amicon (Merck). Proteins were loaded on a 10% SDS-PAGE gel and analyzed by Coomassie Blue staining (Instant; Blue Abcam) or western blot. Western blot was done on a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane with 1:500 rabbit anti-Pic, 1:500 rabbit anti-SepA, or 1:250 rabbit anti-SigA diluted on 1X TBS supplemented with 0.5% of Tween 20 (Merck) and 3% BSA (Euromedex). Membranes were then washed three times in TBS/0.5% Tween 20 and incubated 1 h with 1:10,000 of a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody (Abcam) and washed three times in TBS/0.5% Tween 20. Revelation was assessed using a Super Signal ECL kit (Thermo Scientific) and imaged with a ChemiDoc™ Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad).

Quantitative Real-Time PCR Analysis.

S. flexneri 2a, S. flexneri 5a, and S. sonnei were cultured for 5 h in TSB medium at 37 °C and shaken at 180 rpm. After 15 min of centrifugation at 4,000 rpm, the pellets were used for RNA extraction. Total RNAs were extracted by phenol/chloroform and precipitated. After DNAse treatment, RNAs were reverse transcribed using iScriptTM Reverse Transcription Supermix (Bio-Rad) and cDNAs were amplified by quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR). Reactions were carried out on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad) using the Maxima SYBR Green kit (Thermo Scientific). Oligonucleotides used for qRT-PCR are listed in SI Appendix, Table S2. qRT-PCRs were performed and designed according to the MIQE guidelines (38). The specificity of the oligonucleotides was validated, and the amplification efficiencies of the primer sets were between 90% and 110% with r2 values greater than 0.98. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the ∆∆Ct method. Results were expressed as mean ± SE of an average of three measurements.

Detection of C3 and SPATEs on Bacteria Cell-Surface.

Green fluorescent protein (GFP) bacteria were grown in TSB supplemented with Ampicillin overnight at 37 °C (180 rpm). Bacterial cultures were diluted to OD600nm 0.05 in 90 µL fresh TSB and were grown for 5 h at 37 °C 180 rpm. 35 µL plasma was added to the cultures and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C (180 rpm). The mixes were centrifuged at 3.200 x g, for 10 min at 4 °C, then the pellet was resuspended in 3% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Then, bacteria were washed in 1X PBS and 1X PBS/1% Triton X-100. For primary staining, bacterial pellets were resuspended in 1X PBS/1% Triton X-100/3% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA), 1:500 goat anti-human complement C3 (Thermo Fischer Scientific) and 1:250 rabbit anti-SigA or 1:250 rabbit anti-SepA antibodies and incubated for 1 h at 4 °C. After washing twice in 1X PBS/1% Triton X-100, samples were stained with secondary antibodies by resuspending bacterial pellets in 1X PBS/1% Triton X-100/3% BSA, 1:250 donkey anti-goat Alexa Fluor 568 (Invitrogen), 1:1,000 Goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 647 (Invitrogen) and 1:1,000 DAPI (Life Technologies) and incubated for 1 h at 4 °C in the dark followed by two washes in water. Bacteria were resuspended in water and imaged using a laser-scanning TCS SP8 confocal microscope (Leica) using a 63x objective.

Proteolysis Assays.

Albumin-free plasma was incubated for 18 h at 37 °C with or without 1 µg pure SepA or SigA and samples were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and stained with Instant Blue solution. For mass spectrometry analyses, overnight products were loaded on a 4 to 15% SDS-PAGE precast gel (Bio-Rad), and specific bands (named Band 1 and Band 2) were cut from gels and analyzed by the Strasbourg Esplanade mass spectrometry platform. Additional proteolysis assays were performed by incubating 1 µg of pure human C3 (Merck) with SepA, SigA, and heat-inactivated SigA and SepA for 18 h at 37 °C in 1X PBS. Degradation products were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and stained with Instant Blue solution or western blot analysis using 1:2,000 goat anti-human complement C3 antibody (Thermo Scientific) diluted in 1X TBS/0,5% Tween 20/3% BSA. After overnight incubation, the membrane was washed three times in 1X TBS/0.5% Tween 20 and incubated for 1 h with 1:10,000 horseradish peroxidase-conjugated rabbit anti-goat IgG (Abcam). After three washes, the membrane was revealed using the Super Signal ECL kit.

Mass–Spectrometry Analysis by NanoLC–MS/MS: In-Gel Samples, Database Search, and Protein Validation.

For LC–MS/MS analyses of the in-gel samples, bands were excised from the SDS-PAGE gel, were destained several times in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate containing 50% (v/v) acetonitrile, further dehydrated with 100% acetonitrile, and then reduced with 10 mM DTT for 1 h at 56 °C. Proteins were then alkylated with 55 mM iodoacetamide for 1 h in the dark at room temperature. Gel pieces were washed again with the destaining solutions described above. 80 ng of modified sequencing-grade trypsin (10 ng/µL; Promega) were added to each dehydrated gel sample for overnight digestion at 37 °C. The resulting peptides were extracted twice from the gel pieces with a first solution of 60% acetonitrile and 5% formic acid, followed by a second extraction in 100% acetonitrile. Gel supernatants were finally vacuum-dried in a SpeedVac concentrator. Digested peptides were resuspended in 20 µL of 0.1% formic acid (solvent A) and injected on an Easy-nanoLC-1000 system coupled to a Q-Exactive Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Each sample was loaded on a C-18 precolumn (75 μm ID × 20 mm nanoViper, 3 µm Acclaim PepMap; Thermo-Fisher Scientific) and separated on an analytical C18 analytical column (75 μm ID × 25 cm nanoViper, 3 µm Acclaim PepMap) with a 60 min gradient of solvent B (0.1% of formic acid in acetonitrile).

MS data were searched with Mascot algorithm (version 2.8, Matrix Science) against either the UniprotKB database with Shigella sonnei taxonomy (22.219 sequences, version 2021_02), and the Swiss-Prot database with human taxonomy (20.396 sequences, version 2021_01) or the Swiss-Prot with all taxonomies (568.744 sequences, version 2022_05). The resulting Mascot files were imported into Proline v1.0 package (39) to align the identified proteins. Proteins were then validated on Mascot rank equal to 1, a Mascot score superior to 25, and 1% FDR (False Discovery Rate) on both peptide spectrum matches and protein sets (based on Mascot score). The deposition of the data from the in-gel samples has been submitted to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository, the validation process is ongoing. For SI Appendix, Fig. S5C, the number of observations of each amino acid from the sequences was counted in all the spectra validated with 1% FDR.

Guinea Pig Model of Shigellosis.

The ascorbate-deficient guinea pig model of infection by Shigella was previously described by Skerniskyte et al. (16) and was used in this study to follow prolonged infections (up to 48 h) with S. sonnei WT pGFP, S. sonnei ∆sigA pGFP, S. flexneri 5a WT pGFP, S. flexneri 5a ∆sepA pGFP, S. sonnei ∆sigA pSigA, and S. flexneri 5a ∆sepA pSepA strains. Before the challenge, complemented strains were grown in the presence 500 µM IPTG to maximize the expression of SigA and SepA. In brief, young ascorbate-deficient guinea pig females were intrarectally challenged with 1011 CFU in the exponential phase (OD600nm = 0.6). 48 h postinfection, animals were killed, and biological samples were collected and processed as follows.

CFU counting.

Colons were flushed with 500 µL 1X PBS and plated onto LB agar + Amp for 18 h at 37 °C for luminal CFU counting. To estimate mucosal CFU, 0.5 cm tissue sections of colons were weighted and homogenized in 500 µL 1X PBS with Bead Mill Homogenizer (VWR) and then plated onto LB agar + Amp. Blood samples were also collected in the presence of citrate and 50 µL were plated onto LB agar plates + Amp to estimate CFU/mL in blood.

Tissue labeling and imaging.

1 cm sections of colons were fixed in PFA, washed with a sucrose gradient (from 15% to 30% sucrose), and frozen in OCT blocks. 10 µm sections were obtained using a CM-3050 cryostat (Leica Biosystems). For histological studies, sectioned tissues were stained with hematoxylin-eosin standard protocol and imaged with an Axioskop two transmission light microscope (Zeiss) using 10x or 20x objectives. For immunofluorescence, tissues were washed three times in 1X PBS/0,1% Triton, 1X PBS, water, and stained overnight at 4 °C in humid conditions with primary antibodies. The following antibodies were used for immunofluorescent staining: 1:100 mouse anti-human CD31 (BD Pharmingen), 1:100 mouse anti-human CD34 (Biolegend), 1:2,000 goat anti-human complement C3 antibody (Thermo Fischer Scientific) and 1:1,000 goat anti-albumin antibody (Sigma). The next day, slides were washed three times, and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with 1:1.000 of donkey anti-goat IgG Alexa Fluor 568 (Invitrogen) and Dapi (Life Technologies). Slides were washed again, mounted with ProLongGold® (Invitrogen), and imaged using a laser-scanning TCS SP8 confocal microscope (Leica).

Statistical Analysis.

One-Way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons post hoc test or Two-Way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons post hoc test were used to determine statistical significance with GraphPad Prism v10.2.0 software.
