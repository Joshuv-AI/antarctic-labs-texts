Some Generalizations of Totient Function with Elementary Symmetric Sums Thanks: Dedicated to Bhagawan Sri Sathya Sai Baba.
Udvas Acharjee, N. Uday Kiran

Abstract
We generalize certain totient functions using elementary symmetric polynomials and derive explicit product forms for the totient functions involving the second elementary symmetric sum. This work follows from the work of Tóth [The Ramanujan Journal, 2022] where the totient function was generalized using the first and the k t ​ h k^{th} elementary symmetric polyomial. We also provide some observations on the behavior of the totient function with an arbitrary j t ​ h j^{th} elementary symmetric polynomial. We then outline a method for solving a certain the restricted linear congruence problem with a greatest common divisor constraint on a quadratic form, illustrated by a concrete example. Most importantly, we demonstrate the equivalence between obtaining product forms for generalized totient functions, counting zeros of specific polynomials over finite fields, and resolving a broad class of restricted linear congruence problems . Keywords: Totient Function, Quadratic Forms over Finite Fields, Restricted Linear Congruence, Menon’s identity, Elementary Symmetric Sums. MSC(2020): 11A05 ⋅ \cdot 11A07 ⋅ \cdot 11A25

Introduction

The Euler totient function
\varphi(n)
counts the number of positive integers less than
n
that are relatively prime to
n
. Over time, various generalizations of this classical function have been introduced and rediscovered from different perspectives. Several generalizations are made using polynomials. In 1967, Menon
[
12
]
extended the Euler totient function using a polynomial with integral coefficients
f(x)
by defining

\phi_{f}(n)=\left|\{x:1\leq x\leq n,\,\gcd(f(x),n)=1\}\right|.

Obviously,
f(x)=x
is the classical Euler totient function. Menon’s definition also encompasses several other notable generalizations as special cases:

Schemmel totient function

[
17
]
, obtained by setting

f(x)=x(x-1)\cdots(x-b+1)\quad\text{for some }b\in\mathbb{N}.

Nagell’s totient function

[
13
,
6
]
, given by

f(x)=x(b-x)\quad\text{with }b\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}.

However, this one-variable polynomial approach does not generalize Jordan’s totient function. To address this, Stevens
[
18
]
proposed in 1971 a multivariate generalization. For a system
S=\{f_{1}(x),\dots,f_{m}(x)\}
of
m
integral polynomials, define

\phi_{S}(n)=\left|\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in\{1,\dots,n\}^{m}:\gcd(f_{1}(x_{1}),\dots,f_{m}(x_{m}),n)=1\}\right|.

If each
f_{i}(x)=x
, this recovers Jordan’s totient function.

Very recently, Csizmazia and Tóth
[
7
]
extended Stevens’ construction further to multivariable polynomials
f_{i}:\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{k}\to\mathbb{Z}_{n}
. Their generalized totient function associated with a set
F=\{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\}
is defined as,

\varphi_{F}(n):=\left|\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{k}:\gcd(f_{1}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k}),\dots,f_{m}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k}),n)=1\}\right|.

(1)

This function was proven to be multiplicative and the following product form
[
7
, Theorem 2.1]
was obtained:

\varphi_{F}(n)=n^{k}\prod_{p\mid n}\left(1-\frac{N_{F}(p)}{p^{k}}\right)

(2)

where
N_{F}(p)
is the number of zeros of the set of polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{k}
i.e.,

N_{F}(p)=|\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{k}):f_{i}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\equiv 0\text{ mod }p,\forall f_{i}\in F\}|.

This definition also recovers Jordan’s totient function when
f_{i}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=x_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq k
.

A natural strengthening of this condition demands each polynomial value be coprime to
n
individually:

\phi_{F}(n):=\left|\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{k}:\gcd(f_{1}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k}),n)=\cdots=\gcd(f_{m}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k}),n)=1\}\right|.

(3)

It is clear that if
f=\prod_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}
, then
\phi_{F}(n)=\varphi_{\{f\}}(n)
. Therefore, it follows that
\phi_{F}(n)
is also multiplicative. It is evident that
\phi_{F}(n)\leq\varphi_{F}(n)
.

We give a relation between the two functions
\varphi_{F}(\cdot)
and
\phi_{F}(\cdot)
using the principle of inclusion-exclusion in section (
2
). Therefore, a product form for one of these functions leads to the other as well.

An illustrative example of
\phi_{F}(n)
can be found in Tóth
[
20
]
with

F=\{f_{1},f_{k}\},\quad f_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i},\quad f_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\prod_{i=1}^{k}x_{i},

generalizing the restrictions on two-parameter function introduced by Arai and Carlitz
[
1
,
4
]
. Using convolution identities and induction, Tóth obtained the elegant product formula:

\phi_{F}(n)=n^{k}\prod_{p\mid n}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\left(\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{k}-\frac{(-1)^{k}}{p^{k}}\right),

where the product runs over all primes dividing
n
.

Our work in this paper is inspired by the observation that Tóth’s choice of
f_{1}
and
f_{k}
corresponds to the
1^{st}
and
k^{th}
elementary symmetric sums.

e_{j}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{j}\leq k}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{j}},

for
j=1
and
j=k
.

In this work, we focus mainly on extending the generalization to the case of the second elementary symmetric polynomial
e_{2}
,

e_{2}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k}x_{i}x_{j},

We aim to provide explicit formula for the product form and extend some results to certain other symmetric sums.
For this, we utilize results from
[
10
]
on zeros of quadratic forms over finite fields to derive explicit product formulas for the corresponding generalized totient functions. Since these functions are multiplicative
[
7
]
, it suffices to consider the prime-power case.

Our study relates closely to the classical problem of
restricted linear congruences
, which concerns counting solutions
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
to

a_{1}x_{1}+\dots+a_{k}x_{k}\equiv b\pmod{n}

with constraints

\gcd(x_{i},n)=t_{i},\quad\text{where }t_{i}\mid n,

a problem with deep roots going back to Lehmer
[
9
]
and later work by Rademacher
[
14
]
, Brauer
[
3
]
, Dixon
[
8
]
, Rearick
[
16
]
, Cohen
[
5
]
, and most recently Bibak et al.
[
2
]
who provide general formulas for arbitrary parameters.

Restricted linear congruences closely connect to generalized totient functions. For instance, summing over all
a
with
\gcd(a,n)=1
the solutions to
x\equiv a\pmod{n}
yields the classical totient
\varphi(n)
. Similarly, we observe that Tóth’s formula for
\phi_{\{e_{1},e_{k}\}}(n)
is always divisible by
\varphi(n)
. In fact, our inspiration originates from our observation that the quotient

\phi_{\{e_{1},e_{k}\}}(n)/\varphi(n)

equals the number of solutions to

x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}\equiv m\pmod{n},

subject to the constraints
\gcd(x_{1}\cdots x_{k},n)=1
and
\gcd(m,n)=1
.

We demonstrate similar phenomena hold for more general cases involving
e_{2}
. The approach also simplifies the derivation of Menon-type identities for certain classes of generalized totient functions. This work thus reveals a beautiful interplay between restricted linear congruences, zeros of polynomial systems over finite fields, and product formulas of generalized Euler totient functions.

A Connection Between the Generalized Two Totient Functions:
\varphi_{F}(\cdot)
and
\phi_{F}(\cdot)

Let
F=\{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\}
be a set of
m
polynomials with integer coefficients, and
\varphi_{F}(n)
and
\phi_{F}(n)
be defined as in equations (
1
) and (
3
).
For
F=\varnothing
define
\varphi_{F}(n)=\phi_{F}(n)=0
. For
F\neq\varnothing
define
\varphi_{F}(1)=\phi_{F}(1)=1
.

Theorem 2.0.1

(Relation between two totient functions)
.

: The following identities hold

\phi_{F}(p^{k})=\sum_{J\subseteq F}(-1)^{|J|+1}\varphi_{J}(p^{k})\quad\textnormal{and }\quad\varphi_{F}(p^{k})=\sum_{J\subseteq F}(-1)^{|J|+1}\phi_{J}(p^{k}).

Proof.

By equation (
2
) we have:

\varphi_{F}(n)=n^{k}\prod_{p\mid n}\left(1-\frac{N_{F}(p)}{p^{k}}\right),

where
N_{F}(p)
is the number of zeros of the set of polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{k}
i.e.,

N_{F}(p)=|\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{k}):f_{i}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\equiv 0\text{ mod }p,\forall f_{i}\in F\}|.

Now notice that for
f=\prod_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
,

N_{\{f\}}(p)=|\bigcup_{f_{i}\in F}\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{k}:f_{i}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\equiv 0\text{ mod }p\}|.

By the inclusion-exclusion principle, this clearly means that,

N_{\{f\}}(p)=\sum_{J\subseteq F}(-1)^{|J|+1}N_{J}(p).

So we obtain the following expression:

\varphi_{\{f\}}(p^{k})=p^{k}\left(1-\frac{N_{\{f\}}(p)}{p^{k}}\right)=p^{k}\left(1-\frac{\sum_{J\subseteq F}(-1)^{|J|+1}N_{J}(p)}{p^{k}}\right).

Since
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\subseteq F\\
J\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|J|+1}=1
, therefore we have the identity:

\varphi_{\{f\}}(p^{k})=\sum_{J\subseteq F}(-1)^{|J|+1}\varphi_{J}(p^{k}).

The other identity can be proved in a similar fashion.
∎

3
Totient Function Defined using Elementary Symmetric Polynomials

A quadratic form
f
in
k
indeterminates over a finte field
\mathbb{F}_{p}
is a homogeneous polynomial in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[x_{1},\dots,x_{k}]
of degree 2 or the zero polynomial. The quadratic form
f
can be written as
f(x)=x^{T}Ax
for a symmetric matrix
A
. The quadractic form
f
is non-degenerate if the associated symmetric matrix
A
is non-singular (invertible).

We use the following result from
[
10
]
.

Theorem 3.0.1
.

Let
f
be a non-degenerate quadratic form over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
,
p
odd, in
k
indeterminates. Then for
b\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
the number of solutions
N(b)
of
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=b
in
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
is

N(b)=\begin{cases}p^{k-1}+p^{(k-1)/2}\eta((-1)^{(k-1)/2}b\Delta),&\text{ if }k\text{ is }odd\\
p^{k-1}+\nu(b)p^{(k-2)/2}\eta((-1)^{k/2}\Delta),&\text{ if }k\text{ is }even.\end{cases}

where
\eta
ia the quadratic character of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
and
\Delta=\text{det}(f)
. The integer valued function
\nu
on
\mathbb{F}_{p}
is defined by
\nu(b)=-1
for
b\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}
and
\nu(0)=p-1
.

Using the above result, we obtain a formula for the totient function
\varphi_{\{e_{2}\}}(n)
, where
e_{2}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
denotes the second elementary symmetric sum in
k
variables with
k\geq 2
. The result follows by identifying the appropriate matrix and determining the values of
k
and
p
for which it becomes degenerate.

Theorem 3.0.2
.

For prime
p>2
and
k>1
, the number of solutions,
N_{k}(e_{2},p)
of the congruence:

e_{2}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})=0\text{ mod }p

is:

N_{k}(e_{2},p)=\begin{cases}p^{k-1}+(p-1)p^{(k-1)/2}\eta((-1)^{(k-1)/2}(1-\gcd(k-1,p))),&\text{ if }k\text{ is odd,}\\
p^{k-1}+(p-1)p^{(k-2)/2}\eta((-1)^{k/2+1}(k-1)),&\text{ if }k\text{ is even}.\end{cases}

Also,

N_{k}(e_{2},2)=\frac{1}{4}\left(2^{k+1}+2(\sqrt{2})^{k+1}\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{k\pi}{4}\right)\right).

Proof.

For prime
p>2
, the symmetric matrix
A
associated with
e_{2}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
is given by:

A=\begin{bmatrix}0&2^{-1}&\cdots&2^{-1}\\
2^{-1}&0&\cdots&2^{-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
2^{-1}&2^{-1}&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}_{k\times k}

We split the proof into two cases depending on the symmetric matrix
A
is degenerate or not:

1.

When
A
is non-degenerate. So
\Delta=\text{det}(A)=(-1)^{k-1}2^{-k}(k-1)
. Therefore, using theorem (
3.0.1
)

N_{k}(e_{2},p)=\begin{cases}p^{k-1},&\text{ if }k\text{ is odd}\\
p^{k-1}+(p-1)p^{(k-2)/2}\eta((-1)^{k/2+1}(k-1)),&\text{ if }k\text{ is even}.\end{cases}

2.

When
A
is degenerate, that is, when
\Delta=\det(A)=0
, first note that
k\equiv 1\pmod{p}
. The null space of
A
is given by
a(1,1,\dots,1)^{T}
for
a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
. We will define a set of
k-1
linearly independent vectors
\{e_{2},\dots,e_{k}\}
that doesn’t belong to the span of the vector
(1,1,\dots,1)^{T}
.
Define
e_{j}=(-1,0,\dots,0,1,0,\dots,0)^{T}
where
1
is in the
j
-th row in the vector. We obtain the following:

e_{i}^{T}Ae_{j}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }i=j,\\
2^{-1},&\text{ if }i\neq j.\end{cases}

The defines a non-degenerate
(k-1)\times(k-1)
matrix
\overline{A}
with
\text{det}(\overline{A})=k/2^{k}
. Therefore from theorem (
3.0.1
) we have:

N_{k}(e_{2},p)=\begin{cases}p^{k-1}+(p-1)p^{(k-1)/2}\eta((-1)^{(k-1)/2}),&\text{ if }k\text{ is odd},\\
p^{k-1},&\text{if }k\text{ is even}.\end{cases}

Combining these two cases we get the theorem for
p>2
.

For
p=2
we let a vector
v\in\{0,1\}^{k}
have
j
1’s. The symmetric sum written as a quadratic form gives:

v^{T}Av=\frac{j(j-1)}{2}

which is equal to
0
when
j\equiv 1\text{ mod }4
or
j\equiv 0\text{ mod }4
.
This gives the following form for
p=2
,

N_{k}(e_{2},2)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
j\equiv 0,1\text{ mod }4\end{subarray}}^{k}\binom{k}{j}.

Now this is a sieved sum of the binomial coefficients and therefore we can use its generating function
f(x)=(1+x)^{k}
and the fact that for
\omega_{n}=e^{2\pi i/n}
,

\sum_{j=0}^{n}\omega_{n}^{m}=\begin{cases}n,&\text{ if }n\mid m,\\
0,&\text{ if }n\nmid m,\end{cases}

to obtain a closed form as follows:

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
j\equiv 0\text{ mod }4\end{subarray}}^{k}\binom{k}{j}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j=0}^{3}f(\omega_{4}^{j}).

Substituting
\omega_{4}=i
such that
i^{2}=-1
we obtain,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
j\equiv 0\text{ mod }4\end{subarray}}^{k}\binom{k}{j}=\frac{1}{4}\left(2^{k}+(1+i)^{k}+(1-i)^{k}\right).

We write
(1+i)
in polar form as
\sqrt{2}(\cos\frac{\pi}{4}+i\sin\frac{\pi}{4})
and
(i-1)
as
\sqrt{2}(\cos\frac{-\pi}{4}+i\sin\frac{-\pi}{4})
. We then use the well known De Moivre’s theorem to obtain,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
j\equiv 0\text{ mod }4\end{subarray}}^{k}\binom{k}{j}=\frac{1}{4}\left(2^{k}+2(\sqrt{2})^{k}\cos\frac{k\pi}{4}\right).

Similarly, for the sum with
j\equiv 1\text{ mod }4
we use the generating function
f(x)=x^{3}(1+x)^{k}
. Using the same technique, this would give us,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
j\equiv 1\text{ mod }4\end{subarray}}^{k}\binom{k}{j}=\frac{1}{4}\left(2^{k}+2(\sqrt{2})^{k}\sin\frac{k\pi}{4}\right).

The result is obtained by summing the two terms.
∎

Remark 3.0.1
.

Using the idea given in the proof of theorem (
3.0.2
) for the case
p=2
, we can obtain the number
N_{k}(e_{l},2)
for all positive
l
such that
l\leq k
. Note that the number of solutions ,

of

e_{l}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\equiv 0\text{ mod }2

x_{i}
’s such that
x_{i}=1
. More precisely the value of the function
e_{l}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
with exactly
j

1
is
\binom{j}{l}\text{ mod }2

\binom{j}{l}=0
for
j<l
. This requires us to characterize all such

such that
\binom{j}{l}\equiv 0\text{ mod }2
. Fortunately, Lucas Theorem
[
11
]
, a well known result in number theory and combinatorics, gives us a nice characterization for this. Lucas theroem states the following:

\binom{j}{l}=\prod_{i=0}^{m}\binom{j_{i}}{l_{i}}\text{ mod }p,

where
l=\sum_{i=0}^{m}l_{i}p^{i}
and
j=\sum_{i=0}^{m}j_{i}p^{i}
. Now, for
p=2
, this theorem leads us to conclude that
\binom{j}{l}\equiv 0\text{ mod }2
is and only if
l
is not a submask of
j
i.e. in their binary representations,
j\&l\neq l
. This characterizes the number of ones ,
j
, in the tuple
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
such that
\binom{j}{l}
is even and leads to the following equation:

N_{k}(e_{l},2)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
j\&l\neq l\end{subarray}}^{k}\binom{k}{j}.

As an example, we will obtain a form for the case
l=3
. First note that the condition
j\&3\neq 3
implies that either
j\text{ mod }4\in\{0,1,2\}
. Since the cases for
j\text{ mod }4\in\{0,1\}
have already been evaluated in theorem
3.0.2
, we take up the case
j\equiv 2\text{ mod }4
. Using the same method as before with the generating function
f(x)=x^{2}(1+x)^{k}
, we obatin,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
j\equiv 2\text{ mod }4\end{subarray}}^{k}\binom{k}{j}=\frac{1}{4}(f(1)+f(i)+f(-1)+f(-i))

Evaluting the expression in the right hand side gives us the identity:

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
j\equiv 2\text{ mod }4\end{subarray}}^{k}\binom{k}{j}=\frac{1}{4}\left(2^{k}-2(\sqrt{2})^{k}\cos\frac{\pi k}{4}\right).

From this, and the identities in the theorem
3.0.2
we obtain:

N_{k}(e_{3},2)=\frac{1}{4}\left(3\cdot 2^{k}+(\sqrt{2})^{k}2\sin\frac{\pi k}{4}\right).

3.1
Solutions of Certain Restricted Linear Congruences

For
a_{1},\dots,a_{k},b,n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
n\geq 1
, a linear congruence in
k
-unknowns
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
is of the form

a_{1}x_{1}+\dots+a_{k}x_{k}\equiv b\text{ mod }n.

(6)

By a solution of (
6
) we mean an ordered
k
-tuple of integers modulo
n
, denoted by
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
that satisfies (
6
). Additionally, there might restrictions on the set of the solutions of (
6
). For instance, we might want to solve the linear congruence subject to certain conditions like
\gcd(x_{i},n)=t_{i},(1\leq i\leq k)
where
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}
are positive divisors of
n
. This problem has been dealt by Bibak in
[
2
]
.

In what follows, we will outline a method that solves the restricted linear congruence problem subject to the
\gcd
conditions on certain functions of the unknowns.

Theorem 3.1.1
.

Let
F=\{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\}
be a set of homogeneous polynomials and
g_{k}(m,n)
denote the number of solutions of the restricted linear congruence:

a_{1}x_{1}+\dots+a_{k}x_{k}\equiv b\text{ mod }n

subject to:
\gcd(f_{i}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}),n)=1
for each
1\leq i\leq m
then

g_{k}(b,n)=g_{k}(\gcd(b,n),n)
.

Proof.

Let
\gcd(b,n)=d
and denote
r=b/d
. Consider the map:

(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\mapsto(r^{-1}a_{1},\dots,r^{-1}a_{k}).

Under this map every solution
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
of:

a_{1}+\dots+a_{k}\equiv b\text{ mod }n,

can be uniquely mapped to a solution
(r^{-1}a_{1},\dots,r^{-1}a_{k})
of :

r^{-1}a_{1}+\dots+r^{-1}a_{k}\equiv d\text{ mod }n,

subject to the given conditions.
∎

Theorem 3.1.2
.

Let
F=\{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\}
be a set of homogeneous polynomials and for
\gcd(b,n)=1
let
g_{k}(b,n)
denote the number of solutions of :

a_{1}x_{1}+\dots+a_{k}x_{k}\equiv b\text{ mod }n

subject to
\gcd(f_{i}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}),n)=1
for each
1\leq i\leq m
. Then we have the following:

g_{k}(b,n)=\frac{\phi_{F^{\prime}}(n)}{\varphi(n)}.

where
F^{\prime}=F\cup\{a_{1}x_{1}+\dots+a_{k}x_{k}\}
.

Proof.

Consider the map:

f_{b}:(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\mapsto(bx_{1},\dots,bx_{k})

This is clearly a bijective map from every solution of:

a_{1}x_{1}+\dots+\ a_{k}x_{k}\equiv 1\text{ mod }n

to a solution of

a_{1}x_{1}+\dots+a_{k}x_{k}\equiv b\text{ mod }n

and also preserves the conditions given in the theorem. The identity follows from the definition of
\phi_{F}(n)
.
∎

for odd

proof of theorem

Consider the sets:
