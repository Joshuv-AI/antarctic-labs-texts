Cholinesterase deficiency and anesthesia management: Clinical challenges and coping strategies
Authors: Tiantian Liu, Chaofeng Sun, Song Ye, Qinsong Ye, Ding Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCholinesterase deficiency is a rare genetic disorder that impairs the activity of acetylcholinesterase and butyrylcholinesterase. It causes severe perioperative risks due to prolonged neuromuscular blockade induced by succinylcholine and ester-based anesthetics, and may lead to life-threatening complications such as respiratory depression and cardiovascular instability, thus requiring personalized anesthesia protocols for affected patients. A comprehensive literature review was conducted to synthesize the pathophysiology, genetic basis, and pharmacokinetic impacts of cholinesterase deficiency on anesthetic drugs, and clinical case analyses were performed to validate perioperative management strategies. Genetic mutations such as BCHE c. 695T>A are the primary etiology, and butyrylcholinesterase deficiency is more prevalent and associated with significant anesthetic adverse events. The use of alternative anesthetic agents including non-depolarizing neuromuscular blockers and amide-type local anesthetics, as well as reversal agents such as sugammadex, can significantly reduce perioperative risks. Multimodal monitoring including train-of-four and neurophysiologic monitoring and preoperative screening further optimize safety. Five clinical cases confirmed the efficacy of tailored strategies in diverse surgical settings. Proactive preoperative screening, cholinesterase-independent anesthetic regimens, and interdisciplinary collaboration are pivotal for mitigating risks in cholinesterase-deficient patients. Emerging advances including CRISPR gene therapy and AI-driven pharmacokinetic models hold great promise for precision anesthesia, while genetic counseling remains essential for long-term patient management.

Introduction

Cholinesterase deficiency, characterized by diminished enzyme activity, impairs neurotransmitter metabolism—particularly acetylcholine breakdown—with profound clinical consequences. In anesthesia, butyrylcholinesterase (BuChE) deficiency significantly prolongs the action of neuromuscular blocking agents (e.g., succinylcholine, mivacurium), increasing risks of prolonged paralysis and respiratory complications, necessitating extended postoperative monitoring.[12] This enzyme also serves as a biomarker for neurodegenerative disorders; due to accelerated depletion of acetylcholine, the patient’s activity is reduced and cognitive decline is exacerbated.[34]

Etiologically, the deficiency stems from genetic mutations, environmental exposures (e.g., organophosphate pesticides, nerve agents), or malnutrition, with acute inhibition posing occupational and environmental hazards.[5] Such complexity underscores the need to elucidate molecular mechanisms to guide therapeutic interventions. In perioperative care, impaired drug metabolism elevates anesthesia-related risks, demanding protocol adjustments based on patient-specific cholinesterase profiles.

Continued research into cholinesterase dynamics is critical for advancing neurologic therapeutics and optimizing anesthesia safety. Prioritizing this understanding will enhance both targeted treatments for neurodegenerative conditions and personalized approaches to perioperative management.

This study provides practical and evidence-based frameworks for anesthesiologists, surgeons, and critical care practitioners, helping them identify high-risk patients preoperatively, select optimal anesthetic regimens, and implement effective perioperative monitoring, thus reducing the incidence of anesthesia-related adverse events (e.g., prolonged paralysis, respiratory failure) and improving surgical safety. For patients with cholinesterase deficiency, the personalized management strategies and genetic counseling recommendations proposed in this study can not only ensure perioperative safety, but also provide long-term guidance for their future medical care, avoiding repeated anesthetic risks. Additionally, the study’s summary of emerging therapeutic advances (e.g., CRISPR gene therapy) offers new hope for the fundamental treatment of this genetic disorder, further improving the quality of life of affected patients.

Materials and Methods

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science databases from their inception to January 2025, using the keywords “cholinesterase deficiency,” “butyrylcholinesterase deficiency,” “anesthesia management,” “neuromuscular blockade,” and “sugammadex.” The inclusion criteria were original research, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and clinical case reports focusing on the pathophysiology, genetic basis, and anesthetic management of cholinesterase deficiency; non-English articles and irrelevant studies were excluded. A total of 68 relevant studies were finally included for synthesis and analysis. In addition, five clinical cases of cholinesterase deficiency with anesthetic adverse events from published literatures were collected and analyzed to validate the efficacy of perioperative management strategies, with case data including clinical characteristics, laboratory tests, anesthetic regimens, treatment measures, and outcomes.

Cholinesterase Deficiency: Pathophysiology

Function and mechanism of cholinesterase

Cholinesterases include two primary enzymes: acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and BuChE, distinguished by their substrate preferences.

Genetic basis of cholinesterase deficiency

Cholinesterase deficiencies are primarily caused by genetic mutations disrupting enzyme synthesis or function. AChE deficiency, though rare, stems from AChE gene mutations that reduce or abolish enzyme activity, with mutation type dictating symptom severity.[6] Environmental factors (e.g., organophosphorus pesticides), may also inhibit AChE, mimicking genetic deficiency and necessitating dual diagnostic consideration.[7] While AChE inhibitors are used in Alzheimer’s disease to enhance cholinergic signaling, their efficacy depends on mutation-specific profiles, underscoring the need for personalized therapies.[8]

BuChE deficiency, more prevalent than AChE deficiency, arises from BChE gene mutations and exhibits autosomal recessive inheritance. Enzyme activity ranges from partial to complete loss, with carriers often asymptomatic.[9] Specific variants (e.g., BCHE c. 695T>A [p.Val204Asp]) and compound heterozygous mutations are linked to prolonged neuromuscular blockade after succinylcholine use.[910] Genetic testing is critical, as ~ 8% of Americans face moderate risk of BuChE-related complications.[1112] Clinical heterogeneity is evident, with cases like compound heterozygous BChE mutations in symptomatic patients.[13] BuChE SNPs (e.g., rs3495, rs1803274) may also associate with substance abuse, expanding the gene’s clinical relevance beyond anesthesia.[14]

In summary, unraveling BuChE’s genetic basis is vital for optimizing anesthesia safety and exploring broader health implications. Genetic testing and mutation research remain pivotal for personalized management and pathophysiological insights.

Clinical manifestations of cholinesterase deficiency

Cholinesterase deficiency manifests clinically as congenital myasthenic syndrome (CMS), primarily caused by AChE dysfunction due to mutations in the COLQ gene. This impairs acetylcholine breakdown at neuromuscular junctions, leading to persistent muscle stimulation, weakness, and fatigue. Severe cases may involve early respiratory compromise from impaired neuromuscular transmission.[1516]

CMS symptoms span mild to life-threatening, including dysphagia, respiratory insufficiency, and mobility limitations. Dysphagia increases aspiration risks, often requiring speech therapy, while respiratory muscle weakness may necessitate noninvasive ventilation.[171819] Emerging therapies targeting acetylcholine receptor clustering aim to enhance neuromuscular function.[17]

Diagnosis relies on genetic testing and electrophysiological studies. Although acetylcholinesterase inhibitors are standard treatment, efficacy varies by mutation subtype.[1516] Ongoing research focuses on molecular mechanisms to refine therapeutic strategies, emphasizing personalized approaches to improve quality of life.[151617181920]

In summary, CMS underscores the interplay of genetic defects and neuromuscular dysfunction. Multidisciplinary management—addressing swallowing, respiratory, and mobility challenges—combined with early intervention, remains critical for optimizing outcomes.[1516171819]

Anesthesia drugs and their relationship with cholinesterase

Metabolic pathways of common anesthetic drugs

Anesthetic drugs’ metabolic profiles are critical for individualized administration in cholinesterase-deficient patients, as impaired enzyme activity may alter drug clearance and increase adverse reaction risks. Most clinical anesthetics (propofol, ketamine, sevoflurane) rely on hepatic metabolism, independent of cholinesterase (AChE/BuChE), making them safe alternative options for cholinesterase-deficient patients and the core of tailored anesthetic regimens.

Propofol is primarily metabolized in the liver via glucuronidation and sulfation, with no involvement of cholinesterase in its clearance pathway; this independent metabolic characteristic ensures its stable efficacy and rapid elimination in cholinesterase-deficient patients, making it a first-choice agent for total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA).[2122]

Ketamine undergoes hepatic biotransformation by cytochrome P450 enzymes (CYP2B6, CYP2C9, CYP3A4), and its metabolic process does not depend on cholinesterase. Genetic variations of CYP450 may cause individual differences in ketamine effects, but no interaction with cholinesterase deficiency has been reported, supporting its safe use in this patient population.[2324]

Sevoflurane, a commonly used inhalational anesthetic, is metabolized in the liver to inorganic fluoride and hexafluoroisopropanol (HFIP), with its metabolism unrelated to cholinesterase activity. However, elevated fluoride levels may affect renal function in high-risk patients, and cautious use is still required in cholinesterase-deficient patients with pre-existing kidney disease, with no need for dose adjustment for cholinesterase deficiency alone.[25]

Role of cholinesterase in anesthetic drug metabolism

Cholinesterases (AChE and BuChE) are critical for anesthetic drug metabolism.

AChE terminates neuromuscular signaling by hydrolyzing acetylcholine, ensuring muscle relaxation. Its inhibitors, used in Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s therapies, elevate acetylcholine levels but pose toxicity risks at high doses (e.g., pesticides).[26] AChE also regulates non-neuronal processes like apoptosis and tumor suppression, suggesting anticancer applications.[27]

BuChE metabolizes succinylcholine, a depolarizing muscle relaxant. Deficiencies—caused by genetic mutations, liver disease, or drug interactions—prolong neuromuscular blockade, necessitating anesthetic vigilance.[2829] Succinylcholine additionally elevates cerebral blood flow and intracranial pressure, revealing neurophysiological complexities during anesthesia.[30]

Though slower than AChE, BuChE hydrolyzes substrates like atropine (albeit weakly) and exhibits non-catalytic roles in neural differentiation and detoxification. Its diverse catalytic activities (e.g., aryl acylamidase) underscore broad physiological contributions.[1031]

Understanding these enzymes’ dual metabolic and non-metabolic roles optimizes anesthetic protocols and informs therapeutic innovation, from neurologic disorders to oncology.

Impact of cholinesterase deficiency on anesthetic drug metabolism

Cholinesterase deficiency (genetic or acquired) disrupts anesthetic drug metabolism, as seen in congenital AChE deficiency, where succinylcholine accumulation causes prolonged paralysis and respiratory depression. Drug accumulation risks are influenced by enzyme activity (e.g., CYP2C8’s role in montelukast metabolism[32]), drug interactions (e.g., cytochrome P450 inhibitors altering clopidogrel/tamoxifen efficacy[33]), and physiological changes (e.g., post-surgical pharmacokinetic shifts[34]).

In BuChE deficiency, non-depolarizing agents (e.g., rocuronium) paired with sugammadex mitigate risks, particularly in neuromuscular disorders like myotonic dystrophy. Sugammadex ensures rapid reversal of neuromuscular blockade, reducing postoperative pulmonary complications and offering cost benefits in high-risk populations (e.g., cystic fibrosis).[353637]

Understanding these metabolic and pharmacodynamic interactions is essential for optimizing anesthesia safety in cholinesterase-deficient patients.

Anesthesia management strategies for patients with cholinesterase deficiency

Pre-anesthesia assessment and preparation

Patients with cholinesterase deficiency require meticulous pre-anesthetic evaluation to mitigate risks associated with neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs). A thorough history, including prior anesthetic complications, and physical assessments of neuromuscular/respiratory function are critical. For example, neuromuscular testing may reveal deficits linked to conditions like multiple sclerosis, while respiratory evaluations address ventilatory decline common in neuromuscular disorders.[383940] Laboratory confirmation of cholinesterase levels and specialist consultations (e.g., neurologists) further refine risk stratification.

The availability of sugammadex—a selective binder for steroidal NMBAs (e.g., rocuronium)—is a core preparatory requirement. As the first-choice reversal agent for this patient population, sugammadex demonstrates significant advantages over traditional acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (e.g., neostigmine): it ensures faster, more predictable reversal of neuromuscular blockade.[4142] reduces postoperative respiratory complications and residual paralysis,[43] and has a favorable safety profile with lower rates of nausea/vomiting. Cost-benefit analyses suggest that while sugammadex incurs higher upfront costs, its ability to reduce pulmonary complications may offset long-term healthcare expenditures.[44] This agent’s clinical value is definitive for perioperative safety in cholinesterase-deficient patients, and on-site availability is mandatory for all related surgical procedures.

Selection and dose adjustment of anesthetic agents

In patients with cholinesterase deficiency, anesthetic selection must prioritize avoiding depolarizing NMBAs (e.g., succinylcholine, mivacurium) to eliminate risks of prolonged paralysis due to impaired BuChE metabolism. When muscle relaxation is unavoidable, non-depolarizing NMBAs (e.g., rocuronium) are the preferred alternative, with strict dose minimization and mandatory neuromuscular monitoring. For high-risk populations such as those with myasthenia gravis.[45] sugammadex remains the first-line reversal option, and repeated doses may be necessary for patients with severe neuromuscular disorders (e.g., ALS) to ensure complete recovery.[46]

Total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) with propofol and remifentanil offers a safer alternative for most cases, eliminating NMBA use, while reducing postoperative nausea/vomiting (PONV).[47] and ensuring hemodynamic stability. In pediatric cases, TIVA demonstrates favorable recovery profiles[55], though residual blockade risks persist if NMBAs are administered without neuromuscular monitoring.[4849] Regional anesthesia (e.g., spinal/epidural) further circumvents NMBA reliance, serving as an effective supplementary strategy for tailored anesthetic plans.

Monitoring and support during anesthesia

Patients with cholinesterase deficiency, particularly BuChE deficiency, require vigilant intraoperative monitoring due to heightened risks of prolonged paralysis from NMBAs. Continuous train-of-four (TOF) monitoring is critical to prevent residual paralysis and awareness during emergence.[5051] Standard vital sign tracking (blood pressure, oxygen saturation, end-tidal CO2) should be augmented with continuous non-invasive blood pressure monitoring to mitigate hemodynamic instability.[52]

In surgeries involving neural risk, intraoperative neurophysiologic monitoring (IONM) provides real-time feedback to prevent irreversible nerve damage,[53] necessitating a skilled team for rapid response. Immediate access to emergency support measures—including mechanical ventilation and sugammadex for acute prolonged neuromuscular blockade—is essential. Anesthesia teams must also recognize cholinergic crisis signs (bradycardia, bronchospasm) and administer interventions (atropine, bronchodilators) promptly. A multimodal monitoring and emergency preparedness strategy ensures timely intervention for potential complications, underscoring perioperative safety for this high-risk group.

Clinical case analysis

Successful management cases

A 5-year-old boy undergoing outpatient inguinal herniorrhaphy developed prolonged neuromuscular blockade after mivacurium administration (0.3 mg/kg). Subsequent investigation revealed a markedly low plasma cholinesterase concentration (115 U/L).[54] A 5-year-old girl developed respiratory failure and miosis following succinylcholine administration during anesthesia, suggesting genetic BuChE deficiency. She received fresh frozen plasma, recovered fully after 24 hours of observation and was discharged the next day.[55] A 4-year-old boy with obstructive sleep apnea underwent elective adenotonsillectomy, with failure of spontaneous respiration resumption postoperatively. He was successfully extubated after 7 hours, and subsequent testing revealed a plasma cholinesterase level of 4,561 U/L.[56] A 30-year-old parturient developed prolonged neuromuscular blockade after general anesthesia induction with thiopental and succinylcholine, requiring mechanical ventilation for 6 hours. Postoperative serum cholinesterase was markedly low at 1,123 U/L (normal 4,650–10,440 U/L), suggesting a deficiency.[57] A 51-year-old woman with upper and mid-abdominal pain had a markedly low serum cholinesterase level of 211 U/L (normal 4,000–13,000 U/L); genetic testing revealed compound heterozygous pathogenic BCHE mutations. Gastroscopy was performed without succinylcholine or mivacurium, and her postoperative course was uneventful.[13] The core clinical data, laboratory findings, treatment measures and outcomes of the above five cases are summarized in Table 1 for intuitive comparison and analysis.

Clinical Characteristics and Management of Cholinesterase Deficiency Cases

Case summary and experience sharing

These five cases illustrate the spectrum of BuChE deficiency, ranging from asymptomatic low levels to prolonged neuromuscular blockade requiring mechanical ventilation. Patients presented with varied clinical scenarios, including routine surgeries (herniorrhaphy, adenotonsillectomy), cesarean section, and gastroscopy. The cases highlight the importance of considering BuChE deficiency in patients with prolonged muscle weakness after succinylcholine or mivacurium administration, the utility of preoperative cholinesterase testing and genetic analysis, and the need for individualized anesthetic plans avoiding these agents in susceptible individuals. Management strategies included respiratory support, fresh frozen plasma, and alternative neuromuscular blocking agents. Ultimately, these cases emphasize the importance of early recognition, prompt intervention, and patient education regarding this potentially serious condition.

When prolonged neuromuscular blockade raises suspicion for BuChE deficiency, several key interventions are essential. First, maintain respiratory support until spontaneous recovery or complete hydrolysis of the causative agent. Second, obtain cholinesterase levels for the patient and family members to identify potential deficiencies in others. Third, strictly avoid future exposure to succinylcholine and mivacurium, opting for alternative neuromuscular blocking agents if needed. Fourth, clearly document the diagnosis in the patient’s medical record and educate both the patient and family about the implications for future anesthetic management, emphasizing the importance of informing healthcare providers about this condition. Finally, genetic testing is recommended to confirm the diagnosis, characterize the specific genetic variant, and provide more personalized risk assessment for the patient and their family. This comprehensive approach helps ensure patient safety and informs future anesthetic care.

Latest research advances and future directions

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) hold transformative potential in anesthesiology, enhancing patient monitoring, risk prediction, and personalized anesthesia planning through analysis of large datasets. These technologies, already applied in broader healthcare for imaging optimization, early diagnosis, and drug discovery (e.g., target identification and treatment response prediction),[585960] could similarly revolutionize anesthesia protocols. Challenges include ensuring data quality, addressing ethical concerns (e.g., algorithmic bias), and integrating AI tools into clinical workflows. Responsible adoption demands collaboration among clinicians, data scientists, and regulators to establish safety-focused guidelines. Overcoming these hurdles could position AI/ML as indispensable tools for advancing precision anesthesia and improving outcomes.

Conclusion

Cholinesterase deficiency, a rare genetic disorder, critically impacts anesthesia management due to risks of prolonged neuromuscular blockade and respiratory compromise. Preoperative screening—including family history and prior adverse reactions to NMBAs—is essential to identify at-risk patients. Avoidance of depolarizing muscle relaxants in favor of alternative techniques (e.g., regional anesthesia) enhances perioperative safety.

Anesthesiologists must prioritize ongoing education on evolving guidelines, such as cautious use of non-depolarizing NMBAs, though further trials are needed to confirm their safety in this population. Management requires balancing evidence-based protocols with personalized care, facilitated by multidisciplinary collaboration among anesthesiologists, geneticists, and critical care teams.

In summary, a proactive, patient-centered approach—integrating vigilant screening, tailored anesthetic strategies, and interdisciplinary coordination—is vital to mitigate risks. Advances in pharmacologic and genetic research promise further refinements in care for these high-risk patients.

This study provides practical and evidence-based frameworks for anesthesiologists, surgeons, and critical care practitioners, helping them identify high-risk patients preoperatively, select optimal anesthetic regimens, and implement effective perioperative monitoring, thus reducing the incidence of anesthesia-related adverse events (e.g., prolonged paralysis, respiratory failure) and improving surgical safety. For patients with cholinesterase deficiency, the personalized management strategies and genetic counseling recommendations proposed in this study can not only ensure perioperative safety but also provide long-term guidance for their future medical care, avoiding repeated anesthetic risks. Additionally, the study’s summary of emerging therapeutic advances (e.g., CRISPR gene therapy) offers new hope for the fundamental treatment of this genetic disorder, further improving the quality of life of affected patients.
