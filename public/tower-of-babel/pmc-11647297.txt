The impact of tobacco smoking and smoking cessation on lung cancer and stroke incidence among people with HIV on antiretroviral therapy in South Africa: a simulation modeling study
Authors: Helen D’Couto, Acadia M Thielking, Ronel Sewpaul, Douglas E Levy, Nancy A Rigotti, Stavroula A Chrysanthopoulou, Mark J Siedner, Kenneth A Freedberg, Robin Wood, Emily P Hyle, Krishna P Reddy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWith declining HIV-related mortality, over 20% of people with HIV (PWH) in South Africa are now over age 50 years, and tobacco-related non-communicable disease burden is increasing. We quantified the impact of smoking and smoking cessation on lung cancer and stroke incidence among PWH in South Africa.MethodsUsing a microsimulation model, we simulated 18 cohorts of initially virologically suppressed PWH over their lifetime, categorised by sex, initial age (35 years/45 years/55 years) and smoking status (current/former/never). Smoking status remains constant throughout the simulation; individuals with former smoking status quit at model start. PWH can disengage from HIV care and experience virological rebound. We modelled the relative risk of lung cancer for females (males) with current versus never smoking status as 16.69 (15.83), and for females (males) with former versus never smoking status as 1.99–8.80 (1.90–6.18), depending on age at cessation. Corresponding modelled relative risks of stroke were 1.79 (1.54) for current versus never smoking, and 1.00–1.29 (1.00–1.12) for former versus never smoking. We varied HIV-related and smoking-related parameters in sensitivity analyses.ResultsModelled female (male) PWH who stop smoking at age 45 years experience 61.3% (70.9%) and 35.6% (18.6%) lower cumulative lung cancer and stroke incidence over 25 years compared with people who continue smoking. The proportion alive and lung cancer-free or alive and stroke-free over 25 years would increase by 10.4 (9.5) or 10.5 (8.5) percentage points. In sensitivity analysis, smoking and smoking cessation have a greater impact on lung cancer and stroke cumulative incidence if competing HIV-related mortality risks are lower or if PWH experience higher lung cancer and stroke risk compared with people without HIV apart from smoking.ConclusionSmoking cessation could substantially reduce lung cancer and stroke risk among PWH in South Africa. To reduce the rising non-communicable disease burden among PWH, smoking cessation should become part of routine care of PWH.Keywords: HIV, Tobacco, Global Health

Introduction

Among the >7.5 million people with HIV (PWH) in South Africa, over 20% are aged 50 years or older.1 2 As PWH reach older ages due to increasingly accessible antiretroviral therapy (ART), South Africa faces converging communicable and non-communicable disease (NCD) epidemics.3 PWH are at increased risk of NCD multimorbidity compared with people without HIV due to chronic inflammation, side effects of ART and higher prevalence of substance use.4 5

Tobacco smoking, which was prevalent in approximately 36% of male and 8% of female South Africans in 2016, is a well-established risk factor for many NCDs.6 Some studies report higher smoking prevalence among PWH compared with people without HIV in South Africa and other low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs).6 7 Lung cancer and stroke are two of the most common smoking-associated diseases for which published incidence data are available in South Africa.8 9 Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths in South Africa, while stroke is the top cause of disability and among the leading causes of death among adults.8 9 In analyses of lung cancer and stroke cases in South Africa, incidence occurred at younger ages among PWH compared with people without HIV, even with similar disease severity.10,12

As NCD burden rises among PWH in South Africa and other LMICs, mitigating risk factors such as tobacco smoking is essential.13,15 Although the WHO has emphasised the importance of integrating tobacco control within HIV care, South Africa’s strategic plan for the care of PWH does not highlight tobacco cessation.16,18 Understanding the impact of tobacco smoking and smoking cessation on local disease burden is critical to inform clinical and public health priorities. We used a validated microsimulation model to quantify the impact of smoking and smoking cessation on cumulative lung cancer and stroke risk among PWH on ART in South Africa.

Methods

Analytic overview

We used the Cost-Effectiveness of Preventing AIDS Complications-International (CEPAC-I) model, a validated, discrete-time microsimulation of HIV natural history and treatment, to project the cumulative incidence of lung cancer and stroke among people who currently smoke (CS), people who formerly smoked (FS) and people who have never smoked (NS) in South Africa.19,21 We also projected the proportions of people alive and lung cancer-free, or alive and stroke-free, at 25 years (25 years after model start), choosing this timeframe to align with available data about lung cancer and stroke incidence and the impact of smoking. We used data specific to ischaemic stroke, which accounts for the majority of incident stroke cases among PWH.22 Because virological suppression is prioritised in HIV care guidelines to reduce high near-term morbidity and mortality, we simulated PWH with virological suppression at model start, as done previously.23,25 We simulated 18 cohorts, representing each combination of two sexes (female/male), three ages at model start (35 years/45 years/55 years), and three smoking statuses (CS/FS/NS). Each cohort was homogenous by sex, smoking status and age. To estimate the potential impact of a smoking cessation intervention, we assumed that FS stop smoking at model start, and that smoking status remains unchanged throughout the simulation (ie, CS smoke until death, FS do not relapse and NS do not start smoking). We simulated one million people in each cohort and conducted sensitivity and scenario analyses around key model input parameters.

Model structure

The CEPAC-I model simulates individuals through stages of HIV disease progression and treatment on a monthly cycle until death. Simulated PWH enter the model virologically suppressed on an ART regimen of tenofovir/lamivudine/dolutegravir, as per South African guidelines.23 Each individual experiences monthly probabilities of continued ART adherence, virological non-suppression and disengagement from HIV care, derived from primarily South African data.26,29 CD4 counts, which increase when there is virological suppression, inform monthly probabilities of morbidity and mortality from opportunistic diseases and other HIV-related conditions. Simulated individuals also experience non-HIV-related mortality risks stratified by sex, age and smoking status (described next). Model specifications and other details are in previous publications and at https://mpec.massgeneral.org/cepac-model/.19 20 30

Input parameters

HIV-related characteristics

The mean sex-stratified CD4 cell count at model start for females is 789 cells/µL (SD 318 /µL) and for males is 600 cells/µL (SD 303/µL), based on the Vukuzazi cohort in South Africa.31 Mean adherence to ART is 95.3%, based on distributions from primarily South African data.28 29 The probability of disengagement from HIV care is 0.7%/month, assuming that these cohorts of PWH with initial virological suppression would have a relatively low monthly probability of disengagement as observed among PWH engaged in care for at least 12 months.32 Additional HIV-related input parameters are in table 1.

Smoking-stratified mortality

We previously described the derivation of age-specific and sex-specific non-HIV all-cause mortality rates stratified by smoking status.25 Briefly, we derived mortality rates from South Africa population data, sex-specific and age-specific smoking prevalence from the South Africa Demographic and Health Survey (SADHS), smoking-attributable fraction of deaths from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study and smoking-associated mortality risk ratios from published studies624 33,35 Similar to prior studies, we assumed no smoking-attributable mortality before age 40 years.25 33 36 Based on published data, we assumed the non-HIV-associated mortality risk among FS was the same as that among CS until 5 years after smoking cessation.33 37 38

Sensitivity and alternative scenario analysis

We varied several HIV-related and smoking-related input parameters and assumptions to assess the robustness of model projections (table 1). HIV-related factors were as follows: (1) no disengagement from HIV care; (2) higher probability of disengagement from HIV care (1.6%/month)32; (3) lower or higher CD4 cell counts at model start (100–800/μL); (4) a 5-year earlier shift of lung cancer and stroke monthly incidence to account for potential accelerated incidence among PWH; and (5) increased monthly risk of lung cancer and stroke at lower CD4 counts (where, compared with PWH with CD4 >500/μL, the RR of lung cancer is approximately 2 for PWH with CD4 <500/μL, while for stroke the RR is 3 for PWH with CD4 350–500/μL and 7 for PWH with CD4 <350/μL).10,1222 45 Smoking-related parameters varied were as follows: (1) CS versus NS lung cancer and stroke incidence RRs; and (2) FS versus NS lung cancer and stroke incidence RRs. We also conducted alternative scenario analyses focusing on different starting populations: (1) ART-naïve PWH, where FS quit 2 years after model start (online supplemental methods); and (2) people without HIV. Finally, to further evaluate competing risks from HIV and smoking, we completed a two-way sensitivity analysis of disengagement from HIV care and CS versus NS lung cancer and stroke RRs.

Model validation

Validation of model-projected life expectancy was detailed previously.25 To validate lung cancer incidence, we calculated a weighted average of the lifetime cumulative lung cancer incidence for females and males with and without HIV, based on sex-stratified HIV prevalence and smoking prevalence in South Africa.2 6 Assuming no lung cancer incidence before age 35 years, we compared lifetime cumulative incidence among PWH age 35 years at model start with lifetime cumulative lung cancer estimates from the South African National Cancer Registry.8 53 We similarly compared the weighted average projected lifetime cumulative stroke incidence to estimates from the GBD study.54 Results of model validation comparisons are in the online supplemental results and online supplemental table 1.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct or reporting of our simulation model-based research. Plans were made to disseminate the findings to public health officials in South Africa.

Results

Base case

Among female PWH aged 45 years at model start, 25-year cumulative lung cancer incidence would be 2.9%, 1.1% and 0.2% for CS, FS and NS (table 2 and online supplemental figure 1A); the corresponding estimates for males are 3.2%, 0.9% and 0.2% (table 2 and online supplemental figure 1B). For stroke, the 25-year cumulative incidence for CS, FS and NS would be 6.2%, 4.0% and 4.0% for females, and 4.3%, 3.5% and 3.2% for males (table 2 and online supplemental figure 1C,D). Female (male) FS experience a 61.3% (70.9%) relative reduction (1.8 (2.2) percentage point reduction) in cumulative lung cancer incidence and a 35.6% (18.6%) relative reduction (2.2 (0.8) percentage point reduction) in 25-year cumulative stroke incidence after stopping smoking at age 45 years and remaining abstinent. Compared with continued smoking, smoking cessation at age 45 years results in more people alive and lung cancer-free or stroke-free 25 years later. Among females (males), the percentage point increases are 10.4 (9.5) for being alive and lung cancer-free, and 10.5 (8.5) for being alive and stroke-free (figure 1 and table 2).

Sensitivity and alternative scenario analysis

HIV-related and smoking-related input parameters were varied in sensitivity and alternative scenario analysis for females and males entering the model at age 45 years. Lower competing mortality risks from HIV (eg, higher engagement with HIV care and higher CD4 counts) result in higher lung cancer and stroke cumulative incidence over 25 years and greater reductions in cumulative incidence from smoking cessation (table 3, figure 2). Higher lung cancer and stroke cumulative incidence over 25 years among PWH occurring with less disengagement or higher initial CD4 count is driven by longer survival (ie, people surviving long enough to face the higher monthly and annual risks of lung cancer and stroke at older ages). When assuming no disengagement from HIV care, smoking cessation produces 11.7 and 10.9 percentage point increases for females and males in the proportion of people alive and lung cancer-free at 25 years (compared with 10.4 and 9.5 in the base case); the corresponding percentage point increases for females (males) alive and stroke-free at 25 years are 11.8 (9.7) (compared with 10.5 (8.5) in the base case) (online supplemental table 2 and online supplemental figure 2). Among females with initial CD4 cell count 800/µL compared with those with initial CD4 cell count 100/µL, the percentage point increases in people alive and lung cancer-free at 25 years are 17.7, 21.8 and 23.6 for CS, FS and NS (online supplemental table 2 and online supplemental figure 2).

If PWH face higher monthly incidence of lung cancer and stroke compared with people without HIV, then the benefits of smoking cessation would be even greater than shown in the base case. When shifting incidence rates 5 years earlier among PWH (to account for potential accelerated development of lung cancer or stroke among PWH compared with people without HIV), the percentage point reduction in 25-year cumulative lung cancer incidence from smoking cessation increases to 2.5 among females and 2.9 among males, compared with 1.8 and 2.2 in the base case (figure 2). Corresponding percentage point reductions in 25-year cumulative stroke incidence are 3.5 among females and 1.3 among males compared with base case values of 2.2 and 0.8, respectively (figure 2). When modelling higher monthly lung cancer and stroke incidence for PWH with low CD4 compared with PWH with high CD4, similar trends are observed, although with a smaller magnitude because the modelled cohort (with initial virological suppression on ART) has high CD4 counts; only people who disengage from HIV care and/or experience virological rebound are at risk for lower CD4 count (figure 2).

As smoking-related RRs of lung cancer and stroke are varied, the 25-year cumulative incidence of lung cancer or stroke changes as anticipated. Among CS, varying lung cancer RRs for CS versus NS to lower and higher estimates produces 25-year cumulative lung cancer incidence of 2.5%–3.3% for females and 2.7%–3.7% for males (table 3). When varying lung cancer RRs for FS versus NS to lower and higher estimates, 25-year cumulative lung cancer incidence is 0.9%–1.3% for females and 0.8%–1.1% for males (table 3). Similar trends are observed when varying smoking-associated RRs for stroke (table 3).

In alternative scenario analyses, modelling ART-naïve PWH results in lower 25-year cumulative incidence of lung cancer and stroke and a lower impact of smoking cessation, due to higher competing mortality risks from HIV. Modelling people without HIV yields the opposite (table 3 and figure 2).

In the two-way sensitivity analysis, as smoking-associated lung cancer and stroke RRs increase and disengagement from HIV care decreases, the 25-year cumulative incidence of lung cancer and stroke increase (online supplemental figure 3). With higher smoking-associated RRs of lung cancer and stroke (upper limit of 95% CIs) and no disengagement from HIV care, smoking cessation would increase lung cancer-free and stroke-free survival at 25 years by 11.5 (11.0) and 11.9 (10.0) percentage points for females (males), respectively (compared with base case estimates of 10.4 (9.5) and 10.5 (8.5)) (online supplemental figure 4).

Discussion

Using a simulation model, we projected that 45-year-old PWH in South Africa who have virological suppression and smoke would face a 91%–94% and 12%–42% higher 25-year cumulative risk of lung cancer and stroke compared with PWH who never smoke, despite lower smoking intensity in South Africa compared with high-income countries. We projected that stopping smoking at age 45 years would substantially reduce the 25-year cumulative risk of developing lung cancer by 61%–71% and stroke by 19%–36%. Among PWH aged 55 years, stopping smoking would reduce 25-year cumulative lung cancer incidence by 31%–41% and stroke incidence by 13%–14%.

Similar to a prior study of the burden of NCDs among PWH, we found that populations with the lowest competing mortality risks from HIV would experience the highest lung cancer and stroke burden over 25 years.55 These populations (eg, PWH with higher CD4 counts and higher engagement in care) would also gain the most from smoking cessation in terms of reductions in cumulative lung cancer and stroke incidence. PWH who disengage from care and stop taking ART have greater risks in mortality from advanced HIV disease, including opportunistic diseases. As such, these PWH have a lower probability of surviving to ages at which the monthly and annual risks of lung cancer and stroke are the highest. Aligning with published data, we found a higher modelled relative reduction in stroke incidence from smoking cessation at age 45 years among females than among males, possibly due to differences in competing mortality risks and in the prevalence of other stroke risk factors.37

We conservatively assumed in the base case the same monthly risk of lung cancer or stroke among PWH and people without HIV, given that our simulated population consisted of PWH with virological suppression. However, PWH might face higher risks of lung cancer and stroke than people without HIV due to chronic inflammation and immune suppression.11 45 56 57 South Africa-based and US-based studies report that PWH are diagnosed with lung cancer at younger ages compared with people without HIV.1045,49 In South Africa, another study found that PWH are more likely to present with incurable lung cancer.46 Similarly, prior South African studies found that strokes occur at earlier ages among PWH and are associated with worse outcomes and longer hospital stays.11 12 22 However, these prior studies included people who had not been on ART or had disengaged from HIV care. It is unclear if this higher risk of lung cancer and stroke would be observed among virologically suppressed PWH and long-time ART users, who were the focus of our model analysis. Studies among PWH suggest that lung cancer and stroke risks decrease as CD4 count increases.1112 22 50,52 58 59 As shown in sensitivity analysis, if virologically suppressed PWH face accelerated risks of incident lung cancer and stroke (compared with people without HIV) or if incidence is higher at lower CD4 cell counts, smoking cessation could avert even more lung cancer and stroke cases among PWH than among people without HIV, depending on competing mortality risks.

Our model-projected results suggest that incorporating smoking cessation within HIV care could substantially reduce the cumulative risk of lung cancer and stroke. Interventions that could prevent lung cancer and stroke cases among PWH in South Africa are particularly important considering high morbidity and mortality from these conditions. Due to several factors including a lack of accessible screening tools, delays in seeking diagnosis, and limited technical capacity for diagnosis, over 80% of lung cancer cases in South Africa are diagnosed at stage 3 or 4.60 In the public sector, where the majority of PWH are treated, there is minimal access to lung cancer treatment due to limited medical personnel and prohibitive costs.61 Even among people in South Africa with private health insurance, 5-year survival after lung cancer diagnosis is only approximately 14%.62 For stroke, due to a lack of awareness about signs and symptoms, poor infrastructure and medical personnel shortages, over 80% of patients arrive at medical centres outside of the window for thrombolysis.63 64

Our findings support the WHO’s call to integrate smoking cessation interventions within HIV care in settings such as South Africa.16 The South African National Cancer Strategic Framework’s cancer prevention guidelines also emphasise addressing risk factors, such as smoking, as means to reduce cancer risk.65 PWH in South Africa have relatively frequent opportunities for interaction with the healthcare system, offering chances to deliver smoking cessation interventions within existing infrastructure. In past South African studies, most PWH expressed willingness to stop smoking but lacked cessation guidance, highlighting a critical need for accessible smoking cessation resources.66 Although there are few studies of smoking cessation interventions among PWH in South Africa, studies from other settings have identified efficacious interventions, and trials in South Africa are underway.67,70 Recent studies from similar settings are encouraging: a single-arm pilot trial of a behavioural smoking intervention in Botswana demonstrated the high efficacy and feasibility of integrating cessation resources within HIV care.67 Additional studies have shown the effective integration of NCD care within HIV care.71 72

Our analysis has limitations. As we only focus on two out of many NCDs that are associated with smoking, the reduction in NCD burden from smoking cessation for PWH will inevitably exceed what was quantified in this study. We focused on lung cancer and stroke given available data, whereas data about incidence of other smoking-associated diseases like myocardial infarction and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease are limited in part by barriers in diagnosis. While we accounted for all-cause non-HIV-related mortality by smoking status, we did not account for mortality specifically from lung cancer or stroke given the challenges and limitations of deriving smoking-stratified mortality risks from all causes other than lung cancer or stroke. We focused instead on cumulative incidence of lung cancer and stroke. Our model projects higher lung cancer cumulative incidence compared with National Cancer Registry estimates, though prior studies confirm that lung cancer estimates are prone to under-reporting.8 53 73 74 As most people in South Africa are diagnosed with late-stage lung cancer, it is likely that many cases go entirely undiagnosed.73 In derivations of lung cancer and stroke incidence stratified by smoking status, we applied the RRs of lung cancer and stroke by smoking status from the USA, adjusted to match the average smoking intensity in South Africa.33 40 41 43 While tobacco smoking intensity is a major driver of disease incidence, it is possible that differences in the types of tobacco smoked or unmeasured confounding could produce different smoking-associated lung cancer and stroke risks in the USA and South Africa.33 75 76 For instance, higher prevalence of untreated hypertension in South Africa could produce excess stroke risk that may not be mitigated to the same extent by smoking cessation as in the USA.58 77 78 Our sensitivity analysis results demonstrate the impact of modelling lower or higher smoking-associated risks of stroke. Additionally, the stroke incidence data we used are specific to ischaemic stroke, while the RRs of smoking on stroke incidence include other types of stroke.33 34 We do not anticipate that this would affect relative increases or decreases as the impact of smoking on haemorrhagic and ischaemic stroke are similar.79 80 We evaluated parameter uncertainty but not model structural uncertainty, although the CEPAC model of HIV has been previously validated, and we found good validity of model projections in this analysis with empirical data.19 20 30 Finally, we did not account for the risk of smoking relapse, focusing instead on the benefits of sustained abstinence. The prevention of relapse is critical for maximising the benefits of smoking cessation.

In conclusion, our findings highlight that smoking contributes substantially to lung cancer and stroke risk among PWH in South Africa, and that smoking cessation would greatly reduce this risk. These results support prioritising smoking cessation as part of the comprehensive care of PWH.
