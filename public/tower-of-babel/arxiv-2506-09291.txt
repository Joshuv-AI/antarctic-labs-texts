Competition Complexity in Multi-Item Auctions: Beyond VCG and Regularity
Hedyeh Beyhaghi, Linda Cai, Yiding Feng, Yingkai Li, S. Matthew Weinberg

Abstract
We quantify the value of the monopoly’s bargaining power in terms of competition complexity—that is, the number of additional bidders the monopoly must attract in simple auctions to match the expected revenue of the optimal mechanisms [ Bulow and Klemperer 1996 , Eden et al. 2017 , c.f.,] —within the setting of multi-item auctions. We show that for simple auctions that sell items separately, the competition complexity is Θ ⁡ ( n α ) \Theta(\frac{n}{\alpha}) in an environment with n n original bidders under the slightly stronger assumption of α \alpha -strong regularity, in contrast to the standard regularity assumption in the literature, which requires Ω ⁡ ( n ⋅ ln ⁡ m n ) \Omega(n\cdot\ln\frac{m}{n}) additional bidders [ Feldman et al. 2018 ] . This significantly reduces the value of learning the distribution to design the optimal mechanisms, especially in large markets with many items for sale. For simple auctions that sell items as a grand bundle, we establish a constant competition complexity bound in a single-bidder environment when the number of items is small or when the value distribution has a monotone hazard rate. Some of our competition complexity results also hold when we compete against the first best benchmark (i.e., optimal social welfare).

Introduction

To maximize profit, a monopoly seller can either run a simple auction among potential buyers or restrict buyer access to gain more control over bargaining and negotiation. In their seminal work,
Bulow and Klemperer 1996
show that for single-item auctions under mild regularity assumptions, the latter approach offers no advantage: the seller can achieve at least the same revenue as the optimal mechanism by simply attracting one additional buyer and running a second-price auction.

1

The second-price auction awards the item to the highest bidder and charges them a price equal to the second-highest bid.
This result also suggests that, given limited resources, a firm may benefit more from expanding its market while using simple auctions rather than fine-tuning the selling mechanism based on detailed factors of the environment.

In many practical applications, the seller typically has multiple items to sell, and buyers often have combinatorial values for these items. In such settings, the Bayesian optimal mechanism becomes significantly more complex, even when selling just two items to a single bidder. Specifically, there are known examples where the optimal mechanism may sell each item separately, sell both items as a grand or nested bundle, offer a “discount price” for the bundle, sell lotteries, and other possibly counterintuitive structure
[

.
Given the complexity of designing the optimal mechanism, generalizing the result of
Bulow and Klemperer 1996
to combinatorial auctions becomes even more appealing. This is because the cost of attracting additional buyers is likely to be similar in both settings, while optimizing the mechanisms in combinatorial auctions is considerably more challenging.

Building on this motivation,
Eden et al. 2017
introduce and initiate the study of
competition complexity
in multi-item settings. Specifically, the competition complexity of a simple auction is defined as the number of additional bidders required to ensure that its expected revenue exceeds the Bayesian optimal revenue from the original number of bidders. Under this definition, in the single-item setting, the competition complexity of the second-price auction is 1 for regular bidders
[
Bulow and Klemperer 1996
]
.
In multi-item settings, one classic simple mechanism that does not require detailed knowledge of the environment is the Vickrey-Clarke-Groves (VCG) auction.

Since the theoretically tight bound for the competition complexity of the VCG auction is derived for the family of regular distributions, it is natural to ask the following question:

(Question 1)
Can we obtain improved competition complexity bounds of the VCG auction under stronger distributional assumptions than regularity?

(Question 2)
Can we obtain improved competition complexity bounds by considering other simple mechanisms beyond the VCG auction?

In this paper, we answer Question 1 in the affirmative. Additionally, we make conceptually novel and technically non-trivial progress in partially answering Question 2. In the following subsection, we provide a summary of our results and proof techniques.

1.1
Our Contributions and Techniques

In this subsection we explain the contributions and techniques of this paper.

Our paper focuses on revenue maximization in a classic multi-item setting where bidders are ex ante symmetric and have additive valuation functions. Specifically, each item

Improved competition complexity of the VCG auction.
In the first part of this paper, we focus on the VCG auction and study its competition complexity within the class of
\alpha
-strongly regular distributions. The concept of strong regularity was first introduced by
Cole and Roughgarden 2014
and has since been explored in various contexts within the mechanism design literature
[
Cole and Rao 2017
,
Schweizer and Szech 2019
,
Giannakopoulos et al. 2021
,
Allouah et al. 2022
,
Allouah et al. 2023
,
Giannakopoulos et al. 2023
, e.g.,]
. Specifically,
\alpha
-strongly regular distributions serve as an interpolation between MHR distributions and regular distributions, with
\alpha=1
and
\alpha=0
representing the two extreme cases, respectively.

Recall that both the exponential distribution and the equal-revenue distribution, as discussed in
Examples

1.1
and
1.2
, are paradigmatic (and often provable “worst-case” instances) within the classes of MHR and regular distributions, respectively. Inspired by the performance of the VCG auction in these two examples, along with the established tight bound of competition complexity,
\Theta(n\log(\frac{m}{n}))
, for regular distributions
[
Beyhaghi and Weinberg 2019
]
, it becomes natural and intriguing to investigate the competition complexity of the VCG auction for MHR distributions and, more broadly, for
\alpha
-strongly regular distributions for every
\alpha\in(0,1]
.

As the first main result of this paper,
Theorem

1.1
characterizes the
tight
competition complexity of the VCG auction for
\alpha
-strongly regular distributions given every
\alpha\in(0,1]

3.2

Theorem 1.1

(Competition complexity of VCG for
\alpha
-strongly regular items)
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1]
,

m\geq 1
asymmetric

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}\in\left(\max\left\{\frac{1}{\alpha}-1,1\right\}\cdot n,\frac{11}{\alpha}\cdot n\right].

Furthermore, having

The formal definition of

Since the Bayesian optimal mechanism and its expected revenue are typically complex to characterize, it is common in the literature to compare a given mechanism against a benchmark that provides an upper bound on the Bayesian optimal revenue. Following this approach, we analyze the competition complexity of the VCG auction relative to the welfare benchmark, also known as the first-best benchmark.
Surprisingly, while the competition complexity upper bound is derived with respect to the welfare benchmark, it turns out to be tight even when evaluated against the original Bayesian optimal revenue benchmark. Specifically, we can construct a problem instance where recruiting an additional
\mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}-1
bidders and running the VCG auction is insufficient to suppress the revenue of the Bayesian optimal mechanism achieved with the original
n
bidders.

At a high level, our upper-bound analysis (
Lemma

3.3
) demonstrates that the worst-case instance (with respect to the welfare benchmark) corresponds to the generalized Pareto distribution. Given that bidders have additive valuation functions, the VCG auction essentially reduces to running a second-price auction independently for each item. For any fixed item, its contribution to the welfare benchmark is the expected value of the first-order statistic,

k

(rather than the

k

while its contribution to the VCG auction is the expected value of the second-order statistic.
To establish that the generalized Pareto distribution is the worst case, we employ a multi-step argument. First, we identify a “three-interval structure” (

3.4
) that facilitates a comparison between the first and second order statistics under varying sample sizes. Next, we leverage the tail bounds implied by
\alpha

3.5

Competition complexity of the bundle-based second-price auction.
In the second part of this paper, we study on the bundle-based second-price auction and study its competition complexity for answering Question 2 imposed earlier in our introduction. (See more motivations behind studying the bundle-based second-price auction in

We focus on the additional bidders required in the bundle-based second-price auction to surpass the Bayesian optimal revenue for a single bidder, which is already a challenging task.
Our results for the bundle-based second-price auction are threefold.

8

In

n\geq 2

\Omega(\exp(m))

First, we continue our exploration about the competition complexity when the bidder has MHR valuation distributions for all items. (The formal theorem statement can be found in
Theorem

3.9
.)

Theorem 1.2

(Competition complexity of BSPA for MHR items)
.

For
n=1
bidder and any
m\geq 1
asymmetric MHR items, the competition complexity of the bundle-based second-price auction against the welfare benchmark is at most
3

{\rm BSPA}_{4}\geq{\rm WEL}_{1}

1.2

This provides strong confidence that the bundle-based second-price auction is a natural and promising mechanism to study in the multi-item setting when the bidder has an additive valuation function. 1.1

[
Barlow et al. 1963

Finally, we ask the following question motivated by the original result in

10

10

Recall

While most subsequent works study competition complexity—quantifying the number of additional bidders required for prior-independent mechanisms to beat (or nearly match) the Bayesian optimal mechanism—there are also prior studies
[

Theorem 1.4

(Constant approximation of BSPA for regular items)
.

For
m\geq 1

48\cdot{\rm BSPA}_{2}\geq{\rm OPT}_{1}

We remark that a similar statement fails for the VCG auction.
Specifically, it is known that for every
m\geq 1

O(1/\log(m))

[
Feldman et al. 2018

We establish this 48-approximation for regular items (
Theorem

1.4

[
Babaioff et al. 2020
]
. En route, our analysis framework also shows that for a regular bidder, selling all items as a grand bundle (BRev) is a 4-approximation to selling all items separately (SRev). Combining with the prior result that the better of selling grand bundle or selling items separately approximates the Bayesian optimal revenue
[

Cai et al. 2021

As an interesting comparison, it is known that without the regularity assumption, selling all items as a grand bundle does not guarantee a constant approximation against the Bayesian optimal revenue. In this sense, our result shows that regularity is a sufficient condition for achieving a constant approximation when selling all items as a grand bundle. (The formal theorem statements can be found in

Related Work

Our work connects to several strands of existing literature.

Competition complexity.
The most relevant to our work are results on competition complexity in auctions.
Bulow and Klemperer 1996
initiated the work in competition complexity for a single item.

2
Preliminaries

We consider the setting where there are
m
items and
n
i.i.d. bidders. Bidders have additive valuation functions: given value profile
\{{v}_{j}\}_{j\in[m]}
over
m
items, a bidder’s valuation for any subset
S
of items is
\sum_{j\in S}{v}_{j}
.
The value profile of each bidder is drawn from product distribution
F=\Pi_{j\in[m]}F_{j}
, where
F_{j}
is the marginal value distribution for item
j
.
11
11

11

Unless stated otherwise, we assume that each bidder’s value for each item is realized independently.

By slightly abusing the notations, we use
F_{j}(\cdot)
to denote the cumulative density function (CDF).
We assume that each distribution
F_{j}
is continuous and atomless, so
F_{j}
is a strictly increasing function. Let
f_{j}(\cdot)
denote the corresponding probability density function (PDF).
For any quantile
q\in[0,1]
, we define
F_{j}^{-1}(q)
as the value in distribution
F_{j}
corresponding to quantile
q
.

We introduce the following notations for the welfare and revenue benchmarks in this paper, each in the context of
n
bidders with values drawn i.i.d. from distribution
F
.

•

{\rm OPT}_{n}(F)
: the expected revenue of the Bayesian revenue-optimal mechanism.

•

{\rm BSPA}_{n}(F)
: the expected revenue generated by bundling all items together, and running the second-price auction.

•

{\rm BRev}_{n}(F)
: the expected revenue generated by bundling all items together, and running Myerson’s revenue-optimal single-item auction
[
Myerson 1981
]
.

•

{\rm VCG}_{n}(F)
: the expected revenue of the VCG auction that allocates the items efficiently, which, in the context of additive bidders, is equivalent to selling each item separately through the second-price auction.

•

{\rm SRev}_{n}(F)
: the expected revenue generated by running Myerson’s revenue-optimal single-item auction for each item separately.

•

{\rm WEL}_{n}(F)
: the optimal expected welfare, which can be achieved by the VCG auction.

•

{\rm{CDW}}_{n}(F)
: the expected value of the duality benchmark. See
Section

2.2
for the formal definition.

When it is clear from the context, we omit
F
from above notations.
All five mechanisms and two benchmarks form the hierarchy

\displaystyle{\rm BSPA}\leq{\rm BRev}\leq{\rm OPT}\leq{\rm{CDW}}\leq{\rm WEL}

\displaystyle{\rm VCG}\leq{\rm SRev}\leq{\rm OPT}\leq{\rm{CDW}}\leq{\rm WEL}

with incomparable pairs “VCG vs. BSPA” and “SRev” vs. “BRev”.

2.1
Distributional Assumptions

Our paper considers distributions under the following assumptions, listed in increasing order of restrictiveness: regular,
\alpha
-strongly regular, and monotone hazard rate (MHR).
12
12

12

Here we introduce the distributional assumptions for the univariate distribution
F
. For (joint) product distributions, we say it is regular/MHR/
\alpha
-strongly regular if all its marginal distributions satisfy the corresponding distributional assumption.
Regular and MHR distributions are standard assumptions extensively used in the auction design literature. The
\alpha
-strongly regular distribution, introduced by
Cole and Roughgarden 2014
, serves as a natural interpolation between the regular and MHR assumptions.

Given any (univariate) distribution
F
, let

\displaystyle h({v})\triangleq\frac{f({v})}{1-F({v})}

be the hazard rate for distribution
F
, and let

\displaystyle\varphi({v})\triangleq{v}-\frac{1}{h({v})}

be the corresponding virtual value function.

Definition 2.1

(Regular distribution)
.

A distribution
F
is
regular
if its virtual value function
\varphi({v})
is non-decreasing in
{v}
.

Definition 2.2

(
\alpha
-strongly regular distribution)
.

Given any parameter
\alpha\in[0,1]
, a distribution
F
is
\alpha
-
strongly regular
if its virtual value function
\varphi({v})

is
\alpha
-strongly monotone, i.e.,
\varphi({v})-\varphi({v}^{\dagger})\geq\alpha({v}-{v}^{\dagger})
for all
{v},{v}^{\dagger}
in the support of
F
.
13
13

13

When the virtual value function
\varphi(\cdot)
is continuous, an equivalent statement is that derivative of the virtual value function
\varphi^{\prime}({v})\geq\alpha
.

Definition 2.3

(Monotone hazard rate (MHR) distribution)
.

A distribution
F
is
MHR
if its hazard rate
h({v})
is non-decreasing in
{v}
.

Note that by definition, a distribution
F
is regular if and only if it is
0
-strongly regular, and is MHR if and only if it is
1
-strongly regular.

In this section, we formally define competition complexity and the duality benchmark CDW, which will be useful for bounding competition complexity in combinatorial auctions.

Definition 2.4

(Competition complexity)

In an auction setting with

\displaystyle{\rm AUC}_{n+c}(F)\geq{\rm OPT}_{n}(F)

and

{\rm AUC}
with
n+c
bidders and the Bayesian revenue-optimal mechanism with
n
bidders, respectively.

In the special case of single-item auctions,
Bulow and Klemperer 1996
show that the competition complexity of the VCG auction for regular buyers is
1
.

Theorem 2.1

(
Bulow and Klemperer 1996
)
.

For
n\geq 1

m=1

{\rm VCG}_{n+1}\geq{\rm OPT}_{n}
.

3
Competition Complexity for Welfare

In this section, we first characterize the competition complexity of the VCG auction relative to the welfare benchmark and then show that this bound remains tight even when compared to the optimal revenue benchmark.
We further establish that the competition complexity of selling items as a grand bundle can be unbounded when there are at least two bidders.
For upper bounds on competition complexity, we require only that the distributions are i.i.d. across bidders, allowing for arbitrary heterogeneity and correlation in values across items. In contrast, our lower-bound constructions restrict attention to distributions that are independent across items. Our results rely on a novel bound on the order statistics for
\alpha
-strongly regular distributions.

Definition 3.1

(Competition complexity constant of VCG for
\alpha
-strongly regular items)
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define constant
\mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}
as the minimum integer such that

\displaystyle F_{2:n+\mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}}=\alpha F_{1:n+\mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}}-(1-\alpha)\geq F_{1:n}

where
F
is the generalized Pareto distribution with CDF
F(v)=1-(1+{v})^{-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}
, and
F_{k:n}
is its expected
k
-th highest value given
n
samples.

For
\alpha=1
, let
\mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}
be the minimum integer such that

\displaystyle F_{2:n+\mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}}=F_{1:n+\mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}}-1\geq F_{1:n}

where
F
is the exponential distribution with CDF
F({v})=1-e^{-{v}}
.

Remark:
The equality for deriving
F_{2:n+\mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}}
in the above definition can be directly computed based on the functional form of the distribution.
The constant
\mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}
for
\alpha=1
can be easily computed due to the special structure of the exponential distribution. Specifically, through a simple algebraic calculation,
for the exponential distribution
F
, we have

\displaystyle F_{2:n+\mathcal{C}_{n,1}}=H_{n+\mathcal{C}_{n,1}}\quad\text{and}\quad F_{1:n}=H_{n}

where
H_{n}=\sum\nolimits_{i\in[n]}\frac{1}{i}
is the harmonic number.
Therefore,
\mathcal{C}_{n,1}
is the minimum integer such that

H_{n+\mathcal{C}_{n,1}}-H_{n}\geq 1
.
Note that
\mathcal{C}_{n,1}\to(e-1)\cdot n
when
n\to\infty
.

When
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the dependence of
\mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}
on
n
is less transparent in the definition. We provide an asymptotic bound on
\mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1]
and any
n\geq 1
, the competition complexity constant
\mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}\in\left(\max\left\{\frac{1}{\alpha}-1,1\right\}\cdot n,\frac{11}{\alpha}\cdot n\right]

Proof.

First, observe that for any
\epsilon\in[0,1]
and any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle 1-\epsilon\geq\alpha(1-\epsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}).

(1)

The inequality holds since
1-\epsilon-\alpha(1-\epsilon^{\frac{1}{\alpha}})
is monotonically decreasing in
\epsilon
, and the value of this difference is
0
when
\epsilon=1
.
Therefore, letting
N=\frac{n}{\alpha}
, we have

\displaystyle\alpha F_{1:N}-(1-\alpha)<\alpha F_{1:N}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\alpha\cdot\left(\,1-(1-(1+{v})^{-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}})^{N}\,\right){\,\mathrm{d}}v

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}1-(1-(1+{v})^{-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}})^{n}{\,\mathrm{d}}v=F_{1:n}.

The second inequality holds by applying
Equation

1
with
\epsilon=(1-(1+{v})^{-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}})^{n}
.
This implies that
\mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}>(\frac{1}{\alpha}-1)\cdot n
.

Moreover, note that for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the exponential distribution is also a special case of
\alpha
-strongly regular distribution.
Note that
\mathcal{C}_{n,1}
is the minimum integer such that

H_{n+\mathcal{C}_{n,1}}-H_{n}\geq 1
,
where
H_{n}=\sum\nolimits_{i\in[n]}\frac{1}{i}
is the harmonic number.
This implies that for the exponential distribution,
\mathcal{C}_{n,1}\geq n
for any
n
,
which further provides a lower bound on
\mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}
for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
n\geq 1
.

Next, we will provide the upper bound for
\mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}
.
Note that letting
\tau=n^{1-\alpha}-1
,
we have
(1+\tau)^{-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}=\frac{1}{n}

and

\displaystyle\begin{split}F_{1:n}&=\int_{0}^{\infty}1-\left(1-(1+{v})^{-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\right)^{n}{\,\mathrm{d}}v\\
&\leq\tau+\int_{\tau}^{\infty}n\cdot(1+{v})^{-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}{\,\mathrm{d}}v=n^{1-\alpha}\cdot\left(1+\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\right)-1.\end{split}

(2)

The inequality holds since
(1-\epsilon)^{n}\geq\max\{1-n\epsilon,0\}
for any
\epsilon\in[0,1]
.
In addition, letting
\hat{\tau}=(2n)^{1-\alpha}-1
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}F_{1:n}&\geq\int_{\hat{\tau}}^{\infty}1-\left(1-(1+{v})^{-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\right)^{n}{\,\mathrm{d}}v\\
&\geq\int_{\hat{\tau}}^{\infty}\frac{n}{2}\cdot(1+{v})^{-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}{\,\mathrm{d}}v=(2n)^{1-\alpha}\cdot\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}.\end{split}

(3)

where the second inequality holds since
(1-\epsilon)^{n}\leq 1-\frac{n\epsilon}{2}
for any
\epsilon\in[0,\frac{1}{2n}]
.
Thus for
N\geq n\cdot(\frac{2^{\alpha}}{\alpha(1-\alpha)})^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}
,
by combining
Equations

2
and
3
, we have

\displaystyle\alpha F_{1:N}-(1-\alpha)\geq\alpha(2N)^{1-\alpha}\cdot\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}-1\geq n^{1-\alpha}\cdot\frac{1}{\alpha}-1\geq F_{1:n}.

Note that for
\alpha\leq\frac{1}{5}
, we have
(\frac{2^{\alpha}}{\alpha(1-\alpha)})^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\leq\frac{2.351}{\alpha}
.
Therefore, we have
n+\mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}\leq\frac{2.351n}{\alpha}
for
\alpha\leq\frac{1}{5}
.
For
\alpha\geq\frac{1}{5}
, since
\alpha
-strong regularity is a special case of
\frac{1}{5}
-regularity, we have
\mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}\leq 11n\leq\frac{11n}{\alpha}
.
∎

Theorem 3.2

(Competition complexity of VCG against WEL for
\alpha
-strongly regular items)
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1]
,
n\geq 1
bidders and
m\geq 1
(possibly correlated) asymmetric
\alpha
-strongly regular items, the tight competition complexity of the VCG auction against the welfare benchmark is
\mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}
, i.e.,
{\rm VCG}_{n+\mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}}\geq{\rm WEL}_{n}
.
Moreover, there exists an instance such that
{\rm VCG}_{n+\mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}-1}<{\rm WEL}_{n}
.

Note that our theorem applies to distributions with arbitrary correlations instead of just product distributions.
We prove our theorem by identifying the exact worst case
\alpha
-strongly regular distributions given any parameter
\alpha\in(0,1)
are generalized Pareto distributions.
The procedure for showing that the worst-case distribution for
\alpha=1
is the exponential distribution follows the same steps as before and is therefore omitted in this paper.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
N,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
N>n\geq 1
,
and
\alpha
-strongly regular distribution
F
,
let
\hat{F}
be the generalized Pareto distribution such that

1-\hat{F}({v})=(1+{v})^{-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}
.
If
\hat{F}_{2:N}\geq\hat{F}_{1:n}
,
then
F_{2:N}\geq F_{1:n}
.

The proof of
Lemma

3.3
relies on the following two technical lemmas.

Lemma 3.4

(Three-interval structure)
.

For any distribution
F
, and
N,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
N>n\geq 1
,
there exists

0\leq q^{\dagger}\leq q^{\ddagger}\leq 1

such that

\displaystyle\xi_{2:N}(q)\leq\xi_{1:n}(q)

\displaystyle q\in[0,q^{\dagger}]\cup[q^{\ddagger},1]

\displaystyle\xi_{2:N}(q)\geq\xi_{1:n}(q)

\displaystyle q\in[q^{\dagger},q^{\ddagger}]

where
\xi_{k:n}(q)
is defined as the probability density that
the value with quantile
q
(i.e.,
F^{-1}(q)
)
is the
k
-th highest order statistic among
n
i.i.d. samples from distribution
F
.

Proof.

By definition,

\displaystyle\xi_{1:n}(q)=nq^{n-1}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\xi_{2:N}(q)=N(N-1)(1-q)q^{N-2}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\xi_{1:n}(q)-\xi_{2:N}(q)=q^{n-1}\left(n-N(N-1)(1-q)q^{N-n-1}\right).

Since
(1-q)q^{N-n-1}

is a single-peaked function
(i.e., first increasing and then decreasing),
we finish the proof as desired.
∎

Lemma 3.5

(
Allouah et al. 2022
)
.

For any
\alpha\in[0,1]
,
{v}_{1},{v}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
{v}_{1}\geq{v}_{2}
,

q^{\dagger},q^{\ddagger}\in[0,1]
with

q^{\dagger}>q^{\ddagger}
, and

\alpha
-strongly regular distribution
F
with CDF satisfying that

F({v}_{1})=q^{\dagger},F({v}_{2})=q^{\ddagger}
, we have

\displaystyle 1-F({v})

\displaystyle\geq q^{\ddagger}\cdot\Gamma_{\alpha}\left(\Gamma_{\alpha}^{-1}\left(\frac{q^{\dagger}}{q^{\ddagger}}\right)\cdot\frac{{v}-{v}_{2}}{{v}_{1}-{v}_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle{v}\in[{v}_{2},{v}_{1}]

\displaystyle 1-F({v})

\displaystyle\leq q^{\ddagger}\cdot\Gamma_{\alpha}\left(\Gamma_{\alpha}^{-1}\left(\frac{q^{\dagger}}{q^{\ddagger}}\right)\cdot\frac{{v}-{v}_{2}}{{v}_{1}-{v}_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle{v}\in[{v}_{1},\infty)

where

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha}(v)=\begin{cases}(1+(1-\alpha)v)^{-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}&\alpha\in[0,1)\\
e^{-v}&\alpha=1.\end{cases}

Below we formally prove
Lemma

3.3
and then
Theorem

3.2
.

Proof of
Lemma

3.3
.

Note that for any distribution
F
, we have that

\displaystyle F_{2:N}=\int_{0}^{1}\xi_{2:N}(q)\cdot F^{-1}(q){\,\mathrm{d}}q\text{ and }F_{1:n}=\int_{0}^{1}\xi_{1:n}(q)\cdot F^{-1}(q){\,\mathrm{d}}q.

where
\xi_{k:n}(q)
is defined in
Lemma

3.4
.
In addition,
Lemma

3.4
guarantees that
there exists
0\leq q^{\dagger}<q^{\ddagger}\leq 1

and corresponding value
{v}_{1}>{v}_{2}
such that

\xi_{2:N}(q)\leq\xi_{1:n}(q)

for
q\in[0,q^{\dagger}]\cup[q^{\ddagger},1]

and
\xi_{2:N}(q)\geq\xi_{1:n}(q)
for

q\in[q^{\dagger},q^{\ddagger}]
.
To minimize the difference
F_{2:N}-F_{1:n}
,
we need to minimize
F^{-1}(q)
for

q\in[q^{\dagger},q^{\ddagger}]

and maximize
F^{-1}(q)
for

q\in[0,q^{\dagger}]\cup[q^{\ddagger},1]
.
By
Lemma

3.5
,
the extreme
\alpha
-strongly regular distribution
\tilde{F}

that crosses
({v}_{1},q^{\dagger})
and
({v}_{2},q^{\ddagger})
satisfies

\displaystyle 1-{\tilde{F}}({v})

\displaystyle=q^{\ddagger}\cdot\Gamma_{\alpha}\left(\Gamma_{\alpha}^{-1}\left(\frac{q^{\dagger}}{q^{\ddagger}}\right)\cdot\frac{{v}-{v}_{2}}{{v}_{1}-{v}_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=q^{\ddagger}\cdot(1+\beta({v}-{v}_{2}))^{-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}

where
\beta=\Gamma_{\alpha}^{-1}\left(\frac{1-q^{\dagger}}{1-q^{\ddagger}}\right)\cdot\frac{1-\alpha}{{v}_{1}-{v}_{2}}
.
Let
{v}_{0}
be the constant such that

(1-q^{\ddagger})\cdot(1+\beta({v}_{0}-{v}_{2}))^{-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}=1
.
By shifting the distribution
\tilde{F}
down by
{v}_{0}
,
and scaling it by a factor
(1-q^{\ddagger})^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\cdot\beta
,
the resulting distribution is
\hat{F}
.
Moreover, it is easy to verify that
\hat{F}_{2:N}\geq\hat{F}_{1:n}

if and only if
\tilde{F}_{2:N}\geq\tilde{F}_{1:n}
,
where the latter implies that
F_{2:N}\geq F_{1:n}
by construction.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

3.2
.

By
Lemma

3.3
, for
\alpha\in(0,1)
, it is without loss to consider
\alpha
-strongly regular distribution
\hat{F}
with CDF

1-\hat{F}({v})=(1+{v})^{-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}
,
where the corresponding virtual value is
\alpha{v}-(1-\alpha)
.
Since the expected revenue from the second-price auction is the expected virtual value,
we have
\hat{F}_{2:N}=\alpha\hat{F}_{1:N}-(1-\alpha)
.

Lemma 3.7

\displaystyle\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\text{\bf Pr}\!\left[{\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum\nolimits_{j\in[m]}\max\left\{\,0,{v}_{ij}-\max_{i^{\prime}\neq i}{v}_{i^{\prime}j}\,\right\}-\frac{z_{n}}{n}\right|\geq\epsilon}\right]=0.

(4)

3.2

In this section, we show that, in contrast to the VCG auction, which has a competition complexity

14

14

\alpha
-strongly regular distributions for any

In this section, we study the optimal pricing of the grand bundle (BRev) for a single bidder. We begin by focusing on the setting with regular items. Our main result is as follows.

Theorem 5.1

(Approximation of BRev against OPT for regular items and a single bidder)

In contrast, as shown in

Conclusion and Future Directions

In this work, we study the
competition complexity
in multi-item auction settings. For the Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG) auction, we identify its
tight
competition complexity, denoted by
\mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}
, when item valuations follow
\alpha
-strongly regular distributions, including the MHR case (i.e.,
\alpha=1
). In contrast to prior work that assumes only regularity and yields bounds depending on both the number of items
m
and bidders
n
, our bound
\mathcal{C}_{n,\alpha}
depends solely on
n
and is independent of
m
.
We then turn to the bundle-based second-price auction (BSPA) in the single-bidder setting. For MHR distributions, we show that BSPA has a competition complexity of 3. For regular distributions, we derive competition complexity bounds in cases with two or three items. In addition, we establish constant-factor approximation guarantees for both BSPA and the revenue from selling items as a grand bundle (BRev), under regular and MHR assumptions.
Many of our techniques are general and may be of independent interest.

Future directions.
There are many interesting directions for future research.

First, it would be valuable to extend the competition complexity results of BSPA from the case of two or three items to a general number
m
of items. Note that most parts of our analysis framework (
Lemmas

4.2
,
4.3
and
4.4
) hold for arbitrary
m
. Therefore, one promising approach to establishing a competition complexity of
m
is to generalize
Lemma

4.5
. Going further, an important open question is whether BSPA admits a competition complexity with sub-logarithmic or even constant dependence on
m
in the single-bidder setting.

Second, from a technical perspective, a key challenge in analyzing BSPA is the limited understanding of the distribution of sums of values drawn from regular distributions. By comparison,
Barlow et al. 1963
showed that sums of values from MHR distributions remain MHR. It would be valuable to prove or disprove an analogous result for regular distributions, even in the i.i.d. setting.

Third, as shown in
Theorem

3.8
, for two or more bidders, the competition complexity of BSPA suffers from exponential dependence on
m
, even under uniform valuations. Hence, it would be valuable to design prior-independent mechanisms with improved competition complexity guarantees in the multi-bidder setting. One promising direction could be a hybrid mechanism combining VCG and BSPA, for example, by first running a competition for entry and then selling items separately.

Finally, it would be interesting to improve the constant-factor approximation or identify tight bounds for BSPA and BRev in the single-bidder, regular-items setting.

Appendix A
Motivation for the Bundle-based Second-price Auction

In this section, we provide some motivation why the bundle-based second-price auction (BSPA) could be an interesting mechanism for studying competition complexity in the multi-item setting.

First, selling items as a grand bundle is a simple and practical approach in the multi-item setting.
Babaioff et al. 2020
has identified selling items separately (SRev) and selling items as a grand bundle (BRev) as two simple mechanisms that complement each other in the few-bidders setting that we study. The Bulow-Klemperer-style questions typically emphasize prior-independent mechanisms, and (BSPA) is a natural prior-independent implementation of selling the grand bundle (analogous to VCG vs SRev).

As illustrated numerically in
Example

1.2
and discussed in
Derakhshan et al. 2024
, under the equal-revenue distribution (an “extreme case” within the class of regular distributions), BSPA outperforms VCG and closely matches the Bayesian optimal revenue. Specifically,
Derakhshan et al. 2024
shows that when selling
m
items with equal-revenue distributions to
n
bidders, the expected revenue of BSPA is
nm+\Omega(m\ln(mn))
, while the Bayesian optimal revenue is
nm+O(m^{2}\ln n)
. As a comparison, the expected revenue of VCG for the equal-revenue distribution is
nm
.
This further motivates BSPA as a mechanism of interest in the Bulow-Klemperer setting, which typically focuses on VCG in this literature.
