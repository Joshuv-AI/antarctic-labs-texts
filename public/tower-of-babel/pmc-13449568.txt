Developing a sinusoidal-polynomial fitting model for deriving the structural and biochemical circadian rhythms for different parts of Birch from hyperspectral LiDAR data
Authors: Shao Dong, Yi Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A deeper understanding of circadian rhythms in plants, especially trees, is crucial for uncovering how structural and physiological processes align with daily environmental cycles. However, most studies analyze biochemical changes and positional variations separately, with limited exploration of their coordination within the whole-plant system. Hyperspectral light detection and ranging (HSL) integrates three-dimensional (3D) structural mapping with hyperspectral reflectance, enabling non-destructive assessment of plant biochemistry. Previous work showed that HSL can detect nocturnal vertical canopy displacements with centimeter accuracy, but organ-level rhythmic patterns (e.g., branches vs. leaves) remain poorly studied. Here, we developed a hyperspectral point cloud classification method combining spectral and spatial data to separate branches from leaves and analyze their sleep movements independently. A novel sinusoidal-polynomial fitting model was then proposed to characterize circadian rhythms in both sleep movements and reflectance variations. We applied HSL data from a single birch tree (Betula pendula) collected at 30 distinct HSL measurement times over 24 h to develop a 3D canopy partitioning approach that divides the canopy into nine grids (3 × 3) and ten vertical layers per grid. Results revealed near-24-hour rhythmic patterns (max R² = 0.5841, P < 0.05) and stratified sleep movements: branches exhibited larger amplitudes than the corresponding canopy layers, with the overall maximum movement amplitude occurring shortly before sunrise (04:00–06:30) and recovering after sunrise. The model also effectively characterized diurnal reflectance variations (max R² = 0.5812, P < 0.05). In addition, chlorophyll-related spectral indices exhibited a sinusoidal variation, reaching a minimum around 03:00. These findings highlight the potential of HSL for the joint analysis of structural and biochemical circadian rhythms, providing a non-destructive approach to investigate plant rhythmicity in both structural and physiological domains.

Introduction

Unlike mobile organisms, plants have evolved sessile architectural frameworks optimized for light capture and nutrient uptake, rather than complex anatomy for locomotion [31, 58]. As a result of this sessility, plants must continuously adapt to their environment, regardless of habitat quality [11]. Each plant harbors a distinct circadian clock that optimizes growth across diverse conditions and developmental stages [12, 59, 60]. Notably, plant biological rhythms operate beyond diurnal scales; in perennials, circadian rhythms interact with and are modulated by longer seasonal cycles over lifespans spanning decades [1, 19, 23].

Plant circadian rhythms are governed by an intrinsic circadian oscillator that coordinates diverse physiological processes (McClung et al., 2006) [26]. A defining feature of this system is temperature compensation, which maintains approximately 24-hour cycles across a range of ambient temperatures [55]. Functioning as a central biological timekeeper, the oscillator retains high precision under fluctuating seasonal and climatic conditions. These regulatory mechanisms underscore the evolutionary significance of circadian rhythms in maintaining ecological homeostasis [37].

Extensive research has explored plant circadian rhythms through molecular and transcriptomic approaches [24] (Hu et al., 2024). These cell-autonomous rhythms are sustained by self-regulating transcriptional and post-transcriptional feedback loops, and multicellular plants contain multiple distinct circadian clocks [41]. While some studies have employed remote sensing for non-invasive estimation of circadian rhythms [24, 45], current research remains limited in methodological diversity and lacks integration with mechanistic explanations.

Plant sleep movements, as manifestations of circadian rhythms, contribute significantly to biosphere health and stability [17]. These movements occur across both terrestrial and aquatic flora, with fossilized evidence of leaf herbivory providing some of the earliest indications of such behaviors [34, 52]. In terrestrial plants, nocturnal leaf elevation often reduces dew accumulation, mitigating potential respiratory constraints, while also modulating temperature and growth processes [9, 65]. Furthermore, these rhythmic movements are influenced by diverse stressors and light conditions, with some studies suggesting the involvement of associative learning mechanisms in their regulation [21].

Plant circadian rhythms manifest not only in sleep movements that reshape external morphology, but also in precisely timed biochemical fluctuations that optimize growth [6, 22]. Characterizing these seasonal biochemical changes is critical for estimating global forest carbon fluxes and improving agricultural management [3, 43]. As the primary photosynthetic pigment, chlorophyll strongly influences plant growth, health, and survival, making its quantification key to understanding carbon cycling [20]. Though chlorophyll content varies among species, it is continually renewed within individual plants [54]. Traditional destructive sampling methods are increasingly replaced by high-accuracy spectral inversion techniques driven by advances in remote sensing [10].

However, conventional circadian rhythm studies have largely focused on endogenous molecular mechanisms or descriptive observations of branch movements [7, 50, 64], often overlooking the relationship between reflectance properties and biochemical composition. Most existing plant circadian research emphasizes internal molecular oscillators or downstream gene-expression pathways, while comparatively little attention has been paid to externally observable rhythmic manifestations such as sleep movements or circadian biochemical fluctuations. Yet these macroscopic expressions represent integrative outcomes of internal regulation and environmental forcing, and may contain substantial ecological and physiological information. The lack of explicit quantitative models describing such observable rhythmic patterns remains a notable gap.

Light detection and ranging (LiDAR) enables precise 3D structural mapping by measuring the time-of-flight of laser pulses. Building on this, Hyperspectral LiDAR integrates LiDAR’s 3D mapping capability with continuous spectral reflectance measurements across up to 32 bands [56]. This dual capability has been effective for nitrogen estimation and chlorophyll inversion [16, 57]. Advances in optical components and sensor materials are further enhancing HSL performance, expanding its potential in Earth observation [25]. Despite these advances, HSL and other remote sensing technologies remain underutilized for estimating plant sleep movements and circadian rhythms. Current research largely focused on detecting basic periodic oscillations, with no universal models established [27]. A pioneering HSL study demonstrated the feasibility of tracking birch canopy height variations, but lacked comprehensive analysis of sleep movement dynamics and related processes [48, 49].

This study leverages the unique capabilities of HSL to achieve precise classification of birch point clouds by integrating both spectral and structural dimensions features, eliminating dependence on external reference data. Based on this refined classification, we developed a novel sinusoidal-polynomial fitting model to characterize plant circadian rhythms. The model incorporates components that capture both environmentally influenced variability and persistent oscillatory behavior, providing a structured and interpretable framework for describing how external and internal factors may jointly shape observable circadian patterns. Rather than replacing molecular circadian models or establishing a mechanistic circadian model, this approach aims to bridge high-frequency structural and reflectance measurements with biologically meaningful rhythmic interpretation at the canopy scale. The model was validated using 30 temporal point cloud datasets acquired from a single birch tree over a 24-hour period. To systematically examine vertical and directional variations, we applied a 3D canopy partitioning method by implementing a consistent nine-cell (3 × 3) grid across the canopy and subdividing each grid cell into ten equal-height vertical layers. Remote sensing inversion was then introduced to estimate diurnal reflectance dynamics in vascular plants. This research (1) evaluates the ability of the proposed model to describe circadian-like oscillatory patterns in both canopy movement and reflectance; (2) investigates the potential of HSL for high-resolution, non-invasive estimation of nocturnal tree dynamics; and (3) demonstrates the feasibility of estimating leaf biochemical traits directly from HSL reflectance data, establishing a new approach for advanced studies in plant physiology.

Methods

Materials

This study investigated a silver birch (Betula pendula) located in southern Finland (Kirkkonummi, 60°09′40″N, 24°32′48″E). Birch trees, particularly B. pendula, are characterized by relatively flexible branches whose nocturnal drooping amplitude exceeds that of many species with more rigid branches, while leaf movements remain less pronounced. This characteristic makes birch a representative species for investigating plant circadian and sleep-related movements. Compared with species possessing stiffer branches, such as oaks and pines, the more flexible branches of birch exhibit more pronounced nocturnal motion. This drooping is primarily associated with nighttime water redistribution: as transpiration decreases at night while root water uptake continues, changes in cell turgor pressure occur, causing the branches to temporarily sag under the influence of gravity. Morphological and reflectance data were collected using a hyperspectral laser scanner developed by the Finnish Geospatial Research Institute, which continuously scanned the tree at 30 different HSL measurement times between September 11 and 12, 2013 [48, 49]. The HSL system recorded eight central wavelengths: 545.5, 641.2, 675.0, 711.0, 741.5, 778.4, 978.0, and 1278.0 nm, and produced point clouds containing both 3D spatial coordinates and reflectance intensities across these bands.

The morphology of the birch tree is shown in Fig. 1. Key measurements include its location on a south-facing slope, with scans acquired approximately every hour (15 min per session) and at 40-minute intervals around sunset (19:40) and sunrise (06:30 the following day). The data were classified into 13 feature categories, including reference spheres and reflectance calibration panels, with intensity values calibrated for each different HSL measurement time. It should be noted, however, that the portion of the birch tree shown in Fig. 1 represents only a preliminary classification of the raw data, in which branches and leaves were not strictly separated.

The main technical specifications of the HSL system developed by Hakala et al., [30] are summarized in Table 1. The HSL system is an active hyperspectral LiDAR instrument capable of simultaneously recording range-resolved backscattered signals across multiple discrete wavelength channels, enabling wavelength-dependent reflectance measurements independent of ambient illumination conditions. Reflectance values were derived from calibrated return intensities using reference panels and range correction procedures to ensure comparability across different HSL measurement times. Negative intensity values resulting from noise or dark current were set to zero. According to the experimental description provided by Puttonen et al. [48, 49], measurements between sunset and sunrise were conducted under windless conditions, whereas the remaining daytime scans were assumed to be only minimally affected by wind. The eighth channel (1278.0 nm) was excluded due to its low signal-to-noise ratio and substantial noise in the HSL system.

To ensure the 3D coordinate accuracy of the point clouds for subsequent analyses, this study assessed the positional stability of hyperspectral point cloud data (already preliminarily distance-corrected) across 30 HSL measurement times. During the entire measurement period, both the reference spheres and the silver birch remained fixed in position. Therefore, the centers of the reference spheres obtained through sphere fitting can serve as stable spatial reference points for evaluating the consistency of the multi-temporal scans. Using several standard reference spheres and taking the first HSL measurement time as a baseline, deviations of the fitted sphere centers in the remaining 29 scans were calculated to quantify the positional differences between scans. These deviations primarily reflect the multi-temporal registration accuracy of the point clouds. The results indicate millimeter- or submillimeter-level positional deviations (see Tables 2 and 3).

Specifically, four polystyrene reference spheres, whose scans approximated ideal spheres, were analyzed in each dataset. Applying the least squares method to the 3D sphere equation, optimal center coordinates and radii were fitted for each sphere. Using the first HSL measurement time’s sphere parameters as reference, deviations in centers and radii at subsequent times were computed and averaged to quantify positioning errors. Finally, the 3D coordinates of later different HSL measurement times were corrected based on these errors, ensuring accuracy for further analyses.

Refining point cloud classification

The initial separation of the tree trunk and foliage was performed manually, followed by classification using a normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) threshold of 0.2. Points with NDVI values below 0.2 were assigned to the “woody” category (primarily including the trunk and coarse branches) while the remaining points were labeled as “foliage,” encompassing leaves and finer branches. NDVI values were calculated as the normalized difference between the reflectance intensity of band 6 (778.4 nm) and band 3 (675.0 nm) [44]. However, this approach often misclassified many branches as leaves and vice versa in the raw dataset, preventing a detailed analysis of sleep-related movements.

Given the complex structure of tree crowns and the potential overlap in spectral responses between leaves and woody tissues, we employed a classification approach that integrates both spectral and geometric features of the birch point cloud. Specifically, NDVI derived from spectral information was first used to distinguish vegetated from non-vegetated surfaces. Subsequently, the local curvature of each point was computed to characterize geometric surface variability. Considering the relatively low point cloud density (mean point spacing of 0.99 cm), 40 nearest neighbors were used for curvature estimation. Finally, an empirical threshold of NDVI > 0.4 and curvature > 0.08 was applied to improve the separation between foliage and woody components [4, 29, 51]. While spectral indices capture large-scale differences between vegetated and non-vegetated surfaces, the curvature feature helps improve the fine-scale separation of leaves and branches.

Because no independent ground-truth labels were available in the original dataset, a full quantitative classification accuracy assessment (e.g., confusion matrix metrics) could not be performed. Instead, classification performance was evaluated using a visually interpreted reference region following common practice in remote sensing studies dealing with historical datasets. Specifically, a spatial subset of the point cloud was manually identified through detailed 3D visualization. This region corresponded to a clearly distinguishable woody branch segment where foliage was visually negligible across all observed HSL measurement times. The selected region remained structurally stable and visually identifiable throughout the measurement period despite minor sleep-related movements.

In the initial NDVI-based classification, a substantial portion of points within this region was misclassified as foliage. Therefore, this subset was treated as a pseudo-reference branch-dominated region to examine whether the integrated spectral–geometric method could reduce such obvious misclassification. The proportion of points labeled as woody tissue before and after applying the refined criteria was calculated to illustrate the practical effectiveness of the classification refinement. It should be noted that this evaluation reflects agreement with visually interpreted woody structures rather than validation against an independent ground-truth dataset.

Construction of the sinusoidal-polynomial fitting model

The plant circadian system is a complex endogenous network producing ~ 24-hour rhythms via specific signaling pathways. This intrinsic oscillator can be entrained by environmental cues such as light and temperature [61]. Featuring extensive feedback regulation, it integrates multiple signaling networks, temporally gating plant responses to environmental stimuli within the diurnal cycle. In turn, these pathways modulate the oscillator, forming a bidirectional regulation that ultimately enhances plant growth and adaptive fitness [8].

Although these molecular mechanisms are well documented, few studies have attempted to describe canopy-scale rhythmic patterns using integrated structural and spectral observations. To address this, we propose an interpretable sinusoidal–polynomial fitting model to characterize observed circadian dynamics in canopy structure and reflectance. The model consists of three components: (1) a damped oscillation term characterizing the exogenous amplitude attenuation of periodic fluctuations (e.g., temperature effects), (2) a steady-state oscillation term representing endogenous periodic fluctuations in plants, and (3) a quadratic polynomial term describing the overall trend variation of circadian rhythms. It should be noted that these components are not direct representations of physiological processes, but rather phenomenological descriptors that enable structured decomposition and interpretation of complex temporal signals. The fitting procedure of the sinusoidal-polynomial model consists of: (1) reshaping the temporal vectors and canopy movement data or reflectance data into column vectors and applying Z-score normalization to standardize the variables prior to model fitting; (2) using FFT spectral analysis (pwelch power spectrum) to automatically extract dominant frequencies and generate initial frequency values dynamically, rather than assuming a fixed 24-hour period; (3) scaling polynomial coefficients according to normalization parameters to ensure reasonable initial values and improve convergence. During fitting, the Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm first performs nonlinear least-squares optimization, followed by iterative outlier rejection based on a 3σ residual threshold. If initial fitting fails, parameter constraints are relaxed automatically (e.g., converting attenuation coefficients to absolute values) to reduce sensitivity to initial values. Finally, fitted curves are transformed back to original scales for accuracy evaluation.

Three-dimensional canopy partitioning

In this study, we analyzed the birch tree using a 3D canopy partitioning method, with additional subdivision within each grid to enable a more detailed investigation of motion variations across different orientations and height layers. This fine-scale stratification was necessary to reveal the directional and vertical heterogeneity of sleep movements and reflectance changes in the canopy (Fig. 2). It allowed for separate analysis of chlorophyll content dynamics as well as height changes in branches induced by circadian sleep movements. Importantly, the hyperspectral point cloud data at all 30 HSL measurement times were processed consistently using this workflow, ensuring comparability across temporal observations.

The 3D canopy partitioning was performed using a structured grid-based approach. From a top-down perspective, the canopy was divided into a 3 × 3 horizontal grid centered on the trunk, resulting in nine spatial sectors. The central grid was defined as a square with a side length equal to one-third of the shorter canopy diameter (measured along two perpendicular horizontal directions), with the trunk located precisely at its center. The orientation of the grid was fixed such that one axis was aligned with the laser incidence direction to ensure geometric consistency across different HSL measurement times. The surrounding eight grids were defined to fully cover the remaining canopy area within the laser scanning extent. For vertical stratification, the canopy was further divided into ten uniform height layers from the canopy base to the top, each representing 10% of the total canopy height. These layers were sequentially numbered from Layer 1 (0–10% relative height, bottom of the canopy) to Layer 10 (90–100% relative height, top of the canopy). This combined horizontal and vertical partitioning resulted in a 3D canopy segmentation framework, enabling structural and biochemical circadian analysis across different canopy regions.

Feasibility of leaf chlorophyll content retrieval from the hsl system data using spectral indices

The structure and biochemical composition of plants cause distinct scattering and absorption of radiation, enabling vegetation spectral indices to reliably estimate biochemical parameters through mathematical operations on reflectance values from specific bands [28, 33]. This study employs several well-validated vegetation spectral indices for chlorophyll inversion in the birch, testing whether the HSL system data can effectively retrieve canopy chlorophyll content.

According to Li et al. [39], four categories of spectral indices are commonly used for chlorophyll retrieval: Simple ratio (SR), Modified simple ratio (mSR), Double-difference ratios (DD) and Red edge positions (REP). Among these, SR demonstrates the highest sensitivity to specular reflection, while the other three indices can mitigate specular effects to varying degrees. Both DD and REP indices are mathematically designed to be specular-insensitive, though their actual performance against leaf specular reflection requires experimental verification.

Since LiDAR acquires directional-directional reflectance, the influence of specular reflection cannot be neglected. Based on previous research findings, it evaluated 20 spectral indices from the four major categories for chlorophyll inversion using experimental datasets, ultimately identifying four unified spectral indices suitable for both BRF and DHRF spectra: DD-type (Macc01 and DATT) and REP-type (REPPF and WREPS4). This study primarily employs these four relatively reliable spectral indices for chlorophyll content inversion in the birch canopy.

It should be noted that due to the limited number of available bands (with the 8th band excluded due to excessive noise), reflectance band interpolation was necessary to apply these spectral indices. We fitted the reflectance data from the remaining 7 bands and determined that Piecewise Cubic Hermite Interpolating Polynomial (PCHIP) interpolation provided the optimal fitting performance [2]. However, since the HSL system lacks a corresponding blue light band, the interpolation results still cannot effectively estimate reflectance in the blue light spectrum. Building on this, we further evaluated the potential of the HSL system for retrieving canopy leaf chlorophyll content using the chlorophyll absorption coefficients from the latest PROSPECT-PRO model [18].

Time series analysis

This study utilized time series analysis to investigate the sleep movements of the birch over a 24-hour period, diurnal variations in canopy reflectance, and differences in chlorophyll content across different height layers and orientations. This effective analytical method helps reveal the regularity of circadian rhythms and trends in sleep movements [32]. It aims to identify temporal variations and trends in both reflectance and spatial positioning parameters by analyzing hyperspectral point cloud data. This approach simultaneously enables effective detection of anomalous data points during the observation period. Furthermore, we standardized both the positional information and reflectance data from the point clouds to eliminate random errors, ensuring robust and comparable results.

Accuracy assessment

To evaluate the robustness of the sinusoidal–polynomial model and address potential risks of overfitting given the discrete nature of the 24-hour dataset, a Bootstrap Pairs analysis was conducted. This resampling procedure involved 200 iterations, where each iteration generated a new dataset by sampling the original observations with replacement. For each bootstrap replicate, the model parameters were re-estimated. From the resulting distribution of fitted trajectories, point-wise 95% confidence bands were calculated to quantify the stability of the model against sampling variations and data noise.

Furthermore, the accuracy of the proposed sinusoidal–polynomial fitting model was validated by calculating the coefficient of determination (R²), root mean square error (RMSE), and the F-statistic to evaluate model significance in the analysis of sleep movements and reflectance variations in the HSL data. Result

An example of the reclassified birch point cloud is shown in Fig. 3. Point clouds at all HSL measurement times were reclassified using the integrated spectral–geometric approach rather than relying solely on the NDVI threshold of 0.2 to distinguish branches from leaves. Specifically, points within the spatial range of X: 7.15–7.45 m, Y: 0.5–0.7 m, and Z: −1.2–−0.9 m were examined. As described in Sect. 2.2, this region was visually interpreted as a branch-dominated subset, with foliage being negligible across all observed HSL measurement times. Although slight movements occurred due to sleep behavior and the point cloud density was relatively sparse, the structural characteristics of this branch segment remained clearly identifiable.

Using the integrated classification method that combines spectral and geometric information, most points within this visually interpreted branch region were consistently labeled as woody tissues, with only a small fraction identified as foliage. The proportion of points labeled as woody tissues within this region increased to an average of 95.54%, indicating substantially improved consistency with the visually interpreted branch structure. Across the entire birch point cloud, the proportion of points labeled as woody tissues increased by 14.73% after applying the refined classification criteria. This refinement helped reduce apparent branch–leaf confusion in the dataset and improved the reliability of subsequent circadian movement analyses.

To evaluate the stability of the classification refinement, a sensitivity analysis was performed by varying the nearest neighbor count (k) and the curvature threshold. When the neighborhood size k was adjusted from 30 to 50 (with the curvature threshold fixed at 0.08), the average producer accuracy (PA) within the branch-dominated reference region ranged from 87.66 to 98.54%, while the overall branch proportion (BR) across the entire canopy fluctuated between 23.50 and 20.45%. Similarly, varying the curvature threshold between 0.06 and 0.10 (with k = 40) resulted in a PA range of 89.29 to 99.08% and a BR range of 17.35 to 27.77%. These results indicate that although the classification metrics exhibit some sensitivity to parameter selection—particularly the curvature threshold—the classification consistently achieves high accuracy (PA > 87%) in identifying woody structures compared to spectral-only methods. This analysis demonstrates that the integrated spectral–geometric approach is robust enough to maintain the structural integrity of the branch network across a reasonable parameter space, thereby supporting the reliability of the subsequent circadian movement interpretations.

According to the birch partitioning principles outlined in Sect. 2.4, the birch canopy does not grow uniformly or symmetrically around the trunk. Consequently, some height layers within specific grid sectors contained no canopy points or only a very limited number of points. To minimize potential artifacts and ensure the reliability of the subsequent analyses, these sectors were excluded from the sleep movement analysis and biochemical inversion. Specifically, sectors containing fewer than 100 points were considered insufficient to represent a valid canopy segment and were therefore omitted from further analysis. This threshold was selected to ensure a sufficient point density for reliable canopy representation and to avoid unstable model fitting caused by sparse point distributions. The fitting results of the sinusoidal-polynomial fitting model are shown in Fig. 4. To capture subtle sleep movements of the birch, we normalized the canopy movement data by subtracting the mean Z-coordinates recorded at the first HSL measurement time from all 30 subsequent temporal measurements. Across all directional grid sectors, the model demonstrated statistically significant fitting accuracy (P-value < 0.05), with fitted curves oscillating around the mean canopy movement amplitude for each corresponding layer. Excluding Grid 3 which showed poorer data quality due to insufficient point cloud coverage, the maximum coefficient of determination reached 0.5841. All fitted curves exhibited sinusoidal-like fluctuation patterns with periods approximating 24 h, confirming the model’s effectiveness in characterizing the birch tree sleep movements.

Notably, clear directional heterogeneity was observed among different horizontal canopy sectors. Some grids exhibited mean vertical displacement amplitudes exceeding 10 cm (e.g., Grid 4 and Grid 5), whereas others showed average vertical displacements of approximately 5 cm (e.g., Grid 8 and Grid 9). This indicates that canopy sleep movement amplitudes vary substantially among different orientations. Furthermore, within any given grid sector, although different height layers displayed similar temporal movement trends, their movement amplitudes differed, suggesting vertical heterogeneity in sleep intensity within the same directional sector. In addition, most canopy sectors exhibited their maximum vertical displacement amplitudes at approximately 04:00–06:30 local time, followed by a gradual decrease during the night and a recovery after sunrise. This consistent timing pattern suggests that the nocturnal canopy movement is closely synchronized with the daily light cycle.

The statistical stability and reliability of the model were further validated via Bootstrap analysis. As illustrated in Fig. 5, using canopy Grid 5 as a representative example, the model demonstrates robust parameter estimation despite the noise and variability inherent in the intermediate time regions of the HSL data. Grid 5 was specifically selected for this demonstration because it maintains a sufficient point cloud density across all 10 vertical layers, whereas other grids lack adequate data points in certain layers to support a comprehensive multi-layer analysis. The resulting point-wise 95% curve confidence band remains tight and constrained across the entire time domain. Notably, the confidence band does not exhibit significant widening or ‘flaring’ at the model’s temporal boundaries (time 0 and time 24+), which is a characteristic indicator of model overfitting or parametric instability. The primary fitted curve remains well-centered within this statistical band, indicating that the model provides a stable and reliable representation of the underlying dynamics within the measured parameter space.

In addition to the sinusoidal–polynomial fitting model, several conventional fitting approaches were evaluated using the same processing pipeline. The coefficient of determination, root mean square error, and statistical significance tests were calculated for each model, with the results summarized in Table 4. The single-sine model, sine–cosine model, and single-sine plus polynomial model all produced statistically insignificant fits (P > > 0.05), indicating that these models were unable to adequately capture the circadian movement patterns of the birch canopy. Although the low-order polynomial model achieved statistical significance (P < 0.05), its predictive performance remained inferior to the proposed sinusoidal–polynomial model and lacked the ability to represent periodic circadian behavior.

We quantified birch canopy sleep movement amplitudes across different orientations and height layers during the nocturnal period (sunset to sunrise), calculated as the vertical height difference between the two HSL measurement times, with the results summarized in Table 5. The results show an average canopy vertical height decrease of several centimeters due to sleep movements. Notably, upper canopy layers exhibited greater amplitude, while lower layers showed smaller displacements. Layers within the 30 to 70% height percentiles maintained sufficient point cloud density across all grids. In addition, horizontal directional differences were evident, particularly within lower canopy layers. For example, Grid 2 exhibited substantially smaller mean sleep movement amplitudes within Layer 3 to Layer 5 compared with other canopy sectors. Relative to its symmetrical counterpart (Grid 8) across the trunk center, the average sleep amplitude in Grid 2 was approximately 4 cm lower. Grid 5 (central sector) displayed significantly larger vertical height variations than others, indicating that branches and leaves connected directly to the trunk experience more pronounced sleep movements. This finding aligns with the botanical principle that plants adjust posture to become more perpendicular to the ground during sleep, an adaptive mechanism enhancing survival.

Pearson correlation coefficients of dusk-to-dawn height differences were computed among the nine canopy grid sectors across different parts of the birch canopy. The results in Table 6 suggest that sleep movement rhythms deviate from a uniform pattern and instead display distinct orientation-dependent characteristics. For example, the sleep pattern in Grid 1 correlates more strongly with adjacent Grid 4 but weakly with diagonally positioned Grids 6 and 9. Conversely, Grid 9’s strongest correlation coefficient is with neighboring Grid 6. Although neighboring grids are expected to share similar sleep movement patterns, some directional sectors show markedly different rhythms from their adjacent grids, resulting in unusually low correlations. For instance, Grids 1 and 2 display minimal correlation, while Grid 1’s correlation with Grid 4 is the highest among all pairs. Similarly, Grid 9’s correlation with Grid 6 significantly exceeds that with Grid 8, despite both being neighbors. These findings confirm that within the same height layer, the birch canopy exhibits varying sleep movement amplitudes depending on orientation.

The reflectance values were derived exclusively from canopy points classified as leaves, with non-leaf components (e.g., trunk and branches) excluded from the analysis. We validated the feasibility of chlorophyll content inversion in birch canopy using the HSL system, with results shown in Fig. 8. The study employed chlorophyll-specific absorption coefficients from the PROSPECT-PRO model, derived from hundreds of leaf samples across multiple datasets. Comparing absorption coefficients in the 545–980 nm range with canopy reflectance across corresponding bands, Fig. 8 confirms that the reflectance data from the HSL system effectively capture chlorophyll absorption peaks, demonstrating the system’s reliability for biochemical component inversion, including chlorophyll.

Four commonly used spectral index models were applied to estimate chlorophyll content using reflectance data from leaf-classified canopy points within the 30%–70% relative height interval of the birch canopy. The purpose of this analysis was to explore the feasibility of using HSL-derived reflectance data for biochemical parameter estimation, rather than to perform a rigorous validation of chlorophyll retrieval. The results are summarized in Table 7. Because only seven effective spectral bands were available in the HSL dataset, the reflectance spectra were interpolated using the PCHIP method. No in situ chlorophyll measurements were collected during the 2013 data acquisition campaign; therefore, direct validation of the retrieved chlorophyll values is not possible. In this study, the chlorophyll retrieval is intended as an exploratory assessment of whether HSL-derived reflectance measurements can produce physiologically reasonable estimates of canopy biochemical traits. To evaluate the plausibility of the retrieved values, the estimated chlorophyll content was compared with typical ranges reported in previous studies. Previous research indicates that birch chlorophyll content typically decreases to approximately 10–30 µg/cm² in autumn [36, 46]. Among the models, WREPS4 demonstrated the highest stability, followed by the Datt model, while Macc01 performed poorly. The Macc01 model requires average reflectance in the 380–480 nm range (strongly absorbed by chlorophyll when content ≤ 20 µg/cm²) but the Finnish dataset lacks spectral data below 545 nm. Thus, even with interpolation, the model cannot capture this absorption, causing overestimated and unreliable inversion results. Excluding Macc01’s limitations due to band coverage, the other three models yielded reasonable chlorophyll estimates without significant variation across height layers.

Based on the best-performing WREPS4 model, we further analyzed the temporal variation of chlorophyll content in birch canopy leaves across different height layers and horizontal spatial sectors. Figure 9 illustrates the temporal dynamics of chlorophyll content from Layer 4 to Layer 7, without horizontal grid subdivision. The results indicate that chlorophyll content across different height layers exhibited highly consistent temporal patterns. Over the approximately 24-hour measurement period, chlorophyll content first decreased and then increased, reaching a maximum around 15:15 and a minimum at approximately 03:00, exhibiting an approximately sinusoidal temporal pattern before returning to nearly its initial level after one day. The amplitude of variation within each height layer remained within 1 µg/cm². Differences among height layers were minimal, with a maximum inter-layer difference of approximately 0.2 µg/cm².

Figure 10 presents the temporal variation of chlorophyll content within the same vertical range (Layer 4–Layer 7), but under the horizontal 3 × 3 grid division, without distinguishing vertical layers. Similar to the patterns observed in Fig. 9, chlorophyll content across different spatial directions exhibited consistent circadian trends. However, spatial differences among grids were slightly more pronounced than those among height layers, with maximum inter-grid differences reaching approximately 1.5 µg/cm². The largest differences were observed between Grid 4 and Grid 6, which are symmetrically positioned relative to the trunk-centered sector (Grid 5). These results collectively demonstrate that chlorophyll content in the birch canopy exhibits a clear circadian rhythmic pattern, with measurable differences observed among both vertical layers and horizontal spatial sectors.

Discussion

In this study, the proposed hyperspectral point cloud classification method effectively separated birch branches from foliage, providing a high-fidelity dataset for circadian analysis. Our findings reveal that branch movements exhibit significantly greater amplitudes than those of the corresponding canopy layers. The sinusoidal–polynomial fitting model was then applied to describe these patterns, demonstrating consistent performance in capturing both structural sleep movements and diurnal reflectance variations. The model enables decomposition of observed rhythmic signals into multiple components, facilitating a more structured interpretation of canopy-level temporal dynamics. In particular, the separation into damped and steady oscillatory terms provides a useful way to distinguish between amplitude-varying and persistent periodic patterns observed in the data. The statistical reliability of this framework is further supported by Bootstrap analysis; the narrow 95% confidence bands and stable boundary behavior demonstrate that the model is robust to data noise and does not suffer from significant overfitting, even within the constraints of a 30-point dataset.

It is important to emphasize that this decomposition should be interpreted as phenomenological rather than mechanistic. The associations between model components and external environmental drivers or intrinsic biological regulation are inferred from observed patterns, but are not directly validated in this study. Further work, including controlled experiments or independent physiological measurements, would be required to establish such causal relationships. Within these limitations, the model provides a practical and interpretable framework for analyzing high-frequency canopy-scale observations. By linking structural movement and reflectance dynamics within a unified temporal representation, it contributes to a more comprehensive characterization of plant circadian behavior at the canopy level.

Beyond structural changes, we explored the capability of the HSL system to capture leaf chlorophyll-related spectral dynamics. The estimated chlorophyll proxies exhibited a sinusoidal-like temporal pattern, reaching a maximum at approximately 15:15 and a minimum around 03:00. The relatively consistent temporal trends observed across the canopy suggest that the reflectance-derived signals respond coherently to diurnal environmental or physiological processes. Interestingly, while vertical height layers (Layers 1–10) showed relatively small differences, spatial heterogeneity among the horizontal sectors was more pronounced. The measurable differences observed between symmetrical canopy regions (e.g., Grid 4 vs. Grid 6) may reflect localized micro-environmental influences within the canopy. These results suggest that HSL-derived reflectance measurements have the potential to capture high-frequency variations in canopy biochemical proxies, providing a preliminary exploration of temporal variability in tree physiological indicators.

The studied birch displayed distinct nocturnal sleep rhythms characterized by pronounced periodicity and spatial partitioning. Vertically, the upper canopy layers consistently exhibited the largest amplitudes. This is likely due to the cumulative mechanical effect along the branch length; while leaves undergo subtle repositioning via pulvini-mediated turgor changes, the rigid branches act as levers, amplifying small angular adjustments at the base into substantial vertical displacements at the terminal tips. This explains why branch-leaf interfaces in Acer or Betula often show decimeter-scale movements, whereas individual leaf nyctinasty remains within the centimeter scale [42].

The observed directional differences in sleep movements across different sectors suggest a significant degree of spatial heterogeneity within the canopy. While these orientation-dependent patterns could potentially be linked to the tree’s long-term phototropic adaptation and historical light environment, the current single-tree dataset remains insufficient to establish a definitive mechanistic link between solar exposure and specific movement amplitudes. It is possible that sun-exposed sectors develop distinct structural or physiological traits compared to shaded areas [62], yet such directional variations may also be influenced by other localized factors, such as branch mechanical properties or micro-environmental differences within the canopy [5, 15]. Therefore, these spatial patterns should be interpreted as preliminary observations that highlight the complexity of canopy-scale circadian behavior, necessitating future multi-sample studies with integrated environmental sensors to clarify the underlying drivers.

For perennial plants, circadian rhythms are inherently embedded within broader seasonal cycles. Although this study investigated birch dynamics over a 24-hour timescale, these high-frequency observations represent a baseline “unit” of plant response. Future research should explore how the parameters of our sinusoidal-polynomial model, such as the amplitude and phase of the oscillation, shift as the tree transitions through different phenological stages. As biochemical components vary significantly across seasons [35, 53], understanding the integration of circadian and seasonal rhythms will be key to improving ecosystem carbon cycle models and photosynthetic capacity estimates [14, 63].

This study has two main limitations. First, as the 2013 data acquisition campaign did not include concurrent leaf sampling, our biochemical retrieval currently lacks in-situ ground-truth validation. Consequently, the chlorophyll values reported here should be interpreted as indicative feasibility estimates based on established spectral indices rather than absolute quantifications. Future work must incorporate simultaneous destructive sampling to rigorously validate HSL-based biochemical retrieval. Additionally, while the classification was effective, minor misclassifications of branch or trunk points as foliage may have slightly influenced the inversion accuracy.

Second, the spectral index models require further optimization to accommodate the unique “hotspot” observation geometry of the coaxial HSL system. While the models used here are well-validated for BRF spectra, new model developments specifically tailored for high-frequency HSL data could yield deeper insights. Extending this interpretable framework to diverse vegetation types and longer observation periods remains a promising direction for understanding the complex physiological processes of the forest canopy.

Conclusion

This study proposes an integrated HSL point cloud classification approach and a novel sinusoidal-polynomial fitting model to characterize plant circadian rhythms by analyzing diurnal variations in birch sleep movements and reflectance over a ~ 24-hour period. The classification method effectively separates branches from leaves, revealing that birch branches exhibit larger sleep movement amplitudes than the corresponding canopy layer, with the maximum nocturnal movement amplitude for both branches and the full canopy occurring around sunrise. Using continuous hyperspectral point cloud data from the HSL system, we developed both the nonlinear model and a 3D canopy partitioning framework. This work represents the first remote sensing inversion application to simultaneously estimate circadian reflectance changes and sleep movement rhythms in individual vascular plants. Results demonstrate that the model accurately reveals both movement and reflectance dynamics, uncovering pronounced ~ 24-hour sinusoidal patterns, vertical partitioning, with upper canopy layers showing greater amplitudes and significant directional differences within the same height layer. Across all canopy sectors, the maximum sleep-movement amplitude occurred shortly before sunrise (04:00–06:30), after which the canopy gradually returned to its normal height following sunrise. Furthermore, the study highlights the HSL system’s capability for tracking biochemical-related spectral dynamics. The estimated canopy chlorophyll variations also exhibited a sinusoid-like variation, reaching a minimum around 03:00 at night and a maximum around 15:15 during the daytime measurement period. These results provide a non-destructive remote sensing pathway to explore circadian spectral variations potentially linked to biochemical traits in vascular plants.

This study establishes a framework for applying HSL to estimate vascular plant circadian rhythms. Although the HSL system used here had only 8 spectral bands, advanced systems with 32 bands now cover broader wavelength ranges, enabling more effective tracking of morphological and biochemical changes. While our focus was on chlorophyll-related dynamics in birch canopy, other parameters such as equivalent water thickness and carotenoids also show diurnal variation and could be incorporated in future remote sensing studies with rigorous in-situ validation. Moreover, current estimation of sleep movements, especially in woody parts like branches (lacks detailed analysis), often treating whole canopy without accounting for structural variations such as branch diameter and angle. Integrating these structural factors with biochemical data in future research could deepen understanding of sleep movements and circadian dynamics.
