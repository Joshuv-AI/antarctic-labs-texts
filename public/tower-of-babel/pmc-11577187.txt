Supplementation of precision biotic leads to improved growth performance by modulating the microbiome of broiler chickens fed corn or wheat-based diets☆
Authors: C Bortoluzzi, M Segura-Wang, R Aureli, A Leduc, MA Iuspa, AJ Cowieson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe objective of the present study was to evaluate the effect of a precision biotic (PB; Symphiome®, dsm-firmenich) on the growth performance, nutrient digestibility, litter quality, blood uric acid (UA) concentration, cecal short-chain fatty acids (SCFA) concentration, and ileal and cecal microbiome of broiler chickens fed corn or wheat-based diets. One-day-old broiler chicks were placed in a completely randomized design (2 × 2 factorial scheme) in 4 treatments, 8 replicates/treatment, and 16 birds/replicate. The treatments were a corn or wheat and soybean meal-based diet without or with supplementation of 0.9 kg/MT of PB. All the birds were vaccinated against coccidiosis at the time of placement (Paracox 5). Growth performance was evaluated weekly and at the end of the experiment. At d 35, blood was collected to determine UA concentration and cecal content for SCFA concentration analysis. Ileal digesta was also collected for nutrient digestibility analysis, and ileal digesta and cecal content were collected for microbiome analysis. The data were submitted to two-way ANOVA (P ≤ 0.05), and LSM was used to separate the means in case of interaction. During the starter and grower phases of the study, a better body weight gain (P = 0.0008 and P = 0.04, respectively) was observed in birds fed wheat compared to corn-based diets. From 28 to 36 d of age, the supplementation of PB increased feed intake (P = 0.05), and tended (P = 0.06) to improve the feed conversion ratio by 4.3 points vs non-supplementated birds. Birds fed wheat-based diets had higher (P = 0.02) blood UA than corn based-diets fed birds, and the supplementation of PB led to a reduction (P = 0.02) of blood UA compared to non-supplemented birds. The supplementation of PB changed the abundance of core metabolic pathways of the microbiome, mostly related to protein metabolism, which led to a reduction in blood UA concentration and increase of cecal SCFA concentrations. In conclusion, by beneficially modulating the microbiome, the supplementation of PB was translated into improved growth performance of broiler chickens fed corn or wheat-based diets.Keywords: Broilers, Corn diets, Microbiome, Precision biotics, Wheat diets

Introduction

The worldwide increased restrictions in the use of antimicrobial growth promoters (AGP) are driving the food animal production industry to search and develop novel, precise, and effective solutions that can, at least partially, replace AGP in the diets of the animals. For this, a multidisciplinary approach is needed for understanding the mechanism of action of such substances and how it can be successfully implemented by the poultry industry. For instance, the application of an extensive research platform revolving around the combination of in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo approaches, has led to the development of glycan-based precision biotics (PB). This PB was found to modulate core microbiome pathways most particularly related to protein metabolism and utilization, as well as short chain fatty acid (SCFA) production (Walsh et al., 2021; Bortoluzzi et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2023). The modulation of the microbiome of the gastrointestinal tract (GIT) of chickens, through the use of glycan-based PB, has led to improved growth performance of the animals, and indirectly to a better sustainability profile of poultry operations (Jacquier et al., 2022; Bortoluzzi et al., 2023; Petranyi et al., 2024). Leone and Ferrante (2023) reported that, as opposed to conventional prebiotics, glycan-based PB are less impacted by external factors and may be used with the ultimate goal to also improve welfare, litter, and air quality of poultry houses.

Additionally, the ability to beneficially modulate host physiology as an indirect effect of the modulation of the microbiome is a notable example of the importance in studying the interplay between the host, diet and its microbiome (Clavijo and Flórez, 2018; Sun et al., 2021; Blokker et al., 2022; Lobo et al., 2024). It is known that feed has a strong effect on the composition, diversity, and metabolism of the intestinal bacteria (Kers et al., 2018; Bernard et al., 2024), and therefore, on the feed-efficiency of the animals (Bernard et al., 2024; Petranyi et al., 2024). It has been demonstrated that through the modulation of the intestinal microbiome, PB enhanced the resilience against coccidiosis (Blokker et al., 2022) and infectious bronchitis (Lobo et al., 2024) in broiler chickens, while in laying hens, it improved performance, mortality, and overall resilience during the peak of egg production (Petranyi et al., 2024). Since feed formulation plays a role on the microbiome, it may as well influence the activity of feed additives.

For example, replacement of corn by wheat increases fiber content in the diets of broilers, that may impair the utilization of the dietary energy due to the presence of non-starch polysaccharides (NSP), and influence the intestinal homeostasis (Jiang et al., 2024). The fiber portion of the chicken diets, specially the soluble portion of fiber, plays an important role on the GIT development and function, such as gizzard development and activity, secretion of HCl, enzyme activity, and others, that ultimately enhance nutrient digestibility, performance, and overall intestinal health (Mahmood and Guo, 2020). Furthermore, dietary fiber is essential to the complex relationship between GIT bacteria and mucosal barrier function as low fiber content may damage the mucus layer and predispose the intestine to inflammation and proliferation of pathogenic bacteria (Mahmood and Guo, 2020). Borda-Molina et al., (2021) observed that wheat-based diets promoted several beneficial bacterial groups related to fiber degradation and increased the abundance of microbial genes related to biosynthesis of secondary and carbohydrate metabolites compared to corn-based diets. These authors suggested that in the presence of high fiber concentration, the microbiome needs to establish more intense interactions in order to degrade complex carbohydrates.

Therefore, in the present study, we hypothesized that the supplementation of glycan-based PB would modulate core microbiome metabolic pathways dependent on diet composition, and this effect would be translated into improved growth performance. The objective of the present study was to evaluate the supplementation of glycan-based PB to broiler chickens fed corn or wheat-based diets on the growth performance, ileal nutrient digestibility, uric acid (UA) concentration in the blood, litter characteristics, SCFA production in the caeca, and ileal and cecal microbiome.

Material and methods

Ethical statement

The trial was conducted at the Research Center for Animal Nutrition and Health (dsm-firmenich, Village-Neuf, France, F-68305) according to the official French guidelines for experiments with live animals. The animal ethical and welfare committees approved the proposed design of the study ensuring comfort and welfare of the birds. The study followed the experimental procedure 2 (Prevention and reduction of breeding stress by nutritional prophylaxis) from the project authorization using animals for scientific purposes N°26233. In this study, the procedure was classified with a moderate degree of severity.

Animals, treatments, and diet composition and feed manufacturing

A total of 512 one-day-old male broiler chickens (Ross) were supplied by a commercial hatchery (Couvoir de la Seigneurtière Orvia. 44116 Vieillevigne, France). On the day of arrival, the chickens were divided by weight into groups from which 16 birds were selected randomly to form a pen replicate in a way that all the pens had a similar average body weight at the beginning of the trial. The birds of each replicate were raised in a floor pen with wood shavings as litter and allocated into 4 dietary treatments, with 8 replicates/treatment, and a total of 32 pens of 2.25 m2. The experiment consisted of a completely randomized design, in a factorial scheme (2 × 2), with 2 types of diets (corn or wheat-based), and with or without the supplementation of PB at 0.9 kg/MT which provides 400 mg/kg active dry glycans (Symphiome®, dsm-firmenich, Kaiseraugst, Switzerland).

All the chickens were housed in an environmentally controlled room. The room temperature set at 32 °C for the first week and reduced by 1 °C every two days until the birds reached 36 d. In the first days an infra-red electric heating lamp was placed in each pen. Birds had free access to feed and water. Feed was provided in crumble form at the beginning of the trial (d 1-15) and afterwards in pellet form. The trial lasted for 36 days comprising a 15-d starter, 14-d grower phase and a 7-day finisher phase.

The composition of the basal diets is shown in Table 1. Basal diets included Ronozyme® HiPhos (dsm-firmenich, Kaiseraugst, Switzerland) and Ronozyme® MultiGrain (dsm-firmenich, Kaiseraugst, Switzerland) at 2,000 FTY/kg and 100 FXU/kg, respectively. Raw materials were ground in a hammer mill using a 3 mm sieve, and mixed with other ingredients (minerals, vitamins and amino acids) in a 500 L or 1500 L mixer. After mixing the feed was pelleted (3 × 25 mm) at about 74°C (controlled pelleting temperature) and feed samples were taken for analysis of the products activities. The determination of glucanase, xylanase and phytase activities in the experimental diets were performed by BioPract GmbH.

RONOZYME® HiPhos

With the goal of providing a mild intestinal challenge, coccidiosis vaccine (PARACOX 5) was administered through the feed on the day of arrival of the chicks at the recommended dose. Before use, the vaccine bottle was shaken vigorously for 30 s to allow the resuspension of oocysts. Paracox-5 was diluted in tap water at a rate of about 1,000 doses in 600 mL of water (0.6 ml per bird or 10 ml for 16 birds). A quantity of starter feed (200 g) was placed on a plate on the floor, and 10 ml of the vaccine solution was sprayed on top of the feed. The applicator tank was shaken regularly during spraying to avoid sedimentation of oocysts. When all of the treated feed had been consumed, the normal feeding program was reintroduced.

Measurements

Feed intake (FI) and body weight (BW) were recorded on d 1,15, 28 and 36 to calculate the body weight gain (BWG) and the feed conversion ratio (FCR), respectively for d 1-15, 15-28, 28-36 and day 1-36.

Intestinal content sampling

On d 35, 4 animals per pens were first stunned by electric shock and then sacrificed by cervical dislocation. After opening the coelomic cavity, the content of the intestinal segments was gently collected. Ileal digesta from 4 birds/pen (2/3 distal part of the ileum) for digestibility analyses, and ileal digesta and cecal content samples from 1 bird/pen for microbiome analysis were collected. The ileal digesta samples were pooled for all the birds from one pen. The samples were stored frozen at -20° until analysis.

Ileal digestibility analysis

The analyses of the nutrient content in the feed and digesta samples were performed according to standard methods (VDLUFA, 1976; AOAC, 2006). Crude protein was determined by a nitrogen analyser (FP 528, LECO, St. Joseph, USA) using the Dumas method (CP=N*6.25). Gross energy measurements were performed using an adiabatic bomb calorimeter (C 2000 basic, IKA, Staufen, Germany), and titanium dioxide concentration in feed and digesta were determined by Inductively Coupled Plasma-Optical Emission Spectrometry (ICP-OES, 5100 Dual View, Agilent) with an internal method based on DIN EN ISO 11885:2007 (AOAC, 2006) after sulfuric acid mineralization.

Litter quality assessment

The analysis of the litter was performed after litter collection from 5 distinct points within the pen in the areas avoiding the feeders and the drinkers and homogenisation of the different samples. A subsample of 10 g was solubilized in 100 mL of distilled water, and pH measurements were performed using a pH meter according to Brauer-Vigoderis et al. (2014). For the ammonium concentration determination, a subsample of around 8g of litter was mixed with 100mL of Tris buffer for 10-15 minutes and centrifuged for 15,000G for 15 minutes. The litter ammonium concentrations were then determined by a Biomedical automate COBAS 6000 (Roche Diagnostics, CH-4202 Basel) using the respective Roche Diagnostic kit (NH3L kit).

Blood uric acid concentration

On d 35, blood samples from two birds randomly chosen from each pen were collected in heparin tube from the jugular vein. The plasma concentrations of uric acid (UA) were determined by the mean of a Biomedical automate COBAS 6000 (Roche Diagnostics, CH-4202 Basel) using the respective Roche Diagnostic kits.

Cecal short-chain fatty acid concentration

The concentration of SCFA was analyzed at the Bioanalytic lab at dsm-firmenich, Kaiseraugst, Switzerland. Briefly, after a protein precipitation step to clean up the sample, the supernatant was incubated for derivatization reaction, in presence of O-benzylhydroxylamine hydrochloride (O-BHA) and N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N’-ethylcarbodiimidehydrochloride (EDC). The obtained substances were extracted by liquid-liquid extraction using dichloromethane. The extraction phase was then evaporated to dryness and reconstituted with methanol-water (50-50, v-v) and finally injected into the HPLC-MS/MS system. The detection was done with a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer equipped with electrospray ionization source, in a combined positive and negative mode. Quantification was done by applying dedicated external calibrations (using labeled internal standards). To assess the daily and long-term laboratory performance (accuracy and precision) of the method, standard and quality-control samples are analyzed daily with unknown samples.

Ileal digesta and cecal microbiome analyses

DNA Isolation, library preparation, and sequencing

DNA was extracted from 250 mg of intestinal content by using PowerFecal Pro DNA kit (Qiagen) according to supplier's recommendations, and the DNA concentrations were measured fluorometrically on a Qubit® 4.0 Fluorometer (ThermoFisher Scientific). DNA was fragmented to obtain uniform read lengths of 6-8kb, using Covaris g-TUBE. A total of 400ng of fragmented DNA was used as input for the library preparation. Sequencing libraries were prepared with the Ligation sequencing kit (ONT SQK-LSK109) and multiplexed using the Native Barcoding Expansion (ONT EXP-NBD196) as described by the manufacturer. Sequencing was performed on an Oxford Nanopore MK1C device, using the R9.4.1 (ONT FLO-MIN106D) flow cells.

Data analysis – taxonomic and functional annotation

Data processing, from raw sequencing reads to annotation count matrices, was done through in-house developed workflows. The workflows perform read quality control and removal of reads below 1kb with NanoFilt v2.8.0 of the NanoPack toolset (De Coster et al., 2018), taxonomy annotation with Kraken2 v2.1.2 (Wood et al., 2019) and gene annotation with minimap2 v2.22 (Li, 2021). The annotations were performed using a comprehensive chicken-specific microbial gene reference catalogue developed in-house for optimized sequence mapping, containing around 1,000 species and 9 million genes.

Taxonomic counts were normalized by the total number of reads per sample. Normalized taxonomic counts were used to compare the abundances of specific bacterial groups between the control and PB treated samples. Significant differences between taxonomic groups were evaluated using Maaslin2 (Mallick et al., 2021). Gene counts were normalized within each sample to obtain TPMs and grouped by EC number summing TPMs of all genes contributing to each EC number. Additionally, the EC number abundances were further normalized within each sample dividing by the total gene abundance of the ‘glycolysis/TCA Cycle’ KEGG Module (Bortoluzzi et al. 2023).

To explore the differences between microbiome profiles of treatments, random forest classifier models were used. The analyses were performed in R (R version 4.2.2) with the package ‘randomForest’ (version 4.7-1.1; dimensionality mtry = 2, number of trees = 10,000). As features for the models, the normalized EC values were used, following the same methodology as described in Bortoluzzi et al. (2023). The feature important lists were extracted with the most important metabolic reactions defined by the EC numbers for distinguishing the control and PB treated birds. Metagenomic clustering using the functional annotations was performed with a Local Fisher Discriminant Analysis and the EC number abundances. The Microbiome Protein Metabolism Index (MPMI) was estimated for each sample as described in Bortoluzzi et al. (2023).

Statistical analysis

Growth performance data, short and branch-chain fatty acid concentration, blood uric acid, ileal digestibility, and litter characteristics were analyzed by two-way ANOVA considering the main effect of PB, diet and their interaction (P < 0.05) by using JMP 16.0. Significant differences on MPMI between controls and PB treated samples were assessed using Kruskal-Wallis’ Test (P < 0.05).

Growth performance and nutrient digestibility

Results of the growth performance were summarized for the starter (d1-15), grower (d15-28), finisher (d28-36) and the entire experimental period (d1-36) in Table 3. Initial body weight at day 0 was 38.5 ± 0.11 g. During the starter and grower phases of the study, a significant better BWG (P = 0.0008 and P = 0.04, respectively) was observed in birds fed wheat compared to corn-based diets, and during the starter perid, FI was also higher (P = 0.003) in birds fed wheat-based diets. From 28 to 36 d of age, it was observed that the supplementation of PB increased FI (P = 0.05) by 59 g, and tended to improve the FCR (P = 0.06) by 4.3 points vs non-supplementated birds; wheat-based diets also tended to impair FCR (P = 0.06) vs corn-based diets fed birds. There was no interation nor main effect of diet and PB supplementation of the growth performance of the birds during the entire experimental period (0-36d).

Ileal and cecal microbiome of corn vs wheat-based diets fed broilers

A significant shift in the ileal microbiome metabolic profile of chickens fed corn vs wheat-based diets was observed (Fig. 3A). The main changed pathways are shown in Fig. 3B. The top five metabolic changes observed in the ileum of the birds were related to folate, arginine and proline metabolism, lipoic acid metabolism, amino sugar, and nucleotide sugar metabolism, and zeatin biosynthesis (Fig. 3B). However, no significant differences were observed on the MPMI (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, the cecal metabolic profile was also shifted in corn vs wheat-based diet fed broilers (Fig. 4A). The top five changed pathways were DNA replication, valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation, histidine metabolism, cyanoamino acid metabolism, and fatty acid degradation (Fig. 4B). There were no significant differences between the microbiome of corn vs wheat-based diet fed broiler for the MPMI (Fig. 4C).

Discussion

Within the frame of the present study, we sought to elucidate how PB affects growth performance and different characteristics of the GIT of chickens when supplemented to diets with inclusion of corn or wheat as the main cereal. At the end of the experimental period, the type of diet did not impact the growth performance of the animals. It is important to highlight, however, that all the diets were supplemented with carbohydrases and phytase. In the absence of these enzymes, one could expect a negative impact of the inclusion of wheat to broiler diets due to its higher concentration of antinutritional factors, such as phytate and NSP content when compared to corn-based diets (Veluri et al., 2024a). Furthermore, the inclusion of wheat by itself, increased the ileal digestibility of DM, N, and energy, improved litter quality, increased blood uric acid, and modulated the ileal and cecal microbiome. On the other hand, PB reduced blood uric acid concentration, modulated cecal metabolite production, shifted the ileal and cecal microbiome in both corn and wheat-based diet fed broilers, and numerically improved the performance of the birds. Although different publications have reported the beneficial effect of PB on the performance, GIT functionality, and cecal microbiome of chickens (Walsh et al., 2021; Bortoluzzi et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2023; Petranyi et al., 2024), this is the first study to demonstrate the influence of PB in diets formulated with corn or wheat as the main energy source on the functionality of the microbiome, cecal SCFA, blood UA, and ileal digestibility.

As presented herein, the type of diet did not affect the growth performance of the birds in the overall phase but increased the ileal digestibility of nutrients on d 36. Veluri et al. (2024a) reported that the full replacement of corn by wheat led to an improvement in the ileal digestibility of DM, N, and energy when compared to corn-based diets. This effect may be due to the improved morphological and enzymatic characteristics of the GIT promoted by wheat with consequent enhanced functionality (Shang et al., 2020; Veluri et al., 2024b). However, regardless of the inclusion of an enzyme product, the full replacement of corn by wheat led to lower growth performance (Veluri et al., 2024a). As presented herein, no interaction between diet and PB was observed. The positive effect of PB on the performance of chickens has been documented before (Jacquier et al., 2022; Blokker et al., 2022, Bortoluzzi et al., 2023; Lobo et al., 2024), regardless of carbohydrase supplementation (Jacquier et al., 2022), and despite the overall satisfactory performance of the flock (Yan et al., 2023).

Considering the microbiome modulation, dietary formulation itself, i.e., corn vs wheat-based diets, played an important role on the taxonomic composition as well as the functional profile of both ileal and cecal microbiome. Wheat based diets promoted an increase of Lactobacillus in the ileum (from about 35 to 65 % abundance), and Blautia in the cecal content (from about 8 to 13 %) when compared to corn-based diets. Additionally, significant changes in the functional profile between both diets were also observed, with a greater abundance of carbon and amino acid related pathways in the ileal and cecal microbiome, respectively, of wheat-based diet fed broilers. In a similar work by Borda-Molina et al. (2021) the type of cereal inclusion into the chicken's diet was the main contributor to the changes observed in the microbiome. For instance, dietary wheat inclusion promoted the proliferation of several families responsible for degrading complex carbohydrates, such as Lactobacillaceae and Bifidobacteriaceae (Borda-Molina et al., 2021). It is also important to highlight, that besides the differences in the carbohydrate profile between both types of diets, in the present study, the inclusion of wheat led to an increased inclusion of soybean oil of about 1 % in all diets, that may shift the microbiome taxonomic profile and abundance of functional pathways, as demonstrated by Jiang et al. (2024). These authors demonstrated that by incrementing soybean oil to the diet of chickens, an improved diversity of the intestinal microbiota was observed that also shifted the abundance of carbohydrate and lipid metabolism pathways (Jiang et al., 2024). Hence, it suggests that wheat-based diets promote a more complex microbial interaction in the intestine (Borda-Molina et al., 2021), that could influence the action of feed additives, microbial metabolite production in the caeca, and blood metabolites concentration.

For instance, it is known that UA is the main nitrogenous end product in avian species, and it is predominantly synthesized in the liver from purine metabolism and excreted (>90 %) through the kidneys (Harr, 2002). In birds, it is believed that its high blood concentration offers a potential protective mechanism against oxidative stress (Tsahar et al., 2006). Since UA is the main form of nitrogen (N) excretion in birds, its blood concentration varies considerably with the protein turnover/intake rate (Tsahar et al., 2006). It has been observed that in chickens fed low crude protein diets (17 %), the concentration of UA in the blood was decreased compared to 23 % CP fed broilers, and negatively correlated with blood ammonia concentration (Namroud et al., 2008). In the current study, chickens fed wheat-based diet had an increase in blood UA concentration of 13 % compared to corn-based diets (measured at d 36 when the CP content in both diet types was 18 %). This could be justified by the numerically higher FI in birds fed wheat-based diet (3,605 vs 3,537 g) and, therefore, N intake (calculated 80.9 (corn diet) vs 84.4 (wheat diet) g of N intake on a digestible N basis with a P = 0.02). Another point contributing to this observation could be that wheat-based diets contain more non-bound amino acids and less soybean meal than corn-based diets leading to amino acid imbalance (Liu et al., 2021). Similarly, Petrilla et al. (2018) found that wheat-based diet increased blood UA content, suggesting the superiority of corn in this regard (Liu et al., 2021).

On the other hand, PB supplementation reduced the blood UA concentration by 14 %, regardless of the dietary formulation, and without reduction in FI nor N intake. Therefore, the beforementioned rationale indicates that PB leads to better utilization of non-absorbed amino acids by the intestinal bacteria, reducing ammonia and other detrimental metabolites formation, and improves host protein absorption and utilization as seen by the higher MPMI in this and previous publications (Bortoluzzi et al., 2023, Yan et al., 2023). This effect could lead to better partitioning of amino acids post-absorption for functional or skeletal protein synthesis, for example. In another observation, by modulating microbiome pathways related to protein metabolism, PB diverted ammonia towards asparagine production (7-fold increase in the asparagine production in the cecal content); apart from this, it was suggested that PB also drives the detoxification of ammonia through the urea cycle of the microbiome (dsm-firmenich unpublished data). Therefore, without reducing FI, PB improves microbiome utilization of unabsorbed N that may lead to environmental benefits, such as reduction of ammonia emission as shown by Jacquier et al. (2022).

The supplementation of PB modulated the metabolic pathways of SCFA production (Walsh et al., 2021), and increased the production of SCFA (Bortoluzzi et al., 2023). In the present study, we observed a 20 % numerical increase of butyrate and valerate by PB when supplemented to wheat-based diets. The effects of PB relied mainly on the microbiome protein metabolism with many nitrogen-related pathways changed by its supplementation which increased MPMI in the ileal and cecal microbiome. It is known that certain bacterial groups metabolize non-digested protein and lead to the production of putrefactive substances while other bacterial groups will render the production of short and branched-chain fatty acids (BCFA) (Apajalahti and Vienola, 2016). Until recently the effects exerted by PB on the microbiome was believed to be mainly found in the caeca (Bortoluzzi et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2023) as also observed herein. However, a recent publication reported the effects of PB in other GIT compartments (Petranyi et al., 2024). In the current study, PB strongly modulated the ileal microbiome pathways that led to a dramatic increase in the MPMI when birds were fed wheat-based diets.

Overall, the type of diet did not influence the performance of the birds, even though wheat-based diets promoted better DM, N, and energy digestibility. Type of diet had an impact on the microbiome taxonomic composition of both intestinal sections, and therefore on the abundance of metabolic pathways. The supplementation of PB positively impacted the growth performance of the broilers in both corn and wheat-based diets, with slightly stronger effect on wheat-based diet fed broilers. Moreover, the supplementation of PB changed the abundance of core metabolic pathways of the microbiome, mostly related to protein metabolism, which led to a reduction in blood UA concentration and changed SCFA concentrations. In conclusion, the supplementation of PB beneficially modulated the microbiome of broiler chickens fed corn or wheat-based diets that translated into improved growth performance. Thus, it should be considered as an effective strategy for exploiting the full genetic potential of the intestinal bacteria and of broiler chickens.
