Readiness for telehealth integration in primary healthcare centers: a pilot mixed‑methods assessment from Lebanon’s REMOTE project
Authors: Emile Whaibeh, Jowy Abi Hanna, Lina Jaalouk, Hady Naal, Wael Al-Delaimy, Youssef Bassim, Elie Nemer, Elie Matta, Nada Najem, Lilian Ibrahim, Joseph Zgheib, Georges Abi Tayeh, Myriam Mrad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe healthcare system in Lebanon has faced numerous challenges due to recent crises, significantly impacting its ability to meet population health needs. One of the primary obstacles is the reduced capacity of individuals to seek healthcare services due to financial difficulties, transportation challenges, and the limited availability of providers in certain specialties or geographical areas. This is especially true for vulnerable groups and those living in rural areas where healthcare services are scarce and socioeconomic status is lower.AimThis study aims to assess the readiness of primary healthcare centers (PHCs) in Lebanon to integrate telehealth (TH) services to inform an evidence-based model for telehealth integration in resource-limited settings.MethodologyThe research assessed, from August to September of 2022, five PHCs managed by the Order of Malta, an organization that provides humanitarian and medical assistance across the country, serving vulnerable populations of all backgrounds, across 5 Lebanese governorates. This mixed-methods assessment involved a quantitative cross-sectional survey targeting beneficiaries and a directed content analysis of qualitative interviews conducted with PHC-affiliated healthcare providers, PHC personnel, and beneficiaries.ResultsFindings indicate that telehealth can reduce costs, improve accessibility, and decrease waiting time, addressing key barriers in the current healthcare system. Challenges such as power and internet outages, limited digital literacy, and infrastructural constraints were identified. Despite these obstacles, patients and staff demonstrated a positive reception to telehealth, especially for telemental health services.ConclusionTelehealth presents a scalable solution to enhance primary care in Lebanon’s low-resource settings. Future efforts should prioritize infrastructure upgrades, digital literacy training, and policy support to ensure sustainable implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14859-7.

Background

Introduction

Primary healthcare centers (PHCs), which are health units providing comprehensive packages of services at nominal fees to vulnerable Lebanese, migrants, and displaced populations, are struggling to maintain safe and accessible operations. Financial challenges are at an all-time high, with analysts claiming that Lebanon is currently facing its worst economic crisis since the end of its civil war in 1990. Unsurprisingly, the Lebanese healthcare system has been severely impacted by this crisis, and PHCs are no exception [1]. In fact, between August 2020 and November 2021, PHC consultations increased by 10 to 17% monthly, signaling an increased dependence from vulnerable individuals on PHCs [2]. However, access to healthcare services in Lebanon is hindered by the centralization of services in and around Beirut [3], limited patient awareness and health literacy, widespread societal stigma around essential services like mental and reproductive health services [4], and prohibitive service costs. In recent years, the healthcare system has been grappling with multiple intersecting crises: an influx of refugees, COVID-19, the August 4 Beirut Blast, and the ongoing economic collapse. These factors pushed an already overwhelmed healthcare system to its limits, especially in tandem with the brain drain of professionals, prioritization of more pressing responses, limited resources, as well as other challenges [5]. Therefore, it is crucial to vitalize primary care services by scaling up integrated community-based services.

One potential solution to respond to the aforementioned challenges is the adoption of telehealth (TH) – a modality of remote healthcare services provision relying on the use of information and communication technologies (ICT). PHCs are ideal for TH integration in Lebanon because they have the minimally required ICT, and their catchment areas cover rural governorates, which typically host underserved populations [6]. For instance, many PHCs in Lebanon are usually equipped with the tablets and computers, internet connection, and the IT personnel needed for the TH implementation [7]. For patients in rural settings, TH implementation offers them improved access to high-quality services, reduced wait time, work absenteeism, and transportation cost, and positive perception of clinician competency. It also protects from the disruption of care delivery in the event of nationwide events that can impede access to care. [8] In contrast, for PHCs and medical professionals, TH helps them overcome the issue of limited resources and improves the sharing of medical information between PHCs and physicians, hence reducing unnecessary referrals. [9] Moreover, TH eliminates the practitioners’ need to commute, allowing them to treat patients living in otherwise unreachable areas [10].

However, the implementation of TH in PHCs is not seamless. Issues such as potential regulatory concerns, infrastructural limitations including unreliable internet connection and outdated hardware, limited digital literacy among PHC staff and patients, associated resistance, and others may pose a challenge for the integration of TH [11]. Surprisingly, even though 80% of the Lebanese population owns a smartphone and 76% has access to the internet, TH use has been limited before the COVID-19 pandemic. [12] However, following COVID-19, the Lebanese Order of Physicians encouraged physicians to provide healthcare services remotely when deemed possible. [13] In 2020, Helou et al. explored how physicians adapted to the sudden need for TH and found a significant shift in their perceptions, indicating greater openness and willingness to adopt this model. [14] In 2022, a cross-sectional study by Shaarani et al. investigated the utilization of TH by Lebanese physicians during the COVID-19 pandemic and found that 75.8% used TH during the pandemic, and 40% started doing so because of the pandemic. [15].

Challenges pertaining to the current climate crisis also place extra pressure on the healthcare system. In addition to the increase in demand for healthcare services and the issues in infrastructure, the healthcare sector itself is a big source of greenhouse gas emissions, contributing in some countries to more than 5% of national emissions. [16, 17] Given these challenges, the healthcare sector finds itself in a critical dilemma: adapting to these challenges in an attempt to answer as many patients’ needs as possible, while at the same time, reducing its own environmental impact. [18] To tackle this issue, more innovative healthcare solutions, such as Telehealth, have become subjects of debate since they offer a more environmentally friendly approach to medical consultations. For instance, since TH removes the need for doctors and patients to commute from and to PHCs, TH can significantly cut down on the sector’s carbon emissions [2, 19], all while allowing PHCs to respond to their patients’ medical needs.

However, to date, and despite global interest of health facilities to shift towards adopting TH services, very little evidence exists on the best practices to integrate TH within PHCs in low-resource and conflict-affected settings such as Lebanon. This is likely due to the fact that Lebanon’s healthcare system remains fragile, still recovering from multiple crises (financial, social, and environmental) with limited resources at hand to develop and upscale its services, especially with regards to its primary healthcare network. For this reason, this study is part of the essential research needed to support innovative approaches to strengthen the healthcare system and related services in Lebanon. More specifically, this study represents the initial phase of a larger research project, focusing on the readiness of patients and medical professionals to adopt telehealth services in primary healthcare centers. Subsequent phases, which will examine the state of telehealth post-implementation in PHCs, will be addressed in future papers.

Telehealth services in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs)

Several recent studies and reports have examined telehealth in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region and other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). These studies focused on healthcare access for remote and disadvantaged populations, patient readiness to and satisfaction with telehealth services, as well as the TH benefits as perceived by patients and doctors. For instance, in their review of studies on telemedicine in LMICs during the COVID-19 pandemic, Mahmoud et al. highlighted the main benefits of introducing telemedicine in LMICs, most notably to maintain non-emergency healthcare, facilitate patient access to providers, and lower infection risk that may result from patient-provider contact [20]. Nevertheless, they noted that technological, regulatory, and economic challenges were found in most reviewed studies.

Another study conducted by Alhaidari et al. assessed the satisfaction of pregnant women with telehealth services during COVID-19 in Iraq [21]. Out of all the participants surveyed, more than half (57%) expressed their satisfaction with telehealth consultations, with only 7.9% being dissatisfied with the provided services. More importantly, however, the majority of participants (82.7%) intended to continue using TH services even after the pandemic, suggesting that TH may be a valuable addition to the traditional in-person consultations. The study also highlighted the pressing need to increase access to healthcare services in LMICs, even beyond the pandemic.

Similar results were reported by Murshidi et al., who conducted a cross-sectional study in Jordan to explore the knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions of Jordanians towards the usage and adoption of telemedicine [22]. The study found that half of the surveyed population (50%) was already aware of telemedicine, and more than two-thirds of the participants (68%) were willing to use TH services. In parallel, the study also showed that increased knowledge of and positive attitudes toward TH were proportional to having higher educational levels and living in urban settings, while participants living in the Southern parts of the country displayed poorer attitudes toward TH.

The present study

This research is a pilot project entitled “REvitalizing Medical and health service Offering via TElehealth (REMOTE),” and it aims to document and evaluate the process of integrating TH services into selected PHCs in Lebanon. The selected PHCs are located in the 5 main governorates in Lebanon, all operating under the same management of the Order of Malta – Lebanon, an apolitical humanitarian organization whose network includes 11 community health centers, mobile medical units, elderly daycare centers, and medical and dental laboratories, among others. [23] During this project, PHC personnel were trained and equipped with the knowledge and skills to operate new TH programs that focused on 4 health disciplines: reproductive health, non-communicable diseases, pediatrics, and mental health.

Objectives

The purpose of this study was to assess the readiness of patients, PHC support staff, and medical professionals with TH services. It aligns with global momentum to integrate TH services into health facilities, which is expected to improve access of populations to healthcare services and improve efficiency for the delivery of health services at system-level. The ultimate goal of this project, which includes research, implementation, and assessment phases, is to produce an evidence-based model for TH integration in PHCs in low-resource settings. Even though the current project focuses on the integration of TH within 5 PHCs in Lebanon and on 4 pre-defined health topics, the envisioned model accounts for scale-up potential at the level of healthcare system and for replicability in other contexts with similar cultural and development landscapes.

Context assessment

The first phase of the REMOTE project is to conduct a context assessment examining the readiness of patients, PHC support staff, and medical professionals to the integration of TH in PHC settings. This context assessment was conducted through a mixed-method approach, whereby multiple sources of data were collected, triangulated, and synthesized to overcome critical knowledge gaps in the literature and to inform subsequent implementations. Data was collected from PHCs through interviews with key PHC personnel (Supplementary File 1) to understand their capacities and readiness to adopt TH systems. In parallel, patients participated in interviews (Supplementary File 2) and completed a survey (Supplementary File 3) that examined the existing barriers to their healthcare access and their readiness to adopt telehealth services. The remaining stages of the study include evaluating the effectiveness of this program and utilizing the findings produced in the project to inform the development of the model and subsequent guidelines for TH integration in PHCs. These two stages are outside of the scope of the current study and will be the subject of future publications.

Methodology

Study design

This study follows a mixed-method multiple-case study design. The main goal of this research is to produce evidence that informs a model for future integration of TH in PHCs in low-resource settings, using Lebanon as a case. Therefore, a mixed-method multiple-case study design is the ideal approach, aligning with the study’s aim to examine the implementation of specific TH health focus in PHCs representing pre-defined settings in Lebanon. This design provides enough depth to investigate key aspects associated with this implementation from the context assessment leading up to the synthesis of findings and offers the opportunity to compare findings across health topics and settings of operations. Ultimately, this study provides a solid foundation from the generated evidence to support scaling up and replicating such programs in different settings.

To achieve this goal, this study was conducted in close coordination with implementers of the program through three key phases described above: contextual assessment, program evaluation, and model development. Each phase represents a milestone in this study because it informs (1) critical knowledge gaps in the literature both regarding the context of operations and regarding the experiences and reception of TH integration in PHC, (2) subsequent steps to be conducted in the program based on generated findings, and (3) the overall model.

Study setting

A total of 5 PHCs were targeted, each from one of the 5 main governorates in Lebanon: (1) Beirut and Mount Lebanon, (2) Akkar, (3) North, (4) South, and (5) Bekaa.

Study population

The study population included PHC personnel (administrative staff, nurses, social workers, managers, and clinicians) and patients seeking healthcare services from the target PHCs. Each of the 5 targeted PHCs was regarded as a single case.

Sample size

From each PHC, 5 PHC personnel representing the aforementioned positions and 5 beneficiaries were interviewed at each data collection time point. Therefore, 10 interviews per PHC (50 interviews in total) were conducted before implementation, and similarly throughout the rest of the data collection time points post implementation. Additional interviews were conducted on a case-by-case basis to ensure reaching data saturation.

Sampling methodology

In view of the nature of the present research, it using a multiple-case study design approach, representativeness was not sought in the sampling methodology. Instead, an in-depth approach was prioritized for selected PHCs with the goal of focusing on their operational structures, teams, and patient populations. That said, this research selected 5 centers under the management of one NGO-based PHC. The centers were identified, contacted, and screened to examine their eligibility. The 5 selected PHCs represent different Lebanese governorates and operate under a unified management. While we acknowledge that this may introduce potential selection bias, especially since all selected centers already have a baseline level of infrastructural readiness to engage with TH, we believe that these conditions are appropriate and necessary given the pilot nature of the intervention, even if they limit the generalizability of the results. Once screened and selected, PHCs were asked to provide written authorization to collaborate on this research and to sign a Memorandum of Understanding detailing their roles and rights in this study. Each center was required to identify a nurse, administrative staff, social worker, manager, and clinician to participate in interviews. PHC personnel were purposively selected to represent a variety of roles, while patient participants were approached using a convenience sampling method based on their willingness to participate, following a standardized recruitment script to minimize selection bias. It is important to note that although convenience sampling was used for beneficiaries, efforts were made to diversify the sample in terms of gender, age, nationality, and health conditions.

Data collection

Data collection procedures

Data collection was conducted between the months of August and September of 2022, and it involved quantitative patient surveys and semi-structured qualitative interviews with PHC personnel and patients. Upon selection of PHCs, one focal point from each PHC (the PHC manager) was identified, and they aided the research team in identifying and approaching the study participants.

Patient quantitative data

Quantitative data was collected from patients chosen from the 5 targeted PHCs. The purpose of this survey (Supplementary File 3) was to assess the patients’ baseline satisfaction with PHC services and their readiness for TH. The inclusion criteria are: (1) receiving services at the PHC, (2) being able to provide informed consent, and (3) being willing to complete the questionnaire, and exclusion criteria include: (1) being unable to communicate effectively due to severe impairments and (2) being unable to provide informed consent. Patients were approached by PHC staff and the research team and asked to participate in the research study in accordance with ethical principles.

PHC qualitative data

For the purpose of this study, qualitative data was collected from two sources: semi-structured interviews with PHC personnel and medical professionals (Supplementary File 1) and semi-structured interviews with PHC patients (Supplementary File 2). For each PHC, the PHC manager pre-identified the personnel that needed to be interviewed, and these individual interviews took place in a private room within the PHC. PHC personnel included administrative staff, nurses, social workers, managers, and clinicians. In total, 18 interviews with PHC staff and personnel were conducted, divided as follows: Ain Remmeneh (N = 4), Baraa (N = 3), Kefraya (N = 4), Kobayat (N = 3), and Roum (N = 4). The interviews aimed to investigate the readiness of staff to integrate TH in their operations, TH’s perceived usefulness and potential challenges, and the personnel’s recommendations on how to best implement this modality. Future phases, which focus on the post-implementation evaluation of telehealth in PHCs, will explore the experiences PHC personnel gained by coordinating, managing, and delivering the program. It is worth noting that, due to their unavailability on-site during the visits, some interviews with healthcare providers had to be conducted over the phone, reflecting the pre-existing physician availability challenges in PHCs. While we acknowledge that this modality leads to missing some non-verbal cues, it was still essential to gather insights from them as they are instrumental in the success of TH implementation in PHCs.

In addition to the PHC staff, beneficiaries were interviewed first in order to gauge their comfort with TH and willingness to use it. The total number of patient interviews was n = 37, divided into Ain Remmeneh (N = 8), Baraa (N = 7), Kefraya (N = 10), Kobayat (N = 8), and Roum (N = 6). For all qualitative data, the same analytical approach was used regardless of the mode of interview.

Data management and analysis

Data management

All collected data was protected to the fullest extent possible. For quantitative data, PHCs were advised on using data management software on password-protected computers, restricting access to data for eligible personnel only, and removing patients’ personally identifiable information such as names and phone numbers from datasets to be shared with the research team. In turn, and since data collection tools and mechanisms were standardized across PHCs, the research team combined on a regular basis the datasets retrieved from the PHCs in a master sheet. The datasets were cleaned upon reception and prepared for analysis.

As for the qualitative data, all interviews were audio-recorded should participants consent to it; otherwise, notes were taken instead. All recordings and notes were transcribed and presented on individual Word documents in preparation for data cleaning and qualitative analysis. The transcripts were also de-identified and anonymized.

Data analysis

Qualitative data was analyzed using a directed content analysis approach [24], guided by the OPTIC framework (Fig. 1). The OPTIC framework (Fig. 1) developed by Whaibeh and Mahmoud [25] to represent the pillars of TH practice: (1) Operating site characteristics, (2) Patient population, (3) Tele-clinician considerations, (4) Information and communication technology, and (5) Cultural considerations. Transcripts were first read to identify text segments relevant to the predefined categories. Two researchers independently coded a subset of transcripts to develop the preliminary codebook. Data coding was done using Taguette, a free open-source tool. New codes were added as needed, based on emergent insights. Discrepancies were resolved through discussions with an iterative refinement to the codebook. Themes were developed by grouping the codes. Trustworthiness was achieved by triangulating data sources (staff and patients), peer debriefing sessions, and maintaining an audit trail.

As a supplement to the qualitative findings, quantitative data was also analyzed and reported tata version 12.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Descriptive statistics were conducted to summarize the data, including frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and means, standard deviations, medians, and interquartile ranges for continuous variables. Scores for satisfaction and readiness were calculated by aggregating responses, and their distributions were reported both at the item level and as total scores. To assess normality, Shapiro-Wilk tests were conducted for the outcome variables, which confirmed their non-normal distribution. Accordingly, non-parametric tests were used for the bivariate analyses, including the Mann-Whitney U test for comparisons between two groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test for comparisons among multiple groups, and the Spearman’s rank correlation to assess associations between continuous variables. Variables which were statistically significant and/or conceptually important were then included in multi-variate quantile regression models to identify the predictors of patient satisfaction and telehealth readiness scores. These models were refined iteratively by removing non-significant variables to improve model parsimony and retain predictors that are statistically robust.

The use of multiple PHCs, triangulation of data sources, and the reliance on experienced and well-trained data collectors helped in the reduction of bias and the improvement in the reliability of the collected data.

Data quality control

In order to ensure high data quality, all personnel that were collecting or managing the data underwent training by the research team. PHC personnel were trained on administering the updated data collection tool that included questions on clinical and non-clinical outcomes to be routinely administered to patients and were trained on managing, de-identifying, and sharing this data with the research team. Similarly, members from the research team who were conducting interviews were trained on administering the semi-structured interview guide, probing during interviews, and providing a safe space for participants to share their experiences. All data collected from PHCs underwent data quality checking procedures such as back-checking to assess reliability, consistency, and identify potential issues with the tool and potential inconsistencies in data. Additionally, on-site supervision, and the use of standardized scripts for recruitment and data collection were employed as quality assurance measures.

Ethical considerations

This study first underwent an ethics review at the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Hotel Dieu de France (HDF) (Approval number: CEHDF2047). Once the study was cleared, all research participants and all points of the research activities were first approached informally by the research team or by PHC personnel to inquire about their potential interest in participating in research activities after briefing them about what the study consists of. Upon initial approval, the research team or the PHC personnel explained to the participants in detail why they were being contacted, what their roles and rights were, how their data would benefit them and their communities, and that it would be treated with confidentiality, privacy, and anonymity. Participants were also informed that their names would not be present in the data sheets circulating among team members and that their data would be de-identified and reported in aggregate forms. Participants who took part in semi-structured interviews were first asked about their consent to have the interview audio-recorded for the research team to not miss out on important information that they may have shared. Should participants not consent to being audio-recorded, the researcher team took notes instead. Finally, all research participants were informed about their right to withdraw from the study at any time and for any reason, without it compromising their ability to seek healthcare services at the given PHC or their ability to maintain their jobs at the PHC should they be PHC personnel. All participants were required to sign an informed consent form or indicate their written approval to participate in other anonymized ways such as not using their names or using pseudonyms.

Reflexivity and researcher positionality

The research team included medical doctors and trained public health researchers with experience in qualitative and quantitative research methods, in telehealth, and primary care service delivery. To minimize any potential power dynamics, data collection was led by individuals not affiliated with the PHCs or Order of Malta. Researchers maintained a reflexive stance, acknowledging how their backgrounds and perspectives might influence data interpretation. Detailed field notes and regular debriefings after every field visit were employed to promote consistency and enhance the rigor of the analysis. Transferability was enhanced by conducting interviews with diverse stakeholders across PHCs in five different governorates, using flexible probing to elicit rich, detailed descriptions of the study settings, participants, and contextual factors.

Study results

On the patient level

Patient survey

A total of 294 patients were approached to participate in the assessment, and 281 of them agreed to complete the survey (response rate: 95.6%). The participants were selected from 5 different PHCs across various Lebanese governorates: Ain Remmeneh PHC (N = 49), Kefraya PHC (N = 49), Kobayat PHC (N = 89), Baraa PHC (N = 44), and Roum PHC (N = 63). Sociodemographic characteristics of the surveyed population (N = 281)

The majority of participants attended the PHC at least twice in the past 3 months (~ 72% (N = 202)). Additionally, ~ 72% (N = 199) of interviewees claimed that they did not face any issues contacting or accessing the PHCs. In fact, the majority of patients (95.4%) visited the PHC with no prior appointment, relying solely on walk-in consultations. Most participants reach the PHC via public transportation (~ 62% (N = 173)), some rely on public transport (~ 24% (N = 65)), while others come on foot (~ 16% (N = 43)). When it comes to the main reasons for the PHC visits, the most common responses were to schedule follow-up visits (~ 75% (N = 211)) and receive prescriptions for the medications (~ 69% (N = 193)). In addition, most participants (~ 72% (N = 199) usually succeeded in receiving the medical services that they were seeking. Services from general physicians were the most sought after (~ 41% (N = 116)), followed by cardiologists (~ 23% (N = 65)) and pediatricians (~ 16% (N = 45)).

Participants who were not able to receive services at the PHC primarily reported that the sought-out specialist/doctor was not available (~ 27% (N = 76)) or that the services sought after were not provided by the PHC (~ 27% (N = 76)). In general, patients reported choosing their PHC because of the quality of the services offered (~ 76% (N = 214), the PHC’s accessible location (42% (N = 118)), and the availability of free services (~ 31% (N = 88)). Finally, 86.5% (N = 244) of participants were usually prescribed medication. The source of the prescribed medications varies: 52.7% (N = 149) of interviewees buy their medications from the PHC’s pharmacy, which are offered at a symbolic price while 41.3% (N = 117) reported that they get the medications from external pharmacies. Satisfaction scores of surveyed patients (N = 281) with current PHC services

When it comes to patient satisfaction with healthcare services, the 14-item satisfaction score demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.75). Table 2 shows that overall satisfaction among the 281 participants was high, with a total mean score of 39.8 ± 3 (median = 41, IQR = 38–42) out of a possible 42 points. Participants reported the highest satisfaction with cleanliness and orderliness of primary healthcare centers (PHCs) (98.2% satisfied, mean = 3 ± 0.2), the privacy and confidentiality during consultations (97.2% satisfied, mean = 3 ± 0.2), and the quality of relationship with healthcare providers (96.1% satisfied, mean = 3 ± 0.2). Operational hours also had high satisfaction levels, with 96.1% expressing satisfaction (mean = 2.9 ± 0.3). Additionally, participants were notably satisfied with the information provided by doctors (97.2%, mean = 3 ± 0.2) and ease of access to healthcare providers (88.3%, mean = 2.9 ± 0.4). Lower satisfaction was noted in waiting times for services (74.7% satisfaction, mean = 2.6 ± 0.7) and the availability of prescribed medication (73.7%, mean = 2.7 ± 0.6).

Because the satisfaction score was not normally distributed, we conducted a bivariate analysis to assess associations between satisfaction scores and socio-demographic and health-seeking behavior variables, using Mann-Whitney U test, Kruskal-Wallis test, and Spearman’s rank correlation. The full results of the bivariate analysis can be found in Supplementary File 5. Then, we employed quantile regression (median regression) to model the determinants of satisfaction with current PHC services (Table 3). This approach is robust to non-normal residuals and outliers and estimates the median of the outcome variable conditional on the covariates, making it well suited for skewed distributions. We included all the variables that showed significant or borderline associations in the bivariate analyses and iteratively removed non-contributing predictors based on model performance and parsimony.

Table 4 summarizes participants’ readiness to use TH based on their responses across 25 different survey items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (from “Strongly Disagree” to “Strongly Agree”). The 25-item readiness scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.95. Participants showed overall positive attitudes toward telehealth, reflected by an average total readiness score of 84.77 ± 24.6 (median = 90, IQR = 75–98) out of possible 125 points. The highest-rated item was related to participants’ willingness to inform friends and family about telehealth, with 75% either agreeing or strongly agreeing (mean = 3.91 ± 0.86). Items indicating telehealth could improve healthcare quality (74.7% agreement, mean = 3.81 ± 1.05) and access (75.7% agreement, mean = 3.84 ± 1.03) also scored highly. Most respondents agreed or strongly agreed that telehealth services are trustworthy (68%, mean = 3.69 ± 1), easy to use (69%, mean = 3.72 ± 1.04), and suitable and convenient for their healthcare routine (67%, mean = 3.67 ± 1.01). However, participants expressed concerns about needing to be overly cautious when using telehealth systems, reflected in a lower mean score of 2.8 ± 1.03 and only 23.8% agreement. Neutral responses were substantial for items related to whether telehealth would alter traditional healthcare services or interactions (approximately 30%), indicating participants’ uncertainty about potential disruptions.

In the final quantile regression model (Table 5), readiness to engage with telehealth was significantly associated with clarity of communication, site of care, and marginally with sex. Patients who reported receiving a clear explanation about their condition and treatment from PHC personnel and providers had a 10.5-point higher median readiness score. Compared to the Ain Remmeneh center, used as the reference site, median readiness scores were significantly lower among patients attending Baraa (-11 points, p = 0.001), Kefraya (-26 points, p < 0.001), and Kobayat (-15.5 points, p < 0.001), while no statistically significant difference was observed in Roum. Male participants and non-Lebanese participants had lower readiness compared to their female (-3.5 points, p = 0.056) and Lebanese counterparts (-3.5, p = 0.1); although these associations were not statistically significant. Overall, the model explained 30.6% of the variance in the readiness scores (Pseudo R² = 0.306).

Patient interviews

Findings from patient interviews indicate that patients largely chose their PHCs based on convenience and their relationship with PHC staff. One participant claimed that, “I live nearby in the area, and the services and doctors are both very good” (Patient 1 at Baraa PHC). Patients who are not Lebanese also commended the treatment and services that they receive from the PHC staff: “They make us feel like family, and not like we are from another country” (Patient 3 at Kefraya PHC). Additionally, many participants were happy with the cost of the services provided, though they did note that the prices recently increased due to the economic crisis: “It [the cost] is really good” (Patient 3 at Roum PHC); “I hope it [the cost] stays this way; it is acceptable” (Patient 1 at Baraa PHC); “It [the services] used to be for free, but now the amount is symbolic and reasonable” (Patient 2 at Ain Remmeneh PHC).

For many patients, commuting to the PHC is the biggest challenge, especially if they live in remote areas. As such, many expressed their interest in telehealth services as a solution for this problem: “We would love to have that [TH services]; for example, if I was in the mountains, I wouldn’t have to go down. It would be great” (Patient 1 at Ain Remmaneh PHC). Additionally, interviewees reported that wait times tended to be unpredictable and long, and that they could not always find the needed services and specialties at PHCs. Several participants believed, then, that TH can help mitigate these issues, as it allows them to seek external medical professionals, more specifically, specialties that are not available at the PHC, without having to wait for too long.

Data collected from patient interviews also shows that many participants believe that telehealth services can be very effective when used in specific medical fields, especially in psychology and psychiatry. In a country such as Lebanon, where mental illness is still seen as a taboo subject, many participants appreciated having TH as an alternative option to consult with a psychiatrist. In fact, one patient noted that “I have a divorced cousin who fell into depression, and here in the villages, when you tell people to go and see a doctor, they tell you that nothing’s wrong. So we did our best to convince her to see a doctor online here. She talked to him, and he gave her medicine and changed her lifestyle” (Patient 5 at Roum PHC).

However, while they were willing to use TH, many patients expressed a clear preference for in-person treatment, especially when it comes to earlier stages of contact with their providers. For example, one interviewee reported that TH “would reduce transportation costs, especially since I don’t drive. But I would prefer seeing the doctor face-to-face. I get scared a lot, so I like seeing the doctor face-to-face” (Patient 1 at Kefraya PHC). One interesting observation that was noted during the patient interviews was a lack of understanding of what telehealth services actually are. When asked about their knowledge of medical services provided remotely, several participants claimed that they were familiar with them, mistaking phone consultations for TH services: “My husband had a kidney infection, so he called a doctor here in the area. The center was closed, so she prescribed medicine for him, and we got it from the pharmacy” (Patient 1 at Ain Remmeneh PHC).

Healthcare personnel interviews

In their interviews, medical practitioners and personnel expressed a very positive attitude towards the implementation of TH at their respective PHCs. Many medical doctors were already familiar with TH, with some even using it daily in their private practices, citing transportation costs as the main reason why patients would choose TH over regular consultations: “The needs of the patient were the reason to start telemedicine. Fuel prices, medicine, and the consultation… they all add up to become a big sum of money for the patient to pay” (Doctor at Baraa PHC). Doctors who had previous TH experience explain that, although not all patients were accepting of TH in the initial stage –especially the elderly, the majority were highly satisfied with the services and booked their appointments months in advance: “We started with 2 to 3 patients, but now, they are booking appointments 2 months in advance” (Doctor at Baraa PHC). According to the interviewed healthcare providers, the overwhelming majority of patients stated that TH is effective and sustainable: “Regarding telemental health: 90% of the patients are satisfied with the service, while 10% prefer talking directly to the doctor” (Doctor at Kefraya PHC). Additionally, doctors and staff called for a wider adoption of TH services and the inclusion of a bigger variety of medical services provided, particularly in the field of pediatrics: “We wish to widen telemedicine services to other specialties. Lots of our patients are kids, and we are not always able to have a pediatrician on site” (Nurse at Kefraya PHC).

Nevertheless, some reservations were noted by the healthcare personnel regarding using TH in the consultation process. A few interviewees believed that TH is definitely helpful in the follow-up sessions, but face-to-face consultation is recommended for the initial checkup with the doctor: “It’s important that the patient gets checked/examined physically. In the case of consultation, not examination, it [TH] can be used” (Head nurse at Ain Remmeneh PHC). Unsurprisingly, some interviewees expressed their frustration with the logistics required for TH services, citing internet connection issues, regular power cuts, and the lack of the needed equipment as recurrent challenges: “The problem in the center is the internet connection. During winter/bad weather and days with long power cuts, we face some difficulties with the Wi-Fi. We use our 3G. We also have one laptop in the center, one computer, and one tablet, so we cannot take more than one patient at a time” (Nurse at Roum PHC). Overbooking TH services was also cited as a challenge for PHCs, as doctors and the equipment needed for TH are quite limited and may not be enough to answer all the patients’ needs.

Admin and supporting staff interviews

Findings from the interviews with PHC admin and supporting staff align with the opinions of healthcare personnel, highlighting the challenges as well as the positive attitude of PHC patients and staff towards TH. At the organizational level, PHC representatives and healthcare providers reported that the most common challenges faced at their centers included (1) being understaffed and under equipped, (2) experiencing long waitlists with reduced response capacity, (3) not having all the needed services available, (4) having poor internet connection in remotely located PHCs, and (5) being unable to facilitate transportation of all patients to their centers.

Regarding telehealth integration specifically, PHC representatives mentioned that their centers were largely willing and ready to adopt telehealth, claiming that the needed technology and platforms for TH were available at the center: “As an organization, we have the technology and platforms, we have tablets to record patients’ information” (Coordinator of the center at Roum PHC). At the same time, the reception of telehealth services by PHC patients has been largely positive, as explained by a social worker at Roum PHC: “Especially now that we are helping the patients cut the transportation fees, they no longer care about how they are seeing the doctor; they care about receiving the proper treatment, regardless of the means of communication” (Social worker at Roum PHC).

However, and similarly to previous concerns highlighted in the patients’ and medical professionals’ interviews, one admin worker at the Ain Remmeneh PHC pointed out the limitations of TH services, especially if used for first-time consultations. They believe that TH is more suitable for follow-up visits and mental health consultations: “It [TH] is good, but for the patients who need a physical examination, it’s a limitation. For example, for the psychologist or psychiatrist, TH can be effective, but for a patient that is suffering from pain, consultation cannot be done online. If it’s the patient’s follow-up visit and the doctor has all the needed information about the patient’s situation, then it can be done online” (Admin worker at Ain Remmeneh PHC).

In one of the centers where TH was being used occasionally by some providers in a limited capacity (as opposed to a full, formal, and organized integration), staff reported technical challenges stemming from resource limitations. TH requires better internet connection, better-suited equipment, and clearer workflow systems: “It’s been a year since we started offering telemedicine services at this center, but we suffer from power cuts and internet cuts. In these situations, we rely on our own mobile internet to make do” (Social worker at Roum PHC). Other interviewees mentioned how slow internet connection is hindering the overall workflow, causing them to waste too much time finalizing admin work: “Because of the weak connection in the villages, we are obliged to record the information on paper and then transfer it onto the platform when we get back to the center. It’s wasting our time, but we have no other choice” (Coordinator of the center at Roum PHC). This observation aligns with data collected from healthcare providers and patients who expressed interest in delivering and/or receiving treatment through telehealth.

Discussion

Telehealth practices have a strong potential to improve access to healthcare services, especially for populations living in rural and remote areas. However, these populations, especially in Lebanon, tend to face multiple barriers to engage in telehealth practices given the digital divide caused by limited digital literacy, infrastructural barriers, and several financial restraints. In addition, and as a result of the intersecting crises in Lebanon, access to healthcare has become a challenge to a large segment of the population. Issues related to transportation, finances, and the number of available medical personnel have exacerbated the state of healthcare in Lebanon. Crises related to climate change have also taken their toll on the healthcare sector in Lebanon, straining an already-burdened system further and creating more setbacks that hospitals and PHCs have to tackle.

The patient survey results reveal a diverse patient population with significant socio-economic challenges. Most participants were women (~ 62%), Lebanese (~ 80%), and aged between 18 and 49 years (~ 51%). The majority of participants (~ 82%) reported insufficient income to meet basic needs, emphasizing the financial barriers to healthcare access in Lebanon, especially for remote populations. In fact, the high engagement with PHCs, with approximately 72% of participants visiting at least twice in the past three months, indicates a strong reliance on these centers for healthcare needs. To many participants, PHCs are the only affordable option for medical services, especially after the Ministry of Public Health’s (MoPH) budget suffered severe cuts: from $486 million to just $37 million in 2022 due to the currency crash [26]. As such the demographic profile of the interviewed participants underscores the importance of affordable and accessible healthcare services.

Our multivariate analyses showed that patient satisfaction with PHC services was higher among patients who successfully secured appointments and lower among patients who had longer wait times at the center. Telehealth can act as a solution to these issues by improving patient access to specialized physicians. Moreover, it saves the patients the need of commuting to and from healthcare centers away from where they live to be seen by specialists. Several studies highlight TH’s benefit of greatly addressing transportation barriers for both practitioners and patients and even reducing issues like work absenteeism for patients who cannot risk losing their jobs [27, 28]. By removing the need for the specialist physicians to always be physically present at the PHC, patients can have access to a wider pool of specialized practitioners. Thus, TH enables patients to receive proper medical assistance and significantly reduce costs, allowing them to allocate those savings to other important areas of their medical care.

Our survey showed great readiness for TH, with most patients and providers believing it would improve the quality and accessibility of healthcare services, particularly in light of the socio-economic challenges in Lebanon. This aligns with other research conducted on patient and provider readiness for TH implementation–specifically in China [29], Iran [30], and the USA [31], to name a few. This readiness is essential for the successful implementation of TH, especially given the current barriers in accessing in-person care. However, concerns about altering traditional healthcare interactions and dealing with health problems suggest that patients value personal contact with healthcare providers –at least during the first stages of their treatment. Faced with this challenge, doctors can meet up with the patients face-to-face at the PHC for the purpose of the first medical consultation, then they can rely on TH services for future follow-up sessions with their patients.

When it comes to the various challenges faced by both patients and healthcare providers, interviews with patients, PHC staff, and clinicians highlight the following organizational issues: understaffing, unavailability of specialized healthcare providers, long waitlists, and poor internet connectivity. Challenges related to misconceptions about telehealth modalities also arose from our study surveys, highlighting the need for patient education prior to full-scale implementation. Nevertheless, similar issues were reported even in developed countries such as the USA, where populations living in rural areas have a much more difficult time accessing healthcare services than urban regions. According to DeHart et al., less than 12% of primary care physicians and 8% of specialized physicians offer their services in the rural areas of the USA, marking a huge discrepancy between the availability of services in urban areas and rural regions [32]. Other challenges related to the social aspect of TH (lack of public awareness), organizational difficulties (unavailability of staff and proper training), and technological setbacks (underdeveloped infrastructure, especially in rural areas) were also explored in numerous studies [33] on TH.

Fortunately, however, according to our study, the willingness and readiness of PHC representatives and healthcare providers in Lebanon to adopt telehealth are promising. This aligns with previous studies exploring the usage and implementation of TH in LMICs [34–36]. For instance, research [37] conducted on health professionals’ readiness to implement TH in their healthcare facilities in Ghana revealed that of all the factors usually affecting healthcare provider readiness towards TH, facility readiness and staff knowledge were the most statistically significant. Nevertheless, successful integration will require better infrastructure, equipment, and staff training, especially in a LMIC like Lebanon. Once the TH implementation is achieved successfully, however, PHCs and staff will definitely experience fewer issues, especially in relation to long waitlists and understaffing.

In summary, patients’ preference for PHCs based on convenience and relationships with staff underscores the importance of trust and accessibility in healthcare. Our results show that proper treatment from the PHC staff and the lack of discrimination against patients are essential components that patients consider before choosing a PHC to visit. In parallel, the main attractions of telehealth—easier appointment scheduling, reduced transportation burdens and wait times—align with patients’ needs. The data collected shows that most participants are willing to receive treatment remotely as long as it removes the burden of commuting to and from the center. However, the preference for in-person treatment, especially during initial contact, indicates that telehealth should complement rather than replace traditional care in some situations. This attitude was conveyed in both patient and doctor interviews. The overwhelming majority of medical professionals highlighted the advantages of telehealth services and expressed their willingness to implement TH within their practices. The interviewed doctors emphasized, however, that better internet connection and higher staff readiness are paramount to yield more promising results. A very important finding also emerged from the multivariate analysis for the readiness scores: the most robust predictor for patient readiness for telehealth was clarity of communication. Patients who receive adequate explanations about their conditions and treatment scored higher on readiness, a finding that was echoed in our qualitative interviews as well. This emphasizes the need for tailored implementation strategies that ensure effective patient-provider communication and more patient education initiatives to inform them about what TH entails, how it can be effectively used, its risks and benefits, and how they can access such services.

The current study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the use of self-reported measures introduces reporting bias, particularly in health surveys. Given the ongoing relationship among patients, staff, and medical professionals at the participating PHCs, participants may not have fully expressed their true opinions or experiences, possibly underreporting negative feedback or issues due to social desirability bias or perceived consequences related to their association with the center. Second, the sample size is limited to only five PHCs, one from each of the five governorates in Lebanon, all of which operate under the same management (Order of Malta). Moreover, for the quantitative patient surveys, a non-probabilistic convenience sampling, which restricts the generalizability of findings. Similarly, for the qualitative part, the interviewed study participants may not fully reflect the opinions of other healthcare providers in centers that operate under different management structures or in different regions. However, it is important to note that this study was designed as a baseline assessment pilot study to test the feasibility of TH integration in PHCs rather than to generate nationally representative estimates. Despite these limitations, the results suggest strong readiness and willingness among patients and healthcare providers to adopt TH in PHCs. Our context assessment results underscore the necessity to invest in the needed infrastructure and training in addition to developing supportive policies to ensure equitable, effective, and sustainable implementation. The current study shows that TH should not aim to replace but to complement in-person care in Lebanon through a hybrid model that optimizes provider reach and patient access. Further studies with larger and more diverse samples across different management models and regions are needed to validate and expand upon these findings at the national level.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is one of the first efforts to assess the readiness of PHCs in Lebanon to adopt TH services and evaluate the patient satisfaction with and willingness to adopt this innovative form of medical care. It also marks the beginning of an extensive research initiative, concentrating on the preparedness of patients and medical professionals for telehealth integration in primary healthcare centers. It combines quantitative and qualitative data to produce actionable insights that directly informed the design and deployment of the subsequent phases of the REMOTE project in Lebanon. By engaging with patients, providers, and PHC staff across 5 centers all over Lebanon, our contextual assessment is grounded in real-world practice, and it reflects the practical challenges and opportunities related to TH integration in Lebanon. While recognizing the considerable promise of telehealth, it is crucial to acknowledge potential drawbacks often encountered in LMICs such as Lebanon. These can include issues related to digital literacy, access to reliable internet and technology, and ensuring equitable access for all segments of the population. Nevertheless, our research highlights the considerable potential of telehealth to enhance healthcare accessibility and efficiency in Lebanon, particularly amidst socio-economic challenges and infrastructural limitations. Successfully integrating telehealth within PHCs requires addressing key barriers, including technological inadequacies, organizational challenges, and other challenges.

The readiness and willingness of both patients and healthcare providers indicate promising foundations for telehealth adoption in Lebanon, suggesting that strategic investments in infrastructure, policy, and training could significantly improve healthcare delivery. Ultimately, a balanced, hybrid approach that combines traditional and telehealth services can offer sustainable and equitable healthcare solutions for Lebanon’s diverse and underserved populations. By considering the socio-economic and environmental conditions of the healthcare system in Lebanon, it becomes evident that the adoption of telehealth services is a crucial –and logical– future step in the advancement of this sector. TH promises lower costs for the patients, more accessible services, shorter wait-times, and lower environmental impact. Finally, continued research on this topic is paramount to fully realize the potential of TH in improving the experiences and satisfaction of patients, staff, and medical professionals in Lebanon.
