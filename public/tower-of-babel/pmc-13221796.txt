Acquired Hemophilia A During Prasugrel Therapy After Recurrent Acute Coronary Syndrome
Authors: Shumpei Yao, Naoki Fujisawa, Kenichiro Otsuka, Joji Nagasaki, Takumi Kawase, Yosuke Takahashi, Daiju Fukuda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcquired hemophilia A (AHA), a rare autoimmune bleeding disorder caused by factor VIII inhibitors, may be overlooked in patients receiving antiplatelet therapies.Case SummaryA 60-year-old woman underwent percutaneous coronary intervention for non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, receiving aspirin and prasugrel. After recurrent unstable angina, repeat percutaneous coronary intervention was performed, and dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) was resumed. One month later, she developed diffuse ecchymoses and progressive anemia. Investigations indicated AHA with isolated activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) prolongation, reduced factor VIII activity, and factor VIII inhibitor positivity. DAPT was discontinued, and high-dose corticosteroid was initiated, causing complete remission; aspirin monotherapy was restarted. Unstable angina recurred, and coronary artery bypass grafting was performed.DiscussionUnexplained bleeding with isolated APTT prolongation on DAPT should prompt evaluation for AHA and early hematology consultation.Take-Home MessageRecognize AHA early; stop DAPT when safe, and favor revascularization strategies that minimize prolonged DAPT.

History of Presentation

A woman in her 60s presented with progressive subcutaneous bleeding, purpura, and ecchymoses involving the upper and lower extremities (Figures 1A and 1B). These symptoms developed 1 month after having resumed dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) with aspirin (100 mg/d) and prasugrel (3.75 mg/d) following repeat percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) for in-stent restenosis of the left anterior descending artery (LAD) (Figures 2A and 2B). Past Medical History

Eight months prior, the patient had undergone PCI with a drug-eluting stent (Ultimaster Nagomi; 3.0 × 24 mm) in the proximal LAD for non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (Figures 2A and 2B, Videos 1 and 2). DAPT with aspirin (100 mg/d) and prasugrel (3.75 mg/d) was initiated and continued for 4 months. Subsequently, aspirin was discontinued and prasugrel monotherapy was maintained, following current guidance permitting shortened DAPT followed by P2Y12 inhibitor monotherapy in selected patients to reduce bleeding risk.1 We administered a reduced-dose regimen of prasugrel (3.75 mg maintenance dose), which is approved in Japan.1

Six months after the index PCI, the patient presented with unstable angina. Coronary angiography revealed an in-stent restenosis in the same proximal LAD segment (Figures 2A and 2B). Optical frequency-domain imaging revealed significant luminal narrowing, with a layered neointimal pattern within the stent (Figures 2C and 2D, Video 3). Repeat PCI was successfully performed using a Hiryu Plus balloon (3.0 × 15 mm), followed by drug-coated balloon angioplasty with a SeQuent Please NEO (3.0 × 20 mm) (Figure 2D, Video 4). DAPT with aspirin and prasugrel was resumed. She had no history of bleeding disorders, autoimmune disease, malignancy, hepatic dysfunction, or coagulation disorders.

Differential Diagnosis

Initial laboratory evaluation revealed anemia with a hemoglobin level of 8.1 g/dL and a normal platelet count of 22.0 × 104/μL, excluding thrombocytopenia as the bleeding cause. There was no laboratory evidence of disseminated intravascular coagulation, and liver and renal function tests were within normal limits. Coagulation studies revealed a normal prothrombin time–international normalized ratio, with isolated activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) prolongation (∼44 s), suggesting an intrinsic pathway abnormality. A cross-mixing test was performed to determine the cause of the isolated APTT prolongation. Mixing and incubating the patient's plasma with normal pooled plasma did not correct the prolonged APTT, indicating an inhibitor pattern rather than a coagulation factor deficiency (Figure 1C).

Alternative causes of isolated APTT prolongation were excluded. Heparin contamination was considered unlikely given no heparin exposure and normal antithrombin III (107%; reference range: 80%-130%) and factor X (130%; reference range: 71%-128%) activities. Von Willebrand disease was excluded based on significantly elevated von Willebrand factor activity (260%; reference range: 60%-170%) and antigen levels (203%; reference range: 50%-155%), ruling out von Willebrand factor deficiency. In addition, lupus anticoagulant testing was negative, with a normalized ratio of 0.9 (negative: <1.2). These findings were diagnostic of acquired hemophilia A (AHA), most likely drug induced and temporally associated with thienopyridine antiplatelet therapy, particularly prasugrel.

Investigations

Further coagulation assays revealed significantly reduced factor VIII activity (7 IU/dL, 7%) and a detectable factor VIII inhibitor titer of 10 Bethesda units. Screening for lupus anticoagulants, anticardiolipin antibodies, and antinuclear antibodies was negative. Liver and renal function tests were within normal limits. Contrast-enhanced chest and abdominal computed tomography revealed no occult malignancy. Accordingly, no underlying malignancy or systemic autoimmune disease was found.

Management

Both antiplatelet agents were discontinued for approximately 1 month, and high-dose oral prednisolone (1 mg/kg/d) was initiated, resolving bleeding and normalizing coagulation. After the recovery of factor VIII activity and inhibitor disappearance, aspirin monotherapy was resumed, without bleeding recurrence.

Several months later, the patient developed recurrent exertional chest pain. Coronary angiography revealed complete occlusion of the proximal LAD stent with collateral circulation (Figures 2E and 2F). Given the repeated restenosis and contraindications to thienopyridine therapy, PCI requiring DAPT was considered high risk. Off-pump coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) using the left internal thoracic artery to the LAD was successfully performed, with no complications.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The total follow-up period was 18 months from the initial bleeding event. After initiation of corticosteroid therapy, factor VIII activity was gradually recovered, with APTT normalization. Factor VIII activity showed a stepwise recovery from 7 IU/dL (7%) at admission to the normal range by week 5 and remained elevated after. The factor VIII inhibitor titer decreased from 10 Bethesda units at diagnosis to 2 Bethesda units by week 4 to 5, reaching undetectable levels after 5 weeks and remaining negative throughout follow-up. APTT progressively shortened and normalized, remaining normal for >1 year after discontinuing corticosteroid therapy.

The patient experienced no recurrent bleeding or thrombotic events during follow-up. She remained asymptomatic after CABG, with preserved left ventricular systolic function on serial echocardiography. Follow-up coronary computed tomography angiography confirmed patent bypass grafts and stable cardiac function (Figures 2G and 2H). The patient continued aspirin monotherapy without recurrence of hemorrhagic symptoms.

Discussion

AHA is a rare autoimmune bleeding disorder caused by autoantibodies that neutralize factor VIII activity, with an incidence of approximately 1 to 1.5 cases per million individuals annually.2 Drug-induced AHA accounts for <10% of all cases and is reportedly linked to various medications, including penicillins, interferons, and antiplatelet or antithrombotic agents.1,2 In contemporary PCI practice, potent P2Y12 inhibitors (clopidogrel, prasugrel, and ticagrelor) are frequently used in high–ischemic risk settings.1,3 Although generally well tolerated, immune-mediated hematologic complications, including AHA, have been reported on rare occasions, possibly owing to drug-induced immune dysregulation triggering inhibitor formation.3,4

Previous reports have linked AHA to antiplatelet agents, such as clopidogrel (most common) and ticagrelor (less common).3,5 Clopidogrel shows a strong disproportionality signal for drug-associated AHA, whereas prasugrel shows no such significant associations. Moreover, limited case reports indicate that prasugrel-associated AHA is rare.2 Consequently, our case expands the spectrum of antiplatelet agents potentially linked to AHA, highlighting AHA consideration in unexplained bleeding and isolated APTT prolongation during antiplatelet therapy.

Guidelines recommend bypassing agents (rFVIIa/aPCC) or recombinant porcine factor VIII for clinically significant or life-threatening bleeding.6,7 In our patient, bleeding was extensive but clinically controllable, with high ischemic risk after recurrent acute coronary syndrome (ACS) and recent coronary stenting. Given the thromboembolic complications of hemostatic agents and the patient's residual factor VIII activity (7 IU/dL [7%]) with moderate inhibitor titer (10 Bethesda units), steroid monotherapy was deemed suitable.8 High-dose corticosteroids were prioritized for inhibitor eradication, with close monitoring and a plan to escalate to bypassing therapy or rpFVIII if bleeding worsened or became uncontrolled.8,9

Managing antiplatelet therapy represents a major clinical challenge in patients with coronary artery disease complicated by drug-induced AHA. We classified our patient as being at high bleeding risk according to the Academic Research Consortium for High Bleeding Risk (major criterion: acquired coagulation disorder).10 In our patient, antiplatelet agents were discontinued during active bleeding and reinitiated as aspirin monotherapy after normalizing coagulation parameters. However, given the recurrent ACS owing to in-stent restenosis and the contraindication to thienopyridine therapy, reintervention strategies requiring DAPT were considered high risk. Contemporary ACS guidance supports shortening DAPT and transitioning to single antiplatelet therapy in selected patients with high bleeding risk; however, we avoided rechallenge with P2Y12 inhibitors because prasugrel was the suspected drug, and we continued aspirin monotherapy.1,3 Because a repeat PCI strategy may require renewed DAPT, CABG was selected to achieve durable revascularization, minimizing dependence on prolonged DAPT for a major bleeding disorder. Long-term aspirin use is recommended as standard antiplatelet therapy after CABG.

Conclusions

This case highlights the significance of considering drug-induced AHA in patients presenting with unexplained bleeding and isolated APTT prolongation, despite initial attribution to antiplatelet therapy. Early multidisciplinary collaboration between hematologists, cardiologists, and cardiac surgeons is essential for timely diagnosis, bleeding management, and selection of appropriate revascularization strategies.
