Literacy-sensitive sexual and reproductive health education for adolescents in Ghana: Evidence from a school-based quasi-experimental study
Authors: Jacqueline Nkrumah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study examined how the complexity and format of sexual and reproductive health (SRH) education materials influence adolescents’ functional SRH literacy and its implications for literacy-sensitive SRH education.Study designQuasi-experimental study design.MethodsThis school-based quasi-experimental study was conducted among 317 adolescents aged 11–15 years (grades 7–8) in the Effutu Municipality. Schools were cluster-assigned to standard text, simplified text, picture-enhanced text, or comparison conditions. Participants engaged in six weekly guided reading and facilitated discussion sessions. Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 and STATA 17.0, applying regression-based difference-in-differences (DiD) and fixed-effects models.ResultsOf the 317 adolescents assessed at baseline, 249 completed the intervention. The DiD results showed significant improvements in functional SRH literacy for simplified text (12.28 points; 95% CI: 6.79–17.77; p = 0.001) and picture-enhanced text (10.26 points; 95% CI: 4.37–16.14; p = 0.001), while the standard text showed no significant change. The fixed-effects estimates confirmed similar gains for the simplified (13.87 points; 95% CI: 7.33–20.42; p = 0.001) and picture-enhanced materials (13.06 points; 95% CI: 6.94–19.17; p = 0.001). Although pictorial supports improved textual comprehension, their contribution to numeracy-related literacy was limited and, in some school contexts, elicited sociocultural resistance.ConclusionThe findings illustrate the pedagogical value of simplifying SRH educational materials and support literacy-sensitive school-based health promotion strategies. They also emphasize the need to consider sociocultural context when designing and implementing age-appropriate SRH education, particularly in underserved settings.

Introduction

Adolescents' sexual and reproductive health (SRH) literacy, adapted from Nutbeam's concept, refers to the ability to access, understand, and apply SRH information [1] and is critical for informed decision-making and the adoption of protective health behaviors. [2,3] Low SRH literacy can increase adolescents' vulnerability to misinformation, hinder informed use of SRH services, and contribute to poorer sexual and reproductive health outcomes. [4] SRH literacy also reflects the extent to which health and education systems communicate developmentally and linguistically appropriate information to adolescents. [5,6]

In Ghana, policy frameworks such as the Adolescent Reproductive Health Policy, [7] the National HIV and AIDS, STI Policy, [8] and the Adolescent Health Policy and Strategy [9] endorse multisectoral collaboration and school-based delivery of SRH education. The Ghana Education Service has also expressed commitment to comprehensive sexuality education (CSE) as part of efforts to strengthen adolescent health education. [10] However, implementation of school-based SRH education among adolescents aged 11–18 years has faced challenges, including sociocultural resistance, misinformation, and ongoing debates surrounding the content and scope of sexuality education. These challenges have contributed to gaps between policy commitments and effective delivery of age-appropriate, comprehensive SRH education within school settings.

A major barrier to effective comprehensive sexuality education (CSE) is the limited physical and linguistic accessibility of SRH materials. [11,12] Textbooks and other educational resources are rarely adapted to adolescents’ literacy levels, with most written above the comprehension abilities of students in underserved communities. [13] National and sub-national level assessments show that most Ghanaian adolescents aged 10-14 read below grade level, [14,15] which limits their capacity to engage with SRH content. Although simplified and picture-enhanced educational materials have been shown to improve health literacy in other contexts, [16,17] evidence of their effectiveness within SRH education in Sub-Saharan Africa is limited. [18,19]

A major evidence gap remains regarding the use of literacy-sensitive sexual and reproductive health (SRH) educational materials to support adolescents' functional SRH literacy in sub-Saharan Africa. This study addresses this gap by examining how SRH educational materials of varying textual complexity are associated with adolescents' functional SRH literacy. Specifically, the study aimed to compare changes in functional SRH literacy and textual and numerical comprehension among adolescents exposed to the three educational formats. The findings provide practical insights into the design of literacy-sensitive SRH educational resources and inform strategies for strengthening school-based SRH education, particularly for adolescents with lower literacy skills in underserved settings.

Methods

Study area, design, and population

The study was conducted in Effutu Municipality in Ghana's Central Region, selected due to high adolescent sexual activity (>60%), low awareness of modern contraceptives (27%), [20] and limited access to literacy-sensitive SRH information. [21] Effutu has a population of 83,695, including 20,433 adolescents aged 10–19 across four sub-municipalities (Winneba East and West, Essuekyir-Gyahadze, and Kojo-Beedu North), and 50 Junior High Schools. Using a school-based quasi-experimental design, the study examined how literacy-sensitive SRH materials influenced functional SRH literacy among in-school adolescents within routine educational settings.

Definition of variable

Functional SRH literacy was operationalized based on Mancuso's conceptualization of health literacy [22] as the level at which young adolescents can read, process, and understand available SRH educational materials, including numerical information, needed to make informed SRH decisions. Although health literacy encompasses interactive and critical dimensions, this study focused on functional health literacy, which assesses adolescents' ability to read, process, and understand SRH information in educational materials.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible participants were adolescents aged 11–15 years who were enrolled in grades 7 or 8 and resided in the Effutu Municipality. Participants were required to pass all three screening assessments (foundational literacy, visual acuity, and color perception), obtain parental consent, and provide written assent.

Sample determination and size

The study was carried out in the Effutu Municipality and included all four sub-municipalities. Thirteen communities were conveniently selected based on the presence of a basic school, their location within the same geographical area to facilitate the clustering of schools, and approval from school management. Nearby schools in each sub-municipality were clustered to curtail contamination between study groups. Five communities and schools were selected from Kojo-Beedu North, three each from two communities in Winneba East and Winneba West, and four communities and schools from Essuekyir/Gyahadze. Details of schools and adolescents included in the study are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

A target sample size of 408 adolescents aged 11–16 was projected during the preparatory stage to ensure adequate representation across study groups and to account for anticipated attrition in a school-based intervention. The sample size estimation was informed by a prior health literacy intervention study assessing changes in HIV- and ARV-related knowledge. [23] The sampling also followed established approaches for comparing proportions between groups. [24] It assumed a 5% level of significance (Zα/2 = 1.96), 80% statistical power (Zβ = 0.84), and expected proportions of participants demonstrating knowledge gains in the previous literacy intervention study (intervention group = 0.32; comparison group = 0.061). [23] Premised on the above specifications, the minimum required sample was estimated at 92 participants per group. This figure was adjusted for an expected 10% dropout rate, resulting in a planned allocation of 102 adolescents per group and a total projected sample of 408 participants.

Adolescents underwent a three-stage screening process before enrolment into the intervention. First, foundational literacy was assessed using a 526-word passage adapted from the Progress in International Reading Literacy Study (PIRLS), accompanied by ten comprehension questions. [25] Five numeracy items were also drawn from the Trends in the International Mathematics and Science Study. [26] The 25-min assessment was administered during school hours with approval from school authorities. Scoring followed Ghana's Basic Education Certificate Examination (BECE) grading system, with aggregates 1–2 (70–100% of total marks) representing higher foundational literacy and aggregates 7–9 (0–49% of total marks) representing lower foundational literacy. These categories were used to characterize participants at the two extremes of foundational literacy for subsequent analyses. The second and third stages of the screening included visual acuity and color deficiency screenings conducted by a practicing ophthalmic nurse. Fig. 1 in the supplementary file explains adolescents' participation across study phases.

Intervention materials

A simplified SRH educational resource developed by the author was used as the intervention, material [27] informed by an assessment of SRH literacy needs among in-school adolescents in the Effutu Municipality. [15,16] Existing SRH content from digital and print sources was compiled into a synthesized standard text, which was subsequently simplified and adapted to align with adolescents’ cognitive and reading levels. The development process and validation outcomes have been published elsewhere. [28] Two literacy-sensitive formats were produced: a simplified text and a picture-enhanced version.

The simplified text achieved a mean Flesch Reading Ease Score (FRES) of 82.0, indicating easy readability, compared with 66.0 for the standard text. All three formats, standard, simplified, and picture-enhanced, contained six core lessons covering pubertal changes, sexual activities, abstinence, friendship, sexually transmitted infections, and contraceptive methods. The standard text served as the comparison material (see Supplementary Table 4 for the readability indices).

Data collection tools

The test of Functional Literacy in SRH (TOFL-SRH), developed by the author, was used to assess adolescents’ ability to read and understand SRH information at baseline and post-intervention. Existing instruments were not purposeful, prompting the development of a multiple-choice cloze-based tool, a validated approach for measuring sentence and passage-level comprehension, [29,30] as applied in TOFHLA [31] and OHL-AQ. [32] TOFL-SRH comprised background, literacy, and numeracy components. Development involved randomly selecting eight of the twenty topics from the intervention material to reduce respondent burden. Thirty-five cloze items were constructed from a 682-word passage using rational deletion, including five numeracy questions requiring menstrual cycle calculations based on a two-month calendar. Two parallel forms (TOFL-SRH0 and TOFL-SRH1) were developed. TOFL-SRH1 was identical to TOFL-SRH0 except for the order of the items, which was reshuffled to minimize recall bias. Revisions were guided by Expert reviews and pretesting with 76 adolescents. The pretesting demonstrated acceptable reliability (α = 0.70 and 0.85 for TOFL-SRH0 and TOFL-SRH1, respectively).

Intervention and data collection

A three-stage process was followed for the intervention. First, baseline data were collected employing TOFL-SRH0 by the author and trained research assistants (18–25 min). Second, each sub-municipality and its selected communities and schools were treated as a cluster to reduce crossover and contamination. Sub-municipality/clusters were assigned to four conditions: Winneba East served as the comparison group and continued with routine school activities without receiving the study intervention; Winneba West received the standard text (ST1); Essuekyir/Gyahadze received the simplified text (ST2); and Kojo Beedu North received the picture-enhanced text (PT).

Educational materials were shared one week in advance to ensure transparency and to build parental and school familiarity. Third, weekly peer-supported reading sessions were conducted for six weeks. Adolescents read materials at home and participated in gender-segregated groups of 4–20 during 1-h sessions, involving 40 min of guided reading and 20 min of questions and discussions. In each school, a focal teacher was appointed to coordinate participation. Sessions were facilitated by the author and ten trained near-peer research assistants (five male, five female, aged 21–25 years) using trainer versions with expanded explanations to encourage discussion. The author facilitated sessions in one cluster, conducted at least one monitoring visit to each school during the intervention, and held weekly meetings with the research assistants to review implementation and address challenges. The program ended in week six, followed by end-line assessment in week seven using TOFL-SRH1 (≤25 min). To minimize facilitator bias, implementors were rotated during end-line data collection. Implementation occurred between May and July 2022, with follow-up data collected in September 2022.

Data processing and analysis

Participants' sociodemographic characteristics and functional SRH literacy outcomes were coded and processed using SPSS. Functional SRH literacy scores were calculated by assigning one point for each correct response. Percentage scores were calculated by dividing the total number of correct responses by the number of items and multiplying by 100. Scores were categorized as inadequate (0–59%), moderate (60–74%), or adequate (75–100%). Scores were further disaggregated into textual and numerical components to examine domain-specific gains in comprehension from baseline to endline. Data entry and cleaning were performed, and statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 and STATA version 17.0.

Changes in functional SRH literacy and comprehension outcomes were evaluated using regression-based difference-in-differences (DiD) and fixed-effects linear regression models. The DiD approach compared changes over time between intervention arms and the comparison group, while the fixed-effects model accounted for stable participant- and group-level differences that could influence literacy outcomes, providing conservative estimates. To examine literacy retention beyond the intervention period, a linear mixed-effects model was applied to end-line and 8-week follow-up data from 215 participants. The model included fixed effects for group, time, and group-by-time interaction, with a random intercept for each participant to account for repeated measures. Finally, multivariable linear regression was used to assess associations between participants' demographic characteristics and functional SRH literacy outcomes. A formal assessment of the parallel trends assumption was not feasible because only one pre-intervention measurement was available, which is a limitation of the study.

Participant retention and attrition

Attrition occurred after the baseline assessment, decreasing from 317 to 249 participants, and was driven primarily by school-level withdrawal rather than individual participant dropout. In three schools, participation ceased following concerns raised by some teachers regarding selected SRH topics, particularly condom and sexuality-related content. Baseline equivalence in functional SRH literacy across the four study arms (n = 317) was assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test, which showed no significant differences (χ2(3) = 5.932, p = 0.115), indicating comparable baseline SRH literacy. Missingness was attributable to school-level withdrawal, and analyses were based on complete cases without imputation of missing data.

Discussions

Improving adolescents' SRH literacy is essential to informed decision-making and healthy behavioral choices. This study demonstrates that how SRH information is presented plays a critical role in shaping adolescents’ ability to understand and apply health information, highlighting the importance of literacy-sensitive resource design beyond simply making SRH information available. While 317 adolescents were enrolled, the school-level withdrawal resulted in 249 completing the intervention. These withdrawals reflected sociocultural resistance, stigma, and religious opposition to SRH education in Ghana and similar contexts, as documented in prior studies. [33,34] However, evidence consistently supports the importance of open, age-appropriate SRH communication during adolescence, [35,36] supporting the need for sustained stakeholder engagement to normalize SRH education in schools.

Similar patterns of change in functional SRH literacy were observed across the DiD and fixed-effects analyses. Although participant attrition should be considered when interpreting the findings, the consistency of results across these analytical approaches lends support to the observed intervention-related improvements. While post-intervention trends in functional SRH literacy and the textual comprehension sub-domain were modest, the DiD interaction terms suggested that improvements in the intervention groups exceeded these underlying learning trends. Participants exposed to simplified and picture-enhanced texts consistently showed larger, statistically significant gains across both analytical approaches, with similar patterns observed in each analysis.

While simplified texts improved both domains, gains in numeracy-related SRH understanding were more limited among the picture-enhanced group. One possible explanation is that the picture-enhanced menstrual cycle chart required participants to simultaneously interpret multiple visual and numerical cues. These included colors, day numbers, symbols, and cycle phases, which may have increased cognitive processing demands compared with the conventional calendar used in the simplified text materials. Alternatively, numeracy-related SRH concepts may have remained challenging for adolescents with weaker foundational numeracy skills. These explanations were not directly examined and warrant further investigation. These findings have practical implications for the design of picture-enhanced SRH educational materials. Visual elements should be carefully integrated with numerical information to minimize unnecessary cognitive processing demands when explaining concepts such as menstrual cycles, contraceptive effectiveness, and the use of percentages or likelihoods to communicate pregnancy risk.

Consistent with earlier research, [17,37,38] picture-enhanced materials supported comprehension. However, in sensitive SRH contexts, visual representations should be culturally appropriate, as images depicting sexual or reproductive concepts may be perceived as inconsistent with local social and cultural norms, potentially limiting their acceptability. Foundational literacy remained an important predictor of functional SRH literacy, suggesting that although simplified and picture-enhanced materials supported comprehension across literacy levels, adolescents with stronger foundational reading skills continued to derive greater benefit. [39,40] This finding shows that scaling up literacy-sensitive SRH interventions may require complementary efforts to strengthen foundational literacy, particularly for adolescents with very low literacy skills. Although retention patterns were similar across intervention groups over the eight-week follow-up period, further research is needed to determine how literacy-sensitive SRH communication strategies can best support the long-term retention of functional SRH literacy. Future studies should examine whether repeated exposure to such communication strategies enhances retention over time.

Policy insights

This study has practical implications for adolescent SRH policy and programming in Ghana and similar contexts. Although national policies support comprehensive sexuality education, implementation varies across schools and communities because of sociocultural resistance, religious concerns, and misinformation. The findings suggest that policy attention should move beyond improving access to SRH information to consider how that information is designed and delivered within school settings. Simplified and picture-enhanced materials were associated with better functional SRH literacy, highlighting the value of age-appropriate and literacy-sensitive educational resources, particularly in underserved communities. In resource-constrained settings, these findings may help guide investment toward literacy-sensitive educational materials that produce greater improvements in adolescents' functional SRH literacy. The limited gains in numeracy-related comprehension also highlight the need to strengthen the communication of numerical SRH concepts through literacy-sensitive educational materials.

Study limitations

The study experienced a 21% dropout between baseline and end-line, with an additional 14% attrition at the 8-week follow-up, potentially reducing statistical power and increasing the risk of Type II errors, particularly in the standard-text group. Attrition was influenced by ethical constraints, stigma, and cultural beliefs surrounding adolescent SRH, which also affected participant engagement. In addition, differences in the distribution of public and private schools across study arms may have introduced residual confounding despite the analytical adjustments employed. The study was also conducted in a single municipality, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings with different educational and sociocultural contexts. Although the author developed the intervention materials and led data collection, standardized intervention procedures, validated assessment tools, trained research assistants, and the rotation of implementors during end-line data collection were used to reduce potential bias. Future studies should prioritize early stakeholder engagement, parental involvement, and community sensitization, while using more balanced study groups and multiple or matched control groups to better account for contextual influences and strengthen causal inference.

Conclusion

This study examined how SRH educational materials of varying complexity influence functional SRH literacy among Ghanaian adolescents. The findings demonstrate that the design and presentation of SRH information are important for supporting adolescents' functional SRH literacy. Although sociocultural concerns contributed to attrition, which may have reduced statistical power, simplified and picture-enhanced texts were associated with improved comprehension. These findings support the use of age-appropriate and literacy-sensitive educational materials in school-based SRH education. Future research should examine how the content, format, and delivery of SRH information influence comprehension, retention, and informed decision-making.

Ethical statement

Written approval was obtained from the Municipal Education Service. Educational materials were developed with input from education and health authorities, academics, thematic experts, and basic school pupils. Written permission was obtained from school heads, informed consent from parents, and written assent from adolescents. Participation was voluntary, and participants could withdraw at any time. Each school designated a focal teacher to support study coordination and participant welfare. Although the study posed minimal risk, participants with concerns could speak privately with the focal teacher, who referred issues requiring attention to the head teacher where appropriate. Facilitators discussed study issues during weekly briefing meetings, and the author monitored implementation through school visits. Schools and parents received the intervention materials one week before implementation. Ethical approval was granted by the Ghana Health Service Ethics Review Committee (GHS-ERC006/10/21).
