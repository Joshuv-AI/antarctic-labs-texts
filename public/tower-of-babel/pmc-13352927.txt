Effects of different exercise modalities and doses on arterial stiffness in individuals with overweight and obesity: a systematic review and Bayesian dose–response meta-analysis
Authors: Leiming Di, Mingming Huang, Xinbi Zhang, Qinlong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveArterial stiffness (AS), an independent cardiovascular risk factor, is elevated in individuals with overweight or obesity. Exercise is a key non-pharmacological intervention, yet the comparative efficacy and optimal dose across modalities remain unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of different exercise types and doses on AS in overweight and obese populations.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Web of Science, EBSCO, Cochrane Library, and Embase identified randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published up to August 2025. Bayesian network meta-analysis and dose–response analysis were conducted using Stata 17.0 and R software.Results40 RCTs (n = 2,064) were included, covering five exercise modalities: aerobic exercise (AE), resistance training (RT), combined AE and RT (CT), high-intensity interval training (HIIT), and HIIT + RT. Compared with controls, AE (SMD = − 0.81), RT (–0.48), HIIT (–0.87), and HIIT + RT (–0.95) significantly reduced arterial stiffness, whereas CT showed a borderline reduction (SMD = − 0.39, 95% CI: − 0.79 to 0.00). HIIT + RT ranked highest in efficacy, followed by HIIT, AE, and RT, with CT ranked lowest. Bayesian dose–response analysis demonstrated a nonlinear L-shaped association, with a minimum effective dose of 92 MET-min/week and the overall benefit approaching a plateau at approximately 670 MET-min/week. Modality-specific plateau ranges were approximately 720 MET-min/week for AE, 390 MET-min/week for HIIT, and 550 MET-min/week for HIIT + RT. No significant dose–response relationship was identified for RT or CT.ConclusionsExercise improves arterial stiffness in individuals with overweight or obesity. AE, HIIT, and HIIT + RT showed favorable effects, although the apparent superiority of HIIT + RT should be interpreted cautiously because the available evidence remains limited. The findings support the use of modality-specific dose ranges, rather than a single overall dose estimate, to inform individualized exercise prescription. Further large-scale direct randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-026-01737-2.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of mortality worldwide [1]. Arterial stiffness (AS) is an established independent risk factor for CVD and has gained increasing attention as an early and modifiable biomarker [2]. Elevated AS reduces arterial elasticity, alters pressure wave propagation, disrupts hemodynamics, raises blood pressure, and increases cardiac workload, thereby substantially heightening cardiovascular risk [3, 4]. Because AS reflects subclinical vascular changes preceding overt CVD, it provides a valuable biomarker for early detection and risk stratification, in addition to serving as a promising target for preventive interventions.

Overweight and obesity, typically defined by body mass index (BMI) with region-specific cut-offs [5, 6], are strongly associated with increased AS and early vascular impairment [7–9]. People living with overweight or obesity exhibit significantly greater central and peripheral AS than those with normal weight, and the severity of stiffness rises progressively with BMI across all age groups [10, 11]. Given the global rise in obesity prevalence—affecting more than 800 million adults worldwide—improving AS in this high-risk population is a critical step toward mitigating cardiovascular risk [12]. In this context, AS offers a practical, non-invasive tool for monitoring vascular health and guiding targeted interventions in individuals with overweight and obesity.

Exercise is widely recognized as a cornerstone non-pharmacological strategy for improving cardiovascular health and reducing AS [13, 14]. However, findings regarding aerobic exercise (AE) [15], resistance training (RT) [16], high-intensity interval training (HIIT) [17], and combined interventions (CT) remain inconsistent [18]. These inconsistencies are largely attributable to methodological limitations, small sample sizes, and the predominance of pairwise comparisons, which hinder a comprehensive evaluation of different exercise modalities. Conventional pairwise meta-analyses are limited to direct comparisons between two interventions and cannot simultaneously integrate evidence across multiple exercise modalities. In contrast, network meta-analysis enables the combination of both direct and indirect evidence, allowing for a more comprehensive comparison and ranking of different exercise strategies.Moreover, although the benefits of exercise are well established, the optimal dose—defined by frequency, intensity, and duration—for maximizing improvements in AS remains unclear. Most trials have not systematically quantified dose–response relationships, and existing meta-analyses have rarely addressed this issue. Taken together, these gaps underscore the need for more comprehensive evidence to clarify both the comparative efficacy of different exercise modalities and the dose–response relationship in individuals with overweight or obesity.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the comparative efficacy of various exercise modalities and doses on AS among people living with overweight or obesity by performing a Bayesian network meta-analysis (NMA) combined with a dose–response meta-analysis (MBNMA). To our knowledge, this is one of the first network meta-analysis to integrate both modality-specific and dose-related effects on AS across overweight and obese populations of all ages. The findings are expected to inform evidence-based, personalized exercise prescriptions, enhance vascular health, and ultimately reduce cardiovascular risk in this growing high-risk population.

Methods

Registration

This systematic review and NMA were conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses statement guidelines [19]. The study protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews ID: CRD42024582698.

Literature search strategy

This study was conducted using a systematic search of electronic databases, including PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and EBSCO. Controlled vocabulary terms from MeSH were applied in PubMed and the Cochrane Library, while Emtree terms were used in Embase. The search covered the period from database inception to June 2024, and an updated search was conducted in August 2025 to capture newly published studies.

The search strategy was developed according to the PICOS framework and combined terms related to exercise, overweight/obesity, arterial stiffness, and randomized controlled trials. Exercise-related terms included “exercise”, “physical activity”, “aerobic exercise”, “resistance training”, “strength training”, “high-intensity interval training”, “interval training”, “combined training”, and other relevant free-text terms. Population-related terms included “obesity”, “overweight”, and “obese”. Outcome-related terms included “arterial stiffness”, “pulse wave velocity”, and “PWV”. Study design terms included “randomized controlled trial”, “randomized”, “placebo”, and “clinical trial”. These terms were combined using Boolean operators (AND/OR), with database-specific adaptations as appropriate. The detailed search strategies for each database are provided in Appendix 1.

According to the PICOS framework, the population included individuals with overweight and/or obesity; the interventions included aerobic exercise, group exercise, combined exercise, high-intensity interval training, and stretching; the comparator was no exercise intervention or light stretching; the outcome was pulse wave velocity (PWV); and the study design was randomized controlled trials (RCTs). In addition, the reference lists of relevant reviews and included studies were manually screened to identify additional studies that were not captured through the electronic search.

Inclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) a randomized controlled trial (RCT); (2) subjects were individuals with overweight and/or obesity, defined by the following criteria: overweight as a BMI greater than 25 kg/m² and obesity as a BMI of 30 kg/m² or more in European populations; overweight as a BMI of 24 kg/m² or more and obesity as a BMI of 28 kg/m² or more in Asian populations, with no age limit; (3) the experimental group underwent at least 2 weeks of structured exercise training, in order to exclude acute single-bout exercise studies while retaining short-term repeated training interventions [20]; (4) the control group had no exercise intervention; and (5) the study assessed PWV before and after the exercise intervention, with no restrictions on the method of PWV measurement.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria included: (1) repetitively published studies, literature reviews, letters to the editor, conference abstracts, studies reporting only the acute effects of a single bout of exercise, and animal studies; (2) combinations of exercise interventions with other interventions (e.g., combined diet and exercise interventions that included both a control group and an exercise group without calorie control); (3) non-English language publications; and (4) studies lacking detailed PWV data.

Literature screening and data extraction

Two reviewers independently screened the literature, extracted data, and cross-checked the results. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer. For missing or unclear data, study authors were contacted whenever possible to obtain additional information. During study selection, titles and abstracts were initially screened to exclude clearly irrelevant articles. Subsequently, full texts of potentially eligible studies were assessed to determine inclusion. Data extraction included the following variables: first author, country, publication year, participant characteristics (sample size, gender, and age of each group), details of the intervention (type of exercise, duration, frequency, and session length), and outcome measures. In addition, PWV outcomes were extracted as originally reported and were further classified according to measurement type and vascular territory. For studies that reported the outcome only as “PWV”, the original full text was re-examined, and the measure was classified as central/aortic or peripheral/regional PWV based on the reported measurement pathway, device description, and vascular territory assessed. In the included randomized controlled trials (RCTs), exercise interventions were categorized into five types: (1) AE – aerobic exercise; (2) RT – resistance training; (3) CT –combined resistance and aerobic training; (4) HIIT – high-intensity interval training; and (5) HIIT + RT – combined HIIT and resistance training. Data coding and management

Intervention strategies were systematically categorized into four hierarchical levels. At the first level, interventions were classified as either exercise or control. At the second level (modality level), exercise interventions were categorized according to their primary modality, including AE, RT, CT, HIIT, and HIIT + RT, with the control group retained as a separate category.

At the third level, interventions were further classified according to the specific exercise type performed, including elastic-band training, bodyweight- or machine-based resistance training, walking, cycling, swimming, ball games, and placebo or usual-care conditions used in control groups. At the fourth level, intervention codes were generated by combining the specific exercise type with its corresponding exercise dose. Exercise dose was defined as total energy expenditure and was calculated from exercise duration, frequency, and intensity, expressed as metabolic equivalent task minutes per week (MET-min/week) [26, 27].

Age-appropriate Compendia of Physical Activities were used to assign MET values: the 2024 Adult Compendium for participants aged 19–59 years [26], the Older Adult Compendium for participants aged ≥ 60 years [28], and the Youth Compendium for participants aged < 19 years [29], where applicable. Weekly exercise dose was expressed as MET-min/week. For single-component interventions, dose was calculated as: weekly dose = MET × active exercise minutes per session × sessions per week.

For multi-component interventions (e.g., HIIT or circuit-based resistance training), dose was calculated as: weekly dose = Σ (MET_i × active exercise minutes_i per session) × sessions per week,

where each active exercise component was coded separately according to the reported modality and intensity. To avoid overestimation of exercise dose, all interval time in HIIT and circuit-based resistance training was excluded from the dose calculation. Accordingly, only net active exercise time was included, while warm-up and cool-down periods were also excluded. If session duration increased progressively during the intervention, the mean active exercise duration across the entire intervention period was used. If a study did not report the exact session duration, we imputed the mean duration for that exercise modality based on the included studies.

To improve network connectivity and enable dose–response network meta-analysis, weekly MET-min values were mapped to prespecified dose nodes of 0 (control), 300, 600, 900, 1200, and 1500 MET-min/week. These prespecified dose categories were selected as a pragmatic balance between preserving an interpretable dose gradient, maintaining sufficient data within each category, and ensuring network connectivity; narrower categories were considered likely to result in sparse nodes and reduced model stability, whereas broader categories might obscure potentially important dose-related differences [30–34]. A worked example is as follows: if an adult aerobic exercise intervention was assigned a value of 7.3 MET, and participants exercised for 60 min per session at a frequency of 3 sessions per week, the weekly dose was calculated as 7.3 × 60 × 3 = 1314 MET-min/week, which was then mapped to the nearest prespecified dose node of 1200 MET-min/week.

Exercise-type assignments, and extracted dose data are provided in Appendix 2. Using dose information in this way is consistent with methodological work on incorporating dose effects into network meta-analysis and on using dose–response model-based network meta-analysis to strengthen network connectivity [31].

Risk of bias and GRADE assessment

Two reviewers independently assessed the risk of bias in the included studies using the Cochrane Risk of Bias (ROB) tool, which evaluates seven domains: (a) random sequence generation; (b) allocation concealment; (c) blinding of participants and personnel; (d) blinding of outcome assessment; (e) incomplete outcome data; (f) selective reporting; and (g) other sources of bias. Given the nature of exercise interventions, blinding of participants is often not feasible in practice. Therefore, in line with methodological practices in previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses of exercise interventions [5], participant blinding was excluded from the overall ROB judgment. Instead, blinding of outcome assessors was regarded as a key quality control criterion. We then assigned an overall ROB rating for each study. Studies were rated as having low risk of bias if no domain was judged as high risk and no more than three domains were rated as “unclear risk.” A study was considered to have moderate risk if only one domain was rated as high risk, or if four or more domains were rated as unclear risk with none rated as high risk. All other cases were classified as high risk of bias [35].

The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework was applied to assess the certainty of evidence for both the primary and secondary network estimates [36].

Statistical analysis

Network meta-analysis

To minimize the influence of baseline differences, effect sizes were calculated using the changes in means and standard deviations from pre- to post-intervention. The standard deviation (SD) of change scores was estimated using formulas recommended in the Cochrane Handbook (version 6.3) [37]. Following the PRISMA extension for network meta-analyses [19], a random-effects model within a frequentist framework was applied in Stata 17.0 to pool standardized effect sizes and calculate corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) [38], assessing the impact of different exercise interventions on AS. Given the variability in devices used to assess AS, the standardized mean difference (SMD) was used as the summary effect size. Network plots were generated to illustrate the structure of direct comparisons between interventions. In these plots, the width of connecting lines corresponds to the number of trials, and the size of each node reflects the sample size. Loop-specific inconsistency was assessed by calculating inconsistency factors and their 95% CIs [39]. An inconsistency model was first applied to evaluate global inconsistency; if no significant inconsistency was detected (P > 0.05), a consistency model was adopted for further analysis [40]. To compare the relative effectiveness of interventions, the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) was calculated. SUCRA values range from 0% (least effective) to 100% (most effective), indicating the probability that each intervention ranks best [41, 42]. Funnel plots were used to examine potential publication bias and small-study effects.

Bayesian dose-response meta-analysis

A MBNMA model with random effects was employed to examine the dose–response relationship between exercise interventions and arterial stiffness [43]. No violations of key assumptions in network meta-analysis—including network connectivity [44], consistency, and transitivity [45, 46]—were detected (Appendix 9). Given the heterogeneity of devices used to measure arterial stiffness, SMD were used as the effect size, and 95% credible intervals (CrIs) were calculated to quantify uncertainty.

Various candidate models—including the Emax function, restricted cubic spline, quadratic, and nonparametric models—were compared to identify the best-fitting dose–response relationship between exercise dose and PWV. The Emax model yielded the lowest DIC and was therefore selected as the primary model for modeling the non-linear dose–response relationship within a random-effects framework (Appendix 10).

To assess the expected range of outcomes in future trials, 95% prediction intervals (PIs) were also computed. All MBNMA and dose–response analyses were conducted using the “MBNMAdose” package in R, while dose–response curves were visualized using the “ggplot2” package to enhance interpretability and presentation quality.

Other analysis

To assess the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses were performed by sequentially excluding studies with high risk of bias, small sample sizes (N < 20), and short intervention durations (< 4 weeks). The < 4-week threshold was selected as a more conservative criterion to test whether the main findings remained stable when very short-term interventions, which may reflect early functional vascular responses rather than more stable chronic vascular adaptations, were excluded.

Meta-regression

To further explore potential sources of heterogeneity, additional exploratory analyses were performed. Random-effects meta-regression models were used to examine mean age and mean BMI as continuous study-level moderators. In addition, a subgroup analysis according to geographic region (Asia vs. Non-Asia) was conducted, using region as a study-level proxy for population-specific BMI classification frameworks. These analyses were based on exercise-versus-control comparisons and were intended to assess whether study-level population characteristics might influence the observed intervention effects on arterial stiffness.

Results

Literature search results

The study selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1. The initial database search yielded 6,365 records (6,358 from databases and 7 from reference lists). After removing 1,774 duplicates, 4,591 records were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 4,542 were excluded based on predefined eligibility criteria, and 49 full-text articles were assessed for inclusion. Among them, 21 were excluded with documented reasons, resulting in 28 studies being included. An updated search identified 2,525 additional records. After removing 572 duplicates, 1,953 records remained for screening. Following title and abstract screening, 1,892 were excluded, and 61 full-text articles were retrieved for assessment. Of these, 49 were excluded for specific reasons, and 12 additional studies were included. In total, 40 RCTs met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis.

Characteristics of included studies

A total of 40 RCTs were included, involving 2,064 participants with a mean age range of 10 to 79 years. The most frequently investigated intervention was AE (21 studies), followed by RT (13 studies) and HIIT (13 studies). CT was evaluated in 9 studies, and HIIT + RT in 5 studies. Exercise frequency generally ranged from 2 to 5 sessions per week, while session duration varied across interventions. Outcome assessment methods were heterogeneous across studies, encompassing central/aortic PWV measures (e.g., cfPWV, cPWV, aPWV, and Aortic PWV), peripheral/regional PWV measures (e.g., baPWV, crPWV, rPWV, faPWV, ftPWV, pPWV, and left/right baPWV), and estimated PWV measures (e.g., ePWV). Several studies reported the outcome only as “PWV”; for these studies, the original full texts were reviewed to determine whether the measure more appropriately reflected central/aortic or peripheral/regional arterial stiffness. The detailed classifications are provided in Appendix 2.

Risk of bias and quality of evidence

A total of 24 studies were assessed as having a low risk of bias, 12 studies were rated as having a moderate risk of bias, and 4 studies were deemed to have a high risk of bias. Detailed risk of bias assessments for each study are provided in Appendix 3. Appendix 8 presents the results of the GRADE quality assessment for each pairwise comparison and for the SUCRA-based ranking of interventions. Overall, the certainty of evidence for most comparisons was rated as moderate to low, while the certainty of evidence supporting the SUCRA rankings was rated as moderate. These findings suggest a relatively high degree of confidence in the network estimates.

Inconsistency test

Global inconsistency was assessed using loop inconsistency tests, inconsistency models, and node-splitting methods. The loop inconsistency test showed good agreement across all outcome measures. The inconsistency model results indicated that all outcomes had P-values > 0.05, indicating no significant inconsistency and supporting the use of a consistency model for analysis. Node-splitting analysis showed that the direct comparison between CON and AE remained significantly inconsistent (P = 0.013), whereas all other comparisons showed no evidence of significant inconsistency. Thus, although the overall network was considered acceptable, the AE effect estimates should be interpreted with some caution. (Appendix 5).

Combining effect size analysis and ranking results

Compared to the non-exercise control group, AE significantly reduced arterial stiffness (SMD = − 0.81, 95% CI: − 1.09 to − 0.53, P < 0.01), as did RT (SMD = − 0.48, 95% CI: − 0.85 to − 0.11, P < 0.05), HIIT (SMD = − 0.87, 95% CI: − 1.21 to − 0.53, P < 0.01), and HIIT + RT (SMD = − 0.95, 95% CI: − 1.58 to − 0.33, P < 0.01). In contrast, CT showed a marginal trend toward reducing arterial stiffness (SMD = − 0.39, 95% CI: − 0.79 to 0.00, P = 0.05), although the evidence was not sufficiently robust to support a clear effect (Fig. 3).

Bayesian dose-response meta-analysis results

Overall dose-response relationship of exercise

As shown in Fig. 6, exercise demonstrated a nonlinear L-shaped dose–response association with arterial stiffness. The minimum effective dose was estimated at 92 MET-min/week (SMD = − 0.41, 95% CrI: − 0.76 to − 0.05). The effect size increased progressively with increasing exercise volume and appeared to reach its maximum at approximately 670 MET-min/week.

Dose-response relationships of the five exercise modalities

As shown in Fig. 7, AE, HIIT, and HIIT + RT demonstrated nonlinear L-shaped dose–response associations with arterial stiffness, while no significant dose–response relationship was identified for CT or RT. For AE, the minimum effective dose was estimated at 92 MET-min/week (SMD = − 0.41, 95% CrI: − 0.85 to − 0.02), with the curve approaching a plateau at approximately 720 MET-min/week. For HIIT, the initial effective dose was 73 MET-min/week (SMD = − 0.43, 95% CrI: − 0.92 to − 0.01), and the response gradually plateaued around 390 MET-min/week. HIIT + RT showed a higher minimum effective dose of 110 MET-min/week (SMD = − 0.57, 95% CrI: − 1.22 to − 0.02), with the beneficial effect leveling off at approximately 550 MET-min/week. By contrast, no statistically significant dose-related effects were observed for CT or RT, as the 95% credible intervals remained overlapping with the null across the evaluated dose range.

Sensitivity analysis of the network meta-analysis

To evaluate the influence of studies with high risk of bias, small sample sizes (N < 20), and short intervention durations on the intervention effect, sensitivity analyses were conducted by sequentially excluding these studies. The results indicated that neither statistical significance nor SUCRA rankings were substantially altered, suggesting that the overall findings of this study are robust and reliable (Appendix 7).

Meta-regression

Mean age was further examined as a potential source of heterogeneity using a random-effects meta-regression model. Among the 40 included articles, 34 studies provided sufficient data, contributing 39 exercise-versus-control comparisons. The results indicated that mean age was significantly associated with intervention effect size (β = -0.0101 per year, 95% CI: -0.0156 to -0.0046, P = 0.0003), suggesting that the beneficial effect of exercise on arterial stiffness tended to become greater with increasing age. After inclusion of age as a moderator, the between-study variance was τ² = 0.0173, while residual heterogeneity remained significant (QE = 114.68, df = 37, P < 0.001). Age explained approximately 4.9% of the between-study heterogeneity.

Geographic region was also examined as a potential source of heterogeneity, using Asia versus Non-Asia as a study-level proxy for population-specific BMI classification frameworks. The pooled effect estimate was Hedges’ g = -0.4967 (95% CI: -0.7824 to -0.2110) in the Asia subgroup and Hedges’ g = -0.8695 (95% CI: -1.2050 to -0.5339) in the Non-Asia subgroup. These findings indicate that exercise was associated with improved arterial stiffness in both regional subgroups, with a numerically larger effect observed in the Non-Asia subgroup. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because geographic region was used as a study-level grouping variable rather than a direct measure of BMI classification or ethnicity.

Mean BMI was additionally examined as a potential moderator in 32 exercise-versus-control comparisons with directly usable BMI data. The regression coefficient was β = 0.0141 per kg/m² (95% CI: -0.0546 to 0.0829), suggesting that BMI was not a significant effect modifier of the exercise intervention effect in the present dataset. The corresponding meta-regression bubble plots and subgroup analysis plot are presented in Appendix 13.

Discussion

This network meta-analysis evaluated the relative effectiveness of various exercise interventions on arterial stiffness, with SUCRA rankings used to assess comparative efficacy. The results indicated that all exercise modalities—except for CT—significantly improved arterial stiffness. Among them, HIIT + RT showed the highest probability of being the most effective (SUCRA = 81.8%), followed by HIIT (79.3%), AE (72.7%), and RT (36.4%), with CT ranked lowest (29.2%).

Figure 6 showed a nonlinear L-shaped dose–response association between exercise and arterial stiffness, indicating that vascular benefits emerged at relatively low exercise doses, increased progressively with greater exercise volume, and then approached a plateau. In the overall analysis, the minimum effective dose was estimated at 92 MET-min/week, whereas the maximal benefit appeared to be reached at approximately 670 MET-min/week. These findings suggest that even relatively small amounts of exercise may produce measurable improvements in arterial stiffness, while additional benefit beyond the moderate-to-higher dose range may become less pronounced.

Modality-specific analyses further indicated that this nonlinear pattern varied across exercise types (Fig. 7). AE, HIIT, and HIIT + RT all showed significant L-shaped dose–response relationships, although their plateau ranges differed, with the curves approaching a plateau at approximately 720 MET-min/week for AE, 390 MET-min/week for HIIT, and 550 MET-min/week for HIIT + RT. By contrast, no significant dose-related effects were identified for CT or RT. Therefore, the overall estimate of 670 MET-min/week should not be interpreted as a universal optimal dose for all exercise modalities; for practical purposes, the modality-specific dose ranges shown in Fig. 7 are likely to be more informative.

Finally, sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the results. Excluding studies with high risk of bias, small sample sizes (N < 20), or short intervention durations did not substantially alter the main outcomes.

Comparison with other studies

Previous network meta-analyses based on randomized controlled trials involving individuals with obesity have primarily focused on metabolic indicators such as body weight, glycemic control, or blood pressure [21, 47, 48]. In contrast, the present study emphasizes the effects of different exercise modalities (e.g., HIIT and CT) on arterial stiffness, an important but underexplored cardiovascular risk marker. Arterial stiffness is increasingly recognized as a valuable parameter for assessing and managing cardiovascular risk, particularly among people living with overweight and obesity. Elevated arterial stiffness has been consistently associated with a higher likelihood of experiencing cardiovascular events in this population [49, 50].

In the ranking of intervention strategies, HIIT + RT emerged as the top-performing modality; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because it was supported by only a small number of studies and relied predominantly on indirect evidence. Although no meta-analyses or large-scale randomized controlled trials have yet confirmed its significant effect on PWV or other arterial stiffness indicators, our findings—based on an integration of indirect evidence—suggest that HIIT + RT may offer potential benefits. One possible explanation, proposed in previous literature, is that combined training may exert complementary effects on vascular function. For example, earlier studies have reported that HIIT combined with RT may be associated with greater improvements in inflammatory profiles and cardiorespiratory fitness than either modality alone [51]. Other studies have suggested that HIIT may reduce resting inflammatory markers such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) [52, 53], whereas RT may improve metabolic regulation and vascular function through pathways related to skeletal muscle adaptation and vascular signaling [54, 55]. However, these potential mechanisms were not directly assessed in the present meta-analysis and should therefore be interpreted as hypothetical explanations rather than direct findings of this study.

Apart from HIIT + RT, standalone HIIT also demonstrated a significant effect in reducing arterial stiffness, consistent with previous findings from network meta-analyses [18, 48]. Notably, HIIT appears to provide vascular benefits even at relatively low exercise doses, indicating high time efficiency and favorable clinical feasibility for individuals with limited time availability.

The ranking results further indicated that AE and RT, when implemented individually, can also significantly improve arterial stiffness, in line with prior literature [15, 56]. One possible explanation is that these modalities may influence arterial stiffness through partially distinct physiological pathways. Previous literature has suggested that AE may improve vascular function through mechanisms related to endothelial shear stress, nitric oxide bioavailability, and vascular remodeling [57–59]. Similarly, RT has been proposed to affect arterial stiffness through pathways related to endothelial function and nitric oxide-mediated vasodilation [60].

The AE findings should nevertheless be interpreted with some caution, given the significant inconsistency observed in the node-splitting analysis for the CON versus AE comparison (P = 0.013). This is notable because AE was the most frequently represented intervention in the network. A possible explanation is the substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity across the contributing AE studies. Specifically, AE protocols varied considerably in exercise mode (e.g., walking, running, cycling, treadmill-based training, and swimming), session duration, exercise intensity, and total weekly dose. Participant characteristics also differed across studies, including age, obesity severity, and the presence of comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, or sarcopenia. In addition, arterial stiffness was assessed using different PWV indices across vascular territories, which may have introduced further measurement heterogeneity. Taken together, these factors may have contributed to the inconsistency between direct and indirect evidence for AE. Accordingly, although AE showed a significant overall benefit in the network meta-analysis, its effect estimates should be interpreted with caution.

By contrast, CT did not demonstrate a clearly robust synergistic effect. Although the pooled estimate suggested a possible reduction in arterial stiffness, the result was borderline (SMD = − 0.39, 95% CI: − 0.79 to 0.00, P = 0.05) and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. This finding is consistent with existing systematic reviews and meta-analyses, which suggest that while CT may provide some improvement in PWV, its effects are generally less stable than those of standalone AE, and the overall quality of evidence remains low [61]. Moreover, one study reported that CT did not significantly improve PWV compared with the control group (SMD = − 0.23, 95% CI: − 0.50 to 0.04) [62]. One possible explanation is that CT interventions were substantially more heterogeneous than RT-only interventions in terms of the relative proportion of aerobic and resistance components, exercise order, intensity, and session structure. Thus, a higher total training load in CT may not necessarily translate into a higher effective dose of the component most relevant to arterial stiffness adaptation. Importantly, the CT interventions included in the present review were not uniformly low-dose, suggesting that simple underdosing is unlikely to fully explain the finding. However, expressing CT dose as total MET-min/week may not fully capture the relative distribution and interaction of aerobic and resistance components. In addition, some individual studies have reported beneficial effects of combined training on PWV in specific populations, suggesting that its vascular effects may depend on protocol composition and participant characteristics rather than on total exercise load alone [63–65]. Taken together, these findings suggest that CT may confer some benefit, but the current evidence remains inconclusive.

One possible explanation, suggested by previous literature, is that different exercise modalities may influence arterial stiffness through partially distinct physiological pathways. In individuals with overweight or obesity, AE has been associated with improved endothelial function and more consistent vascular benefits, whereas the effects of RT appear to be more variable, which may partly reduce the overall effect of CT. Although CT integrates features of both AE and RT and is widely recommended for cardiovascular health, some studies have reported limited effects on arterial compliance or blood pressure control, possibly in relation to the intensity and configuration of the RT component [66]. Previous experimental and clinical studies have proposed that exercise-induced changes in shear stress, nitric oxide bioavailability, inflammatory signaling, and endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) activity may contribute to these modality-specific vascular responses [67–69]. However, these potential mechanisms were not directly assessed in the present meta-analysis and should therefore be interpreted as hypothetical explanations rather than direct findings of this study. These mechanistic interpretations are based on prior experimental and clinical literature and should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than direct evidence from the present analysis.

Additionally, systematic reviews suggest that moderate-to-high intensity RT may have limited or even adverse effects on arterial stiffness, highlighting the importance of considering dose–response relationships and the need for individualized exercise prescriptions [70, 71].

This study also revealed a nonlinear dose–response relationship between exercise and arterial stiffness improvement, indicating that more exercise is not always better. Moderate levels of physical activity may provide vascular protection by enhancing endothelial function, reducing chronic inflammation, and alleviating oxidative stress [72]. In contrast, excessive or high-frequency exercise may lead to sympathetic overactivation and fatigue accumulation, which could negate potential vascular benefits [73]. Similar nonlinear relationships have been reported in studies on blood pressure [74] and HbA1c [75], and this study is the first to demonstrate such a pattern with arterial stiffness as a clinical endpoint.

Practical and applied significance

This study employed a network meta-analysis to systematically evaluate the effects of various exercise modalities on arterial stiffness in individuals with overweight or obesity, revealing findings of considerable clinical and translational significance. The results demonstrated that HIIT, RT, AE, and HIIT + RT significantly improved arterial stiffness. HIIT appeared to offer relatively high time efficiency, whereas HIIT + RT also showed promising effects but should be interpreted cautiously given the limited and predominantly indirect evidence base.

Moreover, a nonlinear L-shaped dose–response relationship was identified between exercise volume and arterial stiffness improvement. In the overall analysis, the minimum effective dose was estimated at 92 MET-min/week, and the benefit appeared to approach a plateau at approximately 670 MET-min/week. Modality-specific analyses showed that AE, HIIT, and HIIT + RT all demonstrated significant L-shaped dose–response relationships, whereas no significant dose-related effects were identified for RT or CT. The plateau range differed across modalities, occurring at approximately 720 MET-min/week for AE, 390 MET-min/week for HIIT, and 550 MET-min/week for HIIT + RT.

To improve practical interpretability, these modality-specific plateau doses were translated into illustrative weekly exercise volumes in Appendix 12. For AE, the plateau dose corresponds approximately to 141–170 min/week of moderate-intensity activity or 104–116 min/week of vigorous-intensity activity, which is broadly consistent with current physical activity guidelines. For HIIT and HIIT + RT, the corresponding weekly durations are shorter, suggesting greater time efficiency for improving arterial stiffness. However, these values should not be interpreted as replacements for general physical activity recommendations, which are intended to support broader health outcomes. In addition, the HIIT + RT estimate should be interpreted cautiously because it is based on a relatively limited and predominantly indirect evidence base.

Taken together, these findings suggest that exercise prescriptions for arterial stiffness may be better guided by modality-specific dose ranges than by a single overall dose estimate. By contrast, although HIIT + RT appears promising, stronger practical recommendations for its routine use should await confirmation from larger direct randomized controlled trials. In practice, AE may represent the most accessible option for most individuals, whereas HIIT may be particularly suitable for those seeking a time-efficient strategy and who can safely tolerate vigorous exercise. These dose-informed interpretations may help improve the practical translation of the findings and provide a preliminary basis for more individualized exercise prescription in the management of arterial stiffness risk.

Additional exploratory analyses suggested that age may be an important effect modifier in the relationship between exercise and arterial stiffness. The meta-regression indicated that the beneficial effect of exercise tended to become greater with increasing mean age, suggesting that age may represent a meaningful source of between-study heterogeneity. This finding is biologically plausible, as baseline arterial stiffness, endothelial dysfunction, vascular reserve, and exercise tolerance differ substantially across adolescents, younger adults, and older adults [76, 77]. However, because this analysis was conducted at the study level using mean age rather than individual participant data, it should be interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive. In addition, age explained only a modest proportion of the between-study heterogeneity, while residual heterogeneity remained significant, suggesting that other unmeasured factors also contributed to variability in intervention effects.

Methodological strengths and limitations

The main strength of this study lies in its comprehensive application of network meta-analysis and Bayesian dose–response modeling to systematically compare the effects of various exercise interventions on arterial stiffness in overweight and obese populations. Notably, this is the first study to quantitatively estimate the optimal intervention dose across different exercise modalities. This study design enabled the integration of both direct and indirect evidence, thereby improving the robustness and generalizability of effect estimates and providing a more practical and quantifiable basis for individualized exercise prescription.

Despite the robustness of the findings, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, considerable heterogeneity existed in the outcome measures and assessment methods for arterial stiffness across the included studies, particularly between central arterial indicators (e.g., carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity, cfPWV) and peripheral indicators (e.g., brachial-ankle PWV, baPWV). While cfPWV is widely regarded as the gold standard [78, 79], its measurements may be influenced by factors such as path length estimation, equipment variability, and operator skill. For instance, differences of up to 1.7 m/s have been reported between cfPWV values measured by SphygmoCor and Complior systems [80, 81]. In contrast, baPWV is easier to administer and more reproducible [82, 83], and has been widely adopted in clinical settings; however, it may systematically overestimate path length, potentially inflating PWV values [84].

Relatedly, different PWV indices (e.g., cfPWV, baPWV, crPWV, and ePWV) were pooled in the main analysis. Because these measures reflect different vascular territories and are not fully interchangeable, measurement-site heterogeneity may have influenced the magnitude and clinical interpretation of the pooled estimates. To improve transparency, we provided a descriptive breakdown of studies by PWV type in Appendix 2. However, because the number of studies within several PWV categories was limited and unevenly distributed, a formal subgroup analysis by PWV type was not considered sufficiently robust. Accordingly, the overall findings should be interpreted with caution. In addition, the lack of detailed reporting on measurement instruments and methodological procedures in some primary studies limited our ability to conduct meaningful subgroup or sensitivity analyses based on outcome type. Future research should prioritize the standardization of arterial stiffness assessment methods and encourage more comprehensive reporting of measurement protocols and technical details in original studies to improve the comparative validity and interpretability of future meta-analyses.

Second, the current analysis focused exclusively on exercise-only interventions and excluded all studies involving dietary interventions, whether alone or in combination. This approach enhanced internal validity by reducing confounding, but limits the generalizability of our findings to real-world lifestyle intervention settings. Previous studies suggest that combined exercise and dietary interventions may yield superior improvements in metabolic parameters, blood pressure, and vascular function [85, 86]. Future research should aim to systematically assess the synergistic effects of multifactorial interventions, provided that intervention components are clearly defined.

Third, substantial variability in participant characteristics (e.g., age, BMI, baseline arterial stiffness, and comorbidities), along with small sample sizes and short intervention durations in some studies, may have influenced individual responses to exercise. This could reduce the external validity and statistical precision of the findings. Although subgroup and meta-regression analyses were attempted, insufficient reporting of covariates in the original studies limited deeper exploration. Well-powered RCTs with longer follow-up and more detailed covariate control are needed to clarify how population characteristics influence intervention response.

A further limitation is that expressing complex multi-component interventions such as CT as total MET-min/week may not fully capture the relative contribution, sequencing, and interaction of aerobic and resistance components. Another limitation relates to the possibility of publication bias and related small-study effects. The asymmetry observed in the funnel plot suggests that smaller studies with more favorable findings may have been more likely to be published, which could have inflated the pooled effect estimates and SUCRA-based rankings. This concern may be particularly relevant for modalities supported by relatively few studies but showing favorable rankings, such as HIIT + RT, and to a lesser extent HIIT. We did not perform formal bias-adjustment analyses, because methods such as trim-and-fill were primarily developed for pairwise meta-analysis, whereas network meta-analysis-specific selection models are more assumption-dependent and difficult to apply in relatively sparse networks. Therefore, publication bias should be considered when interpreting the magnitude of the pooled effects and the certainty of the ranking results.

Another limitation is that only English-language publications were included, which may have resulted in language bias and the exclusion of high-quality studies published in other languages, potentially affecting the comprehensiveness and representativeness of the evidence base.

An additional limitation relates to intervention duration. We used ≥ 2 weeks as the minimum inclusion threshold to exclude acute single-bout studies while retaining short-term repeated exercise interventions. However, interventions lasting only 2–3 weeks may be insufficient to produce stable chronic vascular adaptations. Therefore, we additionally performed a sensitivity analysis excluding studies with durations < 4 weeks. This approach was intended to balance inclusiveness with methodological rigor. Nevertheless, the choice of duration threshold may still have contributed to heterogeneity and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Conclusion

This study employed a network meta-analysis and Bayesian dose–response modeling to systematically evaluate the effects of various exercise modalities—including AE, RT, CT, HIIT, and HIIT + RT—on arterial stiffness in overweight and obese populations. The results indicated that AE, HIIT, and HIIT + RT significantly improved arterial stiffness, each demonstrating a nonlinear dose–response relationship. However, the evidence supporting HIIT + RT remains limited and should be interpreted cautiously pending confirmation from larger direct randomized controlled trials. Bayesian dose–response analysis further demonstrated a nonlinear L-shaped association, with a minimum effective dose of 92 MET-min/week and the overall benefit approaching a plateau at approximately 670 MET-min/week. Modality-specific plateau ranges were approximately 720 MET-min/week for AE, 390 MET-min/week for HIIT, and 550 MET-min/week for HIIT + RT. However, uncertainty remains regarding the apparent superiority of HIIT + RT because the available evidence is limited and predominantly indirect. In addition, while RT showed a significant overall effect in the network meta-analysis, its dose–response pattern was not statistically significant and should therefore be interpreted with caution. The evidence for CT was borderline and remains inconclusive. Exploratory meta-regression further suggested that age may be an important effect modifier of the exercise response on arterial stiffness. Future research should prioritize larger direct randomized controlled trials comparing key exercise modalities, age-stratified analyses, longer follow-up to evaluate sustained vascular effects, more standardized assessment of arterial stiffness outcomes, and more precise quantification of dose in complex multi-component interventions.
