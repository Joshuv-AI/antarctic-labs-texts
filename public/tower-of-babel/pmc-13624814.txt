Cadaveric investigation of an ultrasound-guided obturator canal approach for obturator nerve block
Authors: Takayuki Yoshida, Chutikant Vichainarong, Yousuke Nakano, Masaaki Kitada, Tatsuo Nakamoto
Published: 2026

Introduction

Using a subpectineal approach to administer an obturator nerve block involves ultrasound-guided injection of local anesthetic into the subpectineal plane, an intermuscular space between the pectineus muscle and the obturator externus muscle (OEM). Solution administered at the subpectineal plane has been reported to spread retrogradely to the obturator canal [1]. This has led to the assumption that a single subpectineal injection may be sufficient to achieve blockade of all obturator nerve fibers, regardless of their site of division. However, recent clinical and cadaveric studies have demonstrated that injectates administered via the subpectineal approach do not consistently spread into the obturator canal, raising concerns about incomplete blockade [2,3]. To overcome this limitation, we proposed the obturator canal approach, which allows direct needle tip placement in the obturator canal [4]. This approach may permit delivery of even small volumes of local anesthetic directly into the obturator canal, potentially achieving more reliable obturator nerve blockade. We hypothesized that, with this approach, 5 mL of local anesthetic would be sufficient to allow solution distribution throughout the obturator canal.

In this cadaveric study, we conducted a preliminary anatomical feasibility investigation to test this hypothesis in order to inform future clinical research.

Methods

This cadaveric investigation was approved by our institutional ethics committee (Approval Number: 2022197). In six lower limb specimens from four Thiel-embalmed adult cadavers, we replicated the obturator canal approach described previously [4]. The cadavers were placed supine with the hips flexed and externally rotated. An ultrasound transducer was placed on the genitofemoral sulcus to identify the external opening of the obturator canal, located immediately posterior to the inferior margin of the superior pubic ramus. The needle tip was positioned at the distal end of the obturator canal under ultrasound guidance and 5 mL of water-soluble blue dye was injected. Approximately 1–2 hours after dye injection, we dissected the inguinal region and anteromedial thigh of each specimen to evaluate the dye spread.

Results

We observed spread of the blue dye in the pelvic cavity via the obturator canal in all six specimens. In every specimen, the dye stained the obturator nerve both within the pelvic cavity and along the obturator canal.

In bilateral specimens from one cadaver, in addition to the main fasciculus, we identified a superior fasciculus of the OEM. In these specimens, the superior fasciculus was attached tightly to the superior pubic ramus and completely covered the external opening of the obturator canal. In this anatomical configuration, the subpectineal plane did not appear to be continuous with the obturator canal. In this cadaver, the anterior branch of the obturator nerve on the right side passed through a slit within the superior fasciculus of the OEM and proceeded toward the medial thigh after exiting the obturator canal (Fig. 1A). The posterior branch ran in a deeper layer between the superior and main fasciculi of the OEM and did not enter the subpectineal plane (Type 4, Fig. 1C). On the left side, after exiting the obturator canal, both the anterior and posterior branches of the obturator nerve ran between the superior and main fasciculi of the OEM in the medial thigh (Type 3, Fig. 1C).

In the remaining four specimens, the external opening of the obturator canal appeared to communicate directly with the subpectineal plane. In three of these four specimens, the superior fasciculus of the OEM did not exist, and both the anterior and posterior branches of the obturator nerve ran within the subpectineal plane after exiting the obturator canal (Type 1, Fig. 1C), as illustrated in Fig. 1B. In the remaining specimen, only the anterior branch coursed within the subpectineal plane (Type 2, Fig. 1C).

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that the obturator canal approach can deliver even a small volume of solution directly into the obturator canal and moreover, that the OEM may have a superior fasciculus that covers the external opening of the obturator canal.

Spread of the injectate into the obturator canal is considered to be essential for reliably achieving obturator nerve blockade, as all the obturator nerve fibers run through the obturator canal, regardless of anatomical variation. A recent cadaveric study described the distribution of 5 mL of dye injected deep into the pectineus muscle, inferior to the iliopubic ramus, and at the superior edge of the OEM, immediately outside the obturator canal [3]. Those authors did not report the incidence of dye presence in the obturator canal, although it must have been equal to or higher than the 65% incidence noted for the intrapelvic distribution. Similarly, a clinical investigation reported that ultrasound-guided subpectineal injections of 5–10 mL of contrast-mixed local anesthetic resulted in obturator canal spread in only 84% of patients [2]. These findings indicated that injectate volumes of 5–10 mL delivered to the subpectineal plane do not consistently reach the obturator canal in all patients.

Our findings, along with those of another cadaveric study that investigated the topographical relationships between the obturator nerve and the surrounding musculature, have suggested that the OEM in some individuals may exhibit a distinct superior fasciculus that covers the external opening of the obturator canal [5]. In such cases, immediately after exiting the obturator canal, the posterior branch of the obturator nerve runs between the superior and the main fasciculi of the OEM, before entering the interfascial plane between the adductor brevis and magnus muscles [2,5]. In individuals with this anatomy, the subpectineal plane may not be continuous with the obturator canal, and local anesthetics, particularly small volumes, administered using subpectineal injection techniques are unlikely to reach the obturator canal and may fail to block the posterior branch of the obturator nerve.

In summary, given the small sample size and the inherent limitations of a cadaveric investigation, this study could not confirm whether 5 mL of solution administered via the obturator canal approach will consistently distribute throughout the obturator canal or achieve complete obturator nerve blockade in clinical practice.

Nevertheless, our findings suggest that using the obturator canal approach is a promising technique for delivering even a small volume of solution into the obturator canal. Moreover, in the bilateral specimens from a single cadaver, we observed a superior fasciculus of the OEM that covered the external opening of the obturator canal, underscoring the potential importance of canal-targeting approaches for achieving reliable obturator nerve blockade. Our study has provided a basis for planning future studies of these techniques.
