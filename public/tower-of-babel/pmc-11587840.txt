Introduction

COVID-19 disease is a highly contagious disease caused by the coronavirus SARS-CoV-2 that affects lungs, airways and other organs. December 2019 saw the identification of the first case in Wuhan (china). Since then, a number of strains that spread among people have been found, posing a significant risk to public health and economy [1]. Determining hospital length of stay (LoS) in hospitals is critical information for improving hospital resource allocation and emergency preparation in the light of the potential for future COVID-19 outbreaks. The sociodemographic (age, ethnicity, sex, occupation, residence, healthcare insurance), clinical attributes (comorbidities, COVID-19 clinical stage) and availability of resources at initial evaluation have been the only parameters considered in the investigation of factors impacting LoS [2]. Factors unrelated with infection pathology lead reported LoS to differ dramatically between geographic regions rather than the severity of the illness. Researchers have seen that routine laboratory tests can accurately predict the clinical consequences of COVID-19 infection.

This study aimed to assess the appropriate application of novel combined hematology scores in the domain of systemic inflammation [3] as explanatory factors for COVID-19 patients´ duration of hospital stay (namely LoS). The rationale behind it was that some these factors could forecast the severity of COVID-19 and the rate of in-hospital mortality [4, 5], which could be related to the number of days of hospitalization. The relationship of explanatory variables to prolonged hospitalization like comorbidities, insufficient respiratory therapy, age, and, sex is crucially important in low-income countries like Ecuador, which acted as the COVID-19 pandemic´s zero zone in South America. In this study we suggest that the discovery of targeted inflammatory measures that assess functional status and avert complications could also reduce LoS. Only a few indicators such as low serum albumin, elevated LDH levels [6], total leukocyte count, D-dimer, and CPR [7], have been given any thought as LosS predictors. This study went one step further and evaluated combinations of routine hematology tests only for their ability to predict COVD-19 mortality [4].

Methods

Selection of COVID-19 patients

This retrospective cohort study analyzed 2,930 COVID-19 patients admitted from March 13 to June 17, 2020, at Hospital IESS Quito Sur in Quito (Ecuador). Inclusion criteria required patients to have confirmed COVID-19 diagnosis through RT-PCR testing and complete blood count (CBC) values within 48 hours of admission. Exclusion criteria included patients under 18 years of age, those with missing hematological data, or those discharged within 24. All the patients had a positive RT-qPCR test for Sars-CoV-2 (CDC 2019-Novel Coronavirus Real-Time RT-qPCR Diagnostic Panel upper and lower respiratory specimens) and a pneumonia severity index (PSI) > 3, as well as an arterial blood gasometry exam during the triage evaluation. Blood samples for routine laboratory tests were drawn upon admission after a 40-min interval of average. A complete blood count was obtained by rapidly running the blood samples in a Sysmex XN-550™ Hematology Analyzer (Sysmex America Inc., Japan). Hospital length of stay (LoS) defined the total of days that patients spent in the hospital from admission to either home discharge or death.

COVID-19 patient selection

Patient demographic information (age, gender), clinical characteristics (comorbidities such as diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease), and hospital outcomes (length of stay [LoS], ICU admission, mechanical ventilation) were extracted from electronic medical records. Hematological parameters were calculated from CBC data obtained at admission, and CRP levels were included as a marker of systemic inflammation.

Statistics

Statistical analysis of the data was performed using the SPSS software (version 24.0) for Windows (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Hospital length of stay (LoS) defined the total of days that patients spent in the hospital from admission to either home discharge or death. The initial feature selection encompassed an exploratory analysis of preselected hematological scores (the exploratory outcome measures) relevant to COVID-19 severity and in-hospital mortality [4, 5] able to reach the better statistical significance levels, when combined in proportional hazards regressions. The following hematological indices (the primary outcome measures) were then selected: neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), red cell distribution width to-lymphocyte ratio (RLR), red cell distribution width-to-platelet ratio (RPR), leukocyte-to-C reactive protein ratio (Leu-CPR), and lymphocyte-to-C reactive protein ratio (Ly-CPR). The association of risk factors with LoS was assessed in both the univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models with enter method.

In the case of the analysis of the sample nature, the Mann and Whitney test was conducted to compare LoS means between Survivor and non-Survivor groups of patients. The same statistical procedure was applied to analyze sex-dependent effects on survival. The Kaplan-Meier survival estimates was used to estimate the probability of hospital discharge and the log-rank test were used to compare men and women in terms of survival. Cumulative functions of hospital stay and death probability during hospitalization (the secondary outcome measures) time helped compare sex-dependent differences in survival and hazard functions from the survival dataset. Data were presented as mean ± SD. Alpha value was set at 0.05 for statistical significance.

Ethics

Written informed consent and evaluation by a Human Research Ethics Committee was waived, because the study is observational and retrospective in nature (non-experimental research with secondary data, anonymized patients, and descriptive design). The permission to download the data from the digital clinical records system was granted by the CEO of the IESS Hospital Quito Sur, who attested to the protection of patient confidentiality in accordance with the policies established by the Ecuadorian government during the COVID-19 pandemic and the Declaration of Helsinki. We followed the STROBE guidelines.

Results

Nature, distribution, and survival analysis of the sample

LoS mean among those who survived was 7.8 ± 24.0 days, a shorter stay compared to those who died that was 38.6 ± 41.9 days (W = 31338; p < 0.001). The averaged LoS for women and men were 6.4 ± 21.0 and 10.5 ± 27.5 days respectively (W = 981634; p < 0.001). LoS associated risk factors

All the systemic inflammation-inspired hematological markers were substantially associated in the univariate analysis with a longer LoS (Table 1). For example, the probability of a longer hospital stay doubles with the MLR index (HR(IC95%) = 0.460(0.387-0.547); p < 0.001), but it increases tenfold with PLR (HR(IC95%) = 0.460(0.387-0.547); p < 0.001). The multivariable analysis revealed that only MLR and Ly-CPR missed statistical significance. However, the majority of the systemic inflammatory indices had hazard ratios that were nearly 1, which turned out irrelevant from a clinical standpoint. The RPR index was an exception, as it can rise the likelihood of a lengthier hospital stay by up to ten times (HR(IC95%) = 0.092(0.03-0.29); p < 0.001).

Discussion

Because men´s inflammatory responses varied from women´s [8], men had longer LoS, and greater fatality rates than women [9] The length of hospital stay was statistically associated with indicators such as NLR, PLR and Leu-CPR [5, 10] that suggest a hyper-inflammation condition in cases of severe COVID-19. Nevertheless, the results were clinically insignificant and enable to robustly change LoS. It is interesting to note that the most robust statistically significant association with LoS was seen in a RDW-related index like RPR [4]. The higher the RPR values, the lengthier hospital stay. Sex and RPR were found to be as the most clinically significant risk factors in this study for a prolonged hospital stay.

The RDW is one of the most reliable prognostic indicators of COVID-19 hyperinflammation, severity, and mortality in COVID-19 patients [11–14]. Platelets have a significant influence in inflammation [15] and severity in COVID-19 disease [16], thus the platelet count would be a good way to normalize the RDW. Clotting plays a role in cardiovascular problems and stroke [17], both of which are frequent in COVID-19 patients [18, 19]. Although in-hospital mortality in critically ill patients with myocardial infarct conditions [20] and acute ischemic stroke [21, 22] can be predicted by an elevated RPR score, COVID-19 mortality does not follow this pattern [4]. The current research indicates that a greater RPR would be a stronger predictor of a prolonged COVID-19 recovery in the hospital. The underlying causes are still unclear. Given that RDW is a measure of red blood cell size variation as determined by a hematology analyzer, it may serve as a marker of oxidative for stress and tissue hypoxia, both of which are prevalent in severe COVID-19 cases [23]. Variations in platelet counts are crucial in hemostatic and vascular diseases associated to COVID-19 [24], whereas persistent inflammations can alter RDW [25]. Accordingly, in cases with severe COVID-19, a rise in RDW in conjunction with a decrease in platelet counts may indicate the interaction of endothelial dysfunction, coagulation anomalies, and inflammation [26]. Because an elevated RPR score is a strong indicator of death in sepsis patients [27], it is likely that complications from SARS-CoV-2 infection are the reason of this raised score and the lengthier hospitalization.

Potential limitations include the studýs retrospective character, which makes it difficult to infer causality between hematological indices and extended LoS. It is also possible that the results of this single-center study might not be generalized to other healthcare settings. Hematological data collected at admission, might not accurately reflect how the illness progressed. Residual confounding variables like illness severity and treatments cannot be ruled out. Finally, due to the rapid evolution of COVID-19 variants and treatments, the applicability of these findings in the future may be limited.

Nevertheless, the sizable patient population in both cohorts improves the finding´s generalizability This study provided a comprehensive evaluation of inflammatory biomarkers, including the novel RPR index, and their potential value in forecasting LoS. Furthermore, the application of both univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models along with the Kaplan-Meier survival curves enhanced the interpretability of the results. Further prospective studies are needed to confirm these associations and explore the potential for dynamic monitoring of these biomarkers.

Conclusions

Researchers have mostly concluded that the primary factor causing extended hospital stays is clinical complications brought on by COVID-19 infection. According to our findings, novel hematological scores such as RPR, that are indicative of the state of systemic inflammation may be a predictor of these complications before they arise.
