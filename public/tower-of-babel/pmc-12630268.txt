Characteristics and associated factors of insomnia in gynecological cancer patients: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Yi Xie, Shu-han Yang, Su-ying Liu, Guang-hui Zhu, Yan Wang, Li-yuan Fang, Yu-hang Fang, Run-xi Wang, Bai-lu Sui, Ying Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo investigate the characteristics and associated factors of insomnia in patients with gynecological cancer.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted on 195 gynecological cancer patients with insomnia. In addition to sleep quality, general information, gynecological cancer information, concomitant symptoms and biological indicators were investigated.Results① 77.43% of patients were rated as “poor” or “very poor” in terms of sleep quality, with difficulty falling asleep being the main type of insomnia. ② The sleep quality of gynecological cancer patients was related to cancer type, cancer stage, number of chemotherapy cycles, Karnofsky Performance Status score, depression, anxiety, fatigue, pain, and follicle-stimulating hormone. ③Fatigue (OR = 9.228, CI = 2.198–38.750) and pain (OR = 5.077, CI = 1.321–19.515) were significant associated factors for the aggravation of insomnia.ConclusionGynecological cancer patients exhibit high levels of insomnia. Particular attention should be paid to patients with fatigue and pain, with interventions targeting reduction in sleep latency. Clarifying insomnia characteristics and associated factors is helpful to improve sleep quality and provide evidence-based insights for clinical management of gynecological cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00520-025-10148-x.

Introduction

Gynecological cancer refers to malignant tumors occurring in the female reproductive system, primarily including cervical, endometrial, and ovarian cancers, whose incidence and mortality rates rank among the top ten female cancers [1], seriously threatening women's lives and health. With the continuous improvement of therapeutic measures, the overall survival and disease-free survival of gynecological cancer patients have been progressively extended [2–4]. Alleviating symptom burden and improving quality of life have increasingly become central focuses requiring urgent attention among patients, families, clinicians, and researchers [5].

Insomnia represents one of the most prevalent clinical manifestations among gynecological cancer patients, with over 80% experiencing varying degrees of insomnia at diagnosis, during or after treatment [6, 7]. Insomnia in gynecological cancer patients is notably more complex and refractory compared to in the general population [8]. Failure to provide timely and effective management may exacerbate symptom burden, reduce quality of life [9], disrupt the progression of oncology treatment [10], and even shorten survival duration [11]. Emerging evidence demonstrates that gynecological cancer patients with poor sleep quality face a 2.4-fold higher mortality risk compared to those with good sleep quality [12]. Early prevention, identification, and appropriate management of insomnia are therefore critical for the survival of gynecological cancer patients [13].

Previous studies have identified several potential associated factors for insomnia in gynecological cancer patients, including demographic [13], disease-related [6], and symptom-related [14] factors. However, notable limitations exist in current evidence. Firstly, many investigations have been restricted to specific treatment phases, such as chemotherapy [15], radiotherapy [16], or targeted therapy [17]. Yet insomnia affects the entire disease trajectory, not only during specific treatment periods. Secondly, the correlation between insomnia and other symptoms remain underexplored. Insomnia-emotion-fatigue-pain frequently co-occur as a symptom cluster in cancer patients [18], but most existing studies focus solely on the correlation between emotions and insomnia. Lastly, few studies have evaluated biological indicators. While Clevenger et al. examined the correlation between IL-6 and insomnia [19], the pathogenesis of insomnia in gynecological cancer involves multiple mechanisms, including inflammatory responses, hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis dysfunction, and endocrine disruptions [20]. Therefore, more research involving different factors and samples is needed to identify and prevent insomnia in gynecological cancer patients.

This research sought to explore the characteristics of insomnia in gynecological cancer patients and explore the associated factors. This may contribute to future intervention measures for the prevention and management of sleep disturbances in gynecological cancer patients.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional investigation was performed in compliance with the Helsinki Declaration and STROBE Statement [21], with ethical approval obtained from the Ethics Committee of Guang'anmen Hospital, China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences (No. 2024–027-KY). Participants were recruited and assessed from February 2024 to February 2025 at the Oncology Clinic of Guang'anmen Hospital, China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences.

Participants

Eligibility criteria included: ① Histopathologically confirmed ovarian cancer, cervical cancer, or endometrial cancer [22]; ② Diagnosed with insomnia according to the Chinese Classification of Mental Disorders (3rd Edition, CCMD-3) [23], with a total score of ≥ 6 on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) scale; ③ Aged ≥ 18 years; ④ Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) score ≥ 60; ⑤ Being able to cooperate in completing the scale survey; ⑥ Voluntary participation with written consent. The exclusion criteria were: ① Diagnosed with insomnia before being diagnosed with gynecological cancer; ② Accompanied by brain metastasis; ③ Combined infectious and autoimmune diseases; ④ Merged severe primary diseases such as heart, brain, liver, and kidney.

Measures

General information

The collected data included age, height, weight, and KPS score.

Gynecological cancer information

Including cancer type, cancer stage, pathological type, and previous cancer therapy.

Sleep quality

The PSQI scale was administered to assess the sleep status. PSQI total score = Component A (Sleep Quality) + Component B (Sleep Latency) + Component C (Sleep Duration) + Component D (Sleep Efficiency) + Component E (Sleep Disturbance) + Component F (Sleep Medication) + Component G (Daytime Dysfunction) [24]. Each component was scored from 0 to 3 points, with a total score of 0–21 points. Sleep quality was categorized as good (0–5 points), general (6–10 points), poor (11–15 points), or very poor (16–21 points) based on PSQI scoring. The Cronbach's alpha of PSQI was 0.79, and the test–retest reliability was 0.91 [25].

The PSQI total score was dichotomized into two groups for multivariate analysis: PSQI < 16 (general/poor sleep quality) and PSQI ≥ 16 (very poor sleep quality). This cutoff was chosen based on two considerations: (1) the standard PSQI scoring criteria define 16–21 points as “very poor sleep quality”, which represents severe insomnia with high clinical intervention needs; (2) our study focused on identifying factors associated with severe insomnia (a subgroup with unmet clinical needs), as mild-to-moderate insomnia (PSQI < 16) is often managed with basic sleep hygiene guidance.

Depression and anxiety

The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) was used for evaluation, with total scores calculated as the sum of depression and anxiety subscale scores. Each subscale scores were classified into four tiers: 0–7 (normal), 8–10 (mild), 11–14 (moderate), and 15–21 (severe). The internal consistency of the HADS scale was good, with Cronbach's alpha ≥ 0.84 [26].

Fatigue

The Revised Piper Fatigue Scale (PFS-R) was adopted to evaluate the fatigue status. PFS-R total score = behavioral score + emotional score + sensory score + cognitive score. The PFS-R scale graded severity levels as: 0 (no fatigue), 1–3 (mild), 4–6 (moderate), and 7–10 (severe), with good construct validity (r ≥ 0.65) and test–retest reliability (r > 0.86) [27].

Pain

The Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) was employed to quantify pain severity. This 10-cm linear scale is marked by patients according to their subjective pain perception, with greater severity indicated by placements closer to the right end. Pain intensity was categorized as: 0 (no pain), 1–3 (mild), 4–6 (moderate), and 7–10 (severe). The scale achieved excellent reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha = 0.94–0.96 and test–retest reliability = 0.97 [28].

Biological indicators

Serum levels of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), cortisol (COR), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), estradiol (E2), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays.

Statistical analysis

The SPSS 24.0 platform was utilized for statistical computations. Insomnia characteristics

The average of PSQI total score was 13.22 ± 3.16 points, and up to 77.43% of gynecological cancer patients had a PSQI greater than 11 (Fig. 2A, Table 1). According to the PSQI rating criteria, a score exceeding 11 points indicates poor or very poor sleep quality. Multivariate analysis

Forward stepwise selection was applied to develop a multivariate binary unconditional logistic regression model, with PSQI score dichotomized into 0 (PSQI < 16: general/poor sleep quality) and 1 (PSQI ≥ 16: very poor sleep quality). The dichotomization of PSQI at 16 points aimed to prioritize severe insomnia, as this subgroup has a higher burden of sleep disturbance and requires targeted clinical interventions. The above p < 0.05 correlates (KPS, cancer type, cancer stage, number of chemotherapy cycles, depression, anxiety, fatigue, pain, and FSH) were included as independent variables and assigned values (Supplementary Table 2).

Regression analysis identified fatigue and pain as significant associated factors of decreased sleep quality (p < 0.05). Relative to non-fatigued or mildly fatigued individuals, gynecological cancer patients with moderate-to-severe fatigue demonstrated a 9.228-fold elevated risk of insomnia exacerbation (OR = 9.228, 95% CI: 2.198–38.750, p = 0.002; Supplementary Table 3). Similarly, moderate/severe pain was associated with a 5.077-fold higher risk compared to no/mild pain (OR = 5.077, 95% CI: 1.321–19.515, p = 0.018; Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

This survey evaluated the characteristics and associated factors of insomnia in gynecological cancer patients. In addition to sleep quality, we also evaluated general information, disease information, concomitant symptoms, and biological indicators. These findings may contribute to the prevention and alleviation of insomnia in gynecological cancer patients, enhance overall quality of life for affected individuals, and provide a reference for the comprehensive management of gynecological cancer.

Characteristics of insomnia in gynecological cancer

The research indicated that gynecological cancer patients experienced severe insomnia, primarily characterized by difficulty in falling asleep. The mean PSQI score of 13.22 in this study was substantially higher than reported in the general population (9.00) [30], head and neck cancer (9.19) [31], breast cancer (11.9) [32], gastric cancer (11.96) [33], and colorectal cancer (12.75) [34]. These results indicated that insomnia in gynecological cancer patients was more severe compared to both the general population and other cancer patients, necessitating the development of targeted interventions to improve sleep quality. Furthermore, the participants in this study showed prolonged sleep latency as the main manifestation, suggesting that the selection of pharmacological or nonpharmacological therapies that had fast onset, short maintenance time, and mainly shortened the time to fall asleep may achieve better therapeutic effects in improving insomnia in gynecological cancer patients [35].

Associated factors of insomnia in gynecological cancer

Cancer and cancer therapy

Findings demonstrated that insomnia in gynecological cancer patients worsened with the increase of cancer stage and the number of chemotherapy cycles. Consistent with our findings, a 772-case ovarian cancer investigation found that the risk of aggravated insomnia in stage III/IV individuals was 1.09 times that in those with stage I/II [14]. This may be related to the large tumor burden, long treatment duration, numerous complications, and heavy psychological burden in advanced patients. Chemotherapy is a common treatment for gynecological cancer. However, as it attacks rapidly dividing cells indiscriminately, it has certain cytotoxicity. The long-term accumulation of systemic side effects such as nausea, poor appetite, vomiting, fatigue, and palpitations leads to a continuous decline in sleep quality [36]. A study on insomnia in postoperative gastric cancer populations found that as the number of chemotherapy cycles increased to more than 6, the risk of insomnia exacerbation was 3.640 times that when the number was ≤ 6 [33]. Previous studies have shown that chemotherapy-induced insomnia may be associated with the abnormal production and distribution of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the brain [37]. With the long-term use of chemotherapy drugs, patients tend to have an increased tendency towards insomnia [38].

In addition, this study demonstrated that patients with ovarian cancer and lower KPS scores experienced more severe insomnia. Ho et al. reported that insomnia severity in ovarian cancer patients was 6.11-fold higher than in cervical cancer and 5.01-fold higher than in endometrial cancer [39]. Notably, conflicting evidence exists regarding KPS-insomnia associations, with some studies failing to observe correlations [33]. This discrepancy may be attributed to differing classification criteria for KPS score, introducing potential bias.

Concomitant symptoms

This study identified significant associations between insomnia and depression, anxiety, fatigue, and pain in gynecological cancer patients, with fatigue and pain emerging as key associated factors. The clinical symptoms of cancer patients often occur in complex clusters, prompting researchers to investigate the insomnia-emotion-fatigue-pain nexus as “psychoneurological symptom cluster” [40–42]. “Psychoneurological symptom clusters” share a common cytokine-based neuroimmune mechanism, possibly reflecting a spectrum of inflammation-initiated “disease behaviors” [18]. Zhou et al. further demonstrated stronger correlations among insomnia-fatigue-pain (lower abdominal and lumbosacral pain) in the “psychoneurological symptom cluster” of gynecological cancer [43]. These discoveries underscore the critical importance of prioritizing fatigue and pain in clinical management. Moreover, an evaluation framework based on this symptom cluster may serve as a comprehensive clinical management tool for gynecological cancer patients in the future.

Biological indicators

Our results revealed that insomnia severity in gynecological cancer patients correlated with elevated serum FSH levels. Consistent with previous findings, Mohammadi et al. demonstrated that arousal index was positively associated with increasing FSH levels [44]. Specifically, each 1 IU/L increment in FSH was linked to a 0.49-min prolongation of sleep latency [45]. This phenomenon may be attributed to ovarian dysfunction following anti-tumor therapies (surgical castration, radiotherapy, chemotherapy), which disrupts hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis feedback regulation. Resultant pituitary hyperactivity drives FSH elevation [46], which dysregulates sleep–wake cycle homeostasis and circadian rhythm maintenance [47].

Study limitations

Firstly, as a cross-sectional design, this study failed to observe longitudinal changes in sleep quality among gynecological cancer patients, making it impossible to clarify the temporal associations between potential associated factors and insomnia or develop phase-specific interventions. Secondly, sleep-related neurotransmitters such as serotonin and dopamine were not measured, precluding analysis of the correlation between biological indicators and insomnia in gynecological cancer from the perspective of the HPA axis and resulting in a lack of understanding regarding the neuroendocrine mechanism of sleep disturbances. Lastly, the single-center nature of this study may introduce selection bias, necessitating validation through multicenter, large-sample studies to improve the generalizability of findings.

Clinical implications

The identified associations in this study have clinical implications for gynecological cancer patients with insomnia. First, fatigue and pain were the critical associated factors for severe insomnia; thus, integrating fatigue management (e.g., energy conservation training, physical exercise interventions) and standardized pain control (e.g., WHO analgesic ladder) into routine care may indirectly improve sleep quality. Second, ovarian cancer patients and those with advanced disease (Stage III/IV) or > 6 chemotherapy cycles had more severe insomnia, suggesting these subgroups require proactive sleep screening (e.g., regular PSQI assessments). Third, elevated serum FSH levels were associated with worse sleep, indicating that monitoring HPO axis function (especially in patients with ovarian dysfunction after anti-tumor therapies) could help identify high-risk individuals for early sleep interventions. Although cross-sectional data cannot confirm causality, these associations provide actionable targets for symptom cluster management, aligning with the goal of improving quality of life in gynecological cancer patients.

Conclusion

Gynecological cancer patients suffer from severe insomnia, and is mainly characterized by difficulty in falling asleep. This was significantly associated with cancer type, cancer stage, chemotherapy cycles, KPS score, depression, anxiety, fatigue, pain, and FSH levels. Fatigue and pain emerged as critical associated factors for sleep quality decline. These findings may facilitate early identification and prevention of insomnia in gynecological cancer patients, and provide evidence for designing effective intervention measures to strengthen cancer management.
