Patient safety culture in home care and its related factors and outcomes: a scoping review
Authors: Tania Martins, David Bellagamba, Michael Simon, Julie Bucher Andary, Franziska Zúñiga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatient safety culture is crucial to ensuring the quality and safety of healthcare. Assessing safety culture raises awareness of patient safety, identifies areas for improvement, and supports tracking changes over time. Given the increasing growth of home care, the complexity of care, and its unique characteristics, understanding safety culture in home care is essential to informing practice and guiding further research. We therefore aimed to identify instruments used to measure patient safety culture in home care, including its associated factors and outcomes.MethodsWe conducted a systematic search of MEDLINE/PubMed, CINAHL, PsycINFO/Ovid, EMBASE/Ovid, and Web of Science and then a citation tracking in March 2024. Two reviewers independently screened the records for eligibility criteria. We included studies that measured patient safety culture from the perspective of healthcare professionals working in home care. The included studies were narratively summarized, and the findings are presented through descriptive texts and tables.ResultsThe screening of 1922 articles and citation tracking resulted in 13 articles that met the inclusion criteria. Twelve studies employed self-reported questionnaires and one used interviews. The most frequently used questionnaires were adaptations of the Nursing Home Survey on Patient Safety Culture and Safety Attitudes Questionnaire. Commonly assessed dimensions included teamwork, communication, management, and organizational learning, whereas job satisfaction and stress recognition were less frequently assessed. The studies examined the association of both individual (e.g., age, work experience) and work-related factors (e.g., weekly working hours, transformational leadership) with safety culture. The outcomes analyzed included person-centered care, the overall perceptions of patient safety, and the global reporting of patient safety events.ConclusionsDifferent instruments have been used to measure safety culture in home care, but none of them have been specifically developed for home care, and there is no agreement on which dimensions to include. Evidence on the associated factors and outcomes is scarce and inconclusive. Further research is needed to understand the concept of safety culture, including associated factors and outcomes. Applying mixed-methods and longitudinal study designs could provide a more comprehensive understanding of safety culture and support evaluating and developing strategies to improve it in home care.Review registration numberZenodo: 10.5281/zenodo.10779093.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13732-3.

Background

Patient safety culture has become increasingly important for the maintenance of safe healthcare systems. The World Health Organization Global Patient Safety Action Plan 2021–2030 emphasizes the need to “instill a safety culture in the design and delivery of health care” and calls on governments to adopt strategies for establishing a safety culture throughout their health systems, including methods for changing the culture [1, p. 21]. Improved safety culture has been associated with reduced rates of adverse events [2] and improved patient outcomes [3].

The terms safety culture and safety climate have often been used interchangeably, although it has been argued that they refer to different concepts. A review conducted in 2014 identified 51 original definitions of safety culture and 30 definitions of safety climate [4]. Guldenmund defined safety culture as a pattern of individual and organizational behavior based on three layers of shared perceptions and beliefs about safety. The core layer represents the basic and fundamental assumptions about safety; the middle consists of values, beliefs, and attitudes; and the outer layer represents the behavioral manifestations of the underlying safety culture [5]. Safety climate represents the perceptions of the outer and middle layer of safety culture at a given time. It is an indicator of “shared perceptions of safety policies, procedures, and practices in an organization“ [6, p.123]. To simplify the reporting of results, this review will refer to these concepts collectively as safety culture.

The evidence suggests that safety culture is a crucial factor for ensuring quality and safety in patient care. Studies conducted in hospitals have indicated the important role of safety culture in patient safety [7, 8], quality of care [9], and patient experience [10]. Similarly, in nursing homes, safety culture has been linked to quality of care ratings [11]. In a scoping review, 76% of 34 included studies found associations between increased safety culture scores and reduced adverse events [2].

Assessing safety culture is essential to enhancing patient safety. It can be used to raise awareness of patient safety, identify areas for improvement, evaluate patient safety interventions, track changes over time, and perform internal and external benchmarking [12]. Methods for assessment include questionnaires, qualitative measures (e.g., focus groups and interviews), ethnographies, or a combination of these [13]. Self-reported staff questionnaires are the most commonly used method to measure safety culture. One advantage of using questionnaires is that they allow anonymous contributions from staff who may be hesitant to express their views openly [14]. The advantages of qualitative studies include the potential to uncover cultural assumptions, contextual factors, and other relevant aspects of safety culture that cannot be captured through questionnaires [15].

Home care has unique characteristics, such as the provision of care mostly alone at a patient’s homes, an unpredictable environment, and a limited availability of supplies and materials [16]. Despite this and the increasing growth and complexity of care at home [17], we did not find any reviews of patient safety culture specifically in this setting. Most reviews on safety culture have been conducted in hospitals. For example, a systematic review identified 88 surveys used to assess safety culture in hospitals [18]. Few reviews on safety culture have been conducted in primary care, but the concept of primary care is broad and includes distinctive environments (e.g., general practices and community care centers), making it challenging to generalize their findings to the specific context of home care.

Mapping the available evidence on safety culture in home care is crucial for identifying the existing measurement tools used in this setting, identifying gaps in the measurements, and guiding both researchers and practitioners in measuring safety culture in home care. Moreover, understanding safety culture in home care and identifying its related factors and outcomes is essential to designing interventions to change safety culture. Knowledge about safety culture in home care will offer valuable guidance to researchers, policymakers, and practitioners in measuring and designing targeted interventions to improve safety culture and consequently patient safety in this increasingly important healthcare setting.

This scoping review thus aimed to provide an overview of the existing instruments measuring safety culture and its associated factors and outcomes in the home care setting. To guide the synthesis of the evidence, we asked the following research questions:

What instruments or assessment tools have been used to measure how home care professionals rate patient safety culture in home care?What dimensions, items, topics, or themes do these patient safety culture instruments include?What factors and outcomes have been analyzed in association with patient safety culture in home care?

Methods

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the study protocol published on Zenodo [10.5281/zenodo.10779093] [19] and followed the Joanna Briggs Institute’s methodological guidance for conducting scoping reviews [20] and the framework by Arksey and O’Malley [21]. We conducted a scoping review to map and summarize the existing evidence on the assessment of safety culture and its related factors, to identify knowledge gaps, and to inform future research. For reporting we used the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [22].

Eligibility criteria

In accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute’s methodological guidance for scoping reviews, we used the PCC (Population, Concept, and Context) framework and types of evidence sources to define the eligibility criteria [20] (see Table 1).

Information sources and search strategy

We searched the following five databases: Medical Literature Analysis and Retrieval System Online (MEDLINE) (via PubMed), Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), PsycINFO (via Ovid), EMBASE (via Ovid), and Web of Science with relevant keywords. Further articles were searched for through citation tracking (backward and forward) of the reference lists of all the included studies. The search was conducted on March 18, 2024.

Keywords related to safety culture (e.g., safety climate) and home care (e.g., home visits) were used to identify the relevant articles. The text words contained in the titles and abstracts of relevant articles and the index terms used to describe the articles were used to develop a full search strategy for MEDLINE/PubMed. The initial search strategy was revised in collaboration with an experienced information specialist. The search strategy was translated and adapted for the other databases. The full search strategy for each database can be found in Additional file 1.

Selection of sources of evidence

Following the search, we extracted and uploaded all the identified studies into the software Covidence [23] and removed the duplicates. In a first step, following a pilot test of 50 records, two reviewers (DB and TM) independently screened all the titles and abstracts for eligibility. In a second step, full texts were screened by the two reviewers. Any disagreements between the reviewers at any stage of the selection process were resolved through discussion and consultation with a third, more senior reviewer (FZ). The references of the included articles were managed using Endnote (version 20) [24]. The inter-rater agreement between the two reviewers was 95.4% for the title and abstract screening and 94.4% for the full-text screening.

Data extraction

Two reviewers (DB and TM) extracted data independently and in duplicate from the included studies using a data extraction form developed iteratively by the study team and continuously updated. Data extraction was performed using Covidence [23]. We extracted information on the following characteristics: authors, year of publication, title, study design, aim(s), country in which the study was conducted, sample, response rate, eligibility criteria, data collection period, instrument’s characteristics, dimensions covered by the instrument, safety culture results, related factors, outcomes, and comments by the reviewers. The extraction form was piloted and adapted with the first five studies. After that we adapted the extraction form by adding the country in which the study was conducted, the response rate, the number of items included, psychometric properties of the instrument, and how the scale was calculated.

Discrepancies were resolved by two reviewers (DB and TM) through discussions. We did not request any nonreported results from the authors. No critical appraisal of the sources of evidence was conducted.

Data synthesis

The process of the selection of studies was summarized using a PRISMA flow diagram [25]. The data collected from the included studies were summarized using a narrative synthesis with descriptive texts and tables. We report the results here according to our three research questions.

Results

Our search strategy identified 1922 articles. After removing duplicates (n = 687) and screening the titles and abstracts,143 articles were assessed for their eligibility. Two articles were identified via citation tracking. After full-text screening and citation tracking, 13 studies were included in the current scoping review (see Fig. 1).

Characteristics of the included studies

Among the 13 included studies, 12 (92.3%) had a cross-sectional observational design [26–37], and one was a qualitative descriptive study [38] (see Table 2). The studies were conducted in five different countries, most studies having been conducted in Norway (n = 5) [27, 30, 31, 34, 36], followed by Brazil (n = 4) [28, 32, 33, 37], Belgium (n = 2) [29, 35], Sweden (n = 1) [26] and South Korea (n = 1) [38]. With the exception of a qualitative study that only included advanced practice nurses in hospital-based home care [38], all the studies included professionals with different educational backgrounds working in home care [26–37]. Instruments and assessment tools used to measure safety culture

To measure safety culture, the instruments used were the Norwegian version of the Nursing Home Survey on Patient Safety Culture (NHSOPSC) (n = 4) [27, 30, 31, 34], the Brazilian version of the Safety Attitudes Questionnaire – Short Form (SAQ-SF) (n = 3) [28, 32, 33], the SCOPE-Primary Care (SCOPE standing for Systematisch Cultuur Onderzoek Patiëntveiligheid Eerste lijn, Dutch for systematic culture inquiry on patient safety in primary care) (n = 2) [29, 35], the Swedish adaptation of the Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture (n = 1) [26], the Norwegian version of the SAQ-SF (n = 1) [36], and the Safety Attitudes Questionnaire – Ambulatory Version adapted to the Brazilian setting (n = 1) [37] (see Table 3). The qualitative study used individual face-to-face semistructured interviews with a duration of 30–50 min [38].

The questionnaires were adapted to the home care setting mainly by changing their wording; for example, “patient” was changed to “older person,” or “hospital units” to “care units” [26]. All the instruments used 5-point Likert scales as answer options. Scores ranged from 1 to 5 or 0 to 100, except for one instrument, where the answer options were 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 [37].

Included dimensions

The number of safety culture dimensions measured in the cross-sectional studies ranged from two [36] to 11 [26] (see Table 3). The most assessed dimensions were teamwork, communication, management, and organizational/continuous learning. Dimensions such as job satisfaction or stress recognition were only assessed in the Safety Attitudes Questionnaires [28, 32, 33, 37], while, for example, intention to report events was only included in the SCOPE-Primary Care [29, 35]. The number of included items ranged from 13 [36] to 41 [28–30, 33–35].

Characteristics of the included instruments or assessment tools

Of the 12 studies that used questionnaires to assess safety culture in home care, six reported on psychometric properties of the instrument for the analyzed study sample [27, 30–32, 35, 36], such as its internal consistency or concurrent and convergent validity (see Table 4). The most frequently reported psychometric characteristic (n = 6) was internal consistency, as measured by Cronbach’s alpha. All the dimensions for which a Cronbach’s alpha was reported had values ≥ 0.70, with the exception of the dimensions compliance with procedures, training and skills, and nonpunitive response to mistakes evaluated in a study using the Norwegian version of the NHSOPSC [31], teamwork climate, safety climate, and working conditions in a study using the Brazilian version of the SAQ-SF [32], and organizational learning in a study using the SCOPE-Primary Care instrument [35].

Safety culture scores

Of the four studies that used adaptations of the NHSOPSC, two reported an overall safety culture mean score, one of 3.8 (SD 0.4) [range 1–5] [30] and another one of 3.8 (SD 0.6) [range 1–5] [27] (see Table 4). Among the studies using adaptations of the NHSOPSC, the dimensions with the highest percentage of positive responses were the dimensions teamwork, feedback and communication about incidents, and supervisor expectations and actions promoting patient safety in Ree and Wiig [34] with 81.0% positive responses. The dimension with the lowest percentage of positive responses was staffing with 45.0% positive responses in Ree and Wiig [34].

Of the studies that used adaptations of the SAQ-SF, one reported an overall safety culture mean score of 78.0 (SD not reported) [range 0–100] [33], and another a score of 58.8 (SD 9.8) [range 0–100] [32]. Of all the four studies that used adaptations of the SAQ-SF, the dimension with the highest mean score was job satisfaction with mean score of 88.8 (SD not reported) in Lousada et al. [33]. The dimension with the lowest mean score was perception of management with a mean score of 32.4 (SD 18.5) in Oliveira et al. [32].

In the two studies that used the SCOPE-Primary Care, the dimension with the highest percentage of positive responses was organizational learning (71.7%), while the dimension with the lowest percentage of positive responses was handover and teamwork (43.0%) [35].

Factors related to safety culture

In total, six studies [27–30, 32, 36] examined related factors, two examined outcomes [31, 34], and one examined both [26]. Four studies used t tests, analyses of variance, or Kruskal-Wallis tests [28, 29, 36, 37], while the other studies that analyzed the relationship between safety culture factors and outcomes used multiple regression analyses.

Individual factors

Individual factors such as gender, age, work experience, educational background, and professional category were analyzed in association with the overall safety culture or specific safety culture dimensions with mixed overall results (see Table 5). For example, Oliveira et al. [32] found that older age was associated with higher overall safety culture and higher scores in the dimensions of working conditions and safety climate, while Olsen and Bjerkan [36] did not find any significant associations.

Work-related factors

Work-related factors such as type of contract, workload, weekly working hours, autonomy, skill utilization, transformational leadership, participation, work engagement, work pace, and municipality were analyzed in relation with safety culture. Having a statutory work contract was associated with lower scores of overall safety culture (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:\:$$\end{document}= −15.72, p = 0.004) and of the dimension safety climate (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:$$\end{document} = −34.29, p < 0.001) [32]. In Oliveira et al. [32], working more hours per week was associated with lower scores in teamwork climate (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:\:$$\end{document}= −0.53, p = 0.02), while Viksveen et al. [27] found no association with safety culture (no estimates were reported). Outcomes related to safety culture

Safety culture was analyzed in association with the outcomes of person-centered care [31], global patient safety grade rating [26, 34], and the global reporting of patient safety events [26]. Higher scores in the dimensions staffing, feedback and communication about incidents, and communication openness were significantly associated with higher levels of person-centered care [31] (see Table 6). Ree and Wiig [34] observed that higher scores in the dimensions teamwork, staffing, and handoffs were associated with a higher global patient safety grade rating, while Silverglow et al. [26] found that the dimensions communication openness and management support for patient safety were associated with a higher global patient safety grade rating. In addition, in Silverglow et al. [26], higher scores on feedback and communication about errors and management support for patient safety were associated with a higher global reporting of patient safety events.

Discussion

In this scoping review, we mapped and summarized how safety culture has been measured in home care, the dimensions that have been assessed, and the related factors and outcomes that have been analyzed. Despite the growing importance of home care, few studies have assessed safety culture and analyzed its associated factors and outcomes in home care.

Characteristics of the included studies

Different adaptations of five self-reported instruments and one qualitative method (interview) were used to assess safety culture in home care. The predominant use of quantitative methods in home care is in line with previous studies conducted in other healthcare settings. For example, a review of safety culture assessment tools in healthcare identified only one tool that used qualitative methods [13]. In a study on safety culture in hospitals, Churruca et al. [18] found that 95.5% (n = 663) of studies used surveys. The choice of quantitative over qualitative methods might be due to the practicability, anonymity, comparability, and possibility of administering them on large samples and the rapid collection and analysis of the data [39, 40]. Regardless of the advantages of using questionnaires, they also have disadvantages: their ability to provide knowledge on “deep aspects” such as safety culture has been regarded as limited [41]. Adding qualitative methods to quantitative data can allow for a more in-depth understanding of safety culture [39]. For example, Granel et al. [42] used mixed methods of questionnaires, in-depth interviews, and observations to assess safety culture in hospitals.

In addition to the lack of qualitative methods, we did not find any longitudinal or experimental studies, which reflects a lack of the evaluation of safety culture over time and a lack of implementing and evaluating interventions to change safety culture in home care.

Instruments used to measure safety culture

The most frequently used instruments were the adaptations of the NHSOPSC [43] and the SAQ-SF [44]. None of these instruments were specifically designed for home care settings. The main adaptations made for the home care setting were minor wording changes. Due to the unique nature of home care settings, items developed for other settings may not be sufficiently targeted or relevant. For example, the item “resident’s needs are met during shift changes” from the NHSOPSC [43] could be difficult to interpret in the context of home care, as in home care there is no formal shift change or handover as in a nursing home, and visits and shift changes don’t occur simultaneously. The home care worker finishes the patient visit and the next worker starts the next visit. Another item refers to “transfers from hospital”; however, for home care settings, transfers from other settings (e.g., short nursing home stays) may also be relevant. Unlike institutional settings, the work environment of home care workers is unique and varies from home to home. Because home care workers provide care mostly alone in clients’ homes, this limits direct observation of coworkers’ behaviors and shared work practices. As a result, when asked, for example, about teamwork, home care workers may interpret the construct differently than workers in institutional settings. These interpretations may involve collaboration with supervisors, interactions with colleagues, or the way tasks, materials, and information are handed over within the team. The assessment of safety culture in home care may thus benefit from a greater tailoring of the existing instruments to this setting.

Included dimensions

In all 12 quantitative studies, the instruments assessed different dimensions. Teamwork, management, and staffing/working conditions were assessed in all the instruments. Communication and organizational learning were assessed in all the instruments except in the SAQ-SF. Other dimensions were assessed less frequently. For instance, job satisfaction and stress recognition were only assessed in the Safety Attitudes Questionnaire (short-form and ambulatory version). These results may reflect a lack of consensus on what dimensions are necessary to assess safety culture. For example, one study has suggested that the dimension stress recognition, which is assessed in the SAQ-SF [44], does not fit into the construct of patient safety culture [45]. Cooper argued that components such as job satisfaction are “opaque individual psychological components” that are distracting in safety culture research [46] (p.15). A systematic review of studies assessing safety culture in hospitals identified 11 safety culture themes: (1) leadership, (2) perceptions of safety, (3) teamwork and collaboration, (4) safety systems, (5) priorization of safety, (6) resources and constraints, (7) reporting and just culture, (8) openness, (9) learning and improvement, (10) awareness and human limits, and 11) well-being [18]. A smaller number of studies in the systematic review [18] measured the dimension well-being, which in our scoping review was only found in studies that used versions of the Safety Attitudes Questionnaire [44]. One challenge we see with the inclusion of well-being or other similar dimensions is recognizing when we are measuring safety culture and when we are measuring another related construct. Further research is needed to make a clear distinction between what constitutes safety culture dimensions and associated factors or outcomes.

Characteristics of the included instruments

The included studies that used instruments validated for nursing homes or hospital settings, mainly reported internal consistency or no psychometric properties. However, validated instruments might not be valid and reliable in different contexts or populations. Analytical approaches, such as confirmatory factor analysis, provide important evidence concerning the equivalence of the internal structure of an instrument when it is administered in a different context, such as home care [47]. In addition, further tests of validity, such as criterion validity and construct validity, are essential to strengthen the interpretability and meaningfulness of the measurement results.

Regarding the calculation of the safety culture scores, we found some variation. Even among studies that used the same instrument, some calculated mean scores per dimension, while others calculated overall mean scores, and others calculated the percentage of positive responses. Although each scoring system provides important, complementary information, the differences in operationalization and in the reporting of results hinder their comparison. For instance, a study on the variability of scoring strategies that used the Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture [48] found that the results differed based on the scoring method chosen (e.g., percentage of positive responses vs. averaged individual means). Scorying by the percentage of positive responses tended to yield lower values compared to averaged individual means [49]. A consistent scoring approach of the scale scores would enable home care organizations to compare their results, learn from each other, and identify gaps and areas for improvement. Making data accessible as open data would reduce the impact of these differences in operationalization and enable researchers and organizations to reanalyze and compare results consistently across studies.

Factors and outcomes related to safety culture

A small number of studies (n = 7) assessed factors related to safety culture. This may be explained by the fact that the home care setting has received little attention in research compared to other healthcare settings. To develop strategies to change the safety culture in home care, assessing the safety culture and identifying related factors can provide useful information.

The included studies assessed individual and work-related factors as related to safety culture with inconclusive results. This may be due to the lack of use of a theoretical framework, use of different instruments, omission of important covariates in the regression models, use of univariate statistical tests, or use of samples with different characteristics and small sizes.

Nevertheless, the studies offer first insights into safety culture in home care. One study reported a positive relationship between transformational leadership and safety culture, which is consistent with results from other healthcare settings, such as nursing homes [50]. This highlights the potential beneficial impact of transformational leadership in improving safety culture. The WHO Patient Safety Action Plan 2021–2030 further underscores the importance of having leaders who are both skilled and passionate about promoting the right conditions and team cultures for safer care. Leaders should ensure that all systems and procedures meet the highest standards while guiding and motivating the staff to achieve this goal [1].

As most home care staff spend most of their time providing care and services in patients’ homes, the physical distance between staff and leaders can limit their interactions. This distance can make it more challenging to maintain regular and consistent communication of a shared vision for safety, complicating efforts to guide and motivate staff compared with other healthcare settings. The use of digital communication tools can help bridge this distance and improve communication, but it may not be sufficient on its own.

None of the included studies reported interventions for improving safety culture. A scoping review of strategies for improving safety culture in other healthcare settings identified strategies related to communication as the most prevalent, followed by teamwork and active leadership [51]. Given the physical distance between team members in home care, strategies require substantial contextualization and innovative approaches tailored to home care.

In the identified studies, safety culture dimensions were associated with the outcomes person-centered care, patient safety grade rating, and the global reporting of patient safety events. No studies have analyzed patient-reported or patient safety outcomes. Examples of outcomes could include quality indicators, adverse events, patient-reported experiences, or quality of care ratings. The WHO Global Patient Safety Action Plan 2021–2030 calls on healthcare facilities to encourage patients and families to report their experiences and safety outcomes, to establish patient safety reporting mechanisms, and to collect, analyze, and develop action plans for learning and improvement [1]. Since patient engagement and empowerment are recognized as powerful tools for improving patient safety, it is important to include their views in the development of strategies to improve safety culture.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. One strength is the broad scope of the review, which identified not only instruments that assessed safety culture in home care but also related factors and outcomes, which allowed us to identify gaps and guide future research. The literature search was revised in collaboration with an experienced information specialist. It comprehensively covered five different databases with no time or language restrictions. We used a rigorous, systematic, and transparent method as documented in the review protocol. Screening the studies and data charting were conducted by two reviewers independently and in duplicate, enhancing the reliability of the results.

Despite our rigorous approach, one limitation of the study is the remaining risk for selection and publication bias, as it is possible that we missed some relevant papers. We did not search the gray literature, so we might have missed some studies. This exclusion may have reduced the comprehensiveness and completeness of the synthesized evidence. Maybe some primary care studies also included staff providing home care services, but we did not include such studies unless they stated this explicitly. This might have led to the exclusion of studies that also assessed safety culture in home care workers. As no quality assessment of the included studies was performed, the findings of this study should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

This review describes the current state of how patient safety culture has been measured in home care, highlights the dimensions assessed, and provides insights into related factors and outcomes that can help identify key aspects to monitor and guide the development of strategies to strengthen safety culture. Different instruments that were developed for other settings were used to assess safety culture in home care. Discrepancies were found in the dimensions included in the different safety culture instruments, highlighting the need for a clearer understanding of safety culture. Further research using instruments with more setting-specific items that reflect the unique characteristics of home care is needed. Study designs, such as mixed methods and longitudinal designs, should be considered as they may provide deeper insights into safety culture and better inform strategies for improving safety culture in home care. Evidence on the associated factors and outcomes of safety culture in home care is limited and inconclusive. Further investigation of the related factors and outcomes of safety culture in home care, including patient-reported and patient safety outcomes, is needed to inform strategies for improving safety culture in home care settings.
