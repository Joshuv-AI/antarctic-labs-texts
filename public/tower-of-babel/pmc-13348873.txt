Would you perform this procedure under general anesthesia? A scenario-based comparison of dentist and anesthesiologist approaches
Authors: Tuba Çatak, Mustafa Üstün, Müzeyyen Dilşah Demiray, Fatma Selenay Uçaş Yıldız
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGeneral anesthesia is increasingly utilized in dentistry; however, interdisciplinary variability in general anesthesia (GA) indication and risk perception remains insufficiently characterized. This study quantitatively compared dentists’ and anesthesiologists’ decisions regarding general anesthesia indication using standardized clinical scenarios.MethodsThis cross-sectional, scenario-based reader study involved 50 anesthesiologists and 50 dentists who each evaluated 20 standardized clinical scenarios, generating 2,000 decisions. For each scenario, participants selected the anesthesia method (general anesthesia vs. local anesthesia) and classified GA-related risk (low vs. high). A binary generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) was used to identify factors associated with GA indication and GA risk perception. Inter-rater agreement was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa and prevalence-adjusted bias-adjusted kappa (PABAK).ResultsAcross all evaluations, GA was selected in 44.1% of assessments. Anesthesiologists indicated GA more frequently than dentists (48% vs. 40.2%). Specialty was a significant determinant of GA indication, with a pronounced specialty × age interaction (p = 0.009). Pediatric scenarios—particularly those involving impaired cooperation and neurodevelopmental comorbidities—were more frequently associated with GA indication. Perceived GA risk did not differ significantly between specialties, although greater clinical experience reduced perceived GA risk among dentists (p < 0.001). Agreement was moderate for anesthesia method (κ = 0.476) and lower for GA risk (κ = 0.395).ConclusionsDentists and anesthesiologists differ significantly in GA indication despite similar risk perception frameworks. Findings highlight the need for standardized GA decision support tools, improved interdisciplinary communication, and evidence-based patient selection criteria in dental anesthesia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03916-7.

Background

General anesthesia (GA) for dental treatment is intended for patients with serious medical conditions, those requiring extensive procedures, and those who are unable to cooperate [1]. Although the number of patients treated under GA is steadily increasing [2, 3]; disabilities, the presence of comorbid conditions, underlying medical reasons, and possible drug interactions render these patients a high-risk group for GA. The rate of anesthesia-related complications in these patients has been reported to be 20%–30%, and given these circumstances, it is necessary to exercise great caution when planning dental treatment under GA [4, 5]. Considering the costs associated with GA, a comprehensive evaluation of patient suitability for the procedure is of great importance [6]. The American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry also advocates for the prudent use of GA [7].

Although national guidelines define indications and contraindications for GA, these indications are not absolute and often rely more on individual clinical judgments than on objective criteria [8–10]. To our knowledge, no study to date has directly compared dentists’ and anesthesiologists’ opinions on decisions regarding GA indication. The primary hypothesis of this study is that, when presented with the same clinical scenarios, dentists and anesthesiologists make different decisions regarding the indication for GA and that these differences are associated with various factors, such as patient age, systemic disease status, and level of cooperation. We further hypothesized that dentists may tend to interpret indications for GA more broadly or more narrowly than anesthesiologists and that this tendency necessitates the need for standardization in multidisciplinary clinical decision-making processes. This study aimed to compare, using standardized clinical scenarios, dentists’ and anesthesiologists’ decisions on the indication for GA in dental procedures, to compare their perceptual risk classifications concerning GA, and to quantify possible differences between the two disciplines. Additionally, the study explored how patient characteristics within scenario contexts may relate to decision-making patterns.

Materials and methods

Study design

This research is a descriptive-analytical, cross-sectional survey study.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Non-Drug Medical Devices and Drugs Research Ethics Committee of Necmettin Erbakan University Faculty of Dentistry (Application ID: 28020) (KONYA/TURKEY) (2025/694). Participation in the study was voluntary, and the participants’ identities were anonymized. The participants were informed that they had the right to withdraw from the study at any stage.

Participants

The study was conducted at a national level and included dentists and anesthesiologists practicing across Türkiye. Participants were working in various healthcare settings, including university hospitals, training and research hospitals, public hospitals, and private or specialty hospitals. The sample comprised academic physicians, specialists, and general practitioners.

Inclusion criteria (Dentists): Dentists who consented to participate voluntarily and who performed dental treatment under GA in their clinical practice were included in the survey.

Inclusion criteria (Anesthesiologists): Anesthesiologists who actively worked clinically with patients undergoing dental treatment and who consented to participate voluntarily were included. Physicians who did not perform procedures under GA and those who did not consent to participate were excluded from the study.

Data collection instruments

Data were collected via two questionnaires developed on the basis of a literature review and consultations with dentists and anesthesiologists who perform dental GA. The questionnaires for dentists and anesthesiologists comprised 23 and 22 items, respectively; 20 clinical scenarios were common to both questionnaires. The remaining items covered professional experience and work area. The questionnaires were specifically developed to evaluate interdisciplinary differences in decision-making regarding indications for GA in dental procedures. The scenarios were constructed taking into account clinical variables that influence the decision for dental GA (age, type of procedure, cooperation, comorbidity, systemic status, neurodevelopmental/psychiatric conditions, pregnancy, obesity, obstructive sleep apnea syndrome [OSAS], etc.). These scenarios included pediatric, adult, and geriatric groups. This study was not based on a factorial design. The scenarios were constructed to reflect holistic clinical decision-making contexts rather than to disentangle the independent effects of specific clinical variables. Characteristics such as comorbidity, cooperation, psychiatric status, and procedural scope were not coded as independent covariates but were treated as integrated components of each scenario. Age was included as a fixed effect because it was the only quantitatively and systematically varying parameter across scenarios. With this approach, the selections of participants for GA and their risk perceptions were assessed in a realistic yet standardized framework. The study examined perceptual risk classification related to GA rather than objectively measuring clinical risk. In this study, GA risk categories were operationally defined as follows: low GA risk corresponded to ASA physical status I–II with an anticipated easy airway, whereas high GA risk was defined as ASA III–IV and/or the presence of a difficult airway.

Content validity was evaluated by three independent experts (one anesthesiologist and two dentists). The items were rated using a four-point Likert scale (1 = not appropriate, 4 = completely appropriate). Items with a content validity index (CVI) of < 0.80 were revised. Cronbach’s alpha was not applied because the questionnaires consisted of independent items measuring different dimensions. The questionnaires were specifically developed for this study by one anesthesiologist and two dentists with clinical experience in dental general anesthesia and have not been previously published. Content validity was assessed using a four-point Likert scale, and only items with a CVI value greater than 0.80 were included in the final questionnaires. The English versions of the questionnaires are provided as Supplementary Material 4,5 (supplementary material 4: questionnaire designed for anesthesiology and reanimation physicians, supplementary material 5: questionnaire designed for dentists). The original language of the questionnaires was Turkish. Their English versions were prepared solely for publication purposes, and no official back-translation was performed. Data were collected online via Google Forms, and the participants were recruited using a snowball sampling method.

Statistical analysis

In this study, 50 anesthesiologists and 50 dentists each evaluated 20 clinical scenarios and reported, for each scenario, the anesthesia method they deemed appropriate (GA or local anesthesia [LA]) and the perceived risk level related to GA. The GA-related risk perception model was fitted only for scenarios in which the participant selected general anesthesia as the indicated anesthesia method. Thus, the risk analysis was conditional upon GA indication. GA risk perception was therefore analyzed as a binary variable (low vs. high), conditional upon GA indication. All scenarios were evaluated independently by participants, thereby creating a nested (clustered) data structure due to multiple scenarios being answered by the same physician and the same scenario being evaluated by different physicians. This dependency was accounted for in the analyses.

Firstly, physician and scenario characteristics were presented descriptively. The distribution of anesthesia methods by specialty group was reported as frequencies and percentages. In the subgroup limited to physicians who considered GA as a viable alternative, the distribution of GA risk levels by scenario and specialty was examined.

A binary generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with a logistic (logit) link function was used to investigate the factors influencing the indication for GA and GA risk assessment. In the model, the dependent variable was the GA method; physician specialty, experience, the age of the scenario patient, and their interactions were included as fixed effects; random intercepts at the physician and scenario (patient) levels were added to the model to control for dependency. Results were reported as coefficient (β), odds ratio (OR), 95% confidence interval (95% CI), and Wald z. The overall significance of fixed effects was evaluated by chi-square tests. For significant interactions, stratified simple effect analyses and estimated marginal probabilities were calculated.

Inter-rater agreement was assessed using coefficients appropriate to the measurement level of the variable. Cohen’s kappa (κ) and the prevalence-adjusted bias-adjusted kappa (PABAK) were calculated for the anesthesia method and GA risk level. Observed agreement (Pₒ) and its 95% CI were obtained using the bootstrap-percentile method with 1,000 replications. All tests were conducted at a significance level of α = 0.05. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 24.0, Jamovi 2.4, Microsoft Excel 2016, and RStudio 2023.09.0.

Sample size and statistical power

In this study, GLMMs with a binary logistic link (binary logistic GLMM) were used for the binary outcome variable. Because there is no closed-form power analysis for mixed models, the sample size was determined using an upper-bound approach based on logistic regression. A priori power analysis was performed with G*Power 3.1.9.7: a two-sided test, α = 0.05, target power (1 − β) = 0.95, and OR = 1.5 representing a medium effect size were assumed. Under these assumptions, the minimum required sample was calculated as 1,837 observations, yielding an expected power greater than 95%. However, this a priori power calculation was based on standard logistic regression and did not account for clustering at the physician and scenario levels. In the fitted GLMM, the estimated ICCs from random-intercept variance components were 0.15 for physician (variance = 0.56) and 0.55 for scenario/case (variance = 3.98), with the logistic residual variance fixed at π²/3 = 3.29.

In the final study, 50 dentists and 50 anesthesiologists each evaluated 20 clinical scenarios, producing a total of 2,000 observations. Thus, the obtained number of observations exceeded the a priori requirement, and the study provided an adequate level of statistical power.

Results

In order to enable anesthesiologists and dentists to evaluate the anesthesia method they considered appropriate and their perceived GA risk level, 20 different clinical scenarios were discussed in the study (Table 1). Each clinical scenario was accompanied by four standardized response options, which were uniformly applied to all 20 scenarios. The standardized response options were as follows: A.The procedure should be performed under general anesthesia; the GA-related risk is low. B.The procedure is more appropriate under local anesthesia; the GA-related risk is high. C.Despite a high GA-related risk, the procedure should be performed under general anesthesia. D.The procedure can be performed under local anesthesia regardless of GA-related risk.

To evaluate the factors affecting the indication for GA (1: general anesthesia, 0: local anesthesia) while accounting for variability at the physician and patient levels, a binary GLMM with a logistic link was used (Table 4). The model included random intercepts for physician and patient (scenario); physician specialty, experience, scenario patient age, and their interaction terms were included as fixed effects. Physician specialty had a significant effect on the indication for GA. Dentists were less likely than anesthesiologists to indicate GA (β = −0.833, OR = 0.435, 95% CI: 0.285–0.662, p < 0.001). The correlation between GA indication and the scenario patient’s age was marginally statistically significant. Adult scenarios were less likely to be indicated for GA than pediatric scenarios (β = −2.043, OR = 0.130, 95% CI: 0.017–0.994, p = 0.049).

Discussion

The results obtained in the present study indicate that specialty is a statistically significant determinant of the decision to indicate GA for dental treatments. Anesthesiologists were significantly more likely to indicate GA for the same clinical scenarios compared to dentists. This difference might be attributable to divergent risk–benefit perceptions and areas of competency between the two professional groups. Previous studies also report that GA indication is not absolute, considerable variability exists among practitioners, and decisions often depend on clinical preferences rather than objective criteria [10]. Although a significant difference in GA indication between the two specialties was identified in the present study, assessments converged in scenarios with increased clinical complexity. This finding suggests that under critical clinical conditions, both disciplines prioritize patient safety and adopt similar safety-oriented approaches when deciding about GA use. Indeed, it has previously been emphasized that decisions on the use of GA should involve a holistic evaluation of individual, surgical, and anesthesia-related criteria, and such decisions must be made in a multidisciplinary manner [11]. Nevertheless, current literature indicates a lack of universally accepted protocols for GA use in dental procedures [12]. Therefore, the standardization of GA indications in dental care and the development of decision algorithms have been advocated [13]. Our findings corroborate the current need to standardize decision processes for GA indication in dental procedures and to develop clinically applicable algorithms. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution in light of the study design. The scenario-based approach, while enabling standardized comparison, may not fully capture the complexity of real-world clinical decision-making. Furthermore, the cross-sectional nature of the study precludes causal inference, and the observed associations should not be interpreted as cause–effect relationships.

In the present study, pediatric patients—particularly scenarios involving impaired cooperation and neurodevelopmental or psychiatric comorbidities—were more frequently considered appropriate for GA by both physician groups. This finding indicates that GA is more often indicated on clinical grounds in pediatric cases and that patient age is a significant determinant in GA indication decisions. In adult scenarios, the likelihood of indicating GA was lower than in pediatric scenarios. From the anesthesiologists’ perspective, general anesthesia ensures airway control and perioperative stability, whereas for dentists it provides the opportunity to complete comprehensive treatment in a single, controlled session. Although these disciplines operate with different clinical priorities, both may regard general anesthesia as a more feasible solution in pediatric cases. This finding suggests that age functions not merely as a biological variable, but also as a contextual factor that shapes the clinical decision-making framework. This result corroborates the finding of previous studies reporting that a substantial proportion of patients undergoing dental treatment under GA are children requiring treatment for early childhood caries [2–16]. Moreover, the literature reports a clear increasing trend in the use of general anesthesia for pediatric dental procedures over time. Between 2001 and 2024, a total of 4,062 pediatric patients underwent dental treatment under general anesthesia, representing 1.59% of all admissions. Importantly, the proportion of dental treatments performed under general anesthesia has shown a significant rise over the years. This upward trend suggests that general anesthesia is becoming an increasingly preferred approach in pediatric dental care and supports the age-related decision patterns observed in our study, indicating consistency with existing literature [3].

A substantial proportion of mortality associated with dental procedures performed under anesthesia in children has been reported to occur in early childhood [17]. In addition, early exposure to GA might adversely affect neurodevelopmental outcomes [18]. In line with these risks, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration has issued a warning that prolonged or repeated use of anesthetic and sedative agents in children younger than three years of age may affect brain development [19]. These data point to the need to reevaluate behavior-management strategies in pediatric dental cases and to limit GA to appropriately selected patients [20]. Consequently, the approach wherein GA should be considered a last resort when conventional methods have failed is widely accepted [21, 22]. However, the present study revealed that physicians’ perceived risk associated with GA was more pronounced in adult scenarios—particularly those with comorbidities—than in pediatric scenarios. This finding suggests that the risks of dental GA are defined along different axes in different patient groups: in the pediatric population, risk is primarily framed in terms of neurodevelopmental vulnerability and anesthetic sensitivity, whereas in adults, it is mainly framed in terms of comorbidity and perioperative physiological burden. It should be noted, however, that the framework used in this study to categorize GA-related risk may not fully capture the multidimensional and context-dependent nature of clinical risk assessment in routine practice.

No significant difference was found between specialty groups in terms of physicians’ perceived GA risk. This finding suggests that physicians who consider GA as an option conduct risk assessments within a similar clinical framework irrespective of specialty. In scenarios wherein systemic risk was prominent, both groups largely considered GA to be high-risk and preferred LA, indicating that patient safety was prioritized in the decision-making process. The literature emphasizes the critical importance of risk assessment in patients considered for GA in dental procedures [4, 23]. A previous study reported that dental treatment under GA is not recommended in high-risk patients [24]. In this context, although risk perception differed between specialties for some scenarios, the similarity of anesthesia method choices in clearly high-risk situations suggests that decision-making is shaped by a scenario-based risk–benefit assessment rather than by a simple binary (yes/no) approach.

Stratified analyses showed that physician experience was not a determinant of GA risk assessment among anesthesiologists, whereas it was a significant determinant among dentists. The observation that increasing professional experience among dentists was associated with a lower likelihood of rating GA as high-risk indicates a more selective appraisal of risk factors; however, it is not necessarily desirable in all clinical circumstances. In line with these findings, previous studies have reported that dentists’ knowledge of indications, contraindications, and potential complications of GA and appropriate patient selection are critical for reducing complication risk [17, 25, 26].

These findings should be interpreted in light of several methodological considerations. The clinical scenarios used in this study were not externally validated or pilot-tested, which may limit their representativeness. In addition, key clinical determinants—such as comorbidity burden, patient cooperation, and procedural complexity—were embedded within the scenarios rather than modeled as independent covariates, thereby limiting the ability to isolate their individual effects. Furthermore, the use of snowball sampling may have introduced selection bias, as participants were recruited through professional networks, potentially affecting the generalizability of the findings.

Clinical implications

This study fills an important clinical gap by quantitatively demonstrating interdisciplinary differences in indications for GA in dental procedures across multiple variables, including age group, clinical patient characteristics, years of professional experience, and specialty. The increasing use of dental treatment under GA necessitates the examination of the decision processes of the clinicians primarily responsible—anesthesiologists and dentists—when confronted with the same clinical scenarios. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to quantitatively compare the two disciplines’ approaches and risk assessments with respect to dental GA cases. These findings underscore the need to develop standards for patient selection, risk appraisal, and decision-making in dental GA practice, and they support the clinical validity of a multidisciplinary approach. Clinically, these results substantiate the need for guidelines and decision-support tools that will homogenize decision processes, facilitate interdisciplinary communication, and strengthen patient safety.

Limitations

Because clinical decisions in this study were evaluated using standardized scenarios rather than real patients, the variability of decision-making in real-world clinical contexts may not be fully reflected. In addition, as the study was conducted within a single healthcare system, the generalizability of the results to practices in other countries may be limited. The clinical characteristics embedded within the scenarios (e.g., comorbidity, cooperation level, and procedural complexity) were not modeled as independent covariates in the statistical analysis. As a result, the findings primarily reflect overall decision-making patterns rather than the isolated effects of individual clinical determinants. This was a deliberate design choice, as the study aimed to evaluate specialty-related decision-making within holistic clinical contexts rather than to perform a variable-centered causal analysis. However, this approach limits the ability to quantify the independent contribution of specific clinical factors, and future studies using variable-centered or factorial designs may provide more detailed insights. The use of snowball sampling may have introduced selection bias, as participants were recruited through professional networks. Individuals with similar clinical experiences or decision-making tendencies may have been more likely to participate. Additionally, the sample was limited to clinicians actively performing dental general anesthesia, which may have increased the likelihood of including individuals with similar clinical experience and decision-making patterns. This may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader populations of anesthesiologists and dentists, particularly those who do not routinely practice dental GA. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution in terms of external validity. The clinical scenarios were not formally validated through an independent pilot study or external expert panel, which represents a limitation of this study.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that decisions regarding indications for GA in dental procedures differ between dentists and anesthesiologists and are shaped by scenario-based risk–benefit assessments. The results highlight the need for clinical decision algorithms that standardize GA-related decisions and support safe patient selection.
