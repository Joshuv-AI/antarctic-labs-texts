Introduction

A balancing game is a perfect information game played by two players; the Pusher (he) and the Chooser (she). Given a set
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a convex set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the balancing game
G(V,K)
proceeds through a sequence of points
z_{0}=0,z_{1},z_{2},\ldots\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In round
k
of the game Pusher offers a vector
v_{k}\in V
and Chooser selects
\varepsilon_{k}\in\{-1,1\}
and the next position is
z_{k+1}=z_{k}+\varepsilon_{k}v_{k}
. Pusher wins if he can make
z_{k}\not\in K
for some
k
and Chooser wins if she can maintain
z_{k}\in K
for all
k
. Balancing games in various forms were introduced by Spencer
[
9
]
in 1979.

In this paper, we consider the case when
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is finite.

Definition 1.1
.

A set
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called

-closed
if
t\in T
and

imply that either

or

.

Define the set

.
P(V)
is clearly

-closed and so are its translates. Moreover,
P(V)
is centrally symmetric with respect to its center which is the same as its center of gravity. We denote it by
g(V)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in V}v
. In a balancing game one often has to check if
g(V)\in P(V)
or not. According to
[
6
]
(see also
[
5
]
) it is NP-hard to decide if

even if

consists of positive integers.

The connection between

-closed sets and balancing games is based on the following fact. If some

lies in a

-closed set

, then Chooser can guarantee that

for all

. This is basically the only strategy that Chooser can use to win.

Theorem 1.2

(
[

, Theorems 1 and 2]
)

The game

1.

Pusher has a winning strategy if and only if there is no

-closed set

with

.

2.

Chooser has a winning strategy if and only if there is a
V
-closed set
T
with
0\in T\subseteq K
.

We derive some properties of

-closed sets under the condition that

V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\mbox{ is finite and contains no parallel vectors}.

Theorem 1.5
.

If

different values of

Assume

a^{*}
can not separate
x-p+q
and
C
.

This finishes the proof of the claim and of Theorem
1.3
∎

4
A special balancing game

In this section, we analyze the game
G(V,K_{M})
for
V=\left\{v\in\{-1,1\}^{n}:v_{1}=1\right\}
, where
K_{M}
is defined as
\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{i}\leq M\mbox{ for all }i\in[n]\}
, and prove Theorem
1.4
. First, we note the following fact which distinguishes the case when
n
is a power of 2.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
n
be even. Then
\frac{1}{2}\binom{n}{n/2}
is an odd integer if
n
is a power of 2, and an even integer otherwise.

Proof.

Let
k
be the largest integer such that
n\geq 2^{k}
. For an integer
x
, denote by
\nu_{2}(x)
the largest integer
d
such that
2^{d}\mid x
. Then
\nu_{2}(n!)=\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor n/4\right\rfloor+\cdots+\left\lfloor n/2^{k}\right\rfloor
. Thus,

\displaystyle\nu_{2}\left(\binom{n}{n/2}\right)=\nu_{2}(n)-2\nu_{2}(n/2)

\displaystyle=(\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor-2\left\lfloor n/4\right\rfloor)+(\left\lfloor n/4\right\rfloor-2\left\lfloor n/8\right\rfloor)+\cdots+(\left\lfloor n/2^{k-1}\right\rfloor-2\left\lfloor n/2^{k}\right\rfloor)+\left\lfloor n/2^{k}\right\rfloor\geq 1,

with equality if and only if
\left\lfloor n/2^{i}\right\rfloor-2\left\lfloor n/2^{i+1}\right\rfloor=0
for
i=1,\ldots,k-1
. This is equivalent to
n
being a power of 2.
∎

We begin with the strategy for Pusher.

4.1
Pusher’s strategy

By Theorem
1.2
, we only need to show that if there exists a
V
-closed set
T
with
0\in T\subseteq K_{M}
, then
M
must be at least the values given in Theorem
1.4
.

Suppose there exists a
V
-closed set
T
with
0\in T\subseteq K_{M}
, for some
M\in\mathbb{Z}
. The intersection of
T
with
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is also
V
-closed, so we may assume that
T\subset\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, so that
T
is closed (even discrete). Let
C=\cl\conv T
. Since
C\subseteq K_{M}
, it contains no line. Let
\mathbf{1}=(1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
be the all-ones vector. Let
H
be the supporting hyperplane of
C
perpendicular to
\mathbf{1}
, so
H
is of the form
\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x\cdot\mathbf{1}=s\right\}
where
s\geq 0
is the smallest real number such that
C
lies in the half-space
\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x\cdot\mathbf{1}\leq s\right\}
. Here
s\geq 0
follows from
0\in T\subset C
.

Let
C^{\prime}=C\cap H
. Since
C
is closed and convex and
H
is a supporting hyperplane,
C^{\prime}
is non-empty and convex. Since
C^{\prime}\subseteq H\cap K_{M}
is bounded, there is an extreme point
x
of
C^{\prime}
, hence is also an extreme point of
C
. By Theorem
1.3
, there is a translation vector
t\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
x\in t+P(V)\subset C\subset K_{M}
. Since
x
is an extreme point, we have
x\in T\subset\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

We now have a translate
t+P(V)
which intersects the hyperplane
H
and lies completely in
K_{M}
. We note that the smallest cube containing
P(V)
is a translate of
[0,2^{n-1}]^{n}
. This follows from the fact (which is easy to check) that the width of
P(V)
in direction
e_{i}
is
2^{n-1}
for every
i\in[n]
. Then
P(V)
touches all
2n
facets of this cube. It is then clear that if a translate of
P(V)
is contained in
K_{M}
, then the smallest cube containing this translate is also contained in
K_{M}
. Thus the furthest a translate of
P(V)
can be along the
\mathbf{1}
direction is when it is touching all
n
facets of
K_{M}
. Thus, computing the translate of
P(V)
touching all
n
faces of
K_{M}
is a simple task. However, we will show that this translate is not far enough along
\mathbf{1}
to touch
H
for our choice of
M
.

Since
t+P(V)\in K_{M}
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
, we have
t_{i}+\max_{u\in P(V)}u_{i}\leq M
. Recall that
V=\left\{v\in\{-1,1\}^{n}:v_{1}=1\right\}
. For
i=1
, this implies that
t_{1}\leq M-|V|=M-2^{n-1}
. For
i>1
, we have
t_{i}\leq M-2^{n-2}
. We now compute

r:=\max_{u\in P(V)}(u\cdot\mathbf{1})=\sum_{v\in V,v\cdot\mathbf{1}>0}v\cdot\mathbf{1}.

When
n
is odd,

r=\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}(n-2j)\binom{n-1}{j}=\frac{n}{2}\binom{n-1}{\frac{n-1}{2}}+2^{n-2}.

When
n
is even,

r=\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{n}{2}-1}(n-2j)\binom{n-1}{j}=\frac{n}{4}\binom{n}{n/2}+2^{n-2}=\frac{n}{2}\binom{n-1}{n/2}+2^{n-2}.

Since
t+P(V)
intersects
H
, there exists
u\in P(V)
such that
(t+u)\cdot\mathbf{1}=s\geq 0
. On the other hand,

(t+u)\cdot\mathbf{1}\leq nM-(n+1)2^{n-2}+u\cdot\mathbf{1}\leq nM-(n+1)2^{n-2}+r.

Thus, we have

M\geq 2^{n-2}+\frac{1}{n}(2^{n-2}-r)=\begin{cases}2^{n-2}-\frac{1}{2}\binom{n-1}{\frac{n-1}{2}}&\text{if $n$ is odd,}\\
2^{n-2}-\frac{1}{2}\binom{n-1}{n/2}&\text{if $n$ is even.}\end{cases}

Since
M\in\mathbb{Z}
, we get the desired bounds in Theorem
1.4
for all
n
, noting that when
n
is a power of 2, by Lemma
4.1
,
\binom{n-1}{n/2}=\frac{1}{2}\binom{n}{n/2}
is odd. Then,
M\geq\left\lceil 2^{n-2}-\frac{1}{2}\binom{n-1}{n/2}\right\rceil=2^{n-2}-\frac{1}{2}\binom{n-1}{n/2}+\frac{1}{2}
.

4.2
Chooser’s strategy

Next, we construct Chooser’s winning strategy in Theorem
1.4
. From Theorem
1.2
, her strategy is to simply construct a
V
-closed set
T
such that
0\in T\subset K_{M}
.
Note that it suffices to find a translation vector
t\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
0\in t+P(V)\subset K_{M}
. Our translate can be described as follows. First, center
P(V)
at the origin, then translate it along the direction
-\mathbf{1}
for as long as the convex hull of the translated
P(V)
contains the origin. If
n
is not a power of 2, then this translate of
P(V)
will contain the origin. If
n
is a power of 2, then this translate of
P(V)
will not contain the origin for parity reasons, so we need a minor adjustment.

Write
\Sigma(V)=\sum_{v\in V}v=2^{n-1}e_{1}
. Recall that the center of
P(V)
is
g(V)=\frac{1}{2}\Sigma(V)
. We consider the translate

P(V)-g(V)=\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in V}\varepsilon_{v}v:\varepsilon_{v}\in\{-1,1\}\right\}.

(2)

The center of
P(V)-g(V)
is the origin. We now deal with the even and odd cases in Theorem
1.4
separately.

Case 1.
When
n
is odd. Set
M=2^{n-2}-\frac{1}{2}\binom{n-1}{\frac{n-1}{2}}
. We claim that

0\in P(V)-g(V)-\frac{1}{2}\binom{n-1}{\frac{n-1}{2}}\mathbf{1}\subset K_{M}.

These two inclusions follows from (
2
) and the next two claims.

Claim 4.2
.

There exists
\varepsilon_{v}\in\{-1,1\}
for each
v\in V
such that
\sum_{v\in V}\varepsilon_{v}v=\binom{n-1}{\frac{n-1}{2}}\mathbf{1}
.

Proof.

For each
v\in V
, set

\varepsilon_{v}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\sum_{i}v_{i}>0,\\
-1&\text{if }\sum_{i}v_{i}<0,\end{cases}

noting that the sum cannot be 0 since
n
is odd. For each index
i
, the
i
-th coordinate of
\sum_{v\in V}\varepsilon_{v}v
is
A-B
, where

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\#\left\{v\in V:v_{i}=1,\ \textstyle\sum_{j}v_{j}>0\right\}=\sum_{k=\frac{n-1}{2}}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{k}

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\#\left\{v\in V:v_{i}=-1,\ \textstyle\sum_{j}v_{j}>0\right\}=\sum_{k=\frac{n-1}{2}+1}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{k}.

Thus,
A-B=\binom{n-1}{\frac{n-1}{2}}
and hence
\sum_{v\in V}\varepsilon_{v}v=\binom{n-1}{\frac{n-1}{2}}\mathbf{1}
.
∎

Claim 4.3
.

For any
\varepsilon_{v}\in\{-1,1\}
for each
v\in V
, we have
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in V}\varepsilon_{v}v-\frac{1}{2}\binom{n-1}{\frac{n-1}{2}}\mathbf{1}\subset K_{M}
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that for each index
i
, the
i
-coordinate of
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in V}\varepsilon_{v}v
is at most
2^{n-2}
. Indeed, this is true since each
\varepsilon_{v}v
contributes at most 1 to coordinate
i
and there are exactly
2^{n-1}
vectors in
V
.
∎

Case 2.
When
n
is even. This case is more complex due to the existence of the “middle layer”,
V_{0}=\left\{v\in V:\sum_{i}v_{i}=0\right\}
. Let
L\subset\mathbb{Z}^{n}
be the
(n-1)
-dimensional lattice generated by
V_{0}
. Then
P(V_{0})
lies in
L
, which can also be expressed as

L=\left\{u\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}:\sum_{i}u_{i}=0,\quad\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\}\text{ consists of integers that are all even or all odd}\right\}.

Alternatively,
L
is generated by the vectors
\{2(e_{1}-e_{2}),2(e_{1}-e_{3}),\ldots,2(e_{1}-e_{n}),w\}
, for any
w\in V_{0}
.

We show that
P(V_{0})
contains every vector in
L
near its center
g(V_{0})
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
R
be a positive integer such that

\binom{n-2}{\frac{n-2}{2}}>4R(n-1).

(3)

Then for any
u\in L
with
\left\lVert u-g(V_{0})\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq R-\frac{n}{2}
, we have
u\in P(V_{0})
.

Proof.

Recall that
P(V_{0})-g(V_{0})
consists of vectors of the form
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in V_{0}}\varepsilon_{v}v
. For
r>0
, write
B_{r}(v)=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\left\lVert x-v\right\rVert_{2}\leq r\right\}
for the ball of radius
r
centered at
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The proof strategy is to find
U\subseteq V_{0}
such that
P(U)
attains every vector in
L
up to radius
R
from its center. Then, using the remaining vectors
V_{0}\setminus U
, we find an element
x\in P(V_{0}\setminus U)
that is close to its center. Combining, we can attain from
P(V_{0})
every vector in
L
close to its center.

It will be convenient to define
V_{0}^{\pm}=V_{0}\cup-V_{0}=\left\{v\in\pm V:\sum_{i}v_{i}=0\right\}
and the equivalence relation
v\sim v^{\prime}
if
v=\pm v^{\prime}
. We say that
v,v^{\prime}
are
(\sim)
-distinct
if
v\nsim v^{\prime}
.

We shall find
2R(n-1)

(\sim)
-distinct vectors
v_{i}^{(j)+},v_{i}^{(j)-}\in V_{0}^{\pm}
, for
i=2,\ldots,n
and
j=1,\ldots,R
, such that
v_{i}^{(j)+}-v_{i}^{(j)-}=2(e_{1}-e_{i})
. We do this greedily, by finding a pair
v_{i}^{(j)+},v_{i}^{(j)-}
in any ordering of the pairs of indices
(i,j)
, that is distinct from all the other vectors we have found so far.

Suppose we have already found
m
pairs, and it is time to find
v_{i}^{(j)+},v_{i}^{(j)-}
for the
(m+1)
-th pair of indices
(i,j)
. Choose some
v_{i}^{(j)+}
so that
(v_{i}^{(j)+})_{1}=1
and
(v_{i}^{(j)+})_{i}=-1
, then set
v_{i}^{(j)-}
to be identical to
v_{i}^{(j)+}
, except its first and
i
-th coordinate have flipped signs. This satisfies
v_{i}^{(j)+}-v_{i}^{(j)-}=2(e_{1}-e_{i})
. Note that there are
\binom{n-2}{\frac{n-2}{2}}
ways to pick
v_{i}^{(j)+}
, at most
4m
of which will result in a collision between
v_{i}^{(j)+}
or
v_{i}^{(j)-}
with one of the
2m
vectors we previously found. By the assumption on
R
,

\binom{n-2}{\frac{n-2}{2}}>4R(n-1)\geq 4m,

thus this greedy process will be successful in finding all
2R(n-1)
vectors.

We now have
2R(n-1)

(\sim)
-distinct vectors
v_{i}^{(j)+},v_{i}^{(j)-}\in V_{0}^{\pm}
satisfying
v_{i}^{(j)+}-v_{i}^{(j)-}=2(e_{1}-e_{i})
for
i=2,\ldots,n
and
j=1,\ldots,R
. Pick any
w\in V_{0}^{\pm}
distinct from these, then set
U:=\left\{v_{2}^{(1)+},v_{2}^{(1)-},\ldots,v_{n}^{(R)+},v_{n}^{(R)-},w\right\}
.

The set
P(U)-g(U)
consists of vectors of the form
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{u\in U}\varepsilon_{u}u
. To exhibit many vectors from this set, we assert that we will use each pair
v_{i}^{(j)+},v_{i}^{(j)-}
with opposite signs, that is, either
v_{i}^{(j)+}-v_{i}^{(j)-}=2(e_{1}-e_{i})
or
v_{i}^{(j)-}-v_{i}^{(j)+}=2(e_{i}-e_{1})
. Then,
P(U)-g(U)
contains all vectors of the form

a_{2}(e_{1}-e_{2})+a_{3}(e_{1}-e_{3})+\cdots+a_{n}(e_{1}-e_{n})\pm\frac{1}{2}w,

(4)

for any integer
-R\leq a_{i}\leq R
with
a_{i}\equiv R\pmod{2}
. Using the next two claims, we show that all vectors in
(L-g(U))\cap B_{R}(0)
are expressible in this form.

Claim 4.5
.

L-g(U)
consists of all vectors of the form

b_{2}(e_{1}-e_{2})+b_{3}(e_{1}-e_{3})+\cdots+b_{n}(e_{1}-e_{n})\pm\frac{1}{2}w,

where
b_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
b_{i}\equiv R\pmod{2}
.

Proof.

We know that
L
consists of all vectors of the form

b_{2}(e_{1}-e_{2})+b_{3}(e_{1}-e_{3})+\cdots+b_{n}(e_{1}-e_{n})+\varepsilon w,\quad b_{i}\equiv 0\pmod{2},\quad\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}.

Set
z:=R(e_{1}-e_{2})+R(e_{1}-e_{3})+\cdots+R(e_{1}-e_{n})-\frac{1}{2}w
. Since
v_{i}^{(j)+}-v_{i}^{(j)-}=2(e_{1}-e_{i})
and
V_{0}\subset L
, we have

z=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{R}\frac{1}{2}(v_{i}^{(j)+}-v_{i}^{(j)-})-\frac{1}{2}w\in L-g(U)

Thus,
z+L=L-g(U)
consists of all vectors of the form

b_{2}(e_{1}-e_{2})+b_{3}(e_{1}-e_{3})+\cdots+b_{n}(e_{1}-e_{n})\pm\frac{1}{2}w,\quad b_{i}\equiv R\pmod{2}

since
V_{0}\subset L
.
∎

Claim 4.6
.

For any
x\in L
with
\left\lVert x-g(U)\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq R
, we have
x\in P(U)
.

Proof.

From Claim
4.5
, we may write
x-g(U)=a_{2}(e_{1}-e_{2})+a_{3}(e_{1}-e_{3})+\cdots+a_{n}(e_{1}-e_{n})\pm\frac{1}{2}w
. Since
\left\lVert x-g(U)\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq R
, by looking at the
i
-th coordinate of
x
for
i=2,\ldots,n
, we have
|a_{i}|\leq R+\frac{1}{2}
, so in fact we have
|a_{i}|\leq R
. Therefore,
x-g(U)
is of the form (
4
). This implies that
x\in P(U)
.
∎

Each
v\in V_{0}\setminus U
has norm
\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{2}=\sqrt{n}
. Theorem 2.2 in
[
4
]
(or Lemma 4.1 in
[
3
]
) shows that there exists for each
v\in V_{0}\setminus U
, a sign
\varepsilon_{v}\in\left\{-1,1\right\}
such that for
x:=\sum_{v\in V_{0}\setminus U}\varepsilon_{v}v
, we have
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}\leq n
and
\frac{1}{2}(x+\Sigma(V_{0}\setminus U))=\frac{1}{2}x+g(V_{0}\setminus U)\in P(V_{0}\setminus U)\subset L
. Then of course
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq n
.

Given any
u\in L
with
\left\lVert u-g(V_{0})\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq R-\frac{n}{2}
, we have
\left\lVert u-(\frac{1}{2}x+g(V_{0})\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq R
and thus
u-(\frac{1}{2}x+g(V_{0}\setminus U))\in L\cap B_{R}(g(U))
. By Claim
4.6
,
u-(\frac{1}{2}x+g(V_{0}\setminus U))\in P(U)
. Since
\frac{1}{2}x+g(V_{0}(W)\setminus U))\in P(V_{0}\setminus U)
, we have
u\in P(V_{0})
as required.
∎

In the next lemma, we show that
P(V_{0})
contains its center
g(V_{0})
if
n
is not a power of 2. In the case when
n
is a power of 2,
P(V_{0})
contains the closest possible point to its center
g(V_{0})
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Suppose
n
is even.

1.

If
n
is not a power of 2, then
g(V_{0})\in P(V_{0})
.

2.

If
n
is a power of 2 and
n>2
, let
w\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
be given by

w_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }i\not\equiv 0\pmod{4},\\
-3&\text{if }i\equiv 0\pmod{4}.\end{cases}

Then,
g(V_{0})-\frac{1}{2}w\in P(V_{0})
.

Proof.

Recall that
L
is the lattice generated by
V_{0}
.

1.

We first show that
g(V_{0})\in L
. Indeed, its first coordinate is
(g(V_{0}))_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\Sigma(V_{0})_{1}=\frac{1}{2}|V_{0}|
, an integer by Lemma
4.1
. In fact
|V_{0}|={n\choose\frac{n}{2}}
but we don’t need this explicitely. By symmetry and the fact that the sum of the coordinates of
g(V_{0})
is 0, we have for
i=2,\ldots,n
,
(g(V_{0}))_{i}=-\frac{1}{2(n-1)}|V_{0}|
, also an integer. Since
n
is even, all the coordinates have the same parity, thus
g(V_{0})\in L
.

By Lemma
4.4
with
R=n/2
and
u=g(V_{0})
, we have
g(V_{0})\in P(V_{0})
, provided that (
3
) holds. It can be shown that (
3
) holds for
n\geq 14
. For even
n<14
, we explicitly construct the signs
\varepsilon_{v}
in the Appendix, see Lemma
A.1
.

2.

Let
u=g(V_{0})-\frac{1}{2}w
. As above, we first show that
u\in L
. Indeed, it is straightforward to check that
u_{1}=\frac{1}{2}|V_{0}|-\frac{1}{2}w_{1}
and
u_{i}=-\frac{1}{2(n-1)}|V_{0}|-\frac{1}{2}w_{i}
for
i\geq 2
. Furthermore,
\sum_{i}u_{i}=0
and
u_{1}\equiv u_{2}\equiv\cdots\equiv u_{n}\pmod{2}
, thus
u\in L
.

Applying Lemma
4.4
with
R=n/2+3/2
and
u\in L
, checking that (
3
) holds for
n\geq 16
, we have that
u\in P(V_{0})
, as required. We leave the remaining small cases
n<16
in the Appendix, see Lemma
A.2
.

∎

The next two lemmas give Chooser’s strategy for when
n
is not a power of 2, and when
n
is a power of 2. Together with the
n
odd case, this completes the Chooser’s strategy portion of Theorem
1.4
.

Lemma 4.8
.

Suppose
n
is even and not a power of 2. Let
M=2^{n-2}-\frac{1}{2}\binom{n-1}{n/2}
. Then

0\in P(V)-g(V)-\frac{1}{2}\binom{n-1}{n/2}\mathbf{1}\subset K_{M}.

Proof.

The proof of the second inclusion is identical to the proof of Claim
4.3
.

For the first inclusion, from (
2
), it suffices to find
\varepsilon_{v}\in\{-1,1\}
for each
v\in V
such that
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in V}\varepsilon_{v}v=\frac{1}{4}\binom{n}{n/2}\mathbf{1}
. We first set for
v\in V\setminus V_{0}
,

\varepsilon_{v}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\sum_{i}v_{i}>0,\\
-1&\text{if }\sum_{i}v_{i}<0.\end{cases}

Then it is not hard to see that
\sum_{v\in V\setminus V_{0}}\varepsilon_{v}v=\frac{1}{2}\binom{n}{n/2}\mathbf{1}=\binom{n-1}{n/2}\mathbf{1}
. Thus, it suffices to find
\varepsilon_{v}\in\{-1,1\}
for each
v\in V_{0}
such that
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in V_{0}}\varepsilon_{v}v=0
. Equivalently, this says that
g(V_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\Sigma(V_{0})\in P(V_{0})
, which is Lemma
4.7
(1).
∎

Lemma 4.9
.

Suppose
n=2^{k}
for some
k\geq 1
. Let
M=2^{n-2}-\frac{1}{2}\binom{n-1}{n/2}+\frac{1}{2}
. Then there exists
w\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
with
w_{i}\leq 1
for all
i
, such that

0\in P(V)-g(V)-\frac{1}{2}\binom{n-1}{n/2}\mathbf{1}+\frac{1}{2}w\subset K_{M}.

Proof.

The case
n=2
can be directly verified, with
w_{1}=1,w_{2}=-1
. Assume that
k\geq 2
, so that
4\mid n
. Let
w\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
be given by

w_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }i\not\equiv 0\pmod{4},\\
-3&\text{if }i\equiv 0\pmod{4}.\end{cases}

The proof of the second inclusion is identical to the proof of Claim
4.3
. For the first inclusion, following the same arguments as in Lemma
4.8
, it suffices to show that

Let the

. With an
