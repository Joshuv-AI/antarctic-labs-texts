Local times and excursions for self-similar Markov trees
Jean Bertoin, Armand Riera, Alejandro Rosales-Ortiz

Abstract
This work builds upon the recent monograph [ 4 ] on self-similar Markov trees. A self-similar Markov tree is a random real tree equipped with a function from the tree to [ 0 , ∞ ) [0,\infty) that we call the decoration. Here, we construct local time measures L ⁡ ( x , d ​ t ) L(x,\mathrm{d}t) at every level x > 0 x>0 of the decoration for a large class of self-similar Markov trees. This enables us to mark at random a typical point in the tree at which the decoration is x x . We identify the law of the decoration along the branch from the root to this tagged point in terms of a remarkable (positive) self-similar Markov process. We also show that after a proper normalization, L ⁡ ( x , d ​ t ) L(x,\mathrm{d}t) converges as x → 0 + x\to 0+ to the harmonic measure \upmu \upmu on the tree. Finally, we point out that using a local time measure instead of the usual length measure \uplambda \uplambda to compute distances on the tree turn the latter into a continuous branching tree. This is relevant to analyze the excusions of the decoration away from a given level. Many results of the present work shall be compared with the recent ones in [ 22 , 23 ] about local times and excursions of a Markov process indexed by Lévy tree.



Keywords:
Branching process; excursion theory; local time; self-similar Markov tree; spinal decomposition.

Introduction

The notion of local time for a Markov process goes back to Blumenthal and Getoor
[
8
]
in the 60’s.
Roughly speaking, this refers to a functional that measures the time that the Markov process spends at a given point of the state space.
Local times play an important role in stochastic analysis, notably in connection with potential theory and excursion theory.
Our purpose is to investigate an analog in the framework of self-similar Markov trees, a family of random processes that have been introduced recently in
[
4
]
.
These naturally arise in invariance principles for Galton–Watson processes with integer types, and as such, in a variety of scaling limit theorems for certain random structures.

In short, a self-similar Markov tree, for which we often use the acronym ssMt, can be thought of as a (positive) self-similar Markov process in a branching setting. It
consists of a random function called the decoration,
g:T\to[0,\infty)
, which is defined on a random rooted compact real tree
T
.
Figure
1
below may help to visualize a sample of a ssMt.
We can think of
T

as encoding the genealogy of a population that starts from a single ancestor and becomes eventually extinct, where distances on
T
are interpreted as time durations.
Each individual in the population has a positive size that evolves as time passes according to Markovian dynamics, begets children, and eventually dies. We think of
g(t)
as the size of an individual at some time represented by
t\in T
.
In general, a parent begets infinitely many children; however, all but finitely many are minuscule with an extremely short life.
As the name suggests,
(T,g)
is self-similar and satisfies a version of the Markov property.

Our motivation for this work partly stems from the recent article

by two of us, in which the local time at a regular point for a Markov process indexed by a Lévy tree

Another striking difference can be observed by considering values taken at a fixed height in the tree.
The latter always form a null set (in the sense that for any

Despite their dissimilarities, both Markov processes indexed by Lévy trees and ssMt are described by random functions on random continuous trees, and it is thus natural to investigate alike properties.

Any real tree is naturally endowed with a length (or Lebesgue) measure

Self-similar Markov trees in a nutshell

The purpose of this section is to introduce some essential features of ssMt which will be needed for our goals, avoiding as much as possible technical issues when these are not important for a first reading. The celebrated Lamperti transformation establishes a bijective correspondence between real Lévy processes and (positive) self-similar Markov processes. and that the killing rate is positive. Consider first the case when
x=0
.
It follows from (
3.4
)
that there is a sequence
(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of positive real numbers tending to
0
fast enough so that the convergence in the claim holds
P
-a.s.
Now let the Lévy process start from an arbitrary
x\in\mathbb{R}
and apply the Markov property at the first hitting time of
0
.
Since
\ell(0,H_{0})=0
,
all that we need now is to check that, uniformly in
x
, the time spent by
\xi
before time
H_{0}
in an interval
(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
is asymptotically small compared to
\varepsilon\ll 1
with high probability under
P_{x}
.

So consider the Lévy process started from
x
and killed when hitting
0
,
(\xi_{t})_{0\leq t<H_{0}}
. By (
3.6
), the latter is a Markov process with potential density
v^{\dagger}
given by

v^{\dagger}(x,y^{\prime})=v(y^{\prime}-x)-\frac{v(-x)}{v(0)}v(y^{\prime}),\qquad y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}.

Since
v
is uniformly continuous (recall that we assumed that
-\psi(0)>0
so that
v\in\mathcal{C}^{0}
), we can extract from the sequence
(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
a sub-sequence
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n\geq 0}
such that

v^{\dagger}(x,y^{\prime})\leq 2^{-n}\qquad\text{ for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$, $n\geq 0$, and $|y^{\prime}|<\varepsilon_{n}$. }

In particular, we have

E_{x}\left(\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{n}}\int_{0}^{H_{0}}\mathbf{1}_{|\xi_{t}|<\varepsilon_{n}}\mathrm{d}t\right)=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{n}}\int_{-\varepsilon_{n}}^{\varepsilon_{n}}v^{\dagger}(x,y^{\prime})\mathrm{d}y^{\prime}\leq 2^{-n},

and a standard combination of the Borel-Cantelli lemma and the Markov inequality entails that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{n}}\int_{0}^{H_{0}}\mathbf{1}_{|\xi_{t}|<\varepsilon_{n}}\mathrm{d}t=0,\qquad P_{x}\text{-a.s.}

This completes the proof of the first claim in the statement. The second claim is then clear.
∎

3.2
Conditioning on the terminal value

We suppose throughout this section that the killing rate is positive, so that the lifetime
\zeta

is exponentially distributed with parameter
\Lambda(\{-\infty\})=-\psi(0)>0
under
P_{x}
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
It is readily seen from the Fubini theorem that the potential density then also naturally appears in the distribution of the terminal value, i.e. the location of the Lévy process
at the end of its lifetime. Specifically one has

P_{x}(\xi_{\zeta-}\in\mathrm{d}y)=-\psi(0)v(y-x)\mathrm{d}y,\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{R}.

(3.8)

Our main purpose in this section is to rigorously define, and then present some basic properties of, a version of the conditional distribution of the Lévy process given its terminal value. Such questions have been addressed in depth by Fourati
[
11
]
(see also the references therein) in a more general setting than ours, using an approach
à la
Doob combined with so-called Kuznetsov measures. We shall address first conditioning on a finite terminal value
\xi_{\zeta}=y
for
y\in\mathbb{R}
, and we shall then turn our attention to the case “
y=-\infty
” by studying the weak limit as
y\to-\infty
.

3.2.1
Finite terminal value

For any fixed

y\in\mathbb{R}
, the function
x\mapsto v(y-x)
is excessive for the Lévy process and the process
v(y-\xi_{\cdot})
is a
P_{x}
-supermartingale for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
This incites us to define a probability measure
P_{x,y}
on
\mathbb{D}

such that for every
t\geq 0
and every event
A\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
, we have:

P_{x,y}\left(A\cap\{t<\zeta\}\right)=\frac{1}{v(y-x)}E_{x}\left(v(y-\xi_{t})\mathbf{1}_{A}\right).

(3.9)

To make the connection with conditioning on the terminal value,
observe from (
3.8
), the Markov property, and the continuity of the potential density, that the conditional probability

P_{x}\left(A\cap\{t<\zeta\}\mid\xi_{\zeta-}\in(y-\varepsilon,y+\varepsilon)\right)

converges as
\varepsilon\to 0+
towards the right-hand side of (
3.9
).

There is actually a technical difficulty with this approach, namely one has to check that (
3.9
) yields indeed a probability measure
P_{x,y}
on the space
\mathbb{D}
of rcll paths absorbed at
-\infty
(the issue is to establish the left-continuity at lifetime) and that
P_{x,y}
-a.s., the terminal value is
\xi_{\zeta-}=y
.
Nonetheless, this problem can be circumvented in our setting with the following well-known direct construction in terms of killing at a last-passage time.

Lemma 3.2
.

Under the conditional law
P_{x}(\cdot\mid H_{y}<\infty)
, the distribution of the rcll process obtained by killing
\xi
at its last-passage at
y
,

(\xi_{t})_{0\leq t<K_{y}}
, satisfies (
3.9
).

Remark 3.3
.

Plainly for
x=y
, we have
P_{x}(\cdot\mid H_{x}<\infty)=P_{x}
, thanks to (
3.3
).

Proof.

It suffices to observe that
K_{y}>0
if and only if
H_{y}<\infty
, and from (
3.6
) and the Markov property, that

P_{x}(t<K_{y}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t})=v(y-\xi_{t})/v(0).

We infer
that for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, any
t\geq 0
and any event
A\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
, we have

P_{x}\left(A\cap\{t<K_{y}\}\mid H_{y}<\infty\right)=\frac{1}{v(y-x)}E_{x}\left(v(y-\xi_{t})\mathbf{1}_{A}\right).

∎

Since finite-dimensional distributions determine probability measures on
\mathbb{D}
, Lemma
3.2
now makes clear the existence and uniqueness of the law
P_{x,y}
on
\mathbb{D}
satisfying (
3.9
).
We can then check that the family
(P_{x,y})_{y\in\mathbb{R}}
provides the regular disintegration of the law
P_{x}
with respect to the terminal value.

Proposition 3.4
.

For every starting point
x\in\mathbb{R}
, the map
y\mapsto P_{x,y}
with values in the space
of probability measures on the Skorokhod space
\mathbb{D}
endowed with the topology of weak convergence, is continuous, and one has the disintegration identity

P_{x}=-\psi(0)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}v(y-x)P_{x,y}\mathrm{d}y.

(3.10)

Proof.

Consider any sequence
(y_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of real numbers that tends to
y
.
It is plain from (
3.9
) and the continuity of
v
that
P_{x,y_{n}}
converges to
P_{x,y}
as
n\to\infty
, in the sense of finite-dimensional distributions.
On the other hand, the representation of
P_{x,y}
as the law of the Lévy process killed at its last passage time at
y
in Lemma
3.2
enables us to check
the general criterion for tightness in
[
14
, Theorem VI.3.21]
for the sequence
(P_{x,y_{n}})_{n\geq 0}
. Indeed, it allows us to bound from above the probabilities there
computed under the laws
P_{x,y_{n}}
by those computed under
P_{x}
up to a constant factor. We conclude that as
n\to\infty
,
P_{x,y_{n}}
converges to
P_{x,y}

weakly on
\mathbb{D}
. The disintegration in the statement now makes sense and is plain from (
3.8
) and (
3.9
).
∎

We next point at an elementary identity involving the Esscher transform, which will be important for the present study.

Lemma 3.5
.

Take any
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
with
\psi(\beta)<0
and write

\psi^{(\beta)}(\cdot)=\psi(\beta+\cdot)
for the shifted Lévy exponent. Then the following assertions hold:

(i)

\psi^{(\beta)}
is the Lévy exponent of another killed Lévy process, whose law
P^{(\beta)}

is equivalent to
P
with density

\frac{\mathrm{d}P^{(\beta)}}{\mathrm{d}P}=\frac{\psi(\beta)}{\psi(0)}\exp(\beta\xi_{\zeta-}).

(3.11)

Furthermore, the potential density
v^{(\beta)}
of this
\beta
-tilted Lévy process is

v^{(\beta)}(x)=\mathrm{e}^{\beta x}v(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(ii)

For every
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, the conditional laws
P_{x,y}
and
P^{(\beta)}_{x,y}
are identical.

Proof.

(i) It follows from (
3.8
) that the positive random variable
\frac{\psi(\beta)}{\psi(0)}\exp(\beta\xi_{\zeta-})
has expectation
1
under
P
. Thus
(
3.11
) defines a probability measure
P^{(\beta)}
on the space
\mathbb{D}
of rcll paths, which may be referred to as the
\beta
-tilted law.
Take any
t\geq 0
and write
\exp(\beta\xi_{\zeta-})=\exp(\beta\xi_{t})\exp(\beta(\xi_{\zeta-}-\xi_{t}))
on the event
t<\zeta
.
We deduce from the Markov property of
\xi
that for
every event
A\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
, there is the identity

P^{(\beta)}\left(A\cap\{t<\zeta\}\right)=E\left(\exp(\beta\xi_{t})\mathbf{1}_{A}\right).

We then readily infer
(e.g. by computing the characteristic functions of increments) that
P^{(\beta)}
is indeed the law of another Lévy process with Lévy exponent
\psi^{(\beta)}
.
The stated formula for its potential density is immediate.

(ii) The identity
P_{x,y}=P^{(\beta)}_{x,y}
follows readily from (i), (
3.9
) and (
3.11
). ∎

We conclude this section with two more results about the conditioned Lévy process.
The first is a time-reversal identity, which was also discussed by Fourati
[
11
]
.
The opposite
-\xi
of a Lévy process is again a Lévy process; we write

\widehat{P}_{x}
for its law started from
x
, i.e. for the law of
-\xi
under
P_{-x}
.
The following claim is readily seen from the elementary duality lemma
[
7
, Section II.1]
combined with Proposition
3.4
.

Corollary 3.6
.

For any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, the law under
P_{x,y}
of the time-reversed process,

\xi_{(\zeta-t)-},\quad 0\leq t<\zeta,

is
\widehat{P}_{y,x}
.

Finally, an easy consequence of (
3.4
) is that the process
v(y-\xi_{t})+\ell(y,t)
for
t\geq 0
is a
P_{x}
-martingale for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
.
Its terminal value
\ell(y,\infty)=\ell(y,\zeta)
has expectation
v(y-x)
, and more precisely, is exponentially distributed with mean
v(0)
under
P_{x}(\cdot\mid H_{y}<\infty)
.
Roughly speaking, this entails that conditioning on the terminal value can also be interpreted as a killing according to the local time measure.
Here is the precise connection.

Lemma 3.7
.

For every
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, the identity

E_{x,y}\left(A_{\zeta}\right)=\frac{1}{v(y-x)}E_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\zeta}A_{t}\ell(y,\mathrm{d}t)\right)

holds for any bounded
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-predictable process
(A_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.

Proof.

Since the process
v(y-\xi_{t})+\ell(y,t)
for
t\geq 0
is a
P_{x}
-martingale, we have for any bounded
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-progressively measurable process
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
that

E_{x}\big(B_{t}(\ell(y,\zeta)-\ell(y,t))\big)=E_{x}(B_{t}v(y-\xi_{t})).

Lemma
3.2
then enables us to rewrite the right-hand side as

v(y-x)E_{x,y}\left(B_{t}\mathbf{1}_{t<\zeta}\right).

Integrating over
t\geq 0
and applying Fubini’s theorem, we arrive at

E_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\zeta}\ell(y,\mathrm{d}r)\int_{0}^{r}\mathrm{d}tB_{t}\right)=v(y-x)E_{x,y}\left(\int_{0}^{\zeta}\mathrm{d}tB_{t}\right).

This is our claim when the predictable process can be expressed in the form

A_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}rB_{r},\qquad t\geq 0,

for some bounded progressively measurable process
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. The general case follows by a standard argument.
∎

3.2.2
Infinite terminal value

The purpose of this section is to study the limiting behavior of the measures
P_{x,y}
as
y\to-\infty
. This requires an additional assumption of Cramer’s type on the Lévy exponent
\psi
, namely that:

there exists
\varrho<0
such that
\psi(\varrho)=0
.

(3.12)

For the rest of the section we suppose that (
3.12
) is satisfied.
Note from convexity of
\psi
that the right-derivative at
\varrho
is negative,
\psi^{\prime}(\varrho)<0
.
The exponent
\psi
shifted by
\varrho
is still a Lévy exponent, and the first item of Lemma
3.5
admits the following finite-horizon analog :

Lemma 3.8
.

The function
\psi^{(\varrho)}
is the Lévy exponent of another Lévy process which has no killing and drifts towards
-\infty
. Its law
P^{(\varrho)}
verifies for every
t>0

P^{(\varrho)}_{|\mathcal{F}_{t}}=\exp(\varrho\xi_{t})P_{|\mathcal{F}_{t}}.

(3.13)

Furthermore, the potential density
v^{(\varrho)}
of this
\varrho
-tilted Lévy process is bounded and given by

v^{(\varrho)}(x)=\mathrm{e}^{\varrho x}v(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(3.14)

The proof is analogous to that for Lemma
3.5
and we skip the details. The absence of killing stems from
\psi^{(\varrho)}(0)=0
,
and the drift towards
-\infty
from
\psi^{(\varrho)\prime}(0)=\psi^{\prime}(\varrho)<0
. The rest of the section is devoted to establishing the convergence of
P_{x,y}
towards
P_{x}^{(\varrho)}
as
y\to-\infty
.

Proposition 3.9
.

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}
, the following convergence holds weakly with respect to the Skorokhod topology:

\lim\limits_{y\to-\infty}P_{x,y}=P_{x}^{(\varrho)}.

(3.15)

As a consequence, we have also,

\lim\limits_{y\to-\infty}P_{x}(\,\cdot\,|\xi_{\zeta-}\leq y)=P_{x}^{(\varrho)}.

The proof of Proposition
3.9
relies on the representation (
3.9
) of the measures
P_{x,y}
paired with the following asymptotic estimate of the potential density
v
.

Lemma 3.10
.

We have:

\lim_{y\to-\infty}\exp(\varrho y)v(y)=-1/\psi^{\prime}(\varrho).

Proof.

Recalling (
3.14
), the claim can be rephrased as

\lim_{y\to-\infty}v^{(\varrho)}(y)=-1/\psi^{\prime}(\varrho).

(3.16)

Let us first explain why this result should not come as a surprise. The Lévy process with exponent
\psi^{(\varrho)}
is non-lattice, has no killing and has finite mean
\psi^{\prime}(\varrho)<0
. The renewal theorem
[
7
, Theorem I.21]
yields that for every
h>0
,

\lim_{x\to-\infty}\int_{x}^{x+h}v^{(\varrho)}(y)\mathrm{d}y=-h/\psi^{\prime}(\varrho),

(3.17)

in agreement with (
3.16
).

On the other hand, we observe from (
3.6
) and the strong Markov property at
H_{x}
that

\frac{v^{(\varrho)}(x)v^{(\varrho)}(y-x)}{v^{(\varrho)}(0)^{2}}=P^{(\varrho)}_{0}(H_{x}<\infty)P^{(\varrho)}_{x}(H_{y}<\infty)\leq P^{(\varrho)}_{0}(H_{y}<\zeta)=v^{(\varrho)}(y)/v^{(\varrho)}(0).

We now readily see that (
3.16
) follows from (
3.17
) and the fact that
v^{(\varrho)}(y-x)
converges to
v^{(\varrho)}(0)
as
y-x\to 0
.
∎

We have all the ingredients to establish the convergence of
P_{x,y}
as
y\to-\infty
.

Proof of Proposition
3.9
.

Fix
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
t>0
. Recall from (
3.9
) that for every measurable functional
F\geq 0
on the space
\mathbb{D}([0,t])
of possibly absorbed rcll paths indexed by
[0,t]
, we have

E_{x,y}\big(F(\xi_{s},0\leq s\leq t)1_{\zeta>t}\big)=\frac{1}{v(y-x)}E_{x}\big(F(\xi_{s},0\leq s\leq t)v(y-\xi_{t})\big).

(3.18)

for
y\in\mathbb{R}
.
It readily follows from Lemma
3.8
that

\sup_{(y,z)\in\mathbb{R}_{-}\times\mathbb{R}}\exp(-\varrho z)\frac{v(y-z)}{v(y)}<\infty.

Since
E_{x}(\exp(\varrho\xi_{t}))=\mathrm{e}^{\varrho x}<\infty
, dominated convergence applies and Lemma
3.10
yields

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{y\to-\infty}E_{x,y}\big(F(\xi_{s},0\leq s\leq t)1_{\zeta>t}\big)

\displaystyle=E_{x}\big(F(\xi_{s},0\leq s\leq t)\exp(\varrho(\xi_{t}-x))\big)

\displaystyle=E_{x}^{(\varrho)}\big(F(\xi_{s},0\leq s\leq t)\big),

where the second equality stems from Lemma
3.8
. In particular, since under
P_{x}^{(\varrho)}
the process
\xi
does not have killing, from taking
F=1
we infer that
P_{x,y}(\zeta>t)\to 1
as
y\to-\infty
. It then follows that

\lim\limits_{y\to-\infty}E_{x,y}\big(F(\xi_{s},0\leq s\leq t)\big)=E_{x}^{(\varrho)}\big(F(\xi_{s},0\leq s\leq t)\big),

for every every measurable functional
F\geq 0
on
\mathbb{D}([0,t])
, and since
t>0
is arbitrary the desired convergence (
3.15
) follows. The second statement of the proposition is now a straightforward consequence of the disintegration (
3.10
) and (
3.15
).
∎

Remark 4.3
.

One important feature of the function

is that, up to a multiplicative factor of

, it is the occupation density of the decoration. The study of occupation densities of random processes has been conducted in a wide range of settings. For instance, occupation densities have been extensively studied for superprocesses with Brownian displacements, and these results can be further extended to tree-indexed Brownian motion. Concretely, Sugitani
[
25
]
first proved that super-Brownian motion with branching mechanism

admits an occupation density, and this was later extended to general

[
20

, one can derive the existence of an occupation density for Brownian motion indexed by an

-stable Lévy tree. In the special case when
\alpha=2
, this yields the celebrated Integrated super Brownian excursion
f_{\mathrm{ISE}}=(f_{\mathrm{ISE}}(x),x\in\mathbb{R})
, which has played a fundamental role in scaling-limit theorems of discrete trees, and appears in interacting-particle systems and statistical-physics models
[
9
,
13
,
26
]
. The study of
f_{\mathrm{ISE}}
remains an active field of research. For instance, recent work of Le Gall and Perkins shows that

satisfies a stochastic differential equation
[
18
]
. It is worth mentioning that the naïve analogue of the total mass
L(x,T)
in the setting of Brownian motion indexed by a stable tree is almost surely degenerate (taking only the values

or
\infty
), so the construction of local time measures is not as straightforward as in this work, and requires additional care. This task is carried over in
[
22
]
, where a local time measure for general Markov processes indexed by Lévy trees is obtained by an approximation procedure.

the convergence of the measures themselves then follows by standard techniques.
We start with the following version of Proposition

that will be needed in the sequel.

Proposition 5.3
.

We have:

(i)

The shifted cumulant,

\kappa^{(\omega)}(\cdot)=\kappa(\omega+\cdot)

is a Lévy exponent with no killing for which (

) and (

) are satisfied.
Its potential density
\mathrm{w}^{(\omega)}
is continuous and satisfies

\lim_{y\to\infty}\mathrm{w}^{(\omega)}(y)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{y\to-\infty}\mathrm{w}^{(\omega)}(y)=-1/\kappa^{\prime}(\omega).

(ii)

For every

\E_{x^{\prime}}\big(L(x,T)\big)=(x^{\prime}/x)^{\omega}\mathrm{w}^{(\omega)}(\log(x/x^{\prime})).

Proof.

The claims follow readily from Proposition

and Lemmas
3.8

.

The notion of local decomposition for self-similar Markov trees, which should be thought of as a spatial version of the Markov branching property for self-similar Markov trees, plays a crucial role in this section. Let us give a quick introduction; to keep the presentation short, we focus on a particular form and refer to
[
4
, Chapter 4]
for the general theory and technical details.

First, recall that
\mathbb{T}
denotes the space of isomorphism classes of decorated trees
(T,g)
and is equipped with the probability measure
\mathbb{Q}
, which is the law of our ssMt.
Given a closed subtree
T^{\prime}\subset T
with
\uprho\in T^{\prime}
, we are interested in the family of connected components of
T\backslash T^{\prime}
.
Any such component, say
\uptau^{*}
, is attached to
T^{\prime}
at a unique point, say
r\in T^{\prime}
, and
r
is the sole boundary point of
\uptau^{*}
.
So
\uptau=\uptau^{*}\cup\{r\}
is another closed subtree of
T
, which we decorate with

defined by

g_{\uptau}(y)=\left\{\begin{matrix}g(y)&\text{ \, if }y\in\uptau^{*},\\
\ell&\text{ if }y=r,\\
\end{matrix}\right.

where

\ell=\limsup_{\uptau^{*}\ni x\to r}g(x)

is called the germ
6
6

6

In general,
\ell<g(r)
.
of the decoration on
\uptau
.
Then

is again a decorated tree, which is naturally rooted at

and has initial decoration
\ell
; it is referred to as a subtree dangling from
T^{\prime}
.

We say that the subtree
T^{\prime}
induces a local decomposition if we can index the connected components of
T\backslash T^{\prime}
by some countable set

(\uptau^{*}_{i})_{i\in I}
, in such a way that under the law
\mathbb{Q}
and conditionally on the restriction of the decorated tree to

,
(T^{\prime},g_{\mid T^{\prime}})
and on the family
(r_{i},\ell_{i})_{i\in I}

(\uptau_{i},g_{\uptau_{i}})
for

are independent, and each
(\uptau_{i},g_{\uptau_{i}})
has the law
\mathbb{Q}_{\ell_{i}}
.

In what follows, we will apply this notion for
{T}^{\prime}
of the following form for some
0<x<1
:

{T}_{>x}=\big\{t\in T:g(s)>x\text{ for all }s\prec t\big\},

and

{T}_{\neq x}=\big\{t\in T:g(s)\neq x\text{ for all }s\prec t\big\}.

It is readily checked from the same argument as in the proofs of Propositions 4.3 and 4.9 in

that each induces a local decomposition, and we will use the notation

and

for the set of indices appearing in the respective local decomposition. We stress that
there is the identity
\mathcal{L}(x)=\{r_{i}:i\in I_{\neq x}\}
and that
the germ of the decoration at any subtree component of

is always
\ell=x=g(r)

We now state a technical first-moment formula related to the local decomposition induced by

Recall that for every

,

is the root of the component

Lemma 5.4
.

Let

be a non-negative functional and

\E\Big(\sum_{i\in{I}_{>x}}F(\ell_{i})\cdot\ell_{i}^{\omega}\Big)=E^{(\omega)}\left(F(X_{\varsigma_{x}})\right),

where in the right-hand side,

stands for the expectation for the law
Q^{(\omega)}
of a self similar Markov process

(\kappa^{(\omega)};\alpha)

\varsigma_{x}=\inf\{t\geq 0:X_{t}<x\}
is the first passage time below
x

Proof.

The proof relies on the many-to-one formula similar to that in the proof of Theorem
4.4
, but now for a point marked according to the harmonic measure
\upmu

4.6

\mathbb{Q}^{(\omega)}
characterized by the relation:

\E^{(\omega)}(G(T,g,\uprho^{\prime}))=\E\left(\int_{T}G(T,g,t)\upmu(\mathrm{d}t)\right),

(5.2)

for every

measurable function. By (

is a probability measure, and by

\text{the distribution of }g_{\mid\llbracket\uprho,\uprho^{\prime}\rrbracket}\text{ under }\mathbb{Q}^{(\omega)}\text{ is }Q^{(\omega)}.

(5.3)

We stress that since
\kappa^{(\omega)}(0)=0
, the self similar Markov process under
Q^{(\omega)}
dies continuously at

Thus for
\upmu

, we have
g(t)=0
and the decoration along the branch from the root to

x

Getting back to (

t\in T

g(s)\leq x

, and for definiteness by convention we take

An application of (

\E\left(\int_{T}F(\ell_{x}(t))\upmu(\mathrm{d}t)\right)=\E\left(\sum_{i\in{I}_{>x}}F(\ell_{i})\upmu(\uptau_{i})\right)=\E\left(\sum_{i\in{I}_{>x}}F(\ell_{i})\ell_{i}^{\omega}\right).

(5.4)

On the other hand, we get from (

∎

We then make another step towards Theorem

Lemma 5.5
.

The following assertions hold.

(i)

\lim_{x\to 0+}\sum_{i\in{I}_{>x}}\ell_{i}^{\omega}=\upmu(T)
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{Q})
.

(ii)

The family
\big\{x^{\omega}N(x,T):x\in\mathbb{R}\big\}
is uniformly integrable under
\mathbb{Q}
.

(iii)

Moreover, there is the identity

\E\big(N(x,T)\big)=x^{-\omega}\mathrm{w}^{(\omega)}(\log x))/\mathrm{w}^{(\omega)}(0),

and as a consequence,

\lim_{x\to 0+}x^{\omega}\E(N(x,T))=-\frac{1}{\kappa^{\prime}(\omega)\mathrm{w}^{(\omega)}(0)}.

Proof.

(i)
Let
\mathcal{F}_{>x}
be the sigma-field generated by
(T_{>x},g_{\mid T_{>x}})
and
(r_{i},\ell_{i})_{i\in I_{>x}}
.
Note that
\upmu({T}_{>x})=0
since the harmonic measure does not charge the skeleton of
T
(see
[
4
, Proposition 1.12]
).
This fact paired with the local decomposition induced by
{T}_{>x}
and (
5.1
) yields the identity

\mathbb{E}\big(\upmu(T)\big|\mathcal{F}_{{>x}}\big)=\sum_{i\in{I}_{>x}}\ell_{i}^{\omega},\quad\mathbb{Q}\text{-a.s.}

Obviously
\left(\mathcal{F}_{{>x}}\right)_{x>0}
is a descending filtration and the process in the last display is a uniformly integrable martingale with terminal value
\upmu(T)
(observe that
(T,g)
is clearly
\mathcal{F}_{{>0+}}
-measurable).

(ii) Let
\mathcal{F}_{\neq x}
be the sigma-field generated by
(T_{\neq x},g_{\mid T_{\neq x}})
and
(r_{i},\ell_{i})_{i\in I_{\neq x}}
. Then, we have

\sum_{i\in{I}_{\neq x}}\upmu(\uptau_{i})\leq\upmu(T).

Recall that
\ell_{i}=x
for
i\in I_{\neq x}
and
N(x,T)=\#I_{\neq x}
, so the local decomposition induced by
T_{\neq x}
paired with (
5.1
) yields

x^{\omega}N(x,T)=\sum_{i\in I_{\neq x}}\ell_{i}^{\omega}\leq\mathbb{E}\big(\upmu(T)\big|\mathcal{F}_{\neq x}\big).

This entails the claim of uniform integrability.

(iii) We first write

L(x,T)=\sum_{i\in I_{\neq x}}L(x,\tau_{i}).

We next take expectations using the local decomposition induced by
T_{\neq x}
.
Recall from self-similarity that the law of
L(x,T)
under
\mathbb{Q}_{x}
does not depend on
x>0
, and that
N(x,T)=\#I_{x}
. We get

\E(L(x,T))=\E(N(x,T))\E(L(1,T)),

(5.5)

and the claims follow from Proposition
5.3
.
∎

We now have all the ingredients needed to establish the convergence of the normalized total masses.

Proposition 5.6
.

For
y>0
, the following limits hold in
L^{1}(\mathbb{Q}_{y})
:

\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{L(x,T)}{\E(L(x,T))}=\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{N(x,T)}{\E(N(x,T))}=\upmu(T).

Proof.

Plainly, it suffices to prove the result for
y=1
, since by self-similarity, the distributions of
L(xy,T)
and of
N(xy,T)
under
\mathbb{Q}_{y}
do not depend on
y>0
. We also point out that the first limit readily follows from the second. Indeed, since the local time measure
L(x,\mathrm{d}t)
is carried by the level set
\{t\in T:g(t)=x\}
, we can write

L(x,T)=\sum_{i\in I_{\neq x}}L(x,\tau_{i}),

and we deduce from the local decomposition induced by
T_{\neq x}
that the right-hand side is actually
the sum of
N(x,T)
i.i.d. variables distributed as
L(1,T)
under
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
.
By the law of large numbers, we have as
x\to 0+
that there is the convergence in probability under
\mathbb{Q}
,

\lim_{x\to 0+}\frac{L(x,T)}{N(x,T)}=\E(L(1,T))=\mathrm{w}^{(\omega)}(0).

We can easily conclude by an application of Scheffé’s Lemma using Proposition
5.3
(ii) and Lemma
5.5
(iii) that the first limit in the statement indeed holds in
L^{1}(\mathbb{Q})
.

Local decompositions also play a major role in the proof of the second convergence. Beware however that we shall apply them to
T_{>\sqrt{x}}
rather than
T_{>x}
. We need the following slight modification of Lemma
5.5
(i):

\lim_{x\to 0+}\sum_{i\in{I}_{>\sqrt{x}}}\ell_{i}^{\omega}\mathbf{1}_{\ell_{i}\geq x^{2/3}}=\upmu(T)\qquad\text{ in }L^{1}(\mathbb{Q}).

(5.6)

In this direction, we start to observe from the many-to-one formula of Lemma
5.4
that

\E\left(\sum_{i\in I_{>\sqrt{x}}}\ell_{i}^{\omega}\mathbf{1}_{\ell_{i}<x^{2/3}}\right)=Q^{(\omega)}\left(X_{\varsigma(\sqrt{x})}<x^{2/3}\right).

Recall from Assumption
5.1
that
\kappa^{\prime}(\omega)\in(-\infty,0)
, so the Lévy process with exponent
\kappa^{(\omega)}
has a finite strictly negative expectation. The combination of the renewal theorem and the Lamperti transformation entails that as
x\to 0+
,

x^{-1/2}X_{\varsigma(\sqrt{x})}
converges in distribution under
Q^{(\omega)}
, where the limiting variable is strictly positive a.s.
Therefore

\lim_{x\to 0+}\E\left(\sum_{i\in I_{>\sqrt{x}}}\ell_{i}^{\omega}\mathbf{1}_{\ell_{i}<x^{2/3}}\right)=\lim_{x\to 0+}Q^{(\omega)}\left(x^{-1/2}X_{\varsigma(\sqrt{x})}<x^{1/6}\right)=0,

and Lemma
5.5
(i) implies (
5.6
).

Next, we write
N_{i}(x)=N(x,\tau_{i})
for every
i\in I_{>\sqrt{x}}
, so that

x^{\omega}N(x,T)=\sum_{i\in I_{>\sqrt{x}}}x^{\omega}N_{i}(x)=\sum_{i\in{I}_{>\sqrt{x}}}\ell_{i}^{\omega}b_{i}(x),\qquad\text{where }b_{i}(x)={\Big(\frac{x}{\ell_{i}}\Big)}^{\omega}N_{i}(x).

The local decomposition induced by
T_{>\sqrt{x}}
shows that conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{>\sqrt{x}}
, the variables
b_{i}(x)
for
i\in I_{>\sqrt{x}}
are independent,
and the conditional law of each
b_{i}(x)
is that of
y^{\omega}N(y)
under
\mathbb{Q}
for
y=x/\ell_{i}
.

Observing from Lemma
5.5
(iii) that
\E(y^{\omega}N(y))\leq 1
for all
y>0
, we deduce from the calculation above that

\lim_{x\to 0+}\E\left(\sum_{i\in{I}_{>\sqrt{x}}}\ell_{i}^{\omega}b_{i}(x)\mathbf{1}_{\ell_{i}<x^{2/3}}\right)=0.

We can now focus on indices
{i\in{I}_{>\sqrt{x}}}
with
\ell_{i}\geq x^{2/3}
, so
x/\ell_{i}\leq x^{1/3}
, and we have from (

) that

\lim_{x\to 0+}\sum_{i\in{I}_{>\sqrt{x}}}\ell_{i}^{\omega}\frac{\mathrm{w}^{(\omega)}(\log(x/\ell_{i}))}{\mathrm{w}^{(\omega)}(0)}\mathbf{1}_{\ell_{i}\geq x^{2/3}}=-\frac{\upmu(T)}{\kappa^{\prime}(\omega)\mathrm{w}^{(\omega)}(0)},\qquad\text{ in }L^{1}(\mathbb{Q}).

We know from Lemma
5.5
that

\big\{y^{\omega}N(y,T):y\in\mathbb{R}\big\}
is uniformly integrable under

, which enables us to apply

\lim_{x\to 0+}\sum_{i\in{I}_{>\sqrt{x}}}\ell_{i}^{\omega}b_{i}(x)\mathbf{1}_{\ell_{i}\geq x^{2/3}}=-\frac{\upmu(T)}{\kappa^{\prime}(\omega)\mathrm{w}^{(\omega)}(0)},\qquad\text{in }L^{1}(\mathbb{Q}).

Putting the pieces together, we conclude that

\lim_{x\to 0+}x^{\omega}N(x,T)=-\frac{\upmu(T)}{\kappa^{\prime}(\omega)\mathrm{w}^{(\omega)}(0)},\qquad\text{in }L^{1}(\mathbb{Q}),

which established the second limit of the statement.
∎

[

Under

Lemma 5.7

Under

, let

be a sequence of measures on

\nu_{n}(T_{u})\to\upmu(T_{u})

\nu_{n_{k}}(\mathrm{d}t)\to\upmu(\mathrm{d}t),\quad k\to\infty,

In this proof, we argue under
\mathbb{P}
. By a diagonal argument, we can extract a sub-sequence

along which, a.s., we have

\lim_{j\to\infty}\nu_{n_{j}}(T_{u})=\upmu(T_{u}),\quad\text{ for every }u\in\mathbb{U}.

To simplify notation we will assume that
(n_{j})_{j\geq 0}
is just the original sequence
(n)_{n\geq 0}
.
For every

, we also introduce the subset

and we recall from
[

that, a.s.,

is a finite measure and

\upmu\big(\bigcup_{n\geq 0}T^{(n)}\big)=0.

Let us consider an event

of full probability where

) and (

Let us start with some preliminary remarks. First a straightforward application of Portmanteau’s theorem, combined with (

\displaystyle\nu(C)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\nu\Big(\bigcup_{u\in\mathbb{U}^{n}_{C}}T_{u}\Big)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{u\in\mathbb{U}_{C}^{n}}\nu(T_{u}),

. In this direction, for

\lim_{x\downarrow 0}\mathbb{E}\Big(\big|\frac{L(x,T_{u})}{m(x)}-\upmu(T_{u})\big|\wedge 1\Big)=0,

for every

. For the remaining of the proof we fix

\mathbb{P}

6
Excursions away from a level set

Our main purpose in this section is to investigate the level sets

\mathcal{L}(x)=\{t\in T:g(t)=x\},\qquad x>0,

as well as the excursions of the decoration away from
x
.
For the sake of simplicity, we work under
\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{P}_{1}
when the decoration at the root is
g(\uprho)=1
; we further focus on the level
1
and write

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(1).

We stress that level sets
\mathcal{L}(x)
for any
x>0
can then be treated by an application of the local decomposition induced by the sub-tree
T_{\neq x}
in the notation of Section
5
, and that changing the initial decoration to an arbitrary
y>0
is of course an easy matter by self-similarity.

We call the closure of a connected component of
T\backslash\mathcal{L}
an excursion-subtree. Each excursion-subtree is naturally rooted at the closest point to
\uprho
, called the debut of the excursion, and further decorated by the restriction of
g
; we then simply call a decorated excursion-subtree an excursion. Observe that by construction,
the decoration of an excursion at its root always equals
1
. We will soon observe that excursions have also an end and use this end as a natural mark.

We will start the study of excursions with the analysis of those that meet the ancestral segment. Their description involves a stopped Poisson point process, which notably enables us to define the so-called excursion measure
\mathbf{N}^{\bullet}
. We will next show that the level set
\mathcal{L}
equipped
with the local time distance has the structure of a continuous branching tree; this should be viewed as an analog of
[
22
, Theorem 5.1]
for Markov processes indexed by a Lévy tree. Finally, we will describe the family of excursions in terms of a Poisson point process, which is of course reminiscent of the classical result of K. Itô for the excursions of a Markov process away from its starting point, and also of
[
23
, Theorem 3.8]
for Markov processes indexed by a Lévy tree.

6.1
Excursions debuting on the ancestral segment

Recall from Section
2
that we can think of an ssMt as representing a population that evolves when time passes.
The ancestor is labelled by
\varnothing
and associated to an oriented closed segment
S_{\varnothing}\subset T
which we refer to as the ancestral segment and whose left extremity is the root
\uprho
.

Lemma 6.1
.

The following assertions hold
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.

(i)

The decoration

\mathcal{L}_{\varnothing}

(ii)

\mathcal{L}_{\varnothing}

(iii)

\big\{\left(L^{-1}_{\varnothing}(a-),L^{-1}_{\varnothing}(a)\right):a>0\text{ with }L^{-1}_{\varnothing}(a)>L^{-1}_{\varnothing}(a-)\big\}.

Proof.

Let us briefly recall the proof of (i), since a slightly modified argument will be also useful later on.
By the Lamperti transformation, the claim amounts to the well-known fact that a.s. for the Lévy process
\xi

if

is such that either
\xi_{t}=0
or
\xi_{t-}=0
, then actually
\xi_{t}=\xi_{t-}=0
.

Fix any

has a jump with absolute size

\xi^{\prime\prime}
for the compound Poisson process
obtained by summing those jumps as time passes. It follows from the Lévy-Itô decomposition and standard properties of Poisson point processes that

is also a Lévy process, which is independent of
\xi^{\prime\prime}
. Fix any

and consider the
n

\xi^{\prime\prime}
, say
\varsigma
. Recalling from a well-known result due to Hartman and Wintner

that the distribution of

is continuous for any

and
\xi_{\varsigma-}=\xi^{\prime}_{\varsigma}+\xi^{\prime\prime}_{\varsigma-}
; in particular the probability that
\xi_{\varsigma}=0
or
\xi_{\varsigma-}=0
is null.
∎

The contribution of the ancestor to the population is encoded by its reproduction measure

Lemma 6.2
.

One has

\eta_{\varnothing}(\mathcal{L}_{\varnothing}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})=0
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.

Proof.

The construction of the decoration-reproduction process

of the ancestor in
[

, Section 2.2]

consists in applying the Lamperti time-substitution to a pair

, where

is a Lévy process and

\mathbb{R}_{+}\times([-\infty,\infty)\times\mathcal{S}_{1})

Consider any Borel set

with

and write
\varsigma
for the

\mathbf{N}

has an atom in

(i) shows that the probability that either

6.2
Branching structure of level sets of individuals

Recall that from the point of view of a self-similar Markov tree as population model, every individual
u\in\mathbb{U}
is represented by a segment
S_{u}
decorated by a function

and further endowed with a reproduction process

.
It is natural to decompose the level set of the decoration (at level

) in the whole tree as

\mathcal{L}=\bigcup_{u\in\mathbb{U}}\mathcal{L}_{u},

where

\mathcal{L}_{u}=\{t\in S_{u}:f_{u}(t)=1\}

and to introduce the subset of individuals

Expressing the total local time

and applying the Markov property of the decoration on each segment

easily deduce from Proposition

that

\E(\#\mathbb{L})=\mathrm{w}^{(\gamma_{0})}(0)/v(0)<\infty.

(6.3)

We point at the following immediate consequences of Lemmas

.

The main result of this section is that

can be seen as a continuous time branching tree
in the following sense.
Recall first from
[
3

that a (one dimensional) continuous (time Markov) branching process
describes a population where distinct individuals evolve in continuous time, independently, one from the other and according to the same law.
Its dynamics are determined by a sequence
(\beta_{k})_{k\geq 0}
of nonnegative coefficients with
\sum_{k\geq 0}\beta_{k}<\infty
and called the branching rates. Specifically,
\beta_{k}
is the rate at which a typical individual is replaced by

k
new individuals. We stress that for

is essentially irrelevant and can be changed arbitrarily, since the replacement of an individual by another has no significant effects on the model. A continuous time branching process starting from a single ancestor can thus be encoded from a Galton–Watson tree with reproduction law
(\beta_{j}/(\sum_{k\geq 0}\beta_{k}):j\geq 0)
by assigning an independent exponential length with mean
\sum_{k\geq 0}\beta_{k}
to each edge.
One calls a continuous time branching process subcritical when

\sum_{k\geq 0}(k-1)\beta_{k}<0;

(6.5)

this is the case if and only if the total length of its associated tree has finite expectation.

We conclude this section by briefly discussing the relationship between the present results and those obtained in

[
22
,
23
]
. In the later works, the object of study is a continuous Markov process,
indexed by a (fractal) Lévy tree
\tau
- the family of random trees which arise as the scaling limit of (sub-)critical Galton–Watson trees. Informally, such a process
(Y_{t}:t\in\tau)
can be described as follows: conditionally on the
underlying random tree
\tau
, the process evolves as a Markov process along each branch and splits into independent copies at every branching point. In order to guarantee the existence of a non-trivial local time, the existence of a fixed point
x
of the state space which is regular and instantaneous for the Markov process is further assumed.

A central objective of
[
22
]
is the construction of a measure

A(x,\mathrm{d}t)
supported on the level set
\{t\in\tau:Y_{t}=x\}
. In contrast with the present work, this construction
is technical, and the resulting measure is supported on the set of leaves of the tree - instead of the skeleton. The law of the motion along
the segment connecting the root of
\tau
to a point sampled according to
A(x,\mathrm{d}t)
is also identified, and exhibits
similarities with the spinal descriptions obtained in Section
4.3
. The level set
\{t\in\tau:Y_{t}=x\}
thus plays a role analogous to that of
\mathcal{L}
in the present setting. As in this
work, its structure is analyzed by introducing a tree
\widetilde{\tau}
, which is obtained by identifying all pairs of
points
s,t\in\tau
such that
A(x,\llbracket s,t\rrbracket)=0
. It is shown that
\widetilde{\tau}
is itself a
(fractal) Lévy tree, whereas in the present setting
(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}},\widetilde{d}_{L})
is an essentially discrete Lévy tree. In both frameworks, the resulting tree satisfies the branching property in the sense of

[
27
]
.

The second work
[
23
]
is devoted to the study of excursions away from
x
. It is shown
that, when properly indexed using the measure
A(x,\mathrm{d}t)
, the associated point process of excursions
has a Poissonian structure, yielding an analogue of the classical Itô excursion theory.
Moreover, the conditional law of the excursions given
\widetilde{\tau}
is identified by methods that are closely
related in spirit to those employed in the present work.

In particular, the role played here by the measure
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
