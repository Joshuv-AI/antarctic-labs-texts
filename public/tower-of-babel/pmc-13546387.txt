Brain metastases in lung cancer patients: Correlation between radiologic phenotypes and outcome parameters in neurosurgically treated cohort
Authors: Yosef Laviv, Ohad Regev, Andrew A. Kanner, Susana Fichman, Tali Siegal, Shlomit Yust-Katz, Alexandra Benouaich-Amiel, Dror Limon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUp to 50% of Brain metastases (BM) are associated with lung cancer. Different radiologic features such as tumor size, anatomical location and peritumoral edema affect the clinical course. However, their impact on prognosis and survival is controversial.Patients and MethodsA retrospective study of lung cancer patients who underwent surgical resection of solitary BM. Based on pre-operative radiologic features of the BM, the cohort was divided into 3 groups: solid, cystic-necrotic, and purely cystic lesion. Extent of peritumoral edema and the cerebral vascular territory affected were also examined.ResultsThe studied cohort included 89 patients. Following categorization, 24 (27%), 46 (51.6%) and 19 (21.4%) cases were considered solid, cystic-necrotic and purely cystic, respectively. Severity of peritumoral edema (p = 0.019) as well as the vascular territory affected (p = 0.011) were the principal differences between groups. Rate of distal brain recurrence was nearly two-fold higher in the purely cystic subgroup vs. the cystic-necrotic subgroup (p = 0.075). On multivariable analysis, adjusted to vascular territory and edema severity, only purely cystic lesions were significantly associated with increased risk for distal recurrence (HR = 2.92, p = 0.040) and showed a trend towards worse progression–free survival (HR = 2.10, p = 0.089) when compared to solid cases. Overall survival curves showed no significant difference between groups.ConclusionThe radiologic phenotype of BM seems to have limited impact on OS in surgically treated lung cancer patients. Purely cystic BM might be associated with unfavorable risk for distal recurrence. For some outcome parameters, meaningful differences between the 3 radiologic groups were found, suggesting potential clinical implications.

Introduction

Brain metastases (BM) is a common, significant health problem. The incidence of BMs is reported to be 10 times higher than that of primary brain tumors, affecting 10–30% of patients with systemic cancer. 1 Of those, BM from lung cancer are by far the most common. Of patients with BM, lung cancer is the primary tumor in 40 to 50% of cases. 2 Patients with BM commonly have poor prognosis, and untreated patients have a median survival of just 1–2 months. 3 On the other hand, treatment modalities such as surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy and biological or immunological targeted therapy have significantly improved the survival of these patients 4

Two important factors affecting the clinical course of BM in general are the consistency of the lesion; i.e., whether the lesion is solid or cystic, and the extent of peri-lesional brain edema.5,6 These morphologic diversities can affect both therapeutic approaches 7 as well as prognosis and survival.8,9 Purely cystic BM are relatively rare (4.4% of patients) 10 ; hence related clinical data is scarce. In addition, published data usually do not differentiate purely cystic lesions from mixed solid – cystic lesions or necrotic lesions. Studies on cystic BM include small cohorts, and in the neurosurgical literature are limited to describing treatment modalities (e.g. stereotactic radiosurgery with or without minimally invasive evacuation of the cyst).11,12 A recently published data has shown a correlation between BM’s consistency, extent of peri-tumoral edema, molecular profile of the lesion and intracranial vascular territory affected. 13 However, there is a lack of large sample studies comparing the prognosis of solid BMs vs cystic BMs in lung cancer, especially in a cohort of surgically treated patients. 9 We hypothesize that distinct, yet poorly characterized, pathophysiological mechanisms drive varying radiological phenotypes, and that the pathogenesis of purely solid BM fundamentally differs from that of purely cystic lesions. However, data regarding whether these phenotypic variations influence clinical outcomes remain sparse, particularly among neurosurgically managed patients. Accordingly, this study aimed to evaluate the association between distinct radiological phenotypes of BM—specifically lesion consistency, peri-tumoral edema extent, and vascular territory—and a range of clinical and prognostic outcomes in a cohort of surgically treated lung cancer patients.

Material and methods

This study was approved by our Institutional Review Board and conducted in accordance with ethical standards of the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. The design and reporting of this study adhere to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for retrospective cohort studies. A retrospective search of the electronic and computerized medical records was performed to identify all adult patients (age ≥18 years) with histopathological confirmed BM with lung origin treated at the neurosurgical department of a tertiary medical center/. Due to the retrospective, anonymous nature of the study, no consents for participation were required. The study population was derived from a parent institutional registry of 194 patients with 273 BMs evaluated between 2012 and 2022, whose baseline radiological correlations were previously described. 13 While Oxman et al. examined cross-sectional anatomical features across mixed tumor primaries and multi-lesional cases without longitudinal outcome data, the current study isolates a specific, homogeneous surgical cohort of 89 patients with solitary, resected lung cancer BM to investigate long-term brain control (local/distant recurrence), progression-free survival, and overall survival.

Patients included in the study had a pathology-based diagnosis of BM as well as complete medical and radiologic records. Additionally, all patients underwent resection (aiming at gross total resection) of one BM. Exclusion criteria were as follows: patients who underwent prior central nervous system (CNS) therapy, including surgery, radiation, CNS-localized chemotherapy (intrathecal chemotherapy), and systemic immunotherapy for BM; leptomeningeal metastases at presentation; Sellar/parasellar cases; “extra-axial” (dural based) BM; hemorrhagic BM at presentation; small BM (defined as volume <1 cm3); patients with purely cystic lesion that underwent stereotactic aspiration without open resection; patients who underwent resection of >1 BM; cases in which the related vascular territory was not clearly defined (=”watershed” cases); and patients with insufficient data/loss to follow up.

Cystic BM was defined as the presence of a rim enhancing lesion with a central fluid component which can be determined by the radiologic appearance of hypo/hyper intensity on T1/T2-weighted MRI imaging, respectively. The rim of lesion was enhanced on T1-weighted post contrast (gadolinium) MRI. BM were divided into three groups based on their radiological phenotype as follow:

Studied variables

Data on the following parameters were extracted for each of the eligible cases: demographics (age, gender); clinical (symptoms – type and duration, subtype of lung cancer, neuroendocrine tumor, known systemic metastases at diagnosis of BM, time till adjuvant therapy; type of adjuvant therapy); surgical (extent of resection, en block vs. piecemeal resection, fluorescence – guided resection, ventricular entry at resection), radiological (number of BM, side affected, anatomical location, vascular territory involved, hemorrhage at presentation, presence of cyst, type of cyst, tumor’s volume; extent of peri-lesional edema, radiologic phenotype of the tumor); genetic/molecular analysis (lung – anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) translocation, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutation, transcription termination factor 1 (TTF-1) expression, Kirsten rat sarcoma (KRAS) mutation, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression, TP53 mutation) and prognostic parameters (overall survival, progression-free survival, local recurrence, distant recurrence, leptomeningeal spread).

Symptomatology was divided into seizures, symptoms of increased intracranial pressure (headaches, nausea/vomiting, blurred vision, change in consciousness), symptoms due to involvement of eloquent areas (focal symptoms - hemiparesis, sensation deficits, speech difficulties, visual field deficits) and cerebellar-related symptoms. Radiologic variables were either magnetic resonance (preferred) or computed tomography based. Anatomical location was categorized by the involved lobe or the specific site (i.e., basal ganglia, thalamus, hippocampus). Vascular territory was divided into posterior circulation (territories supplied by the basilar, vertebral, superior cerebellar and posterior cerebral arteries) and anterior circulation (territories supplied by the internal carotid, anterior cerebral, and middle cerebral arteries) (Figure 1). Tumor’s volumetric analysis was performed using BrainLab Smart Brush® software (BrainLAB AG©, Munich, Germany). Calculations of tumor volume were based on 3D reconstructions of the tumor. Preoperative neuroimaging was performed using standard diagnostic protocols on 1.5 T (n = 58, 65.2%) or 3.0 T (n = 31, 34.8%) MRI scanners (Siemens Magnetom/GE Discovery). Standard sequences included axial T1-weighted spin-echo pre- and post-contrast (0.1 mmol/kg gadolinium-based contrast agent), axial T2-weighted, and T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images with a slice thickness ≤1.0 mm (3D isotropic acquisitions) or 3.0 mm (2D planar acquisitions). Diagnostic contrast-enhanced CT was utilized for volumetric alignment in 4 patients unable to undergo MRI due to contraindications. Extent of peri-lesional edema was determined by the maximal width (millimeters) of peri-tumoral high intensity signal on T2-weighted FLAIR imaging. Peri-lesional edema was also graded based on the ratio between the edema and the tumor’s maximal diameter as follows: grade 0 – no edema; grade 1 – 1:1 >ratio< 2:1; grade 2 – 2:1≥ratio<3:1; and grade 3 - ≥3:1. Grades 0 or 1 and grades 2 or 3 were defined as light edema and severe edema, respectively (Figure 1). Imaging features were evaluated independently by two senior neurosurgeons (Y.L. and A.A.K.) blinded to clinical outcomes. Interobserver agreement prior to consensus was assessed using Cohen’s kappa ( κ ). For radiological phenotype classification, initial agreement was 92.1% (82/89) cases; Cohen’s κ=0.86 ); for edema severity grading, initial agreement was 94.4% (84/89) cases; Cohen’s κ=0.88 ). All discordant cases (n = 7 for phenotype; n = 5 for edema) were resolved by joint consensus review.

All patients underwent surgical resection. In some cases, according to surgeon’s preference, a fluorescence – guided resection was performed. For this, 5- aminolaevulinic acid (20 mg/kg body weight) in a freshly prepared solution was administered orally 3h before induction of anesthesia. Solutions were prepared by dissolving the contents of a vial (1.5 g) in 60 mL of drinking water. Surgery was performed with a modified neurosurgical microscope (Leica M530 OHX® with fluorescence kit, GmbH, Blackstone, Germany) with which conventional white xenon illumination could be switched to violet–blue excitation light. To accurately determine the extent of resection (EOR) and avoid confounding from non-tumoral surgically induced reactive enhancement, all patients underwent early postoperative contrast-enhanced MRI within 24–48 hours of surgery. Subsequent MRIs performed at 30 days and at 2–4-month intervals were used for radiation planning and longitudinal surveillance. EOR was defined as the preoperative tumor volume minus the postoperative residual tumor volume divided by the preoperative total tumor volume. Gross total resection (GTR) was defined as 100% EOR; near-total resection, as ≥95% but less than 100%, and subtotal resection as ≥80% but <95% EOR.

Histopathology

All histopathologic assessments were conducted by an investigator blinded to the clinical and imaging data. The tumor was identified and selected for analysis. Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) was extracted and used for library preparation based on multiplex polymerase chain reaction amplification using Oncomine solid tumor panel kit. Next generation sequencing was performed on the S5 Ion Torrent system for the identification of mutations in cancer related genes in formalin fixed paraffin embedded (FFPE) tumor tissue. The test was designed to detect mutations in hotspot regions, located within the following exons: EGFR - exons 12,18,19,20,21; KRAS - exons: 2,3,4. Lung BM were considered positive for ALK, TTF1, EGFR, KRAS, TP53 or PDL1 based on molecular analysis for the relevant mutation or the relevant immunohistochemistry staining. A PDL1 expression of <1%, 1–49%, and ≥50% was considered negative, low, and high expression positive, respectively.

Outcome measures

The most updated graded prognostic assessment (GPA) for patients with lung cancer and brain metastases 14 was used as a prognostic index for all patients.

All patients underwent surgical resection of a single BM by one of two neurosurgeons (AK and YL). As a concept, unless recommended otherwise by the treating oncologist, all patients have received adjuvant radiotherapy, preferably in the form of stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) (TrueBeam®, Varian Siemens Healthineers©, USA). In addition, eligible patients have received systemic adjuvant treatment in the form of chemotherapy and/or targeted therapy or immunotherapy, based on clinical decision of the treating oncologists.

Distant parenchymal recurrences were defined as new contrast-enhancing foci located >2.0 cm away from the initial tumor borders. Leptomeningeal dissemination was defined as enhancement or nodular deposits in the cerebral ventricular wall and/or the subarachnoid spaces of the brain or spinal cord. Primary dissemination was defined as spread of the tumor through the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) at the time of diagnosis. Secondary dissemination was defined as new spread through the CSF, not seen on preoperative imaging.

Follow-up imaging was reviewed by a multidisciplinary neuro-oncology tumor board comprising senior neuroradiologists, neuro-oncologists, and neurosurgeons. Progression free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from the date of diagnosis to the first progression according to RANO-BM criteria. 15 To differentiate true progression/recurrence from treatment-related effects (such as radiation necrosis), dynamic susceptibility contrast perfusion MRI (evaluating relative cerebral blood volume, rCBV) and short-interval follow-up MRI (4–6 weeks) were performed in equivocal cases.” OS was defined as the time from the date of diagnosis to death (non-censored) or last follow-up (censored). Patients who were lost to follow-up were excluded.

Qualitative radiological variables (e.g., tumor phenotype, vascular territory, and edema grade) were converted into standardized categorical dummy variables prior to statistical synthesis. Concordance between qualitative image evaluations and quantitative volumetric data was verified across all cases prior to survival modeling.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were employed to analyze the attributes of the study population. Each variable was presented by the most suitable central and dispersion measures: nominal variables were presented by number and percent (%), numerical variables were presented by either mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median and inter-quartile range (IQR). Normal distribution of numerical variables was assessed using histograms, Q-Q plots, Shapiro-Wilk test, and Kolmogorov-Smirnov test.

First, we conducted univariate analysis to assess the clinical and sociodemographic characteristics of the study cohort stratified by the radiological phenotype. For continuous variables we used the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test and for nominal variables we used either Chi-square test of Fisher exact test.

Compare of medians for independent ordinal variables was carried out using the Kruskal-Wallis-Test. Linear regression and Cox-regression models with covariates were used for survival analyses. A multivariate analysis for the different outcome parameters was stratified by variables that were found to be significantly different between the three groups on univariate analysis. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were plotted to calculate OS and PFS. Differences between groups were evaluated using Mantel-Cox log-rank test and are presented in hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence interval (CI). For all analyses, p-values <0.05 are considered significant. All analyses were performed using IBM® SPSS® statistics v29.0.1.0 software as well as R® v4.4.1.

Results

During the study period, 194 patients with 273 BM underwent surgical intervention at our institution. Of these, 184 BM cases were excluded from the analysis as shown in Figure 2. The remaining 89 cases formed the study cohort. They included 55 males and 34 females with a mean age of 63.1 ± 9.0 years (range: 25–89). The median follow-up duration for the overall cohort was 18.4 months (95% CI 14.2–22.6 months). Among surviving patients, the median follow-up calculated via the reverse Kaplan-Meier method was 22.1 months (IQR 12.5–34.8 months).Figure 2.Schematic illustration of study profile.

Primary lung cancer histological distribution was as follows: adenocarcinoma (n = 58), neuroendocrine (n = 13), squamous cell carcinoma (n = 9), small cell (n = 4), large cell (n = 1) and other (n = 4). Thus, most cases (95.5%) were non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). 71.9% of cases were supratentorial and 28.1% were infratentorial. Following categorization according to radiologic phenotypes, 24 (27%), 46 (51.6%) and 19 (21.4%) cases were considered purely solid, cystic-necrotic and purely cystic, respectively.

Adjuvant radiotherapy was given to >75% of patients, with SRS being the main modality. No differences were found between groups regarding adjuvant systemic therapy. Discussion

This study has examined the potential impact of certain anatomical parameters and radiologic – related phenotypes on brain control and survival outcomes in a population of surgically treated lung BM. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that examine such correlations in surgically treated BM cohort. This is a retrospective study. Nevertheless, the studied subgroups were quite homogenous for most of the examined variables, increasing the statistical strength of the research. Importantly, GPA scores (which include KPS levels) were similar between all groups, allowing for a more focused analysis of the radiologic phenotypes themselves. The study shows that OS is not affected by the involved vascular territory or by the radiologic phenotypes of surgically treated BM from lung cancer. There may be a correlation between the purely cystic phenotype and poorer PFS, especially through a negative impact on rates of distant recurrences. These potential correlations are further discussed.

Peri-tumoral edema and vascular territories involved

As expected, we have shown that the severity of peri-tumoral edema affects clinical presentation, as patients with severe peri-tumoral edema had significantly shorter mean period from cancer to BM diagnosis. In addition, patients with severe peri-tumoral edema showed a trend towards shorter periods until local recurrence and were associated with worse PFS on univariate analysis. Nevertheless, we have demonstrated that edema severity is not significantly associated with any other outcome-related parameters. Similar outcome-related findings were demonstrated regarding the affected vascular territories, in which anterior and posterior circulations correlated with severe and light edema, respectively. Significant associations were demonstrated between the affected vascular territories and the different radiologic phenotypes, as previously described. 13

The correlation between peri-tumoral edema and survival in BM cases is controversial. In a survival – analysis study on a cohort of surgically treated BM (mixed cohort of primary tumors), the only two factors that were significantly associated with favorable OS on multivariate analysis were GPA and large peri-tumoral brain edema. 16 The same study also found that patients with small peri-tumoral edema were characterized by greater brain-invasive growth and less angiogenic activity. 16 The possible correlation between the cystic component of brain tumors, peri-tumoral brain edema and angiogenic activity of the tumor is an important research subject but is beyond the scope of current paper. 17 In a study on NSCLC patients undergoing SRS for BM it was found that lower rates of distant brain control (i.e., new disease outside of the radiation field) were significantly associated with low peri-tumoral edema to mass index. 18 It was suggested that BMs showing a relatively large total volume in comparison to peri-tumoral edema may be biologically aggressive. 19 These results are in concordance with the observations found in our current study, especially when considering that minimal edema is associated with the purely cystic phenotype which in turn was shown in this study to be associated with increased risk of distant recurrence. In a study on surgically treated BM (from different primaries) in the posterior fossa it was found that edema volume alone was not affecting OS. 20 These studies and others 21 support the notion that the extent of edema is not necessarily associated with poor prognosis. Edema’s severity seems to be responsible for most of the patients’ symptoms but does not affect OS. 5 Similar conclusion was reached in a study on a cohort of surgically treated BM in NSCLC patients, where the mass-edema index did not impact OS. 22 The data from our current study on the correlation between edema severity and survival supports these conclusions.

Nevertheless, a controversy does exist. A study on cohort of NSCLC patients with BM that were treated with radiotherapy has found that higher peri-tumoral edema volume was associated with an increased risk of intracranial progression and a lesser probability of responding to brain radiotherapy. Major edema was negatively impacted the median OS (HR 2.1; p = 0.001). 23

Taken together with the results of our current study, it is reasonable to conclude that the role of peri-tumoral edema in survival of patients with lung BM may require larger scale prospective studies in order to define it more accurately. Nevertheless, the accumulative data suggest that the impact of peri-lesional edema on OS is minimal, especially in surgically – treated cases. In this regard it should be noted that the Graded Prognostic Assessment for lung cancer BM (e.g. Lung-molGPA index) is a powerful prognostic tool that effectively identifies patients at different risks, yet it does not include peri-lesional edema diameter as a prognostic parameter.14,24

Radiologic phenotypes and survival correlations

Our study shows limited association between BM’s consistency and outcome-related parameters. Purely cystic cases showed a trend towards shorter mean period between cancer diagnosis and BM diagnosis when compared to cystic-necrotic cases. In addition, the rate of distant recurrence was nearly two-fold higher in the purely cystic subgroup (58.8%) compared to the cystic-necrotic subgroup (28.9%), although only mildly higher compared to the purely solid cases. Purely cystic cases were associated with increased risk for both poorer PFS as well as distant recurrence. However, this trend has only achieved significance (HR = 2.92, p = 0.040) in multivariable model controlling for predefined clinical and biological covariates (adenocarcinoma histology, edema severity grade, and vascular territory). Given that the confidence interval lower bound approaches unity and significance is model-dependent, and in the absence of formal power calculations for small subgroup sizes, this finding should be interpreted cautiously as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating signal rather than a definitive prognostic claim. For all other survival outcomes, comparing the different phenotypes in different combinations and definitions showed no other significant differences or meaningful trends. Importantly, none of the phenotypes was associated with significantly improved OS. In our study no significant difference was found between groups regarding the GPA scores. This raises the question regarding the potential differences in the molecular pathways that lead to the different radiological phenotypes described. It should be emphasized that the Lung-molGPA index is a composite clinical prognostic scoring system rather than a direct molecular pathway quantification tool. Radiological phenotypes represent complex, overlapping downstream expressions of multiple biological pathways (e.g., tumor angiogenesis, altered vascular permeability, and central ischemic necrosis). Because scoring systems and single-phenotype analyses evaluate these manifestations individually, there is an inherent risk of overemphasizing individual pathway enrichment where biological mechanisms overlap. Comprehensive multi-omic profiling will be essential to uncouple these shared pathways in future cohorts.

Cystic BM is regarded as an important sign of poor prognosis in some types of cancer. In a study on BM in a cohort of breast cancer patients, the authors have suggested that cystic BM are associated with poorer OS. 8 However, the statistical strength of this study was very limited. Previous study on the prognosis of cystic BM in NSCLC have shown that the OS in patients with targeted therapies in cystic BM was significantly longer than that in solid lesions (47.9 months vs 23.4 months, p = 0.013). On the other hand, PFS was significantly shorter in the cystic group which did not receive targeted therapies, compared to the solid group (6.5 months vs 11.7 months, p = 0.003). 9 This study enphasizes the theoretical prognostic and clinical importance of BM consistency and morphology and similar to our results show the negative association between cystic morphology and PFS. However, it lacks data regarding surgical interventions. In addition, the study does not distinguish purely cystic from cystic-necrotic lesions. Lastly, it should be noted that targeted therapy is only suitable for minority (∼30%) of caner patients with BM. 25 Comparative studies between lung and breast cancer brain metastases26,27 demonstrate that while cystic features portend specific local control challenges across cancer types, the integration of potent, central nervous system-penetrant targeted agents (e.g., EGFR/ALK TKIs in NSCLC, or tucatinib regimens in HER2 + breast cancer) plays a vital role in extending intracranial PFS and preventing distant failure.

Other studies on cystic lung BM have focused on radiotherapy. Brigell et al. compared the local control after brain-directed radiation in patients with cystic vs solid BM and found that patients with cystic BM have poorer local control after receiving stereotactic radiation. 28 In another study, brain radiotherapy shows superior survival benefits for lung cancer patients with solid BMs compared to those with cystic BMs, particularly in EGFR-mutant lung cancer. 29 On the other hand, Wang et al. found no difference in survival times between cystic and solid brain tumors treated with SRS and suggested that radiosurgery may be a suitable treatment option for cystic BM. 7

Our study suggests a correlation between a purely cystic phenotype and an increased risk of distant recurrence, as both the incidence of distant recurrence as well as the time to distant recurrence was less favorable in purely cystic cases. Purely cystic lesions are significantly associated with decreased peri-tumoral edema. 13 As mentioned previously, limited edema was associated with increased brain-invasive growth and higher rates of distant recurrence, which may explain the observed association between a purely cystic phenotype and an elevated risk of distant recurrence. The precise pathophysiology of this phenomenon will require further investigation. Another theoretical explanation may be the surgical technique being used. We excluded cases treated with stereotactic aspiration in order to keep a homogenous cohort. It is possible that open resection of a purely cystic lesion may lead to more spillage of tumor cells and to increased risk of distant recurrence. On the other hand, indications for open resection (e.g., severe mass effect, larger volume, rapid clinical deterioration) may inherently select for biologically aggressive lesions. Consequently, whether the higher distant recurrence observed in purely cystic cases stems from surgical intraoperative cyst rupture/cell spillage or intrinsic tumor aggressiveness cannot be conclusively determined from these data. Cystic brain metastases treated with stereotactic cyst aspiration followed by radiosurgery appear to achieve reasonable local control, 30 but distant brain failure and progression elsewhere in the brain remain a relevant concern in this population. Our review of the literature has failed to find proper comparison regarding the risks associated with open resection vs. stereotactic aspiration of cystic BM and there are no decisive conclusions regarding the preferred surgical intervention. Park et al. 31 have reported a 25% rate of remote progression and Franzin et al. 10 reported a 52% risk of remote progression following aspiration and SRS. On the other hand, only 9% (2/22) of patients treated with Omaya reservoir placement and SRS have shown distant progression. 32 This topic will require further research, including prospective studies comparing resection versus aspiration-radiosurgery strategies.

Clinical implications and future directions

The potential association between a metastasis’s radiologic phenotype and the risk of distant progression may carry significant implications for neuro-oncological practice. Based on our findings, we propose several areas where radiologic phenotyping could inform clinical decision-making, though these should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive recommendations: tailored postoperative surveillance - given the nearly two-fold higher rate of distant recurrence in purely cystic cases compared to cystic-necrotic cases, radiologic phenotyping could help tailor postoperative management. Patients with purely cystic lesions may benefit from closer surveillance with shorter follow-up imaging intervals (e.g., every 6–8 weeks for the first 6 months) to allow for the early detection of distant failures; surgical strategy and planning - the initial surgical approach may be influenced by the radiologic phenotype. While open resection remains a standard for solitary lesions, there is a theoretical risk that resection of a purely cystic lesion may lead to tumor cell spillage, potentially increasing the risk of distant recurrence. In selected cases, clinicians might weigh the benefits of open resection against minimally invasive aspiration followed by SRS; risk-stratified adjuvant therapy - for patients demonstrating a purely cystic phenotype, an aggressive radiotherapy strategy—such as early SRS or whole-brain radiotherapy—may be considered, given the higher risk of distant recurrence in this subgroup and prior suggestions of benefit in cases with small peri-tumoral edema 18 ; patient counseling - cystic morphology may be incorporated as a prognostic factor during surgical planning to aid in counseling patients about the specific likelihood of distant recurrence and to support more individualized decisions regarding adjuvant treatment; molecular and systemic considerations - although our analysis of molecular markers—including EGFR, KRAS, and PD-L1—did not reveal significant phenotypic correlations, the lack of complete molecular data for all cases remains a limitation. Future research should explore whether specific genomic alterations drive the purely cystic phenotype, which could eventually inform the choice of systemic agents in conjunction with local therapies.

Beyond the purely cystic phenotype, future studies should also investigate the role of the edema-mass ratio in multi-centric disease, as our study was limited to solitary metastases. These theoretical implications provide a framework for more powerful, prospectively designed studies to validate the utility of radiologic phenotypes in lung cancer brain metastases. Bridging the gap between qualitative radiological insights and robust prognostic tools requires incorporating granular imaging features into established clinical models (such as the Lung-molGPA). 14 Recent machine learning applications for local control in brain metastases 33 and global collaborative frameworks like the Consortium for Intracranial Metastasis Academic Research 26 highlight the potential of combining volumetric edema ratios, phenotype classifications, and molecular markers into unified predictive algorithms to guide local neurosurgical and radiosurgical interventions. We believe that our results contribute to the growing data on the correlation between radiomics and prognosis and will the improve the accuracy of future deep learning tools.

Limitations

This study is characterized by several limitations, primarily stemming from its retrospective design, which introduces inherent selection and detection biases. Our cohort was intentionally restricted to a highly homogeneous subset—patients with solitary, resectable lung cancer BM (≥1 cm3) and no prior CNS-directed therapy—to maximize internal validity for neurosurgical decision-making and due to the importance of an individually tailored treatment in the current oncologic era. Consequently, these findings may not be generalizable to patients with multiple metastases, very small lesions, or those treated primarily with SRS, whole-brain radiotherapy, or systemic therapy alone. Methodologically, while the radiologic phenotypes were defined by quantitative criteria (<2 mm rim for purely cystic), a degree of imaging overlap may exist. The elevated prevalence of purely cystic lesions (21.4%) relative to unselected populations (∼4.4%) suggests selection effects that could influence outcome associations. For the vast majority of cases, no standardized vascular imaging was done. Thus, in some cases, our interpretation of the possible vascular territory, based on non-vascular imaging, may be inaccurate. We have tried to overcome this bias by excluding cases in a water-shed territory between the two main circulations. Non-uniform surveillance schedules and imaging modalities over 2012–2022 may introduce detection bias for distant recurrence and PFS. Standardized prospective protocols are needed to confirm phenotype-associated risks under harmonized follow-up. Statistically, some analyses were limited by the subgroup sizes and the number of events, particularly regarding PFS and distant recurrence trends. While multivariable models were used to adjust for key confounders like adenocarcinoma histology and edema severity, the exploratory nature of these results—especially those reaching only trend-level significance—requires cautious interpretation. Finally, our molecular data was incomplete for a significant portion of the cohort, limiting our ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding genomic drivers of these phenotypes. As such, our clinical suggestions regarding aggressive adjuvant therapy or intensified surveillance should be viewed as hypothesis-generating frameworks for future prospective validation rather than formal clinical recommendations.

Conclusions

In a selected cohort of lung cancer patients (the vast majority being NSCLC) undergoing resection of a solitary BM, the radiologic phenotypes showed limited impact on OS. While the extent and severity of peri-tumoral edema may affect the clinical timeline, no other effects on outcome parameters were found on multivariate analyses. In this surgical subset, purely cystic BM might be associated with higher risk of distant recurrence and unfavorable PFS; generalization to non-surgical or multiple BM populations require external validation. For some outcome parameters, meaningful differences between the 3 radiologic groups were found. Further studies are needed using our suggested definitions of radiologic phenotypes of BM in order to deepen our understanding of their clinical and prognostic implications.
