Analysis of perioperative period coagulation function affected by anti-seizure medications in epilepsy surgery
Authors: Yue Huang, Dejin Gao, Wanchen Dou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDrug-resistant epilepsy is a chronic condition that refers to epilepsy that does not improve with adequate medication treatment. For such cases, surgical intervention is a crucial strategy. However, in some patients, hemorrhage may occur after the operation. Proper management of anti-seizure medications is essential for perioperative care in epilepsy surgery. It should be noted that certain anti-seizure medications may negatively affect coagulation function. In this study, we aim to summarize the coagulation function of epilepsy patients during the perioperative period and compare the postoperative coagulation function when using sodium valproate and levetiracetam.MethodEighty-two epilepsy patients (age = 30.79 ± 10.30 years old; M:F = 49:33) who underwent surgical treatments in Peking Union Medical College Hospital from April 2019 to April 2023 were enrolled in the study. Coagulation functions were evaluated using laboratory examination. Based on their perioperative medication, they were divided into two groups - sodium valproate and levetiracetam. We used t-test to measure the coagulation function parameters affected by the operation and then used independent t-test to determine the differences in coagulation function between the two groups.ResultAbnormal PT, APTT, Fbg within 3 days after operation were found 84.8%, 28.3%, 93.5% respectively in patients using sodium valproate intravenous preparation, while these parameters were 38.9%, 25.0%, 83.3% respectively in patients using levetiracetam intravenous preparation. The patients who took sodium valproate intravenous preparation get higher value of PT increase in perioperative time compared to patients using levetiracetam intravenous preparation.ConclusionPatients with epilepsy are at risk of experiencing abnormal coagulation function following epilepsy surgery. It has been observed that patients may experience more severe and prolonged prothrombin time (PT) when using intravenous valproic acid preparation during the perioperative period, as compared to using levetiracetam intravenous preparation. These findings highlight the importance of monitoring coagulation function after epilepsy surgery. Surgeons should closely monitor patients who receive sodium valproate treatment during the perioperative period for any potential impact on their coagulation function post-surgery.Keywords: Epilepsy surgery, Perioperative management, Coagulation function, Anti-seizure medications

Patients with epilepsy are at risk of experiencing abnormal coagulation function following epilepsy surgery. It has been observed that patients may experience more severe and prolonged prothrombin time (PT) when using intravenous valproic acid preparation during the perioperative period, as compared to using levetiracetam intravenous preparation. These findings highlight the importance of monitoring coagulation function after epilepsy surgery. Surgeons should closely monitor patients who receive sodium valproate treatment during the perioperative period for any potential impact on their coagulation function post-surgery.

Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder that results from various factors and causes recurrent, episodic, and temporary dysfunction of the central nervous system, defined as a disorder of the brain characterized by an enduring predisposition to generate epileptic seizures, and by the neurobiologic, cognitive, psychological, and social consequences of this condition. It has a substantial impact on the quality of life and imposes a significant burden on society. Epilepsy accounts for 1% of the world’s diseases [1], with more than 30% of cases being refractory to medication [2]. Drug-resistant epilepsy is defined as the failure of two tolerated and appropriately chosen and used AED schedules (whether as monotherapies or in combination) to achieve sustained seizure freedom [3]. Surgical treatment provides the opportunity for drug-resistant epilepsy remission for patients with focal epilepsy whose seizures persist despite anti-seizure medications [4]. With the advancement of related science and technology, several surgical treatment methods have emerged over the past few decades. Surgical treatment methods for epilepsy can be categorized into resective surgery, palliative surgery, neurostimulation, and other techniques. Among these methods, resective surgery for epileptogenic zone is the most common surgical strategy [5]. Although resective surgery has a satisfactory outcome, the possible complications need to be taken into consideration. The common postoperative complications include neurological deficits, wound infections, hemorrhage, and central nervous system infection. These complications may affect the clinical outcome and sometimes pose a threat to life. Previous studies have reported that the rate of postoperative hemorrhage ranges from 1.2% to 3.2% [6]. It has been observed that patients who undergo surgical treatment for epilepsy have a higher potential for abnormal coagulation function, which increases the risks for postoperative hemorrhage. The hemostatic process can be primarily divided into the following steps. Initially, platelets aggregate at the site of bleeding, recruiting additional platelets to adhere to one another and form a primary hemostatic plug known as a platelet plug. This achieves preliminary hemostasis, referred to as primary hemostasis. Subsequently, upon activation of the coagulation system, a series of enzymatic reactions occur that work in conjunction with platelets to form a fibrinous thrombus. Existing studies suggest that anti-seizure medications exert multiple effects on this process. It is important to provide antiepileptic treatment to control status epilepticus during the perioperative period. This requires the administration of intravenous anti-seizure medications to patients. Due to factors such as fasting and fluid restriction prior to surgery, it is necessary to administer preventive intravenous anti-seizure medications. Furthermore, patients often remain in a sedated state postoperatively, which imposes certain limitations on the continuation of oral medications that were prescribed preoperatively. However, there is currently a lack of literature on the coagulation function during the perioperative period of epilepsy surgery, particularly regarding anti-seizure medications management.

Current understanding indicates that certain hepatic enzyme inducers (such as phenytoin, carbamazepine, and phenobarbital) may affect the metabolism of coagulation factors by inducing liver enzymes (e.g.CYP450), thereby increasing the risk of bleeding. Conversely, some enzyme inhibitors (such as valproate) may inhibit hepatic enzymes affecting coagulation factor metabolism and potentially lead to coagulopathy. Newer anti-seizure medications (such as levetiracetam and lamotrigine) are generally considered to have minimal impact on coagulation function. Research has confirmed that long-term oral administration of sodium valproate and other anti-seizure medications adversely affects both platelet count and function [7, 8]. Additionally, some studies have reported that levetiracetam can similarly impair platelet function [9]and coagulation function [10]. Phenytoin may accelerate vitamin K metabolism through hepatic enzyme induction, subsequently inhibiting the activity of clotting factors [11]. However, there remain gaps in research regarding pharmacological management during the perioperative period for patients undergoing craniotomy.

Due to the medication procurement has changed in our hospital, the patients were treated by different intravenous anti-seizure medications. Therefore, we conducted a study to summarize the coagulation function during the perioperative period of epilepsy surgery and to determine whether the use of sodium valproate and levetiracetam during this period has a different effect on coagulation dysfunction.

Method

Samples

This study was conducted in the Department of neurosurgery in the authors’ hospital between April 2019 and April 2023. A total of 82 patients diagnosed with drug-resistant epilepsy and performed surgical treatment were finally enrolled in this study.

Inclusion criteria

Diagnosis of drug-resistant epilepsy

Defined as the inability to achieve sustained seizure freedom (≥ 12 months) despite appropriately selected anti-seizure medications (ASMs) administered at adequate dosages.

Surgical intervention

Preoperative evaluations must demonstrate that scalp EEG or intracranial EEG has captured seizure activity consistent with clinical manifestations. Patients undergoing curative intracranial surgeries (e.g., anterior temporal lobectomy, lesionectomy, hemispherotomy) or palliative procedures (e.g., corpus callosotomy) are included.

Age

Participants must be aged ≥ 16 years (adolescent and adult population) to minimize developmental variability in coagulation profiles.

Perioperative coagulation data availability

A complete baseline coagulation panel, including PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen levels, platelet count, and D-dimer measurements, must be available within the perioperative period.

Exclusion criteria

Confounding coagulation disorders

Known congenital coagulopathies (e.g. hemophilia, von Willebrand disease). Active liver dysfunction (abnormal ALT or AST levels) or end-stage renal disease (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m²).

Pharmacological interference

Recent administration of anticoagulants (e.g., warfarin, direct oral anticoagulants) or antiplatelet agents (e.g., aspirin, clopidogrel) within the 7 days preceding surgery. Concurrent use of non-antiseizure medications that may influence coagulation (e.g., NSAIDs, heparin).

Systemic comorbidities

Presence of active malignancy, sepsis, or inflammatory conditions (CRP > 10 mg/L). Pregnancy or lactation due to hormonal effects on coagulation.

Surgical and data limitations

Emergent surgical interventions or procedures related to traumatic brain injury. Incomplete records of perioperative coagulation monitoring. Loss to follow-up within 30 days postoperatively.

Currently approved anti-seizure medications can be classified into three generations. First-generation anti-seizure medications include carbamazepine (a sodium channel blocker) and valproate (which exhibits a multi-target mechanism). Second-generation representatives comprise lamotrigine (acting on sodium and calcium channels) and levetiracetam (which binds to synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2 A, SV2A). Third-generation anti-seizure medications include brivaracetam (a high-affinity SV2A ligand) and cannabidiol (CBD, which modulates the endogenous cannabinoid system).

According to current evidence-based guidelines issued by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE), carbamazepine is recommended as first-line treatment for focal seizures, whereas valproate is preferred for generalized seizures. Novel anti-seizure medications such as lacosamide are increasingly used as alternative options owing to their favorable tolerability profiles.

When initial drug efficacy is suboptimal, adjustment of monotherapy—through stepwise dose titration and pharmacokinetic optimization—should be attempted. If seizure control remains inadequate, an add-on therapy strategy is recommended. When combining AEDs, it is important to avoid strong enzyme-inducing agents; theoretically, agents with complementary mechanisms of action should be selected to maximize synergistic effects.

In this study, patients on monotherapy were predominantly treated with carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, sodium valproate, or levetiracetam. Those receiving two-drug regimens generally received combinations of the aforementioned agents, with some also prescribed topiramate. Patients on triple therapy were administered three drugs from the listed options, and in some cases, lacosamide was incorporated.

This study was approved by Ethics Committee in the author’s hospital (IRB number Y2087).

Procedures

Patients diagnosed with drug-resistant epilepsy and scheduled for resection or disconnection of the epileptogenic zone underwent routine preoperative evaluation, including laboratory tests such as complete blood count and coagulation profile. Following craniotomy, coagulation parameters—including prothrombin time (PT), international normalized ratio (INR), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), and fibrinogen (FIB) concentration—were measured daily for three consecutive days.

Demographic and clinical data were recorded, including sex, age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, etiology of epilepsy, type of surgery, operative duration, intraoperative blood loss, anti-seizure medications, preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin (Hb) levels, and postoperative complications. These data were used to analyze perioperative coagulation profiles and baseline characteristics.

According to the intravenous anti-seizure medications administered during and after surgery, patients were divided into two groups: the sodium valproate group and the levetiracetam group. Patients in the sodium valproate group received 0.4 g intravenous sodium valproate on the day of surgery and on the first postoperative day. Similarly, those in the levetiracetam group received 0.5 g intravenous levetiracetam on the day of surgery and on the first postoperative day.

According to laboratory examination standards, we considered that PT above 12.6s, INR above 1.14, APTT above 32.5s, Fbg above 3.5 g/L and Hgb below 110 g/L as abnormal. We defined that post-operative PTmax as the maximum value of PT in 3 days after operation. We calculated POD1PT-Preoperative PT to measure the increase in prothrombin time after operation.

Statistical

Data were presented as ratio for classified variables and mean ± SD for continuous variables. One-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to examine the normality of all data. The relationship of coagulation function between pre-operation and post-operation was evaluated by paired samples t-test. We count the amount and proportion of abnormal coagulation function subscale. Then, unpaired t-test were performed to identify the possible difference that affects abnormal coagulation function across the sodium valproate group and levetiracetam group.

All the data were analyzed using IBM SPSS (version 22.0), and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Perioperative coagulation function summary

In this study, patients demonstrated normal coagulation function and hemoglobin levels prior to surgery. However, significant alterations in coagulation parameters were observed postoperatively. On the first day after surgery, abnormal prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), and fibrinogen (FIB) levels were observed in 60.9%, 15.2%, and 17.4% of patients in the sodium valproate group, respectively. In contrast, the levetiracetam group exhibited a lower incidence of abnormalities across all three parameters. Furthermore, a higher proportion of patients in the sodium valproate group (84.8%) exhibited abnormal PT within the first three postoperative days compared to those receiving levetiracetam. Postoperative prothrombin time analysis

Coagulation is initiated through two principal pathways: the tissue factor pathway (assessed by prothrombin time, PT) and the contact activation pathway (evaluated by activated partial thromboplastin time, APTT). These pathways converge through the formation of the prothrombinase complex, which generates thrombin; thrombin subsequently converts fibrinogen into fibrin to form a stable clot. PT is the most widely utilized coagulation test in clinical practice, reflecting the integrity of the extrinsic and common coagulation pathways. It is influenced by the activity of coagulation factors II, V, VII, X, and fibrinogen. PT is particularly useful in assessing the initiation phase of hemostasis [12]. Previous studies have indicated that prolonged PT is frequently observed during the perioperative period [13].

In the present study, a considerable number of epileptic patients exhibited abnormal PT within three days after surgery. A t-test was employed to evaluate whether the choice of perioperative anti-seizure medications was associated with prolonged PT. The results indicated that patients receiving levetiracetam perioperatively exhibited more favorable coagulation profiles than those receiving sodium valproate. Although prolonged PT occurred in both groups, the sodium valproate group demonstrated a significantly greater increase in PT postoperatively. Detailed data are provided in Table 3. Clinically, two patients in the sodium valproate group developed postoperative intracranial hemorrhage and required unplanned reoperation for evacuation of the hematoma. By contrast, no instances of intracranial hemorrhage were observed in the levetiracetam group.

Discussion

In this study, it was found that patients’ coagulation function changes after surgery compared to their pre-operative state. Epilepsy patients, in particular, tend to have prolonged prothrombin time after surgery, which may increase their risk of postoperative bleeding.

This study has revealed that abnormal blood clotting is common in epilepsy surgery patients. Surgeons should closely monitor post-operative coagulation function and intervene when necessary. The coagulation dysfunction mainly affects the extrinsic coagulation pathway in epilepsy patients.

Levetiracetam binds to synaptic vesicle protein SV2A, interfering with the release of the neurotransmitter stored within the vesicle [14]. It is a commonly used first-line choice for seizures in palliative care [15]. Previous studies have found that levetiracetam does not appear to cause clinically significant or relevant hematological adverse events suggestive of underlying hematological disorders [10].

Valproic acid (2-propyl pentanoic acid) is a first-line medication used for the treatment of epilepsy and bipolar disorder [16]. Advanced studies uncovered the potential of valproic acid to interfere with multiple regulatory mechanisms including histone deacetylases, GSK3 alpha and beta, Akt, the ERK pathway, the phosphoinositol pathway, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, GABA, and the OXPHOS system. Previous studies proved that valproic acid might negatively affect coagulation function, especially for patients with chronic drug administration [10, 17]. However, a drug’s acute and chronic administration are entirely different circumstances [18]. It is controversial for surgeons to ban valproate treatment before surgery. Studies did not show an increase in blood loss during craniotomy surgery in patients receiving preoperative valproate [19]. Our study focus on perioperative management. We find that compared to using levetiracetam intravenous preparation, using valproic acid intravenous preparation has more tendency to perform prolonged PT in the short term.

Currently, it has been confirmed that there is an association between the use of sodium valproate (VPA) and acquired coagulation factor deficiencies. Gerstner et al. [20] reported that among patients receiving VPA treatment for epilepsy, the incidence of coagulation factor abnormalities was approximately 4%. However, none of these patients experienced bleeding complications. Psaras et al. [21]found no correlation between VPA treatment and blood loss during neurosurgical procedures for brain tumors. Consequently, some scholars argue that VPA treatment should not be discontinued prior to any neurosurgical intervention. For patients undergoing temporal lobe resection for the treatment of epilepsy, no hematological side effects have been observed with the immediate perioperative use of valproic acid (VPA) [19]. Kurwale et al. indicates that in patients with normal coagulation screening and platelet counts prior to surgery, no clinically significant or relevant bleeding or blood loss was noted. Therefore, it is not recommended that all patients scheduled for surgical procedures discontinue VPA [22]. Therefore, the perioperative application of VPA remains a common choice for epilepsy surgeons during surgical procedures due to its reliable efficacy in controlling seizures and the extensive experience accumulated in its use [23].

Levetiracetam is a second-generation anti-seizure medications, and its clinical application during the perioperative period lacks comprehensive summaries compared to traditional medications such as valproate. Previous studies have focused on the use of levetiracetam and valproate in controlling seizures during epilepsy-related surgical procedures, indicating no statistically significant difference between the two drugs in terms of seizure control rates during this period [24]. However, there has been insufficient research examining their effects on coagulation function during the perioperative phase. Therefore, this study aims to address some of these gaps in knowledge.

According to the findings of this study, although perioperative intravenous administration of sodium valproate was associated with more pronounced coagulation dysfunction compared to intravenous use of levetiracetam, clinical observations did not reveal a significant increase in perioperative bleeding-related complications. This may be attributed to timely monitoring of the aforementioned physiological changes and laboratory abnormalities, as well as appropriate interventions (such as the administration of blood products) when necessary. Therefore, this paper does not advocate for discontinuing or switching medications due to the adverse effects on coagulation arising from intravenous infusion of sodium valproate. Instead, it emphasizes that careful attention should be paid to its impact on coagulation function during perioperative use.

Limitation

The limitations of this study include its nature as a single-center retrospective investigation with a limited sample size. In this retrospective clinical study, we recognize the existence of several potential biases that may influence our findings. Firstly, selection bias could impact the representativeness of the patient population, as our research is restricted to patients who provided complete medical history information. We have endeavored to control for this through stringent inclusion and exclusion criteria, as well as by implementing treatment and documentation processes carried out by the same surgical team to manage certain variables. Secondly, information bias may arise from reliance on potentially incomplete or inaccurate historical medical records. We have made concerted efforts to mitigate information bias using these methods. However, despite our best attempts at controlling for confounding factors, confounding bias may still persist since not all potential confounders can be adequately adjusted for within a retrospective design. In discussing hemorrhage-related complications, platelet function plays an important role; however, due to the nature of this study being based on existing clinical data summary, incorporating discussions related to thromboelastometry and platelet function would necessitate additional invasive testing for patients and increase healthcare costs. Furthermore, obtaining relevant results would require significantly expanding the sample size; thus, this study does not address the impact of anti-seizure medications on platelet function at this time. Due to budgetary constraints, this study is currently unable to monitor blood drug concentrations. If conditions permit in the future, related further research may be conducted to clarify the impact of perioperative anti-seizure medications on coagulation function.

Conclusion

The current study indicated that epilepsy patients are likely to suffer from abnormal coagulation function after epilepsy surgery. In particular, abnormal PT after operation is quite common in epilepsy patients. Patients suffer from more serious prolonged PT when using valproic acid intravenous preparation during perioperative time than using levetiracetam intravenous preparation. During the perioperative period, when administering sodium valproate intravenously for the treatment of epilepsy, it is essential to closely monitor changes in the patient’s coagulation function.
