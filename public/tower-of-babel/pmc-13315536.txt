Baseline Financial Toxicity and Cost-Coping Mechanisms Among Patients Presenting for Radiation Therapy
Authors: Chaewon Hwang, Daniela Reyes, Kian Sahani, Hannah Koo, Katharine Esselen, Michele R Hacker, Joanne W Jang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeFinancial toxicity (FT) is an increasingly recognized barrier to high-quality cancer care, yet data describing FT at the initiation of radiation therapy across cancer types are limited. We evaluated the prevalence of FT and its associations with demographic factors, clinical characteristics, and cost-coping behaviors among patients starting radiation therapy at a tertiary academic center.Methods and MaterialsAdult patients scheduled to begin radiation therapy completed a pretreatment survey as part of a prospective longitudinal study. The survey included the 12-item Comprehensive Score for Financial Toxicity tool, demographic and clinical variables, and cost-coping behaviors. High FT was defined as a Comprehensive Score for Financial Toxicity score ≤22 (top tertile). We used χ2, Fisher’s exact, and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests to compare high and low FT groups and log-binomial regression to estimate risk ratios (RRs) for cost-coping behaviors.ResultsAmong 265 respondents, 35% (n = 94) had high FT. These patients were younger, and more likely to be unmarried, have lower income and education, and speak a language other than English (all P ≤ 0.02). Self-reported race/ethnicity, income, type of cancer, and receipt of chemotherapy also differed by FT (all P ≤ .001). Compared with patients with low/no FT, those with high FT were more likely to reduce or stop work (21% vs 7%; P < .001), delay medical care (RR, 13; 95% CI, 4-43), borrow money (RR, 10; 95% CI, 4-26), and cut back on essential goods (RR, 7; 95% CI, 3-16). Parking and transportation costs also more frequently influenced medical decisions (24% vs 6%; P < .001).ConclusionsHigh FT was common at radiation therapy initiation and associated with social determinants of health and clinical factors. It was strongly linked to behaviors that may negatively impact outcomes, highlighting the need for early FT screening and targeted interventions during radiation therapy planning.

Introduction

Financial Toxicity (FT) refers to the psychological and behavioral burden of health care costs, particularly in cancer care, where treatment complexity and expenses are high. Major contributors to FT include insurance premiums, medical costs, job loss, travel for care, and a personal history of long-term financial instability.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8

FT has also been linked to poorer quality of life, emotional distress, delaying or avoiding care, and increased mortality.9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14 A national survey (1990-2009) found that after a cancer diagnosis, employment fell by 10%, work hours dropped by 200 hours annually, earnings declined by 40%, and family income decreased by 20% within 2 years.15 A study by Zafar et al16 of 245 patients with cancer found that FT not only impacted medication adherence but also led to reduced spending on essential items like food, clothing, and leisure activities, as well as diminished savings for the future. Notably, 42% of participants in the study experienced significant or catastrophic financial burden during their treatment and follow-up period.16 Finally, Yabroff et al13 evaluated over 25,000 cancer survivors in the United States who had filled out the National Health Interview Survey and reported higher mortality risk among those with financial hardship.

To measure and quantify FT, de Souza et al17 developed the Comprehensive Score for Financial Toxicity Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy, version 1 (COST-FACIT, also known as COST), an 11-item validated survey scored 0 to 44; lower scores indicate higher FT. Using the COST tool, studies have shown that FT is associated with demographic factors, as well as treatment and cancer characteristics, and can affect cancer care and outcomes. In head and neck cancer, younger age and low income predicted severe FT and nonadherence to care.18 Among patients with breast cancer, surgery type, employment, medication costs, and marital status were linked to FT.19 At our institution, among patients with gynecologic cancer who had completed radiation therapy, those who were younger and privately insured reported greater FT, which in turn was associated with care delays.20 Patients residing in rural areas also face higher FT, as shown in a radiation oncology study.21

Centralization of complex cancer care, such as complex surgery, systemic therapy, or specialized radiation therapy at large academic centers, increases travel costs, disrupts daily life, and exacerbates FT.16,22,23 Furthermore, minoritized populations bear disproportionate burdens; nearly half of the survivors of cancer report financial hardship, with a higher prevalence among patients who identify as African American (68%) compared with patients who identify as White (44%).24,25

Therefore, we sought to characterize the baseline FT and associated cost-coping strategies reported by patients prior to starting radiation therapy in a large tertiary hospital with specialized capabilities such as CyberKnife (CK) stereotactic body radiation therapy and complex brachytherapy procedures.

Methods and Materials

All patients aged >18 years who were undergoing radiation therapy were eligible to participate. Patients were approached and asked to complete the survey before the start of radiation therapy. This survey was part of a larger prospective survey study in which patients were also asked to complete surveys 1 month and 1 year after completion of radiation therapy. The survey was available in English; patients with other language preferences completed the survey with translators or with the help of family members who were fluent in English.

The survey used here was based on an earlier survey developed by our group,9 which was a pilot study tested and revised based on feedback, and used in subsequent studies.20 The survey for the current study included the COST tool, version 2. Additional questions addressed demographic and disease characteristics, and social determinants of health. We also included questions on loans, savings, and insurance status. The cost-coping strategies included items such as borrowing money, using savings, and cutting back on leisure activities.

Version 2 of the COST tool contains 12 questions pertaining to FT and is scored 0 to 44, with the 12th question not counting toward the total score. Lower COST scores indicate higher FT. In the absence of a standard definition for high FT that is generalizable across cancer types and geographic locations, in our earlier work, we defined high FT as the top tertile of COST scores.9 We used the same approach here in order to isolate the group at highest risk. Thus, high FT was defined as a COST score ≤22, which was the top tertile; low FT was defined as a COST score >22.

Surveys were deemed incomplete and excluded from the analysis if participants responded to fewer than 6 questions on the COST tool. We also excluded surveys that we suspected were incorrectly filled out, as evidenced by the respondent selecting the same answer for all questions, including the 4 reverse-coded questions. A medical record review was conducted to gather additional demographic, disease, and treatment information.

Data are reported as frequency (percent) and median (IQR). We compared high and low/no FT groups with χ2, Fisher’s exact, and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. We used log-binomial regression to estimate risk ratios and 95% confidence intervals. Based on prior literature, we considered age, insurance, income, and employment as potential confounders, and ran adjusted models with all 4 of those variables. P values <.05 were deemed statistically significant. The institutional review board approved this study (protocol #: 2021P001006).

Results

Of the 305 respondents who initiated radiation therapy, we excluded 32 incomplete surveys and 8 surveys that were completed incorrectly. Thus, the analysis included baseline surveys from 265 participants. Demographic information of the respondents is shown in Table 1. Among the 265 respondents, 94 (35%) reported high FT, and 171 reported low/no FT (65%). The median COST score was 16 (12-19) in the high FT group and 32 (27-37) in the low/no FT group. Compared with respondents with low/no FT, those with high FT were younger, more likely to be women, more likely to self-identify as a minoritized race/ethnicity, less likely to have English as a primary language, and less likely to be married or partnered (all P ≤ .02). High FT was also associated with a lower education level, type of insurance, and lower household income (Table 1).

Although cancer stage was not significantly different between the low and high FT groups, FT was associated with cancer type (P ≤ .001; Table 2). Although genitourinary cancer was the most common cancer for both high and low/no FT groups, gynecologic, breast, and gastrointestinal cancers were more common in the high FT group. Receipt of chemotherapy at any time (either prior to or after the baseline survey) was associated with high FT, with 54% of patients in the high FT group receiving chemotherapy versus 34% in the low/no FT group (P = .001). There were no significant differences between the high versus low/no FT groups in having received hormones or surgery at any time or having received radiation prior to the survey. For the radiation course immediately after the survey, the high and low/no FT groups did not differ in duration of radiation therapy, type of radiation, or radiation intent (palliative vs definitive; all P ≥ .05). Patients with high FT had a higher number of radiation fractions (P = .06) and a shorter distance traveled for treatment (P = .05), though the differences were not statistically significant (Table 2).

Patients with high FT were significantly more likely to have taken unpaid time off, stopped working/retired early compared to those with low/no FT (P < .001, Table 3). The cost of transportation or parking was more likely to influence the medical decisions of those who reported high FT (24%) compared to low/no FT (6%, P < .001). Additionally, patients with high FT were significantly more likely to cite medical bills and reduced work capacity as contributing to their FT (both P < .001). Patients with high FT were significantly more likely to employ multiple cost-coping strategies than patients with low/no FT (Table 3).

After adjusting for age, income, insurance, and employment, individuals with high FT were 13 (95% CI, 3-46) times more likely to delay or avoid care, which included medical visits, filling prescriptions, buying over-the-counter medications or medical supplies, chemotherapy, surgery, and radiation therapy. High FT was also associated with a significantly increased risk of borrowing money, using savings, and reducing spending on basic goods and on leisure activities. Additionally, high FT was associated with a greater likelihood of interest in discussing care costs with doctors (Fig. 1).

Given local referral patterns, patients with genitourinary cancer comprised 43% of all respondents, with nearly all (94%) having prostate cancer. Of the patients with prostate cancer, the majority (57%) received CK stereotactic body radiation therapy. Other forms of radiation therapy included external beam radiation therapy (EBRT) and a combination of CK and EBRT (EBRT ± CK). The median number of fractions for those receiving CK was 5 (5-5), while for EBRT ± CK, it was 28 (20-38). Most (76%) of patients with prostate cancer reported low/no FT. We did not observe a significant association between FT and type of radiation therapy. In the high FT group, 50% received EBRT ± CK and 50% received CK alone. In the low/no FT group, 38% received EBRT ± CK and 59% received CK alone (P = .31).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study represents the largest evaluation of baseline FT among patients initiating radiation therapy across a broad range of cancer types. Our results provide further evidence that patients who are historically socioeconomically disadvantaged experienced higher baseline FT. Additionally, we found that those who had higher baseline FT were more likely to engage in behaviors that can negatively impact outcomes, including delaying or avoiding medical care and delaying or avoiding medical visits. Those with higher baseline FT also engaged in behaviors that can negatively impact overall quality of life, including borrowing money, reducing spending on basic goods, using savings, and reducing spending on leisure.

Prior radiation therapy–focused studies, including 2 prospective studies,26,27 have been limited by single-item measures of FT, have only studied a single cancer type,28 or have administered the COST tool at variable time points relative to radiation therapy.21 For example, a phase 1 study using the COST tool reported that African American patients with breast cancer receiving chemotherapy before radiation therapy experienced significantly higher FT, with over 60% reporting meaningful burden.28 Interestingly, one longitudinal study in Germany (N = 170) found that FT in patients undergoing radiation therapy is driven mainly by baseline socioeconomic factors, such as income, employment stability, and pre-existing financial difficulties, rather than tumor-specific characteristics.29

In contrast, our study provides comprehensive baseline COST data from more than 250 patients within a large tertiary hospital in the United States, representing diverse patient ethnicities and races, as well as tumor types. Reported FT prevalence varies widely across studies, reflecting differences in measurement tools, patient populations, and treatment settings. For example, among patients receiving palliative radiation therapy, Harris et al30 reported moderate to severe FT was present in 19% of individuals, whereas baseline FT was observed in 52% of screened patients in a Bronx cohort, using the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire Core-30.26 Other validated measures, such as the Financial Index of Toxicity,31 further highlight variability across settings.

High FT in our cohort was associated with younger age, lower income, unemployment, female sex assigned at birth, non-White race/ethnicity, primary language other than English, and unmarried status. These findings align with national reports documenting disproportionate financial hardship among minoritized patients with cancer.12,13,32, 33, 34 Insurance type was also associated with FT: patients with high FT were more likely to have private insurance or Medicaid, whereas patients with low/no FT were more likely to have Medicare, consistent with prior data showing reduced cost-related barriers to care after the age of 65 years, where Medicare eligibility reduces financial obstacles to care among older cancer survivors.13,35

Clinical factors were also associated with FT. Chemotherapy given at any time was significantly associated with high FT. Cancer type varied by FT: those with low/no FT were more likely to have prostate cancer at baseline, whereas those with high FT were more likely to have gynecologic, thoracic, breast, or gastrointestinal cancers. Evidence on FT differences across cancer types is limited, but prior meta-analyses indicate higher out-of-pocket costs for breast cancer compared with colorectal, lung, and prostate cancers,2 and among gynecologic cancers, ovarian cancer was associated with lower FT than other tumor types.36 A single institution study from Germany showed that patients with pelvic tumors had higher baseline FT than those with prostate or breast cancer, and that FT was not associated with stage at baseline.29

High FT was associated with cost-coping behaviors that can compromise radiation therapy adherence and patient outcomes. Hypofractionated radiation therapy, including stereotactic body radiation therapy, may help reduce financial burden by decreasing treatment visits, though prior findings are mixed in studies of patients with prostate cancer.37, 38, 39 Among patients with prostate cancer, the planned radiation therapy type (CK vs CK ± EBRT) did not differ by FT prior to treatment initiation. Longitudinal analyses will help determine whether treatment modality or visit frequency influences FT over time.

This study has several limitations. First, the survey was administered only in English, which may have reduced participation among patients with non-English language preference who also might have been at elevated risk for FT. Among patients whose primary language was not English, assistance with completing the survey from a family member or caregiver may have affected responses; however, this was a small group of respondents because more than 90% of the cohort had English as their primary language. Second, our decision to define high FT by the top tertile means that the other group includes respondents with a wide range of FT as well as those with no FT at all; it also may limit the ability to compare our results with other studies. Third, the generalizability of the results may be limited by the fact that the study was conducted at a single tertiary academic center with specific referral patterns and that respondents with prostate cancer, who were more likely to have low/no FT, constituted a large proportion of the study population. Finally, because this is a baseline survey prior to receiving radiation, the FT due to radiation and other treatments given after the baseline survey cannot be assessed until the 1-month or 1-year postradiation survey time points. Completion of planned accrual and longitudinal follow-up will strengthen future analyses.

Conclusions

Specific social determinants of health, including younger age, lower income, unemployment, female sex, non-White race/ethnicity, primary language other than English, unmarried status, and private health insurance or Medicaid, along with cancer type and receipt of chemotherapy, were associated with high FT at baseline prior to starting radiation therapy. Additionally, high FT was strongly associated with cost-coping measures, such as delaying or avoiding medical care, borrowing money, or cutting back on essential goods, actions that can significantly compromise both patient outcomes and quality of life. Developing strategies to alleviate the financial burden during radiation therapy is crucial to preserving quality of life, enhancing treatment adherence to life-saving cancer treatments, and improving patient outcomes.
