Learning behavior, student engagement, and academic performance in learning analytics
Authors: Wenwei Huang, Cui Yue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionLearning behavior and student engagement are important educational constructs that are associated with academic performance. Learning analytics provides opportunities to examine these relationships using large educational datasets. However, clear construct separation, appropriate statistical validation, and comparison with alternative predictive models remain necessary.MethodsWe analyzed a publicly available educational analytics dataset containing 14,003 records. After removing 1,534 exact duplicate records, we retained 12,469 unique records. Learning behavior was represented by study hours, online course participation, and assignment completion. Student engagement was represented by attendance, discussion participation, and extracurricular involvement. Academic performance was represented by examination score, while final grade was excluded because its calculation procedure was not documented. We used ordinary least squares regression to examine the statistical associations. We also included an observed variable path model and machine learning models as alternative specifications. We used an 80% training and 20% held-out test split for predictive validation.ResultsLearning behavior showed a small and nonsignificant association with student engagement (β₁ = 0.0017, p = 0.8112). Student engagement showed a negative association with academic performance (γ₁ = −1.2485, p = 0.0014), whereas learning behavior showed a positive association (γ₂ = 1.0075, p = 0.0009). The baseline model showed limited explanatory power (R2 = 0.004). On the held-out test set, the random forest model provided the strongest predictive performance (R2 = 0.564, RMSE = 11.719, MAE = 8.896, and MAPE = 14.013%). The robustness analysis showed similar coefficient magnitudes across the alternative static specifications.DiscussionThe findings indicate statistical associations among learning behavior, student engagement, and academic performance within the analytical dataset. They do not establish causal relationships or temporal relationships because the dataset is cross-sectional and contains no documented temporal ordering or repeated observations. The results support transparent construct definition, comparative model evaluation, and out-of-sample validation in learning analytics research.

Introduction

Academic performance is an important indicator of educational achievement, but its interpretation requires attention to the behavioral and contextual factors that accompany learning (Costa et al., 2024; Aarthi et al., 2026). Student engagement is one of the most established constructs in this area. It is commonly represented through behavioral, emotional, and cognitive dimensions, and its measurement has been associated with academic achievement, persistence, and other educational outcomes (Fredricks et al., 2004; Tu et al., 2026). Learning behavior provides a related perspective because study effort, participation, assignment completion, and learning practices provide observable indicators of how students interact with educational activities (Zhu et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026).

The development of learning analytics has created new opportunities to study these relationships (Shi et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2026). Learning analytics uses data about learners and their educational contexts to examine learning processes and educational outcomes (Siemens, 2013; Luo et al., 2023). Educational data mining provides related computational methods for identifying patterns and making predictions from educational data, while closer communication between learning analytics and educational data mining has been proposed as a means of improving methodological development in the field (Siemens and Baker, 2012; Zhou et al., 2021). Recent studies have therefore examined attendance, online activity, participation, assessment records, and other behavioral indicators as predictors of student outcomes.

A methodological issue arises when several observed indicators are combined to represent broader educational constructs. Composite measures require a clear theoretical definition of the construct, appropriate indicator assignment, and careful interpretation of the resulting scores. Educational measurement research emphasizes that the validity of an inference depends on the evidence and theoretical rationale that support the interpretation of the score (Messick, 1989). This consideration is particularly relevant when learning behavior, engagement, and academic performance are represented by indices derived from different observed variables.

A second issue concerns the distinction between cross sectional association and longitudinal development. Educational development may be studied through repeated observations when the research question concerns change over time, individual trajectories, or accumulated experience. Longitudinal methods such as latent growth curve models are specifically designed for repeated measurements and the analysis of change over time (Duncan et al., 2013). In contrast, the dataset used in the present study contains cross-sectional records without documented temporal identifiers or repeated observations. The present analysis therefore does not estimate individual developmental trajectories or temporal changes.

Longitudinal educational research can also examine temporal relationships when repeated observations and documented time ordering are available (Zhou et al., 2021; Li et al., 2025). Such data may support more advanced temporal models, including fractional order approaches. However, the present dataset is cross-sectional and contains no documented temporal identifiers or repeated observations. Therefore, fractional order modeling is outside the scope of the present empirical analysis. Such models may be relevant to educational research when longitudinal observations provide a genuine temporal sequence and when accumulated historical observations form part of the research question. However, the absence of a temporal variable in the present dataset does not permit direct empirical estimation of individual learning memory or temporal dependence.

The methodological gap this study addresses is therefore narrower than a claim of empirically established fractional memory. Existing educational research provides substantial evidence on student engagement, learning behavior, and academic performance, while learning analytics provides established methods for extracting predictive information from educational records. Less attention has been given to a unified statistical representation of these constructs within a transparent cross-sectional framework that separates construct definition, statistical association, model comparison, and predictive validation. This study addresses this gap by constructing nonoverlapping indices for learning behavior, student engagement, and academic performance and evaluating their statistical relationships using conventional regression and predictive benchmarks.

The study has three objectives. First, it examines the statistical association between learning behavior and student engagement. Second, it evaluates the association between student engagement, learning behavior, and academic performance after adjustment for selected individual and contextual variables. Third, it compares the baseline statistical specification with an observed variable path model and machine learning alternatives and examines the stability of the results under alternative static specifications.

The study’s contribution is therefore empirical and methodological. It provides a transparent cross-sectional analysis of educational indicators within a publicly available dataset, applies explicit construct definitions and validation procedures, and evaluates the resulting relationships against appropriate alternative models. Fractional calculus offers a potential mathematical approach for future longitudinal educational research with repeated observations and explicit temporal ordering. Because the present dataset is cross-sectional and contains no documented temporal identifiers or repeated observations, this study does not estimate fractional differential equations.

Literature Review

The relationship between student engagement and academic performance has been extensively examined within educational psychology and learning analytics research. Student engagement is commonly defined as the degree of behavioral, cognitive, and emotional involvement that students exhibit during the learning process (Papageorgiou et al., 2025; Uzun et al., 2025). Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that highly engaged students achieve superior academic outcomes, exhibit greater persistence, and report stronger learning satisfaction than their less engaged counterparts (Johar et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025; Bergdahl et al., 2024).

Recent developments in learning analytics have enabled researchers to examine engagement through large-scale educational data (López-Meneses et al., 2025; Kaliisa et al., 2025). Learning analytics approaches utilize behavioral indicators such as attendance, assignment completion, online participation, and learning activity records to predict educational outcomes. Johar et al. (2023) reported that learning analytics significantly improves the understanding of student engagement patterns and provides valuable insights for enhancing academic performance. Similarly, Xu et al. (2025) demonstrated that behavioral learning indicators reliably predict student achievement across various educational settings.

The role of learning behavior has also received increasing attention in contemporary educational research. Learning behavior reflects the habits, practices, and strategies students adopt throughout the educational process. Studies have shown that effective learning behaviors, including consistent study effort, active participation, and self-regulated learning practices, contribute positively to academic achievement. Furthermore, motivation educational technology usage have been identified as factors that are associated with both engagement and performance (Al-Zahrani and Alasmari, 2023).

Although substantial progress has been achieved in understanding student engagement and academic performance, most existing studies rely on conventional statistical and machine learning techniques, including regression analysis, structural equation modeling, random forests, and neural networks. These approaches generally assume that educational outcomes depend primarily on current conditions and often do not explicitly represent the association between past learning experiences and current outcomes.

Knowledge acquisition occurs gradually, and current academic outcomes may correspond with learning experiences accumulated over extended periods. Although educational processes may involve changes over time, the present study is restricted to cross sectional data. The available records do not contain repeated observations or documented temporal identifiers. Therefore, the empirical analysis focuses on statistical associations and predictive relationships among learning behavior, student engagement, and academic performance.

Research Gap and Study Contributions

Three gaps are identified in the existing literature. First, student engagement and learning behavior are often examined through separate indicators or predictive models, making it difficult to compare relationships among constructs. Second, composite educational measures require explicit construct definitions and appropriate separation of indicators, but applied learning analytics studies do not always address this issue. Third, longitudinal and memory-based models require repeated observations and temporal ordering, whereas many available educational datasets are cross-sectional.

This study addresses these gaps through a cross-sectional analytical framework. Learning behavior, student engagement, and academic performance are represented by explicitly defined indices with nonoverlapping indicators. We estimate their statistical relationships using ordinary regression and compare them with structural and machine-learning alternatives. Robustness analysis tests whether the principal associations remain stable under alternative static specifications.

The contribution is therefore an empirical and methodological analysis of cross-sectional educational indicators. Future research may use longitudinal datasets with repeated observations to examine temporal relationships that cannot be evaluated with the present data. Future longitudinal studies with repeated observations and documented temporal ordering may provide an appropriate basis for examining temporal and fractional order models.

Methodology

Research design

This study adopts a quantitative cross-sectional research design to examine associations among learning behavior, student engagement, and academic performance. Because the dataset does not contain repeated measurements or a documented temporal variable, the analysis does not estimate temporal changes or dynamic relationships. The Learning Behavior Index, Student Engagement Index, and Academic Performance Index are therefore treated as cross sectional measures, and their statistical associations are examined within the available dataset. The analysis therefore treats the Learning Behavior Index, Student Engagement Index, and Academic Performance Index as cross-sectional measures and examines their statistical associations.

As illustrated in Figure 1, the proposed conceptual framework represents learning behavior, student engagement, and academic performance as related constructs. Learning behavior is represented through study hours, online course participation, and assignment completion. Student engagement is represented through attendance, discussion participation, and extracurricular involvement. Academic performance is represented by examination performance. The framework organizes the statistical associations among these constructs. Because the available dataset contains cross-sectional records without repeated measurements or documented temporal identifiers, the analysis does not establish temporal ordering or causal direction. Therefore, the estimated relationships are interpreted as statistical associations and predictive relationships within the analytical dataset.

Data source and dataset description

The analysis uses the publicly available Student Performance and Learning Style dataset distributed through Kaggle by A. Shamim. The dataset was accessed on 23 August 2026 through the Kaggle repository: https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/adilshamim8/student-performance-and-learning-style. The supplied CSV file contains 14,003 records and 16 variables. The dataset is a secondary, publicly available educational analytics dataset. The authors did not collect it, and they did not conduct primary data collection for this study. The publicly available source does not provide sufficient information about the original participant recruitment, institutional setting, sampling procedure, data collection protocol, or consent procedure. Therefore, the original provenance of the records as observations collected from actual students cannot be independently verified. The records are consequently treated as dataset observations rather than as a verified sample of individual students.

The unit of observation in the present analysis is one row in the supplied CSV file. One record is not assumed to correspond uniquely to one independently recruited or verified student because the original data collection procedure and participant identifiers are not documented in the public source. The dataset is therefore used as a secondary analytical resource for examining relationships among recorded learning behavior, engagement, and academic performance variables.

The raw dataset contains 14,003 records and 16 variables. No missing values are present. We identified 1,534 exact duplicate records by comparing all 16 variables and removed them. No observations were excluded because of missing values, invalid values, or statistical outlier status. After removing duplicates, 12,469 unique records remained for statistical analysis.

The inclusion criterion was a complete record containing all 16 variables within the observed admissible ranges. Exclusion criteria included exact duplicate records, incomplete records, and records containing invalid or impossible values. No observations were excluded according to age, gender, learning style, motivation, stress level, assignment completion, examination score, or final grade.

Variable measurement

This study examines the statistical relationships among learning behavior, student engagement, and academic performance. To operationalize these constructs, variables are classified into three categories: learning behavior variables, student engagement variables, and academic performance variables. Table 1 presents the measurement structure.

Learning behavior variables

Learning behavior represents the recorded study activities and learning practices examined in this study. It is operationalized using three indicators: study hours, online course participation, and assignment completion. These three indicators form the Learning Behavior Index described in Section 2.4.1. Learning Style is retained in the dataset as a categorical variable for descriptive purposes but is excluded from the LBI because its numerical coding from 0 to 3 does not provide an interpretable quantitative order. Higher values are interpreted according to each variable’s measurement scale and represent differences in the recorded learning behavior indicators within the dataset. Learning behavior is treated as a primary explanatory construct within the proposed statistical framework because its recorded indicators are associated with student engagement and academic performance in the analytical dataset.

Student engagement variables

Student engagement reflects the degree of active participation and commitment to educational activities. In this study, engagement is measured through attendance, participation in classroom discussions, and extracurricular involvement. Assignment completion is classified exclusively as a learning behavior indicator to avoid overlap between the learning behavior and engagement constructs. These variables are treated as numerical indicators of engagement within the supplied dataset. Because the original data collection procedure is not documented sufficiently to verify individual participant records, they are interpreted as dataset indicators rather than independently verified measures of individual student behavior.

Student engagement is modeled as an intermediate construct in the statistical specification because its recorded indicators are examined jointly with learning behavior and academic performance. The model does not establish that engagement mediates a causal pathway between these variables.

Academic performance variables

Academic performance is represented by the examination score. Final grade is not included in the primary academic performance measure because the public dataset documentation does not specify how the final grade is calculated or whether the examination score contributes mechanically to its determination. Excluding final grade prevents potential outcome leakage and avoids redundancy between the outcome components.

Control variables

Several control variables are incorporated to account for individual and contextual differences among students. These variables include age, gender, motivation level, stress level, educational technology usage, internet access, and learning resources. Including these factors provides adjustment for observed individual and contextual characteristics that may be associated with the main study constructs. All variables are standardized where necessary to facilitate comparability across different measurement scales and to improve estimation efficiency in subsequent modeling procedures.

Before index construction, continuous indicators are standardized to ensure comparability across measurement scales. This procedure ensures comparability across variables and improves estimation efficiency in subsequent analyses. The weighting procedure is kept identical across all observations and model specifications to ensure comparability of the resulting indices. The weights are treated as measurement weights rather than estimated regression parameters and are therefore not interpreted as causal effects or empirical coefficients. The resulting indices serve as summary measures of the observed indicators in the cross sectional analysis. For each indicator Xj , the standardized value is calculated as Equation 1: Zij=Xij−μjσj (1)

where Xij denotes the observed value of indicator j for observation i , μj denotes the sample mean of indicator j , and σj denotes its sample standard deviation. Equal weighting is then applied to the indicators within each construct. Equal weighting is selected because the dataset documentation provides no theoretical or empirical basis for assigning different weights to the indicators. This procedure places the indicators on a common scale before aggregation. Equal weighting gives each prespecified indicator the same contribution to the corresponding construct and avoids assigning greater importance to any indicator without empirical or theoretical justification. The Learning Behavior Index was calculated as Equation 2: LBIi=ZStudyHours+ZOnlineCourses+ZAssignmentCompletion3 (2)

where LBIi denotes the Learning Behavior Index for observation i , and each Z term denotes the standardized value of the corresponding indicator. Equal weighting is applied to the three indicators, with a weight of 1/3 assigned to each indicator.

The Student Engagement Index (SEI) is constructed using attendance, discussion participation, and extracurricular involvement. Each observed indicator is assigned to one construct only as Equation 3: SEIi=ZAttendance+ZDiscussion+ZExtracurricular3 (3) where SEIi denotes the Student Engagement Index for observation i . Each of the three indicators receives an equal weight of 1/3. The Academic Performance Index (API) is derived from examination scores as Equation 4: APIi=Z(ExamScorei) (4) where Z(ExamScorei) denotes the standardized examination score for observation i. Final grade is excluded from the primary Academic Performance Index because its calculation procedure is not documented in the public dataset source. It cannot be established whether the examination score contributes directly to the final grade. Its exclusion prevents possible mechanical overlap between the outcome components and provides a more conservative definition of academic performance.

Measurement basis of the composite indices

The Learning Behavior Index and Student Engagement Index are treated as formative composite measures rather than reflective latent constructs. The indicators within each index represent distinct dimensions of the corresponding construct and are not assumed to be interchangeable manifestations of a single underlying factor. Therefore, conventional internal consistency statistics, such as Cronbach’s alpha, are not used as the primary measurement criterion for these indices. The measurement basis of the composite indices is instead established through the prespecified conceptual assignment of indicators, nonoverlapping classification of indicators across constructs, z score standardization, and equal weighting described in Section 2.4.1. The resulting scores are interpreted as composite summaries of the recorded indicators rather than as estimates of unobserved latent traits.

As summarized in Table 2, the resulting composite indices provide quantitative measures of the principal constructs used in the cross-sectional statistical analysis. These indices are subsequently incorporated into the statistical models to examine associations among the principal constructs.

Statistical models and comparison framework

The empirical analysis uses a cross-sectional modeling framework because the available dataset contains one record per observation and does not provide documented time identifiers, repeated measurements, or verified individual-level longitudinal trajectories. Consequently, temporal ordering is not imposed on the observations, and the coefficients are interpreted as statistical associations within the analytical dataset rather than as temporal or causal effects.

The baseline empirical specification is estimated using ordinary least squares. Student engagement is specified as a function of learning behavior and the selected control variables as Equation 5: SEIi=β0+β1LBIi+β2TCi+εi (5) where SEIi denotes the Student Engagement Index, LBIi denotes the Learning Behavior Index, Ci denotes the vector of control variables, and εi denotes the residual term.

Academic performance is specified as Equation 6: APIi=γ0+γ1SEIi+γ2LBIi+γ3TCi+εi (6) where APIi denotes the Academic Performance Index, SEIi denotes student engagement, LBIi denotes learning behavior, and Ci denotes the control variables.

The coefficients are estimated by ordinary least squares. The coefficient vector is obtained by minimizing the residual sum of squares. Standard errors, t-statistics, two-sided p-values, and 95% confidence intervals are calculated from the estimated covariance matrix. All principal coefficient estimates are therefore reported with their standard errors, two-sided p-values, and 95% confidence intervals. The same uncertainty measures are reported for the coefficients in the robustness analysis. Uncertainty for the out of sample prediction metrics is assessed by nonparametric bootstrap resampling of the held-out test observations. For each bootstrap sample, R2, RMSE, MAE, and MAPE are recalculated, and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resulting empirical distributions are used to obtain 95% confidence intervals.

Comparison with an observed variable path model

An observed variable path model is included as an alternative structural representation of the relationships among learning behavior, student engagement, and academic performance. The model uses the constructed Learning Behavior Index, Student Engagement Index, and Academic Performance Index as observed composite variables. Learning behavior is specified as a predictor of student engagement, while learning behavior and student engagement are specified as predictors of academic performance. No latent variable measurement model is estimated, and no indicator loadings are estimated. The path model therefore represents a structural reformulation of the observed composite variables used in the regression analysis, rather than an independent latent-variable SEM. The comparison is based on the estimated structural relationships and predictive performance on the held-out test data.

Comparison with machine learning models

Standard machine learning models are used as predictive benchmarks. The benchmark models include random forest regression, gradient boosting regression, and support vector regression. The same outcome variable and predictor information are used across the predictive models.

The analytical dataset is divided into training and test subsets using an 80% to 20% split and a fixed random seed of 42. The training subset contains 9,975 observations, and the held-out test subset contains 2,494 observations. All model parameters are estimated using the training data only. Predictor standardization is based on the training subset and is subsequently applied to the test subset. No information from the test subset is used during model estimation.

The final predictive performance is evaluated on the held-out test subset using R2, RMSE, MAE, and MAPE. This procedure assesses predictive performance on observations not used for parameter estimation. The test set therefore provides a more appropriate assessment of out-of-sample predictive performance than full-sample goodness of fit.

The machine learning models are treated as predictive benchmarks rather than explanatory or causal models. Their purpose is to determine whether the statistical specification provides predictive performance that is competitive with flexible nonlinear algorithms.

Mixed effects models

A mixed effects model is not estimated in the present analysis. Such a model requires repeated observations or identifiable clustered observational units. The available dataset does not provide documented repeated measurements or verified student identifiers. Therefore, a random intercept or random slope structure cannot be specified without imposing unsupported assumptions about the data structure. Mixed-effects modeling is an appropriate alternative for future longitudinal datasets that contain repeated observations for the same students.

Software and implementation

All analyses were conducted in Python 3.14.7. The data processing and numerical calculations were performed using pandas 3.0.5, NumPy 2.5.2, and SciPy 1.18.1. Ordinary least squares regression was implemented using statsmodels 0.14.6. The observed variable path model was implemented using semopy 2.3.11. Random forest regression, gradient boosting regression, support vector regression, data standardization, and train-test splitting were implemented using scikit-learn 1.9.0. The analytical dataset was divided into training and held-out test subsets using an 80% to 20% split with a fixed random seed of 42. The training subset contained 9,975 observations, and the test subset contained 2,494 observations. Predictor standardization was estimated from the training subset and then applied to the test subset. We used no information from the test subset during model estimation.

Ordinary least squares regression was estimated using the standard ordinary least squares implementation in statsmodels. The observed variable path model was estimated with the constructed LBI, SEI, and API entered as observed composite variables. No latent measurement model was estimated. Random forest regression, gradient boosting regression, and support vector regression were implemented with scikit-learn. The final predictive performance of all models was evaluated on the held-out test subset using R2, RMSE, MAE, and MAPE.

Uncertainty for the held-out test performance measures was assessed using nonparametric bootstrap resampling. For each bootstrap sample, R2, RMSE, MAE, and MAPE were recalculated, and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the empirical bootstrap distributions were used to obtain 95% confidence intervals. Statistical estimation procedure

The statistical relationships among the Learning Behavior Index, Student Engagement Index, and Academic Performance Index are estimated using cross-sectional regression analysis. Because the dataset contains no temporal variable or repeated observations, the estimation is performed independently across records, and no temporal ordering is imposed.

Robustness analysis

Robustness analysis examines whether the estimated associations remain stable under alternative static specifications. The baseline model includes LBI, SEI, and the selected control variables. Model A excludes all control variables while retaining LBI and SEI. Model B includes SEI as the principal predictor of API and excludes LBI from the API equation. Model C includes LBI as the principal predictor of API and excludes SEI from the API equation. These specifications test sensitivity to covariate adjustment and to the simultaneous inclusion of the two principal constructs.

The baseline specification includes the Learning Behavior Index (LBI), the Student Engagement Index (SEI), and the selected control variables. The control variables include age, gender, motivation, stress level, educational technology usage, internet access, and learning resources. These variables are included because they represent individual and contextual characteristics that may be associated with the principal study constructs.

Three alternative specifications are considered. Model A excludes the control variables while retaining LBI and SEI. This specification evaluates whether the principal associations remain stable when adjustment for individual and contextual characteristics is removed. Model B includes SEI as the explanatory variable for academic performance, while excluding LBI from the academic performance specification. This model evaluates whether the association between student engagement and academic performance remains stable without simultaneously including learning behavior. Model C includes LBI as the explanatory variable for academic performance while SEI is excluded from the academic performance specification. This model evaluates whether the association between learning behavior and academic performance remains stable when student engagement is not included.

The specifications provide direct sensitivity tests of the main empirical relationships. Model A evaluates sensitivity to the inclusion of control variables, Model B evaluates the stability of the SEI and academic performance relationship, and Model C evaluates the stability of the LBI and academic performance relationship.

For each specification, we compare the estimated coefficients with the corresponding baseline estimates. We report coefficient estimates, standard errors, 95% confidence intervals, and two-sided p-values to assess the magnitude and statistical uncertainty of the estimated associations. Model fit is compared using R2, adjusted R2, RMSE, MAE, and MAPE. The relative change in each principal coefficient is calculated as Equation 15: RCi=∣βi∗−βiβi∣ (15)

where RCi represents the relative change, βi represents the baseline coefficient, and βi∗ represents the coefficient obtained from an alternative specification. Consistent coefficient direction, similar coefficient magnitude, overlapping confidence intervals, and comparable model performance across the specifications are considered evidence of specification stability. Because the analytical dataset is cross sectional and does not contain documented temporal identifiers or repeated observations, the robustness analysis is interpreted as evidence of associative stability rather than causal or longitudinal robustness.

Discussion of findings

The present analysis shows a statistically significant negative association between student engagement and academic performance. After adjustment for learning behavior and the selected control variables, higher SEI values correspond with lower API values (γ₁ = −1.2485, p = 0.0014). This direction differs from much of the educational literature, which generally reports a positive association between engagement and academic achievement. The difference should therefore be interpreted cautiously. One possible explanation is that the SEI combines attendance, discussion participation, and extracurricular involvement, which may not represent examination-focused study effort in the same way. Higher participation may also occur among students who require additional academic support or who face greater academic difficulty. In addition, attendance and discussion participation do not necessarily indicate the amount or quality of preparation for examinations, while extracurricular involvement may represent activities with limited direct correspondence to examination performance. These explanations are only possible interpretations and cannot be tested with the present cross sectional data. The unverified provenance of the dataset also limits confidence in assigning a substantive mechanism to the observed negative association.

Previous studies have reported associations between behavioral indicators, student engagement, and academic performance. Johar et al. (2023) reported that learning analytics provides information about student engagement patterns and their relationship with learning performance. Similarly, Al-Zahrani and Alasmari (2023) reported associations between data-driven educational interventions, instructional personalization, student engagement, and learning outcomes. These findings provide context for the present analysis but do not establish that the associations observed in the present dataset have the same direction or mechanism (Chung et al., 2020; Peng et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2024).

The present analysis does not provide empirical evidence of temporal memory or long term dynamic effects because the dataset contains no documented temporal ordering or repeated observations. The main contribution is therefore a transparent examination of statistical associations among learning behavior, student engagement, and academic performance, along with comparisons with structural and machine learning alternatives. The robustness results indicate that the principal associations remain relatively stable across the alternative static specifications, although overall predictive performance of the linear models remains limited.

From a practical perspective, the results provide descriptive and predictive information about educational indicators. However, the limited predictive performance of the baseline linear model and the cross-sectional nature of the data do not support recommendations based on causal or temporal effects. Future educational applications should combine these findings with longitudinal evidence and validated intervention studies before drawing conclusions about changes in student behavior or engagement.

Conclusion

The association between student engagement and academic performance is negative and statistically significant in the present dataset. After adjustment for learning behavior and the selected control variables, higher SEI values are associated with lower API values (γ1 = −1.2485, p = 0.0014). This direction differs from much of the previous educational literature, which generally reports a positive association between student engagement and academic achievement. The difference should therefore be interpreted cautiously. The present cross sectional analysis does not establish why higher SEI values correspond with lower API values in this dataset, and the observed association should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal relationship.

The baseline linear model shows limited explanatory performance, with R2 = 0.0035 and adjusted R2 = 0.0028. The corresponding RMSE is 17.665, MAE is 15.306, and MAPE is 24.17%. On the held out test data, the baseline model produces R2 = 0.006, RMSE = 17.696, MAE = 15.342, and MAPE = 24.108%. These results indicate limited out-of-sample predictive accuracy for the linear specification. Among the alternative predictive models, the random forest model provides stronger predictive performance than the baseline linear model.

The robustness analysis shows that the principal associations remain relatively stable across the alternative static specifications. Given the unverified provenance of the source dataset and the very limited explanatory power of the baseline linear model, the practical generalizability of these findings to educational policy is limited. The LBI-SEI association remains small and nonsignificant, whereas the SEI-API association remains negative and the LBI-API association remains positive in the specifications in which they are estimated. Model fit statistics show only limited variation across the robustness models.

The findings have limited practical interpretation because the data are cross-sectional and the analysis does not establish temporal ordering or causal mechanisms. The results therefore support identifying statistical associations and predictive patterns rather than claims that changes in learning behavior cause changes in engagement or that engagement causes changes in academic performance.

Several limitations should be considered. The analysis uses a publicly available Kaggle dataset whose original participant recruitment, institutional setting, sampling procedure, and collection protocol cannot be independently verified. The dataset therefore should not be presented as a verified sample of actual students. The raw dataset contained 1,534 exact duplicate records, which were removed before analysis. Several categorical variables are represented by numerical codes without complete publicly available coding documentation. In addition, the dataset contains no documented temporal variable or repeated longitudinal measurements. Consequently, these data cannot empirically evaluate temporal development or individual trajectories. The practical generalizability of the findings is limited. The dataset is publicly available through Kaggle, but its original participant recruitment, institutional setting, sampling procedure, and data collection protocol cannot be independently verified. In addition, the baseline linear model explains only a very small proportion of the variation in academic performance (R2 = 0.004). The findings should therefore be interpreted as dataset-specific statistical associations and predictive patterns rather than as evidence that can be directly generalized to educational policy or broader student populations.

Future research should use longitudinal educational datasets with documented participant provenance, repeated measurements, and explicit temporal identifiers. Such data would permit direct evaluation of temporal relationships and provide an appropriate basis for longitudinal modeling of changes in learning behavior, engagement, and academic performance.
