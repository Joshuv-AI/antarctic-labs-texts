Disintegrating the curve complex
Mladen Bestvina, Kenneth Bromberg, Alexander J. Rasmussen

Abstract
We study a finite sequence of graphs, beginning with the curve graph and ending with a graph quasi-isometric to a tree. There is a Lipschitz map from one graph in the sequence to the next. This sequence was first introduced by Hamenstädt. We prove (as conjectured by Hamenstädt) that the graphs in this sequence are hyperbolic and that the coarse fibers of the maps in the sequence are quasi-trees. This gives an upper bound on the asymptotic dimension of each graph in the sequence and as a result, an upper bound on the asymptotic dimension of the curve graph. Additionally, we show that the action of the mapping class group on each graph in the sequence is acylindrical, and classify the boundary and actions of individual mapping classes for each graph in the sequence.

Introduction

The curve graph
{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)
of a finite type orientable surface
\Sigma

is a crucial tool for studying the coarse geometry of the mapping class group

\operatorname{Mod}(\Sigma)
. The graphs
{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)
were introduced by Harvey in
[
22
]

as an analog of Tits buildings for mapping class groups. From the perspective
of coarse geometry, the most important property of
{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)
is that it is
an infinite diameter
hyperbolic
graph, as proved by Masur-Minsky in
[
32
]
.
Our purpose in this paper is to study
{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)
via
“disintegration.” Our main result is the following. Some of the terms in the Main
Theorem will be explained below.
See the numbered references in parentheses for precise statements of results.

Main Theorem
.

There is a tower of graphs:

{\mathcal{C}}_{m(\Sigma)}(\Sigma)\to{\mathcal{C}}_{m(\Sigma)-1}(\Sigma)\to\cdots\to{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\Sigma)

with the following properties:

•

{\mathcal{C}}_{m(\Sigma)}(\Sigma)
is quasi-isometric to
{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)

(Section
2
).

•

The mapping class group
\operatorname{Mod}(\Sigma)
acts on
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)

by isometries (Section
2
).
Moreover,
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
is hyperbolic and has infinite diameter
(Proposition
5.3
).

•

m(\Sigma)
is a linear function of the complexity of

\Sigma

(Section
2
).

•

The graph
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\Sigma)
at the bottom of the tower is a
quasi-tree (Theorem
2.5
).

•

Each map
{\mathcal{C}}_{k+1}(\Sigma)\to{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
in the tower is 1-Lipschitz,
coarsely surjective, alignment-preserving, and
\operatorname{Mod}(\Sigma)
-equivariant
(Section
2
).

•

The coarse fibers of each
{\mathcal{C}}_{k+1}(\Sigma)\to{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
are
quasi-trees and are quasi-convex in
{\mathcal{C}}_{k+1}(\Sigma)
(Theorem
2.5
).

•

The action of
\operatorname{Mod}(\Sigma)
on each
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
is
acylindrical (and in particular there is a positive
lower bound on the asymptotic translation lengths of loxodromic
elements of
\operatorname{Mod}(\Sigma)
on
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
) (Theorem
11.1
).

•

The Gromov boundary of each
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
is a certain
explicit subset of the space of ending laminations on

\Sigma
(which is the Gromov boundary of
{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)
,

[
26
]
) (Theorem
10.2
).

•

There is an explicit classification of which elements of

\operatorname{Mod}(\Sigma)
act loxodromically on
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
. All other
elements act elliptically (Theorem
11.2
).

See Section
2
for the definition of the graphs
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}

and the definition of coarse fibers.

An important part of the Main Theorem (specifically,
Theorem
2.5
) was first announced by Hamenstädt in a
very inspiring lecture
[
18
]
at MSRI in Fall
2016.
However, a proof
has not yet been circulated.

The graph
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\Sigma)
has been studied before, by Maher-Masai-Schleimer

[
28
]
and by Hamenstädt
[
19
,
20
]

(who called it the
principal
curve graph
). The definitions of
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\Sigma)
differ slightly in the two
papers, but yield quasi-isometric spaces. We will adopt the analog of the
Maher-Masai-Schleimer definition as our working definition, but we
discuss the relationship with Hamenstädt’s definition; see Section

4
. The two papers together prove hyperbolicity and infinite diameter
of
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\Sigma)
, identify the Gromov boundary and loxodromic elements,
show that
{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)\to{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\Sigma)
is
Lipschitz and alignment-preserving, and that the action
is weakly properly discontinuous (
WPD
) (see
[
8
]
for the definition
of WPD; the definition of
alignment-preserving
is given below). Our
contribution to the study of
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\Sigma)
is that it is a quasi-tree,
and that the action is acylindrical
(see
[
10
]
for the definition of acylindricity).
We also prove the corresponding
results for all
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
. The most difficult part of the Main Theorem is the
proof that the coarse fibers of each map are quasi-trees; see Section
8
.
The workhorse in
this proof is an analog of the Masur-Minsky Nesting Lemma
[
32
, Lemma 4.7]
giving a
criterion for when a splitting sequence of train tracks makes progress in

{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
. See Section
7
.

We point out that this tower immediately implies
a bound on asymptotic dimensions:

Corollary 1.1
.

For each
k
,
\operatorname{asdim}{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)\leq k+1
. In particular,

\operatorname{asdim}{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)\leq m(\Sigma)+1
.

This follows from the Bell-Dranishnikov theorem

[
4
]
about asymptotic dimension and induction on
k
.
Thus the tower can be viewed as a strong
witness of the fact that
\operatorname{asdim}{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)<\infty
.

To put this work in context, let us briefly describe some
disintegration results, for other hyperbolic complexes. In
[
16
]
,
Fujiwara-Schleimer constructed a tower interpolating between the arc
and the curve graph of (bordered or punctured)
\Sigma
with coarse
fibers which are quasi-trees of curve graphs of subsurfaces.
A
witness

W
is an essential subsurface that intersects every arc.
There is a notion
of complexity
\xi(W)
, taking on positive integer values, so that
V\subset W

implies
\xi(V)\leq\xi(W)
and equality holds exactly if
V
and
W
are
isotopic. Now let
X_{0}=\mathcal{A}
be the arc graph of the surface

\Sigma
. For
c>0
define
X_{c}
by adding a cone point over
the set of arcs contained
in a witness
W
of complexity
\leq c
, for each such witness
W
.
This yields a tower of 1-Lipschitz maps

\mathcal{A}=X_{0}\to X_{1}\to\cdots\to X_{\xi(\Sigma)-1}.

It turns out
that the last graph
X_{\xi(S)-1}
is quasi-isometric to the curve
graph
{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)
, so the tower yields an interpolation between the arc
and the curve graphs. Moreover, each
X_{c}
is hyperbolic and each

X_{c}\to X_{c+1}
is obtained by coning off a collection
of pairwise disjoint quasi-convex subsets with bounded closest-point
projections to
each other, each of which is quasi-isometric to the curve graph of a witness
of complexity
c+1
. Fujiwara-Schleimer observe that this structure
implies that the arc graph has finite asymptotic dimension, by using

[
4
]
and the fact that curve graphs have finite asymptotic
dimension. In
[
7
]
the first author and Feighn use this structure in
a similar inductive fashion to give an alternative description of the
Gromov boundary of the arc graph, originally due to Schleimer and
Pho-On
[
38
]
.

There is an analogous construction for
\operatorname{Out}(F_{n})
-complexes. In

[
7
]
, the first author and Feighn construct a tower interpolating
between the free splitting and cyclic splitting complexes of the free
group
F_{n}
. All complexes in the tower are hyperbolic and the maps are
Lipschitz and
\operatorname{Out}(F_{n})
-equivariant. The coarse fibers are
quasi-trees of certain relative cyclic splitting
complexes. The existence of interpolating towers between cyclic
splitting and free factor complexes, and between free factor complexes
and quasi-trees is still open.

In both the arc-to-curve-graph and free-to-cyclic-splitting-complex towers,
each map is coning off of a collection of
quasi-convex sets with uniformly bounded projections to each
other. The coarse fibers can then be understood using the projection
complex
[
6
]
constructed from this collection. That is
not
the
case in our Main Theorem. The sets being coned off at each stage,
while quasi-convex, typically have infinite diameter
intersections, and one such set may in fact be properly
contained in another.

We now say a bit more about some terms in
the Main Theorem that may be less familiar to the reader.

Given a train track
\tau\subset\Sigma
, the set of projective
measured laminations it carries is a cell in the space
{\mathcal{PML}}(\Sigma)

of all projective measured laminations, which is a sphere. The
codimension of this cell is the
index

\operatorname{ind}(\tau)
of
\tau
. We
define
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
by starting with
{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)
and adding edges
between curves
\alpha,\beta
provided they are both carried by a
train track of index
>k
. If
m(\Sigma)
is the smallest number such
that train tracks of index
>m(\Sigma)
are necessarily non-filling,
then
{\mathcal{C}}_{m(\Sigma)}(\Sigma)
is quasi-isometric to
{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)
. See Section

2
.

A triple
x,y,z
in a metric space
X

is called
K
-aligned if
d(x,y)+d(y,z)\leq d(x,z)+K
.
We say that a Lipschitz map
f:X\to Y
is
alignment-preserving
if
there exists
K>0
such that whenever
x,y,z
is
0
-aligned,
f(x),f(y),f(z)

is
K
-aligned. If
X
and
Y
are hyperbolic, then an
alignment-preserving map
f
sends
re-parametrized quasi-geodesics
to re-parametrized quasi-geodesics.

The statement that the coarse fibers of
{\mathcal{C}}_{k+1}(\Sigma)\to{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
are
quasi-convex quasi-trees means that for
B\subset{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
bounded,
its preimage in
{\mathcal{C}}_{k+1}(\Sigma)
is contained in a quasi-convex set
which is quasi-isometric to a tree. The quasi-isometry constants
here necessarily deteriorate with the diameter of
B
.

Recall that an
ending lamination
is a filling geodesic
lamination
\Lambda
which is the support of a transverse measure. The space of
ending laminations is identified with the Gromov boundary
\partial{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)

[
26
]
.
The
index

\operatorname{ind}(\Lambda)
of
\Lambda
is the
largest index of a train track that carries
\Lambda
. The Gromov
boundary
\partial{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
of
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
can then be identified with the
space of ending laminations of index
\leq k
with the
coarse Hausdorff topology, which is the quotient of the topology on
{\mathcal{PML}}(\Sigma)

(Theorem
10.2
).

An element
\phi\in\operatorname{Mod}(\Sigma)
is loxodromic in the action on
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
if and only
if it is pseudo-Anosov and its (un)stable lamination has index
\leq k
(Theorem
11.2
).
Thus going down the tower, the set of loxodromic elements decreases, as does
the Gromov boundary.

We note that if
X
is a hyperbolic metric space with finite asymptotic dimension, then
there is an analogous filtration

X=X_{n}\to X_{n-1}\to\cdots\to X_{0}

by hyperbolic graphs, where the coarse fibers are quasi-trees and
n
is the
asymptotic dimension of
X
. This follows from the quasi-median quasi-isometric
embedding of
X
in a product of quasi-trees
proven by Petyt recently in
[
36
]
. Furthermore, Petyt-Spriano-Zalloum
recently proved in
[
37
, Theorem 1.3]
that
{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)
equivariantly
quasi-isometrically embeds in a product of quasi-trees. An immediate corollary
is that there is a tower of graphs for
{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)
for which coarse fibers
are quasi-trees. This recovers certain parts of the main theorem.
We compare
[
37
, Theorem 1.3]
and the main theorem.
On one hand, our main theorem does not prove equivariant quasi-isometric
embedding of
{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)
into a finite product of quasi-trees.
On the other hand
[
37
, Theorem 1.3]
does not give a
bound on the length of a tower of hyperbolic graphs as in our main theorem
and thus cannot be used to bound
\operatorname{asdim}{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)
.
The main advantage of our main theorem is that it produces a tower of
natural hyperbolic graphs with very explicit topological definitions.
These topological definitions yield detailed information on the boundaries
of the associated hyperbolic graphs, the dynamics of their mapping class group
actions, and the structure of their coarse fibers.

Finally, we suggest some ideas for further study. The filtration of
{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)

and the techniques of this paper may be useful for the following purposes. Firstly,
they may be useful for precisely computing the asymptotic dimensions of curve graphs
and mapping class groups. If one could prove that the asymptotic dimension
of
{\mathcal{C}}_{k+1}(\Sigma)
is greater than that of
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
for
k<m(\Sigma)
,
this would show that
the asymptotic dimension of
{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)
is exactly
m(\Sigma)+1
.
One could hope to use this in turn to compute
\operatorname{asdim}\operatorname{Mod}(\Sigma)
exactly.
It is an interesting question whether
\operatorname{asdim}\operatorname{Mod}(\Sigma)
is equal to the
virtual cohomological dimension of
\operatorname{Mod}(\Sigma)
, or indeed whether there exists any
finitely generated group with finite asymptotic dimension and finite virtual cohomological
dimension which are unequal. The current best known bounds on
\operatorname{asdim}\operatorname{Mod}(\Sigma)

are quadratic in the complexity of
\Sigma

[
6
,
3
]
while

\operatorname{vcd}\operatorname{Mod}(\Sigma)
is linear in the complexity
[
21
]
,
so there is currently a mysterious discrepancy between the two bounds.

The filtration of
{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)
also gives a route for
proving geometric properties of curve graphs and subgroups of mapping class
groups by
induction
on the filtration. For instance, one could attempt to use
induction to produce quasi-isometric embeddings of hyperbolic planes
in
{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)
(see
[
27
]
for previous work on this problem)
or to study the structure of certain subgroups of mapping class groups.
As an example, if a subgroup quasi-isometrically embeds in
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\Sigma)

under the orbit map then it must be virtually free. It would be interesting
to know e.g. if Veech groups of quadratic differentials with vertical and
horizontal laminations in
\partial{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
quasi-isometrically embed
in
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
.

The tower

Let
\Sigma
be a connected orientable surface of finite type, without
boundary. We will always assume that
\Sigma
is not sporadic,
i.e. it is not a sphere with
\leq 4
punctures or a torus with
\leq 1
puncture. The goal of this section is to define a tower of graphs
with the curve graph
{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)
at the top and the
principal
curve graph
at the bottom.

We will consider train tracks on
\Sigma
.
We recall a few basic definitions about train tracks, and assume that the reader
is familiar with the basic theory of train tracks. We refer the reader to

[
35
]
for further background.

The train tracks we consider may not be connected,
but unless stated otherwise they will be trivalent (that is,
generic

in the terminology of
[
35
]
).
A train track will always be assumed not to have
(smooth) disks, (smooth) annuli, (smooth) punctured disks,
monogons, or bigons as complementary components.

If
\tau
is a train track, then the edges of
\tau
are called
branches

and the vertices are called
switches
. A
carrying map
from a
geodesic lamination or train track
\sigma
to
\tau
is a map

f:\Sigma\to\Sigma
such that
f(\sigma)\subset\tau
,
f
is homotopic to the
identity, and the derivative of
f
on each tangent line to
\sigma
is injective.
We will denote a carrying map from
\sigma
to
\tau
by
\sigma\to\tau
,
leaving the notation for the map itself implicit. A
train path
is a smooth
immersion of an interval in
\tau
. If
f:\sigma\to\tau
is a carrying map
between train tracks then the image of any branch of
\sigma
is a train path
on
\tau
. Recall that
\tau
is
recurrent
if, for every branch
b
of

\tau
, there is a closed curve carried by
\tau
whose image passes through

b
.
We will generally assume that train tracks are recurrent,
but we will make this hypothesis explicit
wherever necessary.
Additionally, we will sometimes require train tracks to be
transversely recurrent
.
The definition of transverse recurrence is given in
[
35
]
. A track
which is both recurrent and transversely recurrent is called
birecurrent
.

The set
M(\tau)
of measured laminations carried by
\tau
is a polyhedral cone
in Thurston’s measured lamination space
{\mathcal{ML}}(\Sigma)
.
Namely, it is a cone over
P(\tau)
, the projectivization of
M(\tau)
, which has the structure of a compact polyhedron.
We denote by
{\mathcal{S}}(\tau)
the set of simple closed curves carried by
\tau
.
The vertices of
P(\tau)
are represented by simple closed curves lying in

{\mathcal{S}}(\tau)
, which we call
vertex cycles
. We denote the set of vertex
cycles of
\tau
by
V(\tau)\subset{\mathcal{S}}(\tau)
.
According to

[
34
, Proposition 3.11.3]
, a vertex cycle is either an embedded simple
closed curve in
\tau
or a barbell. In particular, a vertex cycle of

\tau
traverses each branch of
\tau
at most twice.
By
[
17
, Corollary 2.3]
, the diameter of
V(\tau)

in
{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)
is bounded in terms of the track
\tau
(and hence the diameter is
also bounded only in terms of
\Sigma
, since there are finitely many train tracks
on
\Sigma
up to the mapping class group action).
Note that if

\sigma
and
\tau
are train tracks and there is a carrying map
\sigma\to\tau
,
then there is an inclusion
{\mathcal{S}}(\sigma)\subset{\mathcal{S}}(\tau)
.

We define the
index
of
\tau
as

\operatorname{ind}(\tau)\vcentcolon=\dim{\mathcal{ML}}(\Sigma)-\dim M(\tau)

i.e. as the codimension of

M(\tau)
in
{\mathcal{ML}}
. If
\tau
is
recurrent and
\sigma\subset\tau
is a proper subtrack, then

M(\sigma)
is a proper face of
M(\tau)
,
and in particular
\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)>\operatorname{ind}(\tau)
.

We next give a combinatorial description of
\operatorname{ind}(\tau)
.
Recall that a train track
\tau
is
orientable

if every branch can be oriented so that every carried
curve has an orientation that agrees with the orientation of every branch it traverses.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
5
, Lemma 3.1]
,
[
35
, Lemma 2.1.1]
)
.

Suppose that
\tau
is recurrent with
v
switches and
b
branches.
If
\tau
is non-orientable then
\dim M(\tau)=b-v=-\chi(\tau)
. If
\tau

is orientable then
\dim M(\tau)=b-(v-1)=1-\chi(\tau)
.

The point of this result is that in the non-orientable case the switch equations are
all independent, and in the orientable case one is redundant.
For the next result, a
maximal
track is a track
which is maximal with respect to inclusions of tracks: its complementary components
are all either triangles or once-punctured monogons. Recall here that we
are excluding sporadic surfaces.
A track is
filling
if it
carries a minimal filling lamination: that is, it carries a minimal lamination
whose complementary regions are ideal polygons with at most
one puncture. If a track is filling, it is necessary that all
complementary regions are polygons with at most one puncture. However,
this condition is not sufficient.
Each such polygon is bounded by finitely many (smooth) train paths (called
sides
)
meeting at switches pointing outward from the polygon (called
corners
).

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\tau
be a filling recurrent train track and let
s_{1},\dots,s_{k}
be the numbers of sides of the complementary polygons of
\tau
.
Let
p
be the number of punctures of
\Sigma
.

(i)

If
\tau
is non-orientable then

\operatorname{ind}(\tau)=2p+\sum(s_{i}-3).

(ii)

If
\tau
is orientable then

\operatorname{ind}(\tau)=-1+2p+\sum(s_{i}-3).

Proof.

Note that
\dim{\mathcal{ML}}(\Sigma)=-3\chi(\Sigma)-p
and that

\chi(\Sigma)=\chi(\tau)+(k-p)
since the number of unpunctured
polygons of
\Sigma\smallsetminus\tau
is
k-p
.
Therefore, if
\tau
is non-orientable,

\operatorname{ind}(\tau)=-3\chi(\Sigma)-p+\chi(\tau)=-2\chi(\tau)-3k+2p

by Proposition
2.1
.
There is one switch of
\tau
for each corner, so the total number of switches is
\sum s_{i}
. Since the train track is generic, each vertex is valence three and the number of edges of
\tau
is
\frac{3}{2}\sum s_{i}
. Therefore

\chi(\tau)=-\frac{1}{2}\sum s_{i}

and

\operatorname{ind}(\tau)=\sum s_{i}-3k+2p=2p+\sum(s_{i}-3).

If
\tau
is orientable, by Proposition
2.1
, the dimension of
M(\tau)
increases by one so

\operatorname{ind}(\tau)=-1+2p+\sum(s_{i}-3).

∎

When the index of a train track is sufficiently large, the train track is necessarily
not filling. The next proposition quantifies the exact upper bound on the index of a filling
track.

Proposition 2.3
.

The largest index
m(\Sigma)
of a recurrent filling track on
\Sigma
is

•

4g-5
if
\Sigma
is closed of genus
g>2
,

•

2 if
\Sigma
is closed of genus
g=2
,

•

4g+p-4
if
\Sigma
is nonsporadic of genus
g
with
p

punctures and
(g,p)\neq(1,2)
,

•

1 if
\Sigma
is the twice-punctured torus.

Proof.

From the formulas above, it follows that the index of a filling track
is maximized when the
number of complementary regions is minimized and the track is non-orientable. For
closed surfaces of genus
g>2
there are non-orientable recurrent
tracks with one complementary component (with
4g-2
sides).
When
g=2
such a train track is necessarily
orientable. When
p>0
the smallest possible number of complementary
components is
p
and there are such non-orientable tracks as long as

(g,p)\neq(1,2)
, in which case such tracks are orientable. The
(non)-existence of tracks as claimed follows from

[
31
]
. Note that the results there are phrased in terms of
singularities of quadratic differentials.
∎

Set
m(\Sigma)
to be the number given by Proposition
2.3
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
0\leq k\leq m(\Sigma)
. Define
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
to be the graph whose
vertices are the isotopy classes of essential simple closed curves on
\Sigma

and whose edges join pairs
\{\alpha,\beta\}
such that there is a track
\tau

with
\operatorname{ind}(\tau)>k
that carries both
\alpha
and
\beta
. Distance in
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
is denoted by
d_{k}
.

We do not require the track
\tau
in Definition
2.4
to be connected.
For any
\alpha,\beta
having disjoint representatives, we may consider
their disjoint union to be a train track
\tau
and we have
\operatorname{ind}(\tau)>m(\Sigma)
.
Thus,
\alpha,\beta
are joined by an edge in
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
for each
k
and the
natural map
{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)\to{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
is 1-Lipschitz. Moreover,

{\mathcal{C}}_{k+1}(\Sigma)\to{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
is also 1-Lipschitz.
Although we have restricted
k
to lie between 0 and
m(\Sigma)
,

{\mathcal{C}}_{-1}(\Sigma)
would naturally be the complete graph, since any two
curves are carried by a common train track. The graph
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\Sigma)
is the

principal curve graph
, so named by Hamenstädt in

[
19
]
.
Hamenstädt’s definition is slightly
different; we are using the one given by Maher-Masai-Schleimer
in
[
28
]
.

Throughout the rest of the paper,
d=d_{{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)}
denotes distance in the
curve graph.
Note that if
d_{m(\Sigma)}(\alpha,\beta)=1
, then
\alpha
and
\beta
are carried by a track
\tau
that doesn’t fill, so
\alpha
and
\beta
don’t fill. In particular,
d(\alpha,\beta)\leq 2
and therefore

{\mathcal{C}}_{m(\Sigma)}(\Sigma)
is quasi-isometric to

{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)
. This yields a tower of graphs with natural 1-Lipschitz maps
that are the identity on the vertices:

{\mathcal{C}}\overset{qi}{\simeq}{\mathcal{C}}_{m(\Sigma)}\to\mathcal{C}_{m(\Sigma)-1}\to\cdots\to{\mathcal{C}}_{1}\to\mathcal{C}_{0}

Our main theorem is:

Theorem 2.5
.

Every
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
is hyperbolic. The principal curve graph

{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\Sigma)
is a quasi-tree. Each map
\mathcal{C}_{k+1}(\Sigma)\to{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
has the property that every bounded set in

{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
is contained in a bounded set whose preimage in
{\mathcal{C}}_{k+1}(\Sigma)
is
a quasi-convex quasi-tree.

We collect some facts about quasi-convex subsets of a

Lemma 3.1

This follows directly from (1).

A metric space is a
quasi-tree
if it is quasi-isometric to a simplicial tree. Manning’s
bottleneck condition
gives a criterion for a geodesic metric space to be a quasi-tree. We use the version described on

[
6

.

Theorem 3.2

(
[
29
, Theorem 4.6]
)
.

Suppose that
X
is a geodesic metric space and
\Delta>0
is a constant such that the
following property is satisfied. For any pair of points
x,y\in X
there is a path
p_{x,y}

from
x
to
y
such that any other path
q
between
x
and
y
contains
p_{x,y}

in its
\Delta
-neighborhood. Then
X
is quasi-isometric to a tree, with quasi-isometry
constants depending only on
\Delta
.

For our applications, there is an extra subtlety in that the metric spaces we will be interested in will not be geodesic and, in fact, will not even be connected. More concretely, we need a version of the bottleneck condition that we can apply to a quasi-convex subgraph of a graph.

Let
X
be a graph and
A
a
Q
-quasi-convex subgraph. For

, we construct a new graph

by adding an edge between any pair of vertices

with
2\leq d(x,y)\leq D
. Let
A_{K}\subset X_{K}
be the subgraph spanned by

, i.e. the largest subgraph of

with the same vertex set as

. Let

, and

be the metrics for

, and

is the subspace metric. Let

be the path metric on

are

As

\tilde{d}_{A}(x,y)\leq d_{A}(x,y)\leq K\tilde{d}_{A}(x,y)

are quasi-isometric. Moreover, a

-connected path in

.

We claim that the diameter of

\displaystyle I_{Q^{*}+2\delta}\left(T^{j_{1}}_{i_{1}},\dots,T^{j_{N-1}}_{i_{N-1}}\right)

4
Hamenstädt’s graphs

Hamenstädt originally constructed a graph related to
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}
using a definition
which does not involve train tracks. We will show that her graphs are in fact
quasi-isometric to the graphs
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}
.

We define an index
\operatorname{ind}(\alpha,\beta)
associated to a pair of curves
\alpha,\beta

in minimal position.
First suppose that
\alpha
and
\beta
fill
\Sigma
. Then the complementary components of
\alpha\cup\beta
are even-sided polygons (since the curves fill) each with at most one puncture.
Any bigon in the complement of
\alpha\cup\beta
has a single puncture.
We say that a component of
\Sigma\smallsetminus(\alpha\cup\beta)
is
large
if it is not a non-punctured quadrilateral.
We label the large components
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}
and let
p
be the number of punctures.
Let
2s_{i}
be the number of sides of
P_{i}
. If
P_{i}
is non-punctured,
then
s_{i}\geq 3
. If
P_{i}
is punctured, it is possible that
s_{i}=1
or
2
.
If, after orienting
\alpha
and
\beta
, all intersection are of the same sign,
we say that
\alpha
and
\beta
are an
orientable pair
and define

\operatorname{ind}(\alpha,\beta)=-1+2p+\sum(s_{i}-3)
.
Otherwise we define
\operatorname{ind}(\alpha,\beta)=2p+\sum(s_{i}-3)
.
Finally, if
\alpha,\beta
don’t fill
\Sigma
,
we define
\operatorname{ind}(\alpha,\beta)=\infty
.

Next, we define
{\mathcal{C}}^{H}_{k}(\Sigma)
to be the graph with vertex set

\mathcal{V}({\mathcal{C}}^{H}_{k}(\Sigma))=\mathcal{V}({\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma))
and such that
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{V}({\mathcal{C}}^{H}_{k}(\Sigma))

are joined by an edge if
\operatorname{ind}(\alpha,\beta)>k
.
This is Hamenstädt’s original definition. Note that if all the complementary
components of
\alpha\cup\beta
are quadrilaterals, hexagons, or punctured bigons,
then there is at least one hexagon or punctured bigon.
Thus, the algebraic intersection number of
\alpha
and
\beta

must be strictly less than the geometric
intersection number, and we have
\operatorname{ind}(\alpha,\beta)=0
.

Denote by
d_{k}^{H}
the metric defined on
\mathcal{V}({\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma))
by the graph

{\mathcal{C}}_{k}^{H}(\Sigma)
. The remainder of this section will be dedicated to proving the
following:

Theorem 4.1
.

If
d_{k}(\alpha,\beta)=1
then
d^{H}_{k}(\alpha,\beta)\leq 2
.
If
d^{H}_{k}(\alpha,\beta)=1
then
d_{k}(\alpha,\beta)=1
.
In particular,
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
and
{\mathcal{C}}^{H}_{k}(\Sigma)
are quasi-isometric.

For the rest of the paper, we will typically fix the surface
\Sigma

and write
{\mathcal{C}}
for
{\mathcal{C}}(\Sigma)
,
{\mathcal{PML}}
for
{\mathcal{PML}}(\Sigma)
, etc.

We note that it may in fact be true that
{\mathcal{C}}^{H}_{k}(\Sigma)={\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
.
The difficulty in proving this is that we don’t have a direct criterion for
checking if
d_{k}(\alpha,\beta)\geq 2
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be a pair of curves that fill
\Sigma
. Then there exists a train track
\tau(\alpha,\beta)
that carries both
\alpha
and
\beta
with
\operatorname{ind}(\tau(\alpha,\beta))=\operatorname{ind}(\alpha,\beta)
.

Proof.

Recall that
\alpha
and
\beta
are chosen to be in minimal position.
A train track with bigons
\tau^{\prime}(\alpha,\beta)
is obtained from
\alpha\cup\beta
by
replacing each intersection point by a large branch at which
\alpha
turns
right and
\beta
turns left.
We will collapse the bigons to form a track
\tau(\alpha,\beta)
. That is, we will
define a quotient map of the surface,
which is a self-homotopy equivalence, so that the image of
\tau^{\prime}(\alpha,\beta)
will be the track
\tau(\alpha,\beta)
. Some care needs to be taken to ensure that the quotient space is still the same surface.

We make the collapsing procedure explicit as follows.
The union
\alpha\cup\beta
is a graph embedded in
\Sigma
. There is thus a dual
graph
(\alpha\cup\beta)^{*}
.
As a graph, all vertices of
\alpha\cup\beta
have valence four and therefore all of
the complementary components of
(\alpha\cup\beta)^{*}
are quadrilaterals. The graph

(\alpha\cup\beta)^{*}
can be realized in
\Sigma
with its punctures filled in.
We place one vertex in each
complementary component of
\alpha\cup\beta
and when the region contains a puncture we
place the vertex of
(\alpha\cup\beta)^{*}
at the puncture.
We can then realize each quadrilateral as a unit square.
These squares define a quadratic differential
q
on
\Sigma
with two corresponding
transverse singular foliations.
The union of lines in the squares parallel to
\alpha
form the
vertical
foliation
while the lines parallel to
\beta
form the
horizontal foliation
.
These foliations have singularities at those vertices of
(\alpha\cup\beta)^{*}

which are incident to more than four squares.
The number of squares that meet at a vertex of
(\alpha\cup\beta)^{*}
is exactly the
number of sides of the corresponding complementary component of
\alpha\cup\beta
.
Each square of
q
contains a single vertex of
\alpha\cup\beta
and each edge
of a square intersects a single edge of
\alpha\cup\beta
.

Recall the train track with bigons
\tau^{\prime}(\alpha,\beta)
defined above. We use
the foliations of

to explicitly construct a train track
\tau(\alpha,\beta)

without bigons. We first replace

\alpha\cup\beta
with a trivalent graph
(\alpha\cup\beta)^{\prime}
by inserting an
edge of length
\sqrt{0.02}
and slope one centered at each vertex of

\alpha\cup\beta
(contained inside a square of the quadratic differential
q
)
and then modifying the original edges to line segments that start and end at the vertices of the slope one edges. After doing this, the vertical edges of
\alpha\cup\beta
will have slope
-9
and the horizontal edges will have slope
-1/9
.
We can then smooth the vertices of
\alpha\cup\beta
to define a train track

\tau^{\prime}(\alpha,\beta)
which has the property that the slopes of all its tangent lines
lie outside the range
\left(-9,-1/9\right)
.
See Figure
1
.

Now fix an irrational slope

on

with possible singularities at the vertices of

Furthermore, since non-punctured bigons of

Under this map, the image of

and the set of complementary components of

∎

Note that in the construction above we built two singular foliations associated to a
quadratic differential
q
that we called vertical and horizontal. The curve
\alpha

is a leaf of the vertical foliation while

is transverse to it. The roles of

\alpha
and

are reversed for the horizontal foliation. We can use either of

. We say that a foliation

is

Lemma 4.3
.

Assume that
\alpha
and
\beta
fill
\Sigma
and that
{\cal F}
is a foliation which is
compatible with
\alpha,\beta
. Let

be a component of

\Sigma\smallsetminus(\alpha\cup\beta)
with
2s

contains

\ell
singularities, which have

s=2-2\ell+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}i_{j}.

Collapsing the leaves of
{\cal F}
in

This tree contains
\ell

s+\ell-\frac{1}{2}\left(s+\sum i_{j}\right)=1

In this section we investigate the basic geometric properties of

and

Let
\tau

.
We say that

is

if every train path through

.
Otherwise

is
small
. A branch is called

if both its half-branches are large,

if both its half-branches are small,
and

otherwise.

We say that a train track
\sigma
is obtained from
\tau
by a

left, central, or right split
(respectively) if
\sigma
is obtained from

\tau
by a move as shown on the left, middle, or right of Figure
3

(respectively). The split is on the large branch labeled 1 in the figure.
In any of these cases, there is a carrying map
\sigma\to\tau
which sends switches
to switches.
We call the branch labeled “1” on the bottom of Figure
3
the
small branch
created by the split
in case of a left split or right split.
The inverse move to a split is called a
fold
(over the small
branch labeled 1 on the bottom of Figure
3
).
After a left or a right split
\sigma\to\tau
,
there is a natural bijection between the branches of
\sigma
and those of
\tau
.
The numbers in Figure
3

indicate that the bijection identifies branches with the same numbers.
The bijection is the “identity” on any branch not lying among those five
which are involved in the split. Thus, we will sometimes identify
the branches of
\sigma
with those of
\tau
via these bijections.

If
\tau
is a track with a

b
and
\tau_{L},\tau_{C},\tau_{R}
are, respectively, the tracks

and right splits of
\tau

.

Corollary 7.20

11
Acylindricity

Our aim in this section is to prove the following theorem:

Theorem 11.1
.

The action of
\operatorname{MCG}(\Sigma)
on
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)
is acylindrical for every
k
.

Recall that for an element
g
of a group
G
acting on the hyperbolic space

asymptotic translation length

\operatorname{ATL}(g)\vcentcolon=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{d_{X}(g^{n}x,x)}{n}

.

We start by proving that there is a positive lower bound on asymptotic
translation lengths of loxodromic elements in
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}
, which is a necessary condition for acylindricity.

Theorem 11.2
.

The mapping class
\phi\in\operatorname{MCG}(\Sigma)
is loxodromic in the action on
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(\Sigma)

if and only if
\phi
is pseudo-Anosov with fixed laminations lying in

\partial{\mathcal{C}}_{k}
. Equivalently,
\phi
is loxodromic if and
only if all its invariant train tracks have index
\leq k
.

depending
only on
\Sigma
such that whenever
\phi
is loxodromic on
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}
, we have
\operatorname{ATL}(\phi)\geq E
on
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}
.

Proof.

If
\phi
is not pseudo-Anosov then it acts elliptically on
{\mathcal{C}}
and hence
also on
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}
.
If
\phi
is pseudo-Anosov then for any
\alpha
, the sequence
\{\alpha,\phi(\alpha),\phi^{2}(\alpha),\dots\}
is a quasi-geodesic in
{\mathcal{C}}
that converges to the stable lamination
\Lambda^{+}
.
Then
\{\alpha,\phi(\alpha),\phi^{2}(\alpha),\dots\}
is a quasi-geodesic in
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}
if
\operatorname{ind}(\Lambda)\leq k
and has bounded diameter if
\operatorname{ind}(\Lambda)>k
. In particular,
\phi
is loxodromic if
\operatorname{ind}(\Lambda)\leq k
and is elliptic if
\operatorname{ind}(\Lambda)>k
. Note that
\phi
is elliptic if and only
\phi^{-1}
is elliptic so this argument implies that
\Lambda^{-}
, the unstable lamination, has the same index as
\Lambda^{+}
.

When
\phi
is loxodromic we need a more subtle argument to get a uniform lower bound on
\operatorname{ATL}(\phi)
.
Let
\tau^{\prime}
be a train track carrying
\Lambda
with
\operatorname{ind}(\tau^{\prime})=\operatorname{ind}(\Lambda)
. Then by

[
1
, Section 3]
we may split
\tau^{\prime}
to a track
\tau

such that
\phi(\tau){\overset{ss}{\longrightarrow}}\tau
. As the tracks
\tau_{n}=\phi^{n}(\tau)
all have index equal to
\operatorname{ind}(\Lambda^{+})
, we have that
\Lambda^{+}
is fully carried by every
\tau_{n}
. Therefore there is an

such that
P(\tau_{m})
is contained in the interior of
P(\tau)
.

The number of proper faces of

is
\leq N
where
N
only depends on
\Sigma
. We claim that if
\gamma
is a vertex cycle of
\tau
then
\phi^{N}(\gamma)
is fully carried by
\tau
. If not there are

such that
\phi^{i}(\gamma)
and

are both contained in the interior of the same proper face

of

. This implies that

. On the other hand since

or, equivalently,

Let

The proof of the more general statement will be an inductive argument. The inductive step will come from the case when

Proposition A.1

Proposition A.9
.

Given
C>0
there exists
C^{\prime}=C^{\prime}(\Sigma,m,C)>0

such that the following holds. Let
\tau,\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{m}
be train tracks with
\tau_{i}{\overset{ss}{\longrightarrow}}\tau
and let
\sigma_{1}\subset\tau_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m}\subset\tau_{m}
be subtracks. Then either

(1)

the diameter of
I_{C}({\mathcal{S}}(\sigma_{1}),\dots,{\mathcal{S}}(\sigma_{m}))
is
\leq C^{\prime}
, or

(2)

I_{C}({\mathcal{S}}(\sigma_{1}),\dots,{\mathcal{S}}(\sigma_{m}))\subset B_{C^{\prime}}({\mathcal{S}}(\sigma_{1})\cap\cdots\cap{\mathcal{S}}(\sigma_{m}))
.

Proof.

Let
{\bf I}=I_{C}({\mathcal{S}}(\sigma_{1}),\dots,{\mathcal{S}}(\sigma_{m}))
,
{\bf\tilde{I}}={\mathcal{S}}(\sigma_{1})\cap\cdots\cap{\mathcal{S}}(\sigma_{m})
and
V_{i}=V(\sigma_{i})
.

We claim there is
C_{1}=C_{1}(\Sigma,m,C)
so
that

{\bf I}\subset B_{C_{1}}\left({\bf\tilde{I}}\right)\cup\bigcup_{i}B_{C_{1}}(V_{i}).

Proof of Claim.
Let
\beta\in{\bf I}
.
Thus there are
\alpha_{i}\in S(\sigma_{i})
so that

d(\alpha_{i},\beta)\leq C
. Let
C^{\prime}
be the constant from Proposition

A.8
with
2C
as
C
. Since
d(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j})\leq 2C
it then follows that either

d(\alpha_{i},\sigma_{i})\leq C^{\prime}
for some
i

or there is
\tilde{\alpha}\in{\mathcal{S}}(\sigma_{1})\cap\cdots\cap{\mathcal{S}}(\sigma_{m})
with

d(\alpha_{i},\tilde{\alpha})\leq C^{\prime}
for all
i
.
In the first case it follows that

d(\beta,\sigma_{i})\leq C+C^{\prime}
and in the second it follows that

\beta\in B_{C+C^{\prime}}\left({\bf\tilde{I}}\right)
.
Either way, the claim is proved with
C_{1}=C+C^{\prime}
.

By Theorem
5.1
there is a
Q>0
such that the sets
S(\sigma_{i})
are
Q
-quasi-convex. It suffices to assume that
C\geq Q
and then by (
2
) of Lemma
3.1
we have that
{\bf I}
is
C+2\delta
-quasi-convex and hence
2C+4\delta+1
-connected. By the claim
{\bf I}
is contained in the union of the
m
sets
B_{C_{1}}(V_{i})
, each of which has uniformly bounded diameter, and the set
B_{C_{1}}({\bf\tilde{I}})
. If
{\bf\tilde{I}}=\emptyset
then this implies that the diameter of
{\bf I}
is uniformly bounded while if
{\bf\tilde{I}}\neq\emptyset
then
{\bf I}
is contained in a uniform neighborhood of
{\bf\tilde{I}}
. In the former case (1) holds while in the latter (2) holds.
∎

Lemma A.10
.

Fix constants
Q
and
C^{\prime}\geq Q+2\delta
and let
X
be a
\delta
-hyperbolic graph. Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be a collection of
Q
-quasi-convex subsets of
X
that is closed under finite intersections. Assume that there exists
D_{X}>0
such that for any
A_{1},\dots,A_{m}\in{\mathcal{A}}
either

(1)

\operatorname{diam}_{X}I_{C^{\prime}}(A_{1},\dots,A_{m})\leq D_{X}
or;

(2)

I_{C^{\prime}}(A_{1},\dots,A_{m})\subset B_{D_{X}}(A_{1}\cap\cdots\cap A_{m})
.

Let
Y
be a
\delta
-hyperbolic graph and
f\colon X\to Y
an injective,
L_{0}
-Lipschitz map. Also assume that there is
L_{1}>0
such that for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
we have

f([x_{1},x_{2}])\subset B_{L_{1}}([f(x_{1}),f(x_{2})]).

Then for any
C>0
there is
D_{Y}=D_{Y}(D_{X},C,L_{0},L_{1},m)
such that:

(A)

\operatorname{diam}_{Y}I_{C}(f(A_{1}),\dots,f(A_{m}))\leq D_{Y}
or;

(B)

\displaystyle I_{C}(f(A_{1}),\dots,f(A_{m}))

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle B_{D_{Y}}(f(A_{1}\cap\cdots\cap A_{m}))

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle B_{D_{Y}}(f(A_{1})\cap\cdots\cap f(A_{m})).

Proof.

We first assume that there are only two sets
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
. The general case will follow by induction.

Let
a_{1},b_{1}\in A_{1}
and
a_{2},b_{2}\in A_{2}
and fix
c
and
d
in the geodesics
[a_{1},a_{2}]
and
[b_{1},b_{2}]
such that
d_{X}(c,d)
minimizes the distance between the two geodesics. In particular, this implies that
B_{2\delta}([a_{1},c])\cap[c,d]\subset B_{2\delta}(\{c\})
with a similar statement for
b_{1}
and
d
. By
\delta
-hyperbolicity we have

[c,d]\subset B_{2\delta}([a_{1},c]\cup[a_{1},b_{1}]\cup[b_{1},d])

so if
d_{X}(c,d)>4\delta
we have
[c,d]\subset B_{4\delta}([a_{1},b_{1}])
and therefore
[c,d]\subset B_{Q+4\delta}(A_{1})
. A similar statement holds for
A_{2}
so if
d_{X}(c,d)>4\delta
we have
[c,d]\subset I_{Q+4\delta}(A_{1},A_{2})
.

Assume we are in case (1) with
x,y\in I_{C}(f(A_{1}),f(A_{2}))
. Then there are
a_{1},b_{1}\in A_{1}
and
a_{2},b_{2}\in A_{2}
with
d_{Y}(f(a_{1}),x),d_{Y}(f(a_{2}),x),d_{Y}(f(b_{1}),y),d_{Y}(f(b_{2}),y)<C
. We choose
c
and
d
as above
and observe that

d_{Y}(x,f(c)),d_{Y}(y,f(d))\leq 3C+L_{1}

and therefore

\displaystyle d_{Y}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d_{Y}(f(c),f(d))+d_{Y}(x,f(c))+d_{Y}(y,f(d))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L_{0}D_{X}+6C+2L_{1}=D_{Y}.

This proves that (A) holds.

Now assume we are in case (2) with
x\in I_{C}(f(A_{1}),f(A_{2}))
and
a_{1},a_{2}
and
c
as before. Again we have
d_{Y}(x,f(c))\leq 3C+L_{1}
. Then fix
d\in A_{1}\cap A_{2}
and set
b_{1}=b_{2}=d
. Therefore if
d_{X}(c,d)>4\delta
then
c\in I_{Q+4\delta}(A_{1},A_{2})
and, as (2) holds, we can further assume that
d
has been chosen such that
d_{X}(c,d)\leq D_{X}
. We then have
d_{X}(c,d)\leq\min\{4\delta,D_{X}\}=D_{X}
and it follows that

\displaystyle d_{Y}(x,f(d))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d_{Y}(x,f(c))+d_{Y}(f(c),f(d))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 3C+L_{1}+L_{0}D_{X}=D_{Y}.

Therefore (B) holds.

Now assume that the lemma holds for any collection of
m-1
sets in
{\mathcal{A}}
and let
A_{1},\dots,A_{m}
be sets in
{\mathcal{A}}
. If
A_{1}\cap A_{2}=\emptyset
then we are in case (1) for the sets
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
so

I_{C}(f(A_{1}),\cdots,f(A_{2}))\subset I_{C}(f(A_{1}),f(A_{2}))

has diameter
\leq D^{2}_{Y}=D_{Y}(D_{X},C,C^{\prime},L_{0},L_{1},2)
. Therefore we can assume that
A_{1}\cap A_{2}\neq\emptyset
. Since
f
is injective we have
f(A_{1}\cap A_{2})=f(A_{1})\cap f(A_{2})
and

\displaystyle I_{C}(f(A_{1}),\dots,f(A_{m}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I_{C}(f(A_{1}),f(A_{2}))\cap I_{C}(f(A_{3}),\dots,f(A_{m}))

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle B_{D^{2}_{Y}}(f(A_{1})\cap f(A_{2}))\cap I_{D^{2}_{Y}}(f(A_{3}),\dots,f(A_{m}))

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle B_{D^{2}_{Y}}(f(A_{1}\cap A_{2}))\cap I_{D^{2}_{Y}}(f(A_{3}),\dots,f(A_{m}))

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle I_{D^{2}_{Y}}(f(A_{1}\cap A_{2}),f(A_{3}),\dots,f(A_{m})).

Therefore
D_{Y}(D_{X},C,C^{\prime},L_{0},L_{1},m)=D_{Y}(D_{X},D^{2}_{Y},C^{\prime},L_{0},L_{1},m-1)
is the desired constant.
∎
