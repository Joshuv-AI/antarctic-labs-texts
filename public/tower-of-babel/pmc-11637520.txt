Getting precise about gender and sex measurement: a primer for epidemiologists

Introduction

Accurately measuring gender and sex is crucial in public health and epidemiology. Iteratively reexamining how variables—including gender and sex—are conceptualized and operationalized is necessary to achieve impactful research. Collecting accurate and appropriate data on gender and sex may present conceptual and logistical challenges, particularly when epidemiologists and researchers are designing studies that are not explicitly focused on these topics and may view gender and sex as ancillary to their research. Epidemiologists are trained to construct and analyze gender and sex variables using a fixed, binary gender ideology such that gender means socially constructed characteristics of women and men, and sex means biological and physiological characteristics that define humans as female and male.1 (These socially constructed characteristics directly relate to inequitable gender power relations, which is a related concept worthy of its own discussions but beyond the scope of this work.2‑4)

While this fixed, binary gender ideology is institutionalized throughout major epidemiologic surveillance systems, both locally and globally,5‑7 it is exclusionary of transgender and intersex people by design. Instead, we understand sex and gender as interactions of encompassing characteristics between areas of genetics; epigenetics; biological, anatomical, physiological, and social environments; cognition; history; and culture. Reexamining gender and sex advances epidemiology toward its goals of health promotion and disease elimination. While we cannot reduce the complexities of sex and gender to simply an issue of measurement, striving to capture these concepts and experiences accurately must be an ongoing dialogue and practice—to the benefit of the field and population health.8

We, a collective of transgender and nonbinary (trans) and cisgender (cis) epidemiologists, interdisciplinary researchers, and scholars, challenge common epidemiologic misconceptions about gender and sex. Challenging these misconceptions is not novel; our critiques draw heavily on prior experts’ scholarship across disciplines (eg, biology, sociology, history, anthropology, and feminist and queer theories). Indeed, challenges and critiques are not new to epidemiology either, but the field has yet to embrace these complexities fully.9‑15 We assert that epidemiology must converse with these disciplines to counteract misconceptions and accurately measure gender and sex in epidemiology. We aim to summarize existing critiques and guiding principles in measuring gender and sex that can be applied in practice.

As epidemiologists, why must we challenge gender and sex misconceptions?

Epidemiologists must critically engage in unlearning and challenging misconceptions to ensure proper measurement. Gender and sex are historically constructed—bound in time, ideologies, sciences, and practices that are constantly evolving, fluid, and reconstructed.16 Gender is the time-varying, cumulative interactions between an individual’s genetics, epigenetics, biological and social environments, cognition, and past experiences that provide a heuristic description of their relationship to the stereotyped roles and behaviors assigned to men and women. This conceptualization includes a total rejection, combination, or new creation of these stereotyped roles and behaviors. Several Indigenous cultures celebrate and embrace gender fluidity,17,18 and archival and contemporary records worldwide reveal nonfixed and nonbinary genders.19,20 The fixed binary gender system (man/masculine; woman/feminine) is an inaccurate, unscientific, colonial, patriarchal system and ideology.21 It enables White cis men to control and keep generational power and wealth21,22 and to police and mistreat femininity, gender fluidity, and gender nonconformity.23,24 Epidemiologic practice reinforces this via study design, data collection, and analytical approaches, such as binary gender options on instruments and treating men as the referent group (and hence default and normal).25

The construction of sex as a binary, dimorphic human characteristic (ie, female or male) is challenged in studies from the genetic, epigenetic, biomedical, neurology, and neuroendocrinology fields.26,27 Rather, sex is a multidimensional, constructed continuum of chromosomal, gonadal, fetal and pubertal hormonal, internal reproductive, external genital, and brain sex27‑29 that is directly influenced by gender throughout the life course.10‑12,14,15,30‑32 The existence and lived experiences of intersex people, or people with variations in sex characteristics (VSC),33,34 and of transgender people (through hormonal and/or surgical changes) further challenge this binary.35 While the conflation of gender and sex is common,36 these concepts are neither interchangeable nor completely separate—they are entangled.11 Careful attention to their operationalization, interaction, multidimensionality, and mutual influence is warranted.12,15,27,37,38 (Some scholars use “sex/gender” to highlight this “irreducible entangled phenomenon,”14,39 but this should not be confused with the conflation of sex and gender).

Prevailing misconceptions of sex and gender measurement erase and misclassify cis, trans, and intersex people/people with VSC. Despite the growing visibility of trans populations globally,40,41 epidemiologic data on trans people are lacking, given outdated and inaccurate measures for gender identity—or complete lack thereof—in national surveillance and survey studies.42,43 Epidemiologists and researchers should recognize and affirm every person’s gender and sex. Trans erasure is a violent practice of “how trans[gender] is managed in culture and institutions, a condition that ultimately inscribes trans[gender] as impossible”44 and removes trans people from records and data. Erasure intentionally or unintentionally dismisses or restrains this population’s significance.42,44 Erasure in epidemiology can happen in design (eg, not considering trans people in the sample while not explicitly excluding them), recruitment (eg, not having inclusive practices or culturally competent staff), data collection (eg, not measuring gender identity or modality), analysis (eg, recoding, collapsing or binning as binary, recategorizing as “other,” removing trans participants due to low sample size, or not disaggregating by sex or gender at all), and dissemination and translation of results (eg, removing the trans/nonbinary sample from tables, presenting nonbinary people as binary, using stereotypically gendered blue/pink/purple in visualization45). These practices misidentify trans people as cisgender and vice versa, lower power to detect differences, and bias prevalence and risk estimates for trans populations, even when large inequities between groups exist.32 Note that the safety of trans and intersex people is paramount, and recruitment and reporting practices that could inadvertently out or identify them must be avoided. Inadvertent identification is especially likely if there are very few trans people in the study or if they live in a setting that criminalizes them. To maintain safety and accurate reporting while still addressing erasure, epidemiologists and data analysts can learn and adopt data reporting practices from study designs that prioritize smaller sample sizes (eg, qualitative studies, case studies). This can be a beneficial approach when describing the data. In these kinds of studies, reporting only pertinent, nonidentifiable information vital to contextualizing the data while meeting the purpose of the study is still highly achievable.

Improving gender and sex measurement and methods in epidemiology and public health

Gender and sex have been theorized, conceptualized, and operationalized in public health and epidemiology for over two decades;10,11,14,32,46 some of this discourse includes trans and other gender identities.12,38 Health researchers and agencies have recommended inclusive measures,5,47‑49 but measuring gender and sex has no gold standard, perhaps since these variables depend on time and context. Pragmatically, research questions, aims, scope, study design, methods, and capacity to collect and analyze data should all influence how to measure gender and sex.12,14,15 That is, as there is no single best practice, investigators must decide which dimensions of sex and gender are relevant to their research questions.

As noted in Table 1, using two-step and/or multistep approaches to ascertain gender and sex variables has advantages. Specifically, combined responses to the multiple questions allow analysts to create new variables that better operationalize sex and gender. Do not report on gender and sex separately when using these methods. There are multiple ways to categorize data from a two-step approach. For example, categories could include transgender women, transgender men, nonbinary people, cisgender women, and cisgender men. Another route could categorize men, women, and nonbinary people. Still, other research inquiries may need further information about past or current organs and body parts (eg, uterus, prostate) in addition to gender identity; body parts should not be assumed in cis nor trans bodies.39 In some cases, given the variety of hormone usage history within trans populations,57 it may be useful to further disaggregate nonbinary people based on their exogenous hormone use (testosterone, estrogen, neither). Additionally, this approach enables epidemiologists and researchers to report nonbinary people based on their sex assigned at birth as well as their gender expression as transfeminine, transmasculine, and neither/agender.

Table 2 presents a sample of recent recommendations using two-step gender and sex questions. When to use a particular version depends on the research question, population, and scope, as well as a balance between the degree of validation needed versus the representativeness of the data for the trans population. If a validated measure for a general population is needed in a national sample, the National Academy of Science, Engineering, and Medicine (NASEM) version5 would work (with limitations; see below). If it is a smaller local study on sexually transmitted infections, mental health, or other issues that disproportionately impact trans people, then more tailored methods are useful (ie, Kidd et al51). Lastly, for a transgender-specific study, the most tailored version would be ideal (ie, Kronk et al56). If a survey item like Kronk’s is used for a general population, it is likely that options such as “questioning” and “X” will have very small sample sizes. This approach risks those respondents being dropped, as described above. Note that these approaches are imperfect and may still misclassify some trans and intersex people and may not be reflective of people of trans experience who may resist labeling their gender modality. (Gender modality “refers to how a person’s gender identity stands in relation to their gender assigned at birth” and includes the categories cisgender and transgender but is not limited to them.38,58) However, they are more affirming than one-step, binary measures and are more likely to identify people of trans experience who do not identify as transgender. Last, we assert that before any ascertainment of gender and sex measurement occurs, epidemiologists and researchers must strive to engage and be in consultation with trans and intersex community stakeholders. For trans- or intersex-specific research, community involvement from the beginning is essential.59

Each of these methods has its strengths and limitations. The strengths of NASEM’s recommendation5 are that it has been validated, it includes transgender people, it recognizes Indigenous gender identities, it recognizes that gender can be time-varying (ie, asking for current gender), and it offers a free-text response. NASEM also recommends a separate item to assess intersex status. Limitations include that it can be othering to trans people (ie, can be read to imply they are neither male nor female); it uses terms often associated with sex for gender (ie, male and female instead of man and woman); it conflates gender identity (ie, woman/female, man/male) with gender modality (transgender, cisgender, agender, etc.)38,58; and it asks sex assigned at birth before gender identity. We do not recommend the latter, as it prioritizes sex over gender identity and can be off-putting to trans people.47 Last, it may exclude those without a birth certificate.

Kidd et al’s, recommendation51 is strong in that it includes transgender people, including those outside a binary, and allows for multiple responses, which can be affirming. The options include “another identity,” which may not be ideal but is superior to “other” or “other identity” options. However, allowing multiple responses may be challenging for epidemiologists and data analysts to parse out. To prevent miscategorization, adding another follow-up question may be more impactful. The follow-up could prompt participants who had answered multiple options to pick the one that is most salient to them. This approach maintains transparency and addresses erasure by reporting all the variances/choices that have been selected. Such pertinent ad hoc approaches would need further validation. Kidd’s version also has limitations, particularly that it is oriented to youth and has not been validated for adults. This measure also prioritizes cisgender modality by not labeling “girl” and “boy” in the first two options but labeling trans options, thus implying cis is the “default” or “normal.” Like NASEM, it asks for sex before gender identity, but unlike NASEM, it does not offer a free-text response. Last, it assumes the current sex is the same as that on the birth certificate and assignment at birth, which is not the case for trans people who have legally changed their sex.

Strengths of Kronk et al’s recommendation56 include that a team of transgender academics designed it and is broadly inclusive of many gender identities. Unlike the others, it foregrounds gender identity over sex in the ordering and places sex and gender terms together to accommodate many ways of identifying (eg, female, woman, girl). The choices are most flexible, allowing for nonbinary, questioning/exploring in gender identity; X and unsure for gender assigned at birth; and choosing not to respond and write-in options for both steps. Kronk’s format uses assigned gender at birth rather than assigned sex at birth, reflecting the practical reality that sex and gender assignment at birth are generally one-to-one (ie, someone assigned male at birth is assumed to be a boy). This use of gender also speaks to the “irreducible entangled phenomenon of sex/gender.”14 Kronk’s major limitation is that it has not yet been validated.

Conclusion

As delineated in this piece, poorly measuring gender and sex has many consequences. From an epidemiologic perspective, it affects a study’s internal validity, leading to measurement error, misclassification, and, thus, potentially invalid inferences. Inaccurately collecting gender and sex variables may also lead to reporting bias, as participants could be more likely to answer incorrectly, decline to participate, or drop out of a study if their identities are not represented. It can, therefore, limit our ability as epidemiologists and members of the public health community to design appropriate interventions that aim to address health inequities accurately. Most importantly, measuring gender and sex incorrectly or poorly has policy, societal, and political implications. Replicating practices that are grounded in the fixed gender binary system and applying those to gender and sex measurements and analysis only reinforces the erasure, othering, and invalidation of intersex, nonbinary, and agender people, and it contributes to the harms of transphobia and cisnormativity.

Additionally, while the scope of this commentary reflects expertise from our authorship team—mostly US-based researchers—it is critical to note that these recommendations may not transcend all cultures and societies, as is true for all Eurocentric patriarchal system ideologies and practices. Additionally, we emphasize the time-varying nature of gender and sex and make room for the evolving developments or new expansions that have yet to permeate the field of epidemiology, including frameworks that capture several dimensions (eg, gender modality, gender trajectory, and binary relation).50,52,60,61 However, as we highlighted, the recommendation to conduct cognitive and pilot testing with local communities before data collection applies to global research, and such approaches can adapt or generate gender and sex constructs in various settings and/or with populations globally.55 We also highlighted several better practices for measuring and incorporating gender and sex variables, including teasing out dimensions of gender and sex through multistep approaches and combining gender and sex variables in analysis (eg, (1) sex assigned at birth: female + gender identity: man = man [assigned female at birth] vs. (2) sex assigned at birth: female + gender identity: man + gender modality: trans = trans man), and relating the measurement choices to the research question, aims, and scope. We also encourage epidemiologists and researchers to minimize bias by being transparent about approaches to collecting and analyzing gender and sex variables.

Improving gender and sex measurements in all subdisciplines of public health, particularly in epidemiology, where data-driven surveillance studies are highly valued, is necessary for health promotion and disease elimination in all populations. Such practices should also permeate all topics within public health and epidemiology, given that gender and sex variables are critical to examining all aspects of health. There are inherent limitations to operationalizing any multidimensional construct to zeros and ones, particularly ones that attempt to capture fundamental aspects of human identities. In epidemiology, though, we make such choices. Given that transphobia, misogyny, and cissexism are root causes of health inequities,1,62 we must interrogate dominant assumptions based on fixed binary definitions of gender and sex. Therefore, effectively addressing health inequities for all genders and sexes is an important goal for epidemiology as a field. Transgender, intersex, nonbinary, and agender people are members of the general population and exist in our studies. Our ethical obligation as epidemiologists and public health scientists is to classify them accurately. This also requires better approaches to sampling and assessing the representativeness of sex and gender diversity in studies. While gold standards may not exist, several existing approaches and less harmful examples recognize and affirm all genders and sexes. Epidemiology as a field must not only accept and improve upon these practices but should also embrace the potential to lead methodological advancements in this area.
