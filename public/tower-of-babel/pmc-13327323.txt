Type 1 diabetes‐specific distress among adults with type 1 diabetes in Japan
Authors: Naoki Sakane, Masayuki Domich, Akiko Suganum, Seiko Sakane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe prevalence and characteristics of diabetes distress (DD) in Japanese adults with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1D) remain unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the prevalence and associated features of DD in this population. A cross‐sectional study was conducted using the Type 1 Diabetes Distress Scale (T1‐DDS), the Problem Areas in Diabetes (PAID) scale, and the Hypoglycemia Fear Survey (HFS). Data from 117 adults with T1D were analyzed. Group comparisons were made using t‐tests and anova, and associations were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to examine the structure of the T1‐DDS.ResultsOf the 144 screened participants, 117 (mean age: 50.6 ± 12.8 years; 44.4% male; mean diabetes duration: 18.1 ± 10.7 years; mean HbA1c: 7.6% ± 0.9%) were included in the analysis. Moderate and high DD were observed in 30.8% and 16.2%, respectively. Participants with high DD had significantly higher PAID and HFS‐Worry/Behavior scores, despite no significant differences in age, sex, diabetes duration, or HbA1c. Among T1‐DDS subscales, Powerlessness and Eating Distress had the highest mean scores. All subscale scores were correlated with total distress (rho = 0.636–0.916) and showed good internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha = 0.784–0.875). CFA supported the seven‐factor structure with marginal‐to‐acceptable model fit.ConclusionDD is prevalent in Japanese adults with T1D. The T1‐DDS can be a useful screening tool for identifying individuals experiencing diabetes‐related emotional burden and guiding personalized interventions for these individuals.Keywords: Diabetes distress, type 1 diabetes mellitus, validity

INTRODUCTION

Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1D) is a chronic autoimmune condition characterized by insufficient insulin production due to destruction of pancreatic beta cells. Effective management of T1D requires lifelong insulin administration, regular blood glucose monitoring, and adherence to structured dietary and physical activity regimens [1, 2]. The term “diabetes distress” (DD) was introduced in psychosocial research literature in 1995 to describe the negative emotional experiences resulting from the ongoing demands of diabetes management. Although researchers were initially cautious about emphasizing DD as a major barrier to self‐care, accumulating evidence, including multiple systematic reviews of interventional studies, has demonstrated that DD is common in people with T1D and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D) across diverse age groups and cultural contexts. DD has been shown to negatively affect emotional well‐being, diabetes self‐care, and glycemic control. Accordingly, routine screening for DD is now recommended in many national diabetes care guidelines [3, 4, 5].

DD encompasses concerns about disease complications, emotional burdens, and the challenges of living with diabetes. Studies have suggested that the sources of DD differ between individuals with T1D and T2D. In adults with T1D, DD often goes unrecognized and is poorly addressed. To better identify and understand the sources of DD in this population, the Type 1 Diabetes Distress Scale (T1‐DDS) was developed [6]. The T1‐DDS is a validated 28‐item instrument consisting of seven subscales: Powerlessness, Management Distress, Hypoglycemia Distress, Negative Social Perceptions, Eating Distress, Physician Distress, and Friends/Family Distress. A mean subscale score ≥2 indicates moderate distress, and mean subscale scores ≥3 indicate high distress. In Japan, the Problem Areas in Diabetes (PAID) scale is widely used in both research and clinical settings. However, standardized subscales tailored to the specific challenges of T1D are yet to be established. Furthermore, the prevalence and characteristics of DD in Japanese adults with T1D remain unclear.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the prevalence of DD and its associated characteristics in Japanese adults with T1D.

METHOD

Participants

All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of National Hospital Organization Kyoto Medical Center (approval number: 21‐090). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after a full explanation of the study. The trial was registered at the University Hospital Medical Information Network (UMIN) Center (registration number: UMIN000056829). Participants were recruited from the National Hospital Organization Kyoto Medical Center, Kyoto, Japan, between January and July 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of type 1 diabetes mellitus, (2) age 20 years or older, and (3) receiving care at one of the collaborating centers. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) current use of anti‐dementia medications and (2) determination by the principal or co‐investigator that study participation would be inappropriate. Patients were classified as having acute‐onset, slowly progressive, or fulminant T1D according to the diagnostic criteria of the Japan Diabetes Society [7, 8, 9]. The sample size was estimated to be 120 for categorical endpoints, based on a descriptive study of sample sizes by Han et al. [10]. To account for an anticipated 10% missing data rate, 134 participants were recruited.

Measures

Data on age, sex, diabetes duration, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) level, body mass index, glucose monitoring (intermittently scanned and real‐time continuous glucose monitoring [isCGM and rtCGM, respectively]), diabetes complications (neuropathy, retinopathy, and nephropathy), and diabetes treatment (continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion [CSII] or sensor‐augmented pumps [SAP]) were collected from the medical records.

Questionnaires

The survey assessments included the T1‐DDS, which consists of 28 items. Each item was answered on a 6‐point Likert scale ranging from “Not a problem” (1) to “A very serious problem” (6). The total scale score was calculated as the mean of the scores for the 28 items. Furthermore, the T1‐DDS includes seven subscales of diabetes distress in people with T1D: Powerlessness, Negative Social Perceptions, Physician Distress, Friends/Family Distress, Hypoglycemia Distress, Management Distress, and Eating Distress [6, 11, 12]. Moderate/high DD was defined as mean score ≥2, while high DD was defined as mean score ≥3.

Additionally, DD was assessed using the 20‐item PAID scale, which is a widely validated and reliable instrument (Cronbach's α = 0.95) [13, 14]. Each item was rated on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“no problem”) to 4 (“serious problem”). The total score was calculated by summing all item scores and multiplying by 1.25, yielding a range of 0–100. Table 1 summarizes the key differences between the T‐DDS and the PAID instruments.

Fear of hypoglycemia was assessed using the Hypoglycemia Fear Survey (HFS) adapted for use in Japan [15]. The HFS has two subscales: the HFS‐Behavior (HFS‐B) and the HFS‐Worry (HFS‐W) subscales. The items were rated on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (always) [16]. Higher scores indicated a higher fear of hypoglycemia.

Data analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were expressed as percentages. Statistical analyses included t‐tests, analysis of variance (anova) with Bonferroni post hoc comparisons, and measurement of Spearman's rank correlation coefficients (rho) to assess associations between variables. Moderate (score = 2.0–2.9) and high DD (score ≥3.0) were calculated from all items of the T1‐DDS. The internal consistency of the Japanese T1‐DDS and its seven subscales was assessed by exploring the inter‐item correlations measured using the Spearman's rank correlation test (rho) and Cronbach's alpha. Owing to the relatively large number of items in the T1‐DDS, acceptable alpha levels were set at 0.90–0.95 for the full scale and >0.70 for the subscales, which correspond to the original US validation study [6]. Standardized confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was applied to test the seven US T1‐DDS subscales against Japanese data. Along with the chi‐square test of model fit, several tests of the fit of the 7‐factor model were used: the comparative fit index (CFI: 0.90–0.95 = “acceptable fit”; >0.95 = “good fit”); the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA: ≤0.05 = “very good fit”; 0.05–0.08 = “good fit”); and the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR: 0.05–0.08 = “acceptable fit”; >0.05 = “good fit”). Factor loadings for individual items were “acceptable” or “good” if >0.40 or >0.70, respectively. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was performed using Stata version XX (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

The non‐distress, moderate‐distress, and high‐distress groups showed no significant differences in age, sex, duration of diabetes, HbA1c level, diabetic complications, glucose monitoring methods, or treatment type. However, participants with moderate or high distress had significantly higher PAID scores as well as HFS‐W and HFS‐B subscale scores than those without distress.

The total and subscale scores of the T1‐DDS showed no significant correlations with age or diabetes duration, except for a weak positive association between the Negative Social Perceptions score and diabetes duration (rho = 0.205, P < 0.05). CFA and factor loadings of T1‐DDS

Model Fit Evaluation: The structural equation model showed an overall marginal‐to‐acceptable fit. The chi‐square test showed a significant result (χ2(324) = 630.376; P < 0.001), which may be attributable to sample size sensitivity. The RMSEA was 0.090 (90% confidence interval: 0.080–0.101), indicating borderline fit. The CFI and Tucker‐Lewis index (TLI) were 0.864 and 0.841, respectively, suggesting an acceptable‐to‐suboptimal comparative fit. In contrast, the SRMR was 0.072, indicating good residual fit. The coefficient of determination (CD) was 1.000, suggesting that the model explained the variance in the observed variables extremely well.

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated the psychometric properties of the T1‐DDS and explored its associations with key clinical and psychosocial variables in Japanese adults with T1D. Among the participants, 30.8% and 16.2% reported moderate and high levels of DD, respectively. Notably, several meaningful associations were observed between the T1‐DDS subscale scores and the related psychosocial measures. These findings support the reliability and convergent validity of the T1‐DDS in this population.

Validation of T1‐DDS

The internal consistency of the overall T1‐DDS and its seven subscales was high, with Cronbach's alpha values ranging from 0.78 to 0.88, and 0.95 for the total distress score. These values are consistent with those of previous validation studies in Western populations [6, 11, 17], supporting the reliability of the scale in Japanese clinical settings. We also evaluated the factorial structure of the T1‐DDS using SEM in a Japanese cohort. The model showed an overall acceptable fit: RMSEA = 0.090 (borderline), TLI = 0.841, and CFI = 0.864, all of which were slightly below the conventional threshold of 0.90. However, the SRMR was within the acceptable range (0.072), and the CD reached 1.000, indicating strong explanatory power. These findings support the general validity of the seven‐factor model but also indicate areas for improvement. The high inter‐factor correlations and borderline fit indices indicate a possible overlap among subdomains or cultural differences in item interpretation. Adaptation or revalidation may be required to optimize the construct validity of the T1‐DDS in the Japanese population.

Correlation of the key clinical features

Among the T1‐DDS subscales, Powerlessness and Eating Distress were the most commonly reported domains, with approximately 70% of the participants experiencing moderate‐to‐high levels of distress in these areas. In contrast, Physician Distress and Friends/Family Distress were less frequently reported. These patterns may reflect the cultural and healthcare system characteristics specific to Japan, where individualized dietary management demands and limited consultation time with healthcare providers may contribute to increased distress. In terms of clinical correlates, the HbA1c level was modestly but significantly associated with Management Distress and Eating Distress, suggesting that perceived difficulties in daily self‐management and eating behaviors may directly influence glycemic control. This finding aligns with the results of a previous study by Strandberg et al. (2015) [18], in which the regimen‐related distress subscale of the DDS‐17 showed the strongest association with glycemic control. Taken together, these results underscore the multidimensional nature of DD in Japanese adults with T1D and highlight the need to incorporate emotional and psychosocial assessments into routine diabetes care. Interventions aimed at strengthening self‐management skills, enhancing emotional resilience, and promoting dietary flexibility may help reduce distress and improve glycemic outcomes. In a large Danish cohort of adults with T1D (age ≥ 18 years), higher T1‐DDS scores were significantly associated with elevated PAID‐20 and HFS‐W scores, as well as higher HbA1c levels. Additionally, DD was more prevalent in younger individuals, females, those with a shorter diabetes duration, and those with diabetes‐related complications [17]. However, in the present study, the total DD score was not significantly correlated with age, duration of diabetes, or HbA1c levels. This discrepancy may reflect ethnic differences or differences in healthcare systems. Furthermore, the moderate correlations observed between the T1‐DDS and HFS‐W subscale scores suggest a strong link between hypoglycemia‐related anxiety and a broader emotional burden, particularly within the Hypoglycemia Distress and Powerlessness domains.

Differences between T1‐DDS and PAID

While both the T1‐DDS and PAID are validated instruments for assessing diabetes‐related emotional distress, they differ notably in their purpose, structure, and clinical utility, and these differences have important implications for research and practice. The PAID scale was originally developed as a global measure of emotional difficulties associated with living with diabetes and is applicable to both type 1 and type 2 diabetes populations. It consists of 20 items and is widely used as a screening tool for overall diabetes‐related distress. Although factor analyses have suggested that the PAID scale encompasses multiple dimensions, such as emotional distress, treatment challenges, food‐related concerns, and social support, it is primarily used to generate a total score that reflects general psychological burden. As such, the PAID scale has limited ability to identify specific sources of distress that require tailored intervention. In contrast, the T1‐DDS was specifically designed to capture the unique psychological challenges faced by adults with T1D. It consists of 28 items across seven conceptually distinct subscales: Powerlessness, Management Distress, Hypoglycemia Distress, Negative Social Perceptions, Eating Distress, Physician Distress, and Friends/Family Distress. This multidimensional structure allows for a more nuanced assessment of distress and facilitates the identification of domain‐specific emotional burdens that can be directly addressed through individualized clinical or psychosocial interventions.

Our findings support the use of the T1‐DDS to identify specific areas of distress that are not explicitly captured by the PAID scale. For example, hypoglycemic and eating distress were particularly pronounced in our sample, underscoring the value of the T1‐DDS in capturing the nuanced emotional experiences of adults with T1D. Although the T1‐DDS and PAID scores were strongly correlated, their structural differences suggest that they may serve complementary roles. The PAID scale may be well‐suited for broad screening purposes, whereas the T1‐DDS provides more detailed information that can inform clinical decision‐making and personalized care planning.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study was its use of robust SEM techniques to evaluate the psychometric properties of the T1‐DDS in a non‐Western context. However, the relatively modest sample size warrants caution when interpreting the results. Emerging evidence suggests that DD and transition readiness are inversely related in youths with T1D undergoing transition to adult care [19]. Interventions aimed at reducing distress may, therefore, enhance transition readiness, and vice versa. Future studies involving larger and more diverse populations are needed to confirm the generalizability of the findings and further explore the bidirectional relationship between emotional burden and healthcare transition readiness.

In conclusion, our findings indicated that DD is common among Japanese adults with T1D. The T1‐DDS proved to be a valuable screening instrument for identifying individuals experiencing diabetes‐related emotional burdens. We assessed total distress and its seven subdomains—Powerlessness, Management Distress, Hypoglycemia Distress, Negative Social Perceptions, Eating Distress, Physician Distress, and Friends/Family Distress. The findings highlighted the need for tailored psychological care based on specific distress profiles. Short‐form tools derived from the PAID‐20 scale, such as PAID‐5 and PAID‐1, have been used for brief screening purposes [20, 21]. In Denmark, the T1‐DDS‐7, a condensed version of the original 28‐item T1‐DDS, has demonstrated reliability for routine screening in adults with T1D [22]. A similar brief tool is required in Japanese clinical settings. Additionally, individuals with T1D have identified glucose prediction technologies as promising for reducing both the fear of hypoglycemia and overall distress [23]. Future studies should explore the integration of CGM and trend arrow data into psychological support strategies to further reduce the burden of diabetes management.
