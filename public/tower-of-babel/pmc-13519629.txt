From static compromise to dynamic design: a polarity modulation paradigm for multiclass veterinary drug residue analysis in complex foods
Authors: Qifan Sun, Hong Lin, Jianxin Sui, Kaiqiang Wang, Xiudan Wang, Limin Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The determination of multiclass veterinary drug residues in food is fundamentally constrained by the irreconcilable conflict between the extremely broad polarity of analytes and the dynamic, heterogeneous nature of complex matrices. Current mainstream sample preparation methods typically rely on static, compromise-driven conditions that limit optimal recovery for polarity-extreme compounds. This review articulates a paradigm shift by establishing the “Dynamic Polarity Window” (DPW) as a framework for rational design. Unlike static methods, DPW enables programmable polarity adjustment across pretreatment stages to match analyte-specific requirements. We critically evaluate conventional techniques through this polarity-centric lens and synthesize a toolkit of programmable strategies—encompassing solvent engineering, salting-out, pH/temperature levers, and microenvironment control—guided by physicochemical parameters. This framework enables the transition from passive, universal extraction to active, on-demand separation, enhancing selectivity and robustness. We finally outline key future directions to bridge the prediction-experiment gap, develop sustainable solvents, and integrate automation for robust residue analysis.

Introduction

Global regulatory standards for drug residues in animal-derived foods are being progressively tightened, with authorities such as the European Commission, the U.S. FDA, and China's Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs establishing increasingly stringent maximum residue limits (MRLs) for antibiotics, β-agonists, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and anthelmintics, as well as certain legacy pesticide residues (China, 2019; Union, 2010; United States Department of Agriculture, 2016). This trend is driven by both evolving toxicological insights—linking even trace-level chronic exposure to gut microbiota disruption and antibiotic resistance gene transfer, and intensifying international trade disputes where “zero tolerance” policies often lead to rejections of non-compliant shipments (Chen, Ying, & Deng, 2019; Okunola, Dennis, & Beghin, 2025; Sadighara et al., 2023). With the continuous tightening of regulatory requirements and the expansion of monitoring scope, current developments in drug residue analysis are increasingly driven by the need to simultaneously achieve high analytical sensitivity and accuracy, broad analytical coverage across diverse compound classes, robustness against complex matrices, and acceptable sample throughput within realistic cost and resource constraints.

The core challenges in veterinary drug residue analysis in animal-derived foods are twofold: first, the extreme complexity of food matrices; second, the wide diversity of target physicochemical properties. Components such as lipids, proteins, carbohydrates and metal ions in food matrices induce significant matrix effects, directly suppressing or amplifying analyte signals, thereby compromising determination accuracy and sensitivity (Raposo & Barceló, 2021). Contributing factors include matrix interference with the entire extraction system, which alters compound distribution patterns and mass transfer capabilities, and cause encapsulation or binding of targets by proteins and fats, thereby hindering extraction. Additionally, matrix components also affect the sensitivity and accuracy of determination instruments (Fagerberg, Zarmpi, Jabbar, & Fotaki, 2021; Thomas, 2007; Wang, Lin, Sui, & Cao, 2013). Consequently, sample preparation has emerged as a critically influential step in trace veterinary drug residue analysis. Furthermore, the development of high-throughput screening pretreatment technologies to address both target compounds and unknown contaminants with diverse properties is another critical direction for future advancements. Addressing these two intertwined challenges requires systematic quantification of the physicochemical properties that govern analyte behavior. Among these, polarity is arguably the most critical yet the most variable parameter across veterinary drug classes, as it governs solubility, partitioning, and matrix interactions.

Since its introduction by Hansch in 1964, the octanol-water partition coefficient (logP) has been widely used as an indicator for predicting drug distribution in the body (Fujita, Iwasa, & Hansch, 1964). However, the polarity range of veterinary drug residues is extremely broad, spanning from highly hydrophilic antibiotics (e.g., neomycin, LogP = −9.0) to highly hydrophobic hormones (e.g., diethylstilbestrol, LogP =5.93), with a polarity difference exceeding 10 Log units between target compounds (NCBI, 2026). The broad polarity range interacts with the multi-phase nature of food matrices, forming a complex system that requires dynamic polarity regulation to achieve simultaneous extraction and purification of target compounds. However, food matrices have unique characteristics, such as high ionic strength, which can interfere with the polarity of polar drugs; excessive lipids may encapsulate and dissolve some drugs; and certain hydroxy compounds may cause polarity drift in drugs (Fagerberg et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2021). Therefore, the fundamental challenge in veterinary drug residue analysis can be summarized as the irreconcilable tension between the extremely broad and fixed physicochemical properties of target analytes (particularly their polarity) and the dynamic, heterogeneous interference environment of complex food matrices (Liang et al., 2024, Raposo and Barceló, 2021). Current mainstream sample preparation methods predominantly attempt to address this conflict with a static, compromised set of conditions, inevitably forcing a trade-off among recovery, selectivity, and throughput (Kaufmann, Butcher, Maden, Walker, & Widmer, 2022; Veiga-del-Baño, Andreo-Martínez, Pérez-Lucas, & Navarro, 2024). To resolve this contradiction systematically, a new paradigm is required—one capable of dynamically and intelligently modulating the microenvironment that interacts with the analytes.

This leads to the central thesis of this review: that establishing a “Dynamic Polarity Window” (DPW) strategy enables a paradigm shift from passive compromise to active design. This paper systematically defines this concept as: a strategy employed during sequential stages of sample pretreatment (such as extraction, purification, and concentration) that actively and temporally modulates the comprehensive polarity of the microenvironment (including dielectric constant, hydrogen bonding capacity, ionic strength, and pH). This enables target analytes spanning a wide polarity range to sequentially enter the “mobile phase” along a predetermined pathway, while selectively trapping key matrix interferents within the “immobile phase.” Its core objective is to transition from passive, compromise-driven universal extraction to active, on-demand programmable separation.

In summary, guided by this DPW framework, this review targets the analysis of drug and metabolite residues with a wide polarity range in animal-derived foods. It critically summarizes preparation technologies and methods centered on “polarity differences,” and explores how to improve extraction efficiency and detection sensitivity through rational strategies such as dynamic polarity modulation. Furthermore, this review synthesizes recent advancements in green solvents and functional materials within this paradigm. Finally, future research directions are proposed. The overarching aim is to provide a systematic perspective for designing next-generation sample preparation strategies capable of actively addressing the increasingly complex analytical demands and evolving regulatory challenges in food drug residue analysis.

Bridging the gap between polarity and complexity: the fundamental challenges of multiresidue analysis

Challenges in high-throughput extraction

High-throughput analysis of veterinary drug residues places stringent demands on sample preparation protocols, requiring analytical robustness to be maintained alongside efficient processing of large sample sets. This dual requirement inherently creates tension between methodological rigor and practical throughput, which becomes increasingly pronounced as analytical scope and sample numbers expand.

Fundamental conflict between “multi-residue” goals and matrix complexity

The fundamental requirement to simultaneously extract multiple drug classes encounters significant obstacles due to extensive polarity variations (logP < −2 to >5) (Halwatura & Aga, 2023). No single solvent system adequately accommodates this breadth, inevitably compromising recovery rates for compounds at both polarity extremes, particularly for polar analytes, which often exhibit low or very low recoveries (Alshammari, Almulgabsagher, Ryder, & Abbott, 2021; Kaufmann et al., 2022). This polarity-mediated selectivity loss directly influences subsequent extraction efficiency and analytical accuracy. More critically, the challenge posed by target compound polarity variations is amplified in complex matrices. Significant differences in matrix composition across various foods (e.g., meat, eggs, fish) cause substantial fluctuations in standardized methods during practical application. The varying co-extraction levels of interferents like fats and pigments lead to unpredictable matrix effects (Raposo and Barceló, 2021), forcing developers to make necessary trade-offs between method universality and data reliability (Chen et al., 2021; Williams, Olomukoro, Emmons, Godage, & Gionfriddo, 2023).

Efficiency-purification trade-off in automated workflows

Matrix complexity directly translates into obstacles for automated implementation. Practical issues like particle clogging and viscosity changes disrupt the continuity of automated workflows (Pan, Zhang, Zhang, & Li, 2014). Essential purification steps—such as multiple phase separations and solvent switching—prove difficult to execute flawlessly on standardized platforms, creating efficiency bottlenecks. These technical limitations, in turn, constrain the achievement of high-throughput goals. Automation bottlenecks further intensify the conflict between purification requirements and processing speed. While simple “dilute-and-inject” approaches are fast, they reduce sensitivity; thorough purification significantly increases time costs (Perestrelo et al., 2019). Balancing these core parameters directly impacts final data accuracy and throughput, becoming a critical decision point in method optimization.

Quality control and data Management in Scaled Processing

As throughput increases, quality control challenges scale disproportionately. Minor variations in solvent batches, consumables, or operational parameters can propagate across large sample sets, undermining batch-to-batch reproducibility (Santana-Mayor, Rodríguez-Ramos, Herrera-Herrera, Socas-Rodríguez, & Rodríguez-Delgado, 2023). Effective high-throughput analysis therefore requires integrated quality control strategies spanning the entire workflow.

Simultaneously, large-scale processing generates substantial data volumes, complicating data handling, traceability, and regulatory compliance. Rapid identification of outliers, source tracking of anomalies, and maintenance of result integrity increasingly exceed the capacity of manual review, necessitating robust data management infrastructures that further elevate technical and economic barriers.

Food matrix characteristics and polarity-related challenges

The accurate quantification of analytes in complex matrices presents substantial challenges due to multifaceted matrix effects that substantially compromise analytical reliability. These effects originate from the system's inherent capacity to dynamically reorganize the extraction microenvironment and the complex competitive interactions between matrix constituents and target analytes, leading to frequently unpredictable deviations from theoretical models. Matrix complexity manifests through intrinsic heterogeneous composition, extensive polarity distribution, and persistent structural heterogeneity that collectively impede efficient extraction (Jangid et al., 2025). Despite advances in analytical methodologies, these fundamental matrix attributes continue to pose significant obstacles to accurate quantification (Williams et al., 2023). Fundamental limitations in microenvironmental control

Current extraction strategies exhibit limited control over the dynamically evolving physicochemical microenvironment within complex matrices. Endogenous electrolytes alter ionic strength in unpredictable ways, simultaneously facilitating nonpolar analyte transfer while destabilizing ionizable species through electrostatic shielding (Li, Zhuang, Lu, An, & Wang, 2021). Native buffering systems frequently counteract external pH adjustments, undermining polarity-based separation strategies (Mennah-Govela, 2020). In addition, hydrophilic macromolecules increase viscosity through hydration effects, thereby creating diffusion barriers that restrict mass transfer (Fig. 1A) (Hirose & Sugano, 2024). Amphiphilic matrix components further complicate extraction by partitioning into organic phases and modifying solvent polarity in situ, thereby reducing reproducibility.

Persistent challenges in matrix interference management

Lipid-dominated systems present particularly intractable challenges due to their strong affinity for hydrophobic analytes (logP>3), resulting in persistent co-solubilization that substantially compromises extraction specificity. Although advanced materials such as covalent organic frameworks, metal–organic frameworks, and DESs show theoretical promise for lipid management (Fig. 1B), their practical application is often limited by cost, reproducibility, and restricted analyte coverage (Chen et al., 2021; Li et al., 2025). Macromolecule-rich environments introduce additional interference through combined hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and steric encapsulation. Approaches such as isoelectric point adjustment and enzymatic treatment offer partial mitigation (Fig. 1C) but frequently introduce secondary challenges, including analyte degradation and added cleanup steps (Chiu et al., 2021; Sun, Zhang, Chen, & Cao, 2024). Furthermore, in sugar-rich matrices such as honey, sulfonamide antibiotics can form stable covalent conjugates with reducing sugars (Fig. 1D). These bound residues evade conventional methods that target only the free parent compounds, necessitating specific hydrolysis steps for accurate quantification and representing a distinct, chemistry-driven interference mechanism (Zhang et al., 2021). Structurally heterogeneous solids present perhaps the most fundamental challenges through their complex spatial compartmentalization that defies conventional extraction strategies. Current integrated approaches combining mechanical disruption with advanced extraction platforms show theoretical promise but practical limitations in reproducibility, scalability, and applicability to real-world samples (Anandharamakrishnan et al., 2023).

Collectively, these challenges indicate that empirical optimization alone is insufficient to reliably address the combined effects of complex food matrices and diverse analyte properties. While existing polarity-modulation extraction protocols have demonstrated practical utility, their performance often reflects a compromise between theoretical selectivity and real-world robustness, leaving persistent gaps between conceptual potential and routine implementation. These interconnected limitations, conceptually summarized in Fig. 2, underscore the need for more deliberate and systematic control of the polarity microenvironment during sample preparation. Applicability of polarity modulation in extremely challenging

For highly challenging matrices such as honey (high sugar content, strong buffering capacity) or egg (high protein content, high viscosity), exogenous polarity modulators may exhibit limited penetration or rapid neutralization. However, the DPW strategy remains applicable when matrix preconditioning is introduced prior to polarity modulation, as such pretreatment facilitates the action of polarity modulators and releases analytes from encapsulation or conjugation, thereby enabling the subsequent polarity-driven extraction to proceed effectively. For honey, acid hydrolysis or acidified extraction has been shown to release sugar-bound residues and overcome matrix buffering effects, enabling effective polarity-driven extraction (Yang, Lin, Liu, & Lin, 2022; Zhang et al., 2021). For egg, protein precipitation or hydrolysis effectively disrupts protein networks and releases encapsulated analytes, thereby facilitating subsequent polarity modulation (Wang et al., 2025). These preconditioning steps have been experimentally validated in routine multi-residue workflows, achieving satisfactory recoveries for previously problematic analytes. Thus, for such extremely challenging matrices, the DPW is not a theoretical construct but a sequential workflow that first releases target analytes and reduces matrix interference through conventional pretreatment steps (e.g., dilution, hydrolysis, or precipitation) before applying the polarity modulation strategies discussed in subsequent sections.

Current sample preparation techniques and their limitations

Analytical workflows in food drug residue analysis typically comprise four key stages: sample pretreatment, extraction and purification, separation, and determination (Fig. 3). Among these, sample preparation—encompassing analyte isolation, purification, and concentration from complex matrices—plays a decisive role in analytical reliability while simultaneously representing the largest source of uncertainty and labor. It has been estimated that sample preparation accounts for approximately 60% of total analytical time and up to 50% of overall analytical error (Agency, 2015; Cámara, 2005). To address the combined challenges posed by complex food matrices and diverse physicochemical properties of analytes, a range of sample preparation strategies has been developed. Liquid-liquid extraction (LLE)

LLE is a classical technique that separates compounds by distributing them between two immiscible solvents. This process relies on the “like dissolves like” principle, where polarity determines the affinity between solvent and analyte. The choice of organic solvent—such as nonpolar hexane or moderately polar ethyl acetate—directly defines the polarity window for extracting analytes (Jiang, Yang, Tian, & Sun, 2023). LLE is effective for nonpolar compounds but less efficient for hydrophilic analytes, which exhibit poor partitioning in conventional organic solvents (Drouin, Rudaz, & Schappler, 2018; Piovesana et al., 2024).

Salt-assisted liquid–liquid extraction (SALLE) partially extends the applicability of LLE by inducing a salting-out effect, whereby increased ionic strength reduces aqueous-phase polarity and weakens analyte solvation, promoting transfer into the organic phase (Hammad, Abdallah, Bedair, & Mansour, 2022; Pasupuleti, Gurrani, Tsai, & Ponnusamy, 2022; Sun et al., 2024). Acetonitrile is particularly well suited to SALLE owing to its broad polarity coverage and its ability to precipitate proteins, thereby providing preliminary cleanup (Valente & Rodrigues, 2015). However, drawbacks include high solvent consumption, limited lipid removal, and automation challenges (Bustamante-Rangel et al., 2024). Viewed through the lens of DPW, the limitations of traditional LLE stem from its static, single-stage polarity configuration. It relies on fixed solvent pairs, providing a singular polarity environment for all analytes that cannot be adjusted in real time. This “one-size-fits-all” approach fails to address the dynamic requirements for differentiated dissolution environments among analytes of varying polarity within complex matrices, resulting in poor recovery rates for polar extremes.

Solid-phase extraction (SPE)

SPE is a widely applied technique for analyte enrichment and matrix cleanup that exploits selective adsorption on solid sorbents (Badawy, El-Nouby, Kimani, Lim, & Rabea, 2022). Compared with LLE, SPE generally offers improved recoveries, reduced solvent consumption, and enhanced purification efficiency (Jalili et al., 2020). Common sorbents—including C18, silica, and alumina—operate through reversed-phase, normal-phase, ion-exchange, or mixed-mode mechanisms, depending on their surface functionalities. These adsorption processes can be reinterpreted as polarity- and interaction-driven selectivity control, whereby analyte retention reflects the balance of hydrophobic, electrostatic, and specific intermolecular interactions.

Despite its versatility, SPE performance is highly sensitive to sorbent selection and elution conditions; inappropriate choices can lead to poor recoveries or irreversible analyte loss. Moreover, the relatively high cost of functionalized sorbents and lower throughput compared with dispersive formats constrain large-scale applications. Variants such as magnetic SPE (MSPE) and matrix solid-phase dispersion (MSPD) address specific limitations by facilitating rapid separation or direct treatment of heterogeneous samples, respectively, but their use remains application-dependent (Jiang et al., 2019, Song et al., 2022, Wang et al., 2022). Although SPE offers greater flexibility through different adsorbents, its operation typically remains a discrete, stepwise static process. Once the adsorbent and eluent are selected, their polarity window is fixed for a single operation. It lacks the ability to achieve continuous, gradient changes in polarity within a single step, making it difficult to simultaneously and efficiently recover and deeply purify compounds across a wide polarity range on a single column.

QuEChERS

QuEChERS (Quick, Easy, Cheap, Effective, Robust, and Safe) is a widely used sample preparation method that was initially developed for the analysis of pesticide residues in fruits and vegetables (Anastassiades, Lehotay, Štajnbaher, & Schenck, 2003). Subsequently, this method has been widely adopted by scientists. The method ingeniously combines the polarity principles of SALLE and d-SPE, and involves extraction using acetonitrile or other organic solvents, followed by the addition of salts such as magnesium sulfate (MgSO₄) and sodium chloride (NaCl) to promote phase separation and remove water. The extracted analytes are then purified using dispersive solid-phase extraction (d-SPE) with adsorbents such as C18, polyamide (PSA), and graphitized carbon black (GCB) (Veiga-del-Baño et al., 2024). Owing to its simplicity, speed, low cost, and compatibility with high-throughput workflows, QuEChERS has become a cornerstone technique in routine multiclass veterinary drug residue analysis (Brandi, Siragusa, Robotti, Marengo, & Cecconi, 2024). Although originally developed for pesticide residue analysis and widely applied across food, environmental, and biological fields, QuEChERS has since been extensively adapted for veterinary drug residue analysis in animal-derived foods. It should be emphasized that modern QuEChERS-based mega-methods have enabled broad analytical scope, enabling the simultaneous determination of hundreds of veterinary drugs spanning wide polarity ranges (Lehotay, 2022; Zhao et al., 2021). It should also be acknowledged that through modifications such as pH adjustment, buffer addition, or polar co-solvents, QuEChERS has been successfully adapted for a range of polar veterinary drugs, often with recoveries comparable to those of dedicated methods (Desmarchelier et al., 2018; Guo et al., 2024). However, this broad applicability is inherently achieved through compromise, and such methods often provide only “just-sufficient” sample preparation conditions rather than truly optimal extraction and cleanup environments for all analyte classes, particularly at the extremes of polarity. In practical applications, quantitative reliability is often ensured through isotope-labeled internal standards and/or matrix-matched calibration to manage matrix-induced signal suppression or enhancement, especially in complex animal-derived matrices (Raposo & Barceló, 2021; Williams et al., 2023). In this sense, many current multiclass mass spectrometry-based methods primarily achieve acceptable analytical performance through calibration-based compensation strategies, rather than through intrinsically optimized extraction and cleanup selectivity.

Consequently, despite its proven versatility and successful adaptations, classical QuEChERS procedures may not always meet increasingly stringent analytical requirements, especially for highly polar, strongly matrix-bound, or extremely hydrophobic compounds (Su et al., 2024). Polarity bias during acetonitrile extraction can reduce recoveries for highly polar or strongly hydrophobic compounds, while non-specific adsorption during d-SPE may result in irreversible analyte losses. Furthermore, the limited capacity of conventional sorbents can be overwhelmed by highly complex matrices, leading to incomplete cleanup. To mitigate these issues, advanced sorbents such as functional polymers, multi-walled carbon nanotubes, and metal–organic frameworks have been explored to enhance QuEChERS performance (Musarurwa, Chimuka, Pakade, & Tavengwa, 2019; Zhou et al., 2024). Deep eutectic solvents (DESs)

DESs are a relatively new class of solvents that have attracted attention in sample preparation due to their unique properties. DESs are eutectic solvents composed of mixtures of two or more components (hydrogen bond donors and acceptors) that form a mixed liquid at a certain temperature. Hydrogen bond donors (HBDs) are typically quaternary ammonium salts, tetraalkylammonium salts, or phosphonium salts, while hydrogen bond acceptors (HBAs) are typically acids, alcohols, amines, or carbohydrates (Tang, An, & Row, 2021). Their properties are similar to those of ionic liquids (ILs), but compared to traditional ILs, they have several advantages, including simple synthesis and easy preparation, low cost, low toxicity, and environmental friendliness (Quintana, Sztapka, Ebinuma, & Agatemor, 2022). Although this early application focused on water samples, the underlying polarity-tunable microextraction concept has since inspired analogous strategies for veterinary drug residues in complex food matrices. DESs can be tailored to have specific polarity, making them suitable for extracting various compounds from food matrices (Omar & Sadeghi, 2022; Wang & Liu, 2025). Wang and Liu (Wang et al., 2025) further summarized the application of DESs in food contaminant detection, highlighting their ability to selectively interact with various target analytes through hydrogen bond networks, enabling effective extraction and matrix cleanup. For example, a magnetic hydrophobic deep eutectic solvent (Fe₃O₄@HDES) has been developed for the simultaneous extraction of sulfonamide and estrogen residues from milk, enhancing extraction selectivity and reusability in complex milk matrices (Hashim et al., 2025). For tetracycline determination in chicken meat, a three-component hydrophilic DES composed of choline chloride and glycolic acid (1:1 M ratio) with 20% water was identified as the optimal extraction system, enabling effective separation of tetracyclines from protein-rich meat matrices (Kurashov et al., 2025). More recently, a 96-well electromembrane extraction platform using a DES composed of 6-methylcoumarin and thymol (1:1, w/w) as the supported liquid membrane enabled the simultaneous determination of 22 veterinary drug residues in honey, milk, and eggs when coupled with LC-MS/MS, demonstrating the scalability of DES-based approaches for high-throughput multi-residue screening (He et al., 2026).

Other extraction methods

Beyond conventional partitioning techniques and adsorption strategies, a series of techniques utilizing physical fields or specialized operating modes offer critical complements for tackling complex food matrices. These physical field-assisted extraction technologies primarily optimize mass transfer processes through energy input. For example, Supercritical Fluid Extraction (SFE) leverages its adjustable polarity to achieve controlled extraction across a range from nonpolar to moderately polar compounds. Pressurized Liquid Extraction (PLE) and Accelerated Solvent Extraction (ASE) enhance solvent penetration under elevated temperature and pressure, weakening matrix interactions and enabling more efficient extraction. Meanwhile, Microwave-Assisted Extraction (MAE) and Ultrasonic-Assisted Extraction (UAE) accelerate the release and diffusion of target compounds through selective heating of polar molecules and cavitation effects, respectively (Pan et al., 2014; Song et al., 2022; Yousefi, Rahimi-Nasrabadi, Mirsadeghi, & Pourmortazavi, 2021). While these methods can improve extraction efficiency and reduce processing time, their widespread adoption is limited by high equipment costs, complex parameter optimization, and restricted versatility. Consequently, they are primarily employed in specialized or research-oriented applications rather than routine regulatory analysis.

In summary, the mainstream sample preparation techniques discussed herein—from classical LLE and SPE to integrated QuEChERS—fundamentally operate on a design philosophy of static compromise when confronting the challenge of multi-residue analysis across a wide polarity range. Whether through fixed solvents, single-mode adsorption mechanisms, or generic partitioning-purification workflows, these methods collectively establish a static “polarity operation window” that seeks to cover the majority of target compounds. This strategy represents a rational and practical engineering solution that balances throughput, cost, and general applicability, rendering these techniques indispensable as high-throughput screening tools. However, this static compromise is achieved at the inherent expense of optimal extraction efficiency and cleanup selectivity for analytes at polarity extremes. Consequently, to achieve truly precise and comprehensive analysis of veterinary drug residues in complex matrices, it is imperative to transcend this paradigm of static compromise. The path forward necessitates a shift toward novel strategies capable of dynamic and precise modulation of the polarity microenvironment during sample preparation.

Theoretical basis and evaluation criteria

Rather than replacing established multiclass analytical workflows, targeted polarity modulation is proposed as a rational framework to shift part of the analytical burden from downstream correction to upstream, mechanism-driven control of analyte–matrix interactions during sample preparation. In many contemporary mass spectrometry–based multi-residue methods, acceptable performance is often achieved through calibration-based compensation strategies, such as isotope-labeled internal standards or matrix-matched calibration (Raposo & Barceló, 2021; Williams et al., 2023). While effective and frequently indispensable, these approaches primarily correct matrix-induced variability at the quantification stage rather than addressing its physicochemical origins during extraction and cleanup (Nasiri et al., 2021).

From an analytical perspective, targeted polarity modulation aims to enhance intrinsic extraction efficiency and selectivity by regulating solvent polarity, ionization state, and intermolecular interactions governing analyte partitioning in complex food systems (Williams et al., 2023). By establishing a controllable “polarity window” aligned with the physicochemical properties of veterinary drug residues, this framework seeks to reduce non-specific co-extraction and irreversible analyte loss, thereby decreasing reliance on post-extraction corrective measures. Importantly, it is intended as a complementary optimization layer that can be readily integrated into existing sample preparation methodologies to improve robustness, recovery, and matrix tolerance at the source. It should be recognized that existing high-throughput sample preparation methods, such as QuEChERS, can perform satisfactorily under appropriate conditions — their success largely depends on the specific analytes, the nature of the food matrix, the practical circumstances, and the required data quality (e.g., detection limits). The purpose of introducing the DPW framework is not to replace these established techniques but to provide a conceptual foundation for theoretical-driven method development. It is envisioned that DPW can be integrated with current methods in a complementary manner, enabling researchers to design sample preparation strategies based on rational polarity modulation rather than solely on empirical optimization. Future studies are encouraged to build upon this framework to develop practical protocols that work in synergy with existing approaches.

Fundamental theory of related parameters

In the preparation process of drug determination, the polarity of drugs and their interaction with solvents are key factors affecting extraction efficiency and separation effectiveness. To accurately predict and optimize these processes, researchers must consider multiple theoretical indicators that provide a deeper understanding of the physicochemical properties of drug molecules, thereby enabling the design of more effective experimental protocols.

Polarity refers to the distribution of electrical charge within a molecule, which determines its ability to engage in electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and dipole–dipole forces. It is a key determinant of a molecule's solubility and its partitioning behavior between phases. In analytical chemistry, polarity is often quantified indirectly through parameters such as the octanol–water partition coefficient. The logP serves as the core descriptor of a compound's hydrophobicity, defined as the logarithmic value of the equilibrium concentration ratio of a molecule between the octanol and water phases. It is a crucial indicator for assessing a molecule's hydrophobicity. LogP can be determined experimentally or predicted computationally using various software packages, which calculate the sum of contributions from hydrophobic groups (such as alkyl chains, aromatic rings) and hydrophilic groups (such as hydroxyl groups, carboxyl groups) in the molecule using the molecular fragment contribution method. Popular methods include CLOGP, ACD/LogP, ALOGP, KOWWIN, KLOGP, XLOGP3, and some deep neural network algorithms (Cheng et al., 2007; Kenney, Paffenroth, Timko, & Teixeira, 2023). For ionizable compounds, the distribution coefficient (logD) is more relevant. It represents the apparent partition coefficient under specific pH conditions, considering the ionized state of the compound (Chemaxon, 2025). It is particularly important for predicting the behavior of ionizable drugs in different media. In practical sample preparation, logP and logD values largely determine the partitioning preference of veterinary drugs between aqueous and organic phases, thereby directly influencing extraction efficiency and co-extraction of matrix components. The calculation of partition coefficient P and distribution coefficient D follows the following formulas (Poole & Poole, 2003):(1)logP=logCOCW(2)logD=logP−log1+10pKa−pHwhere CO is the concentration of the compound in the octanol phase, and CW is the concentration in the aqueous phase when the system is in equilibrium. If logP and the pKa of a compound are known, logD can be calculated at any pH. For polyprotic compounds the equations become more complicated. Details can be found in Pliška, Testa, and van de Waterbeemd (1995).

The acid dissociation constant (pKa) quantifies a compound's tendency to donate or accept protons. Brønsted–Lowry, which reflects the ability of an acid to transfer protons to water to form H3O+, which reflects the strength of the acid. Therefore, the larger the pKa, the smaller the degree of acid dissociation, that is, the weaker the acidity; the smaller the pKa, the greater the degree of acid dissociation, that is, the stronger the acidity (Kerns & Di, 2004). pKa, a fundamental physicochemical property, is a key determinant of a drug's behavior. It governs the ionization state across pH gradients, which directly influences the molecular form in solution and, consequently, critical attributes including solubility, lipophilicity, and stability. The relationship with logD is reflected in the degree of ionization of the compound. The pKa value determines the ionization state of the compound at a specific pH value, and logD reflects the effect of this ionization state on the distribution behavior. Taking acidic compounds as an example, as the pH value increases, the degree of ionization increases and the logD value decreases. LogP characterizes the partition coefficient of neutral molecules, reflecting their inherent hydrophilicity, while logD considers the combined partition coefficient of ionized and molecular states at different pH values, better approximating physiological or food environments. This perspective is widely applied in drug development, as it accounts for drug absorption across membranes in the gastrointestinal tract (Doak, Over, Giordanetto, & Kihlberg, 2014).

The counts of hydrogen bond donors and acceptors are critical indicators of molecular polarity, reflecting a molecule's ability to form hydrogen bonds with water or other polar solvents. Hydrogen bond donors are typically groups containing donor atoms (e.g., oxygen or nitrogen), such as alcohols (-OH) and amines (-NH₂), while hydrogen bond acceptors are atoms or groups that can accept hydrogen bonds, such as carbonyl groups (C=O) and ethers (—O—) (Kenny, 2022). In food testing, the number of hydrogen bond donors and acceptors can influence the solubility and extraction efficiency of compounds in polar solvents.

The dipole moment is a measure of the degree of separation between the positive and negative charge centers in a molecule, reflecting its polarity. The larger the dipole moment, the stronger the polarity of the molecule. The calculation of the dipole moment is typically based on the electronic distribution and geometric structure of the molecule. When analyzing food samples, the dipole moment is a critical reference for selecting an appropriate extraction solvent. Polar molecules (such as alcohols and amines) typically have larger dipole moments and thus exhibit higher solubility in polar solvents (such as methanol or acetonitrile). Non-polar molecules (such as hydrocarbons), on the other hand, have smaller dipole moments and are better suited for dissolution in non-polar solvents (such as hexane or dichloromethane). In sample preparation, molecular dipole moment primarily influences dipole–dipole and hydrogen-bonding interactions with solvents, sorbents, and matrix constituents, thereby contributing to non-specific adsorption and variable recoveries (Nadjamoeddin, Faridah, Andarwulan, Hariyadi, & Khotib, 2024).

The polarizability describes a molecule's ability to polarize under the influence of an external electric field, reflecting the deformability of the molecule's electron cloud. The higher the polarizability, the stronger the molecular polarity. The magnitude of the polarizability depends on the distribution of the molecular electron cloud and the molecular geometric structure. In food testing, polarizability is a critical parameter for evaluating the behavior of molecules in different solvents. Molecules with high polarizability are more likely to form stable solutions in polar solvents, while they may exhibit lower solubility in non-polar solvents (Jianfang Zhang et al., 2024). Higher molecular polarizability enhances dispersion and π–π interactions during extraction and cleanup, which can increase unintended retention on sorbents or co-extraction of hydrophobic matrix components.

Taken together, these physicochemical parameters do not act independently but jointly shape the extraction microenvironment, underscoring the need for coordinated polarity control rather than isolated parameter adjustment.

Synergistic effects in predictive applications

Building upon the fundamental parameters described in Section 4.1, their true predictive power is unlocked when considering their synergistic interactions. These parameters do not exist in isolation but form a complementary network that provides a more holistic and accurate prediction of molecular behavior in complex food matrices than any single descriptor could offer. For instance, when a molecule with a moderate to high logP value also possesses a large number of hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, its aqueous solubility may be higher than what would be predicted based solely on logP, even though the logP ostensibly indicates good extractability into organic solvent phases. In the study by Ensing et al., analysis of polyethers in water revealed that strong solvation and hydrogen-bond network compatibility with the surrounding water molecules can dominate their solubility behavior beyond simple hydrophobicity metrics (Ensing et al., 2019). Thus, in a LLE scenario, reliance solely on logP to gauge partitioning may lead to under-estimation of the fraction remaining in the aqueous phase, and consequently an over-optimistic estimate of recovery into the organic phase. Similarly, a molecule with a relatively large dipole moment suggests stronger interaction compatibility with high-polarity solvents, thereby guiding the selection of solvents with higher polarity indices for extraction. Meanwhile, a molecule characterized by high polarizability tends to present enhanced π-π stacking and van der Waals interactions with aromatic sorbents during cleanup (a factor not captured by log P or dipole moment alone) (Mabesoone, Palmans, & Meijer, 2020; Yang, Jiang, Yang, & Lin, 2018). This cross-referencing of parameters is crucial for avoiding misinterpretations and for building robust predictive models that account for the multifaceted nature of solute-solvent and solute-matrix interactions.

This integrated theoretical framework directly enables the rational design of sample preparation, which manifests in two distinct yet complementary strategic phases: the efficient extraction of the target and its subsequent purification from matrix interferents.

Strategies for high-efficiency extraction of target Analytes

The objective in the extraction phase is to engineer the chemical environment to maximize the transfer of the target analyte from the sample matrix into the extraction phase. The most critical tool in this endeavor is the logD-pH profile, which dynamically visualizes the interplay between ionization and lipophilicity. By consulting this profile, the optimal extraction pH can be identified so that the target analyte predominately exists in its neutral, hydrophobic form, thereby maximising its logD. For example, basic compounds (e.g., quinolones) require an acidic environment (pH < pKa) to suppress ionization, whereas acidic compounds (e.g., penicillins) are best extracted under neutral or alkaline conditions (pH > pKa). Complementing pH control, the principle of solvent polarity-matching is employed. This involves systematically aligning the target's dipole moment and hydrogen-bonding capacity with the polarity index and Hansen solubility parameters of candidate extraction solvents—drawing on modern solute–solvent interaction theory, which emphasises that solvent–solute compatibility (including polarity, H-bond donor/acceptor interactions, and polarizability) is critical for efficient extraction (Katritzky et al., 2004; Novaes, de Faria, Ferraz, & de Aquino Neto, 2023). Furthermore, the salting-out effect can be synergistically employed: by increasing the ionic strength of the aqueous phase, its effective polarity is reduced, thus pushing the partitioning equilibrium toward the organic phase and enhancing extraction efficiency (Inês Maria Valente, Gonçalves, & Rodrigues, 2013).

Strategies for selective clean-up and matrix separation

Upon successful extraction, the strategic focus shifts from attraction to selectivity. The goal of the clean-up stage is to exploit polarity differences to separate the target from co-extracted interferents. The “polarity switching” strategy in SPE is a powerful technique. Here, the pH of the sample loading solution is precisely adjusted to force target analytes into a fully ionized/non-ionized state, thereby freely controlling the passage and retention of analytes and interferents (Badawy et al., 2022). Alternatively, strategies can leverage the intrinsic polarity of interferents themselves. Solvent-based back-extraction is a classic example, where the initial organic extract containing both the target and lipids is shaken with a strongly non-polar solvent like n-hexane. Lipids, being highly soluble in n-hexane, are partitioned into this new phase, thereby purifying the target analyte in the original solvent (Perestrelo et al., 2019).

Quantitative definition of the DPW

To render the DPW concept practically useful, its boundaries must be quantitatively definable. The window is conceptually the range of microenvironmental polarity within which all target analytes achieve acceptable recovery (>80%) while matrix interferents are excluded. This quantification framework rests on three theoretical pillars.

First, the logD-pH relationship provides the rational basis for identifying the optimal extraction pH. Since logD governs the partitioning behavior of ionizable compounds, the pH that maximizes the proportion of neutral species (logD >0) serves as the logical starting point, as neutral forms preferentially partition into organic phases (Desmarchelier et al., 2018). Second, empirical correlations established in multi-residue studies translate logD values into solvent polarity requirements: compounds with logD < −1 require highly polar solvents (water-rich systems), those with logD between −1 and 2 are compatible with moderately polar solvents (acetonitrile, methanol), and those with logD >3 demand low-polarity solvents (ethyl acetate, hexane). (A quantitative scale for solvent polarity—Snyder's P′—is introduced in Section 5.1) Third, the overall window is bounded by the polarity span required to cover the most hydrophilic and most hydrophobic analytes. Importantly, when this span exceeds approximately 4 P′ units—a threshold that signifies extreme polarity diversity—a single extraction becomes fundamentally insufficient, necessitating a sequential two-window strategy (e.g., low-polarity extraction followed by high-polarity extraction after microenvironment adjustment) (Hyde et al., 2017; Sun et al., 2024).

This quantification framework allows researchers to define polarity window boundaries before any laboratory work, replacing empirical screening with rational design. In summary, parameters such as logP and pKa transcend their role as descriptive molecular labels to become predictive and design tools. Their integration into the DPW framework enables the pre-calculation and blueprinting of the ideal microenvironment (e.g., pH, ionic strength, solvent polarity) required for analytes at each preparation stage. This shift from empirical ‘trial-and-error’ to rational design establishes a solid scientific foundation for next-generation strategies. Importantly, this targeted polarity modulation is not intended to replace established multi-residue workflows but to serve as a rational optimization layer integrated within them. It thereby enables more deliberate control of analyte–matrix interactions, enhancing selectivity and robustness in challenging analytical scenarios.

Polarity-modulation optimization strategies

Polarity-modulation optimization in sample preparation for drug residue analysis in food matrices is a strategy that involves the systematic manipulation of physicochemical conditions to exploit polarity differences, aiming to maximize analyte recovery while minimizing co-extraction of interferents. Unlike empirical methods, it relies on quantitative descriptors like logP, logD, and pKa to provide a predictive framework for design.

Building upon the rational design principles established earlier, this chapter frames sample preparation not as a collection of independent steps, but as a process of continuous polarity window modulation. Here, solvent selection, salt effects, pH control, and microenvironmental interactions are interconnected levers that collectively reshape the polarity landscape experienced by analytes. We deconstruct this DPW framework into a series of actionable and sequentially programmable modulation dimensions. These elements form a versatile toolkit that, like assembling modular building blocks, can be tailored to the analyte polarity spectrum and matrix type. By synergistically regulating these dimensions, it is feasible to construct a polarity environment sequence that evolves over the processing steps, thereby systematically addressing the core conflict between analyte diversity and matrix complexity.

Solvent system design

The solvent system serves as the cornerstone for constructing DPW. Through the dynamic variation of its polarity index, hydrogen bonding parameters, and mixing ratios, it provides the primary driving force for differentiated dissolution requirements. The concept of solvent polarity indices originated from the work of Lloyd R. Snyder in the 1970s. Based on Rohrschneider's solubility data, he derived polarity parameters (P′) for a wide range of common solvents (Snyder, 1974). The enduring utility of P′ lies in its simplicity—a single, readily accessible value for most organic solvents. Nevertheless, P′ has well-recognized limitations. As a single-number scale, it condenses multi-dimensional intermolecular interactions into one value, and fails to distinguish hydrogen-bond acidity, basicity, and dipolarity independently. To address these limitations, modern solvatochromic parameters—particularly the Kamlet–Taft scales (α, β, π*)—offer a more nuanced description of solvent polarity and intermolecular interactions (Kamlet & Taft, 1976; Marcus, 1993). However, these advanced models require extensive solute/solvent parameterization and are less practical for routine multi-residue workflows. For most method development purposes, P′ remains a practical first-tier guide due to its simplicity, broad tabulation, and extensive literature support (Guidea & Sârbu, 2020).

When there is a significant polarity gap between the analyte and matrix interferents, the use of a single solvent with appropriately matched polarity indices is sufficient. For example, acetonitrile (polarity index 5.8) effectively extracts moderately polar antibiotics while excluding most non-polar lipid content due to immiscibility (Perestrelo et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2025). For analytes with moderate polarity (e.g., amphiphilic drugs with logP values between 1.5 and 3.0), mixed solvent systems enhance extraction efficiency by adjusting the ratio of polar and non-polar components. To ensure optimal extraction efficiency for analytes of diverse polarity in high-throughput analysis, mixed-solvent systems are often employed. In high-throughput testing, extraction solvents are typically a mixture of acetonitrile and buffered saline solutions. This mixed extraction solution offers several key advantages, including broadening the extraction polarity range, stabilizing pH, chelating metal ions, and improving extraction efficiency (Guo et al., 2024; Santana-Mayor et al., 2023). Citrate buffer and acetate buffer have been widely used by many researchers and have been used in official methods for high-throughput pesticide residue analysis (AOAC Official Method 2007.01 and CEN Standard Method EN 15662) (Perestrelo et al., 2019). In high-throughput testing of veterinary drugs, a 20% EDTA-Mcllvaine buffer solution can improve extraction efficiency more effectively removing proteins or co-extracted components (Sun et al., 2024). Mixtures of acetonitrile with solvents such as ethyl acetate, methanol, dichloromethane, and n-hexane can also be optimized to enhance extraction efficiency of target compounds (Li et al., 2023; Perestrelo et al., 2019). The addition of acidic, basic, or zwitterionic modifiers alters solvent polarity and the ionization characteristics of analytes. Acidification (e.g., formic acid) ionizes basic compounds, increasing their solubility in polar organic solvents, while alkaline additives (e.g., ammonium hydroxide) enhance the extraction of acidic analytes (Santana-Mayor et al., 2023). Salts such as magnesium sulfate can induce a salting-out effect, effectively reducing the solubility of analytes in the aqueous phase and promoting their transfer to the organic phase, a principle widely applied in the QuEChERS method (Perestrelo et al., 2019).

With the promotion of green chemistry concepts, researchers are actively exploring green polar solvents as alternatives to traditional organic solvents. These include bio-based solvents, water-based solvents, ILs, supercritical fluids, and DESs. These solvents not only exhibit excellent solubility but also have lower environmental impact and toxicity. In food analysis, the use of green solvents can reduce environmental pollution and solvent residues, thereby enhancing the reliability of analytical results (Chemat et al., 2019; Meenu et al., 2023).

Salting-out/salt-assisted polarity adjustment

The salt effect serves as a powerful lever for dynamically regulating aqueous phase polarity by altering solution ionic strength, capable of instantaneously shifting the distribution equilibrium of hydrophilic compounds—functioning as a ‘rapid switch’ in window control (Fig. 4). In simple terms, this involves utilizing the strong interactions between salt ions and water molecules to occupy a significant portion of the solvent molecules, preventing them from fully enveloping the polar groups of the solute (Yao, Ladner, Amin, & Perrin, 2025). When examined through the lens of the Hofmeister series, this effect becomes a selective tuning tool rather than merely a physical phenomenon. In this theory, the influence of anions is greater than that of cations. Specifically, kosmotropic (structure-enhancing) ions such as SO₄2−, F−, and PO₄3− enhance salting-out effects, effectively decreasing the solubility of hydrophobic analytes and promoting their transfer into the organic phase—thus narrowing the polarity window toward less polar compounds. Conversely, chaotropic (structure-disrupting) ions such as I− and SCN− promote “salting-in” phenomena, maintaining or even enhancing the solubility of polar or ionized compounds in aqueous media, thereby broadening the window toward more hydrophilic species (Hyde et al., 2017). Therefore, in practice, by judiciously selecting the salt type according to the Hofmeister series, analysts can tailor the extraction window to favor either hydrophobic or hydrophilic drugs within the same workflow, without changing the organic solvent.Fig. 4Schematic of (A) salt-induced liquid-liquid phase separation (Li et al., 2021) and (B) the general principles and strategies for salting-out based on the Hofmeister series (Hyde et al., 2017).Fig. 4

Salting-assisted liquid–liquid extraction (SALLE) combines salting-out with homogeneous liquid–liquid extraction strategies, i.e., adding salt to water and its miscible organic solvent (such as acetonitrile) to induce phase separation while migrating the target polar compounds into the organic phase (Li et al., 2021; Jun Zhang & Xiong, 2019). In practical applications, salt selection must consider not only salting-out efficacy but also the solution's buffering capacity, protective effects on target compounds (e.g., through chelation of metal ions or inhibition of degradation), and chromatographic compatibility (including volatility and ionization inhibition effects) to ensure extraction efficiency and the reliability and accuracy of subsequent analysis (Perestrelo et al., 2019; Yao et al., 2025).

Temperature and pH polarity lever effect

Temperature and pH serve as dynamic parameters that regulate the analyte's intrinsic apparent polarity (e.g., via logD) and solvation capacity, enabling ‘in situ’ and ‘real-time’ fine-tuning of the polarity window. In food testing, precise utilization of temperature and pH can significantly enhance extraction selectivity and concentration efficiency. LogD reflects the distribution behavior of compounds—including all ionized and non-ionized forms—between aqueous and organic phases, and is pH-dependent (Chemaxon, 2025). By finely adjusting solution pH, the charge state of analytes can be altered, thereby influencing their distribution behavior in solvents. For instance, under acidic conditions, analytes bearing protonatable functional groups may become positively charged and migrate toward the aqueous phase; conversely, under neutral or alkaline conditions, they may become neutral or negatively charged and migrate toward the hydrophobic phase. pH adjustment enables selective distribution between different polarity segments. Desmarchelier et al. investigated the optimal extraction pH for 105 compound residues, yielding results consistent with logD predictions (Desmarchelier et al., 2018). Sun et al. modified the solubility of ethoxyquinoline in water and n-hexane via pH adjustment, enabling its separation from lipids (Sun et al., 2025). In food analysis, pH modulation is employed to optimize distribution of polar analytes and enhance recovery rates.

The effects of temperature on solvent polarity and analyte solubility have been extensively studied. Temperature adjustment enables selective extraction and enrichment of target analytes. Water's inherent polarity decreases with rising temperature. Its high dielectric constant (ε) under ambient conditions makes it unsuitable for extracting organic compounds. However, as temperature gradually increases, water's dielectric constant decreases, reducing its polarity and enhancing solubility for nonpolar compounds. Supercritical water extraction leverages water's low polarity at elevated temperatures to dissolve nonpolar compounds (Alghoul, Ogden, & Dorsey, 2017; Rezaei, Eikani, Nosratinia, & Bidaroni, 2023). For instance, when heated to a certain level, water's polarity significantly decreases, enabling it to dissolve nonpolar compounds like multiple pesticides. The higher the hydrophobicity of the compound, the greater the required temperature (Kronholm, Hartonen, & Riekkola, 2007). This temperature-dependent polarization effect is even more pronounced in certain solvent-water mixtures (e.g., ethanol, N, N-dimethylcyclohexylamine) (Berry, Dach, Melhorn, Yip, & Soh, 2022; Rezaei et al., 2023). ILs and DESs can also extract compounds with varying polarities through temperature regulation (Alshammari et al., 2021). By strategically adjusting temperatures in specific extraction solvents, selective extraction and enrichment of target analytes can be achieved, enhancing analytical efficiency and selectivity.

Polarity-directed extraction method based on micelle microenvironment

The micellar microenvironment formed by surfactants is intrinsically dynamic, adapting to changes in concentration and temperature. It thus provides an intelligent, self-assembling nanoreactor for polarity window regulation. The application of surfactants and the micelles they form in sample preparation has emerged as a key strategy in polar-directed methodologies in recent years. Its fundamental principle leverages the amphiphilic nature of surfactant molecules, which self-assemble into micelles upon exceeding the critical micelle concentration (CMC) in solution. This creates a microscopic extraction environment featuring both a hydrophobic core and a hydrophilic surface. Nonpolar analytes can be encapsulated within the hydrophobic core, while amphiphilic molecules distribute in the barrier layer. Polar compounds, meanwhile, adsorb onto the hydrophilic surface via electrostatic interactions, significantly altering target distribution behavior across different phases (Rosen & Kunjappu, 2012). It must be emphasized, however, that micellar extraction is not universally applicable; its adoption depends critically on compatibility with the detection method.

Compared to traditional organic solvent extraction, micellar extraction offers advantages such as tunable polarity environments, environmental sustainability, and operational simplicity. By selecting different surfactant types (e.g., non-ionic, cationic, or anionic), efficient extraction can be achieved for analytes with specific polarity or charge properties (Vakh & Koronkiewicz, 2023). Furthermore, the “microenvironment” provided by micelles exhibits concentration and enrichment effects, effectively reducing matrix interference and enhancing determination sensitivity. Additionally, many surfactants possess low toxicity and good biocompatibility, aligning with the trend toward green analysis (Esteve-Turrillas, Garrigues, & de la Guardia, 2024). Among these, cloud point extraction (CPE) achieves concentration and separation by elevating temperature to induce micellar phase separation, concentrating target analytes within the micellar phase. Micellar extraction, conversely, directly exploits the solubilizing effect of micellar solutions. In food analysis, micellar extraction demonstrates distinct advantages for complex matrices rich in proteins and lipids, reducing matrix suppression of target analytes through competitive binding or dissolution (Du et al., 2022). Furthermore, this method is often combined with techniques like Dispersive Liquid-Liquid Microextraction (DLLME) to form micelle-assisted DLLME, which improves the dispersibility of the extractant and the distribution coefficient of the target compound, thereby further increasing recovery rates and sensitivity. Consequently, surfactant- and micelle-mediated extraction not only expands the scope of polarity regulation but also offers new possibilities for green and efficient food analysis methods (Bedair & Mansour, 2024). A practical concern regarding micelle-mediated extraction is the compatibility of surfactants with LC-MS analysis, as conventional surfactants can cause ion suppression and contamination of the ion source. To address this, the micellar extract should be subjected to post-extraction cleanup prior to instrumental analysis. Common strategies include diluting the coacervate phase with a suitable solvent to reduce viscosity and surfactant concentration, performing back-extraction to transfer analytes into a surfactant-free phase, or, when feasible, solvent evaporation followed by reconstitution in an MS-compatible solvent (Vakh & Koronkiewicz, 2023). With appropriate post-treatment, micelle-mediated extraction has been successfully coupled with LC-MS/MS for the determination of polar drug residues in complex matrices. Computational simulation and intelligent method development

In polarity-directed method development, the distribution behavior of target compounds between different phases is a critical factor determining recovery rate and selectivity. Traditionally, researchers have relied heavily on extensive experimental exploration, a process that is often time-consuming, labor-intensive, and associated with high trial-and-error costs. In recent years, with the advancement of computational chemistry and predictive tools, the computational prediction of solvation parameters and partition coefficients has provided new avenues for methodological optimization. Computational chemistry software or databases can yield physicochemical properties such as logP and logD, along with parameters like solvation free energy and hydrogen bond donor/acceptor capabilities (Ulrich, Goss, & Ebert, 2021; Win, Cheong, & Hopkins, 2023). These computational tools thus enable prediction of target compound distribution behavior in LLE systems or retention characteristics on different SPE media (reverse-phase, normal-phase, ion-exchange). For instance, highly hydrophobic molecules with elevated logP values typically exhibit strong retention on reverse-phase SPE, while logD values dynamically reflect ionization states across pH changes, guiding ion-exchange mode selection. This approach not only enables rapid pre-experimental screening of suitable solvents or adsorbents but also significantly reduces unnecessary experimental iterations, transitioning method development from “experience-driven” to an efficient “theoretical prediction + experimental validation” model. Multiple studies validate this strategy's practicality: Ulrich et al. optimized logP prediction models using deep neural networks, achieving an RMSE of 0.47 logP units—surpassing traditional methods—while enhancing generalization through data augmentation techniques (Ulrich et al., 2021). Another study constructed an RT logD model by integrating retention time, pKa, and logP as pre-trained features, effectively improving logD prediction performance in pharmaceutical analysis scenarios (Wang et al., 2023). Furthermore, studies evaluating logP/logD prediction accuracy indicate that accounting for ionization states and pKa corrections enhances logD prediction reliability, particularly crucial when developing complex sample preparation strategies (Bergazin et al., 2021). Some applications further demonstrate the practical feasibility of the “prediction+validation” method in the analysis of veterinary drug residues. For ionisable compounds, pH-controlled DLLME based on pKa/logP achieved recoveries of ochratoxin A in cereals of 81.2–90.1% (Campone, Piccinelli, Celano, & Rastrelli, 2012). More directly, Sun et al. used logD to guide pH adjustment, enabling selective separation of ethoxyquin from lipid interferences in aquatic products (Sun et al., 2025). Beyond logD-based predictions, our ongoing work employs orthogonal experiments (salt, pH, acetonitrile) and XGBoost modelling for seven veterinary drugs; the interaction term acetonitrile proportion × logD emerged as the dominant feature for condition recommendation, substantially reducing empirical screening. Despite these successes, the predictive power of logP/logD models for complex food matrices warrants caution. Most models are trained on aqueous-organic partitioning data and do not account for matrix-specific interferences such as protein binding, lipid solubilization, or ion pairing, which can significantly alter analyte partitioning behavior in real food samples (Schackmuth & Kerrigan, 2023). Moreover, prediction accuracy is considerably lower for highly polar, polyprotic, or strongly ionized compounds, with mean absolute errors often exceeding 1 log unit for such species (Bergazin et al., 2021). Therefore, while computational prediction is a valuable tool for narrowing the experimental design space, it should be viewed as a guide rather than a substitute for experimental validation, particularly when dealing with previously uncharacterized matrix–analyte combinations. The most reliable strategy remains a hybrid approach: computational screening followed by targeted confirmatory experiments.

In contrast, Design of Experiments (DoE) methods emphasize revealing interactions among multiple factors through statistical tools during experimentation. For instance, optimizing conditions such as solvent ratios, pH, and salt concentrations using Response Surface Methodology (RSM) or Central Composite Design (CCD) efficiently determines optimal operating windows. Existing research has employed DoE to systematically optimize adsorbent combinations and extraction conditions, rapidly establishing preparation methods for veterinary drug residues in food matrices (Almeida et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2024). While DoE inherently relies on experimental data, its integration with computational prediction significantly enhances optimization efficiency, making the development of polarity-directed methodologies more scientific and precise. In summary, computational prediction methods for solvation parameters and partition coefficients provide theoretical support for rational preparation system design, while statistical optimization tools like DoE play a complementary role in the experimental phase. Together, they offer a green, efficient, and cost-effective technical pathway for polarity-directed strategies in complex food matrices.

From this perspective, polarity modulation is not a single adjustment step but an integrated design logic that spans multiple stages of sample preparation. By sequentially controlling the polarity window through solvent systems, ionic strength, pH conditions, and local microenvironmental interactions, sample preparation can be rationally adapted to different analytical objectives, ranging from broad surveillance to polarity-extreme or ultra-trace determinations. Importantly, this framework is not intended to replace established multiclass methodologies, but to complement them by shifting part of the analytical burden from downstream correction toward upstream, mechanism-driven control of analyte–matrix interactions. In doing so, it provides a transferable conceptual basis for more robust, selective, and interpretable sample preparation strategies in veterinary drug residue analysis.

Challenges and outlook

Toward predictive design: closing the simulation-experiment gap

A major challenge lies in the gap between predicted polarity parameters and actual experimental outcomes, as non-ideal solvent effects and protein binding in complex matrices often cause deviations from theoretical models, introducing uncertainty in method development (Aliagas, Gobbi, Lee and Sellers, 2022, Schackmuth and Kerrigan, 2023). Future progress depends on closing the loop between simulation and experiment. This requires moving beyond static descriptors like logP, toward integrating molecular dynamics simulations to model free energy changes in specific matrix-solvent microenvironments. Coupling these simulations with data-driven models refined by experimental feedback is essential to evolve from heuristic trial-and-error to genuinely predictive, rational design of extraction protocols.2.Material-driven selectivity: the need for “smart” cleanup

Material-driven selectivity: the need for “smart” cleanup

The insufficient selectivity during extraction, which amplifies matrix effects and compromises accuracy (Carriera, Di Fiore, Iannone, Maio, & Avino, 2025; Eddahby, Chahine, & Hssaini, 2025), underscores a material science challenge. The solution lies not only in new sorbents but in stimuli-responsive (“smart”) materials. The future development of polymers or hybrid frameworks—whose surface properties can be reversibly switched by triggers like pH or temperature—is critical. Such polarity-tunable sorbents would enable dynamic capture and release of analytes within a single workflow, directly translating the dynamic window paradigm into practical cleanup tools.3.Reconciling green chemistry with performance

Reconciling green chemistry with performance

The pursuit of green, sustainable practices is paramount, yet replacing traditional solvents with alternatives like ethanol or deep eutectic solvents (DESs) often compromises polarity control or universality (Ferreira & Sarraguça, 2024; Meher & Zarouri, 2025). Moreover, not all DESs are inherently green; their environmental impact depends critically on the choice of components (Hayyan et al., 2013). Therefore, the central goal must shift from simple substitution to engineering genuinely green, polarity-tunable liquid systems, e.g., DESs composed of naturally occurring, biodegradable components (Azzouz & Hayyan, 2023; Omar and Sadeghi, 2022).4.Enabling intelligent and high-throughput platforms

4.Enabling intelligent and high-throughput platforms

Enabling intelligent and high-throughput platforms

Technical bottlenecks in automation persist, as most advanced polar separation strategies remain manual, ill-suited for large-scale determination. While emerging platforms like digital microfluidics show promise, challenges in solvent compatibility and standardization remain (Jiang, Zhuang, Guo, Sohan, & Yin, 2023; Nilghaz et al., 2021). Moreover, practical issues such as precipitation, clogging, and memory effects—particularly when high salt concentrations or micellar systems are used—pose additional challenges for automated fluidic platforms. To address these physical fluidics limitations, practical measures include pulse flow to mitigate clogging (Dincau, Tang, Dressaire, & Sauret, 2022), low-salt or salt-free polarity modulation (e.g., switchable hydrophilicity solvents) (Zollo, Tassaing, Salmon, & Medina-Gonzalez, 2024), and modular chip design that isolates the polarity modulation stage (Cai et al., 2023). Furthermore, modular microfluidic platforms that integrate multiple polarity modulation mechanisms (e.g., electrowetting, on-chip phase transitions) can be developed. These improvements and measures would enable the automation of “programming” polarity sequences, making high-throughput, dynamic precision a practical reality.

Future development will thus converge on two complementary tracks: the rational design of adaptive materials and solvents guided by advanced computation, and the physical integration of these elements into intelligent, miniaturized platforms. Success in this interdisciplinary endeavor will mark the true transition from a theoretical blueprint to a practical, next-generation framework for sample preparation.

Conclusion

The analysis of multiclass veterinary drug residues in complex food matrices requires sample preparation strategies that can accommodate large polarity differences among analytes while controlling diverse matrix interferences. This review presents targeted polarity modulation as a coherent conceptual framework that links physicochemical descriptors—such as logP/logD, pKa, dipole moment, hydrogen-bonding capacity, and polarizability—with extraction and cleanup behavior. By integrating these parameters into a unified polarity window concept, the framework supports more systematic and predictable method development, offering complementary advantages to established workflows such as QuEChERS, particularly for polarity-extreme compounds and challenging matrices.

While polarity-directed strategies provide clear benefits, challenges remain in bridging theoretical predictions with real-system behavior, balancing green chemistry with analytical performance, and translating these approaches into automated, high-throughput platforms. Continued progress will depend on the development of predictive models that account for multiphase matrix interactions, the design of genuinely sustainable solvent systems, and the establishment of standardized evaluation metrics for polarity-modulation extraction.

Looking forward, the DPW framework holds promise for addressing several persistent challenges in regulatory food safety monitoring. By providing a rational basis for sequential extraction design, it could reduce the need for separate protocols for polarity-extreme compounds and facilitate rapid response to emerging contaminants. The integration with automated, programmable platforms offers a pathway toward more adaptable, efficient, and forward-looking food safety systems. Ultimately, by shifting sample preparation from empirical optimization toward mechanism-informed design, targeted polarity modulation supports the development of more selective, robust, and sustainable workflows, contributing to improved analytical reliability and enhanced food safety assurance.
