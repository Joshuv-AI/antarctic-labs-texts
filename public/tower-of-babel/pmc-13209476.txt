A Discrepancy Between Evidence and Practice: Underuse of Cemented Hemiarthroplasty for Femoral Neck Fractures and Its Association With Surgeon Training Background
Authors: Hamed Tayyebi, Paulo Castaneda, Clayton Hui, Haroon Kisana, Janyne Mallender, Sorka Deeyor, Eric Pinkston, Michael McKee, Niloofar Dehghan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Despite extensive evidence demonstrating lower complications with cemented hip hemiarthroplasty (HHA), many orthopaedic surgeons continue to use noncemented HHA implants for the management of femoral neck fractures in elderly populations. In this study, we aimed to validate these findings within our cohort and to examine whether this evidence is reflected in current surgical practice.Methods:We conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients aged older than 50 years who underwent HHA for intracapsular femoral neck fractures between 2009 and 2018 across a multicenter hospital system. Intraoperative and postoperative surgical complications were compared between cemented and noncemented implants. Surgeon data, including fellowship training and case volume, were collected to assess associations with implant selection.Results:A total of 4407 patients (mean age 81 years, mean follow-up 4106 days) were included, with 496 (11%) receiving cemented and 3911 (89%) noncemented HHA. Intraoperative and postoperative periprosthetic fractures were significantly lower in the cemented group (HR = 0.57, 95% CI, 0.32-0.82, P = 0.047; and HR = 0.39, 95% CI, 0.17-0.77, P = 0.021, respectively). Use of cemented over noncemented HHA was significantly higher among trauma surgeons (36.4%) compared with arthroplasty surgeons (4.3%, P = 0.012), and general orthopaedic surgeons or surgeons with nonrelated fellowship training (7.1%, P = 0.029).Conclusion:Consistent with previous studies, our findings reaffirm the superior clinical outcomes of cemented HHA. Nevertheless, noncemented implants remained the predominant choice within our hospital system—even among fellowship-trained surgeons. This highlights a persistent gap between evidence and practice, emphasizing the need for stronger integration of evidence-based guidelines into surgical decision making.Level of Evidence:Level III.

The proximal femur region plays a critical role in weight bearing and locomotion, making injuries in this area particularly debilitating.1,2 Femoral neck fractures are prevalent among the elderly and have a substantial effect on mobility and overall quality of life.3-5 Surgical intervention is often needed to address displaced femoral neck fractures,6,7 and HHA is a standard surgical option, which can be done using either cemented or noncemented techniques.8

The choice between cemented and noncemented HHA for treating displaced femoral neck fractures has been the subject of extensive research.9,10 Cemented HHA is attributed to lower rates of periprosthetic fractures, but it comes with risks associated with cement use, including potential respiratory compromise.9,10 Conversely, noncemented HHA has been shown to increase the rates of periprosthetic fractures and the need for revision surgeries.9,10 Multiple recent publications have compared cemented and noncemented HHA in the treatment of intracapsular hip fractures in elderly populations.9,11-14 These studies notably elucidated the advantages of cemented implants, including decreased intraoperative fractures, decreased postoperative pain, and no difference in mortality or respiratory complications.

Despite this high-level evidence, a persistent practice gap remains: A substantial proportion of surgeons continue to favor noncemented implants, particularly in the United States.15,16 Although the use of cement has become the standard of care in Europe,17 in the United States, surgeons predominantly use noncemented implants,17 despite evidence to suggest higher complications. Surgeon experience could also be regarded as another factor driving the use of noncemented HHA.18 Anecdotally, we have observed the preference for noncemented implants in our local practice, a large referral center, where we treat many patients for complications of noncemented HHA in the community, such as periprosthetic fractures and dislocation due to subsidence. This disconnects between evidence and clinical practice, which raises concerns about variability in care quality and highlights the need to better understand the drivers of implant selection.

This study has two primary objectives. First, we aim to validate prior findings by comparing clinical outcomes between cemented and noncemented HHA for displaced femoral neck fractures in our hospital system. Second, we aim to evaluate the discrepancy between the best available evidence and real-world clinical practice within our large, multicenter health system. Specifically, we examine the frequency of cemented vs. noncemented hemiarthroplasty use in geriatric hip fractures and analyze whether surgeon training and subspecialty are associated with implant choice.

Methods

Study Design

A retrospective chart review of patients within our academic and nonacademic hospital system with more than 30 sites were completed between 2009 and 2018. International Classification of Disease 9 and 10 (ICD-9 and ICD-10) codes and Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes were used to identify patients. Institutional review board approval was obtained before data collection. Inclusion criteria were age older than 50 years, sustaining an intracapsular femoral neck fracture (ICD-9820.00-820.19, ICD-10 S72.000-S72.099), availability of outcomes data ≥90 days of documented clinical follow-up or recorded death within 365 days in the health-system vital-status registry, and treatment with HHA (CPT 27236). Patients were excluded if an HHA was done for a pathologic fracture or in the setting of a revision procedure, if patients underwent a total hip arthroplasty (THA), and those with incomplete charts or surgical reports.

Data Collection

Patient charts were reviewed, and data were collected, including baseline characteristics, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, comorbidities, surgical details, complications, and information regarding the operating surgeon. The recorded complications included intraoperative and postoperative periprosthetic fractures, infection, dislocations, mortality (7-day, 30-day, 90-day, and 1-year), and revision surgery.

Intraoperative and postoperative fractures were identified according to documentation in surgical notes, radiology reports, and postoperative imaging. Intraoperative fractures included any cortical or metaphyseal disruption detected during implant insertion and recorded by the operating surgeon. Minor calcar cracks that were identified intraoperatively but did not compromise implant stability and were managed with cerclage wiring were not classified as periprosthetic fractures for the purpose of this analysis. Postoperative fractures were defined as periprosthetic fractures occurring along or adjacent to the femoral stem and confirmed on postoperative radiographs or cross-sectional imaging during follow-up.

The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was calculated for each patient based on the listed comorbidities and was used to evaluate the effect of these conditions on the patient's overall survival. The CCI score ranged from 0 to 37, with a higher score indicating a higher risk of mortality.19 Data acquisition was completed using the financial identification number (FIN) to avoid duplicate incidences by medical record number and to capture a contralateral femoral neck fracture as a separate event.

Surgeon characteristics were obtained from institutional credentialing databases. Surgeons were categorized based on their fellowship training into three groups: (1) trauma fellowship-trained, (2) joint reconstruction fellowship-trained, and (3) Other: no fellowship or fellowship training unrelated to hip surgery (e.g., hand, foot and ankle, and spine). Surgeon case volume was also documented based on the HHA procedures for femoral neck fractures performed by each surgeon during the study period (2009-2018).

Outcomes

The study had two main aims. For aim 1, the primary outcome was the rate of surgical complications in the cemented and noncemented groups, including intraoperative and postoperative periprosthetic fractures, surgical time, mortality, revision surgery, dislocation, and deep infection. Mortality was assessed in several timeframes: immediate (within 7 days of surgery), short-term (within 30 days of surgery), mid-term (within 90 days of surgery), and long-term (within 1 year of surgery).

For aim 2, the primary outcome was the association between implant selection and the surgeon's fellowship training background.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.0.0.0. Categorical data were summarized with frequencies and percentages, while quantitative data were presented as means and standard deviations. A median split approach was used to categorize the patients based on the year of surgery. Univariate analysis was done using chi-square tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for categorical data, and Student t-tests for numerical variables. Multivariable analysis was conducted using binary logistic regression, incorporating variables with a P-value <0.1 from the univariate analysis. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

In total, 4,646 charts were identified with the defined search criteria and 4,407 patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria were included (Figure 1). Of these, 496 patients were treated with cemented HHA (11.3%) and 3911 with noncemented HHA (88.7%). Patients were 69% female, with a mean age of 81 years.

Effect of the Surgeon's Training

HHA procedures were done by 147 surgeons within our health system. Based on their training background, 113 surgeons (76.9%) had nonrelated fellowship training or no fellowship training, 11 surgeons (7.5%) had trauma fellowship training, and 23 surgeons (15.6%) had joint reconstruction fellowship training. The total number of surgeries done by the first, second, and third groups was 3499, 149, and 759 patients, respectively.

In total, 129 surgeons (87.8%) performed noncemented HHA more frequently than cemented procedures. Thirteen surgeons (8.8%) predominantly used cemented implants, while five surgeons (3.4%) used an equal number of cemented and noncemented implants. Use of cemented over noncemented HHA was significantly higher among trauma surgeons compared with arthroplasty surgeons (36.4% vs. 4.3%, P = 0.012), and general orthopaedic surgeons or surgeons with nonrelated fellowship training (36.4% vs. 7.1%, P = 0.029). Discussion

The results of this study, with more than 4400 patients, showed a higher rate of periprosthetic fractures in the noncemented versus cemented HHA, both intraoperatively and postoperatively. Patients treated with cemented HHA were sicker, with a higher CCI; however, they had a lower risk of periprosthetic fractures and similar mortality rates compared with noncemented HHA. The study also illustrated that in our hospital system, most of the HHAs performed were noncemented (89% vs 11%). Given the large body of evidence in support of cemented HHA in the elderly and the mean age of 81 in our cohort, this number is surprisingly high. When analyzing surgeon expertise, we found that most of the procedures (76.9%) were done by general orthopaedic surgeons or surgeons with nonrelated fellowship training, such as hand, spine, sport, foot and ankle surgeons. Among surgeons with a joint reconstruction fellowship (15.6%), noncemented HHA was chosen in 91.3% of cases. Trauma surgeons opted for cemented HHA more frequently than the other two groups (36.4%). However, trauma surgeons represented a small proportion of the total cohort, accounting for only 11 of 147 surgeons.

The outcomes of cemented versus noncemented HHA for femoral neck fractures have been extensively studied, with numerous clinical trials and meta-analyses published on the subject.10,20,21 A meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials in 20199 reported a markedly lower rate of complications with cemented implants. Compared with noncemented hemiarthroplasty, cemented implants had significantly better 12-month postoperative hip function (OR 0.52, 95% CI, 0.31-0.88; P = 0.01), substantially lower postoperative periprosthetic fracture rates (OR 0.09, 95% CI, 0.02-0.38; P = 0.001), and lower intraoperative fracture rates (OR 0.29, 95% CI, 0.13-0.68; P = 0.004). Another meta-analysis in 202322 also reported lower rate of complications and better functional outcomes with the use of cemented implants. In addition, various registry studies from across the world have also emphasized improved outcomes and lower complication rate, particularly intraoperative and postoperative fracture, in cemented versus noncemented HHA implants.15,23-26 Consistent with the previous reports, this study also demonstrated superior outcomes associated with cemented HHA.

Although the rate of postoperative periprosthetic fractures was markedly higher in the noncemented group, the overall revision surgery rate did not differ markedly between the two cohorts. This apparent discrepancy can be explained by several factors. First, not all periprosthetic fractures necessitated surgical revision—some were managed conservatively depending on fracture pattern, implant stability, and patient comorbidities. Second, surgeons may have adopted different thresholds for revision surgery based on patient frailty or institutional practice patterns.

Various registry studies from across the world have also emphasized improved outcomes in cemented versus noncemented HHA implants. A Norwegian Hip Fracture Register (NHFR) database study reported an increased risk of revision in the noncemented group.23 Moreover, they reported a 2.1 times increased risk of revision surgery with noncemented HHA with a 1-year hazard rate ratio of 2.0 (95% CI, 1.6-2.5). Also using the NHFR, Kristensen et al.24 found a higher overall risk of revision surgery for any reason after noncemented HHA, with the highest risk for revision surgery associated with periprosthetic fracture (HR 5.1; 95% CI, 3.5 to 7.5; P < 0.001). The Finnish registry data reported similar mortality but higher mechanical complications with noncemented implants (3.7% vs 2.8%, P < 0.001), as well as a higher rate of revision surgery (1.7% vs 0.95%; P < 0.001) and periprosthetic fractures (1.2% versus 0.52%; P < 0.001) during the first 90 days.25 Data from the Dutch Arthroplasty Register found no difference in mortality, but a significantly higher rate of revision (5.1% in noncemented versus 3.1% in cemented), reasons including stem loosening (P < 0.001) and periprosthetic fracture (P < 0.001).13 Similarly, the Australian Mayo Clinic Joint Replacement Database has also reported a high rate of revision surgery with noncemented implants compared with cemented counterparts.26 In the United States, a large single-system study with 12,491 patients, reported a markedly higher cumulative revision surgery rate in the noncemented group (3.0% versus 1.3%, HR: 1.7),15 primarily due to periprosthetic fractures. Consistent with the findings of previous studies, this study also demonstrated superior outcomes associated with cemented HHA.

The use of cemented HHA for femoral neck fractures has shown an increasing trend across several countries over the past decade. National registry data from Sweden, Norway, the Netherlands, Australia, and New Zealand show a clear decline in the use of noncemented hemiarthroplasty after 2012, corresponding to growing evidence favoring cemented implants.17 Sweden eliminated noncemented use entirely by 2016, while other countries showed varied rates, with the Netherlands still using it in 20% of cases by 2018.17 Despite the global shift toward cemented hemiarthroplasty, noncemented implants continue to be used with a high frequency in the United States.17 Kaiser Permanente Hip Fracture Registry reported that 48% of HHA procedures for hip fractures in the United States are noncemented implants.15 In this study, nearly 89% of HHA procedures were done using the noncemented method. The reason for such a discrepancy between evidence and practice in the United States is not clear.

There may be several reasons why many surgeons in the United States continue to prefer the noncemented HHA approach despite the literature supporting the superior safety of cemented HHA. According to a recent study by Ryan et al.,27 only 37% of residents in the United States considered their training in cement techniques to be comprehensive, and 17% felt their training was inadequate or nonexistent. Although 96% of residents felt prepared to implant a noncemented femoral implant, only 82% felt adequately trained to properly place a cemented implant. Consequently, inadequate training may be a key reason for the lower tendency to perform cemented HHA. To evaluate this hypothesis, we analyzed the correlation between the selection of cemented HHA and the surgeon's level of training. Our findings indicate that most of the procedures (76.9%) were done by surgeons who's fellowship training does not include HHA, with only 7% of these surgeons opting for cemented over noncemented HHA. This may confirm that surgeons with less training may be more inclined to choose the noncemented approach. Providing additional training could lead to a greater preference for cemented hip hemiarthroplasty in the future. However, 91.3% of joint reconstruction fellowship-trained surgeons also favored the noncemented approach over the cemented technique. Trauma surgeons demonstrated a higher preference for cemented HHA, with 36.4% selecting this approach more frequently than noncemented HHA. Nevertheless, this group represented a small proportion of the overall cohort (11 of 147 surgeons). These findings suggest that even among highly trained surgeons, clinical decisions may still be influenced by factors beyond evidence-based guidelines. One potential explanation is that evidence-based medicine has not yet been fully integrated into clinical practice, with surgeon preference continuing to outweigh research-based recommendations. If this is the case, the development and enforcement of standardized protocols may be necessary to ensure adherence to best practices and improve patient outcomes.

Another reason for noncemented implants may be to decrease the surgical time. Since femoral neck fractures commonly occur in the elderly with multiple comorbidities, surgeons may perceive a shorter surgical time with an noncemented prosthesis as preferable.21 Differences between healthcare systems and emphasis placed on operating room efficiencies may also contribute to differences across the continents.

Other factors, such as potential occupational hazards associated with cement exposure, have also been proposed as contributing to the underutilization of cemented hemiarthroplasty.28,29 These include concerns about exposure to methyl methacrylate monomer vapors, allergic or irritant reactions, and theoretical long-term respiratory or reproductive risks among operating room staff.28,29 However, the validity of these concerns has not been substantiated by empirical evidence.30 The results of our study and many others, however, do not show an increased risk of mortality with a cemented implant. Moreover, given the higher risk of intraoperative and postoperative fractures and higher rate of revision surgery demonstrated by multiple studies, the lower surgical time is outweighed by the higher risk of complications.

Some surgeons preferentially use noncemented HHA in younger, more active, or healthier patients. This study demonstrates that the ages of patients in the cemented and noncemented groups were the same. However, the cemented group had a higher CCI, indicating a greater level of comorbidity and overall health challenges compared with the noncemented group. Notably, even healthier patients who underwent noncemented hemiarthroplasty exhibited a higher risk of periprosthetic fractures, underscoring that cemented fixation provides a protective benefit not only in frail individuals but also in medically stable elderly patients—contrary to the common misconception that healthier patients are better suited for noncemented implants. Importantly, for physiologically younger or more active elderly patients, THA may represent a more suitable treatment option than hemiarthroplasty because it provides better long-term functional outcomes and lower risk of acetabular erosion.31,32 This further underscores the need for guideline-directed implant selection tailored to patient characteristics.

Other factors, such as potential occupational hazards associated with cement exposure, have also been proposed as contributing to the underutilization of cemented hemiarthroplasty.28,29 Regardless of the reasons, this study indicates that the use of cemented implants is much lower than ideal in our hospital system. In addition, our results reveal that even surgeons with an adequate training background favor an noncemented procedure. We believe there is a need to investigate the causes of this discrepancy and address them moving forward to help improve our patient outcomes. We plan to use the results of this study to develop a clinical guideline in our hospital system to help guide practice and lower complications.

This study has several limitations. As a retrospective chart review, it inherently includes biases related to patient selection, implant choice, surgical technique, and postoperative care. The study reports on outcomes in our specific geographic region and may not be representative of the entire country; however, we have included sites from busy urban centers and rural regions to ensure a heterogeneous population representative of our state. Second, there could be a bias introduced by a loss to follow-up, although this would be similar between both groups. Third, surgeon training level, the type of implant used by different surgeons, and the time to surgery could not be controlled.

Conclusion

Consistent with previous studies, our findings reaffirm the superior clinical outcomes associated with cemented hemiarthroplasty, particularly regarding reduced rates of intraoperative and postoperative periprosthetic fractures. Despite this, noncemented implants remained the predominant choice within our hospital system, including among surgeons with relevant fellowship training. The preference for noncemented implants in the United States has been well documented, and this persistent divergence from evidence-based recommendations underscores a critical gap between research and real-world practice. These results highlight the urgent need for targeted educational initiatives, institutional protocols, and system-wide efforts to promote adherence to established best practices and improve patient outcomes.
