Abstract
In this paper, we define an underlying data generating process that allows for different magnitudes of cross-sectional dependence, along with time series autocorrelation. This is achieved via high-dimensional moving average processes of infinite order (HDMA( ∞ \infty )). Our setup and investigation integrates and enhances homogenous and heterogeneous panel data estimation and testing in a unified way. To study HDMA( ∞ \infty ), we extend the Beveridge-Nelson decomposition to a high-dimensional time series setting, and derive a complete toolkit set. We exam homogeneity versus heterogeneity using Gaussian approximation, a prevalent technique for establishing uniform inference. For post-testing inference, we derive central limit theorems through Edgeworth expansions for both homogenous and heterogeneous settings. Additionally, we showcase the practical relevance of the established asymptotic theory by (1). connecting our results with the literature on grouping structure analysis, (2). examining a nonstationary panel data generating process, and (3). revisiting the common correlated effects (CCE) estimators. Finally, we verify our theoretical findings via extensive numerical studies using both simulated and real datasets. Introduction

Panel data analysis has seen its popularity in the past thirty years or so. Comprehensive reviews have been conducted at different stages, while the literature evolves. See, for example,
Arellano and Honoré (2001)
,
Petersen (2008)
,
Chudik and Pesaran (2015)
,
Hsiao (2022)
, etc. Among all challenges raised in different surveys, this article aims to offer a unified framework and a set of toolkit to

1.

simultaneously test homogeneity (i.e.,
\mathbb{H}_{0}
) vs. heterogeneity (i.e.,
\mathbb{H}_{1}
);

2.

develop valid inference under either
\mathbb{H}_{0}
or
\mathbb{H}_{1}
, and account for both weak and strong cross-sectional dependence (WCD and SCD), as well as the dependence along the time dimension.

In what follows, we review the relevant literature, point out the challenges, and then highlight our contributions.

We start with the hypothesis testing about homogeneity against heterogeneity, which has always been a central topic in empirical studies. Without loss of generality, we consider a simple setup as follows:

\displaystyle x_{it}=\mu_{i}+\epsilon_{it},

(1)

where
x_{it}
,
\mu_{i}
, and
\epsilon_{it}
are all scalars,
(i,t)\in[N]\times[T]
,
[L]\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,L\}
for any given positive integer
L
,
N
stands for the number of individuals, and
T
stands for the total number of periods.
More often than not, the key hypotheses are

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{0}:\mu_{i}=\mu\text{ for all }i;\quad\quad\mathbb{H}_{1}:\mu_{i}\neq\mu\text{ for some }i.

(2)

Sometimes, the hypotheses are even more straightforward, e.g.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{0}:\mu_{i}=0\text{ for all }i;\quad\quad\mathbb{H}_{1}:\mu_{i}\neq 0\text{ for some }i.

The model (
1
) extends the location model of
Lazarus et al. (2021)
to panel data settings. To settle (
2
), a large literature (
Pesaran and Yamagata, 2008
;
Gao et al., 2020
; and references therein) adopts the quadratic test statistics. However, as explained in
Fan et al. (2015)
, the tests based on quadratic forms often suffer from low powers, and fail to detect the sparse alternatives. Therefore,
Fan et al. (2015)
and
Yu et al. (2024)
provide power enhanced test statistics to tackle this issue.

To the best of our knowledge, the above literature largely (if not all) ignores the dependence along both dimensions. As time series autocorrelation (TSA) has been well discussed in the literature (see
Fan and Yao, 2003
;
Gao, 2007
), we justify the necessity of accounting for the cross-sectional dependence (CD) here. As surveyed by
Chudik and Pesaran (2015)
, CD is likely to be the rule rather than the exception, and it sometimes goes beyond WCD due to omitted variables as documented in
Giglio and Xiu (2021)
. It is then reasonable to call for a complete toolkit set that is robust to the presence of dependence.

To better present our motivations, we start with four datasets, of which
N
and
T
stand for the number of individuals and the number of time periods respectively.

Dataset 1
: the U.S. macroeconomic dataset assembled by

Dataset 2
: the climate data of 37 stations from the U.K. Meteorological Office.

Dataset 3
: the bank equity return data constructed by
Baron et al. (2021)
.

Dataset 4
: the realized volatility data of 16 international stock markets.

We shall provide numerical evidences demonstrating that the four datasets exemplify both WCD and SCD, highlighting the need for a unified framework to model these varying dependencies.

Four datasets offer examples of CD with different magnitude. The issue at hand is certainly a cause for concern, as inferring
\mu_{i}
of (
1
) is a cornerstone of data analysis (e.g.,
Hamilton, 1994
, Chapter 3
;
Lazarus et al., 2021
;
Cocci and Plagborg-Møller, 2024
). Without a grasp of the dependence magnitude, the existing literature offers scant guidance on how to infer the homogenous/heterogeneous means, let alone more intricate scenarios including homogenous/heterogeneous trends (
Robinson, 2012
;
Wu et al., 2023
). See Example
1
and Example
2
of Section
2
for the purpose of demonstration. To our knowledge, although
Chudik and Pesaran (2015)
formalize the definitions of WCD and SCD, only Assumption 3 of
Gonçalves (2011)
addresses this issue using a set of high-level conditions. Yet, the underlying data generating mechanism remains underexplored. In related research,
Robinson (2011)
,
Robinson and Thawornkaiwong (2012)
and
Lee and Robinson (2016)
impose a linear system structure to represent cross-sectional dependence. This approach, while convenient in theory, is infeasible in practice due to the fact that there is no natural ordering in the cross-sectional dimension. When solely WCD is of interest, Assumption C of
Bai and Ng (2002)
, which regulates dependence across cross-sections and time using various moments, is often cited. Nonetheless, the underlying data generating process remains somewhat obscure.

Considering the aforementioned points, our contributions in this paper are as follows:

1.

First, we define an underlying data generating process that allows for different magnitude of CD, along with TSA. This is achieved via high-dimensional moving average processes of infinite order (HDMA(
\infty
)), which automatically generalizes the spatial structure introduced by Robinson and his co-authors in recent years. The framework is important in the sense that as noted by
Brockwell and Davis (1991, p. 187)
and
Fan and Yao (2003, pp. 33 & 190)
, the Wold decomposition theorem ensures a formal linear representation exists for any stationary time series with no deterministic components, and HDMA(
\infty
) naturally incorporates this result into a panel data framework.

2.

To the best of our knowledge, HDMA(
\infty
) has not been carefully explored in the literature of panel data analysis. Our setup and investigation significantly integrates and enhances both homogenous and heterogeneous panel data modelling and testing (such as
Pesaran, 2006
;
Pesaran and Yamagata, 2008
;
Fan et al., 2015
;
Yu et al., 2024
). To study HDMA(
\infty
), we extend the BN decomposition (e.g.,
Beveridge and Nelson, 1981
;
Phillips and Solo, 1992
) to a high-dimensional time series setting, and derive a complete set of toolkit.

Our development offers theoretical justification for some high level assumptions of the literature (e.g.,
Gonçalves, 2011
, Assumption 3
,
Bai and Ng, 2002
, Assumption C
), and also generalizes Assumption 2 of
Pesaran (2006)
and many extensions since then. Additionally, our investigation complements the work of
Fan et al. (2015)
, who specifically study cases where
\frac{T}{\sqrt{N}}\to 0
, by considering a broader range of scenarios and relaxing the independence assumptions employed in
Pesaran and Yamagata (2008)
and
Yu et al. (2024)
.

3.

We exam homogeneity against heterogeneity using Gaussian approximation, a prevalent technique for establishing uniform inference (e.g.,
Chernozhuokov et al., 2022
, and references therein). For post-testing inference, we derive Central Limit theorems through Edgeworth expansions for both homogenous and heterogeneous settings. Notably, the demand for Gaussian approximation in panel data analysis has been increasing recently, as exemplified in Section 4 of
Su et al. (2024)
and Section 4 of
Liu et al. (2024)
. Our study also contributes to this research direction by providing a set of foundational conditions and deriving a set of useful basic results.

4.

We showcase the practical relevance of the established asymptotic theory by (1). connecting our results with the literature on grouping structure analysis such as those surveyed in
Bonhomme and Manresa (2015)
and
Su et al. (2016)
, (2). examining a nonstationary panel data generating process presented in
Phillips and Moon (1999)
, and (3). revisiting the common correlated effects (CCE) estimators of
Pesaran (2006)
. Typically, when investigating nonstationary panel data, one has to impose cross-sectional independence such as
Phillips and Moon (1999)
,
Dong et al. (2021)
and
Huang et al. (2021)
due to technical constraints. Our study offers a set of complete toolkit to account for the dependence of unit root precesses.

5.

Finally, we evaluate our theoretical findings via extensive numerical studies using both simulated and real datasets.

2.

Bootstrap statistics for both homogeneous and heterogeneous cases
. The key technique for investigating our bootstrap statistics relies on the properties of the

-dependent time series. In deriving Edgeworth expansions for the homogeneous and heterogeneous bootstrap statistics, we adopt the proof strategy of

By applying the asymptotic results for each segment (established in Lemmas

and

Firstly, we explain the necessity of accounting for WCD and SCD in a unified framework. For simplicity, suppose that

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mu\cdot\mathbf{1}_{N}+\mathbf{B}(L)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t},

(4)

where

\mathbf{B}(L)\coloneqq\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{B}_{\ell}L^{\ell}

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{\ell}=(\mathbf{b}_{\ell 1}^{\dagger},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{\ell N}^{\dagger})=(\mathbf{b}_{\ell 1}^{\sharp},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{\ell N}^{\sharp})^{\top}.

(5)

The HDMA(
\infty

CD:
\text{Cov}(x_{i1},x_{j1})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{b}_{\ell i}^{\sharp\top}\mathbf{b}_{\ell j}^{\sharp}

\text{Cov}(x_{it},x_{is})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{b}_{\ell+t-s,i}^{\sharp\top}\mathbf{b}_{\ell i}^{\sharp}

To see the difference between WCD and SCD, we provide the following examples.

Example 1
.

When
\mathbf{B}_{0}=\mathbf{I}
and
\mathbf{B}_{\ell}=\mathbf{0}
for
\ell\geq 1
, we have
\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mu\cdot\mathbf{1}_{N}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
, which gives a set of i.i.d. panel data over both dimensions. This is an extreme case of WCD, and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{NT}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(x_{it}-\mu)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{NT}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{it},

which will help us to drive the asymptotic distribution. In this case
\|\mathbf{B}_{0}\|_{2}=1<\infty
.

Example 2
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{x}_{t}
is generated as follows.

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mu\cdot\mathbf{1}_{N}+\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}&\cdots&\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}&\cdots&\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\\
\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t},

which infers
\mathbf{B}_{0}=\mathbf{1}_{N}(\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}/\sqrt{N})
and
\mathbf{B}_{\ell}=\mathbf{0}
for
\ell\geq 1
. Apparently, each time series
\{x_{it}\mid t\in[T]\}
is perfectly correlated with the others, and simple algebra gives that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N\sqrt{T}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(x_{it}-\mu)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{NT}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{it}.

Therefore, SCD requires a different normalizer to derive the asymptotic distribution, which directly affects the construction of the confidence interval in practice. Notably,
\|\mathbf{B}_{0}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{1}_{N}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{N}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top})}=\sqrt{N}
, which diverges as
N\to\infty
.

A few findings emerge in view of both examples. First, (
4

and offers detailed data generating process for
Gonçalves (2011, Assumption 3)
. Second, inferring

\mathbf{B}_{\ell}

(b)

\limsup_{N}\sqrt{\frac{N}{L_{N}}}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{1}<\infty
, where
\mathbf{B}\coloneqq\mathbf{B}(1)
.

Assumption
1
.1 is standard. See
Saulis and Statulevicius (1991, Chapter 1)
for detailed definition of cumulants. In general,
\mathbf{B}_{\ell}\neq\mathbf{0}
for
\ell\geq 1
, so we need to regulate the elements of
\mathbf{B}_{\ell}
using Assumption
1
.2, which ensures that after suitable normalization, the Spectral norms of
\mathbf{B}_{\ell}
’s are summable. Obviously, Assumption
1
.2 is satisfied for both Examples
1
and
2
. While
L_{N}\in(N,N^{2})
, the magnitude of cross-sectional dependence is in between both cases. By Lemma
A4
of the appendix, Assumption
1
entails that

\displaystyle\sigma_{x}^{2}=\lim_{N}\frac{1}{L_{N}}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{N},

which does not only incorporate long run covariance along the time dimension, but also accounts for WCD and SCD automatically.

Having these conditions in hand, we present the first main result of this paper.

Theorem 1
.

Under Assumption
1
, as
(N,T)\to(\infty,\infty)
,

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}}\left|F_{NT}(u)-\Phi(u)-\frac{\beta_{3}}{6}(1-u^{2})\phi(u)\right|=O\left(NT\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{B}\|_{1}}{\sqrt{L_{N}T}}\right)^{4}\vee\frac{1}{T^{2}}\right),

where
F_{NT}(u)\coloneqq\Pr(\widetilde{S}_{NT}\leq u)
,
\beta_{3}
is defined in (
A.12
) for the sake of presentation, and

|\beta_{3}|=O\left(NT\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{B}\|_{1}}{\sqrt{L_{N}T}}\right)^{3}\vee\frac{1}{T^{3/2}}\right).

If
\kappa_{3}=0
, then
\beta_{3}=0
.

Theorem
1
gives an Edgeworth expansion for
\widetilde{S}_{NT}
, and a considerably simplified form will be presented in Corollary
1
under a slightly more restrictive condition. The detailed definition of
\beta_{3}
is omitted here due to its cumbersome notation. It is worth mentioning that if the skewness of
\varepsilon_{it}
is 0 (i.e.,
\kappa_{3}=0
), the term
\frac{\beta_{3}}{6}(1-u^{2})\phi(u)
automatically vanishes. Then
F_{NT}(u)
converges to
\Phi(u)
in a much faster rate. In general, we do not have
\kappa_{3}=0
, so we keep the statement of Theorem
1
as it is.

It is noteworthy that for either Example
1
or Example
2
, the result reduces to

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}}\left|F_{NT}(u)-\Phi(u)-\frac{\beta_{3}}{6}(1-u^{2})\phi(u)\right|=O\left(\frac{1}{NT}\vee\frac{1}{T^{2}}\right),

where
|\beta_{3}|=O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{NT}}\vee\frac{1}{T^{3/2}})
. Sequentially, for both examples, Theorem
1
gives the following Berry-Esseen bound:

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}}\left|F_{NT}(u)-\Phi(u)\right|=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}(\sqrt{N}\wedge T)}\right),

which says that, for the panel data with TSA and WCD/SCD, the Berry-Esseen bound is usually at the order of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{NT}}
unless
N\gg T
such that
\frac{T}{\sqrt{N}}\to 0
. The presence of the term
\frac{1}{T^{3/2}}
is due to the BN decomposition which introduces a truncation residual along the time dimension. In a typical panel data setting
N\asymp T
, the truncation residual is negligible. Our result complements the work of
Fan et al. (2015)
, who specifically study cases where
\frac{T}{\sqrt{N}}\to 0
, by considering a broader range of scenarios and relaxing the independence assumptions employed in
Pesaran and Yamagata (2008)
and
Yu et al. (2024)
.

With a minor additional restriction, the following corollary holds.

Corollary 1
.

Under Assumption
1
, as
(N,T)\to(\infty,\infty)
and
\frac{N}{T^{2}}\to 0
,

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}}\left|F_{NT}(u)-\Phi(u)-\frac{\beta_{3}^{*}}{6}(1-u^{2})\phi(u)\right|=O_{P}\left(\frac{\sqrt{N}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{1}^{2}}{L_{N}T}\right),

where

\beta_{3}^{*}\coloneqq\frac{\kappa_{3}}{\sigma_{x}^{3/2}L_{N}^{3/2}T^{1/2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\mathbf{b}_{j}^{\dagger})^{3}

with
\mathbf{b}_{j}^{\dagger}
being the
j^{th}
column of
\mathbf{B}
, and
\kappa_{3}
is defined in Assumption
1
.1.

The condition
\frac{N}{T^{2}}\to 0
is rather common in the literature of panel data analysis, and is apparently fulfilled given
N\asymp T
. Using Assumption
1
.2, it is straightforward to see that
\frac{\sqrt{N}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{1}^{2}}{L_{N}T}=\frac{N\|\mathbf{B}\|_{1}^{2}}{L_{N}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}T}\to 0.

Bootstrap Inference
— Below, we provide a bootstrap procedure to conduct inference. Given the nature of panel data, an intuitive thought is to draw a set of random variables, say,
\{\zeta_{it}\mid i\in[N],t\in[T]\}
under certain restrictions, and construct the bootstrap counterpart of
S_{NT}
as follows:

\displaystyle\widetilde{S}_{NT}^{*}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sigma_{x}\sqrt{L_{N}T}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(x_{it}-\mu)\zeta_{it}.

It is then straightforward to obtain that

\displaystyle E^{*}(\widetilde{S}_{NT}^{*2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma_{x}^{2}L_{N}T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mu\cdot\mathbf{1}_{N})^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mu\cdot\mathbf{1}_{N})

(7)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{\sigma_{x}^{2}L_{N}T}\sum_{k=1}^{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T-k}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mu\cdot\mathbf{1}_{N})^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{t,t+k}(\mathbf{x}_{t+k}-\mu\cdot\mathbf{1}_{N}),

(8)

where
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t}=(\zeta_{1t},\ldots,\zeta_{Nt})^{\top}
,
\mathbf{W}_{ts}=E[\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{s}^{\top}]
for
t\neq s
, and
\mathbf{W}_{0}\coloneqq\mathbf{W}_{tt}
for simplicity. Correspondingly, we can write
\widetilde{S}_{NT}^{2}
as follows:

\displaystyle\widetilde{S}_{NT}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma_{x}^{2}L_{N}T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mu\cdot\mathbf{1}_{N})^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{N}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mu\cdot\mathbf{1}_{N})

(9)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{\sigma_{x}^{2}L_{N}T}\sum_{k=1}^{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T-k}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mu\cdot\mathbf{1}_{N})^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{N}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}_{t+k}-\mu\cdot\mathbf{1}_{N}).

(10)

Comparing the right hand sides of
E^{*}(\widetilde{S}_{NT}^{*2})
and
\widetilde{S}_{NT}^{2}
, it is obvious that we need (
7
) and (
8
) to mimic (
9
) and (
10
) respectively. As
\mathbf{1}_{N}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}
has rank one, by Lemma
A1
the most obvious form of
\mathbf{W}_{ts}
should be

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{ts}=\alpha_{ts}\mathbf{1}_{N}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top},

where
\alpha_{ts}
is a scalar varying with respect to the distance between
t
and
s
. Therefore, we conclude that, to have a valid bootstrap procedure, one should replace
\zeta_{it}
with
\zeta_{t}
to ensure there is no cross-sectional variation. The finding nicely fits our study, as we assume no prior information about the magnitude of CD. By doing so, we preserve the dependence along the individual dimension in the bootstrap draws, and also avoid imposing certain order on individuals implicitly.

Formally, the bootstrap procedure is as follows.

1.

Draw the bootstrap version of
S_{NT}
by

\displaystyle\widetilde{S}_{NT}^{*}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sigma_{x}\sqrt{L_{N}T}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(x_{it}-\mu)\zeta_{t},

where
\zeta_{t}
is an
m
-dependent time series.

2.

Repeat the above procedure
R
times to obtain the sampling distribution of
\widetilde{S}_{NT}^{*}
.

Accordingly, we impose the following assumption.

Assumption 2
.

1.

Let
a(\cdot)
be a symmetric Lipschitz continuous kernel defined on
[-1,1]
such that
a(0)=1
and
\lim_{|x|\to 0}\frac{1-a(x)}{|x|^{q_{a}}}=C_{q_{a}}
for
q_{a}\in\{1,2\}
and
0<C_{q_{a}}<\infty
. Assume
\limsup_{N}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\ell^{q_{a}}\cdot C_{N\ell}<\infty
, and
\int_{-1}^{1}a(u)\exp(-\mathsf{i}ux)\mathrm{d}u\geq 0
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

2.

Let
E[\zeta_{t}]=0
,
E[\zeta^{2}_{t}]=1
,
E|\zeta_{t}|^{4}<\infty
, and
E[\zeta_{t}\zeta_{s}]=a(\frac{t-s}{m})
for
\forall t,s\in[T]
, where
m
satisfies
m\rightarrow\infty
and
\frac{m}{\sqrt{T}}\rightarrow 0
as
T\rightarrow\infty
.

Assumption
2
is a typical assumption in the literature of dependent wild bootstrap. We refer interested readers to
Shao (2015)
for a comprehensive review of this line of research. Several conventional kernel functions satisfy such conditions. For example, for the Bartlett kernel,
q_{a}=1
and
C_{1}=1
; for the Parzen, Tukey-Hanning, QS kernels, and the trapezoidal functions,
q_{a}=2
and the values of
C_{2}
vary and all satisfy
C_{2}<\infty
. See
Andrews (1991)
for comments on different kernel functions. Notably, when the Bartlett kernel is adopted, the condition
\limsup_{N}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\ell^{q_{a}}\cdot C_{N\ell}<\infty
reduces to Assumption
1
.2.a.

Using Assumptions
1
-
2
, the following lemma and theorem hold for the bootstrap procedure.

Lemma 2
.

Under Assumptions
1
-
2
, as
(N,T)\to(\infty,\infty)
, the following results hold:

1.

E^{*}[(\widetilde{S}_{NT}^{*})^{2}]=1+o_{P}(1)
;

2.

E^{\ast}[(\sum_{t=(s-1)m+1}^{sm}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{t}\zeta_{t})^{2}]=O_{P}(mL_{N})
, for
s=1,\ldots,\lfloor\frac{T}{m}\rfloor
;

3.

E^{\ast}[(\sum_{t=\lfloor\frac{T}{m}\rfloor m+1}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{t}\zeta_{t})^{2}]=O_{P}(mL_{N})
.

Lemma
2
summarizes some basic statistical properties to facilitate the investigation on the bootstrap statistic for the homogeneous cases. With them in hand, we present the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Under Assumptions
1
-
2
, as
(N,T)\to(\infty,\infty)
,

1.

\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\text{Pr}^{*}(\widetilde{S}_{NT}^{*}\leq u)-\Phi(u)-\frac{1}{6\widetilde{\sigma}^{\ast 3}}(1-u^{2})E^{\ast}[\widetilde{S}_{NT}^{*3}]\phi(x)\right|=O_{P}\left(\frac{m}{T}\right)
;

2.

\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\text{Pr}^{*}(\widetilde{S}_{NT}^{*}\leq u)-\Pr(\widetilde{S}_{NT}\leq u)\right|=O_{P}\left(\sqrt{\frac{m}{T}}\right)
;

3.

\text{MSE}(\widetilde{\sigma}^{\ast 2})=\frac{2m}{T}\int_{-1}^{1}a^{2}(u)du+\frac{C_{q_{a}}^{2}}{\sigma_{x}^{4}m^{2q_{a}}}\Delta_{q_{a}}^{2}+o_{P}(m^{-2q_{a}})+o\left(\frac{m}{T}\right)
;

where
\widetilde{\sigma}^{\ast 2}=E^{\ast}[\widetilde{S}_{NT}^{*2}]
and
\Delta_{q_{a}}=L_{N}^{-1}\sum_{s=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}|s|^{q_{a}}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{\ell}\mathbf{B}_{\ell+|s|}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{N}
.

Theorem
2
.1 establishes the first-order Edgeworth expansion for the bootstrap statistic
\widetilde{S}_{NT}^{*}
, extending the results of
Tikhomirov (1981)
to the panel data framework. Conditional on the sample, the rates of the first two results of Theorem

are optimal, as the bootstrap draws
\{\zeta_{t}\}
are
m
-dependent time series data only. Theorem
2
.3 demonstrates that the bootstrap covariance estimator can consistently estimate the true covariance, and infers that MSE is minimized at

m_{\text{opt}}=\big(\frac{C_{q_{a}}\Delta_{q_{a}}}{2\sigma_{x}^{4}\int_{-1}^{1}a^{2}(u)du}\big)^{2/(2q_{a}+1)}T^{1/(2q_{a}+1)}.

2.2

\mathbb{H}_{1}

Under
\mathbb{H}_{1}

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbf{B}(L)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t},

We then explore the second option under (
11
) when inferring heterogeneity. Mathematically, it means that one is concerned with
\max_{i}\widetilde{p}_{i}
rather than any individual
\widetilde{p}_{i}
. This is useful, as there is an increasing literature concerning about the uniform inference in the panel data setting (e.g.,
Liu et al., 2024
;
Su et al., 2024
). The following result contributes to this line of research.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\{\mathbf{z}_{t}\mid t\in[T]\}
be independent Gaussian random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
E[\mathbf{z}_{t}]=\mathbf{0}
and
\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{z}_{t})=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^{\top}
. Let Assumption
1
.1 hold and
\max_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\ell\|\mathbf{b}_{\ell i}^{\sharp}\|_{2}<\infty
. As
(N,T)\to(\infty,\infty)
,

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\Pr\left(\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\right|_{\infty}\leq u\right)-\Pr\left(\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{z}_{t}\right|_{\infty}\leq u\right)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O\left(\left(\frac{N^{2/J}(\log N)^{5}}{T}\right)^{1/4}+\sqrt{\frac{N^{2/J}(\log N)^{3-2/J}}{T^{1-2/J}}}\right).

Theorem
5
establishes a Gaussian approximation within the panel data framework. Notably, this approximation remains valid irrespective of whether the cross-sectional dependence is WCD or SCD. Also, it is worth mentioning that under the sub-Gaussian condition of
\varepsilon_{it}
, the term
N^{2/J}
in the above theorem can be replaced by
\log N
. Thus if we impose an exponential tail assumption,
N
can even diverge at an exponential rate of
T
.

To have a practically feasible version of Theorem
5
, we need to know
\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{z}_{t})=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\eqqcolon\boldsymbol{\Omega}
. Thus, define the high-dimensional long-run covariance matrix estimator by

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t,s=1}^{T}a\left(\frac{t-s}{\widetilde{m}}\right)(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\overline{\mathbf{x}})(\mathbf{x}_{s}-\overline{\mathbf{x}})^{\top}\quad\text{with}\quad\overline{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{t},

and accordingly, define the Gaussian multiplier bootstrap approximate by

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}^{1/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{z}_{t}^{*}\right|_{\infty},

where
\{\mathbf{z}_{t}^{*}\mid t\in[T]\}
is a vector of i.i.d.
N
-dimensional Gaussian random variables with
\mathbf{z}_{t}^{*}\sim N(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{N})
.

Theorem 6
.

Let Assumption
1
.1 hold with
J>4
, and let Assumption
2
.1 hold. Suppose that (1)
\max_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\ell^{q_{\alpha}}\|\mathbf{b}_{\ell i}^{\sharp}\|_{2}<\infty
, (2)
\frac{N^{2}T\log T}{(T\widetilde{m}\log N)^{J/4}}\to 0
, and (3)
\widetilde{m}\asymp T^{1/(2q_{\alpha}+1)}
. Then

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathrm{Pr}^{*}\left(\left|\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}^{1/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{z}_{t}^{*}\right|_{\infty}\leq u\right)-\Pr\left(\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\right|_{\infty}\leq u\right)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O_{P}\left((\log N)^{5/4}(\widetilde{m}/T)^{1/4}\right)+O\left(\left(\frac{N^{2/J}(\log N)^{5}}{T}\right)^{1/4}+\sqrt{\frac{N^{2/J}(\log N)^{3-2/J}}{T^{1-2/J}}}\right).

Theorem
6
builds upon the robust inference of high-dimensional covariance matrix estimation presented in
Gao et al. (2025)
and the continuity of the maximum of a Gaussian distribution. Similar to Theorem
5
, Theorem
6
accommodates various types of CD.

The condition
\max_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\ell^{q_{\alpha}}\|\mathbf{b}_{\ell i}^{\sharp}\|_{2}<\infty
is slightly more restrictive than those required in Assumption
3
. This is not surprising, as we now need to investigate the uniform inference rather than derive CLT for any individual. Nevertheless, we only require an algebraic decay rate of the temporal dependence.
\widetilde{m}\asymp T^{1/(2q_{\alpha}+1)}
corresponds to the optimal level of bandwidth in terms of minimizing the asymptotic mean squared error of each element in
\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}
. The condition
\frac{N^{2}T\log T}{(T\widetilde{m}\log N)^{J/4}}\to 0
imposes restrictions on
(\widetilde{m},N,T)
jointly, which are easy to realize. If
\varepsilon_{it}
is sub-Gaussian,
J
can be arbitrarily large. Then the restrictions can be much simplified.

In the following subsection, we show that the above results offer the theoretical framework for the purpose of inference.

2.3
Test Statistic

According to the results established in Section
2.1
and
2.2
, we are now ready to consider an
L_{\infty}
-based test statistic:

Q_{NT}=\max_{i}\sqrt{T}(\overline{x}_{i}-\overline{x}),

where
\overline{x}_{i}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{it}
and
\overline{x}=\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{it}
. Under
\mathbb{H}_{0}
, assume
N\leq L_{N}<N^{2}
which implies that
\overline{x}-\mu=o_{P}(1/\sqrt{T})
, then simple algebra yields that

Q_{NT}=\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mu\cdot\mathbf{1}_{N})\right|_{\infty}+o_{P}(1).

Hence, we can calculate the critical value of
Q_{NT}
using Theorem
6
. In addition, our test statistic can detect a class of sparse local alternatives at the rate
T^{-1/2}
. Specifically, under the sparse local alternatives such that
\mathbb{H}_{1}:\ \mu_{i}=\mu+a_{T}\delta_{i}
with
a_{T}=1/\sqrt{T}
and
|\delta_{i}|>0
for some
i
(our
L_{\infty}
-based test is still powerful even only one individual violates the null hypothesis), we have

Q_{NT}=\max_{i}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(x_{it}-\mu)+\delta_{i}\right)+o_{P}(1),

and thus
Q_{NT}
diverges to infinity if
\sqrt{T}a_{T}\to\infty
. We now state the following proposition.

Proposition 1
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
.1 hold with
J>4
. Additionally, suppose that (1)
\max_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\ell^{q_{\alpha}}\|\mathbf{b}_{\ell i}^{\sharp}\|_{2}<\infty
, (2)
\frac{N^{2}T\log T}{(T\widetilde{m}\log N)^{J/4}}\to 0
, (3)
\widetilde{m}\asymp T^{1/(2q_{\alpha}+1)}
and (4)
\overline{x}-\mu=o_{P}(1/\sqrt{T})
. Then under the null hypothesis

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\Pr(Q_{NT}\leq u)-\mathrm{Pr}^{*}\left(\left|\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}^{1/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{z}_{t}^{*}\right|_{\infty}\leq u\right)\right|=o_{P}(1),

where
\{\mathbf{z}_{t}^{*}\mid t\in[T]\}
is a sequence of i.i.d.
N
-dimensional Gaussian random vectors with
\mathbf{z}_{t}^{*}\sim N(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{N})
.

The distributional approximation established in Proposition 1 enables us to explore the known features of the partial sum of the bootstrapped versions of Gaussian samplers for inferential purposes.

3
Extensions

In this section, we consider three extensions. We firstly connect the above investigation with the literature on grouping structure analysis such as those surveyed in
Bonhomme and Manresa (2015)
and
Su et al. (2016)
. In the second extension, we relax some restrictions imposed on the panel data unit root processes of
Phillips and Moon (1999)
. Finally, we revisit the model of
Pesaran (2006)
and the corresponding CCE estimators using the results established above.

3.1
Connection with Grouping Analysis

In this subsection, we explain how the above study can be connected with the current literature on grouping structure analysis. The numerical implementation of grouping can be easily done via
K
-mean or the agglomerative hierarchical clustering algorithm (
Hastie et al., 2009
). In what follows, we explain how the above results are connected with the grouping analysis.

Note that
\mu_{i}
can be estimated by
\overline{x}_{i}
as in Section
2.3
, so
\mu_{i}
is already known approximately. To proceed, we introduce the grouping structure, and impose the necessary conditions. Denote
J_{0}
sets of indices (i.e.,
\mathscr{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{G}_{J_{0}}
) such that

\displaystyle\cup_{j=1}^{J_{0}}\mathscr{G}_{j}=[N],\quad\mathscr{G}_{j_{1}}\cap\mathscr{G}_{j_{2}}=\emptyset\text{ for }j_{1}\neq j_{2},\quad\text{and}\quad\sharp\mathscr{G}_{j}\asymp N\text{ for }\forall j,

where
J_{0}\geq 1
is a fixed positive integer, and
\sharp\mathscr{G}_{j}
stands for the cardinality of
\mathscr{G}_{j}
.

Assumption 4
.

Suppose that

1.

\displaystyle\max_{j\in[J]}\max_{i\in\mathscr{G}_{j}}|\mu_{i}-\overline{\mu}_{j}|\leq c_{N}
with
c_{N}\to 0
, where
\overline{\mu}_{j}
’s are fixed numbers;

2.

\displaystyle\min_{j_{1}\neq j_{2}}|\overline{\mu}_{j_{1}}-\overline{\mu}_{j_{2}}|\geq c_{0}>0
, where
c_{0}
is a constant.

Typically, one assumes that
\mu_{i}\equiv\overline{\mu}_{j}
for all
i\in\mathscr{G}_{j}
, so
c_{N}\equiv 0
. Assumption
4
.1 relaxes this restriction slightly. Assumption
4
.2 requires all groups to be well partitioned. If
J_{0}
is known, we can estimate the group structure as follows:

\displaystyle(\widehat{\mathcal{G}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=\argmin_{\mathcal{G},\boldsymbol{\nu}}S(\mathcal{G},\boldsymbol{\nu}),

(12)

where
S(\mathcal{G},\boldsymbol{\nu})\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J_{0}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{j}}|\overline{x}_{i}-\nu_{j}|^{2}
,
\mathcal{G}\coloneqq(\mathcal{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{J_{0}})
,
\boldsymbol{\nu}\coloneqq(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{J_{0}})
,
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}\coloneqq(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{J_{0}})
, and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\coloneqq(\widehat{\nu}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\nu}_{J_{0}})
. Here
\mathcal{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{J_{0}}
are
J_{0}
sets of indices such that

\displaystyle\cup_{j=1}^{J}\mathcal{G}_{j}=[N],\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{G}_{j_{1}}\cap\mathcal{G}_{j_{2}}=\emptyset\text{ for }j_{1}\neq j_{2}.

In (
12
),
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
estimate
\mathscr{G}\coloneqq(\mathscr{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{G}_{N})
and
\overline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\coloneqq(\overline{\mu}_{1},\ldots,\overline{\mu}_{J_{0}})
, respectively.

Since
J
is not known in practice, we estimate
J_{0}
using the following information criterion:

\displaystyle\widehat{J}=\argmin_{J\leq J^{*}}\text{IC}(J),

(13)

where
\text{IC}(J)\coloneqq S(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{\mid J},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\mid J})+\rho_{NT}J
,
J^{*}
is a user-specified large fixed constant, and
\rho_{NT}
is a tuning parameter satisfying that

\displaystyle\rho_{NT}\to 0\quad\text{and}\quad\rho_{NT}/(\sqrt{\log(N)/T}+c_{N})\to\infty.

In (
13
),
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\mid J}\coloneqq(\widehat{\nu}_{1\mid J},\ldots,\widehat{\nu}_{J\mid J})
and
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{\mid J}\coloneqq(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{1\mid J},\ldots,\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{J\mid J})
are obtained via (
12
) by assuming the number of groups being
J
. A natural choice of
\rho_{NT}
is
[\log(N+T)]^{-1}
.

Proposition 2
.

Let Assumptions
1
-
3
hold. Then the following results hold:

1.

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[N]}|\overline{x}_{i}-\sum_{j\in[J_{0}]}\overline{\mu}_{j}I(i\in\mathscr{G}_{j})|=O_{P}(\sqrt{\log(N)/T}+c_{N})
.

2.

Given
J_{0}
,
\Pr(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{j}=\mathscr{G}_{j})\to 1
for all
j\in[J_{0}]
, where
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{j}
is obtained via (
12
).

3.

When
J_{0}
is unknown,
\Pr(\widehat{J}=J_{0})\to 1
, where
\widehat{J}
is obtained via (
13
).

The first result of this proposition is evident building on the investigation in Section
2.2
and Assumption
4
.1. After successfully partitioning the individuals, one can further investigate the homogeneity/heterogeneity for the
j^{th}
group, e.g.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{0}:\mu_{i}\equiv\overline{\mu}_{j}\text{ for all }i\in\mathscr{G}_{j}

using the results developed in Sections
2.1
-
2.3
.

3.2
Nonstationary Panel Data

We now revisit the nonstationary panel data model studied in
Phillips and Moon (1999)
. Consider the following data generating process:

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{t}=\mathbf{y}_{t-1}+\mathbf{B}(L)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t},

(14)

where
\mathbf{y}_{t}=(y_{1t},\ldots,y_{Nt})^{\top}
,
\mathbf{y}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
without loss of generality, and
\mathbf{B}(L)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
is the same as that in (
4
). As presented in Section 2 of
Phillips and Moon (1999)
, when studying nonstationary panel data, a key quantity is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{NT^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}y_{it}^{2}=\frac{1}{NT^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{t},

(15)

which is also the foundation of some basic results of
Bai et al. (2009)
and
Dong et al. (2021)
. Using the results of Section
2
, we are now able to account for the cross-sectional dependence of these unit root processes, and present the following proposition.

Proposition 3
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds with
L_{N}=N
, and let further that
\lim_{N}\frac{1}{N}\|\mathbf{B}\|^{2}\to b
. Then as
(N,T)\to(\infty,\infty)
,
\frac{1}{NT^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}y_{it}^{2}\to_{P}\frac{b}{2}
.

Previously, one has to impose cross-sectional independence such as
Phillips and Moon (1999)
,
Dong et al. (2021)
and
Huang et al. (2021)
due to technical constraints.

3.3
CCE Estimators

We now revisit the model of
Pesaran (2006)
and the corresponding CCE estimators using the results established above. Accordingly, we let
L_{N}\equiv N
in what follows. The model is as follows:

\displaystyle y_{it}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{it}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{f}_{t}+\epsilon_{it},

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{it}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{f}_{t}+\mathbf{v}_{it},

where only
\{(y_{it},\mathbf{w}_{it})\mid i\in[N],t\in[T]\}
are observable,
\mathbf{w}_{it}
is a
k\times 1
vector,
\mathbf{f}_{t}
is an
m\times 1
vector, and the dimensions of the other variables are defined accordingly. Both
m
and
k
are finite. The model admits a vector form:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}_{i}=\mathbf{W}_{i}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}+\mathbf{F}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i},

where we have
\mathbf{Y}_{i}=(y_{i1},\ldots,y_{iT})^{\top}
,
\mathbf{W}_{i}=(\mathbf{w}_{i1},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{iT})^{\top}
,
\mathbf{F}=(\mathbf{f}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{f}_{T})^{\top}
, and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}=(\epsilon_{i1},\ldots,\epsilon_{iT})^{\top}
. In the homogenous setting (e.g.,
Westerlund, 2018
and references therein), one further assumes that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\equiv\boldsymbol{\theta}\text{ for all }i\in[N].

(16)

It is then natural to question whether (
16
) holds practically.

To exam (
16
), we briefly review the CCE approach. When eliminating the unobservable factor structure, the CCE approach utilizes the following form:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}y_{it}\\
\mathbf{w}_{it}\end{pmatrix}=\left[\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}&\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathbf{0}\\
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}&\mathbf{I}_{k}\end{pmatrix}\right]^{\top}\mathbf{f}_{t}+\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_{it}\\
\mathbf{v}_{it}\end{pmatrix},

(17)

and for notational simplicity, we rewrite (
17
) as

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{it}=\mathbf{C}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{f}_{t}+\mathbf{u}_{it},

where the definitions of
\mathbf{z}_{it}
,
\mathbf{C}_{i}
, and
\mathbf{u}_{it}
are self-evident. Simple algebra yields that

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{Z}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}\overline{\mathbf{C}}+\overline{\mathbf{U}},

where
\overline{\mathbf{C}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{C}_{i}
,
\overline{\mathbf{Z}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{Z}_{i}
with
\mathbf{Z}_{i}=(\mathbf{z}_{i1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{iT})^{\top}
, and
\overline{\mathbf{U}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{U}_{i}
with
\mathbf{U}_{i}=(\mathbf{u}_{i1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{iT})^{\top}
. Consequently, the CCE estimators of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
are respectively defined by

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathbf{W}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{\overline{\mathbf{Z}}}\mathbf{W}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{\overline{\mathbf{Z}}}\mathbf{Y}_{i}\quad\text{for}\quad\forall i\in[N],

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{W}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{\overline{\mathbf{Z}}}\mathbf{W}_{i}\right)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{W}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{\overline{\mathbf{Z}}}\mathbf{Y}_{i},

(18)

where
\mathbf{M}_{\overline{\mathbf{Z}}}=\mathbf{I}_{T}-\overline{\mathbf{Z}}(\overline{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}\overline{\mathbf{Z}})^{+}\overline{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}
.

The key part of the CCE approach is that
\mathbf{M}_{\overline{\mathbf{Z}}}
offers a good approximation of
\mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{F}}
, because

\overline{\mathbf{C}}^{+}=\overline{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}(\overline{\mathbf{C}}\,\overline{\mathbf{C}}^{\top})^{-1}

under the conditions: (1)
\overline{\mathbf{C}}
has full row rank, and (2)
\overline{\mathbf{U}}
is asymptotically negligible.

Based on (
18
), we construct the following quantities for all
j\in[k]

\displaystyle Q_{j}=\max_{i}\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}(\mathbf{W}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{\overline{\mathbf{Z}}}\mathbf{W}_{i})(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}),

where
\mathbf{e}_{j}
is a selection vector as defined in Section
1
. We further let

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t,s=1}^{T}a\left(\frac{t-s}{\widetilde{m}}\right)\widehat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{jt}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{js}^{\top},

where
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{j1},\ldots,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{jT})=(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}}_{1,1}\circ\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}}_{1,1+j},\ldots,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}}_{N,1}\circ\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}}_{N,1+j})^{\top}
,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}}_{i}=\mathbf{M}_{\overline{\mathbf{Z}}}\mathbf{Z}_{i},
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}}_{i,j}
stands for the
j^{th}
column of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}}_{i}
.

To facilitate the development, we impose the following conditions.

Assumption 5
.

1.

(1) Suppose that
\mathbf{f}_{t}
is covariance stationary with absolute summable auto covariances, and
\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\mathbf{F}\to_{P}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{f}}>0
. (2)
\overline{\mathbf{C}}\to_{P}\mathbf{C}
, where
\mathbf{C}
has full row rank
m
. (3)
\frac{1}{T}\overline{\mathbf{U}}^{\top}\overline{\mathbf{U}}=O_{P}(\frac{1}{N})
,
\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\overline{\mathbf{U}}=O_{P}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{NT}})
and
\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{V}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{F}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}=\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{V}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}+O_{P}(\frac{1}{T})
for
\forall i
. (4)
\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{V}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{i}\to_{P}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{V}_{i}}>0
for
\forall i
, and
\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{V}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{i}\to_{P}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{V}}>0
.

2.

Suppose that
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}=(\epsilon_{1t},\ldots,\epsilon_{Nt})^{\top}\coloneqq\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{B}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-\ell}
fulfills Assumption
1
, and the conditions of Theorem
6
. Let
\mathbf{v}_{t}\coloneqq f(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-p})
, where
p
is fixed, and
f(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-p})
admits a linear combination of
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-p}
.

The first two conditions of Assumption
5
.1 are standard. In the third condition,
\frac{1}{T}\overline{\mathbf{U}}^{\top}\overline{\mathbf{U}}=O_{P}(\frac{1}{N})
and
\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\overline{\mathbf{U}}=O_{P}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{NT}})
are rather standard in view of the development of Section
2.1
, and the following expansions:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\overline{\mathbf{U}}^{\top}\overline{\mathbf{U}}=\frac{1}{TN^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{U}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{N}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{t}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\overline{\mathbf{U}}=\frac{1}{TN}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{f}_{t}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{t},

where
\mathbf{U}_{t}=(\mathbf{u}_{1t},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{Nt})^{\top}
. The requirement
\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{V}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{F}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}=\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{V}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}+O_{P}(\frac{1}{T})
can be verified by the development of Section
2.2
. Assumption
5
.2 allows
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}
and
\mathbf{v}_{t}
to be weakly dependent.

Under these condition, the following proposition holds.

Proposition 4
.

Suppose that Assumption
5
holds and
N\asymp T
. Then for all
j\in[k]

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\Pr(Q_{j}\leq u)-\mathrm{Pr}^{*}\left(\left|\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}_{j}^{1/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{z}_{t}^{*}\right|_{\infty}\leq u\right)\right|=o_{P}(1),

where
\{\mathbf{z}_{t}^{*}\mid t\in[T]\}
is a sequence of i.i.d.
N
-dimensional Gaussian random vectors with
\mathbf{z}_{t}^{*}\sim N(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{N})
.

According to Proposition
4
, all
Q_{j}
’s should not fall in the rejection region yielded by the bootstrap draws under (
16
). We further examine this result in the simulation studies.

On top of these extensions, we may also revisit the time trend analyses of
Gao and Hawthorne (2006)
,
Chen et al. (2012)
,
Robinson (2012)
and
Wu et al. (2023)
, which are of course mathematically involved due to the nonparametric nature. We leave them for future study.

4
Simulation

In this section, we conduct simulation studies to examine the theoretical results about testing and inference of Section
2
, and also verify our argument about the CCE estimators in Section
3.3
.

Simulation 1
(Testing) — The data generating process (DGP) is simplified as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+\rho_{x}\mathbf{x}_{t-1}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\nu}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{t},

where
t=-200,\ldots,0,1,\ldots,T
,
\rho_{x}=0.3
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}\coloneqq(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{N})^{\top}
,
\mathbf{x}_{t}\coloneqq(x_{1t},\ldots,x_{Nt})^{\top}
, and
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{t}\coloneqq(\nu_{1t},\ldots,\nu_{Nt})^{\top}
. As well understood, the AR(1) process admits an MA(
\infty
) representation thus suiting the definition of (
4
). To introduce cross-sectional dependence, we let
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\nu}=\{\rho_{\nu}^{|i-j|}\}_{N\times N}
. The observations from
t=-200,\ldots,0
are burn-in sample in order to eliminate the impact of the initial value. We let
\{\nu_{it}\}
be i.i.d. over both
i
and
t
, and be generated in three cases:

Case 1.

\nu_{it}\sim N(0,1)
;

Case 2.

\nu_{it}\sim t_{8}
, where
t_{8}
stands for a
t
-distribution with a degree freedom 8;

Case 3.

\nu_{it}\sim\Gamma(2,2)-1
, where
\Gamma(2,2)
stands for a Gamma distribution with a shape parameter 2 and a scale parameter 0.5. Therefore,
\Gamma(2,2)-1
has mean 0.

Case 1 is a symmetric distribution with thin tails; Case 2 is a symmetric distribution with heavy tails; and Case 3 is an asymmetric distribution. To exam the size and power of the proposed test in Section
2.3
, for each case we consider three scenarios for
\boldsymbol{\mu}
:

(a).

\mu_{i}\equiv 0
for all
i
;

(b).

\mu_{1}=\frac{4}{\sqrt{T}}
, and
\mu_{i}\equiv 0
for
i\geq 2
;

(c).

\mu_{1}=1
, and
\mu_{i}\equiv 0
for
i\geq 2
.

Scenarios (a)-(c) are designed to examine size, local power, and global power respectively.

Notably, the above DGP is a special case of
Pesaran and Yamagata (2008)
and
Yu et al. (2024)
. Thus, for the purpose of comparison, we also consider the approaches of these two papers (referred to as PY and YYX respectively). To put everything on equal footing, we consider the null of
Yu et al. (2024)
(i.e.,
\mathbb{H}_{0}:\mu_{i}\equiv 0
for all
i
), and modify the test statistic of
Pesaran and Yamagata (2008)
accordingly in an obvious manner. For the sake of space, we refer interested readers to their papers for detailed implementation. As PY and YYX methods calculate the asymptotic variances neglecting the dependence of the residuals (e.g.,
Pesaran and Yamagata, 2008
, Eq. (14)
and
Yu et al., 2024
, Eq. (2.7)
), we anticipate some distorted size or power. Additionally,
Yu et al. (2024)
rely on the Gaussian assumption, so we anticipate the DGPs of Cases 2 and 3 will further distort size or power. For our method (referred to as GLPY), we calculate
Q_{NT}
under the null for each dataset, and obtain the 95% confidence interval (denoted by
\text{CI}_{\infty}
) via
|\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}^{1/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{z}_{t}^{*}|_{\infty}
based on 399 bootstrap replications. After
R
replications, we calculate the rejection rate as follows:

\displaystyle\Delta_{test}=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{j=1}^{R}I(Q_{NT,j}\not\in\text{CI}_{\infty,j}),

where the subindex
j
stands for the corresponding values obtained in the
j^{th}
simulation replication. Similarly, we will report the rejection rates for PY and YYX approaches. For our method, we expect that
\Delta_{test}
is sufficiently close to 0.05 for the scenario (a), is reasonably close to 1 for the scenario (c), and is in between 0 and 1 for the scenario (b). For simplicity, we let
a(\cdot)
be Bartlett kernel, and take suggestions from
Gao et al. (2025)
to set
\widetilde{m}=\lfloor 1.75T^{1/3}\rfloor
for simplicity. Additionally, we let
R=1000
,
N\in\{100,150,200\}
,
T\in\{200,400\}
, and
\rho_{\nu}\in\{0.5,0.95\}
.

The results are summarized in Table
1
. Overall, our approach has reasonable size, local power, and global power irrespective to the magnitude of CD (i.e., the value of
\rho_{\nu}
) as expected. Due to omitting dependence, PY and YYX methods tend to over reject, which is evident in view of the rejection rates of scenario (a) of Cases 1-3.

5
A Case Study

Heterogeneous expectations, which may arise in financial markets because investors interpret and react to available information differently, are crucial for understanding variations in asset prices, portfolio allocations, and market dynamics. As a result, the study of heterogeneous expectations has become an important focus in recent years, with a number of works highlighting its implications for financial theory and practice
(
Ameriks et al., 2020
;
Brunnermeier et al., 2021
;
Giglio et al., 2021
, see, for example,)
.
Notably,
Giglio et al. (2021)
document significant and persistent cross-sectional variations in individual investors’ return expectations, which can only be partially explained by demographic factors. Similarly,
Dahlquist and Ibert (2024)
reveal substantial heterogeneity in equity return expectations among institutional investors and investment consultants and it can be linked to asset managers’ portfolios. These findings challenge traditional finance models based on homogeneous rational expectations and motivate the development of alternative frameworks.

In this study, we revisit some prior work in this area and formally test the heterogeneity in equity return expectations using the newly proposed inference method. Furthermore, we investigate the relationships between equity premium expectations and equity valuations, as explored by
Dahlquist and Ibert (2024)
.

5.1
Variables and Data

Following
Dahlquist and Ibert (2024)
, we examine three types of subjective return expectations held by asset managers for U.S. equities. The equity return expectation (
ere_{it}
) is defined as the (geometric) nominal equity return forecast for large-cap U.S. equities over a 10-year horizon, as reported in public disclosures
1
1

1

As noted by
Dahlquist and Ibert (2024)
, the forecast horizons for return expectation data range from 1 to 50 years. However, most asset managers provide forecasts close to a 10-year horizon. Consequently, this study focuses on 10-year horizon forecasts.
. The equity premium expectation over yield (
epey_{it}
) is calculated by subtracting the horizon-matched log nominal Treasury yield from the nominal equity return expectation. Similarly, the equity premium expectation over cash (
epec_{it}
) is derived by subtracting the expected annualized return on cash (over the next 10 years) from the equity return expectation.

For the equity valuation, we utilize the cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio (
cape_{t}
), defined as the log ratio of real equity prices to real earnings averaged over the past decade. Introduced by
Campbell and Shiller (1988)
, this measure of equity valuation is widely recognized in the finance literature
(
Jagannathan and Liu, 2019
, see, for example,)
. Additionally, we include the past 12-month return of the S&P 500 index (
pr_{t}
) to capture momentum effects and the horizon-matched Treasury yield (
rf_{t}
) to account for the risk-free rate.

The data used to construct these variables are available on the website maintained by the authors of
Dahlquist and Ibert (2024)
. By focusing on a fixed 10-year forecast horizon, we obtain a panel dataset comprising observations from 45 asset managers across 109 months, covering the period from November 1997 to April 2021. Descriptive statistics for these variables are presented in Table
4
.

5.2
Model Specifications and Inference

To analyze the heterogeneity in equity return expectations, we estimate three econometric models, each progressively incorporating more explanatory variables to account for the potential determinants of the expectations:

Model 1:

y_{it}=\mu_{i}+\epsilon_{it}
;

Model 2:

y_{it}=\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}cape_{t}+\epsilon_{it}
;

Model 3:

y_{it}=\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}cape_{t}+\gamma_{i}pr_{t}+\delta_{i}rf_{t}+\epsilon_{it}
,

where
y_{it}\in\{epey_{it},epec_{it},ere_{it}\}
. Model 1 is the baseline model that captures the individual-specific mean levels of equity return expectations, where
\mu_{i}
represents the time-invariant mean for each asset manager
i
. In Model 2, the equity return expectations are modeled as a function of the
cape_{t}
, with
\beta_{i}
representing the sensitivity of each manager’s expectations to equity valuations.
Dahlquist and Ibert (2024)
reveal countercyclical expectations for U.S. equity returns, by showing that the expectations are negatively associated with
cape_{t}
. In this study, we further investigate whether the relationship between return expectations and valuations differs across managers. The inclusion of
pr_{t}
and
rf_{t}
in Model 3 further enables us to examine whether these additional factors, such as historical market performance, can help explain cross-sectional differences in expectations.

For each specification, we conduct hypothesis tests to evaluate the presence of heterogeneity in key parameters. Specifically:

1.

We test
\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}
:
\mu_{i}=\mu
for all
i
in Model 1;

2.

We test
\mathbb{H}_{0}^{2}
:
\alpha_{i}=\alpha
and
\mathbb{H}_{0}^{3}
:
\beta_{i}=\beta
for all
i
respectively in Models 2 and 3.

These tests provide evidence regarding both the variation in average return expectations and the diversity in how managers incorporate fundamental and market-driven information into their forecasts. Rejecting these null hypotheses would indicate the heterogeneity of return expectations among the institutional investors and investment consultants.

5.3
Estimation and Testing Results

We first estimate three specifications of equity return expectations using both heterogeneous and homogeneous regression models. The estimated coefficients for each model, along with their 95% confidence intervals, are presented in Table
5
. Across all models, the cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio is found to be significantly negatively associated with asset managers’ forecasts of equity returns and premiums, indicating countercyclical patterns in equity return expectations: when equity valuations are higher, asset managers expect lower future returns. This result aligns with the findings of
Dahlquist and Ibert (2024)
and contrasts with the procyclical expectations among retail investors revealed by
Greenwood and Shleifer (2014)
.

Comparing the outcomes from heterogeneous and homogeneous estimations, we can identify noticeable differences. In particular, most homogeneous coefficient estimates for the intercept and
cape
are larger (in absolute values) than the corresponding average values from heterogeneous models. This discrepancy highlights the importance of allowing for heterogeneity in the data. Homogeneous models, by assuming no diversity across asset managers, may overestimate the influence of equity valuation on return expectations.

Furthermore, both estimation approaches consistently suggest that past returns exert no significant influence on asset managers’ expectations regarding future equity premiums. This finding suggests that asset managers may not rely on recent return trends when forming long-term return expectations, potentially due to the forward-looking nature of their strategies. It also indicates that their expectations are primarily driven by fundamental factors, such as valuations and macroeconomic conditions, rather than past market performance. Importantly, these results hold consistently across all three measures of equity return expectations.

To further investigate the heterogeneity in asset managers’ expectations for U.S. equity returns, we perform a sequence of heterogeneity tests as outlined in Section
5.2
. The results of these tests are summarized in Panel A of Table
6
. For Model 1, the null hypothesis of homogeneous means is rejected at the 0.01 significance level for all three types of expectations: equity returns, equity premium over yield, and equity premium over cash. This indicates substantial variation in the average levels of return expectations across asset managers.

For Models 2 and 3, the heterogeneity in expectations remains significant even after accounting for equity valuations, past returns, and risk-free rates. The results for
\mathbb{H}_{0}^{3}
further reveal that the relationship between equity return expectations and equity valuations varies significantly across institutional investors and investment consultants. This finding indicates that differences among asset managers extend beyond simple average return expectations; they also exhibit diverse sensitivities to fundamental factors such as equity evaluations. Collectively, these findings confirm the presence of heterogeneity in U.S. equity premium expectations, which aligns with recent literature emphasizing the role of diverse beliefs and preferences in shaping market outcomes
(see,
Brunnermeier et al., 2021
, among others)
.

In order to illustrate the heterogeneity test results for the intercept in each model, we provide the distributions of bootstrap test statistics in Figure

Pesaran and Yamagata (2008)
(PY) and the homogeneity test of intercepts developed by

\mathbb{H}_{0}^{3}
and
\mathbb{H}_{0}^{2}

To adapt this framework to our setting, we modify their statistics to test heterogeneity against an unknown constant by specifying the intercept in the null as its homogeneous estimator. This adjustment ensures comparability with our proposed methodology while maintaining the robustness of their test.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce an underlying data generating process that allows for different magnitude of CD, along with TSA. This is achieved via high-dimensional moving average processes of infinite order (HDMA(
\infty
)), which automatically generalizes the spatial structure introduced by Robinson and his co-authors in recent years. The framework is important in the sense that as noted by
Brockwell and Davis (1991, p. 187)
and
Fan and Yao (2003, pp. 33 & 190)
, the Wold decomposition theorem ensures a formal linear representation exists for any stationary time series with no deterministic components, and HDMA(
\infty
) naturally incorporates this result into a panel data framework.

Our setup and investigation significantly integrates and enhances both homogenous and heterogeneous panel data modelling and testing (such as
Pesaran, 2006
;
Pesaran and Yamagata, 2008
;
Fan et al., 2015
;
Yu et al., 2024
). To study HDMA(
\infty
), we extend the BN decomposition (e.g.,
Beveridge and Nelson, 1981
;
Phillips and Solo, 1992
) to a high-dimensional time series setting, and derive a complete set of toolkit. It is worth mentioning our investigation complements the work of
Fan et al. (2015)
, who specifically study cases where
\frac{T}{\sqrt{N}}\to 0
, by considering a broader range of scenarios and relaxing the independence assumptions employed in
Pesaran and Yamagata (2008)
and
Yu et al. (2024)
.

We exam homogeneity against heterogeneity using Gaussian approximation, a prevalent technique for establishing uniform inference (e.g.,
Chernozhuokov et al., 2022
, and references therein). For post-testing inference, we derive Central Limit theorems through Edgeworth expansions for both homogenous and heterogeneous settings. Notably, the demand for Gaussian approximation in panel data analysis has been increasing recently, as exemplified in Section 4 of
Su et al. (2024)
and Section 4 of
Liu et al. (2024)
. Our study also contributes to this research direction by providing a set of foundational conditions and deriving a set of useful basic results.

We showcase the practical relevance of the established asymptotic theory by (1). connecting our results with the literature on grouping structure analysis such as those surveyed in
Bonhomme and Manresa (2015)
and
Su et al. (2016)
, (2). examining a nonstationary panel data generating process presented in
Phillips and Moon (1999)
, and (3). revisiting the common correlated effects (CCE) estimators of
Pesaran (2006)
. Typically, when investigating nonstationary panel data, one has to impose cross-sectional independence such as
Phillips and Moon (1999)
,
Dong et al. (2021)
and
Huang et al. (2021)
due to technical constraints. Our study offers a set of toolkit to account for the dependence of unit root precesses.

Finally, we verify our theoretical findings via extensive numerical studies using both simulated and real datasets.

Throughout the proofs, we suppose that without loss of generality,
\mu\equiv 0
for the homogenous case, and
\mu_{i}\equiv 0
for all
i\in[N]
for the heterogeneous case. We shall not mention them again unless misunderstanding may arise.

This document is structured as follows: Appendix
A1
points out that the proposed inference methods can accommodate unbalanced panels after some necessary modifications; Appendix
A2
provides some foundational facts used throughout the proofs; Appendix
A3
contains all preliminary lemmas; Appendix
A4
presents the theoretical proofs of the main results; and Appendix
A5
details the proofs of the preliminary lemmas.

A1
Unbalanced Panel Data

More often than not, one encounters unbalanced panel data practically. Given the missing proportion is asymptotically negligible, the proposed inference methods can accommodate unbalanced panels after some necessary modifications.

For instance, we let
\mathbb{T}_{i}
denote the sample set for individual
i
and
T_{i}\coloneqq\sharp\mathbb{T}_{i}
. The
L_{\infty}
-based test statistic is then redefined as

Q_{NT}=\max_{i}\left|\sqrt{T_{i}}(\overline{x}_{i}-\overline{x})\right|,

where
\overline{x}_{i}=\frac{1}{T_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}x_{it}
and
\overline{x}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{T_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}x_{it}
.

Additionally, the high-dimensional long-run covariance matrix estimator for unbalanced panels is defined as
\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}\coloneqq(\widehat{\Omega}_{in})
, where

\displaystyle\widehat{\Omega}_{in}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{i}T_{n}}}\sum_{t\in\mathbb{T}_{i}}\sum_{s\in\mathbb{T}_{n}}a\left(\frac{t-s}{\widetilde{m}}\right)(x_{it}-\overline{x}_{i})(x_{ns}-\overline{x}_{n}),

and the Gaussian multiplier bootstrap approximation is accordingly defined by

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}^{1/2}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{1}}}\sum_{t\in\mathbb{T}_{1}}z_{1t}^{*},\cdots,\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{N}}}\sum_{t\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}z_{Nt}^{*}\Big)^{\top}\right|_{\infty},

where
\{z_{it}^{*}\mid t\in\mathbb{T}_{i}\}
is a sequence of i.i.d.
N(0,1)
.

For the CCE estimation, the estimators and test statistics for unbalanced panels can also be updated. Let
\mathbb{N}_{t}
contain the indices for individuals that have valid observations at time
t
and let
N_{t}
be the number of indices in this set. Additionally, for each
i
, define
\overline{\mathbf{Z}}_{\cdot i}\coloneqq(\overline{\mathbf{z}}_{t_{1}},\cdots,\overline{\mathbf{z}}_{t_{T_{i}}})^{\top}
, where
t_{1},\cdots,t_{T_{i}}\in\mathbb{T}_{i}
and
\overline{\mathbf{z}}_{t}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N_{t}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{t}}\mathbf{z}_{it}
.

The CCE estimators of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
can be then defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathbf{W}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{\overline{\mathbf{Z}}_{\cdot i}}\mathbf{W}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{\overline{\mathbf{Z}}_{\cdot i}}\mathbf{Y}_{i}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for}\hskip 9.24994pt\forall i\in[N],

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{W}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{\overline{\mathbf{Z}}_{\cdot i}}\mathbf{W}_{i}\right)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{W}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{\overline{\mathbf{Z}}_{\cdot i}}\mathbf{Y}_{i},

where
\mathbf{M}_{\overline{\mathbf{Z}}_{\cdot i}}=\mathbf{I}_{T}-\overline{\mathbf{Z}}_{\cdot i}(\overline{\mathbf{Z}}_{\cdot i}^{\top}\overline{\mathbf{Z}}_{\cdot i})^{-1}\overline{\mathbf{Z}}_{\cdot i}^{\top}
.

The heterogeneity test statistic is then given by

\displaystyle Q_{j}=\max_{i}\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}(\mathbf{W}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{\overline{\mathbf{Z}}_{\cdot i}}\mathbf{W}_{i})(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}),

where
\mathbf{e}_{j}
is a selection vector. For constructing the bootstrap statistics, we use
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{j}=(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{j,in})
, where

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{j,in}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{i}T_{n}}}\sum_{t\in\mathbb{T}_{i}}\sum_{s\in\mathbb{T}_{n}}a\left(\frac{t-s}{\widetilde{m}}\right)\widehat{\xi}_{j,it}\widehat{\xi}_{j,ns},

where
\widehat{\xi}_{j,it}
denotes the
t
-th element of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}}_{i,1}\circ\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}}_{i,1+j}
, with

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}}_{i}=\mathbf{M}_{\overline{\mathbf{Z}}_{\cdot i}}\mathbf{Z}_{i},
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}}_{i,j}
standing for the
j^{th}
column of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}}_{i}
. The distribution of
Q_{j}
is then approximated by

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}_{j}^{1/2}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{1}}}\sum_{t\in\mathbb{T}_{1}}z_{1t}^{*},\cdots,\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{N}}}\sum_{t\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}z_{Nt}^{*}\Big)^{\top}\right|_{\infty},

where
\{z_{it}^{*}\mid t\in\mathbb{T}_{i}\}
is a sequence of i.i.d.
N(0,1)
.

A2
Some Facts

We present a few facts in this section, which will be repeatedly used in the proofs.

On Cumulant
— Note that for a generic cumulant, we have for a constant
c

\displaystyle\kappa_{r}(cx)=c^{r}\kappa_{r}(x).

(A.1)

We refer the interested reader to
Saulis and Statulevicius (1991)
for more details about cumulants.

On BN Decomposition
— Simple algebra shows the Beveridge and Nelson (BN) decomposition under the high-dimensional (HD) setting is as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}(L)=\mathbf{B}-(1-L)\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(L),

(A.2)

where
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(L)\coloneqq\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\ell}L^{\ell}
with
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\ell}\coloneqq\sum_{k=\ell+1}^{\infty}\mathbf{B}_{k}
.

On Dependence
— To calculate CD, for
\forall i,j\in[N]
it is easy to obtain that

\displaystyle E[x_{i1}x_{j1}]=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{b}_{\ell i}^{\sharp\top}\mathbf{b}_{\ell j}^{\sharp}.

To calculate TSA, for
t>s
we write

\displaystyle E[x_{it}x_{is}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left[\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{b}_{\ell i}^{\sharp\top}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-\ell}\right)\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{b}_{\ell i}^{\sharp\top}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{s-\ell}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left[\left(\sum_{\ell=t-s}^{\infty}\mathbf{b}_{\ell i}^{\sharp\top}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-\ell}\right)\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{b}_{\ell i}^{\sharp\top}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{s-\ell}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left[\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{b}_{\ell+t-s,i}^{\sharp\top}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{s-\ell}\right)\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{b}_{\ell i}^{\sharp\top}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{s-\ell}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{b}_{\ell+t-s,i}^{\sharp\top}\mathbf{b}_{\ell i}^{\sharp}.

To calculate CD + TSA, for
t>s
we write

\displaystyle E[x_{it}x_{js}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left[\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{b}_{\ell i}^{\sharp\top}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-\ell}\right)\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{b}_{\ell j}^{\sharp\top}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{s-\ell}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left[\left(\sum_{\ell=t-s}^{\infty}\mathbf{b}_{\ell i}^{\sharp\top}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-\ell}\right)\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{b}_{\ell j}^{\sharp\top}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{s-\ell}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left[\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{b}_{\ell+t-s,i}^{\sharp\top}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{s-\ell}\right)\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{b}_{\ell j}^{\sharp\top}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{s-\ell}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{b}_{\ell+t-s,i}^{\sharp\top}\mathbf{b}_{\ell j}^{\sharp}.

On Cumulants
— Recall the notation of Section
1
. Let
\widetilde{\phi}(x)
be the characteristic function of a standard normal distribution. We further define its cumulants by
\gamma_{r}
for
r\geq 1
. We now consider a generic distribution function
G(x)
with a characteristic functions
\chi(u)
and cumulants
\beta_{r}
. Then by Taylor expansion, we have

\log\frac{\chi(u)}{\widetilde{\phi}(x)}=\log\chi(u)-\log\widetilde{\phi}(x)=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}(\beta_{r}-\gamma_{r})\cdot\frac{(\mathsf{i}u)^{r}}{r!}

and

\displaystyle\chi(u)=\widetilde{\phi}(x)\cdot\exp\left\{\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}(\beta_{r}-\gamma_{r})\cdot\frac{(\mathsf{i}u)^{r}}{r!}\right\}.

(A.3)

On Exponential function
— By Taylor expansion,

\displaystyle\exp(u)=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{r}}{r!}.

(A.4)

Taylor theorem yields that

\displaystyle\exp(u)-\sum_{r=0}^{k}\frac{u^{r}}{r!}=\frac{\exp(\widetilde{u})}{(k+1)!}u^{k+1},

(A.5)

where
\widetilde{u}
is between 0 and
u
.

Preliminary Lemmas

In this appendix, we provide some useful preliminary lemmas. The first three lemmas are either obvious or have been carefully proved in the literature, so we do not provide the proofs herewith. We will provide the proofs for Lemmas
1

.

Lemma A1
.

A matrix
\mathbf{A}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
has rank one if and only if it can be written as the outer product of two nonzero vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(i.e.,
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^{\top}
).

Lemma A2

(Esseen’s smoothing Lemma)
.

Let
H(\cdot)
be a distribution with 0 expectation and characteristic function
\chi(\cdot)
. Suppose
H(x)-G(x)
vanishes at
\pm\infty
and that
G(\cdot)
has a derivative
g(\cdot)
such that
|g|_{\infty}\leq m
. Finally, suppose that
g
has a continuously differentiable Fourier transform
\xi(\cdot)
such that
\xi(0)=1
and
\xi^{(1)}(0)=0
. Then

\displaystyle|H-G|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-a}^{a}\left|\frac{\chi(u)-\xi(u)}{u}\right|\mathrm{d}u+\frac{24m}{\pi\cdot a}

where
a>0
.

See Chapter XVI, section 3 of
Feller (1971)
for details about Esseen’s smoothing Lemma.

Lemma A3
.

Let
\mathsf{i}
be the imaginary unit. Then for
\forall\theta\in[0,1)

\displaystyle\sup_{x}\left|\exp(\mathsf{i}x)-\sum_{j=0}^{r}\frac{(\mathsf{i}x)^{j}}{j!}\right|\leq\min\left\{\frac{2}{r!}|x|^{r+\theta},\frac{|x|^{r+1}}{(r+1)!}\right\}.

See
Tikhomirov (1981, 3.2)
for example.

\displaystyle\eqqcolon

where the definition of

Proof of Theorem
1
.

First, we denote the following notation to facilitate the development. Let

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}=(\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\dagger},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{N}^{\dagger})\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\ell}=(\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}_{\ell,1}^{\dagger},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}_{\ell,N}^{\dagger})

(A.8)

where
\mathbf{B}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\ell}
have been defined in (
A.2
) already.

By Lemma
A4
, we write

\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathbf{x}_{s}=\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{t}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{t\ell}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\ell},

where the definition of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{t\ell}
is the same as that in Lemma
A4
. Thus,
\widetilde{S}_{NT}
can also be written as

\widetilde{S}_{NT}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{x}\sqrt{L_{N}T}}\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{T\ell}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\ell}\eqqcolon\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\ell},

where
\frac{1}{\sigma_{x}\sqrt{L_{N}T}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{T\ell}\eqqcolon\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}_{\ell}
, and we have suppressed
N
and
T
in
\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}_{\ell}
for notational simplicity.

To proceed, denote by
\psi_{NT}(u)
the characteristic function of
\widetilde{S}_{NT}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\psi_{NT}(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left[\exp\left(\mathsf{i}u\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\ell}\right)\right]

(A.9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{\ell=-\infty}^{T}\prod_{j=1}^{N}E[\exp(\mathsf{i}u\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\overline{\mathbf{b}}_{\ell,j}^{\dagger}\varepsilon_{j\ell})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{\ell=-\infty}^{T}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\psi(\widetilde{b}_{\ell j}u),

where
\overline{\mathbf{b}}_{\ell,j}^{\dagger}
stands for the
j^{th}
column of
\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}_{\ell}
,
\widetilde{b}_{\ell j}\coloneqq\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\overline{\mathbf{b}}_{\ell,j}^{\dagger}
, and the second equality follows from
\{\varepsilon_{it}\}
being i.i.d. over both dimensions.

Using (
A.9
), we are able to calculate the
r^{th}
cumulant
\beta_{r}
of
\widetilde{S}_{NT}
for
r\geq 1
. Obviously, we have
\beta_{1}=0
and
\beta_{2}=1
. For
r\geq 3
, write

\displaystyle\beta_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-\mathsf{i})^{r}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{r}}{\mathrm{d}u^{r}}\log\psi_{NT}(u)|_{u=0}

(A.10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{T}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(-\mathsf{i})^{r}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{r}}{\mathrm{d}u^{r}}\log\psi(\widetilde{b}_{\ell j}u)|_{u=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{T}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\widetilde{b}_{\ell j}^{r}(-\mathsf{i})^{r}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{r}}{\mathrm{d}u^{r}}\log\psi(u)|_{u=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\kappa_{r}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{T}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\widetilde{b}_{\ell j}^{r}+\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{0}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\widetilde{b}_{\ell j}^{r}\right),

where the second equality follows from (
A.9
), and the third equality follows from (
A.1
). For the second term on the right hand side of (
A.10
), we note that

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{0}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\widetilde{b}_{\ell j}^{r}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\sigma_{x}^{2}L_{N}T)^{r/2}}\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{0}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\mathbf{b}_{T\ell,j}^{\dagger}|^{r}

(A.11)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\sigma_{x}^{2}L_{N}T)^{r/2}}\left(\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{0}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\mathbf{b}_{T\ell,j}^{\dagger}|^{2}\right)^{r/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\sigma_{x}^{2}T)^{r/2}}\left(\frac{1}{L_{N}}\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{0}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{T\ell}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{T\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{N}\right)^{r/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{1}{T^{r/2}}\right),

where
\mathbf{b}_{T\ell,j}^{\dagger}
stands for the
j^{th}
column of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{T\ell}
, the second inequality follows from the fact that for a vector
\mathbf{x}
,
|\mathbf{x}|_{p_{1}}\leq|\mathbf{x}|_{p_{2}}
for any
p_{1}>p_{2}\geq 1
, and the last equality follows from the proof of Lemma
A4
.2. Thus, (
A.10
) and (
A.11
) together infer that

\displaystyle\beta_{r}=\kappa_{r}\sum_{\ell=1}^{T}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\widetilde{b}_{\ell j}^{r}+O\left(\frac{1}{T^{r/2}}\right).

(A.12)

Recall the definitions of (
A.8
), and write further that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\sigma_{x}^{2}L_{N}T)^{r/2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{T}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\mathbf{b}_{T\ell,j}^{\dagger}|^{r}

(A.13)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2^{r-1}}{(\sigma_{x}^{2}L_{N}T)^{r/2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{T}\sum_{j=1}^{N}[|\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\mathbf{b}_{j}^{\dagger}|^{r}+|\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}_{T-\ell,j}^{\dagger}|^{r}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{r-1}}{(\sigma_{x}^{2}L_{N})^{r/2}T^{r/2-1}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\mathbf{b}_{j}^{\dagger}|^{r}+O\left(\frac{1}{T^{r/2}}\right),

where the first inequality follows from

Proof of Lemma
1
.

First, we note that the Probabilist’s Hermite polynomials

\left\{H_{n}(x)\mid(-1)^{n}\exp\left(\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}x^{n}}\widetilde{\phi}(x)\ \text{ for }\ n\geq 0\right\}

have the following generating function:

\exp\left(xu-\frac{u^{2}}{2}\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}H_{n}(x)\frac{u^{n}}{n!}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\phi(x)H_{n}(x)\frac{u^{n}}{n!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2}+xu-\frac{u^{2}}{2}\right)

(A.63)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-u)^{2}}{2}\right).

We now apply the Fourier transformation to both sides of (
A.63
), and note that the right hand side can be further written as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(\mathsf{i}w)\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-u)^{2}}{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x=\exp\left(\mathsf{i}wu-\frac{1}{2}w^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp(\mathsf{i}wu)\widetilde{\phi}(w)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{\phi}(w)\frac{(\mathsf{i}wu)^{n}}{n!}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{\phi}(w)(\mathsf{i}w)^{n}\cdot\frac{u^{n}}{n!},

where the first equality is obvious in view of the characteristic function of the normal distribution, and the third equality follows from (
A.4
).

By comparing the Fourier transformation of both sides of (
A.63
), the result follows immediately.
∎

Proof of Lemma
A4
.

(1).a The expression
\mathbf{B}(L)=\mathbf{B}-(1-L)\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(L)
of (
A.2
) is the so-called BN decomposition from
Phillips and Solo (1992)
. We then write

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathbf{x}_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{t}[\mathbf{B}-(1-L)\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(L)]\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{s}-\sum_{s=1}^{t}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(L)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{s}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(L)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{s-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{s}-\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(L)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}+\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(L)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{0}.

By Assumption
1
.2,

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{N}{L_{N}}}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\ell}\|_{2}\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{N}{L_{N}}}\sum_{k=\ell+1}^{\infty}\|\mathbf{B}_{k}\|_{2}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\ell\sqrt{\frac{N}{L_{N}}}\|\mathbf{B}_{\ell}\|_{2}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\ell\cdot C_{N\ell}<\infty.

(1).b According to (1).a, write

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathbf{x}_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{s}-\sum_{\ell=0}^{t-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-\ell}-\sum_{\ell=t}^{\infty}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-\ell}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{-\ell}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\sum_{\ell=1}^{t}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\ell}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{t}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{t-\ell}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\ell}-\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{t+\ell}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{-\ell}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{-\ell}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{t}(\mathbf{B}-\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{t-\ell})\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\ell}-\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{t+\ell}-\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\ell})\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{-\ell}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{t}(\mathbf{B}-\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{t-\ell})\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\ell}-\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{0}(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{t-\ell}-\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{-\ell})\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\ell}\eqqcolon\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{t}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{t\ell}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\ell}.

(2). Note that for a given vector
\mathbf{v}
,
|\mathbf{v}|_{2}\leq|\mathbf{v}|_{1}
in which
|\mathbf{v}|_{j}
with
j=1,2
defines its
L^{j}
norm. In connection with the fact that
\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{N}{L_{N}}}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\ell}\|_{2}<\infty
of the first result, we obtain that
\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{N}{L_{N}}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}<\infty.
We are now able to write

\displaystyle E\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_{N}}}\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{0}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{T\ell}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\ell}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L_{N}}\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{0}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{T\ell}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}_{T\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L_{N}}\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{0}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{T-\ell}-\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{-\ell})(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{T-\ell}-\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{-\ell})^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{N}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2}{L_{N}}\sum_{\ell=T}^{\infty}\|\mathbf{1}_{N}\|_{2}^{2}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{2}{L_{N}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\|\mathbf{1}_{N}\|_{2}^{2}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{4N}{L_{N}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}<\infty.

(3). By the first result of this lemma, we have

\displaystyle E\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_{N}}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{T-\ell}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\ell}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L_{N}}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{T-\ell}\right)\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{T-\ell}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{N}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{\frac{N}{L_{N}}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{T-1}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\ell}\|_{2}\right)^{2}\leq\left(\sqrt{\frac{N}{L_{N}}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\ell}\|_{2}\right)^{2}<\infty.

The proof is now completed.
∎

Proof of Lemma

.
