Enhanced immunogenicity of the rabies virus glycoprotein fused to a dendritic cell-targeting peptide
Authors: Atousa Bakhsheshi, Fattah Sotoodehnejadnematalahi, Alireza Gholami, Mehdi Ajorloo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The dendritic cell-targeting peptide (DCP) trimer sequence has previously shown to enhance specific IgG induction. This study aims to investigate the expression of rabies virus glycoprotein (RVG) in eukaryotic cells through cloning in the pCDNA3.1 + vector, alongside the fusion of a DCP. The DCP trimer sequence was fused to the C-terminal of the RVG to improve the immune response. The construct was designed to include a His-tag for protein purification and an enterokinase (EK) cleavage site for separation of the tag from the recombinant protein. The RVG gene was amplified from the rabies virus genome via RT-PCR, and cloning was performed using BamHI and EcoRI restriction enzymes. The recombinant plasmid (RVG- pCDNA3.1+) was transfected into BHK-21 cells using lipofection, and the expression of the recombinant protein was confirmed through SDS-PAGE and western blotting using anti-His antibodies. The His-tagged recombinant protein was purified using Ni-NTA resin. Immunogenicity of the recombinant protein was investigated through mouse inoculation and analysis of the serum samples using RFFIT and ELISA methods. The results indicated successful cloning and expression of the RVG-DCP fusion protein in eukaryotic cells, with potential applications in rabies vaccine development. The results suggests a novel method for enhancing the immunogenicity of viral glycoproteins through the use of dendritic cell-targeting peptides.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51181-y.

Introduction

Rabies disease burden and current vaccines

The rabies virus (RV) is a neurotropic virus causing fatal encephalitis in humans and other mammals1. Since there is no cure for rabies, the pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is the most important action to protect people with high occupational risk and health care workers in rabies endemic regions2. The commercial vaccines currently used are dependent on injecting multiple doses, and they are restricted in long-lasting immunity3.

Rabies virus glycoprotein-based subunit vaccines

The rabies virus glycoprotein (RVG) plays a crucial role in the virus’s ability to infect host cells by mediating viral entry through receptor binding4. Due to the importance of RVG in immunity against viral infection, it has become a key target for vaccine development5. Traditionally, viral glycoproteins are expressed in prokaryotic or eukaryotic systems to generate antigens for vaccine production6. However, these vaccines have proven ineffective. The RVG expressed in yeast failed to elicit a protective immune response in mice, likely due to improper folding of the final product7. In contrast, the baculovirus-derived glycoprotein produced in insect cells has demonstrated immunogenicity8,9. Baculovirus-expressed RVG requires extensive purification due to co-purifying insect cell proteins, baculoviral components, and differences in glycosylation patterns that may affect downstream immunogenicity and regulatory acceptance. Enhancing the immunogenicity of these glycoproteins remains a challenge.

Dendritic cell-targeting strategies

Dendritic cells (DCs) are professional antigen-presenting cells that play a vital role in initiating immune responses10. Targeting peptides, such as dendritic cell-targeting peptides (DCP), have been shown to enhance the activation of DCs, thereby improving antigen presentation and subsequent immune response11. Recent studies have demonstrated the potential of fusion proteins, combining viral antigens with DCPs, in inducing stronger immune responses12. A trimer sequence of dendritic cells targeting peptides (DCP1) was identified using a phage display system, which improves the specific IgG induction when fused to the C-terminal of antigen proteins and peptides. It has been reported that fusion of this peptide can improve the vaccine efficiency even in absence of adjuvants13.

The rationale and objective of the present study

The current study presents the cloning of the RVG gene along with the DCP1 sequence into a eukaryotic expression system, namely the BHK-21 cell line. We hypothesize that the fusion of DCP1 with RVG would enhance the immunogenicity of the recombinant protein, leading to improved immune responses and supporting vaccination strategies.

Methods

The constructs and primers

A rabies virus glycoprotein (RVG) expressing construct was designed for expression of the glycoprotein fused to three DCP1s using a GGGS flexible linker in eukaryotic cells and obtained from a gene synthesis service (Generay, China). The sequences of an enterokinase (EK) site and His-tag were embedded at C-terminal of the recombinant protein. The sequence map of the designed construct containing cloning site for the RVG (between BamHI and EcoRI) is presented in Fig. 1 which was furtherly cloned into pCDNA3.1+ (rpCDNA3.1+).

In-silico analysis of the recombinant protein stability

The GGGS wich is a flexible linker was embedded in the fusion protein sequence and the concluded model was investigated using the I-TASSER online software to obtain the 3D model of the recombinant RVG-DCP protein. The protein structure was analyzed using SAVES V6.1 PROCHECK. Ramachandran plot was used to assess stability of the amino acid sequence. The ProtParam was used for protein characteristis assessment.

Cloning of RVG into the designed construct

The RVG gene was amplified from the PV2061 strain of rabies virus genome using RED mastermix (A180303, Ampliqon, Denmark), following RNA extraction using total RNA extraction kit (YT9065, YTA, Iran) and cDNA synthesis (YT4500, YTA, Iran). The primers were designed to amplify the RV glycoprotein from the sequence of the rabies virus strain PV-2061 (Genebank: JX276550.1) (F: GAGGATCCAGACTCAAGGAAACATGGTT, R: ACGAATTCCTCTCTCAGTCCGGTCTCAC) using Generunner and IDT Oligoanalyzer software. Three important modifications were made in the sequence of the primers compared to the original RVG sequence (Genebank: JX276550.1). First, the Kozak sequence was embedded in the forward primer, second, the stop codon was removed from the reverse primer, and third, the reading frame of the glycoprotein was matched with the EK and His tag. Both the PCR product and the rpCDNA3.1 + were digested using BamHI and EcoRI enzymes (Thermo USA), and after a cleanup step using a PCR cleanup kit (FAPCK 001, Favorgen, Iran), the ligation reaction was carried out using T4 ligase (EL0011, Thermo, USA). The cloning of RVG into rpCDNA3.1+ (RVG-rpCDNA3.1+) was verified using colony PCR, quick check and sequencing method (Pishgam, Iran). The colony PCR was carried out using the same primers and mastermix, as those used for the RVG PCR amplification.

Protein expression in eukaryotic cells

BHK-21 cells were obtained from the National Cell Bank of Iran, and seeded in a 6-well plate at ~ 70–80% confluency one day prior to transfection. The RVG- rpCDNA3.1 + plasmid was transfected into BHK-21 cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (11668019, Themo, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the plasmid DNA was diluted in DMEM (12100046, Gibco, USA) medium and mixed with the lipofectamine reagent at a 1:1 ratio, followed by a 15–20 min incubation at room temperature to allow complex formation. The DNA–lipid complexes were then added dropwise to the cells in serum-free DMEM. After 4–6 h of incubation, the medium was replaced with complete growth medium containing 10% FBS (26140079, Gibco, USA), and cells were further incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 72 h and harvested for downstream assays. The 72-h post-transfection time point was selected based on preliminary optimization experiments and common results for transient expression in BHK-21 cells. The cells were lysed using lysis buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, 1% Triton X-100, 150 mM NaCl, and 1 mM PMSF), and the supernatant was used after clarification (10,000 RPM at 8 °C for 30 min) for further analysis using SDS-PAGE and Western-blot analyses. The clarification was performed For Western blotting, proteins were resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto nitrocellulose membrane. Membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST (20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween20), and incubated with an HRP-conjugated anti-His antibody (SB-019662, Sinabiotech, Iran) overnight at 4 °C. Band development was performed using 3,3′-Diaminobenzidine (DAB) containing 20 mM Tris, 0.1% DAB (D8001, Sigma, USA), 0.05% H2O2.

The purification of the recombinant protein

Metal ion affinity chromatography was employed to purify the recombinant protein through the histidine tag. The clarified cell lysate was applied to a Ni-NTA column (88221, Thermo, USA) utilizing a binding buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, and 40 mM imidazole), followed by washing with 20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, and 100 mM imidazole, and elution with buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, and 500 mM imidazole. The final concentration of the purified protein was assessed utilizing the Bradford kit (DB0017-625a, DNAbiotech, Iran) conforming to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Immunization of mice

The purified recombinant protein was quantified using the Bradford method. The recombinant protein dissolved in PBS buffer was used for injecting antigens formulated with Aluminum hydroxide gel (21645-51-2, Brenntag, USA) as an adjuvant in BALB/c mice. A negative control group was also included in the experiment and was inoculated using adjuvant and PBS. Eight BALB/c mice with 4–6 weeks age, 10–18 g, and of a single gender (male) were selected for each group of vaccination. The adjuvant was used in the antigen formulation at a 1:2000 dilution. The injections were performed on days zero and seven. The first group was immunized intraperitoneally with 10 µg the purified recombinant protein and the control group with 1 ml of the commercial veterinary rabies vaccine (Rabisin, Germany) both containing the adjuvant. The mice were followed up for three weeks, and any case with illness was excluded from the study. Since two mice were excluded from the recombinant protein vaccination group, six mice were chosen from each group for blood sampling and immunogenicity assessment (Fig. 2).

Virus neutralizing antibody test

Blood samples were taken on day 21, and the serum was separated using a 2000 rpm centrifuge for 15 min after blood clotting. The virus-neutralizing antibodies (VNAs) were assessed using the Rapid Fluorescent Focus Inhibition Test (RFFIT) following standard protocols recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO)5. Briefly, serial dilutions of the heat-inactivated serum samples were incubated with a fixed dose of rabies virus (challenge virus standard, CVS-11 strain) for 90 min at 37 °C. After incubation, the mixture was added to BHK-21 cells and further incubated for 20–24 h at a 37 °C incubator supplied with 5% CO2. The cells were then fixed with cold acetone and stained with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated anti-rabies nucleocapsid antibody (3572114, Bio-Rad, USA). Fluorescent foci were visualized under a UV microscope, and the 50% endpoint titer was calculated using the Reed–Muench method. This method estimates the dilution at which 50% of microscopic fields were protected from infection by interpolating between consecutive dilutions demonstrating percentages higher and lower than the 50% neutralizing threshold14. Titers were expressed in International Units per milliliter (IU/mL) by comparison with a reference serum with known potency.

Cytokine profile associated with Th1-skewed immune responses

Cytokine profiles during Th1 activation were analyzed to compare the immune response elicited by the recombinant rabies virus glycoprotein (rRABV-G) with the commercial rabies vaccine. Experimental mice serum was collected on day 14 following the second injection. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits were applied to measure the levels of interleukin-12 (IL-12) (FK-1323, Fantibody, China), interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) (KPG-MIFNY, Karmania Pars Gene, Iran), and interleukin-2 (IL-2) (KPG-MIL2, Karmania Pars Gene, Iran) based on the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, 96-well plates coated with capture antibodies were treated with standards and serum samples, followed by incubation with streptavidin-HRP and biotin-conjugated detection antibodies. Following the addition of TMB substrate, absorbance at 450 nm was determined using a microplate reader. Cytokine concentrations were calculated from standard curves prepared with recombinant cytokines and are given in pg/mL. Duplicate samples were run for all samples, and the results are given as mean ± SD.

Quantification of anti-RABV glycoprotein IgG

A mouse anti-RABV glycoprotein antibody IgG titer serologic assay kit (RAS-T157, Acrobiosystem, Switzerland) was used to measure the induced IgG levels. For qualitative analysis, the serum samples were diluted by a 1:100 ratio and 100 µl of the diluted sample was added in each well of the plate and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Each well was washed three times for 1 min. Each well was incubated in a dark place with 100 µL HRP-Conjugated Antibody working solution for 1 h at 37 °C. After three steps of washing, 100 µL of the substrate was added to each well and incubated for 20 min at 37 °C. At the final step, 50 µL of the stop solution was added and incubated for 3 min. The absorbance at OD450 was read, and 0.1 was considered the cut-off value. To reduce the background noise, the OD630 was subtracted from the OD450 to reach the final OD. For quantitative assessment of the antibody titer, a two-fold dilution of serum samples was prepared from 1:100 to 1:25,600. The lowest dilution that resulted in an OD over the cut-off was considered the antibody titer.

The statistical analyses

All the data were analyzed for the normality test using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Unpaired T-test and Mann–Whitney were used to compare the means of two groups. All the statistical analyses were assessed using GraphPad Prism V8.

Equipment and settings

Gel electrophoresis images were acquired using a Vilber Quantum Gel Documentation System (Vilber, France). Image acquisition was performed using the accompanying BioVision software under standardized exposure conditions. All acquisition parameters (including exposure time and sensitivity settings) were kept consistent across comparable experiments to ensure reliable comparisons. Western blot membrane images were captured using a high-resolution digital camera under uniform lighting conditions. Care was taken to avoid overexposure and signal saturation during image acquisition. For figure preparation, images were processed using Adobe Photoshop (Adobe Systems). Processing steps were limited to cropping, alignment, and labeling for clarity and presentation. Cropping was performed only to remove irrelevant regions of the membrane (e.g., lanes corresponding to unrelated experiments), and full-length, uncropped images with visible membrane edges have been provided in the Supplementary Information.

Results

in-silico analysis of the recombinant protein

The modeling was accomplished using I-Tasser, and the results concluded a proper model ( C-score = − 1.08). The best identical analogue in PDB was 6TMR crystal structure, which was related to Mokola virus glycoprotein in its monomeric post-fusion conformation15. The results from the ProtParam server showed that the recombinant protein has an estimated half-life of 30 h in mammalian cells, and the related molecular weight is about 63 kDa. The theoretical half-life of the RVG was similarly estimated to be 30 h in mammalian cells by the ProtParam. The Ramachandran analysis showed only 2.8% of the amino acids were placed in the disallowed region (Fig. 3).

The RVG-DCP fusion protein was successfully expressed in BHK-21 cells as confirmed by western blot analysis, where a protein band of the expected molecular weight was observed (Fig. 5). Purification of the recombinant protein was achieved using Ni-NTA resin, resulting in a high-purity protein suitable for further analysis. The purified RVG-DCP fusion protein showed the expected size and demonstrated good immunoreactivity with anti-His antibodies in western blot analysis.

Discussion

The current study illustrates the effective cloning, expression, and immunogenicity improvement of a rabies virus glycoprotein (RVG) fused to a trimer of dendritic cell-targeting peptides (DCP) in BHK-21 cells, using the pCDNA3.1 + vector. The fusion protein RVG-DCP led to increased virus-neutralizing antibodies (VNAs) production in BALB/c mice compared to a commercial vaccine control. One millilitre of inactivated rabies virus made with aluminium hydroxide, which could produce more than one IU VNA with a single injection, was included in the commercial vaccine. The NIH method, the gold standard for evaluating rabies vaccine immunity, was used to design the vaccination schedule16. The assessment was done utilizing the Rapid Fluorescent Focus Inhibition Test (RFFIT). These results are representative of the promise of DC-targeting strategies in breaking the limitations of standard rabies vaccines, such as needing several doses and providing suboptimal long-term immunity. By utilizing computational modeling, eukaryotic expression, and immunologic assays together, this strategy is consistent with more recent trends in vaccine development focused on the rapid presentation of antigens to dendritic cells (DCs)10. DCs are essential for triggering adaptive immune responses. The methods used here began with gene synthesis and RT-PCR amplification of RVG from the viral genome4,17. The inclusion of a Kozak sequence within the forward primer improved translation initiation. Stop codon deletion and frame alignment allowed seamless integration into the DCP trimer with a GGGS linker, enterokinase (EK) tag, and His-tag for purification. The design follows accepted practices in recombinant protein engineering, where flexible linker usage minimizes steric hindrance and conformational integrity is upheld18.

In-silico estimations by I-TASSER and ProtParam showed the protein to be stable with a molecular mass of about 63 kDa and a half-life of 30 h in mammalian cells. This was supported by a Ramachandran plot that showed only 2.8% of residues in disallowed regions. Such computational validation is required to prevent expression failures, as seen in earlier attempts in the case of RVG expressed in yeast, which were undermined by false folding and lack of immunogenicity6. Expression within BHK-21 cells using lipofection produced RVG-DCP protein detectable by SDS-PAGE and Western blotting with anti-His antibodies. This underscores the value of this cell line in producing glycosylated viral antigens that closely resemble native forms. BHK-21 cells offer an advantage over prokaryotic systems by facilitating post-translational processes necessary for RVG’s receptor-binding activity and immunogenicity7,19. Ni-NTA affinity chromatography-based purification achieved a purified recombinant protein suitable for immunogenicity studies, 600 mM imidazole elution, which is appropriate for downstream immunogenicity testing. This is a crucial step as the contaminants would interfere with the immune assays, and the EK site allows for tag removal in clinical applications.

The VNA immunogenicity assessment indicated markedly higher VNA titers in RVG-DCP-treated mice (mean potency ~ 1.5 IU) than in the commercial vaccine (mean ~ 0.5 IU; p = 0.0022), when using aluminum hydroxide as an adjuvant. RFFIT, a WHO-recommended assay for VNA quantification, involved incubation of CVS-11 strain dilutions and serum with measurement of fluorescent foci in BHK-21 cells, standardized to a 10 IU reference serum5,20,21. This enhanced response is consistent with enhanced antigen presentation by DCP trimers, which induces effective antigen cross-presentation to CD8 + T cells and robust humoral immunity11,13. Similar improvements have also been noted in fusion proteins where DC-targeting motifs improve vaccine potency by attracting DCs without adjuvants22–24. An example includes a recombinant rabies virus that encodes MIP-1α-fused RVG, inducing enhanced DC recruitment and VNA titers in mice, consistent with our findings23,25. Traditional inactivated vaccines, though safe, have a tendency to require boosters due to waning immunity, as proven by studies showing VNA levels dropping below those needed for protection within months2,26. DC-targeting fusions like RVG-DCP, however, can induce more long-lasting responses by inducing Th1-skewed immunity, as proven by increased IgG titers in comparable systems27. This is supported by structural analyses of RVG trimers, in which stabilization of them in pre-fusion form increases exposure of neutralizing epitopes3,15,28. Moreover, our use of a trimeric DCP design increases binding affinity, similar to the way multimeric ligands in nanoparticle vaccines facilitate DC maturation29,30. This study may reflect that fusing a dendritic cell-targeting peptide (DCP) trimer to the C-terminal of the rabies virus glycoprotein (RVG) significantly enhances its immunogenicity when expressed in BHK-21 cells. The recombinant RVG-DCP protein induced markedly higher levels of key Th1 cytokines—IL-12, IL-2, and IFN-γ—compared to a commercial veterinary rabies vaccine, indicating robust dendritic cell activation and cellular immune response31. Additionally, ELISA results showed elevated anti-RVG IgG titers in mice immunized with RVG-DCP, suggesting a humoral immunity induction. The higher anti-RVG IgG titer was witnessed in both quantitative and qualitative ELISA methods, while only the qualitative one was significantly different. These findings suggest that DCP fusion is a promising strategy for developing more effective rabies vaccines with enhanced immune activation and potentially reduced reliance on adjuvants. Rabies vaccine induces a Th1-skewed immune response, as has been shown by a multitude of studies32,33. The Th1 subset of CD4 + T cells secretes cytokines typically linked with inflammation, including IFN-gamma and TNF, and favors cell-mediated immune responses34. This Th1 dominance is reinforced by Lang et al., who found that dogs vaccinated for rabies developed CD4⁺ effector memory (TEM) and CD4⁺CD8⁺ TEMRA cells with a strong Th1 phenotype35. Schnee et al. demonstrated that a rabies glycoprotein vaccine composed of mRNA elicited strong CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell responses in both mice and pigs and that CD4⁺ cells were instrumental in the production of neutralising antibodies36. Wen et al. supported this pathway by genetically modifying rabies viruses to express dendritic cell-activating molecules, which enhanced IFN-α and CD86 expression and consequently innate and adaptive immunity23. Humoral immunity is accountable for neutralisation, whereas cellular immunity and, more precisely, Th1-mediated responses are significant for long-term protection and viral clearance in the central nervous system37. In conclusion, the Th1 pathway is the cornerstone of effective rabies vaccination, with IFN-γ-producing CD4⁺ T cells and activated dendritic cells orchestrating a robust immune defense. In this study, the significantly higher levels of IFN-γ, IL-2 in case of adaptive, and IL-12 in case of innate/APC-associated immunity ensure a comprehensive comparison with commercial vaccines and provide insight into an efficient rabies protection.

There are still some limitations. Information regarding the exact dosing of the commercial vaccine was not available, and the comparison of a fixed recombinant protein dose to a whole-virus vaccine would be more reliable if the virus or glycoprotein content of the commercial vaccine were considered. On the other hand, since the RVG alone (without DCP) was not included as a control in the investigations, the immunity enhancement cannot be definitively attributed to DCP. The study used a two-dose immunization regimen in a limited number of five per group, which is unlikely to account for variability; multi-scale trials in alternative models, including dogs and non-human primates, will need to be performed to test cross-protection against a range of RABV strains. The adjuvant dependency was not completely removed, even though DCP’s adjuvant-sparing ability is promising29,38. Furthermore, although BHK-21 expression yields glycosylated proteins, GMP scale-up will need modifications, e.g., transition into HEK293 systems7. Future studies, including the investigation of the level of VNAs during the period of time and comparing with conventional vaccines, are needed to prove the longer immunity induced by RVG-DCP. Taken together, these findings represent a meaningful step forward in the improvement of rabies vaccination strategies. Rabies is a serious global threat, with over 59,000 human deaths occurring annually, as reported by Hampson et al. in 2015, and remains the most reliable estimation in the absence of robust worldwide reporting systems1,39. Increased effectiveness is possible by integrating DC-targeting with mRNA or viral vectors, as seen in mRNA vaccines triggering rapid VNAs40. Challenge studies post-vaccination and epitope mapping should be explored in future research to further maximize the fusion design, even potentially extending this across other lyssaviruses or zoonotic pathogens. Cumulatively, the RVG-DCP fusion presents a strong technical solution to immunogenicity boost, taking advantage of DC biology to drive rabies prevention towards reduced booster requirements, and adjuvant-free formats.

Conclusion

In this study, we have successfully cloned and expressed the rabies virus glycoprotein fused with a dendritic cell-targeting peptide in eukaryotic BHK-21 cells. This fusion protein has the potential to enhance the immunogenicity of rabies vaccines by improving antigen presentation and subsequent immune response. The approach described here provides a new strategy for developing more effective vaccines against rabies and potentially other viral diseases.
