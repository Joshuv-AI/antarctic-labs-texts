C-Reactive Protein and Risk of Low Back Pain: A Univariable and Multivariable Mendelian Randomization Study
Authors: Shalin Feng, Jun Mou, Xiaoqin Wang, Changping Mu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the causal associations of genetically predicted C-reactive protein (CRP), major depressive disorder (MDD), body mass index (BMI), and smoking initiation with low back pain (LBP) using Mendelian randomization (MR).MethodsSummary-level GWAS data for exposures and LBP (FinnGen R8) were obtained. Instrumental variables were selected at a strict genome-wide threshold (P < 5×10−8) and harmonized. Two-sample univariable MR was performed primarily using random-effects inverse-variance weighted (IVW). Multivariable MR (MVMR) assessed independent effects adjusting for confounders, evaluated via conditional F-statistics. Sensitivity analyses included Cochran’s Q, MR-Egger, and leave-one-out diagnostics.ResultsUnivariable MR showed that genetically predicted circulating CRP was associated with an increased risk of LBP (IVW: β = 0.100, 95% CI: 0.040 to 0.160, P = 0.001), corroborated by the weighted median estimator. Conversely, genetically predicted MDD showed no causal association with LBP (β = −0.039, P = 0.752). In MVMR, instrument strength was robust (conditional F: CRP = 61.37, BMI = 23.30, smoking = 14.39). Elevated CRP remained an independent risk factor for LBP (β = 0.093, 95% CI: 0.016 to 0.170, P = 0.018). Additionally, smoking initiation exhibited an independent causal effect (β = 0.268, 95% CI: 0.081 to 0.455, P = 0.005), while BMI showed no direct effect (β = 0.129, P = 0.128).ConclusionThis study provides genetic evidence consistent with an independent causal association between elevated CRP levels and LBP risk, alongside smoking initiation. Genetically predicted MDD and BMI did not exhibit independent causal effects on LBP.Keywords: Mendelian randomization, C-reactive protein, low back pain, major depressive disorder, multivariable analysis

Introduction

LBP represents a highly prevalent global burden, and its clinical definition varies across studies, with some definitions requiring pain persistence for more than three months. Out of 354 conditions evaluated between 1990 and 2017 LBP was identified as the leading cause of global disability.1 Approximately 70% to 80% of the population will experience at least one episode of low back pain during their lifetime,2 with an estimated 5.0% to 10% of these individuals subsequently developing symptoms.3 Persistent pain often triggers adverse cognitive and behavioral adaptations, severely disrupting daily activities, amplifying psychological burdens, and paradoxically exacerbating the subjective perception of pain.4 Consequently, LBP not only exerts a profound disabling impact and incurs exorbitant healthcare expenditures but also fundamentally compounds the suffering of affected individuals.

Within the multifactorial etiological network of LBP, low-grade systemic inflammation has emerged as a cornerstone biological mechanism.5 CRP, a hallmark biomarker of systemic inflammation, has been positively correlated with LBP, spinal stiffness, and intervertebral disc degeneration across numerous observational cohorts.6–8 Conversely, a subset of epidemiological investigations has failed to detect a statistically significant association between CRP and LBP. In particular, a recent Mendelian randomization study by Suri et al9 found no evidence supporting a causal effect of CRP on chronic musculoskeletal pain conditions, which contrasts with other genetic explorations highlighting the nominal genetic impact of systemic inflammatory markers on spinal discomfort.10 These discrepancies likely stem from variations in phenotypic definitions, divergent sample sizes, differences in instrument selection thresholds, or ancestral heterogeneities thereby underscoring the critical necessity for a more rigorous, multivariable genetic framework to clarify this relationship.

Beyond systemic inflammation, psychiatric and psychological indices are also strongly implicated in the pathogenesis and progression of LBP.4 While the prominent clinical comorbidity between MDD and pain has been extensively documented, the underlying direction of causality remains a subject of intense debate. Observational studies consistently demonstrate that individuals with depressive traits exhibit a substantially elevated risk of developing LBP; however, conclusions derived from current Mendelian randomization frameworks remain inconsistent.11 Certain genetic explorations support a direct causal role of depression on LBP risk, whereas alternative models show that these causal effect vectors vanish entirely upon the adjustment of extraneous confounders. Whether depression indirectly compromises spinal health via downstream inflammatory cascades or operates through an entirely independent pathogenic axis remains largely elusive.

Furthermore, actionable lifestyle factors, including elevated BMI and smoking initiation, have been robustly linked to genetic liabilities for LBP.12–14 Obesity acts as a dual pathogen affecting the spine through both mechanical overloading and metabolic neuroinflammation,15,16 while smoking initiation is closely linked to systemic oxidative stress and accelerated intervertebral disc degeneration.17 Nevertheless, these behavioral and metabolic traits engage in intricate, bidirectional interactions with both circulating CRP and depressive phenotypes, rendering traditional univariable analyses inherently incapable of isolating their independent etiologies.18,19

In summary, although CRP, MDD, BMI, and smoking initiation have each been individually implicated in the landscape of LBP, their precise independent configurations and relative weights within this causal web have yet to be systematically evaluated. Currently, there is a distinct lack of a unified MR framework capable of simultaneously incorporating these exposures while robustly partitioning out mutual horizontal pleiotropy and confounding. To bridge this knowledge gap, the present study utilized summary-level metrics from large-scale genome-wide association studies to deploy a combination of univariable and multivariable MR approaches. Our objective was to systematically quantify the independent direct causal vectors of these interrelated factors on LBP risk, with the ultimate goal of refining the etiological understanding of back pain and providing a solid genetic blueprint for targeted prospective clinical interventions.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE-MR reporting guidelines and adopted a two-sample Mendelian randomization design to systematically evaluate the causal effects of CRP, MDD, BMI, and smoking initiation on LBP. The MR design relies on three core assumptions: the instruments must be robustly associated with the exposure (relevance); independent of confounders (independence); and affect the outcome exclusively through the exposure (exclusion restriction). The overall analytical framework consisted of three levels: first, univariable Mendelian randomization was performed to estimate the total effects of each factor on LBP; second, multivariable Mendelian randomization was conducted by simultaneously incorporating CRP, BMI, and smoking initiation into a regression model to estimate the independent causal effect of CRP after adjusting for confounders; finally, the robustness of the results was assessed through heterogeneity tests, horizontal pleiotropy tests, and leave-one-out sensitivity analysis. All genetic instrumental variables were selected using the genome-wide significance threshold (P < 5×10−8) and subjected to quality control through linkage disequilibrium clumping (r2 < 0.001, window = 10,000 kb) and instrument strength testing (F-statistic > 10).

Data Sources and Study Population

The summary-level genetic statistics for this two-sample and multivariable Mendelian randomization study were obtained from publicly accessible genome-wide association study databases.

Specifically, genetic instruments for circulating CRP levels were extracted from a GWAS meta-analysis performed by Ligthart et al (2018) via the CHARGE Consortium, including approximately 204,402 individuals of European descent (IEU OpenGWAS ID: ieu-b-35).

Genetic variants associated with MDD were retrieved from the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium meta-analysis by Wray et al (2018), which included 135,458 cases and 344,901 controls of European ancestry (ID: ieu-a-1188). Notably, this GWAS partially overlaps with the UK Biobank cohort; however, the estimated overlap fraction relative to the total FinnGen R8 outcome sample size is minimal (< 5%). Given the high univariable F-statistics of all retained instruments, type 1 error inflation attributable to sample overlap bias is considered negligible.

For the primary outcome, summary statistics for LBP were obtained from the FinnGen research project (R8 release, phenotype ID: finn-b-M13_LOWBACKPAIN), comprising 13,178 cases rigorously defined by International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) code M54.5, and 164,682 controls of Finnish ancestry.

For multivariable MR (MVMR) adjustment, summary data for BMI were obtained from the GIANT Consortium study by Locke et al (2015) (ID: ieu-a-2, N ≈ 339,000 European individuals), and genetic instruments for smoking initiation (phenotyped as “ever being a regular smoker”) were derived from the GSCAN Consortium analysis by Liu et al (2019) (ID: ieu-b-4877, N ≈ 460,000 European participants).

All data are de-identified, aggregated summary statistics released for public use. As the original GWAS obtained appropriate institutional review board approvals and informed consent, no additional ethical approval was required for this secondary analysis (Table 1).

Instrumental Variable Selection and Quality Control

This study utilized a two-sample Mendelian randomization framework, wherein genetic variants served as instrumental variables to predict the exposures of interest. Single nucleotide polymorphisms associated with circulating CRP and MDD were extracted at the standard genome-wide significance threshold (P < 5×10−8) to ensure instrument validity. A rigorous linkage disequilibrium clumping process was executed using the 1000 Genomes Project European reference panel (r2 < 0.001, window = 10,000 kb) to guarantee instrument independence. The strength of each individual SNP was quantified using the F-statistic according to the formula F = (β/SE)2, and any candidate variant with F < 10 was excluded. After harmonization with the outcome dataset, 54 independent SNPs were retained as valid IVs for circulating CRP, and 4 SNPs were retained for MDD. All constituent instruments exhibited univariable F-statistics substantially greater than 10.

Method Description

Univariable Mendelian Randomization Analysis

To evaluate the causal effects of genetically predicted CRP and MDD on LBP, two-sample univariable MR analyses were performed separately.

The multiplicative random-effects IVW method was used as the primary analysis, which accounts for potential heterogeneity and pleiotropy. Four complementary methods were also applied to test the robustness of the estimates against horizontal pleiotropy: MR-Egger regression (which tests and corrects for directional pleiotropy), weighted median estimator (which provides consistent estimates if at least 50% of the weight comes from valid instruments), simple median, and weighted mode.

Causal effect sizes were expressed as Beta coefficients (β) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All analyses were performed using the TwoSampleMR package (v0.7.5) in R (v4.5.2). Statistical significance was set at two-sided P < 0.05.

Multivariable Mendelian Randomization Analysis

To evaluate whether the causal effect of CRP on low back pain was independent of metabolic and behavioral pathways, we performed multivariable MR adjusting for BMI and smoking initiation. MDD was purposefully excluded from this multivariable model because our preceding univariable analysis demonstrated a strictly null total causal effect of genetic liability to MDD on low back pain (P = 0.752), rendering further direct effect estimation redundant. Candidate genetic variants associated with CRP, BMI, and smoking initiation were extracted at a strict genome-wide threshold (P < 5×10−8) and pooled together. After unified clumping (r2 < 0.001, window = 10,000 kb) and mutual harmonization across all exposures and the outcome, 129 shared SNPs were incorporated into the final multivariable IVW model. To systematically assess instrument strength in the joint model, conditional F-statistics were calculated using the Sanderson et al method, incorporating a phenotypic correlation matrix to account for instrument covariance. Additionally, multiple testing for primary analyses was evaluated against a Bonferroni-corrected threshold (P < 0.0125 for four exposures). To ensure robust findings, a priori statistical power calculations were performed using the mRnd web-based tool. Given our sample sizes and instrument strength, the study possessed >80% power to detect the observed odds ratios for significant LBP exposures at an alpha level of 0.05. The conditional F-statistics for CRP (61.37), BMI (23.30), and smoking initiation (14.39) were all substantially higher than the conventional threshold of 10, confirming the robust substantially reducing concerns about weak instrument bias.

Sensitivity Analyses

To systematically evaluate the robustness of our causal estimates and detect potential violations of the core MR assumptions, multiple sensitivity complementary metrics were performed.

First, Cochran’s Q statistic was utilized to assess horizontal heterogeneity among the instrumental variables in both univariable and multivariable frameworks. A P-value <0.05 was considered indicative of significant heterogeneity, justifying our default deployment of the multiplicative random-effects IVW model to accommodate variant-specific effect cross-variance.

Second, the MR-Egger intercept test was applied to quantify and evaluate directional horizontal pleiotropy. An intercept P-value > 0.05 demonstrated the statistical absence of directional pleiotropic bias, confirming that the genetic variants affect the LBP outcome exclusively through the specified exposures.

Finally, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was executed by systematically omitting each individual SNP in an iterative manner and re-calculating the remaining IVW estimates to verify whether the global causal vector was disproportionately driven or biased by any single, highly influential genetic instrument.

Additionally, the Mendelian Randomization Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) global test was implemented to detect potential horizontal pleiotropy and outlying variants, with outlier-corrected estimates computed if significant distortion was identified.

All sensitivity diagnostics were computed via the TwoSampleMR package.

Result

Univariable Mendelian Randomization Estimates

Genetically predicted higher circulating CRP levels (54 SNPs) were significantly associated with an increased risk of low back pain. Under the random-effects IVW model as the primary analysis, a one-standard-deviation increase in circulating CRP corresponded to a β of 0.100 (95% CI: 0.040 to 0.160, OR = 1.105, 95% CI: 1.041 to 1.173, P = 0.001). This directional risk was consistently supported by the weighted median estimator (β = 0.112, 95% CI: 0.014 to 0.209, OR = 1.118, P = 0.025). MR-Egger regression yielded a directionally concordant estimate (β = 0.038, 95% CI: –0.061 to 0.138, P = 0.451). Full estimates are detailed in Table 2.

In contrast, univariable MR exploration of genetic liability to MDD (4 SNPs) did not reveal a statistically significant causal effect on low back pain under the primary IVW model (β = −0.039, 95% CI: −0.280 to 0.202, OR = 0.962, P = 0.752). This null finding was corroborated by complementary sensitivity estimators, including weighted median and MR-Egger regression (all P > 0.05, Table 2), confirming the absence of a direct causal link between depressive traits and low back pain risk.

Multivariable MR Results

The forest plot illustrates the independent causal effects of CRP, BMI, and smoking initiation on the risk of low back pain derived from multivariable MR analysis comprising 129 shared SNPs (Figure 4). Genetic liability to increased CRP levels maintained a significant independent causal effect on low back pain (β = 0.093, 95% CI: 0.016 to 0.170, OR = 1.097, 95% CI: 1.016 to 1.185, P = 0.018). Notably, smoking initiation also exerted a significant independent causal effect on low back pain (β = 0.268, 95% CI: 0.081 to 0.455, OR = 1.307, 95% CI: 1.084 to 1.576, P = 0.005). Conversely, genetically predicted BMI showed no independent association with low back pain after adjustment (β = 0.129, 95% CI: −0.037 to 0.294, OR = 1.137, P = 0.128). These results indicate that both elevated CRP levels and smoking behaviors contribute independently to the risk of low back pain.

Sensitivity Diagnostics

Cochran’s Q statistic indicated no significant heterogeneity among the updated CRP instruments (Q = 40.61, P = 0.894). The MR-Egger intercept test showed no evidence of directional horizontal pleiotropy (intercept = 0.005, 95% CI: −0.001 to 0.011, P = 0.101). The MR-PRESSO global test did not identify any significant outliers (P = 0.411; 0 outliers detected). The distortion test was not applicable because no outliers were identified. Leave-one-out analyses further confirmed that no single variant disproportionately drove the causal estimates. Collectively, these sensitivity diagnostics support the robustness of our primary causal estimates.

Discussion

The present study leveraged a comprehensive two-sample and multivariable Mendelian randomization framework to evaluate the genetically proxied causal effects of CRP and MDD on the risk of low back pain. Our findings provide genetic evidence consistent with an independent causal contribution of elevated circulating CRP levels to low back pain risk, alongside smoking initiation. Rather than absolute assertions, our updated multivariable framework indicates that systemic inflammation and smoking operate via distinct pathophysiological pathways. Specifically, smoking-induced oxidative stress, vascular damage, and capillary vasoconstriction may directly accelerate intervertebral disc degeneration, independent of baseline systemic inflammatory status. In contrast, no robust evidence was found supporting a direct causal influence of genetic liability to MDD or BMI on low back pain in the multivariable model.

Our positive findings strongly resonate with previous observational epidemiological cohorts reporting elevated inflammatory markers in back pain patients,20–23 advancing the field by verifying the forward causal directionality at the genomic level.7,24 Notably, some previous univariable MR investigations, including a recent study by Suri et al (2025), exploring the association between CRP and chronic musculoskeletal pain conditions have yielded null conclusions. We postulate that such discrepancies largely stem from differences in phenotype definitions, ancestral sample sizes, and instrument selection thresholds. While Suri et al evaluated broadly defined chronic pain cohorts, our investigation focused on lumbar spine disorders from the FinnGen registry. Furthermore, our study implemented a unified instrument clumping procedure (P < 5×10−8) in a multivariable context, achieving conditional F-statistics well above 10 and effectively substantially mitigating concerns about weak instrument bias and confounding.

Mechanistically, CRP, functioning as a critical downstream biomarker of the interleukin-6 (IL-6) signaling pathway, may chronically accumulate and directly sensitize nociceptors in the dorsal root ganglia, or alternatively induce microvascular endothelial dysfunction thereby accelerating intervertebral disc degeneration, driving intractable back pain from the peripheral level.25,26 Furthermore, our null finding regarding MDD suggests that the frequently observed clinical comorbidity between depression and back pain is likely attributable to downstream symptomatic overlap, shared environmental stressors, or reverse causality, rather than a direct shared genetic pleiotropic pathway.27,28

These findings carry potential translational implications. Since systemic inflammation is validated as an independent risk node for LBP, future intervention strategies may look beyond traditional mechanical analgesia. Pharmacological interventions targeting pro-inflammatory pathways may offer novel preventative or disease-modifying avenues for patients with refractory back pain.29,30 Additionally, within the scope of clinical nursing and disease management, exploring high-sensitivity CRP as a potential high-risk stratification biomarker, coupled with targeted lifestyle interventions like anti-inflammatory diets and structured exercise, holds tangible public health significance for mitigating long-term disability risks.31

The strengths of this investigation lie in its rigorous MR design, which inherently circumvents the reverse causality and residual confounding biases ubiquitous in traditional observational studies.32,33 Furthermore, leveraging outcome data from the FinnGen R8 repository, rigidly defined by ICD-10 diagnostic codes, guarantees the clinical precision of the phenotype.34 However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, all utilized GWAS summary data were restricted to individuals of European descent, potentially limiting the universal generalizability of our conclusions to other ancestral populations.35,36 Finally, CRP represents only one predominant node within the systemic inflammatory cascade; future investigations utilizing tissue-specific cytokine GWAS data are warranted to map the complete pathological landscape.

Conclusions

This study suggests that elevated circulating CRP levels and smoking initiation are independent genetic risk factors for low back pain, whereas genetically predicted depression and BMI did not show statistically significant independent causal effects in the present analysis. Anti-inflammatory approaches and smoking cessation strategies may provide complementary pathways for the prevention and clinical management of low back pain.
