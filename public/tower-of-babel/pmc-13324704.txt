Smart library personalized resource proactive recommendation system integrating user profiling and knowledge graphs
Authors: Fei Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe resource recommendation service of smart libraries is facing a dual dilemma of imprecise perception of user needs and passivity in recommendation patterns. Existing approaches mostly adopt classical paradigms of collaborative filtering or content-based filtering, exhibiting notable limitations in the following aspects: user profiles primarily rely on single-dimensional borrowing or retrieval behavioral data without integrating academic characteristics such as disciplinary backgrounds and research stages; knowledge graphs are mostly confined to collection metadata without embedding disciplinary ontologies and citation networks, limiting the mining of cross-domain resource semantic associations; and recommendation mechanisms lack context-aware proactive push capabilities, with recommendation timing being disconnected from readers’ research rhythms and interest evolution trajectories. To address these issues, this paper proposes a smart library personalized resource proactive recommendation system that integrates multi-dimensional user profiling with a disciplinary knowledge graph. At the user modeling level, the system incorporates readers’ borrowing records, retrieval logs, disciplinary backgrounds, and temporal behavioral patterns, employing a multi-head attention mechanism to achieve adaptive weighted fusion of multi-source heterogeneous features, and capturing the dynamic evolution trajectory of user interests through a gated recurrent unit. At the knowledge representation level, a library resource knowledge graph that fuses collection metadata, disciplinary ontologies, and citation networks is constructed, with a graph attention network employed for embedding learning of higher-order semantic relationships. Compared with GCN, GAT can adaptively assign differentiated aggregation weights to different neighbor nodes, making it more suitable for the library knowledge graph where different relation types contribute unevenly to recommendation. At the recommendation strategy level, a context-aware proactive recommendation mechanism based on interest drift detection is designed, which determines the optimal push timing by monitoring temporal changes in user profiles. The interest drift threshold is optimized through grid search on the validation set, and three types of contextual signals — access context, research stage, and interest drift — are fused through a gated aggregation mechanism to jointly trigger proactive push. Experimental results on a university library dataset demonstrate that the proposed method achieves 16.02% and 11.64% on Recall@10 and NDCG@10, respectively, representing improvements of 4.87% and 5.23% over the best-performing baseline methods. The push acceptance rate of proactive recommendations reaches 38.9%, significantly outperforming the random push strategy. Introduction

With the deep penetration of the Internet of Things, cloud computing, and artificial intelligence technologies into the library domain, traditional libraries are undergoing a paradigm transformation driven by technology. As a next-generation library service model, the smart library has a core objective of achieving deep integration of resource management and user services through intelligent technology1. In this transformation process, how to precisely identify the latent needs of readers from vast amounts of heterogeneous digital resources and present resources to target users in a timely manner through proactive push has become a critical issue for enhancing the effectiveness of library knowledge services. However, most current library resource recommendation systems still predominantly operate on passive-response architectures — that is, the recommendation process is triggered only after a reader explicitly initiates a retrieval request. This mode is unable to capture the dynamic evolution trajectory of user interests, presenting significant bottlenecks in both resource utilization and service satisfaction.

In the domain of personalized recommendation research, collaborative filtering and content-based filtering methods have been widely applied, yet both face the challenges of data sparsity and cold start2. To alleviate these issues, the introduction of knowledge graphs as auxiliary information into recommendation systems has become an important trend in recent years, with a large number of studies demonstrating their ability to significantly enhance recommendation accuracy and interpretability3. In the area of knowledge graph embedding representation learning, graph attention networks have been widely used to model higher-order semantic associations between entities, enabling adaptive propagation and aggregation of knowledge through dynamic attention mechanisms4. Meanwhile, user profiling, as the core means of characterizing user preferences and behavioral features, is evolving from static modeling based on explicit feedback toward dynamic modeling that incorporates multi-source implicit behaviors5.

In the library context, some scholars have attempted to combine collaborative filtering algorithms with content features to improve the accuracy of collection recommendations6, while others have utilized deep learning techniques to optimize the personalized retrieval of academic resources7. Despite these advances, existing work still falls short in the following respects: the construction of user profiles primarily relies on single-dimensional borrowing or retrieval behavioral data, without integrating academic characteristics such as readers’ disciplinary backgrounds and research stages, making it difficult to comprehensively characterize the composite academic needs of readers; the construction of knowledge graphs is mostly confined to collection metadata, without embedding disciplinary ontological hierarchies and cross-resource citation networks, which limits the ability to mine cross-domain resource semantic associations; and recommendation mechanisms lack context-aware proactive push capabilities, with recommendation timing being disconnected from readers’ research rhythms and interest evolution trajectories, failing to achieve forward-looking resource delivery.

To address these research gaps, this paper proposes a smart library personalized resource proactive recommendation system that integrates multi-dimensional user profiling with a disciplinary knowledge graph. The overall research framework of this system is illustrated in Fig. 1. Specifically, at the user profiling level, this paper synthesizes readers’ borrowing records, retrieval logs, disciplinary backgrounds, and temporal behavioral patterns, employing attention mechanisms to dynamically fuse multi-source heterogeneous features and generate multi-dimensional profile representations that reflect the evolutionary trend of user interests. At the knowledge graph construction level, this paper not only performs entity extraction and relationship modeling on the metadata of library collections (such as authors, subject headings, classification numbers, etc.), but also integrates disciplinary ontological knowledge and cross-domain citation associations to construct a semantically rich library resource knowledge graph, employing a graph attention network for representation learning to obtain high-quality entity embedding vectors. At the recommendation mechanism level, a context-aware proactive recommendation strategy is designed that determines recommendation timing and delivers personalized resources without requiring explicit retrieval requests from users by monitoring the reader’s current research stage, access context, and interest drift signals. Related Work

Current research status on smart library resource recommendation

Resource recommendation services in smart libraries have received sustained attention in recent years, with the research focus shifting from traditional collection management toward intelligent, user-centered knowledge services. At the macro level, relevant studies have systematically reviewed the conceptual connotations, technological dimensions, and service functions of smart libraries, pointing out that smart libraries encompass three core elements — intelligent devices, smart users, and smart services — and that their technical architecture involves 53 key technologies including the Internet of Things, artificial intelligence, and big data8. In the specific practice of resource recommendation, personalized service models based on collaborative filtering represent one of the most widely applied technical approaches. Some studies have constructed user-based and item-based collaborative filtering recommendation algorithms on cloud computing platforms, maintaining recommendation accuracy between 75% and 88%9. For the university library scenario, some scholars have proposed an intelligent resource recommendation framework based on fuzzy logic and deep learning, attempting to address the inability of keyword search engines to provide personalized and context-aware resource suggestions10. However, the current methods in library resource recommendation are still largely confined to the classical paradigm of collaborative filtering or content-based filtering, and fail to deeply exploit the disciplinary semantic associations among library collection resources, which limits the quality of recommendations in academic knowledge service scenarios.

User profiling construction methods

User profiling is a key prerequisite for personalized recommendation, with its core lying in extracting stable and discriminative preference representations from users’ multi-faceted interaction data. In terms of methodological evolution, user modeling has undergone a paradigm shift from early stereotypical models to representation learning methods driven by deep learning, with implicit data collection, multi-behavior modeling, and the integration of graph data structures becoming mainstream trends11. Within the deep learning framework, recurrent neural networks have been employed to model the long- and short-term interests in user behavioral sequences, and improved LSTM networks are capable of capturing the dynamic drift characteristics of user preferences over time12. In hybrid recommendation models, some studies have combined user-based and item-based collaborative filtering with neural collaborative filtering, recurrent neural networks, and TF-IDF content analysis, effectively improving recommendation accuracy and relevance while addressing data sparsity13. For educational scenarios, some scholars have constructed a multi-feature fusion book recommendation model based on deep neural networks, jointly training user embedding vectors using readers’ borrowing history and resource attribute information14. Furthermore, a recent comprehensive survey on on-device recommender systems has highlighted that model lightweighting and privacy-preserving local computation are emerging as key considerations for next-generation recommendation architectures, which also has implications for user profiling construction on resource-constrained library terminal devices15. These studies provide useful references for user profiling construction in the library domain, but also expose a common limitation of current work — user feature modeling in the library scenario predominantly relies on single-dimensional behavioral data and has not yet fully integrated structured knowledge features such as readers’ disciplinary backgrounds and research stages to characterize their composite academic needs.

Applications of knowledge graphs in recommendation systems

Knowledge graphs introduce rich auxiliary information into recommendation systems through the structured expression of relationships between entities, and have been proven to effectively alleviate data sparsity and cold start problems16. In terms of technical approaches to knowledge graph-enhanced recommendation, existing methods can be categorized into three main classes: embedding-based methods, path-based methods, and propagation-based methods17. Among these, graph attention networks have demonstrated excellent performance in knowledge-aware recommendation due to their ability to perform adaptive weighted aggregation of neighborhood nodes. Some studies have modeled relationship propagation in knowledge graphs using dynamic attention mechanisms, combined with multi-level contrastive learning strategies to extract high-quality user and item feature representations18. In the area of heterogeneous information fusion, some scholars have proposed a recommendation approach based on heterogeneous networks and dynamic knowledge graphs, achieving relation-enhanced aggregation of multi-modal nodes through an improved multi-head attention mechanism and mining users’ hierarchical latent interests through a knowledge propagation network19. A systematic survey of knowledge graph embedding techniques classifies existing methods by embedding strategy and application scenario, and points out that current methods still exhibit deficiencies in modeling multi-interest distributions of users and in representation quality under sparse data conditions20. The collaborative modeling of graph neural networks and knowledge graphs is also an active research direction in recent years; recommendation algorithms based on graph convolutional networks have significantly improved recommendation accuracy and diversity through information propagation on heterogeneous graphs21. At the level of semantic understanding of knowledge graphs, some studies have applied knowledge graphs to frequent subgraph mining to construct user profile vectors that preserve original semantics, achieving a good balance between recommendation accuracy and interpretability22. The opportunities and challenges facing knowledge graphs themselves have been comprehensively analyzed from the perspectives of knowledge acquisition, knowledge completion, and knowledge fusion23. In addition, recent advances in knowledge graph reasoning have explored context-aware reinforcement learning methods to enhance multi-hop reasoning capabilities under few-shot scenarios, demonstrating the potential of adaptive representation learning for capturing fine-grained entity semantics, which provides methodological inspiration for entity embedding learning in library knowledge graphs24. Although the above works have achieved significant results in general recommendation scenarios, few studies have incorporated disciplinary ontological knowledge and library collection metadata into the scope of knowledge graph construction, nor have they combined knowledge graph embeddings with multi-dimensional dynamic user profiles for joint optimization, which limits the application potential of knowledge graphs in personalized library recommendation.

Research progress on proactive recommendation mechanisms

Unlike traditional passive-response recommendations, proactive recommendation systems are capable of automatically pushing recommendation results to users when the current context is appropriate, without waiting for users to make explicit requests. Context awareness is the technical foundation for achieving proactive recommendation; a relevant systematic review reviewed 90 studies published between 2014 and 2024, summarizing the main categories of methods for integrating contextual information into recommendation systems25. In terms of technical means for context modeling, attention mechanisms have been widely used to adaptively focus on key parts of input data; some studies employ sparse self-attention mechanisms combined with location-category information to improve training efficiency and personalization accuracy in point-of-interest recommendation26. The comprehensive evolutionary trajectory of recommendation systems indicates that the iterative progression from traditional filtering methods to deep learning models, and further to graph neural networks and large language models, is continuously expanding the capability boundaries of recommendation systems27. In the library and education domains, existing work has explored adaptive learning path generation methods based on knowledge graphs and deep learning, utilizing graph attention networks and Transformers to model the hierarchical and semantic relationships of knowledge and dynamically recommending personalized resources to learners28. However, existing research on proactive recommendation is primarily concentrated in areas such as point-of-interest recommendation and news push in mobile scenarios, and proactive recommendation mechanisms specifically designed for the library scenario remain largely unexplored. The academic information needs of library users possess unique characteristics such as periodicity, stagedness, and disciplinary focus; existing methods have not yet established context-aware proactive push strategies that are coupled with users’ long-term research trajectories and interest evolution rhythms.

In summary, although the four research directions of smart library recommendation, user profiling construction, knowledge graph-enhanced recommendation, and proactive recommendation mechanisms have each made progress, the deep integration of multi-dimensional dynamic user profiling, disciplinary knowledge graphs, and context-driven proactive recommendation has not yet been achieved. Current work either focuses on algorithmic improvements while neglecting the domain-specific characteristics of the library scenario, or attends only to a single technical dimension while lacking system-level method integration. Existing approaches are unable to simultaneously meet the dual demands of academic libraries for precise user need characterization and proactive knowledge service delivery — this is precisely the research gap that this paper aims to fill.

System framework and methodology

Overall system architecture design

The smart library personalized resource proactive recommendation system integrating user profiling and knowledge graphs proposed in this paper consists of four core modules, and its overall architecture is illustrated in Fig. 2. The bottom layer is the data acquisition and preprocessing module, which is responsible for extracting readers’ borrowing records, retrieval logs, electronic resource access behaviors, and basic reader attribute information from the library integrated management system, and performing cleaning, denoising, and normalization processing on the raw data. On this basis, the two parallel core modules of the system are responsible for the dynamic construction of multi-dimensional user profiles and the construction and representation learning of the library resource knowledge graph, respectively. The user profiling module maps readers’ multi-source heterogeneous behavioral data into a unified embedding representation space, and achieves adaptive feature fusion and interest evolution modeling through attention mechanisms; the knowledge graph module integrates collection metadata, disciplinary ontological knowledge, and cross-resource citation associations into a semantically rich heterogeneous graph structure, employing a graph attention network for entity embedding learning. The top layer is the context-aware proactive recommendation engine, which comprehensively calculates the user–resource matching degree using user profile vectors and knowledge graph entity embeddings, while simultaneously monitoring the user’s current context state to determine the optimal recommendation timing, and ultimately completes the proactive delivery of personalized resources without requiring the user to initiate an explicit retrieval request.

Dynamic construction model for multi-dimensional user profiles

The quality of user profiles directly determines the depth and accuracy with which the recommendation system understands reader needs. Unlike existing studies that rely solely on a single behavioral dimension to model user preferences29, the multi-dimensional user profiles constructed in this paper encompass four feature subspaces: static attribute features (including the reader’s department, major, and academic level), borrowing behavior features (including borrowing frequency, distribution across borrowing categories, and borrowing time series), retrieval interaction features (including retrieval keyword sequences, click-browsing trajectories, and resource dwell time), and disciplinary background features (including the disciplinary domain of the reader’s published papers and the research directions of the projects they have participated in). In the knowledge graph construction process, entity recognition employs a named entity recognition method based on pre-trained language models to extract entities from the title, abstract, and keyword fields of resources33, while relation extraction is accomplished through a combination of MARC field rule mapping and pattern matching-based automated methods. After construction, the knowledge graph requires representation learning to obtain high-quality entity embedding vectors. This paper employs a Graph Attention Network (GAT) as the core architecture for graph embedding learning. Compared with Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN), which aggregate neighbor information using fixed and uniform weights, GAT introduces a learnable attention mechanism that adaptively assigns differentiated aggregation weights to different neighbor nodes. This characteristic is particularly well-suited for the library resource knowledge graph, where the semantic contribution of different relation types varies significantly — for instance, the “belongs to classification” relation and the “cited by” relation have fundamentally different informational importance for characterizing a resource’s academic relevance, and GAT can automatically learn to emphasize the more informative neighbors while suppressing less relevant ones. GAT has demonstrated excellent performance in knowledge graph-based recommendation tasks, with its key advantage lying in the ability to effectively filter out less informative entities through multi-stream attention propagation34. The core challenge of proactive recommendation lies in “recommendation timing determination”37. Recent research has demonstrated that intention-aware proactive recommendation strategies, which actively predict the optimal moment and content for push delivery, significantly outperform passive approaches in terms of user acceptance. Inappropriate push timing severely reduces user acceptance. The context-aware recommendation timing determination strategy designed in this paper fuses three types of contextual signals: access context signals (whether the reader is currently in the physical library space or on an online retrieval platform), research stage signals (inferring the stage — literature review, method exploration, or paper writing — in which the reader is engaged based on recent borrowing and retrieval behaviors), and interest drift signals (detecting whether significant shifts in user interests have occurred by comparing the cosine similarity between the recent behavioral window and the historical profile).

Specifically, the three types of contextual signals are fused through a gated aggregation mechanism to jointly determine whether to trigger proactive recommendation. The access context signal serves as a prerequisite gate: proactive push is only activated when the reader is currently in an active access state (i.e., within the physical library space or on an online retrieval platform). On this basis, the research stage signal is used to adjust the type distribution of recommended resources — for example, when the system infers that a reader is in the literature review stage, it prioritizes recommending review articles and foundational monographs; when the reader is in the method exploration stage, methodological and applied resources are given higher priority. The interest drift signal serves as the core trigger condition for proactive push, determining whether the reader has entered a new research direction exploration period.

Experiments and results analysis

Dataset and experimental setup

The experimental data in this paper originate from the operational records of a comprehensive university library from January 2021 to June 2024, encompassing reader borrowing records, OPAC retrieval logs, electronic resource access behaviors, and basic reader attribute information. After data cleaning, 12,836 valid readers, 187,452 collection resource entries, and 1,024,573 borrowing and interaction behavior records were retained. To construct the knowledge graph, 216,891 entities and 583,647 relation triples were extracted from the collection MARC data. The dataset was randomly split into training, validation, and test sets in an 8:1:1 ratio. The detailed statistical information of the dataset is shown in Table 3.

Evaluation metrics and baseline methods

This paper evaluates the system from two dimensions: recommendation accuracy and proactive recommendation timing effectiveness. Recommendation accuracy employs three widely used ranking metrics — Recall@K, NDCG@K, and MRR — where K takes values of 10 and 20. Proactive recommendation timing effectiveness is measured by the Push Acceptance Rate (AR), defined as the proportion of cases in which users actually click on or borrow resources after receiving a proactive recommendation.

To validate the effectiveness of the proposed method, the following six representative baseline methods are selected for comparison: (1) BPR-MF, a matrix factorization model based on Bayesian Personalized Ranking; (2) DeepFM, a feature interaction model combining factorization machines with deep neural networks; (3) RippleNet, a recommendation model based on knowledge graph preference propagation; (4) KGAT, a recommendation model based on knowledge graph attention networks; (5) KGCL, a recommendation approach integrating knowledge graphs and contrastive learning; (6) SLKG, a knowledge graph recommendation method for smart libraries. Experimental results and comparative analysis

The overall performance of each method on recommendation accuracy metrics is shown in Table 5. The proposed method achieves the best performance across all evaluation metrics. Compared with the best-performing baseline KGCL, the proposed method improves Recall@10 by 4.87%, NDCG@10 by 5.23%, and MRR by 3.41%. This performance gain can be attributed to the synergistic effect of the multi-dimensional user profiling and knowledge graph embedding modules: the knowledge graph module mines cross-domain resource semantic associations through disciplinary ontologies and citation networks that go far beyond what traditional metadata descriptions can capture, while the multi-dimensional user profiling module provides fine-grained user need representations that enable the matching network to more precisely align readers with academically relevant resources. It is noteworthy that BPR-MF and DeepFM, which do not use knowledge graphs, clearly lag behind knowledge graph-enhanced methods on all metrics, further validating the necessity of modeling semantic associations among collection resources for improving recommendation quality. In the comparison between the library-specific method SLKG and the proposed method, the latter outperforms the former by 6.52% on Recall@20, with the gap primarily attributable to SLKG’s reliance on only partial user profile information and its lack of GRU-based dynamic interest evolution modeling capability, which limits its ability to capture readers’ evolving academic needs over time.

Ablation study and parameter sensitivity analysis

To quantify the contribution of each core module to the overall system performance, five ablation variants are designed for comparative experiments: (1) w/o UP, removing the multi-dimensional user profiling module and using only user ID embeddings; (2) w/o KG, removing the knowledge graph embedding module and retaining only user–resource interaction information; (3) w/o ATT, replacing the multi-head attention fusion mechanism with average pooling; (4) w/o GRU, removing GRU temporal modeling and using only static profiles; (5) w/o PRO, removing the proactive recommendation timing determination module and adopting a random push strategy. Several key findings can be drawn from the ablation results. The removal of the knowledge graph module leads to a 19.5% decrease in Recall@10 (from 16.02% to 12.89%), which is the largest performance decline among all ablation variants, indicating that the semantic association information between resources provided by the knowledge graph plays a core supporting role in improving recommendation accuracy. This is because the knowledge graph, through the integration of disciplinary ontologies and citation networks, mines cross-domain resource correlations that traditional metadata-based methods cannot capture — for example, two resources that belong to different Chinese Library Classification numbers but share common subject headings and citation relationships can be semantically linked through higher-order paths in the knowledge graph, enabling the model to recommend relevant resources that would otherwise be invisible to conventional approaches. The removal of the user profiling module results in a 15.9% decrease in Recall@10, making its impact second only to the knowledge graph module. When the attention mechanism is replaced by average pooling, NDCG@10 decreases by 9.6%, indicating that adaptive feature-weighted fusion is superior to simple equal-weight aggregation in distinguishing the importance of profile features across different dimensions. The removal of the GRU temporal module reduces MRR from 9.90% to 9.35%, and the push acceptance rate also declines, which validates the timeliness contribution of dynamic interest evolution modeling for capturing users’ current preferences.

In the ablation variant that removes the proactive recommendation timing determination module (w/o PRO), the recommendation ranking metrics remain consistent with the full model (since this module does not affect the ranking score computation), but the push acceptance rate drops sharply from 38.9% to 22.6%, a decline of 42%. This striking contrast reveals the practical application value of the context-aware recommendation timing determination strategy — even when the recommendation content is exactly the same, inappropriate push timing will still be rejected by users, and the interest drift detection-based recommendation timing determination strategy designed in this paper aligns push behavior with the rhythm of users’ intrinsic information needs by identifying the critical window in which readers enter a new research direction exploration period, thereby significantly enhancing the effectiveness of proactive recommendations in practical applications. Discussion and conclusion

Results discussion and key findings

The proactive recommendation system integrating user profiling and knowledge graphs proposed in this paper outperforms existing baseline methods across multiple evaluation dimensions, and the internal logic of this result can be explained from the perspective of the technical architecture. The introduction of the knowledge graph module enables semantic associations among collection resources that extend far beyond traditional metadata descriptions — when this module is removed in the ablation experiment, Recall@10 decreases by 19.5%, the most significant performance decline among all ablation variants, indicating that higher-order semantic connections between resources are the primary driving factor for improving recommendation accuracy. As analyzed in Sect. 4.3, this is because the knowledge graph integrates disciplinary ontologies and citation networks to mine cross-domain resource correlations that traditional metadata-based methods cannot capture, enabling the model to recommend semantically relevant resources beyond direct categorical matches. The contribution of the multi-dimensional user profiling module is reflected in the differentiated gains across user groups of different activity levels: in the low-activity user group, the proposed method achieves a Recall@20 improvement of 7.63% over the best-performing baseline KGCL, far exceeding the 3.12% improvement in the high-activity group. This indicates that profile dimensions such as disciplinary background and static attributes can effectively compensate for the deficiency of interaction signals when behavioral data are scarce, thereby playing a critical buffering role in cold start scenarios. The experimental results of the context-aware proactive recommendation mechanism reveal a problem that has long been overlooked in library recommendation research: the quality of recommendation content and the appropriateness of recommendation timing are two mutually independent and indispensable factors in determining user acceptance. The ablation variant w/o PRO has recommendation ranking scores completely consistent with the full model, yet the push acceptance rate drops sharply from 38.9% to 22.6%, a decrease of as much as 42%. This contrast powerfully demonstrates that even when recommendation content fully matches user needs, push at an inappropriate moment will still be rejected by users, and the interest drift detection-based recommendation timing determination strategy designed in this paper aligns push behavior with the rhythm of users’ intrinsic information needs by identifying the critical window in which readers enter a new research direction exploration period, thereby significantly enhancing the effectiveness of proactive recommendations in practical applications. The inverted-U-shaped curve exhibited by the interest drift threshold in the parameter sensitivity analysis further corroborates this judgment — both overly sensitive push triggering () and overly conservative triggering strategies () lead to declines in acceptance rates; only at can the system achieve the optimal balance between push frequency and push quality.

Research limitations

The experimental data in this paper originate from the operational records of a single university library; although the dataset reaches a scale of tens of thousands of users and millions of interaction records, the disciplinary coverage, reader composition, and collection resource types of this dataset are all constrained by the actual circumstances of a specific university, which introduces certain uncertainty regarding the transferability and generalizability of the experimental conclusions to other types of libraries such as public libraries and specialized libraries. Reader groups in public libraries differ significantly from those in university libraries in terms of the availability of disciplinary background information and the regularity of borrowing behaviors; the disciplinary background feature subspace designed in this paper may require adaptive adjustments or replacement with other available user attribute dimensions in such contexts. It should be noted that the use of a single-dataset experimental setting in this study is primarily due to the privacy-sensitive nature of library operational data — borrowing records, retrieval logs, and reader attribute information all constitute personal sensitive data, and there are currently no publicly available benchmark datasets that simultaneously contain the multi-source heterogeneous data (borrowing records, OPAC logs, electronic resource access behaviors, disciplinary background information, and collection metadata for knowledge graph construction) required by the proposed system. Future work will seek to validate the generalizability of the proposed method through multi-library collaborative experiments or the construction of desensitized benchmark datasets.

At the technical implementation level, the knowledge graph construction process currently relies on a semi-automated pipeline that combines rule-based mapping of MARC fields with entity recognition based on pre-trained language models, meaning that the quality of the knowledge graph is to some extent constrained by the completeness and standardization of collection metadata. When the cataloging quality of collection data is uneven or there are large numbers of missing fields, both the accuracy of entity recognition and the coverage of relation extraction in the knowledge graph may decline, thereby affecting the performance of downstream recommendation tasks. Furthermore, the proactive recommendation timing determination strategy in this paper is primarily based on a single trigger mechanism of interest drift detection and has not yet incorporated more fine-grained contextual factors (such as academic semester stages, examination cycles, and research project milestones); future work can build a richer multi-dimensional context model on this basis to further improve the precision of recommendation timing.

In addition, the proposed system integrates multiple deep learning sub-modules including multi-head attention, GRU, multi-layer GAT, and MLP matching network, resulting in a total of approximately 19.4 million trainable parameters (as shown in Table 8). While this model scale is manageable on high-performance GPU servers, it may pose deployment challenges for small and medium-sized libraries with limited computational resources. Future work should explore model lightweighting strategies, such as knowledge distillation, model pruning, or low-rank approximation, to reduce the parameter count and inference cost while preserving recommendation accuracy, thereby lowering the deployment barrier for resource-constrained library environments.

Furthermore, this paper directly utilizes readers’ original borrowing records, retrieval logs, and access behaviors for user profiling construction and model training without incorporating explicit privacy protection mechanisms. In practical deployment, such behavioral data constitutes personal sensitive information subject to data protection regulations. The raw behavioral sequences may inadvertently reveal readers’ research interests, ideological inclinations, or personal reading preferences, posing potential privacy risks. Future work should integrate privacy-preserving techniques such as differential privacy for noise injection during user profile computation, or federated learning frameworks that enable collaborative model training across multiple libraries without sharing raw user data, thereby achieving a balance between recommendation performance and reader privacy protection.

Future work outlook

In terms of model architecture optimization, the powerful capabilities demonstrated by large language models in natural language understanding and knowledge reasoning provide a new technical pathway for further upgrading library recommendation systems. Future work can explore embedding large language models as enhancement components for knowledge graph completion and user intent understanding within the recommendation framework proposed in this paper, leveraging their deep semantic understanding of academic texts to automatically expand entity relationships in the knowledge graph and generate more precise descriptions of user needs, thereby achieving simultaneous improvements in both the richness of knowledge representations and the granularity of user modeling. In terms of model lightweighting, as discussed in Sect. 5.2, the current model’s parameter scale (approximately 19.4 million) may hinder deployment in resource-constrained library environments; future work should investigate knowledge distillation, model pruning, and low-rank approximation techniques to reduce the computational cost while preserving recommendation accuracy, enabling broader adoption across libraries of varying scales. In terms of privacy protection, as noted in Sect. 5.2, the direct use of raw behavioral data for modeling poses potential privacy risks; federated learning-based collaborative recommendation across multiple libraries is a promising solution to this challenge — multiple libraries can jointly train recommendation models through secure aggregation of model parameters without sharing raw user data, both protecting reader privacy and expanding the knowledge coverage of the system. Additionally, differential privacy mechanisms can be introduced during user profile computation to provide formal privacy guarantees for individual readers.

In terms of application scenario expansion, the technical framework proposed in this paper has the potential for generalization to other knowledge service domains. Scenarios such as digital museums, online education platforms, and academic search engines face the dual challenge of resource semantic association modeling and personalized user need matching; the proactive recommendation approach integrating user profiling and knowledge graphs proposed in this paper can provide a methodological reference for these domains. Future empirical research should also deploy this system in multiple libraries of different types and conduct real-user experiments, collecting user satisfaction feedback and long-term usage behavior data on proactive recommendation services through A/B testing, in order to comprehensively evaluate the service effectiveness and user experience of the system in actual operating environments.
