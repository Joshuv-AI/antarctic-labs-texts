Nisotirostide (LY3457263), a novel NPY2 receptor agonist for type 2 diabetes and obesity: From discovery to clinical proof of concept
Authors: Daniel A Briere, Jennifer K Leohr, Helle Linnebjerg, Avinash Muppidi, Meghan Antonellis, Patrick Antonellis, Todd M Suter, Elizabeth Smith LaBell, Daniel Lopes, Mridula Dogra, Tamer Coskun, Jorge Alsina-Fernandez, Kieren J Mather
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/ObjectivesNisotirostide is a novel selective NPY2 receptor agonist under development as adjunctive therapy for people with type 2 diabetes (T2D) on glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RA).MethodsNisotirostide binding affinity and potency were determined against human NPY2 receptor. Effects of nisotirostide on body weight, food intake, body composition, glucose, and insulin were evaluated in mouse models. A Phase 1 study in healthy participants and participants with T2D on 1.5 mg once-weekly dose of dulaglutide evaluated safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics, and pharmacodynamics, following a single subcutaneous dose of nisotirostide ranging from 0.025 to 1 mg.ResultsNisotirostide potently and selectively activated NPY2 receptor signaling in vitro. In mice, nisotirostide reduced body weight by up to 12% as monotherapy and up to 31% when combined with GLP-1 RA, demonstrating synergism. Nisotirostide-treated mice showed improved glucose control, independent of weight loss. In Phase 1 clinical study (N = 65), PK of nisotirostide supported a once-weekly dosing regimen. A 1 mg dose of nisotirostide in monotherapy reduced mean body weight by 0.75 kg versus placebo (ns); when added to dulaglutide, up to 2.58 kg reduction was observed versus dulaglutide alone (p < 0.05). Gastrointestinal adverse events, such as nausea and vomiting, were the most commonly reported with nisotirostide; these were dose-dependent and mild-to-moderate in severity.ConclusionsNisotirostide demonstrated potent, selective NPY2R agonism with a PK profile supporting once-weekly dosing. Preclinical metabolic benefits, including weight-independent glucose improvements, translated clinically, and a manageable tolerability profile supports further development as adjunctive GLP-1 RA therapy for T2D and obesity.ClinicalTrials.gov number: NCT04641312Keywords: PYY, Type 2 diabetes, NPY receptor, Weight management, Glycemic control

Introduction

Recent developments in the management of type 2 diabetes (T2D) include innovative therapies that target multiple mechanisms to improve patient outcomes [1,2]. Among these, therapeutics based on gut-derived hormones, such as glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP), have led to long-acting GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RA) or dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists (GIP/GLP-1 RA) [3]. These agents have shown significant clinical benefits, including better glycemic control, weight reduction, and improvements in cardiometabolic health [3,4]. Nevertheless, many patients fail to meet or maintain their HbA1c goals over time [5]. In response, emerging therapies utilizing other gut hormones with similar or complementary actions to GLP-1 and/or GIP are being explored for their therapeutic potential for use as adjunctive therapy along with GLP-1 RAs or GIP/GLP-1 RAs [6].

Peptide YY (PYY) is a 36-amino acid gut-derived peptide of the pancreatic polypeptide family that may serve a complementary role to existing GLP-1-based therapies [[7], [8], [9]]. It functions primarily as an anorectic agent to regulate satiation and food intake by binding to five receptors in humans: Neuropeptide Y (NPY) 1 R, NPY2R, NPY4R, NPY5R, and NPY6R [7,10]. PYY1-36 is predominantly secreted during fasting and binds to all NPY receptors, while PYY3-36 is the major postprandial circulating form of PYY. Unlike GLP-1 and GIP, for which DPP-IV cleavage results in inactivation, enzymatic processing of PYY1-36 by DPP-IV generates PYY3-36, which reduces affinity for other NPY receptor subtypes while preserving high affinity for NPY2R, thereby enhancing NPY2R selectivity and bioactivity [7,11,12]. Consequently, whereas PYY1-36 binds all NPY receptor subtypes, PYY3-36 functions as a selective NPY2R agonist, through which it exerts its effects on energy and glucose regulation [7,13]. Correspondingly, an infusion of normal postprandial concentrations of PYY3-36 led to significantly decreased appetite and up to 33% reduction in food intake over 24 h among people with or without obesity [10,14,15].

GLP-1 and PYY3-36 are released together from gastrointestinal L-cells in response to the same stimuli, indicating they may have complementary roles [16]. Co-infusions of native PYY3-36 and GLP-1 have demonstrated synergistic effects on food intake inhibition, weight reduction, and gastric emptying in preclinical and clinical settings, suggesting a potential for combination therapy to similarly augment the effects of GLP-1 in humans [[17], [18], [19]]. Additionally, PYY3-36 is thought to improve insulin sensitivity and regulate glucose homeostasis independently of weight loss [19]. These direct effects may be further augmented by weight reductions whether achieved though PYY3-36 alone or in combination with GLP-1.

Importantly, until recently, studies investigating the potential of PYY3-36 alone or in combination with GLP-1 were limited by the relatively short half-life of the native PYY3-36 (approximately 15 min), which necessitated the use of continuous infusions [20]. As with PYY3-36, the short half-life of native GLP-1 rendered it impractical as a pharmacologic agent. This limitation was overcome through half-life extension in GLP-1 RAs and GIP/GLP-1 RAs, enabling once-weekly subcutaneous dosing [21,22].

Efforts to develop long-acting PYY3-36 analogs were subsequently undertaken to overcome these pharmacokinetic limitations, with the promise that a long-acting analog could enable exploration of combination approaches [17,18,[23], [24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29]]. Variant screening of PYY3-36 to optimize NPY2R selectivity, potency, and peptide stability identified selective NPY2R analogs that improved glucose metabolism in db/db mice. When combined with a semaglutide surrogate, these analogs showed superior glycemic control in diabetic rats and greater than additive reductions in body weight in diet-induced obese (DIO) mice relative to semaglutide surrogate alone [18]. Moreover, a long-acting PYY3−36 analog, PYY1875, in combination with semaglutide induced additional body weight reduction in obese rats, exceeding weight loss seen with high-dose semaglutide [26]. While efficacy with long-acting PYY3-36 analogs and GLP-1 RAs in patients with obesity or overweight is encouraging, there remains an unmet need for treatment options for patients with T2D who are unable to achieve adequate glycemic or weight control with GLP-1 RAs alone. Among the long-acting NPY2R agonists that have been evaluated clinically, BI 1820237 has demonstrated additive effects on gastric emptying when combined with low-dose liraglutide in men with overweight or obesity, though formal efficacy endpoints, such as body weight, were not assessed in this Phase 1 study [30]. Investigating the cooperative efficacy of GLP-1 RAs and other gut peptide-based therapeutics, such as long-acting PYY analogs (NPY2R agonists) in these patients, may help identify potential alternative combination treatments for glycemic control and weight management.

Nisotirostide (LY3457263) is a novel, synthetic peptide analog of PYY3-36 with selective NPY2 receptor agonist activity [31]. To address the inherently short half-life of native PYY3-36, nisotirostide was engineered with a fatty acid conjugate that facilitates reversible binding to serum albumin, thereby prolonging its circulating half-life. Nisotirostide is under development as an adjunctive therapy for people with T2D on established GLP-1 RA therapy. Here, we describe the receptor affinity, pharmacokinetics, and in vivo characterization of nisotirostide, followed by results from a first-in-human Phase 1 clinical trial (NCT04641312).

Methods

Preclinical methodology

Nisotirostide, PYY3-36, and a surrogate of dulaglutide were synthesized at Eli Lilly and Company using automated peptide synthesizers. In vitro cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) accumulation, [35S]GTPγS recruitment/binding, and competitive radioligand binding assays were performed to determine functional potency (EC50) and binding affinity (Ki) of nisotirostide for the human NPY receptor 2 (NPYR2). Radioligand binding was also performed for the mouse NPY receptors. Receptor selectivity for nisotirostide was assessed using radioligand binding assays for the human NPY receptors NPYR1, NPYR4, and NPYR5. For binding, cAMP and GTPγS assays, nisotirostide was solubilized in dimethyl sulfoxide. All assays were performed in the absence of albumin to allow direct comparison to native peptides without the confounding influence of albumin binding. Pharmacodynamic (PD) studies were performed in lean C57 B l/6 mice, db/db mice, and DIO C57BL/6NTac mice to assess treatment effects on food intake, body weight change, body composition, fasting glucose and insulin, and Homeostatic Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR). Lean mass and fat mass were quantified using quantitative nuclear magnetic resonance (QNMR). Additional experimental details are provided in the Supplemental Methods.

Clinical methodology

Clinical study design and participants

A Phase 1, double-blnd, placebo-controlled, randomized, single-ascending dose study was conducted in healthy participants (Part A) and in participants with T2D on a stable dose of dulaglutide (Part B) at two centers in Germany. Single subcutaneous (SC) doses of nisotirostide were administered in five dose levels in Part A and three in Part B. Part A enrolled healthy participants aged 20–45 years with body mass index (BMI) 18.5–35 kg/m2. Part B enrolled participants aged 20–68 years with T2D diagnosed ≥6 months before screening, BMI 27–40 kg/m2, stable body weight for 3 months (<5% change), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) 7.0–9.5% (53–80.3 mmol/mol), and receiving a stable dose of metformin, or DPP-4 inhibitor with or without metformin, or a GLP-1 receptor agonist (liraglutide, immediate release exenatide, lixisenatide, or dulaglutide) with or without metformin for at least 3 months prior to screening. Individuals who were not on a stable treatment of 1.5 mg once-weekly (QW) dulaglutide for at least 3 months required a run-in period. Participants receiving a GLP-1 receptor agonist other than dulaglutide, or a DPP-4 inhibitor, discontinued that treatment upon initiating dulaglutide; DPP-4 inhibitors were subject to an appropriate washout period before study drug administration. Participants not previously on dulaglutide received 0.75 mg for 1 week, followed by 1.5 mg QW for 5 weeks (6-week run-in period). Participants already on a stable 0.75 mg QW dose of dulaglutide underwent a 5-week run-in period with 1.5 mg QW dulaglutide. Individuals with a history of or current cardiovascular, respiratory, hepatic, renal, gastrointestinal, endocrine (apart from T2D in Part B), hematological, or neurological disorders, or who had undergone any form of bariatric surgery were excluded. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Randomization, masking, and study design

Participants were randomly assigned to receive either nisotirostide or placebo. Part A evaluated single SC doses of nisotirostide (0.025=1 mg) or placebo in five consecutive cohorts in healthy participants. Part B evaluated single SC doses of nisotirostide (0.075=1 mg) or placebo in three cohorts in participants with T2D on 1.5 mg QW dulaglutide. Each cohort included 6 participants receiving nisotirostide and two receiving placebo. The investigator and participants remained blnded to treatment assignment throughout the study. Nisotirostide was administered as 1 mg/2 mL formulations with volume-matched placebo (0.9% sodium chloride). Dulaglutide was administered as 1.5 mg/0.5 mL single-use pen.

Clinical outcomes

The primary objective of the study was to investigate the safety and tolerability of single dose of nisotirostide in healthy participants (Part A) and in participants with T2D on a stable dose of dulaglutide (Part B) by assessing treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) and serious adverse events (SAEs). Additional safety assessments included laboratory assessments, vital signs (systolic and diastolic blood pressure [SBP, DBP], pulse rate), and electrocardiograms (QT interval corrected using Fridericia's formula [QTcF]). Injection site reactions (ISRs) were evaluated using prospective, solicited assessments at multiple predetermined timepoints post-dose. Pain, as part of the ISR evaluation, was assessed using a visual analog scale (VAS).

A secondary objective was to characterize the pharmacokinetics (PK) of nisotirostide. The exploratory objective was to assess the pharmacodynamics (PD) of nisotirostide in terms of changes in body weight, oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), and gastric emptying.

Plasma samples obtained in the study were assayed for nisotirostide concentrations using a validated liquid chromatography high-resolution accurate mass spectrometry method. Body weight assessments were performed on Day 1 (prior to nisotirostide dosing) and throughout the study period. Gastric emptying and OGTT assessments were performed on Day −1 (baseline; prior to nisotirostide dosing) and Day 3, after the maximum observed drug concentration (Cmax) of nisotirostide.

The Phase 1 clinical trial (NCT04641312) was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences International Ethical Guidelines, and the International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practices Guideline. Statistical analyses

Preclinical studies

In vitro data are shown as geometric mean and standard error of the mean (SE), while in vivo data are presented as the mean ± SE. For in vivo studies, groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett's test, with vehicle as the comparator unless noted. In chronic studies, statistical comparisons evaluated differences on the last day of treatment. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05. Formal evaluation of synergism employed the Bliss Independence method [32]. If the interaction contrast test and all pairwise comparisons between the control, each single agent-treated group, and the combination-treated group were statistically significant (p < 0.05), and the observed combination mean percent bodyweight change from baseline was more negative than the expected additive response, then the combination effect was declared greater than additive (synergistic).

Analyses of clinical data

The sample size used was customary for Phase 1 studies that evaluate safety and tolerability endpoints and was not based on planned statistical inferences for pharmacodynamic data.

Safety parameters were summarized using standard descriptive statistics, and analyses were conducted for all enrolled participants who received study intervention, regardless of whether they completed all protocol requirements. PK analyses were conducted using standard noncompartmental methods in Phoenix® Version 8.1 among participants who received a nisotirostide dose and had measurable nisotirostide concentrations.

Log-transformed Cmax and area under the concentration curve (AUC(0-∞)) of nisotirostide were evaluated using a power model, where log-dose acted as an explanatory variable. Estimate ratios of dose-normalized least square geometric mean and 90% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. To assess gastric emptying, 1 g of acetaminophen was administered for the determination of Cmax and AUC using standard noncompartmental analysis. The ratio of the Cmax and AUC were calculated on Day 3 compared to baseline (Day-1). These baseline-adjusted Cmax and AUC were log-transformed and analyzed using an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) model to evaluate treatment effects. For OGTT, participants were administered 75 g of glucose and the glucose and insulin postprandial excursion (change from premeal AUC0–2hr) were calculated by trapezoidal rule using standard noncompartmental analysis. The ratio was calculated for the post-dose assessment on Day 3 compared to baseline (Day −1).

Role of the funding source

Eli Lilly and Company funded the study. The study sponsor was involved in the study design, data collection, data review, data analysis, and report drafting.

Results

Chemical structure and preclinical characterization of nisotirostide

Nisotirostide (LY3457263) is a single fatty acid acylated PYY3-36 analog consisting of a C-terminally amidated linear polypeptide with 34 amino acids in the main chain with approximately 80% sequence identity to native PYY3-36 (Figure 1A). Residues are numbered according to native human PYY1-36. The main chain is conjugated at the position 7 lysine residue to one ε-aminohexanoate unit (Ahx), one α-Glu residue (Glu), one γ-Glu residue (γGlu), and a saturated linear 18-carbon diacid (C18 diacid), which facilitates albumin binding as a pharmacokinetic half-life extension while permitting desired pharmacological properties to enable once-weekly subcutaneous dosing. The amino acid substitution profile was optimized to enhance NPY2R selectivity over NPY1R, NPY4R, and NPY5R relative to native PYY3-36. Nisotirostide is a single enantiomer, comprised of amino acids with (S) configuration only.

To assess the intrinsic potency of nisotirostide for the native PYY3-36 receptor, cells expressing human NPY2R (hNPY2R) were used in cAMP accumulation assays as well as [35S]GTPγS recruitment/binding assays. Figure 1B,C shows representative concentration response curves comparing native PYY3-36 with nisotirostide on hNPY2R, assessed through a functional cAMP and [35S]GTPγS recruitment/binding assay, respectively. Table S1 presents the functional potency of nisotirostide for hNPY2R in repeated studies. In the functional cAMP assay, nisotirostide (IC50: 75.9 pM, SEM = 4.4, n = 3) had similar potency for hNPY2R as native PYY3-36 (IC50: 138 pM, SEM = 27, n = 3). In the [35S]GTPγS recruitment/binding assay, nisotirostide (EC50: 78.6pM, SEM = 11.8, n = 4) showed higher potency for the human NPY2 receptor than native PYY3-36 (EC50: 478 pM, SEM = 36, n = 4). Together, these demonstrate that nisotirostide has high potency and affinity at hNPY2R.

To demonstrate selectivity for hNPY2R versus other NPY receptors, competitive radioligand binding assays for hNPY2R, hNPY1R, hNPY4R, and hNPY5R were utilized. Figure 1D–G shows representative concentration response curves for native PYY3-36 versus nisotirostide on hNPY2R (Figure 1D), hNPY1R (Figure 1E), hNPY4R (Figure 1F), and hNPY5R (Figure 1G). Table S2 presents the binding affinities of nisotirostide for human NPY receptors in repeated studies. In line with both cAMP and [35S]GTPγS recruitment/binding assays, nisotirostide (Ki: 11.0 pM, SEM = 2.6, n = 5) had similar competitive radioligand binding to hNPY2R as PYY3-36 (Ki: 8.99, SEM = 1.17, n = 5), but enhanced selectivity from other NPY receptors. At hNPY1R, nisotirostide (Ki: 420,000 pM, SEM= NC, n = 1/3) had >450-fold reduced affinity versus PYY3-36 (Ki: 877 pM, SEM = 51, n = 3). At hNPY4R, nisotirostide (Ki: 6,260,000 pM, SEM = 233,000, n = 3) had >30-fold reduced affinity versus PYY3-36 (Ki: 18,700 pM, SEM = 3990, n = 4). At hNPY5R, nisotirostide (Ki: 59,000 pM, SEM = 12,900, n = 4) had >20-fold reduced affinity verses PYY3-36 (Ki: 2740 pM, SEM = 806, n = 4). Together, these competitive binding data demonstrate that nisotirostide has similar affinity to human NPY2R as native PYY3-36, while being highly selective from other human NPY receptors relative to the native ligand PYY3-36.

In support of utilizing nisotirostide in mouse models, binding assays were performed for mouse NPY receptors. Nisotirostide had similar affinity for mouse NPY2R (Ki: 3.9 pM, SEM = 0.6, n = 7) as PYY3-36 (Ki: 5.1 pM, SEM = 1.7, n = 8) but similar (mNPY4R) or reduced (mNPY1R and mNPY5R) affinity for other NPY mouse receptors compared to native PYY3-36 (Table S3).

Initial profiling of the physiological effects of nisotirostide was performed in lean animals by measuring changes in food intake and body weight for 3 days following a single SC injection of 10, 30, or 100 nmol/kg. Nisotirostide showed effects on reducing cumulative food intake at the 2 highest doses and led to a dose-responsive reduction in body weight (Fig. S1).

Dose-dependent reductions in food intake were observed following nisotirostide treatment for up to 4 days post treatment, while combinations of nisotirostide and the dulaglutide surrogate showed prolonged suppression of food intake for as long as 8 days post treatment (Figure 2A). Vehicle treatment resulted in a mean increase in body weights of +3%, dulaglutide surrogate alone showed no change (0%) from starting body weight (not significant versus vehicle), while nisotirostide treatment alone showed dose-dependent reductions in body weight of −1% (1 nmol/kg; not significant versus vehicle), −3% (3 nmol/kg; p < 0.05 versus vehicle), and −12% (10 nmol/kg; p < 0.05 versus vehicle). Notably, combined treatment of nisotirostide and the dulaglutide surrogate resulted in body weight changes of −18% (1 nmol/kg; p < 0.05 versus vehicle), −26% (3 nmol/kg; p < 0.05 versus vehicle), and −31% (10 nmol/kg; p < 0.05 versus vehicle), demonstrating synergistic changes in body weight versus either treatment alone; synergy confirmed by statistical methods (Figure 2B). To further showcase the synergism, final body weight reductions of cohorts treated with vehicle, nisotirostide at 3 nmol/kg, dulaglutide surrogate at 1 nmol/kg, or co-administration of nisotirostide at 3 nmol/kg and dulaglutide surrogate at 1 nmol/kg were shown, alongside a calculated sum of effects of nisotirostide or dulaglutide surrogate alone (Figure 2C). Additionally, DIO mice treated with nisotirostide alone or in combination with the dulaglutide surrogate demonstrated improvements in body composition, with the majority of body weight loss coming from fat mass as opposed to lean mass (Figure 2D).

A HOMA-IR score was then calculated (G). P < 0.05 using one-way ANOVA versus vehicle (⁎). All data are expressed as mean +/− SEM with n = 6.

Following 14 days of treatment, only the 10 nmol/kg dose of nisotirostide reduced body weight (p < 0.05 versus vehicle) (Figure 2E). However, all tested doses of nisotirostide (1, 3, and 10 nmol/kg) showed significant (p < 0.05 versus vehicle) improvements in fasting glucose (Figure 2F), fasting insulin (Figure 2G), and resulting HOMA-IR (Figure 2H). Dulaglutide surrogate treatment (1 nmol/kg) resulted in no significant change in fasting body weight, glucose, insulin, or HOMA-IR. Nisotirostide at 1 and 3 nmol/kg showed significant (p < 0.05 versus vehicle) improvements in fasting glucose, insulin, and HOMA-IR, despite no significant change in fasting body weight, suggestive of a potential weight-independent improvement in glucose control with nisotirostide (Figure 2E–H).

To demonstrate the effects of nisotirostide on weight and glucose control in a hyperglycemic mouse model, we performed dose–response studies using diabetic db/db mice (Fig. S2). As observed with DIO mice, chronic treatment with nisotirostide in db/db mice resulted in dose-dependent reductions in daily food intake, with significant (p < 0.05 versus vehicle) reductions observed at 10 and 30 nmol/kg (Fig. S2A), and significant body weight lowering (p < 0.05 versus vehicle) with nisotirostide doses of 3, 10, and 30 nmol/kg (Fig. S2B). Nisotirostide at 10 and 30 nmol/kg showed significant daily glucose lowering (p < 0.05 versus vehicle), with reductions appearing as early as 1-day post-dose (Fig. S2C). Following chronic treatment of nisotirostide for 10 days, db/db mice were fasted overnight, followed by assessment of fasting components on Day 11. Nisotirostide at 1 and 3 nmol/kg resulted in no significant change in fasting body weight (Fig. S2D) or fasting glucose (Fig. S2E), however displayed a significant change (p < 0.05 versus vehicle) in fasting insulin (Fig. S2F) and HOMA-IR (Fig. S2G). Nisotirostide doses of 10 and 30 nmol/kg significantly lowered fasting body weight, glucose, insulin, and subsequently HOMA-IR (p < 0.05 versus vehicle). Lower doses of nisotirostide showed improvements in fasting insulin and HOMA-IR, despite no significant changes in fasting body weights, potentially indicating a weight-independent improvement in glucose control.

Nisotirostide in healthy participants and participants with T2D

Participant disposition

The study enrolled participants across two parts (Figure 3). In Part A, 43 healthy participants were enrolled, 41 received nisotirostide or placebo, and 40 completed the study (Figure 4A). In Part B, all 24 enrolled participants received nisotirostide or placebo and completed the study (Figure 4B).

Pharmacokinetics

Maximum nisotirostide concentrations (Cmax) occurred within 27–52 h post-dose (Table 2A and B), with a mean terminal half-life of approximately 8–10 days. Intersubject variability in Cmax and AUC(0-∞) was 34% or less. Dose proportionality was confirmed across the evaluated dose range in both healthy participants and participants with T2D, with slope estimates for AUC(0-∞) of 1.04 (90% CI: 0.84, 1.28) and Cmax of 0.95 (90% CI: 0.77, 1.18) in healthy participants, and AUC(0-∞) of 1.10 (90% CI: 0.87, 1.38) and Cmax of 1.11 (90% CI: 0.95, 1.29) in participants with T2D. Small differences in pharmacokinetic parameters were observed between healthy participants and participants with T2D, likely attributable to body weight differences.

Participants who required dulaglutide titration completed a 6-week lead–in period prior to nisotirostide dosing in Part B, during which body weight decreased by approximately 2 kg before stabilizing prior to nisotirostide dosing, ensuring that subsequent body weight changes could be attributed to the addition of nisotirostide (Fig. S3).

Nisotirostide added to dulaglutide resulted in dose-dependent reductions in body weight (Figure 5B). A single dose of 1 mg nisotirostide +1.5 mg dulaglutide reduced body weight compared with placebo +1.5 mg dulaglutide from Day 4 through Day 42, with statistically significant placebo-adjusted differences on Days 7 (−1.49 kg; 95% CI: −2.71, −0.27), 14 (−2.00 kg; 95% CI: −3.35, −0.65), and 28 (−2.58 kg; 95% CI: −4.23, −0.93); differences at other timepoints did not reach statistical significance.

Oral glucose tolerance test

Nisotirostide treatment did not significantly alter postprandial glucose or insulin excursion compared with placebo in healthy participants (data not shown).

In participants with T2D, the addition of nisotirostide to dulaglutide reduced postprandial glucose excursion compared to placebo + dulaglutide at the 0.25 mg and 1 mg dose levels, while no reduction was observed at 0.075 mg (Figure 5C). The 1 mg nisotirostide +1.5 mg dulaglutide group had an approximately 22% points lower postprandial glucose excursion compared to placebo + dulaglutide; formal statistical comparison of glucose AUC was not performed.

Postprandial insulin excursion ratios were comparable for the 0.075 and 0.25 mg nisotirostide +1.5 mg dulaglutide compared and placebo +1.5 mg dulaglutide group, while the 1 mg dose +1.5 mg dulaglutide had a numerically smaller insulin excursion ratio compared to the placebo + dulaglutide group (0.99 vs 1.41) (Figure 5D).

Gastric emptying

In healthy participants, no notable effects on gastric emptying were observed at nisotirostide doses ≤0.075 mg. Acetaminophen Cmax decreased by approximately 20% at nisotirostide dose levels of 0.25 mg and 0.5 mg, and by 27% at the 1 mg dose level, compared to placebo (p < 0.05) (Figure 5E and Table S4). Acetaminophen AUC or tmax were not impacted.

In participants with T2D receiving placebo +1.5 mg dulaglutide, no discernible effects on acetaminophen Cmax, AUC, or tmax were observed on Day 3 compared to baseline day, consistent with the known attenuation of GLP-1-mediated gastric emptying slowing with prolonged dulaglutide exposure.

When nisotirostide was added to dulaglutide, acetaminophen Cmax was reduced by approximately 19–20% on Day 3 at doses of 0.25 mg and 1 mg compared to placebo +1.5 mg dulaglutide; however, these differences did not reach statistical significance (Figure 5F and Table S4). Acetaminophen AUC or tmax were not impacted.

Safety profile

In healthy participants (Part A), TEAEs were reported in 17 (41.5%) participants, and all were mild-to-moderate in severity. Dose-related trends were observed with higher frequency of TEAEs at the higher nisotirostide doses. TEAEs reported in more than two participants were nausea, vomiting, and headache (Table 3). Local ISRs, assessed by prospective injection site evaluation, were reported in 16 (39.0%) participants, with pain as the most frequent ISR.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that nisotirostide is a novel, long-acting NPY2R agonist with potent and selective activity at the human NPY2R and comparable receptor affinity to native PYY3-36. In preclinical models, nisotirostide demonstrated dose-dependent glucose lowering effects in diabetic db/db mice within one day of treatment. Acute and chronic administration reduced food intake and body weight across various mouse models, and combination treatment with a surrogate of dulaglutide produced synergistic body weight reductions in DIO mice. Nisotirostide also significantly improved fasting glucose, fasting insulin, and HOMA-IR in non-diabetic DIO mice and obese diabetic db/db mice at doses that did not produce significant weight loss, suggesting metabolic benefits independent of weight reduction. While this preclinical glycemic dataset was intended to characterize nisotirostide and was not designed to provide comprehensive mechanistic data, future studies could evaluate this relationship.

Consistent with the preclinical profile, the Phase 1 clinical study demonstrated effects of nisotirostide on body weight, postprandial glucose metabolism, and gastric emptying, with the most pronounced effects observed when added to dulaglutide in participants with T2D. A single 1 mg dose of nisotirostide added to dulaglutide resulted in a maximum placebo-adjusted body weight reduction of −2.58 kg in participants with T2D, while monotherapy in healthy participants resulted in a numerically smaller reduction of 0.75 kg that did not reach statistical significance. The extent to which the combination effects reflect complementary or additive contributions of nisotirostide beyond dulaglutide alone warrants further clinical evaluation.

The observed body weight reductions were accompanied by improvements in postprandial glucose excursion along with a numerically lower insulin excursion (0.99 vs 1.41) with the 1 mg dose of nisotirostide in participants with T2D, though these observations are based on a small number of participants at this dose level and should be interpreted accordingly. Two mechanisms could contribute to this glycemic improvement. First, the dose-dependent gastric emptying delay observed with nisotirostide may reduce the rate of postprandial glucose appearance, thereby attenuating postprandial excursions. Second, preclinical data demonstrated improvements in fasting glucose, fasting insulin, and HOMA-IR at doses that did not produce significant weight loss, suggesting a weight-independent mechanism of glycemic benefit that may contribute to the clinical observations. Together, these findings suggest that the glycemic improvements observed after a single dose of nisotirostide may extend beyond what would be expected from weight loss alone, though confirmation in a multiple-dose study with adequate sample sizes will be needed.

Nisotirostide reduced acetaminophen absorption surrogate of gastric emptying. Acetaminophen Cmax was significantly reduced by approximately 20–27% for nisotirostide doses ≥0.25 mg in healthy participants. Acetaminophen AUC was largely unchanged across doses, indicating a slowing of gastric emptying rate without impacting the extent of total absorption. In participants with T2D receiving background dulaglutide treatment, similar acetaminophen Cmax reductions were observed at the 0.25 mg and 1 mg doses, though these did not reach statistical significance, likely reflecting the small group sizes in Part B. The magnitude of gastric emptying effects observed with nisotirostide is comparable to that reported for long-acting GLP-1 RAs after multiple doses [33].

Multiple NPY2R agonist programs have now demonstrated that combining PYY-pathway activation with GLP-1-based therapies enhances metabolic outcomes, both preclinically and clinically [26,30,34]. In mice, while the magnitude of NPY2R agonist monotherapy effects varied across the programs, all three clinical-stage NPY2R agonists — nisotirostide, PYY1875, and BI 1820237 — showed greater body weight reduction in combination with GLP-1 RAs than with either agent alone [26,34]. Together, these reports highlight the additive/synergistic potential of NPY2R agonism along with GLP-1-based therapies on metabolic disease in rodents.

When evaluated in humans, the Phase 1 study with PYY1875 showed transient reductions in body weight with no dose response when added to semaglutide in people with obesity or overweight but without T2D [26]. There are several key differences in study design that limit direct comparison with the present study. The PYY1875 study enrolled people with obesity or overweight but without T2D, whereas the present study evaluated people with obesity or overweight and T2D, a population that typically exhibits an attenuated response to incretin-based weight loss therapy. Furthermore, in the Phase 1 PYY1875 study, the GLP-1 RA (semaglutide) and PYY analog were initiated simultaneously as an initial combination, whereas in the present study, participants stabilized on dulaglutide during a 6-week lead–in period prior to the addition of nisotirostide. This add-on approach ensured that body weight had plateaued on GLP-1 RA therapy before nisotirostide was introduced, allowing the incremental effect of NPY2R agonism to be more clearly distinguished. BI 1820237 demonstrated additive gastric emptying effects with low-dose liraglutide in men with overweight or obesity [30]. To our knowledge, nisotirostide is the first NPY2R agonist to demonstrate statistically significant body weight reduction and glycemic improvement in participants with T2D on established GLP-1 RA therapy, directly supporting its utility as an adjunctive treatment and isolating the incremental contribution of NPY2R agonism beyond a GLP-1 RA alone. The −2.58 kg body weight reduction observed after a single dose of nisotirostide added to dulaglutide is encouraging in the context of a T2D population, where even modest weight reductions carry disproportionate metabolic benefit and where the attenuated response to weight loss pharmacotherapy sets a higher threshold for demonstrating incremental efficacy. While this single dose effect falls short of the 5–10% reductions associated with sustained improvements in glycemic control and cardiovascular risk [30], it provides a promising foundation for multiple-dose studies at higher dose levels where cumulative weight loss effects would be expected. The combination of early weight loss and potentially additive glycemic effects may be particularly valuable in a population where metabolic burden is high.

The pharmacokinetic profile of nisotirostide, characterized across the dose range evaluated, demonstrated a prolonged half-life of 8–10 days, consistent with its fatty acid conjugation and supporting once-weekly subcutaneous administration. This dosing frequency aligns with current GLP-1 RA treatment paradigms and may facilitate patient adherence in a combination regimen. Across these studies, the tolerability of NPY2R agonists has been a consistent challenge [26,30,35]. Earlier PYY analogs were associated with a high incidence of treatment-emergent adverse events, particularly gastrointestinal events, among people with obesity or overweight [35]. Similarly, BI 1820237 was associated with transient nausea and vomiting at higher doses in men with overweight or obesity [30], and Wulff et al. reported that PYY1875 was not well tolerated, with gastrointestinal AEs common among participants receiving the PYY3-36 analog [26]. In the present study, gastrointestinal AEs with nisotirostide were mild-to-moderate in severity and dose-dependent. Following a single dose, the combination with dulaglutide did not appear to meaningfully exacerbate the gastrointestinal burden beyond what is expected with GLP-1 RA therapy alone. While these findings are encouraging, this was a single dose study, and the tolerability profile with repeated administration remains to be established. Titration strategies will be explored in subsequent studies to optimize tolerability at higher dose levels.

The data presented here have several limitations. Preclinical experiments relied on animal models that may not fully replicate human physiology. In particular, differences in NPY2R selectivity and receptor biology between mice and humans mean that findings may be directional rather than quantitatively predictive. The clinical study was a single dose study with a relatively small sample size, and the durability of the observed pharmacodynamic effects with repeated dosing remains to be established. Sample sizes were small overall, and particularly so for pharmacodynamic endpoints in Part B, where group sizes for OGTT and gastric emptying assessments were as small as n = 3, limiting the statistical power to detect differences and the reliability of point estimates and should be considered strictly hypothesis-generating. The use of different populations to evaluate monotherapy and combination effects limits conclusions about whether the combined effects of nisotirostide and dulaglutide are additive, synergistic, or driven by disease-related differences in drug response. The formal claim of synergy in this manuscript therefore rests on the preclinical DIO mouse data, where synergy was evaluated using the Bliss Independence method in a controlled within-study design, and is not inferred from cross-population comparisons between Parts A and B of the clinical study. The acetaminophen absorption method used to assess gastric emptying, while widely used, has inherent variability that may have limited the ability to detect significant differences. Future multiple-ascending-dose and Phase 2 studies in larger populations will be needed to confirm these findings and establish the optimal dose, dosing regimen, and clinical benefit of nisotirostide in combination with GLP-1 RA therapy. Such studies should also include a nisotirostide monotherapy arm in patients to more directly characterize the contribution of NPY2R agonism independent of background GLP-1 RA therapy.

Conclusion

In summary, nisotirostide demonstrated potent and selective NPY2R agonism with a pharmacokinetic profile supporting once-weekly administration. The preclinical metabolic benefits, including weight-independent improvements in glucose control, translated to the clinical setting, where nisotirostide added to dulaglutide produced significant reductions in body weight in participants with T2D following a single dose. These findings, together with a manageable tolerability profile, support the clinical development of nisotirostide as adjunctive therapy to GLP-1 RA based treatment for people with T2D and obesity.
