Reinforcement of skin flap tracheal reconstruction with porous titanium nickelide implants: A novel approach to persistent tracheal defect closure
Authors: Evgeny B Topolnitskiy, Alex A Volinsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe study objective was to evaluate the safety and efficacy of porous titanium nickelide mesh implants for reinforcing skin flap reconstruction of persistent tracheal defects after prolonged tracheostomy and laryngotracheal reconstruction.MethodsThis retrospective case series included 60 patients with persistent tracheal defects treated between June 2013 and December 2020. An experimental group (n = 17) received external porous titanium nickelide implant reinforcement, and a control group (n = 43) underwent conventional skin flap closure without reinforcement. The experimental group was characterized by significantly more severe tracheomalacia (P < .001), reflecting the nonrandomized, clinician-directed selection protocol. Primary outcomes included flap stability, airway patency, and complication rates. Secondary outcomes included granulation tissue formation and need for revision surgery.ResultsAll 17 experimental group patients achieved stable tracheal reconstruction without implant-related complications (erosion, infection, or displacement) during a median follow-up of 48 months (range, 12-120 months). Flap instability on dynamic bronchoscopy was absent in the experimental group versus 18.6% in controls (P = .046). Granulation tissue formation occurred more frequently in the experimental group (58.8% vs 27.9%; P = .047). However, all cases were limited in size, did not compromise airway patency, and were successfully managed with a single session of argon plasma coagulation. No restenosis occurred in either group.ConclusionsPorous titanium nickelide reinforcement provides durable biomechanical support for skin flap tracheal reconstruction, preventing flap instability and maintaining airway patency in a higher-risk cohort selected for severe tracheomalacia. This technique is a safe and effective option when primary tracheal resection is not feasible. The selection bias inherent to this nonrandomized series should be considered when interpreting superiority claims.Key Words: persistent tracheal stoma, shape memory alloy, titanium nickelide, tracheal reconstruction, tracheal stenosis, tracheostomy complications

Reinforcement of skin flap tracheal reconstruction with porous NiTi implants.

Central Message

Porous titanium nickelide reinforcement stabilizes skin-flap tracheal reconstruction and maintains airway patency in patients with persistent tracheal defects and tracheomalacia.

Perspective

Persistent tracheal defects after prolonged cannulation present significant surgical challenges. When primary resection is not feasible, skin flap closure reinforced with porous titanium nickelide implants offers a viable alternative that prevents collapse and maintains long-term airway stability.

Prolonged tracheostomy cannulation is a necessary intervention for patients requiring extended mechanical ventilation or airway protection, but chronic cannulation can produce persistent anterior tracheal wall defects that fail to close spontaneously after decannulation.1, 2, 3, 4 These defects disrupt breathing, impair phonation, and create a pathway for aspiration and infection.5 They frequently arise as the terminal stage of laryngotracheal reconstruction for cicatricial tracheal stenosis, itself a well-recognized complication of prolonged intubation occurring in approximately 10 to 20% of affected patients.6, 7, 8, 9

Circular tracheal resection with primary anastomosis remains the gold standard for tracheal stenosis,10,11 but persistent defects with extensive anterior wall destruction and preserved lateral and posterior walls are often not amenable to resection.12 In such cases, anterior wall reconstruction using local skin-muscle flaps is required. This approach, originally described by Lawson and Grillo13 in 1970, faces the fundamental challenge of providing adequate structural rigidity to prevent collapse during inspiration, particularly in patients with tracheomalacia or significant cartilage loss.14 Autologous cartilage grafts address the structural deficit but carry donor site morbidity, graft resorption risk, and limited availability for larger defects.15,16 Reconstruction with titanium plates has been described but carries concerns regarding rigidity-induced erosion.17 Tissue engineering approaches remain largely experimental.18, 19, 20

Nickel-titanium (NiTi) shape memory alloys possess superelasticity, corrosion resistance, and excellent biocompatibility that have supported their clinical use in cardiovascular and orthopedic applications.21,22 In tracheal surgery, NiTi has been used primarily as intraluminal stents, which are associated with granulation overgrowth, migration, and erosion.23, 24, 25, 26 An extraluminal porous NiTi scaffold offers a paradigm shift: structural support without intraluminal contact. Luo and colleagues27 demonstrated in a canine model that NiTi mesh patches successfully supported tracheal wall reconstruction with fibrous tissue ingrowth and no migration. Building on this, we developed a clinical protocol using porous NiTi implants fabricated by self-propagating high-temperature synthesis to reinforce skin flap reconstruction.28,29

The objective of this study was to evaluate the safety, efficacy, and long-term outcomes of this reinforcement strategy in patients with persistent tracheal defects after prolonged cannulation and laryngotracheal reconstruction.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Ethics

This retrospective case series was conducted at Siberian State Medical University from June 2013 to December 2020 (Institutional Review Board Protocol #18/1152/2013, dated April 22, 2013). All patients provided written informed consent. The study conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patient Selection

Inclusion criteria included the following: (1) persistent tracheal defect with failure of spontaneous closure more than 4 weeks after decannulation; (2) anterior wall defect 1.5 cm or more craniocaudally as the final stage of laryngotracheal reconstruction for cicatricial stenosis after T-tube stent removal; (3) intact lateral and posterior tracheal walls; (4) absence of active tracheobronchitis; and (5) 6 months or more from initial tracheostomy indication. Exclusion criteria included concurrent tracheoesophageal fistula, circumferential stenosis requiring resection, malignant airway disease, active stomal infection, severe comorbidities precluding general anesthesia, and inadequate tissue quality for flap harvest.

Sixty patients met inclusion criteria. The decision to use NiTi reinforcement was based on clinical assessment of anterior wall instability and tracheomalacia severity: The key criterion was the adequacy of skin flaps combined with insufficient muscle reinforcement or elevated risk of postreconstruction skin flap collapse. This selection was nonrandomized and based on individual surgeon judgment. Seventeen patients received reinforced reconstruction (experimental group); 43 patients underwent conventional flap closure (control group). The experimental group had significantly more severe tracheomalacia (P < .001), a selection bias discussed below.

Implant Fabrication

Porous NiTi implants were manufactured via self-propagating high-temperature synthesis.28,29 Implants were fabricated as arched mesh structures to conform to anterior tracheal wall curvature; dimensions (typically 50 mm × 10 mm × 1 mm) were customized from preoperative computed tomography (CT) measurements and trimmed intraoperatively. Implants were sterilized by autoclaving at 134 °C for 18 minutes.

Surgical Technique

Step 1: Defect assessment. Size and extent of the anterior wall defect were documented.

Step 2: Flap design. In the experimental group, cutaneous flaps were designed for implant interposition; in the control group, skin-muscle-cutaneous flaps were harvested. Flaps provided approximately 5 mm overlap on all defect margins.

Step 3: Internal lining. The medial skin surface was rotated inward to line the reconstructed tracheal segment. Edges were sutured to tracheal mucosa with interrupted 4-0 polyglactin sutures, knots tied externally to minimize intraluminal granulation.

Step 4: Implant placement (experimental group). The arched NiTi implant was positioned on the external surface of the rotated flap and secured with absorbable sutures to lateral walls and peritracheal tissues without penetrating the tracheal lumen.

Step 5: External closure. Bilateral flap skin surfaces were approximated with interrupted 3-0 polyglactin (muscle layer) and 4-0 nylon (skin). In the experimental group, the implant was fully covered by subcutaneous tissue and skin. No drains were placed.

Postoperative Management and Follow-up

All patients received perioperative cefazolin (2 g IV at induction, 1 g every 8 hours × 48 hours), proton pump inhibitors, humidified oxygen, nebulized bronchodilators and mucolytics, and early chest physiotherapy. Patients were extubated in the operating room and monitored for 24 hours before ward transfer.

Follow-up visits occurred at 2 weeks, 1, 3, 6, and 12 months, and then annually. Each visit included clinical assessment, flexible laryngotracheoscopy, and spirometry. CT imaging was performed at 6 months and as clinically indicated. The primary outcome was stable airway patency without restenosis requiring intervention. Secondary outcomes included complications (infection, emphysema, implant exposure/displacement), granulation tissue formation, and need for revision surgery.

Outcome Classification

Good: Complete defect closure, stable airway without stridor, normal voice, no respiratory limitation during daily activities.

Satisfactory: Closed defect with minimal exertional stridor, acceptable voice, minor limitation not requiring intervention.

Unsatisfactory: Persistent stomal opening, significant stridor, or restenosis requiring additional surgery or chronic cannulation.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median with range. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons used Fisher exact test (categorical) and Mann–Whitney U test (continuous). All analyses were performed using R version 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Patient Demographics

Sixty patients were included. The experimental group (n = 17) comprised 9 women and 8 men, median age 54 years (range, 28-71). The control group (n = 43) comprised 24 women and 19 men, median age 51 years (range. 21-73). Groups were comparable in age (P = .42), sex (P = .89), cannulation duration (P = .38), and defect size (P = .21). However, tracheomalacia severity differed significantly; 41.2% of experimental patients had severe tracheomalacia versus 4.7% of controls (P < .001), confirming that higher-risk patients were preferentially selected for implant reinforcement (Table 1).

Operative Details

All procedures were completed without intraoperative complications. Median operative time was shorter in the experimental group (30-80 minutes) than in controls. Estimated blood loss was minimal in both groups (range, 3-30 mL).

Primary Outcomes

All 17 experimental group patients achieved stable tracheal reconstruction with patent airways. No implant-related complications (erosion, infection, or displacement) occurred during median follow-up of 48 months (range, 12-120 months).

Complications

Table 2 summarizes complications. Soft tissue emphysema occurred in 2 experimental (11.8%) and 5 control (11.6%) patients (P = 1), resolving with conservative management in all cases. One control group patient (2.3%) required revision surgery and ultimately achieved a good outcome.

Postoperative complications

Granulation tissue at the suture line occurred more frequently in the experimental group (58.8% vs 27.9%; P = .047). All experimental group cases were small (<3 mm diameter), asymptomatic, and resolved with single-session argon plasma coagulation at the 1-month follow-up visit. Of note, granulation tissue requiring intervention occurred in 58.8% of experimental patients versus 9.3% of controls (P < .001). This higher intervention rate reflects the tissue response at the implant-suture interface rather than intrinsic NiTi toxicity, and all cases resolved definitively with a single bronchoscopic treatment. No recurrence was observed.

Airway Stability

Dynamic bronchoscopy at 6 months revealed stable anterior tracheal wall throughout the respiratory cycle in all 17 experimental patients (100%), with no inward flap collapse during forced inspiration. In the control group, 8 patients (18.6%) demonstrated subtle inward movement during deep inspiration, although none developed respiratory symptoms or required intervention. This difference was statistically significant (P = .046).

Long-Term Outcomes

Median follow-up was 48 months (range, 12-120) for experimental and 42 months (range, 12-108) for control patients (P = .38). No restenosis occurred in either group. CT at 6 months confirmed proper implant positioning in all experimental patients with no displacement or erosion; long-term imaging showed complete fibrous encapsulation. Hair growth from rotated skin flaps was noted endoscopically in 6 experimental patients (35.3%) and 14 control patients (32.6%; P = 1), managed with periodic endoscopic trimming.

Functional outcomes were classified as good in 16 experimental patients (94.1%) and 40 control patients (93%). One patient in each group had a satisfactory outcome due to mild exertional dyspnea, not requiring intervention.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that extraluminal reinforcement with porous NiTi implants is safe and durable for skin flap reconstruction of persistent tracheal defects. No implant-related complications occurred over a median 48-month follow-up, and all experimental group patients maintained stable, patent airways. The technique conferred superior biomechanical stability on dynamic bronchoscopy compared with conventional flap closure, a clinically meaningful finding given that this group harbored significantly more severe tracheomalacia at baseline.

Selection Bias and Interpretation

A critical interpretive caveat is that patient selection was nonrandomized. Experimental group patients had significantly more severe tracheomalacia (41.2% severe vs 4.7% in controls; P < .001), meaning the implant cohort was inherently higher risk. The experimental cohort, despite having markedly more severe tracheomalacia, demonstrated stable airway reconstruction with no implant-related complications, no revision surgery, and uniformly preserved anterior wall stability on dynamic bronchoscopy. These findings support the potential value of the reinforcement technique, although the nonrandomized design limits the strength of causal conclusions. A prospective randomized trial would provide higher-quality evidence; however, withholding a potentially beneficial intervention from high-risk patients raises ethical challenges.

Biomechanical Rationale for Extraluminal Nickel-Titanium

The fundamental challenge in reconstructing persistent tracheal defects is providing structural rigidity while maintaining a smooth internal lining. Strap muscle flaps alone provide insufficient rigidity in patients with moderate-to-severe tracheomalacia.17 Autologous cartilage grafts address this deficit but carry donor site morbidity and risk of resorption.15,16 Intraluminal stents are associated with complication rates of 30 to 50%, including granulation overgrowth, migration, and erosion into mediastinal structures.23, 24, 25, 26 External NiTi scaffolding avoids these intraluminal risks while exploiting the material's superelastic properties: At body temperature, NiTi undergoes up to 8% strain recovery upon unloading,30 allowing accommodation of respiratory motion, coughing, and swallowing without permanent deformation. The arched implant configuration provides anteroposterior support while permitting lateral flexibility, matching tracheal biomechanics.

Tissue Integration and Biocompatibility

The porous architecture (60-65% porosity; 100-300 μm pore diameter) promotes fibrous tissue ingrowth, incorporating the implant into surrounding tissues within 3 to 6 months and preventing migration.31,32 NiTi's spontaneous surface titanium oxide layer limits nickel ion release,33 and its biocompatibility in orthopedic, cardiovascular, and thoracic reconstruction applications is well established.31, 32, 33 Our zero-complication rate from the implant itself across 17 patients with extended follow-up is consistent with this established profile.

Granulation Tissue: Mechanism and Management

The higher rate of granulation tissue requiring bronchoscopic intervention in the experimental group (58.8% vs 9.3%; P < .001) warrants transparent discussion. This granulation occurred at the suture line where the implant was fixed to adjacent tissues, most likely reflecting a localized response to suture material under micromotion at the implant-tissue interface during early healing, rather than NiTi-specific toxicity. All cases were small (<3 mm), asymptomatic, resolved with a single session of argon plasma coagulation, and did not recur. This compares favorably to the persistent, recurrent granulation typical of intraluminal stents, which frequently mandate repeated interventions or stent explantation.34 Future refinements in suture material or implant fixation may reduce this occurrence.

Limitations

The key limitations are as follows: (1) retrospective, nonrandomized design with significant baseline differences in tracheomalacia severity between groups; (2) small sample size, potentially insufficient to detect rare complications; (3) single-center experience limiting generalizability; (4) absence of objective pulmonary function testing, flow-volume loops, or formal exercise testing, because future studies should incorporate these quantitative measures; (5) operator-defined outcome classification; and (6) follow-up beyond 10 years remains limited. A prospective randomized trial incorporating formal pulmonary function testing and blinded endoscopic assessment would substantially strengthen the evidence base.

Clinical Implications and Patient Selection

Based on this experience, NiTi reinforcement should be considered for patients with (1) moderate-to-severe tracheomalacia; (2) defects more than 15 mm craniocaudally; (3) failed primary flap closure; (4) poor tissue quality from radiation or chronic infection; or (5) high respiratory demand. The technique represents a middle ground between the technical demands of tracheal resection35,36 and the biomechanical limitations of flap closure alone, and is particularly valuable when resection is not feasible due to disease extent or patient factors.

Conclusions

Porous titanium nickelide reinforcement of skin flap tracheal reconstruction is safe and effective for managing persistent tracheal defects, achieving durable airway patency without implant-related complications during median 48-month follow-up. The technique provides superior biomechanical stability on dynamic bronchoscopy compared with conventional flap closure, a finding particularly notable given that the experimental cohort harbored significantly more severe tracheomalacia. The higher rate of granulation tissue requiring bronchoscopic intervention is a recognized and manageable phenomenon that resolved definitively in all cases. The inherent limitations of this nonrandomized retrospective series preclude unqualified superiority claims, but the safety and functional outcomes justify considering this technique for selected patients with persistent tracheal defects, especially those with moderate-to-severe tracheomalacia or large anterior wall defects. Prospective randomized studies incorporating objective pulmonary function measurements and blinded evaluation are warranted.
