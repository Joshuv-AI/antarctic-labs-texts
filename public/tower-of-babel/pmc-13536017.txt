Managing headache in primary care: choosing therapies that treat the whole patient
Authors: Brian T Burroughs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo provide a practical, comorbidity-aligned approach to headache management in primary care, emphasising therapies that can address headache and coexisting chronic conditions while reducing polypharmacy and medication interaction risk. Narrative clinical review using targeted literature searches and guideline synthesis. Contemporary clinical guidelines, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, randomised controlled trials and other clinically applicable evidence addressing the diagnosis and management of headache disorders in adults were prioritised. Evidence relevant to primary care, comorbidity-aligned treatment selection, non-pharmacologic interventions, medication-overuse headache and office-based procedures was emphasised. PubMed/MEDLINE and major headache guideline sources were searched through 7 August 2026. Reference lists of selected publications were also reviewed to identify additional clinically relevant evidence. Most headache disorders can be managed in primary care using a structured approach that confirms the headache phenotype, screens for secondary causes, identifies comorbidities and aligns treatment with the patient’s broader clinical needs. Selecting therapies that benefit both headache and coexisting conditions may reduce medication burden and improve adherence and tolerability. Non-pharmacologic interventions and structured follow-up are important components of care. Recognition and management of medication overuse can improve outcomes, while selected office-based procedures may provide additional options when used with appropriate attention to evidence limitations, patient selection and clinician competence. Comorbidity-aligned headache care offers primary care clinicians a practical framework for individualising treatment, limiting unnecessary medication layering and integrating pharmacologic and non-pharmacologic strategies. This approach may improve headache outcomes while supporting management of the patient’s overall health. Introduction

Headache disorders are among the most common and disabling problems encountered in primary care. Globally, headache disorders rank among the leading causes of years lived with disability, with migraine responsible for a disproportionate share of disability among working-age adults.1 In the USA, most outpatient visits for headache occur in primary care settings, placing primary care clinicians at the centre of diagnosis, longitudinal management and prevention of disease progression.2

Despite this central role, headache care is often fragmented. Many patients face long waits for neurology, and some receive ‘headache-only’ treatment that focuses on episodic rescue while comorbid conditions (sleep disruption, anxiety/depression, obesity, chronic pain, hypertension) continue to amplify headache frequency and disability. Migraine and other primary headache disorders commonly coexist with depression, anxiety, sleep disorders, obesity, chronic pain syndromes and cardiovascular risk factors.3–6 When headache is treated as an isolated complaint, clinicians may add medications on top of existing regimens without addressing overlapping indications, increasing the risk of adverse effects, interactions and medication-overuse headache.2 7–9

Primary care clinicians are uniquely positioned to lead headache care because the required skill set includes risk stratification, chronic disease follow-up, medication titration, deprescribing, behavioural counselling and care coordination. These responsibilities match core primary care strengths.2 This narrative clinical review provides a practical, comorbidity-aligned approach to diagnosing and treating common headache disorders in primary care, emphasising therapies that fit the whole patient rather than a single symptom.

Methods

This narrative clinical review was informed by targeted searches of PubMed/MEDLINE and the websites of the International Headache Society, American College of Physicians, US Department of Veterans Affairs and Department of Defense, European Academy of Neurology, American Headache Society and US Food and Drug Administration. Searches were updated through 7 August 2026 and emphasised English-language sources published from 1 January 2019 through 7 August 2026. Older landmark guidance and pharmacology references were retained when they remained clinically relevant. Search concepts combined headache, migraine, tension-type headache, cervicogenic headache, medication-overuse headache, primary care, prevention, comorbidity, non-pharmacologic treatment, nerve block, epilepsy, safety and drug interaction.

Consistent with principles outlined in the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA), evidence selection prioritized clinical guidelines, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and major comparative studies addressing decisions relevant to primary care.10 Sources were selected for clinical relevance, recency, methodological rigour and applicability to comorbidity-aligned care. When recommendations differed, the most recent high-quality guidance was prioritised and uncertainty was described rather than assuming equivalence. This was not a systematic review; no protocol was registered, exhaustive study identification was not attempted and no de novo risk-of-bias or certainty assessment was performed.

Headache disorders commonly managed in primary care

Most primary headache presentations can be diagnosed clinically and managed in primary care after urgent secondary causes are excluded. Selected secondary syndromes, including cervicogenic headache and medication-overuse headache, are also commonly recognised and managed in this setting.2

Migraine

Migraine without aura is characterised by recurrent attacks lasting 4 to 72 hours. The headache has at least 2 of 4 core characteristics: unilateral location, pulsating quality, moderate to severe intensity, and aggravation by or avoidance of routine physical activity. Attacks are accompanied by nausea or vomiting, or by both photophobia and phonophobia.11 Chronic migraine should be suspected when headache occurs on 15 or more days per month for at least 3 months, with migraine features on at least 8 days per month.2 3 For primary care clinicians, the best medication is often the one that addresses migraine plus the patient’s comorbidity profile.

Tension-type headache

Tension-type headache typically presents as bilateral, pressing or tightening pain of mild to moderate intensity that is not worsened by routine physical activity.2 Although often less disabling than migraine, chronic tension-type headache can substantially impair quality of life and frequently coexists with anxiety, depression and poor sleep.

Acute treatment generally begins with acetaminophen or an nonsteroidal anti-inflamatory nti-inflamatory drug (NSAID), with explicit limits to reduce medication-overuse risk. For frequent or chronic tension-type headache, amitriptyline has evidence for prevention. Aerobic exercise, progressive strengthening, physical therapy and behavioural strategies can complement medication when sleep, stress, mood or musculoskeletal factors contribute.12

Cervicogenic headache

Cervicogenic headache is a secondary headache attributed to a cervical source and typically presents as referred head pain, often unilateral, that is provoked by neck movement or sustained posture. Examination may show reduced cervical range of motion, reproduction of pain with cervical palpation or movement, and associated neck or shoulder dysfunction.

Initial management emphasises targeted physical therapy, therapeutic exercise and selected manual therapy, while addressing posture and ergonomics. Evidence suggests possible improvement in headache intensity, frequency and disability, although many trials have important risk-of-bias limitations.13 Imaging is guided by trauma, neurologic findings, systemic symptoms or other red flags rather than the diagnosis alone. Persistent diagnostic uncertainty or failure of conservative treatment may warrant specialty or interventional assessment.

Medication-overuse headache

Medication-overuse headache is a common, under-recognised cause of chronic daily headache. It develops when patients use acute headache medications frequently, typically 10 or more days per month for triptans, opioids or combination analgesics, or 15 or more days per month for simple analgesics, over at least 3 months.7–9 Medication-overuse headache perpetuates a cycle of escalating headache frequency and escalating medication consumption; identifying it early is one of the highest-yield interventions in primary care.

Red flags and diagnostic reassurance

Primary care clinicians should screen for secondary headache features at the initial visit and whenever patterns change. Red flags include thunderclap onset; new headache after age 50; a progressive or dramatically changing pattern; focal neurologic deficits; altered mental status; fever, weight loss or known malignancy; immunosuppression; pregnancy or the postpartum state; papilledema or visual loss; and headache provoked by exertion or Valsalva manoeuvres.2 14 In the absence of red flags, diagnostic confidence allows timely treatment without routine neuroimaging.

Why comorbidity alignment improves outcomes

Comorbidity-aligned prescribing is not simply a convenience strategy; it targets the drivers of headache disability and chronification. Sleep disruption and mood disorders can lower the threshold for attacks, increase pain sensitivity and reduce coping capacity, magnifying functional impairment even when headache frequency is unchanged.3–6 Similarly, obesity and metabolic dysregulation are associated with higher headache frequency and increased risk of progression to chronic migraine; selecting a preventive that supports weight goals can improve both headache and overall cardiometabolic risk.3 5 Chronic pain conditions share overlapping mechanisms of central sensitisation and may increase reliance on analgesics that promote medication-overuse headache.4–6

This is where primary care excels: clinicians can treat headache while simultaneously improving sleep hygiene, anxiety control, blood pressure and weight trajectory, often with a single well-chosen preventive and a simplified acute plan. When the preventive ‘fits’ the patient’s broader disease state, adherence improves, and fewer add-on medications are needed.

Preventive therapy: aligning migraine prevention with chronic disease management

Preventive therapy should be considered when attacks are frequent, disabling, prolonged, refractory to acute therapy or when acute medication use approaches medication-overuse thresholds.2 15 Preventive selection in primary care should be driven by comorbidity fit and tolerability as much as by migraine phenotype. When starting prevention, set expectations: benefits typically build over weeks, and the goal is fewer headache days, less severe attacks, reduced rescue use and improved function.

Migraine with hypertension or cardiovascular risk

Beta-blockers (eg, propranolol, metoprolol) are established migraine preventives and practical first-line choices when hypertension, tachycardia or situational anxiety coexist.2 15 They can reduce migraine frequency while improving blood pressure control. Start low and titrate slowly, particularly in patients prone to fatigue or orthostasis; avoid in asthma with bronchospasm, bradycardia or conduction disease. If fatigue, exercise intolerance or mood worsening emerges, reassess the tradeoff and consider switching to an ARB.

Angiotensin receptor blockers, particularly candesartan, have evidence for migraine prevention and are often well tolerated.16 ARBs are attractive when a patient needs blood pressure therapy and has comorbid diabetes or kidney disease, or when beta-blockers are not tolerated. When selecting any antihypertensive for migraine prevention, consider baseline blood pressure, orthostatic symptoms and fall risk, particularly in older adults.6

Migraine with depression, anxiety, insomnia or chronic pain

Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) and serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) can address migraine prevention while treating common comorbidities that amplify headache disability. TCAs such as amitriptyline are useful when insomnia or chronic pain is prominent and can be effective at low bedtime doses.2 17 However, anticholinergic effects, weight gain and next-day sedation can limit tolerability, especially in older adults.6 18

SNRIs such as venlafaxine can be considered when anxiety or depression is prominent and anticholinergic burden is undesirable.17 18 Their migraine-prevention evidence is less established than that for several traditional preventives, making comorbidity fit particularly important. The goal is to select a therapy that can improve mood or pain amplification while also reducing migraine frequency, rather than adding an antidepressant solely for headache.

Migraine with obesity or metabolic disease

Topiramate is effective for migraine prevention and may support weight loss, making it useful when obesity is a key comorbidity.2 15 Cognitive adverse effects, including word-finding difficulty, impaired attention or memory, and slowed processing, can limit adherence.19 20 Use gradual dose titration and ask directly about cognitive function at follow-up; reduce the dose or discontinue treatment if adverse effects interfere with work, driving or daily activities. Counsel about paresthesias, hydration, nephrolithiasis, metabolic acidosis and teratogenicity. Potential effects on hormonal contraception depend on dose and formulation, so patients with pregnancy potential should receive individualised counselling.19 20

Migraine with epilepsy

Migraine frequently coexists with epilepsy, creating opportunities for coordinated treatment but also additional safety concerns.21 Topiramate may address both conditions when its cognitive, renal, metabolic and reproductive risks are acceptable. Valproate can also prevent migraine and treat epilepsy, but weight gain and major reproductive risks substantially limit its role. Concomitant topiramate and valproate increases the risk of hyperammonemia and encephalopathy; avoid the combination when possible or coordinate closely with neurology.19–21

Migraine with polypharmacy, medication intolerance or interaction risk

CGRP-targeted therapies (monoclonal antibodies and gepants used for prevention) offer migraine-specific prevention with generally favourable tolerability and low interaction burden.22 23 These agents can be valuable when comorbidities make traditional preventives undesirable, including low baseline blood pressure, fatigue, high cognitive demands or complex medication regimens. Contemporary reviews support their expanding role within integrated migraine management.22 23

In primary care, major barriers include high acquisition cost, prior authorisation, insurance restrictions and limited availability. These barriers are especially pronounced in low-income and middle-income countries, where newer migraine-specific therapies may be unaffordable or unavailable.24 Clinicians can improve approval success by documenting headache frequency, disability and prior preventive trials, including dose, duration and reasons for discontinuation.15

Acute therapy in context: match efficacy to safety

Acute therapy should be individualised based on headache severity, nausea/vomiting, cardiovascular risk and medication-overuse risk.2 NSAIDs and acetaminophen remain appropriate for mild to moderate attacks, but frequent use contributes to medication-overuse headache; educate early that ‘effective’ does not mean ‘unlimited.’7–9

Triptans are effective for moderate to severe migraine but are generally avoided in patients with established coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease or significant uncontrolled cardiovascular risk.2 For patients who cannot take triptans or who do not respond, non-vasoconstrictive options such as gepants can be considered.23

Non-pharmacologic care: foundational, not optional

Non-pharmacologic care should accompany acute and preventive medication rather than be reserved for treatment failure. A headache diary can clarify phenotype, headache days, acute medication use, menstrual or sleep associations, and response to treatment. Clinicians should encourage a consistent sleep schedule, regular meals, adequate hydration, caffeine consistency or reduction when intake is excessive, and gradual aerobic or strengthening exercise.12

Cognitive behavioural therapy, relaxation training, biofeedback, mindfulness-based approaches and physical therapy may help selected patients, although the certainty and magnitude of benefit vary by intervention and headache type.12 Rather than prescribing broad trigger-avoidance lists, use the diary to identify reproducible, modifiable patterns and avoid unnecessary dietary or activity restriction.

Medication-overuse headache and deprescribing: high-yield primary care work

Medication-overuse headache is one of the most impactful diagnoses clinicians can make in patients with chronic headache, and it is an area where primary care’s longitudinal relationship is a major advantage. Frequent acute medication use becomes both a response to and a driver of headache frequency.7–9 The most important clinical move is to name the cycle in non-judgmental language: the medications provide short-term relief but maintain the long-term pattern.

Patients should expect a temporary worsening of headache during withdrawal, often accompanied by nausea, vomiting, sleep disturbance, restlessness, anxiety, tachycardia or blood pressure changes. Symptoms commonly last 2 to 10 days, although the course varies by the medication overused and the patient’s comorbidities.7–9 Preparing patients for this interval can reduce abandonment of the plan and unnecessary urgent care use. Deprescribing is not only about stopping overused rescue medications. It also includes reassessing legacy medications started years ago, duplicate therapies and drugs that worsen comorbidities (eg, weight-promoting agents in obesity, sedating agents in fall-risk patients). Primary care clinicians can often improve headache outcomes by simplifying the regimen and selecting a more comorbidity-aligned preventive.

Procedural options in primary care: greater occipital nerve blocks

Peripheral nerve blocks may provide short-term pain relief and can serve as a bridge while preventive therapies are titrated.28 Greater occipital nerve blocks are among the office-based procedures that appropriately trained primary care clinicians may consider within local scope, credentialing and emergency-preparedness requirements.

Small trials and meta-analyses suggest short-term benefit from greater occipital nerve blocks for acute migraine and possibly as adjunctive treatment in chronic migraine, but certainty remains limited and protocols vary.28–30 Selected uses include severe attacks when nausea limits oral absorption or a bridge during withdrawal from overused acute medications. When to refer

Emergency evaluation

Thunderclap onset, an acute focal neurologic deficit, altered mental status, fever with meningismus, papilledema or acute visual loss, pregnancy or postpartum headache with concerning secondary features, or suspected acute vascular or infectious disease requires emergency assessment rather than routine outpatient referral.14

Expedited specialist assessment

Progressive headache, an abnormal neurologic examination, atypical or prolonged aura, a new headache after age 50, cancer or immunosuppression, diagnostic uncertainty, or a suspected trigeminal autonomic cephalalgia or other secondary syndrome warrants prompt imaging and/or neurology involvement according to the clinical context.2 14

Routine collaborative referral

Referral is reasonable after multiple adequately dosed and timed preventive trials have failed or were not tolerated; for chronic migraine requiring onabotulinumtoxinA or other advanced procedures; for refractory medication-overuse headache; when epilepsy, pregnancy potential or drug interactions complicate treatment; or when a procedure exceeds the clinician’s training or local scope.2

Referral should be framed as collaboration, not transfer of responsibility. Primary care clinicians remain central after specialty involvement because management of sleep, mood, blood pressure, weight, medication reconciliation, and medication-overuse risk often determines whether improvement is sustained.

Conclusion

Headache disorders are a routine part of primary care practice and should be managed as such. A comorbidity-aligned approach can reduce unnecessary medication layering while improving the fit between treatment and the patient’s broader health goals. Accurate phenotype recognition, non-pharmacologic care, medication-overuse management, structured follow-up and timely referral are foundational. Selected office-based procedures such as greater occipital nerve blocks may expand options when evidence limitations, clinician competence and local scope are considered. With intentional prescribing and longitudinal follow-up, primary care can deliver effective, patient-centred headache care while using specialty collaboration where it adds the most value.
