Wasserstein Distributionally Robust Performative Prediction
Siyi Wang, Zifan Wang, Karl H. Johansson

Abstract
Performativity means that the deployment of a predictive model incentivizes agents to strategically adapt their behavior, thereby inducing a model-dependent distribution shift. Practitioners often repeatedly retrain the model on data samples to adapt to evolving distributions. In this paper, we develop a Wasserstein distributionally robust optimization framework for performative prediction, where the prediction model is optimized over the worst-case distribution within a Wasserstein ambiguity set. We allow the ambiguity radius to depend on the prediction model, which subsumes the constant-radius formulation as a special case. By leveraging strong duality, the intractable robust objective is reformulated as a computationally tractable minimization problem. Based on this formulation, we develop distributionally robust repeated risk minimization (DR-RRM) and repeated gradient descent (DR-RGD), to iteratively find an equilibrium between distributional shifts and model retraining. Theoretical analyses demonstrate that, under standard regularity conditions, both algorithms converge to a unique robust performative stable point. Our analysis explicitly accounts for inner-loop approximation errors and shows convergence to a neighborhood of the stable point in inexact settings. Additionally, we establish theoretical bounds on the suboptimality gap between the stable point and the global performative optimum. Finally, numerical simulations of a dynamic credit scoring problem demonstrate the efficacy of the method.

Introduction

Stochastic optimization constitutes the foundation of modern machine learning, where prediction is framed as identifying a model parameter that minimizes expected risk
[
1
]
. This paradigm has been widely applied in fields such as recommendation systems
[

, automated trading

For instance, in credit scoring, applicants may strategically adjust their financial profiles to improve their approval chances under the current scoring rule. Similarly, in traffic routing, predicting congestion and suggesting optimal routes alters driver behavior, which creates new traffic patterns. This concept is formalized as performative prediction
[

, which has been generalized to various domains, including constrained optimization
[

, data-driven control
[

, reinforcement learning
[

, and game settings

. If this dependency is ignored, performativity manifests as a form of distribution shift.

Model retraining is essential for adapting to evolving data distributions. In practice, learners often rely on empirical distributions constructed from finite samples, which leads to overfitting and suboptimal out-of-sample generalization.
To mitigate this risk, distributionally robust optimization (DRO) has emerged as a robust alternative

,

,

to traditional empirical risk minimization.
Instead of optimizing for a single empirical distribution, DRO minimizes the worst-case risk over a prescribed ambiguity set centered at the empirical distribution. This paradigm ensures that the model remains valid under limited samples while preventing over-sensitivity to outliers. Moreover, leveraging duality results, many DRO formulations can be recast into computationally tractable convex programs
[

]

However, they overlook RGD, which is essential for high-dimensional settings where exact minimization is computationally intractable.
We bridge these gaps by developing a Wasserstein DRO framework via Lagrangian relaxation, and providing comprehensive convergence guarantees for both DR-RRM and DR-RGD. Specifically, we allow the ambiguity radius to vary with the decision model, which enables tighter robustness bounds as the model stabilizes.

Additionally, while our work shares conceptual similarities with decision-dependent DRO
[
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
, a critical distinction lies in the objective. Decision-dependent DRO typically focuses on solving static, single-stage optimization problems and establishing their tractable dual reformulations. In contrast, we focus on the dynamic stability of the learning algorithms and aim at establishing convergence to a robust performative stable point, an equilibrium in the dynamic interaction between performative distribution and model retraining.

In this work, we investigate Wasserstein distributionally robust performative prediction and provide both algorithmic solutions and theoretical analyses.
The prediction model is optimized by minimizing the performance risk, which is defined as the worst-case expected loss over a Wasserstein ambiguity set centered at the empirical distribution.
We allow the ambiguity radius to depend on the prediction model, which subsumes the constant-radius formulation as a special case.
Since direct computation over the decision-dependent ambiguity set is generally intractable, we adopt a Lagrangian relaxation.
Leveraging strong duality results
[
31
]
, we reformulate the DRO problem into a min-max optimization over worst-case realizations.
Building on concepts from
[
6
]
, we define the robust performative stable point and the robust performative optimum. The global performative optimum is typically intractable, as both the distribution and the loss depend on the decision variable. Consequently, we focus on finding a robust performative stable point.
To achieve this, we develop DR-RRM and DR-RGD algorithms for the formulated min-max optimization. Both methods operate iteratively: at each step, the learner samples from the current distribution and solves a robust surrogate function to identify adversarial perturbations. Based on these estimates, the model parameters are updated by explicitly solving the risk minimization problem (DR-RRM) or by constructing a gradient estimate to take a descent step (DR-RGD). Furthermore, we show that both algorithms converge under standard regularity conditions such as smoothness and Lipschitz continuity. Specifically, our analysis accounts for the approximation error introduced by solving the adversarial perturbations. We demonstrate that in the presence of approximation error, the iterations converge to a bounded neighborhood of the equilibrium. In the ideal case where the error is zero, the algorithms converge to a unique robust performative stable point. Finally, we derive a bound on the distance between the stable point and the global performative optimum in terms of both parameter distance and performative risk.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:
(1) Framework: We establish a distributionally robust performative prediction framework and convert the intractable DRO objective into a solvable min-max problem via strong duality.
(2) Algorithm: We develop DR-RRM and DR-RGD algorithms to iteratively solve the robust performative stable point.
(3) Convergence analysis: Under standard regularity assumptions, we prove that both algorithms converge to a unique stable point in the exact setting, and to its bounded neighborhood in the presence of approximation errors.
(4) Suboptimality guarantee: We derive theoretical bounds on the distance and risk gap between the accessible performative stable point and the global performative optimum.

II
Problem Formulation

II-A

Performative prediction

Let
\theta\in\Theta
denote the model parameter and
\Theta
the parameter space. Let the random samples
\xi\in\Xi
be drawn from a distribution
\mathbb{P}
, where
\Xi\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a compact and convex set.
Denote the diameter of the admissible set
\Xi
as
D_{\xi}=\sup_{x,y\in\Xi}\|x-y\|
.
Performative behavior means that the deployment of a predictive model will affect the observed distribution, denoted by
\mathbb{P}(\theta)
.
Given a loss function
l(\theta,\xi):\Theta\times\Xi\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the prediction performance is evaluated by performative risk, which is defined as the expected loss over the distribution
\mathbb{P}(\theta)
it induces:

{\rm PR}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathbb{P}(\theta)}[l(\theta,\xi)].

(1)

The decision
\theta_{\rm po}
is performative optimal if it minimizes the performative risk:

\theta_{\rm po}={\arg\min}_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathbb{P}(\theta)}[l(\theta,\xi)].

(2)

Note that the performative risk (
1
) depends on both the model parameter
\theta
and the induced distribution
\mathbb{P}(\theta)
. However, this distribution is typically unobservable before deploying the parameter
\theta
, making it difficult to solve the performative optimum (
2
).
Consequently, the notion of performative stability is introduced as an alternative solution concept, where model parameters are consistent with the distribution they induce. Formally, a decision
\theta_{\rm ps}
is performatively stable if it satisfies:

\theta_{\rm ps}={\arg\min}_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathbb{P}(\theta_{\rm ps})}[l(\theta,\xi)].

Namely, a performative stable decision
\theta_{\rm ps}
minimizes the expected loss on the distribution
\mathbb{P}(\theta_{\rm ps})
resulting from deploying
\theta_{\rm ps}
in the first place.
It is noteworthy that performatively stable decisions are fixed points of risk minimization. Crucially, performative stability and performative optimality do not generally coincide: a stable classifier may not be optimal, and an optimal classifier need not be stable
[
6
]
.

II-B

Distributionally robust optimization

In real-world scenarios, the distribution of interest
\mathbb{P}_{0}(\theta)
is often unobservable. Typically, we only have access to datasets that contain finite samples independently drawn from the distribution
\mathbb{P}_{0}(\theta)
, which yields an empirical estimate
\hat{\mathbb{P}}(\theta)
. To mitigate the risk of distribution mismatch, we employ a DRO method that minimizes the worst-case risk over an ambiguity set centered at the distribution estimate. To characterize this set, we utilize the Wasserstein distance to quantify the discrepancy between the candidate and empirical distributions:

\rm{W}\big(\mathbb{P}(\theta),\hat{\mathbb{P}}(\theta)\big)=\inf_{\mathbb{D}\in\Gamma(\mathbb{P},\hat{\mathbb{P}}(\theta))}\mathbb{E}_{(\xi,\zeta)\sim\mathbb{D}}[c(\xi,\zeta)],

where
\Gamma(\mathbb{P},\hat{\mathbb{P}}(\theta))
denotes the set of joint distribution of random variables
(\xi,\zeta)
with
\xi\sim\mathbb{P}
and
\zeta\sim\hat{\mathbb{P}}(\theta)
, and the function
c:\Xi\times\Xi\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
evaluates the transportation cost. Following standard conventions in the literature, we adopt the squared Euclidean distance

c(\xi,\zeta)=\|\xi-\zeta\|^{2}

(3)

as the transportation cost.
Denote
\mathcal{D}(\Xi)
as the set of all distributions supported on
\Xi
.
The ambiguity set
\mathcal{B}_{\rho(\theta)}(\hat{\mathbb{P}}(\theta)):=\{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{D}(\Xi):{\rm W}(\mathbb{P},\hat{\mathbb{P}}(\theta))\leq\rho(\theta)\}

contains all distributions within
\rho(\theta)
-distance from
\hat{\mathbb{P}}(\theta)
and
\rho(\theta)\geq 0
is a user-specified radius. Generally, a larger
\rho
represents higher robustness and a more conservative model. Letting
\rho(\theta)
decrease as
\theta
increases reflects progressively tighter robustness bounds as the model stabilizes.

Given the ambiguity set, the distributionally robust performative prediction is formulated as

\mathop{\arg\min}_{\theta\in\Theta}\sup_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\rho(\theta)}(\hat{\mathbb{P}}(\theta))}\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathbb{P}}[l(\theta,\xi)].

(4)

\theta
by minimizing the worst-case risk over a Wasserstein ambiguity set. The deployment of
\theta
then updates the observed data distribution
\hat{\mathbb{P}}(\theta)
and the risk assessment for the next iteration.

Although the primal DRO problem (

[

]
.
To ensure computational tractability, we leverage its Lagrangian relaxation
[

]
, with a penalty parameter
\lambda(\theta)

\mathop{\arg\min}_{\theta\in\Theta}\sup_{\mathbb{P}}\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}_{\xi\sim\mathbb{P}}[l(\theta,\xi)]-\lambda(\theta){\rm{W}}(\mathbb{P},\hat{\mathbb{P}}(\theta)),

(5)

where
\lambda(\theta):\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
varies with the decision
\theta
.
Generally,
\lambda(\theta)
is inversely related to
\rho(\theta)
, such that a larger
\lambda(\theta)
indicates a smaller ambiguity set and less robustness.

Main results

As discussed, finding the performative optimum is generally intractable. Following the framework of
[
6
]

In Section

Lemma 2
.

[

Let
\mathbb{P}_{x}

\mathbb{P}_{y}

W_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{x},\mathbb{P}_{y})=\frac{1}{K}\sup_{\|f\|_{L}\leq K}\{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathbb{P}_{x}}[f(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathbb{P}_{y}}[f(y)]\},

for any fixed

The following assumption restricts the sensitivity of the distribution map

.

Assumption 1

Let the distribution

be
\varepsilon

\theta

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\zeta}l(\theta,\zeta)-\nabla_{\zeta}l(\theta^{\prime},\zeta)\|\leq L_{\zeta\theta}\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|,

denote the gradients of

Assumption 3
.

For any fixed

, the loss function

.

.

Denote

III-A

Performative risk minimization

This section introduces the repeated risk minimization for distributionally robust performative prediction and analyzes its convergence to the performative stable point.

At each iteration
t
, we only observe a realization of the true distribution
\mathbb{P}(\theta_{t-1})
resulting from the previous model, which constitutes the empirical distribution estimate
\hat{\mathbb{P}}(\theta_{t-1})
. Then, the algorithm iteratively updates the model by minimizing the performative risk on this empirical distribution. Thus, the repeated risk minimization processes as

\theta_{t+1}=\mathop{\arg\min}_{\theta\in\Theta}\ \mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\hat{\mathbb{P}}(\theta_{t})}f(\theta,\xi)

(8)

with initial value
\theta_{0}
.
Note that the repeated risk minimization procedure is consistent with the definition of performative stability in (
7b
), which means that the solution via recursive retraining (
8
) converges to a performative stable point.

We now detail the algorithm procedure.
At each iteration
t
, we sample
\xi_{t}
from the empirical distribution
\hat{\mathbb{P}}(\theta_{t})
uniformly. By Lemma
1
, we compute a maximizer
z_{t}
for the inner objective function:

\displaystyle z_{t}

\displaystyle={\arg\max}_{\zeta\in\Xi}\phi(\theta_{t},\xi_{t},\zeta),

(9)

where the adversarial perturbation
z_{t}
represents realizations of the worst-case distribution.
Ideally, if
\phi(\theta_{t},\xi_{t},\zeta)
admits a closed-form solution, an exact maximizer
z_{t}
can be derived.
However, when the explicit form is unknown or the maximization is computationally intractable,
z_{t}
is obtained numerically such that
\|z_{t}-\zeta^{\ast}(\theta_{t},\xi_{t})\|\leq\epsilon
, where
\zeta^{\ast}(\cdot)
denotes the set of exact maximizers and the tolerance
\epsilon>0
.
We retain the
\epsilon

Finally, based on the robust objective
\phi(\theta_{t},\xi_{t},z_{t})
, we update the model parameter
\theta_{t}
via

\theta_{t+1}=\mathop{{\arg\min}}_{\theta\in\Theta}l(\theta,z_{t})-\lambda(\theta)c(\xi_{t},z_{t}).

(10)

It is noteworthy that
\xi_{t}
and
z_{t}
both depend on
\theta_{t}
.
To summarize, as outlined in Algorithm
1
, the procedure iteratively identifies adversarial perturbation
z_{t}
and updates the model parameters by minimizing risk evaluated on these perturbed samples.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced a distributionally robust performative prediction framework grounded in empirical data distributions. To address the inherent discrepancy between the empirical and true distributions, we employed a decision-dependent Wasserstein ambiguity set centered on the empirical distribution to encompass the true distribution. By leveraging Lagrangian relaxation and strong duality, we transformed the robust objective into a computationally tractable form, allowing the optimization problem to be solved directly at the sample level.
Within this framework, we defined robust performative stable points and the robust performative optimum. We developed two iterative algorithms: repeated risk minimization and repeated gradient descent to find the stable points. We proved that under standard regularity conditions, both algorithms converge to a stable point in the exact setting, or to a bounded neighborhood thereof in the presence of approximation errors. Furthermore, we established theoretical bounds on the parameter distance and risk gap between the attainable robust stable points and the global performative optimum. These results demonstrate that the distributionally robust performative prediction framework effectively mitigates the impact of strategic manipulation while maintaining robust performance under distributional uncertainty.
