Adoption of robot-assisted minimally invasive surgery across surgical specialties in France: a nationwide population-based study
Authors: Anaïs Havet, François Bettega, Violaine Fernandez, Clara Bouvard, Cécile Payet, Jean-Christophe Lifante, Christophe Tresallet, Laurent Brunaud, Xavier Armoiry, Marie Viprey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Robotic minimally invasive surgery has been used in France for nearly two decades, with monitoring enabled by the National Health Data System (SNDS) since March 2020. No French study has comprehensively described robotic procedures, including ENT, at a fine geographical scale. We aimed to assess the use of robotic and non-robotic minimally invasive surgery over time and across French departments (administrative subdivisions), describe patient and healthcare facility characteristics and investigate determinants of robotic surgery use. We identified adults undergoing robotic or non-robotic minimally invasive procedures in urology, gynaecology, digestive, thoracic, and ENT specialties between January 2017 and June 2023. From July 2020, robotic surgery use was described by specialty, quarter and department, and temporal trends were assessed using SARIMA models. Determinants of robotic surgery use were identified by specialty using multivariable logistic regression models. Between January 2017 and June 2023, 914,350 minimally invasive surgeries were performed, including 432,505 from July 2020 onwards, of which 19.2% were robotic. This proportion varied by specialty (urology: 63.6%, thoracic surgery: 18.5%, ENT: 16.8%, gynaecology: 9.7%, digestive surgery: 6.5%). Across all specialties, the proportion of robotic surgeries increased by 0.66% points per quarter. Minimally invasive surgery volume and robotic surgery use varied substantially across departments. Robotic surgery use was associated with patient sociodemographic characteristics and facility characteristics (facility type, number of beds and surgical volume). Robotic surgery use is increasing in France, with substantial variation across specialties, departments, patient and healthcare facility characteristics. Further studies on safety and effectiveness are needed to complement real-world monitoring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11701-026-04081-2.

Introduction

The introduction of robotic technology has progressively broadened the application of minimally invasive surgery, offering enhanced precision and control of surgical movements, especially during complex procedures and in anatomically confined spaces [1, 2]. Initially developed for urological surgery, robotic surgery has since been adopted by a wide range of surgical specialties, including digestive, gynaecological, thoracic, and ear, nose and throat (ENT) surgery [3]. This adoption is reflected by the increasing number of robotic systems in French healthcare facilities, rising from 135 in 2017 to approximately 300 in recent years according to industry data [4, 5].

Although robotic minimally invasive surgery has been in development in France for nearly two decades, robotic assistance has only been identifiable in the French National Health Data System (SNDS), a healthcare reimbursement database, since 2019 for selected procedures, and traceability has only been mandatory since March 2020 [4]. Based on available estimates, around 30,000 robotic surgeries are performed annually in France [6], and the proportion of procedures performed using robotic assistance varies depending on the specialty and type of procedure [7].

To date, few studies have described the use of robotic surgery in France. One study based on national hospital discharge data described the use of robot-assisted radical prostatectomies in 2020 [8]. Subsequently, the National Academy of Surgery described robotic surgery activity between 2021 and 2022 across all surgical procedures recorded in the French databases, with the exception of ENT procedures, as well as regional disparities and early postoperative outcomes. The results showed that robotic surgery was expanding in France but that its adoption remained highly uneven across territories, suggesting inequalities in access [4, 9].

Building on these previous works, we aimed to use the SNDS over a longer observation period to provide a more comprehensive assessment of the adoption and use of robotic surgery at a finer geographical scale, across French departments (administrative subdivisions). Our study also extended previous analyses by including ENT procedures, accounting for socioeconomic deprivation at patients’ place of residence, and investigating patient and healthcare facility characteristics and temporal factors associated with the use of robotic surgery.

Hence, from a health services research perspective, this study focused on the adoption and use of robotic surgery rather than on its clinical effectiveness or comparative outcomes. The first objective was to assess the use of robotic and non-robotic minimally invasive surgery across all procedures recorded in the SNDS (urology, gynaecology, digestive surgery, thoracic surgery, and ENT surgery) over time and across departments between July 2020 and June 2023. The secondary objectives were to describe the characteristics of patients undergoing minimally invasive surgery and the healthcare facilities involved and to investigate factors associated with the use of robotic surgery.

Methods

Study design and population

We identified adults aged ≥ 18 years who underwent minimally invasive surgery (robotic or non-robotic) in metropolitan France between January 2017 and June 2023 in urology (kidney, upper urinary tract and prostate), gynaecology (uterus), digestive surgery (stomach, colon and rectum), thoracic surgery (lung and mediastinum) and ENT surgery (larynx, pharynx and tongue) (see Supplementary Table 1 for CCAM codes).

During the study period, three COVID-19 lockdown periods occurred in France (17 March-11 May 2020; 30 October-15 December 2020; 3 April-2 May 2021). Although robotic assistance in minimally invasive surgery has been recorded in the SNDS since March 2020, analyses of robotic surgery use started in July 2020, after the first COVID-19 lockdown, because surgical activity during this period was heavily disrupted and did not reflect usual practice [10].

For each patient, we identified their first minimally invasive surgery, with or without robotic assistance, performed between January 2017 and June 2023. The index date corresponded to the date of this first surgery. Multiple procedures within the same surgical specialty during the index surgery were included, explaining why the number of procedures exceeded the number of patients. Patients were excluded if they (i) underwent minimally invasive surgery involving multiple surgical specialties, (ii) underwent minimally invasive surgery in the four years preceding the index date, (iii) had inconsistent identifiers and death data, (iv) underwent lymph node clearance with an unidentified specialty, and (v) underwent an aberrant procedure (i.e. prostate surgery in women or gynaecological surgery in men).

Data source

This study was based on data from the SNDS, a medico-administrative database used to monitor healthcare utilisation in France, covering nearly the entire population enrolled in the French health insurance system (approximately 99% of French residents). It combines individual demographic characteristics with records of both outpatient and hospital care. Diagnoses are documented using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), whereas medical and surgical procedures are identified through the French Common Classification of Medical Procedures (CCAM) [11].

Variables

Patient characteristics included age, sex, comorbidity profile assessed with the Charlson index [12], and socioeconomic deprivation measured using the French Deprivation Index (FDep). Derived from the 2015 census, FDep classifies residential areas into five quintiles of deprivation, with Q5 corresponding to the highest level of deprivation [13].

Healthcare facility characteristics included facility type (university hospital, hospital centre and private-not-for-profit-centre, and private-for-profit-centre), number of beds (< 100, 100–200 and > 200 beds) and surgical volume (i.e., total facility-level surgeries across all specialties over the study period, per 100 minimally invasive procedures). In the absence of comprehensive data on the availability of robotic platforms in French hospitals, we considered a hospital to have access to robotic surgery if it performed at least 10 robotic procedures per calendar year. This pragmatic threshold was chosen to identify hospitals with regular robotic activity. A threshold of 10 annual procedures has also been used to define very low-volume centres in a previous French study [14].

Statistical analysis

We first plotted the quarterly number of minimally invasive surgeries between January 2017 and June 2023. All subsequent analyses covered the period from July 2020 to June 2023. We then plotted the quarterly number and proportion of robotic surgeries, and described the number of facilities with a robot by semester according to the number of surgical specialties, from the second semester of 2020 (S2 2020) to the first semester of 2023 (S1 2023). To assess temporal trends in the proportion of robotic procedures between July 2020 and June 2023, seasonal autoregressive integrated moving average (SARIMA) models were fitted for all surgical specialties combined and separately for each specialty. The coefficient of the temporal regressor represented the estimated average monthly change in the proportion of robotic procedures. For ease of interpretation, these estimates were multiplied by three, and reported as changes in percentage points per quarter with standard errors and p values. We also described, overall, by speciality and organ, the number and proportion of minimally invasive surgeries (total, non-robotic and robotic).

We mapped robotic surgery use at the departmental level, with all surgical specialties combined, to assess territorial disparities in access. This finer geographical scale allowed us to provide a more detailed assessment of geographic variation than previous studies conducted at the regional level [4, 9]. Patient and healthcare facility characteristics were described according to surgery type and specialty.

The determinants of robotic surgery use were investigated by surgical specialty using multivariable logistic regression models. Covariates included: patient characteristics (age, sex, FDep and Charlson score), healthcare facility characteristics (facility type, number of beds, surgical volume and French region) and temporal factors (season of surgery, with summer defined as June, July, and August ; COVID-19 lockdown period, defined as whether surgery was performed during the second or third COVID-19 lockdown). Results were presented as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). The main analysis used a threshold of 10 robotic procedures per calendar year to define facilities with a robotic system, and a sensitivity analysis used a threshold of 50 procedures.

Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and proportions, whereas continuous variables are described using means and standard deviations. All analyses were conducted with SAS® software (version 7.15 HF9). Geographic visualisations were produced in R (version 4.3.1) using the sf package [15].

Results

Use of minimally invasive surgeries

Between January 2017 and June 2023, a total of 914,350 minimally invasive surgeries were performed in France (Supplementary Fig. 1). The number of surgeries followed a seasonal pattern, peaking at the beginning of the year and declining during the summer. During the first COVID-19 lockdown, we observed a decrease in the number of minimally invasive surgeries and an increase in the proportion of robotic surgeries (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Between July 2020 and June 2023, 432,505 minimally invasive surgeries were performed including 82,840 robotic-assisted surgeries (19.2%) (Fig. 1). The proportion of robotic surgeries varied by specialty: 63.6% in urology (N = 52,506/82,607), 18.5% in thoracic surgery (N = 5,676/30,681), 16.8% in ENT surgery (N = 533/3,171), 9.7% in gynaecology (N = 10,962/113,591), and 6.5% in digestive surgery (N = 13,163/202,455). Robotic procedures were most commonly used for prostate surgery (72.8%), rectal surgery (12.3%), mediastinal procedures (37.5%), and tongue (19.0%) (Table 1). Between July 2020 and June 2023, the proportion of robotic procedures increased progressively, with an estimated increase of 0.66% points per quarter. Quarterly increases were 1.06% points in urology, 0.31 in digestive surgery, 0.43 in gynaecology, 1.19 in thoracic surgery, and 0.93 in ENT surgery (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Figs. S3-S7).

Characteristics of operated patients and healthcare facilities

Urology

Most of patients undergoing a minimally invasive surgery were men (robotic surgery: 88.6%; non-robotic surgery: 79.1%). Among patients undergoing non-robotic surgeries, 39.5% were aged 60–69 years and 35.3% were ≥ 70 years; among robotic surgeries, these percentages were 45.8% and 29.9%, respectively. The mean number of comorbidities was approximately 3 in both groups. Patients undergoing minimally invasive surgery more often lived in the least deprived areas (Q1-Q3 of FDep, robotic surgery: 64.8%; non-robotic surgery: 58.1%). Most of patients underwent surgery in private for-profit centre (robotic surgery: 47.9%, non-robotic surgery: 60.4%) (Table 2). Among facilities performing minimally invasive surgeries in urology (N = 476), 42.9% had acquired a robot, including 47.1% of private for-profit centres (Table 3). Among university hospitals performing minimally invasive surgeries in urology (N = 63), 69.8% performed robot-assisted procedures, compared with lower proportions in other facility types (Supplementary Table 2).

Robotic surgery use was higher among patients aged 50 years or older (ORs ranging from 1.49 to 2.54) than among those aged under 50 years and lower in the most deprived areas (Q4-Q5 of FDep, OR 0.87, 95% CI 0.83–0.91). No association was observed with the number of comorbidities. Robotic surgery use was higher in hospital centres or private non-profit centres than in university hospitals (OR 1.10, 95% CI 1.04–1.17) and in facilities with fewer than 100 beds than in those with more than 200 beds (OR 1.65, 95% CI 1.43–1.91), but lower in facilities with 100–200 beds than in those with more than 200 beds (OR 0.75, 95% CI 0.70–0.81). Surgical volume was also positively associated with robotic surgery use (OR 1.04, 95% CI 1.03–1.05). Robotic surgery use was lower in all regions than in “Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes” (ORs ranging from 0.20 to 0.79), during summer, autumn, and winter than in spring (ORs ranging from 0.87 to 0.94), and during the COVID-19 lockdown period (OR 0.89, 95% CI 0.84–0.94) (Supplementary Table 3).

Gynaecology

In total, 37.5% of women in the robotic surgery group and 46.0% in the non-robotic surgery group were under 50 years. In both groups, most women had ≤ 1 comorbidity and lived in the least deprived areas (Q1-Q3 of FDep: 64.3% and 61.2%). Around 46% of women underwent robotic surgery in university hospitals, while 47.3% of women undergoing non-robotic surgery were treated in private for-profit centres (Table 2). Among facilities performing minimally invasive surgeries in gynaecology (N = 680), 31.8% had acquired a robot, including 42.6% of private for-profit centres (Table 3). Among university hospitals performing minimally invasive surgeries in gynaecology (N = 84), 65.5% performed robot-assisted procedures, compared with lower proportions in other facility types (Supplementary Table 2).

Robotic surgery use was higher among patients aged 50 years or older (ORs ranging from 1.19 to 1.26) than among those aged under 50 years, and increased with the number of comorbidities (OR 1.12, 95% CI 1.08–1.15). No association was observed with FDep. It was lower in hospital centres or private non-profit centres (OR 0.60, 95% CI 0.57–0.63) and private for-profit centres (OR 0.23, 95% CI 0.22–0.25) than in university hospitals, and in facilities with 100–200 beds (OR 0.50, 95% CI 0.45–0.56) and fewer than 100 beds (OR 0.35, 95% CI 0.22–0.52) than in those with more than 200 beds. It also decreased with surgical volume (OR 0.87, 95% CI 0.86–0.88) and varied across regions (ORs ranging from 0.63 to 3.08). Robotic surgery use was lower during the COVID-19 lockdown period (OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.80–0.92), and no significant association was found with season (Supplementary Table 3).

Digestive surgery

In both groups, a high proportion of patients undergoing minimally invasive digestive surgery were women (robotic surgery: 61.3%; non-robotic surgery: 65.2%) and were under 50 years of age (robotic surgery: 35.6% non-robotic surgery: 46.6%), and lived in the least deprived areas (Q1-Q3 of FDep, robotic surgery: 59.9%; non-robotic surgery: 57.5%). The mean number of comorbidities was 1.9 in the robotic surgery group and 1.56 in the non-robotic surgery group. Around 41% of patients undergoing robotic surgery were treated in university hospitals, whereas patients undergoing non-robotic surgery were more often managed in private for-profit centre (54.7%) (Table 2). A total of 29.4% of facilities which performed minimally invasive digestive surgeries had acquired a robot, including 44.8% of private for-profit centres (Table 3). Among university hospitals performing minimally invasive digestive surgeries (N = 88), 54.5% performed robot-assisted surgeries, a higher proportion than in other facility types (Supplementary Table 2).

Robotic surgery use was higher among patients aged 50 years or older (ORs ranging from 1.21 to 1.36) than among those aged under 50 years, and increased slightly with the number of comorbidities (OR 1.03, 95% CI 1.01–1.05) and with FDep (Q4-Q5, OR 1.14, 95% CI 1.09–1.19). No association was observed with sex. It was lower in hospital centres or private non-profit centres (OR 0.47, 95% CI 0.45–0.50) and private for-profit centres (OR 0.27, 95% CI 0.25–0.28) than in university hospitals, and in facilities with 100–200 beds than in those with more than 200 beds (OR 0.88, 95% CI 0.80–0.96), but it was higher in facilities with fewer than 100 beds (OR 5.40, 95% CI 4.38–6.63). It also decreased with surgical volume (OR 0.88, 95% CI 0.87–0.89), and varied across regions (ORs ranging from 0.31 to 2.50). Robotic surgery use was lower in autumn (OR 0.95, 95% CI 0.90–1.00) and winter (OR 0.90, 95% CI 0.86–0.95) than in spring, and during the COVID-19 lockdown period (OR 0.87, 95% CI 0.82–0.92) (Supplementary Table 3).

Thoracic surgery

More than half of patients undergoing minimally invasive thoracic surgery were men (robotic surgery: 52.4%; non-robotic surgery: 56.2%). In both groups, most were aged 60 years or older (robotic surgery: 72.8%; non-robotic surgery: 70.0%), had an average of three comorbidities, and lived in the least deprived areas (Q1-Q3 of FDep, robotic surgery: 62.6%; non-robotic surgery: 61.0%). Approximately half of the patients underwent robotic or non-robotic thoracic surgery at a university hospital (Table 2). Among the 198 facilities performing minimally invasive thoracic surgeries, 41.4% (N = 82) had a robot, including 39.0% of private for-profit centres (Table 3). Among university hospitals performing minimally invasive thoracic surgeries (N = 58), 55.2% carried out robot-assisted surgeries, a higher proportion than in other facility types (Supplementary Table 2).

Robotic surgery use was lower in men (OR 0.88, 95% CI 0.81–0.95), higher among patients aged 50 years or older (ORs ranging from 1.33 to 1.38) than among those aged under 50 years, and higher in the most deprived area (Q4-Q5 of FDep, OR 1.11, 95% CI 1.03–1.19). No association was found with the number of comorbidities. It was higher in hospital centres or private non-profit centres (OR 1.38, 95% CI 1.27–1.50) and lower in private for-profit centres (OR 0.77, 95% CI 0.70–0.84) than in university hospitals. It was also lower in facilities with 100–200 beds than in those with more than 200 beds (OR 0.39, 95% CI 0.30–0.50), decreased with surgical volume (OR 0.92, 95% CI 0.90–0.94), and varied across regions (ORs ranging from 0.03 to 31.61). Robotic surgery use was lower in summer (OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.76–0.91) than in spring and during the COVID-19 lockdown period (OR 0.79, 95% CI 0.71–0.87) (Supplementary Table 3).

ENT surgery

Most individuals undergoing minimally invasive ENT procedures were men (robotic surgery: 70.9%; non-robotic surgery: 68.5%). In both groups, about 60% of patients were aged 60 years or older, with an average of three comorbidities. A higher proportion of patients undergoing minimally invasive surgery resided in the least deprived areas (Q1-Q3 of FDep, robotic surgery: 67.2%; non-robotic surgery: 58.3%). A total of 68.5% of patients underwent robotic ENT surgery in university hospitals (34.0% in non-robotic group) (Table 2). Only 16% of facilities (N = 54) which performed minimally invasive ENT surgeries had acquired a robot, including 46.3% of university hospitals (Table 3). Most facilities performed their ENT surgeries without robot assistance (Supplementary Table 2).

Robotic surgery use was lower among patients aged 50 years or older (ORs ranging from 0.50 to 0.62) than among those aged under 50 years. No association was found with sex, the number of comorbidities or FDep. It was lower in hospital centres or private non-profit centres (OR 0.57, 95% CI 0.42–0.77) and in private for-profit centres (OR 0.05, 95% CI 0.03–0.09) than in university hospitals, and decreased with surgical volume (OR 0.87, 95% CI 0.81–0.92). It was also higher in facilities with 100–200 beds than in those with more than 200 beds (OR 5.27, 95% CI 1.94–13.90) and varied across regions (ORs ranging from 0.29 to 6.76). No association was found with season or the COVID-19 lockdown period (Supplementary Table 3).

For each specialty, the sensitivity analysis using a threshold of 50 robotic procedures per year yielded broadly consistent results with the main analysis, with the most notable differences observed for healthcare facility characteristics in urology (Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first French nationwide study to assess robotic surgery use across all surgical specialties recorded in the SNDS, including ENT procedures, over a longer observation period, while investigating factors associated with robotic surgery use. We observed a progressive increase in the use of robotic assistance in minimally invasive surgeries over time, with varying levels of adoption depending on surgical specialty and French department. In the multivariable analysis, robotic surgery use was associated with patient sociodemographic characteristics and healthcare facility characteristics, including facility type, number of beds, surgical volume and region.

During the first COVID-19 lockdown, we observed a decline in minimally invasive surgeries, including robotic surgeries, while the proportion of robotic procedures reached its highest level. Previous French studies also reported a decrease in robotic surgical activity; however, oncological procedures declined less than non-oncological procedures, which may explain the higher proportion of robotic procedures during this period. These findings reflect changes in healthcare organisation, with priority given to urgent and cancer-related surgeries during the first lockdown [10, 16–18]. Consequently, we conducted our analyses from July 2020, after the first lockdown [19].

We showed that the proportion of robotic surgeries increased over time across all surgical specialties, consistent with the results reported by Saiydoun et al. between 2021 and 2022 in France [9]. Our findings were also consistent with established models of surgical innovation diffusion (IDEAL framework). The progressive increase in robotic surgery, with adoption exceeding 20% in 2022, suggests that robotic surgery may have reached a “tipping point” in its diffusion, beyond which adoption may accelerate [19].

Between July 2020 and June 2023, urology accounted for 63.6% of all robotic surgeries, and the use of robotic assistance in this specialty was already high by July 2020 compared with others. This suggests that robotic surgery had long been well integrated into urology, particularly for radical prostatectomies, thereby contributing to the predominance of male patients [20]. In contrast, the adoption of robotic assistance in gynaecology, thoracic surgery and ENT surgery remains more debated [21–23], and the heterogeneity of indications in digestive surgery may also partly explain the lower uptake of robotic surgery in this specialty [24].

Moreover, the number of facilities performing robotic surgery across two or more specialities increased during this period. This trend may reflect a transition from specialty-specific adoption towards the development of multidisciplinary institutional robotic platforms. It may be related to the high cost of robotic systems, encouraging shared use across specialties to optimise utilisation and improve economic efficiency [25, 26]. Future integration of artificial intelligence (AI) may further enhance the value of these platforms by improving surgical precision and intraoperative decision-making [27].

We observed geographical variations in the use of robotic surgery across French departments. These findings were consistent with those of a previous French study that reported regional disparities at a larger geographical scale [9]. Spatial differences in the number of minimally invasive surgeries may be related to the distribution of healthcare facilities, with higher volumes observed in university hospitals mainly located in large metropolitan areas. In some French departments, high proportions of robotic surgery were observed despite low overall procedure volumes, likely reflecting the presence of one or a few specialised centres. This territorial heterogeneity may also reflect differences in healthcare accessibility, the presence of experienced surgical teams and deprivation levels in patients’ areas of residence.

Robotic surgery use varied according to patient characteristics, including age, sex and socioeconomic deprivation. In particular, robotic surgery use was higher among patients living in less deprived areas in urology. Studies from the United Kingdom and the United States have also reported greater demand for robotic assistance among patients in less deprived areas and a higher concentration of robotic centres in these areas [28, 29]. In addition, in France, although robotic-assisted surgery is not associated with a specific reimbursement, higher physician fees in private settings may lead to out-of-pocket payments, which could further contribute to socioeconomic disparities in access [27]. Robotic surgery use also varied according to healthcare facility characteristics, including facility type, number of beds, surgical volume, and region. In gynaecology and digestive surgery, a higher proportion of patients undergoing robotic surgery were treated in university hospitals, which may partly reflect differences in case-mix, as patients with more complex indications may be more likely to be referred to academic centres with greater technical resources and training activities [30, 31]. In ENT surgery, robotic surgery was also mainly performed in university hospitals, which may reflect their particular role in introducing and training surgeons in new surgical techniques in a specialty where robotic surgery has developed more recently.

Temporal factors were also associated with robotic surgery use, with generally lower use in summer, autumn, and winter than in spring, as well as during the COVID-19 lockdown period. Other factors, including patient case-mix, hospital competition, technical resources, and surgeons’ experience and training, may also influence robotic surgery use but could not be directly assessed in our study.

Several limitations of this study arise from the use of the SNDS, a medico-administrative database. Although robotic-specific CCAM codes have only been available since March 2020, restricting the observation period, our study covers a longer timeframe than previous French studies. Although some digestive and other specialties (e.g., inguinal hernia repair, cardiac, vascular, and breast surgeries) are recorded in the SNDS, robotic procedures cannot be specifically identified in the absence of dedicated robotic codes. The smaller number of procedures in ENT surgery may also have limited the precision and interpretation of the results for this specialty. As robotic procedures do not receive a specific reimbursement tariff from the French Health Insurance, their identification in the SNDS may be incomplete [5, 32, 33]. Nevertheless, the high cost of robotic systems and the need to monitor their use may encourage accurate coding.

Conclusion

The use of robotic surgery is gradually increasing in France, with adoption varying across surgical specialities, healthcare facility types and geographical area. Continued real-world studies are warranted to monitor its use, assess long-term safety and effectiveness, and inform clinical practice and health policy. As a perspective, we will compare the occurrence of complications and mortality between robotic and non-robotic in gynaecology and thoracic surgery using propensity score analyses and target trial emulation.
