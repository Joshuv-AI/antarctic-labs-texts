Prevalence and Sex-Specific Distribution of Electrocardiographic Variants in Normotensive Nigerian University Students: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Jovita E Egharevba, James Nelson Okema, Clinton Uche Nwanaga, Peter Chukwuma Onyebuagu, Grace Acomo, Ivaan Pitua, Felix Bongomin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElectrocardiographic (ECG) variants occurring in the absence of known cardiac disease are common in healthy young adults but remain poorly characterized in sub-Saharan African populations. Contemporary ECG reference standards are predominantly derived from Caucasian cohorts, and their direct application to individuals of African descent risks misclassification of physiologically normal findings as pathological, with consequent unnecessary investigations and healthcare burden.ObjectiveTo determine the prevalence and sex-specific distribution of ECG variants among apparently healthy, normotensive undergraduate students at a Nigerian federal university, and to assess associations between ECG variant status and demographic variables.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 102 normotensive undergraduate students (55 males, 47 females; mean age 22.4 ± 2.8 years) at the Federal University of Technology, Owerri (FUTO), Imo State, Nigeria. Resting 12-lead ECGs were acquired using standardized protocols and interpreted by a blinded, board-certified cardiologist applying pre-specified diagnostic criteria. Sex-stratified comparisons used chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests with Bonferroni correction. Logistic regression assessed independent predictors of key variants.ResultsExcluding normal sinus rhythm, at least one ECG variant was identified in 71 participants (69.6%; 95% CI 59.8–78.3%). Juvenile T-wave pattern was the most prevalent variant (37.3%; 95% CI 27.9–47.4%), followed by high QRS voltage (22.5%; 95% CI 14.7–32.0%). Juvenile T-wave pattern was more prevalent in females (48.9% vs 27.3%; p=0.02 uncorrected; OR 0.41, 95% CI 0.19–0.91), while high QRS voltage predominated in males (30.9% vs 12.8%; p=0.02 uncorrected; OR 3.18, 95% CI 1.18–8.55); neither finding survived Bonferroni correction (threshold p<0.006) and both are considered exploratory. Twelve participants (11.8%) exhibited borderline QTc prolongation; structured clinical evaluation confirmed no true pathological QTc prolongation in any participant. Early repolarization was absent in this cohort. Age and BMI were not independently associated with variant prevalence.ConclusionECG variants occur in approximately 70% of healthy, normotensive predominantly Igbo university students in south-eastern Nigeria and demonstrate sex-specific patterns. These findings contribute population-specific descriptive data and highlight the need for ethnically informed ECG interpretation to reduce diagnostic misclassification in this population. Multi-center studies across Nigerian and West African cohorts are required to confirm and generalize these observations.Keywords: electrocardiographic variants, population-specific ECG data, Nigerian youth, sex differences, QRS voltage, juvenile T-wave pattern

Introduction

The resting 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) is the most widely used non-invasive investigation in cardiovascular medicine.1 Its role in diagnosing arrhythmias, conduction disorders, myocardial ischemia, and channelopathies is well established.1,2 However, a substantial proportion of clinically healthy individuals demonstrate ECG patterns that deviate from standard textbook norms without any evidence of underlying structural or functional cardiac disease.2,3 These represent normal physiological variation, ECG variants occurring in the absence of known structural or functional cardiac disease rather than pathological findings.

The prevalence of such variants in healthy young adult populations has been reported in the range of 40–60% in predominantly Caucasian cohorts.2,3 Compelling evidence demonstrates significant ethnic variation in ECG morphology: variants such as early repolarization, juvenile T-wave pattern, and high QRS voltage occur substantially more frequently in individuals of African descent than in Caucasian populations.3–5 Despite this, most contemporary ECG reference standards and interpretation guidelines continue to be derived from predominantly European and North American populations.

This ethnic mismatch has important clinical consequences. In the absence of population-specific reference data, ECG findings that represent normal physiological variation in individuals of African ancestry may be misclassified as potentially pathological. Such misinterpretation can lead to unnecessary echocardiographic evaluation, ambulatory monitoring, exercise stress testing, and, in some clinical or occupational contexts, inappropriate restriction from physical activity or employment.4,5 In resource-limited healthcare settings, these avoidable investigations impose a disproportionate burden on patients and health systems.

Nigeria carries one of the highest cardiovascular disease burdens in sub-Saharan Africa yet remains substantially underrepresented in electrocardiographic epidemiology research6–8 University students, aged predominantly 18–30 years, represent a population segment in whom accurate ECG interpretation is particularly consequential both for pre-participation cardiovascular screening and for informing clinical decisions during this critical period of emerging cardiovascular risk. The Federal University of Technology, Owerri (FUTO), situated in Imo State, south-eastern Nigeria, has a predominantly Igbo student body, providing an opportunity to generate population-specific ECG reference data relevant to this major Nigerian ethnic group.

The objective of this study was to determine the prevalence and sex-specific distribution of ECG variants in apparently healthy, normotensive undergraduate students at FUTO, and to assess their associations with age, sex, and body composition. Findings are contextualized within the current literature on ethnicity-related ECG variation.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This cross-sectional descriptive study was conducted at the Department of Human Physiology, School of Basic Medical Sciences, FUTO, Imo State, Nigeria, over a four-week period in September 2025. Ethical approval was granted by the FUTO School of Basic Medical Sciences Ethics and Research Committee (Approval No. FUT/COM/SBMS/REC/Vol1/002, October 27, 2023). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study Population and Eligibility

The target population comprised apparently healthy, normotensive undergraduate students aged 18–30 years enrolled at FUTO. Stratified random sampling was applied, with stratification by faculty and year of study to achieve proportional representation across the institution. Eligible participants were registered undergraduates who were normotensive at screening (systolic blood pressure <140 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure <90 mmHg), and who provided written informed consent. Participants were excluded if they reported a history of cardiovascular disease, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic systemic illness, or use of any cardiovascular-active medication; were pregnant or lactating; or had evidence of acute illness on the day of assessment. Self-reported physical activity level was recorded as low, moderate, or high using a standard validated single-item question to explore its potential confounding effect on ECG variant prevalence. Tribal and ethnic affiliation was also recorded as a secondary variable.

Sample Size Estimation

Sample size was calculated using the formula n = Z2p(1−p)/d2, assuming a 95% confidence level (Z=1.96), an expected variant prevalence of 35% based on prior literature in African youth,9 and a margin of error of 9.5%, yielding a minimum of 100 participants. A total of 102 participants were enrolled to account for potential incomplete data. The authors acknowledge that this sample size, while adequate for primary prevalence estimation, is insufficient to establish robust reference standards and is underpowered for subgroup analyses involving lower-prevalence variants. Sex-stratified results for variants with fewer than 10 events per group are regarded as exploratory.

Data Collection

Demographic and Anthropometric Assessment

Age, sex, year of study, ethnic affiliation, and self-reported physical activity level were recorded using a standardized questionnaire. Height was measured barefoot using a calibrated stadiometer; weight was recorded on a calibrated electronic scale with participants in light clothing. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m) squared.

Blood Pressure Measurement

Blood pressure was measured in the seated position after ≥5 minutes of rest using a calibrated automated digital sphygmomanometer (Omron M6 Comfort, Omron Healthcare, Kyoto, Japan), applied to the left arm at heart level. Two readings were recorded at least five minutes apart and averaged to confirm normotensive status.

Electrocardiographic Recording

Resting 12-lead ECGs were recorded using a Schiller AT-10 Plus electrocardiograph (Schiller AG, Baar, Switzerland), calibrated daily at 1 mV = 10 mm. Recordings were performed in a quiet, temperature-controlled room (22–24°C) with participants supine after ≥5 minutes of rest. All recordings were obtained between 08:00 and 12:00 hours to minimize circadian variation in QTc and autonomic tone, consistent with established standardization protocols.10 Electrode placement followed AHA/ACC/HRS/ISCE recommendations.10 ECGs were acquired at 25 mm/s and 10 mm/mV. Tracings with significant artefact were repeated.

Electrocardiographic Interpretation

All ECGs were interpreted by a single board-certified cardiologist with subspecialty training in electrophysiology, who was fully blinded to all participant demographic, clinical, and anthropometric data. A standardized interpretation protocol was applied prospectively. Rhythm, heart rate, PR interval, QRS duration, QRS axis, QT/QTc interval, and wave morphology were systematically assessed in every tracing. Interval measurements (PR interval, QRS duration, QT interval) and QRS axis were obtained from the automated analysis of the Schiller AT-10 Plus electrocardiograph, with manual confirmation by the interpreting cardiologist in all cases where automated measurements appeared artefactual or inconsistent with visual inspection of the tracing.

Pre-specified diagnostic criteria for each variant, defined a priori, were as follows:

Juvenile T-wave pattern was defined as isolated T-wave inversion confined to leads V1–V3 (and optionally V4), without ST-segment deviation, pathological Q waves, or clinical symptoms.2,11 High QRS voltage was assessed using Sokolow–Lyon criteria (SV1 + RV5 or RV6 ≥35 mm) and Cornell criteria (RaVL + SV3 ≥28 mm in males; ≥20 mm in females) applied independently; participants meeting either criterion were classified as having high QRS voltage, and those meeting both are reported separately. Because echocardiographic data were not obtained, findings are reported as “high QRS voltage” without asserting absence of left ventricular hypertrophy—an acknowledged limitation. Borderline QTc prolongation was defined as QTc ≥450 ms in males and ≥460 ms in females by the Bazett formula; Fridericia correction was also applied to all affected participants, and participants with QTc ≥480 ms were referred for cardiology review. First-degree AV block was defined as a PR interval >200 ms on standard leads, stable across three consecutive complexes. Early repolarization was defined as J-point elevation ≥1 mm in ≥2 contiguous inferior or lateral leads with upward-sloping ST segment in the absence of ischemic symptoms, with anterior leads V2–V3 excluded per this pre-specified protocol consistent with inferolateral early repolarization criteria.12 J-point elevation was measured at the junction of the QRS complex and ST segment, referenced to the PR segment baseline, using manual calipers on paper tracings at standard 25 mm/s speed. We acknowledge that this conservative threshold and exclusion of anterior leads may underestimate true early repolarization prevalence.12 Heart rate variants: sinus bradycardia (<50 bpm), borderline bradycardia (50–59 bpm), sinus tachycardia (>100 bpm), and borderline tachycardia (90–100 bpm).

To assess inter-rater reliability, a random sample of 20 ECGs (~20% of the total) was independently re-interpreted by a second blinded cardiologist (F.B.) using the same protocol. Cohen’s kappa was calculated for each variant; overall inter-rater agreement was substantial (kappa=0.82, 95% CI 0.71–0.93). Normal sinus rhythm is reported as a baseline characteristic and is not classified as an ECG variant.

Statistical Analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Continuous variables are expressed as means ± standard deviations (SD); categorical variables as frequencies and percentages with 95% confidence intervals (Wilson score method). Sex-stratified comparisons used chi-square tests for categorical variables, with Fisher’s exact test applied when any expected cell count was <5, and independent-samples t-tests for continuous variables after confirming normality by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. To account for multiple comparisons across eight sex-stratified ECG variant tests, Bonferroni correction was applied (corrected threshold p<0.006); uncorrected p-values are reported alongside for transparency. Binary logistic regression, including sex, age, BMI, and resting heart rate as covariates, assessed independent predictors of juvenile T-wave pattern and high QRS voltage; model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test and Nagelkerke R2. Post-hoc power calculations were performed for the two primary sex comparisons. All tests were two-tailed.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Of 150 students approached, 102 met eligibility criteria and completed all study procedures (response rate 68%). The cohort comprised 55 males (53.9%) and 47 females (46.1%). Mean age was 22.4 ± 2.8 years (range 18–30). Mean BMI was 23.1 ± 3.5 kg/m2. All participants were normotensive (mean SBP 118.2 ± 8.5 mmHg; mean DBP 76.3 ± 7.2 mmHg). The cohort was predominantly Igbo (88.2%). Physical activity levels were: low 28.4%, moderate 51.0%, and high 20.6%; activity level did not differ significantly between sexes (p=0.18). Resting heart rate was significantly higher in females than males (74.0 ± 10.2 vs 68.9 ± 9.7 bpm; p=0.01). No other baseline characteristics differed significantly by sex (Table 1).

ECG Variant Prevalence

Normal sinus rhythm was present in 91 participants (89.2%; 95% CI 81.6–94.5%). Excluding normal sinus rhythm, at least one ECG variant was identified in 71 participants (69.6%; 95% CI 59.8–78.3%). Thirty-one participants (30.4%) had ECGs without identifiable variants. The most prevalent variants were juvenile T-wave pattern (37.3%), high QRS voltage (22.5%), and borderline QTc prolongation (11.8%; reported separately). First-degree AV block was identified in seven participants (6.9%). Early repolarization was systematically assessed in all participants; no cases were identified. Characterization of Key Variants

Juvenile T-wave pattern (isolated T-wave inversions in leads V1–V3 without ST-segment deviation or pathological Q waves) was identified in 38 participants; all were asymptomatic with no personal or family history of cardiac disease. Lead-specific distribution of T-wave inversions was as follows: isolated V1 inversion only, 14 participants (36.8% of those with juvenile T-wave pattern; 13.7% of total cohort); V1 and V2 inversion only, 16 participants (42.1%; 15.7% of total); V1 through V3 inversion, 8 participants (21.1%; 7.8% of total). High QRS voltage was present in 23 participants: 15 met Sokolow–Lyon criteria only, four met Cornell criteria only, and four met both simultaneously. Because echocardiographic data were not obtained, findings are reported as “high QRS voltage” throughout this manuscript; no claim is made regarding the presence or absence of true left ventricular hypertrophy, an acknowledged limitation. First-degree AV block was identified in seven participants with PR intervals ranging from 202 to 218 ms; all were asymptomatic without clinical evidence of structural heart disease. Early repolarization was absent in this cohort.

Borderline QTc Prolongation: Clinical Evaluation and Disposition

Twelve participants (11.8%; four males, eight females) demonstrated borderline QTc prolongation by Bazett formula. Fridericia correction was applied to all 12 cases; three female participants retained borderline QTc prolongation on Fridericia correction (QTcF ≥460 ms). All 12 participants underwent structured clinical evaluation. Four were referred for formal cardiology review including echocardiography and Holter monitoring; no pathological condition was identified in any referred participant. Long QT syndrome was formally excluded in the two participants with QTc ≥480 ms. No true pathological QTc prolongation was confirmed in this cohort. Individual dispositions are presented in Table 3.

Sex-Stratified Analysis

Sex-stratified comparisons identified nominal (uncorrected) differences for juvenile T-wave pattern and high QRS voltage. Juvenile T-wave pattern was more prevalent in females (48.9%) than males (27.3%; χ2=5.24, p=0.02 uncorrected; OR 0.41, 95% CI 0.19–0.91). High QRS voltage was more prevalent in males (30.9%) than females (12.8%; χ2=5.31, p=0.02 uncorrected; OR 3.18, 95% CI 1.18–8.55). However, neither finding survived Bonferroni correction (corrected threshold p<0.006); both are therefore considered exploratory sex-specific trends. Post-hoc power for these two comparisons was 74% and 71%, respectively. No other sex differences were observed. Sex-stratified data are presented in Table 4.

Associations with Demographic Variables

Age, analysed continuously, was not associated with variant prevalence (Pearson r=0.12, p=0.23). BMI did not differ between participants with at least one variant (23.4 ± 3.2 kg/m2) and those without (22.6 ± 3.8 kg/m2; t=1.02, p=0.31). Blood pressure showed no significant correlation with any ECG variant (all r<0.15, p>0.05). Resting heart rate was significantly lower in the three participants with sinus bradycardia (54.3 ± 4.2 bpm) compared to the remainder (71.8 ± 10.1 bpm; p<0.001). Self-reported physical activity level was not a significant predictor of high QRS voltage (high vs low/moderate: 28.6% vs 21.3%; p=0.54).

Logistic Regression

Binary logistic regression for juvenile T-wave pattern, including sex, age, BMI, and resting heart rate as covariates, identified female sex as the only significant independent predictor (adjusted OR 0.38, 95% CI 0.17–0.86, p=0.02; Hosmer–Lemeshow p=0.68; Nagelkerke R2=0.10). For high QRS voltage, male sex was the only significant independent predictor (adjusted OR 3.04, 95% CI 1.12–8.24, p=0.03; Hosmer–Lemeshow p=0.72; Nagelkerke R2=0.09). Age and BMI were not significant predictors in either model.

Discussion

This study provides the first systematic assessment of ECG variant prevalence in a Nigerian university student population employing explicit pre-specified diagnostic criteria, blinded dual-interpreter reliability assessment, and sex-stratified analysis with multiple comparison correction. Among 102 normotensives, predominantly Igbo undergraduate students at FUTO, south-eastern Nigeria, 69.6% demonstrated at least one ECG variant, a prevalence substantially exceeding rates in Caucasian populations (40–60%)2,3 and consistent with findings in other African-descent cohorts.4,9,13

The two most clinically important findings are the high overall prevalence of juvenile T-wave pattern and the sex-specific patterning of variant distribution. Juvenile T-wave pattern was observed in 37.3% of participants and was more prevalent in females (48.9% vs 27.3%; p=0.02 uncorrected, exploratory). These figures align closely with prevalences of 25–40% reported in African American populations,4,13 and contrast markedly with Caucasian prevalences typically below 5%.13 This ethnic divergence carries direct clinical implications: T-wave inversions in leads V1–V3 in an asymptomatic African-descent individual are substantially more likely to represent physiological variation than early cardiomyopathy or Brugada pattern, and ECG interpretation must incorporate population context.

High QRS voltage was identified in 22.5% of participants using Sokolow–Lyon or Cornell voltage criteria, with male predominance (30.9% vs 12.8%; p=0.02 uncorrected, exploratory), consistent with established sex-related differences in cardiac mass and wall thickness. Critically, all affected participants were normotensive and without clinical evidence of structural heart disease, confirming prior observations that voltage criteria for left ventricular hypertrophy carry high false-positive rates in young African individuals.9,13 Future studies integrating echocardiography are essential to validate voltage-based criteria in this population.

The complete absence of early repolarization in this cohort, a pattern reported in 3–15% of African-descent populations in other studies, is noteworthy.12,14 Several factors may contribute to this null finding. First, the conservative ≥1 mm J-point elevation threshold applied in this study is among the most stringent used in epidemiological research; lower thresholds (≥0.1 mV) used in some studies yield higher prevalence rates. Second, the pre-specified exclusion of anterior leads V2–V3 from the definition means that early repolarization patterns confined to the anterior wall would not have been captured. Third, J-point elevation is known to be sex-, age-, and lead-dependent, and a uniform threshold applied across all leads may underestimate true prevalence, particularly in females. Fourth, genuine population heterogeneity within Nigerian ethnic subgroups this cohort being predominantly Igbo may yield different variant frequencies compared with pan-ethnic West African or diaspora samples. Future studies in this population should consider applying multiple early repolarization definitions in parallel, including anterior leads, to better characterize prevalence across definitional approaches.

Comparison with other young adult cohorts contextualizes our findings. A recently published cross-sectional study of healthy Vietnamese university students reported lower overall prevalence of T-wave inversions and a different sex-distribution profile, suggesting that ECG variant patterns may reflect ethnic-specific physiological differences even within broad geographic regions.15 In contrast, our T-wave inversion prevalence of 37.3% is substantially higher, consistent with the well-established higher prevalence of this finding in populations of African descent. Regarding sex-specific patterns, Sheikh et al demonstrated pronounced sex differences in ECG findings among elite black and white athletes, with black male athletes showing particularly high rates of voltage criteria and repolarization variants.5 Although our population is non-athletic, the directional consistency of sex-specific QRS voltage differences between our cohort and the athlete data supports the biological plausibility of sex-related cardiac electrophysiological differences across fitness levels in African-descent individuals. These cross-cohort comparisons underscore the importance of both ethnicity and sex as determinants of ECG morphology and reinforce the need for population- and sex-specific interpretation frameworks.

The sex-specific trends for juvenile T-wave pattern and high QRS voltage were consistent across chi-square and logistic regression analyses after adjustment for age, BMI, and heart rate, although neither survived Bonferroni correction at the pre-specified threshold of p<0.006. These findings are therefore exploratory and hypothesis-generating; they nonetheless support the case for investigating sex-specific ECG interpretation thresholds in African-descent populations in future adequately powered studies, analogous to the sex-specific QTc thresholds already embedded in current guidelines.

The structured evaluation of all 12 participants with borderline QTc prolongation is a methodological strength. The finding that no participant had confirmed pathological QTc prolongation after repeat ECG, Fridericia correction, medication and electrolyte review, and cardiology referral in high-risk cases supports a cautious clinical approach: borderline QTc values in asymptomatic young adults should prompt structured evaluation rather than immediate restriction or diagnosis.16

From a public health perspective, the finding that approximately 70% of apparently healthy Nigerian Igbo university students exhibit at least one ECG variant has direct implications for cardiovascular screening programs. Clinicians interpreting ECGs in this population should appreciate that juvenile T-wave inversion in V1–V3, high precordial QRS voltage, and borderline QTc values frequently occur in the absence of known cardiac pathology. Population-specific descriptive data of the kind generated here are important inputs for developing evidence-based local guidelines, though larger confirmatory studies are required before formal normative standards can be established.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths include standardized ECG acquisition with daily calibration, prospective pre-specified diagnostic criteria for each variant, blinded dual-reader inter-rater reliability assessment (kappa=0.82), sex-stratified comparisons with Bonferroni correction applied, structured clinical evaluation of all participants with borderline QTc, and logistic regression with model fit statistics.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the sample of 102 students from a single institution is predominantly Igbo and cannot be generalized to Yoruba, Hausa-Fulani, or other major Nigerian populations, nor to West African populations broadly. Second, the sample size, while adequate for primary prevalence estimation, is insufficient to establish robust reference standards and is underpowered for subgroup and sex-stratified analyses; neither sex difference finding survived Bonferroni correction, and all such comparisons are exploratory. Third, echocardiographic data were not obtained, precluding formal exclusion of structural LVH. Fourth, only resting ECGs were performed. Fifth, Bazett formula QTc correction is susceptible to over-correction at faster heart rates, addressed by Fridericia sensitivity analysis. Sixth, the conservative early repolarization definition (≥1 mm threshold; exclusion of anterior leads V2–V3) may underestimate true prevalence; future studies should apply parallel definitions. Seventh, lead-specific distribution of T-wave inversions (V1-only, V1–V2, V1–V3) was characterized post-hoc and should be prospectively specified in future studies. Eighth, sinus arrhythmia, PACs, and PVCs were noted during interpretation but not formally enumerated as discrete variants; their omission from prevalence reporting may affect completeness of ECG variation data. Ninth, the cross-sectional design precludes assessment of variant stability over time or long-term prognostic significance. Tenth, volunteer bias cannot be excluded.

Conclusions

ECG variants occur in approximately 70% of apparently healthy, normotensive undergraduate students at a south-eastern Nigerian federal university with a predominantly Igbo ethnic composition. Juvenile T-wave pattern and high QRS voltage are the most prevalent variants and demonstrate sex-specific distributions, though these differences did not survive Bonferroni correction and should be considered exploratory pending confirmation in larger studies. These findings contribute population-specific descriptive data relevant to ECG interpretation in young Nigerian Igbo adults and reinforce the clinical importance of ethnically informed electrocardiographic assessment. Borderline QTc prolongation, identified in 11.8% of participants, warrants structured clinical evaluation rather than automatic classification as benign or pathological. Multi-center and longitudinal studies across Nigeria’s diverse ethnic populations are needed before evidence-based national ECG reference standards can be established.
