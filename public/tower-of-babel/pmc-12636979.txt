Exploring self-management behaviour clusters and predictors in community-dwelling older adults with declined intrinsic capacity: a cross-sectional study using TPB-HAPA integration
Authors: Yinan Zhao, Xiaoyang Li, Li Chen, Chi Zhang, Jintao Luo, Juan Yang, Qin Zhou, Siliang Wu, Hui Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe decline in intrinsic capacity (IC) among older adults poses significant challenges to healthy ageing. Despite the importance of self-management in enhancing IC, research on self-management patterns and their predictors among older adults with declined IC remains limited.PurposeThis study aimed to explore clusters of self-management behaviours and their predictors among older adults with declined IC using an integrated theoretical framework combining the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB) and the Health Action Process Approach (HAPA).MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted in two community health centres in China from December 2022 to June 2023. A convenience sample of 308 older adults with declined intrinsic capacity was recruited based on WHO-recommended criteria. Self-management behaviours were assessed using the Chronic Disease Self-Management Behaviour Scale. HAPA constructs (self-efficacy, outcome expectancy, risk perception, planning) and TPB constructs (subjective norms, behavioural attitudes, perceived behavioural control, behavioural intention) were measured via validated questionnaires refined through expert consultation. Unsupervised k-means clustering was applied to identify behavioural subgroups, validated by principal component analysis. Inter-subgroup differences were examined using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) or rank-sum tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables.ResultsThree distinct self-management behaviour subgroups were identified among older adults with declined intrinsic capacity: Hesitant to Act (n=196), Norm-Dependent (n=42) and Assertive Decisiveness (n=70). Significant differences were observed across subgroups in demographic characteristics, including gender (p=0.017) and education (p=0.005), as well as in HAPA/TPB construct scores, including perceived behavioural control and planning (all p<0.001).ConclusionsThis study identifies three distinct patterns of self-management behaviours among older adults with declined IC. The findings highlight the need for tailored interventions targeting the specific challenges faced by each subgroup. Future research should validate these findings in larger samples and explore causal relationships between variables.

Introduction

Intrinsic capacity (IC), as the cornerstone of healthy ageing, encompasses several dimensions, including mobility, cognitive function, sensory function, nutritional status and depressive status,1 2 and is an independent predictor of disability and death in older adults.3 To this end, the WHO issued the Integrated Care of Elderly People (ICOPE) guidelines in 2017 aiming to preserve the IC and thereby ensuring a high quality of life through a whole life course perspective in older adults.1 The ICOPE guidelines specify a set of measurement tools for community settings, including the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), the Mini-Nutritional Assessment (MNA), and the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB), enabling timely detection of declined IC and appropriate interventions in older adults. The second edition of ICOPE4 expands assessments to include social engagement and environmental factors but retains the five core domains.4 This study utilised the original guidelines for IC assessment to focus on these core domains, aligning with the research emphasis on functional declines.

Self-management is an important strategy to maintain and enhance the IC of older adults. Personalised behavioural change strategies, including goal-setting, health education, establishing self-efficacy, making plans, providing support and resources, and monitoring and feedback, have been used to improve health awareness and self-management behaviours among patients (aged 65 and above) with chronic diseases such as diabetes,5 hypertension6 and breast cancer.7 In parallel, community interdisciplinary collaboration and home care models, as well as telehealth management technology (also known as mHealth technology—medical or public health practice that is delivered with the support of mobile phones, patient monitoring devices and other wireless devices8) in self-management of older adults9 have been adopted to comprehensively enhance self-management behaviour levels, which can lead to improved health status and reduce outpatient visits, hospitalisations, healthcare utilisation and costs. Multiple factors can affect the self-management behaviour in older adults, including situational characteristics (people, problems, environments) and individual factors (knowledge, experience, skills, values).10 Meanwhile, mHealth technology provides an effective medium to improve self-management behaviours of older adults.9

Nevertheless, the focus on the declined IC was much less, and chronic diseases are the main focus of self-management behaviours in older adults.11 12 This might be because the detection and monitoring of the declined IC in community IC timely remain largely unrecognised, and the focus of primary healthcare is still on diagnosis and treatment of diseases.

It has been reported that the integrated model (derived from the theory integration approach) can explain and predict self-management behaviours more comprehensively and accurately compared with separated models such as self-efficacy and behaviour change theory.1316 In particular, Richards enhanced the explanatory power towards the exercise intention than the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB) alone by integrating the Extended Parallel Process Model and the TPB15 via predictors of health behaviour change such as obesity, diet and exercise. Similarly, Ahadzadeh improved the overall validity of prediction in mobile health use behaviours by integrating the Health Belief Model and Technology Acceptance Model among Malaysian urban women.14

Therefore, this study aims to comprehensively understand and identify clusters of self-management behaviours among older adults with declined IC and to examine the predictors of these behaviours using an integrated theoretical framework that combines the TPB and the Health Action Process Approach (HAPA). We believe the integration of TPB and HAPA would provide a comprehensive framework and enhance behavioural prediction accuracy because the TPB explains the intention formation phase, while HAPA captures the transition from intention to behaviour, including planning, action and maintenance. Understanding clusters of self-management behaviours among older adults with declined IC would facilitate more effective intervention strategies to enhance self-management capabilities in this vulnerable population, thereby promoting healthy ageing and reducing the burden on healthcare systems.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a cross-sectional study conducted from December 2022 to June 2023. Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling method from two community health service centres in Zhuzhou City, Hunan Province, China. Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling method from two community health service centres located in different administrative districts (Tianyuan District and Hetang District) to enhance geographic diversity. This study follows the STROBE guidelines for cross-sectional studies.17

The inclusion criteria were: (i) permanent residents of the community aged 60 years and above who would be living in the community in the next 6 months; (ii) at least one of the five domains (mobility, cognitive function, sensory function, nutritional status, depressive status) scored one on the WHO-recommended intrinsic capacity instrument,18 assessed following the original ICOPE guidelines (participants could have impairments in one or multiple domains); and (iii) informed consent and signing of the informed consent form. The exclusion criteria were: (i) suffering major diseases, such as malignant cancer, severe cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases and the need for major organ transplants; (ii) severe impairment in any of the five dimensions of intrinsic capacity, with a score of ≤9 in the cognitive domain of the MMSE, a score of ≥10 in the depression domain of the Geriatric Depression Scale-15, a score of <7 in the mobility functioning of the SPPB, a score of <8 in the nutritional domain of the MNA Short Form, a score of ≥8 in the hearing domain of the Hearing Handicap Inventory for the Elderly - Screening Version (HHIE-S),1923 and a score of ≥24 in the hearing domain of the HHIE-S; (iii) Participating in other health-related intervention studies.

The sample size of this study is 5 to 10 times the number of questionnaire entries,24 the independent variable of this study is 41 entries, and considering that 10% of the questionnaires are invalid, at least 226 cases need to be in this study to be collected.

Measurements

Self-management behaviour

The Chronic Disease Self-Management Behaviour Scale (CDSMS) was developed by the Centre for Patient Education and Research at Stanford University based on Bandura’s Self-Management Efficacy Theoretical Framework and is designed to effectively assess patients' behavioural performance in self-management.25 The scale consists of 3 dimensions: motor exercise, cognitive symptom management practices and communication with physicians. An equidistant scale was used, with higher scores being associated with better self-management behaviours. Score = (actual score on the scale/highest possible score on the scale) × 100%, with ≥80% being high, 61%–79% being moderate and ≤60% being low. The translation and cultural adaptation of the Chronic Disease Self-Management Behaviour Scale were first conducted by Fu in a randomised controlled trial of the Chronic Disease Self-Management Programme in Shanghai, China.26 The culturally adapted version has been validated and widely used in older Chinese populations. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the Chinese version is 0.788, with those of its subscales ranging from 0.785 to 0.884.27

Independent variables

The HAPA model motivational stage constructs including self-efficacy, outcome expectancy and risk perception. The TPB model includes indirect constructs such as subjective norm, behavioural attitudes, perceived behavioural control, behavioural intention and planning.

The operational definitions are as follows:

Behavioural Intention refers to their subjective willingness to engage in self-management. Behavioural attitudes encompass their positive or negative feelings and perceptions towards self-management. Subjective norms reflect the perceived pressure from significant others or groups on whether to undertake self-management. Perceived Behavioural Control assesses their belief in the ease of implementing self-management practices. Self-efficacy measures their confidence in successfully carrying out self-management behaviours. Outcomes Expectations represent their subjective projections of the subsequent benefits from self-management. Perceived risk captures the degree to which they perceive harm from risky scenarios. Planning denotes whether older adults devise strategies for when, where and how to execute their self-management plan, including contingencies for anticipated challenges.

The researcher designs the initial questions of the questionnaire based on the literature review and the opinions of experts in the field related to research and scale development and then measures and approves the validity and reliability of the questionnaire.2832 To determine the validity, both surface validity (qualitative and quantitative types) and content validity (quantitative type) were used.

The Delphi expert consultation method was used to further refine the instrument, involving 12 experts with backgrounds in geriatric nursing (n=4), public health (n=3), clinical geriatrics (n=3) and health psychology (n=2). Five indicators (expert positive coefficient, representativeness, authority coefficient (Cr), coefficient of variation (Cv) and opinion coordination degree) were used to evaluate the process. The process included 3 rounds of consultation. Consensus was defined as Cr ≥ 0.70, Cv ≤ 0.25, and a coordination coefficient ≥ 0.30.33 Furthermore, cognitive interviews were conducted with eight older people from the target group. During the interviews, the researcher actively guided and recorded the responses of the respondents to obtain feedback on the accuracy of expression, legibility and comprehension of the questionnaire entries. Their comments were taken on board and included in the questionnaire if there were questions.34

The researcher used Cronbach’s alpha coefficient to assess the reliability of the questionnaire developed by the researcher. For this purpose, the researcher administered the prepared pilot questionnaire to 30 persons from the target group, and Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated after the completion of the questionnaire. For all the constructs, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was above 0.7, so the reliability of the instrument used in this study was optimal.34

Sociodemographic indicators

Sociodemographic indicators include demographic information such as age, sex, marital status and educational attainment as well as socioeconomic status such as occupation, household composition and economic income.

Data collection

During the data collection process, from June 2022 to November 2022, research staff collaborated with Community Healthcare Service Center (CHSC) administrators to obtain a list of older adults meeting basic age and residency criteria from electronic health records, and trained nurses at each CHSC then conducted brief telephone or on-site pre-screening to assess cognitive status and exclude those with acute illnesses. To mitigate interviewer bias, all data collectors receive standardised training (4 hours) on the study protocol, questionnaire administration methods, and neutral probing techniques. The training includes role-playing exercises to ensure consistency in questioning style. Inter-rater reliability is assessed by having 5% of the interviews (n=7) independently rated by two trained researchers, yielding an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.92 for key variables, indicating high consistency.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Statistical analysis

Data are entered into the database using a double-entry method via EpiData software. Entries are reviewed for accuracy. Missing data across key variables are minimal (0.3%–1.6%): self-management behaviours (3 cases), HAPA/TPB constructs (4 cases) and intrinsic capacity (2 cases). Missing values are imputed using multiple imputation via the “mice” package in R software.

To identify subgroups of older adults with declined intrinsic capacity based on self-management behaviour mechanisms, an unsupervised clustering method is employed. Prior to clustering, to address potential scale discrepancies across different variables, all input variables (behavioural attitudes, perceived behavioural control, outcome expectations, behavioural intentions and plans) were standardised using Z-scores. Subsequent unsupervised clustering was conducted based on these standardised variables. Results are visualised using the “tidyverse” package (version 1.3), “ggplot2” package (version 3.3.2), and “plotly” package (version 4.9.2.2) in R software (version 4.0.3). Data are analysed using the “car” and “mediation” packages. Subgroup characteristics are compared using ANOVA or rank-sum tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Bonferroni post hoc tests were applied to adjust for multiple comparisons and identify specific pairwise differences between subgroups. All tests are two-sided with α=0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Behavioural and Ethics Committee of Xiangya School of Nursing, Central South University (No.E202258). Formal administrative permissions were obtained from both participating community health centres. It upheld principles of voluntariness, confidentiality, impartiality, beneficence and non-maleficence. Participants were informed of voluntary participation, anonymity and the right to withdraw. Data were used exclusively for research, with emphasis on mental health privacy. Equal opportunities and unbiased information were provided. Participants could consult researchers for health-related clarifications. This ensured ethical and legal conduct, prioritising participant rights and privacy.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 317 questionnaires were distributed, yielding 308 valid responses (effective response rate: 89.87%). Participants were predominantly female (61.0%), with ages distributed across 60–69 years (35.3%), 70–79 years (37.9%) and 80 years or older (26.8%). The inclusion of older age groups was deliberate, given the study’s emphasis on age-related declines in intrinsic capacity, particularly in those with greater functional reserve reductions. Educational levels varied, with the majority having elementary or middle school education. Pre-retirement occupations were mostly non-agricultural (73.1%), and monthly incomes were generally modest, aligning with regional norms (median approximately 3000 yuan). Most participants were married (82.5%), and health status reflected a burden of chronic conditions (mean Charlson Comorbidity Index: 2.94±4.80). Descriptive analysis of clusters

Cluster analysis identified three distinct subgroups among community-based older adults with declined intrinsic capacity, based on behavioural mechanisms from the HAPA and TPB models. The elbow plot (figure 1) demonstrates an optimal clustering at k=3, where the total within-cluster sum of squares stabilises. The Principal Component Analysis (PCA) plot (figure 2) projects the dataset into a two-dimensional space defined by the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2), which together capture 75.67% of the total variance (PC1: 64.4%; PC2: 11.3%), thereby enabling effective dimensionality reduction. Three distinct clusters are clearly visualised: Cluster 1 (orange, solid circles) aggregates in the upper-right quadrant, primarily along the positive axes of PC1 and PC2; Cluster 2 (blue, hollow circles) occupies the intermediate region, spanning across both axes; and Cluster 3 (green, solid triangles) concentrates in the lower-left quadrant, mainly along the negative axes of PC1 and PC2. This visualisation highlights substantial variance explained by the principal components, underscoring the robustness of the subgroup distinctions.

Significant differences were observed in gender and educational attainment across the three self-management subgroups: Hesitant to Act (Subgroup 1), Norm-Dependent (Subgroup 2) and Assertive Decisiveness (Subgroup 3). Subgroup one demonstrated a higher proportion of females (χ² =8.13, p=0.017); however, post hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni adjustment revealed no significant differences. Discussion

This study employed cluster analysis to identify three distinct subgroups of self-management behaviours among community-dwelling older adults with declined IC, each characterised by unique behavioural tendencies, demographic profiles and psychological correlates. These findings reveal the heterogeneity of self-management practices in this population and bridge theoretical frameworks such as HAPA and TPB by demonstrating how behavioural attitudes, perceived control, subjective norms and self-efficacy interact to form adaptive or maladaptive patterns.

We observed that the first subgroup, named ‘Hesitant to act group’, was primarily composed of married adults aged 70–79 with elementary education, exhibiting high behavioural attitudes and perceived control but low subjective norms and outcome expectations. This dissonance suggests a gap between personal capability and confidence in behavioural outcomes, rooted in diminished self-efficacy—a finding consistent with empirical research linking self-efficacy to proactive health behaviour.35 36 Practically, these individuals may benefit from targeted interventions that strengthen outcome expectations, such as peer-led workshops showcasing successful self-management stories and structured social support programmes to mitigate environmental barriers.37 38 For health professionals, this subgroup highlights the need to prioritise confidence-building strategies alongside skill development in clinical and community settings, such as incorporating brief motivational interviewing during routine check-ups to reframe outcome expectations and collaborating with local community centres to host monthly peer-sharing sessions.39 40

Distinct from the first group, the second subgroup, named “Norm-dependent group”, comprised mostly women aged 80 years and older. This group prioritised subjective norms but demonstrated weaknesses in self-determination (low behavioural attitudes, perceived control and planning). This pattern aligns with research on social influence in ageing, emphasising that older adults may rely on external cues when internal motivation is compromised.41 42 To address this, interventions should focus on fostering self-determination through goal-setting training and problem-solving exercises,43 44 while engaging family and community networks to reinforce autonomy-supportive norms. Public health policies could integrate these insights by designing community-based programmes that balance social support with opportunities for independent decision-making, tailored to the needs of frail older adults.

The third subgroup, called “Assertive Decisiveness group”, exhibiting high scores across all behavioural dimensions, embodies proactive self-management, reflecting strong self-efficacy and internal locus of control—key markers of successful ageing.45 Their resilience points to modifiable factors (e.g., positive outcome expectations, strong social integration) that can inform interventions for other subgroups. Specifically, community-based initiatives could facilitate the dissemination of this subgroup’s self-monitoring competencies to broader older adult populations through structured practical workshops, with members of this subgroup serving as peer instructors.46 Clinically, this subgroup highlights the value of leveraging existing strengths in ageing populations to develop preventive strategies.

Integrating these lenses contextualises subgroup differences within a broader understanding of ageing and behaviour change, connecting findings to overarching behavioural mechanisms beyond classification. Beyond subgroup characteristics, these findings deepen understanding of behavioural dynamics in this population: observed variability in self-management aligns with literature indicating age-related IC decline is not unidimensional but interacts with psychosocial factors to influence adaptability,47 as seen in how self-efficacy, social norms, structural barriers and individual cognitions collectively modulate health behaviours—an underemphasised dynamic in IC-focused studies.

Strength and limitation

A significant strength of this study lies in its use of cluster analysis to identify distinct subgroups of older adults based on their self-management behaviours, providing a nuanced understanding of the diverse needs and challenges faced by this population. The inclusion of demographic and behavioural characteristics adds depth to the analysis, allowing for more targeted interventions and support. Notably, the study’s application of HAPA and TPB to frame behavioural mechanisms enhances its theoretical rigour. Several limitations should be noted. First, the reliance on self-reported data may introduce recall and social desirability biases, and potential issues with instrument validity (e.g., measurement precision of behavioural attitudes) could affect result interpretation. Second, the sample’s demographic homogeneity (e.g., regional concentration) raises concerns about selection bias, limiting generalisability. Third, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, as temporal relationships between psychological factors and self-management behaviours remain unexamined. Finally, due to data collection conditions, this study only used the Charlson Comorbidity Index to assess health status and did not record specific disease types in detail, which somewhat limited in-depth analyses of the associations between specific health statuses and subgroups of self-management behaviours.

Conclusion

This study identifies three distinct subgroups of self-management behaviours among older adults with reduced intrinsic capacity, shedding light on the heterogeneous nature of their self-care practices and the factors influencing self-management efficacy, while emphasising the need for subgroup-specific interventions. These findings support the development of targeted, person-centred strategies to strengthen community-based integrated care, promote healthy ageing and alleviate healthcare burdens. Future research is required to validate the identified subgroups in larger and more diverse populations, clarify potential causal relationships, and explore the dynamic evolution of subgroup characteristics using interdisciplinary methods. Such efforts will enhance the translational value of this research and its potential impact on population health.
