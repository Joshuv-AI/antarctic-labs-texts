Photon-Counting CT in Cardiovascular Imaging: From Technical Promise to Clinical Translation
Authors: Yaping Ge, Peipei Zhang
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the review by Rajiah et al. [1] titled “Photon-Counting CT in Cardiovascular Imaging: Clinical Applications.” The authors are to be commended for providing a timely and clinically relevant overview of photon-counting CT (PCCT) in cardiovascular imaging. In particular, their discussion of ultrahigh-resolution imaging, multienergy reconstructions, improved assessment of calcified plaques and stents, myocardial applications, and strategies to reduce radiation dose offers valuable insight into the expanding role of PCCT in contemporary cardiovascular CT practice.

We would like to offer several comments that we hope will further strengthen the clinical interpretation of this review.

First, although this article provides a convincing summary of the technical advantages of PCCT over conventional energy-integrating detector CT, much of the discussion focuses on image quality surrogates, including reduced blooming artifacts, improved visualization of stent struts, enhanced iodine contrast, and better delineation of small vessels. These imaging improvements are promising; however, it remains unclear whether they translate into patient-centered outcomes. A clearer distinction between technical superiority and demonstrated benefits in downstream management, prognosis, or cost-effectiveness would help readers interpret the current evidence more cautiously and avoid overextending early findings.

Second, greater attention to standardization and reproducibility would enhance the translational value of this review. The authors noted that PCCT performance is influenced by multiple acquisition and reconstruction parameters, including scan mode, kernel, iterative reconstruction strength, slice thickness, matrix size, tube voltage, and contrast protocol. This complexity raises an important point: some of the reported advantages of PCCT may be highly sensitive to the protocol and, to some extent, specific to individual platforms. For quantitative applications, such as plaque characterization, virtual calcium scoring, and iodine-based myocardial assessment, harmonization across centers and vendors is essential before these methods can be implemented more widely in routine practice [2].

Third, the applicability of PCCT in challenging real-world populations warrants further discussion. The review acknowledges that ultrahigh-resolution mode may be less suitable for larger patients because of increased image noise. It also notes that smaller collimation may increase susceptibility to motion artifacts. These limitations are particularly relevant in cardiovascular imaging, where obesity, tachyarrhythmia, severe calcification, renal dysfunction, prior interventions, and limited breath-holding often coexist. Because PCCT is often presented as particularly useful in technically demanding cases, a more explicit framework indicating which patients are most likely to benefit from ultrahigh-resolution mode, standard multienergy mode, or combined modes would be highly valuable for clinical adoption [3].

Fourth, we believe that this review could more clearly distinguish between established clinical applications and those that remain emerging or investigational. The sections on plaque characterization, material decomposition, late iodine enhancement, extracellular volume quantification, and molecular imaging are informative and forward-looking; however, these applications are not supported by the same level of clinical validation. Stratifying the evidence into mature, promising, and exploratory domains would improve the practical value of the review and help readers align their expectations with the current state of the evidence.

In summary, Rajiah et al. [1] have provided an excellent and thought-provoking review of PCCT in cardiovascular imaging. We believe that greater emphasis on outcome validation, protocol standardization, real-world patient selection, and the maturity of the evidence base would further strengthen the article and support the responsible clinical translation of this rapidly evolving technology.
