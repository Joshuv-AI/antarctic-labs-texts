Oxygen-Plasma Defect Engineering of Epitaxial Graphene on 4H-SiC for Enhanced NO2 Sensing
Authors: Xuan Thang Trinh, Anh Tuan Nguyen, Tham Thi Hong Nguyen, Tuan Quoc Dang, Hoai Duy Tran, Phuong Thanh Nguyen, Tuan Minh Dao, Ly Huu Truong, Cuong Quoc Le, Trung T. Pham, Vu Nguyen-Si, Huynh Thien Ngo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Developing sensitive, low-power, and manufacturable NO2 gas sensors is crucial for environmental and health monitoring. Epitaxial graphene on 4H-silicon carbide (Graphene@4H-SiC) is an ideal, device-ready platform, but its intrinsically inert surface limits its sensing performance. Here, we present a complete workflow that combines a scalable oxygen plasma treatment for defect engineering with the fabrication of a fully operational chemiresistive sensor device. The optimized sensor, treated at 150 W, demonstrates a nearly 8-fold response enhancement to NO2 compared to its pristine counterpart. It exhibits excellent recovery and sensitive detection down to 10 ppm at a low operating temperature of 70 °C. Crucially, a synergistic approach combining extensive experimental characterization with first-principles simulations reveals the underlying mechanism. Density functional theory calculations identify carbon vacancies as overwhelmingly potent adsorption sites, with a binding energy (−7.97 eV) and charge transfer far exceeding that of pristine graphene or common oxygen functional groups. The excellent correlation between the experimental performance peak and the theoretical superiority of vacancy defects provides a validated understanding of the enhancement. This work bridges the critical gap between atomic-scale theory and practical device performance, establishing a rational design pathway for high-performance graphene sensors.

Introduction

Nitrogen dioxide (NO2), a highly toxic gas resulting from fossil fuel combustion, poses a significant threat to both the environment and public health, contributing to acid rain and severe respiratory illnesses. , The imperative for continuous, real-time monitoring of its ambient concentration has driven the demand for sensor technologies that are not only highly sensitive but also compact, energy-efficient, and suitable for large-scale integration into modern monitoring networks.

Graphene, with its atomic thinness and exceptional electronic properties, has been identified as an auspicious material for next-generation chemical sensors. − However, the chemically inert nature of its pristine basal plane leads to weak, nonspecific interactions with gas molecules, often resulting in modest sensor performance. , To overcome this, “defect engineering” has emerged as a critical strategy to functionalize the graphene surface, creating highly reactive sites that enhance molecular binding and charge transfer, thereby amplifying the sensing signal. −

Significant progress has been made in developing graphene-based NO2 sensors using various material platforms. Graphene synthesized by chemical vapor deposition (CVD) offers high quality but necessitates a complex transfer process from its growth substrate, which can introduce contamination and structural damage, posing a challenge for reproducible, large-scale device fabrication. − On the other hand, sensors based on chemically reduced graphene oxide (rGO) are scalable but often exhibit performance variability due to their inherent structural inhomogeneity. Consequently, a significant experimental research gap exists in developing a complete workflow that combines a device-ready, high-quality graphene platform with a scalable, precisely tunable postprocessing technique to optimize sensing performance reliably. −

Concurrently, theoretical studies employing density functional theory (DFT) have provided invaluable insights into the atomic-scale mechanism of NO2 sensing. It is well-established that NO2 interacts weakly with pristine graphene. − Subsequent studies have explored how specific defects, such as hydroxyl or epoxy groups, can be more favorable adsorption sites. − However, a theoretical research gap remains. Few studies have systematically compared the adsorption mechanisms across a comprehensive range of plasma-relevant defects (including critical defects like carbon vacancies). More importantly, there is a scarcity of research that directly links these fundamental theoretical predictions to the experimentally measured performance of an entirely fabricated sensor device, leaving a disconnect between atomic-scale understanding and practical application.

This work presents a synergistic approach combining advanced experimental methods with first-principles simulations to address these interconnected gaps. Our work is based on epitaxial graphene films grown directly on 4H-silicon carbide (4H-SiC) wafers. This transfer-free method involves the high-temperature sublimation of silicon from the substrate’s surface, allowing the remaining carbon atoms to reconstruct into a high-quality graphene lattice and create a scalable, device-ready platform for sensor fabrication. We employ a controlled oxygen plasma treatment to engineer its surface. A complete sensor device was fabricated and demonstrated to operate effectively at a low temperature of 70 °C. Critically, our comprehensive DFT simulations directly corroborate its enhanced performance, which evaluates different defect types and identifies the primary mechanism driving the sensitivity. This study thus provides a validated, end-to-end workflow for the rational design of high-performance graphene sensors, bridging fundamental insights with practical device performance.

Experimental Details

Epitaxial Graphene Synthesis

Epitaxial few-layer graphene (FLG) was grown on the Si-face of 4H-SiC substrates (5 × 5 mm2) via high-temperature thermal sublimation, a process illustrated conceptually in Scheme a. The synthesis was conducted in a custom vacuum system equipped with a diffusion pump (Scheme a), which was capable of achieving a base pressure of 5 × 10–6 Torr. During growth, a 4H-SiC sample placed in a graphite boat was resistively heated to 1600 °C for 5 min. At this temperature, silicon atoms preferentially sublimate from the (0001) surface, allowing the remaining, excess carbon atoms to reconstruct into a graphitic lattice. This process forms a continuous FLG film covalently bonded to the substrate, eliminating the transfer step and issues of extrinsic strain common in deposition-based methods. , A constant argon purge (100 sccm) maintained the chamber pressure at approximately 1 × 10–3 Torr. This controlled environment regulates the Si sublimation rate, resulting in the self-assembly of surface carbon atoms into a continuous, terraced FLG film.

Oxygen-Plasma Defect Engineering

Following synthesis, the as-grown FLG samples underwent a controlled defect engineering step using an oxygen plasma (Scheme b). This modification was performed in a PlasmaPro 80 system. With a constant O2 flow of 100 sccm at a base pressure of 2.30 × 10–4 Torr, the RF power (with a 5 V DC chuck bias) was varied (50, 100, 150, and 200 W) for a fixed exposure time of 30 s. This procedure created a series of samples with increasing defect densities, designed to maximize the density of active adsorption sites while preserving the film’s overall electrical continuity. To ensure the reproducibility of this defect engineering process, three independent samples were fabricated and tested for each plasma condition. While the plasma parameters (power, time, flow) are precisely controlled, we acknowledge that defect creation is an inherently stochastic process. The data presented in this manuscript represent a complete and representative data set from one of these samples, chosen to maintain the integrity of the experimental results. The observed trends in defect density and sensing performance were consistently confirmed across all samples.

Materials Characterization

Surface morphology was examined by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, Hitachi S-4800). Topography and height statistics were obtained using atomic force microscopy (AFM, Park Systems XE7, True Non-Contact mode, 3 × 3 μm2 scan area). Scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) resolved the terrace structure and the graphene honeycomb lattice for the pristine film. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS, HORIBA H7593) was used to determine the elemental composition, and Raman spectroscopy (LabRAM, HORIBA Scientific) was employed to quantify the level of structural disorder via the D/G intensity ratio.

Gas-Sensing Measurements

The FLG films were fabricated into chemiresistive sensor devices to evaluate gas-sensing properties using a standard CMOS-compatible process. Starting with a 5 × 5 mm specimen of Graphene@4H-SiC, interdigitated electrode patterns were defined using photolithography with an AZ5214-E image reversal photoresist. Subsequently, a 500 nm-thick silver (Ag) film was deposited onto the surface via e-beam evaporation. We selected Silver (Ag) as the contact material as it provides an excellent balance of being more cost-effective than Au or Pt, while offering high thermal stability and full compatibility with our fabrication process, forming a reliable ohmic contact. The final electrode structure, consisting of contact pads and sensing bars, was completed after a lift-off process in which the photoresist was removed. The resulting device, with well-defined metal contacts on the graphene surface, was then ready for gas sensing tests.

The final device performance was evaluated in a sealed, temperature-controlled test chamber (Scheme b). Gas flows were managed by mass-flow controllers (MFC) to deliver precise concentrations of NO2 diluted in a N2 carrier gas. The final mixed gas concentration was independently verified by an external commercial NO2 detector (EVIKON) placed in-line before the test chamber to ensure the accuracy of the concentration values delivered to the sensor. The device was first stabilized at 70 °C for each measurement under pure N2. After stabilization, the device was exposed to the target NO2 concentration for 120 s, followed by a 180-s recovery period under pure N2. The electrical resistance was recorded continuously using a Keithley 2400 SourceMeter.

All measurements were conducted at a controlled operating temperature of 70 °C. Our primary goal was to minimize power consumption, and preliminary studies determined that 70 °C was the minimum temperature required to provide sufficient thermal energy for the full and rapid desorption of NO2 molecules.

Computational Details for Adsorption Modeling

We modeled NO2 adsorption on pristine epitaxial graphene (iG) and plasma-modified graphene (mG). For iG, a defect-free 7 × 7 graphene sheet was used. For mG, three representative defect motifs were considered separately: epoxy (mG_O), hydroxyl (mG_OH), and single-carbon vacancy (mG_VC). Each model comprised a 7 × 7 graphene supercell containing one NO2 molecule and 20 Å of vacuum on both sides along Oz to avoid spurious interactions. Therefore, the four adsorption systems were iG+NO2, mG_O+NO2, mG_OH+NO2, and mG_VC+NO2.

Initial adsorption poses were located by classical molecular dynamics using LAMMPS with the Interface Force Field (IFF). Simulations employed an isothermal NVT ensemble at 343 K, utilizing an Andersen thermostat, a 1.0 fs time step, and a 5.0 ns trajectory per system. Representative low-energy configurations from the trajectories were selected as starting points for electronic-structure calculations.

First-principles calculations were performed with FHI-aims. All systems were treated with the PBE exchange-correlation functional, the “tight/tier-1” numerical atom-centered basis, and the many-body dispersion (MBD) scheme to capture long-range van der Waals interactions with the graphitic surface. Brillouin-zone sampling used a Γ-centered 3 × 3 × 1 k-point grid. Atomic positions were relaxed until residual forces were below a stringent threshold while keeping the in-plane lattice vectors fixed to the 7 × 7 graphene reference. Binding energies were computed as EB=E(NO2+surface)−E(surface)−E(NO2) 1 with negative Eb indicating exothermic adsorption. Bader charge analysis was carried out using Critic2 on the converged electron densities to quantify charge transfer between NO2 and the substrate.

The percentage response and recovery were computed as %Response=|R0−RgR0|×100 2 %Recovery=|Ra−RgR0−Rg|×100 3 where R0 is the baseline resistance in pure N2, R g is the resistance at the end of the exposure window, and Ra is the resistance after the specified recovery period. Results and Discussion

Surface Morphology and Topography

The surface morphology of the graphene is critical for its gas sensing performance. We used FE-SEM to visualize how oxygen plasma alters this morphology at different powers. This helps identify a processing power that creates useful defect sites without destroying the film.

Figure captures the plasma power-dependent evolution of the FLG morphology. The as-grown film, shown at two different magnifications in panels (a) and (b), consists of large, laterally continuous platelets and terraces with well-defined step edges. This morphology is characteristic of multilayer graphene produced by the sublimation of the 4H-SiC substrate. The onset of plasma etching is evident after 30 s of oxygen plasma exposure. At a low power of 50 W (panel c), the smooth terraces fragment into a textured network of smaller domains, indicating the initial activation of edges and the creation of defects on the basal plane. As the power increases to 100 W (panel d), this fragmentation intensifies, creating a denser network of cracks across the surface. A dramatic morphological transition occurs at 150 W (panel e). The film is restructured into a high-density array of vertically oriented flakes or “nanowalls.” This suggests a more aggressive etching mechanism, where oxygen plasma undercuts the graphene layers along grain boundaries and defect sites, causing significant local delamination and lifting of the flakes. This condition appears to maximize the creation of exposed edge sites. Finally, at 200 W (panel f), the process transitions from restructuring to net ablation. The graphene film is largely removed, exposing wide patches of the underlying SiC substrate. This outcome signifies overetching, where the material removal rate, resulting from physical sputtering and chemical erosion, exceeds the rate of controlled functionalization.

FE-SEM of FLG morphology versus plasma power. (a, b) As-grown film shows continuous platelets and terraces. (c–e) 50–150 W yield fragmentation and crack networks; (f) 200 W exposes SiC, indicating overetch. Scale bars and magnifications are shown on panels.

This analysis reveals a clear transition from mild surface fragmentation to aggressive etching as plasma power increases. The 150 W condition creates a high-density array of vertical flakes, maximizing the exposed edge sites. This morphology represents an optimal structure for sensing before the film is destroyed at higher powers.

The morphological evolution observed in the FE-SEM images, from smooth terraces to a fragmented, high-surface-area structure, is the physical manifestation of the defect engineering process. This energetic O2 plasma treatment not only creates a high density of reactive edge sites but is also known to introduce a combination of oxygen-containing functional groups (such as epoxy and hydroxyl) and structural point defects onto the graphene lattice. Crucially, the high-energy ion bombardment can physically eject carbon atoms, creating carbon vacancies. As demonstrated in subsequent sections, these vacancy sites, in conjunction with the functionalized edges, are hypothesized to act as potent adsorption centers for NO2 molecules, facilitating the charge transfer necessary for an enhanced sensing response.

Figure presents the AFM topography scans that track these changes. The as-grown FLG (a) displays a well-ordered surface, characterized by the smooth, atomically flat terraces typical of epitaxial growth. The maximum height variation across this pristine surface is minimal, ∼ 2.7 nm, and detailed profile analysis reveals interlayer steps of 3.5 ± 0.08 Å, confirming the graphitic nature of the film. The first stage of plasma treatment at 50 W (b) induces a distinct topographical shift. The large terraces are replaced by a dense, granular texture, indicating that the plasma has uniformly initiated etching across the entire basal plane. This fragmentation leads to a quantifiable increase in surface roughness and an expansion of the vertical height span to ∼ 4.2 nm. This trend of increasing topographical complexity accelerates at higher powers. The morphology becomes highly disordered at 100 W (c), with larger and more vertically pronounced features, resulting in a greater height variation of ∼ 6.1 nm. This clearly transitions from surface fragmentation to more aggressive, three-dimensional etching. Finally, the scan at 150 W (d) provides stark quantitative evidence of the severe ablation observed in SEM. The maximum height span explodes to ∼ 13 nm, a range that signifies the formation of deep pits reaching the SiC substrate, leaving behind only isolated, tall remnants of the original graphene film.

AFM topography (3 × 3 μm2) of as-grown and plasma-treated samples. (a) As-grown FLG sample. (b) 50 W treated sample. (c) 100 W treated sample. (d) 150 W treated sample.

While SEM reveals film fragmentation, AFM provides quantitative data on surface topography and roughness. This analysis measures the increase in surface complexity, which is directly related to the area available for gas sensing. The root-mean-square (RMS) roughness was calculated from the AFM data for each sample to quantify this topographical evolution. The results show a monotonic increase in surface roughness with plasma power, rising from 0.412 nm for the pristine film to 0.678 nm at 50 W, and then reaching 1.084 and 1.817 nm for the 100 and 150 W samples, respectively. This quantitative trend directly measures the increasing surface area and complexity.

These AFM results provide quantitative evidence that surface roughness increases with plasma power. This trend directly supports the visual evidence of fragmentation and aggressive etching observed in the SEM analysis. This confirms the creation of a more topographically complex surface, which is desirable for enhancing gas-surface interactions.

We employed STM for atomic-resolution imaging to confirm the intrinsic crystalline quality of the starting material prior to plasma modification. This technique probes the local density of electronic states, allowing for direct visualization of the carbon lattice. A high-quality, well-ordered baseline is essential for evaluating the effects of subsequent defect engineering.

Figure presents the STM analysis of the as-grown FLG. The large-area scan in panel (a) reveals a high-resolution view of the terraced landscape, showing atomically sharp and well-defined step edges characteristic of the epitaxial growth process on SiC. This observation is in excellent agreement with the larger-scale SEM and AFM images. The atomic-resolution image in panel (b) provides proof of the film’s graphitic nature. It clearly resolves the hexagonal honeycomb lattice, the signature of sp2-hybridized carbon atoms arranged in a graphene sheet. The image confirms that the pristine film consists of large, highly crystalline, well-ordered domains. The STM results, therefore, validate the high quality of the as-grown epitaxial graphene. This establishes a crucial baseline, confirming the starting material is a low-defect, well-ordered crystalline film. This pristine structure serves as the ideal platform for the controlled introduction of defects through subsequent oxygen-plasma treatment.

STM of as-grown FLG. (a) Terrace landscape on 4H-SiC. (b) Atomic-resolution honeycomb lattice confirming ordered sp2 domains.

The collective microscopy analyses (FE-SEM, AFM, and STM) provide a comprehensive picture of the film’s topographical evolution. Starting from a highly crystalline, well-ordered epitaxial graphene baseline, the oxygen plasma treatment enables the deliberate engineering of the surface morphology, progressing from mild fragmentation to the creation of high-surface-area vertical nanostructures. Next, we employ surface-sensitive spectroscopic techniques to understand the chemical and structural consequences of this physical transformation.

Chemical Composition and Structural Integrity

To investigate the chemical changes corresponding to the observed morphological evolution, we performed energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). This analysis aims to confirm the incorporation of oxygen onto the graphene surface and to track the relative elemental changes, which can provide further evidence of the etching process.

Figure presents the EDS results. The spectrum of the as-grown film (a) shows a surface composed almost entirely of carbon (77 at. %) and silicon (23 at. %) from the underlying substrate, with negligible oxygen content. As the plasma power increases to 50 W (b) and 150 W (c), two critical trends emerge, as summarized in the plot in panel (e). First, the atomic percentage of carbon steadily decreases, while that of silicon increases, which is consistent with the gradual etching of the graphene film and the increasing exposure of the SiC substrate. Second, and more importantly, the oxygen content rises monotonically, reaching a maximum of 3.3 at. % at 150 W. This provides direct evidence that the plasma treatment successfully functionalizes the surface by incorporating oxygen. A sharp change occurs at 200 W (d): the carbon content plummets, and silicon becomes the dominant element, providing quantitative confirmation of the severe film ablation. Concurrently, the oxygen content drops significantly, indicating that the material is being removed faster than it can be functionalized.

EDS analysis across plasma powers. (a–d) Spectra of as-grown, 50, 150, and 200 W. (e) Atomic% of C, O, and Si versus power.

Therefore, the EDS analysis confirms the physical etching of the carbon film and its chemical functionalization with oxygen. The fact that the oxygen content peaks at 150 W provides strong chemical evidence for an optimal processing window. Beyond this power, the etching mechanism becomes too destructive, removing the desirable oxygen-containing functional groups and the carbon lattice. While EDS does not provide chemical bonding information, the increase in the oxygen signal following O2 plasma treatment is compelling evidence for incorporating oxygen-containing functional groups onto the graphene lattice. This finding confirms the chemical modification of the surface.

While EDS confirms elemental changes, Raman spectroscopy is essential for probing the structural integrity of the sp2 carbon lattice. This technique is susceptible to disorder and defects within the graphene framework. The analysis focuses on the intensity ratio of the defect-activated D peak to the graphitic G peak (D/G), which serves as a quantitative measure of the induced disorder.

Figure shows the Raman spectra evolution. The as-grown sample (a) exhibits a spectrum characteristic of high-quality, few-layer graphene: an intense G peak (∼1580 cm–1), a sharp 2D peak (∼2700 cm–1), and a nearly absent D peak (∼1360 cm–1), yielding a very low initial D/G ratio of 0.07. This confirms the pristine film’s high crystalline quality, which agrees with STM results. The D peak intensity systematically grows with power upon plasma exposure, as seen for the 50 W (b) and 150 W (c) samples. As plotted in panel (e), this results in a monotonic increase of the D/G ratio, which reaches a maximum of 0.23 at 150 W, signifying the successful and controlled introduction of lattice defects. However, the trend reverses at 200 W (d), and the D/G ratio drops sharply to 0.09. Notably, this spectrum also shows a new peak from the 4H-SiC substrate.

Raman evolution with plasma power. (a–d) Representative spectra. (e) D/G ratio versus power. (f) 2D/G ratio versus power.

The nonmonotonic behavior of the D/G ratio is a critical finding. The initial increase to 150 W corresponds to the introduction of sp3-type defects into the sp2 lattice. The subsequent sharp decrease at 200 W signifies a transition from defect engineering to amorphization and aggressive ablation, where the graphitic lattice is so heavily damaged that it can no longer be considered “defective graphene.” This spectroscopic evidence, combined with the emergence of the substrate signal, powerfully corroborates the microscopy and EDS data, cementing the conclusion that 150 W represents the optimal power for maximizing functional defects while preserving the core structural integrity of the graphene film.

Notably, the trend of the D/G ratio, which quantifies lattice disorder, perfectly mirrors the oxygen concentration trend observed in EDS. This correlation suggests that introducing these oxygen-containing species primarily contributes to the measured increase in structural defects, effectively acting as sp3-type functionalization sites on the sp2 carbon framework.

The comprehensive experimental characterization has confirmed that oxygen plasma treatment effectively engineers the graphene surface, introducing a high density of defects and oxygen-containing functional groups. To gain a more profound, mechanistic understanding of how these specific sites interact with NO2 molecules and enhance the sensing response, we performed first-principles density functional theory (DFT) calculations.

Theoretical Modeling of the NO2 Adsorption Mechanism

To elucidate the underlying mechanism of the enhanced sensing performance, we used DFT to model the adsorption of a single NO2 molecule on four distinct surface models: pristine (ideal) graphene (iG), graphene with an epoxy defect (mG_O), a hydroxyl defect (mG_OH), and a single carbon vacancy (mG_VC). These calculations aim to determine the preferred adsorption geometries, binding energies (Eb), and net charge transfer (ΔQ). These parameters are critical for identifying the most effective sites for capturing NO2 and generating a strong electronic signal.

Figure illustrates the DFT-optimized adsorption geometries, while Table provides the corresponding calculated energies and charge transfers. For pristine graphene (iG), the NO2 molecule resides relatively far from the surface (Figure (a-b)), indicating a weak interaction. This is quantified by a modest binding energy of −0.483 eV, characteristic of physisorption, with a baseline net charge transfer of −133.7 me. Introducing an epoxy group (mG_O), a common oxygen defect, does not improve this interaction; the binding is slightly weaker (Eb = −0.396 eV) with no additional charge transfer. In contrast, the hydroxyl group (mG_OH) is a more favorable adsorption site (Figure e, f). It results in a significantly stronger interaction with a binding energy of −0.913 eV and a slightly enhanced charge transfer. The most dramatic effect is observed for the carbon vacancy (mG_VC). Here, the NO2 molecule is drawn into the defect site, with one of its oxygen atoms forming a chemical bond with the graphene lattice (Figure (g-h)). This chemisorption event is characterized by a powerful binding energy of −7.974 eV and a significant net charge transfer of −140.6 me.

These simulation results provide a clear and fundamental explanation for the experimentally observed enhancement in sensing. The calculations reveal a distinct hierarchy of adsorption strength: Vacancy ≫ Hydroxyl > Pristine ≈ Epoxy. The carbon vacancy is identified as the preeminent active site for NO2 detection, offering both exceptionally strong binding for efficient molecular capture and the most significant charge transfer, which is essential for a robust sensor signal. While hydroxyl groups contribute to improved sensitivity, the dominant role of vacancy defects provides a powerful mechanistic basis for why plasma-induced “defect engineering” is so practical for creating high-performance graphene-based gas sensors.

The theoretical simulations in Section provide a robust mechanistic foundation for our experimental findings. The DFT calculations confirmed that vacancy and hydroxyl defects (shown to be created by the plasma treatment in Sections and ) act as highly favorable sites for NO2 adsorption, predicting a significantly enhanced interaction. This theoretical insight strongly suggests that the engineered films, particularly the one treated at 150 W, should exhibit superior gas sensing performance.

Having established the physical, chemical, and theoretical basis for enhanced sensing, we present the experimental gas sensing performance of the fabricated devices. These measurements were conducted to validate the effectiveness of the plasma-induced defect engineering approach directly. A self-consistent picture built from convergent evidence elucidates the mechanism behind the enhanced sensing performance. First, EDS confirms the successful incorporation of oxygen onto the surface, peaking at 150 W. Second, Raman spectroscopy shows that the structural defect density follows the same trend. Third, our DFT simulations predict that plausible oxygen-containing defects, such as hydroxyl groups, alongside carbon vacancies, act as potent adsorption sites. Finally, the measured sensor response itself peaks at 150 W. The convergence of these independent experimental and theoretical findings provides a robust and validated conclusion that plasma-induced functionalization is the key driver of the observed performance enhancement.

The primary finding, highlighted in Figure (a) and quantified in Figure (b) and Table , is that the sensor response to 60 ppm of NO2 demonstrates a remarkable and monotonic improvement with increasing plasma power. The response magnitude rises from a modest 1.86% for the pristine sensor to 5.08% (50 W), 8.46% (100 W), and reaches a maximum of 14.65% for the 150 W-treated sensors (an almost 8-fold enhancement). As expected, introducing defects increases the film’s baseline resistance (R0 in Table ) from ∼ 124 Ω for the pristine sample to ∼ 291 Ω for the 150 W sample due to increased charge carrier scattering. The dynamic response curves for the treated samples (Figure c-e) show fast, stable, and highly reversible signals. This excellent reversibility is quantified in Table , where all sensors exhibit recovery rates greater than 97%, with the optimal 150 W sensor achieving a 100% recovery rate. Crucially, the high sensitivity of the optimized sensor is demonstrated in Figure (f), which shows clear, distinguishable responses to NO2 concentrations as low as 10 ppm.

To further probe the sensor’s practical limits, tests were conducted down to 5 ppm. At this concentration, the sensor provided a clear and reproducible signal, distinguishable from the baseline. However, at concentrations below 5 ppm, the signal-to-noise ratio was insufficient, and the output signal fell within the statistical error margin of our measurement system. Therefore, we define the reliable experimental limit of detection (LOD) for our current sensor and setup as 5 ppm, which aligns with critical environmental monitoring thresholds.

The experimental sensing data unequivocally validate the success of the oxygen plasma treatment as a method for enhancing the performance of graphene-based NO2 sensors. The dramatic improvement in response is a direct manifestation of the engineered defect sites, which, as confirmed by our earlier characterization and DFT modeling, act as potent adsorption centers that facilitate a much greater charge transfer with NO2 molecules than a pristine lattice. The combination of a significantly enhanced response, complete reversibility, and high sensitivity to low concentrations confirms that the 150 W plasma treatment is an optimal strategy for transforming epitaxial graphene into a high-performance chemical sensing platform.

To contextualize our findings, Table provides a benchmark comparison of our optimized 150 W sensor against other representative graphene-based NO2 sensors from recent literature. While some composite materials or nanostructures may achieve higher absolute response values (often at higher temperatures or with complex synthesis), our device demonstrates a highly competitive, stable, and fully reversible signal on a scalable, transfer-free epitaxial platform. The ability to achieve this robust performance at a low operating temperature highlights the success and practicality of our defect-engineering approach for integrated device applications.

While this study significantly enhances the NO2 response and recovery, further investigations are warranted to assess the sensor’s practical potential fully. Conclusions

In this work, we have successfully demonstrated and explained a complete workflow for enhancing the NO2 sensing performance of epitaxial graphene through a controlled oxygen plasma treatment. By fabricating and testing fully operational chemiresistive sensor devices, we confirmed that this defect engineering strategy is highly effective. The sensor treated at an optimal plasma power of 150 W exhibited a remarkable, nearly 8-fold increase in response to NO2 compared to the pristine graphene device, while also maintaining excellent (100%) recovery and demonstrating high sensitivity to concentrations as low as 10 ppm at a low operating temperature of 70 °C.

A comprehensive, multifaceted analysis combining surface characterization (SEM, AFM, STM), compositional investigation (EDS, Raman), and theoretical modeling (DFT) was employed to elucidate the underlying mechanism. The experimental results revealed that the 150 W plasma treatment creates an optimal density of surface defects and oxygen-containing functional groups. Our first-principles simulations provided a crucial mechanistic insight, identifying that specific defects, particularly carbon vacancies, act as overwhelmingly potent adsorption sites for NO2 molecules, with significantly higher binding energies and greater charge transfer than a pristine lattice. The excellent agreement between the experimental performance peak and the theoretical predictions provides strong evidence that the sensing enhancement is driven by the creation of these highly active sites. This study presents a scalable and effective method for fabricating high-performance graphene gas sensors, bridging the gap between fundamental surface science and practical device applications.
