Beyond points: conceptual challenges in anatomy-based mapping of acupoints
Authors: Chaeyeon Yeo, Seung-Nam Kim
Published: 2026

Acupoints have traditionally been defined as discrete locations on the body surface. However, increasing anatomical, physiological, and mechanistic evidence suggests that therapeutic needling effects are mediated by interactions with underlying anatomical substrates rather than by surface location alone.1,2 In particular, recent studies have demonstrated that stimulation at specific acupoints such as ST36 can engage defined neural pathways, including the vagal–adrenal axis, highlighting the anatomical basis of acupoint specificity.3

In this study, anatomy-based acupoint mapping refers to the process of linking standardized acupoint locations to identifiable musculoskeletal, neural, vascular, and connective tissue structures within a three-dimensional spatial framework, for purposes of systematic representation, interdisciplinary communication, and database construction. This approach is not intended to replace existing clinical or theoretical interpretations of acupoints, but to establish a reproducible anatomical basis upon which such interpretations can be more precisely grounded. Importantly, the issue is not that anatomy-based mapping is misguided, but that it may be incomplete when treated as the sole defining framework for acupoint identity.

This anatomical perspective has important implications for clinical practice, education, and research. In clinical settings, phenomena such as Deqi are often interpreted as arising from interactions between the needle and neural or connective tissue structures.4 Accurate anatomical understanding is also essential for safe needling and for minimizing adverse events associated with neurovascular injury, as demonstrated by recent quantitative assessments of needling depth safety margins.5 Furthermore, anatomically explicit descriptions of acupoints are increasingly required to improve reproducibility and facilitate communication across disciplines.6

Growing international interest in anatomy-based acupoint representation is reflected in large-scale initiatives such as the TARA project, which aims to define acupoints through standardized anatomical and ontological frameworks.7 Building on this recognition, Lee et al. demonstrated that acupoint depth is inherently three-dimensional, involving underlying neurovascular structures that surface coordinates cannot adequately capture.8 Kang and Chae further proposed that acupoints function as ontologically emergent constructs shaped by clinical, theoretical, and cultural frameworks, rather than as fixed anatomical entities.9 These contributions clarify why conceptual extension is needed, yet the specific challenges of practical implementation have not been systematically examined. In constructing an anatomy-linked acupoint database on a previously developed 3D anatomical platform,10 we encountered non-trivial conceptual challenges not addressed by existing surface-based or ontology-based definitions.

To our knowledge, this commentary presents the first systematic attempt to identify and classify these challenges from an implementation perspective. As illustrated in Fig. 1 and summarized in Table 1, these challenges can be grouped into six categories. First, multiple anatomical structures may coexist within a single acupoint region, making one-to-one mapping difficult. Second, the anatomical targets engaged during needling vary depending on insertion depth, reflecting the layered organization of tissues. Third, variations in needle trajectory, including angle and direction, can lead to different anatomical interactions at the same surface location. Fourth, anatomical targets exist at different hierarchical levels that should not be treated as equivalent. Fifth, inconsistent anatomical nomenclature introduces ambiguity in defining and integrating anatomical data. These six categories should not be read as isolated observations, but as an integrated framework defining the structural boundaries of anatomy-based acupoint mapping, and thereby the minimum requirements for a more adequate representational system. Acupoints are not fixed surface coordinates, but three-dimensional anatomical configurations defined by spatial relationships between multiple structures along the needle trajectory. Accordingly, anatomy-linked acupoint databases should be designed to incorporate multi-target associations, depth- and trajectory-dependent parameters, hierarchical anatomical organization, and standardized terminology.

More broadly, these considerations may improve the precision of acupuncture education, enhance the clarity of needling descriptions in research reporting, and support safer and more reproducible clinical practice. The present framework is therefore intended as a starting point rather than a complete solution, with complementary physiological and clinical perspectives remaining essential.

In conclusion, the six categories proposed here constitute a structured framework for identifying the conceptual limitations of anatomy-based acupoint mapping, limitations that emerged not from theoretical analysis alone, but from the practical constraints of real implementation. By defining these requirements systematically, this framework provides a foundation for developing more precise and multidimensional acupoint representations in research, education, and practice.
