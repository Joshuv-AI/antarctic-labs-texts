Clinical and spinopelvic outcomes of Transforaminal versus Anterior Lumbar Interbody Fusion at L5-S1: a short-term ambispective cohort study
Authors: Silvia Suárez-Monsalve, Nelcy Rodríguez Malagón, Santiago Campos-Fajardo, Diego Andrés Rosselli Cock, Juan Carlos Pérez Rodríguez, Jaime Eduardo Becerra Ospina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDesignObservational ambispective cohort.ObjectivesTo compare the clinical and radiological effectiveness of Transforaminal Lumbar Interbody Fusion (TLIF) versus Anterior Lumbar Interbody Fusion (ALIF) in patients with chronic low back pain and single-level L5–S1 degenerative disc disease.MethodsAn analytical ambispective cohort was conducted at two high-complexity private clinics in Bogotá, Colombia, between 2019 and 2024, including adults with chronic low back pain (≥3 months) and symptomatic L5–S1 degenerative disc disease undergoing minimally invasive fusion. The exposure was the surgical technique (TLIF vs. ALIF). The primary outcome was change in Visual Analog Scale (VAS) pain score, with secondary outcomes including changes in segmental (L5–S1) and regional (L1–S1) lumbar lordosis. Analyses included descriptive, bivariate, and multivariable models (multiple linear and ordinal logistic regression), adjusting for baseline imbalances such as BMI, facet joint signs, and spinal canal stenosis.Results300 patients were included (120 TLIF, 180 ALIF), 61.7% female, mean age 45 years. Pain reduction was comparable (mean VAS reduction 6 points TLIF vs. 5 points ALIF; p = 0.0026; 95% CI: −1.19 to −0.25). Segmental lordosis gain was greater with ALIF (median 4.8° vs. 1.0°; p = 1.32 × 10−6; 95% CI: 2.31 to 5.38), and remained significant after adjustment (ALIF 3.83° greater; p = 2.4 × 10−6; 95% CI: 2.27 to 5.40), while regional lordosis changes did not differ (p = 0.161). Obesity appeared to modify the effect, with greater segmental correction in obese TLIF patients. Complication rates were low and similar (TLIF 5% vs. ALIF 7.2%).ConclusionsTLIF and ALIF provide comparable clinical improvement, while greater lordotic correction with ALIF does not yield additional meaningful pain relief. TLIF is a safe, efficient, and potentially cost-effective alternative, supporting its use as a first-line option for monosegmental L5–S1 fusion.Keywords: Low back pain, Degenerative disc disease, Lumbar interbody fusion, Transforaminal approach, Anterior approach, Lordosis restoration

Introduction

Chronic low back pain (CLBP) is a highly prevalent condition affecting the adult population, with an estimated lifetime prevalence of 60-85%, regardless of sex, race, or age(Rubin, 2007). It represents a frequent cause of medical consultation and a leading cause of prolonged work disability and disability pensions in developed and developing nations(Meucci et al., 2015; Corbett et al., 2007). Beyond its physical manifestations, CLBP is associated with significant psychological and mood disturbances that, combined with activity limitations, substantially impair quality of life (Bailly et al., 2015).

Structural abnormalities of the spinal column rank among the most common etiologies of chronic low back pain. The spine must maintain alignment within an equilibrium zone relative to the pelvis and lower extremities to support erect posture and unassisted gait. This relationship is defined by spinopelvic parameters that must remain within certain physiological ranges to minimize energy expenditure(Ames et al., 2012). When these parameters exceed established thresholds, secondary musculoskeletal adaptations are required to achieve mechanical compensation, resulting in increased energy demand(Schwab et al., 2010). These compensatory changes can produce global spinal malalignment, leading to pain and functional impairment.

The lumbosacral intervertebral level (L5-S1) is the most frequently affected level of the lumbar spine, primarily because of its location at the base of the spinal column and the substantial mechanical loads it bears (Kos et al., 2019). This level is commonly affected by degenerative disc disease (DDD), which progressively destroys disc structure, causing nuclear prolapse beyond the intervertebral space and resulting in nerve root compression. This mechanical compression generates local pain and radicular symptoms extending to the lower extremities, which may be accompanied by neurological deficits in strength and sensation(Hemanta et al., 2016).

When conservative management fails to provide adequate symptom relief, surgical intervention becomes the appropriate therapeutic alternative. Spinal fusion surgery aims to correct spinal deformities, alleviate pain, and restore quality of life by creating arthrodesis of degenerated levels to stabilize affected segments, eliminate pain-generating movements, and provide direct and indirect neural decompression while restoring lumbar lordosis and correcting spinal deformity (Mobbs et al., 2015).

Multiple surgical approaches have been developed to achieve these goals(Tropiano et al., 2017). Anterior lumbar interbody fusion with supplemental posterior fixation (ALIF 360°) has been widely considered a preferred approach for discogenic low back pain at L5-S1, based on its anatomical advantages (Pavlov et al., 2004): the anterior approach allows direct visualization of the anterior disc space, complete discectomy, and placement of large interbody cages with broad endplate contact, facilitating disc height restoration, lumbar lordosis recovery, and spinopelvic alignment correction (Hsieh et al., 2007; Bassani et al., 2020). Transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF) offers the advantage of a single posterior approach, avoiding anterior abdominal dissection and the associated access-related morbidity(Rosenberg and Mummaneni, 2001). Both techniques are indicated for managing axial somatic or radicular pain, with or without spondylolisthesis, when conservative treatment has failed(Rothrock et al., 2018).

However, TLIF has been traditionally employed more frequently at L4-L5 and superior levels, with limited application at L5-S1, reserved for specific cases such as patients with elevated body mass index or anatomical abnormalities precluding ALIF approach, including previous abdominal surgery or unfavorable vascular anatomy(Faundez et al., 2009).

In our clinical practice, we observed that TLIF yielded clinical results comparable to ALIF in terms of pain reduction and functional improvement, while avoiding the abdominal approach and its specific contraindications. Based on these observations and the potential practical advantages of TLIF in resource-limited settings, we designed this study to compare the short-term clinical and radiographic outcomes of TLIF versus ALIF in a cohort of patients with symptomatic L5-S1 degenerative disc disease treated at two private high-complexity institutions in Bogotá, Colombia, during the 2019–2024 period.

Background

Pathophysiology of degenerative lumbar disease and surgical indications

Chronic low back pain (CLBP), defined as pain persisting for more than three months in the lumbosacral region with or without radicular symptoms, is most commonly driven by degenerative spinal pathology(Freburger et al., 2009; Hart, 1979; Diamond and Borenstein, 2006)–(Freburger et al., 2009; Hart, 1979; Diamond and Borenstein, 2006). Degenerative disc disease (DDD), characterized by progressive disc structural deterioration, loss of disc height, and potential neural compression, represents a major contributor, leading to axial pain and/or radiculopathy(Kos et al., 2019; Barrey et al., 2007). This degenerative cascade is frequently associated with lumbar spinal stenosis, resulting from combined disc degeneration, facet arthropathy, ligamentum flavum hypertrophy, and, in some cases, spondylolisthesis. Radiologically, stenosis is defined by reduction of the dural sac diameter (typically <10 mm on axial MRI) with loss of the surrounding cerebrospinal fluid space, and is commonly graded using systems such as Lee-Schizas (Reid et al., 2019; Deer et al., 2019; Andresen et al., 2002)– (Reid et al., 2019; Deer et al., 2019; Andresen et al., 2002). Disc degeneration severity is assessed using the Pfirrmann classification on MRI(Pfirrmann et al., 2001). Together, these conditions constitute the primary structural basis for lumbar instability and surgical indication.

Spinopelvic parameters

Spinopelvic alignment is critical for surgical planning and outcome assessment. In this study, regional lumbar lordosis (RLL), measured from L1 to S1, and segmental lumbar lordosis (SLL) at L5–S1 were evaluated as key parameters of sagittal balance restoration(Schwab et al., 2010; Okpala, 2019; Furlanetto et al., 2018; Kapustka et al., 2020)–(Schwab et al., 2010; Okpala, 2019; Furlanetto et al., 2018; Kapustka et al., 2020) (Fig. 1). RLL typically ranges from 40° to 60° in adults, while SLL averages 15° ± 3°, serving as essential references for assessing physiological alignment and the degree of correction achieved after surgery.

Measurement of lumbar lordosis on pre-operative radiographs. The image on the left represents regional lumbar lordosis (RLL, L1–S1), and the image on the right shows segmental lumbar lordosis (SLL, L5–S1).

Anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF)

ALIF provides direct anterior access to the disc space, allowing complete discectomy and placement of large interbody cages with broad endplate contact, facilitating lordosis restoration and fusion(Mobbs et al., 2015; Vieli et al., 2019). It is most commonly performed at L5–S1 due to favorable anatomical access and is indicated in selected patients with degenerative disc disease, segmental instability, foraminal stenosis, and low-grade spondylolisthesis. Careful patient selection is required given potential vascular risks and limitations in patients with prior abdominal surgery, unfavorable vascular anatomy, severe peripheral vascular disease, or solitary kidney(Malham et al., 2014; Phan et al., 2017; Lakchayapakorn, 2008; MayField; Jeswani et al., 2012)–(Malham et al., 2014; Phan et al., 2017; Lakchayapakorn, 2008; MayField; Jeswani et al., 2012). Although obesity is not an absolute contraindication, it may increase technical complexity of the retroperitoneal approach.

At the institutions where this study was conducted, the entire procedure, including the retroperitoneal approach, was performed exclusively by spine surgeons without the need for vascular or general surgery teams, optimizing operative efficiency and resource utilization. ALIF is routinely supplemented with posterior instrumentation to enhance segmental stability and fusion rates(Pavlov et al., 2004). Preoperative evaluation of iliac vessel anatomy is essential to minimize vascular complications, particularly at the L5–S1 level, where an intervascular distance of approximately 2–3 cm is generally considered adequate to allow safe cage placement without increasing the risk of vascular injury(Jeswani et al., 2012).

Transforaminal Lumbar Interbody Fusion (TLIF)

TLIF is a posterior approach that allows interbody fusion through the neuroforamen, minimizing neural retraction and posterior soft tissue disruption(Park et al., 2005). The indications for TLIF at L5-S1 are broader than historically recognized. According to current literature, TLIF is appropriate for symptomatic DDD, including discogenic low back pain, foraminal stenosis, segmental instability, and low-grade spondylolisthesis refractory to conservative management(Simone et al., 2024). Importantly, TLIF also represents the preferred technique when ALIF is contraindicated, including patients with prior abdominal surgery, unfavorable vascular anatomy (MayField), obesity, or other anatomical barriers to retroperitoneal access. Recent evidence supports expanding TLIF utilization at L5-S1 in carefully selected patients, demonstrating comparable clinical outcomes to ALIF when curved cages are positioned optimally within the intervertebral space to maximize lordosis restoration. Contraindications include severe epidural scarring, active spinal infection, and complex nerve root anatomy precluding safe foraminal access(Rosenberg and Mummaneni, 2001; Shin and Choi, 2015).

Surgical techniques and implants

Both ALIF and TLIF in this study were performed using minimally invasive techniques, which have been associated with reduced morbidity and improved recovery(Mobbs et al., 2012; Fairbank and Pynsent, 2000). Interbody cages play a central role in restoring disc height, alignment, and promoting fusion. ALIF cages provide a larger endplate contact area (780-1140 mm2) and greater intrinsic lordosis (7-12°)(KYOCERA), facilitating sagittal alignment restoration. In contrast, TLIF cages (typically banana-shaped) provide anterior column support and indirect decompression when properly positioned (Fairbank and Pynsent, 2000; Choi et al., 2018; Shin and Choi, 2015; KYOCERA; Vialle et al., 2022; Ding et al., 2022).

At the institutions where this study was conducted, curved TLIF cages were systematically used due to their superior ability to restore segmental height and lordosis compared to straight cages (3.4 mm vs. 2.2 mm height gain) (Choi et al., 2018). In all cases, interbody fusion was supplemented with a combination of autologous and heterologous bone graft, aiming to enhance fusion potential and optimize segmental and regional lordosis restoration.

Relevant literature synthesis

The 32 identified articles, published between 2001 and 2025, included 27 observational studies (mean sample size 223 patients, follow-up 3-24 months) predominantly conducted in the United States, Western Europe, and East Asia, along with 2 systematic reviews (2012, 2015) and 3 meta-analyses (2018, 2024 and 2025).

Regarding clinical outcomes, the majority of studies demonstrated similar pain improvement with both techniques (Hsieh et al., 2007; Faundez et al., 2009; Kim et al., 2009, 2010; Karamian et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2022; Alhaug et al., 2024)– (Hsieh et al., 2007; Faundez et al., 2009; Kim et al., 2009, 2010; Karamian et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2022; Alhaug et al., 2024). Some reports noted superior pain relief with TLIF in radicular pain presentations (Alhaug et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2017; Moses et al., 2021) and better functional scores and patient satisfaction, though recent large population studies found no clinically significant differences in patient-reported outcomes (PROMs) between ALIF and TLIF at short and medium-term follow-up despite radiographic differences (Daniels et al., 2025; Lavu et al., 2025).

Concerning radiographic outcomes, most studies favored ALIF for regional lumbar lordosis (RLL) and segmental lumbar lordosis (SLL) restoration(Hsieh et al., 2007; Zhu et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2017; Robert et al., 2014; Platz and Halm, 2021; Ahlquist et al., 2018; Leveque et al., 2022; O'Connor et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023)–(Hsieh et al., 2007; Zhu et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2017; Robert et al., 2014; Platz and Halm, 2021; Ahlquist et al., 2018; Leveque et al., 2022; O'Connor et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023). However, both techniques achieved similar interbody fusion rates(Alhaug et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2023; Phan et al., 2015) without significant differences in pseudoarthrosis rates (Liu et al., 2023). Recent studies with prolonged follow-up reported lower adjacent segment degeneration rates following ALIF compared to TLIF, potentially explaining higher reoperation rates in TLIF patients(Huang et al., 2025). Conversely, some authors such as Alhaug, Bouchard and Kuang (48, 63, 64 found no significant differences in lordosis gain between techniques.

Recent meta-analyses provide conflicting perspectives. Ajiboye et al. (2018). reported superior SLL correction with ALIF (2.5-8° vs. 1.4-2.5°; 95% CI: 1.7-5.7; p < 0.001) and greater RLL improvement with ALIF (5.4-9.9° vs. −2 to 1.4°; 95% CI: 2.7-5.3; p < 0.001), though with severe heterogeneity (I2>92%). Daher et al. (2024). Noted that despite statistical ALIF superiority in functional metrics, differences were not clinically meaningful, with similar complication rates supporting TLIF as the preferred alternative given its single posterior approach. The 2025 meta-analysis by Daniels et al. reported higher overall complication and reoperation rates with TLIF versus ALIF, with variability dependent on technique (open vs. MIS) and surgical volume(Daniels et al., 2025).

Until recently, literature has favored ALIF for radiographic balance restoration, though this radiographic advantage does not consistently translate into clinically significant patient benefit, and heterogeneity in results is attributable to factors including interbody cage height and angulation, underlying pathology (particularly high-grade spondylolisthesis or severe spinal stenosis), study design, and baseline patient characteristics.

Knowledge gap identification

Available literature demonstrates ALIF superiority for spinopelvic parameter restoration. However, prior comparative studies have attributed several potential procedural considerations to ALIF relative to TLIF. Faundez et al. (2009) and Hee et al. (2001) reported greater intraoperative blood loss with anterior-posterior fusion (approximately 200 mL additional) and longer operative times associated with the dual-stage approach (30–50 additional minutes). Some series have documented higher costs of the ALIF construct and some reports have noted prolonged hospitalization compared to TLIF(46). It should be noted, however, that at the L5-S1 level specifically, ALIF is generally considered a well-tolerated and expedient procedure when performed by experienced spine surgeons, and some of the operative time and blood loss differences described in the literature may be attributable to study-specific case mixes, surgeon experience, and institutional volume rather than representing universal characteristics of the technique. These variables were not directly measured in this cohort and therefore cannot be confirmed from our data.

Regarding Latin American literature, few studies specifically address these techniques in regional populations. An illustrative example is the Hospital Universitario San Ignacio Neurosurgery Service experience (2014-2018), which reported favorable ALIF outcomes with 1.7% complication rates when performed by experienced spine surgeons without additional vascular surgery support(Lindado et al., 2022).

The majority of published studies do not comprehensively integrate clinical and radiographic outcomes with technique-specific factors in Latin American patient populations. This study aims to provide a comparative analysis of these surgical techniques in a Colombian cohort, offering complementary evidence for hypothesis generation regarding regional behavior, particularly in populations with similar demographic profiles and healthcare system structures, which remain underrepresented in international literature.

Study design

Observational analytical ambispective cohort study in adult patients with chronic low back pain and L5-S1 degenerative disc disease undergoing surgical fusion with either TLIF (exposed group) or ALIF (non-exposed group) at two high-complexity private clinics in Bogotá, Colombia, between 2019 and 2024.

Justification for study design

For comparative evaluation of health interventions, the randomized controlled trial represents the methodological ideal(Lozano et al., 2015). However, given feasibility constraints and the fact that patients had already undergone surgery at the participating institutions before institutional ethics approval (March 2023), an observational analytical cohort design was selected. This design permits evaluation of the association between exposed and comparison groups relative to frequency and magnitude of clinical and radiological outcomes, estimating effect measures such as mean differences between groups in a homogeneous data source setting with controlled costs and defined timeline (Ruiz et al., 2015; Song and Chung, 2010).

Surgical technique assignment followed a structured institutional decision algorithm. ALIF was the preferred approach when retroperitoneal access was anatomically feasible, more specifically: when patients had no prior major abdominal surgery, no unfavorable vascular anatomy on preoperative imaging (iliac intervascular distance >2 cm at L5-S1), and there were no other contraindications to an anterior approach. TLIF was selected when ALIF was contraindicated, or when surgeon and patient preference favored the single posterior approach. We acknowledge that non-randomized assignment contributes to potential selection bias, which is the primary methodological limitation of this study. In a randomized controlled trial, many of the patients assigned to TLIF in this cohort would likely have been excluded as ineligible for ALIF. Their inclusion reflects real clinical practice, strengthens the external validity of the TLIF arm for the population in which TLIF is most commonly used, but simultaneously introduces substantive group heterogeneity that limits direct comparability. Although propensity score matching or inverse probability of treatment weighting would strengthen causal interpretability, it is reasonable to highlight that this requires exhaustive and uniformly documented baseline characterization of all variables including treatment assignment, surgeon, institution-level factors and standardized vascular anatomy classification, which was not achievable as this was a retrospective cohort. A multivariate approach was therefore employed using regression models adjusted for covariates with the greatest bivariate group difference and clinical plausibility as confounders. It should also be noted that these models are exploratory and associative and therefore they cannot certainly establish causal inference.

Sample size calculation

Given the non-randomized observational nature of this study with the intent to compare clinical and radiological effectiveness between exposed (TLIF) and non-exposed (ALIF) participants, the sample size was calculated as an exercise using online methodology (https://powerandsamplesize.com/calcs/) based on the expected proportion of patients achieving radiographic lordosis improvement (≥5°). Parameters were derived from prior meta-analyses(Ajiboye et al., 2018) and systematic reviews (Phan et al., 2015) reporting ALIF proportions of 0.7 and TLIF proportions of 0.4.

Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05 with statistical power (1-β) = 0.80 and TLIF:ALIF assignment ratio of 1:1.5, reflecting institutional procedural frequency. Using the normal approximation for comparing two proportions with unequal assignment ratios. The minimum required sample was approximately 35 TLIF and 53 ALIF patients (total 88).

However, considering the observational study nature, anticipated 20% follow-up loss, multivariate and stratified analysis requirements, and to approximate the sample size to mean values reported in comparable observational studies (∼223 patients), the final sample was expanded to 120 TLIF and 180 ALIF patients.

Variables and data collection

Independent variables detailed in tables include demographic characteristics, preoperative clinical variables, imaging parameters, intraoperative and hospitalization variables, postoperative follow-up variables, and postoperative imaging parameters. Data were primarily obtained from electronic health records. Spinopelvic parameters were measured and classified by the principal investigator using institutional imaging platforms.

Outcome variables

Clinical outcome was defined as improvement in pain measured by the difference between preoperative and 3-month postoperative Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores (ΔVAS = preoperative VAS - postoperative VAS). Positive differences indicate pain reduction, analyzed as clinical delta (ΔVA), providing an objective, reproducible measure. A between-group difference of ≥1 point in VAS was considered the minimal clinically important difference for the purposes of this study.

Radiological outcomes evaluated changes in lumbar lordosis, both segmental and regional. Angular correction was defined as the difference between postoperative and preoperative angles, expressed as angular gain in degrees (ΔLordosis = postoperative angle - preoperative angle). Measurements were performed on lateral weight-bearing radiographs using standardized techniques ensuring pre- and postoperative measurement comparability.

Data collection and management

Although the sample size was calculated, a consecutive population-based recruitment of all patients meeting the selection criteria was performed without randomization until the predetermined sample size was achieved. Sociodemographic and clinical data were collected from electronic health records of both institutions by the principal investigator. Diagnostic images and spinopelvic parameter calculations for pre- and postoperative periods were reviewed directly on institutional imaging platforms. All information was stored in a secure REDCap® database to ensure data protection and confidentiality, with storage performed exclusively by the principal investigator. Patient identity was protected by excluding sensitive identifiers. Clinical experts provided oversight, problem-solving, and feedback throughout the process.

Statistical analysis

Exploratory analysis was performed to identify missing or unusual data, followed by descriptive and bivariate analysis to evaluate variable behavior regarding distribution and group homogeneity. For variables lacking normal distribution, results are reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). For associations between ordinal variables or nonlinear relationships, the Spearman correlation coefficient was employed. Given consecutive rather than randomized recruitment, baseline group imbalances were anticipated. Potential confounding sources were identified through bivariate analysis; obesity, moderate-to-severe spinal canal stenosis, and facet joint signs demonstrated the greatest result variation and clinical relevance. Based on these associations and clinical relevance, variables for inclusion in adjustment models were defined. Other variables potentially associated with outcomes, including baseline VAS, Pfirrmann grade, spondylolisthesis grade, cage angle and surgeon/institutional variability were considered. However, uniform documentation of variables across the full retrospective period was not available, limiting their inclusion. The impact of this restriction on residual confounding cannot be fully excluded as is acknowledged as a limitation. Bivariate analysis of independent variables

Independent variables are described according to the intervention group. Data are presented as n (%), mean (SD), or median (IQR), as appropriate to the variable distribution.

Outcomes were analyzed through multivariate models selected according to variable nature: multiple linear regression for continuous outcomes; ordinal logistic regression for ordinal outcomes; and logistic regression for dichotomous outcomes. Ordinal logistic regression was specifically selected for categorical variables with inherent order, preserving variable hierarchy without assuming equidistant category spacing, inappropriate under linear models. Models were adjusted for variables demonstrating baseline imbalances to control for potential confounding and obtain precise effect estimates between surgical technique and outcomes. All statistical analyses were performed in R and R Studio version 4.0.3 (R Core Team, 2020).

Ethical considerations

This project strictly adhered to national and international guidelines for human subjects research. Nationally, Resolución 8430 (1993) of the Colombian Ministry of Health was followed(de Colombia, 1993). Internationally, the Declaration of Helsinki (64th General Assembly, Fortaleza, Brazil, October 2013) (World Medical Asociation (AMM), 2013) and ethical guidelines by the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences (CIOMS) in collaboration with the World Health Organization were observed (Consejo de Organizaciones Internacionales de las Ciencias Médicas (CIOMS), 2014).

This study was classified as “minimal risk research” per Colombian Resolution Article 11, as data collection from medical records involved no intentional intervention or modification of clinical course or patient decisions. Sensitive data collection was avoided. Given the ambispective evaluation with a minimum 3-month follow-up, patient identification documents were recorded in the database. Written informed consent was not required.

Before data collection and analysis, the project received ethics committee approval from both participating institutions and the Ethics and Research Committee of Hospital Universitario San Ignacio and Pontificia Universidad Javeriana in their ordinary session of February 23, 2023 (Acta N° 3/2023).

Results

Exploratory analysis

Data was collected and stored in the REDCap® database following ethics approval in March 2023. Surgical scheduling records from both institutions (from March 2019 until completion of the 300-patient target) were reviewed to identify potential participants. Final enrolled patients underwent surgery in May 2024 with subsequent 3-month postoperative follow-up. The timeline was extended due to the reduced operative volume of this pathology during the COVID-19 pandemic.

During pandemic periods, TLIF was systematically utilized more frequently during specific intervals, primarily related to limited institutional availability of ALIF implants due to cost restrictions. However, individualized data regarding specific non-availability intervals of ALIF materials were unavailable, precluding precise stratified analysis by this factor.

Selection criteria application, exhaustive medical record review, diagnostic imaging evaluation, and information registration were performed entirely by the principal investigator.

The cohort implemented systematic operative strategies including the use of curved transforaminal cages positioned as anteriorly as possible within the L5-S1 intervertebral space, reinforced with autologous and heterologous bone graft. Operative indications, surgical team composition, operative materials, postoperative analgesic management, timing of physical therapy initiation, hospital stay duration, and postoperative follow-up were standardized to the extent possible for both groups.

Exploratory data analysis identified no missing data. Notably, 96% of patients were treated at the first clinic, reflecting reduced surgical frequency at the second institution during the COVID-19 pandemic. Patients from both centers were included to complete the sample, given that surgery and follow-up were performed by the same specialists at both centers. Atypical data review identified implausible values and corrected digitization errors immediately. One notable outlier was a patient with markedly prolonged hospitalization (42 days) due to surgical site infection evolving to meningitis requiring multiple procedures with favorable resolution. Additionally, one patient in the TLIF group developed pseudoarthrosis associated with interbody cage displacement, which required surgical reintervention. No cases of cage subsidence were identified.

Patient distribution revealed 96% treated at the first institution. The population comprised 61.7% women (n = 185) with a mean age of 45 years (SD ± 12.3). Most frequent occupations were homemaker (16.3%; n = 49) and manual laborer (15.7%; n = 47). Comorbidities were absent in 89%, with 5% reporting osteoarthritis; 47.7% had a prior abdominal surgery history.

Descriptive analysis

Population characteristics are presented according to the nature and distribution of each variable. Continuous variables are reported as mean (SD) or median (IQR), as appropriate, and categorical variables as n (%).

Obesity was present in 44 patients (14.7%), while 18 (6%) were overweight. Regarding pain characteristics, 58.7% reported mixed pain, 36.3% somatic pain, and 5% radicular pain. Pain duration median was 24 months (range 3-240 months). Preoperative pain intensity median on VAS was 8/10 (IQR 1). Pain lateralization was slightly left-predominant (43.3%) versus right-sided (42%). Physical examination findings included paravertebral palpation tenderness (76%), axial movement pain (93%), and rotational movement pain (43%). Radicular stretch signs occurred more frequently (39.3%) than facet stress signs (20%). Muscle strength was preserved in 78.7%, with 18.7% demonstrating partial (4/5) strength. Approximately half (45.7%) retained normal sensation; paresthesias were most common (35.3%) among those with sensory changes.

Disc disease was classified as Grade IV in 39.7% and Grade III in 33.7%. Moderate-to-severe spinal canal stenosis was present in 30% (n = 90), while 34.3% had Grade I spondylolisthesis and 9.67% Grade II spondylolisthesis.

Hospital stay duration was 1 day in 90.7%, with the notable 42-day exception mentioned above from the ALIF group with serious infectious complications. Complications were absent in 93.7%; surgical site infections occurred in 3.3% (n = 10) and wound dehiscence in 0.7% (n = 2). Single cases of neuropathic pain, dural tear, ICU admission, post-fusion sacroiliitis, adjacent segment disease, and postoperative myasthenic crisis in a patient with myasthenia gravis occurred. One TLIF patient (0.3%) with cage displacement developed pseudoarthrosis requiring reoperation. Residual pain intensity median was 2/10 (IQR 1-3).

Preoperative spinopelvic parameters: SLL demonstrated normal distribution (mean 17°, SD ± 7°); postoperative measurement showed non-normal distribution (median 19.7°, IQR 15.4-23.2), likely reflecting procedure-related concentration toward physiologic normality. RLL maintained normal distribution preoperatively (mean 49.8°, SD ± 14.9°) and postoperatively (mean 49.4°, SD ± 12.5°), suggesting more stable global variation less susceptible to procedural disruption.

Bivariate analysis

TLIF and ALIF group comparisons identified differences presented in Table 2. Obesity was substantially more prevalent in the TLIF groýp (23.3% vs. 8.9%), reflecting the institutional decision algorithm whereby obesity complicating retroperitoneal access was a primary indication for TLIF. Notably, all ALIF patients had favorable vascular anatomy on preoperative imaging, compared with 90% of TLIF patients. This also illustrates the role of vascular anatomy in technique selection. Moderate-to-severe spinal canal stenosis was more frequent in ALIF (34.4% vs. 23.3%), however, any-grade spondylolisthesis was more frequent in TLIF patients (43.3% vs. 49.9%). Facet joint stress signs were more frequent in TLIF (25.8% vs. 16.1%). The distribution of disc degeneration grades was broadly comparable between groups: high-grade disc disease (Pfirrmann IV-V) was present in 62.5% of TLIF patients and 63.3% of ALIFpatients, with ALIF showing a higher proportion of Pfirrmann V (26.1% vs 19.2%). ALIF enrolled more women (64.4% vs. 57.5%), and had a slightly higher osteoarthritis rate (6.1% vs. 3.3%). TLIF patients had a greater mean age (46.1 vs. 44.3 years; SD ± 12.4). These baseline differences are consistent with confounding by indication driven by the surgical decision algorithm and support the need for multivariate adjustment in outcome analyses.

The most frequent pain type was mixed axial and radicular pain in both groups, more so in TLIF (61.7% vs. 56.7%). Pain duration median was equal for both groups, though the average was 1.7 months longer in TLIF (39 vs. 37.3 months). Pain lateralization was predominantly left in TLIF (44.2%) versus balanced in ALIF.

Physical examination findings of paravertebral tenderness, axial movement pain, and rotations were greater in the ALIF group, as were radicular symptoms. Facet stress signs were more common in TLIF. Muscle strength was minimally more compromised in ALIF (0.8%). Paresthesias were similarly distributed.

High-grade disc disease was more common in ALIF (26.1% vs. 19.2%), but TLIF was more associated with moderate-to-severe spinal canal stenosis (34.4% vs. 23.3%) and low-grade spondylolisthesis (49.2% vs. 43.3%).

Hospital stay was longer in ALIF (mean 1.6 vs. 1 day), influenced by the noted 42-day outlier. ALIF demonstrated a higher complication rate (7.2% vs. 5%), including surgical site infection, dehiscence, neurological injury, dural tears, and ICU admission. TLIF had cases of cage displacement with pseudoarthrosis requiring reoperation, post-fusion sacroiliitis managed with percutaneous sacroiliac block, and adjacent segment disease. No vascular injuries occurred in TLIF.

Bivariate analysis confirmed baseline group differences in BMI, spinal canal stenosis, and facet joint signs, which were the variables with the greatest absolute difference between groups and strongest clinical plausibility as confounders. This justifies their inclusion as adjustment covariates in multivariate models and guiding selection of appropriate statistical tests for outcome analysis.

Table 3 describes outcome variable behavior by group. Both groups demonstrated similar preoperative VAS levels (median 8/10 [IQR 7-9] TLIF vs. 7/10 [IQR 6-8] ALIF). Postoperatively, both showed marked pain reduction, achieving 2/10 (IQR 1-4 TLIF vs. 2-3 ALIF). VAS change was greater in TLIF (median 6, IQR 4-7) versus ALIF (median 5, IQR 3-6). Results were reported as median and IQR, given non-normal variable distributions, using measures unaffected by extreme values.

Bivariate analysis of dependent (outcome) variables

Outcome variables are described according to the intervention group and reported as median and IQR (Q1–Q3).

Preoperative spinopelvic parameters showed similar medians: SLL 17° (IQR 12.5-23) TLIF versus 16.1° (IQR 12-21.1) ALIF; RLL 51.3° (IQR 40.7-60.8) TLIF versus 50.3° (IQR 38.5-59.6) ALIF.

Interbody cage specifications need to be detailed, given their impact on spinopelvic parameters. TLIF procedures predominantly employed banana-shaped cages with 6-8° angulation, 10-12 mm height, and 26-28 mm width, positioned as anteriorly as possible within the L5-S1 space to optimize segmental lordosis restoration, with the remaining intervertebral space filled with heterologous bone graft promoting stability and fusion. This implant strategy achieved modest but consistent lordosis gain. Patients with greater degenerative compromise, anatomical limitations, or decreased angular correction tolerance employed neutral banana cages with 0-6° angulation. ALIF procedures utilized wedge-design cages with 10-15° incorporated lordosis and average 12-14 mm heights, exploiting anterior access for superior disc height and sagittal alignment restoration. Selected patients with elevated pelvic incidence received hyperlordotic cages up to 20° to optimize sagittal balance compensation.

Parameter change analysis demonstrated greater SLL increase with ALIF (median 4.8°, IQR 1.2-7.7) compared to TLIF (median 1°, IQR -4.1-4.5). RLL changes were discrete in both groups (median −0.3° [IQR -7.1-5.4] TLIF versus 2.2° [IQR -6.7-6.9] ALIF). Negative spinopelvic parameter values reflect normal curvature inversion (kyphosis) or pelvis-column discrepancy from degenerative disease, disc height loss, or insufficient surgical correction, representing real biomechanical alterations rather than measurement error. Wilcoxon non-parametric testing comparing group medians found significant differences in SLL change but not RLL change.

Multivariate linear and ordinal regression analysis

Bivariate analysis of clinical and radiographic variables identified notable and clinically relevant differences in weight, facet stress signs, and spinal canal stenosis. These findings, with documented literature plausibility, suggested these variables could function as potential confounders and warranted inclusion in multivariate analyses as adjustment covariables.

Given evidence from systematic review(Jiang et al., 2012) and meta-analyses (Ajiboye et al., 2018; Daher et al., 2024) demonstrating the largest differences in angular parameters (particularly segmental lordosis) between techniques, ΔSLL was defined as the primary outcome for multivariate analysis. This continuous variable quantifies the magnitude of angular correction achieved post-intervention, supporting multiple linear regressions to evaluate the independent surgical technique effect.

The model objective was to determine whether surgical technique (TLIF vs. ALIF) maintained an independent association with segmental lordosis gain after simultaneously considering potential confounders.

As shown in Table 4, the initial unadjusted linear analysis revealed a surgical technique associated with two outcomes. VAS pain change was significantly greater in TLIF (p = 0.0026; 95% CI: −1.19 to −0.25). Similarly, L5-S1 lordosis change was significantly greater in ALIF (p = 1.32e-06; 95% CI: 2.31 to 5.38). Conversely, L1-S1 lordosis change (ΔRLL) showed no relevant differences (p = 0.214; 95% CI: −0.90 to 4.02), indicating the absence of a statistically significant association.

In adjusted multivariate analysis accounting for imbalanced variables (BMI, facet joint signs, spinal canal stenosis), surgical technique maintained a significant effect on VAS change and SLL. Discussion

Interpretation of results

Both procedures achieved significant comparable pain reduction (6-point VAS change in TLIF versus 5-point change in ALIF) without clinically relevant differences. Stratified by obesity, non-obese patients demonstrated a 0.7-point VAS reduction differential (p = 0.004; 95% CI: −1.3 to −0.2) and obese patients a 0.9-point differential (p = 0.07; 95% CI: −2.0 to 0.1), without differential technique impact.

Radiographically, ALIF demonstrated greater segmental lordosis correction (approximately 4.8° versus 1° TLIF). This difference persisted both in non-obese (ΔSLL: −3.7°; p = 1.87e-05; 95% CI: −3.3 to −2.0°) and obese patients (ΔSLL: −4.6°; p = 0.03; 95% CI: −8.6 to −0.5°), though without additional clinical benefit translation.

Potential residual confounding impact requires consideration. The analyzed population represented relatively young patients (mean age ∼45 years) with low comorbidity prevalence and predominantly normal BMI range. This homogeneous clinical profile might have attenuated the effects of variables such as age or comorbid burden on outcomes, limiting detection of technique-exclusive differences. Although multivariate adjustment and stratified analysis were performed, residual confounding from variables not included in regression models cannot be excluded. Of particular note, this cohort encompasses a broad spectrum of disc degeneration severity (Pfirrmann II-V, with 3.3% grade II, 33.7% grade III, 39.7% grade IV, 23.3% grade V) and spondylolisthesis grades (34.3% grade I, 9.7% grade II, and higher grades 2%). Surgical considerations, expected biomechanical correction and clinical trajectory of TLIF and ALIF differ meaningfully across this spectrum, particularly between patients with no spondylolisthesis and those with grade II or higher slippage, as well as those between mild to advanced disc degeneration. Stratified analyses by Pfirrmann grade or spondylolisthesis grade were not pre-specified in the original protocol and the subgroup sample sizes that would result from such stratification are not sufficient to support a stable regression estimate, performing them post hoc would risk spurious findings. This represents a recognized limitation of the current study. Validated functional outcome measures such as ODI, SF-36, and EQ-5D were not included, as they were not systematically collected in all patients from the beginning of the cohort, which may limit a comprehensive interpretation of clinical improvement.

Collectively, results suggest TLIF and ALIF provide comparable clinical outcomes in the short term, at 3-month follow-up in this population, with ALIF's greater radiographic gain not necessarily conferring additional clinical benefit. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as the absence of standardized functional scales limits the ability to establish full clinical equivalence between techniques. These findings support TLIF as an effective, potentially cost-effective alternative to ALIF, particularly in younger patients with low comorbidity burden.

Comparison with the literature

Contrasting this cohort's results with published literature confirms that ALIF typically achieves greater SLL gain, as suggested by diverse studies (Hsieh et al., 2007; Bassani et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2009; Robert et al., 2014; Jiang et al., 2012). However, in this population, with confounding control, the clinical relevance of this difference is diminished. Cases occurred where lordosis reduced instead of increasing (negative ΔSLL) with equivalent pain improvement, suggesting ALIF's radiological advantage does not necessarily imply clinical benefit universally.

Regarding RLL, most studies report significant TLIF-ALIF differences(Hsieh et al., 2007; Kim et al., 2009; Ajiboye et al., 2018; Jiang et al., 2012; Wu et al., 2021). Conversely, this cohort found comparable RLL outcomes between techniques. This resembles reports by Kuang(Kuang et al., 2017), Alhaug (Alhaug et al., 2024), and Bouchard (Bouchard et al., 2024), concluding that despite larger cage size and anterior positioning as in this cohort, practical differences are minimal or clinically irrelevant (Vialle et al., 2022).

Concordantly, 2024 studies question whether ALIF radiological and clinical advantages, though statistically significant, translate into patient-perceptible clinical benefits(Daher et al., 2024). Daniels' meta-analysis supports this questioning, noting that ALIF-TLIF differences in patient-reported outcomes do not exceed minimal clinically important difference thresholds.

Regarding complications, some studies reported higher ALIF frequency with prolonged hospitalizations(Faundez et al., 2009; Karamian et al., 2022; Phan et al., 2015; Hee et al., 2001). This cohort demonstrated similar complication rates and hospitalization duration between interventions, though with greater ALIF variability. These findings suggest equivalent safety profiles in this context, although TLIF seems to offer a lower risk threshold.

Analysis of potential error sources

Like all observational cohort studies, this work faces methodological limitations potentially affecting internal validity. The most significant is the non-randomized assignment of patients to surgical techniques, which introduces a potential for selection bias and confounding by indication. Bivariate analysis identified baseline imbalances between groups; notably higher obesity prevalence in TLIF and higher moderate-to-severe spinal stenosis in ALIF, which were incorporated into multivariate adjustment. However, additional confounders including baseline VAS, Pfirrmann grade, spondylolisthesis grade, cage design and surgeon experience, were not uniformly documented or included in models, and their residual influence cannot be excluded.

Approach selection was influenced by clinical considerations. Patients with ALIF contraindications, such as unfavorable vascular anatomy, multiple prior abdominal surgeries with peritoneal adhesion risk, inguinal herniorrhaphy with mesh, abdominal aortic aneurysm, or abdominal trauma sequelae, received TLIF. In a randomized trial, these cases would likely be excluded. However, this cohort included them, given comparable pathology and outcome development potential as the broader population, reflecting real clinical practice despite introducing group heterogeneity.

Additionally, variables reported in the literature, such as operative time and intraoperative blood loss, were not recorded, limiting adjustment for their potential influence. Health record-based data collection may introduce information bias through unstandardized documentation or complication underreporting. To mitigate this, indirect perioperative impact indicators, including hospital stay, blood transfusion necessity, and ICU transfer, were incorporated. Radiographic measurements were performed by the principal investigator with expert supervision; while initial measurements were blinded, postoperative blinding was not feasible due to identifiable surgical features on imaging, which may introduce measurement bias.

Another potentially informative outcome measure is the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) (Fairbank and Pynsent, 2000), which would have enhanced the assessment of functional improvement. However, its inclusion was not feasible, as validated functional scales (including ODI, SF-36, and EQ-5D) were not systematically collected from the start of the cohort in 2019. Retrospective collection was avoided to prevent recall bias in baseline status. This lack of standardized functional measures may limit a comprehensive interpretation of clinical outcomes. This study cannot make claims regarding functional outcomes, disability or quality of life, and the observed VAS equivalence should not be extrapolated to comprehensive clinical equivalence. Notably, prior studies by Daher(Daher et al., 2024) and Alhaug (Alhaug et al., 2024) similarly reported no clinically significant differences between TLIF and ALIF based on ODI.

Identification of new hypotheses

The initial 3-month follow-up window, while useful for capturing immediate postoperative outcomes, is insufficient to evaluate several outcomes of paramount importance in spinal fusion surgery. Fusion success, cage subsidence, cage mobilization, adjacent segment disease, and reoperation rates require a minimum of 12-24 months of follow-up for meaningful assessment. Cage subsidence is a particularly well-recognized complication of lumbar interbody fusion that is dependent on patient-specific factors, technique-specific factors and implant characteristics. Future prospective studies should incorporate measurement of these parameters, as well as the incorporation of validated functional scales (ODI, EQ-5D, SF-36), radiographic fusion assessment, cage migration surveillance and minimum 12-month follow-up to provide a comprehensive comparison. Other outcomes warranting prospective investigation include return-to-work intervals, neurological recovery, adjacent segment disease rates and the learning curve effect for each technique across surgical volume strata.

Clinical utility of results

This study supports TLIF as an effective alternative comparable to ALIF for symptomatic L5-S1 degenerative disc disease management. TLIF offers practical advantages, including a single surgical team requirement, avoidance of abdominal approach, and reduced vascular risk, potentially resulting in lower costs. These features, without compromising desired outcomes, make TLIF particularly relevant in resource-limited scenarios such as Colombia(Taboada et al., 2022).

Knowledge gaps addressed by results

To the best of our knowledge, and based on searches conducted in major scientific databases, this represents one of the largest reported Latin American—and Colombian—cohorts (n = 300) evaluating surgical techniques for the management of L5–S1 disc disease, providing insight into local experience with TLIF and ALIF. These findings contribute to addressing a regional gap in the literature, demonstrating clinical and imaging outcomes comparable to those reported worldwide.

Conclusions

Until recently, literature has favored ALIF results regarding spinopelvic parameter improvement and sagittal balance restoration without significant clinical result differences versus TLIF. However, ALIF presents potential procedural considerations, including increased operative time, elevated costs, greater intraoperative blood loss, potentially prolonged hospitalization, and higher intraoperative complication rates, as well as specific contraindications such as prior abdominal surgery, obesity, or unfavorable vascular anatomy.

This study suggests that TLIF is an effective and safe technique in alternative to ALIF, with comparable outcomes in patients with CLBP and L5-S1 disc disease. Although ALIF managed a higher SLL correction, its clinical impact was limited. Meanwhile, RLL showed no statistically significant differences between techniques. Clinical results, including the reduction of preoperative pain, as well as complication rates, were equivalent among both groups.

Most published studies do not comprehensively integrate clinical and radiographic outcomes with technique-specific factors, potentially introducing bias and fostering partial-data-based conclusions. This study provides a global analysis of the outcomes of surgical techniques in a Colombian cohort, offering valuable information for hypothesis generation regarding regional behavior in similarly demographic and healthcare-system populations.

As one of the largest Latin American cohorts to perform this comparison, our study provides relevant evidence to inform surgical decision-making in the region. Our findings suggest that TLIF may represent an efficient, cost-effective, and low-risk alternative, making it a valuable option in healthcare systems with limited resources.
