Family participation in dignity interventions for patients with cancer receiving palliative care: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Qian Cai, Hangsai Wang, Yue Zhang, Liuqing Wang, Le Zhao, Xinfen Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe global burden of cancer is escalating, with Asia accounting for over half of cancer-related deaths worldwide. As cancer often diminishes patients’ quality of life and sense of dignity, dignity-related interventions have gained prominence in palliative care for patients with cancer. However, a more in-depth exploration of the involvement of families, as the fundamental social units in Confucian Asian cultures, and cultural considerations is currently lacking. This scoping review focuses specifically on patients with cancer receiving palliative care and aims to offer a comprehensive synthesis of the existing evidence on family participatory dignity interventions, addressing the need for a holistic understanding of this emerging field to guide future research and clinical practice.Methods and analysisThis scoping review will be meticulously structured according to the five-stage framework established by Arksey and O’Malley, complemented by the scoping review methodology of the Joanna Briggs Institute. Our search will encompass a comprehensive array of databases, including PubMed, Embase, The Cochrane Library, CINAHL, Web of Science, PsycINFO, Scopus, CNKI and Wanfang Data, from their inception up to August 2024, targeting both English and Chinese relevant literature. To ensure a thorough exploration, we will also delve into grey literature via OpenGrey, Google Scholar and citation chaining. This scoping review will include all types of quantitative or mixed methods designs and qualitative studies. We will extract data on study design, sample size, intervention details, outcome measures and any other relevant information. The screening process will be conducted by two independent reviewers, who will meticulously assess the titles and abstracts, followed by a full-text review to select relevant studies. Discrepancies will be resolved through consensus discussions with a third reviewer. Data extraction will be executed using a standardised tool, and the findings will be systematically presented in tabular form with an accompanying narrative to summarise all relevant interventions, their characteristics, outcomes and key findings.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval for this scoping review is not required, since the methodology merely involves the collection and review of publicly available literature. Our findings will not only be presented and discussed in a peer-reviewed article but also shared at conferences relevant to the topic.Trial registrationOur scoping review protocol has been formally registered with the Open Science Framework. Registration details can be accessed at the provided link: https://osf.io/fyhrm/.

Introduction

The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) reported alarming data in 2022 that there were 19.96 million new cancer cases and 9.74 million cancer-related deaths worldwide, with projections estimating a rise to 35.3 million new cases by 2050.1 The burden of cancer incidence and mortality is severe and escalating. Notably, new cancer cases in Asia account for 49.2% of the global total, with deaths surpassing half of the worldwide total at 56.1%.1 In China, there were 4.82 million new cancer cases in 2022, representing 24.2% of the global total, and 2.57 million deaths, constituting 26.4% of the global mortality rate.1 2 With increasing population growth and ageing, the global population is expected to rise from 8 billion in 2022 to 9.7 billion by 2050,3 with the proportion of those aged 65 and over increasing from 10% to 16%.4 Age-related cancer incidence and mortality will significantly contribute to the intensifying global cancer burden, including in China.57

Despite considerable progress in treating the somatic symptoms of patients with cancer, the disease often forces patients to confront a range of distressing symptoms such as pain and discomfort (including nausea, constipation and respiratory difficulties) as it progresses,8 9 leading to a markedly diminished quality of life. With the uncertainty of the future and a myriad of physiological, psychological, social and economic issues arising from treatment, patients’ sense of value and meaning is severely compromised, causing immense suffering.1012 Additionally, the dependency on others for care may restrict or prevent opportunities for independent engagement in daily and social life, putting patients at high risk of losing their dignity.13 Dignity is conceptually defined as the intrinsic value of a person combined with self-respect and respect from others. A study reported that most patients with early and advanced cancer experienced some level of loss of dignity, with 71% experiencing mild loss of dignity, 18% experiencing moderate loss and 5% experiencing severe loss of dignity.14

Helping patients with cancer undergoing palliative care to achieve a sense of dignity has always been one of the most confounding challenges.15 Dignity therapy, a brief and personalised form of psychotherapy developed by Chochinov, is frequently used in palliative care for patients with cancer to enhance their sense of dignity and alleviate their suffering.16 17 This approach aims to improve the experience of patients whose lives are threatened by illness and to mitigate psychological, emotional and existential distress.18 The prominence of dignity therapy in the field of oncology is growing due to its ability to enhance the meaning, direction and dignity in the lives of patients and their families.19 However, there is a need for more comprehensive perspectives encompassing the diverse viewpoints of patients, family members and healthcare professionals, as well as insights from cultural backgrounds. A previous study indicated that effective communication between patients and their families can promote positive reflections on the grief experience for family caregivers,20 and can mitigate patients’ psychosocial distress and alleviate the subsequent grief experienced by family members.21 Conversely, inadequate patient–family communication was reported as a contributing factor to psychological stress, evoking feelings of loneliness and hopelessness and ultimately diminishing the quality of life for patients with cancer and their family members.2224

In the Confucian Asian cultures, the family rather than the individual is regarded as the fundamental unit of life, and family members serve as the fundamental source of social, economic and emotional support for patients,25 26 thereby influencing patients’ perception of dignity. Particularly in China, where there is a shortage of nursing professionals, family caregivers (usually the patient’s spouse, offspring or other relatives) play a critical role in caring for patients.27 The care and support from family members are effective means to maintain the patient’s dignity and alleviate their psychological burden and suffering.28 Studies involving family caregivers indicated that patients’ sense of dignity could be enhanced through strengthening family integrity, the perception of being loved and mutual compassion, which involved the cultivation of filial support and understanding gained through open communication with their family members.29 30 Therefore, the family must become the guiding principle for dignity-enhancing interventions in the Asian context, and family caregivers are essential resources in providing dignified interventions for patients with cancer undergoing palliative care.31

Based on the assumption that there is a relationship between the participation of caregivers and the sense of dignity of patients with cancer receiving palliative care, some researchers, building on the clinical foundation of dignity therapy and an empirical understanding of dignity-related issues among Asian families facing terminal illness, have developed a novel ‘Family Dignity Intervention’ (FDI).32 Family participatory dignity interventions adopt a patient–family dyadic model rather than a solitary treatment approach. As opposed to traditional interventions, family-related dignity interventions require the joint participation of family caregivers and patients, facilitating the establishment of a family-centred communication platform.33 34 Engaging in dialogue about life experiences between patients and caregivers is beneficial for patients, allowing them to express their emotions and adjust their mental state. It also provides caregivers with the opportunity to express their inner feelings, prompting them to reflect on their past with the patient and recognise their significance and sense of duty. Such interventions align more closely with the philosophy of palliative care as defined by the WHO,35 which emphasises not only improving the quality of life for patients but also for their family members.

However, there is no consensus regarding the involvement of families in dignity interventions, and the field lacks a holistic view and a comprehensive review of the existing literature. Engaging families in current dignity practices remains a challenge, and there is a dearth of studies demonstrating how such involvement can be effectively achieved. Identifying the current evidence base for family-involving interventions is considered the inaugural step towards instituting new routines that prioritise family participation in dignity care. Scoping reviews provide a feasible means for gathering and synthesising diverse evidence and effectively showcasing the expanse and complexity of a field,3638 which is especially beneficial for integrating evidence from varied and heterogeneous sources.

This study aims to offer a broad overview of the literature encompassing every facet of family participatory dignity interventions, such as the types of interventions, their structural components and outcomes. By adopting less strict inclusion criteria to incorporate a variety of research study types, including developmental and experimental studies, the review ensures a comprehensive and holistic perspective. Given these considerations, a scoping review is deemed the most appropriate methodology for the current study. Since undertaking a scoping review is a complex endeavour, a priori protocol would be essential for preparing and conducting the review, facilitating the avoidance of potential issues throughout the process. In this paper, a comprehensive protocol for our study is described.

Objectives

This scoping review aims to present a broad overview and synthesis of the research on family participation in dignity interventions for patients with cancer receiving palliative care, meticulously mapping and summarising the evidence from both published and grey literature to delineate the characteristics and outcomes of such involvement.

Methods and analysis

Scoping reviews are an emerging research methodology, paralleling the systematic reviews in structure yet diverging in purpose and methodologies.39 Given the expansive nature of our study’s aims, including synthesising the current literature on family participation in dignity interventions for patients with cancer receiving palliative care, and mapping these findings to inform future research in this area—a scoping review is more appropriate than a systematic review.36 39 Guided by the foundational methodological framework established by Arksey and O’Malley,40 enhanced by Levac et al,41 and further outlined in the Joanna Briggs Institute Reviewer’s Manual,37 our review will methodically progress through stages: (1) identifying the research question; (2) identifying the relevant studies; (3) study selection; (4) charting the data; and (5) collating, summarising and reporting the results. Each stage will be delineated with precision in the subsequent narrative.

Our protocol has been registered within the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/fyhrm/). Furthermore, both the current protocol and the forthcoming scoping review will adhere to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines,42 ensuring methodological rigour and clarity in our reporting.

Stage 2: identifying relevant studies

Our search strategy is grounded in established methodologies for conducting scoping reviews,38 43 with the aim of identifying a comprehensive array of literature, including both peer-reviewed and grey literature, to ensure a diverse representation of contexts. Recognising the multifaceted nature of ‘family participation’ and ‘dignity interventions’, which are terms with varied definitions and interpretations, we confront the challenge of delineating their intersection. To navigate this, we will use key terms to inform previous systematic reviews of family participation and dignity interventions. Our inclusion criteria will be stringent, focusing solely on articles that explicitly address the convergence of these two concepts, thereby ensuring the review’s scope remains both broad and focused.44

Databases and search strategy

Our search will encompass a spectrum of electronic bibliographic databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, CINAHL (via EBSCO host), Cochrane Library, Embase and Psych INFO (via OVID), the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI, for Chinese literature) and WAN FANG DATA (for Chinese literature). Grey literature search will be conducted through platforms like Google Scholar and OpenGrey. The reference lists of pertinent papers will also be searched via citation chaining through Scopus to identify additional eligible studies.45 46

We will employ a three-step search strategy to develop a systematic search strategy. The first step involves a preliminary search on PubMed, which has been completed. This will be followed by an analysis of the text in the titles and abstracts of the identified studies, along with the index terms applied to them. The selection of key terms will be collaboratively determined by two authors (QC and HW) in consultation with a research librarian.

The subsequent step will encompass a conclusive literature search using all identified keywords and index terms across the nine databases. This search will be iterative, with the potential to incorporate additional keywords and search terms as we gain a deeper understanding of the evidence base. The guidance of a research librarian will be instrumental in refining our search strategy, ensuring the use of pertinent keywords and Medical Subject Headings terms (see table 1). Our search will be exhaustive, covering articles and grey literature up to August 2024 in both English and Chinese. A detailed search strategy for PubMed is available in the online supplemental file 1.

In the third step, we will meticulously review the reference lists from the studies and articles we have identified in search of any additional studies that may merit inclusion. Each title and abstract will be carefully assessed for relevance to our review. For those studies that are preliminarily deemed eligible, we will procure the full-text articles for an in-depth evaluation. Should there be any ambiguities or a need for supplementary information, we will not hesitate to engage with the authors of the original studies to seek clarification.

Stage 3: study selection

During this phase, we will specify and refine our inclusion and exclusion criteria, adhering to the PCC (Population, Concept Context) framework as advocated by the Joanna Briggs Institute. The stringent application of these refined criteria ensures that the articles selected are pertinent to our research inquiry.

Exclusion criteria

The following exclusion criteria will be applied: (1) duplicate published studies; (2) conference proceedings and abstracts, study protocols, letters, discussions or editorials; and (3) studies with incomplete data and no returns after contacting authors.

Screening

The screening process for this scoping review will consist of two phases. Initially, two independent reviewers will assess the titles and abstracts against our inclusion criteria. Subsequently, the full text of all potentially relevant articles will be retrieved for detailed screening by a researcher for the next phase, which is full-text screening. The same two reviewers will use the above eligibility criteria to select full-text studies. Any discrepancies encountered during these stages will be resolved through discussion or referral to a third reviewer.

A PRISMA-ScR flow diagram will be employed to delineate the screening decision process, monitor the number of studies included at each selection phase (see online supplemental file 2) and specify the rationale for excluding articles during the full-text screening.47 This flow diagram, alongside the finalised inclusion and exclusion criteria, will be incorporated into the manuscript that presents the results of our final scoping review.

Prior to finalisation, all the feedback from the researchers will be carefully considered for updating the data extractions from the included studies. If the data extraction tool requires any revision, it will be discussed and decided on by the research team. Any alterations will be explicitly documented in the final scoping review. In instances where essential data are absent from the studies or reports, attempts will be made to contact the original authors. If missing data are unavailable, the characteristics will be noted as either unknown or missing.

Stage 5: collating, summarising and reporting the results

The objective of this scoping review is to present a comprehensive overview report that synthesises and summarises the progress of all available research. Guided by the PRISMA-ScR guidelines,47 the data garnered in the stage 4 will be methodically organised and concisely summarised, ensuring alignment with the review’s research queries and objectives, and interpreted within the context of knowledge gaps, future research needs/directions/recommendations, practice and policy. Notably, this review will not undertake a systematic quality assessment or risk of bias evaluation, as the intent of this scoping review is to provide an overview of the existing literature rather than a critical appraisal of the literature to answer a specific question.39 42

Relevant information will be tabulated, charted and reported to map the distribution of included studies chronologically and geographically, as well as the types, aims, characteristics, providers, recipients and outcomes of reported interventions. Descriptive numerical summary, such as number of studies included, types of study design and characteristics of study populations, will be used to elucidate the relationship between the findings and the review’s objectives. Qualitative data will be summarised by using Braun and Clark’s thematic analysis (an analysis method flexible and appropriate to apply to a complex account of data) as recommended.48 49

Consultation

Consultation is an optional step, but it is recommended to involve stakeholders and/or experts in the process. Engaging stakeholders can help validate preliminary results and provide fresh perspectives on the topic. In this upcoming study, stakeholders will be consulted in stage 5. A panel of experienced health professionals, healthcare leaders and academics will be invited to provide their insights via email or virtual meetings.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public will not be involved in the design or writing of the research plans or the drafting of the scoping review, as the primary objective is to systematically map and synthesise existing literature on family participation in dignity interventions for patients with cancer receiving palliative care. However, a panel of family members of patients with cancer receiving palliative care will review the results following the publication of the scoping review protocol. We believe that involving family members at this stage will validate and enrich our findings within a national context, ensuring their relevance to families.

ETHICS AND DISSEMINATION

Given that our scoping review relies exclusively on data extracted from previously published studies, documents and publicly available scientific literature, ethical approval is not required for its conduct. The synthesized findings of this review will be submitted for publication through a peer-reviewed article in a scientific open-access journal. Additionally, our results will be presented and shared at relevant scientific conferences and at workshops for research and operational stakeholders, facilitating translation into further research and operational practices.
