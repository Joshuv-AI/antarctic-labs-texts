A southward differentiated impactor forms the tapered shape of the South Pole–Aitken impact basin on the Moon
Authors: Shigeru Wakita, Brandon C. Johnson, Jeffrey C. Andrews-Hanna, Gabriel Gowman, Thomas M. Davison, Gareth S. Collins, Carys A. Bill, Simone Marchi, Amanda M. Alexander, William F. Bottke, Alexander J. Evans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The South Pole–Aitken (SPA) basin-forming impact was a critical event in the Moon’s history. Despite being the oldest and largest acknowledged basin, critical details including the impactor’s size, nature, direction, and fate of the ejecta remain uncertain. Here, we simulate SPA’s formation and resultant crustal structure. We find that SPA’s observed shape of an ellipse tapered toward the south is best reproduced by a 260-km-diameter differentiated impactor striking with a north-to-south trajectory. In this scenario, the impact disperses lunar mantle materials in the cross-range and the downrange directions away from the lunar farside. Much of the mantle ejecta collapses into the basin interior, consistent with the distribution inferred from gravity. These results suggest that the Artemis landing sites near the lunar south pole should contain abundant SPA ejecta including mantle materials. Samples returned from these regions should therefore reveal the age of SPA and the composition of the lunar mantle.

INTRODUCTION

At greater than 2000 km in diameter, the South Pole–Aitken impact basin (SPA) is the largest and oldest confirmed basin on the Moon (1, 2). The basin is elongated in one direction, presumed to be the downrange direction, suggesting either a northward or southward impact trajectory (2). The ejecta from an impact of a northward trajectory has been suggested as a solution to the crustal thickness asymmetry between the Moon’s nearside and farside [e.g., (3, 4)]. Impactor core material may be responsible for magnetic anomalies observed around the northern rim of SPA (5) or gravity anomalies within the basin (6). Recent work, however, suggests that a southward impact trajectory is more likely based on the shape and the structure of the basin. Large basins on the Moon and other solid bodies (e.g., Mars and Pluto) are ellipses that taper in the downrange direction (7). SPA’s tapering toward the south, a steeper crustal thickness gradient toward the north, and the presence of a thorium- and iron-rich deposit toward the southwest of SPA beyond the basin rim support a southward impact trajectory (7). The shape of the SPA basin was evaluated using a combination of topography, Bouguer gravity, and gravity gradients, excluding any regions in which the basin outline has been substantially modified by other large impacts or is otherwise indistinct (7). Multiple choices for the basin outline all yielded a shape tapering to the south at >2σ level. If this southward direction is correct, the Artemis missions, which will target the south pole region, will be well placed to sample SPA ejecta and reveal the history of the Moon. There is, therefore, a need to revisit the formation of SPA considering a southward impact.

Previous three-dimensional (3D) impact simulations of SPA formation suggested that the impactor diameter was 200 to 400 km with an impact angle of 30° to 45° (5, 8, 9). Assuming a south-to-north impact, these simulations mainly investigated the distribution of ejecta at resolutions of >10-km cell size. Nevertheless, the resulting modeled basins include a thickened annulus of crust in the basin outside the central zone of crustal thinning, which is not observed at SPA (4). Previous 3D models have not examined the relationship between the impact direction and the observed SPA crustal thickness distribution including its tapered shape and observed ejecta distribution, which requires higher resolution and longer run times to make robust comparisons. Here, we conduct high-resolution (5-km cell size) 3D simulations of the formation of SPA using iSALE-3D (10, 11). If the large impactor that produced SPA formed early enough, heating from the decay of short-lived radionuclides (e.g., 26Al) is expected to result in its differentiation (12–14). We simulate impacts of both differentiated and undifferentiated bodies obliquely striking a Moon-like target and examine the resultant crustal thickness and ejecta distributions. More details are provided in Materials and Methods.

RESULTS

Reproducing the tapered shape of SPA basin

Our impact simulation that produces a final basin that best fits the tapered elliptical shape of SPA (7) is of a 260-km-diameter differentiated body that strikes the Moon at 13 km/s and a 30° impact angle. Note that 30° is measured from the horizontal direction (i.e., 90° is a vertical impact). We assume that the impactor core is half of the impactor radius, which corresponds to 29.2% in total mass. This size is at the higher end of the estimated core radius, which is 40 to 50% of the differentiated parent body radius assuming an initial composition of an ordinary chondrite (15). A time series of SPA formation is shown in Figs. 1 and 2. The impactor’s upper portion is highly deformed but continues on nearly its initial trajectory in a process called decapitation [e.g., (16, 17); see Figs. 1B and 2]. Thus, the impactor striking at a shallow impact angle does not fully penetrate into the Moon. Material is ejected (Fig. 1C), and then, the transient crater collapses under the force of gravity, thereby producing a large asymmetric central uplift (Fig. 1D). After the collapse of the uplift (Fig. 1E), crustal material flows toward the basin center until the basin reaches its post-impact state ~3 hours after the impact (Fig. 1F). The distance traversed by crustal materials can be up to 800 km toward the center of SPA. Note that our best-fitting case is comparable with previous work of 200 to 420 km at 15 km/s with 30° to 60° (8) but slightly different from 350 to 400 km at 12 to 16 km/s with 30° to 45° in (9). Here, we focused on the crater planform in crustal thickness to determine a best-fit.

We use crustal thickness from our simulations to determine the basin shape (e.g., rim planform shape and location; see Materials and Methods). With our resolution of 5 km, this approach will reflect the final topography, since the final basin should be isostatically compensated (18) but is more robust than comparing topography directly. The basin planform takes the shape of a tapered ellipse and matches the observed basin planform well (Fig. 3A). Our best-fit simulation produces a tapered basin that is slightly wider than SPA’s shape based on Bouguer gravity and gravity gradients (7) (see also Fig. 3, E and F). Based on SPA’s observed gravity data and topography, the averaged crustal thickness profile along the SPA’s long axis exhibits a steeper slope toward the north compared to the south (7). Our averaged crustal thickness profile exhibits fluctuations, and it is hard to distinguish quantitatively the slopes in uprange and downrange directions (Fig. 3C). Nonetheless, a slightly steeper slope in the uprange direction may suggest that our simulations also favor a southward impact trajectory as suggested by Andrews-Hanna et al. (7).

To explore the effect that the impactor’s core has on the basin shape, we simulate the same impact but without an impactor core (260-km-diameter impactor hitting at 13 km/s with 30° impact angle). Note that this lowers the mass of the coreless impactor, which is assumed to be composed completely of dunite (see Materials and Methods). While a differentiated impactor produces a basin planform well described by a tapered ellipse-like shape for SPA, an undifferentiated impactor produces a more circular basin (see Fig. 3, A and B). In addition, the crustal thickness gradient in SPA’s uprange and downrange directions are similar for an undifferentiated impactor, which is also inconsistent with observations (Fig. 3D). The decapitation of the impactor occurs in both simulations regardless of the presence of a core (Fig. 2). In the case of the differentiated impactor, however, the dense core continues to excavate a shallow zone in the downrange direction during the decapitation process. It is this continued excavation by the core that results in the tapering of the planform in the downrange direction (Fig. 3). This enhanced excavation is absent in case of the undifferentiated impactor, which explains the contrasting final basin shapes. Thus, our simulation indicates that the impactor’s core is responsible for the tapered shape of SPA.

Effect of impact angle and velocity

We also vary the impact velocity from 10 to 16 km/s to explore the effect of impact velocity on basin shape. We use this range despite modeling results that indicate that the mean impact speed is 20 km/s (19) for lunar projectiles 4.35 billion years ago, a prospective age for SPA basin formation [e.g., (20)]. Our rationale comes from a geometric model describing large elliptical basin formation from Andrews-Hanna and Zuber (21) [see also (22)]. Cratering efficiency, or the ratio between final crater and impactor sizes, decreases for lower impact velocities. A lower cratering efficiency implies that crater growth is arrested at an earlier time when the crater planform has less time to circularize relative to the highly elongated projectile footprint, or area of direct contact between the impactor and target. Based on this, we expect that lower impact velocities will lead to more tapering and elongation, and therefore, the projectile speed of SPA was likely lower than the predicted mean. As we vary the impact velocity, we use crater scaling laws to estimate the size of impactor that will produce the same-sized crater as our best-fitting simulation, i.e., the smaller size for the higher impact velocity (see Materials and Methods). We find that the 10-km/s simulation produces a similar shape to our best-fit case, although the tapering is more exaggerated (fig. S1A). Conversely, the-16 km/s impact results in a more circular shape, which is inconsistent with SPA’s shape (fig. S1B).

Our best-fit favoring a-13 km/s impact velocity implies that the SPA impactor was on a low inclination Earth-like orbit before it struck the Moon. Given the collisional and dynamical evolution of leftover planetesimals, the most likely source of the SPA impactor probably originated within the Mars zone rather than within the Venus-Earth zone (19, 23). The bodies in the Mars zone were transported there and/or formed in situ during the early solar system (24–26). These bodies may have undergone differentiation due to the decay heat of 26Al. Since the building blocks of Mars could be similar to ordinary and enstatite chondrites (27–29), a potential impactor originating from the Mars zone could have a similar composition. Lunar samples from Apollo landing sites find variety of composition originating from impactor materials (30). Future sampling in and around SPA basin could provide compositional information on the SPA impactor, helping us understand its origin.

Decapitation of a differentiated impactor, which requires a relatively shallow impact angle, is necessary to produce the tapered shape of SPA. Our best-fitting case of 30° is consistent with the shallower angle of previous work (5, 8, 9). In contrast, the steeper impact angle case of 35° begins to produce a relatively larger and circular-shaped basin and fails in forming a tapered-shaped basin as observed (fig. S2B). The shallower angle case of 25° produces a tapered shape basin that is substantially smaller than SPA (fig. S2A). These findings imply that the decapitation of the impactor core is essential for producing tapered-shaped basins. A bigger impactor than implied by impactor scaling laws may be necessary for these shallower impacts, but it would also likely create a basin that is too large in the cross-range direction. At this shallower angle, the core does not contribute substantively to the cratering process (fig. S3). Alternatively, for those shallow impact angles, our simulation may not resolve the decapitation process well even with our high resolution of 5 km; the best-fit impactor’s core radius of 65 km is 13 cells (see also Supplementary Text and fig. S4). While our current results indicate 30° as the best-fit case, this might change with a much higher resolution simulation and a more extensive exploration of impact parameters (e.g., impact angle, impactor size, and its core size) and target properties (e.g., preimpact temperature profile).

Distribution of mantle ejecta

Understanding the distribution of mantle ejecta around SPA is of great interest to future sample return efforts. Substantially more material is ejected in the downrange direction compared to the uprange direction as expected [e.g., (31–33); see Fig. 1C]. We find that the mantle material is distributed ~550 km beyond the final topographic rim in the downrange direction and 650 km in the cross-range direction, while there is no mantle ejecta in the uprange direction (Fig. 4A). The ejecta pattern is reminiscent of the butterfly-like distal ejecta pattern, which is characterized by two “wings” of ejecta in cross-range direction and usually found as a result of grazing impacts (9, 16, 31, 34, 35). For comparison, mantle-bearing ejecta inferred from gravity data [supplementary figure 5 of (7); (36)] extends ~400 km beyond the basin rim west of the basin in the cross-range direction and 250 km south of the basin rim. Mantle-bearing ejecta is not expected from the gravity data beyond the north rim of the basin. Mantle ejecta from an impactor with a core is distributed further in both the cross-range and downrange direction compared to the impactor without a core (Fig. 4B).

In the later stages of crater formation (from 3000 s until the end of the simulation, ~3 hours), the thickened crust in the ejecta blanket collapses into the basin, thins, and transports mantle-bearing ejecta atop across much of the basin floor interior to the final topographic rim (see Fig. 5 and movie S1). Because this zone may be dominated by the impact melt sheet and any crust and ejecta material transported into this zone might be expected to homogenize with the melt, the central part of the SPA basin could be devoid of observable mantle-bearing ejecta. The center of the basin is also dominated by material excavated from shallower depths (<50 km; see below and Fig. 6 and fig. S5). Alternatively, a lower thermal gradient would reduce the inward collapse of the ejecta leaving an exposed melt pool at the basin center. Limited inward collapse of the ejecta blanket is supported by the observation work of a subtle peak ring of thickened crust at the inner edge of the zone of mantle-bearing ejecta (7, 36). Our model indicates that impact melt is distributed around the basin center (fig. S6). These results agree well with gravity data interpretation of mantle-bearing ejecta extending up to ~700 km interior to the present-day topographic rim (7, 36), a uniform density zone ~1300 km in diameter that has been interpreted as the solidified melt sheet in the basin center. The central impact melt zone in our model is narrower than inferred from the gravity data and is preferentially located in the downrange direction (fig. S6). We suspect that this discrepancy could be a result of post-impact processes, such as viscous spreading of the hot buoyant melt sheet.

Collapse of the central uplift also pushes mantle material onto the surface (Fig. 5). Asymmetry in the central uplift means that less mantle material is pushed downrange than in the cross-range direction. Mantle is also pushed up over the crust in the uprange direction, with the thickened crust collapsing into the basin. This prediction explains why the observed gravity anomalies are consistent with mantle-derived material interior to the uprange rim, where no mantle-derived ejecta is expected (7, 36).

We can also probe the provenance depth of the mantle ejecta. The uppermost mantle ejecta (from 50 to 70 km from the surface) is broadly distributed (Fig. 6A), while the deeper mantle ejecta (>90 km from the surface) is preferentially distributed in the cross-range direction (Fig. 6C). The maximum depth of excavation is closely related to the decapitation of the differentiated impactor that produces the observed tapering of the crater planform. The differentiated impactor extends excavation in the downrange direction, but the excavation is shallower and more limited in lateral extent. The maximum depth of excavation is 70 km for ejecta that lands in the downrange direction and 100 km for ejecta that lands in the cross-range direction.

DISCUSSION

Our results indicate that a differentiated impactor is necessary to produce the tapered planform of the SPA basin. We expect, however, that any large density contrast between the interior and exterior of the impactor could produce a similar shape. For example, a rocky body could potentially have a highly porous outer layer, while a differentiated icy body could have a rocky core. Such impactors would have a lower average density than the impactors considered in this work. They would yield a reduced cratering efficiency. Thus, including the effect of a high porosity outer layer or an ice layer could enhance elongation and tapering. Inclusion of these effects would expand the allowable parameter space for SPA-forming impacts to include higher impact angles and velocities. Note that a porous outer layer would be more consistent with leftover planetesimals from the terrestrial planet region than an icy outer layer (19).

Other basins with similar tapered shapes are located on the Moon, Mars, and Pluto (7). We postulate that they were also produced by bodies with large density contrasts between their interiors and exteriors. Insights into the nature of these basin-forming impactors might be gleaned from new impact simulations capable of resolving this tapering effect (fig. S4). While the impactor’s core and its decapitation is essential to form the tapered-shaped basin (fig. S2), its core-mantle ratio could have an important effect on the resulting basin shape. If the impactor’s core is molten, the behavior of decapitation and the resultant basin shape could change. While the fate of the impactor’s core is beyond the scope of this paper and needs further investigation, our best-fitting simulation indicates it is unlikely to be located within the basin (6). Instead, it would land away from the basin and/or escape from the Moon (see figs. S3 and S4 and movie S3). Therefore, the magnetic anomalies within and around the SPA basin may require an explanation other than the magnetization of material from the SPA forming impactor’s core (5).

Our best-fitting simulation shows that the lunar mantle ejecta distribution is similar to the butterfly-like pattern of distal ejecta found for craters produced by low-angle impacts [e.g., (16, 31)], while the distribution of mantle ejecta varies according to their original depth (Fig. 6). Note that the “wings” of the butterfly-like ejecta are distributed in cross-range direction (i.e., northwest and northeast of SPA; Fig. 4), consistent with observations of enhanced FeO (37). Assuming a thinner preimpact crust to the southwest of the SPA impact site, as global crustal thickness trends imply, we would expect thicker deposits of mantle material to extend further from the basin to the southwest, while any mantle ejecta should be thinner and remain closer to the basin to the east. This distribution is consistent with the observed distribution of gravity anomalies [supplementary figure 5 of (7); (36)], which have been interpreted to arise from mantle-rich ejecta. These anomalies extend further from the basin beyond the rim to the southwest where the crustal thickness is <50 km, but are only found interior to the rim to the northeast where the crustal thickness reaches 70 km (7, 36).

Although it is not clear if this aspect of the simulations would persist at higher resolution, the excavated mantle material is predicted to be present as tens of kilometer scale discrete bodies, consistent with observed gravity anomalies (7, 36). The total volume of mantle ejecta from our best-fitting simulation, 4.2 × 106 km3, is slightly less than the estimated value from the gravity data [~5 × 106 km3; (36)]. The typical modeled thickness of mantle ejecta of a few kilometers is similarly consistent with the thickness as suggested by the gravity data. Tracking tracers from a depth greater than 35 km to emulate a thinner crust suggests that there would be 1.3 × 107 km3 of mantle ejecta (see fig. S5) consistent with previous estimates from Citron et al. (9). The intuitive result of more mantle ejecta being produced with a thinner crust is consistent with mantle ejecta extending to greater distances to the southwest of SPA where the preimpact crust is thought to be thinner (7, 36).

The distribution of Th-rich material outside the basin can only be reconciled with the expected ejecta distribution (7, 36, 38, 39), if it was not uniformly distributed at the time of impact, as mantle ejecta is expected on both sides of the basin, and material just below the crust is predicted to be the most widely distributed. Th-rich and Ti-poor material distributed in the southwest ejecta of SPA may be material excavated from a late-stage, discontinuous, thin magma ocean between the crust and mantle (7). To the east of the basin, the observation of gravity anomalies from mantle-derived material in the rim region (7, 36), where no Th excess is found, can only be explained if the Th-rich material was absent below the basin in this region, consistent with the proposed discontinuous late-stage magma ocean (7). Although subsequent impact craters and basins have modified the ejecta blanket of SPA, there is no evidence for substantial contamination of the surface compositional signature (7). For instance, the continuous ejecta of the Orientale basin (40, 41) lies largely outside of the mantle-bearing ejecta of SPA (36), and most of the eastern ejecta of SPA is mapped as either Nectarian or pre-Nectarian materials (40, 41), in which impact gardening would have vertically mixed the material to provide a more representative composition.

Our work suggests that NASA’s Artemis III mission, which will send astronauts to the Moon, is likely to sample SPA ejecta, if it lands as planned in the south polar region of the Moon (42). This region is near the south rim of SPA. For the previously assumed south-to-north impact, the Artemis landing region just beyond the postcollapse topographic rim would be devoid of ejecta from the lunar mantle. In contrast, for a north-to-south trajectory, our models predict that the Artemis III mission will land downrange of the impact point assuming our interpretation of a southward trajectory is correct. If a north-to-south impact produced SPA, the Artemis III mission may land within the ejecta deposit that contained excavated mantle material by the SPA-forming impact. Our model indicates that this region has 350-m thick of mantle materials on average, but some locations have up to ~3 km emplaced (see the black circle in Fig. 4A). Much of this material may have been excavated from depths >90 km (Fig. 6). This ejecta lands at high velocity and is expected to mix with crustal material through the process of ballistic sedimentation (43). This material has also undergone billions of years of regolith gardening and mixing. Despite this mixing, samples from south-pole region are likely to contain SPA mantle ejecta. Thus, returned samples from Artemis landing site could constrain the age of SPA in addition to the composition of the Moon’s mantle.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Numerical methods

We performed 3D impact simulations using the iSALE-3D shock physics code (10, 11) including recent improvements to its parallel efficiency and treatment of multiple materials (44). iSALE-3D uses a solver as described in Hirt et al. (45) and includes a strength model (46–48). While the iSALE-3D code solves the energy conservation equation, it is not strictly enforced. To examine the formation of SPA basin and its crustal thickness, we applied 5-km resolution for the high-resolution zone regardless of the impactor size. Our impactor diameter of 230 to 400 km corresponds to resolutions of 23 to 40 cells per projectile radius (CPPR). Note that iSALE has a high-resolution zone and an extension zone, where cells incrementally increase by 3% from the previous cell up to a maximum cell size of 100 km (20 times larger than the cell size in the high-resolution zone). We used a limited high-resolution zone to save the computational resources but simulated the whole target (i.e., Moon) within the extension zone. Our high-resolution zone ranges [−1800 km, 1500 km] for x (horizontal), [0 km, 1650 km] for y (depth), and [−1445 km, 555 km] for z (vertical) direction, respectively. The impact point is the origin (0,0,0) and the oblique impactor travels from positive x to negative x. Note that our simulations assume symmetry in the cross-range direction. We varied the impact angle from 15° to 90°. We also scaled the impactor diameter to form the comparable transient crater sizes as impact angle, and impact velocity were varied [e.g., (49, 50)]. Our fiducial impact velocity is 13 km/s, which is lower than the mean velocity expected for the SPA impactor of 20 km/s (19). We estimated the fiducial impact’s effect on the Moon’s rotation and orbit (see Supplementary Text).

We assumed that the Moon is composed of three different materials—core, mantle, and crust. We used the analytical equation of state (ANEOS) of iron for the core, dunite for the mantle, and granite for the crust, respectively (51–55). We also used iron core and dunite mantle for the differentiated impactors. We varied the impactor radii from 230 to 400 km to produce a transient crater of similar size according to the impact angle and impact velocity (49, 50). For example, we considered a 300-km-diameter impactor for 30° at 10 km/s and a 240-km-diameter impactor for 35° at 13 km/s. The iron core size is fixed at half of the impactor radius, which is on the high end of estimates for the ordinary chondritic parent body (15). This corresponds to 29.2% of the total mass in our best-fitting case. Our assumption of half radius core also corresponds to the mass fraction of iron oxides in chondrites (56–59) and the core fraction of thermally evolved planetesimals (12–14, 60). Our Moon-like target has a radius of 1740 km, an iron core radius of 350 km, a crustal thickness of 50 km, and a mantle layer 1340 km thick. The choice of 50-km-thick crust is motivated by having the crust resolved by 10 computational cells. Our fiducial temperature profile of the target, with a temperature gradient of 50 K/km up to a maximum mantle temperature of 1400 K, follows the best-fit case in previous work 2D simulations (18). This setting corresponds to a lithospheric thickness of 22 km. This high temperature gradient is consistent with an impact early in lunar history when the post-magma ocean cumulates remain warm. Later, after the completion of magma ocean solidification, the equilibrium conductive temperature gradient would have been much lower. Note that a lower thermal gradient and higher mantle temperature may produce similar results (61) and rheologies that include transient weakening like acoustic fluidization could also reduce the thermal gradient needed to reproduce SPA (62). If the Moon has a lower temperature gradient of 30 K/km (a lithospheric thickness of 37 km) and the same mantle temperature of 1400 K, it results in a relatively circular planform with an annulus of thickened crust, which is inconsistent with observation (fig. S7). An annulus of thickened crust was also produced in previous 2D work with this cooler thermal profile (18). Exploring these effects in more detail with 3D simulations could suggest alternative temperature profiles suitable for SPA (63).

To compare with observations, we calculated the crustal thickness and tracked the distribution of mantle ejecta using tracer particles. For the crustal thickness, we first found the surface of the target along a radial line through the middle of each surface cell. Next, we found the crust-mantle interface in the radial direction toward the center of the target. Last, we calculated the distance between these locations considering the material fraction of each cell. Note that we made the crustal thickness zero if the surface material is mantle with no crust underlying it. To compare the crustal thickness with observed data by Andrews-Hanna et al. (7), we considered a basin-centered polar projection; we calculated the distance and angles from the basin center that is located 850 km downrange from the impact site. When we outlined the basin rim, we considered the first location that has a crustal thickness of 50 ± 5 km (where 5 km is the size of one cell) in every 10° in the polar coordinate. In an attempt to avoid isolated islands of high crustal thickness, we ignored locations with less than seven adjacent points that meet this criterion. The arbitrary choice of seven adjacent points works fine for most cases; however, this approach still identifies isolated islands of high crustal thickness as the part of the outline of the basin at times (see fig. S1). For the thickness of mantle ejecta, we used tracer particles to examine the ejecta landing sites. iSALE emplaces one tracer particle in each cell before the impact and tracks their location throughout the impact. Note that we limited the total number of tracer particles to save the computational resources; their initial locations were [−1215 km, 550 km] for x, [0 km, 1000 km] for y, and [−500 km, 260 km] for z directions, respectively. We regarded surficial mantle ejecta as being located within three cells from the surface and above three cells from the crust-mantle boundary to avoid any artificial effect around the boundaries. We averaged the ejecta within a square area of 40 km by 40 km to calculate the ejecta thickness. The spatial resolution of 5-km cell size over the impactor and basin was the highest resolution achievable with the available computational resources. While this resolution is higher than previous 3D simulations [e.g., (8)] and allows us to adequately resolve crustal material, we note that it only affords a resolution of 10 cells over the thickness of the crust and is lower than previous 2D simulations, which used a 1- to 2-km cell size [e.g., (18, 64)]. We did not fully investigate the influence of spatial resolution on our results as a higher resolution was unfeasible, and a lower resolution would not have been sufficient to resolve the crust (see Supplementary Text). The spatial resolution implies a minimum impactor resolution of 26 CPPR. Experience from previous iSALE-2D simulations suggests that this is sufficient to adequately resolve shock pressure decay, crater size, and large-scale structure [e.g., (65)]. While the spatial resolution of the crust is sufficient to resolve the major zone of crustal thinning under the basin, including its planform, we expect that a higher spatial resolution would reveal finer-scale crustal deformation and reduce the ~5-km uncertainty on the crust-mantle boundary position under the basin. We also expect that a higher spatial resolution would result in thinner and more continuous deposits of ejecta and exhumed mantle.
