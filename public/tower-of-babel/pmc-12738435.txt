A cytokine-based serum proteomic score is a prognostic biomarker for immunotherapy in head and neck cancer
Authors: Md Saiful Islam Saif, Kartik Sehgal, Ze Zhang, Rondi A. Butler, John K. Wiencke, Annette M. Molinaro, Julianna Miller, Hannah G. Stolrow, Keisuke Shirai, Lucas A. Salas, Devin C. Koestler, Robert Haddad, Brock C. Christensen, Karl T. Kelsey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Despite advances with anti-programmed death (PD)-1-based immunotherapy for recurrent/metastatic head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (R/M HNSCC), identifying who will benefit from these therapies remains challenging. In a prospective, multicenter study, serum levels of 92 cytokines were measured in pretreatment sera collected on 38 (discovery cohort) and 40 (validation cohort) patients with R/M HNSCC undergoing anti-PD-1-based immunotherapy. A prognostic cytokine score comprised of four cytokines (CXCL1, MCP-4, IL6, CD40) was developed using Cox elastic net modeling within the discovery cohort and showed concordance indices of 0.71 and 0.79 for overall survival (OS) in the discovery and validation cohorts, respectively. High cytokine scores were linked to worse OS (p < 0.01, adjusted HR = 2.07, 95% CI = 1.26–3.41). Further, the cytokine score outperformed established biomarkers including TMB and PD-L1 CPS. Our results suggest that serum cytokine proteomic score may serve as prognostic biomarker for anti-PD-1 therapy in R/M HNSCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-025-04271-1.

Introduction

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) arises in the oral cavity, pharynx, and larynx. In 2025, 72,680 new HNSCC cases and 15,980 deaths are estimated in the USA [1]. Despite multimodality treatment regimens, 40–60% of patients with locoregionally advanced HNSCC experience recurrence and/or distant metastatic disease [2, 3]. The major therapeutic breakthrough of the last decade in the management of recurrent/metastatic (R/M) HNSCC has been anti-programmed death (PD)-1-based immunotherapy [4–6]. Objective response rates in the platinum-refractory setting with nivolumab and pembrolizumab were 13.3 and 14.6% in the CHECKMATE-141 and KEYNOTE-040 trials, respectively [4, 5]. In the KEYNOTE-048 study, response rates varied from 17 to 23% and 36 to 43% depending upon the regimen (monotherapy or combination with chemotherapy) [6]. The four-year overall survival was 15–19% [7]. Notably, patients whose tumors respond to anti-PD-1 therapy-based treatment can have remarkably durable disease control, highlighting the need for robust biomarkers of response and resistance that are available at the time of treatment initiation [5, 6].

The PD-L1 combined positive score (CPS), developed using tissue specimens, is the most utilized and only US FDA-approved companion biomarker for anti-PD-1 therapy in R/M HNSCC. The efficacy and survival outcomes with pembrolizumab monotherapy were higher in subgroups with a higher PD-L1 CPS, leading to FDA approval of pembrolizumab monotherapy only in the PD-L1 CPS ≥ 1 subgroup [6, 8]. Similar results were observed for treatment with pembrolizumab-chemotherapy [8]. PD-L1 expression can be heterogeneous intra-tumorally and have differential expression between two different tumor lesions within the same patient and thus may not accurately reflect the entire tumor biology when evaluated on a single specimen [9–11]. Other tissue-based biomarkers used to predict treatment response include tumor mutational burden (TMB), mismatch repair deficiency, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, and inflammatory gene expression signatures [9]. TMB and T cell-inflamed gene expression profiles have been significantly associated with objective responses [12]. The VIGex gene expression signature platform has been proposed to optimally categorize tumors by RNA sequencing as hot, intermediate-cold, and cold [13]. Non-tumor-based approaches to predict long-term immunotherapy response may include assessment of immune-related adverse effects [14, 15]; however, standardizing clinical scoring of adverse events is challenging. Hence, there remains a need for reliable, noninvasive biomarkers that predict with precision, either robust responses or resistance to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy.

Analysis of the peripheral blood components can provide reliable data on circulating proteins, including circulating tumor-derived products and immune cells. Recent work has reported that thrombocytosis and change in mean platelet volume (MPV) after one round of immunotherapy may predict outcome in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [16]. Circulating blood biomarkers also have been used to better understand the dynamics of cancer-associated immune alterations in response to immunotherapy [11, 17–20]. Increased neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) in pretreatment peripheral blood have been associated with worse clinical outcomes with anti-PD-1 therapy across several cancer types [21–31]. In addition, platelet count and the ratio of platelets to other blood components have been shown to be predictive of outcome in initial standard of care treatment for head and neck cancer [32, 33]. These observations highlight recent work suggesting that tumor-educated platelets may be a biomarker useful for monitoring cancer progression [34, 35]. Further evidence suggests that peripheral blood biomarkers, including routine blood tests [20] exosomes [36], circulating tumor DNA [37], cell-free DNA [38], cell-free RNA [39], may provide important information relevant for assessing immunotherapy response. Pretreatment peripheral levels of cytokines such as IL6 and IL8 have been associated with poor outcomes in NSCLC and melanoma [18, 40–42]. In another study utilizing machine learning in NSCLC, integrated evaluation of changes in peripheral blood cell counts and associated ratios outperformed the predictive ability of PD-L1 expression and TMB for clinical outcomes [43]. The majority of these studies, however, were conducted in patients with other more common cancers where immunotherapy has been available for longer, and to date, there is a paucity of data exploring immune-related biomarkers in R/M HNSCC [44].

To address this gap, we are conducting a prospective, ongoing, multicenter study collecting peripheral blood samples in patients with R/M HNSCC before treatment with anti-PD-1-based immunotherapy [45–47]. In prior work, we generated peripheral blood immune profiles by DNA methylation cytometry to identify pretreatment peripheral immune biomarkers of response to anti-PD-1 therapy [47]. In contrast to flow cytometry where fresh specimens are needed, methylation cytometry can utilize banked samples without requirement for intact cells, providing a substantial advantage in terms of scalability and cost-effectiveness. We hypothesized that platelet-associated biomarkers would be more informative in this setting, since degranulated platelets in sera may enhance the detection of potentially crucial cytokines associated with tumors. Hence, we assessed serum rather than plasma cytokines as prognostic biomarkers of immunotherapy response in this study. Recognizing that predictive strength can be gained through the combination of cytokines, we also developed and validated a cytokine score, benchmarking its prognostic performance against established blood and tissue-based biomarkers used to predict the immunotherapy response in HNSCC.

Materials and methods

Study population

The study population being enrolled in this multicenter prospective study has been previously described [45, 46]. Briefly, eligible patients were 18 years or older with a diagnosis of R/M or advanced incurable HNSCC who were starting a new anti-PD-1 immunotherapy-based regimen. Patients with carcinoma in situ, cutaneous malignancies, and salivary gland cancers were not eligible for study participation. We initially studied 38 patients before treatment initiation (“discovery cohort”), followed by the next 40 patients (also before treatment initiation), hereafter referred to as the “validation cohort.” There were no restrictions on prior lines of therapy. HPV status was evaluated in oropharyngeal cases as standard of care by p16 testing and confirmed by direct HPV testing by RNA in situ hybridization if p16 positive. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards of the participating institutions. All procedures were conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and met all local regulatory requirements.

Serum cytokine measurement

Whole blood and serum were collected from all patients via venipuncture on the same day before the initiation of anti-PD-1-based therapy. Serum samples were analyzed for cytokine levels using the Olink® Target 96 Immuno-Oncology panel, which measured 92 proteins associated with biological processes and immune function and response to diseases, focusing on cancer. Additional information regarding this technology and the 92 proteins profiled on this panel is available at https://olink.com/products-services/target/immune-response-panel/. Annotations related to the Olink nomenclature of the 92 proteins considered in this study are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Data were preprocessed using Olink® NPX Manager software and provided in an arbitrary log2-scaled unit called normalized protein expression (NPX). NPX is used for relative quantification and is defined by normalizing and log2-transforming the CT (cycle threshold) values obtained from the quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). Proteins with ≥ 25% of samples below Olink’s limit of detection (LOD) in either the discovery or validation cohort were excluded, resulting in 82 cytokines for analysis. The LOD is defined as the lowest measurable level of an individual protein calculated as three fixed standard deviations above the average for the negative controls on the plate. More information about LODs is available at https://olink.com/faq/how-is-the-limit-of-detection-lod-estimated-and-handled.

Two of the 38 samples in the discovery cohort and 6 of the 40 samples in the validation cohort profiled using this panel were not successfully detected and thus were excluded from subsequent statistical analysis. These samples failed Olink’s internal quality control (QC) due to low signal intensity or poor overall assay performance. These determinations were made by Olink based on their standard QC criteria for assessing whether samples were successfully detected or failed quality control. After excluding non-detected samples, 36 patients from the discovery cohort and 34 from the validation cohort were available for analysis. Batch effect correction was performed using the Olink_normalization function provided in OlinkAnalyze R package (https://rdrr.io/cran/OlinkAnalyze/src/R/olink_normalization.R). To facilitate the removal of the batch effect, eight samples from the discovery cohort were replicated in the validation cohort following the guidelines of Olink (https://cran.rproject.org/web/packages/OlinkAnalyze/vignettes/bridging_introduction.html). These replicate samples were then utilized during normalization to identify and correct batch-related variation. Supplementary Fig. 1 shows PCA plots across cohorts before and after removing batch effects. Whole blood was utilized for DNA methylation-based cytometry. Detailed descriptions of cytokine measurement, methylation-based cytometry, and clinical assessments—including platelet counts, MPV, TMB, and PD-L1 CPS are available in Supplementary Items 1, 2, and 3, respectively.

Whole-blood DNA methylation assessment and methylation-based cytometry

DNA was extracted using the Zymo Quick DNA Miniprep Plus kit following the manufacturer’s instructions and quantified using a Qubit dsDNA Broad Range Assay kit (Thermo) per the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA was sent to the Avera Institute for Human Genetics for DNA methylation quantification using the Illumina Infinium Methylation EPIC array platform. The resulting data were preprocessed using minfi [48], ENmix [49], and SeSaMe [50] R/Bioconductor packages following previously described steps [51]. After removing poor-quality samples, high-quality methylation data were available for 36 patients in the discovery cohort and 31 patients in the validation cohort.

Methylation-based cell-type deconvolution, also known as methylation cytometry, was performed on each sample to estimate the immune cell profile of the samples [51]. A comprehensive list of the 12 immune cell types and summary measures of their estimates for both discovery and validation cohort samples are provided in Supplementary Table 2. For each sample, deconvolution estimates below the LOD for a given cell type were imputed with the LOD for that specific cell type [52]. Following imputation, deconvolution estimates for each sample were rescaled to sum to one. A complete blood count (CBC) analysis was also conducted on the same whole-blood sample that was used for DNA methylation profiling, allowing us to estimate the cell counts (106 L−1) of each of the 12 immune cell types by taking the product of deconvolution estimate for that cell type (e.g., predicted cell-type proportion) and the total white blood cell count measured for that sample.

Assessment of platelet count and mean platelet volume

CBC, including platelet counts and MPV, were collected from fresh peripheral blood and drawn as part of the standard of care by the institution’s clinical laboratory personnel using automated instrumentation.

Tumor mutational burden and PD-L1 CPS measurements

TMB was obtained through clinically directed comprehensive genomic profiling of patients’ tumor samples via commercially available FoundationOne CDx (Foundation Medicine) or Oncopanel, the Dana Farber /Brigham Cancer Center platform. TMB was reported as the number of mutations per megabase harbored by tumor cells. TMB information was unavailable for 11 patients in the discovery cohort and 23 patients in the validation cohort. PD-L1 CPS was measured by immunohistochemical quantitative analysis using Dako22C3 pharmDxTM companion diagnostic assay at Foundation Medicine or institutional assay at DFCI using E1L3N clone (Cell Signaling Technology). It was reported within a range of 0–100. PD-L1 CPS information was missing for only one patient in the cohort.

Clinical outcomes

Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from the start of anti-PD-1-based therapy to the time of death or censoring. Progression-free survival (PFS) was calculated as the duration from the start of anti-PD-1-based therapy until the occurrence of tumor progression, death, or censoring.

Statistical analyses

Data processing and analysis were conducted using R (v4.3.0). To examine the relationship between the levels of individual cytokines and patient outcomes, we fit a series of Cox proportional hazards (Cox-PH). Multivariable Cox-PH models were adjusted for patient age, sex (female as reference), smoking status (ever versus never, never as the referent group), and Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (0 as reference). The proportionality assumption of the Cox-PH model was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals [53]. Resulting p-values were adjusted for multiple testing by calculating the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) [54]. To identify the top cytokines for predicting survival outcomes (e.g., OS and PFS), Cox elastic net (Coxnet) penalized machine learning models were fit to OS and PFS separately, using all 82 cytokines that withstood quality control filtering. Coxnet models were fit using the glmnet function in R in the glmnet R package [55], and the cv.glmnet function was used to optimize regularization parameters. Based on the optimized regularization parameters, cytokines with nonzero coefficients were identified as the top cytokines. For each model fit, the linear predictor, hereafter referred to as the cytokine score, was calculated based on the cytokines with nonzero coefficients. Prognostic performance was assessed by calculating Harrell’s concordance index (c-index) [56, 57]. Patients were divided into low and high cytokine scores (based on the median score), and survival differences between groups were evaluated using the log-rank test and visualized Kaplan–Meier plots. A flowchart describing the framework of cytokine scores is provided in Supplementary Fig. 2. Differences in platelet count, PD-L1 CPS, and TMB between these two groups were analyzed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Spearman correlations were used to assess the relationship of cytokine scores with platelet count and immune cell count. Additionally, the Spearman correlation between all 82 cytokines and platelet count, immune cell counts, PD-L1 CPS, and TMB, was calculated.

Demographics and clinical characteristics of patients with HNSCC

In the discovery cohort, follow-up times ranged from 27 to 960 days; in the validation cohort, this range was from 21 to 646 days. In the discovery cohort, 14 (39%) of 36 patients died and 28 (78%) of 36 patients experienced either disease progression or death during follow-up. In the validation cohort, 10 (29%) of 34 patients died, and 19 (56%) of 34 patients experienced either disease progression or death. The median platelet count and MPV were comparable in the discovery and validation cohorts (Table 1). Kaplan–Meier Survival curves for OS and PFS in the discovery and validation cohort were similar, except for a longer PFS interval for the validation cohort and an overall longer follow-up duration in the discovery cohort (Supplementary Fig. 3). DNA methylation-based deconvolution estimates of 12 peripheral blood immune cell types with estimated cell counts (cell/μL) in both the discovery and validation cohorts were similar.

Cytokine levels and their association with immunotherapy outcomes

The distributions of cytokine levels for each of the 82 cytokines in the discovery and validation cohorts that passed quality control filtering are displayed in Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5. We examined the correlation between platelet count and cytokine levels since serum is generated after platelet degranulation as part of clot formation (Supplementary Table 2–3). A Spearman correlation greater than 0.30 was observed for 29 cytokines from the discovery cohort and 23 from the validation cohort (12 were overlapping). Similar analyses of 12 immune cell counts and 82 cytokines revealed weak correlations between cytokine levels with all immune cell counts except neutrophil count (Supplementary Table 2–3).

Next, Cox-PH models were applied to each cytokine to examine the association between individual cytokines and patient outcomes. In the discovery cohort, 10 cytokines were nominally significantly associated with OS (unadjusted p < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 4). These included: CXCL1, MCP-4, IL6, CD40, DCN, PD-L1, PTN, Gal-1, CXCL11, and CCL20. While none of these survived adjustment for multiple testing, all had an HR > 1.0, indicating that increased levels were associated with poor overall survival. After adjustment for age, sex, smoking status, and ECOG performance status, CCL20 and CD40.L were statistically significantly associated with OS (nominal p < 0.05); however, again, neither survived adjustment for multiple testing (Supplementary Table 4). In the validation cohort, 14 cytokines, including TNFRSF12A, CXCL13, NCR1, PGF, IL10, IL6, CD4, CXCL9, MMP12, VEGFA, ADA, TNFRSF4, MCP-3, and CSF-1, were nominally significantly associated with OS before adjusting confounders, and all 14 had HR > 1.0 (Supplementary Table 5). After adjustment for confounders, 19 cytokines were significantly associated with OS in the validation cohort; however, none retained significance after FDR correction (Supplementary Table 5).

For PFS in the discovery cohort, only MCP-4 (also known as CCL13) was nominally significantly (unadjusted p < 0.05) associated with PFS (Supplementary Table 6), and no cytokines were significant after adjusting for potential confounders. In the validation cohort, MCP-3 and CXCL1 were significant in univariable models, while MCP-3, CXCL1, CASP8, CRTAM and IL6 were significant in the multivariable analysis of PFS (Supplementary Table 7). However, none of the cytokines remained significant after FDR correction. Across both cohorts, IL6 and CXCL1 repeatedly appeared nominally significant in more than one analysis, either in OS or PFS (Fig. 2). Predicting OS and PFS using a cytokine score with validation across patient cohorts

To evaluate whether the collection of cytokines might improve our prognostic ability, a Coxnet model was fit to the discovery cohort, separately for both OS and PFS. Cytokines identified as predictors for OS included: CXCL1, MCP-4, IL6, and CD40 and for PFS: EGF, ANGPT1, ADGRG1, MCP-1 (also known as CCL2), MCP-4, GZMH, KIR3DL1, MCP-2 (also known as CCL8), Gal-1, CXCL10, CCL23, TNFRSF4, CCL17, PTN, IFNγ, and LAG3. All cytokines selected in the OS models exhibited HR > 1.0, while 5 of the 16 in the PFS models exhibited the opposite direction of association (CXCL10, CCL23, TNFRSF4, CCL17, MCP-1, and IFNγ). The cytokine score calculated for OS and PFS had a c-index of 0.71 and 0.87, respectively, in the discovery cohort. To examine the biological relevance and generalizability of the cytokine score, we next utilized these discovery cohort-trained models to predict validation cohort survival outcomes. In the validation cohort, we observed a c-index of 0.79 for OS and 0.43 for PFS, demonstrating that the cytokine score validated for OS; however, this was not true for PFS.

In an effort to further define the cytokine score, we stratified patients in the discovery cohort into high and low groups based on the median cut-point (7.14) of the validated cytokine score. Patients with a high score had significantly poorer overall survival than patients with a low cytokine score (log-rank p < 0.01, Fig. 3A). This finding also was significant in a univariable Cox-PH considering cytokine as a continuous variable (unadjusted HR = 2.38; 95% CI = (1.31, 4.27)). However, this finding was not statistically significant in a multivariable Cox-PH (adjusted HR = 1.72; 95%CI = (0.93, 3.18)) (Fig. 3A). A similar analysis conducted in the validation cohort, using the same cutoff as the discovery cohort, showed a pattern very similar to that in the discovery group. In the validation cohort, both univariable and multivariable Cox-PH were statistically significant with an unadjusted HR of 2.35 (95% CI = (1.22, 4.52)) and an adjusted HR of 3.81 (95% CI = (1.5, 9.64)) (Fig. 3B). Notably, this significance persisted even after adjusting for platelet count. A sensitivity analysis was conducted to ensure the findings’ robustness by capping the censored time in the discovery group to a maximum of 646 days (consistent with the maximum censored time observed in the validation cohort). This analysis showed results consistent with the use of uncensored data (Fig. 3A and B), demonstrating that differences in follow-up times between the cohorts did not influence the findings.Fig. 3Kaplan–Meier survival analyses stratified by cytokine scores for OS in the discovery and validation cohorts. Prognostic value of the cytokine score compared to established biomarkers

To improve analysis power, we merged the cohorts (totaling of 70 patients) to assess the association of the validated cytokine score with clinical parameters. As expected, univariable and multivariable Cox-PH remained significantly associated with OS, with analysis of an unadjusted HR of 2.31 (p < 0.01, 95% CI = (1.22, 3.51)) and an adjusted HR of 2.07 (p < 0.01, 95% CI = (1.26, 3.41)) (Table 2). Univariable Cox-PH models fit individually exhibited the highest c-index for the cytokine score (0.73) versus platelet count (0.66), TMB (0.62), and PD-L1 CPS (0.56) (Fig. 4A). Multivariable Cox-PH models demonstrated the c-index improved from 0.73 (clinical factors) to 0.78 (adding platelet count) and 0.81 (adding cytokine score) (Fig. 4A). Considering the challenge of the multivariable model due to our sample size, we further analyzed the univariable model of all six covariates and two additional covariates, including neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR). We then adjusted the cytokine score with only one covariate at a time, so each multivariable model had only two covariates (Supplementary Table 8). Similarly, the cytokine score showed a modest association with platelet count (ρ = 0.59; Fig. 5E). Additionally, a separate Spearman analysis comparing the cytokine score with immune cell counts, TMB, PD-L1 CPS, MPV, NLR, and PLR revealed a moderate correlation with neutrophil count (ρ = 0.44), monocyte count (ρ = 0.38), and PLR (ρ = 0.34) (Supplementary Table 9). In contrast, weak correlations were observed with other immune cell types, TMB, PD-L1 CPS, MPV, and NLR. Discussion

Research seeking predictive and prognostic biomarkers of response to immunotherapy in R/M HNSCC has, to date, had modest success, primarily relying upon tumor-associated markers that have significant limitations in clinical application. Going forward, as perioperative anti-PD-1 immunotherapy has become the new standard of care in the curative intent setting for locoregionally advanced disease [58], it remains critical to discover improved biomarkers that can be easily used in the clinic, such as peripheral blood proteins that are reflective of the interaction of the tumor and the immune system. To accomplish this, we analyzed cytokines in serum collected before the administration of anti-PD-1-based immunotherapy using the Olink Immuno-Oncology panel in R/M HNSCC.

Cytokine levels (individual and combinations) were modestly associated with survival outcomes, although the results were inconsistent across the discovery and validation cohorts. However, a Cox elastic net machine learning model based on a combination of cytokines CXCL1, MCP-4, IL6, and CD40 yielded a validated cytokine score that can predict OS, with a high cytokine score associated with poor survival. Our cytokine score outperformed existing immunotherapy response biomarkers, including TMB and PD-L1 CPS, and other potential prognostic biomarkers, including platelet count, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and mean platelet volume MPV [21–23, 30, 31, 47, 59–63] based on the C-Index of Cox-PH analyses.

Several studies have reported that pretreatment serum IL6 in newly diagnosed patients with HNSCC could be a valuable biomarker to predict recurrence and overall survival [64–67]. Recently, a decrease in plasma IL6 level correlated with improved PFS in patients with NSCLC on anti-PD-1 therapy [65]. Low serum levels of IL6 (and VEGF) were associated with better clinical outcomes in patients with HNSCC treated with cetuximab [66]. Interestingly, IL6 has also been suggested to be a potential therapeutic target in immuno-oncology, hoping to diminish its ability to regulate myeloid suppressor cells, which may be a major factor in hampering a positive response [67]. High expression levels of CXCL1 (also known as GRO-alpha) have been linked with advanced stage and worse survival in uterine cervical cancer [68]. Multiple studies have identified CXCL1 as a potential predictive biomarker for patients with NSCLC [69], colorectal cancer [70], and bladder cancer [71]. Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that CXCL1 expression is significantly elevated in HNSCC tissue compared to normal tissue, and high CXCL1 level is associated with poor overall survival outcomes [72]. However, CXCL1 has not been well studied in the immunotherapy setting. MCP-4 has been reported to be overexpressed in ovarian cancer, and its expression level has been associated with prognosis [73]. Elevated serum MCP-4 has been reported to be a prognostic and predictive biomarker for the detection of metastases in colorectal cancer [74]. Moreover, it has also been associated with gastric cancer [75]. CD40, a member of the tumor necrosis factor family, has been established as a reliable tumor marker in various cancers [76, 77] and has emerged as a promising target for immunotherapy [78]. To date, we are unaware of any reports of MCP-4 or CD40 as prognostic biomarkers in immunotherapy.

Notably, all of the cytokines comprising the score (CXCL1, IL6, MCP-4, and CD40) have been found to be elaborated by activated platelets [79–81] or enriched by their presence, as evidenced by the soluble form of the IL6 receptor [82]. These observations provide additional evidence that platelets might give rise to tumor-supportive inflammation [82]. This may reflect the concept of “tumor-educated platelets” where megakaryocytes (the source of platelets) and/or platelets themselves are altered by cancer and cancer-associated molecules, potentially giving rise to novel, tumor-supportive cytokine profiles in the platelet granules. During the ex vivo clotting process, cytokines contained in granules are released into sera, leading to consistently higher concentrations of platelet-related cytokines compared to plasma [83]. We found a modest correlation between platelet count and cytokine levels; specifically, the levels of the four cytokines in the cytokine score (CXCL1, IL6, MCP-4, and CD40) were moderately correlated with the patient’s platelet count. In multivariate analyses of OS, we included platelet counts and found that cytokine scores remained statistically significant. Previous studies showed that platelet counts alone, although associated with cancer risk [84], do not capture the cancer-related reprogramming of megakaryocytes and thrombopoiesis that support tumorigenesis [85]. Our results support the additional predictive value of platelet-related cytokines over platelet counts. Furthermore, consistent with previous studies, we observed that patients with low cytokine scores had a low PLR, which was associated with better survival [21–25]. In contrast, although earlier research reported that lower MPV was associated with poor survival [63], our study did not identify a significant relationship between MPV and survival, and no differences in MPV were observed between low versus high cytokine score groups.

Additionally, pretreatment neutrophil counts showed a moderate positive correlation with the cytokine score, consistent with the known roles of these cytokines in neutrophil recruitment and activation [86–88]. These correlations align with prior reports that elevated circulating IL6 and CXCL1 reflect systemic neutrophilic inflammation [86–88]. In line with the prior literature, we observed that patients with low cytokine scores had lower neutrophil count and NLR and correspondingly better survival [61]. Taken together, our results emphasize the importance of understanding the sources of the cytokines, and our findings could be related to prior observations relating platelet number, PLR, neutrophil count, and NLR as predictors of cancer or cancer response [89, 90].

Our findings have clinical and translational relevance. However, this study has some limitations; our discovery cohort had a relatively small sample size, which may explain why the PFS cytokine score was not validated. Larger sample sizes could potentially identify additional biomarkers as prognostic for the immunotherapy response in HNSCC. It is important to note that the PFS, an outcome in this study, was assessed via investigators using routine clinic follow-up periods and may be biased by clinical schedules. However, as a treatment benefit measure, OS is likely considerably less biased. Additionally, while we adjusted for potential confounders, the sample size in the discovery cohort limited our ability to detect interactions between cytokines and immune cell parameters and reduced the robustness of multivariable models. In addition, serum sample collection was not prespecified in the original study protocol, and the decision to obtain and analyze these samples was made shortly after study initiation. Consequently, several early-enrolled participants were not included in this analysis. While our study focused on developing a noninvasive, blood-based biomarker, we acknowledge that correlating the serum cytokine profile with tumor immune microenvironment features (e.g., TLS (tertiary lymphoid structures), TIL (tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes), or immune activation signatures) would provide valuable insight. However, due to the recurrent/metastatic nature of our cohort, the cost and logistical constraints of this multicenter study, such analyses were beyond our current scope. Future studies combining serum and tumor data will help better understand the biological role of the cytokine score. Additionally, we acknowledge that methylation-based cytometry is an indirect method and may not fully capture the complexity of immune profiling achievable with direct flow Cytometry and Fluorescence-Activated Cell Sorting (FACS). In future studies, we plan to further explore the association between pretreatment blood-derived DNA methylation biomarkers and cytokine scores, survival, and treatment response in patients with R/M HNSCC receiving ICI therapy [91]. Our results emphasize that large-scale biomarker trials, indispensable to evaluate the ultimate diagnostic performance of serum cytokines, are needed to promote translation into the clinical setting.

In conclusion, when taken individually, pretreatment serum cytokine levels were not strongly associated with outcomes with anti-PD-1-based therapy in R/M HNSCC. However, the prognostic cytokine score of OS derived from this study was validated and outperformed established biomarkers. A focus on platelet-related cytokines in serum may yield novel insights and enhance the clinical utility of these proteins as biomarkers of outcomes to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy.
