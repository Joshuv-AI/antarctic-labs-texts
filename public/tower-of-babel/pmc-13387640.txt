“Hallucinations” of artificial intelligence tools misleading patients and families
Authors: Zvi Weizman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLarge Language Models offer significant benefits in healthcare, including guidance for patients and families. One of the risks associated with these tools is the presentation of inaccurate information, known as “Hallucinations.” This study highlights three cases where these tools misled patients and families by providing false information, which could cause harm. Therefore, physicians must review and verify any Artificial intelligence (AI)-generated content, as these tools cannot reliably differentiate between accurate and false information.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) provides significant benefits for pediatric patients and their families. These Large Language Models (LLMs), such as ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, Gemini, Grok, and others, enhance access, personalization, and continuity of care. These tools offer 24/7 support by answering common health questions and advising patients and parents on when to seek medical help. Personalized health applications assist families in managing medication adherence, growth milestones, and developmental progress. AI-powered educational tools deliver age-appropriate health information tailored to specific conditions. Additionally, LLM chatbots can help identify developmental delays or health issues early through screening tools and predictive analytics, potentially enabling earlier interventions and improved outcomes. These technologies also support care coordination by helping families navigate complex healthcare systems, and ensuring vital health information remains accessible across different providers and settings.[1]

However, these tools carry serious risks. One key concern is the chance of providing incorrect health information. AI chatbots can “hallucinate,” generating false or misleading data that appears accurate and logical but is wrong, made up, or lacking evidence. Examples include fabricated facts, fake citations, nonexistent people or groups, and inaccurate statistics.[2]

When patients and families rely on these tools for health-related information, hallucinations pose serious risks, such as spreading misinformation that can lead to harmful medical decisions, causing unnecessary anxiety or false reassurance, and eroding trust in healthcare resources. This issue is especially critical because AI-generated misinformation often sounds convincing, making it particularly dangerous for anxious parents seeking quick answers or for teenagers who may prefer anonymous online advice over speaking directly with healthcare providers.[3] This report presents three cases where patients and families were misled by LLM chatbots. By sharing these examples, our report warns clinicians and patients about the dangers of this problem. It aims to raise awareness and emphasizes the importance of verifying any AI-provided information with other sources rather than accepting it as the absolute truth.

Case Descriptions

Case 1

A 16-year-old boy with recurring abdominal pain traveled to China during a routine follow-up. While abroad, he experienced a severe episode of abdominal pain and consulted a local alternative medicine practitioner, who prescribed an herbal remedy containing Aristolochia (birthwort) extract. Unsure about the treatment, the patient asked a popular commercial chatbot for guidance, which reassured him that the remedy was safe and harmless. The therapy was stopped after 3 days due to no improvement and the onset of severe nausea. When he returned home, the patient discovered that Aristolochia contains aristolochic acid, a potent nephrotoxin associated with irreversible kidney damage and even acute kidney failure. It has also been linked to cancer. This herb is banned in many countries because of its toxicity.[4]

Case 2

The parents of a 14-year-old girl with cerebral palsy and multiple orthopedic deformities in her left leg were seeking information about a specialized orthopedic surgeon capable of performing single-event multilevel surgery (SEMLS). In complex cases with several anatomical issues, such as the hip, knee, and ankle, it is better to treat all problems in one surgical session to reduce the number of surgeries and have only one recovery period. These procedures are complex and need teams with extensive experience in each part of the surgical plan.[5] The parents used a popular commercial LLM chatbot to find a reputable surgical center worldwide with the necessary expertise. However, verification showed that the surgical center, institution, and named surgeons were completely fabricated.

Case 3

This case involved a potential acetaminophen overdose caused by incorrect medication recommendations from an LLM. A healthy 7-year-old boy developed an acute upper respiratory infection with a high fever, runny nose, and significant nasal congestion. His mother, a single parent with limited healthcare access in a rural area, used a commercially available LLM chatbot for treatment advice. The AI suggested using multiple over-the-counter medications simultaneously without recognizing duplicate active ingredients. As a result, the child received several acetaminophen-containing products, leading to a total daily dose that exceeded the recommended maximum for children and posed a serious risk of liver toxicity. Fortunately, the medications were stopped after 2 days without any negative effects.

Discussion

The rapid development of artificial intelligence, especially large language models, is changing healthcare with both exciting opportunities and serious dangers. One of the biggest risks is the production of false or inaccurate medical information, which directly threatens patient safety. This report highlights three cases where popular commercial LLM-based chatbots misled patients, and their families with fake medical content.

LLM hallucinations are an inherent feature of current model architectures, arising from their probabilistic design, and training methods rather than being a correctable flaw. As one researcher has noted, hallucinations are a part of LLM design, not a bug.[6] Nonetheless, ongoing research aims to find strategies for reducing hallucination rates.[7] Physicians must therefore critically assess any AI-generated clinical content, as these systems cannot reliably distinguish between accurate and fabricated output. Responsible deployment of AI in healthcare requires clear disclaimers to users, explicit advice to verify AI-generated information with qualified professionals, and ongoing monitoring and auditing of AI outputs for accuracy.[8]

The increasing research on AI hallucinations in medicine is vital for raising awareness among doctors and patients about the dangers of blindly relying on LLM outputs in clinical settings. Because healthcare involves high stakes and little room for error, these failures need careful investigation. Recent efforts have begun to measure these risks more precisely. One study evaluated six popular LLMs using 300 clinical vignettes created by physicians and found hallucination rates as high as 83% when no safety measures were implemented; interestingly, the models not only accepted false information but also often expanded on it, offering confident explanations for nonexistent medical conditions. Importantly, the same study showed that even a simple intervention, like a single alert prompt warning the model that its input might be inaccurate, nearly halved hallucination rates, demonstrating the practical value of prompt engineering as a safety tool in clinical settings.[9] On a larger scale, a global survey of clinicians revealed that 91.8% had personally encountered medical hallucinations, and 84.7% believed they could harm patients.[10] These findings highlight the urgent need to widely share this evidence so that both clinicians and patients can approach AI-generated medical information with the critical thinking it requires.

This manuscript is limited by the small number of cases presented. Nonetheless, these three instances serve as a valuable reminder for clinicians, patients, and families about the real dangers posed by AI hallucinations, how to recognize them, and how to guard against misinformation by cross-referencing AI-generated content with established sources. The medical literature on AI hallucinations has addressed their mechanisms, classifications, and potential risks, but it remains largely theoretical, relying on benchmark evaluations and simulated scenarios rather than documented patient encounters. This creates a critical gap: theoretical frameworks explain the phenomenon broadly but provide little actionable guidance for clinicians and patients based on actual clinical experience. Authentic case reports help fill this void by showing how AI-generated misinformation appears in practice, assisting physicians in identifying similar situations, guiding the development of practical safeguards, and raising awareness among healthcare providers and the public. In this way, real-world case documentation functions similarly to adverse event reporting in pharmacovigilance, turning isolated incidents into shared knowledge that can lead to systemic improvements in patient safety. More clinical research is needed to develop practical guidance for clinicians and patients on how to navigate and reduce the risks of AI hallucinations in healthcare.
