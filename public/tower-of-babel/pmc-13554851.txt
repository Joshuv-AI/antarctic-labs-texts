Fixing the Valve but Not the Flow
Authors: Niti Dalal, Aabha Divya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: aortic regurgitation, aortic root thrombosis, left ventricular assist device, mechanical circulatory support, transcatheter aortic valve replacement

Long-term therapy with durable left ventricular assist devices (LVADs) has transformed advanced heart failure from a terminal condition into a long-term, device–patient partnership between device and patient. However, this progress has introduced new complications at the interface between the continuous pump flow and the heart's native anatomy. The case by Stegmann et al1 is compelling because it demonstrated that aortic regurgitation (AR), aortic root thrombosis, previous cardiac surgery, and an inaccessible outflow graft are interdependent consequences of a complex LVAD physiology. The authors report an LVAD patient with significant AR in whom transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) was unsuitable due to an enlarged annulus and aortic thrombus, leading to a hybrid strategy involving thrombus removal, an innovative temporary balloon occlusion of the outflow tract, and surgical aortic valve replacement.1 This case highlights that we can replace the aortic valve more readily than we can correct the abnormal flow state that damaged it. Without attention to that flow state, valve replacement risks becoming a technically successful but physiologically incomplete solution.

The central lesson of this case is that the aortic root remains a clinically active structure during continuous-flow LVAD support. It is tempting to conceptualize the LVAD as bypassing the native left ventricle and aortic valve sufficiently to make these structures secondary. This view is increasingly untenable. Aortic insufficiency is common during continuous-flow support and is associated with clinical deterioration and adverse outcomes.2 This is because the aortic valve is exposed to altered transvalvular pressure, reduced opening, stasis, and recurrent mechanical stress. Therefore, current guidelines recommend intervention once AR becomes more than mild and hemodynamically significant.3

The second lesson is that AR becomes far more complex when it coexists with thrombus. In the reported case, a dynamic thrombus extended from the noncoronary sinus into the ascending aorta. Aortic root thrombosis is found in roughly 5% of LVAD recipients and is characteristically associated with the noncoronary cusp and a persistently closed aortic valve.4 The presence of a thrombus with significant AR changes the treatment algorithm. Transcatheter therapy, although an attractive option to fix AR, should not be reflexive. TAVR is increasingly appealing for high-risk LVAD patients with AR, particularly when redo sternotomy carries substantial risk. However, in this case, the patient's enlarged annulus and thrombus made TAVR unsafe because of the risks of device migration, paravalvular leakage, and embolization. Experience with pure native AR in LVAD recipients confirms that noncalcified valves, annular dilation, and limited anchoring remain important challenges.5 Dedicated regurgitation valves, such as the JenaValve Trilogy, are promising, as shown in the ALIGN-AR (Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation with the Trilogy Valve for Symptomatic Native Aortic Regurgitation) trial,6 and could spare selected LVAD patients the need for a redo operation.7 The authors' decision to proceed with redo surgical aortic valve replacement was an application of risk stratification. Formal guidelines and expert consensus documents support structured management of aortic valve disease in mechanical circulatory support, but they cannot substitute for anatomy-specific decision-making in cases of this complexity.

The report's most practical contribution is its hybrid operative strategy. Because the device had been implanted through a lateral thoracotomy with the outflow graft anastomosed to the descending aorta, the graft could not be reached for clamping, and an unclamped graft would fill the ventricle the instant the pump stops. Rather than dissecting it, the authors occluded the graft with a balloon passed retrograde from the femoral artery. This is simple, fast, and reproducible, and it deserves to become a default option in the redo toolkit whenever an LVAD has been placed by lateral thoracotomy. Equally important was the sequence of thrombus management. The authors used brief circulatory arrest, unilateral antegrade cerebral perfusion, retrograde cardioplegia, aortotomy, direct removal of wall-adherent thrombus, subsequent antegrade cardioplegia through cleared coronary ostia, and only then standard surgical valve replacement.

Finally, of note is that the patient avoided neurological adverse events and was discharged to rehabilitation but suffered an early bioprosthetic valve thrombosis 1 week after surgery. This outcome is clinically important because it suggests that correcting AR does not necessarily normalize the abnormal root hemodynamics induced by continuous-flow support. Surveillance after intervention should therefore extend beyond assessment of paravalvular leak or valve gradients. It should include aortic valve opening, root washout, recurrent thrombus formation, anticoagulation adequacy, right ventricular loading, and the broader flow geometry created by the outflow graft. Computational work has shown that graft geometry can alter aortic wall flow patterns and promote thrombogenicity.8 That disturbed flow field does not disappear simply because a prosthetic valve has been seated. Early antithrombotic management and scheduled computed tomography surveillance should therefore be considered, with a low threshold for recurrent valve or root thrombosis.

This raises a subtle but important question: if replacing the valve doesn't fix the flow issue that caused the damage, is valve replacement truly a durable solution for these patients? For many, it remains the most practical option, and conservative management of recurrent thrombosis was reasonable in this case. However, the underlying problem where an outflow-driven flow field causing both regurgitation and thrombosis, must be addressed more ambitiously. Strategies should include preserving aortic valve opening, individualized pump-speed management, careful assessment of outflow graft orientation, and, when appropriate, transplantation. Although this case will not alter guidelines, it contributes a reproducible bailout technique and, importantly, demonstrates that, in the LVAD aorta, the next challenge and the elegant solution can be the same event.

Ultimately, Stegmann et al1 remind us that mature LVAD medicine is no longer only about pump durability. It is also about the evolving biology of the native heart and great vessels under artificial flow. The once bypassed aortic valve, by the logic of mechanical support, has re-emerged as a determinant of outcomes. The aortic root, which was assumed to be passive, may become thrombogenic. And the outflow graft, placed to avoid one operative risk, may shape the risks of the next operation. This case is not just a report of a successful redo of a surgical aortic valve replacement; it also strongly advocates for personalized, imaging-guided, hybrid approaches to managing late LVAD complications.
