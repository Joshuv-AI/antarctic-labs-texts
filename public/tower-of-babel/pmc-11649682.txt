Prognostic accuracy of blood cell count ratios in predicting adverse outcomes in crush syndrome patients
Authors: Taner Karlidag, Olgun Bingol, Baran Sarikaya, Omer Halit Keskin, Atahan Durgal, Guzelali Ozdemir
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rationale behind this study was the lack of an accurate predictor to facilitate treatment management and anticipate prognosis in crush syndrome. Our research aimed to investigate the following: (1) Is there a correlation between the ratios of blood cell counts upon admission and the adverse outcomes of crush syndrome? and (2) if such a correlation exists, what are the precise thresholds of blood cell count ratios for predicting these adverse outcomes? In this single-centered and retrospective design study, we analyzed all patients admitted to our hospital after Kahramanmaras Earthquake. The data on length of stay in the ICU and mortality status were obtained using our hospital system. Ratios of blood cell count were calculated and recorded at the time of admission. The NLR values in admission were significantly higher in patients with amputation, patients who received intensive care unit (ICU), and patients who died. (p < 0.001, p < 0.001 and p = 0.001, respectively). The MLR values were significantly increased in patients who underwent amputation and patients who received ICU (p < 0.001 and p = 0.003, respectively). NAR values in admission were significantly higher in patients with amputation, patients who received intensive care unit (ICU), and patients who died (p < 0.001, p < 0.001, and p = 0.002, respectively). In addition, according to binary logistic regression analysis, with a CLR value of > 109.844, the risk of amputation increases 8.188 fold; with a CLR value of > 64.518, the possibility of ICU admission requirement increases 10.117 fold, and with a CLR value of > 116.00, the risk of death increases 5.519 fold. Ratios of blood cell count such as NLR, MLR, NAR, and CLR offer a reasonable prognostic accuracy in predicting adverse outcomes and mortality in patients with crush syndrome. Therefore, for better disaster management in the future, the determination of these values at admission should be used as an adjunct tool for predicting prognosis in patients with crush syndrome.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-82035-0.Keywords: Earthquake, Crush syndrome, Mortality, Prognosis, Blood cell count ratios, PredictorSubject terms: Orthopaedics, Trauma

Introduction

Earthquakes are catastrophic natural disasters that have a global impact, causing numerous fatalities and injuries each year within a short period1,2. In contrast to other types of catastrophic events, an earthquake is characterized by the significant dissipation of energy it involves3. It is primarily mechanical energy; therefore, most victims suffer crush injuries4. Furthermore, collapsed buildings prolong extrication time and increase the severity of crush injuries.

Crush syndrome, a prevalent cause of mortality following seismic events, is characterized by systemic manifestations resulting from muscle crush injuries. These injuries may arise due to the immediate effects of physical trauma or ischemia-reperfusion injury, which occurs as a consequence of prolonged compression affecting various regions of the body5. Crush syndrome can result in mortality due to complications such as acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and multiple organ failure syndrome (MODS)6. The mortality rate for crush syndrome patients requiring dialysis treatment has been documented to exceed 40%7. Research indicates that the incidence of crush syndrome among earthquake victims is estimated to range from 2 to 15%8. While the overall occurrence of crush syndrome in all trauma patients has been documented within the same range, it can escalate to as high as 30% specifically in the context of earthquake-related injuries9. Furthermore, the high-energy impact leads to increased pressure within the muscle compartments, restricting blood flow and causing a condition known as acute compartment syndrome (ACS)10. Crush injuries predominantly affect the extremities and are often associated with ACS11. In managing this patient population, interventions such as fasciotomy or amputation may be necessary.

Various blood cell count ratios have drawn considerable attention over the past decade. These biomarkers are decisively linked to systemic inflammation resulting from various types of fractures, polytrauma, carcinomas, and a wide array of musculoskeletal disorders. Their diagnostic and prognostic significance is critical across multiple surgical fields. The C-reactive protein to Lymphocyte Ratio (CLR) has been associated with mortality rates following displaced femoral neck fractures and serves as a predictive marker for periprosthetic joint infection12,13. The neutrophil-to-albumin ratio (NAR) has emerged as a prognostic biomarker after traumatic brain injury and is correlated with early mortality in patients with hip fractures14,15. The monocyte-lymphocyte ratio (MLR) has been identified as a predictor of 30-day mortality following hip fractures and functions as a prognostic tool for patients with various types of malignancies16,17. Furthermore, the Neutrophil to Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR) has been linked to adverse outcomes, including amputation and early mortality in patients requiring urgent revascularization due to acute limb ischemia. It has also been associated with in-hospital mortality during the acute phase of severe trauma18,19. Previous research has explored the relationship between crush syndrome and mortality and other adverse outcomes, highlighting certain electrolyte imbalances and trauma scores20,21. To our knowledge, no study has yet investigated the relationship between these blood cell count ratios and adverse outcomes following crush syndrome. The motivation for this study stems from the absence of a reliable predictor to enhance treatment management and forecast outcomes in cases of crush syndrome, which is characterized by a diverse range of prognostic possibilities.

Our research was designed to explore the following objectives: (1) To determine whether a correlation exists between the ratios of blood cell counts upon admission and the adverse outcomes associated with crush syndrome; and (2) If such a correlation is identified, to ascertain the specific thresholds of blood cell count ratios that can effectively predict these adverse outcomes.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a single-center retrospective review of all patients admitted to our hospital after the Kahramanmaras Earthquake between February 6, 2023, and April 5, 2023. Our institutional review board (University of Health Science Ankara Bilkent City Hospital Ethical Committee) approved the design and protocol of the current study (E1-23-3402). All researchers have signed the latest version of the Helsinki Declaration. Informed consent forms were obtained from all the patients assigned to the study. This study is reported based on Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE)22.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

A total of 812 patients were admitted to our hospital in the aftermath of the earthquake. This study encompassed all earthquake victims seeking medical intervention at our facility and receiving a crush syndrome diagnosis. The exclusion criteria applied were (1) patients who were not diagnosed with crush syndrome, (2) initially admitted to another institution, (3) patients with vascular injury requiring surgical intervention, (4) patients with active infection, and (5) patients with missing data (Fig. 1).

Patient care and surgical interventions

Following the devastating event, a considerable number of patients from the earthquake region were admitted to our facility, which serves as a tertiary referral center. The majority of the admitted individuals presented with compromised general health and compartment syndrome caused by advanced crush syndrome. Crush syndrome, characterized by a combination of crush injury and systemic symptoms such as shock, acidosis, and ARF23, was diagnosed by internal medicine specialists. Our internal medicine department implemented preventive measures, mainly focusing on addressing traumatic rhabdomyolysis and ARF during regular patient visits. Patients who developed ARF were evaluated for the need for hemodialysis treatment.

Urgent fasciotomy has become the standard procedure for managing ACS. However, in cases where fasciotomy is contraindicated, such as when extrication time is prolonged, limb sensation is lost, and there is no active movement24, primary amputations were also performed, taking into account the risk of rhabdomyolysis and infections, mainly due to delayed fasciotomy interventions24–26. The decision to proceed with primary amputation was made based on the surgeon’s experience and consultation with cardiovascular surgery, especially in life-threatening sepsis cases where limb salvage was not feasible25. Infectious disease specialists were consulted, and appropriate antibiotic therapies were administered to patients. The open wounds of the patients were treated with the cooperation of the Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery Department. In cases where infection could not be adequately controlled despite multiple debridements and antibiotic therapy, or when achieving sufficient blood circulation was not possible, amputations were carried out at the proper level in collaboration with divisions of infectious disease and cardiovascular surgery. Patients with worsening general health were closely monitored and treated in the intensive care unit (ICU).

Data collection

Demographic data of the patients, such as age, sex, and comorbidities, were recorded. The data on length of stay in the ICU and mortality status were obtained using our hospital system. For the purposes of this study, we defined prolonged ICU stay as an ICU length of stay of 10 days, which is consistent with previously reported literature27 All blood results, including inflammatory mediators, were collected at the time of admission. Ratios of blood cell count such as NLR, MLR, C-reactive protein (CRP) to Lymphocyte ratio (CLR), and Neutrophile to Albumin ratio (NAR) were calculated and recorded. To ensure impartiality, an autonomous research aide meticulously documented all data about the ongoing study.

Outcome of interest

The primary outcome of this study is to reveal the influence of blood parameters at the time of admission on the adverse consequences of crush syndrome. For this purpose, patients were examined in three main headings according to their prognosis: (1) mortality, (2) extremity amputation, and (3) ICU admission requirement. To give a cut-off value in blood parameters that affect these three consequences using the ROC (Receiver Operating Characteristics) curve and a multivariate logistic regression analysis as a secondary outcome.

Statistics

The statistical analysis was carried out using the SPSS version 22.0 software program. Categorical variables were represented by percentage and frequency values, while numerical variables were described using mean, standard deviation, and min-max values.

The quantitative data did not display a normal distribution based on the Shapiro-Wilk Test, so non-parametric test procedures were employed. The Mann-Whitney U Test and Spearman’s Rho Correlation Test were utilized to examine the relationships between parameters. The chi-square test was applied to analyze categorical data. A multivariate logistic regression analysis model, with final diagnosis as the outcome variable, was used to assess the effects on the ROC curve of a combination of diagnostic variables. ROC curves were calculated for each of the available diagnostic tests together with the corresponding area under the curve values (AUC), which were compared as per Delong et al. (1988) for calculation of the Standard Error of the AUC and of the difference between two AUCs. Youden’s index was utilized to identify the optimal diagnostic threshold point for the NLR, MLR, CLR, and NAR ratios.

The results were evaluated within the 95% confidence interval, and statistical significance was considered for p < 0.05.

Results

After exclusion criteria were applied, the remaining 94 patients ( 45 male and 49 female), with a mean age of 38.17 ± 15.3 years (range 18–85 years), were included in the study. During the follow-ups, a total of 21 patients (22.3%) underwent amputation of any extremity, 54 patients (57.4%) were received to the ICU, and 13 patients (13.8%) died (Fig. 1). The number of patients who received fasciotomy due to acute compartment syndrome was fifty-seven. The assessment of the patient’s blood parameters was conducted categorically across three distinct prognosis groups: (1) patients who underwent amputation compared to those who did not, (2) patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) versus those who were not, and (3) patients who were deceased in comparison to those who survived.

Amputation

There was no significant difference in age and sex between the patient group with and without amputation (p = 0.229 and p = 0.284, respectively). The NLR, MLR, CLR, and NAR were significantly higher in patients who underwent amputation (p < 0.001, p < 0.001, p = 0.011, and p < 0.002, respectively). ROC analysis calculated the cut-off NLR value for amputation as 7.005 for this group. The area under the curve (AUC) was 0.817 with 0.857 sensitivity and 0.667 specificity (Fig. 2A). The MLR cut-off value was 0.566, AUC was 0.792, and sensitivity and specificity were 0.810 and 0.700, respectively (Fig. 2B). The cut-off CLR value was 109.844 for amputation. The AUC was 0.750 with 0.583 sensitivity and 0.848 specificity (Fig. 2C). The NAR cut-off value was 0.453, the AUC was 0.894, and sensitivity and specificity were 0.857 and 0.867, respectively (Fig. 2D). ICU admission

Table 2 represents detailed information on patients with ICU admission. During the follow-up period, no significant difference was determined between the groups regarding age and gender when comparing patients who received ICU care with those who did not require ICU admission (p = 0.668 and p = 0.950, respectively). NLR, MLR, CLR, and NAR were statistically significantly higher in ICU patients (p < 0.001, p = 0.003, p = 0.001, and p < 0.001, respectively). We also compared the patients who received ICU regarding the length of stay in ICU, and NLR, MLR, and NAR were statistically significantly higher in patients with prolonged ICU stay (p = 0.003, p = 0.001, and p = 0.032, respectively). Interestingly, total protein and albumin values were also significantly higher in patients with prolonged ICU stay (p = 0.020 and p = 0.006, respectively). ROC analysis calculated the cut-off NLR value for ICU admission requirement as 7.078 for this group. The AUC was 0.750 with 0.722 sensitivity and 0.750 specificity (Fig. 3A). The MLR cut-off value for ICU admission requirement was 0.497, with 0.682 AUC, and sensitivity and specificity were 0.704 and 0.625, respectively (Fig. 3B). The cut-off CLR value was 64.518 for amputation. The AUC was 0.766 with 0.833 sensitivity and 0.632 specificity (Fig. 3C). The NAR cut-off value was 0.447, the AUC was 0.769, and sensitivity and specificity were 0.574 and 0.875, respectively (Fig. 3D).

Mortality

There was no difference in age and gender between deceased and control patients (p = 0.218 and p = 0.894, respectively). NLR, CLR, and NAR were statistically significantly higher in patients with mortality (p = 0.001, p = 0.021, and p = 0.002, respectively) (Table 3). According to ROC analysis, the cut-off NLR value was 10.651 for the mortal group. The AUC was 0.779 with 0.769 sensitivity and 0.728 specificity (Fig. 4A). The cut-off CLR value was 116.00, AUC was 0.736, and sensitivity and specificity were 0.700 and 0.778, respectively (Fig. 4B). The NAR cut-off value was 0.518 for the mortal group. The AUC was 0.767 with 0.692 sensitivity and 0.790 specificity (Fig. 4C). The mortality statistics are presented in Table 3.

Binary logistic regression analysis

Table 4 presents comprehensive data pertaining to the binary logistic regression analysis. The independent variables NLR, MLR, CLR, and NAR were evaluated in relation to three prognostic categories within a logistic regression model. A stepwise methodology was employed to develop this model, which assessed the likelihood of amputation, the necessity for ICU admission, and mortality. Notably, among all assessed variables, only CLR exhibited a significant effect on enhancing the model’s explanatory power. The result of the analysis showed an odds ratio of 8.188 (95% confidence interval 1.39–48.17) for the association of amputation and CLR, 10.117 (95% confidence interval 1.19 to 85.93) for the relation of ICU admission requirement and CLR, and, 5.519 (95% confidence interval 1.01–30.13) for the association of mortality and CLR. These results showed that with a CLR value of > 109.844, the risk of amputation increases 8.188 fold; with a CLR value of > 64.518, the possibility of ICU admission requirement increases 10.117 fold, and with a CLR value of > 116.00, the risk of death increases 5.519 fold.

Discussion

The findings of this study indicate that the CLR value serves as a highly reliable predictive indicator for amputation, the necessity for ICU admission, and mortality in patients with crush syndrome. Additionally, NLR, MLR, and NAR values have been identified as statistically significant predictors of adverse outcomes related to crush syndrome.

Neutrophils serve as the primary agents in the innate immune system28. Upon activation, they initiate the release of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which possess potent antimicrobial and immune-modulating properties. However, excessive local concentrations of NETs can cause substantial tissue damage, resulting in subsequent organ dysfunction29. Lymphocytes play a crucial role in regulating a proper inflammatory response, and their decrease due to apoptosis can lead to an inconvenient state of inflammation. Zahorec et al.30 reported that the NLR value might indicate a patient’s response to an inflammatory insult. When faced with stress, neutrophil levels increase in response, but when the stress becomes overwhelming, it induces apoptosis in lymphocytes30.

A recent observational cohort study demonstrated that NLR measured upon admission to the ICU was associated with mortality in patients without sepsis31. Coelho et al. reported that elevated preoperative NLR was associated with early mortality and amputation in patients with acute limb ischemia18. Karadeniz et al. concluded that a high NLR value was a significant risk factor for one-year mortality in elderly patients operated for hip fractures32. Many recent studies demonstrated that NLR value emerged as an independent predictor for postoperative complications and mortality in non-orthopedic surgeries33,34. The current study found that the NLR value was significantly higher in patients with adverse outcomes following crush syndrome.

Monocytes play a vital role in the innate immune system and serve as a connecting link between the adaptive immune system by presenting antigens to lymphocytes35. The MLR has been proposed to be related to the survival rate in patients with malignancy10. Moreover, Bingol et al. reported that the admission MLR is valuable data for predicting mortality in geriatric hip fracture patients24. In this study, the admission MLR value was statistically correlated with adverse outcomes such as amputation, ICU admission requirement, and prolonged ICU stay.

The serum albumin levels indicate both the nutritional status and organ function, wherein inflammation causes a decrease in hepatic albumin secretion and increases the generation of pro-inflammatory substances36,37. The biological processes of albumin have yet to be comprehensively defined. However, its association with shorter hospital stays, decreased mortality, and complication rates for patients with acute conditions is well-established38. Several recent studies have proposed using the NAR value as a predictor of prognosis39,40. For example, Hu et al. concluded that elevated neutrophil percentage to albumin ratio (NPAR) values were correlated with an increased risk of early mortality in patients with sepsis37. The study found that elevated NAR values were linked with adverse outcomes in patients with crush syndrome, consistent with the literature.

CRP is an acute-phase protein synthesized by the liver, and its levels increase in response to inflammation. The inverse relationship between elevated CRP levels and a decrease in lymphocyte cell count during inflammation may account for the high diagnostic value of CLR. Recent studies on CLR have predominantly focused on its prognostic implications for various cancers and its potential role in disease progression of COVID-1941,42. However, no research has explored the relationship between CLR and crush syndrome. This study found that the elevated admission CLR value correlated with amputation, ICU admission requirement, and mortality. Moreover, according to binary logistic regression analysis, with a CLR value of > 109.844, the risk of amputation increases 8.188 fold; with a CLR value of > 64.518, the possibility of ICU admission requirement increases 10.117 fold; with a CLR value of > 116.00, the risk of death rises 5.519 fold.

This study acknowledges several limitations. First, it employed a retrospective design; therefore, the findings necessitate verification through randomized controlled trials. Furthermore, this design introduces the possibility of selection bias regarding patient selection by the authors. To mitigate such bias, stringent inclusion and exclusion criteria were established. Second, the study was conducted at a single center and involved a relatively small sample size. However, to ensure standardization, it was essential to exclude patients who had initially sought treatment at different healthcare institutions. Third, our evaluation was confined to short-term outcomes following crush syndrome. Consequently, we lacked access to data regarding patients who may have experienced adverse events post-discharge. Lastly, within the study cohort, it was not possible to completely differentiate between three distinct adverse outcomes experienced by patients.

Conclusion

Ratios of blood cell count such as NLR, MLR, NAR, and CLR offer a reasonable prognostic accuracy in predicting adverse outcomes and mortality in patients with crush syndrome. Additionally, the study identified the CLR ratio as an independent predictor, establishing specific thresholds for predicting adverse outcomes in patients diagnosed with crush syndrome. To enhance disaster management practices in the future, the assessment of these values upon patient admission should be employed as an auxiliary instrument for prognostic prediction in individuals suffering from crush syndrome. However, further studies involving larger cohorts are necessary to validate these ratios in the future.
