Investigating outcomes of coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery: a single-center retrospective study in Jordan
Authors: Emad Hijazi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery brings significant cardiac benefits but also risks post-operative complications, including cognitive impairments and infections.Materials & methodsWe analyzed 108 patient records between 2011 and 2021 at the Princess Muna Al-Hussein Cardiac Center, Irbid, Jordan. The primary endpoint was the occurrence of postoperative events.ResultsFew patients (8.3%) faced complications like stroke and infections, with age and ICU stay length as significant risk factors. Patients with complications often experienced left ventricular impairments and required inotropic support.ConclusionThe study highlights the need for understanding CABG surgery complications to improve outcomes, emphasizing preventive care and personalized follow-up.Keywords: Coronary artery bypass graft, CABG, postoperative complications, infection, Jordan

Introduction

Coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) is considered as a first line treatment for multivessel disease and in left main coronary artery disease [1]. It is a commonly performed surgery since it has established a significant improvement in these patients in terms of cardiac function and coronary vascularization [2]. CABG can prolong survival in patients suffering from stable coronary disease, while percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) cannot. PCI is more focused towards managing flow-limiting lesions, while CABG limits new infarcts by ensuring blood flow distal to occluding lesions [3]. Although there has been decline in cardiac revascularization, over 200,000 patients are undergoing CABG in the United States every year. Still, CABG is a high-risk procedure with a 30-day morbidity rate of 14%. Almost 14% of Medicare post-CABG patients are readmitted within 30 days of discharge [4]. Despite the low mortality rate, CABG is associated with post-operative cognitive impairments such as delirium and other complications that could make daily activities difficult [5,6].

There are several postoperative complications, most of them are responsible for emergency patient visits and readmissions including postoperative infections, congestive heart failure, and chest discomfort. Postoperative infections include surgical site wound infections, pneumonia, urinary tract infections, and deep sternal wound infections, which include mediastinitis, pericarditis, and myocarditis [4]. Post-operative complications of CABG may affect many systems other than the cardiovascular system [4]. Mediastinitis is one of these infections, and it has higher incidence in patients with diabetes and higher body mass index [7]. Infection at the site of surgery is another important outcome to pay attention to [8]. Risk factors for developing these outcomes are more likely in cases of diabetes mellitus, frequent central venous catheters, and body mass index [9]. Patients should be followed up upon discharge to prevent the incidence of such events.

Perioperative morbidity and mortality have been linked to three categories of risk factors: patient characteristics, provider characteristics, and postoperative factors. Patient characteristics include age, female sex, African American ethnicity, higher body surface area, and previous myocardial infarctions within one week. Also, comorbidities including femoral/popliteal disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), congestive heart failure, calcified ascending aorta, and others. As for the provider-related factors, they include surgeon volume (less than 100 cases a year). Postoperative factors such as the discharge to nursing home and the length of hospital stay more than 5 days [4].

In the field of cardiac surgery, post-operative infections pose a significant challenge. These range from superficial site infections to more serious complications like deep sternal wound infections (DSWI). While DSWI occurs in only 1–2% of cardiac surgery patients, its impact is severe, with mortality rates of 6–30% compared to 2% for cardiac surgery overall. Even when treated successfully, DSWI increases long-term mortality risk. Additionally, chronic post-sternotomy pain affects a substantial portion of patients, often leading to emergency department visits. These issues underscore the importance of infection prevention and long-term care in cardiac surgery [3,4].

The distinction between DSWI and superficial wound infection (SSWI) is crucial. Only the skin, subcutaneous tissue, with or without pectoralis fascia are involved in SSWI, with an incidence of 0.5–8%. As for DSWI, it is usually uncommon, and it carries serious consequences and mortality. Despite these consequences, there are currently no guidelines for the prevention and treatment of DSWIs after cardiac surgery [3,10].

Although the presence of postoperative events after CABG was reported. The associated factors are still in need for further research. In this study, we aim to investigate the possible complications following CABG, with the focus on postoperative infections. Understanding these complications will help in determining the right follow-up program and to establish the utmost patient care.

Materials and methods

Study design

This is a unicentric, retrospective study from medical records of the King Abdullah University Hospital (KAUH), the main hospital in north Jordan from January 2011 to December 2021. The Institutional Review Board (IRB) at our hospital institute and university approved the study (approval #257-2018). The study was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and informed consent was waived by the IRB committee due to the retrospective nature of the study. Patient’s data were anonymized and stored confidentially.

Demographic data were collected for age, sex, and body mass index (BMI), clinical data for the history of hypertension (HTN), diabetes mellitus (DM), smoking, previous myocardial infarction (MI), respiratory illness, history of cerebrovascular accidents (CVA), surgical variables including type of surgery, choice of surgery, time of anesthesia, surgery, aortic cross-clamp (ACC), cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), and post-operative variables of complications, reopening, and length of stay.

Study participants

The study excluded patients suffering from severe carotid artery disease who had not undergone surgery and who, after consultation with a cardiologist, chose stenting even with partial revascularization to prevent stroke complications. Our institutional cardiac surgery follow-up protocol dictated the examination timeline for patients: pre-surgery, post-extubation in the cardiac intensive care unit (CICU), during daily rounds in the ward, one-week post-discharge in the clinic, and three to four weeks post-surgery. Patients were followed-up for one month, during the ICU and in the clinics after their discharge.

Surgical techniques

The skin was incised, followed by midline sternotomy, and electrocauterization was employed to cut through pericardial and presternal tissues. Bone wax was applied. The aorta was palpated gently to detect any aortic calcification to ensure avoidance of the cannulation site. Care was taken during heart manipulation to prevent squeezing and while placing the cross clamp, even when partial, non-circumferential aortic calcification was unavoidable.

Aortic cross-clamping with a partial aortic clamp during proximal graft anastomosis was not substituted. The utilized technique involved a single aortic cross-clamp for proximal graft anastomosis. Cardiopulmonary bypass was initiated with a solitary right atrial two-stage cannula and an ascending aorta perfusion cannula. Standard bypass management encompassed membrane oxygenators, arterial line filters, systemic hypothermia to a minimum of 32 °C, and non-pulsatile flow at 2.4 l/min/m2, with a maintained mean arterial pressure above 50 mm Hg.

Intermittent antegrade crystalloid cardioplegia with elevated potassium concentrations was used for myocardium protection. Anticoagulation was attained using 300 U/kg of heparin to ensure the activated clotting time exceeded 480 seconds and was reversed by protamine at the procedure’s conclusion. Standard placement of mediastinal and left pleural chest drains was implemented in all patients. Sternum closure was achieved with steel wires, and Vicryl 2.0 dyed suture was used in two layers to close the subcutaneous tissues, thereby preventing the creation of dead spaces.

A subcuticular closure was performed using absorbable Vicryl undyed (polyglactin 910) for the intracutaneous closure group. Prophylactic measures included IV TAZOCIN 4.5g administered 30 minutes pre-operation, followed by either IV TAZOCIN 4.5g, or 2.25 g in cases of renal impairment every six hours. For patients allergic to penicillin, 1-g IV vancomycin was administered every 12 hours until drain removal. Postoperatively, patients were monitored in the cardiovascular intensive care unit until they achieved hemodynamic stability and were extubated. Drains were retained until observed drainage was less than 100 cc in a 12-hour period.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were described using mean ± standard deviation (SD) if the data were normally distributed according to the Shapiro-Wilk test, or median (Range) if the data deviated from normality. Categorical variables were described using frequencies (percentages %). To analyze the correlation between demographic, clinical, and operative variables with mortality risk groups, Wilcoxon (Mann–Whitney U) t was used for continuous variables, chi-squared X^2, and the fisher-exact test was used for categorical variables if the category count was <5. A significant difference was considered with a p-value <0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using R statistical language (Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patients’ characteristics

A total of 108 post-CABG surgeries were enrolled in this study. Of which, 92 (85.2%) were males and with a median (IQR) age of 58.5 (52–80). Median BMI was 28 (20–48), with 59 (54.6%) patients with history of smoking. Most patients had a history of HTN 73.1% (79/108) and DM 54.6% (59/108). Thirty patients (27.8%) had respiratory illnesses. Eight (7.4%) patients also had valve surgery, 4 (3.7%) had more than one valve involved, and 5 (4.6%) patients had both CABG and valve surgery. The majority of patients (93.5%) had elective CABG surgery, while only 7 (6.5%) patients had urgent CABG surgery. while no patient was presented with a CVA (Table 1).

In this study of 108 patients, 9 (8.3%) experienced post-operative complications. Demographic analysis revealed no significant difference in gender distribution between groups (p = 0.6), with males predominating in both (86 vs. 78%). Patients with complications were significantly older (68 ± 8 vs. 57 ± 12 years, p = 0.010). Although not statistically significant, urgent surgeries were more frequent in the complication group (22 vs. 5.1%, p = 0.10). Comorbidities were prevalent across both groups, with hypertension (89 vs. 72%, p = 0.4) and diabetes mellitus (67 vs. 54%, p = 0.5) being common. Notably, patients with complications had a significantly higher rate of previous myocardial infarction (56 vs. 23%, p = 0.049). Intraoperative characteristics, including anesthesia time, surgery time, cardiopulmonary bypass time, and aortic cross-clamp time, did not differ significantly between groups. However, post-operative outcomes showed marked differences. Patients with complications exhibited higher rates of LV impairment (78 vs. 37%, p = 0.030) and inotrope use (78 vs. 33%, p = 0.012). They also required longer mechanical ventilation (27.0 ± 30.0 vs. 10.9 ± 8.8 hours, p = 0.045), experienced extended ICU stays (5.50 ± 0.71 vs. 1.72 ± 1.10 days, p = 0.009), and had prolonged overall hospital stays (10.00 ± 2.83 vs. 4.99 ± 0.99 days, p = 0.011). While not reaching statistical significance, there was a trend towards higher rates of surgical reopening (22 vs. 4.0%, p = 0.078) and reintubation (11 vs. 1.0%, p = 0.2) in the complication group (Table 4).

Discussion

CABG has become one of the most performed cardiac surgeries. This is largely due to its effectiveness and safety compared to other interventions in the setting of coronary artery diseases [11,12]. However, adverse events have occurred in many cases, which increased the mortality rate significantly. Postoperative complications after CABG have been reported in the literature, but the contributing factors require further investigation. Gaining insight into these complications will support the development of effective follow-up programs and optimize patient care. In this study, we analyzed the outcomes of 108 post-CABG patients who had the surgery performed in our hospital. The patients’ demographic data were studied to correlate these outcomes with other underlying etiologies.

The patients in our cohort were grouped into two groups based on whether they have experienced postoperative complications. Mainly, one patient had a post-operative stroke, and three patients had post-operative infections, two of which had a surgical site wound and infection, and one had a deep sternal wound infection, these findings are consistent with the literature [13,14]. A meta-analysis by Gao et al. has pooled 37,444 patients who underwent CABG surgery and estimated a rate of surgical site wound infection of around 3%. In their analysis, they have found that patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease have a higher risk of developing postoperative infections (OR, 1.27; 95% CI, 1.01–1.60, p = 0.04) [15].

We have found postoperative infections in 3.8% and DSWI in 0.93% of patients. Compared to Omran et al., they have found an incidence of 0.22% for DSWIs in 9201 patients who underwent CABG [16]. They have also found significant association with female gender, hypertension, DM, prolonged intubation, and re-explorative operations after bleeding. We have found hypertension in 89% of our patients, DM in 67%, and a higher exposure for mechanical ventilation, which might explain the difference in DSWI incidence.

The prevalence of stroke was 0.9% in our study. Stroke is considered a life-threatening complication following CABG and it is associated with higher rates of morbidity and mortality. Post-operative stroke has a higher incidence in post CABG (1.4–3.8%) than in general surgery [17]. This high incidence of this serious event indicates the importance of understanding the underlying etiologies and associated risk factors. There several risk factors of postoperative stroke following cardiac surgeries of which is previous history of stroke [18]. The proposed guidelines from leading cardiothoracic surgical societies recommend that surgery within 48 hours of a stroke is appropriate when there is a compelling surgical need. However, if feasible, delaying surgery up to 4 weeks is recommended for patients with a large territorial stroke or intracranial hemorrhage [19,20]. Previous study by Sultan et al. on 10,250 patients, found a 14.9% mortality rate among those who experienced a symptomatic postoperative stroke following cardiac surgery. Similarly, Matthews et al. showed the stroke-related mortality rate was 30% [21,22]. These findings highlight the critical importance of preventing postoperative stroke, as its occurrence appears to be associated with higher mortality rates.

A meta-analysis carried by Ganesh et al. [23] compared between the incidence of post-operative stroke following CABG and percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). They reported that the PCI had a significantly lower risk of stroke compared to the CABG. This indicates that the risk of developing a stroke should be considered when deciding the revascularization method. In a study conducted by et al. on stroke rates between CABG and PCI showed that stroke rate was 0.4% in patients who had PCI compared to 1.1% in those who had CABG at 30-days postoperatively (HR: 0.33, p-value <0.001), and 2.6% in the PCI group compared to 3.2% in the CABG group after 5-years of follow-up (HR: 0.77, p-value = 0.027) [24]. However, the increased risk of stroke following CABG compared to PCI was limited to patients with multivessel disease and diabetes. Laimoud et al. investigated the predictors of early and late postoperative stroke using multivariate regression showing that carotid artery stenosis, postoperative atrial fibrillation, cardiopulmonary bypass time, prior cerebral stroke, and chronic kidney disease were significant predictors of early postoperative stroke following CABG. While carotid artery stenosis, combined CABG and valve surgery, chronic kidney disease, atrial fibrillation, prior stroke, and HbA1c levels were significant predictors of late postoperative stroke [25]. In the SYNTAX trial, they compared the risk of postoperative stroke between PCI and CABG showing that CABG was not significantly associated with higher rate of postoperative stroke (OR: 1.67, p-value = 0.09) [26].

Stroke may evoke from other post-operative outcomes. Post-operative atrial fibrillation has been associated with a long-term prognosis following surgery. Oraii et al. [27] conducted a study on patients without previous history of atrial fibrillation who went on CABG. They assessed the effect of post-operative atrial fibrillation on overall mortality and stroke. Although the late stroke risk was similar between patients with and without post-operative atrial fibrillation, post-operative atrial fibrillation was associated with increased risk of mid-term mortality and stroke. A study by et al. investigated the risk of stroke and postoperative atrial fibrillation after CABG showing that postoperative atrial fibrillation had a rate of 6.3% for developing cerebrovascular accidents, compared to 3.7% in patients with normal sinus rhythm, indicating that postoperative atrial fibrillation as an independent predictor of cerebrovascular accidents (HR: 1.53, p-value = 0.025) [28]. It has been shown that atrial fibrillation-related strokes are linked to poorer clinical outcomes and higher mortality than non-atrial fibrillation-related strokes. The more severe clinical and imaging outcomes in atrial fibrillation-related strokes are suggested to be the result from larger volumes of severely hypo-perfused tissue, leading to increased infarct size and a higher risk of hemorrhagic transformation [29,30].

Infections play a key role in increasing the morbidity and mortality of patients who undergo CABG. Many types of infection may evolve. Off of these include mediastinitis which was found to significantly increase the mortality rate in the first-year post CABG as reported by Braxton et al. [31]. Although the development of this infection is not well understood, some risk factors have been reported to be associated with this complication. These factors include obesity or increased body mass index, diabetes mellitus, heart failure, COPD, low ejection fraction (EF), renal failure, and improper stabilization of the sternum [31–33]. A study by Zukowska et al. investigated the postoperative CABG outcomes in patients who developed postoperative infections showing that pneumonia was the cause of infection in 4.6% of patients, while bloodstream infection accounted for 1.9% of patients. Additionally, they showed that 10% of patients who did not survive, had postoperative infections from pneumonia, and 3.9% had bloodstream infection [34].

Another form of infection is surgical wound infections (SWI). This complication has been studied by Spelman et al. in which they collected data from 693 patients who undergone CABG and 12-months follow-up. Some of the patients in this study developed sternal wound infections, infections at the site of conduit harvest in arms or legs, and post-operative bacteremia. Spelman et al. concluded that a pre-operative strict weight control and diabetic control would improve the outcomes significantly and increase the risk of developing these infections [35]. Alghamdi et al. investigated the risk factors of post-cardiac surgery wound infections showing that smoking, diabetes mellitus, male sex, and multiple operations during a single hospital stay are associated with higher risk of postoperative infections following cardiac surgery [36].

Post-operative infections put the patients and health care system in a clinical and economic burden. For this, health care providers must consider the possible complications following CABG and whether the benefits of the surgery outweigh the trade-off cost of the associated complications. The use of a broad range prophylactic antibiotics has markedly reduced the risk of infections in post CABG. Other than the use of prophylactic antibiotics, glycemic control in diabetic patients. These preventive measures have shown an effective way of minimizing the adverse effects post-surgery, and with maintaining the clinical outcomes for the patients [37].

Prevention of post-operative stroke is more difficult and challenging compared to post-operative infections. Many studies have been constructed to see what are the possible preventive measures of stroke that can be applied to post CABG patients. Schulman et al. [38] carried out a study to evaluate the efficacy of applying early anticoagulation drugs such as Warfarin in cases of atrial fibrillation to prevent stroke. The role of this anticoagulant was limited and is associated with increased risk of bleeding in the pericardium for the hospitalized patients. Previous studies have demonstrated that epi-aortic scanning during heart surgery can be effective in decreasing the incidence of perioperative brain injury. A study by Zingone et al. showed that using epi-aortic scanning decreased the rate of postoperative stroke from 3.3 to 1.1%. In addition, Yamaguchi et al. showed that no patient had postoperative out of 909 patients after implementing epi-aortic scanning [39,40].

Additionally, postoperative complications were associated with higher rates of previous myocardial infarction. This finding is in line with previous studies, showing that postoperative graft failure and perioperative myocardial infarction are significant complications following CABG surgery with an incidence of CABG failure ranges from 5 to 14% [41].

To evaluate the importance of our results even further, we constructed two supervised machine learning models, which is by far the first to our knowledge in these settings. The demographic, clinical, intra-operative, and post-operative variables were fitted as features in the model in association with mortality. The model has shown that the highest contributing features were the post-operative complications, AAC time, age, CPB time, and hyperthermia. This indicates that post-operative stroke or infection are of great value in determining the mortality of post CABG patients, which is concordant with what is in the literature. Models developed for specific procedures offer a potential solution for more accurate risk estimation in high-risk groups. While the predictive variables for mortality following cardiac surgery remain consistent, the key factor is the weight assigned to each variable, tailored to the specific outcome and patient group [42].

Our findings have several clinical implications for patients undergoing CABG surgery in Jordan. First, our results indicate that older patients and those with a history of myocardial infarction are at a higher risk for postoperative complications, including left ventricular impairment and prolonged ICU and hospital stays. These findings highlight the need for more careful preoperative assessment and postoperative monitoring in these patients. Additionally, our findings showed a higher incidence of complications in patients requiring urgent surgeries, therefore, optimizing patient stability before surgery may improve their outcomes. The detailed postoperative follow-up protocol and management techniques presented in our study can be served as a model for other centers to improve postoperative outcomes in similar patient populations.

Despite the incorporation of machine learning in this study as a validation method, this study has some limitations that need to be further investigated. First, the size of the cohort is not large enough to establish a robust association. Second, the cohort consisted of males predominantly, which makes it difficult to see if variation in gender revels different outcomes. Also, this study was conducted in a single center (unicentric), where patients tend to have to a certain degree similar conditions. These limitations need to be considered and validated to make a generalized and well-established study.

Conclusions

In this retrospective study, we investigated the post CABG complications alongside the clinical outcomes that we desire to maintain from the surgery. In conclusion, post-operative stroke and post-operative infections play key roles in affecting the mortality and morbidity rates of the patients. Understanding these adverse effects that may occur is an important aspect to focus on and to keep in mind while deciding what type of revascularization technique to carry, the preventive measures to apply and the follow-up program for the patients.
