Investigating the factors associated with induced abortion in six nations from the global south
Authors: Brittany E. Hayes, Amy Adamczyk, Noreen Ali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn many low- and middle-income countries, abortion is an important part of fertility control for unintended pregnancies, even when it is technically illegal. Yet, we have relatively little information on the cross-national personal characteristics associated with the odds that a woman will obtain an abortion.MethodsWe merge six Demographic and Health country surveys (i.e., India, Cambodia, Indonesia, Cameroon, Côte d’Ivoire, and Albania) to examine the correlates of individual-level factors and abortion decisions in the last five years. By assessing a set of diverse lower income nations across the world, we can see if the factors we think would matter based on prior research (i.e., education, economic standing, number of children, urbanicity, and religion) are important for explaining abortion decision-making across these societies.ResultsEducation and number of children appear to be robust and important risk factors for abortion behaviors. We find fewer associations between personal economic standing, urbanicity, and religion.ConclusionsWomen across nations do not face the same barriers in accessing abortion, and do not have the same drivers in obtaining one. Country-specific policies need to developed.

Abortion is a debated and stigmatized topic in many nations. Given the divisive tone that undercuts conversations around abortion, there tends to be ongoing changes to abortion laws even in rich nations that have advanced healthcare systems (i.e., the United States [1]). While roughly 20 countries have made abortion outright illegal (e.g., Malta; Egypt; Philippines) women still access abortion care, albeit care that may not be safe. For these reasons there are likely individual factors that shape abortions behaviors beyond national legislation that prohibits the procedure. Indeed, research has shown that women face a multitude of micro-level challenges that affect their abortion behavior, including financial ability, lack of support systems, and religious stigma [2–4].

Few studies have examined abortion behaviors across several nations at once[5–9], especially in low and middle-income nations[10–14]. Country-specific analyses that are conducted in isolation are unable to consider if individual factors shaping abortion behavior are consistent across nations in a way that can identify trends. At the same time while low and middle-income countries share similarities when compared to higher income nations, such societies also have unique histories and differ across socio-structural and cultural dimensions[15, 16]. As a result, there may be more differences than trends when considered in tandem. Further, few studies have used time-period specific data to disentangle the temporal order between women’s personal characteristics and abortion decisions. For questions regarding abortion behaviors, temporal order is critical to uncovering the underlying mechanisms, as individual correlates may change throughout the life course and further shape behaviors. Absent is a consideration of whether women’s personal characteristics within a clear snapshot of time are consistent (or not) across different religious, cultural, and economic landscapes, especially when accounting for risk factors often considered among more developed nations.

Although most of the countries in our study come from the Global South where nations are frequently grouped together, these low and middle-income nations differ in their religious cultures, economic standing, and level of development [16]. By including six different nations in one analysis we can identify trends, but also differences, across abortion behavior. Research has shown that the association between individual characteristics and outcomes can vary in strength and direction depending on the national context. For instance, in some country-specific analyses a woman’s decision-making ability increases her risk of experiencing violence by her partner[17, 18], but in others it is a protective factor [19]. The equivocal evidence regarding many factors associated with abortion behaviors raises questions about how these processes vary across diverse nations typically grouped together.

Utilizing standardized Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS), we examine individual-level patterns in abortion behaviors and their associated factors across six countries: India, Cambodia, Indonesia, Cameroon, Côte d’Ivoire, and Albania. By assessing six diverse low and middle-income nations countries through standardized surveys, we can see if the factors we think would matter based on prior research are important for explaining abortion decision-making. Knowing the consistency in these predictors enhances claims around generalizability and contributes to the evidence-base on abortion behaviors. If predictors remain significant and in the same direction across diverse societies, cross-national programming can be developed. Yet, if predictors are not consistent it would illuminate the need for country-specific programming and offer direction about what it should entail.

Country Context

The six nations included in analyses are drawn from Asia (i.e., India, Cambodia, and Indonesia), Africa (i.e., Cameroon, Côte d’Ivoire), and Europe (i.e., Albania). The three Asian nations are diverse in regards to religion and include majority Hindu (India), Buddhist (Cambodia), and Muslim (Indonesia) populations [21–23]. Compared to the global average, historically, these three Asian nations have demonstrated gender inequality in terms of the Gender Inequality Index (GII), which provides detail on disparities between men and women in regards to reproductive health, empowerment, and the labor market (India = 0.437; Cambodia = 0.486 Indonesia = 0.439)[24]. Across these dimensions, India has a much lower maternal mortality rate compared to (103 per 100,00 live births) both Indonesia (173 per 100,00 live births) and Cambodia (218 per 100,00 live births) [24]. The fertility rate, or the total births per woman, hovers around 2 children in these three nations (India = 2.0; Cambodia = 2.6; Indonesia = 2.1)[58]. While Indian (41.00%) and Indonesian (51.00%) women aged 25 and older tend to have at least some secondary education, Cambodian women differ in that roughly 16.4% of women aged 25 and older have at least some secondary education [24].

When considering the African nations, Cameroon has a majority population that practices Christianity [25]. Côte d’Ivoire has large populations that practice either Islam (42.5%) or Christianity (39.8%), offering a greater diversity of religious affiliation relative to other countries in the analysis [26]. Both Cameroon and Côte d’Ivoire have higher maternal mortality rates (i.e., 438 and 480 per 100,00 live births, respectively) [24] and fertility rates (both nations have an average of 4.3 live births per woman) than the Asian and European countries included in our sample[58]. These nations also have higher levels of gender inequality than the Asian nations included here (Cameroon = 0.555; Côte d’Ivoire = 0.612). Finally, many women in these nations aged 25 and older do not have at least some secondary education (Cameroon = 24.50%; Côte d’Ivoire = 13.50%)[24].

Finally, Albania, located in Europe, is a majority Muslim nation [27]. The country fairs well in terms of gender parity in that it has the lowest GII (i.e., 0.116) and hence lowest level of gender inequality in the sample. Likewise, the majority of women aged 25 and older have at least some secondary education (82.2%) and there are low rates of maternal mortality (i.e., 8 deaths per 100,00 live births) [24]. Fertility rates were also the lowest in Albania with women having roughly 1.3 births[58].

While these nations are diverse, they share some similarities, including majority religion. Both Albania and Indonesia are majority Muslim. Many nations have high rates of maternal mortality and are ranked quite low in terms of gender equality. When compared to nations located in Asia and Africa, Albania provides a contrast where abortion was largely legalized during the Soviet era but where the rate of maternal mortality is quite low [28] and there are higher levels of gender equality [24].

Further, these nations differ in their abortion laws. In Albania and Cambodia, abortion is permitted on request during the first trimester. In India, women can get abortions based on broad social and economic grounds in addition to rape, incest, or fetal abnormality. Cameroon, Indonesia, and Côte d’Ivoire have the most restrictive laws, only allowing it for extreme reasons, like to preserve the mother’s health or in instances of rape [29]. By this, abortion is not legal on demand within gestational limits in these three nations. Given their diverse laws, it remains an empirical question if similar sociodemographic abortion risk factors matter across this set of countries. If so, we may be capturing information about key individual-level forces shaping abortion decisions for women across the world, enhancing the generalizability of the conclusions around the correlates of abortion behaviors[20].

Individual factors and abortion

Educational attainment

Several studies have found that women with higher levels of education are more likely to report an induced abortion than those with lower levels of education[4, 9, 30–32]. Women with increased exposure to higher education may have greater abortion risk because they may be more knowledgeable about and have greater access to the procedure [9]. Women with more education may also have greater career ambitions and may be less willing to have an unintended pregnancy disrupt their long-term plans. Relatedly, Yemane, Korsa, and Jemal [33] found that the least educated Ethiopian women had the lowest likelihood of abortion compared to others. Likewise, Ghanaian and Mozambique women with some education had higher abortion risk than those with no education [34, 35]. Consistently and when considering India in particular, research has found that Indian women with higher levels of education have higher abortion odds [10].

Conversely, other studies have found a negative association between education and abortion. Among Ghanaian women, pregnancy termination odds are lower for residents with higher educational levels than those with none [34]. Interestingly, Dankwah and colleagues [36] found that Ghanaian women with average levels of education were more likely to report having had an induced abortion compared to their more and less-educated counterparts. Finally, contrary to these associations, Ahinkorah and colleagues [37] found no relationship between education and abortion decisions for Ghanaian women. The conflicting findings across these studies highlights the complexity of the education association. The temporal order between these two processes may not be captured with lifetime data. But also, many of these nations may have low rates of women enrolling in secondary education, which may further shape these processes.

While there is conflicting evidence on the association between education and abortion behavior, we hypothesize that women with more education will have greater access to abortion resources and career ambitions, which will increase their odds of reporting an abortion. This is because much of the conflicting evidence comes from only one nation. While we cannot tease out the mechanisms, these ideas lead to our first hypothesis:

H1:Higher levels of education will be associated with increased odds of reporting an abortion in the last five years.

H1:Higher levels of education will be associated with increased odds of reporting an abortion in the last five years.

H1:Higher levels of education will be associated with increased odds of reporting an abortion in the last five years.

Social class, wealth, and economic standing

Previous scholarship has suggested that financial standing is also important for understanding abortion behaviors [10, 38, 39]. Wealthier women can afford safe abortions and may be enticed to pursue a higher standard of living, making the idea of fewer children more appealing. In a meta-analysis, Chae and colleagues [5] found that, out of 19 low and middle-income countries, wealthier women in 12 of these nations were more likely to report an abortion compared to their poorer counterparts. Consistent with this conclusion, being richer increased the likelihood of abortion among Ghanaian women as well as Indian women [3, 10, 40]. This could be because poorer people cannot afford or manage the logistics of finding an abortion, and in particular, a safe abortion. Building on this argument, poorer Nepalese women were more likely to undergo an unsafe abortion than richer residents [4]. Collectively, it seems wealthier women may be more aware of their abortion rights, have safer abortion access, or desire smaller families[4], which lead to us to our next hypothesis that proposes a positive association between wealth and abortion:

H2: Compared to poorer women, wealthier ones will have greater odds of reporting an abortion in the last five years.

H2: Compared to poorer women, wealthier ones will have greater odds of reporting an abortion in the last five years.

H2: Compared to poorer women, wealthier ones will have greater odds of reporting an abortion in the last five years.

Number of children

The number of children a respondent already has may also affect future abortion decisions. In a cross-national macro study of both economically developed and poorer nations, women with two or more children had higher abortion odds compared to those with none [41]. Consistent with this cross-national study, county-specific research has found that Ghanaian and Nepalese women with more children are more likely to seek an induced abortion compared to those with none [3, 30, 42]. Number of children may be related to maternal health, fertility preferences, or financial/emotional resources. When women have more children, it may deplete their economic resources. Additionally, they may feel unable to adequately care for additional ones and seek termination to prevent lowering their family’s standard of living and limiting the amount of emotional and physical support available for existing children. Nyarko and Potter [3] argue that this could be because women who already have children likely hold stronger ties to their existing ones. Likewise, they may have already met their fertility preferences.

Conversely, Seidu and colleagues [35] found that women with four or more children were 56% less likely to terminate a pregnancy than those with fewer children. Zahan and Feng [43] also found that Bangladeshi women with no children had higher chances of pregnancy termination when controlling for age, education, wealth, and area demographics. This negative relationship between number of children and abortion behaviors may relate to religious, personal, or cultural reasons. Furthermore, in contrast to the previous reasoning, it is possible that individuals with many children have the resources to afford more. Additionally, these findings may be context-specific, necessitating the need to include standardized measures across several nations, as we do in the current study. Because the nations included in our analyses are largely drawn from the Global South, it is very likely that the number of children is associated with balancing economic resources leading to a positive association. These ideas lead to the third hypothesis:

H3: If a woman already has a child, the odds of reporting an abortion within the last five years will increase.

H3: If a woman already has a child, the odds of reporting an abortion within the last five years will increase.

H3: If a woman already has a child, the odds of reporting an abortion within the last five years will increase.

Urban and rural areas

Where an individual lives may be important for determining abortion access and subsequent abortion behavior. Research has shown that living in an urban area increases abortion odds. The desire to have children and actual fertility rates are often higher among rural residents than their urban counterparts [44]. For example, Ghanaian and Nepalese women living in urban areas have higher odds of reporting an abortion than those living in rural areas [3, 40, 42, 45]. In a sample of recently pregnant women from five countries, abortion rates were lower among rural dwellers than those living in urban areas, when controlling for other variables [9]. When considering the nations in our study, previous research has found that induced abortion is more common among urban women in India, Albania, and Cambodia than those from more rural areas[5, 7, 10, 14]. It may be that those living in urban areas have increased access to abortion providers, hold more liberal views, or have smaller support networks that could help care for a pregnancy brought to term. Indeed, some research has found that women from urban areas have weaker family ties and receive less financial help from their kin than rural women [46]. Regardless of the specific mechanisms, prior work on the nations included in these analyses suggest a positive association between urbanicity and abortion [5, 7, 10, 14] leading to the fourth hypothesis:

H4: Women living in urban areas will have higher odds of reporting an abortion in the last five years than those from rural areas.

H4: Women living in urban areas will have higher odds of reporting an abortion in the last five years than those from rural areas.

H4: Women living in urban areas will have higher odds of reporting an abortion in the last five years than those from rural areas.

Religion

Religion is one of the strongest factors shaping views about abortion [2, 47]. [59] People who are regularly engaged with their religion and find it important are less likely to report that abortion is acceptable [48]. More religious people also have more conservative sex-related behaviors. For example, very religious women are less likely than others to have premarital sex [49]. Further, as an individual’s religiosity increases, their number of sexual partners decreases, further limiting their odds of an unplanned pregnancy[50], and possibly abortion.

While this work has focused on religiosity more generally, religious affiliation alone may shape sex-related behaviors. Adamczyk and Hayes [51] found that ever-married Muslims were less likely than ever-married Christians to report premarital sex. Extending this to abortion behaviors, research has demonstrated that women of Islamic faith have lower odds of terminated and unintended pregnancies [4, 32, 34, 37, 45]. While most religions disapprove of abortion, it may be that Muslim women have lower odds of an abortion than their Christian counterparts because they are less likely to engage in activities that make an abortion necessary or desirable [51]. These activities may include premarital sex, extramarital affairs, multiple sex partners or being sexually active longer before marriage. Because our sample includes nations that differ across three major world religions (i.e., Christianity, Islam, and Buddhism) as well individual-measures of religious affiliation, we can disentangle the role of religion in shaping abortion odds. Given Muslim women may be less likely to engage in sex behaviors that necessitate an abortion, we hypothesize the following:

H5: Muslim women will have lower odds of reporting an abortion in the last five years than women of other religions.

H5: Muslim women will have lower odds of reporting an abortion in the last five years than women of other religions.

H5: Muslim women will have lower odds of reporting an abortion in the last five years than women of other religions.

Materials and methods

We use data from the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS), which are nationally representative surveys conducted in over 90 low to middle income countries. Data administrators typically collect data in each country every five years and focus on demographic and health trends. Some nations include optional modules that focus on other key topics, such as female genital mutilation or vaccination. Within each nation, the sampling strategy is typically a stratified two-stage cluster design. The first stage focuses on enumeration areas, and the second stage collects a sample of households from the selected areas. Because of this strategy, the samples in each country are meant to be representative at the national, regional, and resident level.

While 62 nations had information on pregnancy resolution, this measure includes abortions, miscarriages, and stillbirths. A much smaller number of countries had data on abortion behavior. Amongst these countries, only six had information on whether the respondent had an abortion in the last five years: India, Albania, Cambodia, Cameroon, Côte d’Ivoire, and Indonesia. Limiting the time-period to five years provides consistency within the responses and, therefore, analyses. Further, the use of the five-year recall period – rather than lifetime – can disentangle some of the temporal order questions between independent variables and the outcome measure. Consequently, while our nations represent a convenience sample, the data within each country reflects a nationally representative sample. We leverage the nationally representative nature of these surveys from the most recently available dataset that had information on abortion to ascertain trends in correlates of abortion behaviors across six very different nations that are all defined as low to middle income. Institutional Review Board approval was obtained and determined to be not human subjects.

Dependent variable

The outcome measure focused on whether or not the respondent had an induced abortion in the last five years. First, respondents were asked if they ever had a pregnancy that miscarried, was aborted, or ended in a stillbirth. If they said yes, they were asked when that pregnancy ended. If it was in the last five years, they were then asked how many months pregnant they were and if the pregnancy ended in spontaneous miscarriage, induced abortion, or stillbirth. This item was coded whereby 1 = induced abortion in the last five years and 0 = did not have an abortion in the last five years. Because the abortion question is embedded within a series of general inquiries about pregnancies and includes specific requests for dates and duration, administrators are more likely to have accurately captured abortion behavior than if they had asked the question outside of this question context. To our knowledge, there is only one existing study that has examined induced abortion, albeit for recently pregnant women, at the individual level across a handful of DHS countries [9].

Independent variables

Education was measured as a categorical variable where no education served as the reference category. Three measures were included: primary education (some or all), secondary education (some or all), and more than secondary education.

The total number of children ever born was categorized into a set of dummy variables (i.e., one, two, three, or four or more) where no children was the reference.

Wealth was also assessed with a set of four dummy variables (i.e., poorer, middle, richer, and richest) where poorest is the reference category. Wealth is a standardized measure developed by the DHS and accounts for the respondent’s standard of living within the nation. It is not a comparison of wealth across nations. Whether the respondent lived in an urban (= 1) or rural area (= 0) was included as a binary measure.

Religion was captured in all countries except Indonesia where it was not measured at all. The measurement of religion is variable across DHS surveys though all nations included Muslim and forms of Christianity. We included a series of country-specific measures related to religion where Muslim serves as the reference category for all analyses that examine the association between religion and abortion behaviors.

We include two important control variables - marital status and age. Marital status was measured as a binary measure where 1 = currently married or in a union, and 0 = single, divorced, or widowed. Age was included as a set of dummy variables (i.e., 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, 45–49) where the youngest group of women (15–19) were the comparison group. For Albania only, the DHS did not report any women, age 15–19, as having obtained an abortion. We double checked the data to make sure we had not made any errors when merging, and our process was clean. Bearak et al. (2022) used the same DHS data as us to estimate abortion rates across countries [60]. Consistent with our estimates, they found that across Europe Albania had one of the smallest unintended pregnancy rates, which tend to be correlated with low abortion rates. Because there is no variation in the outcome for Albanian women between the ages of 15–19, for just this country, we combined the first two categories, so it ranges from 15 to 24. Surveys were conducted across a two - year span in Albania, Cameroon, and Côte d’Ivoire and over three years in India. For these countries, we included survey year as a binary measure.

Analytic plan

Given the binary nature of our dependent variable and our country-specific approach, we estimated six separate logistic regression models for the odds of reporting an abortion in the last five years. Weights, as recommended by the DHS, were used for analyses and account for complex sample designs. Such weighting procedures adjust for clustering, stratification, and weights. Missing data tends be minimal in the DHS. Across the six countries analyzed, less than 1% were missing. Therefore, we listwise deleted across the entire sample.

First, we present key descriptive statistics. We then move into the multivariate results. Because we only have six nations, we do not have enough power to examine societal-level factors. Additionally, India had a large sample (N = 672,582). By contrast, Côte d’Ivoire included 9,957 women. We are unable to analyze a pooled sample with all countries because India would have had an out-sized influence. Instead, we look for trends across countries based on the direction and size of key correlates. We then present marginal means.

Educational levels also varied across nations. Less than 1% of women had no education in Albania. Similarly, 1.67% of Indonesian women had no education. These are quite low estimates when compared to 53.08% of women in Côte d’Ivoire who had none. These baseline estimates of education provide important context to the interpretation of multivariate findings. Further, they are consistent with the metrics provided by agencies[24], reinforcing the representativeness of these datasets.

Multivariate results

Before detailing the country-specific findings, we first recognize the consistency of findings across models. Across three of the six countries, having more than one child was associated with higher odds of reporting an abortion in the last five years. Indeed, this was an incredibly strong association in Albania. Indonesia and Cote d’Ivoire were the only two countries where we did not see this association. We see modest effects in Cameroon but not of the same magnitude as India, Albania, and Cambodia. Education was an equally important correlate.

India

Compared to women without any education, Indian women with primary education, secondary education and more than secondary education had higher odds of reporting an abortion in the last five years. Relative to women in the lowest wealth category, higher levels of wealth were associated with greater odds of reporting an abortion. Up until age 34, older women are more likely to obtain abortions than women age 15–19. However, beginning at age 40 when fertility has dropped, older women become less likely than the youngest ones to have reported an abortion in the previous five years. Married women had higher odds of reporting an abortion than those who were not married. Urban Indian women also had higher odds than those who lived in rural areas. Finally, compared to Muslim respondents, Indian women without a religion, and Hindu respondents had higher odds of an abortion. We did not find any significant differences between Muslim respondents and Christians or Buddhists.

Albania

To reiterate, the majority of women in Albania had at least some education. Significant differences only emerged between women with secondary education when compared to those without any education. In this case, Albanian women with secondary education had lower odds of reporting an abortion than those with no education. The only other significant item in the model was the number of children ever born. Women with one, two, or three or more children had substantially higher odds of reporting an abortion than those without any children.

Cambodia

When considering the association between education and abortion in Cambodia, female residents who had more than a secondary education had lower odds than those without any education. The wealthiest respondents had higher odds of reporting an abortion than the poorest ones. As in India, women between the ages of 20 and 34 are more likely to obtain an abortion than the youngest women, who were between the ages of 15 and 19. But, as women’s fertility substantially declines after age 40, we no longer see a difference in abortion reporting between the youngest and oldest women in the sample. Married Cambodian women had higher odds of reporting an abortion than women who were not currently married. Cambodian women who lived in urban areas had higher odds of reporting an abortion than women from rural areas. When compared to Muslim respondents, Christians and Buddhists had higher odds of reporting an abortion.

Cameroon

Women in Cameroon who had a primary or secondary education had higher odds of reporting an abortion when compared to those without any education. We do not find the same wealth-related differences for Cameroon women. Married Cameroonians had lower odds of reporting an abortion when compared to women who were not currently married. When compared to Muslim respondents, Christians, those without any religion, and those who identified with another religion had higher odds of reporting an abortion.

Côte d’ivoire

Compared to those without any education, women in Côte d’Ivoire who had primary, secondary, or more than secondary education had higher odds of reporting an abortion. The wealthiest women were more likely to report an abortion in the previous five years than the poorest ones. The youngest women (i.e., age 15–19) had lower odds of obtaining an abortion than women in the older age categories. Finally, compared to Muslim respondents, Christians, those without any religion, and those who identified with another religion were all associated with higher odds of reporting an abortion. These findings around religion are consistent with the results from Cambodia and Cameroon.

Indonesia

Indonesian women with secondary education and more had higher odds of reporting an abortion than Indonesians with no education. Beginning around age 30, we see that older women were more likely to report an abortion in the previous five years than the youngest women. As noted above the Indonesian survey did not ask about religion.

Supplemental analyses of women aged 30 to 49

Given the positive association between abortion risk and at least some of the educational categories in four of our six countries, we wanted to better assess the correct casual order. Some respondents, for example, could have been in school when they got pregnant. If they did not get an abortion, then having a child could have disrupted their educational plans. In this scenario, the causal relationship between abortion risk and educational attainment would run in the opposite direction with getting an abortion increasing educational attainment.

To assess the causal order, we narrowed our analyses to women aged 30 to 49 because the majority of women would have likely completed their education by then. This focus allows us to disentangle temporal processes between education and abortion behaviors (see Appendix A). For the majority of the analyses, the associations remain or become stronger. This is true for India, Cambodia, Cameroon, and Indonesia. Within these nations, it is likely that education increases the odds of a woman reporting an abortion in the last five years. For women in Côte d’Ivoire, those with more than secondary education becomes marginally significant, albeit in the same direction.

The greatest change emerged in Albania. In the initial analysis, women with a secondary education had lower abortion odds than those with no education, which is the opposite of our hypothesized direction. However, very few Albanian women under the age of 50 have no education (less than 1%). When the sample was restricted to women aged 30 to 49, the direction of the counterintuitive relationship remained, but the statistical significance dropped to 0.10.

Discussion

We consider the correlates of abortion behaviors across six low-to-middle income nations. Education and number of children appear to be robust and important risk factors for abortion behaviors in the majority of countries. We find fewer associations between personal economic standing, urbanicity, and religion. Including nations from the Global South contextualizes how these factors may operate within each unique cultural context while unpacking the consistency in associations across nations [52]. We do this among countries that have been treated similarly because of their classification on the world stage (i.e., the majority located in the Global South) but who have unique histories and cultures. This approach allows us to identify trends but also differences across the nations. Additionally, by considering abortion behaviors in the last five years we begin to disentangle the temporal order between some cross-national factors and abortion. This allows us to ascertain generalizability of these associations.

First, there was variability in the prevalence of abortion reporting within the last five years across nations. Indonesia (0.2%) and Albania had the lowest prevalence (0.7%) while abortion prevalence was much higher in Côte d’Ivoire (8.20%). This is somewhat surprising given research has found especially high rates of abortion in some Soviet Union countries[53, 54]. However, as noted above, other studies using the DHS have found that across Europe Albania had one of the lowest unintended pregnancy rates, which tend to be correlated with fewer abortions [60]. The low abortion prevalence is not surprising within Indonesia – where abortion is quite restricted. The interesting contrast stems between Cameroon, Indonesia, and Côte d’Ivoire – three nations that have quite restrictive laws – but where abortion was much more prevalent in the African nation.

Birth control prevalence tends to be lower in poorer nations and when women have higher levels of gender inequality [55]. Some forms of birth control (e.g., condoms) require their partner’s cooperation. Additionally, in poorer places more reliable forms of birth control (e.g., pill, IUD) can be expensive, hard to access, and not well-known [56]. Furthermore, abortion being illegal, women and their providers may avoid detection, and officials may be hesitant to act, especially relative to other “illegal” acts. As a result, when these women find themselves with an unanticipated pregnancy, abortion may become an important option to manage fertility. These simple descriptive statistics indicate there is variability across nations that have the same legal standards for abortion.

As more nations change their laws around abortion[29], it is important to consider the individual correlates of such behaviors to inform programming. If predictors remain significant and in the same direction across diverse nations, cross-national programming can be developed. If on the other hand, predictors are not consistent it would illuminate the need for country-specific programming and offer direction on what that programming should entail. Our findings demonstrated largely consistent findings for the direction between education (H1) and number of children (H3) with abortion behaviors. In line with H1, at least some of the higher education categories were associated with increased odds of reporting an abortion in four of the nations: Indonesia, India, Cameroon, and Côte d’Ivoire. In contrast, education was not consistently significantly associated with abortion in Albania and Cambodia. Instead, we see negative relationships with abortion behaviors between those with secondary education (compared to none) in Albania and those with higher education (compared to none) in Cambodia. These latter findings are illuminating. The majority of Albanian women had secondary education, suggesting there may be a ceiling effect within this nation. In contrast, Cambodian women often did not have more than secondary education, suggesting a potential backlash effect. Future research needs to examine these mechanisms by including measures of historical context, local access, or indices of globalization effects. Multilevel models across nations with sufficient power are also a worthwhile area for future research.

In regard to wealth (H2), there was a positive and significant association in one of the nations: India. There was also a significant positive association between the richest respondents (compared to the poorest) in Cambodia and Côte d’Ivoire. While our data cannot determine if wealthier women may be more aware of their rights regarding abortions, have safer access, or be more ambitious in obtaining employment[4], it is clear that financial standing is important for understanding the decision to obtain an abortion [10, 38, 39]. Knowing how wealth, and potentially access, relate to abortion behaviors is a key metric for providers and engaging in outreach to more vulnerable populations.

There was not a significant association between any of the levels of wealth and abortion behaviors in Albania, Cameroon, and Indonesia. With regards to Albania, other studies have suggested that some former Soviet Union nations may have developed an abortion culture resulting from wide government supported abortion access, hospital subsidies when women got abortions verses other forms of fertility control, and minimal religious objection in these officially atheistic countries[53, 54]. Fewer personal factors were associated with abortion decisions in Albania relative to other countries, suggesting that individual characteristics may matter less when the culture is more accepting. For Indonesia, the wealth coefficient is in the anticipated direction but does not reach statistical significance. It may be related to the fact that Indonesia has the largest economy is Southeast Asia and has made gains in the last few decades [57]. More research is needed to better understand the lack of a statistical association between wealth and abortion behavior in Indonesia. Finally, abortion is highly restricted in Cameroon, suggesting wealth and resources may not even provide access to an abortion.

H3 focused on the association between current motherhood and obtaining an abortion. The relationship was quite strong when it was significant– in three of the six nations women with more than one child were more likely to have reported an abortion in the last five years. Findings from Cameroon also approached this trend. While some analyses suggest there is a negative relationship between number of children and abortion behaviors[35, 43], it might be related to how this item is coded. For instance, Seidu and colleagues [35] used four or more children as their cut-off. Zahan and Feng [43] considered Bangladeshi women without children. It might be that having one child is a major life transition compared to when women have four or more. As such, the number of children may be related to maternal health, fertility preferences, or financial/emotional resources.

Inconsistent with prior research, urbanicity was only significant and positively associated with abortion behaviors in two of the nations: India and Cambodia. Although it was in the anticipated direction, this item was not statistically significant in the remaining four nations, offering limited support for H4. It may be that globalization has transformed family structure whereby even individuals in rural areas have weaker family ties and receive less help [46]. This may be especially true in Asian nations, like India, that outsources labor to wealthier nations across the Global North limiting the ties individuals have in rural areas. Likewise, abortion access may be widely available in rural and urban places, even when it is technically illegal. These findings raise questions for future studies about access and abortion attitudes, especially in rural areas.

Finally, the analyses support H5 in that Christians were more likely than Muslims to report an abortion in the last five years in Cambodia, Cameroon, and Côte d’Ivoire. Conversely, H5 was not supported in Albania – a nation with a large Muslim population but whose history is deeply influenced by Communism, where religion may be less paramount. While DHS data collection effort for Indonesia did not include a measure of religion, it has the world’s largest Muslim population [23]. Religion is an especially sensitive topic, and 87% of Indonesians affiliate with Islam, which may be why survey administrators did not collect religion information [61]. The diversity of religions in Côte d’Ivoire and the majority Christian population in Cameroon may be driving the prevalence when compared to Indonesia even though the nations have the same laws. This work shows that while the majority religion can be an important factor, the nation’s religious makeup and political history (i.e., communism) may influence this association.

While we did not develop any hypotheses about the association between age and abortion odds, we included age as a control variable in our models. We found that across all of the countries, except Albania, abortion odds where generally higher among women in their 20 s and 30 s compared to the youngest women in the sample. Compared to the youngest women, these slightly older women are likely more sexually active, which would shape the odds of both unintended pregnancies and induced abortions. But, as women move past their prime fertility years beginning in their mid to late 30s, we found that in some countries (i.e., India, Cambodia, and Cameroon) abortion odds were either lower than the teens, or there were no longer any statistically significant differences between these groups. For Côte d’Ivoire and Indonesia, we found that the oldest women had higher abortion odds than the teens. More research is needed to understand the factors shaping abortion odds for older women, even as their fertility and thus risk of an unintended pregnancy declines[59].

Our study has some limitations that merit mention. First, our abortion measure was limited to induced abortion in the last five years. It does not speak necessarily to the prevalence of other types of abortions. While it is possible the respondent’s level of education changed during that time frame, our sample focuses on women aged 15 and over. Many of the women in these nations have completed their schooling, if they attended school at all. Additionally, supplemental analyses were limited to women age 30 to 49. By age 30, formal education for most women is likely to have ended. The results from our supplemental analysis are very similar to the original ones. Hence, it appears that women’s education likely precedes abortion behavior, as opposed to an abortion increasing educational attainment, which might be more common in countries with longer educational trajectories and higher overall levels of educational attainment.

While our selection of measures and focus on the last five years attempts to disentangle temporal order, we cannot be certain. We were limited to the nations that included specific measures on abortion within the last five years. While 62 nations appear to capture data on pre-term pregnancy resolutions, miscarriages and stillbirths are not the same as induced abortion. It is important for future research to capture termination decisions and contraceptive use that may be related to the need for an abortion. Finally, abortion is a delicate issue, especially in places where it is technically illegal. Hence, women may feel compelled to respond in socially desirable ways [49]. In the DHS, the question about induced abortion emerges only after women have reported all recent pregnancies and how and when they ended, which should reduce reporting bias. Likewise, by focusing on only the previous five years, the recall window is shorter, which should also improve reporting but may nonetheless be affected by recall bias. Nevertheless, we cannot be sure that some women chose not to report their induced abortions. Finally, we only include six nations in our analyses which limit the generalizability of the results.

Conclusion

The prevalence of abortion in the last five years is variable across these poor and middle-income nations and does not appear to be related to laws that may (not) restrict abortion. Further, while there was some consistency across correlates, including education and the number of children, there was also quite a bit of variability across nations. Such findings suggest the importance of country-specific programming when considering public health questions around abortion behaviors. This also begins to question claims around generalizability when it comes to abortion decisions[20]. Women across nations do not face the same barriers in accessing abortion, and do not have the same drivers in obtaining one. Therefore, country-specific policies need to developed.
