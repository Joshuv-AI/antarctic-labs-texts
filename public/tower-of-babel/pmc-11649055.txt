Primitive Myxoid Mesenchymal Tumor of Infancy: A Lost Battle
Authors: Nitin James Peters, V Manasa Reddy, Amanjit Bal, Arihant Jain, Anjali Gupta, Renu Madan, Jai Kumar Mahajan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPrimitive myxoid mesenchymal tumor of infancy (PMMTI) is a low to intermediate-grade, poorly differentiated myofibroblastic tumor and is characterized by its tendency to recur locally. It commonly occurs in the 1st year of life and is predominantly seen in the axial skeleton and rarely in the retroperitoneum. We report one such case of PMMTI, which is the second case reported in English literature.

INTRODUCTION

Primitive myxoid mesenchymal tumor of infancy (PMMTI) is a rare mesenchymal tumor first described by Alaggio et al., with only a few cases reported to date.[1] It is characterized by aggressive local infiltration, rare distal metastasis, and resistance to chemotherapy. The treatment strategy mainly involves radical surgery to achieve clear margins. It is characterized histologically by a diffuse myxoid background with delicate vascularity and positive reactivity to immunohistochemical markers such as CD99, CD117, and nestin.[2] Herein, we report a patient with PMMTI and the temporal profile of the disease.

CASE REPORT

A 7-month-old boy was brought to our outpatient clinic with a history of swelling in the lower abdomen for 1 week which was incidentally detected by his parents. There was no history of pain or sudden increase in size. On examination, it was an 8 cm × 7 cm firm to hard, nontender, mobile mass in the left lumbar region.

Routine blood investigations were normal. Ultrasonography showed a large solid mass measuring 8.7 cm × 7.5 cm in the left paraspinal region pushing the left kidney anterolaterally but separate from it. Subsequently, a contrast computed tomography abdomen was performed for better delineation and it revealed a large well-circumscribed, heterogeneous soft-tissue mass measuring 8.4 cm × 6.6 cm × 8.9 cm in the left anterior pararenal space. The mass was seen compressing on the left kidney and left renal vein but with no obvious infiltration. There was no macroscopic fat or calcification, and the differential diagnosis of sarcoma, extrarenal Wilms, extra-adrenal neuroblastoma, and inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor was contemplated.

Given the tumor being well encapsulated with no obvious infiltration into the surrounding structures, the decision was made to proceed with upfront resection. Intraoperatively, a 10 cm × 5 cm well-defined, encapsulated retroperitoneal mass was found related superiorly to the tail of the pancreas and spleen when displacing the left kidney laterally. Feeding vessels were ligated and the mass was excised in toto, without any intraoperative rupture, after separating it from the adjacent structures [Figure 1a and b]. The patient had an uneventful postoperative course.

Upon gross examination, the tumor measured 11 cm × 9 cm × 4 cm in size with a bosselated outer surface and areas of congestion. Histological examination showed an encapsulated tumor with negative margins and tumor cells arranged in nests, sheets, and alveolar patterns separated by thin fibrous septae. Tumor cells were largely monomorphic with round nuclei, fine chromatin, inconspicuous nucleoli, and moderate eosinophilic to clear cytoplasm. The background matrix in a few areas showed extensive myxoid changes [Figure 2a-d]. On immunohistochemistry, tumor cells were positive to cyclin D1, SATB2, and TLE-1, weakly positive to BCL-2, and negative to desmin, myogenin, myoD1, WT1, HMN45, CD99, synaptophysin, and PHOX2B [Figure 3a-d]. These features were suggestive of a primitive myxoid tumor of infancy.

At 1.5 years of follow-up, the patient presented again with a same-sized lump and high-grade fever. He was reevaluated and diagnosed to have a tumor recurrence. After a multimodal tumor board discussion, a laparotomy was planned and the tumor was excised. There was a tumor rupture on the medial aspect. The rest of the tumor was excised piecemeal. He remained well for 6 months when he presented with hematemesis, an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy showed a large tumor recurrence. Hemostatic radiotherapy was administered, with a limited response. At reevaluation, there was a large abdominal recurrence with liver infiltration. Palliative care was contemplated and the family agreed to it. The patient succumbed to the disease almost 3 years after the initial diagnosis.

DISCUSSION

Undifferentiated pediatric sarcomas are a subset of unclassified soft-tissue tumors comprising approximately 5% of all pediatric sarcomas.[2] Until recently, PMMTI was variably categorized as congenital infantile fibrosarcoma or infantile myofibromatosis. However, specific immunohistochemical stains and genetic analysis have distinguished it from this group and resulted in important advances affecting its treatment and prognosis.

PMMTI is a distinct soft-tissue tumor first described by Alaggio et al. in 2006 usually occurring in the 1st year of life.[1] It is a locally aggressive tumor on the spectrum of myofibroblastic disease having a long, indolent course, and is notable for its local recurrences. However, distant metastases are rare. It commonly involves the somatic soft tissues of the trunk, the head-and-neck region, and the extremities.[3] It very rarely occurs in the retroperitoneum and only one other case has been previously described by Kao et al.[4]

PMMTI was initially included in the category of congenital infantile fibrosarcoma, infantile myofibromatosis, or desmoid fibromatosis due to clinicopathological overlap. Cytogenetic studies include the absence of the t(12;15) ETV6-NTRK3 fusion gene and the lack of mutations in the CTNNB1 gene. These are characteristics of congenital infantile fibrosarcoma and desmoid fibromatosis, respectively, and facilitate its differentiation.[3] Internal tandem duplication of B cell CLL/lymphoma 6 (BCL6)-interacting corepressor (BCOR) exon 15 and YWHAE-NUTM2B fusions have been described recently in PMMTI. Similar fusions have also been noted in some soft-tissue undifferentiated round cell sarcoma and clear cell sarcoma of the kidney.[5]

These tumors are 2–15 cm in size grossly and have a gray-white fleshy surface that is firm peripherally. Microscopically, they are composed of spindled areas and areas of primitive polygonal and round cells embedded in a diffuse myxoid background with a delicate vascular network. The cytoplasm is pale/eosinophilic. Immunohistochemistry is essential in the diagnosis of PMMTI. In this lesion, the cells tend to show positive reactivity to CD99, CD117, and nestin consistent with the primitive nature of the tumor, a positive or diffuse reactivity for vimentin, and negative reactivity to myoid, lipoblastic, histiocytic, and neural markers such as smooth muscle actin, muscle-specific actin, desmin, S-100 protein, or myogenin. It is important to differentiate PMMTI from other sarcomas as the prognosis and management may differ.[3] By far, radical surgical excision which may require partial amputations or extensive dissection is the preferred mode of management. It is imperative to ensure negative margins as the most notable risk factor for local recurrence is margin positivity. Interestingly, in our case, despite negative margins, there was a recurrence. This tumor is unresponsive to chemotherapy, making treatment of primary tumors and recurrences difficult. To date, radiation therapy has not been implemented as a treatment modality and the long-term outcome for these patients is unclear.[5]

CONCLUSION

PMMTI is a rare tumor and the correct diagnosis based on immunohistochemistry helps in prognostication and management. Surgical excision is the cornerstone of treatment; however, the tumor is extremely aggressive and usually fatal.
