Changing Epidemiology of Severe Pneumococcal Pneumonia in Adults at a Tertiary Hospital between 2010 and 2024
Authors: Sang-Ho Choi, Jin Won Huh, Sang-Bum Hong, Heungsup Sung, Kyung-Hyun Do, Sang-Oh Lee, Chae-Man Lim, Younsuck Koh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background The epidemiology of pneumococcal pneumonia can be influenced by vaccine implementation, serotype replacement, and viral circulation. We aimed to investigate 15-year trends in the epidemiological and clinical characteristics of severe pneumococcal pneumonia patients requiring intensive care unit (ICU) admission.Methods We analyzed data from a prospective cohort of adult patients with severe pneumonia admitted to the 28-bed medical ICU of a 2,700-bed tertiary hospital in South Korea between 2010 and 2024. Temporal changes in incidence, resistance to antimicrobial agents, frequency of coinfections, and mortality were evaluated across three 5-year periods.Results Among 2,511 ICU patients with severe pneumonia, 140 (5.6%) had pneumococcal infections. The proportion of pneumococcal cases decreased from 9.9% in 2010–2014 to 4.2% in 2015–2019, and further to 2.2% in 2020–2024 (p<0.001 for trend). Conversely, ceftriaxone non-susceptibility rates rose sharply from 4.8% to 16.7% and 45.5% across the same periods (p<0.001). Coinfections were frequent and increased significantly, largely due to respiratory viruses, with rates of 48.9%, 61.8%, and 77.8%, respectively (p=0.02). Influenza was the predominant co-pathogen during 2015–2019, whereas severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) was most frequent in 2020–2024. The 90-day mortality rates remained stable over time (29.5%, 38.2%, and 27.8%, p=0.64).Conclusion In this single-center ICU cohort, the proportion of severe pneumococcal pneumonia cases decreased over the 15-year period, whereas ceftriaxone non-susceptibility and viral coinfections became more frequent. These trends should be interpreted cautiously given the lack of serotype and individual vaccination data and incomplete susceptibility results. Continued surveillance of pneumococcal disease, antimicrobial resistance, and respiratory coinfections is warranted.

Introduction

Streptococcus pneumoniae remains a leading cause of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) requiring hospitalization and intensive care unit (ICU) admission worldwide [1,2]. The introduction of pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCV10 and PCV13) has substantially reduced the burden of invasive pneumococcal disease, particularly among children, with moderate indirect benefits in adults [3]. However, the emergence and increasing prevalence of non-vaccine serotypes have raised concerns regarding serotype replacement and associated antimicrobial resistance [4-6]. In South Korea, sequential introduction of PCV10, PCV13, and PCV15 and the inclusion of the 23-valent pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine (PPSV23) for older adults have been accompanied by nationwide increases in vaccine coverage [7]. Meanwhile, the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic has further altered respiratory pathogen dynamics through widespread infection-control measures and shifts in viral epidemiology [8-10], potentially influencing the incidence and characteristics of pneumococcal pneumonia. During this period, pneumococcal vaccination was strongly promoted as part of respiratory infection prevention strategies [11,12].

Despite these developments, data on how vaccination, evolving resistance, and pandemic-related changes have collectively affected the epidemiology of severe pneumococcal pneumonia in critically ill adults remain limited. Previous international and Korean studies have largely focused on pneumococcal CAP in general adult populations or invasive pneumococcal disease, particularly with respect to serotype distribution, vaccine impact, and antimicrobial resistance [13-16]. Although ICU-based studies of severe pneumococcal pneumonia have been reported internationally [1,17], longitudinal analyses evaluating temporal changes in incidence, antimicrobial resistance, and respiratory viral coinfections remain scarce. Moreover, to our knowledge, no Korean ICU-based study has evaluated long-term epidemiological changes in severe pneumococcal pneumonia. To address this gap, we analyzed 15-year trends in the incidence, clinical manifestations, antimicrobial resistance, and respiratory coinfections of severe pneumococcal pneumonia among adults admitted to a tertiary medical ICU in South Korea, including temporal changes during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Materials and Methods

1. Study design, setting, and data collection

We analyzed data from a prospective cohort study conducted in the 28-bed ICU of Asan Medical Center, a 2,700-bed tertiary referral hospital in Seoul, South Korea. Since January 2010, all critically ill adults (aged ≥16 years) admitted with a diagnosis of severe pneumonia have been prospectively enrolled in this cohort [18,19]. For the present analysis, we included patients with severe community-acquired pneumococcal pneumonia admitted between January 2010 and December 2024. Demographic characteristics, comorbidities, immunocompromised status, and clinical data—including disease severity scores (Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation [APACHE] II and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment [SOFA]), microbiologic results, and outcomes—were prospectively recorded using a standardized case report form. The primary outcomes were the incidence and 90-day mortality of severe pneumococcal pneumonia.

2. Definitions

Pneumonia was diagnosed based on new pulmonary infiltrates on chest imaging plus ≥2 compatible clinical findings (fever, cough, sputum production, dyspnea, or prescription of antimicrobial therapy). Severe pneumonia was defined as the presence of septic shock requiring vasopressor support or the need for invasive mechanical ventilation [20]. Pneumococcal pneumonia was defined as pneumonia with S. pneumoniae isolated from respiratory specimens (sputum, endotracheal aspirates, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, or pleural fluid) or blood cultures, or detected by urinary antigen testing. CAP was defined as onset before or within 48 hours of hospital admission. Immunocompromised status included solid organ or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, recent cytotoxic chemotherapy within 6 months, or immunosuppressive drug use (including corticosteroids) within the previous month. Appropriate empirical antimicrobial treatment was defined as the administration of one or more in vitro active agents against S. pneumoniae before antimicrobial susceptibility results became available.

3. Microbiological testing

All isolated pneumococcal strains were screened for penicillin resistance with 1 μg of oxacillin disks, and the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of penicillin and ceftriaxone were determined by the E-test (AB Biodisk, Solna, Sweden). Susceptibility status was interpreted according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute breakpoints for S. pneumoniae [21]. Non-meningitis breakpoints were applied for penicillin and ceftriaxone. Respiratory viral pathogens were detected using multiplex reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) assays performed on nasopharyngeal swabs, endotracheal aspirates, or bronchoalveolar lavage specimens. The Anyplex II RV16 Detection Kit (Seegene Inc., Seoul, Korea) was used from 2010 to 2017; the Allplex Respiratory Panel Assay (Seegene Inc.) from 2018 to 2022; and the Bio-Fire FilmArray Pneumonia Panel (BioFire Diagnostics, Salt Lake City, UT, USA) was implemented in July 2023. Bacterial detections by the BioFire FilmArray Pneumonia Panel alone were not considered causative pathogens, except for atypical bacterial pathogens, including Legionella pneumophila, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, and Chlamydia pneumoniae.

4. Statistical analyses

For temporal trend analysis, the 15-year study period was divided into three 5-year phases: period 1 (2010–2014), period 2 (2015–2019), and period 3 (2020–2024). Demographic and clinical characteristics, microbiological findings, antimicrobial resistance, coinfections, and outcomes were compared across periods. Categorical variables were compared across periods using the chi-square test, while trends in continuous variables over time were assessed using linear regression for trend. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a significance threshold set at p<0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software version 29.0 K for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

5. Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Asan Medical Center (IRB No. 2010-0079). The need for informed consent was waived because of the observational design and the use of de-identified clinical information.

Results

1. Enrolled patients and overall pathogen distribution

During the study period, a total of 2,511 adult patients were admitted to the medical ICU with a diagnosis of severe pneumonia, comprising 889 patients in 2010– 2014, 807 patients in 2015–2019, and 815 patients in 2020–2024. Blood cultures and sputum or endotracheal aspirate cultures were routinely performed in nearly all patients throughout the study period, with testing rates ranging from 99.3% to 100% and from 98.7% to 100%, respectively, whereas the pneumococcal urinary antigen test was performed in 90.9%, 95.4%, and 84.5% of patients during 2010–2014, 2015–2019, and 2020–2024, respectively (Supplementary Table S1). One or more pathogens were detected in 1,805 (71.9%) patients. Bacterial pathogens were detected in 945 (37.6%), viral pathogens in 936 (37.3%), fungal pathogens in 294 (11.7%), and mycobacterial pathogens in 66 (2.6%) patients. S. pneumoniae was identified in 140 (5.6%) patients. S. pneumoniae ranked fourth among bacterial pathogens, following Klebsiella pneumoniae (8.2%), Staphylococcus aureus (7.2%), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (6.2%).

2. Demographics and clinical characteristics

Table 1 presents demographic and clinical characteristics of 140 patients with severe pneumococcal pneumonia. The median age was 70.0 years (interquartile range [IQR], 63.0 to 76.0), and 76.4% of the patients were male. The most common underlying condition was diabetes mellitus (32.1%), followed by structural lung disease (24.3%) and solid cancer (21.4%). Thirty-six patients (25.7%) were classified as immunocompromised. The distribution of comorbidities, immunocompromised status did not differ significantly across the three study periods. Pneumococcal pneumonia occurred year-round but was most frequent during the winter and spring months.

3. Temporal trends in incidence, clinical manifestations, and antimicrobial susceptibility

The number and proportion of severe pneumococcal pneumonia cases declined significantly over time (p<0.001 for trend) (Figure 1). The proportion of pneumococcal cases decreased from 9.9% (88/889) in 2010–2014 to 4.2% (34/807) in 2015–2019, and further to 2.2% (18/815) in 2020–2024 (p<0.001 for trend). Table 2 summarizes the clinical manifestations, laboratory findings, and antimicrobial susceptibility. Most patients (97.1%) required mechanical ventilation, and 69.3% presented with septic shock at ICU admission. The median APACHE II and SOFA scores were 26.0 (IQR, 22.0 to 32.0) and 10.0 (IQR, 8.0 to 13.0), respectively. Pneumococcal bacteremia was present in 22.1% of cases. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed in 77 culture-positive cases (55.0%). Penicillin non-susceptibility rates increased from 16.7% in 2010–2014 to 25.0% and 36.4% in 2015–2019 and 2020–2024, respectively (p=0.14 for trend); ceftriaxone non-susceptibility rates increased markedly from 4.8% to 16.7% and 45.5%, respectively (p<0.001 for trend). When stratified by recent healthcare exposure, penicillin and ceftriaxone non-susceptibility rates were numerically higher among patients with recent healthcare exposure, but the differences were not statistically significant. Ceftriaxone MICs were measured by E-test in 46 isolates, and the corresponding MIC histogram is shown in Figure 2. Levofloxacin non-susceptibility was 9.8% in 2010–2014 and 8.3% in 2015–2019, and then showed an increasing trend to 36.4% in 2020–2024.

4. Temporal changes in coinfection rate

The distribution of respiratory co-pathogens is summarized in Table 3. The overall coinfection rate increased significantly over time, from 48.9% in 2010–2014 to 61.8% in 2015–2019 and 77.8% in 2020–2024 (p=0.02 for trend). This increase was mainly driven by viral coinfections, which rose from 37.5% to 55.9% and 61.1%, respectively (p=0.02 for trend). Rhinovirus predominated from 2010 to 2014, whereas influenza virus and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) were the leading co-pathogens from 2015 to 2019 and 2020 to 2024, respectively. Among six patients with severe pneumococcal pneumonia after implementation of the BioFire FilmArray Pneumonia Panel in July 2023, one had respiratory viruses detected; both endemic human coronavirus (HCoV) and influenza A virus were positive by the BioFire FilmArray Pneumonia Panel and by respiratory viral multiplex polymerase chain reaction. Bacterial coinfections were present in 20.0% of cases, showing an increasing trend over time (p=0.053 for trend). P. aeruginosa and S. aureus were the most common bacterial co-pathogens, and P. aeruginosa was co-identified in 27.8% of cases during 2020–2024.

5. Empirical antimicrobial treatment and outcomes

In the 140 patients with severe pneumococcal CAP, β-lactam plus fluoroquinolone (62.9%, n=88) was the most frequently used empirical regimen, followed by glycopeptide plus β-lactam plus fluoroquinolone (17.9%, n=25), β-lactam plus azithromycin (5.0%, n=7), and glycopeptide plus β-lactam (4.3%, n=6). Among the 77 culture-positive patients in whom the appropriateness of empirical antimicrobial treatment could be assessed, the rate of inappropriate empirical treatment was 7.1% in 2010–2014, 4.2% in 2015–2019, and 36.4% in 2020–2024, showing a significant increase in the most recent period (p=0.03). Table 4 summarizes the main outcomes of the 140 patients. The overall 90-day mortality was 31.4%. Mortality rates remained relatively stable across study periods, being 29.5% in 2010–2014, 38.2% in 2015–2019, and 27.8% in 2020–2024 (p=0.64 for trend). Kaplan–Meier survival curve analyses also showed no significant difference among the three periods (p=0.50) (Figure 3). The median duration of ICU stay was 9.0 days (IQR, 5.0 to 20.8), and the median hospital stay was 25.0 days (IQR, 14.0 to 48.3), with no significant temporal change.

Discussion

We investigated temporal trends in the epidemiology and clinical features of severe pneumococcal pneumonia in adults over a 15-year period. The incidence of pneumococcal pneumonia among ICU-admitted patients declined substantially, whereas ceftriaxone non-susceptibility and viral coinfections increased. Despite these shifts, disease severity and 90-day mortality remained stable over time. These findings highlight the evolving nature of pneumococcal infections in the post-vaccine era and underscore the continuing importance of surveillance to guide prevention and treatment strategies, particularly in the context of changing respiratory virus circulation during and after the COVID-19 pandemic.

Our study demonstrated a marked decline in the incidence of severe pneumococcal pneumonia during the study period. This decrease parallels the nationwide implementation of pneumococcal vaccination in South Korea. PCV10 and PCV13 were introduced in 2010 and incorporated into the national childhood immunization program in May 2014, followed by PCV15 in 2023, which replaced PCV10 in April 2024. For adults aged 65 years and older, the PPSV23 was implemented as part of the national vaccination program in May 2013. PCV coverage among children reached approximately 96% in 2024 [7], while PPSV23 coverage among older adults exceeded 60% by 2017 [5]. These high coverage rates likely contributed to the observed reduction in severe pneumococcal infections through both direct protection and herd immunity. In addition, COVID-19 pandemic-related infection-control measures may have further reduced pneumococcal transmission during 2020–2024. The reduced incidence has persisted even after the major waves of COVID-19 subsided, suggesting that both vaccination and strengthened respiratory hygiene practices have contributed to the sustained decline in severe pneumococcal disease.

Our findings should be interpreted in conjunction with those of a previous Korean catchment population-based study by Heo et al. [22] That study covered 2011–2014, which largely corresponded to the first period of our analysis, and reported that pneumococcal pneumonia accounted for 14.4% of medically attended CAP, with an estimated annual incidence of 42.2 to 49.4 cases per 100,000 persons; the annual incidence of invasive pneumococcal disease ranged from 4.1 to 6.5 cases per 100,000 persons. Similarly, in our study, the proportion of pneumococcal pneumonia among ICU-admitted patients with severe pneumonia was highest during 2010–2014, at 9.9%. Together, these findings suggest that pneumococcal disease represented a substantial clinical burden in Korea during the early 2010s. The apparent difference in trends may be explained by differences in study design and denominator: Heo et al. [22] estimated population-based incidences of pneumococcal diseases, whereas our study evaluated the relative contribution of S. pneumoniae among ICU-admitted patients with severe pneumonia over a longer, 15-year period.

Although the incidence of severe pneumococcal pneumonia has declined, resistance to penicillin and ceftriaxone has increased markedly. Several studies have demonstrated that, although the overall incidence of pneumococcal infections decreased following the introduction of PCV13, infections caused by non-vaccine serotypes have increased relatively, and this shift has been linked to increasing antimicrobial resistance [3-6,23]. In England and Wales, for example, a British group demonstrated a rapid increase in non-vaccine serotypes such as 8, 15A, and 23B after the introduction of PCV13, many of which exhibited multidrug resistance [6]. Likewise, the Pneumococcal Serotype Replacement and Distribution Estimation (PSERENADE) global analysis involving >100,000 invasive isolates showed sustained reductions in vaccine-type disease but increasing proportions of non-vaccine serotypes associated with β-lactam resistance [3]. A recent Korean study reported ceftriaxone non-susceptibility in 34.8% of 92 pneumococcal isolates collected in 2015 [24]. Another large-scale Korean study, analyzing 411 pneumococcal isolates from 16 institutions between 2017 and 2019, revealed that non-PCV13 and non-PPSV23 serotypes accounted for 71.3% and 40.6%, respectively. Among 96 multidrug-resistant isolates, non-PCV13 serotypes 23A, 11A, and 15B accounted for high proportions (18.8%, 16.7%, and 8.3%, respectively). Major multidrug-resistant non-PPSV23 serotypes included 23A (18.8%) and 23B (7.3%) [25]. These findings suggest that growing burden of antimicrobial resistance could be linked to the expanding circulation of non-vaccine serotypes. Our study adds to this evidence by demonstrating these trends specifically among adults with severe pneumococcal pneumonia, a population that has been less well characterized in longitudinal clinical studies.

We found that the rates of coinfection, particularly viral coinfection, increased significantly over time. Influenza virus and SARS-CoV-2 were the most common co-pathogens during the periods of 2015–2019 and 2020–2024, respectively. During 2020–2024, SARS-CoV-2 was co-identified with S. pneumoniae in 27.8% of cases, consistent with previous reports that S. pneumoniae can be identified as a bacterial co-pathogen in patients with COVID-19. This finding suggests that the predominant circulating respiratory viruses in each period appeared to play a notable role in the occurrence of pneumococcal pneumonia. Coinfection between influenza virus and S. pneumoniae has long been recognized as a classic example of viral–bacterial synergy, in which influenza-mediated epithelial damage, altered mucociliary clearance, and upregulation of host adhesion receptors facilitate pneumococcal invasion [26,27]. Recent studies have also demonstrated that S. pneumoniae remains a frequent bacterial co-pathogen in patients hospitalized with COVID-19 [28,29]. In South Korea, the 2015–2019 period was marked by relatively high influenza activity, with approximately 3.7 times more influenza cases compared to 2010–2014 [30]. This surge may have contributed to the increased frequency of influenza–pneumococcus coinfection during that time. During the COVID-19 pandemic, widespread mask use, social distancing, and travel restrictions markedly reduced the circulation of respiratory viruses such as influenza and respiratory syncytial virus [8,31,32]. These measures likely led to a increase in viral–bacterial coinfections overall. Together, these observations underscore the dynamic interplay between respiratory viruses and S. pneumoniae and highlight the importance of coordinated surveillance and vaccination strategies addressing both pneumococcal and viral pathogens to reduce the burden of severe pneumonia.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single tertiary care center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other hospitals or community settings with different patient populations. Second, pneumococcal isolates were not available, precluding serotype determination and molecular characterization that could clarify the relationship between emerging non-vaccine serotypes and resistance. Third, changes in diagnostic practices over the study period may have affected the estimated incidence of severe pneumococcal pneumonia and viral coinfections. The use of the pneumococcal urinary antigen test was slightly lower during 2020–2024 than in earlier periods, which may have influenced the detection of pneumococcal pneumonia. In addition, the BioFire FilmArray Pneumonia Panel was implemented in July 2023 and may have affected respiratory virus detection; however, as described in the Results, no respiratory virus was detected solely by the BioFire FilmArray Pneumonia Panel after its implementation, suggesting that its introduction was unlikely to have substantially influenced the observed viral coinfection rate. Finally, as only about half of the cases yielded cultured isolates, antimicrobial susceptibility testing was restricted to these cases, which may have introduced selection bias and affected the estimated resistance rates. Culture-positive patients, particularly those with bacteremia or a high bacterial burden, may differ clinically from patients diagnosed solely by urinary pneumococcal antigen testing. Therefore, the observed antimicrobial resistance rates may have been overestimated and should not be interpreted as representing all patients with severe pneumococcal pneumonia.

In conclusion, severe pneumococcal pneumonia accounted for a decreasing proportion of ICU-admitted cases of severe pneumonia over the 15-year study period, whereas antimicrobial non-susceptibility and respiratory coinfections were increasingly observed. These trends should be interpreted cautiously because serotype, vaccination, and complete susceptibility data were unavailable. Ongoing surveillance remains important in this changing epidemiological context.
