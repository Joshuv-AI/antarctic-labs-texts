Real-world estrogen receptor alpha 1 (ESR1) testing patterns and results for ER+/HER2- metastatic breast cancer in the United States, 2018–2024
Authors: Jane Meisel, Tim Pham, Clara Chen, Ayush Kris, James Roose
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo understand historical and recent ESR1 testing rates, and when ESR1 mutations emerge during first-line (1 L) treatment.MethodsThis retrospective, observational cohort study used the Flatiron Health Research Database (FHRD) and the Flatiron Health-Foundation Medicine metastatic breast cancer (mBC) Clinico-Genomic Database (CGDB). Adult patients with a confirmed diagnosis of hormone receptor-positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative mBC from 1/1/2018 to 6/30/2024 were included. ESR1 testing patterns and test results were descriptively analyzed.ResultsAmong 7772 patients with mBC in the FHRD who initiated 1 L therapy, tumor ESR1 mutation status was evaluated for 222 (3%) patients at baseline (≤ 90 days before 1 L) and 1355 (17%) during 1 L. The percentage of patients who had an ESR1 test result reported during 1 L increased over time (11% in 2018-19, 19% in 2020-21, 22% in 2022-24). Median time from 1 L start to first ESR1 test was 7.4 months (mos) among tested patients. A positive test result was reported for 29/222 (13%) patients tested at baseline and 240/1355 (18%) tested during 1 L. Most (60%) tests during 1 L used tissue specimens, while the remaining 40% were liquid biopsies, and the median time from specimen collection to result reporting in 1 L was 28 (IQR:10–84) days. Focusing on time periods wherein specimens were provided, ESR1 test positivity was 6.7% (76/1,127) for specimens provided at baseline, 23% (15/65) for specimens provided 9 to 12 months into 1L therapy, 38% (26/69) for those provided 15 to 18 months into 1L, and 40% (38/94) for those provided from 18 to 24 months into 1L.ConclusionsESR1 mutations can be detected at any time interval during 1L.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10549-026-08060-x.

Introduction

Female breast cancer (BC) is the second most common cancer: in 2025, an estimated 316,950 women in the US will be diagnosed with BC, and an estimated 42,170 women will die from the disease [1–3]. Estrogen receptor alpha (ER⍺), encoded by the ESR1 gene, and its cognate ligand estrogen are key factors in the development and progression of luminal breast cancers. Breast cancers characterized by ER expression (ER+), and a lack of HER2 overexpression or amplification (ER+/HER2-), are the most common BC subtype and represent approximately 70% of all BC cases, and after relapse, account for most BC-related deaths [4, 5].

Endocrine therapies (ET) that disrupt ER signaling in ER-positive breast cancers are effective targeted treatments in both early-stage and metastatic BC (mBC) [2, 6]. However, many patients eventually develop resistance to ET and progress on first-line (1 L) therapy within 2–3 years [7–9]. Mutations in the ESR1 gene, particularly in the hormone binding domain, have been shown to be associated with development of resistance to ET and with poor prognosis and worse outcomes [10–14], but are rare at mBC diagnosis [15], and detected in less than 5% of patients at the start of 1 L. However, among patients with recurrent disease previously treated with aromatase inhibitor (AI) or who are resistant to ET, and among those with exposure to ET in the first-line setting, ESR1 mutations (ESR1m) can be detected in approximately 40% of patients [16, 17].

The prognostic and predictive implications of ESR1 mutations are studied in the literature and have driven the approval of novel SERDs in 2 L [18]. In 2023 the FDA approved elacestrant, an oral Selective Estrogen Receptor Degrader (SERD) for postmenopausal women or adult men with ER+/HER2-, ESR1-mutated, advanced or mBC with disease progression following at least 1 line of ET, and approved the Guardant360 CDx assay as a companion diagnostic device based on results from the randomized, open-label, active-controlled, multicenter EMERALD trial (NCT03778931) [19, 20]. Novel oral SERDs promise greater ER-degrading potential in patients compared with the first approved SERD fulvestrant [21]. Treatment guidelines were updated in 2023 to recommend evaluating ESR1m status using next-generation sequencing (NGS) or by assessing circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) using PCR testing at progression following prior lines of ET [22]. The oral SERD Imlunestrant was approved for the same indication in 2025 [23]. Recently, patients in the randomized phase II SERENA-2 study (NCT04214288) that received the next generation SERD camizestrant showed significantly longer PFS versus fulvestrant among patients with ER+/HER2- mBC who had previously progressed on ET, including among patients with ESR1m [24].

Switching treatment upon detection of an ESR1m before radiologic progression represents an alternative treatment strategy. The proof-of-concept phase 3 PADA-1 study (NCT03079011) demonstrated that switching from AI to fulvestrant while maintaining the CDK4/6i palbociclib when an ESR1m is detected in ctDNA significantly improved PFS [25], while patients who received fulvestrant plus palbociclib as 2 L therapy in a crossover cohort had short PFS. Results from a preplanned interim analysis of the phase 3, double-blind, randomized SERENA-6 trial (NCT04964934), which evaluates a strategy of switching endocrine therapy upon emergence of ESR1m detected in ctDNA during 1 L treatment, demonstrated that switching to camizestrant while maintaining the CDK4/6i led to a significant improvement in PFS from the time of randomization (i.e., at the time of ESR1m detection in the absence of progression) compared with continued AI plus CDK4/6i (Hazard Ratio:0.44; 95% CI:0.31–0.60; p < 0.0001) [26].

A growing body of clinical evidence highlights the potential value of detecting ESR1m prior to radiologic progression, and recent clinical trials have explored mutation-guided switching approaches aimed at improving disease control prior to radiologic progression. However, real-world data on ESR1 testing and results, including on the types and timing of testing conducted, timing of ESR1m emergence during 1 L therapy, and the associations between ESR1 testing and results with patient characteristics is limited. Understanding real-world testing patterns and patient characteristics will help identify the most effective testing frequency and duration for detecting early resistance to the 1 L standard of care (AI+CDK4/6i) and build awareness of the dynamics of ESR1m to guide treatment strategies in the 1 L setting. This study aims to understand recent and historical ESR1 testing rates, when ESR1m emerge during 1 L, and the patient characteristics with ESR1m detected during 1 L therapy.

Methods

Study design, data source, and population

This retrospective, observational cohort study used the deidentified US-based, electronic health record (EHR)-derived Flatiron Health Research Database (FHRD), and, for select analyses, the Flatiron Health-Foundation Medicine mBC Clinico-Genomic Database (FH-FMI CGDB) (Supplemental Methods) [27–29]. Patients in the FHRD who had a diagnosis of ER+/HER2- mBC (de novo or recurrent) between January 1, 2018 and June 30, 2024 were included in the study. Eligible patients were aged 18 years or older at mBC diagnosis and diagnosed with ER+/HER2-negative BC based on test results closest to mBC diagnosis among results reported up to 60 days after mBC diagnosis. Patients were excluded if they had evidence of another non-BC diagnosis in the 90 days before mBC diagnosis date, received a clinical study drug before or after mBC diagnosis, or had evidence indicating missing treatment data at initiation of mBC therapy. The FH-FMI CGDB was used only for the secondary study population of patients diagnosed with ER+/HER2- mBC from 2018 to March 2024 whose tumors underwent Foundation Medicine (FMI) comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) (i.e., NGS) after mBC diagnosis (Supplemental Methods).

Data were captured from initial diagnosis until the end of the patient’s record, death, or the date of data cutoff (September 30, 2024), whichever was earliest. The pre-index period included all patient data from before the date of mBC diagnosis (the index date), including neoadjuvant and adjuvant therapies, and characteristics at initial diagnosis. The follow-up period was from the mBC diagnosis date to the date of death, end of patient-follow-up or data cutoff. Where applicable, the baseline period refers to the 90-day period before and including the start of first-line therapy for mBC (Supplemental Figure. 1).

Variables and endpoints

Two distinct dates associated with each ESR1 test were used for different analytical purposes (Supplemental Methods). The result date, (i.e., the date the ESR1 test result was reported), was used to characterize real-world clinical testing patterns and clinical practice. The specimen collection date, defined as the date on which a patient’s tissue or blood sample was obtained (regardless of when the sample was tested and result reported), was used in analyses evaluating the timing of ESR1m emergence relative to the start of therapy, to better reflect changing tumor biology. Testing may have been conducted on non-contemporaneous samples (e.g., archived samples collected at diagnosis or before start of 1 L).

In the primary study population, ESR1 test data, including timing of specimen collection and test results, test type, and laboratory name were abstracted from unstructured EHR data. Consistent with previous clinical studies [17, 23], only results that indicated a mutation (e.g., deletion/insertion, substitution, point/missense mutation, truncation) were considered ESR1m; variants of unknown significance, amplifications, and rearrangements were not. Patient characteristics and treatment data are described in the Supplemental Methods.

In the secondary study population only, ESR1 test data and results were linked directly from FMI NGS test data. Tumors were considered to harbor ESR1m if any of the following 11 short variant missense mutations within the ligand-binding domain considered in clinical studies were detected: D538G, Y537S, E380Q, Y537C, L536H, L536R, L536P, Y537N, Y537D, S463P, and V422del [17, 23].

Positivity calculations

Test positivity was assessed based on result date (to indicate the proportion of patient tumors known to have a mutation at key milestones in their treatment), as well as based on specimen collection date (regardless of when the test was conducted and the result was made available, to understand the patient’s tumor biology and prevalence of ESR1m as-of the date of specimen collection). Co-mutation rates with other actionable biomarkers (BRCA, PIK3CA, AKT1, PTEN) were reported as a percentage among the patients whose tumor harbored ESR1m and for whom a known result (i.e., neither indeterminate nor pending) was available for the other marker of interest. Emergence of ESR1m was reported based on the date of specimen collection relative to 1 L start date in 3–6 month time intervals, and excluding patients in whose tumor an ESR1m was already detected in an earlier interval.

Statistical methods/analyses

This study was descriptive, no formal hypothesis testing was conducted, and descriptive statistics were used for all analyses (Supplemental Methods). Bias due to missing data was minimized via collection of information from unstructured sources (i.e., review of clinical notes). When assessing emergence of ESR1m over time, the date of specimen collection was used, as this reflects changing tumor biology over time, rather than retesting of a non-contemporaneous specimen. To understand whether representativeness of tested patients presented a risk of bias to the study, characteristics of patients who were tested and not-tested were summarized. Statistical analyses were implemented using R statistical software version 4.1.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [28].

Results

Participants

A total of 8,581 patients with mBC were included in the primary study population (Fig. 1), of whom 2,943 (34%) were diagnosed in 2022 or later, and 7,772 (90.6%) had documented 1 L therapy. Among the 7,772 patients with evidence of treatment, 3,263 (42%) received AI+CDK4/6i as 1 L therapy for mBC (Supplemental Figure. 2). A total of 2,312 patients with mBC from the secondary study population were included (Supplemental Table 1).

Patient characteristics

Patient characteristics for the primary study population are described in Table 1. The median age of patients was 66 (IQR:57–75) years, 98.6% were female, 64% were White, 77% received care in the community setting, and 16% had evidence of liver metastases at mBC diagnosis. ESR1 testing patterns

Among the 8,581 patients in the primary study population, 2,917 (34%) patients had evidence of an ESR1 test with a result reported during the pre-index or follow-up period (Table 2). Among the 7,772 patients who initiated 1 L therapy, 222 (2.9%) had an ESR1 test result reported within the 90 days before 1 L therapy start, 1,355 (17%) were tested during 1 L therapy, and 1,086 (14%) during 1 L therapy and before the first clinician-documented progression. Clinical triggers for ESR1 testing during 1 L were not systematically captured in the EHR data. In routine practice, testing may be prompted by a range of factors, including clinical suspicion of progression, emerging symptoms, or physician preference; however, these drivers could not be evaluated in the current analysis. Among 4,207 patients who initiated 2 L therapy, 993 (24%) were tested on or before 2 L therapy start date (Table 2). The median time from specimen collection to result date during 1 L was 28 days (IQR:10–84).

ESR1 testing patterns overall, and by year of metastatic breast cancer diagnosis

The percentage of patients whose tumor was tested during 1 L increased over time, from 11% among those diagnosed in 2018-19 to 19% in 2020-21 and 22% in 2022-24, even before accounting for shorter follow-up in recent years. Focusing on the specimen collection date rather than the date the test was reported, most patients (88%) did not provide a specimen for ESR1 testing during 1 L, and among the 963 patients that did, 90% provided 1 specimen and 10% provided 2 or more specimens. Among patient tumors tested during 1 L therapy, 69% (935/1355) were tested within 60 days of specimen collection. Across 1,595 tests conducted during 1 L therapy, tissue (60% vs. 39% liquid) specimens were the most common specimen type, and most (96%) were NGS tests, while 3% were RNASeq, and 1% other modalities. The most common testing providers were Foundation Medicine (29%), and Guardant (25%) (Supplemental Table 2).

Among the 120 patients in the secondary study population (for whom deeper genomic data was available) whose tumors were found to harbor ESR1m based on a test result reported during 1 L, the most common ESR1 variants were D538G (52, 43%), Y537S (36, 30%), and E380Q (28, 23%). Multiple variants were observed in 23 (19%) patients, most commonly D538G, Y537S (3, 2.5%). No alteration captured on the NGS panel (beyond the 11 considered to represent ESR1m in this study) was observed in > 1% of patients (Supplemental Table 3).

Result timing

Median time from mBC diagnosis to first ESR1 test among patients in the primary study population was 8.2 (IQR:2.0-22.8) months. Among tested patients, the median time from 1 L therapy start to first ESR1 test (during 1 L or a later line) was 7.4 (IQR:1.1–21.4) months overall, and 18.9 (IQR:6.7–38.2) months for patients diagnosed in 2018-19, decreasing to 8.4 (IQR:1-22.5) months for those diagnosed in 2020-21, and 1.7 (IQR:0.4–7.7) months for those diagnosed in 2022-24 (Table 2).

ESR1 test positivity

A positive test result was reported for 29/222 (13%) patients tested at baseline and 240/1355 (18%) patients tested during 1 L therapy. Among the subset who initiated 2 L therapy, 185/993 (19%) tested positive before 2 L therapy start. Of the 5,127 patients with recurrent mBC who initiated 1 L therapy, 48% (2,437/5,127) had evidence of receiving AI in the neoadjuvant or adjuvant setting, among whom 3% (76/2,437) had their tumors tested within 90 days before 1 L therapy start, of which 22% (17/76) were found to harbor ESR1 m. Positivity was 0% (0 positive/10 tested) among 219 patients who received prior chemotherapy, 9% (5/57) among 1,935 without evidence of neoadjuvant/adjuvant therapy, and 8% (5/64) among 2,645 diagnosed with de novo mBC (Table 3). Positivity was 10% (2/20 among 720 patients with < 1 year of prior neoadjuvant or adjuvant ET, 7% (1/14) among 569 patients with 1–2 years of prior ET, and 21% (12/58) among patients with 2 or more years of prior ET (Supplemental Table 4). Among the 240 patients whose tumors were found to harbor ESR1m while receiving 1 L therapy, co-mutations detected included PIK3CA (38%, 58/153 tested), AKT1 (4%, 6/141 tested), BRCA1 and/or BRCA2 (10%, 14/143 tested), and PTEN (2.8%, 1/36 tested with a tissue sample) (Supplemental Table 5).

Emergence of ESR1 mutations

Based on the date each tested specimen was provided (regardless of when the test result became available), ESR1 test positivity was 6.7% (76/1,127) for specimens provided at baseline, and increased to 23% (15/65) for specimens provided 9 to 12 months into 1 L therapy, then to 38% (26/69) for those provided from 15 to 18 months into 1 L therapy, and further to 40% (38/94) for those provided from 18 to 24 months into 1 L therapy (Fig. 2; Supplemental Table 6).

Characteristics of patients with and without ESR1 test during 1 L

Patients whose tumors underwent ESR1 testing during 1 L therapy (17.4%, 1,355/7,772) compared to those not tested were of a similar age (median age 64 vs. 66 years), treated more often in community practices (83% vs. 76%), and had a higher proportion with ECOG performance status of 0 at 1 L therapy start (35% vs. 27%). Demographic factors, such as sex, race, socioeconomic status, and ethnicity, between the tested and untested groups, and clinical characteristics, such as menopausal status and presence of liver metastases at diagnosis, were numerically similar (Table 1). When comparing the characteristics of patients whose tumors had ESR1m to those with a negative ESR1 test result during 1 L, no differences were observed in race, liver metastases, and menopausal status (Supplemental Table 7).

Discussion

This is the first real-world study that aimed to understand recent and historical ESR1 testing rates, when ESR1m emerge during 1 L therapy, and the characteristics of patients with ESR1 mutated ER+/HER2- mBC. Testing rates were low during 1 L, and remained low in recent years, though testing early during 1 L therapy has increased in the most recent period studied (2022 and later). Although the shorter median time from 1 L start date to first ESR1 test in the recent time period reflects shorter follow-up, the share of patients with a test observed was also higher (22% for those diagnosed in 2022–2024 versus 19% in 2020–2021). Taken together, these findings indicate that at least as many patients’ tumors were tested in the first 1.7 months of 1 L therapy among those diagnosed in 2022–2024 as in the first 8.4 months of 1 L therapy for those diagnosed in 2020–2021. ESR1 test positivity increased over the course of 1 L therapy, with higher positivity in specimens collected at later time intervals, consistent with longer exposure to endocrine therapy. While positivity was lower early in 1 L compared to later (8.5% at 0–6 months vs. 40% at 18–24 months; Supplemental Table 6), ESR1m were nonetheless observed in specimens collected across all time intervals, indicating that emergence can occur throughout the course of treatment. These findings highlight the dynamic nature of ESR1m emergence in real-world practice. The findings of this study indicate that ESR1m can emerge at any point during 1 L treatment, suggesting that repeated, frequent ESR1 testing can help detect ESR1m early, consistent with the SERENA-6 trial results, where 50% of ESR1m were detected with 1 test, and up to 90% of ESR1m were detected with 2–5 tests [23]. These findings are consistent with emerging interest in ctDNA-based monitoring approaches to detect ESR1m during 1 L therapy.

Identifying ESR1m during 1 L therapy may help inform treatment strategies prior to overt disease progression, with the potential to optimize the duration of clinical benefit in 1 L. Disease progression has been associated with deterioration in PRO and HRQoL, highlighting the importance of strategies that may delay progression. In this context, findings from the SERENA-6 trial demonstrated that switching to camizestrant in 1 L upon detection of ESR1m was associated with a significant improvement in PFS (HR = 0.44 p < 0.0001), and PFS2 (HR 0·63 p = 0·0037), and a delay to deterioration in PROs and HRQoL compared with continued AI plus CDK4/6 [21, 27–30]. While some methodological concerns regarding the interpretation of PFS2 have been raised [31], early mutation-guided switching strongly aligns with the paradigm of anticipatory adaptive oncology, aiming to intercept endocrine resistance early and maximize 1 L disease control [32]. However, the optimal timing of ESR1 testing remain to be established [33].

Because ESR1m emerge over time due to exposure to ET, it is important to consider both the date on which a specimen was collected (i.e., the specimen collection date), as well as the date the test result became available (i.e., the result date). For a quarter of tests reported during 1 L, more than 12 weeks elapsed between specimen collection and result date. To ensure the findings of this study reflected both testing patterns (i.e., when testing was conducted), and ESR1 test positivity (i.e., the percentage of patients whose tumors harbor a mutation at a given point in time), both specimen collection date and result date were used in the analyses as appropriate.

Patients whose tumors underwent ESR1 testing were more likely to be treated in community (non-academic) oncology practices compared with those not tested (83% vs. 76%; Table 1). However, as the underlying database was predominantly composed of patients treated in community setting, and the study was not designed to evaluate determinants of testing, these descriptive differences should be interpreted with caution. Observed differences may also reflect variation in documentation or data capture across practice setting rather than true differences in clinical practice.

The findings presented in this study are approximately consistent with the kinetics studies of PADA-1, which found that cumulative incidence of ESR1m was around 40% before or at progression, but instantaneous incidence was uneven over time, with fewer mutations detected before 6 months and after 30 months compared to the period from 6 to 30 months [34]. Although not a perfect analog as the number of specimens tested is small and possibly not representative, in this study 9% of specimens collected in the first 6 months into 1 L therapy harbored ESR1m, and positivity increased to as high as 40% in patients tested from 18 to 24 months into 1 L, before decreasing later in 1 L. This reinforces the importance of ESR1m as a mechanism of endocrine resistance during 1 L therapy but also highlights that there are other mechanisms of resistance. A comprehensive evaluation of the relative contribution of different resistance mechanisms and their implications for clinical outcomes was beyond the scope of this study.

Limitations

Testing for ESR1 in much of the study period was not standard of care and presumably not intended to monitor for ESR1m emergence. The potential reasons for ESR1 testing and treatment changes (other than progression) were not captured, and could include assessing eligibility for a trial or the presence of other actionable biomarkers (e.g., PIK3CA, AKT1, PTEN). Most patients’ tumors were never tested, and among those tested, most were tested only once. The majority of tests used tissue specimens which may be less sensitive than the guideline-recommended liquid specimens which enable less-invasive, serial monitoring of ctDNA [19, 35]. There were few specimens collected in some time periods studied in 1 L, limiting precision of positivity estimates. As this was not a surveillance study, tested tumors cannot be assumed to be a representative sample and positivity rates should be understood as prevalence among those tested. If tumors were tested because of worsening disease or signals of endocrine resistance that were not followed by a treatment change nor documented as progression, then ESR1 positivity during 1 L reported here may be an overestimate. Indeed, higher positivity was observed at baseline than in other studies [11, 17, 29, 36], possibly reflecting rapid progression on prior adjuvant AI, while positivity of specimens collected later in treatment here was consistent with kinetics in PADA-1 [34]. Lastly, real-world progression (i.e., clinician documentation indicating worsening of disease or increased tumor size) [37] was used to define ESR1 testing before 1 L progression.

Conclusion

These findings indicate that ESR1m can be detected at any time interval during 1 L therapy, with the probability of detecting ESR1m increasing over time. The low rates of testing observed during 1 L highlight an opportunity for improved testing surveillance to identify patients whose tumors harbor ESR1m and are resistant to AI therapy. Doing so can inform appropriate treatment selection to delay disease progression and improve patient outcomes.
