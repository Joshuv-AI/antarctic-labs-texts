Research Participation During a National Educational Disruption: Insights From a Natural Experiment in Medical Education
Authors: Joon Sung Shin, Yeon-Ju Huh, Narae Yoon, Hyun Bae Yoon, Joo Youn Oh, Sun Jung Myung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmersive research experiences are widely recognized as valuable for medical students. However, opportunities for extended research are often constrained by dense curricula. In 2024, the Republic of Korea experienced an inevitable nationwide suspension of medical education for about one year, allowing some students to engage in full-time research. From this unique situation, the present study aimed to explore the medical students’ motivations for research participation, their self-perceived outcomes, and the types of institutional support they have identified as necessary to ensure equitable engagement.MethodsA mixed-methods design was employed. Survey 1 collected data from the entire student body during the leave of absence to investigate research participation rates and support needs. Survey 2 examined the research experiences of students who conducted full-time research. Focus group interviews were conducted with purposively selected student representatives, and the integrated results were analyzed thematically.ResultsA total of 99 students responded to the survey (response rate: 16.9%), and 25 (25.3%) reported participating in research during their leave of absence. Students motivated by intrinsic interest engaged for longer periods and reported greater self-perceived competency gains, particularly in data analysis and communication, whereas non-participants most often cited barriers to access and a need for more structured information. Thematic analysis of focus group interviews underscored the pivotal role of mentorship, early curricular exposure, and faculty role modeling.ConclusionFull-time, faculty-supervised research during an educational disruption provided transformative learning opportunities across motivation types. Embedding structured, early research exposure into the curriculum and ensuring transparent access to opportunities may enhance equity, strengthen professional identity formation, and support the sustainability of the clinician–scientist pipeline.Keywords: Research Participation, Faculty-Supervised Research, Mentorship, Professional Identity Formation, Educational Disruption, Medical Education

INTRODUCTION

Engagement in research has long been recognized as an integral component of medical education.1 Research participation not only strengthens scientific reasoning, data analysis, and critical appraisal skills but also deepens understanding of the principles underpinning evidence-based practice.2,3 Beyond technical competencies, research experiences contribute to professional identity formation, allowing students to envision roles as clinician–scientists and to build mentorship relationships that can shape career trajectories.4,5,6

Internationally, structured programs illustrate these benefits: intercalated degrees in the United Kingdom, optional research years in the United States, and mandatory scholarly projects in Canada have all been shown to increase research productivity, stimulate academic career interest, and enhance confidence in scholarly work.7,8,9,10 Concurrently, it has been observed that experience in voluntary research participation is associated with elevated levels of self-perceived research competence and interest in research participation.11 However, opportunities for extended research remain limited in undergraduate medical education, where the intensity of preclinical and clinical training leaves little room for sustained scholarly activity. As a result, many students interested in research are unable to translate this interest into meaningful projects.12

At our institution, structured research exposure within the formal curriculum is primarily provided through the Undergraduate Research Program (URP), a 10-week block course offered in the second year of the medical curriculum. In contrast, the 2024 leave of absence created protected time for extended, voluntary engagement in faculty-supervised projects, resembling an unstructured research year in duration (mean 8.6 months) and intensity (approximately 30 hours per week) but occurring without a dedicated institutional program or curricular credit.

In 2024, the Republic of Korea experienced an unprecedented disruption in medical education when nationwide disputes over proposed reforms to medical school admissions led most students to take a voluntary leave of absence lasting nearly a year.13 This prolonged suspension generated widespread concern about the continuity of clinical training, professional identity development, and long-term workforce stability.14 At the same time, it created an unusual educational opportunity—a kind of “natural experiment” in which students, temporarily freed from the rigid structure of the curriculum, could pursue activities rarely feasible during ordinary training, including immersive, faculty-supervised research.2

The educational disruption of 2024 underscored both the potential and the challenges of research engagement. Students with prior exposure or established networks accessed research opportunities more readily, while others reported barriers such as limited information about laboratories or a lack of faculty connections. These inequities reflect global patterns, where access to research often depends more on individual initiative and informal mentorship than on systematic institutional support.2,15

The present study examined how medical students from one college in Korea engaged in research during this exceptional period. Specifically, we aimed to identify the motivations that led students to participate, the competencies and professional identity outcomes they perceived as a result, and the institutional supports they considered most necessary to promote equitable participation. By situating these findings within the international discourse on undergraduate medical research, we seek to illustrate how lessons from an unplanned disruption can inform broader curricular innovation. Ultimately, we argue that integrating structured, accessible, and longitudinal research opportunities into medical education—whether during stable or disrupted periods—may be essential to strengthening student development and sustaining the clinician–scientist pipeline worldwide.

METHODS

Study design and setting

This mixed-methods study comprised two components. The quantitative component used surveys to examine the prevalence, characteristics, and outcomes of research participation, while the qualitative component employed focus group interviews to explore motivations, experiences, and perceived challenges that could not be fully captured through surveys.

The study was conducted at Seoul National University College of Medicine (SNUCM), which admits approximately 150 students each year into a 2-year premedical program followed by a 4-year medical curriculum. During the 2024 disruption in medical education, 804 out of 893 SNUCM students (90.0%) took a leave of absence, with only 10 to 20 students per grade participating in classes. SNUCM emphasizes research within its curriculum and offers a sequence of research-oriented courses, including a 10-week block course—the URP—in the second year of the medical curriculum.

Quantitative study

Participants

Survey 1 was distributed online to all 585 enrolled medical students between 25 February and 2 March 2025 via Google Forms. The questionnaire included items on students’ life circumstances, mental health, career perspectives, engagement in research, and desired institutional support. It required approximately 15 minutes to complete. Students were invited by text message, and participation was voluntary.

Survey 2 was conducted among students attending a physician–scientist integration workshop held in December 2024. The questionnaire, developed by the research team, consisted of 28 items addressing demographics, motivations for participation, and self-reported outcomes measured on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). The survey required approximately 10 minutes to complete and was administered on a voluntary basis. Sixty-two students responded of these, 52 who had engaged in research during 2024 were included in the analysis. Both surveys were exempted from Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval (IRB No. 2508-165-1672).

Statistical analysis

In Survey 1, demographic characteristics were compared between research participants and non-participants. In Survey 2, respondents were divided into two groups according to their primary motivation: an “interest-motivated” group and a “non-interest-motivated” group, based on prior studies demonstrating that outcomes vary according to intrinsic motivation.16 Comparative analyses were conducted between the two groups regarding demographic and research-related characteristics. For categorical variables, χ2 tests, Fisher’s exact test, or the Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test were used as appropriate. Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and analyzed with either Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Two-tailed P values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Given the exploratory nature of the study, no adjustments were made for multiple comparisons.

Qualitative study

Data collection

Focus group interviews were conducted between April and May 2025 to explore students’ research experiences and their adjustment following the 2024 leave of absence. Purposeful sampling identified one student representative from each academic year who had served as a class representative either in 2024 or at the time of the study.17 Four sessions were held during lunch breaks, each lasting approximately 1 hour. Interview questions, developed by the research team and reviewed by a medical education expert, focused on motivations for research, perceived benefits, challenges, and support needs. Informed consent was obtained for participation and audio recording. All sessions were moderated by researchers not involved in student evaluation.

Data analysis

Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and analyzed using thematic analysis.18 Meaningful statements were coded, organized into coherent subthemes, and refined into overarching themes. Coding and theme development were conducted iteratively by the research team and further refined through discussion. Three additional meetings with external medical education experts were held to ensure credibility and interpretive rigor.

Ethics statement

The study was exempted from IRB of SNUCM (IRB No. 2508-165-1671). Informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

RESULTS

Characteristics of the research participants (Surveys 1 and 2)

Among the 99 students who responded to Survey 1 (response rate: 16.9%), 25 (25.3%) reported having participated in research. The proportion of female students was higher among research participants than among non-participants (P = 0.044) (Supplementary Table 1).

In Survey 2, among the 52 students who engaged in full-time research in 2024, 37 (71.2%) were male, and the largest proportion were second-year medical students (32.7%) (Table 1). Regarding motivation, 27 students (51.9%) reported pursuing research out of personal interest, 18 (34.6%) cited career advancement or academic aspirations, 6 (11.5%) noted the availability of time, and 1 (1.9%) was influenced by a faculty recommendation. No significant differences in age, sex, or year of study were observed between the interest-motivated and non-interest-motivated groups.

Research activity characteristics (Survey 2)

The mean duration of full-time research was 8.6 months (Table 2). According to the results of Survey 2, respondents indicated that they engaged in research activities at SNUCM laboratories without interruption due to regular curricular obligations during their leave of absence (February 2024 to December 2024). The university did not offer any distinct research programs for students on leave during this period, and student participation in research was voluntary. The mean weekly research hours amounted to 30.24 hours, which corresponds to an average of 6.05 hours per day based on a 5-day workweek. The most prevalent research field was basic medical science (50.0%).

Perceived needs for institutional support (Survey 1)

Across all respondents, the most frequently reported need for institutional support was access to information about research laboratories and mentors (49.5%). This was more strongly emphasized by non-participants (56.8%) than by participants (28.0%; P = 0.013) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Research participants, on the other hand, more frequently identified the need for dedicated research space and for workshops or courses to strengthen research skills (P = 0.012 and P = 0.003, respectively).

Thematic analysis of students’ experiences with research participation

Motivations for research participation

Intrinsic academic interest: Students frequently described an intrinsic interest in research as their primary motivation.

“There was no set deadline, so I was able to freely try out the experimental design I’d wanted to test. It took a long time, but I know it was meaningful.”

“I’m looking to get some experience in an industrial setting. Since hospitals also partner with medical device companies, I’d like to experience the industrial side as well.”

Career orientation and professional aspirations: For some students, research engagement was closely tied to long-term career goals, particularly in academia or clinical research.

“I’m aiming to become a clinical professor at a hospital and work on artificial intelligence, so I’ve been doing research.”

Exploratory participation and aptitude testing: Others pursued research to test their aptitude or to gain exposure, even without a strong initial interest. Internships and laboratory experiences provided opportunities for such exploration:

“About a third of my class went to the lab, and only a few of us stayed the whole time. I think it was a great experience. Some friends who had never considered research joined our lab as interns.”

“Most of them realized research wasn’t for them, and some said, ‘I can’t come in on a Sunday and feed cells.’”

Outcomes of research participation

Scholarly skill development and productivity: Several students noted that they were able to complete and publish work that had not been feasible during shorter curricular blocks such as the URP. They highlighted the autonomy to design experiments and write manuscripts as especially rewarding. Others, however, expressed regret about unfinished projects due to uncertainty regarding the duration of the leave.

“URP was too short to finish my manuscript, but during this leave, I continued for about four months and published it.”

“If I had been sure how long the leave would last, I could have completed more projects.”

Mentorship and role modeling in research socialization: Advisors were frequently cited as role models who shaped students’ career aspirations and deepened their understanding of the academic curriculum upon returning to school.

“I’ve been in the lab since premedical school, and I came to really respect my advisor.”

Professional identity formation and confidence building: Students reported that their research experiences contributed to developing their identities as researchers and strengthened their confidence.

“I kept doing my research at the lab where I did URP during my second year, and I also got some hands-on experience working at a startup focused on a related field.”

“When I returned to school, my peers who had been in research seemed much more confident in their field.”

Support factors

Curricular scaffolding through prior research exposure: Prior curricular exposure played a key role in facilitating participation. Students who had completed the URP found it easier to identify laboratories and connect with mentors.

“URP really helped me connect with labs. Third- and fourth-year students have more access because of that course.”

Enhanced access through reduced informational and structural barriers: By contrast, students without such prior experience—particularly those in the early years—struggled due to limited information and fewer connections.

“I was so lucky to be connected to the lab, which made it a breeze for me to participate in the research. I know that pre-med students and lower-year students faced some challenges because they didn’t have that support network.”

“Our seniors were in labs a lot, but my class had fewer connections with professors, so fewer of us joined labs.”

Need for formalized communication and guidance channels: Participants emphasized the need for structured communication mechanisms between faculty and students to promote broader accessibility:

“It would have been helpful if lab information had been shared systematically, perhaps through student representatives.”

“I wish there had been some guidance on how to participate in research activities. If they had provided guidance during my leave of absence, it would have felt like regular school classes.”

DISCUSSION

This study examined how medical students in the Republic of Korea engaged in full-time research during a nationwide disruption of medical education. Our findings imply that participation in faculty-supervised projects—even outside a formal curriculum—can provide substantial educational benefits while also revealing challenges that should be addressed in future program design.

Students who were intrinsically motivated to conduct research generally participated for longer periods and reported stronger gains in competencies such as data analysis and scientific communication. These findings align with international studies showing that motivation is closely associated with research productivity and confidence.2,19 Notably, students who did not initially express a strong interest also reported meaningful learning experiences, including a new appreciation for scholarship and, in some cases, a growing interest in academic careers. This implies that well-designed research opportunities can broaden participation across the student body, transforming uncertainty or passive interest into sustained scholarly engagement.20,21

Research experiences also influenced professional identity formation. While some students came to view themselves more clearly as future scientists, others maintained a primarily clinician-centered identity. This divergence reflects a well-documented tension between clinical and research roles.22 Mentorship and faculty role modeling were critical in bridging this gap, because supervisors not only offered technical guidance but also demonstrated how scholarly work can be integrated into a clinician’s career.22 Such interactions highlight the importance of mentoring relationships in sustaining the physician–scientist pipeline, particularly in contexts in which role models in translational or academic medicine are less visible.23,24,25

In this context, the leave-of-absence research experience contrasted with URP in both length and structure. Whereas URP is a time-limited, curriculum-embedded block, the leave period allowed sustained immersion and iterative work cycles that students perceived as necessary to complete manuscripts. At the same time, because the university did not offer a distinct program for students on leave, access to opportunities appeared to rely heavily on prior exposure and social networks.

This study also underscored inequities in access to research opportunities. Students with prior exposure to structured courses—such as short-term research methodology blocks—were better positioned to transition into full-time projects. By contrast, those lacking information or laboratory connections faced substantial barriers. Similar inequities have been documented globally; access to research often depends more on personal networks than on transparent institutional systems.2,15 To address these disparities, medical schools must provide clear, systematic information about available projects and mentors, as well as infrastructure such as dedicated research space and workshops in essential skills like statistics and communication.19,26 Ensuring that these supports are accessible to all students is essential for equitable participation.15

Approximately one-third of students expressed a shared need for structured research programs, such as internships, regardless of prior research experience. The findings of this study imply that early exposure not only heightens students’ interest but also improves access to research-related information, thereby facilitating further participation. This indicates that extracurricular research programs—in which students voluntarily engage—may serve as catalysts that transform initial curiosity into active involvement. However, as demonstrated by international models, sporadic or short-term experiences alone are insufficient to sustain long-term engagement in research.27,28 Beyond extracurricular opportunities, longitudinal and structured programs—integrated across both preclinical and clinical training—are more effective in fostering sustained participation, carrying meaningful implications for curricular design.

The finding that research participation experience enhances interest in a research career suggests the importance of early exposure to research. In Japan, undergraduate medical students are provided with early research opportunities and career support programs to strengthen the physician-scientist pipeline.29 In Korea, while medical students exhibit a high level of interest in research participation, concerns regarding career stability motivate them to refrain from pursuing research careers.30 However, the prevailing support policies for physician-scientists in Korea predominantly center on residency support or full-time PhD coursework support projects for medical school graduates who have already opted for a research career. Integrated physician-scientist training programs for undergraduate support are operated by only a few medical schools.30

Lee et al.31 likewise noted that early research exposure for undergraduate medical students is necessary for the development of initiative as a countermeasure to the reality of insufficient research participation, despite students’ positive attitudes toward research. Since 2023, SNUCM has administered the Continuum Research Program, a micro-degree that incorporates URP. According to preliminary analysis of the Continuum Research Program, students who engaged with research during the early stages of their medical school curriculum exhibited proficiency in independent research.32 These findings underscore the necessity of curricular design emphasizing early exposure to research curricula.

There are, however, two main limitations of this study. First, it was conducted at a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of its findings. Second, reliance on self-reported data introduces the possibility of recall or social desirability bias. Nonetheless, the use of two complementary surveys provided a broader perspective, combining individual experiences with perceptions of institutional support.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that immersive research during a period of educational disruption can offer transformative learning opportunities. Such experiences not only strengthen technical competencies but also shape professional identity, foster mentorship relationships, and broaden career perspectives. By drawing on these findings when designing research programs at other medical schools, institutions may better support continuity in students’ learning and contribute to strengthening the future clinician-scientist workforce.
