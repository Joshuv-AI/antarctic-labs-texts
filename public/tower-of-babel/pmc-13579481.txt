The Impact of Respiratory Syncytial Virus Undertesting on Estimates of Disease Burden and Vaccine Effectiveness in a Large Integrated Healthcare System
Authors: Bradley K Ackerson, Brandon Chia, Negar Aliabadi, Gabriella Goodwin, Jeff Slezak, Vennis Hong, Qing Liu, Sally Shaw, Sabrina Welsh, Julie A Stern, Banshri Kapadia, Brigitte C Spence, Joseph A Lewnard, Gregg Davis, Evan J Zasowski, Michael Aragones, Ashley Miller, Michael Dutro, Erica Chilson, Elisa Gonzalez, Robin Hubler, Luis Jodar, Bradford D Gessner, Elizabeth Begier, Sara Y Tartof
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfrequent standard-of-care (SOC) testing for respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) in adults with acute respiratory illness (ARI) contributes to underestimated disease burden.MethodsWe compared RSV prevalence and RSV vaccine effectiveness (VE) among 14 044 adults ≥60 years with ARI emergency department (ED)/hospital admissions receiving SOC RSV testing vs those with SOC or salvaged respiratory specimen RSV testing.ResultsRSV was identified in 139 (0.99%) specimens by SOC testing alone vs 483 (3.44%) via SOC combined with salvage testing.ConclusionsPrecision of VE against RSV-related ARI ED/hospital admissions improved substantially with inclusion of salvage testing compared to SOC testing alone (92% [67%–98%] vs 90% [29%–99%]).

The ability of respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) to cause severe disease in adults was first recognized following outbreaks associated with significant morbidity and mortality among adults in long-term care facilities [1]. Subsequently, RSV was found to cause serious illness among healthy, community-dwelling older adults and high-risk younger adults. A landmark study of cohorts of high-risk adults and healthy adults aged ≥65 years in the United States (US) in 2005 followed prospectively for acute respiratory illness (ARI) over 4 seasons found RSV infection in 3%–7% of community-dwelling, healthy older adults, 4%–10% of high-risk adults, and 16% of older adults hospitalized with cardiopulmonary infections [2]. Furthermore, a recent study estimated that RSV infection in adults ≥65 years of age results in approximately 159 000 hospitalizations and 9500–12 700 deaths annually [3].

Estimates of the burden of RSV disease in adults in the US are limited by several factors including infrequent standard-of-care (SOC) testing for RSV, particularly among older adults [4, 5]. A recent study found that only 4.3% of US adults aged ≥65 years with lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI)-related hospitalization received RSV testing across 937 hospitals, and that 78.4% of hospitals performed RSV tests in <25% of LRTI-related hospitalizations [5].

Accurate estimates of RSV disease burden in older adults are needed to assess the potential public health impact of preventive and emerging treatment strategies against RSV disease. Therefore, we assessed RSV prevalence and vaccine effectiveness (VE) from SOC RSV testing alone compared to SOC RSV testing plus testing of salvaged specimens that were initially tested only for influenza and/or COVID-19 in a cohort of adults ≥60 who presented to the emergency department (ED) or were hospitalized for ARI.

METHODS

Study Design, Setting, and Population

This observational, retrospective, cohort study was conducted at Kaiser Permanente Southern California (KPSC), an integrated health-care organization composed of 16 hospitals and 226 medical offices, which provides comprehensive prepaid health services for >4.8 million members across Southern California. KPSC members broadly reflect the racially and sociodemographically diverse Southern California population [6]. All aspects of clinical care received at KPSC facilities, including diagnoses, clinical notes, laboratory tests, vaccinations, and prescriptions, are linked by a unique identifier in the patient's electronic health record (EHR). Insurance claims submitted for reimbursement capture care received from external healthcare providers. Respiratory pathogen testing, including testing targeting only influenza and/or COVID-19 or respiratory pathogen panel testing, is ordered at the discretion of providers at KPSC. We included members aged ≥60 years with an ED or hospital admission for ARI identified by encounter International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes between November 24, 2023 (start of salvage specimen collection) and April 9, 2024 (end of RSV season defined by <3% of swabs testing positive for RSV by PCR for 2 consecutive weeks) (Supplementary Table 1).

SPECIMEN PROCESSING AND TESTING

Standard-of-Care Testing

Nasal/nasopharyngeal (NP) swabs had been collected during routine care ≤14 days before through ≤3 days after the ED/hospital admission and tested by Roche Diagnostics cobas eplex Respiratory Pathogen 2 panel (RP2 panel) multiplexed nucleic acid test for the detection of 16 viral and 2 bacterial targets.

Salvage Respiratory Specimen Testing

Remnant NP swabs that had been collected during routine care ≤14 days before through ≤3 days after the ED/hospital admission and tested by polymerase chain reaction for SARS-CoV-2 and/or influenza but not RSV were identified by research assistants and stored at −20 °C. Specimens that tested negative for SARS-CoV-2 and/or influenza were salvaged for respiratory pathogen testing using the Roche Diagnostics cobas eplex RP2 panel multiplexed nucleic acid respiratory pathogen test.

Vaccination Status

RSV vaccination status was extracted from participants' vaccination records in the KPSC EHR that captures all vaccines administered within the health system, supplemented with data from California's Immunization Registry to which healthcare providers are legally required to report RSV vaccinations. Exposure was any RSV vaccine administered at 16 KPSC hospitals and their associated clinics at no cost based on FDA-authorized indications. Members who received RSV vaccine ≥21 days prior to the ARI ED/hospital admission were considered vaccinated. Recipients of RSV vaccine <21 days before an ARI ED/hospital admission were excluded. This 21-day window was selected to mitigate the risk of including events from adults whose symptoms developed during the 14-day antibody development window and therefore had not yet had adequate time for vaccine to confer protection. As such, an additional 7 days were added to account for viral incubation, symptom onset, and ED/hospital admission [7, 8].

Statistical Analysis

RSV burden was estimated by positive tests in the numerator and all ARI encounters (tested and non-tested) in the denominator, ensuring that changes in RSV positivity reflect true case identification rather than differential testing. The magnitude difference was calculated as the ratio of the RSV positivity using SOC and salvage testing vs SOC testing alone (Table 2).

As described previously, RSV VE against ARI ED/hospital admission was estimated via a test-negative case control design, comparing the odds of prior RSV vaccination between individuals testing positive for RSV as cases and individuals testing negative for RSV as controls [9, 10]. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% CI were calculated using multivariable logistic regression and the Wald method, respectively. Prespecified and model-specified covariates included age, sex, self-reported race/ethnicity, encounter month, modified Charlson Comorbidity Index, and healthcare use in the year prior to the ARI ED/hospital admission. Vaccine effectiveness was calculated as [1 − OR] × 100%. VE was estimated against RSV-related ARI ED/hospital admissions using 2 study populations: those tested for RSV via SOC and, separately, those tested for RSV via SOC or salvaged specimens. Participants were required to have ≥1 year of health plan membership to assess comorbidities. Analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute). The KPSC Institutional Review Board approved this study and waived the informed consent.

RESULTS

Among 14 044 patients aged ≥60 years with ARI ED/hospital admissions, 6536 (47%) patients had either a SOC or salvaged nasal or nasopharyngeal swab tested for RSV and were included in the analysis. (Table 1) Of these, 1725 (26.4%) were tested for RSV under SOC, while 4811 (73.6%) were only tested after salvage. Comorbidities were common among study patients (12 709 [90.5%] had ≥1 Charlson comorbidity), and most were aged ≥75 years [7710 (54.9%)]. Slightly fewer study patients came from the hospital setting [5872 (41.8%)] than from the ED [8172 (58.2%)]. Patients who had an NP swab collected were more likely to be older (55.6% vs 54.3% ≥ 75 years), have ≥3 Charlson comorbidities (70.0% vs 61.1%), have ≥2 hospital or ED encounters in the past year (15.5% vs 9.9% and 35.8% vs 28.4%, respectively), be immunocompromised (12.9% vs 10.5%), and be tested in the hospital (46.9% vs 37.4%), and less likely to have received RSV vaccine (6.3% vs 7.1%) than participants with ARI ED/hospital admissions who were not tested.

Compared with SOC specimens, salvaged specimens were more likely to be from patients who were younger (46.6% vs 38.3% 60–74 years of age), have fewer comorbidities including congestive heart failure (33.9% vs 38.6%), malignancy (16.1% vs 19.4%), and renal disease (39.9% vs 43.7%), and less likely to be immunocompromised (11.4% vs 17.3%) or be tested in the inpatient setting (39.4% vs 67.8%) (Table 1).

By SOC RSV testing alone, 139 of 1725 patients (7.1%) were identified as RSV positive vs 483 of 6536 patients (7.4%) identified as RSV positive by SOC plus salvaged specimen testing, corresponding to a 3.47-fold greater proportion (Table 2). Across all demographic factors including age, sex, race/ethnicity, comorbidities, vaccination status, immunocompromised status, and encounter setting, 2.2–5.3-fold more cases of RSV were identified when including salvage testing compared to SOC testing only. The greatest difference was observed in participants who were seen in the ED, were 60–74 years of age, had fewer comorbidities, and were non-White.

In the VE analysis, when limited to participants identified by SOC testing only, 138 (8.2%) cases and 1550 (91.8%) controls were included, compared to 480 (7.4%) cases and 5977 (92.6%) controls when salvage specimens were evaluated alongside SOC testing (Supplementary Table 2). Estimated RSV VE was similar but more precise when salvage specimens were included than when analyses included SOC testing only (92% [67%–98%] vs 90% [29%–99%]).

The most common ARI discharge diagnosis codes among participants with SOC testing were those for RSV and unspecified pneumonia (42.5%) and acute hypoxemic respiratory failure (30.2%), while those for participants with salvage testing were codes for cough (31.4%) and for RSV and unspecified pneumonia (24.1%) (Supplementary Table 3).

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates that SOC testing identifies only a fraction of RSV-associated ARI requiring ED or hospital care, largely because clinicians infrequently order RSV testing in older adults. We found that RSV testing of salvaged nasal/NP samples from participants with ARI who were tested for influenza or COVID-19 but not for RSV increased the overall detection of RSV by nearly 3.5-fold. Of great importance, markedly greater detection of RSV through testing of salvaged swabs was also observed among participants at the highest risk of severe RSV disease, including individuals ≥75 years of age and those with Charlson comorbidity index ≥3, diabetes, CHF, chronic pulmonary disease, or immunocompromise. While testing for RSV among persons with ARI ED or hospital admissions in our setting was low (12.3%), it mirrors that seen in many other inpatient settings [5]. Likewise, the overall rate of testing for respiratory viruses within the KPSC patient population was comparable to or greater than that observed in many other settings [11]. Therefore, the impact of incomplete RSV testing on the identification of RSV-associated ARI ED/hospital admissions observed in this study is likely generalizable to many other settings.

In addition to low testing rates, suboptimal detection of RSV using NP swabs in some patients with lower NP viral loads, such as older adults and patients tested after they have developed lower respiratory tract disease, may contribute to under-ascertainment of RSV disease [12]. The decline is even greater when insensitive tests are used, particularly rapid antigen tests that are often utilized in many settings due to their convenience, low cost, and the availability of rapid results [13]. Several studies have demonstrated that the addition of serologic testing and testing of sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage, or saliva increase detection of RSV disease by between 1.5- and 2.1-fold over NP testing alone, even when highly sensitive molecular methods are used [3, 14]. Furthermore, using previously validated methods, a recent study found that adjusting for frequency of testing for RSV and the estimated sensitivity of NP testing increased the estimated burden of RSV-associated outpatient, ED, and hospital ARI encounters by 3.5 to 5-fold among institutions within the RSV-NET [15]. Low RSV testing rates observed in our study and in many other settings likely reflect lower provider awareness of severe RSV-associated disease in adults [5].

Interestingly, the VE point estimates of RSV vaccine against RSV-associated ARI ED/hospital admissions among members with RSV identified through SOC testing was similar to VE estimates after inclusion of participants identified through salvage testing, indicating that case classification was consistent across SOC and salvage testing approaches, but precision (90% [29%–99%]) was substantially improved with the inclusion of salvage specimen RSV testing (92% [67%–98%]). Furthermore, the comparable proportion of participants who tested positive for RSV through SOC or with the inclusion of salvage samples (8.2% vs 7.4%) suggests that these populations were similar. Overall, these findings suggest unchanged validity with the use of salvage specimens, supporting the analytic value of including salvage specimens to reduce misclassification and improve statistical precision in VE analysis. However, participants with SOC testing were older and had more comorbidities, including cardiopulmonary disease, malignancy, renal disease, and immunocompromise compared to those with salvage testing that could bias VE estimates downward in those with SOC testing. In addition, ARI discharge diagnosis codes suggest that members with SOC testing were most likely to have pneumonia (42.5%) or acute respiratory failure with hypoxemia codes (30.2%), while those with salvage testing were most likely to have cough (31.4%) or pneumonia codes (24.1%), suggesting that those with SOC testing may have been somewhat sicker compared to those with salvage testing.

Accurate estimates of RSV disease burden are important to determining the potential benefit of vaccines and emerging therapeutics against RSV disease in populations at high risk of severe disease [11, 16]. However, RSV testing under SOC appears to substantially underestimate the burden of RSV disease. The pattern of SOC RSV testing observed in this study is concentrated among older hospitalized adults with more comorbidities whereas salvage specimens reflect broader respiratory testing practices for influenza and COVID-19 [17]. This may, in part, be due to under recognition by many providers of the ability of RSV to cause serious disease similar to that caused by influenza and COVID-19 in older adults. In addition, some providers may choose not to test for RSV due to the lack of anti-viral therapy against RSV [18]. Furthermore, lower rates of SOC testing in adults 60–74 years of age, older adults with fewer comorbidities, and Black and Hispanic patients suggest that underestimation of RSV disease burden may be even more pronounced in some demographic groups than others. Nonetheless, even after including salvage testing, our estimated number of RSV-associated ARI ED/hospital admissions was likely low for several reasons including reduced sensitivity of NP testing in adults with RSV disease, initiation of the study in November after the start of the start of the RSV season in October in our region, and only half of older adults with ARI ED/hospital admissions were tested for any respiratory pathogen [19]. Likewise, burden of disease associated with other respiratory pathogens is likely also underestimated by limited SOC respiratory pathogen testing for similar reasons. However, while RSV disease burden estimates may be improved by routine or salvage RSV testing of adults with ARI ED/hospital admissions, non-targeted testing would be costly. Furthermore, results from salvage testing would likely be delayed, possibly limiting their clinical utility. Nonetheless, our findings suggest that broader RSV SOC testing targeted to older adults with ARI ED/hospital admissions who are at greatest risk of severe RSV disease, including those with comorbidities including chronic cardiopulmonary disease, diabetes, malignancy, end-stage renal disease, and immunocompromise could improve early detection of RSV disease among these high-risk individuals. Early detection of RSV and other respiratory viruses also may support early initiation of medications against the identified pathogen, including RSV, as newer therapeutic agents become available [16, 17, 20]. Improved RSV disease burden estimates may increase provider awareness of the potential of RSV infection to cause severe disease, which may in turn enhance RSV vaccine uptake. Knowledge of viral pathogen coupled with strong antimicrobial stewardship has the potential to reduce unnecessary antibiotic use [21]. In addition, better identification of RSV in adults hospitalized with RSV may allow clinicians to implement early intervention, including chest physiotherapy, bronchodilators, anti-inflammatory treatments, and physical therapy, that could reduce the long-term consequences of RSV disease that may be even greater than those following influenza [4, 17].

CONCLUSIONS

Identification of RSV-associated ARI ED/hospital admissions by SOC RSV testing alone is infrequent and could be improved substantially by broader testing for RSV among patients with ARI. Furthermore, VE estimates against ED and hospital ARI encounters with RSV were more precise when salvage testing was incorporated, supporting the inclusion of salvage specimens in VE analyses. Accurate estimates of the burden of RSV disease are important to determine the potential benefit of vaccines and emerging therapeutics to prevent or treat RSV disease [3, 16, 22], especially in populations susceptible to severe disease. In addition, improved estimates of RSV prevalence can enhance recognition of disease burden and may improve RSV vaccine uptake.
