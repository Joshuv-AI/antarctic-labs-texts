Comparing the p p -independence number of regular graphs to the q q -independence number of their line graphs
Yair Caro

Abstract
Let G G be a simple graph and let L ⁡ ( G ) L(G) denote the line graph of G G . A p p -independent set in G G is a set of vertices S ⊆ V ⁡ ( G ) S\subseteq V(G) such that the subgraph induced by S S has maximum degree at most p p . The p p -independence number of G G , denoted by α p ​ ( G ) \alpha_{p}(G) , is the cardinality of a maximum p p -independent set in G G . In this paper, and motivated by the recent result that independence number is at most matching number for regular graphs [ 5 ] , we investigate which values of the non-negative integers p p , q q , and r r have the property that α p ​ ( G ) ≤ α q ​ ( L ⁡ ( G ) ) \alpha_{p}(G)\leq\alpha_{q}(L(G)) for all r-regular graphs. Triples ( p , q , r ) (p,q,r) having this property are called valid α \alpha -triples . Among the results we prove are: • ( p , q , r ) (p,q,r) is valid α \alpha -triple for p ≥ 0 p\geq 0 , q ≥ 3 q\geq 3 , and r ≥ 2 r\geq 2 . • ( p , q , r ) (p,q,r) is valid α \alpha -triple for p ≤ q < 3 p\leq q<3 and r ≥ 2 r\geq 2 . • ( p , q , r ) (p,q,r) is valid α \alpha -triple for p ≥ 0 p\geq 0 , q = 2 q=2 , and r r even. • ( p , q , r ) (p,q,r) is valid α \alpha -triple for p ≥ 0 p\geq 0 , q = 2 q=2 , and r r odd with r = max ⁡ { 3 , 17 ​ ( p + 1 ) 16 } r=\max\Big\{3,\frac{17(p+1)}{16}\Big\} . We also show a close relation between undetermined possible valid α \alpha -triples, the Linear Aboricity Conjecture, and the Path-Cover Conjecture.

Introduction

Throughout this article, all graphs will be considered non-trivial, undirected, simple, and finite. In general, we will follow graph terminology and notation presented in
[
23
]
. Specifically, let
G
be a graph with vertex set
V(G)
and edge set
E(G)
. The order and size of
G
will be denoted by
n(G)=|V(G)|
and
m(G)=|E(G)|
, respectively. When the context is clear, we will use

and
m
instead of
n(G)
and

. We will denote the

and the

of

by
\alpha(G)
and

, respectively. The independence and matching numbers are two of the oldest studied invariants in graph theory and are related by the following two theorems (among many others) given in
[
5
]
.

Theorem A
(
[
5
]
)

If
G
is a graph with minimum degree
\delta
and maximum degree
\Delta
, then

\delta\alpha(G)\leq\Delta\mu(G),

and this bound is sharp.

Corollary A
(
[
5
]
)

If
G
is an
r
-regular graph with

, then

\alpha(G)\leq\mu(G),

and this bound is sharp.

Notably, graphs attaining equality in Corollary
A
, which was initially posed as a conjecture by the program
TxGraffiti

[
8
,
9
]
, have been wholly characterized (see
[
17
,
24
]
). The main aim of this paper is to expand and generalize Corollary

.

1.1
Generalized Independence and Matching

Let
p
and
q
denote non-negative integers, and let
r
denote a positive integer. The
line graph
of
G
, denoted
L(G)
, is the graph whose vertex set is the edge set of
G
, where two vertices in
L(G)
are adjacent if and only if the edges they correspond to in
G
are incident (share a vertex). A set of vertices
S\subseteq V(G)
is called a
p
-independent set
of
G
if each vertex in
S
is adjacent to at most
p
other vertices in
S
. The cardinality of a maximum
p
-independent set in
G
is the
p
-independence number
of
G
, and is denoted by
\alpha_{p}(G)
. The
0
-independence number of
G
is precisely the independence number; that is,
\alpha(G)=\alpha_{0}(G)
. A set of edges
M\subseteq E(G)
is called a
q
-matching
of
G
if each edge in
M
is incident to at most
q
other edges in
M
. The cardinality of a maximum
q
-matching of
G
is called the
q
-matching number
of
G
, and is denoted by
\mu_{q}(G)
. The
0
-matching number of
G
is precisely its matching number; that is,
\mu(G)=\mu_{0}(G)
.

It is clear from these definitions that the
q
-matching number of
G
is identical to the
q
-independence number of its line graph
L(G)
. Namely, for every
q
, we have
\mu_{q}(G)=\alpha_{q}(L(G))
, and for this reason, we will now refer to the
q
-independence number of
L(G)
, without explicitly mentioning the
q
-matching number. Using this notation, we may rewrite the inequality stated in Corollary
A
as
\alpha_{0}(G)\leq\alpha_{0}(L(G))
, which implies
\alpha_{p}(G)\leq\alpha_{q}(L(G))
for any
r
-regular graph with
p=0
,
q\geq 0
, and
r\geq 1
– a notion that corresponds to the validity of the triple
(p=0,q\geq 0,r\geq 1)
of integers which is generalized in the next section. This fact naturally leads to the following general question:

Question 1

For which values of
p
,
q
, and
r
is it true that all
r
-regular graphs satisfy the inequality

\alpha_{p}(G)\leq\alpha_{q}(L(G))?

When considering Question
1
, it is only meaningful to consider
r\geq 1
, since the line graph
L(G)
is not defined for any 0-regular graph
G
.

1.2
Contributions

Our first main contribution is to answer Question
1
for many instances of

,
q
, and

. We call a triple
(p,q,r)
that is valid for Question

a

\alpha
-triple
; the only meaningful valid

-triples

and link well-known open problems in graph theory to the triples we could not prove.

As a consequence of considering Question

, reduces to the statement of Corollary

.

Theorem B

If

is an
r
-regular graph with

, then

\alpha_{p}(G)\leq\alpha_{p}(L(G)).

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we provide known results, propositions, and lemmas needed to prove our main results. In Section

, we answer Question

for several varieties of triples. Section

shows a relation between the unverified triples and the well-known Linear Arboricity and Path-Cover Conjectures. Finally, in Section

2.1
Graph Factor Lemmas

Recall that a

of
G
is a spanning subgraph

of

. A
k
-factor
of

is a spanning
k
-regular subgraph of

. The following two theorems are due to Petersen
[
20
]
and Tutte
[
22
]
, respectively.

Theorem 1
(
[
20
]
)

If
G
is a
2r
-regular graph, then
G
has a
2k
-factor for every integer
k
, where
0<k<r
.

Theorem 2
(
[
22
]
)

If
G
is an
r
-regular graph and
k
is an integer satisfying
0\leq k\leq r
, then there exists a spanning subgraph
H
of
G
such that
k\leq d_{H}(v)\leq k+1
for each vertex
v\in V(G)
.

Next, recall that a
[k-1,k]
-factor
of
G
is a spanning subgraph
H
of
G
such that for every vertex
v\in V(G)
, the degree of
v
in
H
satisfies
k-1\leq d_{H}(v)\leq k
. A

[k-1,k]
-factor
, denoted
R
-
[k-1,k]
-factor, is a spanning subgraph
H
of
G
, such that for every vertex
v\in V(G)
, the degree of
v
in
H
satisfies
k-1\leq d_{H}(v)\leq k
, and no two vertices of degree

are adjacent in

. Note, every
[k-1,k]
-factor contains a
R
-
[k-1,k]
-factor, stated formally with the following result.

Lemma 3

Every
[k-1,k]
-factor contains a
R
-
[k-1,k]
-factor as a subgraph.

Proof.
Let
G
be a graph with a
[k-1,k]
-factor, say
H
. If no two vertices of degree
k
in
H
are adjacent in
H
, then we are done since this factor is already a reduced
[k-1,k]
-factor. If
H
contains any adjacent vertices of degree
k
, choose two such vertices and delete the edge between them until no such pair remains. The subgraph obtained by removing these edges is spanning and an
R
-
[k-1,k]
factor of
G
.
\Box

As a consequence of Theorem
2
and Lemma
3
, we have the following result.

Lemma 4

If
G
is an
r
-regular graph where
r\geq 3
is an odd integer, then
G
contains a
R
-
[2,3]
-factor.

We will also need the following strengthening of Theorem

given by Kano
[
19
]
.

Theorem 5
(
[
19
]
)

Let
j
be a positive integer. If
k\leq\frac{2(2j+1)}{3}
, then every
(2j+1)
-regular graph has a
[k-1,k]
-factor each of whose components are regular.

The following result bounds the maximum degree of the line graph of a reduced
[k-1,k]
-factor and states a lower bound on the number of edges contained in such a factor.

Lemma 6

If
G
is a graph of order
n
and
H\subseteq G
is a
R
-
[k-1,k]
-factor, then the following inequalities hold:

(1)

\Delta(L(H))\leq 2k-3

(2)

|V(L(H))|=|E(H)|\geq\frac{n(k-1)}{2}

Proof.
We first prove (1). Let
G
be a graph and
H
be a
R
-
[k-1,k]
-factor of

. Clearly, for every edge
e\in E(H)
in the
R
-
[k-1,k]
-factor, the degree in the line graph is at most

since no two vertices of degree

are adjacent in this factor. To see (2), note

, which implies,

|E(H)|\geq\frac{n(k-1)}{2}.

\Box

2.3
Graph Independence Bounds

We will also need to use lower and upper bounds on the generalized independence number we present in this section. To begin, we first prove a lemma that can also be derived from the
degree sequence index strategy
(
DSI-strategy
) introduced in
[
6
]
; see also
[
13
]
for another alternative proof and an extensive study of generalized independence in graphs. However, we provide direct proof from first principles to stay as self-contained as possible.

Lemma 9

If
G
is an
r
-regular graph of order
n
and
0\leq p\leq r
, then

\alpha_{p}(G)\leq\frac{nr}{2r-p},

(1)

and this bound is sharp. If in addition
p<r
, then

\alpha_{p}(G)\leq\frac{nr}{r+1}.

(2)

Proof.
If
r=p
, then (
1
) is trivial, as
\alpha_{p}(G)\leq n
. Thus, we may assume
r>p
. Let
G
be an
r
-regular graph of order
n
with
r=p+t
, where
t\geq 1
. Next, let
A\subseteq V(G)
be a maximum
p
-independent set of
G
and let
B=V(G)\setminus A
. Thus,
|A|=\alpha_{p}(G)
and
|B|=n-\alpha_{p}(G)
. Let
e(A,B)
denote the set of edges with one endpoint in
A
and the other in
B
. Since
A
is a
p
-independent set, if
v\in A
, then
v
can be incident with at most
p
edges whose other endpoint is also in
A
. Thus, each vertex in
A
contributes at least
r-p=t
edges to the count
e(A,B)
. Hence,
e(A,B)\geq t\cdot|A|
. On the other hand, since
G
is
r
-regular, if
v\in B
, then
v
can be incident with at most
r
edges whose other endpoint is in
A
. Thus, each vertex in
B
contributes at most
r
edges to the count
e(A,B)
, and so
e(A,B)\leq r\cdot|B|
. Hence,

t\cdot|A|=t\cdot(n-|B|)\leq e(A,B)\leq r\cdot|B|.

Rearranging this expression, we obtain

\alpha_{p}(G)=|A|\leq\frac{nr}{r+t}=\frac{nr}{2r-p},

establishing inequality (
1
). Using
p<r
establishes inequality (
2
).

We next show that inequality (
1
) is sharp. Consider the bipartite graph
G=[A,B]
with
|A|=2m
, where each vertex in
A
has degree
r-p
, and each vertex in
B
has degree
r
. Next, pack a
p
-factor into
A
and denote the resulting graph by
G^{\prime}
. Note that for
|A|=2m
, the complete graph
K_{2m}
has a 1-factorization, and so, you take
p
1-factors to pack into
A
. From the cardinalities of
A
and
B
, it follows that
(r-p)\cdot|A|=r\cdot|B|
, while
|A|+|B|=n
. Thus,

(r-p)\cdot|A|=r\cdot(n-|A|).

Rearranging this equality, we obtain

|A|=\frac{nr}{2r-p}.

Since
A
is a
p
-independent set of
G^{\prime}
, inequality (
1
) is sharp.
\Box

Remark 10

A bipartite graph
G
, as claimed in the proof above, is constructed, for example, by taking
2t
copies of
K_{r,r-p}
and can be made connected, if one wishes, by switching operations. We mention here for later use that if
2r-p
is not divisible by three, then for
2t
, where
t
is not divisible by three, the number of vertices of the graph
G
constructed above is not divisible by three.

We next focus on bounding the generalized independence number of the line graph
L(G)
in terms of the order of the graph
G
. To start, the following lemma bounds the 1-independence number of the line graph.

Lemma 11

If
G
is a graph of order
n
, then

\alpha_{1}(L(G))\leq\frac{2n}{3},

and this bound is sharp.

Proof.
Observe that a maximum 1-independent set in
L(G)
corresponds to a subgraph of the form
xP_{3}\cup yP_{2}
in
G
which gives a 1-independent set in
L(G)
of cardinality
2x+y
. Clearly,
3x+2y\leq n
. Hence,

\alpha_{1}(L(G))=2x+y\leq 2x+\frac{n-3x}{2}=\frac{n+x}{2}\leq\frac{2n}{3},

as
x\leq\frac{n}{3}
. To see that this bound is sharp, take
G
to be any graph with a
P_{3}
-packing.
\Box

Next we focus on bounding the the 2-independence number of the line graph
L(G)
of
G
from below in terms of the order
n
of
G
.

Lemma 12

Let
G
be an
r
-regular graph with
r\geq 2
and order
n
.

(1)

If
r
is even, then
\alpha_{2}(L(G))=n
.

(2)

If
r\geq 3
, then
\alpha_{2}(L(G))\geq\frac{(17n-2)}{18}
.

Proof.
Let
G
be an
r
-regular graph with
r\geq 2
and order
n
. We first prove (1). Let
r
be even. Then, by Theorem
1
,
G
has a 2-factor, say
F
, with exactly
n
edges in
G
. The edges of
F
in
G
form a 2-independent set in
L(G)
, which implies
\alpha_{2}(L(G))\geq n
. Suppose
\alpha_{2}(L(G))\geq n+1
for some
r
-regular graph
G
. In
G
, there exists a set
F
of at least
n+1
edges, such that the corresponding vertices in
L(G)
, denoted by
L(F)
, form a set of
n+1
vertices in
L(G)
that induces a subgraph with maximum degree at most two. This is impossible as in the subgraph induced by
F
in
G
there must be a cycle with an attached edge forming degree three in
L(F)
. Thus,
\alpha_{2}(L(G))=n
completing the proof of (1).

We next prove (2). Let
r=3
and observe that in this case the graph
G
has
m(G)=\frac{3}{2}n
edges. We next remove edges from
G
so that the resulting graph contains no vertex of degree three. By Theorem
7
, there exists a matching, say
M\subseteq E(G)
, such that
|M|\geq\frac{4n-1}{9}
. Note that the number of
M
-saturated vertices in
G
is
2|M|
. Therefore, there is at most
n-2|M|
vertices which are not the endpoint of any edge in
M
. For each vertex in
G
which is not the endpoint of an edge in
M
, we delete one edge. Hence, we delete at most

|M|+(n-2|M|)=n-|M|\leq n-\frac{4n-1}{9}=\frac{5n+1}{9},

edges before we arrive at a graph with no degree three vertex. That is, the resulting subgraph of
G
, say
H
, satisfies
\Delta(H)=2
, and also,

m(H)\geq\frac{3}{2}n-\frac{5n+1}{9}=\frac{27n-10n-2}{18}=\frac{17n-2}{18}.

Since
\Delta(H)=2
, the edges of
H
form a 2-independent set in
L(G)
. Thus,

\alpha_{2}(L(G))\geq m(H)\geq\frac{17n-2}{18}.

We now consider
r\geq 5
. By Theorem
5
we may assume
G
contains a
[2,3]
-factor that splits
V(G)=V(G_{2})\cup V(G_{3})
where
G_{2}
is 2-regular graph on
n_{2}
vertices and
G_{3}
is 3-regular graph on
n_{3}
vertices and
n_{2}+n_{3}=n
. Thus,

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}(L(G))

\displaystyle\geq\alpha_{2}(L(G_{2}))+\alpha_{2}(L(G_{3}))

\displaystyle\geq n_{2}+\frac{17n_{3}-2}{18}

\displaystyle=\frac{18(n-n_{3})+(17n_{3}-2)}{18}

\displaystyle=\frac{18n-n_{3}-2}{18}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{17n-2}{18},

which completes the proof of statement (2).
\Box

3
Main Results

This section presents our main results addressing Question
1
. To establish a foundation for the general case, we begin by examining the almost trivial scenario of 1-regular graphs.

Proposition 13

If
G
is a
1
-regular graph, then

\alpha_{p}(G)\leq\alpha_{q}(L(G)),

with equality if and only if
p=0
.

Proof.
For
r=1
, the 1-regular graph
G
of order
n
, consists of
n/2
disjoint copies of
K_{2}
, and so, the line graph
L(G)
consists of
n/2
disjoint copies of
K_{1}
. Therefore, for
r=1
,
\alpha_{0}(G)=\alpha_{q}(L(G))
for
q\geq 0
. However, for
p\geq 1
we observe
\alpha_{p}(G)=n
. Thus,
\alpha_{p}(G)\leq\alpha_{q}(L(G))
holds for 1-regular graphs if and only if
p=0
.
\Box

Corollary 14

(p,q\geq 0,r=1)
is a valid
\alpha
-triple if and only if
p=0
.

The intriguing cases of Question
1
arise for
r
-regular graphs with
r\geq 2
, and the remainder of this section is dedicated to addressing these cases. Each subsection concludes with a corollary or observation on valid or invalid
\alpha
-triples, and we highlight open cases where Question
1
remains unresolved.

3.1
When
r
is Even

When
r\geq 2
is even, we may apply Petersen’s Theorem and obtain our first nontrivial valid
\alpha
-triple.

Theorem 15

If
G
is an
r
-regular graph with
r
even, then,

\alpha_{p}(G)\leq\alpha_{q}(L(G)),

whenever
p\geq 0
and
q\geq 2
.

Proof.
Let
G
be an
r
-regular graph with
r
chosen to be even. In this case, Theorem
1
implies that
G
has a 2-factor, say
H\subseteq G
, and moreover,
|E(H)|=n
. Since
H
is a 2-factor in
G
,
L(H)
is a 2-regular graph in
L(G)
. Thus,
V(L(H))
is a 2-independent set, implying
\alpha_{p}(G)\leq n=\alpha_{2}(L(G))
for all

, and we are done.
\Box

Corollary 16

(p\geq 0,q\geq 2,r=2k)
is a valid
\alpha
-triple for all integers
k\geq 1
.

By Corollary
16
, it follows that Question
1
is resolved whenever
r\geq 2
is even and
q\geq 2
. The remaining cases when
r\geq 2
is even are
q=0
and when
q=1
, of which we address in general in the next two subsections.

3.2
When
q=0

For
q=0
it is clear that
\alpha_{0}(L(G))=\mu(G)\leq n/2
. Thus, for fixed
r
and
p\geq r
, there are no valid
\alpha
-triples since
\alpha_{p}(G)=\alpha_{r}(G)=n>\alpha_{0}(L(G))
in this case. Next recall, by Lemma

, that for

,

\alpha_{p}(G)\leq\frac{nr}{2r-p},

and that this bound is sharp – constructions achieving sharpness are given in the proof of Lemma

. Take
G
to be one of the graphs achieving equality in Lemma

, and observe that whenever

,

\alpha_{p}(G)=\frac{nr}{2r-p}>\frac{n}{2}\geq\alpha_{0}(L(G))=\mu(G).

Hence,
(p\geq 1,q=0,r\geq 1)
is never a valid
\alpha
-triple. Combined with Corollary
A
, this implies that the only valid
\alpha
-triples with

are
(p=0,q=0,r\geq 1)
.

Corollary 17

(p=0,q=0,r\geq 1)
is a valid
\alpha
-triple.

3.3
When

With the following theorem we show all triples of the form
(p,q\geq 3,r\geq 2)
are valid
\alpha
-triples.

Theorem 18

If
G
is an
r
-regular graph with
r\geq 2
and

, then

\alpha_{p}(G)\leq\alpha_{q}(L(G)),

for

.

Proof.
Let
G
be an
r
-regular graph of order
n
with
r\geq 2
.
If
r
is even, then we are done by Theorem
15
. Thus, we may assume that

is odd. Since
\alpha_{p}(G)\leq n
for all

, and
\alpha_{3}(L(G))\leq\alpha_{q}(L(G))
for all
q\geq 3
by monotonicity, it suffices to show that
\alpha_{3}(L(G))\geq n
.

If
r=3
,
G
is a
[2,3]
-factor (with no degree 2 vertices). Then, by Lemma
3
,
G
has a
R
-
[2,3]
-factor. If
r\geq 5
, then Lemma
4
implies that
G
has a
R
-
[2,3]
-factor. In both cases, we are assured that
G
contains a
R
-
[2,3]
-factor, say
H\subseteq G
. Then, by Lemma
6
,
|E(H)|\geq n
and
\Delta(L(H))\leq 3
. Thus,
V(L(H))
forms a 3-independent set in
L(G)
with cardinality at least
n
. That is,

n\leq|V(L(H))|=|E(H)|\leq\alpha_{3}(L(G)).

and the proof is complete.
\Box

Corollary 19

(p\geq 0,q\geq 3,r\geq 2)
is a valid
\alpha
-triple.

3.4
When
p\leq q<3

We now consider Question
1
for the difficult case when
p\leq q<3
. More specifically, the following theorem shows all triples of the form
(p\leq q,q<3,r\geq 2)
are valid
\alpha
-triples.

Theorem 20

If
G
is an
r
-regular graph with
r\geq 2
, then

\alpha_{p}(G)\leq\alpha_{q}(L(G)),

whenever
p\leq q<3
.

Proof.
Since the case of
p=q=0
was already established, we only address the cases with
q\geq 1
in this proof. Thus we aim to establish that the inequality is true when
(p,q)\in\{(0,1),(0,2),(1,1),(1,2),(2,2)\}
. Furthermore, we will frequently appeal to the monotonicity property of the
k
-independence number – namely, that
\alpha_{k}(G)\leq\alpha_{k+1}(G)
for every non-negative integer
k
. To this end, assume
G
is an
r
-regular graph with
r\geq 2
. We consider two separate cases:
r=2
and
r\geq 3
.

Case 1:

r=2
.

In this case,
G
is a
2
-regular graph, and therefore a disjoint union of cycles. It follows immediately that
L(G)=G
, and thus we have

\alpha_{2}(G)=|V(G)|=\alpha_{2}(L(G)).

Consequently, by the monotonicity of the
k
-independence number,

\alpha_{0}(G)\leq\alpha_{1}(G)\leq\alpha_{2}(G)=\alpha_{2}(L(G)),

implying that
(p,q)\in\{(0,2),(1,2),(2,2)\}
are valid pairs. Additionally, we note that
\alpha_{1}(G)=\alpha_{1}(L(G))
, so

\alpha_{0}(G)\leq\alpha_{1}(G)=\alpha_{1}(L(G)),

validating the pairs
(p,q)\in\{(0,1),(1,1)\}
, again by appeal to monotonicity of
\alpha_{k}
.

Case 2:

r\geq 3
.

Here, we only need to explicitly address the cases
(p,q)=(1,1)
and
(p,q)=(2,2)
; as the remaining pairs follow by monotonicity of
k
-independence.

Subcase 2.1:

p=q=1
.

Let
A
be a largest
1
-independent set in
G
, chosen to maximize the number of edges in the induced subgraph
G[A]
. Thus, no additional edge can be added to
A
without violating
1
-independence, and clearly,
|A|=\alpha_{1}(G)
. Let
B=V(G)\setminus A
. By definition of
1
-independence, each component of
A
is either an isolated edge (
K_{2}
) or an isolated vertex (
K_{1}
).

Since
r\geq 3
, every vertex in
A
has at least one neighbor in
B
, ensuring
B
is nonempty. The choice of
A
yields two useful properties:

•

Property I:
Each
K_{2}
component contributes exactly
2(r-1)
edges to
B
, while each
K_{1}
component contributes exactly
r
edges to
B
.

•

Property II:
Each vertex in
B
has at most
r
neighbors in
A
.

Next, let
S
be the set of components of
A
and construct a bipartite multigraph
H
with vertex set
S\cup B
, where each vertex in
S
corresponds uniquely to one component of
A
. A vertex
u\in S
is connected to
v\in B
by edges whose multiplicity equals the number of vertices in the corresponding component of
A
adjacent to
v
.

Let
D\subseteq S
and consider the edges in the induced bipartite subgraph
H[D,N(D)]
. Property II implies there are at most
r|N(D)|
edges going from
N(D)
to
D
. However, Property I guarantees at least
r|D|
edges going from
D
to
N(D)
, giving the inequality

r|N(D)|\geq r|D|.

This implies
|D|\leq|N(D)|
. Thus, Hall’s theorem ensures the existence of a matching from
S
into
B
, assigning to each component of
A
a distinct neighbor in
B
. Using this matching, each component of type
K_{2}
extends to
P_{3}
, and each component of type
K_{1}
extends to
P_{2}
, forming a
1
-independent set of the same cardinality as
A
in
L(G)
. Thus, the pair
(1,1)
is valid and by monotonicity,
(0,1)
also follows.

Subcase 2.2:

p=q=2
.

Let
A
be a largest
2
-independent set in
G
, again chosen to maximize the number of edges in
G[A]
. Thus, no further edge can be included without breaking
2
-independence, and
|A|=\alpha_{2}(G)
. Set
B=V(G)\setminus A
. By maximality, the induced subgraph on
A
consists of disjoint cycles, paths, and isolated vertices. Because
r\geq 3
, each vertex in
A
must have at least one neighbor in
B
, ensuring
B
is nonempty. Since cycle components remain unchanged under the line-graph transformation (i.e.,
L(C_{k})=C_{k}
), we may restrict attention solely to non-cycle components. This choice of
A
yields two useful properties:

•

Property III:
The two end-points of each path component collectively contribute exactly
2(r-1)
edges to
B
, and each isolated vertex (
K_{1}
) contributes exactly
r
edges to
B
.

•

Property IV:
Each vertex in
B
has at most
r
neighbors in
A
.

Let
S
be the set of non-cycle components of
A
and construct a bipartite multigraph
H
with vertex set
S\cup B
, where vertices in
S
correspond uniquely to the non-cycle components of
A
. Connect
u\in S
to
v\in B
with multiplicity equal to the number of endpoints (or single vertex, in the
K_{1}
case) adjacent to
v
in
G
.

Let
D\subseteq S
and consider the edges in the induced subgraph
H[D,N(D)]
. Property IV implies there are at most
r|N(D)|
edges going from
N(D)
to
D
. However, Property III ensures at least
r|D|
edges going from
D
to
N(D)
. This gives the inequality

r|N(D)|\geq r|D|,

which implies
|D|\leq|N(D)|
. Thus, Hall’s theorem ensures the existence of a matching from
S
into
B
, assigning to each non-cycle component of
A
a distinct neighbor in
B
. This matching enables each path component to extend by one additional vertex and each isolated vertex to form an edge, which when taken together with the cycle components of
A
, produces a
2
-independent set with the same cardinality as
A
in
L(G)
. Hence, the pair
(2,2)
is valid, and monotonicity immediately validates the pairs
(1,2)
and
(0,2)
.

All cases and subcases are now complete, establishing the theorem.

\Box

(p\leq q,q<3,r\geq 2)

and Corollary

, we deduce the appealing corollary below, which confirms Corollary

. Namely, for all

.

When

This section addresses some of the remaining cases not covered by Theorem
20
when
q=2
, namely when
p\geq 3
.

Theorem 23

If
G
is an
r
-regular graph with odd
r
such that
r\geq\max\Big\{3,\frac{17(p+1)}{16}\Big\}
, then

\alpha_{p}(G)\leq\alpha_{q}(L(G)),

whenever
p\geq 0
and
q\geq 2
.

Proof.
Let
G
be an
r
-regular graph of order
n
with
r\geq 3
, where
r
is odd. If
q\geq 3
, then we are done by Theorem
18
. If
q=2
and
p\in\{0,1,2\}
, then we are also done by Theorem
20
. Thus, we may assume
q=2
and
p\geq 3
. Hence,
r
is odd with
r\geq 17(p+1)/16>3
. Next observe,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\frac{rn}{2r-p}&\leq&\frac{17n-2}{18}\\
&\Updownarrow&\\
18rn&\leq&(2r-p)(17n-2)\\
&\Updownarrow&\\
18rn&\leq&34rn-4r-17pn+2p\\
&\Updownarrow&\\
16rn&\geq&17pn-2p+4r.\end{array}

Thus, since
r\geq 17(p+1)/16
,

16rn\geq 17n(p+1)\geq 17pn-2p+4r\iff 17np+17n\geq 17pn-2p+4r.

Hence,
17n\geq 4r–2p
, which is a true inequality since
n\geq r+1
(recalling
G
is
r
-regular). By Lemma
9
and Lemma
12
, we finally observe,

\alpha_{p}(G)\leq\frac{rn}{2r-p}\leq\frac{17n-2}{18}\leq\alpha_{2}(L(G)),

completing the proof of the theorem.
\Box

3.6
When
q=1

For
q=1
, many non-valid
\alpha
-triples exist. For example, consider any
r
-regular graph, say
G
, which attains the equality

\alpha_{p}(G)=\frac{rn}{2r-p},

where we refer the reader to the proof of Lemma
9
for one such construction of the graph
G
. Next recall Lemma
11
, which states that

\alpha_{1}(L(G))\leq\frac{2n}{3},

for any graph
G
of order
n
. We observe,

\frac{rn}{2r-p}>\frac{2n}{3}\iff 3rn>4rn-2pn\iff 3r>4r-2p\iff 2p>r,

Thus, choosing
2p>r
yields

\alpha_{1}(L(G))<\frac{rn}{2r-p}=\alpha_{p}(G).

Therefore, no triple of the form
(p\geq 0,q=1,1\leq r<2p)
is a valid
\alpha
-triple. Furthermore, by Remark
10
, we also observe that for
2r-p
not divisible by 3, the same argument above gives that
(p\geq 0,q=1,1\leq r\leq 2p)
is also not a valid
\alpha

4
Relation to Linear Arboricity and Path-Cover Conjectures

The only open cases for
q=2
are when
p\geq 3
and odd
r
with
3\leq r<17(p+1)/6
. As we shall see, the missing cases are closely related to two famous conjectures: The Linear Arboricity Conjecture and the Path-cover conjecture.

To illustrate this, recall that in 1970, Harary introduced the concept of
linear arboricity
as a covering invariant on graphs
[
14
]
. A
linear forest
is a graph in which each component is a path, and the linear arboricity of a graph
G
, denoted
la(G)
, is the minimum number of linear forests whose union is
G
. The following conjecture
[
1
]
, open since 1980, is of fundamental importance in research on linear arboricity.

Conjecture 25
(The Linear Arboricity Conjecture
[
1
]
)

The linear arboricity of an
r
-regular graph is
\lceil\frac{r+1}{2}\rceil
.

Assuming the Linear Arboricity Conjecture is true, if
G
is an
r
-regular graph with odd
r
and
r\geq 3
, then
G
can be covered by
\frac{r+1}{2}
linear forests. This covering contains, by averaging, a linear forest
F
with at least,

\frac{|E(G)|}{la(G)}=\frac{(rn/2)}{((r+1)/2)}=\frac{rn}{r+1}

edges, which form a 2-independent set in
L(G)
. Therefore, if the Linear Arboricity Conjecture is true, then
\alpha_{q}(L(G))\geq\alpha_{2}(L(G))\geq\frac{rn}{r+1}
for all
q\geq 2
. Moreover, if
r>p
, then Lemma
9
(2) implies
\alpha_{p}(G)\leq\frac{rn}{r+1}
. These two facts lead to the following observation.

Corollary 26

If
G
is an
r
-regular graph with
r\geq p+1\geq 3
and we assume the Linear Arboricity Conjecture true, then

\alpha_{p}(G)\leq\alpha_{q}(L(G)),

for all
q\geq 2
.

The Linear Arboricity Conjecture has been confirmed for
r\in\{1,2,3,4,5,6,8,10\}
(see
[
10
]
). Assuming the linear arboricity, the triple
(p\geq 0,q=2,r\geq p+1)
is a valid
\alpha
-triple, while
(p\geq 0,q=2,3\leq r\leq p)
is not a valid
\alpha
-triple because for
p\geq r\geq 1
,
\alpha_{p}(G)=n
while
\alpha_{2}(L(G))=n
if and only if
G
has 2-factor. As is well known (see
[
3
]
), there exist
r
-regular graphs with odd
r\geq 3
that have no
k
-factor for
1\leq k\leq r-1
, and in particular, no 2-factor. For such graphs,
\alpha_{2}(L(G))<n
. For example, let
k
be odd and construct a graph
G
of order
k+2
whose complement has degree sequence
2,1,....,1
. Next take
k
copies of
G
together with an isolated vertex, say
u
, where we attach
u
to vertices of
G
with degree
k-1
in
G
; a graph that has no nontrivial factor. Thus, If the Linear Arboricity Conjecture is true for all odd
r
, then the determination of valid
\alpha
-triples in case
q=2
is complete.

The
path cover number
of
G
, denoted by
\rho(G)
, is the minimum number of vertex-disjoint paths required to cover the vertices of
G
. In
[
16
]
, Magnant and Martin conjectured the following, a conjecture which remains open in general but has been proven for
r
-regular graphs with
0\leq r\leq 6
(see
[
12
,
16
,
18
]
).

Conjecture 27
(The Path-Cover Conjecture
[
16
]
)

If
G
is an
r
-regular graph of order
n
, then
\rho(G)\leq\frac{n}{r+1}
.

Suppose
G
is a graph of order
n
that is coverable by
k
paths, say
P_{1},\dots,P_{k}
, each with order
n_{i}
, for
i=1,\dots,k
, respectively. Since every vertex in
G
is in exactly one path in this covering, we have
n_{1}+\dots+n_{k}=n
. Furthermore, since the path
P_{j}
has exactly
n_{j}-1
edges, the total number of edges in this path covering is
(n_{1}-1)+\dots+(n_{k}-1)=n-k
. Hence,
\alpha_{2}(L(G))\geq n-\rho(G)
. Thus,
if we assume the Path-Cover Conjecture is true and
G
is an
r
-regular graph with
r>p
, then

\alpha_{2}(L(G))\geq n-\frac{n}{r+1}=\frac{rn}{r+1}\geq\alpha_{2}(G),

where the right-hand side of this inequality follows by Lemma
9
(2).

Assuming the Path-Cover Conjecture true,
(p\geq 0,q=2,r\geq p+1)
is a valid
\alpha
-triple, while
(p\geq 0,q=2,3\leq r\leq p)
is not a valid
\alpha
-triple as for
p\geq r\geq\alpha_{p}(G)=n
while
\alpha_{2}(L(G))=n
if and only if
G
has 2-factor. But again, and as before, it is well known that there are
r
-regular graphs with odd
r\geq 3
that have no
k
-factor for
1\leq k\leq r-1
and for these graphs
\alpha_{2}(L(G))<n
. Thus, if the Path-Cover Conjecture is true for all odd
r
, then the determination of valid
\alpha
-triples in case
q=2
is complete.
