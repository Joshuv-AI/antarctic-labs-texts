Multi-sensor observer-based residual learning with Auto-Permutation Feature Importance for fault diagnosis of multistage centrifugal pumps under variable pressures
Authors: Saif Ullah, Muhammad Farooq Siddique, Jong-Myon Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent progress in deep learning has improved fault diagnosis, but such models typically demand large, labeled data and struggle with overfitting when data is limited or imbalanced. Collecting data under faulty machine conditions is challenging, as artificially inducing faults can damage other parts. Additionally, feature extraction pipelines often produce high-dimensional, redundant representations that hinder interpretability and efficiency. To overcome these challenges, this paper introduces a sensor-fused and data-efficient framework for centrifugal pump fault diagnosis under varying pressure conditions. The proposed method uses an autoregressive (AR) observer to model normal-class signals across multiple sensors and extract residuals indicative of faults. These residuals are further processed to compute statistical and spectral descriptors such as RMS and band power. To remove irrelevant features and reduce dimensionality, an Auto-Permutation Feature Importance mechanism (Auto-PFI) is used, yielding a compact and discriminative feature set. Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) is then trained for class-wise density estimation and fault classification. The framework is validated on datasets acquired at 3, 3.5, and 4 bar pressure levels achieving accuracies of above 99% across all pressures, and its performance is benchmarked against single-sensor setups and state-of-the-art method. Visualization tools including t-SNE, ROC curves, and confusion matrices confirm the robustness and generalization of the approach. Results demonstrate that integrating AR-based residual modeling with Auto-PFI and GMM classification offers a reliable, interpretable, and low-data-demanding solution for centrifugal pumps fault diagnosis.Keywords: Centrifugal pump, Vibration signals, Observer, Gaussian mixture model, Fault diagnosis, Permutation feature importanceSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Multistage centrifugal pumps (MCPs) are widely used in industries to convert electrical energy into mechanical energy through multiple impellers that move fluid efficiently. These pumps are a key component of industries like oil refining, mining, and power generation1. Because of their widespread use, any fault in MCPs can lead to energy loss, production delays, high repair costs, and even safety hazards. A recent survey showed that spending just 10,00020,000 USD on health monitoring could save up to 500,000 USD annually, highlighting the importance of timely maintenance2. This makes early and accurate fault diagnosis a top priority for centrifugal pump systems.

Faults in MCPs can generally be divided into two categories: mechanical faults (MFs) and fluid flow-related issues3. Although these may be interconnected, mechanical faults tend to occur more often. Among them, around 34% are related to the mechanical seal (MS), which serves as a critical component in ensuring fluid containment and shaft alignment. Besides MS faults, impeller defects are also common, and both types of faults may affect the pump’s performance or fluid flow dynamics4. These faults can appear as hard faults, which are obvious and easy to detect, or soft faults, which gradually deteriorate pump performance without immediate functional failure. Detecting soft faults at an early stage is especially important to reduce long-term damage and associated costs.

Soft defects caused by a faulty MS may include fluid leakage, fretting, and shaft wear, while impeller issues can disrupt fluid dynamics and introduce mechanical instability5,6. To address these minute conditions effectively, this study focuses on detecting early-stage soft faults, particularly those caused by mechanical seal holes (MSH), mechanical seal scratches (MSS), and impeller defects.

One of the most effective tools for monitoring MCP conditions is the analysis of vibration signals. Faults introduce abrupt changes in mechanical stiffness, which in turn generate impulsive and non-stationary vibration responses7. These changes often appear in the frequency domain but are masked by noise due to their low energy levels8. Furthermore, the vibration signals from faulty machines show time-varying statistical behavior, making them non-stationary and harder to interpret9. Standard Fourier transforms work well for stationary signals but struggle in such cases.

To improve signal clarity, methods like blind source separation (BSS) have been used, but they require reference signals and have limited flexibility in real-world scenarios10. Time-frequency representations such as the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) and wavelet transform have shown better performance in analyzing non-stationary signals11,12. However, STFT faces a trade-off between time and frequency resolution due to its fixed window size13,14. Wavelet transforms offer better adaptability to non-stationary impulses and have been widely used to preprocess MCP vibration signals and extract relevant features15–17. Nevertheless, wavelet-based methods are sensitive to the choice of mother wavelet and can introduce artifacts. Empirical mode decomposition has been proposed as an adaptive alternative but is prone to mode mixing and interpolation errors18. S-transform has also emerged as a solution to combine the strengths of both WT and STFT19.

Wavelet coherence analysis is another advanced technique that helps detect frequency-locked signals related to faults by generating coherograms, often followed by deep learning for classification20. Accurate classification depends not only on preprocessing but also on effective feature extraction. Discriminative features from time, frequency, and time–frequency domains must be carefully selected to reflect the nature of faults21,22. Deep learning methods have gained attention for automating feature extraction and classification23–26 and have shown promising results in fault diagnosis for centrifugal pumps27. However, soft faults in MCPs introduce additional complexity due to fluid structure interaction. Traditional features extracted from raw signals often fail to capture these interactions, especially when signal quality is poor or when operating under low-pressure conditions. Time-domain features may not be sensitive enough to detect early-stage faults, and frequency-domain features are often blocked by noise from microstructural vibrations. Another issue is that most of the deep learning models require many samples for a better generalization and reliable performance. Another problem is that all the features extracted are not necessary to be beneficial for the study. Past studies have also explored feature selection and dimensionality reduction using methods like Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA)28,29. While PCA captures overall signal variations, it fails to account for class separability. LDA improves on this but still requires large, labeled datasets and faces challenges in optimizing intraclass discrimination30. Furthermore, recent studies also show that fault diagnosis becomes more challenging when operating conditions vary across domains. Jian et al.31 proposed an imbalance-aware prototype-guided contrastive learning framework that demonstrated how distribution drift and underrepresented fault classes degrade performance across changing operating states. Related work on open-set domain generalization introduced dual-level weighted mechanisms and targeted data augmentation to cope with unknown or unmatched operating domains, highlighting that fault features often shift when conditions change32. A two-stage semi-supervised domain generalization approach further combining imbalance correction with domain-invariant feature extraction to manage label scarcity and cross-domain drift simultaneously33. In addition, long-tailed multi-domain generalization has been explored using moment-matching and balanced contrastive strategies to stabilize feature distributions under unequal class frequencies and variable conditions34. In addition, Zhang et al.35 proposed a generalizable diagnosis framework based on synthetic and adversarial sample learning, where a modified 1-D diffusion model generates samples for unseen working states and adversarial samples are injected using fast-gradient perturbations to enhance robustness against distribution shifts. This approach significantly improves performance in gearbox diagnostics under unseen operating conditions. Complementary to this, Han et al.36 introduced a health-aware joint scale-distribution learning strategy for unsupervised anomaly detection, where intrinsic EL-image patterns are modeled through scale learning and compact embeddings are constructed to improve discriminability under contaminated or nonstationary data scenarios.Beyond these domain-centric methods, Siddique et al.37 introduced a semi-supervised Vision Transformer framework that leverages pseudo-label generation, uncertainty-aware refinement, and teacher–student knowledge distillation to enhance robustness when labeled samples are limited. Although the model achieved high accuracy across milling cutting tool datasets, its reliance on iterative pseudo-label refinement and transformer-based architecture requires substantial computational resources and large-scale unlabeled data. Collectively, these studies confirm that distribution shift, class imbalance, and domain variability significantly affect diagnostic performance under varying operating conditions, while most state-of-the-art methods depend on deep multi-stage architectures that demand extensive training effort and sizable datasets.

To overcome these issues, this paper introduces a residual-based preprocessing framework that models the normal behavior of MCPs using an autoregressive (AR) observer. This observer captures the expected response of the system under healthy conditions, and any deviation (residual) highlights possible faults. By extracting residuals from multiple sensors, we amplify the fault signatures while suppressing background interference. These residuals are then processed to extract statistical features across time and frequency domain. Rather than relying on manual selection, we use an automated permutation feature importance (Auto-PFI) strategy to identify the most informative features. This ensures interpretability and avoids overfitting. For final classification, a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) is utilized to model the probability distributions of different fault types. GMMs are well-suited for noisy, low-sample scenarios and provide soft decision outputs based on likelihood scores. The proposed residual-based learning pipeline further addresses the challenges of variable operating pressures, class imbalance, and redundant high-dimensional representations by generating pressure-robust residuals and selecting only the most discriminative features for reliable fault discrimination. Experimental setup

To facilitate fault diagnosis experiments, a dedicated test environment was established featuring a PMT-4008 MCP (manufactured by Hanil, Gwangju, Republic of Korea), operated by a 5.5 kW electric motor. The system was equipped with a comprehensive control interface that allowed fine control over key parameters such as temperature, flow rate, speed, and power, along with a visual display and multiple operational switches. The pump system was integrated with two vertically elevated water storage units; a main tank and a buffer tank to guarantee a net positive suction head (NPSH) at the pump’s inlet. These tanks ensured stable water flow and minimized cavitation risk. The hydraulic circuit connecting the pump and tanks utilized steel pipelines fitted with pressure gauges and manual valves, ensuring accurate monitoring and safe flow regulation throughout the test duration. Sensor placement

To capture high-fidelity vibration signals from the MCP, three PCB 622B01 piezoelectric accelerometers were strategically affixed to the pump casing and key internal components. These sensors were mounted closer to the pump casing and positioned near the impeller and mechanical seal region. All sensors were secured with adhesive to ensure stability during operation at 1733 rpm.

The sensors operated with a sensitivity of 100 mV/g ± 5% and supported a frequency bandwidth of 0.4210 kHz, effectively covering the expected vibration spectrum. Their analog signals were digitized using a National Instruments NI-9234 data acquisition module, which offered 24-bit ADC resolution across four analog input channels, capable of handling input signals up to 13.1 MHz.

Fault conditions

To generate training data for fault classification, the following faults were artificially introduced into the system using controlled modifications:

Mechanical seal hole

Two mechanical seals with 38 mm inner diameter were used. For the MSH condition, a 2.8 mm diameter and 2.8 mm depth hole was drilled into the rotating component of the seal. The stationary component remained untouched to isolate the faulty source.

Mechanical seal scratch

For the MSS fault, a deliberate scratch of 10 mm length, 2.5 mm diameter, and 2.8 mm depth were etched into the rotating surface, once again keeping the stationary part untouched to maintain fault specificity.

Impeller corrosion

To replicate impeller corrosion, a region of metal was removed from a cast-iron impeller, inducing an imbalance and surface discontinuity. This artificial defect was 18 mm long, 2.5 mm wide, and 2.8 mm deep, inscribed within a broader impeller diameter of 161 mm.

These fault conditions were selected to reflect real-world deterioration mechanisms in industrial pumping systems and were carefully designed to preserve the structural integrity of surrounding components, allowing clean and isolated signal capture from each fault type. Proposed method

The proposed fault diagnosis framework for MCP comprises six primary stages, as illustrated in Fig. 4. First, vibration signals are acquired from MCPs under multiple fault types and pressure levels. An AR observer, trained on normal signals, models healthy behavior and generates residuals by subtracting predicted outputs from actual signals. From these residuals, a rich set of features is extracted, pruned through correlation analysis, and ranked using PFI. Finally, a GMM classifies faults based on the selected features, using probabilistic inference to handle class overlaps and severity variations. Each stage is described step by step below:

Multi-sensor feature fusion under variable pressures

In this work, the three vibration sensors are first processed independently to preserve their individual sensitivity to fault-related dynamics. Each sensor signal is modeled using the autoregressive observer to generate a residual sequence that highlights deviations from normal behavior. Treating sensors separately prevents interference arising from differences in amplitude scaling, phase response, and pressure-dependent vibration characteristics across 3, 3.5, and 4 bar conditions. Statistical and frequency-domain features are then extracted from each sensor’s residuals, ensuring that local and global information related to pump faults is retained.

Feature-level fusion is performed by concatenating the three sensor feature vectors into a unified representation. This late fusion approach is preferred over early fusion because operating pressure variations affect each sensor differently and combining raw or residual signals directly tends to amplify distribution drift. By fusing features after residual generation, the model works with normalized, pressure-robust descriptors. The Auto-PFI module then filters the fused set, removing redundant or pressure-sensitive features and retaining the most discriminative ones for downstream classification. Although the framework does not explicitly model domain alignment, the combination of residual normalization and feature-importance-based selection effectively mitigates pressure-induced drift, offering a computationally efficient and interpretable alternative to deep domain generalization methods.

Gaussian mixture model

The classification is handled by a GMM, a probabilistic unsupervised model that can also be applied for semi-supervised tasks. GMM assumes that the feature space is a weighted sum of several multivariate normal distributions. Each fault class is associated with one Gaussian component. Training the GMM involves maximizing the likelihood of the feature data under the mixture distribution. This is done using the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm. The E-step calculates the soft class assignment (responsibility) for each sample. The M-step updates the parameters (means, covariances, and priors) based on these assignments.

Results and discussion

The proposed framework introduces a sensor-fused and data-efficient approach for centrifugal pump fault diagnosis under varying pressure conditions. By using an AR observer to extract residuals across three sensors and refining these through Auto-PFI before classification with GMM, the method achieves high discriminability while maintaining interpretability. To further assess the advantage of using multi-sensors, an ablation study is conducted where the same framework was trained using data from only one sensor (Sensor 1, Sensor 2, or Sensor 3). This isolates the effect of sensor fusion, demonstrating the performance trade-offs when discarding multi-sensor integration. Furthermore, another ablation study is performed highlighting the effectiveness of the Auto-PFI in the proposed method.

For comparison, a state-of-the-art method is used that adapts the Multiscale Permutation Entropy with Local Linear Tangent Space Alignment (MPE-LLTSA) approach. The method captures nonlinear dynamics and embeds signals into a tangent space for fault discrimination38. However, its dependence on handcrafted entropy features and dimensionality reduction introduces sensitivity to noise and pressure variations, which may limit its discriminative power in complex fault scenarios.

The method is also compared with Swarm Intelligence Optimization Entropy (SIOE) based model with XGBoost classification. Here, entropy parameters are optimized using African Vulture Optimization and logistic chaos theory, enabling adaptive feature extraction39. XGBoost then performs classification by using robust ensemble learning. While this method improves entropy-based fault detection and demonstrates higher adaptability than static entropy measures, it remains constrained by entropy’s sensitivity to local variations and lacks the residual-modeling capability of the proposed approach.

The method is further compared with a recently proposed Multi-scale Hybrid Domain Adaptation with Attention (MHD AA) network, which combines multi-scale convolutional feature extractors with a multi-channel attention mechanism to capture fault-related structures from different receptive fields40. The network also incorporates a hybrid domain adaptation strategy to align distributions between source and target domains, making it suitable for unsupervised rotating machinery diagnosis under varying operating conditions. When applied to the centrifugal pump dataset, the model produces the performance values shown in Table 3 and the feature distribution trends illustrated in Fig. 8, where the t-SNE visualization exhibits partial clustering but still reveals noticeable overlap between MSH and MSS samples. While the multi-scale and attention components enrich local feature representation, the reliance on domain adaptation alignment limits its ability to fully discriminate highly similar mechanical seal faults, especially when noise and pressure variations distort the underlying feature manifolds. Moreover, since MHD AA does not incorporate residual-based modeling or parameter-optimized feature selection, its generalization remains inferior to the proposed approach.

To evaluate the diagnostic performance of the proposed framework, four commonly used metrics are employed: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-score. Accuracy measures the proportion of correctly classified samples but may become misleading when the dataset is imbalanced because it is dominated by majority classes. Precision quantifies how many of the predicted fault samples are truly correct, which is important for avoiding false alarms in industrial monitoring. Recall measures the proportion of correctly detected fault samples relative to the total actual samples of that class, making it critical for identifying weak or early-stage faults. The F1-score, defined as the harmonic mean of Precision and Recall, provides a balanced assessment when class distributions are uneven42. Table 3 summarizes the performance across all methods. The proposed framework consistently performed better as compared to both ablation and comparison models, achieving accuracies of 99.64%, 99.29%, and 99.67% at 3, 3.5, and 4 bar pressures, respectively. Precision, recall, and F1-scores remained above 99.2% across all operating conditions, confirming robustness to pressure variations. By contrast, the ablation study results dropped to 96.40% (Sensor 1), 97.84% (Sensor 2), and 96.04% (Sensor 3). This indicates that while individual sensors contribute useful information, fusion markedly improves reliability. Sensor 2 provided slightly stronger results among the single-sensor setups, but none matched the fused configuration. When the proposed method was used without using Auto-PFI, although utilizing all the three sensors, the method achieved an accuracy of 96.04%, precision 96.68%, recall and F1 score 95.58% and 96% respectively.

The confusion matrices in Fig. 10 highlight that the proposed method maintains better classification across classes and pressures, with only a few confusions observed. The better performance of the proposed method stems from three key aspects. First, autoregressive residual modeling captures deviations from normal behavior, providing fault-indicative features that entropy-based methods cannot directly extract. Second, Auto-PFI effectively filters redundant features, ensuring compact yet highly discriminative input for classification. Third, sensor fusion integrates complementary information from multiple sources, overcoming weaknesses of individual sensors and enhancing robustness under pressure variations. Together, these design choices explain why the proposed method achieves better accuracy and separability, outperforming both single-sensor ablations and state-of-the-art entropy-based approaches.

Conclusion and future recommendations

This study proposed a sensor-fused framework for MCPs fault diagnosis under varying pressure conditions. By integrating AR residual modeling, Auto-PFI, and GMM based classification, the method achieved better performance, with accuracy exceeding 99% across 3, 3.5, and 4 bar datasets. The ablation study confirmed that multi-sensor fusion performs better than the single-sensor setups, while comparative analysis against state-of-the-art entropy-based methods further highlighted the robustness of the proposed approach. Visualization through confusion matrices, t-SNE, and ROC curves validated the robustness, separability, and generalization capability of the framework.

The key advantage lies in combining residual-driven fault indicators with adaptive feature selection and sensor fusion, which collectively enhance discriminative power and resilience to operating variations. These contributions establish the framework as a reliable and interpretable solution for intelligent condition monitoring of centrifugal pumps. In future work, the framework can be extended to other rotating machinery and real-time monitoring scenarios and can be further strengthened by incorporating noise-perturbation robustness tests, unseen-condition generalization experiments, and extended perturbation studies, with potential integration into industrial digital twin platforms for predictive maintenance.
