Conservative Management of Breast Implant-Associated Infections: Identifying Risk Factors for Failure
Authors: Idoia Bilbao, María Pascual, Miguel Sogbe, Francisco Carmona-Torre, Trinidad Baselga, Manuel Pina, Iñigo Pineda, José Ramón Yuste, José L del Pozo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Background. Breast implant-associated infections remain a challenging complication, with limited high-quality evidence to guide management. Implant removal is often considered the standard approach, although conservative strategies aimed at implant salvage may be successful in selected patients. We conducted a retrospective observational study of 42 patients diagnosed with breast implant-associated infection at a tertiary hospital between 2000 and 2024. All patients initially received antibiotic therapy without implant removal. Clinical, surgical, and microbiological variables were analyzed to identify predictors of treatment failure, defined as the need for implant removal. Logistic regression and Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were performed. Conservative management achieved infection control in 66.7% of patients, while 33.3% required implant removal. The most common symptoms were erythema (90.5%), pain (88.1%), and swelling (76.2%). Fistula was present in 33% and was strongly associated with treatment failure in univariable analysis (P = .003), with a high odds ratio (7.5) in multivariable analysis. Elevated C-reactive protein and culture positivity correlated with poor outcomes. Staphylococcus aureus (36.8%) and Staphylococcus epidermidis (31.6%) were the most frequently isolated pathogens, although outcomes did not differ by microorganism. Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated a median failure-free survival of 191 months (almost 16 years), highlighting the potential long-term durability of conservative therapy in selected patients. Conservative management may represent a valid therapeutic option for breast implant-associated infections in selected patients, particularly in the absence of fistula and persistent drainage. Prospective studies with larger cohorts and standardized protocols are needed to refine predictive models and establish evidence-based selection criteria for conservative therapy.

Prosthetic breast implantation—commonly performed for esthetic augmentation, correction of breast asymmetry, and post-mastectomy reconstruction in breast cancer patients—is a well-established surgical procedure [1]. Infections associated with breast implants occur in 1.1%–2.5% of esthetic surgeries and in up to 35% of post-mastectomy reconstructions [1–3]. Risk factors for breast implant infection have not been clearly defined in prospective studies with adequate follow-up. However, available evidence suggests that infection risk may be influenced by surgical factors (such as incision type and surgical indication), implant-related characteristics, and patient comorbidities, including obesity, diabetes, renal failure, active skin disorders, and tobacco use [1, 4–6].

Currently, there is no consensus on the optimal management of breast implant infections. To date, no randomized controlled trials have evaluated the optimal management of breast implant-associated infections, and current evidence is mainly based on observational studies.

Traditionally, management has consisted of antibiotic therapy and/or explanation with delayed reimplantation; however, some authors advocate for more conservative approaches aimed at implant salvage [7–10]. Despite this, implant removal is often regarded as the most effective strategy, primarily due to the presence of bacterial biofilm, which hinders eradication without device removal [11]. Nevertheless, surgical intervention may be associated with prolonged hospitalization, increased healthcare costs, and considerable psychological impact on the patient.

Certain clinical variables may help predict the likelihood of success with conservative management. Accordingly, the objective of our study was to identify predictors of failure in the conservative treatment of breast implant-associated infections, with the aim of establishing criteria to guide the decision for surgical removal.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

We conducted a retrospective observational study of 42 patients diagnosed with breast implant-associated infection at the Clínica Universidad de Navarra between January 2000 and December 2024.

All patients had breast implants placed either for prophylactic or therapeutic reconstruction, or for esthetic purposes. Initial management in all cases consisted exclusively of antibiotic therapy, administered orally or intravenously. The primary outcome variable was treatment outcome, defined as follows: treatment failure was considered in patients who did not respond to antibiotic therapy and required surgical intervention to control the infection, whereas treatment success was defined as complete resolution of the infection with antibiotics alone. Implant removal was considered a clinical endpoint rather than a purely microbiological failure, reflecting multidisciplinary decision-making.

The variables analyzed included patient age and the presence of comorbidities such as obesity, diabetes mellitus, and chronic kidney disease. We recorded the type of breast reconstruction (therapeutic, prophylactic, or esthetic) and its association with adjuvant treatments, including lymphadenectomy, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. Implant-related characteristics and surgical factors—such as drain placement, incision site, and implant positioning—were also assessed.

Clinical presentation was evaluated according to local and systemic signs and symptoms, including erythema, pain, hematoma, edema, fistula (defined as a sinus tract communicating with the implant), persistent drainage (defined as ongoing or recurrent wound discharge documented in the medical record that required continued local wound care), fever, elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, and leukocytosis, as well as the time interval from implantation to infection onset. Breast ultrasound examinations were performed to detect periprosthetic fluid collections, which were drained whenever feasible.

Microbiological culture results were reviewed when available, and the most frequently isolated pathogens were analyzed. The duration of antibiotic therapy was recorded, with particular attention to the use of agents with potential anti-biofilm activity (quinolones, trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, daptomycin, linezolid, rifampin, and doxycycline). Follow-up duration was documented for all patients.

Statistical analyses were performed using the Chi-square test and Student's t-test, as appropriate. Logistic regression modeling was applied to identify variables independently associated with treatment failure. In addition, survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier method to assess the time to the event of interest in patients undergoing conservative management. The event was defined as failure of conservative treatment, specifically the need for implant removal. Censored cases were defined as patients who did not experience the event by the end of the follow-up period. This analysis allowed estimation of survival probabilities, as well as median and mean times to treatment failure.

The most frequently reported symptoms were erythema (90.5%), pain (88.1%), and swelling (76.2%), whereas fever occurred in 40.5% of cases. Fistula was present in 33% of patients, persistent drainage occurred in 38.1%, while capsular contracture (11.9%) was less common. Time from implantation to infection onset showed substantial variability, with a mean interval of 318 days (10.5 months) and a maximum of 2994 days (approximately 8 years) and was therefore considered a surrogate marker of infection chronicity. Breast ultrasound examinations were performed to assess for periprosthetic fluid collections; findings were normal in 12 patients (28.6%), and ultrasound-guided drainage was performed in 23 (54.8%). Implant removal was ultimately required in 10/23 (43.5%) patients who underwent drainage compared with 4/19 (21.1%) who did not undergo drainage (OR 2.89, 95% CI .73–11.43), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .125)

All patients initially received conservative management with antibiotic therapy without implant removal. Implant preservation was achieved in 66.7% of cases, whereas 33.3% required implant removal due to treatment failure. Most patients (66.7%) had a history of breast cancer, reflecting the high prevalence of infections among individuals undergoing post-cancer breast reconstruction. Most treatment failures occurred within the first year after infection diagnosis. Notably, 13 of the 14 patients who required implant removal had a history of breast cancer and reconstructive surgery. Early failures were also more frequent among patients presenting with persistent drainage (10/16 vs 4/26) or fistula formation (9/14 vs 5/28). In contrast, patients without these high-risk features often achieved durable long-term implant retention, supporting the presence of clinically heterogeneous subgroups within the cohort.

Kaplan–Meier analysis (Figure 1) showed that, among the 42 patients managed conservatively—ie, with implant preservation and antibiotics alone—66.7% remained free from treatment failure during follow-up, while 33.3% experienced failure. The mean failure-free survival was 164.1 months, and the median was 191 months, indicating that half of the patients remained complication-free for nearly 16 years.

In the univariable analysis (Table 1), both surgical reconstruction (P = .039) and a history of breast cancer (P = .011) were significantly associated with treatment failure. These findings suggest that patients with breast cancer or those who have undergone reconstructive surgery may be at higher risk of unsuccessful conservative management, ultimately requiring implant removal. In univariable analysis, both fistula (P = .003) and persistent drainage (P = .002) were significantly associated with conservative treatment failure. In multivariable analysis, persistent drainage (OR 33.07; P = .005), fistula (OR 15.01; P = .005), and mean CRP levels (OR 1.20 per mg/dL; P = .018) were independently associated with conservative treatment failure (Table 2). The most frequent isolated pathogens were Staphylococcus aureus (36.8%) and Staphylococcus epidermidis (31.6%). While culture positivity correlated with failure, the specific pathogen isolated did not appear to influence outcomes. All patients initially received empirical antimicrobial therapy with ceftazidime plus daptomycin. Daptomycin was deliberately selected due to its activity against biofilm-forming Gram-positive organisms. Antimicrobial therapy was subsequently individualized and de-escalated according to microbiological results, antimicrobial susceptibility testing, and patient tolerance; in 40 cases (95.2%), the de-escalated regimen included biofilm-active antibiotics, with no significant differences between groups (P = 1.000).

The median duration of antibiotic therapy was 83 days (range, 14–196 days). The mean time from infection onset to initiation of antibiotic therapy was 86 days (range, 60–101).

Among patients with treatment failure, implant removal occurred at a mean of 23 days (range, 4–171 days) from initiation of antibiotics, always during ongoing antibiotic treatment. A longer duration of intravenous antibiotic therapy was significantly associated with treatment failure, likely reflecting more severe infections in which prolonged IV therapy represented an intensified but unsuccessful attempt to preserve the implant.

DISCUSSION

Our findings support conservative management as an effective strategy for most patients with breast implant-associated infection, often allowing a prolonged implant-retention period without the need for additional surgery. However, this approach is generally considered less appropriate in patients with sepsis, implant exposure, persistent drainage, or fistula formation. In this context, our results provide additional support for the role of these features as markers of conservative treatment failure.

Despite its clinical importance, robust evidence to guide the management of breast implant infections is lacking. No randomized controlled trials or large prospective cohorts have been conducted to date, and current recommendations rely largely on small case series and expert opinion. Standard management of breast implant related infections typically begins with prompt initiation of empiric antibiotic therapy, followed by close clinical monitoring to determine whether implant removal or salvage is warranted. In cases requiring implant removal, systemic antimicrobial therapy is generally recommended for approximately 14 days in typical bacterial infections, and for several months in the setting of mycobacterial infections [3]. Reimplantation may be considered 3 to 6 months after completing antibiotic treatment, following an antimicrobial-free interval to confirm complete resolution of infection [4]. However, given that implant removal can be costly and psychologically distressing for patients, antimicrobial therapy without explanation is sometimes attempted and may represent a valid option in selected cases [7, 11, 12].

In our study, a longer duration of intravenous antibiotic therapy was significantly associated with failure of conservative management, likely reflecting more severe infections in this subgroup. Nevertheless, our findings also suggest a potential role for a staged approach—starting with intravenous therapy and transitioning to oral antibiotics once clinical stability is achieved—as a feasible strategy in selected patients. Given the absence of standardized guidelines and the marked clinical heterogeneity of these infections, antimicrobial duration should be individualized rather than guided by fixed timeframes. Accordingly, therapy in our cohort was tailored to clinical course, microbiological findings, and treatment response, resulting in substantial variability in duration that should be interpreted descriptively rather than as a standardized recommendation. Furthermore, the timing of implant removal should not be considered a surrogate marker of microbiological failure, but rather a surgical decision based on overall clinical assessment.

Infections caused by virulent pathogens such as S. aureus or fungi, or those showing delayed clinical improvement (eg, persistent fever), often require implant removal to achieve cure [1, 12]. Although S. aureus (36.8%) and S. epidermidis (31.6%) were the most frequently isolated pathogens in our series, treatment outcomes did not differ by pathogen type. This suggests that the need for surgical intervention may not be solely dependent on the specific microorganism isolated, contrary to common expectations [1, 11]. Nonetheless, given the predominance of staphylococcal species, empiric therapy should ensure adequate coverage against these organisms to maximize the likelihood of successful conservative management. Despite the high prevalence of local inflammatory signs—such as pain, erythema, and swelling—only the presence of a fistula was significantly associated with conservative treatment failure in univariable analysis. This finding suggests that common local signs are not predictive of outcome, whereas fistula formation could represent a marker of likely failure. Although this association did not reach statistical significance in multivariable analysis, the elevated odds ratio (7.50) supports its potential clinical relevance when considering patients for non-surgical therapy. Although definitive selection criteria cannot be established from our data, fistula formation and persistent drainage may serve as clinically relevant warning signs when considering implant salvage. In addition, higher CRP levels were independently associated with failure, supporting the role of systemic inflammatory response as a surrogate marker of infection severity. A history of breast cancer and reconstructive surgery was also associated with treatment failure in univariable analysis. Taken together, these findings suggest that persistent drainage, fistula formation, and elevated inflammatory markers identify patients at higher risk of unsuccessful conservative management and should prompt close clinical reassessment. However, conservative management can still be a viable strategy in selected cases.

Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated a gradual decline in infection-free survival over time, providing valuable insight into the long-term durability of this approach. Although the median failure-free survival was 191 months (almost 16 years), substantially longer than the follow-up periods typically reported in implant-related infection studies [7–10], most explanations occurred within the first year, indicating an early period of higher risk. These findings further support the presence of clinically heterogeneous subgroups within breast implant-associated infections, with early failures clustering predominantly among patients with breast cancer undergoing reconstructive surgery and presenting with persistent drainage or fistula formation. Conversely, patients without these high-risk features often achieved durable long-term implant retention, suggesting that careful patient selection may be critical when considering conservative management strategies. Importantly, patients who remained explant-free beyond this initial phase tended to experience durable long-term outcomes, as reflected by the subsequent flattening of the survival curve.

This extended observation window enabled us to capture both early and late failures, thereby reducing the risk of underestimating long-term complications and underscoring that conservative management can achieve durable infection control in appropriately chosen patients. Due to the retrospective design and the consistent initial conservative strategy adopted in our cohort, the comparative effectiveness of one-stage exchange versus implant retention could not be assessed. Surgical exchange should therefore continue to be regarded as a valid therapeutic option within a multidisciplinary, individualized treatment framework. Moreover, the retrospective design, limited sample size despite the prolonged study period, and lack of standardized treatment protocols constitute key limitations. In addition, potential temporal changes over the 24-year study period may have influenced infection risk and case mix, but these factors were not systematically recorded and could not be formally analyzed. Collectively, these limitations highlight the need for prospective studies to validate our findings and refine clinical criteria for patient selection.

CONCLUSION

In our cohort, conservative management achieved infection control in 66.7% of patients with breast implant-associated infections.

Persistent drainage, fistula formation, and elevated CRP levels were independently associated with conservative treatment failure in patients with breast implant infection. These findings highlight the importance of integrating local clinical signs and systemic inflammatory markers into early risk stratification. While conservative management remains a viable option in selected cases, particularly in the absence of fistula, the presence of high-risk features should prompt timely reassessment and consideration of alternative management strategies. To confirm these findings, larger prospective studies with standardized treatment protocols are warranted to validate predictive factors, refine risk stratification models, and establish evidence-based criteria for patient selection in conservative therapy.
