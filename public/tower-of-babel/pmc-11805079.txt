Effect of Bleaching on Surface Roughness of White Spot Lesions Treated with Different Modalities of Resin Infiltration: An Vitro Study
Authors: Aidel Abdullah Alshahrani, Afra Elrashid
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:This experimental study evaluated the effect of resin infiltrant (RI) application modalities and bleaching on surface roughness (SR) of the RI white spot lesions (WSLs).Methods:Ninety-six extracted sound premolar teeth were selected, and their coronal part was embedded in a self-cure acrylic resin block. After demineralization, the samples were randomly divided into four study groups (n = 24) based on treatment modalities used: Group 1, etched + RI; Group 2, 1 time etched + 2 times RI; Group 3, 2 times etched + 1 time RI; and Group 4, 2 times etched + 2 times RI. Later all teeth were subjected to bleaching. The SR was assessed using an optical profilometer, post-bleaching, and RI in all groups.Results:Two-way ANOVA confirmed the significance of “Treatment” and the interaction effect between “Group” and “Treatment.” While the t-test confirmed significant differences in SR when compared to the effect of RI and bleaching among the groups (P < 0.05).Conclusion:Bleaching can negatively impact the SR of resin-infiltrated WSLs and affect it significantly.

INTRODUCTION

White spot lesions (WSLs) are typically precursors to enamel caries and remain a major clinical issue despite preventative measures. The resin infiltration (RI) remains the best WSL treatment.[1]

RI methods enhance restorative dentistry, especially for WSLs. Optimizing clinical results and customizing patient therapies requires an understanding of RI modalities. Since bleaching may modify infiltrants’ physical properties, it’s crucial to study how it impacts resin SR. Examining the interplay between bleaching agents and RI surfaces can provide critical insights into maintaining the durability and appearance of treated lesions. This study evaluated RI modalities and bleaching effects on SR of enamel WSLs, aiming to guide clinical practice and improve patient care.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

The study was conducted at King Saud University’s Dental Biomaterial Research Laboratory, and Riyadh Elm University (REU). Ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of REU (FPGRP/2021/644/660/648). G-power determined sample size by considering α = 0.05 and β = 80%, it was presumed to use 24 samples per group. Ninety-six sound premolars were extracted for orthodontic purposes at REU’s Department of Orthodontics, thoroughly cleansed with tap water and disinfected for 7 days, and subsequently immersed in 0.1% aqueous thymol solution. The coronal part of each tooth was separated and embedded in self-cure acrylic resin using a polyvinyl chloride mold, demineralized with carboxymethylcellulose for 7 days in a transparent plastic container, and stored in an incubator at 37°C to create an artificial lesion on the buccal surface. Samples were washed with distilled water and blotted dry following demineralization. The working area under testing was the central region of the buccal side of the tooth.

Sample teeth were randomly divided into four study groups (n = 24) based on treatment modalities used.

Group 1: Etching + infiltrant application (n = 24)

Group 2: 1-Etching + 2-times infiltrant application (n = 24)

Group 3: 2-times Etching + 1-time infiltrant application (n = 24)

Group 4: 2-times Etching + 2-times infiltrant application (n = 24)

The lesions were treated with a 15% hydrochloric acid solution (ICON-Etch) for 2 minutes, then rinsed and dried, with the number of etching applications varying based on the group. Etching was followed by ethanol (ICON-Dry) application for duration of 30 s. The treated area was then air-dried. ICON-Infiltrant was applied to the tooth surface, left for 3 minutes, removed, and light-cured for 40 seconds. Double RI application was repeated for an additional minute followed by another round of light-curing for 40 seconds. The roughened enamel surface was polished using composite resin discs and cups for a smooth finish, and SR was measured (T2).

For bleaching initially, a layer of 0.5 to 1.0 mm thickness of Opalescence® Boost PF 40% (Ultradent Products, USA) was applied for 20 min on the surfaces of the teeth and wiped with a cotton pellet. The bleaching agent was washed with deionized water for 1 min, dried, and stored in an incubator at 37°C for 24 hours before assessing the SR (T3). A 3D optical non-contact surface profilometer (Contour GT, Bruker, Campbell, CA, USA) assessed SR. The measurements used a standard 5× objective camera lens. Vision64 (v 5.30) application software (Bruker, Campbell, CA, USA) improved SR parameter control and measurement accuracy. All the readings were obtained from the same predetermined region of interest.

The study utilized SPSS version 28 software for statistical analysis, employing a paired sample t-test to compare control and experimental subgroups. Two-way ANOVA and Tukey’s post-hoc multiple comparison tests were conducted to compare the means of different groups, with a significance level of P < 0.05.

RESULTS

In Group 1, SR was 1.87 ± 0.41 after resin infiltration (T2) and it increased to 2.72 ± 0.68 μm post-bleaching (T3), and this difference was statistically significant (P < 0.05). Similarly, the mean SR values in other groups were also less at T2 as compared to T3 but in Groups 2 and 3, this difference was insignificant, whereas in Group 4, it was significant (P < 0.05) [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

This study recommends using a non-contact surface profilometer for SR evaluation due to its high-resolution and faster measurement capabilities without damaging the specimen.[2] SR analysis reveals the dynamic nature of RI and other treatments on dental materials, highlighting the importance of understanding these variations across different treatment stages in line with the previous studies.[34]

The study reveals that RI alone in Group 1 leads to a smoother surface, while bleaching increases it, highlighting the sensitivity of RI to bleaching application as observed by different investigators.[56] The SR values in Group 2 and Group 3 are similar despite variations in etching and infiltrant applications and bleaching presence, attributed to a complex interplay of factors. The depth of resin penetration and its interaction with enamel can significantly affect SR, potentially outweighing the impact of infiltrant applications and bleaching agents. This is in agreement with Ulrich et al.[7]

The research reveals that SR outcomes are influenced by etching, infiltrant application, and bleaching, with variations across treatment stages and groups due to microstructural changes, wear, material interactions, and tooth characteristics as observed previously.[58] Natural variations in enamel, like hardness or mineral content, can affect how enamel reacts to RI and subsequent treatments, affecting SR.[5]

The study’s in vitro experimental design may hinder translation to clinical practice, while a larger sample size and diverse clinical scenarios could provide a more comprehensive understanding.

Conclusion: Surface roughness (SR) varied among RI treatment modalities, with Group 2 having the lowest roughness between T2 and T3 treatment modalities, and Group 4 experiencing the most damaging effect of bleaching.
