Plant growth regulators enhance growth and photosynthetic performance in endangered Firmiana Kwangsiensis seedlings
Authors: Zilong Ouyang, Xianglu Jia, Hongying Liao, Tian Xu, Mei Yang, Li Gong, Ting Xu, Zhiyong Su, Xiaoyu Gao, Quanguang Yang, Lei Zhang, Dahong Su, Weichao Teng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Firmiana kwangsiensis, a species endemic to China’s Karst region, is listed as one of China’s 120 key protected wild plants with extremely small populations. Climate change and human activities have severely restricted the survival and distribution of wild F. kwangsiensis communities, making urgent rescue and conservation measures imperative. To elucidate the growth-promoting mechanisms of plant growth regulators (PGRs) on F. kwangsiensis seedlings, a foliar spray experiment was conducted, and morphological, leaf, stem, and photosynthetic traits were measured. The results showed: (1) PGRs significantly enhanced morphological, stem, and photosynthetic indices, improved leaf traits, and promoted seedling growth and development; (2) Tight linkages existed between photosynthetic and growth traits in seedlings, with net photosynthetic rate (Pn) and chlorophyll content (measured as SPAD values) identified as key drivers of growth; (3) PGRs improved seedling quality by enhancing photosynthetic-growth interactions; (4) PGRs exhibited a “low-concentration promotion and high-concentration inhibition” effect, with highest growth observed under 100 mg·L− 1 NAA treatment, followed by 200 mg·L− 1 IAA and 50 mg·L− 1 SA. This study provides novel insights for the conservation and management of F. kwangsiensis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07118-0.

Introduction

Global climate change and other factors have led to frequent extreme weather events, severely disrupting plant growth and development, eroding plant habitats, and posing significant challenges to global forestry development and ecological conservation [1–4]. Particularly, rare and endangered plants face intensified threats to their survival and proliferation [5–7]. As seedling survival and growth directly determine the species’ survival and the establishment and propagation of future communities, the seedling stage is critical for the growth and development of rare and endangered plants. Extensive studies have shown that seedlings of rare and endangered plants are highly vulnerable to multiple stressors that restrict their growth. For instance, climate change has imposed greater temperature and moisture limitations on the distribution of Dacrydium pierrei seedlings, leading to a decline in its population resources [8]; Excessively shaded environments severely inhibit the photosynthetic processes of Horsfieldia hainanensis seedlings, resulting in stunted growth [9]; Growth of the endangered plant Saussurea esthonica seedlings is constrained by mineral nutrient and water availability [10]; And acid rain conditions (especially at pH 2.5 and 4.0) significantly suppress stem and leaf biomass accumulation in seedlings based on the study of the endangered Horsfieldia hainanensis in southern China [11]. Thus, ensuring healthy seedling growth has become a decisive factor in the conservation of rare and endangered plants [12, 13]. Targeted measures must be implemented to improve seedling growth performance, optimize physiological states, and accelerate seedling establishment, thereby mitigating the inhibitory effects of adverse environmental factors to enhance the conservation and management.

Firmiana kwangsiensis is classified as a Level I nationally protected wild plant and recognized as one of China’s 120 Plant Species with Extremely Small Populations (PSESP) according to the List of Nationally Key Protected Wild Plants issued by China’s National Forestry and Grassland Administration in 2021 (www.forestry.gov.cn/main/3954/20210908/163949170374051.html). Based on the IUCN Red List Categories and Criteria (Version 3.1, www.iucnredlist.org), its conservation status is designated as Critically Endangered (CR). While existing studies have addressed aspects such as its geographical distribution [14], pollination ecology [15], and genetic traits [16], research on seedling conservation remains notably absent, particularly regarding methodologies and management practices for breeding during the critical seedling stage.

Plant growth regulators (PGRs) are extensively utilized in China’s agricultural and forestry sectors [17, 18], and are recognized as a key strategy for enhancing crop yield and quality in 21st-century agriculture [19, 20]. As an exogenous seedling management approach, PGRs offer simplicity, accessibility, and high efficiency, demonstrating significant potential for application in the cultivation of rare and endangered plants [9, 21–23]. Plant growth traits are critical for evaluating plant growth performance [24, 25], which include morphological traits (e.g., plant height, ground diameter, crown breadth), leaf traits (e.g., leaf area, leaf count, leaf thickness, specific leaf weight), and stem traits (e.g., internode length, internode count) and more. Photosynthetic traits such as net photosynthetic rate (Pn) and transpiration rate (Tr) reflect light utilization efficiency [26]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that PGRs effectively regulate plant growth and photosynthetic traits, improving cultivation outcomes. For example, 50 mg·L− 1 6-BA (6-Benzylaminopurine) and GA3 (Gibberellic Acid 3) increased lily (Lilium × elegans ‘MayTime and Fangio’) leaf area by 44–69%, while 50 mg·L− 1 IAA (Indole-3-acetic acid) enhanced flower number per stem [27]; PGRs (IAA, GA3, SA (Salicylic acid)) boosted photosynthetic efficiency and antioxidant enzyme activity in mustard, promoting growth [28]. Further research indicates that PGRs enhance rubisco enzyme activity and photosynthetic rate [29, 30], alongside increases in Tr, stomatal conductance (Gs), and intercellular CO2 concentration (Ci) [31]. The growth-promoting mechanisms of PGRs likely involve their role in regulating cellular processes (e.g., division, elongation, differentiation) and morphogenesis [32]. F. kwangsiensis was identified as a heliophilous species, but the intense shaded conditions in its natural habitat frequently impede normal seedling development. Previous studies have also shown that shading significantly affects the growth of F. kwangsiensis seedlings [33]. We hypothesize that PGRs could ameliorate light limitation by stimulating vertical growth to enhance light acquisition efficiency. To investigate the effects of PGRs on F. kwangsiensis seedlings and identify optimal PGR types and concentrations, our study selected five PGRs (NAA (1-Naphthaleneacetic acid), IAA, GA3, SA, 6-BA) at three concentrations (50, 100, 200 mg·L− 1) for foliar spray experiments, aiming to: (1) Assess the impact of PGRs on F. kwangsiensis seedling growth and evaluate the persistence of these effects; (2) Explore linkages between photosynthetic and growth traits to reveal mechanisms underlying PGR-induced seedling quality improvement; (3) Evaluate the comprehensive efficacy of PGR treatments. The findings will advance understanding of PGR effects on F. kwangsiensis seedlings, elucidate mechanisms for enhancing seedling quality, and provide scientific guidance and experimental foundations for cultivating rare and endangered plant species.

Materials and methods

Treatments

The experiment was conducted at the nursery of Nanning Botanical Garden (22°47′50.14″N, 108°24′16.60″E), located in a subtropical monsoon climate zone characterized by abundant sunlight, warm temperatures, ample rainfall, long summers, and short winters. The site has an annual average temperature of 21.8 °C, annual precipitation of 1,453.4 mm, and annual sunshine duration of 1,480.4 h. Seeds were collected from approximately 17-year-old F. kwangsiensis trees in the Nanning Botanical Garden. Sowing was carried out on August 23, 2023, in nursery pots (height: 15 cm; diameter: 12 cm), with germination observed on August 30, 2023. Our preliminary cultivation observation showed that F. kwangsiensis enters a dormancy phase during winter (December-February), during which exogenous applications of fertilizers or growth regulators are contraindicated. From March onward, Nanning’s spring season commences with abrupt temperature increases, creating optimal conditions for active growth. This period is suitable for pot-up operations (transferring to larger containers) and fertilizer application. So, by early March 2024, 300 seedlings were obtained and subsequently transplanted into larger pots (height: 25 cm; diameter: 20 cm), with one seedling per pot. The substrate consisted of peat soil and yellow loam at a 1:1 volumetric ratio, with a total weight of 6 kg per pot. PGRs are usually sprayed directly onto the leaves [34], and numerous previous studies have demonstrated the efficacy of foliar application of PGRs (such as IAA, GA₃, NAA, etc.) [35, 36]. After a 2-week acclimatization period, uniformly healthy seedlings (plant height: 25.75 ± 4.07 cm; ground diameter: 0.66 ± 0.18 cm) were selected for the PGR spray experiment. The experiment included 16 treatments: control (CK, distilled water), 1-Naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA: 50, 100, 200 mg·L− 1; labeled N50, N100, N200), indole-3-acetic acid (IAA: 50, 100, 200 mg·L− 1; labeled I50, I100, I200), gibberellic acid 3 (GA3: 50, 100, 200 mg·L− 1; labeled G50, G100, G200), salicylic acid (SA: 50, 100, 200 mg·L− 1; labeled S50, S100, S200), 6-benzylaminopurine (6-BA: 50, 100, 200 mg·L-1; labeled B50, B100, B200) (Table 1). The concentrations in this study were defined based on previous research [37, 38] and similar preliminary experiments conducted by our team [39]. Each pot represented one biological replicate, with eight replicates per treatment, totaling 128 seedlings. The experiment followed a completely randomized design (CRD) with a unifactorial design including one control and multiple treatment groups. Foliar sprays were applied three times (mid-March, mid-April, and mid-May 2024), with each spray ensuring full leaf wetness without dripping. Sampling followed a two-stage longitudinal design (Mid-June and Mid-November 2024; Fig. 1). In Stage 1, all eight seedlings in each treatment were measured. Then, three representative seedlings per treatment were selected (those closest to the mean growth value) and labeled. These identical seedlings were measured again during Stage 2 to track individual responses.

Index determination method

Determination of the growth traits

Growth traits were categorized into morphological, leaf, and stem traits.

Morphological traits: Measurements were taken at both stage 1 and stage 2. Plant height (PH) was measured with a measuring tape (accuracy: 0.1 cm). Crown breadth (CW) was estimated from the projected diameter of the canopy (accuracy: 0.01 m2). Ground diameter (GD) was measured using vernier calipers (accuracy: 0.01 mm). Since F. kwangsiensis seedings currently exhibit only a main trunk without lateral branches and exhibit a thick base that tapers toward the apex, their morphology can be considered approximately conical. Trunk volume (TV, cm³) was calculated using Eq. (1). Increments (△, stage 2– stage 1) were calculated for plant height, ground diameter, crown breadth, and trunk volume. Leaf traits: Measurements were taken at stage 2. Leaf length (LL) and width (LW) were measured using a measuring tape (accuracy: 0.1 cm). Leaf area (LA) was determined with a Ci 203 leaf area scanner (Li-Cor, USA; accuracy: 0.01 cm²). Leaf thickness (LT) was measured with vernier calipers (accuracy: 0.01 mm). Total leaf count (TLC) was recorded via manual counting. Leaf count increment (△TLC) was calculated as the difference between stage 2 and stage 1 totals. When sampling, leaves from the 7th–9th node positions (counting from the apex) were collected, sealed in airtight bags, and promptly transported to the laboratory. Samples were oven-dried at 70–80 °C until constant weight was achieved. Leaf dry weight (LDW) was measured using a 0.01 g precision balance. Specific leaf weight (SLW) = LDW/LA. Leaf tissue density (LTD) = LDW/(LA × LT). All measurements were replicated three times. Three technical replicate measurements were taken from each of the 3 biological replicates (n = 3). And the mean of three technical replicates per biological replicate served as its measured value.

Stem Traits: Measurements were taken at stage 2. Maximum and minimum internode lengths (MaxIL, MinIL) were measured using vernier calipers (accuracy: 0.01 mm). Total internode count (TIC) was recorded via manual counting. Total internode length (TIL) was measured from the apex to the basalmost internode with a measuring tape (accuracy: 0.1 cm). Average internode length (AIL) = TIL /TIC. Stem traits were assessed through single measurements per biological replicate (n = 3), where each measurement constituted a replicate value.

Determination of the photosynthesis traits

Photosynthetic traits: Measurements were taken at stage 2 by a Li-6800 portable photosynthesis system (Li-Cor, USA). All measurements were made from 8:00 to 11:00 on a sunny day under a light intensity of 600 µmol·m− 2·s− 1, temperature of 25 ℃, CO2 concentration of 400 µmol·mol− 1, flow rate of 500 µmol·s− 1(C0), relative humidity of 75%. The net photosynthetic rate (Pn, µmol·m− 2·s− 1), stomatal conductance (Gs, mol·m− 2·s− 1), intercellular CO2 concentration (Ci, µmol·mol− 1), and transpiration rate (Tr, mmol·m− 2·s− 1) were automatically recorded. Chlorophyll content (Chl) was estimated using a SPAD-502 m (Minolta Camera Co. Ltd., Osaka, Japan) according to the method of Abdelhamid et al. [40]. Water-use efficiency (WUE) was calculated as Pn/Tr and stomatal limitation value (Ls) was calculated as (1-Ci/C0) × 100%, and non-stomatal limit value (Ci/Gs) was calculated as (Ci/C0)×100%, where, C0 is the atmospheric CO2 concentration (400 µmol·m− 2·s− 1) [41]. Three technical replicate measurements were taken from each of the 3 biological replicates (n = 3). And the mean of three technical replicates per biological replicate served as its measured value.

Qualification index of plant growth, morphological, leaf, stem and photosynthesis traits

Using the total dataset approach [42], and referencing methods from previous soil quality studies [43, 44], relevant indicators across traits were selected to calculate morphological quality index (MQI), leaf quality index (LQI), stem quality index (SQI), photosynthetic quality index (PQI), growth quality index (GQI), and total quality index (TQI). Quality indices were adapted from soil science methods because all calculation steps are established fundamental methods in plant research [45–47], these indices can be used to evaluate the effects of different treatments on F. kwangsiensis seedlings: First, based on biological principles, each indicator is standardized using a membership function: growth-promoting indicators are assigned positive values, while growth-inhibiting indicators receive negative values, thereby determining the direction of each indicator’s impact on growth and obtaining membership degrees. Subsequently, these membership degrees are subjected to dimensionality reduction via principal component analysis, lowering the dimensionality for each treatment. Finally, a weighted scoring calculation yields the Quality indices, which thus more accurately reflects the effectiveness of each PGR treatment. First, all selected indicators were normalized to dimensionless values between 0 and 1. For positive-impact indicators (where higher values are better), normalization followed Eq. The following quality indices were computed: TQI, GQI, MQI, LQI, SQI, and PQI, where GQI was calculated using morphological, leaf, and stem traits, TQI was calculated using morphological, leaf, stem, and photosynthetic traits.

Data processing

The values presented in the figures are the means ± standard deviations (SD). Data were first tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test (P < 0.05) and homogeneity of variance using the Levene test (P < 0.05). Significant differences in plant growth, photosynthetic traits and quality index between different treatments were analyzed by using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Multiple comparisons were corrected using Tukey’s HSD in SPSS 25.0 software. Bar charts and regression analysis charts were drew using Origin 2019b software (OriginLab Development Core Team) to compare the overall situation of different treatments based on a set of traits. Pearson correlations between Photosynthetic traits with morphological traits, leaf traits and stem traits were analyzed and visualized using Origin 2019b software, the same. The Mantel test correlation heat maps were produced using an online website (www.chiplot.online).

Results

Effects of PGRs on morphological traits of F. kwangsiensis seedlings in stage 1

During stage 1, significant differences (P < 0.05) were observed among treatments for plant height, ground diameter and trunk volume, while crown breadth showed no significant variation (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2). For plant height, the I200 treatment showed the highest value (128.23 cm), while the CK treatment showed the lowest value (81.13 cm) (Fig. 2a). For ground diameter, the S50 treatment showed the highest value (21.94 mm), while the N200 treatment showed the lowest value (15.73 mm) (Fig. 2b). For trunk volume, the S50 treatment showed the highest value (154.57 cm³), while the CK and N200 treatment showed the lowest values (55.35 and 56.54 cm³, respectively) (Fig. 2d). Overall, PGRs significantly enhanced the morphological traits of F. kwangsiensis seedlings in stage 1, demonstrating a pronounced growth-promoting effect.

Effects of PGRs on morphological traits of F. kwangsiensis seedlings in stage 2

During stage 2, significant differences (P < 0.05) were observed among treatments for plant height, ground diameter, crown breadth, and trunk volume (Fig. 3). For plant height, the N50, N100, I50, I100, I200, G50, G100 and S50 treatment showed the highest values (165.70, 174.80, 165.07, 175.53, 180.53, 172.87, 171.80 and 169.73 cm, respectively), while the B200 treatment showed the lowest value (124.10 cm) (Fig. 3a). For ground diameter, the S50 treatment showed the highest value (40.48 mm), while the CK treatment showed the lowest value (29.06 mm) (Fig. 3b). For crown breadth, the I200 treatment showed the highest value (0.94 m²), while the N200 and I50 treatment showed the lowest values (0.65 and 0.65 m², respectively) (Fig. 3c). For trunk volume, the S50 treatment showed the highest value (732.11 cm³), while the CK treatment showed the lowest value (294.32 cm³) (Fig. 3d). Significant differences (P < 0.05) were also observed in increments of plant height, crown breadth, and trunk volume, while increments of ground diameter showed no significant variation (P > 0.05). For plant height increment, the N100 treatment showed the highest value (69.67 cm), while the B100 treatment showed the lowest value (29.27 cm) (Fig. 3a). For crown breadth increment, the G100 treatment showed the highest value (0.26 m²), while the I50 treatment showed the lowest value (0.01 m²) (Fig. 3c). For trunk volume increment, the S50 treatment showed the highest value (577.54 cm³), while the CK, N200, B100 and B200 treatment showed the lowest values (238.97, 252.73, 266.99 and 238.86 cm³, respectively) (Fig. 3d). Overall, PGRs significantly enhanced both the morphological traits and their increments in stage 2, further promoting seedling growth.

Figure 4 revealed strong positive correlations among morphological traits and their increments across both stages, with numerous significant relationships (P < 0.05). This indicates synergistic interactions among the morphological traits of F. kwangsiensis seedlings. Notably, similar trends were observed between stage 1, stage 2, and their increments, suggesting that the growth-promoting effects of PGRs persisted over the 5-month experimental period. Effects of PGRs on leaf traits of F. kwangsiensis seedlings in stage 2

During stage 2, significant differences (P < 0.05) were observed among treatments for leaf length, leaf width, leaf area, leaf thickness, leaf dry weight, specific leaf weight, total leaf count, and leaf count increment, while leaf tissue density showed no significant variation (P > 0.05) (Fig. 5). For leaf length and leaf width, the CK treatment showed the highest values (34.20 and 37.13 cm, respectively), while the B100 treatment showed the lowest values (20.83 and 21.90 cm, respectively) (Fig. 5a, b). For leaf area, the CK treatment showed the highest value (415.65 cm2), while the I200, G50, S50, S200, B50, B100 and B200 treatment showed the lowest values (197.72, 179.25, 209.32, 193.24, 191.88, 170.61 and 182.66 cm2, respectively) (Fig. 5c). For leaf thickness, the S50 treatment showed the highest value (0.28 mm), while the CK and N200 showed the lowest values (0.18 and 0.18 mm, respectively) (Fig. 5d). For leaf dry weight, the CK treatment showed the highest value (1.94 g), while the G50 treatment showed the lowest value (1.01 g) (Fig. 5e). For specific leaf weight, the I200 and S50 treatment showed the highest values (6.73 and 6.75 mg·cm⁻², respectively), while the CK treatment showed the lowest value (4.70 mg·cm⁻²) (Fig. 5g). For total leaf count, the I200 treatment showed the highest value (39.00 leaves), while the B100 and B200 treatment showed the lowest values (26.33 and 24.33 leaves, respectively) (Fig. 5h). For total leaf count increment, the N100 treatment showed the highest value (18.33 leaves), while the B200 treatment showed the lowest value (2.00 leaves) (Fig. 5i). Overall, PGRs enhanced leaf thickness and leaf specific weight of F. kwangsiensis seedlings but reduced leaf length, width, and area in stage 2, demonstrating their role in strategically modulating leaf traits to optimize growth under controlled conditions.

Effects of PGRs on stem traits of F. kwangsiensis seedlings in stage 2

During stage 2, significant differences (P < 0.05) were observed among treatments for maximum internode length, minimum internode length, internode count, total internode length, and average internode length (Table 2). For maximum internode length, the N100 and G100 treatment showed the highest values (70.62 and 71.74 mm, respectively), while the CK and B200 treatment showed the lowest value (48.51 and 47.85 mm, respectively). For minimum internode length, the I200 treatment showed the highest value (10.53 mm), while the CK and N200 treatment showed the lowest values (5.22 and 5.30 mm, respectively). For internode count and total internode length, the I200 treatment showed the highest values (49.00 mm and 146.10 cm, respectively), while the CK treatment showed the lowest values (33.33 mm and 78.10 cm, respectively). For average internode length, the G100 treatment showed the highest value (3.49 mm), while the CK treatment showed the lowest value (2.35 mm). Effects of PGRs on quality indices of F. kwangsiensis seedlings in stage 2

During stage 2, significant differences (P < 0.05) were observed among treatments for TQI, GQI, MQI, LQI, SQI and PQI (Fig. 8). Overall, a subset of PGR applications elevated TQI, GQI, MQI, SQI and PQI values, demonstrating their comprehensive regulatory role in seedling growth.

Regression analysis of the 16 treatments revealed highly significant positive correlations (P < 0.001) among GQI, PQI, and TQI (Fig. 9), with the strongest relationship observed between TQI and GQI (correlation coefficient = 0.97, R²=0.94) (Fig. 9c). Notably, all treatments increased PQI, demonstrating that PGRs universally enhanced photosynthetic processes in F. kwangsiensis. Most treatments (except B100 and B200) simultaneously improved TQI and GQI (Fig. 9a, b, c), indicating broad growth benefits. Although both the B100 and B200 treatments increased PQI, the B200 treatment showed a greater reduction in GQI (Fig. 9a), resulting in a TQI lower than that of the CK treatment. In contrast, B100 showed a higher TQI than CK, thereby improving seedling quality.

The mechanism by which PGRs enhance TQI of F. kwangsiensis lies in their simultaneous improvement of both PQI and GQI (Fig. 9d). On the one hand, high photosynthetic capacity drives superior carbon fixation, fueling further growth; on the other hand, robust growth foundation (e.g., structural and biomass development) provides essential support for photosynthetic processes. These two components exhibit synergistic interactions: enhanced photosynthesis accelerates growth, while improved growth sustains photosynthetic efficiency, collectively promoting seedling establishment. Over the long term, photosynthetic efficiency (representing growth rate) and growth foundation (reflecting seedling growth quality) jointly determine the future performance of seedlings. PGRs optimize this positive feedback loop by synchronizing photosynthetic and growth enhancements, thereby improving cultivation outcomes. This dual-action mechanism not only accelerates seedling development but also elevates seeding quality, offering critical support for the conservation and management of endangered F. kwangsiensis.

Key photosynthetic drivers of seedling growth in F. kwangsiensis

Mantel’s test was employed to investigate relationships between photosynthetic traits with GQI and PQI in F. kwangsiensis seedlings (Fig. 10). In total, photosynthetic traits act synergistically to regulate seedling growth and quality, with Pn and Chl serving as key drivers. This underscores the importance of optimizing photosynthesis and chlorophyll retention for enhancing F. kwangsiensis cultivation and conservation outcomes.

Discussion

PGRs exert Five-Month persistent effects on seedlings of F. kwangsiensis

PGRs are pivotal tools in cultivation management, with appropriate application demonstrating significant growth-promoting effects, including enhanced developmental rates, seedling quality, and environmental adaptability [48–50]. In this study, foliar PGRs application increased plant height, ground diameter, and trunk volume in stage 1 seedlings, while also improving crown breadth in specific treatments, indicating accelerated growth. This growth promotion may be attributed to: PGRs enhance plant development by modulating hormone levels (e.g., auxins, cytokinins, and gibberellins), elevating antioxidant enzyme activities (e.g., SOD, POD), boosting photosynthetic enzyme function (e.g., phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPC) and ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase-oxygenase (Rubisco)), stimulating chlorophyll synthesis, improving photosynthetic capacity (e.g., Pn, Gs, Ci, Tr), promoting cell differentiation/elongation, and enhancing root cell vitality [28, 51–54]. These findings align with reports by Prakash et al. [55]. and Megu et al. [56]. However, unlike prior research, we further evaluated plant growth five months after discontinuing application. Our results demonstrate persistent effects lasting at least five months, suggesting that PGRs may exhibit persistent effects over an extended duration. In a study on the native plant Acer mono, PGRs were also found to enhance antioxidant enzyme activity and alter endogenous hormone ratios, thereby increasing rooting rates and rejuvenating the plants [57]. In the threatened species Eucommia ulmoides, increases in tree height and biomass were also observed [23]. However, these parameters decreased in some high PGR concentrations treatments (e.g., N200, B200), may be attributed to: (1) Disruption of cellular redox homeostasis and oxidative damage. (2) Induction of stomatal closure, reducing photosynthetic efficiency. (3) Suppression of cell division/elongation and root development, impairing water/nutrient uptake [58–62]. Notably, five months post-treatment (stage 2), most PGR-treated seedlings maintained higher values in plant height, ground diameter, trunk volume, and crown breadth compared to the control (CK). Across both stages, these parameters and their increments showed strong positive correlations, with certain treatments surpassing CK in crown breadth and trunk volume increments. Previous studies have indicated the improving efficacy of PGRs, which aligns with our findings [55, 56]. However, unlike prior research, we further evaluated plant growth five months after discontinuing application, our results demonstrate persistent effects lasting at least five months, this suggests a persistent promotive effect of PGRs over time. Interestingly, the B50 treatment showed superior plant height in stage 1 (overall treatments) but lost this advantage in stage 2. Conversely, N100 exhibited superior plant height in stage 2 than in stage 1 (overall treatments). This may be attributed to its initially higher concentration in stage 1 (above the optimal level but below the inhibitory threshold): over time, NAA was gradually metabolized by the plant, causing its internal concentration in F. kwangsiensis to decrease to a level more conducive to growth [63]. Although N50 demonstrated some growth-promoting effects on PH in Stage 1, its efficacy diminished as the plant utilized it over time [64]. This is reflected in the lower ΔPH value compared to N100 (a similar trend was observed for ΔGD, ΔCW, and ΔTV). Conversely, the clearly excessive concentration of N200 produced inhibitory effects as early as Stage 1, which adversely impacted subsequent growth even after NAA application ceased. This differential response may also relate to F. kwangsiensis’s absorption and utilization strategies for different PGRs, suggesting N100 aligns best with its metabolic patterns– however, this hypothesis requires further validation. In summary, our results demonstrate that five months after discontinuing PGR application, N100 proved most effective for promoting PH in Stage 2. There is a stark difference in height increments between the two treatments (N100: 69.67 cm vs. B50: 32.07 cm). Further analysis revealed that treatments with higher increments in specific metrics—such as N100 (plant height), S50 (ground diameter), I200 (crown breadth), and S50 (trunk volume)—also achieved higher absolute values in stage 2. This variability might be due to: (1) N100 likely decreased to levels most conducive for F. kwangsiensis PH over time. (2) S50 achieved the highest GD and TV values among all treatments as early as Stage 1, while I200 similarly showed the highest CB value in Stage 1. We speculate that this initial growth advantage may account for their persistent superiority in subsequent development, and may stem from differences in PGR types, concentrations, and species-specific absorption/utilization efficiencies [65–67]. However, due to the limited existing research in this area, this remains a preliminary interpretation requiring further validation. Collectively, our results demonstrate that PGR-induced morphological enhancements in F. kwangsiensis seedlings persist over five-month time. Given that prior growth performance directly dictates subsequent developmental trajectories [68], the early-stage advantages observed here suggest that PGRs may exert prolonged positive effects on seedling growth.

To further elucidate the effects of PGRs, we comprehensively measured leaf, stem, and photosynthetic traits. The results revealed that PGRs exerted significant regulatory impacts on these traits. For leaf traits, PGR application reduced LL, LW, LA, and LDW—contrary to findings in most studies [35, 69]—but increased LT, SLW, TLC, and △TLC, aligning with reports by Demir et al. [70]. and Silva et al. [71]. These shifts indicate PGR-induced modifications in leaf morphological strategies. Specifically, reductions in LL, LW, LA, LDW, along with increases in LT and SLW, serve as photoprotective responses to adaptations to hotter, drier, and high-irradiance karst environments, thereby ensuring sustained photosynthetic functionality [72, 73]. Furthermore, the increased TLC and ΔTLC enable seedlings of F. kwangsiensis to achieve greater carbon gain, facilitating rapid seedling establishment [74]. For stem traits, PGR treatments enhanced stem elongation and developmental vigor. We hypothesize that PGRs alter nutrient allocation patterns in F. kwangsiensis seedlings, prioritizing stem biomass investment. This adaptation likely improves ecological niche occupancy and competitive capacity, which is critical for enhancing survival advantages [75]. For photosynthetic traits, PGRs elevated Pn, Gs, Ci, Tr and Chl, collectively photosynthetic capacity. LT, a determinant of photosynthetic capacity, correlates with advanced tissue differentiation and higher photosynthetic rates in thicker leaves [76]. SLW (biomass per unit leaf area) exhibited strong positive relationships with Pn, Gs and Tr, suggesting that higher SLW enhances gas exchange and light-use efficiency [77]. Plants in arid-prone environments usually adopt drought-resistant strategies by developing thicker leaves, reduced LA, and elevated SLW to mitigate water loss and environmental stressors [78, 79]. Such traits are particularly adaptive in Karst rocky ecosystems [80]. While large, thin leaves maximize light interception, they compromise stress tolerance and create understory shading, reducing spatial efficiency [81]. In this study, PGRs shifted F. kwangsiensis leaf trait strategies toward thicker leaves, higher SLW, and increased leaf count rather than area—a phenotype favoring drought resilience and sustained photosynthesis. However, as no water stress was applied in this study, such adaptive advantages remain hypothetical. Concurrently, PGRs promoted morphological adaptations, including expanded CW, increased PH, and AIL, which alleviated inter-leaf shading and improved spatial resource utilization. Enhanced photosynthetic capacity and reduced stomatal/non-stomatal limitations further stabilized growth under environmental fluctuations. Collectively, PGRs induced beneficial morphological and physiological modifications in F. kwangsiensis seedlings, including stem development, optimized leaf traits, and enhanced photosynthetic performance. These effects persisted for at least five months, demonstrating persistent improvements in environmental adaptability—a critical factor for the conservation of this endangered Karst-endemic species.

Photosynthetic-Growth synergy enhances TQI in F. kwangsiensis seedlings

Photosynthesis is particularly critical for plant growth, and is the key process by which plants accumulate biomass through carbon fixation [82, 83]. Pn directly determines carbon assimilation and organic matter accumulation [84]. Gs reflecting the degree of stomatal opening, regulates gas exchange (CO2 uptake and H2O release) and thus governs photosynthetic, respiratory, and transpiration processes. Higher Gs correlate with enhanced photosynthetic efficiency [85]. Ci serves as a critical indicator of CO2 availability within leaf mesophyll cells [86]. Tr drives water and nutrient uptake from roots to aerial tissues via the transpiration-cohesion-tension mechanism [87]. In arborescent species like F. kwangsiensis, Tr sustains hydraulic lift to upper canopy regions, ensuring apical growth, while simultaneously cooling leaves through evaporative heat dissipation to mitigate photodamage under intense irradiance [88]. Chl, which can be estimated using SPAD meter readings, is indispensable for light harvesting and photosynthetic electron transport [89]. However, shading and drought stress significantly reduce Chl and constrain photosynthetic capacity in F. kwangsiensis seedlings, representing primary environmental stressors for this species [33, 90]. In this study, PGR treatments universally elevated Pn, Gs, Ci, Tr and Chl (except B200, which reduced Pn and Chl). Similar effects were observed in the rare and endangered plant Paris polyphylla. This phenomenon can be attributed to the more effective suppression of chlorophyll metabolism enzymes (inhibiting degradation) and reduction of PIF3 activity (promoting biosynthesis), thereby facilitating photosynthesis [91]. Furthermore, PGRs enhance photosynthetic efficiency by boosting the activity of key photosynthetic enzymes (e.g., PEPC and Rubisco) and stimulating chlorophyll biosynthesis [28, 53]. However, within the same class of PGRs, higher PGR concentrations reduced photosynthetic parameters (except IAA), likely attributable to concomitant disruptions in cellular redox homeostasis, oxidative damage, and induction of stomatal closure, collectively compromising photosynthetic efficiency (as evidenced in Pn, Chl, and Ci measurements) [60–62]. Strong positive correlations among these parameters (Pn–Gs: r = 0.72; Pn–Chl: r = 0.68) indicated synergistic interactions, where elevated Chl, stomatal aperture, and transpiration collectively enhanced CO2 assimilation and hydraulic efficiency. As the Karst ecosystems serves as the primary habitat for F. kwangsiensis, it exhibits a pronounced climatic pattern characterized by distinct alternating wet and dry seasons. Despite reduced WUE under PGR treatments, the high Tr observed here aligns with the wet-season rainfall patterns of Karst ecosystems, where abundant moisture facilitates growth consolidation. In this study, PGRs alleviated both stomatal and non-stomatal limitations (e.g., enhanced chloroplast CO2 utilization), suggesting the potential to improve drought resilience during dry-season aridity in theoretical. Nevertheless, as the research was limited to nursery settings, the efficacy of drought resistance under natural field conditions requires additional verification. Collectively, PGRs enhance photosynthesis and sustain elevated growth rates of F. kwangsiensis seedlings.

Growth traits facilitate the establishment of ecological niche advantages and the maintenance of community stability [92]. Quality indices can be employed to assess the comprehensive level of growth traits. In this study, morphological characteristics, stems, and leaves collectively constituted the growth foundation of F. kwangsiensis seedlings. The results demonstrated that PGRs enhanced the morphological, stem, and photosynthetic quality indices of the seedlings. Improved canopy light interception triggered leaf structural modifications, specifically reduced leaf area and increased leaf thickness, as photoprotective responses to elevated light intensity [68]. Simultaneously, both TQI and GQI showed improvements.

Regression analysis revealed that PQI effectively fitted with both GQI and TQI, underscoring the importance of photosynthesis in seedling development. Mantel’s test further indicated synergistic interactions among photosynthetic parameters, collectively regulating the growth and quality of F. kwangsiensis seedlings, with Pn and Chl emerging as key drivers of growth. Plant growth and development fundamentally depend on light energy absorption and utilization [93], while developmental level reciprocally influence photosynthetic capacity [94]. Enhanced photosynthesis promotes growth and seedings quality, whereas mature growth structures conversely stabilize photosynthetic processes, creating a positive feedback loop. This mutually reinforcing mechanism between photosynthetic and growth traits elevates seedings quality, with PGRs amplifying this virtuous cycle to accelerate growth manifestation.

In this study, PGRs stimulated plant growth, and increased both leaf count and thickness, which were crucial to boost photosynthetic performance [74, 95]. Leaf count directly influences whole-plant photosynthate accumulation, while leaf-level efficiency determines canopy carbon gain [74]. Increased mesophyll thickness represents the primary driver of enhanced net photosynthetic rates [96]—a phenomenon mechanistically linked to: (1) Chloroplast ultrastructural modifications (thylakoid realignment, grana stacking density) [97], and (2) Mesophyll cellular adaptations (cell layer stratification, palisade cell height, endopolyploidy) [98, 99]. PGRs concurrently enhance leaf count (via bud division stimulation [100–102]) and thickness through: (1) Direct cell elongation (increasing epidermal/parenchymal tissues), and (2) Canopy optimization (improved PH/TIC/TIL reduces mutual shading). Post-PGR height growth triggered photoprotective leaf thickening via enhanced light interception [73].

Relative to the CK, PGRs induced simultaneous enhancement of growth and photosynthetic parameters, reflected in elevated GQI, PQI, growth traits (CW, SLW, MinIL, TIC, TIL, AIL) and photosynthetic traits (Gs, Ci, Tr). These findings align with Horsfieldia hainanensis [9] and Nothofagus spp [22]. PGRs synergistically enhance photosynthetic efficiency and nutrient uptake efficiency to promote plant growth, while simultaneously driving leaf cell and lateral bud differentiation, ultimately leading to significant increases in plant biomass [28, 54]. Furthermore, PGRs activate the ascorbate-glutathione (AsA-GSH) cycle, regulate amino acids, and maintain membrane/enzyme homeostasis [103, 104]. Therefore, main conclusions can be summarized: (1). High photosynthetic capacity determines superior carbon fixation, promoting growth, while robust growth base provides solid support for photosynthetic processes; (2). Long-term seedling quality is codetermined by photosynthetic efficiency (growth rate) and growth quality (growth base); (3). The mechanism by which PGRs improve TQI involves dual enhancement of GQI and PQI. In conclusion, the synergistic interaction between photosynthetic and growth parameters significantly drives total quality improvement in F. kwangsiensis seedlings. PGRs potentiate this positive interplay, ultimately elevating the seedlings quality through coordinated optimization of photosynthetic performance and growth base.

Concentration selection of PGRs is critical

PGRs play a vital role in global horticultural and agricultural industries, widely employed to enhance plant growth and quality. Commonly used PGRs such as GA3, NAA, IAA, and 6-BA have been extensively reported for their significant efficacy. For instance, Moin et al. [105]. identified optimal growth promotion in Anoectochilus roxburghii with 100 mg·L− 1 GA3 and 50 mg·L− 1 NAA. Similarly, Patel et al. [106] demonstrated that a combined treatment of NAA + GA3 (40 ppm + 50 ppm) enhanced growth traits in Solanum melongena. 6-BA has been proven effective in promoting maize growth [107] and improving apple quality [1], while IAA enhances rice growth [108]. Numerous studies also confirm that PGRs elevate photosynthetic capacity and growth rates in plants [53, 109]. In this study, PGRs exhibited pronounced growth-promoting effects, aligning with previous findings. However, efficacy varied significantly across types and concentrations. For example, IAA treatments consistently resulted in significantly higher TQI compared to CK, with TQI progressively increasing and growth promotion intensifying at higher concentrations, suggesting that further IAA concentration optimization may maximize efficacy. However, excessive PGRs affected plant growth (such as morphological, foliar, stem, and photosynthetic traits), similar conclusions were observed in previous studies [58, 61, 62]. While the N100 treatment markedly elevated TQI, N200 showed no significant difference from CK. NAA promotes growth at optimal concentrations. However, increasing concentrations progressively diminish this stimulatory effect, ultimately inducing growth inhibition at excessive concentrations. Research confirms that while moderate NAA concentrations enhance growth, excessive application disrupts auxin signaling pathways, triggering growth suppression. This phenomenon is exemplified in Rehmannia glutinosa, where excessive NAA concentrations downregulate auxin response factors (ARFs) and AUX/IAA gene expression, thereby disrupting cell division-differentiation balance and impairing morphogenesis [110]. In this study, the N100 treatment significantly increased TQI, whereas N200 showed no significant difference from the CK. This differential response reflects N100’s alignment with F. kwangsiensis’s growth, whereas the N200 concentration provides no measurable growth advantage. We infer that concentrations exceeding 200 mg·L⁻¹ would increasingly dominate inhibitory effects, yielding TQI reduction. These results underscore the necessity of precise and rational selection of PGR types and concentrations to optimize cultivation efficiency.

SA has been documented to exert both growth-promoting and growth-inhibiting effects. At moderate concentrations, SA significantly enhances seedling quality [111, 112]. The underlying mechanisms involve: (1). SA-induced increases in PH, GD, and TLC, contributing to robust seedling development; (2). SA stimulation of root system architecture, including root hair formation, lateral root proliferation, and root elongation, thereby improving water/nutrient uptake and growth [112, 113]; and (3). SA-mediated promotion of bud differentiation [114] and photosynthetic efficiency [115], synergistically reinforcing growth through root-leaf coordination. This study demonstrating that SA elevates TLC, GQI, and PQI in F. kwangsiensis seedlings, likely via root-leaf synergy. Conversely, excessive SA concentrations induce stress tolerance but suppress growth [116], attributed to SA-mediated inhibition of gibberellin (GA) signaling, triggering growth arrest and stress adaptation [117]. In this study, SA treatments enhanced seedling growth, suggesting both appropriate concentration ranges and the absence of environmental stressors under controlled cultivation conditions. And the lower concentration of salicylic acid (SA, 50 mg·L⁻¹) exhibited significantly enhanced treatment efficacy, leading to a notably higher TQI. This improvement manifested through significant increases in multiple parameters compared to S100 and S200 treatments, including morphological traits (PH, GW and TV), leaf traits: (LT, TLC and ΔTLC), stem traits: (MIL) and photosynthetic traits: (Pn, Gs, Tr and Chl).

Similar to SA’s dual effects, excessive concentrations of auxins (e.g., IAA) [118], paclobutrazol (Cultar) [119], and other PGRs are widely reported to inhibit growth. Although most PGRs significantly improved TQI in F. kwangsiensis seedlings (except N200, G200, B100, B200), NAA, GA3, SA, and 6-BA exhibited declining TQI trends with increasing concentrations, suggesting a concentration threshold beyond which growth promotion transitions to phytotoxicity. Conversely, IAA treatments may benefit from further concentration optimization to maximize efficacy. This occurs because IAA plays a critical role in promoting cell elongation: it softens cell walls, enabling turgor pressure to drive cellular expansion [100]. This hormone maintains apical dominance by suppressing lateral bud growth, redirecting energy to vertical growth, and stimulating adventitious root formation to promote rooting [120]. Furthermore, IAA regulate the differentiation of vascular tissues (such as xylem and phloem), ensuring these tissues efficiently transport water and nutrients [121]. IAA acts as an activator of ascorbate oxidase, an enzyme involved in the ascorbate-glutathione (AsA-GSH) cycle. This process reduces ascorbate levels, which is crucial for maintaining redox homeostasis essential for stable plant growth [103]. However, the upper threshold concentration requires future determination.

These findings underscore that F. kwangsiensis growth allocation is exquisitely sensitive to exogenous PGRs, with concentration being a pivotal determinant. Rational PGR selection and precise concentration control are imperative in cultivation and management practices to avoid counterproductive overapplication.

Comprehensive evaluation of PGRs efficacy

In the production practice, distinct functional priorities exist among PGRs. For example: GA3 excels in seed germination promotion and fruit yield enhancement [122]; IAA regulator cell division, elongation, and xylem formation, yet suffers from rapid degradation and high cost [123]; NAA is a broad-spectrum PGR which induces adventitious root formation, enhances metabolism, and boosts photosynthesis [124]; 6-BA, a cytokinin, promotes lateral bud growth and stress tolerance [107]; SA exhibits multifaceted bioactivity, including stomatal regulation and improved disease/drought resistance [125]. PGRs regulate plant development through hormonal signaling pathways, serve as the primary molecular signals regulating cell division and expansion [126]. Auxin mediates phosphorus-zinc homeostasis and NAA-enhanced root elongation [127], regulates division via SKP2A [128]and exhibits reciprocal regulation with cytokinin (CTK) [129]: CTK influences auxin (IAA) biosynthesis while auxin modulates CTK levels in by inducing CKX, suppressing IPT and activating biosynthetic genes [130]. Gibberellin triggers receptor-DELLA binding, enabling SCFSLY1 -mediated DELLA degradation and subsequent PIF release to drive shade-avoidance/elongation [131]. Auxin and GA jointly regulate stem negative gravitropism by antagonizing XET expression [132]. CTK drives shoot meristem proliferation through KNOX/STIP/WUS [133], and AIM1-regulated SA synthesis impacts meristem activity [134]. While this study evaluated PGR effects on F. kwangsiensis seedlings through morphological, leaf, stem, and photosynthetic traits using TQI, it remains limited in assessing tissue development, microstructural changes, metabolic processes, lateral bud differentiation, stress resilience and hormonal signaling pathways. For instance, while N100 showed a significantly higher TQI value compared to the others, their comparative efficacy in stress tolerance remains unaddressed.

To address practical needs, we classified treatments via TQI-based cluster analysis, grouping regimens with similar growth-promoting efficacy. Results were revealed that: treatments N100, I200, and S50 were classified into Level 1 (TQI = 0.66 ~ 0.71), demonstrating optimal growth promotion characterized by tall plant stature, thickened stems, dense foliage with robust leaf thickness, and enhanced photosynthetic performance. Level 2 (TQI = 0.43 ~ 0.56) included G100, G50, I100, S100, N50, B50, I50, S200, and G200, which exhibited moderate yet significant growth-enhancing effects. In contrast, Level 3 (TQI = 0.24 ~ 0.33) comprised B100, N200, CK (control), and B200, showing negligible growth promotion compared to higher-tier treatments. For F. kwangsiensis cultivation, prioritization follows Level 1 > Level 2 > Level 3. Crucially, regardless of PGR type, concentration optimization is paramount: insufficient concentrations yield marginal effects, while excessive concentrations induce inhibition. These findings provide actionable guidelines for precision dosing in nursery management. Our findings indicate that in forest nurseries management, appropriate application of PGRs can facilitate the successful establishment of rare and endangered plant seedlings, thereby improving propagation efficiency. PGRs theoretically stimulate seedling growth, enabling plants to occupy higher ecological niches, reduce shading and capture more sunlight, and thus strengthen their competitive abilities. However, since this experiment was exclusively conducted in artificially managed nurseries, further comprehensive evaluation of field applications—particularly in in-situ and ex-situ conservation contexts—remains necessary.

Conclusion

PGRs significantly enhanced the growth and development of F. kwangsiensis seedlings by promoting morphological and stem development, improving leaf traits, and elevating photosynthetic capacity and environmental adaptability. These effects persisted for at least five months. The underlying mechanism lies in the PGR-mediated positive interaction between photosynthetic and growth parameters, synergistically reinforcing seedling quality. In practical management, precise concentration control is critical: suboptimal concentrations induce negligible effects, while excessive concentrations induce growth inhibition. Of the tested treatments, 100 mg·L⁻¹ NAA showed the best overall performance for seedling quality, making it the recommended treatment for conservation nurseries. These regimens are recommended as priority strategies for enhancing F. kwangsiensis nursery production. This study provides critical insights for optimizing conservation and management strategies of botanical resources in Karst ecosystems in China.
