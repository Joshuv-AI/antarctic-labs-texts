From lesion to circuit modulation: A narrative review of psychosurgery in refractory obsessive–compulsive disorder, treatment-resistant depression, and pathological aggression
Authors: Héctor Aceituno, Juan José Valero, Juan Ignacio López Urdaneta, René Mena-González, Eugenio Cerezo-Prado, José Vásconez-Fabre, José Villamediana-Rodríguez, Anthuan Hazkour, Francisco Rico-Fernández, Jennifer Vela-Poveda, Luisana Maldonado-De Santiago
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: To synthesize the available clinical evidence on psychoneurosurgical techniques in refractory obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD), treatment-resistant depression (TRD), and refractory pathological aggression.Methods: A structured narrative review of prospective studies published between 1999 and 2026, identified in PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, and Scopus, with a complementary search in Google Scholar. Randomized controlled trials, quasi-experimental studies with a control group, and prospective cohorts with a minimum 6-month follow-up, standardized outcome measures, and a minimum sample size of 10 patients were prioritized; for refractory pathological aggression, given the scarcity of trials, selected prospective and retrospective series were additionally included.Results: Both stereotactic ablative techniques (capsulotomy, cingulotomy, gamma radiosurgery, focused ultrasound) and deep brain stimulation (DBS) constitute therapeutic options for refractory patients. DBS of the anterior ventral limb of the internal capsule (vALIC) and gamma ventral capsulotomy (GVC) achieve response rates of 50–62% in refractory OCD. In TRD, DBS of the subcallosal cingulate and the vALIC shows response rates of 40–55% in long-term open phases, although sham-controlled trials have yielded predominantly null results in their blind phases. For pathological aggression, DBS of the posteromedial hypothalamus reports improvements of 63–91%, although on a largely uncontrolled evidence base. These figures should be interpreted with caution given the heterogeneity of designs, response thresholds, and follow-up duration.Conclusion: Contemporary psychoneurosurgical techniques constitute reasonable therapeutic options for patients with severe refractory psychiatric disorders, with moderate efficacy profiles and acceptable safety in specialized centers, although the strength of the evidence is unequal across conditions and is weaker in aggression. Multicenter controlled trials and prolonged prospective follow-up are required.

INTRODUCTION

Historical context

Psychosurgery originated in the prefrontal leucotomies developed by Egas Moniz in 1935 and popularized by Walter Freeman and James Watts through the transorbital lobotomy during the 1940s. Performed without stereotactic guidance and on imprecise indications, these procedures produced devastating outcomes that led to the widespread discrediting of psychiatric neurosurgery.[19,48,62] However, the development of stereotaxy and neuroimaging enabled an evolution from extensive lesions toward interventions focused on the specific neural circuits implicated in psychopathology.[40]

A parallel line of inquiry, less prominent in most contemporary reviews, addressed pathological aggression and its relationship to limbic and temporal-lobe dysfunction. From the 1950s to the 1970s, the Spanish neuroscientist José Delgado pioneered chronic intracranial electrical stimulation in animals and humans and introduced the implantable “stimoceiver,” a device that allowed deep cerebral structures to be stimulated and their electrical activity recorded in freely moving subjects.[12,48] In parallel, at Harvard, the neurosurgeon Vernon H. Mark and the psychiatrist Frank R. Ervin, together with William H. Sweet, combined depth-electrode recording, deep brain stimulation (DBS), and stereotactic amygdalotomy in patients with intractable psychomotor seizures and uncontrolled violence, work synthesized in their influential and controversial monograph Violence and the Brain (1970).[16,35] These investigators explicitly conceptualized “pathological aggression” as, in selected cases, a manifestation of demonstrable temporal-lobe and amygdalar pathology amenable to functional neurosurgery, thereby anticipating the contemporary rationale for the limbic–hypothalamic targets employed in refractory aggression today.[16]

The modern era of stereotactic psychosurgery began with the anterior cingulotomy described by Ballantine in the 1960s and the anterior capsulotomy introduced by Leksell, procedures that demonstrated efficacy with a substantially better safety profile than the lobotomies.[29] It should be emphasized, however, that electrical stimulation of deep cerebral structures for behavioral and psychiatric purposes long predates the modern DBS era: building on Delgado’s work, stimulation of subcortical targets was explored from the 1950s onward, chronic DBS was first implemented for chronic neuropsychiatric conditions by Bechtereva and colleagues in 1967, and the technique was subsequently consolidated for movement disorders during the 1980s.[12,48] Against this background, the sham-controlled series of chronic electrical stimulation of the anterior limb of the internal capsule (ALIC) reported by Nuttin et al. in 1999 did not inaugurate DBS as such, but rather marked the beginning of the contemporary era of reversible and adjustable neuromodulation applied to refractory obsessive– compulsive disorder (OCD), which constitutes the point of departure for the present review; the earlier foundational stimulation and ablative literature is accordingly treated as historical background.[42]

Technological evolution

Contemporary psychosurgical techniques fall into two broad families:

Ablative techniques

Include radiofrequency thermocoagulation (capsulotomy, cingulotomy), gamma knife radiosurgery (GVC), magnetic resonance-guided laser interstitial thermal therapy, and magnetic resonance-guided focused ultrasound (MRgFUS). These techniques generate irreversible but highly focused lesions in defined anatomical targets.[15,31]

Neuromodulation techniques

Principally DBS, which allows continuous and adjustable electrical modulation of neural circuits through implanted electrodes. Its main advantage is reversibility and the possibility of post-implant parameter optimization.[61]

Clinical rationale: Refractoriness

The concept of refractoriness constitutes the cardinal criterion for considering neurosurgical interventions. In OCD, it is defined as the persistence of severe symptoms (Yale–Brown Obsessive–Compulsive scale [Y-BOCS] ≥24, Y-BOCS) despite adequate use of at least three selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors at maximum tolerated doses, antipsychotic augmentation, and cognitive-behavioral therapy with exposure and response prevention over a minimum period of 5 years.[5] In treatment-resistant depression (TRD), failure of multiple adequate pharmacological trials, including electroconvulsive therapy, is required, with a current depressive episode lasting at least 2 years.[63] For pathological aggression, refractoriness implies failure of conventional pharmacological and behavioral management with persistence of severe self- and hetero-aggressive behaviors that compromise the integrity of the patient and their environment.[56]

Principal neurosurgical targets

The anatomical targets used in psychosurgery are grounded in cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical circuit models for OCD and depression, and in limbic-hypothalamic circuits for aggression: [1,19]

Refractory OCD

ALIC/ventral limb of the internal capsule (vALIC), nucleus accumbens (NAcc), bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST), subthalamic nucleus (STN), anterior cingulate.

Treatment-resistant depression

Subcallosal cingulate (SCC/Cg25), ventral capsule/ventral striatum (VC/VS), vALIC.

Pathological aggression

Posteromedial hypothalamus (PMH), amygdala, NAccs.

Objectives of the review

The objectives of the present narrative review are: (a) To synthesize the prospective clinical evidence on ablative and neuromodulatory psychosurgical interventions in refractory OCD, TRD, and refractory pathological aggression; (b) to compare the efficacy and safety of ablative techniques and DBS; (c) to appraise the methodological quality of the available evidence; and (d) to discuss the ethical considerations and future directions of the field.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Review design

A structured narrative review was conducted based on systematic searches completed in May 2026. The strategy was designed to identify prospective studies on the efficacy and safety of psychosurgical interventions in the three conditions of interest.

Search strategy

The search combined bibliographic databases with manual screening of the reference lists of relevant studies and reviews:

PubMed/MEDLINE and Web of Science

Structured search using MeSH terms and free text combined for each condition.

Scopus

Title, abstract, and keyword search to broaden coverage beyond MEDLINE-indexed literature.

Google scholar

Complementary search and citation tracking (backward and forward) of the included studies.

The search terms included combinations of: “psychosurgery,”, “deep brain stimulation,”, “capsulotomy,”, “cingulotomy,” “gamma knife,” “focused ultrasound,” “obsessive–compulsive disorder,” “treatment-resistant depression,” “pathological aggression,” and “refractory aggressiveness.” Language filters (English, Spanish, Portuguese, and French) and a time period (January 1999–May 2026) were applied. The start year does not imply that DBS originated in 1999, electrical stimulation of deep cerebral structures for psychiatric and behavioral purposes dates back to the 1950s–1960s (Delgado, Heath, Bechtereva), but was chosen because 1999 marks the beginning of the contemporary era of reversible, sham-controlled neuromodulation for refractory OCD reported by Nuttin et al.; [42] the earlier foundational stimulation and ablative literature is addressed in the historical introduction rather than in the systematic synthesis.

Representative database-specific search strings. The following strings illustrate the strategy for each condition (dates limited to 1999–2026; adapted syntactically to each interface). The strategy was replicated, with equivalent term mapping, in Web of Science and Scopus, and complemented by forward/backward citation tracking in Google Scholar.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Adult population (≥18 years); confirmed diagnosis of refractory OCD, TRD, or refractory pathological aggression; ablative or DBS psychosurgical interventions with a primary psychiatric indication; designs comprising randomized controlled trials (parallel, crossover, sham-controlled), quasi-experimental studies with a control group, and prospective cohorts; minimum follow-up of 6 months; minimum sample size of 10 treated patients; and publications in English, Spanish, Portuguese, or French. Exceptionally, for refractory pathological aggression, where no randomized trials exist, selected prospective and retrospective case series with n ≥10 and standardized outcomes were admitted, explicitly acknowledging their lower level of evidence. For this indication only, the adult-age restriction was relaxed: because refractory pathological aggression is frequently associated with intellectual disability and early-onset behavioral disorders, several of the available series comprise pediatric, adolescent, or mixed-age cohorts. Such cohorts were therefore admitted for the aggression stream, and any age-dependent findings (e.g., the more pronounced improvements reported in pediatric patients) are flagged explicitly in the corresponding section and are not extrapolated to the adult evidence base for OCD and depression, which remained restricted to patients ≥18 years.

Exclusion criteria

Individual case reports or series with n < 10; exclusively pediatric populations in the OCD and depression streams (this restriction was not applied to the pathological-aggression stream, as justified in the inclusion criteria); primarily neurological indications without a psychiatric objective; non-surgical techniques (transcranial magnetic stimulation [TMS] and transcranial direct current stimulation [tDCS]); and editorials, letters, or conference abstracts. In OCD and depression, where prospective and randomized designs are available, purely retrospective studies were additionally excluded from the study.

Outcomes of interest

The prespecified outcomes were: (a) Symptom severity measured by validated scales (Y-BOCS, Y-BOCS, for OCD; 17-item hamilton depression rating scale [HDRS-17], or Montgomery–Åsberg depression rating scale [MADRS], for depression; overt aggression scale [OAS], or Modified OAS [MOAS], for aggression); (b) response rate, defined as a ≥35% reduction in Y-BOCS for OCD, ≥50% in HDRS-17/MADRS for depression, and ≥50% in OAS/MOAS for aggression; (c) remission rate, operationally defined as Y-BOCS ≤14 in OCD, HDRS-17 ≤7 or MADRS ≤10 in depression, and the absence of clinically significant aggressive episodes in aggression; (d) quality of life; (e) global functioning; (f) neuropsychological profile; (g) safety and adverse events; and (h) frequency/severity of aggressive episodes. When a study used different thresholds, the original definition was respected and indicated in the synthesis.

Methodological quality assessment

To contextualize the strength of the evidence, the methodological quality of the studies was appraised in a structured manner, matching each tool to the corresponding design: the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool version 2 (RoB 2) was applied to randomized controlled trials; the risk of bias in non-randomized studies of interventions (ROBINS-I) tool to non-randomized studies with a comparator (including pre/post designs and secondary connectomic analyses); and the Newcastle-Ottawa scale (NOS) to cohorts and observational series. Given the narrative character of the review, this appraisal, conducted by the authors, was conceived as an interpretive aid to the synthesis rather than as a formal risk-of-bias assessment oriented toward meta-analysis.

Data synthesis

Given the clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies (variability in surgical targets, response criteria, measurement instruments, and follow-up duration), no formal quantitative meta-analysis was performed. The evidence was synthesized narratively, organizing the findings by clinical condition and surgical technique. Consistent with the narrative design, no formal grading of the certainty of evidence (e.g., GRADE) was applied; the relative strength of the findings is discussed qualitatively in light of the methodological quality of the studies that support them.

RESULTS

Study selection

Of the approximately 550 records reviewed (around 168 for refractory OCD, 185 for TRD, and 197 for refractory pathological aggression), an estimated 430 were excluded after title and abstract screening as off-topic, non-primary, or duplicate, leaving approximately 120 articles assessed in full text. Of these, about 64 were excluded for the following reasons: sample size below 10 or single case reports (n ≈ 22); editorials, letters, or reviews without primary data (n ≈ 12); primarily neurological indications without a psychiatric objective (n ≈ 10); purely retrospective design in the OCD and depression streams (n ≈ 9); non-surgical techniques (TMS/tDCS) (n ≈ 8); and follow-up shorter than 6 months (n ≈ 3). After applying the eligibility criteria described, 56 primary studies were selected for the narrative synthesis: 24 on refractory OCD, 21 on TRD, and 11 on pathological aggression [Figure 1]. By design, they comprised 10 randomized controlled trials (parallel-group, crossover, or sham-controlled), 10 non-randomized studies with a comparator group, and 36 open prospective cohorts and series. Consistent with the narrative design, the intermediate counts are approximate. Methodological quality was appraised with RoB 2 (randomized trials), ROBINS-I (non-randomized studies with a comparator), and the NOS (observational studies).

NOS summary

Of the 46 non-randomized studies (assessed with NOS and, where a comparator group was available, also with ROBINS-I), 19 (41%) achieved high quality (≥7 stars), 23 (50%) moderate quality (5–6 stars), and 4 (9%) low quality (<5 stars). The principal limitations were the absence of a non-exposed comparator group (comparability domain) and variability in follow-up duration.

RoB 2 summary

Of the 10 randomized controlled trials assessed with RoB 2, 5 (50%) showed low risk of bias across all domains, 4 (40%) raised “some concerns” (mainly in the domains of deviations from the intervention and missing data), and 1 (10%) showed high risk (BROADEN, owing to early termination for futility and potential attrition bias). The domains with the highest frequency of concerns were D2 (deviations from intended interventions, related to the difficulty of maintaining blinding in DBS studies due to perceptible stimulation side effects) and D3 (missing data, linked to prolonged follow-up with patient loss). The non-randomized studies with a comparator, including the pre/post designs of hypothalamic DBS and the secondary connectomic analyses, were assessed with ROBINS-I and mostly showed a moderate risk of bias, attributable to residual confounding and participant selection inherent to these designs.

Implications for the interpretation of the results

Overall, the methodological profile of the included literature reveals a field that has moved from uncontrolled case series toward prospective and randomized sham-blinded designs, although structural limitations persist that condition the strength of the available evidence. The predominance of moderate-quality observational studies (50%) reflects the ethical and logistical constraints inherent to psychosurgery research, small populations, the impossibility of randomization in ablative procedures, and prolonged follow-up with inevitable loss of participants. Nevertheless, the 41% of observational studies of high quality and the 50% of trials with low risk of bias indicate that reference centers have progressively adopted rigorous methodological standards. The principal threats to internal validity identified – absence of non-exposed comparators, difficulty maintaining effective blinding in DBS, and attrition rates in long-term follow-up – should be considered when weighing the response and remission rates presented in the subsequent sections. In particular, the early termination of the BROADEN trial[26] for futility constitutes a critical finding that qualifies the interpretation of the efficacy of subcallosal DBS in TRD and underscores the need for more prolonged stabilization phases before assessing the primary outcome. These methodological findings justify caution in generalizing the results and reinforce the need for multicenter trials with expanded samples, optimized blinding protocols, and intention-to-treat analyses as future standards to consolidate the evidence base in psychosurgery.

Refractory OCD

Anterior capsulotomy

Anterior capsulotomy, performed by radiofrequency thermocoagulation or gamma radiosurgery, has been one of the most studied ablative techniques for refractory OCD. A comparative review of the literature (20 studies, 170 patients) found that anterior capsulotomy (108 patients) produced a mean 51% reduction in the Y-BOCS score, with a mean follow-up of 61 months, exceeding the 40% reduction observed with VC/VS DBS (62 patients; 19-month follow-up). Patients undergoing capsulotomy showed a 9% higher probability of achieving remission than those treated with DBS.[43]

GVC, the modern variant of the technique using Gamma Knife radiosurgery, was evaluated in the first double-blind randomized controlled trial published by Lopes et al.,[32] which demonstrated the superiority of radiosurgery over the sham procedure. Subsequent studies with extended follow-up reported response rates of 56–60% in experienced centers.[37,46] Refinement of the technique has substantially reduced complications (frontal edema, radionecrotic cysts) through optimization of dose and stereotactic targets.[37]

Bilateral anterior cingulotomy, another classic ablative technique, has demonstrated response rates of approximately 40–56% across reported series, with a generally favorable safety profile. Compared with capsulotomy, cingulotomy shows lower efficacy but also a lower risk of cognitive and behavioral adverse effects.[29] The limbic leucotomy strategy (combination of cingulotomy and subcaudate tractotomy) achieves socialization rates of 75% in operated patients with minimal pharmacological support in series using staged interventions.[29,40]

Deep brain stimulation

vALIC/VC/VS/NAcc DBS

The most extensively studied target for DBS in OCD is the anterior ventral capsule and ventral striatum region, for which a broad body of accumulated experience and specific reviews exists.[4,9,27] The largest prospective cohort study to date included 70 patients with refractory OCD treated with vALIC DBS, reporting a mean 40% reduction in Y-BOCS. In the long-term follow-up of 50 patients from this cohort, the response rate (≥ 35% Y-BOCS reduction) reached approximately 52% with benefits sustained at 5 years.[13,22] An international prospective multicenter study of 30 patients reported a 42% improvement in Y-BOCS.[61]

BNST DBS

Stimulation of the BNST has emerged as a promising target. A double-blind sham-controlled randomized trial demonstrated significant efficacy, with reduction of obsessive–compulsive symptoms during the active stimulation phase relative to the sham phase.[38] Luyten et al.[33] reported favorable results in a prospective cohort of patients with severe OCD, with response rates of 50%.

Subthalamic nucleus (STN) DBS

The randomized crossover trial by Mallet et al.[34] in 16 patients reported a 25% reduction in Y-BOCS during active versus sham stimulation, with level II evidence. Although the effect size was smaller than at other targets, subthalamic stimulation demonstrated additional benefits on associated checking symptoms.[34]

According to the consensus statement of the World Society for Stereotactic and Functional Neurosurgery, DBS for OCD using multipolar electrodes implanted in the anterior ventral capsule region (including the BNST and NAcc) is considered an emerging but not yet established therapy, partially meeting the criteria of two randomized controlled trials with an acceptable risk–benefit ratio.[61]

Focused ultrasound

MRgFUS represents the newest technique for bilateral thermal capsulotomy in OCD. Kim et al.[30] published a prospective study with 2-year follow-up demonstrating a significant reduction in Y-BOCS. Hamani et al.[23] reported long-term safety and efficacy results confirming the feasibility of this minimally invasive approach, which requires neither cranial incision nor exposure to ionizing radiation. MRgFUS enables real-time monitoring of temperature and lesion localization, offering theoretical advantages over gamma radiosurgery in terms of precision and intraoperative feedback.[15,40]

Treatment-resistant depression

Subcallosal cingulate (SCC) DBS

The SCC (Brodmann area 25/SCC) has been the most investigated target for DBS in TRD.[51] The initial open-label studies by Holtzheimer et al.[25] reported response rates of 58% and remission of 42% at 24 months. However, the multicenter sham-controlled randomized trial BROADEN[26] was terminated prematurely after a futility analysis that showed no significant difference between active and sham stimulation at 6 weeks of the controlled phase.

Nevertheless, a long-term follow-up of the BROADEN study and other cohorts has provided evidence of sustained efficacy. A pooled analysis of 172 patients implanted across 5 studies demonstrated a mean reduction of 43% in MADRS at 12 months and 53% at 24 months, with response rates of 46% and 55%, respectively.[24] Crowell et al.[11] reported, in their long-term follow-up, response rates of 50% maintained beyond 4 years post-implant.

A recent Canadian trial of 35 participants using a Balaam crossover design found no significant between-group differences during the 6-month controlled phase, but during the subsequent open phase, observed response rates of 65.7% at 12 months, 69% at 18 months, and 73.1% at 24 months post-implant were achieved.[18] Alemany et al.[3] reported response rates of 75% and remission of 62.5% with follow-up of up to 11 years, demonstrating the durability of the therapeutic effect.

A significant advance was published by Alagapan et al.[2] in Nature, describing an electrophysiological biomarker based on SCC local field potentials that objectively tracks patient recovery, achieving 90% response and 70% remission at 24 weeks in 10 participants with biomarker-guided programming.

The connectomic approach to surgical planning, developed by Riva-Posse et al.,[47] demonstrated that target selection by individualized tractography (involving the forceps minor, uncinate fasciculus, cingulum, and fronto-striatal fibers) achieved response rates of 81.8% and remission of 54.5% at 1 year in 11 participants.

Ventral capsule/ventral striatum (VC/VS) and vALIC DBS

The sham-controlled trial by Dougherty et al.[14] with VC/VS DBS showed no statistically significant difference between active and sham stimulation during the 16-week controlled phase, although 20% of participants in the active group responded versus 14.3% in the sham group. The overall response rate after the open phase was 23% at 12 months.[14,52]

In contrast, the trial by Bergfeld et al.[7] with vALIC DBS in 25 patients showed significantly superior results. After a 52-week open optimization phase, 40% of patients responded (≥50% HDRS-17 reduction). In the subsequent double-blind crossover phase, scores during active stimulation (HDRS-17 =13.6) were significantly lower than during sham stimulation (HDRS-17 =23.1, P < 0.001), providing the first robust randomized evidence that vALIC DBS produces genuine symptom reduction and not a placebo effect.[7]

The long-term follow-up reported by Van Der Wal et al.[58] confirmed the persistence of the clinical response, with stability of benefits over several years of chronic stimulation.

Ablative procedures for depression

Anterior capsulotomy for TRD has been less studied than for OCD. Subramanian et al.[54] reported significant clinical improvements after bilateral anterior capsulotomy in patients with TRD, with sustained follow-up. Anterior cingulotomy has likewise shown efficacy in refractory depression, with clinically significant responses in approximately 40–50% of patients across the published series. Shields et al.[50] prospectively evaluated stereotactic ablative surgery for intractable major depression, confirming medium-term benefits.[50,53]

Refractory pathological aggression

Compared with the evidence base for refractory OCD and TRD, the literature on psychosurgery for pathological aggression is substantially weaker and must be interpreted with corresponding caution. No randomized or sham-controlled trials exist for this indication; the available evidence derives almost exclusively from uncontrolled, heterogeneous prospective and retrospective series, several of which involve small samples and lack independent, blinded outcome assessment.[10,19] Three features further condition the interpretation of these data. First, a large proportion of the operated patients have severe intellectual disability and, in several series, are children or adolescents, so that both the decision to operate and the appraisal of benefit rest heavily on surrogate (caregiver- or clinician-reported) outcomes rather than on patient self-report. Second, the instruments used (OAS, MOAS, inventory for client and agency planning [ICAP], and Yudofsky scales) capture observable behavior but remain susceptible to observer expectation in unblinded designs. Third, and most importantly, the intervention raises distinctive ethical concerns, regarding the decision-making capacity of the patient to provide valid consent, the reliance on legally authorized representatives, the risk of neurosurgery being used as an instrument of behavioral control, and the historical shadow of coercive “psychosurgery for violence,” that demand especially stringent safeguards, independent ethical oversight, and transparent reporting.[16,19] These considerations, revisited in the neuroethics section below, should be borne in mind when weighing the high response figures reported for the hypothalamic and amygdalar procedures that follow.

Posteromedial hypothalamus DBS

DBS of the PMH constitutes the most studied intervention for refractory pathological aggression. Torres et al.[56] published the first long-term results in 6 patients with severe erethism treated with bilateral PMH DBS, reporting significant improvement in 5 of 6 patients with prolonged follow-up.

The largest series was reported by Benedetti-Isaac et al.,[6] comprising 19 patients with refractory aggression treated with PMH DBS, constituting the largest prospective cohort published for this indication. Vidarte OAE et al.[59] reported results in 11 patients with intellectual disability and severe aggressive behaviors, demonstrating a 63% reduction in the MOAS with a mean follow-up of 4 years. Nine of 11 patients showed a significant decrease in the severity of aggressive behavior.

A tractographic study by Torres et al.[57] identified the anatomical structures involved in the therapeutic effect: the PMH, the ventral tegmental area, the dorsal longitudinal fasciculus, and the medial forebrain bundle appear to be encompassed within the stimulation field. The largest multicenter analysis, published by Gouveia et al.[21] in eLife, with data from multiple international centers, reported that 91% of patients responded positively to treatment, with more pronounced improvements in the pediatric population. Probabilistic mapping revealed an optimized surgical target in the posterior–inferior–lateral region of the posterior hypothalamic area.

A recent systematic review by Cojazzi et al.,[10] which included 10 studies with a total of 60 patients, reported a mean 68% improvement in the OAS and a 38.3–80% improvement in the ICAP, with complications in only 6.7% of patients.

Hypothalamotomy and amygdalotomy

Ablative procedures for aggression include posteromedial hypothalamotomy and stereotactic amygdalotomy. García-Muñoz et al.[17] evaluated, in a clinical trial, the combination of unilateral central amygdalotomy associated with posteromedial hypothalamotomy by radiofrequency thermocoagulation, demonstrating a statistically significant reduction in aggressive behavior over 36 months of follow-up according to the Yudofsky global aggression scale. The most frequent side effects were somnolence and reduced sexual behavior. A relevant finding was that symmetric unilateral lesions produce the same clinical effect as bilateral ones.

Romero et al.[49] described bilateral hypothalamotomy plus dominant amygdalotomy with Gamma Knife radiosurgery as a non-invasive alternative, reporting a marked decrease in the frequency and severity of aggression after 25 months of follow-up. Gouveia et al.[20] published a case series with bilateral radiofrequency amygdala ablation for intractable aggressive behavior, demonstrating decreased aggression and improved quality of life without clinically significant adverse effects.

Mpakopoulou et al.[39] conducted a review of stereotactic amygdalotomy for severe aggressive disorders, documenting that the procedure has been used since its earliest descriptions with variable but generally favorable results in carefully selected patients.

Safety and adverse effects

Ablative procedures

The adverse effects of ablative techniques include, for capsulotomy, transient cerebral edema, transient apathy, weight gain, and, in gamma radiosurgery, a risk of delayed radionecrotic cyst formation (substantially reduced with modern dosing protocols). Cingulotomy presents a more favorable safety profile, with generally transient adverse effects (headache, transient confusion). Detailed neuropsychological evaluations suggest that cognitive function does not deteriorate and may even improve in some domains after treatment.[29,37]

DBS

Adverse events associated with DBS are divided into surgical (intracranial hemorrhage, device infection, electrode migration) and stimulation-related (hypomania/mania, sleep disturbances, paresthesias, weight changes). In DBS for OCD, the most frequent effects are transient hypomania and behavioral changes during parameter optimization.[4,9,27] In DBS for depression, the most concerning events include suicidal ideation and completed suicide, with a suicide incidence of 0.55/100 patient-years in the pooled analysis of SCC DBS.[24] In hypothalamic DBS for aggression, the complication rate has been 6.7% according to the available systematic review.[10]

Long-term follow-up

Evidence on long-term outcomes is growing but still limited. For OCD, Graat et al.[22] reported stability of the response at 5 years with vALIC DBS in 50 patients. For depression, Alemany et al.[3] demonstrated sustained efficacy up to 11 years with SCC DBS, and the pooled analysis by Himes et al.[24] confirmed continued improvements up to 5 years. For aggression, Torres et al.[57] and Vidarte OAE et al.[59] reported maintained benefits with 4-year follow-up, with symptomatic deterioration upon depletion of the neurostimulator batteries and full recovery after their replacement, confirming the dependence of the therapeutic effect on continuous stimulation. DISCUSSION

Comparative efficacy across techniques

The synthesis of the evidence reveals differentiated efficacy profiles according to condition and technique. In refractory OCD, both ablative techniques (gamma capsulotomy, with response rates of 56–62%) and vALIC DBS (52%) achieve clinically relevant outcomes, with capsulotomy showing a slight advantage in long-term remission relative to neuromodulation.[37,43] In TRD, DBS of the SCC and vALIC shows sustained response rates of 40–55%, although sham-controlled trials have produced heterogeneous results that warrant a nuanced interpretation.[7,26] In pathological aggression, DBS of the PMH stands out with response rates of 63–91%, the highest of the three conditions, although supported by evidence of lower methodological quality.[10,21]

Target selection and the connectomic approach

One of the most relevant conceptual advances of the last decade is the transition from target selection based on standardized anatomical coordinates toward an individualized connectomic approach. The work of Riva- Posse et al.[47] demonstrated that tractography-guided surgical planning, directed at the confluence of specific tracts (forceps minor, uncinate fasciculus, cingulum, and frontostriatal fibers), substantially increases response rates in SCC DBS. This logic of “target as network” rather than “target as point” has consolidated as the dominant framework,[19] and its extension through electrophysiological biomarkers, such as the local field potentials described by Alagapan et al.,[2] anticipates an era of adaptive closed-loop neuromodulation.

Ablation versus neuromodulation

The choice between ablative techniques and DBS entails a balance among reversibility, adjustability, cost, and risk. DBS offers the advantage of reversibility and the possibility of post-implant parameter optimization but carries the risks associated with an implanted device (infection, battery replacement, hardware failures) and a considerably higher cost.[41,44] Modern ablative techniques, in particular MRgFUS and gamma radiosurgery, offer minimally invasive or nonincisional approaches with comparable efficacy, at the cost of lesion irreversibility.[15,23] The dependence of the therapeutic effect on continuous stimulation, evidenced by symptomatic deterioration on battery depletion,[59] constitutes an additional argument in favor of ablative techniques in patients with limited access to specialized follow-up.

Quality of evidence and limitations

Despite the advances, the evidence base presents structural limitations. The predominance of studies without random allocation (46 of 56) over randomized trials (10 of 56) reflects the ethical and logistical constraints of psychosurgical research. Sample sizes are characteristically small, the heterogeneity of response criteria hinders direct comparison, and the difficulty of maintaining effective blinding in DBS studies, due to the perception of paresthesias or other stimulation effects, introduces a risk of intervention-deviation bias.[14,26] The early termination of the BROADEN trial for futility[26] exemplifies how designs with brief controlled phases may underestimate the efficacy of interventions whose effect unfolds over months.

Safety and neuroethical considerations

Patient selection and informed consent

The psychosurgical indication requires a rigorous multidisciplinary evaluation that confirms genuine refractoriness, rules out comorbidities that would contraindicate the procedure, and ensures valid informed consent. The decision-making capacity of the patient with severe psychiatric illness must be carefully assessed, and independent ethics committees play a central role in safeguarding patient rights.[19]

Personality and identity changes

A recurrent neuroethical concern is the possibility that neuromodulation of limbic circuits may alter dimensions of personality or the patient’s sense of identity. The prospective evidence on this matter is reassuring but nuanced: in a cohort of 22 patients with TRD undergoing SCC DBS, a significant decrease in neuroticism and an increase in extraversion were documented over 15 months, changes that correlated with the antidepressant response.[45] The authors interpret these changes predominantly as a “state effect,” i.e., the normalization of traits previously distorted by depression itself, rather than as an alteration of the patient’s core identity. Nevertheless, the boundary between restoring an “authentic self ” and inducing modification of personality remains the subject of philosophical and clinical debate and underscores the need to incorporate standardized measures of personality and identity in prospective follow-up.[19]

Historical legacy and stigma

The field carries the historical burden of the abuses of the lobotomy era. Methodological transparency, publication of negative results, and responsible public communication are essential to differentiate contemporary psychosurgery, evidence-based, focused, and regulated, from its discredited antecedents.[19,40]

Clinical implications

For the clinician, the available evidence suggests that referral for psychosurgical evaluation should be considered in patients with genuinely refractory OCD, depression, or aggression after conventional options have been exhausted, ideally within reference centers with experience and prolonged follow-up protocols. The choice of technique and target should be individualized, considering the patient’s profile, the desired reversibility, and the resources available for long-term follow-up.

Strengths and limitations of the review

Among the strengths of this review is its integrative scope: it addresses, within a single comparative framework, three refractory psychiatric conditions – OCD, TRD, and pathological aggression – and the two major technical families – ablative and neuromodulatory – an uncommon combination in a literature usually fragmented by condition or by technique. The broad temporal window (1999–2026) allowed the incorporation of very recent evidence, including trials, biomarkers, and connectomic analyses published in 2025–2026. Although of narrative design, the work incorporates a structured appraisal of methodological quality that matches each tool to the corresponding design (RoB 2 for randomized trials, ROBINS-I for non-randomized studies with a comparator, and the NOS for observational studies), a level of scrutiny uncommon in narrative reviews. The synthesis organized by condition and the comparative tables facilitate clinical reading, and the explicit integration of the neuroethical dimension, personality and identity changes, informed consent, and historical legacyprovides a perspective that transcends mere efficacy. Finally, the conclusions were calibrated prudently, in keeping with the actual strength of the available evidence.

As limitations, the narrative design implies that study selection, although transparent, did not follow a fully registered systematic protocol (no PROSPERO registration and no independent duplicate screening); although a PRISMA-style flow diagram and representative database-specific search strings are now provided to improve transparency, a selection bias cannot be entirely ruled out, and the intermediate counts in the flow diagram are approximate. The search strategy did not include fully exhaustive Boolean strings for every database nor a systematic screening of clinical trial registries, with possible omission of gray literature or non-indexed studies. No meta-analysis or formal grading of the certainty of evidence (GRADE) was performed, so the comparisons between techniques are qualitative. The substantial heterogeneity in designs, response thresholds, measurement instruments, and follow-up duration limits the direct comparability of the reported rates. The quality appraisal was performed by the authors without a formal inter-rater agreement metric. Finally, the available evidence is dominated by uncontrolled studies, particularly in aggression, supported largely by case series and pre/post designs; potential publication bias was not formally assessed, and the language (four languages) and period (from 1999) restrictions may have excluded relevant classic ablative literature.

Future directions

Adaptive closed-loop neuromodulation

The development of closed-loop DBS systems, capable of adjusting stimulation in real time according to physiological biomarkers, constitutes the most promising frontier of the field. The SCC electrophysiological biomarker described by Alagapan et al.[2] and the BNST theta-coherence biomarkers identified in depression[60] open the possibility of personalized and dynamic neuromodulation.

Predictive biomarkers of response

The identification of preoperative biomarkers that predict response would optimize patient selection. The classifier based on SCC glucose metabolism measured by fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography predicted DBS response with 80% accuracy by cross-validation, and the post-treatment reduction in metabolism correlated with symptomatic improvement (r = 0.509, P = 0.031), constituting the first imaging biomarker for patient selection in SCC DBS.[8]

Target refinement through tractography

Surgical planning based on individualized tractography is emerging as a standard, enabling the lesion or stimulation to be directed at the confluence of critical tracts rather than at fixed anatomical coordinates.[20,47]

Non-incisional techniques

The refinement of MRgFUS and gamma radiosurgery offers the prospect of psychosurgery without craniotomy, reducing surgical risks and broadening the accessibility of these interventions.[15,23]

CONCLUSION

Contemporary psychoneurosurgical techniques, both ablative and neuromodulatory, constitute reasonable therapeutic options, supported by the evidence of unequal strength, for patients with refractory OCD, TRD, and refractory pathological aggression who do not respond to conventional interventions. The available prospective evidence documents response rates of 50–62% in OCD (gamma capsulotomy and vALIC DBS), 40–55% in depression, and 63–91% in aggression; however, these ranges derive from heterogeneous studies and the upper values stem largely from uncontrolled open series, so they should be interpreted as indicative rather than comparable. The safety profile is acceptable when procedures are performed in specialized centers with rigorous patient selection.

Nonetheless, the evidence base remains limited by small sample sizes, methodological heterogeneity, and a scarcity of randomized controlled trials, particularly in pathological aggression. Advances in the connectomic approach, predictive biomarkers, and closed-loop neuromodulation anticipate an era of increasingly personalized and precise psychosurgery. Consolidation of the field demands a commitment to rigorous methodological standards, transparency in the communication of results, and ongoing neuroethical vigilance that honors the lessons of the past and protects the rights of a particularly vulnerable population.
