Influence of qNOX-guided remifentanil titration on intraoperative dosing and postoperative pain in total knee arthroplasty: a pilot randomized trial
Authors: Fengrong Liu, E Wang, Bei Huang, Donglin Yang, Xiaowei Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate nociception monitoring may optimize opioid administration during total knee arthroplasty. We aimed to evaluate whether the quantium nociception index (qNOX)-guided remifentanil infusion reduces opioid consumption and improves postoperative analgesia.MethodsIn this single-blind randomized trial, 84 patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty received either qNOX-guided (M group, n = 42) or conventional remifentanil infusion (R group, n = 42). Primary outcome was the incidence of Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) ≥ 5 within 40 min postoperatively. Secondary outcomes included intraoperative remifentanil/propofol consumption, Quality of Recovery (QoR)-15 questionnaire, postoperative nausea and vomiting, hospital stay, and hemodynamic stability.ResultsM group demonstrated the following: Lower resting VAS ≥ 5 incidence (16.7% vs. 55.6%; relative risk [RR] 0.30, 95% CI 0.15 to 0.63; P = 0.023). The qNOX-guided strategy also led to a significant reduction in intraoperative remifentanil consumption (0.071 vs. 0.083 µg·kg⁻1·min⁻1; P = 0.001) and a faster time to extubation (median 16 vs. 18 min; P = 0.009). No differences were observed in propofol consumption, QoR-15 scores, or the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting.ConclusionqNOX-guided analgesia reduces intraoperative remifentanil requirements, accelerates recovery, and improves early postoperative pain control without increasing adverse events.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR2500107430. Registered August 11th, 2025, retrospectively registered.Keywords: Analgesia monitoring, Opioid stewardship, Orthopedic anesthesia, Recovery quality, QNOX

Introduction

Background and rationale

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) can markedly enhance knee joint function and patients’ quality of life. Nevertheless, the substantial trauma incurred during the procedure frequently results in significant postoperative pain. Consequently, postoperative dissatisfaction rates of up to 20% have been linked to perioperative pain in this surgical intervention [1–3]. Currently, the adjustment of remifentanil infusion dosage during the perioperative period depends mainly on objective indicators such as the patient’s hemodynamics. This subjective approach carries two main risks: opioid overdosing, which may predispose patients to opioid-induced hyperalgesia [4, 5], and insufficient dosing, which can result in inadequate analgesia and exaggerated stress responses [6–8].

These challenges highlight the need for objective intraoperative nociception monitoring tools. Noxious stimulation activates the sympathetic nervous system, reduces parasympathetic tone, and induces cortical activity changes detectable by electroencephalogram (EEG) [9, 10]. Autonomic-based indices, including the Nociception Level Index (NOL), Surgical Pleth Index (SPI), Analgesia Nociception Index (ANI), and pupillometry, combine multiparametric autonomic signals to estimate the nociception–antinociception balance [11, 12]. Among them, qNOX-guided remifentanil infusion is proved to improve perioperative outcomes in TKA [13, 14]. A pilot randomized trial in colorectal surgery further showed that combining bispectral index (BIS) and NOL monitoring reduced volatile anesthetic consumption and facilitated faster extubation, although postoperative pain and opioid requirements were not significantly improved [15]. These findings suggest that autonomic-based monitoring strategies, while useful for anesthetic titration, may not fully capture the complexity of nociception. This limitation has driven interest in EEG-derived approaches that directly reflect cortical and subcortical responses to noxious stimuli.

The qNOX, derived from EEG and EMG analysis, provides a complementary approach to predict the likelihood of patient responsiveness to noxious stimuli [16, 17]. Unlike BIS, which reflects the hypnotic state, qNOX directly addresses antinociceptive adequacy. Given the high-pain nature of TKA, combining autonomic and EEG-based indices may offer a more clinically relevant strategy for intraoperative nociception monitoring.

Objectives

We aimed to evaluate whether qNOX–guided remifentanil titration could improve perioperative outcomes in patients undergoing TKA. We specifically hypothesized that this approach would (1) reduce intraoperative opioid consumption, (2) lower early postoperative pain scores, and (3) avoid excessive opioid exposure that might contribute to opioid-induced hyperalgesia, thereby providing a more balanced and patient-centered analgesia strategy compared with conventional hemodynamic guidance.

Methods

Trial design and setting

This study was designed as a prospective, single-center, single-blind, randomized controlled superiority trial with a 1:1 allocation ratio to compare qNOX–guided remifentanil titration with conventional hemodynamic-guided management in patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty. The trial was conducted at the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University, Changsha, China, between September and November 2024.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (initial approval No. LYEC2024-0397). In accordance with institutional requirements, the protocol underwent annual ethics review and was renewed under Approval No. LYEC2025-K0027. Both approval numbers refer to the same protocol. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The trial was retrospectively registered at Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No.: ChiCTR2500107430) after patient enrollment had begun but before any data analysis was undertaken. The study protocol had obtained prior ethics approval, and retrospective registration was completed to ensure compliance with international standards for clinical trial transparency. We acknowledge that the timing of registration is a limitation; however, this approach ensured that the trial protocol was publicly available prior to the analysis phase.

Participants and eligibility criteria

Participants were admitted to the hospital the day before surgery and were educated about the use of patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) and VAS. Participants completed the QoR-15 questionnaire under the direct supervision of the investigator[18].

Inclusion criteria

Patients undergoing total knee replacement surgery were eligible if they met the following conditions: (1) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–II; (2) body mass index (BMI) between 18 and 27.9 kg/m2; and (3) age ≥ 18 years.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if they met any of the following conditions: (1) long-term use of opioid analgesics, beta blockers, anticonvulsants, or antiepileptic drugs; (2) known allergy to any of the study drugs; (3) frontal and facial skin damage or other reasons that prevented electroencephalograph monitoring; (4) emergency surgery; (5) inability to complete VAS scores; (6) admission to intensive care unit (ICU) after surgery postoperatively.

Randomization and blinding

An independent investigator generated the random allocation sequence (1:1 ratio) using R software (version 3.6.1, R Development Core Team, Vienna, Austria) and stored it in a password-protected file. The allocation was revealed to the attending anesthesiologist immediately before induction to ensure concealment. Patients, family members, postoperative assessors, and data analysts were blinded. The anesthesiologist was aware of group assignment to deliver the intervention but did not participate in outcome assessment. In the control group, qNOX values were obscured on the monitor.

Interventions

Five minutes before entering the operating room, all patients received a femoral nerve block guided by ultrasound, operated by the same anesthesiologist. The nerve block regimen consisted of 20 ml of 0.3% ropivacaine. Once the effectiveness and consistency of the nerve block were confirmed for all patients, they proceeded to the operating room. Upon entering the operating room, SpO2, invasive blood pressure monitoring, and electroencephalograph monitoring were sequentially established. Additionally, electrode patches were applied to the patients’ foreheads to monitor the qNOX and quantium consciousness index (qCON) (MaygreenBispro™, Maygreen, Shenzhen, China).

Induction

According to the patient’s weight and basic condition, the anesthesiologist injected midazolam (0.04 mg·kg⁻1), etomidate (0.2 mg·kg⁻1), vecuronium bromide (0.1 mg·kg⁻1), and sufentanil (0.4 μg·kg⁻1) intravenously into the patient for anesthesia induction, and then placed a laryngeal mask to maintain mechanical ventilation. Following induction, all participants received mechanical ventilation with air enriched with 60% oxygen and at a tidal volume of 8 ml per kg of predicted body weight. Respiratory parameters were fine-tuned to maintain end-tidal carbon dioxide levels between 4 and 5.3 kPa and end-expiratory positive pressure at 3 cmH2O. We treated systolic BP ≤ 90 mmHg with metadrenaline.

Maintenance

The anesthesia was maintained using a continuous intravenous micro-pump infusion. The anesthetic agents administered included propofol, mivacurium chloride, and remifentanil. Propofol was continuously infused at an initial rate of 4 mg·kg⁻1·h⁻1, and the infusion rate was adjusted based on qCON to maintain a value between 40 and 60. Mivacurium chloride was continuously infused at a rate of 0.3 mg·kg⁻1·h⁻1.

The dose adjustment mode for maintaining remifentanil infusion varied according to different groups:

Group R (control): qNOX values were partially obscured, preventing the anesthesiologist from directly observing the qNOX values. The remifentanil infusion rate was adjusted based on the average blood pressure, with an initial infusion rate of 4 µg·kg⁻1·h⁻1 and subsequent adjustments to maintain the blood pressure within ± 20% of the average value measured 5 min before anesthesia induction.

Group R (control): qNOX values were partially obscured, preventing the anesthesiologist from directly observing the qNOX values. The remifentanil infusion rate was adjusted based on the average blood pressure, with an initial infusion rate of 4 µg·kg⁻1·h⁻1 and subsequent adjustments to maintain the blood pressure within ± 20% of the average value measured 5 min before anesthesia induction.

Group M (intervention): Remifentanil infusion was titrated according to the average qNOX value, aiming to maintain the qNOX value between 30 and 50 per 5 min. The initial remifentanil infusion rate was 4 µg·kg⁻1·h⁻1, with adjustments of 0.5 µg·kg⁻1·h⁻1 as needed.

Group M (intervention): Remifentanil infusion was titrated according to the average qNOX value, aiming to maintain the qNOX value between 30 and 50 per 5 min. The initial remifentanil infusion rate was 4 µg·kg⁻1·h⁻1, with adjustments of 0.5 µg·kg⁻1·h⁻1 as needed.

Throughout this manuscript, the intervention is referred to as “qNOX-guided analgesia.” It is important to note that the qCON was monitored in all patients in both groups for the purpose of titrating propofol infusion to maintain an adequate depth of hypnosis (qCON target 40–60). Therefore, qCON monitoring constituted a background standard of care for hypnotic management. The experimental intervention was specifically the use of the qNOX index to guide remifentanil titration in the M group, compared to conventional hemodynamic guidance in the R group.

Postoperative management

Drug infusions were discontinued following surgical closure. Subsequently, the patient was transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) where they received intravenous neostigmine (1 mg), atropine (0.5 mg), and flumazenil (5 mg). After the patient was conscious, completely recovered spontaneous breathing, and maintained pulse oxygen saturation greater than 94% under the condition of breathing air, and the airway protective reflex recovered, the laryngeal mask was removed.

In PACU, patients with nausea and vomiting received 16 mg of ondansetron intravenously. PCA was performed for patients with VAS ≥ 5 after surgery (PCA scheme: hydromorphone 8 mg + butorphanol 4 mg + ondansetron 16 mg, volume 100 ml, infusion rate 1 ml/h, PCA 1 ml/time, locking time 15 min). The duration of the PCA is 24 h. Meanwhile, PCA use times and the occurrence of adverse complications such as nausea and vomiting, respiratory depression, delayed extubation, hemodynamic instability, and corresponding symptomatic treatment were recorded. Patients with pain scores ≥ 7 were supplemented with intravenous tramadol 50 mg.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the incidence of resting VAS pain score ≥ 5, assessed verbally by patients and recorded by a blinded investigator at 10-min intervals during the first 40 min in the PACU. A positive outcome was defined as any VAS ≥ 5 within this period.

Secondary outcomes included the following: (1) continuous micropump doses of remifentanil and propofol during surgery; (2) Quality of Recovery-15 scores assessed on the morning of postoperative day 1; (3) resting VAS pain scores at 24 h postoperatively (± 2 h), assessed verbally by patients and recorded by a blinded investigator; (4) total hospital length of stay; (5) and incidence of adverse events (nausea, vomiting, respiratory depression, delayed extubation, hemodynamic instability).

Sample size calculation

The sample size calculation was based on a pre-trial, unpublished pilot study conducted at our institution. This pilot data indicated an incidence of moderate-to-severe pain (VAS ≥ 5) in the post-anesthesia care unit of 47% in patients undergoing TKA with femoral nerve block under conventional hemodynamic-guided analgesia. In a small cohort receiving qNOX-guided analgesia, this incidence was observed to be approximately 15%. We therefore aimed to detect this substantial and clinically meaningful reduction in our definitive trial. Assuming a two-sided α of 0.05 and a power (1-β) of 90%, the calculated sample size was 38 per group. Accounting for a potential 10% dropout rate, we aimed to enroll 42 patients per group, with a total of 84. It is important to note that this pilot study was primarily powered for the primary outcome, and therefore may have been underpowered to detect statistically significant differences in the secondary outcomes, which should be interpreted as exploratory. Assuming the probability of type I error was α, a two-sided test was required, α = 0.05, and the probability of type II error was β, β = 0.10, i.e., the power was 90%. The sample size of a single group was calculated to be 38. Considering the statistical loss to follow-up rate of 10%, 42 samples were finally included in each group. A total of 84.

Statistical analysis

All analyses followed the intention-to-treat principle. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were compared using Student’s t-test, and non-normally distributed data with the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. The primary outcome (incidence of VAS ≥ 5 within 40 min in the PACU) was compared between groups using chi-square testing, and relative risks (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals were estimated. Missing data were handled using complete-case analysis (no imputation). Two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed with R (version 3.6.1) and SPSS (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Result

We enrolled a total of 84 patients meeting the eligibility criteria for TKA under general anesthesia. All patients were randomly assigned to receive remifentanil infusion either determined by qNOX value (group M) or by routine hemodynamic management (group R).

Anesthesiologists administered remifentanil according to the allocated protocol, ensuring full adherence to the study design. Postoperative VAS assessments were performed as planned by trained investigators. All randomized patients completed the study and were included in the final analysis, with no losses and exclusions after randomization (Fig. 1). The trial was completed as planned without early termination. Concomitant perioperative care, including standardized general anesthesia (induction and maintenance drugs), femoral nerve block, intraoperative monitoring, and postoperative patient-controlled analgesia, was comparable between groups. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between groups, with no significant differences (Table 1).

Intraoperative outcomes

Group M exhibited a significantly shorter time to extubation compared with group R (16.00 [13.75–19.00] minutes vs. 18.00 [16.00–21.00] minutes; P = 0.009). Urine output was higher in group M (172.62 (140.00–192.50) ml vs. 144.76 (117.50–170.00) ml; P = 0.003). Remifentanil consumption was lower in group M (0.071 ± 0.017 mg·kg−1·min−1 vs. 0.083 ± 0.014 mg·kg−1·min−1; P = 0.001). The qNOX index was significantly lower in group M (48.30 ± 2.62 vs. 50.05 ± 3.23; P = 0.008). No significant differences were found in other indexes (Table 2).

Postoperative outcomes

Within the first 40 min in the PACU, the cumulative incidence of moderate-to-severe pain (VAS ≥ 5) was significantly lower in group M than in group R (16.7% vs. 55.5%; relative risk [RR] 0.30, 95% CI 0.15 to 0.63; P = 0.023; Table 3). Consistently, boxplot analysis showed that VAS scores at PACU arrival (T1) were significantly lower in group M, whereas no between-group differences were observed at subsequent time points (T2–T6; Fig. 2). No significant differences were found in other postoperative observation indexes, including QoR-15 scores, length of stay, or adverse events such as nausea, respiratory depression, delayed extubation, or hemodynamic instability (Table 3).

Discussion

As a pilot randomized trial, the primary goal of this study was to assess the feasibility and potential efficacy of qNOX-guided analgesia and to generate hypotheses for future larger-scale investigations. Consequently, the findings, particularly regarding secondary outcomes, should be interpreted as exploratory.

Our findings show that using qNOX guidance led to better control of intraoperative nociception, as indicated by a lower qNOX index, decreased remifentanil needs, and quicker extubation times and better management in hemodynamic changes. These intraoperative benefits had significant clinical implications in the immediate postoperative phase, with patients guided by qNOX experiencing significantly lower levels of moderate-to-severe pain in the PACU. The observed reduction in postoperative pain incidence and opioid consumption aligns with the growing body of literature supporting nociception monitoring technologies [15, 19–24]. However, the benefits found in other similar studies, including a reduced incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) [21], were not presented in our study. This might be due to differences in monitoring protocols or surgical populations.

An intriguing secondary finding was the increased urine output observed in the qNOX-guided group. Although this study was not designed to investigate renal outcomes, this finding should be interpreted as exploratory and generates a hypothesis for future research. We propose that more precise control of nociception may have led to an attenuated surgical stress response and improved hemodynamic stability, potentially enhancing renal perfusion. This hypothesis is tentatively supported by our observation of more stable diastolic blood pressure in the qNOX group at T4. However, this remains speculative, as we did not measure stress hormones (e.g., cortisol, catecholamines) or utilize advanced hemodynamic monitoring to directly assess renal blood flow. Consequently, this observation necessitates validation in future studies specifically designed to investigate the effects of qNOX-guided anesthesia on perioperative renal function and fluid balance [25–27].

It is noteworthy that high doses of remifentanil can induce opioid-induced hyperalgesia (OIH), a factor considered in the study’s design. Despite the use of remifentanil in both groups, nerve block anesthesia was employed to minimize opioid consumption. The group guided by qNOX avoided OIH risks associated with overdose by ensuring precise dose regulation for adequate analgesia. Previous research indicates that remifentanil can lead to OIH and acute opioid tolerance at infusion rates exceeding 0.1 µg kg−1 min−1 [28], with increased postoperative opioid consumption observed when infusion rates surpass 0.25 µg kg−1 min−1 [29, 30]. In this study, the remifentanil infusion rates in all groups remained below the threshold for OIH, suggesting that OIH may not be the primary influencing factor within the routine clinical dose range.

These findings have important implications for clinical practice, suggesting that qNOX-based infusion regimen for regulated remifentanil can enhance the precision of intraoperative analgesia while reducing overall anesthetic requirements. Future research should prioritize three main areas. Firstly, conducting larger multicenter trials to confirm the validity of these findings across various surgical populations. Secondly, exploring the potential economic advantages of qNOX-guided anesthesia, including reduced drug expenses and quicker recovery times. Lastly, investigating whether the enhancements in early recovery parameters lead to significant differences in long-term outcomes like persistent postoperative pain or functional recovery. The advancement of adaptive, closed-loop systems that combine qNOX monitoring with other depth-of-anesthesia indicators could signify the next breakthrough in tailored perioperative management.

We used an ultrasound-guided femoral nerve block as part of our perioperative analgesic regimen. This practice is consistent with the recent SIAARTI guidelines [31], which emphasize that ultrasound guidance improves needle visualization, increases procedural safety, and reduces avoidable neurovascular injury during regional anesthesia. It also supports the guideline’s recommendation to incorporate multimodal, opioid-sparing analgesia in lower-limb orthopedic surgery.

In addition, remifentanil titration based on qNOX provided an objective indication of the patient’s nociceptive response rather than relying solely on blood pressure or heart rate changes. This approach may help avoid both inadequate and excessive opioid administration during surgery.

Taken together, our protocol combined a guideline-recommended nerve block technique with objective nociception monitoring. This is broadly in line with the safety-focused perioperative strategy highlighted in the SIAARTI document and may contribute to a smoother early recovery. We also acknowledge that some of the observed benefits—such as reduced opioid use and better early pain control—may partly be attributable to the standardized nerve block itself. Future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to determine how nociception monitoring and regional anesthesia can best be integrated to improve patient care [31].

In conclusion, our findings strongly support the superiority of qNOX-guided remifentanil administration compared to conventional methods, enhancing intraoperative anesthetic control and early postoperative recuperation. Although additional studies are warranted to elucidate underlying mechanisms and wider clinical implications, these results underscore the increasing acknowledgment of nociception monitoring as a beneficial asset in contemporary anesthesia. The incorporation of such modalities into enhanced recovery pathways could signify a pivotal advancement in refining perioperative results.

Limitation

Several limitations should be taken into account when interpreting these findings. The study’s single-center design and moderate sample size could restrict the generalizability of the results. Moreover, concentrating solely on immediate postoperative outcomes raises uncertainties regarding the potential longer-term recovery advantages. Furthermore, the standardized anesthetic approach outlined in the study protocol, although enhancing internal validity, may not encompass the adaptability necessary for various clinical situations.

Furthermore, this trial was registered retrospectively in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry after patient enrollment began. This delay resulted from administrative coordination issues during the start-up stage. We acknowledge that this does not fully meet international standards for prospective registration and may affect the transparency and credibility of the results. Nonetheless, the study protocol received full approval from the ethics committee before any patient was enrolled (Approval No. LYEC2024-0397), and the trial was conducted strictly according to the pre-established protocol. All pre-defined primary and secondary outcome measures are reported in this article. We recognize this as a methodological limitation and will ensure prospective registration of trials in future research.

Moreover, while the sample size was determined using pre-trial pilot data for the primary outcome, it is probable that the study lacked sufficient power for the secondary outcomes. Consequently, the results pertaining to secondary outcomes should be viewed as preliminary and approached with care, aiming to propose hypotheses for forthcoming extensive studies.

Clinical relevance of the findings

While the absolute reduction in mean remifentanil consumption (0.012 µg·kg⁻1·min⁻1) may appear modest at first glance, its clinical importance becomes apparent when considered in a broader context. First, when extrapolated over a typical procedure duration (e.g., 150 min), this translates to a total dose reduction of approximately 1.8 µg/kg in the qNOX-guided group. More importantly, this optimized dosing was associated with several tangible clinical benefits: a statistically significant and patient-centered reduction in the risk of early moderate-to-severe pain (RR 0.30), and a faster time to extubation by a median of 2 min. We posit that it is the synergistic effect of these improvements—more precise analgesia, reduced opioid exposure, accelerated recovery of consciousness, and better early pain control—that constitutes the true clinical value of qNOX guidance. This pattern suggests a shift towards a more efficient and balanced anesthetic state, which is a key goal in enhanced recovery protocols.
