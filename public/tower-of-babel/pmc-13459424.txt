Scimitar syndrome and horseshoe lung presenting with syncope and hemoptysis in late childhood, management challenges, and outcomes: a case report
Authors: Mahdieh Khorashadizadeh, Mahdieh Vahedi, Armin Doostparast, Maryam Ghandhari, Amir Behforouz, Sara Ghahramani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHorseshoe lung (HL) and scimitar syndrome are rare congenital cardiopulmonary anomalies that may coexist, posing complex diagnostic and management challenges. The following case presents the clinical manifestation of repeated hemoptysis episodes and syncope, a combination that has rarely been reported in this context. Moreover, there were treatment challenges regarding the utility of minimally invasive techniques in the iatrogenic occlusion of aberrant vessels, which has more recently been suggested in the literature to yield comparable outcomes compared with open surgeries.Case presentationWe described the case of a 15-year-old Iranian boy who presented with syncope and hemoptysis following physical activity. Imaging findings were consistent with concomitant HL, partial anomalous pulmonary venous return (PAPVR), right lung hypoplasia, and scimitar syndrome. The patient successfully underwent transcatheter arterial embolization (TAE); however, another episode of hemoptysis occurred after three weeks. This recurrence indicated the failure of TAE and necessitated a right lower lobe segmentectomy. After the surgical excision, the patient showed improvement in respiratory status, with no further episodes of hemoptysis. He was discharged on postoperative day seven. At the 2-month follow-up, there were no signs of recurrence or complications. Nonetheless, the patient was scheduled for subsequent open-heart repair of the PAPVR defect.ConclusionsThis case highlights that when HL or scimitar syndrome is identified, clinicians should be aware of associated congenital cardiopulmonary and vascular anomalies and perform a comprehensive clinical exam and multimodality imaging. Moreover, although TAE may yield outcomes comparable to open surgery with faster recovery and fewer postoperative complications, its effectiveness is questionable in patients with complex underlying conditions. Therefore, although the choice between the minimally invasive and invasive managements requires a multidisciplinary approach, it is recommended that patients with complications such as infections, bronchiectasis, large lumen aberrant vessels, multiple aberrant vessels, or unusual anatomies be planned for surgical resection of sequestrated lung tissue to avoid potentially less effective interventions.Keywords: Horseshoe lung, Scimitar syndrome, Hemoptysis, Transcatheter arterial embolization, Case report

Background

Horseshoe lung (HL) is a rare congenital pulmonary malformation with a midline parenchymal isthmus connecting the basal segments of the right and left lungs [1]. This isthmus lies posterior to the heart and maintains an anterior position relative to the esophagus, thoracic aorta, and spinal cord [2]. HL is often associated with unilateral lung hypoplasia, which has mostly (approximately 80% of cases) been documented on the right side [3, 4].

Moreover, HL with right lung hypoplasia has been reported to be mostly (80–85%) combined with cardiopulmonary anomalies, including scimitar syndrome, which is a variant of partial anomalous pulmonary venous return (PAPVR) [3, 5]. Scimitar syndrome is the abnormal drainage of one or more pulmonary veins into the hepatic portion of the inferior vena cava (IVC) [6]. Pulmonary arteries’ hypoplasia, cardiac dextroposition, HL, pulmonary sequestration, and other cardiac anomalies such as atrial or ventricular septal defects, aortic coarctation, or tetralogy of Fallot have also been reported in combination with scimitar syndrome [7, 8]. The exact incidence of scimitar syndrome is unknown due to its asymptomatic presentation in some patients; however, estimations range from 1 to 3 per 100,000 live births [7]. The clinical presentation of patients with HL and scimitar syndrome can vary from asymptomatic incidental findings to infantile onset of severe respiratory distress and heart failure [5, 8]. Imaging examinations, particularly chest X-ray radiographs and computed tomography (CT), play a significant role in the initial evaluation of suspected patients [9].

Here, we describe a 15-year-old patient with a late and uncommon presentation of repeated hemoptysis and syncope who was ultimately diagnosed with concomitant HL and scimitar syndrome. This case highlights that if HL or scimitar syndrome is detected, it is necessary that clinicians be aware of associated congenital cardiopulmonary and vascular anomalies and undertake a comprehensive clinical examination and multimodality imaging, as these defects are often associated with a wide range of congenital anomalies.

Case presentation

A 15-year-old Iranian boy was admitted to the emergency department following a sudden, transient episode of decreased level of consciousness during school sports activities, consistent with exercise-induced syncope. He had no relevant past medical, surgical, or family history of cardiovascular diseases. Before the event, he reported generalized weakness and palpitations. The vital signs upon physical examination were as follows: blood pressure = 110/70 mmHg, heart rate = 96 bpm, respiratory rate = 18 breaths/min, temperature = 36.8 °C, and oxygen saturation = 98% on room air. The other findings were also unremarkable except for the detection of a cardiac murmur.

Accordingly, the patient underwent a comprehensive evaluation to elucidate the reason behind syncope. The electrocardiogram (ECG) findings were all unremarkable, with no visible abnormality. The tilt test results were normal, not supporting the diagnosis of vasovagal syncope. An exercise test was also performed, which revealed no significant abnormalities. The transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) findings revealed right ventricular enlargement with an ejection fraction of 70% and mild tricuspid regurgitation. Therefore, the physician suspected cardiac causes for syncope, potentially due to cardiac failure to provide sufficient output flow during the physical activity. Therefore, the patient was referred to a tertiary referral center for further evaluation and management. Meanwhile, the patient experienced repeat episodes of hemoptysis, approximately one-fourth of a cup each time, occurring on three separate occasions, and was subsequently admitted to the pediatric pulmonology department ward. Episodes were not associated with respiratory symptoms such as cough, dyspnea, or fever, and no identifiable triggers were noted. He had no prior history of hemoptysis or other bleeding disorders, and there was no family history of lung disease or coagulopathy. In physical examination, he was stable with vital signs in normal ranges. The general examination also did not reveal bleeding from other sites. Laboratory investigations, including CBC, creatinine, coagulation profile, and urine analysis, were all within normal ranges.

A chest X-ray radiograph revealed a curvilinear vascular shadow in the right lower lung zone, a mild volume loss in the right lung, and mild dextrocardia (Fig. 1).

Posteroanterior chest radiograph shows a curvilinear vascular structure in the right lower lung zone, associated with mild volume loss in the right lung. The cardiac silhouette is mildly displaced toward the right

A transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) showed right atrial and ventricular enlargement with preserved function, two right pulmonary veins draining anomalously into the IVC and subsequently into the right atrium (RA), a small patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), mild pulmonary and tricuspid regurgitation (PR and TR, respectively), and mild pulmonary hypertension (PH) (TR pressure gradient (PG): 52 mmHg, PR PG: 22 mmHg, RA volume index: 42 cc/m2, mid right ventricle (RV) diameter: 49 mm).

Given the presence of both bronchopulmonary and cardiovascular abnormalities, a CT angiography (CTA) was performed as the next step. CTA findings at pulmonary window setting revealed a PAPVR and right heart enlargement. Additionally, there was a coil-shaped density within a branch from the celiac trunk that extended toward the right hemithorax, suggesting pulmonary sequestration and HL. An ectatic bronchiolar structure associated with the sequestration was also noted, which may be responsible for the repeated hemoptysis episodes (Fig. 2).

These findings were confirmed by a perfusion/ventilation nuclear scan, which identified a perfusion defect within the abnormal pulmonary segment that confirmed the diagnosis of sequestration (Fig. 3).

Ventilation/perfusion scintigraphy confirming pulmonary sequestration with ventilation/perfusion mismatch and horseshoe lung in scimitar syndrome. a Planar projection images (anterior, posterior, right lateral, left lateral, right posterior oblique, right anterior oblique, left posterior oblique, left anterior oblique) demonstrating reduced perfusion in the right lower lung zone/basal segments, with relatively preserved ventilation. b Axial single-photon emission computed tomography/computed tomography images (left: perfusion, right: ventilation) showing absent perfusion uptake in the affected right basal parenchyma (green arrow, left) and preserved ventilation in the same region (green arrow, right), indicating ventilation/perfusion mismatch. c Coronal single-photon emission computed tomography/computed tomography images (left: perfusion, right: ventilation) confirming the perfusion defect (green arrows, left) and preserved ventilation (green arrows, right). d Axial single-photon emission computed tomography/computed tomography images (left: perfusion, right: ventilation) illustrating a band of pulmonary parenchyma bridging the right and left lungs (green arrows), located posterior to the pericardium and anterior to the descending aorta and esophagus, diagnostic of horseshoe lung

The combination of intralobar pulmonary sequestration and PAPVR was suggestive of scimitar syndrome. The patient initially underwent transcatheter arterial embolization (TAE) to treat the sequestration. The procedure entailed selective catheterization of the aberrant arterial supply to the sequestrated lung segment, followed by deployment of embolization coils to achieve complete occlusion of blood flow as the procedural endpoint. This procedure aimed to stop the blood supply to the sequestrated lung tissue and prevent further hemoptysis. There was a main aberrant vessel that was inspected during the procedure, which was aimed to be occluded. The hemodynamic parameters were measured as follows: aorta pressure: 100/65 mmHg, pulmonary artery (PA) pressure: 220/100 mmHg, left ventricle (LV) pressure: 100/0–10 mmHg, left atrium (LA) pressure: 270/30–140 mmHg, RV pressure: 40/0–10 mmHg, RA pressure: 130/30–80, RA oxygen saturation (SpO2): 93%, RV SpO2: 87%, PA SpO2: 87%, LA SpO2: 100%).

The TAE operation was finished successfully, and post-embolization angiography confirmed complete occlusion of this aberrant vessel (Fig. 4). The patient's postoperative course was uneventful. However, the patient had repeated hemoptysis after three weeks, which necessitated a surgical segmentectomy. Accordingly, a right-sided thoracotomy was performed, the abnormal pulmonary artery branches were ligated, and the sequestered lung segment was carefully removed (right lower lobe segmentectomy). This procedure was performed to eliminate the source of hemoptysis and correct the anatomical abnormalities. The surgically resected lung was also carefully assessed post-resection to ensure adequate expansion and function. Finally, the removed lung tissue underwent histopathological examination, which revealed non-functioning lung tissue with abnormal systemic arterial blood supply. Furthermore, there was evidence of dilated airways, thickened bronchial walls, alveolar hemorrhage, chronic inflammatory infiltrates, fibrosis and scarring in the affected areas, collectively consistent with lung sequestration and diffuse bronchiectasis.

Postoperative course

Following the right lower lobe segmentectomy, the patient was transferred to the pediatric ICU in stable condition. He remained hemodynamically stable, with no need for pressor support. Chest tube output was minimal, and the patient was extubated on postoperative day 2. Postoperative imaging confirmed proper lung expansion and resolution of the sequestration. The patient showed gradual improvement in respiratory status, with no further episodes of hemoptysis.

The patient was discharged on postoperative day 7 in good condition, with follow-up appointments scheduled to monitor recovery. At the 2-month follow-up, the patient was doing well with no signs of recurrence or complications. Given the ongoing cardiac abnormalities, he was planned for subsequent open-heart repair of the PAPVR defect to fully resolve the underlying cause and prevent recurrence of symptoms.

Discussion

We described a rare presentation of a 15-year-old boy diagnosed with HL combined with scimitar syndrome. In contrast to the infantile cases, where symptoms begin in the early years after birth, the patient was asymptomatic until 15, and the presentation of syncope and recurrent hemoptysis was extremely uncommon. Therefore, this patient was likely a case of the childhood/adult form of scimitar syndrome, complicated by associated congenital cardiopulmonary and vascular anomalies, collectively known as congenital pulmonary venolobar syndrome (CPVS). CPVS is a rare condition that encompasses a group of cardiac and pulmonary congenital abnormalities, which may occur in various combinations. These abnormalities include anomalous pulmonary venous drainage (particularly scimitar syndrome with hypogenetic right lung), pulmonary sequestration with systemic pulmonary vascular supply, and HL [10, 11].

HL, described in 1962 by Spencer, is a congenital malformation characterized by the fusion of the basal segments of both lungs through a parenchymal isthmus [12]. The diagnosis of HL is also based on the radiologic visualization of this connection between the lungs [4]. The exact cause of this condition is not fully understood, but there are two main theories. One suggests that it results from the fusion of lung tissue between the right and left lungs. The other theory proposes that it occurs due to a failure of the splanchnic mesoderm to separate properly, which leads to an abnormal attachment between the lungs and the pleural cavities [2, 13].

HL's clinical presentations vastly differ, from no obvious complaint to respiratory symptoms such as wheezing, unexplained dyspnea, recurrent lower respiratory infections, or signs of pulmonary hypertension and heart failure. The clinical severity usually depends on the degree of pulmonary hypoplasia and the complexity of the associated vascular anomalies [3, 14, 15].

HL frequently coexists with right lung hypoplasia and scimitar syndrome [16]. Scimitar syndrome, also called hypogenetic lung, was first described in 1836 during the autopsy of an infant with dextrocardia and right lung hypoplasia [8], and was called scimitar by Halasz et al. in 1956, who likened the distinctive curvilinear shape of the anomalous vein at the base of the right lung during radiologic examination to a scimitar sword [17]. Scimitar syndrome has also been reported to have a higher incidence among males than in females, in accordance with the current case description [18].

Based on the onset of symptoms, scimitar syndrome could be classified as infantile and childhood/adult [14]. The infantile form is more often associated with thoracic anomalies and pulmonary hypertension and presents with more severe manifestations, including respiratory distress and cyanosis; however, the adult form is mostly asymptomatic, and patients might be asymptomatic for years or merely experience mild exertional dyspnea [8, 19, 20].

There is no universally accepted diagnostic criterion or an imaging modality of choice to detect scimitar syndrome, and the diagnosis is mostly made based on the combination of clinical evaluations and radiologic examinations. The current literature supports the utility of plain chest X-ray, multi-detector CT (MDCT), echocardiography, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), CT angiography, and even pulmonary catheterization in the diagnosis of scimitar syndrome [8, 21]. The initial suspicion of scimitar syndrome in plain chest X-rays often takes place due to the scimitar sign, which has been reported in 70–82% of cases. Nonetheless, some other studies suggest RV enlargement, variations in the right lung’s lobar pattern, dilated pulmonary arteries, and dextrocardia as the most common co-findings [8, 22]. Herein, the evaluation is recommended to be complemented through CT, and more specifically, contrast-enhanced MDCT [9].

Although rare, there is some evidence regarding the occurrence of hemoptysis in patients with scimitar syndrome [23–28]. A review of the series estimated the incidence of hemoptysis to be approximately 6% among these patients [20]. Vascular and pulmonary abnormalities in scimitar syndrome are often complex [23]. Herein, it should be noted that pulmonary sequestration is an abnormal development of lung tissue that has no connection to the bronchial tree or the pulmonary arteries, which potentially results in hemoptysis or infections and necessitates surgical resection of sequestrated lung parenchyma [29, 30]. Hemoptysis is also believed to result primarily from systemic blood flow to the right lung, which is under higher pressure than the pulmonary circulation [23]. Additionally, Huang et al. reported that anomalous arteries often lack a muscular layer, which may account for sudden fatal hemoptysis. They suggested that even asymptomatic patients should be considered for treatment, given that they are at higher risk of hemoptysis secondary to pulmonary hypertension and heart failure resulting from a systemic artery-to-pulmonary vein shunt [25]. Treatment options for scimitar syndrome need a multidisciplinary approach, vary from case to case, and depend on the onset and severity of symptoms, associated co-morbidities, anatomical positions, and the preference of the surgeon [14]. Conservative management with close observation and follow-up could be considered for patients who are asymptomatic or have mild symptoms, minor left-to-right shunting, and whose PA pressures are within normal ranges [14, 31]. Otherwise, in cases with severe respiratory symptoms, pulmonary hypertension, heart failure, pulmonary sequestration, recurrent infection, and hemoptysis, among others, resectional or corrective surgical approaches, including redirecting the abnormal pulmonary veins, removing sequestered lung segments, or repairing associated cardiac defects, are indicated. Lobectomy or pneumonectomy is only reserved for patients presenting with recurrent infections, extensive bronchiectasis, thrombosed intra-atrial baffles, persistent hemoptysis, or severe hypoplasia of the right lung. [14, 32, 33]. Beyond surgical management, TAE is a relatively newer and minimally invasive technique that is safe and effective in treating scimitar syndrome, especially in patients experiencing massive or recurrent hemoptysis [23, 25].

Although TAE may provide temporary control with a better and faster recovery and fewer complications owing to its minimally invasive nature, recurrence can occur, especially when pulmonary sequestration is complicated by infections, bronchiectasis, bleeding disorders, large lumen aberrant vessels, multiple aberrant vessels, or unusual anatomies [34, 35]. Therefore, the medical team should take the patient-specific characteristics into account for proper treatment planning. As was shown in this case, TAE failed to provide a permanent solution, likely due to the combination of pulmonary sequestration, extensive bronchiectasis, and PAPVR. Therefore, from this case, we can learn that surgical excision in complex cases is a better choice from the beginning in order not to delay treatment and to avoid unnecessary interventions. Yet, future studies are still needed to validate these findings, and they should more comprehensively investigate the efficacy of TAE against surgical resections in a large population and take into account that surgeons should be aware of the limited efficacy of TAE in such patients.

Furthermore, it should be noted that although the patient showed good progress with no recurrence of symptoms at the 2-month follow-up, the follow-up period is relatively short. Recurrence of symptoms can occur later, and longer follow-up, ideally at least 6–12 months, is needed to more comprehensively assess the long-term effectiveness of the interventions. However, the patient or his family did not revisit the physician team for repeat evaluations, likely due to the resolution of symptoms. Therefore, further studies with extended follow-up are essential to better understand the durability of the treatment outcomes and to ensure that recurrence is promptly identified and managed.

Conclusion

This case highlights a late-childhood presentation of HL with scimitar syndrome, a combination that is well documented but with rare clinical manifestations of hemoptysis and syncope. While imaging findings were consistent with the expected anatomical patterns, the limited efficacy of TAE in preventing repeated hemoptysis underscores an important clinical insight: although TAE may provide temporary stabilization, definitive surgical intervention might be deemed necessary. Therefore, a careful preoperative evaluation, multidisciplinary approach, and coordinated planning among pulmonologists, cardiologists, interventional radiologists, and thoracic surgeons are essential to choose the best management approach, optimize outcomes, and guide decision-making in such complex congenital cardiopulmonary disorders.
