Maternal–neonatal vitamin D gradient at birth in Ilorin, Nigeria: a comparative cross-sectional study
Authors: Ojuawo Ayodeji, Ojuawo Ayodele, Adesiyun Omotayo, Solomon Ariyibi, Biliaminu Sikiru, Gbenga Popoola, Adebowale Ayobola, Ayokunle Osonuga, Uthman Okikiola Adebayo, Tolutope Adebimpe Oso, Oluwatobi Babajide Ayelaagbe, Afis Akanni Agboola, Don Eliseo Lucero-Prisno III
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCord-blood vitamin D concentrations at birth typically trail maternal levels, but the magnitude of this maternal-neonatal gradient and its implications for supplementation policy remains poorly characterised in sub-Saharan Africa.ObjectiveTo measure the absolute and relative maternal– neonatal vitamin-D gradient at birth among maternal–neonate pairs in Ilorin, North-Central Nigeria.MethodsIn a comparative cross-sectional study, we enrolled 92 healthy parturients and their singleton neonates at University of Ilorin Teaching Hospital (8.3 °N). Paired maternal venous and umbilical-cord sera were analysed for total 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D) using Calbiotech® ELISA. Vitamin-D status was classified using exact measured serum 25(OH)D values as deficient (< 20 ng mL⁻¹), insufficient (20 to 29 ng mL⁻¹), or sufficient (≥ 30 ng mL⁻¹). Paired t-tests, Pearson correlation, McNemar χ², and one-way ANOVA were used to assess maternal–cord differences, associations, and gestational-age effects.ResultsMean maternal 25(OH)D was 28.54 ± 11.01 ng mL⁻¹; while mean cord level was 24.85 ± 9.28 ng mL⁻¹, yielding an absolute gradient of 3.69 ng mL⁻¹ (95% CI 1.0–6.4; t = 2.46, p = 0.015) and a maternal: cord ratio of 1.20 ± 0.29. Concentrations were positively correlated (r = 0.498, p < 0.001). Deficiency was uncommon (2.2% in both groups), yet insufficiency affected 52.2% of mothers and 65.2% of neonates; status discordance was significant (McNemar χ² = 8.97, p = 0.003). Early pre-term neonates (28–< 32 wk) had the highest mean cord 25(OH)D concentration (29.33 ± 8.98 ng mL⁻¹), which remained below the sufficiency threshold of 30.0 ng mL⁻¹, although gestational-age differences were not statistically significant (ANOVA p = 0.19).ConclusionsA modest but clinically relevant maternal–neonatal vitamin-D shortfall persists at birth in semi-equatorial Ilorin, leaving two-thirds of neonates merely insufficient. Maternal sufficiency alone is an unreliable proxy for neonatal adequacy.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Vitamin D is increasingly recognised as a pleiotropic hormone that supports placental calcium transport, modulates maternal immunity and influences fetal skeletal development [1]. Recent reviews concluded that low antenatal 25-hydroxy-vitamin D (25(OH)D) (< 50 nmol L⁻¹) doubles the odds of pre-eclampsia and gestational diabetes and is linked to pre-term birth and reduced birth-weight, whereas supplementation lowers many of these risks [1, 2].

During pregnancy, the body undergoes significant physiological change, including requirements and metabolism of vitamin D, creating a risk for its insufficiency and deficiency. Vitamin D deficiency in pregnancy is a common problem worldwide with rates ranging between 5 and 90% depending on the country [2]. This has prompted calls for routine vitamin-D assessment and supplementation during pregnancy, yet consensus on the serum threshold that guarantees neonatal sufficiency remains elusive [3, 4].

Maternal vitamin D circulates mainly as 25(OH)D bound to vitamin-D-binding protein [5]. At the placenta, 25(OH)D is taken up by trophoblastic receptors, where it may undergo local activation by CYP27B1 to 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D or catabolism by CYP24A1 [6]. A proportion of maternal 25(OH)D crosses into the fetal circulation, supporting skeletal mineralisation, immune development, and calcium homeostasis [7].

Because neonatal vitamin D status at birth depends on placental transfer during pregnancy, cord-blood concentrations at delivery mirror—but rarely equal—maternal levels. A 2022 systematic review pooling more than 30 cohorts estimated a pooled maternal-neonatal correlation of r ≈ 0.70 and showed that cord 25(OH)D values are typically 10–30% lower than maternal concentrations, a consistent shortfall now referred to as the maternal–neonatal vitamin D gradient [8].

Defining the magnitude of this gradient in different populations is clinically important: if neonatal levels systematically trail those of the mother, supplementation targets for pregnant women must be set high enough to safeguard the neonate. Unfortunately, data from sub-Saharan Africa are scarce but suggest that sizable gradients can exist despite year-round ultraviolet exposure.

In Lagos, southern Nigeria, only 4.8% of mothers were vitamin-D-deficient (< 50 nmol L⁻¹), yet 29.5% of their neonates met the deficiency threshold, implying substantial placental attrition of 25(OH)D [9]. Among Black South-African women delivering in Johannesburg, mean cord-blood concentrations were roughly 80% of maternal values (41.9 ± 21.0 vs. 57.0 ± 29.7 nmol L⁻¹) [10]. These observations challenge the assumption that abundant sunlight alone guarantees neonatal sufficiency and raise questions about the transfer efficiency of vitamin D at the maternal–placental interface.

North-Central Nigeria, with its unique blend of cultural practices, dietary patterns and semi-equatorial latitude, has not yet been investigated. Understanding the maternal–neonatal gradient here could inform context-specific supplementation policies, particularly for high-melanin populations in sunlight-rich regions where universal antenatal supplementation is often considered unnecessary. This study therefore aims to quantify the absolute (ng mL⁻¹) and relative maternal–neonatal vitamin D (maternal-to-cord ratio) gradient at birth among maternal–neonatal pairs in Ilorin, North-Central Nigeria. The findings of this study can guide evidence-based antenatal vitamin D supplementation strategies.

Methods

Study design and setting

This comparative cross-sectional study enrolled parturient and their neonates. Stratified sampling with optimum allocation based on monthly delivery figures for the labour suite was used to recruit maternal–neonatal pairs. The final sample comprised 84 mothers and 84 neonates, matching the calculated minimum plus a 10% attrition allowance.

Participants

Inclusion criteria were (i) singleton (uncomplicated) pregnancy, (ii) gestational age ≥ 28 completed weeks (confirmed by early-pregnancy ultrasound or reliable last-menstrual-period dating), (iii) intention to deliver at the study hospital and (iv) written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were multiple gestation, known maternal chronic kidney or liver disease, endocrinopathies affecting calcium–vitamin-D metabolism, current high-dose vitamin-D supplementation (> 1000 IU Day⁻¹) or refusal of venepuncture. Other consideration include: neonate with birth complications, Maternal use of drugs influencing vitamin-D metabolism (anti-TB agents, chemotherapy, anticonvulsants), calcium or vitamin-D supplements, Maternal comorbidities affecting vitamin D (hypertension, diabetes, renal/liver failure, malabsorption, parathyroid disease) and neonatal asphyxia or major congenital malformations.

Ethical approval

The study protocol (reference UITH/EC/204/19) was approved by the University of Ilorin Teaching Hospital Ethics Review Committee, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision).

Data collection

Maternal variables—age, tribe, education, occupation, socio-economic class (Ibadin revised score), weight and height—were collected at the last antenatal visit. Body Mass index was computed as kg m⁻². Neonatal variables—birth-weight (Way Master bassinet scale), crown–heel length (Mediguard infantometer) and head-circumference (inelastic tape)—were measured within two hours of birth, each to the nearest 0.1 cm or gram. Gestational age was determined from the last menstrual period corroborated by the Ballard score and categorised as early pre-term (28–<32 wk), late pre-term (32–<37 wk) or term (37–42 wk).

Blood sampling and handling

At delivery, 2 mL maternal venous blood (antecubital) and 2 mL cord venous blood (umbilical stump) were drawn into ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid tubes under aseptic conditions. Maternal samples were collected immediately after admission for delivery, while cord samples were obtained immediately after birth from the clamped umbilical stump. Samples were centrifuged at 2,000 rpm for 5 min for separation of plasma, and aliquots were stored at − 20 °C until analysis. All samples were labelled with unique study identifiers and processed on the day of collection.

Laboratory measurement of 25(OH)D

Serum 25(OH)D concentrations were quantified using sandwich enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (Calbiotech®, CA, USA; catalogue VD220B), which detects both 25(OH)D₂ and 25(OH)D₃. Optical density was read using a calibrated microplate reader, and concentrations were calculated from the assay standard curve. The assay’s analytical sensitivity was 2.0 ng mL⁻¹; intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were 6.4% and 8.1%, respectively, as verified with Bio-Rad trilevel controls run on every plate. The laboratory participates bi-annually in the Vitamin D External Quality Assessment Scheme.

Covariates

Maternal demographic and obstetric data (age, parity, pre-pregnancy body mass index, booking status, socio-economic class) were abstracted from antenatal records. A structured interviewer-administered questionnaire captured dietary vitamin-D sources and sunlight-exposure practices (duration of direct sun per day, clothing coverage), although these exposure data were not analysed for the present report. Gestational age was determined by obstetric ultrasound performed before 20 weeks or, when unavailable, by last-menstrual-period corroborated with Ballard scores at birth. Infant anthropometry (weight, crown–heel length, head circumference) was measured within 30 min of delivery using calibrated SECA® scales and ShorrBoard stadiometers.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted in SPSS version 20. Normality of continuous variables was checked with Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests and Descriptives as means ± SD (or medians ± Interquartile Range (IQR) and proportions summarised the data. To get the maternal–neonatal gradient, a paired-samples t-test compared maternal and cord means; the absolute difference and maternal-cord ratio were computed for every pair. Pearson correlation assessed the maternal–cord 25(OH)D relationship while Spearman correlation related cord 25(OH)D to gestational age. One-way ANOVA compared mean cord 25(OH)D across gestational-age groups; Bonferroni post-hoc tests were planned for overall p < 0.05. Paired categorical change was done to assess the shift in vitamin-D status from mother to neonate was tested with a McNemar χ². Finally, two-tailed significance was set at α = 0.05; 95% confidence intervals accompany effect estimates.

Quality assurance

All phlebotomists received refresher training on sample handling; centrifuges were calibrated monthly. Ten per cent of samples were blinded duplicates; duplicate-pair coefficients of variation averaged 7.2%. Data-entry verification found < 0.5% transcription error.

Results

Participant flow and baseline characteristics

During the 12-month recruitment window, 92 consenting mother–neonate pairs satisfied the inclusion criteria and provided complete maternal and cord-blood samples. Mean maternal age was 29.73 ± 5.46 years; mean gestational age at delivery was 36.89 ± 3.45.

Maternal and neonatal 25-hydroxy-vitamin D concentrations

Table 1 shows that maternal vitamin D levels at birth were generally higher than those in cord blood. The median maternal level was 28.89 ng/mL (IQR: 21.80–32.70), compared to 24.00 ng/mL (IQR: 17.00–32.00) in neonates. Similarly, the mean maternal level (28.54 ± 11.01 ng/mL) exceeded the mean cord blood level (24.85 ± 9.28 ng/mL). Cord blood values demonstrated a lower minimum and greater variability (5.0–55.0 ng/mL) compared with maternal levels (21.8–76.3 ng/mL), indicating relatively lower and more variable vitamin D status among neonates at birth. Maternal values were higher than cord blood values in most paired samples. Paired-samples t-test analysis confirmed a statistically significant difference between maternal and neonatal levels (t = 2.46, p = 0.015), with a mean absolute difference of 3.69 ng/mL (95% CI: 1.0–6.4) and a maternal-to-neonatal ratio of 1.20 ± 0.29. Additionally, maternal and neonatal vitamin D concentrations were positively correlated (Pearson r = 0.498, p < 0.001). These findings indicate a significant maternal-to-neonatal gap of approximately 3.7 ng/mL (ratio ≈ 1.2:1).

Vitamin-D status categories

Table 2 shows vitamin-D status categories (Deficient, Insufficient and Sufficient). > 50% of mothers and 60% of neonates were only “insufficient,” even though true deficiency was uncommon. The paired distribution differed significantly (McNemar χ² = 8.97, p = 0.003). The later test showed a significant imbalance between maternal and neonatal vitamin-D categories (χ² = 8.97, p = 0.003). Many more mothers who were sufficient had babies who were only insufficient than the other way round. Maternal sufficiency therefore does not guarantee neonatal sufficiency.

Cord-blood 25(OH)D by gestational age

Table 3 shows that mean cord-blood vitamin D levels varied across gestational age groups, with the highest levels observed in early pre-term neonates (29.33 ± 8.98 ng/mL), followed by term neonates (24.35 ± 9.24 ng/mL), and the lowest levels in late pre-term neonates (23.47 ± 9.21 ng/mL). Although early pre-term neonates demonstrated the highest mean concentrations, the overall difference across groups was not statistically significant (ANOVA F = 1.69, p = 0.19). This trend suggests that neonatal vitamin D levels may vary with gestational age, with a possible tendency toward higher levels earlier in gestation.

Discussion

Our data confirms a consistent yet modest maternal–neonatal vitamin D gradient at birth. On average maternal serum 25(OH)D exceeded neonatal concentrations yielding a maternal-to-cord ratio of 1.2. Nearly all (97%) mother–neonate pairs displayed higher maternal values than neonatal values, but the two measures remained positively correlated. In contrast to outright deficiency, which was uncommon, more than half of mothers and almost two-thirds of neonates were vitamin-D-insufficient, underscoring a sizeable “grey zone” of sub-optimal status. From our result, significant McNemar imbalance, suggests that maternal sufficiency may not translate into neonatal sufficiency.

Our gradient aligns closely with reports from two other sub-Saharan cohorts. In Lagos, Nigeria, maternal deficiency was only 4.8% whereas neonatal deficiency reached 29.5%, implying a larger absolute drop despite similar latitude [9]. In Johannesburg, South Africa, mean cord-blood 25(OH)D represented ~ 80% of maternal values [10]—almost identical to our ratio when converted to nmol L⁻¹. Together these African datasets suggest that abundant year-round sunlight does not abolish the maternal–neonatal gradient and that dark skin phenotype, cultural dress norms and limited antenatal supplementation may combine to widen it. Globally, a systematic review of 95 studies found maternal–neonatal 25(OH)D correlations to be universally positive yet neonatal levels consistently 10–30% lower than maternal levels [14], situating our findings well within the international range.

An unexpected observation was that very-pre-term neonates (28–31 weeks) exhibited the highest cord-blood 25(OH)D concentrations, whereas levels declined steadily toward term. Similar inverse relationships between gestational age and neonatal vitamin D have been noted in Scandinavian [15] and Asian [16] cohorts and may reflect preferential placental transfer in early gestation, haemodilution of 25(OH)D as neonatal blood volume expands late in pregnancy, or accelerated utilization by rapidly growing late-gestation neonates. Our data cannot adjudicate among these mechanisms but the authors caution against assuming that prematurity necessarily confers better or poorer vitamin-D status.

Placental transport of 25(OH)D is mediated by megalin–cubilin–D-binding protein endocytosis. Recent ex-vivo perfusion experiments show that the placenta actively metabolizes 25(OH)D, converting a fraction to 24,25-dihydroxy-vitamin D and 1,25-dihydroxy-vitamin D, thereby reducing the amount reaching the neonates [6]. Also, the same study found that perfused human placentas exposed to 25(OH)D rapidly up-regulates genes for megalin and CYP24A1, diverting a fraction of 25(OH)D into local metabolism thereby limiting neonatal delivery [6]. High maternal melanin content and limited ultraviolet-B exposure particularly in veiled or hijab-wearing women curtail cutaneous vitamin-D synthesis [17, 18]. Genotypic variation in the vitamin-D-binding protein gene and in placental hydroxylase enzymes such as CYP27B1 and CYP24A1 has also been shown to modulate the efficiency of maternal-to-neonatal vitamin-D transfer [5, 19].

Achieving maternal sufficiency alone may be inadequate for neonatal sufficiency. Modelling our 18% attrition suggests pregnant women might need serum targets ≥ 36 ng mL⁻¹ to secure neonatal levels ≥ 30 ng mL⁻¹. This aligns with recent meta-analytic report that prenatal vitamin-D supplementation of > 400 IU Day⁻¹ improves birth-weight, austism spectrum disorders, Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder, reduces pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes amongst other poor neonatal/obstetric outcomes [2].

According to the World Health Organisation guidelines in Vitamin D supplementation [20], routine oral vitamin-D supplementation is not currently recommended for all pregnant women because the quality and consistency of benefit-of-dose evidence remain low [20]. The guideline instead encourages context-specific research to clarify dosing and target groups. We observed from our study that two-thirds of neonates remain insufficient despite generally adequate mothers. This finding bolsters the case for revisiting national guidelines in high-melanin, low-latitude settings. Indeed, in high-melanin, sun-rich populations such as Ilorin, the high prevalence of neonatal insufficiency warrants re-evaluating local supplementation thresholds rather than adopting blanket global policies.

A pragmatic interim strategy could involve point-of-care testing: a German validation study showed the i-CHECK™ device had 88% sensitivity and 85% specificity for detecting 25(OH)D < 50 nmol L⁻¹ versus Liquid Chromatography–Tandem Mass Spectrometry [21], enabling dose-tailored antenatal supplementation where laboratory infrastructure is limited.

Future multi-centre studies should incorporate serial measurements across gestation to define the temporal dynamics of placental transfer and examine genetic polymorphisms in vitamin-D-binding protein and placental hydroxylases. Randomised trials comparing higher dosing of antenatal supplementation with current standard dosing in dark-skinned African populations are also warranted, with neonatal sufficiency as a primary endpoint.

The key strengths in our study include the use of paired maternal and neonatal samples analysed in the same laboratory with strict quality control, minimising inter-assay variability, and recruitment across all seasons, which reduces confounding by seasonal ultraviolet flux. Limitations must be acknowledged. First, the single-centre design may limit generalisability beyond semi-urban North-Central Nigeria. Second, dietary intake and supplement use were self-reported and not quantified biochemically. Third, we did not measure vitamin-D-binding protein or free 25(OH)D, which evidence argues more accurately reflect bioavailable vitamin D for placental transfer [22]. Finally, the cross-sectional nature precludes causal inference regarding sunlight exposure or other determinants.

In conclusion, a modest yet clinically meaningful difference in vitamin D levels between mothers and their newborns persists in semi-equatorial Ilorin, resulting in approximately two-thirds of neonates being vitamin D insufficient at birth. Our findings suggest that current antenatal supplementation thresholds may be too low for high-melanin populations and that maternal serum 25(OH)D levels likely need to exceed 35 ng mL⁻¹ to guarantee neonatal adequacy.
