Variability in Hospital-Onset Bacteremia in Ontario
Authors: Olivier Bedard-Lapointe, Xuejiao Shelly Wei, Nick Daneman, Kevin Brown, Fizza Manzoor, Mona Golmohammadzadeh, Dominik Mertz, Zain Chagla, Jennifer A. Flemming, Sudeep S. Gill, Anthony D. Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionWhat is the variability in hospital-onset bacteremia or fungemia (HOB) rates across acute-care hospitals in Ontario, Canada?FindingsIn this cohort study of more than 3 million admissions across 114 acute care hospitals, HOB rates ranged from 0.39 episodes per 1000 admission-days for the 10th percentile to 2.27 episodes per 1000 admission-days for 90th percentile. In a generalized linear mixed-effects model that adjusted for patient and hospital characteristics, the rate ratio was 1.43 per 1 SD across hospitals.MeaningThere is significant variability in HOB rates across hospitals, suggesting it may be a useful quality metric in discriminating between hospital performances.

Introduction

Hospital-onset bacteremia and fungemia (HOB) has emerged as a potential hospital-level quality metric for health care–associated infections.1 It is commonly defined as a positive blood culture for bacterial or fungal organisms obtained on or after hospital day 4, with the day of admission defined as hospital day 1.1

Compared with traditional infection prevention and control metrics, HOB offers several practical advantages. It can be ascertained using routinely collected laboratory data without the need for manual record review and captures a broader spectrum of hospital-acquired infections from different sources.1,2,3,4 By shifting the focus from narrowly defined, source-specific events toward a more inclusive measure of hospital-acquired bloodstream infections, HOB may better reflect the cumulative impact of in-hospital care processes.3,5 The adoption of central line–associated bloodstream infection as a publicly reported quality metric was associated with a marked reduction in infection rates, illustrating the potential for surveillance-based measurement to drive improvements in patient safety and motivating interest in broader quality metric measures such as HOB, which are mostly preventable events according to prior studies.4,6

Reflecting this growing interest, HOB has been proposed as a voluntary health care–associated infection quality metric in the US, and practical guidance for HOB prevention programs has been published.7,8 However, HOB has not yet been adopted as a standardized quality metric for comparing performance across hospitals.

A key requirement for the uptake of any hospital quality metric is its ability to meaningfully discriminate performances between hospitals beyond random variation and differences in patient case mix, thereby enabling identification of settings where in-hospital care processes may warrant closer evaluation or targeted improvement efforts.8 To date, evidence describing hospital-level variation in HOB has largely been limited to selected academic centers, often within intensive care unit (ICU) settings,2,4 and data from broader, population-based health care systems remain sparse.9

We, therefore, sought to describe and compare HOB rates across acute-care hospitals in Ontario, Canada, leveraging population-based administrative and laboratory data from a universal health care system to validate the ability of HOB to discriminate between hospitals as a quality metric. We hypothesized that there would be significant variability in HOB rates across Ontario hospitals.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a population-based, retrospective cohort study in Ontario, Canada, using administrative health databases housed at ICES, formerly known as the Institute for Clinical Evaluative Sciences. Ontario is the second largest province in Canada with a population of approximately 16 million people. Ontario has a publicly funded health care system administered through the Ontario Health Insurance Plan (OHIP) to not-for-profit hospitals. ICES is an independent, nonprofit research institute whose legal status under Ontario’s health information privacy law allows it to collect and analyze health care and demographic data, without consent, for health system evaluation and improvement. The Queen’s University Health Sciences and Affiliated Teaching Hospitals Research Ethics Board approved this study (TRAQ No. 6044236). We reported this study using the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines10 by fulfilling items on the checklist for each section.

Data Sources

The following ICES databases were used for this study: Discharge Abstract Database, Same Day Surgery, National Ambulatory Care Reporting System, OHIP Claims Database, Registered Persons Database, Postal Code Conversion File, Ontario health care institutions, Canadian Organ Replacement Registry, Ontario Laboratories Information System (OLIS), and other ICES-derived cohorts. These datasets were linked using unique encoded identifiers and analyzed at ICES.

For context, Discharge Abstract Database, Same Day Surgery, and National Ambulatory Care Reporting System contain data from encounters at hospitals. OLIS is the large centralized electronic repository of laboratory results for hospital and outpatient facilities. Under the Laboratory and Specimen Collection Centre Licensing Act, Ontario laboratories are required to report test results to OLIS. More than 95% of all clinical microbiology laboratories in the province report culture and susceptibility results to OLIS.11 In Ontario, there were 140 public hospital corporations in 2023,12 and OLIS microbiology data captured 136 acute-care hospitals in 2024. Hospital sites and hospital years that were not available in the OLIS database were excluded from the analysis.

Study Population

The study population included all adult patients with a valid OHIP card who were admitted to an acute-care hospital in Ontario between January 1, 2017, and September 30, 2024. Patients needed to have at least 3 years of OHIP eligibility for data on comorbidities. All eligible admissions between the specified study start and end date were included. Blood culture data were obtained from OLIS for the period from January 1, 2017, through December 31, 2024.

To ensure that patients were at risk for HOB, we restricted the cohort to hospital admissions with a length of stay of 4 days or longer; admissions in which patients were discharged or died before hospital day 4 were excluded. Patients could be included more than once if they were discharged and subsequently readmitted during the study period, with each admission treated as a separate and unique observation.

We only included admissions to acute-care hospitals and excluded admissions to mental health facilities, complex continuing care hospitals, and rehabilitation facilities. To ensure stable hospital-level estimates without extreme outliers due to small number of blood cultures taken, we prespecified the analysis to be restricted to acute-care hospitals that submitted more than 500 blood cultures annually to OLIS during the study period, including only hospital-years meeting this threshold.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was HOB, defined as a positive blood culture for any bacterial or fungal organism collected on or after hospital day 4, with admission day as hospital day 1, consistent with standard definitions.3 This definition aligns with the intended use of HOB as a surveillance-based quality indicator relying on routinely collected laboratory data. Each HOB episode was defined by the date of the first positive blood culture yielding an organism not isolated from blood cultures collected during hospital days 1 through 3.

For patients with multiple positive blood cultures during the same hospital admission, HOB was counted as a single episode per admission, regardless of the number of positive cultures, organisms identified, or timing after hospital day 4. This approach was chosen to reflect the intended use of HOB as a surveillance-based quality indicator and to avoid misclassifying persistent or recurrent bacteremia from the same source as distinct events. Similarly, a prior study had defined HOB as the first positive blood culture.9 All organisms identified in blood cultures were included in the primary analysis, including those considered possible skin commensals. The inclusion of all positive blood cultures regardless of organism was consistent with the definition from prior studies on HOB as a quality metric.2,4 This definition was meant to reflect how automated data from hospitals would practically be collected and analyzed.

In a sensitivity analysis, we included only blood cultures that grew at least 1 organism typically regarded as a true pathogen and not a potential skin contaminant (eTable 1 in Supplement 1).13,14 There were no secondary outcomes.

Covariates

We examined both hospital-level and admission-level patient characteristics as covariates. Hospital-level characteristics included urban vs rural, teaching hospital status, total number of acute-care non-ICU beds, and total number of ICU beds. We also included hospital blood culture testing prevalence (proportion of admissions that had any blood culture drawn on or after hospital day 4) as well as hospital blood culture testing intensity (number of total blood cultures drawn on or after hospital day 4 divided by total admissions that had any blood culture performed anytime during admission).9

Patient characteristics included age, sex, admission calendar year, receipt of long-term dialysis, presence of active malignant neoplasm recently diagnosed or receiving active treatment, transplant recipient status (solid organ, stem cell, or bone marrow), and admission for major surgery. Major surgery was defined using Canadian Classification of Health Interventions codes (eTable 2 in Supplement 1).15

Statistical Analysis

For descriptive analysis of continuous variables, we used mean (SD) or median (IQR) when appropriate. Interdecile ranges and IQRs were used to describe HOB rates across hospitals. We used counts and frequencies to describe categorical variables.

HOB rates were described as a rate based on number of events per 1000 admission-days for each hospital site. For each patient, the first 3 days from admission and days after first episode of HOB were omitted from this denominator of admission-days. As a visual representation of variation across hospital sites and outliers, the HOB rate for each hospital was plotted in a funnel plot.

We used a generalized linear mixed-effects model (GLMM) with a Poisson distribution and log link to model variation in HOB rate across hospitals. The logarithm of admission-days was included as an offset term to account for differences in exposure time across observations. A 2-level hierarchical approach was used with admissions nested within hospitals. Each admission was analyzed as a unique observation, and within-patient clustering was not considered. Hospital was included as a random effect through a random intercept, allowing the baseline HOB rate to vary across hospitals and accounting for the clustering of observations within hospitals. The random-effects covariance structure was specified as variance components, with a single variance parameter estimated for the hospital-level random intercept and independence assumed between hospitals (eAppendix in Supplement 1). Model parameters were estimated using the Laplace approximation. The model included fixed effects for patient and hospital characteristics and a hospital-specific random intercept. Residual between-hospital variation was quantified by the SD of the hospital-specific random intercept.

The adjusted HOB rate was estimated using a model-based estimated-to-expected approach, where estimated counts included hospital random effects and expected counts were derived from fixed effects only. Adjusted rate for each hospital was calculated as the ratio of estimated to expected events multiplied by the overall crude rate.

All confidence intervals were 95% CIs. All statistical tests were 2-sided with a P < .05 significance level. Complete case analysis was done. All analyses were conducted using SAS statistical software version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

Results

Study Population

Between January 1, 2017, and September 30, 2024, we identified 3 325 900 eligible hospital admissions (median [IQR] age, 71 [57-81] years; 1 727 228 female patients [51.9%]) across 114 acute-care hospitals in Ontario (Figure 1). Twenty-two hospitals (eTable 3 in Supplement 1) were excluded because they had 500 or fewer blood cultures per year throughout the entire study period.

Hospital and Patient Characteristics

Among the 114 included acute-care hospitals, 79 (69.3%) were in urban settings, and 96 (84.2%) were nonteaching institutions. Hospitals had a median (IQR) of 66 (27-185) acute-care non-ICU beds and a median (IQR) of 6 (3-21) ICU beds. The median (IQR) blood culture testing prevalence was 7.2% (5.4%-9.8%). HOB Rates

In total, 61 452 HOB episodes were observed over 29 721 745 admission-days across all included hospitals, corresponding to an overall rate of 2.07 episodes per 1000 admission-days. Across the 114 acute-care hospitals, the HOB rates were 0.39 episodes per 1000 admission-days for the 10th percentile, 0.71 episodes per 1000 admission-days for the 25th percentile, 1.08 episodes per 1000 admission-days for the 50th percentile, 1.68 episodes per 1000 admission-days for the 75th percentile, and 2.27 episodes per 1000 admission-days for the 90th percentile. Funnel plots demonstrated marked hospital-level variation in HOB rates, with several hospitals outside the 95% and 99.8% control limits (Figure 2). Of the 61 452 patients with HOB episodes, 9353 (15.2%) had a subsequent positive blood culture, which may be a second HOB episode later during hospital stay that was not included in this analysis.

Multivariable Model for Between-Hospital Variation in HOB

In the GLMMs that adjusted for patient and hospital characteristics (Table 2), variables significantly associated with higher HOB rates included younger age, male sex, earlier admission calendar year, long-term dialysis, active malignant neoplasm, transplant recipient status, major surgery, and higher blood culture testing prevalence. The variance in HOB rates between hospitals decreased from 0.45 to 0.36 after adjusting for admission-level patient characteristics and decreased further to 0.13 after adjusting for hospital characteristics. Therefore, patient case mix and hospital-level characteristics statistically accounted for 71.1% of HOB variance between hospitals. After adjusting for patient and hospital characteristics, there remained significant variation between hospital with a random intercept SD of 0.36 (95% CI, 0.29-0.42), which corresponded to a rate ratio of 1.43 (95% CI, 1.34-1.52) per 1 SD. This translates to hospitals 1 SD above the mean (84th percentile) having approximately twice the adjusted HOB rate as hospitals 1 SD below the mean (16th percentile). Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analysis that only included positive blood cultures growing an organism typically regarded as a true pathogen showed similar results. The overall HOB rate was 1.04 episodes per 1000 admission-days. Across the 114 acute-care hospitals, the HOB rates were 0.24 episodes per 1000 admission-days for the 10th percentile, 0.43 episodes per 1000 admission-days for the 25th percentile, 0.65 episodes per 1000 admission-days for the 50th percentile, 0.96 episodes per 1000 admission-days for the 75th percentile, and 1.24 episodes per 1000 admission-days for the 90th percentile (eFigure in Supplement 1). In a GLMM (eTable 4 in Supplement 1), the random intercept SD was 0.32 (95% CI 0.25-0.38), which corresponded to a rate ratio of 1.38 (95% CI, 1.28-1.46) per 1 SD.

Discussion

In this population-based cohort study of 3 325 900 hospital admissions across 114 acute-care hospitals in Ontario, we observed marked variation in HOB rates between hospitals. The HOB rates ranged from 0.39 to 2.27 episodes per 1000 admission-days between the 10th and 90th percentile, and this heterogeneity persisted after adjustment for patient case mix and hospital-level characteristics. The persistence of hospital-level variation beyond measured patient case mix and hospital characteristics supports a key criterion for consideration of HOB as a hospital-level quality indicator.

This study leveraged population-based administrative and laboratory data to capture HOB across all acute-care hospitals in Ontario, encompassing both community and academic settings. Inclusion of 114 hospitals and more than 3.3 million hospital admissions over nearly 8 years enabled precise estimation of hospital-level HOB rates and a reliable assessment of between-hospital variation. The population-wide scope of the data that includes the vast majority of hospitals within a region enhances the generalizability of these findings and minimizes selection bias inherent to single-center or network-based studies.

Our findings build on prior studies evaluating HOB as a hospital-level quality metric. Previous work has largely been conducted in selected centers, predominantly academic hospitals, and has often focused on ICU populations.2 In a multicenter study of 80 ICUs across 16 hospitals in the US and Canada, Rock et al2 demonstrated marked variability in HOB rates across sites compared with central line–associated bloodstream infection. Our study extends these observations by examining HOB at a population level across 114 acute-care hospitals in a publicly funded health care system, including both community and academic institutions, and across all inpatient settings rather than selected units.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, our definition of HOB included all positive blood cultures, including organisms commonly considered possible contaminants, as the available data did not permit adjudication of true bloodstream infection vs contamination. This approach is consistent with prior studies and reflects its intended use as a surveillance-based quality indicator rather than a clinically adjudicated infection outcome.2,4 Importantly, reliance on routinely collected laboratory data without the need for detailed record review is a key feature that supports the feasibility and scalability of HOB for hospital-level measurement. It is reassuring that sensitivity analysis including only true pathogens in blood cultures showed consistent results. Positive blood cultures due to contamination in and of itself is a quality marker that hospitals should intervene to minimize, so inclusion of contaminated blood cultures in HOB measure would not invalidate it as a quality marker.

Second, as with any observational study, residual confounding by unmeasured patient or hospital characteristics may remain and could contribute to the observed variation in HOB rates across hospitals. Nevertheless, we adjusted for a range of clinically relevant patient and hospital characteristics known to be associated with bloodstream infection risk,4,9 and between-hospital variability remained evident after this adjustment, suggesting that the observed heterogeneity is not solely explained by measured case mix.

Third, we defined HOB as a single episode per hospital admission, regardless of organism or number of positive cultures. The intended purpose was to avoid inflation of HOB rate owing to the small proportion of patients who had persistent or recurrent bacteremia with many positive blood cultures from the same source. Sicker and more complicated patients would be more likely to have multiple HOB events. This likely underestimates the variability for tertiary or quaternary hospitals that care for sicker and complex patients.

Fourth, we excluded 22 hospitals that had 500 or fewer blood cultures per year throughout the entire study period, which can lead to imprecise and possibly extreme HOB rates. These hospitals were rural and had very few beds (eTable 3 in Supplement 1). The exclusion of these hospitals likely led to fewer outliers and less observed variability. Thus, our study findings would not be representative of these rural small hospitals.

Fifth, our study included hospitals with a median of 66 acute care beds and 6 ICU beds. The study findings may not be generalizable to settings with substantially bigger hospitals.

Conclusions

In this population-based cohort study of 3 325 900 hospital admissions across 114 acute-care hospitals in Ontario, we observed marked variability in HOB rates across hospitals, persisting after adjustment for measured patient and hospital characteristics, which has important implications. Although this study was not designed to identify causal mechanisms, the persistence of between-hospital variation suggests that differences in local care processes or practices may contribute to HOB risk and represent potential opportunities for prevention and quality improvement efforts.2,4

Future studies are needed to further evaluate HOB against additional criteria required of a reliable hospital quality metric. Work is needed to better characterize the preventability of HOB events, their associations with patient-important outcomes, and their impact on health care utilization and costs. In parallel, intervention-based studies assessing the implementation of HOB-focused prevention programs will be important to determine whether HOB surveillance and prevention programs can meaningfully reduce HOB rates and improve patient outcomes. Together, such studies will help establish whether HOB can move beyond surveillance to support effective and actionable quality improvement initiatives.
