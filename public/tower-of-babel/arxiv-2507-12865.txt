Abstract
. 1.
Introduction and statement of the results

Euler investigated planar curves
\gamma
of constant density joining two given points and having minimum moment of inertia with respect to the origin
[
6
, p. 53]
. In polar coordinates
r=r(\theta)
, these curves are minimum of the energy

\int_{\gamma}r^{\alpha}\,ds=\int_{\theta_{1}}^{\theta_{2}}r^{\alpha}\sqrt{r^{\prime 2}+r^{\prime 2}}\ d\theta,

where
\alpha
is a parameter. In fact, Euler found the extremals of this energy for two explicit parameters. Indeed, if
\alpha=1/2
, the solution is an equilateral hyperbola. If
\alpha=2
, the solution is
ar^{3}=\mbox{sec}(3\theta+b)
,
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, it is necessary that the two points
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
subtend an angle of less than
\pi/3
at the origin. For any
\alpha
, explicit parametrizations of the extremals can be found
[
2
,
9
]
. The case
\alpha=-1
was considered by Bonnet in
[
1
]
. For
\alpha=2
, Mason proved that given two points of the extremal curve, the extremal curve is an absolute minimum of the energy. In case that
|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|\geq\pi/3
, then the absolute minimum of the energy is the curve formed by the two lines between both points with the origin
[
7
]
.

Recently, Dierkes and Huisken have extended this problem in arbitrary dimensions
[
5
]
. In this paper we consider the case of surfaces in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Let
\Sigma
be a connected, oriented surface and let
\Phi\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an immersion of
\Sigma
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. For
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, define the energy

E_{\alpha}[\Sigma]=\int_{\Sigma}|\Phi|^{\alpha}\,d\Sigma,

where
d\Sigma
denotes the area element induced on
\Sigma
. Critical points of
E_{\alpha}
are called
\alpha
-stationary surfaces
and we say simply stationary surfaces if we do not want to emphasize the value of
\alpha
. Assuming that
0\not\in\Phi(\Sigma)
, the Euler-Lagrange equation associated to
E_{\alpha}
is

(1)

H=\alpha\frac{\langle N,\Phi\rangle}{|\Phi|^{2}},

where
H
and
N
are the mean curvature and the unit normal vector of
\Sigma
, respectively. Notice that if
\alpha=0
then stationary surfaces are minimal surfaces. From now, we will discard the case
\alpha=0
. In
[
5
]
, the authors investigated stability of spheres and minimal cones as well as minimizers of
E_{\alpha}
. See also
[
3
,
4
]
.

We find all
\alpha
-stationary surfaces in the family of isoparametric surfaces. Recall that an isoparametric surface is a surface with constant Gauss curvature and constant mean curvature. In
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the isoparametric surfaces are planes, spheres and circular cylinders. An easy computation shows the following

(1)

Planes are stationary surfaces if and only if they contain the origin
0\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. This holds for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

(2)

The only stationary spheres are the following:

(a)

Spheres centered at
0
. Here
\alpha=-2
.

(b)

Spheres containing
0
. Here
\alpha=-4
.

(3)

Circular cylinders are not stationary surfaces for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

In
[
5
]
, the authors focus on closed stationary surfaces, proving that if
\alpha>-2
, there are not closed stationary surfaces and if
\alpha<-2
, then the closure of any
\alpha
-stationary surface must contain
0
. Finally, if
\alpha=-2
, spheres are the only closed stable surfaces: see
[
5
, Thm. 1.6]
. In fact, for
\alpha=-2
the hypothesis on stability can be dropped because an argument of comparison with spheres centered at the origin together the maximum principle proves that closed
-2
-stationary surfaces must be spheres. Definitively, spheres are the only closed
\alpha
-stationary surfaces and this only holds when
\alpha=-2
.

In the class of stationary surfaces, we point out that the previous two types of examples have constant Gauss curvature
K
: planes with
K=0
and spheres with
K\not=0
. The objective of this paper is to prove the converse and characterize these examples as the only stationary surfaces with constant Gauss curvature.

Theorem 1.1
.

Planes and spheres are the only stationary surfaces with constant Gauss curvature.

This theorem will be proved in Sects.
3
and
4
because we distinguish the case
K\not=0
and
K=0
, respectively.

Furthermore, two more results are proved, where we replace the hypothesis on the Gauss curvature and assuming that a principal curvature is constant or that the mean curvature is constant.

Theorem 1.2
.

Planes and spheres are the only stationary surfaces with a constant principal curvature.

Theorem 1.3
.

Planes and spheres are the only stationary surfaces with constant mean curvature.

The proofs will be done in Sects.
5
and
6
respectively. The techniques use the Gauss and Codazzi equations in local coordinates of a surface given by lines of curvature. This will be introduced in Sect.
2
. In some steps during the proofs, it will be required long and hard computations of derivatives as well as manipulations with polynomials. In this paper we employ
Mathematica
©
to compute these symbolic calculations
[
10
]
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we show local computations of the curvature on a surface when the coordinates are given by lines of curvature in open sets free of umbilic points. The next calculations and results hold for the proofs of all results of this paper.

Let
\Sigma
be an orientable surface immersed in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Denote by
\nabla
and
\overline{\nabla}
be the Levi-Civita connections of
\Sigma
and

\mathbb{R}^{3}
, respectively. Denote by
N
the Gauss map of
\Sigma
. Let
\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma)
be the space of tangent vector fields of
\Sigma
. The Gauss and Weingarten formulas are, respectively,

(2)

\begin{split}\overline{\nabla}_{X}Y&=\nabla_{X}Y+h(X,Y),\\
\overline{\nabla}_{X}N&=-SX,\end{split}

for all
X,Y\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma)
, where
h\colon T\Sigma\times T\Sigma\to T^{\perp}\Sigma
and
S\colon T\Sigma\to T\Sigma
are the second fundamental form and the shape
operator, respectively. The Gauss
equation and Codazzi equations are

\begin{split}\langle R(X,Y)W,Z\rangle&=\langle h(Y,W),h(X,Z)\rangle-\langle h(X,W),h(Y,Z)\rangle\\
(\nabla_{X}S)Y&=(\nabla_{Y}S)X,\end{split}

where
R
is the curvature tensor of
\nabla
. The mean curvature
H
and the Gauss curvature
K
are defined by

H=\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2},\qquad K=\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2},

where
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
are the principal curvatures of
\Sigma
.

In an open set
\Omega\subset\Sigma
of non-umbilic points, consider a local
orthonormal frame
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
by line of curvature coordinates: see for example,
[
8
, Ch. 3]
. The expression of the shape operator
S
is

(3)

\left\{\begin{split}Se_{1}&=\kappa_{1}e_{1}\\
Se_{2}&=\kappa_{2}e_{2}.\end{split}\right.

Since
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
is an orthonormal frame, if
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma)
then

\left\{\begin{split}\nabla_{X}e_{1}&=\omega(X)e_{2}\\
\nabla_{X}e_{2}&=-\omega(X)e_{1},\end{split}\right.

where
\omega(X)=\langle\nabla_{X}e_{1},e_{2}\rangle=-\langle\nabla_{X}e_{2},e_{1}\rangle
. Then we have

(4)

\left\{\begin{split}&\nabla_{e_{1}}e_{1}=\omega(e_{1})e_{2},\quad\nabla_{e_{1}}e_{2}=-\omega(e_{1})e_{1}\\
&\nabla_{e_{2}}e_{1}=\omega(e_{2})e_{2},\quad\nabla_{e_{2}}e_{2}=-\omega(e_{2})e_{1}.\end{split}\right.

Using the Codazzi equations, we have

(5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}e_{2}(\kappa_{1})=(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2})\omega(e_{1})\\
e_{1}(\kappa_{2})=(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2})\omega(e_{2}),\end{array}\right.

where we stand for
e_{i}(f)
the derivative of a smooth function
f\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
along the principal directions
e_{i}
,
i=1,2
. In the set
\Omega
, we have
\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}\not=0
. The Gauss equation gives the following expression of the Gauss curvature
K
,

(6)

K=\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}=-e_{1}\left(\frac{e_{1}(\kappa_{2})}{\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}}\right)+e_{2}\left(\frac{e_{2}(\kappa_{1})}{\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}}\right)-\frac{e_{1}(\kappa_{2})^{2}+e_{2}(\kappa_{1})^{2}}{(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2})^{2}}.

Denote by
\Phi\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
the immersion of stationary surface. Let decompose the position vector
\Phi
in its tangent
\Phi^{\top}
and normal part
\Phi^{\perp}
with respect to
\Sigma
. Let

\Phi^{\top}=\gamma e_{1}+\mu e_{2},

for some smooth functions
\gamma
and
\mu
. Notice that

\gamma=\langle\Phi,e_{1}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}e_{1}(|\Phi|^{2}),\quad\mu=\langle\Phi,e_{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}e_{2}(|\Phi|^{2}).

Analogously, the normal part is
\Phi^{\perp}=\langle N,\Phi\rangle N
, which by the stationary surface equation (
1
), is

\Phi^{\perp}=\frac{H|\Phi|^{2}}{\alpha}N.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a subset of non-umbilic points of an
\alpha
-stationary surface
\Sigma
. Then the functions
\gamma
and
\mu
satisfy the following equations

(7)

\left\{\begin{split}e_{1}(\gamma)&=\mu\omega(e_{1})+\frac{H|\Phi|^{2}}{\alpha}\kappa_{1}+1\\
e_{2}(\gamma)&=\mu\omega(e_{2})\\
e_{1}(\mu)&=-\gamma\omega(e_{1})\\
e_{2}(\mu)&=-\gamma\omega(e_{2})+\frac{H|\Phi|^{2}}{\alpha}\kappa_{2}+1\\
0&=e_{1}(\frac{H|\Phi|^{2}}{\alpha})+\gamma\kappa_{1}\\
0&=e_{2}(\frac{H|\Phi|^{2}}{\alpha})+\mu\kappa_{2}.\end{split}\right.

Proof.

If
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma)
, then
\overline{\nabla}_{X}\Phi=X
. The Gauss and
Weingarten formulas (
2
) imply

\begin{split}X&=\overline{\nabla}_{X}\Phi=\overline{\nabla}_{X}(\Phi^{\top}+\Phi^{\perp})=\overline{\nabla}_{X}\Phi^{\top}+\overline{\nabla}_{X}\Phi^{\perp}\\
&={\nabla}_{X}\Phi^{\top}+h(X,\Phi^{\top})+X(H\frac{|\Phi|^{2}}{\alpha})N-H\frac{|\Phi|^{2}}{\alpha}SX.\end{split}

Taking the tangent and the normal part of the above expression, we obtain, respectively,

(8)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\nabla}_{X}\Phi^{\top}-H\frac{|\Phi|^{2}}{\alpha}SX=X\\
h(X,\Phi^{\top})+X(H\frac{|\Phi|^{2}}{\alpha})N=0.\end{array}\right.

Equations (
7
) are obtained by substituting in (
8
)
X
by
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
in the previous two identities and, next, writing the coordinates with respect to
\{e_{1},e_{2},N\}
. In the case
X=e_{1}
, and using (
4
), we have

\begin{array}[]{ll}e_{1}&={\nabla}_{e_{1}}(\gamma e_{1}+\mu e_{2})-H\frac{|\Phi|^{2}}{\alpha}\kappa_{1}e_{1}\\
&=e_{1}(\gamma)e_{1}+e_{1}(\mu)e_{2}+\gamma\omega(e_{1})e_{2}-\mu\omega(e_{1})e_{1}-H\frac{|\Phi|^{2}}{\alpha}\kappa_{1}e_{1}.\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{ll}0&=h(e_{1},\gamma e_{1}+\mu e_{2})+e_{1}(H\frac{|\Phi|^{2}}{\alpha})N\\
&=\left(\gamma\kappa_{1}+e_{1}\left(\frac{H|\Phi|^{2}}{\alpha}\right)\right)N.\end{array}

This gives the first, third and fifth equations in (
7
). The other three equations of (
7
) are obtained by putting
X=e_{2}
into (
8
).
∎
