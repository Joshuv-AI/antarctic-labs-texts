“We treat the symptom, not the system”: a qualitative study exploring connections between food insecurity and disease-related malnutrition in Canada
Authors: Fareeha Quayyum, Anjana Rao, Katherine L Ford, Jessica Carmila John, Jenneffer Rayane Braga, Emily Nicholas Angl, Aisha Lofters, Leah Gramlich, Alyssa Kelly, Rahul Jain, Sumantra Ray, Celia Laur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFood insecurity is a lack of physical and/or economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food that meets dietary needs and food preferences. Disease-related malnutrition (DRM) occurs in the context of illness or its treatment and is associated with inadequate nutrient intake and the inflammatory processes of diseases or medical conditions. Food insecurity and DRM both pose significant health risks, are prevalent and have similar underlying foundations, yet minimal research has explored these connections. The objective was to explore the complex relationship between food insecurity and DRM to inform system change, research, intervention and education supporting both topics.MethodsWe conducted online semistructured interviews and focus groups with clinicians, researchers/academics and staff from health systems and community organisations purposefully recruited from across Canada. A codebook supported inductive thematic analysis. Following the interviews/focus groups, participants were sent a list of research questions which they rated by priority level, actionability, feasibility and health equity considerations.ResultsInterviews (n=7) and focus groups (2; n=5) were conducted with 12 participants from four provinces. There was an even split between clinicians (n=4), researchers/academics (n=4), management (n=4) or representatives (n=3) from health system or community organisations (n=4). Half (n=6) self-identified as providing more input on DRM, and n=8 on food insecurity. The overarching theme was how structural inequities connected food insecurity and DRM. Participants discussed the need for ethical screening for both, but only if results could connect to services beyond food charity and if screening was unbiased and respectful. The need for system change was reported, alongside opportunities to support practice change through research, intervention and education. A list of research questions was generated.ConclusionsThis study reveals that food insecurity and DRM are fundamentally linked by structural inequities, necessitating a shift from food charity toward ethical, system-wide intervention. Addressing these interconnected issues requires integrated research and policy changes to support equitable health outcomes.Keywords: Malnutrition

Introduction

Food insecurity, defined as inadequate physical, social and/or economic access to safe, culturally appropriate and nutritious food to meet an individual’s dietary needs and preferences for an active and healthy lifestyle, is a well-established social determinant of health in Canada and globally.1–3 Health Canada categorises food insecurity as marginal, moderate or severe, with increasing severity linked to worse health outcomes.1 4 Food insecurity in Canada reached historic levels in 2024, with the Canadian Income Survey reporting that 25.5% of households (approximately 10 million people, including 2.5 million children) were food insecure, the highest prevalence in two decades.5 6 This increase was particularly pronounced for visible minority populations (27.3% in 2022 to 32.2% in 2023), Indigenous populations aged 15 years and over (35.7% in 2022 to 38.6% in 2023) and recent immigrants (10 years or less) aged 15 years and over (28.4% in 2022 to 34.4% in 2023), reflecting structural inequities.7 In Toronto, the Who’s Hungry 2024 report documented 3.49 million food bank visits in the prior year (a 273% increase from before the COVID-19 pandemic).8 This report estimated that more than one in ten Toronto residents relied on food banks, with 83% of those who accessed food bank services reporting inadequate food intake over the previous year.8 Household food security is measured in Canada through national surveys.6 7 There is debate whether clinicians should screen individuals for food insecurity, using tools such as the two-question Hunger Vital Sign tool, as there is concern regarding how to provide support if food insecurity is identified.9–13

Disease-related malnutrition (DRM) occurs in the context of illness or its treatment and is associated with inadequate nutrient intake and the inflammatory processes of diseases or medical conditions.14 Nutrition risk screening is recommended, using validated tools such as the Canadian Nutrition Screening Tool,15–19 followed by diagnosis and treatment.16 20–23 The Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition consensus presents a framework for diagnosing malnutrition. After nutrition screening and assessment, a diagnosis of malnutrition can be made when there is presence of a phenotypic criteria (eg, non-volitional weight loss, low body mass index, reduced muscle mass) and etiological criteria (eg, reduced intake or absorption and/or disease-related inflammation).20 In Canada, nearly half of adults and one-third of children admitted to medical or surgical hospital wards were malnourished on admission.24 Hospital malnutrition in Canada is reported to cost US$2 billion/year.25 Nutrition risk, which can be a precursor to overt malnutrition, is also widespread in the community with a third of Canadians aged ≥65 identified as at risk of malnutrition, with risk status predicting subsequent hospitalisation and mortality.26

There is limited direct evidence regarding the connections between DRM and food insecurity; however, what is known about household food insecurity in Canada suggests escalated risk for DRM.27 For example, food insecurity can reduce dietary quality, lower energy intake and drive trade-offs between food and other material essentials, thereby heightening the risk of DRM.28–30 Similarly, DRM may exacerbate food insecurity through disability, reduced work capacity and increasing healthcare costs, alongside the impact of the disease (chemotherapy, surgery, medical cost, side effects, etc).24 26 29 31 Despite bidirectional risk, current screening pathways keep food insecurity and DRM separate.

This study aimed to explore this complex relationship between food insecurity and DRM in community, hospital and during the transition from hospital to home, to provide direction for research, intervention, education and system change.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study involved semistructured interviews and focus groups with clinicians, researchers/academics and staff from health system/community organisations who have experience related to food insecurity and malnutrition in Canada. Following interviews/focus groups, an online survey of research questions for future work, developed based on interviews/focus groups, was sent for rating by participants.

Qualitative description was used to understand and describe participants’ perspectives regarding the connections and complexity of food insecurity and DRM.32 Qualitative description aims to stay close to the data, providing a thorough description of experiences. This approach is relevant for understanding and describing participants’ concerns, thoughts, feelings and attitudes related to experiences in healthcare.32

The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Studies (COREQ) checklist was followed .20

Recruitment

An environmental scan was conducted to identify individuals and organisations focused on DRM and food insecurity across Canada for purposive sampling. The scan included advanced online searches with relevant key words (food insecurity etc), and linked searches from relevant organisations (ie, linking to a partner organisation). Organisations and/or individuals that had a focus on both food insecurity and nutrition care were considered, recognising some would have a stronger focus on one area than the other. Organisations were then grouped by relevance and diversity (urban and rural settings, setting type, national vs local representation etc), with those deemed most relevant by the team contacted by e-mail. The first round of recruitment included 24 organisation. 12 additional organisations were identified as potentially relevant, but were not contacted once similar ideas were being heard during interviews. National organisations were prioritised over those with a local remit. Organisations were contacted using publicly available contact details, with each organisation deciding the most relevant individual to participate. Individuals, including researchers with expertise on these topics, were also contacted. Following initial contact, up to two follow-up emails were sent. Snowball sampling was used within interviews and focus groups by asking for recommendations for who else to contact at the end of the discussion. Two participants were recruited through snowball sampling.

Data collection

Participants were informed about the details of the project, then provided informed consent electronically and completed an online demographic survey. All focus groups and interviews were conducted online through a video conference software, Zoom. Focus groups and initial interviews were both conducted by an experienced female qualitative researcher (PhD trained) with a background in malnutrition and implementation science (CL). Subsequent interviews were co-conducted by a female graduate level trainee (FQ). FQ was the notetaker on all focus groups, and either CL or FQ was a notetaker during interviews, depending on who conducted the interview. CL had a prior relationship with some participants. All participants had the option to be interviewed solely by FQ, who had no prior relationship with participants. Interviews/focus groups lasted between 40 min and 1.5 hours.

Semistructured focus group and interview guides were developed by the team (dietitians, food insecurity experts, researchers and clinicians) and piloted for usability with the first two focus groups and first two interviews to confirm that questions would elicit responses aligned with our research question. Questions for both interviews and focus groups focused on the perceived connection between DRM and food insecurity, gaps in care, ethical screening processes, impact of structural racism, power dynamics, and opportunities for policy change (see box 1 for details). Throughout the discussion, participants were invited to think of potential research questions for future work. An active interviewing style was followed,33 with interviews treated as conversations. No repeat interviews were conducted. Interviews were completed until FG and CL felt similar ideas were being heard regarding the primary research question, even with diverse participants. Field notes were taken by CL and FQ to capture contextual details and emerging insights. Audio recordings for focus groups and interviews were transcribed verbatim through the video conferencing software and confirmed by FQ. Identifiers were removed and pseudonyms assigned. Data were organised using NVivo V.12. Transcripts were not returned for correction.

Following qualitative data collection, the team developed an online survey in Qualtrics with a list of research questions based on preliminary results. Participants were asked to rate each question on a 9-point Likert scale based on being high priority, feasible, action-oriented and equity focused. Each research question was presented with notes describing potential methods.

Analysis

Qualitative data were analysed with inductive thematic analysis by FQ and AR. A codebook was developed by CL and FQ, then confirmed by AR. A qualitative descriptive approach was followed, staying close to the data.32 Following organisation of data using NVivo V.12, discussions were held between FQ, AR and CL to identify key themes and subthemes supported by relevant quotes. Written results were drafted by CL and confirmed by FQ and AR, before confirmation with other authors.

Descriptive analysis was conducted for the research question survey with questions ranked by their total score and by category (high priority, feasible, action-oriented and equity focused).

Structural inequities connect food insecurity and DRM

The overarching theme was how DRM and food insecurity were distinct yet strongly connected by structural inequities.

Participants explained that DRM has traditionally been treated as a clinical diagnosis, managed in a healthcare setting. Yet, the structural inequities underlying DRM were reported to be rarely considered, leading to the medicalisation of the treatment intervention which could mask upstream causes. Some upstream causes were reported to strongly overlap with causes of food insecurity, such as housing insecurity (ie, lack of access to refrigeration, cooking facilities, or space for meal preparation), income instability (ie, inability to afford food, or food that supports their clinical conditions), lack of transportation (ie, inability to get to the grocery store or carry groceries home), geographic disparities (ie, lack of access to a grocery store especially in more rural areas), alongside institutional structural racism and poverty (ie, how clustering of poverty experiences can dictate where someone lives, which may limit access to grocery stores or healthcare).

These system-level factors connecting DRM and food insecurity were reported to be exacerbated by the disconnect between healthcare and community settings. Participants reported that even if DRM or food insecurity were identified in a clinical setting, little could typically be done once the individual was discharged or left their appointment. Although some participants reported development of programmes for direct food delivery (eg, delivery of a food basket after discharge) or providing connections with food charities (eg, food bank or pantry), overall, these were felt to be unsustainable, short-term solutions that did not address the root causes.

Participants acknowledged how not everyone with DRM is food insecure, and not everyone who is food insecure has DRM. Subsequently, food insecurity can contribute to DRM, yet not all cases of DRM stem from food insecurity. “They’re related, but they’re separate.” Participant 3

Similarly, participants reported how food insecurity does not always result in diagnosable DRM, and that DRM may only appear in severe cases of food insecurity. Food insecurity was thought to precipitate or exacerbate DRM, especially in chronic illness, postsurgical recovery or delayed healing.

Although these underlying connections were clear, participants acknowledged how food insecurity and DRM were addressed by different professionals (dietitians for DRM; social work for food insecurity), which reinforced fragmented care that could lead to poorer health outcomes. Participants strongly reported the need to address the system causes (poverty, racism, housing insecurity, etc), not just the symptoms.

Need for ethical screening

Screening for DRM and/or food insecurity without adequate access to follow-up support was reported as unethical and harmful, exacerbating the power imbalance between a patient and the screener, with the potential to leave patients feeling exposed, judged and unsupported. Participants also discussed the potential benefits when screening could be connected to some support, even if that support would not address the root cause.

Participants reported worrying that screening was futile without actionable follow-up, such as access to a social worker, food resources or support for filing taxes. Particularly for food insecurity, even if short-term interventions were provided, participants acknowledged many of these interventions would not address the underlying cause. Food charity was not enough, but clinicians still wanted to support their patients and their families.

There was debate about whether clinicians change their treatment recommendations based on food insecurity screening results. Some clinician participants reported tailoring their clinical guidance based on the patient’s reality (ie, less expensive meal options). Others acknowledged that fully addressing food insecurity was ‘too big’ for any one clinician, but clinicians could still be respectful and provide a care plan tailored to the individual’s circumstances. Some participants strongly opposed these suggestions, indicating that the clinician could not change their recommendations enough to justify the vulnerability and potential harm posed by being asked food insecurity questions.

Conducting unbiased and respectful screening

If screening can connect to support beyond food charity, participants strongly recommended that all patients should be screened for DRM and food insecurity, rather than specific populations or criteria which may perpetuate stigma and biases.

Participants acknowledged there may already be a strong power differential between patients and clinicians, which screening may exacerbate. Trauma-informed and relationship-centred approaches were recommended to mitigate these power imbalances, using wording that was neutral, non-judgemental and avoided jargon. The way questions were asked was important with some clinicians reporting changing the wording of validated screening questions to make them easier to understand. Screening questions also needed to be asked in environments that were seen as ‘safe’ (eg, ensuring children cannot hear the conversation). Clinically, screening tools for food insecurity, and sometimes DRM, were discussed as a conversation starter to help build a strong relationship; however, this made accurate data collection to help drive system change more difficult if questions were asked in difficult ways or there was inconsistent reporting.

When asked if DRM and food insecurity screening questions should be asked together, there was general agreement that combined questions could provide a more comprehensive view of patient needs. It was reported that malnutrition screening questions do not necessarily get to the root causes of malnutrition, and food insecurity questions could help identify some of those underlying factors. However, the interventions informed by the answers to screening questions may be different and the plans for what to do if someone is identified as at risk need to be carefully considered.

Where screening should occur was also discussed, recognising that support in the community was essential. Screening for both a few days before hospital discharge was also suggested as acute and chronic food insecurity could impact recovery if a patient had difficulty accessing food after discharge.

Influencing system change

Participants discussed the need for systemic change, acknowledging that both food insecurity and DRM are driven by complex social, economic and political factors. These multisectoral challenges were reported to be further disadvantaged through a siloed government structure, and the recognition that food insecurity is not purely health related. Successful influence on policy change was reported to hinge on framing issues in terms of patient benefit, economic impacts and presenting ‘actionable’ policy solutions. Policymakers were said to respond to clear, quantifiable and politically palatable data, with a need for key messages to be communicated clearly. Storytelling and strategic messaging were highlighted as ways to tell a stronger, more humanistic story of individual experiences with both food insecurity and DRM; however, there was concern that considering both together might risk oversimplification, potentially ignoring the deeper structural and/or clinical needs and thus resorting back to food charity.

Authentic community engagement and participatory research were reported to be critical for developing effective and equitable policy to address food insecurity and DRM. Genuine, non-tokenistic involvement of knowledge users, particularly individuals with lived and living experience, and individuals from marginalised groups, including Indigenous populations, was reported as essential to help ensure the right questions were asked, and relevant solutions tested. Co-design methods were encouraged to ensure alignment with community priorities, and to develop multisectoral solutions that integrate healthcare with social supports, housing, education and cultural safety, all of which are undermined by fragmented, underfunded systems and a lack of representation in policymaking.

Opportunities to support practice change through research, intervention and education

The list of research questions (online supplemental 1) provides direction for future research, with questions such as how to explore public/patient perspectives on food insecurity screening, quantitative exploration of the connection between food insecurity and DRM, and ways to improve messaging for policymakers. No one question was most relevant, feasible, high priority and equity oriented. Participatory methods were encouraged.

Participants suggested opportunities for interventions and education (table 3). While there were concerns about screening without system level changes, some local solutions were suggested outside of food charity that may provide some relevant, typically local, support. Some participants discussed implementation of medical-financial navigators in hospital and community settings to assist with tax filing and accessing disability savings plans or other benefits, especially for families with children or chronic conditions. The proposed intervention aimed to connect people with available services that may be unknown or difficult to access within the complex Canadian system.

Participants acknowledged logistic and financial barriers to intervention implementation, particularly how even if a pilot programme were beneficial, there is a lack of infrastructure to support these programmes long-term or at scale. Overly prescriptive interventions were also reported to risk limiting patient autonomy, placing too much on what people eat rather than ensuring access to food. Most interventions were said to focus on short-term solutions rather than addressing the underlying causes. Individuals advocating for system change were also reported to be frustrated by the slow progress, feeling that despite years of strong evidence, nothing changed, leading to advocacy burnout.

Alongside system change, trauma-informed cultural safety training about structural inequities was recommended by some participants who reported how some clinicians, including dietitians, felt uncomfortable and underprepared to discuss sensitive topics such as systemic racism. This discomfort was reportedly tied to the strong impact of a clinical model which focuses on treating symptoms without addressing underlying social and structural drivers. Others highlighted that many clinicians were already prepared to have these sensitive conversations but lacked awareness about what resources were available. Therefore, before widespread screening, participants suggested training about how to discuss these complex issues and increased awareness of referral pathways and resources. Participants also hoped that this training and awareness may support clinicians to become partners in advocating for systemic change.

Discussion

Critical insights were provided by participants about the interconnected nature of DRM and food insecurity, highlighting the urgent need for systemic change and policy interventions to address underlying structural inequities. Our findings align with the suggestion that although there is little evidence regarding the interface between DRM and food insecurity, all that is known about food insecurity in Canada suggests it escalates risk for DRM.27 Results also align with careful consideration about if and how screening questions are asked, as they can be accompanied by embarrassment, stigma, or a sense of guilt in taking resources from others with greater need.34

Participants identified structural inequities as the primary driver connecting DRM and food insecurity, which is consistent with research which explores each topic individually. The sociodemographic factors associated with an increased risk of household food insecurity include race, education, household structure and homeownership status.35–37 For example, rates of household food insecurity are higher in Black, Filipino and Arab households compared with white households36 37 and Indigenous households are 1.5 times more likely to experience food insecurity than non-Indigenous households.35

Results also align with repeated calls for system level changes to address food insecurity and how food charity is not enough.8 12 28 30 34 In line with the Wilson and Junger principles of screening, screening should only be conducted when there is a treatment that can be offered for the condition.38 All participants echoed this concern, with some leaning towards no screening, while others highlighted local initiatives that they felt provided enough support to justify screening. This result reflects the ongoing ethical tensions about food insecurity screening, who should conduct the screening, who should be screened and whether not asking the questions could be seen as ‘ignoring’ the situation, while acknowledging that if the questions are asked but no changes are made, this can lead to harm. In the USA in 2025, the US Preventative Services Task Force found that for primary care, current evidence for screening was not sufficient to balance the benefits and harms of screening for food insecurity.13 The position statement from Dietitians of Canada suggests it is the role for dietitians to discuss financial barriers with their clients, be aware of available resources, and provide realistic, achievable, and person-centred nutrition and health advice, but does not explicitly mention food insecurity screening.12 This lack of clear recommendations on screening contrasts with DRM screening and assessment, as nutrition risk screening is recommended using validated tools such as the Canadian Nutrition Screening Tool,15–19 21 followed by assessment and treatment.16 20–23 Some work has been conducted to add food insecurity questions into nutrition risk screening tools, however few of these tools address the underlying causes, as also discussed by participants. When deciding to screen for food insecurity and/or DRM, the ethical complexity should always be acknowledged, ensuring that if questions are asked, there is an adequate response that could benefit the individual or household being screened. A 2025 Canadian systematic review found that food voucher programmes may reduce household food insecurity, but food boxes, community gardens, school food, hunting and fishing and food charity programmes had little or no effect.39 The review also stated how comprehensive public policy addressing economic deprivation may be more likely to reduce household food insecurity than food-based interventions.39 Although there is recognition of the role of individual healthcare professionals in support DRM and food insecurity, addressing both needs system-level solutions.

To support system change, international nutrition-focused organisations are increasingly recognising the connection with food insecurity. In 2016, the United Nations General Assembly proclaimed the Decade of Action on Nutrition (2016–2025; extended to 203040), which aimed to end malnutrition in all its forms at global, regional and national levels.41 The Nutrition Decade aims for sustained political leadership internationally, with recommendations such as ‘strengthened governance and accountability for nutrition’.42 In Canada, DRM has been a priority of the Canadian Nutrition Society subgroup, the Canadian Malnutrition Task Force (CMTF), which has been involved in work to understand the prevalence of malnutrition in acute care,24 25 the development of a care pathway to support screening and treatment,16 43 as well as the launch of a national mentor-champion programme.44 To help drive this work, the Canadian Nutrition Society and the CMTF registered a commitment to the Nutrition Decade for policy approaches to address DRM called CANDReaM (Creating Alliances Nationally for policy to address Disease Related Malnutrition).27 Within CANDReaM, multinational key informant interviews regarding DRM and/or health policy were conducted.45 While these discussions focused on DRM, food insecurity was continually raised, highlighting how DRM and food insecurity would converge in the community and provided recommendations on how these concepts could be weaved together with other social determinants of health.45 These results align with the increasing focus on food insecurity from nutrition organisations, such as the 2024 position paper from Dietitians of Canada.12 Further work is needed to engage with international experience and policy recommendations to address food insecurity and DRM.

Limitations

This project focused on the experiences of community and health system decision makers, and clinicians, and does not represent the views of individuals with lived experience with food insecurity or DRM. Future work will follow-up on selected research questions, using participatory approaches which actively engage individuals with lived experience in ways that are respectful and collaborative. Although we encouraged participants to focus on the ‘disease-related’ aspect of malnutrition, participants may have been discussing malnutrition more broadly. Although participants acknowledged the need to focus on Indigenous communities, collaborative and Indigenous-led efforts are needed to further explore this topic. Our participants had varied roles, and each brought diverse experiences; however, given the vast geographic and socioeconomic range of the population, our sample is relatively small for mapping this topic widely.

Conclusions

The nuanced relationship between food insecurity and DRM is deeply intertwined through shared structural inequities. To effectively address these dual challenges, health systems must move beyond traditional food charity toward integrated, ethical screening processes that link patients to sustainable supports. Systemic change, driven by cross-sectoral research, targeted education, and policy intervention, is essential to mitigate the health risks associated with both food insecurity and DRM.
