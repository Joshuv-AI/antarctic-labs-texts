Tumor-microenvironment-on-a-chip: the construction and application
Authors: Hanzheng Xu, Jiangtao Wen, Jiahua Yang, Shufen Zhou, Yijie Li, Ke Xu, Wei Li, Sen Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrently, despite the vast amounts of time and money invested in cancer treatment, cancer remains one of the primary threats to human life. The primary factor contributing to the low treatment efficacy is cancer heterogeneity. The unclear molecular mechanisms underlying tumorigenesis, coupled with the complexity of human physiology, and the inability of animal models to accurately replicate the human tumor microenvironment, pose significant hurdles in the development of novel cancer therapies. Tumor-microenvironment-on-chip (TMOC) represents a research platform that integrates three-dimensional cell culture with microfluidic systems, simulating the essential components and physiological traits of the in vivo tumor microenvironment. It offers a dynamic setting within the chip system to study tumor progression, potentially heralding a breakthrough in cancer research. In this review, we will summarize the current advancements in this platform, encompassing various types of TMOCs and their applications in different types of cancer. From our perspective, the TMOC platform necessitates enhanced integration with tissue engineering techniques and microphysiological environments before it can evolve into a more refined preclinical model for cancer research.Keywords: Tumor microenvironment, Microfluidics, Tumor chip, Cancer modeling, In vitro models

Introduction

According to the latest report from the World Health Organization (WHO), cancer accounts for one-sixth of global deaths, and global cancer cases will increase by 60% by 2040, reaching approximately 81% in developing countries [1]. The high mortality rate of cancer indicates that it seriously threatens human life and health, so it is urgent to develop more effective therapeutic drugs [2]. In fact, in the process of clinical oncology research, only about 5% of anti-cancer drugs enter phase I clinical trials and are finally approved by the FDA [3], and more than 80% of drug candidates stopped at the development stage [4]. Clinical screening has extremely low success rates. Moreover, the total cost of a drug entering the market is estimated to be around billions of dollars, and the market risks are extremely high.

The low success rate of anticancer drugs entering clinical trials demonstrates certain predictive shortcomings of current preclinical models for disease and drug screening [5], primarily due to specific differences between experimental models and in vivo physiology and biology. Traditional preclinical models for anticancer drug screening are mainly divided into two categories: in vitro cell models (two-dimensional cell models and three-dimensional cell models) and in vivo animal models. Prior to animal testing, all test compounds should undergo culture studies using in vitro cell models [6].

Two-dimensional (2D) cell culture models are the most commonly used in vitro models, which have the advantages of simple culture procedures, low cost and can be used for high-throughput drug screening [7, 8]. However, the main limitation of 2D cell culture models is that their monolayer culture environment does not reproduce the 3D cell microenvironment, resulting in 2D cell culture models having insufficient predictive power for in vivo drugs [3]. Three-dimensional (3D) cell culture models have been improved in terms of morphology, cell behavior monitoring, response to drug stimulation and physiological research, it can more accurately reflect in vivo conditions than two-dimensional cell culture models. However, 3D cell culture technology cannot reproduce some key features of the cell microenvironment, such as tissue-tissue interfaces, concentration gradients, and certain mechanical cues [9]. As the gold standard of cancer biology, animal models are usually used for preclinical efficacy and pharmacology testing. The limitations and challenges of animal models mainly come from species-specific differences between animals and humans, which lead to differences in aspects such as physiology and cell biology of drug research [10]. This ultimately leads to inconsistencies between animal models and clinical trial results. Therefore, new technology platforms that can accurately simulate the microenvironment of human organs are urgently needed to optimize in vitro tumor models to cope with the shortcomings of traditional cell and animal models [11].

In response to these shortcomings of in vitro methods, the microfluidic platform of "Organ-on-a-chip" microfluidic platforms are gradually being revolutionized [6]. Organ-on-a-chip is a bionic microengineering device that combines microfluidics and 3D cell culture technologies, which can simulate the basic physiological characteristics and complexity of organs [12]. Through accurate microfluidic design, microfluidic devices can reconstruct the physiological dynamic characteristics of tissue in a controllable environment, such as flow pressure, shear stress, substance transport and drug action, providing a platform for the creation and simulation of cellular microenvironment [13]. Besides, Organ-on-a-chip devices have good device performance and bionic physiological microenvironment, they have the advantages of high-throughput testing, reducing the cost of in vitro experiments, animal ethical advantages and plastic repeatability [14]. It can provide more targeted research strategies for the diagnosis and treatment of patients [15]. On the other hand, With the rapid development of organ-on-a-chip technology, organ-on-a-chip technology has also been applied to the construction of in vitro models of tumors, providing a new experimental platform for tumor research [16]. Tumor-on-a-chip has become a promising platform derived from organ-on-a-chip technology with unprecedented advantages in studying cancer biology and personalized patient treatment [17]. In this review, we first present the current status and limitations of current standard preclinical models, while we highlight the development of the emerging microfluidic chip technology and its advantages for application in oncology. Then, we emphasize the applicability of tumor-on-a-chip platform for recapitulating tumor microenvironment. After that, we specify the different types of TMOC, mainly including fibroblasts-on-a-chip, immune cells-on-a-chip, endothelial cells-on-a-chip and mixed cells-on-a-chip. These TMOCs can simulate in vivo microphysiological features and the composition of relevant elements in the tumor microenvironment to better study the mechanisms of tumor development. Then, we discuss the application of TMOC in different cancers, including breast cancer, lung cancer, liver cancer, colorectal cancer, and others. Finally, we also review the current challenges and future prospects of tumor-on-a-chip. This review will further understand current advances in cancer therapy based on tumor-on-a-chip technology and its applications in cancer biology and anti-cancer therapy.

Conventional approaches and novel experimental platforms to tumor modeling

In vitro cell culture models

Before conducting in vivo animal experiments, it is imperative to utilize 2D or 3D cell culture models for initial in vitro experimental studies (Fig. 1). The 2D cell culture model remains widely employed in high-throughput drug screening owing to its low cost, ease of operation, cost-effectiveness, and high reproducibility [18]. However, its drawbacks are also substantial, particularly the absence of cell–cell and cell-extracellular matrix interactions, which translates to a lack of tissue specificity [19] and insufficient in vitro predictive capability, ultimately leading to inconsistent drug screening results when compared to in vivo experiments [20]. Furthermore, the artificial conditions of the 2D cell culture model constrain the investigation of numerous cell behaviors crucial to disease progression, such as immunosuppression and cell metastasis. These limitations have spurred the development of 3D cell culture models.

Conventional approaches and novel experimental platforms to tumor modeling

3D cell culture models is a technique to co-culture different kinds of cells with different materials with 3D structure in vitro (Fig. 1). The aim of this technology is to enable cells to migrate and grow in the three-dimensional spatial structure of the carrier, so as to form a three-dimensional cell-carrier complex [21]. Specific models are Tumor spheroids, 3D ECM models and Organoid. For example, tumor spheroids which defined as self-assembled 3D cell aggregates are largely used to study solid tumor biology since they reproduce several in vivo features of tumors [22]. They have been generated through traditional methods including hanging droplets, cubical or semi-spherical chambers, and static liquid overlays [16]. They can reproduce multiple in vivo characteristics of tumors. Spheroids are considered more reliable models for various applications, including drug screening and cancer mechanism research. 3D cell culture models visualize organized cellular structures [23] and contain different cell types can reproduce the cell-to-cell and cell-to-ECM physicochemical interactions between tumor cells and the surrounding tissue microenvironment [24]. In addition, nutrient and oxygen gradients, multicellular composition and their hierarchical structure [25] are similar to some of the characteristics of solid tumors and can provide microphysiological environments for cells to improve the accuracy of cell responses to drugs. However, 3D cell culture models are usually static systems and do not incorporate mechanical force simulations (e.g. shear stress, physical stress, tissue deformation, etc.) [26]. In addition, these models lack vascularized structures, resulting in an inability to visualize tissue-to-tissue interactions that affect tumor progression and metastasis. In summary, 3D cell culture models can better simulate tumor microenvironment (TME) than 2D models, but there are still some key important factors are not replicated. Such as physiological characteristics at the organ level: cell or tissue interfaces, mechanical flow gradients, physical shear stress and vascular perfusion. How to make up for these factors, the current application of microfluidic technology and organoid model development let us see hope.

In vivo animal models

Animal surrogates are frequently regarded as the gold standard in in vivo testing for drug development, as they can emulate the physiological complexity of human diseases at the organism level (Fig. 1) [2]. However, current animal models fall short of fully replicating the physiological conditions associated with human diseases [27] and often fail to provide accurate human drug responses at the pathophysiological level [28], leading to inadequate translation of preclinical results. This discrepancy is primarily attributed to species-specific differences between mice and humans. Moreover, developing a suitable animal model is not only expensive and time-consuming [29] but also raises ethical concerns. In fact, most animal models lack the human tissue microenvironments and a fully functional immune system [10], thereby often failing to accurately predict human drug responses [29].

To better mimic tumor growth and metastasis, replicating the heterogeneity observed in human tumors, researchers have begun xenotransplanting human tumors into the corresponding organ sites in mice [30]. One such approach involves establishing patient-derived xenograft (PDX) tumor models from primary tumor transplantations, which retain most of the characteristics of the primary tumors. These models enable disease modeling and drug screening experiments to be conducted on patient-derived tumor samples under physiological conditions [31]. PDX models exhibit good predictive clinical efficacy. However, experimental evidence suggests that PDX models may lose cancer-related genes during serial passages, resulting in genomic differences from the parental tumors [32]. Therefore, the stability of their genomes must be taken into consideration. Furthermore, PDX models take several months to generate and have low implantation success rates, introducing variability in preclinical animal protocols.18 Hence, PDX models require optimization to enhance their predictive capacity for future treatment responses in human clinical trials [33]. Cell Line-Derived Xenografts (CDX) model is a tumor model constructed by transplanting a cell line-derived allograft tumor model with tumor cell lines into nude mice or NSG mice [34]. It has a similar construction pattern to PDX, but because the tumor cell line is artificially purified cell line with multiple generations, the CDX model cannot maintain the heterogeneity of tumor tissue, and its biological characteristics and efficacy evaluation are low in clinical similarity [35]. Besides, Genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs) have contributed significantly to cancer research. In contrast to cancer cell inoculation models, GEMMs develop de novo tumors in a natural immune-proficient microenvironment [36]. However, due to the differences in mouse and human biology, gene editing may limit the complexity of genes and the long production cycle and high cost, which limits the use of GEMMs [37].

Organoid

Due to the shortcomings of these current preclinical models, there has been a great deal of interest in new preclinical experimental platforms such as organoid models. Organoid is a three-dimensional 'micro-organ model', a 3D cell culture generated from stem or organ progenitor cells [29]. At present, common organoids include retina, prostate, brain, liver, kidney, heart, blood vessels and other organoids (Fig. 1) [38–41]. These organoids encapsulate the genotypes, phenotypes, and cellular functions of their respective organs, marking a major breakthrough in biological modeling [42]. Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) are capable of encapsulating certain specific functions of organs with stable phenotypic and genetic profiles and allow for long-term in vitro culture [28]. Compared to traditional 2D cell culture models, organoid models contain multiple cell types, have tighter cell-to-cell communication links, and can better represent cell-to-cell interactions [43] and therefore can be better used to model organ histogenesis and pathophysiological states. In addition, tumor organoid derived from tumor stem cells and other cells present in TME can mimic tumor characteristics in vitro, thus revealing tumor heterogeneity. In conclusion, tumor organoid hold great promise for cancer modelling and basic clinical research. Compared to traditional models, organoid models are still in the exploratory stage, and their physiological characteristics and genetic stability are not yet clear [44]. Therefore, they have limitations in reflecting the characteristics of specific human cells. In addition, due to the immaturity of current technology, organoids are subject to cell death due to changes in their environment and limitations in oxygen and nutrition, which limits their clinical application [45].

Microfluidic chip systems

The recently developed microfluidic organ-on-a-chip (alternatively known as Organ-on-a-Chip) represents a groundbreaking experimental platform for tumor modeling [9]. It marks a significant milestone in the integration of microfluidic technology with cell biology research [46]. This technology excels at reconstructing in vivo physiological characteristics at the organ level [47]. By replicating the composition of physiological microenvironments, such as cell or tissue interfaces, mechanical flow gradients, physical shear stress, and vascular perfusion, it can recreate the structure and function of tissues and organs, which holds immense significance for studying organ physiology and pathology [48].

With the rapid advancements in microfluidic technology, researchers have increasingly integrated cancer research with this technology due to its advantages, including low cost, portability, and high sensitivity (Fig. 1) [6]. In recent years, the development of tumor-on-a-chip systems has been accelerated by constructing tumor microenvironments on microfluidic chips. Tumor-on-a-chip stands as another frontier in Organ-on-a-Chip scientific research [19]. The latest tumor-on-a-chip system has emerged as a novel tool for cancer research, capable of precisely controlling the tumor microenvironment and accurately reproducing cell complexity, tumor ecology [23], tissue specificity [18], and other related aspects within a controlled setting (Table 1).

Tumor-Microenvironment-On-A-Chip (TMOC): a suitable choice for reproducing tme

Reproducing TME

The formation and development of tumor is influenced by many factors, of which the tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a key role in influencing tumor development. TME is a complex, interacting and highly heterogeneous environment. the TME is wide-ranging and includes numerous cell types, growth factors, and cell-associated physical properties. each component of the TME has the potential to contribute to local tumor development, proliferation, metastasis and treatment resistance [51]. In the tumor microenvironment, the tumor cells themselves, other non-cancerous cells such as stromal cells [52], immune cells, endothelial cells, and the extracellular matrix surrounding these cells change and evolve with the different stages of tumor progression, allowing the tumor cell population to develop and spread.The study of the tumor microenvironment has also further confirmed that carcinogenesis develops as a result of interactions between tumor cells and the non-cellular (ECM) and cellular components of the TME [53]. In order to understand the different underlying mechanisms of tumor growth and metastasis, researchers need to comprehensively analyse the multiple exchanges between tumor cells and their adjacent microenvironment [54]. The stromal components of tumors provide their structural features, such as cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), a key stromal component contributing to tumor heterogeneity, which can generate and remodel the ECM surrounding the tumor [55] and are central players in the deposition, and modification, of the ECM environment. In addition to this, the heterogeneity of CAFs predicts poor prognosis of cancer [56]. Different types of CAFs have different functional roles in the tumor microenvironment [57], CAFs play a role in promoting angiogenesis, negative regulation of anti-tumor immunity, and tumor invasion and metastasis. It has been reported that CAFs can support the tumor growth and invasion by secreting some cytokines and growth factors, such as transforming growth factor-b (TGF-b), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), insulin-like growth factor-I (IGF-I) and thus [58].

Reproducing TME has always been an important challenge in establishing experimental cancer models. The complexity of TME hinders a comprehensive understanding of TME in traditional in vitro models. If the synergistic relationship between TME and tumor can not be solved, it will lead to inefficient treatment. In addition, the research progress of tumor and environmental biology also highlights the importance of using appropriate experimental models to accurately express tumor microenvironment [26]. Recently, the development of tissue engineering and biomanufacturing technology has been able to incorporate the relevant factors of TME into the in vitro tissue equivalent model [51], while the development of laboratory chips and microfluidic devices provides a new experimental platform for the reproduction of TME, which brings great hope for bridging the transformation gap between preclinical and clinical [59].

For the current cancer research, cancer heterogeneity has always been a major problem for researchers. The true reproduction of the dynamics of the tumor microenvironment is considered to be a key factor in evaluating the accuracy and specificity of anticancer drugs [23]. At present, the rapid development of microfluidic technology provides the possibility to reconstruct physiological dynamic characteristics in a controlled environment [47]. By combining tissue engineering techniques with microfluidic chip, the tumour structure, cell and tissue, extracellular matrix, vascular system and physical shear are partially reconstructed on microfluidic chips [9], thus enabling the reconstruction of the complexity of the tumour microenvironment by reflecting intercellular connections, structural specificity and mechanical interactions. In addition, by constructing ECM hydrogels to mimic the communication crosstalk of the ECM barrier, and steadily perfusing the medium at low flow rates in microfluidic channels, cells can be cultured dynamically for long periods of time, which is essential to accurately replicate human physiology [60]. In cancer research, the combination of microfluidic technology and cancer modeling research has also promoted the development of tumor chip system. The latest tumor chip is a kind of chip system that can reproduce the tumor microenvironment at the tissue and organ level. This chip platform can introduce the key components related to the tumor microenvironment. In addition, some physical and biochemical factors that affect tumor development can also be simulated on the chip, such as mechanical shear force, vascularized environment, biochemical oxygen gradient and niche factors (Fig. 2) [61]. The key advantage of tumor chip system in cancer research is that it can reproduce the tumor microenvironment as completely and truly as possible [62]. In a word, the tumor chip platform will provide an alternative method to summarize different aspects of tumor microenvironment for the existing experimental methods in vitro and in vivo. Relying on its accurate reproduction of tissue specificity, for the applicability of reproducing tumor microenvironment and reflecting the physiological response of anticancer drug therapy through cancer modeling, it can be used as an indispensable transformation tool from laboratory to clinic in the future [16].

Suitability of TMOC for TME reproduction

Cell–cell communication and signal transduction in TMOC

Cell–cell communication and signal transduction are key processes linking cell–cell interactions, These interactions are hijacked to promote cancer development [63]. Cells are connected by direct contact or by using ligand-receptor interactions. Cell communication consists of two fundamental processes: cell signaling for signal generation and cell-to-cell transmission, and cell signal transduction for receiving and processing signals [64]. By constructing microscale channels and chambers, TMOC technology can simulate the microenvironment of cells in the body, including physical contact between cells, chemical signal gradients, and fluid flow. Such simulations can help to more accurately investigate the mechanisms of intercellular communication and signal transduction (Fig. 2).

For example, in the breast cancer microenvironment, the relevant literature reported that the cytokines CXCL12 and epidermal growth factor (EGF) secreted by stromal cells jointly regulate the migration of breast cancer cells, which revealed the potential role of cytokines and growth factors in the regulation of tumor cell migration [65]. Moreover, some researchers have developed a microfluidics platform to culture a uniform endothelium on a porous membrane for chemical transport, and investigated how breast cancer cells adhere to the endothelium through CXCL12-CXCR 4-dependent signaling for further metastatic process, which well illustrates the signaling communication mechanism of tumor cells during metastasis [66]. Understanding the interactions between different cells and cancer cells in the tumor microenvironment is a prerequisite for successful and personalized anticancer therapy. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), Hwang HJ et al. cocultured pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma tumor spherores and pancreatic stellate cells with a 3D co-culture chip system, and found that the increased invasiveness and drug resistance were mediated by the interaction between tumor cells and stellate cells through various secreted factors (IL-6, IL-8, IGF-1, EGF, etc.) [67]. The study is also helpful in exploring cancer metastasis and in the drug evaluation of matrix-targeted drugs. Together, these chip platforms are able to simulate key features of the TME, such as the cancer metastatic cascade and cell–cell / cell-ECM interactions. By growing tumor cells and stromal cells on these platforms, researchers can observe and analyze the cell-to-cell communication and conduction mechanisms.

Suitable materials for preparing TMOC:

There are many materials used in TMOC manufacturing, including inorganic materials such as silicon and glass, polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), polyurethane methacrylate (PUMA), elastomers such as thermosetting polyester (TPE), and polycarbonate (PC), polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) and polystyrene (PS) plastics [68]. Among these materials, PDMS is widely used by virtue of its biocompatibility, oxygen permeability, transparency, flexibility, low cost and simple processing [69]. However, because PDMS is a hydrophobic polymer that also absorbs drug molecules, its use is limited in some cases [70]. Recent research trends have reported that these problems can be further overcome by using alternative polymers (e. g.non-absorptive elastomers and transparent polyester) and through 3D printing processes [71, 72].

In addition, good biocompatible materials can be used to build extracellular units (ECM), such as collagen, hyaluronic acid and other natural biomaterials, which have excellent biocompatibility and degradation, and can simulate the composition and structure of the extracellular matrix [73]. Synthetic peptides and hydrogels: such as PEG (PEG glycol) -based hydrogels, they can mimic different extracellular matrix environments by adjusting the crosslinking degree and composition [74]. However, there is still a gap between the reported hydrogel materials and the ECM in the tumor in terms of stiffness, morphology and viscoelastic energy, and we need to choose a more biomimetic ECM to construct the tumor microenvironment ECM [75]. Some hybrid materials are also often used in TMOC, combining natural ECM components with synthetic polymers to produce materials with better mechanical properties, biodegradation, and cellular interaction capabilities, such as collagen-polyethylene glycol (PEG) composites. Furthermore, the acellular matrix, derived from tissues or organs, provides an abundance of native ECM components while eliminating cellular antigens. These substrates can be used as templates or scaffold for TMOCs [76].

Therefore, the choice of materials depends on the specific study objectives, the desired TMOC properties, and the compatibility with the studied cells and molecules. By carefully selecting and combining these materials, researchers can prepare TMOC that approximate the complexity and dynamics of the native tumor microenvironment.

Different types of Tumor-Microenvironment-On-A-Chip (TMOC)

Tumor microenvironment is an extremely complex physiological environment, which is mainly composed by blood and lymph vessels, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, immune cells, cytokines, extracellular vesicles, and extracellular matrix [77]. Cancer cells can connect with their surroundings and interact with each other to lead to the further development of cancer. Therefore, understanding the interaction between the components of the tumor microring is very important for the study of cancer treatment. In recent years, microfluidic chip has been widely used in tumor research because it can accurately control and restore a variety of factors in tumor microenvironment [4] (including vascularization simulation, metastasis and invasion, cell–cell and cell-ECM interaction, etc.). In this section, based on the microfluidic chips composed of different cells, we introduce different types of TMOC (Fig. 3). They can be used to study the interactions between tumor cells and stromal cells, endothelial cells, immune cells and acellular components, so as to clarify the mechanism of further development and metastasis of cancer caused by these effects.

TMOC technique

The TMOC platform is an advanced research platform that integrates microfluidic technology and cell culture technology, aiming to simulate and study the complex microenvironment around tumors. TMOC uses a microfluidic chip to build a complex microfluidic channel network that simulate the vasculature and lymphatic system around tumors, enabling precise delivery of nutrients and efficient excretion of metabolic waste [78]. At the same time, various cell types, including tumor cells, immune cells, and endothelial cells, were cultured in the chip to simulate the cell interactions in the tumor microenvironment. In addition, to simulate the matrix components in the tumor microenvironment, TMOC provides a real growth environment for cells through incorporation into extracellular matrix scaffold structures (e. g., collagen, etc.) [79]. Combined with advanced imaging technology and data analysis methods, the researchers conducted real-time dynamic monitoring of the cell behavior and drug response in the chip, providing rich experimental data for the researchers.

As the current exploratory preclinical experimental model, its application prospects are also very broad. The TMOC platform can be used to study key processes such as cell interactions, signaling, and metabolic changes in the tumor microenvironment, and provide important clues to reveal the mechanisms of tumor development and development [80]. By simulating the tumor microenvironment, the TMOC platform can be used to evaluate the distribution, penetration, and efficacy of targeted drugs or nanoparticles in tumor tissues, accelerating the drug development process [81]. In terms of personalized medicine, with the combination of patient-derived tumor cells and immune cells, the TMOC platform can be used to evaluate patients' responses to specific treatment options and provide a scientific basis for the formulation of personalized medical programs.

Fibroblasts-on-a-chip

Different growth factors and cytokines secreted by cancer cells have been reported to induce the transformation of normal stromal cells into CAFs. CAFs as one of the most abundant non-cancer cell types in TME, they play an important role in tumor metastasis and invasion, profoundly influencing the behavior of cancer cells [82].

Guocheng et al. reported a co-culture technique to investigate the role of intercellular communication based on a microfluidic chip system that enables unidirectional communication of 3D cultured cells (Fig. 3A) [83]. In this study, the authors investigated the link between tumor cells and stromal cells by comparing the effects of two different types of breast cancer cells (invasive MDA-MB-231 and non-invasive MCF-7 cells) on stromal cells (MRC-5 fibroblasts and mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs)). It was shown that invasive MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells induced MRC-5 fibroblasts and MSC MSCs into cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) more efficiently by unidirectional stimulation. In addition, the conversion of MCR-5 fibroblasts to CAFs was blocked by injection of TGF-β1 antibody, demonstrating that TGF-β1 secreted by breast cancer cells is one of the growth factors that contribute to CAF conversion. This microfluidic chip also provides a useful tool for the study of intercellular communication.

EndMT is known to be a transitional process from an endothelial phenotype to a mesenchymal phenotype [84]. CAFs can be generated from transdifferentiated endothelial cells through endothelial-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) and increase the migration of metastatic cells [85]. The differentiation of endothelial cells into CAFs is induced by cancer cell-derived exosomes, and differentiated CAFs can promote the invasive migration of cancer cells. Kimin et al. developed a 3D microfluidic model containing differentiated CAFs to monitor the formation of cancer invasion sites through CAFs induced by exosomes isolated from three types of cancer cell lines (Melanoma, Squamous carcinoma and Breast carcinoma) (Fig. 3B) [86]. The authors found that cancer cell-derived exosome-induced CAFs (ecCAFs) were more invasive to cancer cells than CAFs induced by cancer cells(cCAFs). Analysis of the genetic data showed that all three types of CAFs differentiated from cancer cell-derived exosomes were enriched in ECM, which promotes the invasive process.

Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) play an important role in local tumor stromal remodeling, promoting tumor development, invasion, and treatment resistance. Chemokines CXCL12 secreted by CAFs have a direct role in the development of cancers such as breast cancer. Based on the application of a three-dimensional in vitro microfluidic model, Jacob C.et al. demonstrated that CXCL12 secreted by CAFs promotes tumor angiogenesis through paracrine signaling increases vascular permeability and promotes tumor angiogenesis, ultimately leads to metastasis (Fig. 3C) [87]. Furthermore, they evaluated the autocrine effect of CXCL12 on contraction and remodeling of the ECM by CAFs and suggested that CXCL12 does not control fibroblast contractility through an upstream autocrine signaling mechanism.

Immune cells-on-a-chip

Immune cells have been integrated into microfluidic chips to study the association of immune cells (T cells, NK cells, macrophages) with tumor cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME) to facilitate research advances in cancer immunotherapy. Jaehyun et al. developed a multilayered blood vessel/tumor tissue chip (MBTC) that allows for the study of T cells infiltration into tumor tissue processes (Fig. 4A) [88]. It was found that tumor cells may play an important role in regulating T cells with tumor infiltration and tumor cells can induce endothelial cell incompetence by producing vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which in turn inhibits T cell extravasation. At the same time, the authors applied anti-VEGF drugs to block VEGF secreted by tumor cells to restore EC (endothelial cells) incompetence and thus enhance T cell infiltration, which validated the clinical effectiveness of immunotherapy with anti-VEGF drugs.

Macrophages play an important role in influencing tumor development, including promoting tumor cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and other roles in shaping the tumor microenvironment. According to the polarization status of macrophages, there are two different phenotypes of macrophages [89], M1 and M2, which play different roles in the tumor microenvironment, while M1 phenotype macrophages are involved in cell killing and can limit tumor progression, and M2 macrophages promote tumor development [90]. Current studies on how macrophages affect the tumor microenvironment still require more refined in vitro models to validate. Ye et al. revealed the effects of different phenotypes of macrophages on tumor behavior by using tumor-on-a-chip (TOC) system (Fig. 4B) [91]. The authors found that M1 macrophages inhibited tumor growth and tumor-induced angiogenesis by producing CXC motif chemokines (CXCL9, CXCL10), while M2 macrophages stimulated tumor cell migration, and this stimulatory behavior was mainly mediated by the secretion of MMP7 and ANGPT2 by M2 macrophages.

The immune system has great potential for cancer therapy. Most cell-based immunotherapies rely on CD8 + T cells and NK cells with cytotoxic capabilities [92]. NK cells express the antibody receptor CD16, enabling them to recognize antibody-coated tumor cells as targets in a process known as antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxic action (ADCC) [93]. Jose M et al. proposed a microfluidic model for the study of NK cell immunotherapy and ADCC effects (Fig. 4C) [94]. Using this model, the authors compared the penetration of therapeutic antibodies and NK cells into tumor spheroids and found that therapeutic antibodies had a delayed penetration into tumor spheroids due to the tumor cell–cell barrier effect. In contrast, NK cells exhibited significant convergent migration and entered the spheroids faster than the therapeutic antibodies. Also, by studying the communication between tumor cells and NK cells, the authors found that NK cells expressed multiple receptors for different chemokines secreted by tumor spheroids (e.g. CXCL12-CXCR4, CCL28-CCR3), validating the hypothesis of the cause of NK cell convergent migration.

Endothelial cells-on-a-chip

The tumor vascular system influences the tumor microenvironment (TME) and nourishes the cancer, promoting its development and metastasis. Endothelial cells are the main types of cells commonly used to construct vascular networks. Tumor vascular networks can be constructed by co-culture of endothelial cells and tumor cells. Forming perfusable vascular network models in vitro has been a challenge to refine the construction of tumor microenvironments. Recently, perfusion organ and tumor models have been developed on microfluidic chips, which provides an in vitro model more consistent with tumor physiology for evaluating tumor cell activity and anticancer drug efficacy in tumor microenvironment [95].

Yuji et al. presented a tumor chip platform with a spheroid model containing a perfusable vascular network to assess intratumoral tumor activity (Fig. 5A) [96]. The proliferative activity of tumor cells in tumor spheroids was detected to be significantly accelerated by perfusing the engineered vascular network for more than 24 h. By comparing tumor cell activity after administration under different conditions, administration of anticancer drugs under perfusion conditions did not cause any dose-dependent reduction of tumor cells, contrary to the results observed under static conditions. The importance of the perfused vascular network for the assessment of tumor activity was further confirmed. Zhiwei et al. simulated tumor microenvironment by using perfusion-capable vascularized tumor sphere chips (Fig. 5B). They demonstrated that the PHD inhibitor DMOG prevented the degradation of normal blood vessels while enhancing the delivery and distribution of the anticancer drugs paclitaxel and cisplatin in an esophageal cancer (Eca-109) tumor sphere model [97]. It was demonstrated that DMOG could protect normal blood vessels and improve the delivery of anticancer drugs to the tumor site to enhance the efficacy of chemotherapy. Yizhong et al.demonstrated the advantages of human vascularized microtumor models in studying the effects of anticancer drugs on endothelial cells and tumor cells (Fig. 5C) [98]. As a validation, they tested fluorouracil, vincristine and sorafenib in perfusable and non-perfusable microorgan and microtumor models. The study showed that human ex vivo vascularized microtumor models are a chip platform to study the replicability of anticancer drugs compared to traditional endothelial or tumor cell monolayer cultures, confirming the advantages of perfusable vascular networks in drug safety evaluation.

Mixed cells-on-a-chip

The tumour microenvironment (TME) is a highly complex physiological microenvironment that has a critical impact on tumour progression and metastasis [99]. The interactions between tumour cells and surrounding non-tumour cells contribute to the processes of angiogenesis, invasion and metastasis and immune escape in TME. To promote the development of "precision medicine" in bladder cancer treatment, Peng fei et al. constructed a microfluidic system that can mimic the bladder cancer microenvironment (Fig. 6A) [100]. The authors used four representative cell types (stromal cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells and macrophages) as component cells of the tumour microenvironment to allow the four cell types to interact with each other while co-cultured with bladder cancer cells to simulate the bladder cancer microenvironment. The authors used the system to simulate the migration of macrophages into bladder cancer cells, thereby validating the recruitment of macrophages to the tumour site. In addition, the authors reproduced the activation process of macrophages in vivo by testing Arg-1 expression in TME. This microfluidic system provides a fundamental and informative tool for modelling TME and facilitating the development of personalised treatments for bladder cancer. Oncology drug screening has been a key step in anti-cancer drug development, and the success rate of drug development screening has been low due to the complexity of TME. In recent years, the demand for breast cancer drug screening has gradually increased. Despite there are many deficiencie in drug screening at this stage, the market development potential has been sufficient. Marie et al. designed a 3D co-culture tumor-microenvironment-on-a-chip capable of reconstructing the physiological microenvironment of breast cancer based on a microfluidic system (Fig. 6B) [101]. The authors used HER2 + breast cancer and trastuzumab (an antitumor drug targeting the HER2 receptor) as a basis to study the dynamic drug-to-cell and cell-to-cell associations in the tumor microenvironment. Trastuzumab was found to inhibit cancer cell proliferation, while also increasing cancer-immune interactions and inducing an anti-tumor ADCC immune response. In addition, the authors identified the inhibitory effect of CAFs on cancer-immune interactions and suggested that CAFs may antagonize the effect of trastuzumab and increase trastuzumab resistance. This model can be used for basic research and preclinical testing of drugs focusing on TME. Chun-Wei et al. developed an L-TumorChip system, a three-layer microfluidic chip tumor platform that integrates key components of the tumor microenvironment, including tumor vascularization and stromal cell environment (Fig. 6C) [5]. Using this platform, the authors revealed the impact of different stromal cells on cancer cell progression and real-time drug response by simulating tumor-stromal cell and tumor-endothelial cell interactions. The authors observed the dynamic response of stromal cells to doxorubicin by monitoring caspase-3 activity, mainly found that the co-culture of doxorubicin with CAFs or MSCs delays pharmacokinetics, resulting in a lower apoptotic response as reflected in caspase-3 activity. This chip system contributes to the study of drug screening and learning of pharmacokinetics (Table 2).

Applications of TMOC in different cancers

Tumor-microenvironment-on-chip (TMOC) systems which are developed on the basis of tumor structure, tumor microenvironment (TME) and tumor biology can be used to address many tumor-related questions (Table 3) [102]. For example, the study of mechanisms of tumor development through basic tumor structure modeling, screening of drug candidates through drug toxicity testing analysis, and current emerging research directions, including personalized tumor therapy and drug delivery assessment, all of these applications play an important role in the development of tumor research [103]. Although the chip system is still in its early stage, it has great potential to replace the traditional models used in tumor research and has a good prospect to deal with cancer diseases, which are a serious threat to human life and health, in the future.Next we will describe the different applications of TMOC systems in different cancer diseases.

Breast cancer

Breast cancer is known to be one of the major diseases afflicting women and one of the leading causes of death for women in the world, and it is a serious threat to women's life and health [104]. Breast cancer is highly heterogeneous and its heterogeneity is the result of interactions between tumor cells and associated stromal cells, extracellular matrix (ECM) and biochemical mechanical cues [105]. Among them, CAF and ECM play a key role. CAF can drive ECM remodeling by various interactions between CAF themselves with the extracellular matrix (ECM) and signaling molecules. The ECM provides structural support and functional properties to the cellular system. Also, an increased deposition of different tpyes of collagens will lead to a stiffer ECM and tumorigenic environment during breast tumor formation [104].

To investigate the effects of ECM components (fibroblasts and ECM proteins) on tumor cells in breast cancer, Lugo-Cintrón et al. constructed a 3D microfluidic model to simulate tumor cell invasion [106]. The team found that cancer cells migrated significantly more in the fibronectin-rich stroma than in the normal collagen stroma, regardless of the type of fibroblasts co-cultured (human mammary fibroblasts (HMF) and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF)). At the same time, in co-culture with HMF, more MMP was secreted in the fibronectin-rich matrix than in the normal collagen matrix. In contrast, the substrate composition did not significantly alter MMP secretion when co-cultured with CAF. In addition, the use of MMP inhibitors was found to be effective in inhibiting the migration of cancer cells in the collagen matrix, which was not effective in the fibronectin-rich matrix. In conclusion, this model can be used to study the role of stromal cells and extracellular matrix (ECM) in the tumor microenvironment in influencing cancer cell migration. Truong et al. developed a microfluidic tumor model device to study the invasive mechanism of tumor-stromal interactions on breast cancer cells [107]. By studying the interaction between fibroblasts (CAF and NF) of different phenotypes from patients and invasive SUM-159 breast cancer cells, it was found that NF and CAF exhibited different effects, NF showing tumor suppressive behavior and CAF showing tumor promoting behavior. In addition, through transcriptome analysis of SUM-159 breast cancer cells, the team identified a gene(GPNMB) that promotes breast cancer invasion, which is induced by CAF, and the pro-invasive effect of CAF can be attenuated by knocking down GPNMB. In conclusion, this microfluidic model can be used to study the molecular pathways of tumor-stromal interactions and facilitate the study of invasive mechanisms of breast cancer cells.

Lung cancer

Statistics show that about 350 people die every day from lung cancer, almost 2.5 times the number of deaths from the second leading cause of cancer death (colorectal cancer) [108]. Lung cancer can be broadly classified into small cell lung cancer (SCLC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). In recent years, as the rapid development of tumor chip system for lung cancer research, compared with traditional in vitro cell culture, tumor on-chip system can be prepared to simulate the physiological microenvironment of the lung, with its ability to cover different cell types and ECM (extracellular matrix), and can simulate the physical and chemical properties of the microenvironment such as mechanical pressure, soluble factors and oxygen gradient [109]. The tumor-on-chip system has been widely used in the physiology and disease related to lung cancer [110, 111]. A three-dimensional vascularized lung cancer chip (VLCC) was designed by Sangun et al. [112] The team used decellularized lung extracellular matrix (ldECM) to develop this chip system. ldECM not only has an extracellular matrix (ECM) component, but also can induce blood vessel formation, allowing the structural structure of blood vessels to be incorporated into the chip system. Thus, mechanical and biochemical characteristics and vascular features in the tumor microenvironment are presented, and solid lung cancer is successfully simulated. In addition, the team performed drug screening tests using Dox and showed that VLCC is more sensitive to dose-dependent drug effects than traditional 2D culture systems.The VLCC also has advantages over traditional 2D culture systems in terms of drug efficacy and mechanistic analysis. Bryan A. et al. designed a microfluidic lung cancer chip system that can summarize in situ lung cancer growth, treatment response and tumor dormancy [113]. The team demonstrated that this chip system can accurately mimic lung cancer-specific growth patterns, cytokine secretion patterns, and allow analysis of molecular-level regulation of drug action by the lung cancer microenvironment, which is highly advantageous for revealing lung cancer cell growth, invasion mechanisms, and drug treatment responses in the tumor microenvironment. These cancer chip models can help to discover new drug targets as well as cancer therapeutic approaches.

Statistically, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for 80–85% of all lung cancer types. For advanced NSCLC patients with positive EGFR gene drive, epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs) are currently the preferred therapeutic agents [114]. However, due to individual differences in lung cancer patients, the efficacy of different targeted drugs is also different. Therefore, in order to improve the efficacy of EGFR-targeted drugs, preclinical drug screening is of great significance for drug efficacy evaluation. Jianfeng et al. prepared a 3D microfluidic lung chip model and used two-dimensional (2D) well plates and 3D lung chips to evaluate the effects of different EGFR-targeting drugs (gefitinib, afatinib and axitinib) on lung cancer cells [115]. It was found that oseltinib was more effective in targeting than afatinib and gefitinib at the same concentrations. Besides, 3D lung chip is better than 2D well plates in evaluating the efficacy of drugs, and the results are closer to the clinical data. Because for primary tumor cells, 3D lung chip has the advantage of accurately simulating physiological microenvironment. The microfluidic lung chip model prepared by the team is important for personalized lung cancer treatment and drug efficacy assessment.

Colorectal cancer

Colorectal Cancer (CRC) is one of the deadliest cancers worldwide, and the survival rate for patients with metastatic colorectal cancer is extremely low, less than 15% [116]. Metastasis and progression of colorectal cancer (CRC) is a complex process in which various types of cells from the primary tumor metastasize by invading surrounding tissues and infiltrating blood vessels, exuding into other organs, and growing into secondary metastatic tumors [117]. To explore all aspects of colorectal cancer metastasis and progression, microfluidic tumor chip system provides a good platform support. They can not only simulate the tumor microenvironment, but also establish a relationship with patients with the chip. Carly et al. developed a colorectal cancer chip model that mimics the colorectal structure and tumor microenvironment (TME) [118]. By simulating the physical mechanical force intestinal peristalsis, the team found that HCT116 (colorectal cancer cells) invasion was significantly increased under peristaltic conditions compared to static conditions. In addition, combining the effects of different cellular interactions on HCT116, it was found that HUVEC (human umbilical vein endothelial cells) had a driver-promoting effect on HCT116 invasion. The effect of cytokines secreted by CAF (cancer-associated fibroblasts) on HCT116 was also analyzed by using conditioned medium (CM) from CAF lines of different patient origins. They found that the cytokines secreted by CAF significantly promoted the invasion of HCT116. This chip well simulated the early metastasis of colorectal cancer as well as explored the mechanism of metastasis. In addition, Louis Jun Ye Ong et al. introduced PDX-derived 3D tumor spheroids into an integrated microfluidic device to construct a multiple tumor organ-on-a-chip device [119]. The team tested the therapeutic response of five different standard of care (SOC) chemotherapeutic agents by using the chip device on tumor spheroids derived from three colorectal cancer (CRC) patients. The results showed that the therapeutic outcomes tested from the chip device closely are correlated with the matched PDX model. This demonstrates that the chip device can be used to predict the response to drug therapy performed by the PDX model and may reduce the reliance on preclinical animal models in the future.

Given the lack of therapeutic efficacy in colorectal cancer (CRC) and the current need to improve the delivery efficiency of anticancer drugs, Carvalho et al. developed a 3D microfluidic chip model that can simulate the colorectal tumor microenvironment and evaluate the dynamic delivery efficiency of anticancer drug nanoparticles [120]. Using fluorescent labeling technology, they enabled GEM (gemcitabine) drug-loaded nanoparticles to act as a real-time tracking tool to obtain in vivo positional imaging. They found that GEM could be efficiently released from the nanoparticles and taken up by cancer cells while retaining the active anticancer drug in soluble form. Also, the chip model is able to analyze the expression of relevant genes, such as Casp-3, Ki-67, and MMP-1, which can assess the effects of cytotoxic compounds. In conclusion, this tumor chip model represents the direction of development for precise nanotherapy.

Liver cancer

Liver cancer is a common cause of death from malignant tumours worldwide [121], and its high clinical mortality rate is a serious threat to human life and health. The main reason for the high failure rate of current clinical trials is that traditional cell culture models as well as animal models cannot accurately mimic the tumor microenvironment of liver cancer [122], and the prediction of safety and efficacy in clinical studies is inadequate. Besides, the screening of drug candidates unable to advance rapidly because the toxicity testing of drug compounds cannot be accurately predicted [123]. Therefore, we need to establish reliable models in vitro to compensate for the shortcomings of traditional deficiencies of models and facilitate clinical treatment and drug candidate development for liver cancer. In recent years, organ-on-a-chip models have been used as a platform for drug candidate screening due to the ability to mimic drug-induced toxicity in vivo. David et al. developed a 3D multi-organ in vitro chip model, in which the team co-cultured bronchial epithelial cells with liver spheroids to mimic the physiological environment of the lung and liver [124]. They demonstrated the ability of the model to modulate drug toxicity using aflatoxin B1 (AFB 1), which can accurately test the toxicity of drugs as well as the potential toxicity of their metabolites, this chip model provides a new platform for screening drug candidates. In addition, in order to better simulate the tumor microenvironment (TME), Siming et al. integrated two components of decellularized liver matrix (DLM) and gelatin methacrylate (GelMA) to develop a three-dimensional liver tumor chip [125]. The chip not only mimics some of the key mechanical properties of the ECM, but also contains relevant growth factors and biophysical cues. In addition, the team also used this tumour chip for drug toxicity assessment which showed a dose-dependent response to the toxicity of acetaminophen and sorafenib. The present toxicity data is more closely mimicked the condition in vivo that provides a more accurate assessment of drug toxicity. Thus, drug toxicity can be assessed more accurately by improving the fidelity of ECM simulations, and this chip model also provides a new platform for screening drug candidates.

Other cancers

Among other cancer diseases, pancreatic cancer is also of great concern. Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) accounts for 90% of pancreatic cancers and is considered to be the most lethal of all cancers [126]. The overall patient population with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) shows a high degree of tumor heterogeneity, which makes clinical treatment of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma complex and unpredictable, resulting in low success rates in clinical trials. Recently, Haque et al. developed a tumor chip device by integrating PDO (patient-derived organoid) and stromal cells on a microfluidic device [127]. The authors used the device to simulate the tumor microenvironment of PDAC, while significantly increasing the chemotherapeutic effect of anticancer drugs by targeting stromal cells, confirming the deteriorating effect of stromal cells on PDAC and further validating that this tumor chip device can be used to test the efficacy of anti-cancer drugs.

In addition, gastric cancer (GC) is a common malignant tumour with a high mortality rate worldwide [128]. Gastric cancer can be classified into intestinal and diffuse types based on histomorphological features, and different therapeutic strategies are required during treatment based on different pathological features. Jang et al. developed a microfluidic three-dimensional model of gastric cancer and assessed the interaction and drug resistance between gastric cancer cells and ECM by using diffuse and intestinal types of gastric cancer cell lines [129]. The study revealed that epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and pro-metastasis genes were upregulated in this gastric cancer model, while drug resistance-associated molecules were also significantly upregulated. The results confirmed that the chemosensitivity of the anticancer drug 5-fluorouracil may be associated with pro-metastasis and drug resistance-associated genes in gastric cancer. This microfluidic gastric cancer model provides a platform for studying gastric cancer development and advancing therapeutic target development.

In recent years, pericyte therapy (ACT) has begun to be attempted as an immunotherapeutic approach for melanoma, but the role of NK-92 lymphocytes in the tumor microenvironment and the causal mechanisms affected are currently unknown to researchers. Laia et al. designed a melanoma chip model to assess the important role of fibroblasts in lymphocyte resistance to tumor [130]. The authors co-cultured melanoma spheroids with fibroblasts and tested the migration of NK-92 cells to the tumor region in the chip model. It was shown that fibroblasts inhibited the recruitment of NK-92 cells to melanoma spheroids and reduced the cytotoxicity of NK-92 cells, which further validated that the effect of fibroblasts on NK-92 cells inhibited the efficacy of ACT. This chip model also provides a practical platform to study the specific mechanisms of ACT in solid tumors (Fig. 7).

Applications of TMOC in different cancers

Concluding remarks

In our article, we describe the development of traditional methods and novel experimental platforms for tumour modelling in recent years, and we highlight the suitability of TMOC for TME reproduction. Furthermore, we discuss cell–cell communication and signal transduction within TMOC systems, as well as suitable materials for their preparation. We review the advancements in TMOC over the past few years, where numerous studies have facilitated the visualization of tumor development in TME by replicating the primary components of the tumor microenvironment on these chips. TMOC integrate tumor cells with various other cell types, including fibroblasts, immune cells, endothelial cells, and mixed cellular components, thereby providing us with deeper insights into the interplay between tumor cells and their surrounding environment. Additionally, we outline the applications of TMOC technology across different cancer types. Cancer model construction, tumor development mechanism research, drug screening, drug toxicity analysis, and personalized treatment all stand to benefit from this technology, offering potential advancements in future cancer treatment.

Regarding the study of TMOC, we emphasize its ability to simulate pathophysiology and intercellular communication by constructing a biological microenvironment akin to that found in vivo. This enables researchers to utilize this technology platform to simulate and investigate key features of the tumor microenvironment [3]. Furthermore, when combined with the flexibility of microfluidics, TMOC can be tailored to in vitro environmental conditions, making it more compatible with applications in anticancer drug development and tumor modeling. Thus far, TMOC platforms exhibit great potential for accurately simulating organs and tumor microenvironments in vitro [15]. In the future, these platforms could serve as viable alternatives to traditional preclinical models, significantly aiding drug discovery and preclinical research.

With the continuous advancement of preclinical research, TMOC systems based on microfluidic technology are progressively evolving and gaining prominence. Compared to traditional preclinical models, TMOC offers unique advantages, such as synthetic biology at the cellular, tissue, and organ levels, providing physiological environment components like mechanical flow gradients, physical shear stress, and vascular perfusion. Portability and high sensitivity are also among its strengths. However, to eventually replace traditional animal models, the new preclinical model of TMOC must still overcome numerous limitations and challenges [16]. Currently, the future development of TMOC faces hurdles related to cell sourcing, microenvironment composition, chip material, and design. Firstly, human solid tumors exhibit heterogeneity. Compared to immortalized cell lines, primary cells derived from patients' tumor tissues are a superior choice for reproducing tumor heterogeneity. However, ethical constraints and difficulties with in vitro proliferation limit the use of primary cells. Secondly, due to the lack of engineering technology and the heterogeneous nature of the tumor environment, there are still limitations in reproducing TME on a chip. Additionally, given the dynamic changes in human physiology, the design requirements for TME in chip devices have become significantly more demanding. In microfluidic devices, the multi-culture system used to simulate TME must provide the necessary living environment for cells, including nutrients, oxygen levels, medium pH, temperature, and other conditions. Moreover, setting the acellular environment poses challenges, such as determining the extracellular matrix (ECM) composition, balancing flow velocities, and managing shear stress. Another pivotal issue is the design and materials used in TMOC. Currently, PDMS is the primary material for chip manufacturing, with its biocompatibility, permeability, and cost-effectiveness as its strengths. However, its tendency to adsorb hydrophobic molecules may impact the pharmacological activity of drugs, thereby limiting its widespread application. It has been reported that copolymer or biomaterial 3D bio-printing can mitigate this limitation of PDMS, and future explorations may uncover new biocompatible materials.

In conclusion, despite the limitations and challenges in the development of the TMOC platform, it still holds the potential to serve as a platform for personalized tumor therapy, advancing cancer treatment. With the emergence of new technology, the latest trend in TMOC research aims to establish the construction of tumor microenvironments on a more comprehensive, multi-organ level. To achieve these objectives, we require the collaboration of various disciplines, such as Organoids and Chip technology, to accelerate the development and application of the tumor chip platform. Additionally, the tumor chip platform can provide a more precise detection platform for antineoplastic drug research and development, potentially replacing current preclinical test models and reducing the need for animal and clinical research.
