Integrating multi-omics and clinical management in breast cancer: a comprehensive review
Authors: Xiangxiang Cao, Yunchang Yang, Pengbo Wang, Jinjin Wang, Yike Zhao, Zichun Zhang, Jiaqi Jiang, Hanwen Zhang, Xinyao Kang, Huigen Feng, Guojie Ji, Huanhuan Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnlike conventional reviews that often treat molecular layers as isolated domains, this review uniquely integrates genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data to bridge the gap between advanced tumor microenvironment (TME) profiling and actionable clinical management. Its objective is to provide a definitive translational roadmap that decodes TME reprogramming, surmounts therapeutic resistance, and optimizes personalized oncology. We dissect the multifaceted mechanisms driving therapeutic resistance and highlight the paradigm-shifting role of liquid biopsy (ctDNA, CTCs, and exosomes) in dynamically monitoring minimal residual disease (MRD).Keywords: Breast cancer, Multi-Omics, Molecular mechanisms, Precision therapies, Tumor microenvironment

Background

Breast cancer remains a formidable global health challenge, accounting for 11.6% of all cancer incidences and 6.9% of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. Its profound biological complexity is driven by molecular heterogeneity, traditionally categorized by receptor expression into Luminal, HER2-overexpressing, and triple-negative (TNBC) subtypes [2]. Malignant transformation and metastasis—the cause of over 90% of breast cancer fatalities—are primarily fueled by abnormal signaling cascades (e.g., hyperactive PI3K/AKT/mTOR and MAPK pathways) [3–5] and dynamic tumor microenvironment (TME) remodeling [6].

While precision medicine has revolutionized clinical management, adaptive therapeutic resistance remains a critical bottleneck. For instance, although novel targeted agents (e.g., palbociclib, olaparib) and endocrine therapies have significantly expanded therapeutic options [10–12], up to 30% of early-stage patients ultimately progress to fatal bone or visceral metastases [13]. Furthermore, nearly 30% of HR+ patients develop acquired resistance driven by ESR1 mutations or compensatory pathway activation [5–9]. Overcoming these obstacles requires detecting minimal residual disease (MRD) prior to macroscopic relapse, an area where emerging diagnostic paradigms like liquid biopsy and AI-enhanced imaging show immense clinical promise [14, 15].

However, existing literature often treats molecular mechanisms and clinical diagnostics as isolated silos. To bridge this translational gap, this review uniquely integrates multi-omics data—encompassing genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics. By synthesizing how proteomic validations of genomic alterations and real-time metabolomic profiling directly inform clinical decision-making, we provide a definitive, highly translational roadmap for decoding TME reprogramming, overcoming therapeutic resistance, and optimizing personalized oncology.We specifically focus on HR+/HER2 − and triple-negative breast cancers, where multi-omics integration has yielded the most actionable clinical breakthroughs.

In particular, three aspects set this work apart. First, we emphasize proteomic validation of genomic alterations, demonstrating how static mutations translate into druggable protein activities. Second, we incorporate real-time metabolomic profiling as a functional readout for therapeutic response that precedes radiographic changes. Third, we provide an integrated analysis of liquid biopsy biomarkers (ctDNA, CTCs, exosomes), showing how each modality compensates for the blind spots of the others. Together, this cross-layer synthesis captures a translational perspective that is often lost when these domains are reviewed in isolation.

The clinical impact of such integration is already emerging. For instance, the I-PREDICT trial demonstrated that multi-omic-informed molecular tumor boards can match patients to individualized combination therapies, significantly improving progression-free survival. Similarly, the SAFIR02-BREAST trial used genomic and transcriptomic profiling to guide targeted therapy in metastatic breast cancer, illustrating real-world clinical utility [219, 220]. These examples underscore the central premise of this review: that multi-omics integration is not merely a research aspiration but an increasingly actionable clinical tool.

Molecular classification in the multi-omics era

Traditionally, breast cancer classification relied heavily on morphological features, categorizing tumors into in situ lesions (e.g., ductal carcinoma in situ [DCIS]) and invasive carcinomas (e.g., invasive ductal and lobular carcinomas) [16–37]. Historically, pathologists also cataloged rare histological variants such as mucinous, medullary, and inflammatory breast cancers [38–50]. However, from a precision oncology perspective, morphological descriptions offer limited actionable insight.

Today, the paradigm has shifted toward molecular and multi-omic stratification. Breast cancer is dynamically classified by the multi-omic profiling of hormone receptors (HR) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), alongside transcriptomic signatures (e.g., PAM50), into Luminal A, Luminal B, HER2-enriched, and Basal-like (frequently overlapping with TNBC) subtypes. This molecular framework, continually refined by integrating genomic mutations, transcriptomic reprogramming, and proteomic signaling networks, is the current cornerstone guiding targeted therapeutic interventions and predicting treatment resistance (Table 1).

Pathogenesis of breast cancer: multidimensional molecular networks and clinical translation

The pathogenesis of breast cancer involves complex dysregulation across multiple levels of molecular regulation, including genetic alterations, epigenetic aberrations, dysregulated signaling pathways, metabolic reprogramming, and interactions within the tumor microenvironment. These mechanisms intertwine to drive tumor initiation, progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance. The following section systematically elucidates the core pathogenic mechanisms of breast cancer, bridging molecular insights with clinical translation.

Multi-omic profiling of oncogenic signaling pathways

DNA damage repair (DDR) pathways and genomic instability

BRCA1/2 Mutations: BRCA1 and BRCA2 are critical components of the homologous recombination repair (HRR) pathway for DNA double-strand break repair [51]. Beyond HRR, BRCA1/2 are involved in cell cycle checkpoint regulation [52, 53]. Mutational analyses reveal that BRCA1 also participates in transcription-coupled base excision repair of oxidative DNA damage [54]. Recent studies demonstrate that BRCA1 interacts with multiple proteins involved in DNA end-joining, mismatch repair, replication, and DNA damage signaling, underscoring its broad role in the DNA damage response network. Loss-of-function mutations in BRCA1/2 impair HRR, leading to genomic instability and a markedly elevated risk of breast cancer (Fig. 1) [55].

BRCA1/2 germline mutations and their role in the DNA damage response. Pathogenic variants in these pathways lead to homologous recombination deficiency (HRD), providing the critical clinical rationale for synthetic lethality via PARP inhibitors

Women harboring BRCA1 mutations face a lifetime breast cancer risk of up to 72%, predominantly developing TNBC. HRR deficiency renders BRCA1/2-mutant tumors sensitive to DNA-damaging agents (e.g., anthracyclines, platinum compounds) and novel therapeutics targeting DNA repair, such as PARP inhibitors (PARPi). PARPi act by blocking single-strand break repair and trapping PARP at DNA damage sites, exploiting synthetic lethality to selectively kill BRCA-deficient tumor cells [56–59]. For instance, the OlympiAD trial reported a median progression-free survival (PFS) of 19.3 months with olaparib, significantly surpassing 17.1 months in the chemotherapy group [60].

TP53 Mutations: TP53 is the most frequently mutated tumor suppressor gene in breast cancer, with mutations detected in approximately 30% of cases and over 80% of basal-like subtypes [61]. TP53 mutations disrupt the transcriptional regulation of genes involved in DNA repair, cell cycle arrest, chromatin remodeling, and apoptosis. Mutant p53 proteins not only lose tumor-suppressive functions but may gain oncogenic properties, such as enhanced proliferation and migration [61, 62]. TP53 inactivation fosters unchecked cell cycle re-entry, suppresses geroconversion (a senescence-like G1 arrest), and inhibits assembly of the DREAM complex (DP, RB-like, E2F, and MuvB), thereby evading cell cycle control. Notably, targeted therapies like APR-246 are under clinical evaluation for restoring wild-type p53 function and inducing apoptosis in p53-mutant tumors [63, 64].

Moderate-penetrance genes

Pathogenic germline mutations in genes such as PALB2, CHEK2, and ATM confer a 2–4-fold increased risk of breast cancer. PALB2 mutations, in particular, disrupt HRR via defective BRCA2 interaction, and are significantly associated with TNBC and familial breast cancer [65].

Somatic driver mutations

The PI3K/AKT/mTOR Signaling Axis: Beyond mere genomic sequencing, parallel phosphoproteomic profiling is crucial for validating the hyperactivation of downstream effectors in the PI3K/AKT/mTOR cascade. This multi-omic integration bridges the gap between static genetic alterations and dynamic, actionable protein targets in the clinic. PIK3CA mutations are present in 30%–40% of breast cancers, leading to constitutive activation of the PI3Kα isoform (p110α), which promotes cell growth, survival, metabolism, and migration via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. The most frequent mutations occur at three hotspots: p.E542K and p.E545K (helical domain) in exon 10, and p.H1047R (kinase domain) in exon 21. The SOLAR-1 trial demonstrated that alpelisib (a PI3K inhibitor) combined with fulvestrant significantly prolonged PFS in patients with PIK3CA mutations by 7.9 months [66–69].

ESR1 Mutations: First identified in 1997, ESR1 mutations gained clinical attention following genomic sequencing of metastatic breast cancer (MBC) in 2013 [70, 71]. Given the prevalence of ER+ breast cancer, endocrine therapy (ET) targeting ER significantly reduces mortality. However, ET resistance remains a major clinical hurdle. ESR1 mutations contribute to resistance through ER downregulation, crosstalk with growth factor receptors, subclonal genomic alterations, and gain-of-function mutations [72, 73]. ESR1 mutations are detected in 20%–40% of MBC patients previously treated with aromatase inhibitors (AIs), and mutation frequency varies by metastatic site, with higher rates in visceral compared to bone metastases (Fig. 2) [74–76].

HER2 Heterogeneity and Proteomic Mapping: Recent multi-omic integration, particularly combining spatial transcriptomics with mass spectrometry-based proteomics, has revealed significant intratumoral HER2 heterogeneity [207]. This transcriptomic-proteomic discordance critically influences targeted therapy efficacy and drives clonal selection during treatment. Approximately 20% of breast cancers exhibit HER2 overexpression as determined by immunohistochemistry [77]. HER2, a member of the ERBB receptor family, promotes tumor cell proliferation and metastasis through aberrant signaling activation [78, 79].

Recent advancements in mass spectrometry-based spatial proteomics have mapped the intricate protein-protein interaction networks driving targeted therapy resistance. For example, quantitative proteomic assessments have successfully stratified ‘HER2-low’ cohorts, revealing that even low levels of targetable receptor protein expression can yield significant clinical responses to next-generation ADCs, thereby expanding the therapeutic window for previously ineligible patients [207, 215, 216].

This proteomic refinement directly translates into clinical practice: patients with tumors reclassified as HER2-low can now receive trastuzumab deruxtecan, which demonstrated a 50% reduction in the risk of disease progression or death in the DESTINY-Breast04 trial [175].

Epigenetic dysregulation

Aberrant DNA methylation

DNA methylation is mediated by DNA methyltransferases (DNMT1, DNMT3A, DNMT3B), adding methyl groups to cytosine residues without altering DNA sequence [80]. Abnormal methylation patterns in breast cancer include global hypomethylation of repetitive elements, hypermethylation of CpG island promoters in tumor suppressor genes (TSGs), and gene-specific hypomethylation [81]. TSG hypermethylation facilitates unchecked proliferation and disease progression [82–84]. Notably, around 50% of breast cancers also exhibit hypomethylation, driven by either active demethylation of oncogenes or passive loss of methylation maintenance, resulting in oncogene activation [85, 86] (Fig. 3).

Hypermethylation in breast cancer: Elevated DNA methylation at oncogenic loci (e.g., ESR1) drives uncontrolled proliferation. Histone modification dysregulation

Post-translational histone modifications—methylation, acetylation, ubiquitination, sumoylation, and phosphorylation—regulate chromatin structure and gene expression [87]. Loss of H3K27me3, for example, is linked to EZH2 overexpression, which silences differentiation genes (e.g., CDH1) via the Polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2), maintaining cancer stem cell phenotypes [88, 89].

Non-coding RNA regulation

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are short (18–26 nucleotides), evolutionarily conserved non-coding RNAs that regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally. They act as oncogenes or tumor suppressors in cancer development. miR-21, one of the most commonly dysregulated miRNAs in breast cancer, promotes tumorigenesis by targeting genes involved in proliferation, angiogenesis, and invasion [90–95]. Beyond miRNAs, the complex regulatory frameworks of breast cancer are deeply modulated by other non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), particularly long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and circular RNAs (circRNAs). Recent comprehensive analyses highlight that specific lncRNAs, such as HOTAIR, MALAT1, and NEAT1, play pivotal roles in regulating cell proliferation, apoptosis, and therapeutic resistance. Furthermore, dysregulation of these ncRNAs is intrinsically linked to the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). By managing the intricate molecular pathways of EMT at various levels, EMT-associated ncRNAs not only drive the metastasis of initial tumors to remote organs but also hold significant promise as early-stage diagnostic biomarkers. Despite their undeniable vast functions in cancer progression, translating these ncRNAs into clinical therapy remains challenging, partly due to the absence of sufficient validations in advanced in vivo mouse models [96, 97].

Dysregulation of signaling pathways

Hormone receptor signaling reprogramming

ER signaling is a primary driver in most breast cancers and serves as a key target for endocrine therapy [98]. ER, a ligand-dependent transcription factor, modulates the expression of factors such as GRHL2 and DCL1, influencing tumorigenesis [99]. Aberrant activation of FOXA1, a pioneer factor in ER-positive cancers, also contributes to cell proliferation and differentiation, although its downstream mediators remain poorly defined [100].

Growth factor receptor pathway alterations

Fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) signaling plays essential roles in mammary gland development, tissue homeostasis, tumorigenesis, and metastasis. Dysregulation of FGFRs, their ligands, and co-factors is associated with therapy resistance. As a subclass of receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs), FGFRs—along with IGFR and VEGFR—activate downstream signaling cascades (e.g., RAS-MAPK) upon ligand binding, contributing to resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors [101–104] (Fig. 4).

Metabolic reprogramming and tumor microenvironment (TME) interactions

Aberrant glycolysis (Warburg effect)

The Warburg effect describes a metabolic hallmark of cancer, wherein proliferating cells convert glucose to lactate even under aerobic conditions [105, 106]. This process involves glucose uptake via GLUT transporters, glycolytic conversion to pyruvate, and subsequent lactate formation by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), with export via monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs) [107]. Hexokinase (HK), especially HK2, is a key glycolytic enzyme highly expressed in tumor cells, promoting lactate accumulation and acidification of the microenvironment, thereby suppressing antitumor immunity [108, 109] (Fig. 5).

Aberrant glycolysis and mitochondrial metabolism in cancer cells. The resulting lactate accumulation and microenvironmental acidification actively suppress antitumor immunity, presenting novel targets for precision metabolic interventions. Lipid metabolism rewiring

Recent research has linked dysregulated lipid metabolism—including fatty acid synthesis, elongation, desaturation, and oxidation—to cancer progression. Key enzymes such as ATP citrate lyase (ACLY), acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC), fatty acid synthase (FASN), carnitine palmitoyltransferase (CPT), and various acyl-CoA dehydrogenases (e.g., ACADS, ACADM, ACADL, ACADVL) are upregulated in tumors. Notably, CPT1B, the rate-limiting enzyme of fatty acid β-oxidation (FAO), contributes to chemoresistance via the STAT3–CPT1B–FAO axis, making lipid metabolism enzymes potential therapeutic targets [110–114].

Tumor microenvironment (TME) regulation

The TME comprises cancer cells, immune cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and extracellular matrix components. c-MYC serves as a central regulator by modulating angiogenesis and cell proliferation through TME interactions [115]. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) promote immune suppression by secreting TGF-β to recruit regulatory T cells (Tregs) and upregulate PD-L1, thereby enhancing tumor progression [116, 117]. Notably, immune cell heterogeneity plays a pivotal role in dictating therapeutic responses. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) frequently polarize towards an immunosuppressive M2-like phenotype, secreting IL-10 and TGF-β to orchestrate an immuno-cold niche [210]. Concurrent single-cell analyses reveal diverse subpopulations of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), particularly inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs), which structurally deposit dense collagen matrices that impede drug penetration and immune cell infiltration [211]. Advancements in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics have profoundly decoded this TME complexity. These high-resolution tools reveal how cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and macrophages physically remodel the matrix and secrete cytokines, actively exhausting CD8 + T cells and building formidable barriers against emerging immunotherapies [206].

These high-resolution insights are directly translatable to clinical decisions. For example, the presence of tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) within the TME has been correlated with improved responses to immunotherapy in TNBC, guiding patient stratification. Furthermore, therapeutic strategies targeting FAP+ immunosuppressive CAFs are now entering clinical settings, and the polarization status of TAMs is being used to select candidates for CSF1R inhibitor combinations [221, 222]. These examples illustrate how TME profiling is moving from descriptive biology to actionable oncology.

Furthermore, dynamic lipidomic profiling has demonstrated that overactivation of fatty acid oxidation (FAO) heavily correlates with immune-cold phenotypes. Real-time metabolomic tracking of circulating oncometabolites in patient serum can now predict resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors weeks before radiological progression, offering a highly actionable therapeutic window [217, 218].

3.5 Bridging molecular pathogenesis and diagnostics

Understanding these intricate multi-omic networks—from genomic instability to metabolic reprogramming—lays the crucial foundation for modern clinical management. To effectively target these specific molecular aberrations, oncologists require highly sensitive, real-time diagnostic modalities. The following section explores how traditional imaging is evolving into multi-omic molecular diagnostics to capture this complex tumor biology.

Diagnostic approaches for breast cancer

Multimodal imaging in breast cancer diagnostics

While imaging is pivotal for morphological evaluation and lesion localization, optimizing modality selection requires balancing diagnostic yield against risks like radiation-induced DNA damage, a notable concern even with the low-dose X-rays of conventional mammography (MG) [120]. To enhance accessibility and mitigate costs without sacrificing image detail, synthesized mammography (SM) has emerged as a robust alternative to digital mammography (DM) [119], though MG accuracy remains inherently limited by individual breast density [118]. To overcome such anatomical limitations, the clinical focus has decisively shifted toward multimodal integration. In ultrasonography (US), combining conventional assessments with contrast-enhanced US (CEUS) significantly clarifies intralesional vascularity [121, 122]. Furthermore, quantitative perfusion analysis via CEUS effectively characterizes solid cystic lesions [123], and its multimodal integration drastically reduces diagnostic uncertainty for complex, non-mass abnormalities [124].Beyond localized morphological assessments, computed tomography (CT) remains indispensable for comprehensive staging and metastasis detection [125]. Specifically, abdominal CT demonstrates high diagnostic accuracy for monitoring visceral spread in stage III or HER2-positive breast cancers [126]. When coupled with modalities like color Doppler US, multimodal CT strategies significantly enhance the detection of regional and distant lymph node metastases [127–129]. Functional imaging via single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) further complements this by characterizing pathophysiological tumor metabolism. Recent breakthroughs in peptide-based, breast cancer-targeted radiopharmaceuticals have markedly elevated SPECT’s sensitivity and specificity [130]. By fusing this functional data with anatomical CT, and incorporating emerging tools like artificial intelligence and hardware optimization, SPECT/CT hybrid platforms now provide the high-dimensional precision required for personalized oncology [131, 132].

Advanced diagnostic and multi-omic monitoring modalities

Genomic Sequencing technologies have mapped comprehensive mutational landscapes of breast cancer, identifying critical driver mutations such as PIK3CA and ESR1, including rare but actionable variants. This facilitates personalized therapeutic strategies, especially in advanced-stage disease [133]. Moreover, transcriptome-based molecular classification systems—such as those tailored to TNBC—have enhanced the development of individualized chemotherapy regimens [134].

Epigenetics, particularly studies on DNA methylation and chromatin remodeling, have uncovered silencing mechanisms involving genes such as BRCA1/2. These findings have not only supported early detection strategies but also fueled the development of epigenetic-targeted therapeutics [135].

Single-cell technologies have provided a microscopic lens into tumor heterogeneity by elucidating dynamic interactions between immune and stromal components within the tumor microenvironment. These insights are reshaping prognostic assessment models and guiding the design of combinatorial therapies [136].

Nanotechnology-based drug delivery systems, such as functionalized exosomes and biomimetic liposomes, have improved tumor-targeted drug accumulation, thereby enhancing chemotherapeutic efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity [137].

Aptamer-based smart probes, exemplified by HER2-specific recognition elements, offer integrated diagnostic and therapeutic (theranostic) functionalities, expanding the toolkit for precision intervention [138, 139].

Emerging molecular diagnostics, including CRISPR-Cas12a–based detection systems, have overcome the sensitivity limitations of conventional methods. These platforms allow ultra-early detection of low-abundance mutations in circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), thereby accelerating the clinical translation of liquid biopsy techniques [140].

Collectively, these technological advancements have constructed a full-spectrum clinical management framework that spans molecular subtyping, real-time monitoring, and precision targeting. This paradigm marks the transition of breast cancer care into a multi-omics–driven era of precision oncology.

Liquid biopsy and minimal residual disease (MRD) monitoring

To overcome the spatial and temporal limitations of traditional tissue biopsies, liquid biopsies have emerged as a cornerstone of precision oncology. This approach primarily analyzes circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), circulating tumor cells (CTCs), and tumor-derived exosomes, offering a non-invasive, multi-omic snapshot of the tumor’s dynamic evolution.

Circulating Tumor DNA (ctDNA) and CTCs: ctDNA profiling is highly effective for detecting minimal residual disease (MRD) and monitoring therapeutic response. Clinical validations of tumor-informed ctDNA assays (such as those targeting patient-specific clonal mutations) have demonstrated exceptional performance. In early-stage breast cancer, serial ctDNA monitoring has achieved analytical sensitivities exceeding 89% for predicting clinical relapse, often detecting MRD with a median lead time of 8 to 10 months prior to radiographic progression [141, 142]. Complementary to ctDNA, CTCs provide critical cellular and transcriptomic information. The FDA-approved CellSearch system has established that the presence of ≥ 5 CTCs per 7.5 mL of blood is robustly associated with decreased progression-free and overall survival in metastatic breast cancer, acting as an independent prognostic biomarker [143].

Exosomes: In addition to ctDNA and CTCs, tumor-derived exosomes play a critical role in intercellular communication and pre-metastatic niche formation. These nanoscale extracellular vesicles encapsulate a highly stable, multi-omic payload of proteins, lipids, and ncRNAs (including miRNAs and lncRNAs). Because exosomal lipid bilayers protect their genomic and proteomic cargo from enzymatic degradation in the bloodstream, they offer significantly higher stability than free-floating ctDNA. Emerging diagnostic platforms leveraging exosomal multi-omic signatures show high sensitivity (often > 90%) in distinguishing breast cancer patients from healthy cohorts, positioning them as highly promising biomarkers for ultra-early diagnosis and real-time mapping of the tumor microenvironment [144].

Comparative Efficacy of Liquid Biopsy Biomarkers: Comparatively, ctDNA assays demonstrate remarkable analytical sensitivity (exceeding 89%) and high specificity (up to 99%) for MRD detection, often anticipating clinical relapse with a lead time of 8 to 10 months [212]. CTCs, while offering lower numerical sensitivity (typically 50–60% in early stages) compared to ctDNA, provide invaluable transcriptomic and proteomic cellular information that ctDNA cannot capture [143]. Conversely, exosomes offer a unique advantage in molecular stability due to their protective lipid bilayers, routinely achieving over 90% diagnostic sensitivity, though their clinical specificity remains challenged by the lack of standardized isolation protocols [213]. Integrating these three modalities uniquely compensates for individual assay limitations.

Translational Relevance and Clinical Implementation: Despite their immense translational relevance, the routine clinical implementation of liquid biopsies faces several formidable barriers. High sequencing costs and the lack of robust assay standardization across different multi-omic platforms currently limit their widespread accessibility. Furthermore, stringent regulatory considerations—such as the need for large-scale, prospective clinical utility trials to secure FDA approval and standardized reimbursement frameworks—must be addressed before liquid biopsy can seamlessly transition from a specialized research tool to an integrated standard of care in routine oncology practice [214].

However, a balanced assessment must acknowledge key limitations. First, ctDNA assays may yield false-negative MRD results in patients with low tumor burden or non-shedding tumors, and clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) can cause false positives. Second, CTC enumeration in early-stage disease lacks sensitivity, limiting its MRD role. Third, exosome-based diagnostics still lack standardized isolation protocols and clinical-grade validation. Additionally, high costs and the absence of large-scale prospective utility trials prevent regulatory approval and insurance coverage. Overcoming these hurdles requires multicenter harmonization efforts and consensus guidelines to integrate liquid biopsy reliably into routine care.

Genetic testing

The widespread application of multi-gene panel testing has markedly improved the precision of early-stage breast cancer management. By integrating risk stratification with adjuvant treatment planning, these genetic assays enhance the clinical utility of traditional pathological parameters; however, their translational application still requires further standardization [145].

BRCA1 and BRCA2 are the most critical pathogenic genes associated with hereditary breast cancer. The presence of deleterious mutations in these genes significantly influences treatment decisions. Tumors harboring such mutations show marked sensitivity to DNA-damaging agents, particularly PARP inhibitors. Nevertheless, poor adherence to testing recommendations results in up to 30% of carriers remaining undiagnosed and untreated [146].

Advancements in molecular diagnostics have promoted the clinical adoption of expanded gene panels beyond BRCA1/2, encompassing genes such as PALB2 and ATM. Despite this progress, the interpretation of variants of uncertain significance (VUS) remains a major clinical challenge, as their pathogenic potential is not yet fully understood [147].

Genetic analysis has become deeply integrated into the entire breast cancer care continuum. Firstly, it facilitates intensified screening and prophylactic interventions in high-risk populations. Secondly, it serves as a predictive biomarker for targeted therapies such as PARP inhibitors, with objective response rates reaching up to 65% in patients harboring pathogenic mutations [148].

Epidemiological studies have revealed that among breast cancer survivors meeting NCCN criteria for genetic testing, only 38% undergo formal genetic counseling. While 92% of those tested report discussing genetic risk with family members, the overall clinical uptake of direct-to-consumer genetic testing remains below 5% [149].

Treatment

Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy remains a primary treatment for certain cancers, despite its strong side effects, which often raise patient concerns. Recent advancements in combination therapies, such as chemotherapy combined with targeted therapies and immunotherapies, have both improved efficacy and reduced side effects [150]. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) plays a significant role in treating HER2-positive and TNBC, as it can shrink tumor size, reduce the risks associated with breast-conserving surgery, and help guide subsequent treatment strategies [151]. In early-stage breast cancer, adjuvant chemotherapy effectively reduces the risk of recurrence and death, though its applicability should be assessed based on the patient’s overall health status and other factors [152]. For metastatic breast cancer, chemotherapy does not offer a cure but remains valuable in alleviating symptoms and extending survival [153]. However, the long-term side effects of chemotherapy, such as cardiac toxicity, cognitive dysfunction, and neurotoxicity, warrant careful attention [154]. Additionally, chemotherapy resistance has become a major challenge in breast cancer treatment. Research indicates that specific molecular markers may be linked to resistance, providing a new direction for personalized treatments [155]. For instance, the combination of platinum-based drugs and PARP inhibitors in TNBC has shown promising preclinical results, reflecting the trend towards diversified and more precise chemotherapy regimens [156].

Endocrine therapy

Endocrine therapy is the cornerstone of treatment for hormone receptor-positive (HR+) breast cancer. It primarily targets the ER signaling pathway to significantly suppress cancer cell proliferation and progression, effectively reducing recurrence rates and improving survival outcomes, making it a standard treatment for both early and late-stage breast cancer [157, 158]. Clinically, endocrine therapy for early-stage breast cancer typically lasts for five years, with treatment duration extended for high-risk patients, particularly postmenopausal women, where aromatase inhibitors have shown superior efficacy compared to tamoxifen [159, 160].

In late-stage breast cancer, endocrine therapy is often combined with targeted therapies to overcome resistance and enhance efficacy. The introduction of CDK4/6 inhibitors (such as palbociclib, ribociclib, and abemaciclib) has significantly enhanced the anti-tumor effects of endocrine therapy by blocking the cell cycle [161, 162]. Furthermore, the combination of mTOR inhibitors and PI3K inhibitors with endocrine therapy holds great promise, offering new directions for improving patient prognosis [163, 164].

However, endocrine therapy still faces the major challenge of resistance. Research shows that specific ESR1 gene mutations and compensatory signaling pathway activations are primary drivers of aromatase inhibitor resistance. Clinically, identifying these mutational profiles via serial ctDNA monitoring directly informs the decision to switch patients to oral Selective Estrogen Receptor Degraders (SERDs, such as elacestrant) or novel targeted combinations, ensuring that therapeutic regimens adapt in real-time to the tumor’s clonal evolution [165–168]. Ongoing studies are actively exploring new endocrine drugs and combination therapies to improve the durability and efficacy of treatment [167, 168].

Heavy ion therapy

Heavy ion therapy, particularly carbon ion therapy, demonstrates significant advantages in breast cancer treatment due to its high linear energy transfer (LET) characteristics. It provides stronger tumor cell-killing effects while reducing damage to surrounding healthy tissues, ensuring more thorough eradication of localized cancer cells [169]. The Heavy Ion Therapy Center (HITC) at Yonsei Cancer Center in Korea, the first carbon ion therapy center equipped with fixed beams and superconducting rotational machines, has confirmed the significant clinical effectiveness and utility of carbon ion therapy for tumor treatment [170]. Clinical trials show that carbon ion therapy in partial breast irradiation for early-stage breast cancer yields good therapeutic outcomes with minimal adverse reactions. Although it requires a longer treatment duration, its persistence and safety provide a foundation for future studies [171]. Despite its high costs and technological complexity limiting global application, carbon ion therapy is expected to play an increasingly important role in breast cancer treatment as technology advances [172].

Targeted therapy

Targeted therapy has become a crucial treatment approach in recent years for breast cancer. With the continuous development of targeted drugs, the treatment of breast cancer with targeted therapies has become more refined and comprehensive. Targeted drugs inhibit specific growth pathways in tumor cells, effectively suppressing tumor development. For HER2-positive breast cancer, the use of monoclonal antibodies such as trastuzumab and pertuzumab has significantly improved patient survival and overall survival rates [173]. For tumors harboring germline BRCA1/2 mutations (predominantly TNBC), multi-omic profiling of homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) has successfully guided the clinical application of PARP inhibitors. Landmark trials, such as OlympiA and OlympiAD [9, 60], have cemented the role of PARP inhibitors (e.g., olaparib and talazoparib) in significantly prolonging both progression-free and overall survival by exploiting synthetic lethality in DNA repair-deficient cancer cells [174].

Furthermore, antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) have revolutionized the targeted therapy landscape by integrating highly specific monoclonal antibodies with potent cytotoxic payloads to deliver precision strikes against tumor cells. Currently, several FDA-approved ADCs have reshaped clinical paradigms, whereas others remain under active clinical investigation. Most notably, quantitative proteomic reassessments of HER2 expression have birthed the ‘HER2-low’ clinical category. The FDA-approved trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd) demonstrated unprecedented survival benefits in this cohort during the landmark Phase III DESTINY-Breast04 trial, reducing the risk of disease progression or death by 50% (Hazard Ratio 0.50) and significantly extending median progression-free survival (9.9 months vs. 5.1 months for chemotherapy), representing a massive paradigm shift [175]. However, critical evaluation of this trial highlights that while survival is significantly prolonged, the emergence of acquired resistance and severe toxicities—particularly interstitial lung disease (ILD)—necessitate stringent clinical monitoring. Similarly, the FDA-approved Trop-2-targeted ADC sacituzumab govitecan (SG) showed marked efficacy in the ASCENT trial by efficiently bypassing traditional chemoresistance mechanisms in heavily pretreated TNBC [175–177]. Yet, its clinical tolerability is frequently limited by high rates of neutropenia and diarrhea. Concurrently, investigational ADCs such as datopotamab deruxtecan (Dato-DXd) are undergoing rigorous Phase III evaluations (e.g., TROPION-Breast01), offering promising preliminary efficacy with differentiated toxicity profiles [208]. Current research is also actively exploring novel targets (such as HER3, LIV-1) and bispecific ADCs to overcome existing treatment barriers [178]. In HR+/HER2- advanced breast cancer, transcriptomic and genomic profiling of cell cycle dysregulation has established the combination of CDK4/6 inhibitors (palbociclib, ribociclib, and abemaciclib) with endocrine therapy as the gold-standard first-line treatment. Extensive clinical data reveal that this blockade comprehensively halts the G1-to-S phase transition, effectively doubling progression-free survival compared to endocrine therapy alone [179]. Similarly, PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway inhibitors have shown promising applications in this subtype [180].

However, targeted therapy still faces the challenge of resistance. Research indicates that mutations in the PI3K/AKT pathway may lead to resistance to HER2-targeted therapy [181]. Therefore, a deeper understanding of resistance mechanisms is critical for developing more effective treatment strategies.

Immunotherapy

Immunotherapy has shown significant efficacy in breast cancer, especially in cases where conventional treatments have limited success. In TNBC, the landmark Phase III KEYNOTE-522 trial established pembrolizumab with neoadjuvant chemotherapy as the standard of care, significantly improving pathological complete response (pCR) rates to 64.8% (compared to 51.2% with placebo) [203]. Nevertheless, a critical limitation remains the identification of precise predictive biomarkers. Multi-omic analyses demonstrate that relying solely on PD-L1 immunohistochemistry is clinically insufficient to predict immune-related adverse events or long-term therapeutic durability. Therefore, comprehensive transcriptomic immune signatures (such as tumor mutational burden and specific T-cell inflamed gene expression profiles) offer far superior predictive value for immunotherapy response [204]. This predictive mechanism is closely linked to CXCL13 + T cell-mediated immune activation and the enrichment of pro-inflammatory macrophages (CXCL9/CXCL10 overexpression) [182, 183]. For HER2-positive subtypes, research has shifted toward multi-modal immunomodulation, including the synergistic application of immune checkpoint inhibitors, HER2-targeted ADCs, CAR-T therapies, and bispecific antibodies (such as PD-1/HER2) [184, 185]. As detailed in Sect. 3.4, the immunosuppressive TME severely limits therapeutic efficacy. Consequently, modern immunomodulatory strategies are now targeting the M2 phenotype of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and blocking fibroblast-derived TGF-β to therapeutically reverse this ‘immune-cold’ state, significantly enhancing cytotoxic T cell infiltration [186]. Finally, genomic-driven screening of public neoantigens (such as TP53/PIK3CA mutation-derived epitopes) lays the foundation for developing universal TCR-T therapies, overcoming technical bottlenecks in personalized treatment [187].

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) therapy

Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) has gained increasing attention in clinical practice and is playing an increasingly important role in breast cancer treatment. Studies have shown that certain active components in Chinese herbs can significantly interfere with intracellular signaling cascades in cancer cells. For instance, the active compound in Huangqin has been found to block the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, which is crucial for tumor cell growth. Inhibition of this pathway can effectively suppress the spread of breast cancer cells [188, 189]. Furthermore, combining Chinese medicine with chemotherapy yields enhanced therapeutic effects. For patients undergoing surgery, a combination of Western and Chinese medicine not only leads to more noticeable tumor shrinkage but also reduces the severe anemia and thrombocytopenia often induced by chemotherapy [190, 191]. For TNBC, Chinese medicine improves the patient’s immune status, alters the tumor microenvironment by modifying immune-suppressive macrophages, and facilitates the infiltration of cytotoxic T cells into the tumor area, resulting in a fourfold improvement in therapeutic outcomes [192]. Notably, Huangqi (Astragalus) has demonstrated potential in enhancing the efficacy of radiotherapy by effectively eliminating drug-resistant cancer cells, doubling the therapeutic effect of radiation. It has also been shown to reverse drug resistance in tumors, restoring their sensitivity to chemotherapy in experimental settings [193].

Combination therapy

As mentioned earlier, the combination of different treatment modalities has shown significant therapeutic benefits, particularly in cases where conventional single treatments are less effective, such as TNBC. In addition, the combination of RET inhibitors with CDK4/6 inhibitors and endocrine therapy in ER+ breast cancer, as well as the use of inetetamab with pyrotinib and chemotherapy, and apatinib combined with trastuzumab and albumin-bound paclitaxel in HER2-positive breast cancer patients with brain metastases, have all demonstrated significant therapeutic effects and notable suppression of resistant tumor cell growth [194–196]. It is well-known that radiotherapy can cause neurotoxicity; however, when combined with novel targeted drugs, it can enhance efficacy while controlling toxicity [197]. The integration of various treatment approaches allows for better management of breast cancer metastasis, recurrence, drug resistance, and heterogeneity, while also minimizing the side effects of treatments, providing patients with better therapeutic options and prognoses (Table 4) [198].

Therapeutic limitations and mechanisms of resistance

Despite the remarkable success of precision therapies, acquired drug resistance remains the primary cause of breast cancer mortality. Through a multi-omics lens, therapeutic resistance is rarely driven by a singular genetic event; rather, it manifests as a complex evolutionary adaptation characterized by subclonal selection, epigenetic regulation, and profound signaling pathway rewiring [201]. Under the selective pressure of targeted therapies, pre-existing drug-resistant subclones outcompete sensitive populations (clonal evolution). This process is heavily modulated by epigenetic regulation, such as genome-wide hypomethylation or specific histone modifications (e.g., EZH2 overexpression), which dynamically alter chromatin accessibility to promote an EMT-associated, therapy-tolerant persister cell state. Concurrently, profound signaling pathway rewiring allows tumors to bypass therapeutic blockades [72].

Endocrine and Targeted Therapy Resistance: In HR+ breast cancers, prolonged endocrine therapy frequently induces selective pressure that results in ESR1 ligand-binding domain mutations (e.g., Y537S, D538G), leading to constitutive, estrogen-independent receptor activation [74, 165]. Concurrently, transcriptomic plasticity allows tumors to bypass CDK4/6 inhibition via the compensatory hyperactivation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR or FGFR signaling pathways [199]. Similarly, in HER2 + disease, profound intratumoral spatial heterogeneity, dynamic internalization of target antigens, and payload tolerance pose significant barriers to antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), driving the survival of resistant subclones [200].

Immunotherapy Resistance and the TME: The efficacy of immunotherapy is profoundly limited by the tumor microenvironment (TME). Multi-omic profiling has identified that “immune-cold” breast cancers employ severe metabolic reprogramming to evade immune surveillance. The Warburg effect leads to excessive lactate accumulation and hypoxia within the TME, which physically and metabolically paralyzes infiltrating cytotoxic CD8 + T cells while recruiting immunosuppressive regulatory T cells (Tregs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [201]. Overcoming these multi-layered resistance mechanisms remains the most critical challenge in modern oncology, necessitating the shift towards dynamic, multi-omic monitoring (such as serial ctDNA tracking) to anticipate adaptive resistance before macroscopic clinical progression occurs.

Future directions in breast cancer treatment

AI-enhanced diagnostic models

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) with multi-modal imaging and radiomics holds significant promise for improving early-stage breast cancer detection [202]. AI models, particularly those utilizing deep learning algorithms, have the capacity to process large datasets from diagnostic imaging and clinical records, which can substantially increase the accuracy of early detection. Moreover, these AI-driven approaches can facilitate dynamic monitoring of residual molecular disease and micrometastases. By integrating patient-specific molecular profiles with real-time monitoring, AI could pave the way for more personalized treatment strategies, allowing for tailored therapeutic interventions that are both effective and timely.

Multi-omics integration in therapy

A major challenge in breast cancer therapy lies in the underutilization of multi-omics data, including genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic profiles. These datasets, when integrated, could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the tumor’s heterogeneity and its molecular evolution. Future studies should focus on developing methods to seamlessly combine these diverse datasets for clinical application, with particular attention to utilizing single-cell RNA sequencing technologies. This approach may offer insights into tumor microenvironment (TME) interactions and mechanisms of therapeutic resistance, thereby identifying novel targets for intervention and improving the precision of therapeutic regimens.

Advancements in immunotherapy

Immunotherapy, despite showing promising results in breast cancer, faces substantial challenges, especially in TNBC, which exhibits significant immune evasion mechanisms. To overcome these hurdles, future research must focus on optimizing immunotherapy regimens through combinations of immune checkpoint inhibitors, bispecific antibodies, and chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapies. Additionally, efforts to reprogram the TME to promote immune cell infiltration could enhance the effectiveness of immunotherapies, particularly in immune-cold tumors that are traditionally resistant to such treatments.

Innovations in targeted therapy

Recent innovations in antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), designed to target specific tumor markers such as HER2, Trop-2, and ESR1 mutations, offer exciting new possibilities for overcoming drug resistance in breast cancer. Further investigation into the role of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in therapy resistance could uncover additional therapeutic targets. The development of novel targeted agents, particularly those aimed at difficult-to-treat subtypes like TNBC, could enhance the precision of breast cancer therapy and improve clinical outcomes.

Integrating traditional and modern therapeutic modalities

The potential for integrating Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) with modern oncological therapies presents a promising avenue for breast cancer treatment. Compounds derived from herbal sources have shown potential in modulating key molecular signaling pathways involved in tumor growth, metastasis, and immune evasion. Multi-center, randomized clinical trials are necessary to assess the clinical efficacy of combining TCM with modern cancer therapies. Such trials could provide the evidence required for broader integration of TCM into standard breast cancer treatment regimens.

Metabolic reprogramming in cancer therapy

Metabolic alterations play a critical role in the pathogenesis of breast cancer, particularly in the context of glycolysis and lipid metabolism. Future research should prioritize targeting these metabolic pathways, including the Warburg effect and lipid metabolism, to reduce tumor resistance and increase the sensitivity of tumors to conventional therapies. By targeting the metabolic reprogramming that supports cancer cell survival and proliferation, new therapeutic strategies could be developed to complement existing treatment modalities.

To achieve true multi-omic integration, genomics and transcriptomics must be complemented by real-time metabolomic profiling. High-throughput techniques, such as mass spectrometry (MS) and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy of patient serum, empower oncologists to monitor circulating oncometabolites (e.g., lactate, kynurenine) and lipidomic shifts during therapy. Because metabolic flux changes within hours to days of drug administration, metabolomics provides a near-instantaneous readout of therapeutic efficacy, far outpacing delayed radiographic or genomic (ctDNA) indicators. This allows for dynamic, real-time clinical decision-making, prompting the immediate switch to targeted metabolic inhibitors or alternative therapies before macroscopic relapse occurs [205].

Emerging technologies for precision oncology

The next frontier in precision oncology relies heavily on the integration of emerging spatial and single-cell multi-omic technologies. Advanced spatial transcriptomics and highly multiplexed spatial proteomics (e.g., Imaging Mass Cytometry) offer unprecedented, spatially resolved maps of the tumor-immune interface, identifying exact molecular niches that foster drug resistance. Furthermore, the integration of these high-dimensional datasets with advanced artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning algorithms will be paramount. Future clinical workflows must leverage these AI-driven multi-omic models not only to discover novel actionable targets but also to dynamically tailor individualized therapeutic regimens in real time, ultimately actualizing the true promise of precision oncology [209].

Real-world implementation challenges and limitations

Despite the tremendous promise of multi-omics and precision oncology, several real-world implementation challenges persist. Firstly, economic and accessibility barriers: comprehensive multi-omic sequencing (e.g., spatial transcriptomics, serial ctDNA monitoring) entails exorbitant costs and requires sophisticated bioinformatics infrastructure, limiting its widespread adoption in resource-constrained or community oncology settings. Secondly, regulatory hurdles: transitioning novel multi-omic signatures from the laboratory to standard-of-care requires rigorous, large-scale prospective trials to meet strict FDA and EMA regulatory approvals.

Furthermore, inherent limitations exist within current methodologies and this review. While liquid biopsies provide a systemic snapshot, they may still fail to capture the entirety of extreme spatial intratumoral heterogeneity. Additionally, this review, while comprehensive, is inherently limited by the rapid, almost daily evolution of the oncology landscape; specific therapeutic guidelines and investigational trial data discussed herein are subject to swift updates. Overcoming these barriers requires a concerted global effort toward standardizing assays, decentralizing high-throughput technologies, and reforming insurance reimbursement frameworks to ensure equitable access to precision medicine.

Discussion and conclusions

Despite the progress, several critical issues warrant discussion. First, the integration of multi-omics into clinic faces significant standardization hurdles; for instance, variant interpretation across platforms remains inconsistent, and metabolomic reference ranges are not yet clinically defined. Second, while PD-L1 and TMB are used as biomarkers for immunotherapy, their predictive power is imperfect, and immune-cold tumors may need alternative strategies like epigenetic priming. Third, the quality of evidence supporting TCM in breast cancer remains predominantly from small, single-center studies, necessitating rigorous multi-center RCTs. Finally, the cost-effectiveness of widespread multi-omic testing must be demonstrated to ensure equitable access. Only by critically addressing these gaps can multi-omics truly transform from a research tool into a standard-of-care component.

Taken together, the evidence reviewed here demonstrates that breast cancer, as a leading global health threat to women, demands an integrated understanding of its epidemiological, molecular, and clinical dimensions. This review has systematically summarized the multidimensional risk factors and molecular networks driving pathogenesis, and detailed how technological advances have driven a paradigm shift from traditional macroscopic evaluation to high-resolution, multi-omic molecular diagnostics. This integration has dramatically enhanced the early detection of minimal residual disease and reduced diagnostic latency. In parallel, treatment has evolved from conventional monotherapies to precision regimens combining targeted therapy, immunotherapy, novel endocrine agents, and traditional Chinese medicine, significantly improving patient outcomes. However, challenges such as drug resistance, metastasis control, and individualized treatment remain urgent issues.

Moving forward, emerging technologies such as single-cell and spatial transcriptomics will further unveil the tumor’s internal diversity, refining molecular subtyping beyond the current Luminal/HER2/TNBC framework. The ultimate conquest of breast cancer will require not only technological innovation and global collaboration, but also a transition from “disease treatment” to “health management.” For researchers and clinicians, strengthening the interface between basic research and clinical translation is essential to building dynamic treatment decision-making systems. For the public, maintaining regular physical activity, avoiding obesity, ensuring adequate sleep quality, reducing alcohol consumption, and maintaining proper vitamin intake remain foundational preventive measures. With continued interdisciplinary effort, the promise of precision oncology can be translated into tangible survival benefits for all patients.
