Ligation-assisted endoscopic mucosal resection versus endoscopic submucosal dissection for small gastrointestinal deep-layer lesions: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Qian Zhang, Ying Zhou, Pengyan Pan, Haixia Hu, Zhiming Xiao, Rui Liu, Decai Zhang, Anliu Tang, Minzi Deng, Xiaoyan Wang, Jingbo Li, Wei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare the clinical outcomes and hospitalization expenses of ligation-assisted endoscopic mucosal resection and endoscopic submucosal dissection for gastrointestinal deep-layer lesions measuring ≤1.0 cm.MethodsThis retrospective single-center cohort study included 486 patients with gastrointestinal deep-layer lesions detected using endoscopic ultrasonography. Eligible lesions measured ≤1.0 cm and were identified using endoscopic ultrasonography as originating from the muscularis mucosae, submucosa, or muscularis propria. Baseline characteristics and treatment outcomes were compared between the ligation-assisted endoscopic mucosal resection and endoscopic submucosal dissection groups before and after 1:1 propensity score matching. Multivariable ordinal logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with hospitalization expense categories.ResultsAfter 1:1 propensity score matching, 336 patients were included in the matched cohort, with 168 patients in each group. No significant differences were observed in the en bloc resection rate, R0 resection status, incidence of procedure-related complications, duration of hemostatic drug use, and length of hospital stay between the ligation-assisted endoscopic mucosal resection and endoscopic submucosal dissection groups. Hospitalization expenses were significantly lower in the ligation-assisted endoscopic mucosal resection group than in the endoscopic submucosal dissection group after matching (renminbi 7900 (interquartile range, 6500–10,000) vs. 10,600 (interquartile range, 8700–13,200); p < 0.001). In adjusted ordinal logistic regression analyses, ligation-assisted endoscopic mucosal resection remained significantly associated with lower hospitalization expense categories in both primary adjusted model without actual length of hospital stay (adjusted odds ratio, 3.52; 95% confidence interval: 2.34–5.29; p < 0.001) and the sensitivity model further adjusted for actual length of hospital stay (adjusted odds ratio, 3.31; 95% confidence interval: 2.17–5.06; p < 0.001).ConclusionsLigation-assisted endoscopic mucosal resection was associated with comparable short-term clinical outcomes and lower observed hospitalization expenses compared with endoscopic submucosal dissection in selected patients with gastrointestinal deep-layer lesions measuring ≤1.0 cm. Prospective multicenter studies with long-term follow-up are needed to confirm these findings.Keywords: Gastrointestinal deep-layer lesion, endoscopic mucosal resection, endoscopic submucosal dissection, ligation-assisted endoscopic mucosal resection, propensity score matching

Introduction

Gastrointestinal cancers remain major contributors to cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide, with a high disease burden across multiple anatomical sites. According to 2023 statistics, 1,958,310 new cancer cases and 609,820 cancer deaths were projected to occur in the United States, with gastrointestinal malignancies accounting for a substantial proportion of cancer-related deaths. Colorectal cancer is among the most commonly diagnosed and fatal gastrointestinal malignancies, followed by pancreatic, liver, gastric, and esophageal cancers.1–3 With the increasing use of population-based screening and endoscopic examination, more early gastrointestinal neoplasms and small gastrointestinal lesions are being detected in clinical practice. 4 Some of these lesions arise from deeper layers of the gastrointestinal wall, including the muscularis mucosae, submucosa, and muscularis propria, and require individualized endoscopic treatment strategies.

Treatment strategies for gastrointestinal malignancies, early gastrointestinal neoplasms, and selected small gastrointestinal lesions vary according to lesion size, location, depth, and malignant potential and may include endoscopic resection, laparoscopic surgery, open surgery, or, in selected malignant cases, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy.5–8 In recent years, endoscopic resection has become an important minimally invasive treatment for premalignant and early malignant gastrointestinal lesions as well as selected small lesions without evidence of metastasis. Complete en bloc resection is generally recommended for selected early gastrointestinal lesions when technically feasible and clinically appropriate.9–11 Endoscopic resection is highly effective when performed by experienced endoscopists for appropriate indications and is now considered a first-line treatment for selected colorectal lesions without invasive cancer.12,13 Endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) and endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) are two commonly used endoscopic techniques for complete removal of gastrointestinal lesions without metastasis.14–16 Endoscopic ultrasonography is useful for characterizing gastrointestinal subepithelial lesions and assessing their layer of origin before endoscopic treatment. 17 Several EMR techniques, including snare EMR, underwater EMR, injection-assisted EMR, cap-assisted EMR, and ligation-assisted endoscopic mucosal resection (EMRL), are used for selected small gastrointestinal lesions without evidence of metastasis. Compared with conventional polypectomy, EMR may enable resection with adequate lateral margins in selected gastrointestinal lesions. In contrast, ESD allows more precise en bloc resection and is often selected for larger lesions, lesions suspected of superficial submucosal invasion, or lesions considered difficult to remove using other endoscopic resection techniques.18,19 Consensus statements and guidelines generally support ESD for selected gastrointestinal epithelial lesions suspected of limited submucosal invasion, particularly when lesions are >2 cm. However, the optimal endoscopic approach for lesions <2 cm remains controversial. Japanese guidelines suggest that EMR may be performed for lesions <2 cm in selected outpatient settings. 18 In the United States, ESD is recommended as a first-line treatment for visible, endoscopically resectable superficial gastric neoplasia, whereas EMR may be considered for lesions measuring ≤1 cm.19,20 Chinese and European guidelines provide limited specific recommendations regarding the optimal endoscopic approach for gastrointestinal lesions measuring 2 cm or smaller, especially those arising from deeper layers.21,22 The European society of gastrointestinal endoscopy (ESGE) guideline on gastrointestinal subepithelial lesions emphasizes the role of endoscopic ultrasonography in characterizing lesion size, location, originating layer, echogenicity, and morphology and highlights that management should be individualized according to diagnostic certainty, lesion features, and malignant potential. 23 In addition, advanced techniques such as endoscopic full-thickness resection and submucosal tunneling endoscopic resection have been reported for selected gastrointestinal subepithelial lesions, particularly lesions suspected to arise from the muscularis propria layer.24,25 Broader changes in gastrointestinal disease epidemiology, including the increasing incidence of early-onset colorectal cancer, further highlight the importance of early detection and individualized management of gastrointestinal lesions. 26

Traditionally, ESD has been used for gastrointestinal lesions arising from deeper layers or those considered difficult to resect completely by conventional EMR. However, for selected gastrointestinal deep-layer lesions measuring ≤1.0 cm, EMRL may theoretically offer comparable clinical outcomes with lower hospitalization expenses. Evidence directly comparing EMRL and ESD in this specific subgroup remains limited. Therefore, this retrospective study aimed to compare the clinical outcomes and hospitalization expenses of EMRL and ESD in patients with gastrointestinal deep-layer lesions measuring ≤1.0 cm. The findings may help inform individualized procedure selection and support the development of more tailored treatment strategies for small gastrointestinal deep-layer lesions.

Materials and methods

Data source and study population

This retrospective single-center cohort study used data from the inpatient medical record information management system of the Department of Gastroenterology. Consecutive hospitalized patients aged 18 years or older who underwent EMRL or ESD for gastrointestinal deep-layer lesions between 2012 and 2022 were identified from the database and screened according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. No random sampling was performed. Eligible lesions measured ≤1.0 cm and were assessed by endoscopic ultrasonography as arising from the muscularis mucosae, submucosa, or muscularis propria. Each patient contributed only one lesion to the analysis.

Patients were excluded if they had evidence of metastasis to intra-abdominal lymph nodes or distant organs, coagulopathy, severe cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease, severe respiratory disease, severe hepatic or renal dysfunction, or incomplete clinical data. After applying these criteria, a total of 486 patients with 486 eligible lesions were included in the final analysis. Of these, 258 patients underwent EMRL and 228 patients underwent ESD.

This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement. 27

Endoscopic procedures

Both EMRL and ESD were performed by experienced endoscopists according to lesion characteristics and institutional practice. For EMRL, the lesion was suctioned into a transparent cap or ligation device, followed by deployment of an elastic band at the lesion base to create a pseudo-polyp. The pseudo-polyp was then resected using a snare with electrosurgical current. Because this retrospective study covered a 10-y period, the specific ligation device, snare type, and electrosurgical settings varied during the study period and were selected by the endoscopist according to lesion characteristics, equipment availability, and institutional practice at the time of the procedure. Wound closure with endoscopic clips was performed when considered necessary by the endoscopist. For ESD, circumferential mucosal incision and submucosal dissection were performed according to standard ESD principles, with hemostasis and wound management performed as needed during the procedure.

Outcome and predictor variables

The primary outcomes were R0 resection status and hospitalization expenses. R0 resection was defined as histologically complete resection with negative lateral and vertical margins, whereas RX was defined as indeterminate histological margin status. Secondary outcomes included the duration of hemostatic drug use, length of hospital stay, and incidence of procedure-related complications, including bleeding and perforation. Baseline and clinical variables included endoscopic procedure type, age, sex, body mass index, lesion size, lesion location, lesion morphology, and tissue depth, as assessed by endoscopic ultrasonography. Candidate variables for ordinal logistic regression included endoscopic procedure type, sex, lesion location, tissue depth, age, body mass index, and actual length of hospital stay. Actual length of hospital stay was included only in the sensitivity model because it may partly mediate the association between procedure type and hospitalization expenses.

Statistical methods

Propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model that included baseline variables potentially associated with treatment selection, including age, sex, body mass index, lesion size, lesion location, lesion morphology, and tissue depth. Final histopathological diagnoses were determined from resected specimens after the procedure and were therefore not included in the propensity score model; they are presented descriptively in Table 1. Nearest-neighbor matching without replacement was performed at a 1:1 ratio using a caliper width of 0.2 times the SD of the logit of the propensity score. Balance between the two groups before and after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences, with a value of less than 0.1 indicating adequate balance. Propensity score matching (PSM) was conducted using the ‘MatchIt’ package in R software version 4.3.0. A total of 336 patients were retained in the 1:1 matched cohort, including 168 patients in each treatment group. Patients who were not successfully matched were excluded from the propensity-matched outcome comparisons but were retained in the full-cohort regression analyses. Therefore, clinical outcome comparisons were primarily performed in the matched cohort, whereas ordinal logistic regression analyses were conducted in the full cohort of 486 patients to preserve statistical power and evaluate factors associated with hospitalization expense categories.

Continuous variables were compared using Student's t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson's chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Fisher's exact test was used when expected cell counts were small. Model stability was assessed before regression analysis, and no evidence of complete or quasi-complete separation was observed. Associations between clinical variables and hospitalization expense categories were assessed using Pearson's chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Hospitalization expenses were examined in two ways. First, actual hospitalization expenses were treated as continuous data for unadjusted between-group comparisons. Because the distribution of hospitalization expenses was skewed, expenses were summarized as median (interquartile range (IQR)) and compared between the EMRL and ESD groups using the Mann–Whitney U test. Second, for ordinal logistic regression analyses, hospitalization expenses were categorized into three ordered groups according to the distribution of expenses in the cohort and clinically interpretable thresholds: lower expense category (renminbi (RMB), <8000), middle expense category (RMB, 8000–12,000), and higher expense category (RMB >12,000). Ordinal logistic regression analyses were performed in the full cohort of 486 patients to identify factors associated with lower hospitalization expense categories. Higher odds ratios (ORs) indicated a greater likelihood of belonging to a lower hospitalization expense category. The proportional odds assumption was assessed before model fitting using the Brant test, and no significant violation was detected (p > 0.05). Variables considered clinically relevant or associated with hospitalization expenses in univariable analyses were included in multivariable ordinal logistic regression models. The primary adjusted model included endoscopic procedure type, sex, lesion location, tissue depth, age, and body mass index. Because actual length of hospital stay may partly mediate the association between procedure type and hospitalization expenses, it was not included in the primary adjusted model. A sensitivity model was then fitted by further adjusting for actual length of hospital stay. Age, body mass index, and actual length of hospital stay were entered as continuous variables. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.0 and the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software (version 25.0, IBM Corp.; Armonk, NY, USA). All p-values were two-sided, and p <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki of 1975, as revised in 2024, and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Third Xiangya Hospital of Central South University, Changsha, China (Approval Number: 320210221; Approval Date: 20 July 2021). The requirement for written informed consent was waived because only retrospective, deidentified clinical data were used.

Results

Baseline characteristics and treatment distribution

A total of 486 patients with gastrointestinal deep-layer lesions measuring 1.0 cm or smaller were included in this study. Of these, 258 patients (53.1%) underwent EMRL, and 228 patients (46.9%) underwent ESD. Overall, 206 patients (42.4%) were male and 280 patients (57.6%) were female.

Before PSM, the mean age was 47.4 years in the EMRL group and 48.2 years in the ESD group. The mean body mass index was 22.1 kg/m2 in the EMRL group and 22.5 kg/m2 in the ESD group. The mean lesion size was 0.64 cm in the EMRL group and 0.68 cm in the ESD group. Lesions were located in the upper gastrointestinal tract in 228 patients (46.9%) and in the lower gastrointestinal tract in 258 patients (53.1%). Flat elevated morphology was the most common lesion morphology, observed in 344 patients (70.8%), followed by protruded morphology in 131 patients (27.0%) and depressed morphology in 11 patients (2.3%). Regarding tissue depth assessed by endoscopic ultrasonography, 320 lesions (65.8%) were assessed as arising from the submucosa, 71 lesions (14.6%) from the muscularis mucosae, and 95 lesions (19.5%) from the muscularis propria. Final histopathological diagnoses included neuroendocrine tumor, gastrointestinal stromal tumor, leiomyoma, ectopic pancreas, inflammatory lesion, and other subepithelial lesions. Final histopathological diagnoses are presented descriptively in Table 1. Because final histopathological diagnosis was determined after resection, it was not included in the propensity score model. Among baseline variables, between-group differences before matching were mainly observed in sex distribution, lesion morphology, and tissue depth, with additional minor imbalances in some baseline variables as reflected by standardized mean differences.

Clinical outcomes of EMRL and ESD

In total, 486 patients with 486 eligible lesions were analyzed, including 258 patients treated with EMRL and 228 patients treated with ESD. After 1:1 PSM, 336 patients were included in the matched cohort, with 168 patients in each group. Table 1 presents baseline characteristics and final histopathological diagnoses before and after PSM. Before matching, baseline imbalances were observed in sex, lesion morphology, and tissue depth. After matching, all baseline covariates included in the propensity score model were adequately balanced between the EMRL and ESD groups, with standardized mean differences below 0.1. Final histopathological diagnoses are presented descriptively in Table 1 and were not included in the propensity score model. In the matched cohort, no statistically significant differences were observed between the EMRL and ESD groups in en bloc resection rate, R0 resection rate, incidence of procedure-related complications, duration of hemostatic drug use, or length of hospital stay. En bloc resection was achieved in 165 patients (98.2%) in the EMRL group and 167 patients (99.4%) in the ESD group (p = 0.623). The R0 resection rates were 97.6% in the EMRL group and 98.8% in the ESD group (p = 0.685). Procedure-related complications occurred in 3.0% of patients in the EMRL group and 1.8% of patients in the ESD group (p = 0.723). The distribution of hemostatic drug use was also comparable between the two groups (p = 0.536), as was the length of hospital stay (p = 0.284). However, hospitalization expenses were significantly lower in the EMRL group than in the ESD group after matching (RMB 7900 (IQR), 6500–10,000) vs. 10,600 (IQR, 8700–13,200); p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Hospitalization expenses were lower in the EMRL group than in the ESD group

Among all 486 patients, 116 patients (23.9%) had hospitalization expenses exceeding RMB 12,000. In the unmatched cohort, hospitalization expenses were significantly lower in the EMRL group than in the ESD group (RMB 7800 (IQR, 6400–9800) vs. 10,900 (IQR, 8800–13,700); p < 0.001). This difference remained statistically significant after PSM (RMB 7900 (IQR, 6500–10,000) vs. 10,600 (IQR, 8700–13,200); p < 0.001) (Table 2). To further explore variables associated with hospitalization expense categories, relevant clinical factors were analyzed in subsequent association and ordinal logistic regression analyses.

Factors associated with hospitalization expenses

To explore variables associated with hospitalization expense categories, clinical factors were first examined using univariable analyses. The distribution of hospitalization expense categories differed significantly according to tissue depth (p < 0.001), whereas procedure-related complications (p = 0.469), duration of hemostatic drug use (p = 0.400), and length of hospital stay (p = 0.110) were not significantly associated with hospitalization expense categories (Table 3).

In the full cohort of 486 patients, ordinal logistic regression was then performed to identify factors associated with lower hospitalization expense categories. The proportional odds assumption was not violated according to the Brant test (p > 0.05). In the crude model, EMRL was significantly associated with a higher likelihood of belonging to a lower hospitalization expense category compared with ESD (crude OR, 4.18; 95% CI: 2.89–6.04; p < 0.001). Submucosal lesions were less likely to belong to a lower hospitalization expense category than muscularis propria lesions in the crude model (crude OR, 0.47; 95% CI: 0.30–0.72; p < 0.001). In the primary adjusted model without actual length of hospital stay, EMRL remained significantly associated with lower hospitalization expense categories (adjusted OR, 3.52; 95% CI: 2.34–5.29; p < 0.001). After further adjustment for actual length of hospital stay in the sensitivity model, this association remained statistically significant (adjusted OR, 3.31; 95% CI: 2.17–5.06; p < 0.001). Other variables, including sex, lesion location, tissue depth, age, and body mass index, were not independently associated with lower hospitalization expense categories in the primary adjusted model. In the sensitivity model, actual length of hospital stay was also not independently associated with lower hospitalization expense categories (Table 4).

Discussion

Our results suggest that EMRL was associated with comparable short-term clinical outcomes and lower observed hospitalization expenses compared with ESD in selected patients with gastrointestinal deep-layer lesions measuring ≤1.0 cm. In this retrospective cohort of 486 patients, 258 patients underwent EMRL and 228 patients underwent ESD. After 1:1 PSM, 336 patients were included in the matched cohort, with 168 patients in each group. EMRL and ESD showed comparable en bloc resection rates, R0 resection status, procedure-related complication rates, duration of hemostatic drug use, and length of hospital stay. However, hospitalization expenses were significantly lower in the EMRL group than in the ESD group. This association remained significant in both primary adjusted model and the sensitivity model further adjusting for actual length of hospital stay, suggesting that the lower hospitalization expenses associated with EMRL were not fully explained by differences in hospital stay.

In the present study, lower gastrointestinal tract lesions accounted for a slightly higher proportion of cases than upper gastrointestinal tract lesions. The distribution of gastrointestinal lesions may vary according to lesion type, anatomical site, population characteristics, screening practices, and regional clinical patterns. Previous epidemiological studies have also suggested that the anatomical distribution of gastrointestinal lesions may differ across countries and populations.28–30 These differences highlight the importance of considering local disease patterns and clinical practice settings when selecting endoscopic treatment strategies.

Current guidelines and consensus statements suggest that endoscopic treatment decisions should be guided by lesion characteristics, technical feasibility, operator experience, and procedure-related risk.19,20–23 Although ESD is generally supported for selected gastrointestinal epithelial lesions, particularly larger lesions or those suspected of limited submucosal invasion, the optimal endoscopic approach for very small deep-layer lesions remains insufficiently defined. For gastrointestinal subepithelial or deep-layer lesions, other advanced endoscopic approaches, including endoscopic full-thickness resection and submucosal tunneling endoscopic resection, have also been described for selected lesions.24,25 However, these techniques require specific expertise and careful patient selection, and their roles may differ according to lesion location, size, originating layer, and suspected pathology. Therefore, for very small deep-layer lesions, the choice between EMRL, ESD, and other advanced resection techniques should be individualized rather than based on lesion size alone. In the present study, 228 patients (46.9%) underwent ESD despite having gastrointestinal deep-layer lesions measuring ≤1.0 cm.

This finding suggests that ESD is still commonly selected for some small deep-layer lesions in clinical practice, possibly because of concerns regarding lesion depth, complete resection, or technical difficulty. EMR is a well-established endoscopic resection technique generally used for selected small gastrointestinal lesions. In addition, a randomized controlled trial in early Barrett's neoplasia demonstrated no clear clinical benefit of ESD over EMR when lesions were amenable to both techniques. 31 Therefore, EMRL may be considered in carefully selected patients with gastrointestinal deep-layer lesions measuring ≤1.0 cm, but its role relative to ESD requires confirmation in prospective studies with long-term follow-up.

Previous studies have suggested that EMRL may offer advantages over ESD for selected small superficial gastrointestinal lesions; however, evidence remains limited for small gastrointestinal deep-layer lesions. With regard to procedure-related complications, reported perforation rates during endoscopic resection vary across techniques, ranging from 0.05% for polypectomy, 0.58%–0.8% for EMR, and 2%–14% for ESD. Reported delayed bleeding rates are approximately 1.6% for polypectomy, 1.15%–1.7% for EMR, and 0.7%–2.2% for ESD.32–35 In the present study, EMRL and ESD showed no significant difference in procedure-related complication rates after PSM. The R0 resection rates were also comparable between the two groups. These findings suggest that, for carefully selected gastrointestinal deep-layer lesions measuring 1.0 cm or smaller, EMRL may achieve similar short-term clinical outcomes to ESD.

A key finding of this study was that EMRL was consistently associated with lower hospitalization expenses. After PSM, hospitalization expenses remained significantly lower in the EMRL group than in the ESD group. In ordinal logistic regression, EMRL was significantly associated with a higher likelihood of belonging to a lower hospitalization expense category in the crude model, the primary adjusted model, and the sensitivity model further adjusting for actual length of hospital stay. Because length of hospital stay may partly mediate the relationship between procedure type and hospitalization expenses, it was not included in the primary adjusted model but was included in the sensitivity model. The persistence of the association after this additional adjustment suggests that the observed expense difference was not fully explained by hospital stay alone. These findings may reflect differences in procedural complexity, resource use, and treatment-related costs. However, because hospitalization expenses were measured as nominal charges and were not standardized for calendar year, inflation, or temporal changes in hospital charging policies, the magnitude of the observed expense difference should be interpreted cautiously.

Strengths and limitations

This study focused on a specific and clinically relevant subgroup of patients with gastrointestinal deep-layer lesions measuring 1.0 cm or smaller, for whom the optimal endoscopic procedure remains insufficiently defined. By directly comparing EMRL and ESD in this subgroup, the study provides evidence that may help inform individualized procedure selection. In addition, PSM was used to reduce baseline imbalance between the EMRL and ESD groups, thereby strengthening the comparability of clinical outcomes and hospitalization expenses between the two procedures. Furthermore, hospitalization expenses were evaluated using both direct group comparisons and ordinal logistic regression models, including a sensitivity model that further adjusted for actual length of hospital stay.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective single-center study, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, long-term follow-up data, including recurrence, repeat intervention, and salvage treatment, were not available; therefore, the long-term comparative effectiveness of EMRL and ESD could not be evaluated. In addition, procedure time was not consistently recorded in the retrospective database and therefore could not be reliably analyzed. Third, although PSM was used to reduce baseline imbalance, residual confounding could not be completely excluded because treatment selection may have been influenced by lesion characteristics, endoscopic ultrasonography findings, endoscopist judgment, and procedural feasibility.

Fourth, gastrointestinal deep-layer lesions comprise heterogeneous pathological entities with different biological behaviors and treatment considerations. Although final histopathological diagnoses were reported descriptively in Table 1, pathology-specific subgroup analyses were not performed because of the limited sample size within several diagnostic categories.

Fifth, tissue depth was assessed preoperatively by endoscopic ultrasonography, and EUS-based assessment may not completely correspond to the final histological layer of origin. Lesions suspected to originate from the muscularis propria may also have influenced treatment selection and introduced residual selection bias. Sixth, although actual hospitalization expenses were compared as continuous data in unadjusted analyses, they were categorized for multivariable ordinal logistic regression. A multivariable continuous-cost regression model was not performed; therefore, categorization may have resulted in loss of information and reduced statistical efficiency. Seventh, although actual length of hospital stay was assessed in a sensitivity model, its role as a potential mediator between procedure type and hospitalization expenses should be interpreted with caution. Eighth, hospitalization expenses were not standardized for calendar year, inflation, reimbursement structures, or temporal changes in hospital charging policies over the 2012–2022 study period. Therefore, calendar-time confounding may have influenced the observed expense comparisons. Finally, although the sample size was larger than that of many single-center studies on this specific subgroup, larger multicenter studies are needed to validate these findings.

Conclusions

In this retrospective single-center cohort study, EMRL was associated with comparable short-term clinical outcomes and lower observed hospitalization expenses compared with ESD in selected patients with gastrointestinal deep-layer lesions measuring 1.0 cm or smaller. However, because of the lack of long-term follow-up, pathological heterogeneity, and potential residual confounding, definitive conclusions regarding comparative effectiveness cannot be drawn. Further large-scale, multicenter prospective studies with long-term follow-up are needed to confirm these findings.
