Diagnostic accuracy of AI-augmented renal ultrasound for degenerative kidney disorders: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mohammadreza Elhaie, Abolfazl Koozari, Amirsaman Soleimani Nasab, Fatemeh Taheri Dehaghi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated artificial intelligence (AI) and radiomics applied to renal ultrasound for the diagnosis, staging, and prognosis of degenerative kidney disorders.MethodsPubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and gray-literature sources were searched without date or language restrictions. Eligible studies applied AI or radiomics to renal ultrasound and reported diagnostic, staging, or prognostic outcomes. Risk of bias and methodological quality were assessed using the Prediction model Risk Of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST), Checklist for Artificial Intelligence in Medical Imaging (CLAIM 2024), and Radiomics Quality Score 2.0 (RQS 2.0). Random-effects models were used for diagnostic accuracy synthesis.ResultsThirty-one studies were included. Machine-learning models achieved pooled sensitivity of 0.86 (95% confidence interval 0.82–0.90) and specificity of 0.83 (0.79–0.87); deep-learning models achieved sensitivity of 0.89 (0.84–0.93) and specificity of 0.85 (0.81–0.91). Heterogeneity was substantial and external validation was uncommon.ConclusionsAI-augmented renal ultrasound shows promising diagnostic performance, but heterogeneous populations, limited calibration, and predominantly internal validation constrain clinical generalizability. Prospective multicenter external validation is required.

Introduction

Degenerative kidney disorders encompass conditions such as chronic kidney disease (CKD) [1]. Ultrasound imaging has emerged as a cornerstone diagnostic tool in nephrology due to its noninvasive nature, real-time capabilities, cost-effectiveness, and lack of ionizing radiation, enabling the assessment of renal morphology, vascularity, and parenchymal changes [2]. However, conventional ultrasound interpretation relies heavily on subjective visual assessment by clinicians, which can be limited by inter-observer variability, challenges in quantifying subtle textural abnormalities, and difficulties in early detection of degenerative processes [3].

Despite these advancements, the literature on AI and radiomics in ultrasound for degenerative kidney disorders remains fragmented, with varying methodologies, sample sizes, and validation approaches across studies [4,5]. This systematic review aims to critically appraise and synthesize the existing evidence on the application of AI and radiomics in ultrasound imaging for the diagnosis, monitoring, and management of degenerative kidney disorders.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [6]. The review protocol was prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD420261277386. No amendments were made to the protocol during the review process. The methods adhered to established standards for systematic reviews in medical imaging and artificial intelligence applications, ensuring transparency, reproducibility, and minimization of bias.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were selected based on the Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome, and Study Design (PICOS) framework to define inclusion and exclusion criteria. The PICOS elements are detailed in Table 1.

Information sources and search strategy

Electronic databases were searched from inception to the date of the final search, with no date or language restrictions. The following databases were included: PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Library. Gray literature sources, such as arXiv, medRxiv, and conference proceedings from relevant societies, were also screened. Reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were hand-searched for additional citations. The full search strategy, categorized by PICOS elements, is presented in Table 2.

Study selection

Citations were imported into EndNote 21 (Clarivate Analytics) for deduplication. Two independent reviewers (initials to be added) screened titles and abstracts for relevance using Rayyan (rayyan.ai), a web-based tool for systematic review management. Full-text articles were retrieved for potentially eligible studies and assessed against the PICOS criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (initials to be added). Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were documented. The study selection process is illustrated in a PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1).

PRISMA flow diagram.

Data collection and items

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers using a standardized, piloted form in Microsoft Excel. Extracted items included: study characteristics (author, year, country, design, sample size); population details (age, sex, disorder type, disease stage); intervention specifics (AI algorithm type, radiomics features used, ultrasound modality [e.g., B-mode, Doppler]); comparator details; outcomes (primary and secondary metrics with confidence intervals); validation methods (e.g., cross-validation, external cohorts); and funding sources/conflicts of interest. For radiomics studies, additional items included feature selection methods, software used, and adherence to the Radiomics Quality Score 2.0 (RQS 2.0). Discrepancies in data extraction were resolved by consensus [7]. Potential overlap between study populations was assessed by comparing study centers, recruitment periods, participant characteristics, sample sizes, ultrasound platforms, and author groups. When publications appeared to originate from potentially overlapping cohorts, they were treated as separate reports only when they evaluated distinct imaging approaches, outcomes, or independently defined datasets; possible residual cohort overlap was considered when interpreting the evidence.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias in included studies was evaluated using the Prediction model Risk Of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST) for prognostic modeling studies [8]. PROBAST comprises four main domains (participants, predictors, outcome, and analysis) and studies were classified as having low, high, or unclear risk of bias and applicability concerns. Assessments were conducted independently by two reviewers, with disagreements arbitrated by a third. No studies were excluded based on risk of bias; instead, results were interpreted in light of these assessments.

Methodological quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using the RQS 2.0 [7] and the updated Checklist for Artificial Intelligence in Medical Imaging (CLAIM 2024) [9]. RQS 2.0 builds on the original Radiomics Quality Score proposed in 2017, expanding it to reflect current needs in radiomics research and integrating Radiomics Readiness Levels (RRLs), which define nine stages from early exploratory research to clinically deployable radiomics tools; only radiomics studies were assessed with RQS 2.0. CLAIM 2024, which consists of 44 items covering title/abstract, introduction, methods (data, ground truth, model development, and evaluation), results, and discussion, was applied to all AI studies. For each study, every item was judged by two reviewers, with disagreements between the two resolved by consensus.

Certainty assessment

The certainty of evidence for key outcomes was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [10].

Statical analysis

A meta-analysis was conducted on the subset of studies reporting sufficient homogeneous data for diagnostic accuracy outcomes, focusing on the performance of AI models in detecting and classifying degenerative kidney disorders via ultrasound. Due to the observed heterogeneity in study designs, populations, and validation methods, random-effects models were employed using RevMan 5.4 software.

Results

Study selection

The comprehensive literature search across multiple databases and gray literature sources identified a total of 5,319 records. After deduplication, 2,454 unique records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 2,177 were excluded as they did not meet the initial relevance criteria based on the PICOS framework (e.g., unrelated imaging modalities, non-degenerative renal conditions, or non-original research). This left 277 full-text articles for detailed eligibility assessment.

During full-text review, 246 articles were excluded for the following reasons: 127 did not involve ultrasound as the primary modality; 66 focused on non-degenerative kidney disorders (e.g., acute injuries or infections); 35 were review articles, editorials, or conference abstracts without sufficient data; and 18 lacked quantitative AI/radiomics outcomes. No studies were excluded due to language or publication date, in line with the unrestricted eligibility criteria. Ultimately, 31 studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis. Furthermore, meta-analyses were performed on 28. The study selection process is summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1).

Study characteristics

The 31 included studies encompassed a diverse range of designs, primarily retrospective (n = 16) and prospective (n = 10) cohort studies, with two cross-sectional analyses, one mixed prospective/retrospective study, and two studies with unreported designs. Sample sizes varied considerably, from small cohorts of 31 participants (e.g., pediatric studies) to larger datasets exceeding 1,000 patients or images (e.g., in CKD classification tasks) [4,11–18,20–22,25–40]. Study characteristics, including patient demographics, are summarized in Table 3. Validation strategies were predominantly internal, including random train-test splitting and k-fold cross-validation. Only a small minority of studies evaluated model performance in truly independent external cohorts. Consequently, the reported performance estimates largely reflect internal model validity rather than established transportability across institutions, populations, or ultrasound platforms.

Diagnosis of degenerative kidney disorders

Ten studies focused on diagnostic classification of degenerative kidney disorders versus healthy or non-CKD controls. Machine learning models incorporating radiomics features from B-mode ultrasound achieved AUCs ranging from 0.82 to 0.96. Deep learning approaches generally demonstrated superior performance, with ResNet-based models reporting AUCs of 0.90–0.92 and accuracies up to 96% in large cohorts [15,17,20,29,38]. Multimodal models combining B-mode with elastography or Doppler parameters further improved early detection, particularly in diabetic kidney disease, where sensitivities of 85–92% were achieved [30,39]. In transplant-related degeneration, machine learning yielded AUCs of 0.94–0.97, although sample sizes were small. A pediatric pilot study demonstrated the feasibility of ultrasound radiomics for differentiating acute kidney injury from chronic kidney disease, although the small sample size limits generalizability [13]. Deep learning models consistently outperformed traditional machine learning, especially for early-stage disease where conventional ultrasound interpretation is limited. Detailed study-level methodology and performance metrics are provided in **Supplementary Table S1**.

Classification of disease severity

Nineteen studies evaluated severity classification, predominantly grading fibrosis [11,12,16,18,25,26], IFTA [4,14,21,31,33], CKD staging [32,34,37], glomerulopathy [21,35], chronicity index [22]. Radiomics-based machine learning models classified mild versus moderate-to-severe fibrosis with AUCs of 0.85–0.98 and accuracies of 85–95% in prospective Chinese cohorts [11,12,14,16,18,21,25,26] Deep learning convolutional neural networks (CNNs) excelled in multi-class IFTA grading (0–3), achieving patient-level accuracies of 88–92% in retrospective analyses of over 6,000 images [33].

In DKD, radiomics nomograms differentiated mild from moderate-severe glomerulopathy with AUCs up to 0.85 in 100–200 patient groups, primarily utilizing B-mode radiomics combined with clinical factors [21]. Hybrid models combining ultrasound modalities with radiomics improved early RF classification, with sensitivities exceeding 80% for minimal vs. mild changes [21]. Subgroup analysis by validation rigor showed modest performance drops (AUC 0.03–0.12) in studies with external cohorts, underscoring generalizability challenges. Machine learning/radiomics approaches were more common for feature-based tasks, while deep learning handled image-heavy classification effectively.

Prognosis and prediction of progression

Two studies [28,36] focused on prognostic applications or longitudinal monitoring, predicting progression risks or functional changes. In pediatric posterior urethral valves, deep learning models on ultrasound images forecasted risk of CKD progression with concordance indices of 0.70–0.82, where ensemble models integrating clinical variables outperformed imaging alone. In adult CKD and DKD, studies primarily predicted current disease status or function (e.g., eGFR or histological grade) rather than future survival. Pediatric prognostic models for posterior urethral valves achieved an AUC of 0.85 (at 5 years) for CKD progression risk stratification using multi-center data. For transplant degeneration, prediction of rejection yielded AUCs of 0.97, though limited to small retrospective samples [40]. Prognostic performance was moderate overall, with deep learning showing advantages in predicting longitudinal outcomes from baseline imaging, but studies generally lacked robust external validation and long-term follow-up.

Risk of bias and applicability concerns

Most included prediction model studies showed low risk of bias and low applicability concerns for participants, predictors, and outcomes, but several had important limitations in the analysis domain. Overall, a meaningful proportion of studies were judged at high or unclear overall risk of bias, mainly driven by suboptimal handling of sample size, overfitting, and validation procedures. Methodological quality assessment

All 31 included AI studies were evaluated using the CLAIM 2024 checklist, demonstrating overall good reporting quality with a mean adherence of 66.1% (SD = 11.1%, range 45.2%–90.5%) across applicable items. The median adherence was 64.3%, indicating that most studies adequately reported the majority of recommended AI-specific methodological elements. Six CLAIM items showed suboptimal reporting (adherence <50%), primarily related to data de-identification methods (Item 11), handling of missing data (Item 12), rationale for reference standard (Item 15), inter- and intrarater variability (Item 18), intended sample size (Item 19), initialization of model parameters (Item 24), ensembling techniques (Item 27), robustness or sensitivity analysis (Item 30), testing on external data (Item 33), failure analysis (Item 37), and availability of software/data (Item 43). These gaps suggest that while basic methodological transparency is generally adequate, advanced considerations such as model interpretability, generalizability documentation, and fairness evaluation remain underreported in the current literature. The full Quality Assessment scoring for each included study is provided in Supplementary Appendix 1 (Excel format).

The 13 radiomics studies [4,11–14,16,20–22,26,27,29,35] were assessed using RQS 2.0, achieving a mean score of 16.9 out of 56 points (30.2%, SD = 9.1%), with scores ranging from 9 to 25 (16%–44.6%). The median score was 15.0 points (26.8%), indicating that most studies fulfilled approximately one-quarter of the RQS 2.0 criteria. Studies performed best at RRL1–RRL4 levels, consistently reporting inclusion criteria, image protocols, feature reduction strategies, and internal validation procedures, while higher readiness levels (RRL5–RRL9) were rarely met. Critical deficiencies were observed in feature robustness testing, image harmonization, open science practices (data/code sharing), explainability methods, fairness evaluation, and real-world usability assessment. Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence for the principal pooled diagnostic outcomes was assessed using the GRADE framework. Overall certainty was judged to be low for the principal machine-learning and deep-learning diagnostic accuracy outcomes. The main reasons for downgrading were substantial statistical inconsistency, methodological limitations identified by PROBAST, marked clinical indirectness arising from heterogeneous disease populations and reference standards, and the predominance of internal rather than external validation. Imprecision was less prominent because the pooled estimates generally had relatively narrow confidence intervals. Publication bias was not evident for the machine-learning analysis based on Egger’s test (p = 0.21), whereas formal assessment was limited for smaller deep-learning analyses. These ratings indicate that although the pooled diagnostic estimates are promising, confidence in their generalizability to routine clinical practice remains limited.

Qualitative synthesis

Given the substantial heterogeneity across studies in patient populations, target conditions, ultrasound protocols, device vendors, reference standards, and AI methodologies, a mixed-methods approach was adopted. Quantitative meta-analysis was performed on 28 studies with extractable diagnostic accuracy data using random-effects models. Complementing this, a narrative synthesis grouped findings by clinical application (CKD detection/staging, fibrosis/IFTA phenotyping, diabetic kidney disease, transplant-related degeneration, and pediatric nephropathies), algorithmic paradigm (radiomics-based machine learning vs. deep learning), and validation strategy. The evidence base is heavily concentrated in adult CKD and renal fibrosis, with both deep learning and radiomics approaches showing promising performance. However, most studies relied on internal validation only, with limited external testing, inconsistent acquisition parameters, and variable outcome definitions. These factors constrain generalizability and indicate that the current evidence remains developmental rather than ready for widespread clinical deployment.

Meta-analysis results

Heterogeneity was quantified with the I2 statistic, and publication bias was assessed via funnel plots and Egger’s test for analyses with ≥10 studies. Subgroup analyses were performed by AI type to explore performance differences. Twenty-seven unique studies contributed 28 model-specific entries to the quantitative synthesis, Overall, 19 studies provided extractable data for machine learning models [4,11,12,14,17,18,21,22,25–27,29,31,32,35,36,40], and 9 for deep learning models, with outcomes primarily centered on binary classification tasks [14,15,19,20,28,30,33,38,39].

Machine learning models

The random-effects summary receiver operating characteristic analysis for ultrasound-based machine learning models demonstrated overall good diagnostic discrimination, with the summary operating point located at an estimated sensitivity of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.82–0.90) and a false-positive rate of 0.17 (specificity of 0.83; 95% CI: 0.79–0.87). The clustered distribution of individual study estimates in the upper-left quadrant of the ROC space suggests consistently high sensitivity with moderate-to-high specificity across studies; however, the 95% predictive region was substantially broader than the 95% confidence region, indicating meaningful between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 78% for sensitivity and 82% for specificity) and potential variability in real-world performance across clinical settings. This heterogeneity is likely attributable to differences in case-mix, reference standards, feature engineering/selection, thresholding strategies, and validation rigor.

Forest plots further illustrated the variability in accuracy metrics, with sensitivity estimates spanning 0.65–1.00 and specificity spanning 0.51–1.00, often accompanied by variable confidence interval widths reflective of sample size disparities. Several studies reported comparatively lower specificity, while others achieved near-perfect estimates, patterns compatible with methodological variations. No significant publication bias was detected (Egger’s test p = 0.21). Collectively, these findings support the potential clinical utility of machine learning approaches, particularly radiomics-enhanced models, for detecting degenerative kidney disorders on ultrasound, while underscoring the need for standardized reporting and robust external validation to mitigate heterogeneity and enhance generalizability (See Figures 4 and 5).

Deep learning models

For deep learning–based ultrasound models, the random-effects SROC analysis indicated strong overall diagnostic discrimination, with the pooled summary point positioned in the upper-left portion of the ROC space, corresponding to a high sensitivity of 0.89 (95% CI: 0.84–0.93) and a low false-positive rate of 0.13–0.15 (specificity of 0.85–0.87; 95% CI: 0.81–0.91). Most individual study estimates concentrated near this region, suggesting generally favorable performance; nonetheless, the 95% predictive ellipse was considerably larger than the 95% confidence ellipse, reflecting substantial between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 72% for sensitivity and 76% for specificity) and implying that performance may vary across populations, ultrasound protocols, and imaging settings.

Consistent with the SROC findings, the forest plots revealed wide ranges in accuracy metrics, with sensitivity spanning 0.59–0.99 and specificity spanning 0.61–0.97, alongside variable confidence interval widths likely related to differences in dataset size, case-mix, model architecture, threshold selection, and validation strategies. Egger’s test showed no evidence of publication bias (p = 0.34). Overall, the evidence highlights the promise of deep learning for ultrasound-based assessment of degenerative kidney disorders, with potentially superior discrimination compared to traditional machine learning in image-heavy tasks, while emphasizing the importance of more consistent methodologies and rigorous external validation to improve reproducibility and generalizability.

Discussion

Overview of the evidence base and principal findings

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized evidence from 31 studies evaluating artificial intelligence and radiomics applications in ultrasound for degenerative kidney disorders. The quantitative meta-analysis of 28 diagnostic studies revealed robust performance, with machine learning models achieving pooled sensitivity of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.82–0.90) and specificity of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.79–0.87), while deep learning models demonstrated superior discrimination with sensitivity of 0.89 (95% CI: 0.84–0.93) and specificity of 0.85 (95% CI: 0.81–0.91). These findings suggest that AI-augmented ultrasound has promising diagnostic performance; however, the magnitude and generalizability of this benefit remain uncertain because of substantial clinical and statistical heterogeneity. Importantly, diagnostic detection, disease-severity classification, histopathological phenotyping, and longitudinal prognostic prediction represent distinct clinical questions; accordingly, these applications are interpreted separately throughout the narrative synthesis rather than regarded as interchangeable measures of AI performance. Across the included evidence, a consistent pattern emerged: models that quantify parenchymal microstructural and functional information (e.g., radiomics texture, viscoelasticity, microvascular flow) generally outperformed conventional B-mode assessment alone, particularly for tasks where morphological changes are subtle (early CKD; early fibrosis; early diabetic nephropathy). For example, a deep learning radiomics approach using routine grey-scale ultrasound improved CKD detection and early-stage discrimination compared with radiologists, with strong performance for CKD vs non-CKD classification (AUC 0.918) and notably higher AUCs than senior readers for early stages (e.g., G1 and G2) [20]. Similarly, radiomics-clinical nomograms for renal pathology phenotyping (e.g., diabetic kidney disease glomerular status) improved discrimination relative to clinical-only or radiomics-only models, supporting the view that ultrasound-derived quantitative features provide complementary information to standard clinical indices [21]. At the same time, the evidence base remains dominated by single-center cohorts and internal validation strategies, which constrains inference about real-world generalizability. This tension—promising discrimination within curated datasets versus uncertain transportability—recurs across disease types and outcome definitions. The pooled estimates should nevertheless be interpreted as broad summary measures rather than disease-specific diagnostic benchmarks. The included studies addressed clinically distinct targets, including CKD detection and staging, renal fibrosis/IFTA assessment, diabetic kidney disease, transplant-related abnormalities, and pediatric nephropathies. These conditions differ in pathophysiology, reference standards, disease prevalence, and ultrasound manifestations; consequently, a single pooled estimate cannot be assumed to represent identical diagnostic performance across all clinical settings. The quantitative findings should therefore be considered complementary to the disease- and task-specific narrative synthesis presented in this review.

Performance by AI algorithm type

Subgroup analysis revealed that deep learning approaches consistently outperformed traditional machine learning across multiple clinical applications. Among machine learning algorithms, XGBoost emerged as the dominant classifier in head-to-head comparisons, achieving primary dataset AUCs of 0.97 in Chen et al. fibrosis grading study [18] and external validation AUCs of 0.95 in Wu et al. multicenter investigation [16]. Random Forest models also demonstrated strong performance, particularly in Wu’s study where it outperformed six competing algorithms including SVM, Multilayer Perceptron, and Naive Bayes [16]. However, this apparent algorithm superiority warrants cautious interpretation. The dominance of tree-based ensemble methods (XGBoost, Random Forest, LightGBM) may reflect their robustness to high-dimensional, relatively small-sample radiomics datasets rather than capturing true biological signal superiority. Additionally, potential publication bias exists—studies may preferentially report their best-performing classifier while underreporting negative results from alternative algorithms tested during model development.

Multimodal fusion approaches consistently outperformed single-modality models across included studies. Chen and Wu et al. demonstrated that integrating grayscale ultrasound radiomics (AUC: 0.79), color Doppler radiomics (AUC: 0.70), and shear wave elastography (AUC: 0.74) achieved a combined AUC of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.82–0.94) for renal fibrosis classification, with reclassification analysis showing NRI of 65.5% and IDI of 12.4% compared to baseline models [12]. Qin similarly reported that ultrasound-based radiomics models (AUC: 0.894–0.919) outperformed both conventional color Doppler ultrasound parameters (AUC: 0.686–0.784) and isolated deep learning approaches (AUC: 0.670–0.880) [14].

An emerging methodological advance identified in this review is viscoelasticity imaging. Zhu et al. introduced Sound Touch Viscosity technology, which measures both tissue elasticity and viscosity parameters based on the Voigt biomechanical model, achieving an AUC of 0.932 for CKD diagnosis. Notably, viscosity parameters contributed approximately 81.5% of the model’s predictive weight and were elevated in CKD patients compared to controls substantially outweighing elasticity measurements alone [17]. This represents a evolution beyond conventional shear wave elastography and suggests that biomechanical tissue characterization may capture pathophysiological changes earlier in the disease process than morphological or echogenicity features.

Model transparency and interpretability

The transparency and interpretability of the evaluated AI approaches also differ substantially and should be considered alongside diagnostic performance. Conventional radiomics-based machine-learning models generally provide greater opportunities for examining selected imaging features and their relative contributions through coefficients or feature-importance estimates, although complex ensemble algorithms such as Random Forest and XGBoost are not intrinsically transparent. Deep-learning models may achieve stronger image-level discrimination but are typically less directly interpretable because relevant representations are learned within multiple latent network layers. Post-hoc explainability approaches, including feature-attribution methods, saliency or attention maps, and model-specific visualization techniques, can partially clarify model decisions; however, such approaches were inconsistently reported across the included literature. Multimodal and hybrid models introduce an additional tradeoff, as integration of clinical, radiomic, hemodynamic, and elastographic information may improve performance while simultaneously increasing model complexity. Consistent with our CLAIM 2024 and RQS 2.0 assessments, interpretability, explainability, failure analysis, and availability of software or data remain important reporting gaps. Future studies should therefore report not only diagnostic accuracy but also the basis of model predictions, feature-selection procedures, preprocessing steps, model architecture, decision thresholds, uncertainty, explainability analyses, and failure modes to enable reproducibility, clinician trust, and meaningful comparison between competing models.

Comparative performance across clinical targets

Several studies focused on fibrosis grading (including interstitial fibrosis/tubular atrophy), reflecting the clinical importance of noninvasive alternatives to biopsy. A multi-modality approach combining grayscale radiomics, color Doppler radiomics, and shear wave elastography (SWE) improved discrimination versus any single modality (multi-modality AUC 0.88 vs 0.74 for SWE, 0.79 for grayscale radiomics, 0.70 for color Doppler radiomics) [12]. In parallel, nomograms integrating B-mode and strain elastography (STE) radiomics with clinical variables produced high AUCs for IFTA stratification (e.g., AUC 0.93 training; 0.83 validation for mild-to-moderate vs severe), with statistically gains over clinical-only and single-modality radiomics models [4]. These convergent findings suggest that fibrosis is particularly amenable to feature fusion approaches that capture both tissue biomechanics and heterogeneous texture signatures.

Moving upstream in disease, a prospective fusion deep learning model targeting minimal (≤10%) versus mild (11–25%) fibrosis reported improved clinical benefit on decision curve analysis and offered feature attribution indicating that microvascular perfusion (SMI) dominated model contribution (65.4%), followed by strain elastography (27.5%), while grayscale ultrasound contributed least (7.1%) [21].

This is clinically coherent: early fibrosis may not manifest as overt morphological change on B-mode, making perfusion- and elasticity-sensitive modalities more informative.

For CKD screening and staging, the deep learning radiomics model trained on cortex-focused ROIs demonstrated superiority to radiologist interpretation for early stages while achieving comparable performance for advanced disease—an important distinction because late-stage CKD is often easier to recognize due to more evident structural changes [21]. This pattern supports a practical interpretation: AI may add the most value where human readers have the greatest difficulty—namely, early-to-intermediate disease where changes are diffuse and subtle.

In diabetic kidney disease, an ultrasound radiomics-clinical nomogram improved test cohort discrimination for glomerular lesion status (AUC 0.850) relative to either clinical factors alone (AUC 0.729) or radiomics alone (AUC 0.761), with decision curve analysis indicating higher net benefit for the combined approach [21]. This aligns with the broader observation that in systemic diseases (e.g., diabetes), clinical biomarkers and imaging capture different facets of pathology and should be modeled jointly. These observations should also be interpreted within the broader cardiorenal and metabolic context of CKD. Diabetes, hypertension, obesity, systemic inflammation, vascular disease, and other cardiovascular risk factors contribute to renal microvascular dysfunction and parenchymal remodeling and may therefore influence ultrasound-derived structural, hemodynamic, and radiomic signatures. AI models that integrate imaging features with relevant clinical and laboratory variables may consequently provide more clinically meaningful risk characterization than imaging-only approaches. Future studies should explicitly account for these systemic determinants during model development and validation.

Evidence also extended beyond adult CKD/fibrosis. A pediatric pilot explored radiomics for distinguishing AKI vs CKD—an unmet need given the limitations of clinical and biochemical markers alone in children [13]. Additionally, radiomics-based classification of glomerulopathy subtypes (e.g., membranous nephropathy vs IgA nephropathy) achieved AUCs >0.7 across multiple classical classifiers, indicating feasibility but also suggesting room for improvement and the need for larger, multi-institutional datasets [35]. Pediatric evidence should be interpreted separately from the predominantly adult literature. Only a small proportion of the included studies evaluated children, and pediatric renal disease differs substantially from adult CKD in etiology, renal maturation, disease prevalence, imaging characteristics, and clinical reference standards. The pooled findings from the predominantly adult evidence base therefore should not be directly extrapolated to pediatric practice, and dedicated multicenter pediatric validation studies are required.

Why multimodality and feature fusion tend to outperform single-source models

Multimodal fusion approaches consistently outperformed single-modality models by integrating complementary physiological signals from B-mode texture and structure, hemodynamics (color Doppler or superb microvascular imaging), and biomechanics (shear wave or strain elastography) [12]. In key studies, combining grayscale and color Doppler radiomics with elastography markedly improved discrimination and reclassification for renal fibrosis compared with any individual modality [21].

This superior performance arises because fusion models capture multiple facets of early tissue remodeling that are incompletely reflected by single techniques, while also increasing robustness against known measurement variability [18].

Calibration and external/geographic validation

Beyond discrimination, calibration and independent validation are essential for determining whether an AI model is clinically reliable and transferable. Most included studies emphasized discrimination metrics such as AUC, sensitivity, and specificity, whereas calibration was insufficiently evaluated or reported. A model may demonstrate strong discrimination while still systematically overestimating or underestimating the probability of disease in a new population; therefore, discrimination alone does not establish clinical reliability. External validation was also uncommon, with only a small minority of studies testing models in genuinely independent cohorts. Geographic validation is particularly important because differences in disease prevalence, patient case-mix, ethnicity, ultrasound equipment, acquisition protocols, and institutional practice may substantially alter model performance. Future studies should therefore evaluate discrimination together with calibration using measures such as calibration plots, calibration slope and intercept, and, where appropriate, the Brier score, followed by prospective validation in geographically and institutionally independent populations. Where performance deteriorates across settings, model recalibration should be considered before clinical implementation. These methodological requirements are critical for meaningful comparison of competing AI models and for determining their transportability to routine clinical practice.

Limitations of current evidence

Several methodological limitations substantially constrain interpretation of the pooled estimates. Statistical heterogeneity was high for the principal diagnostic outcomes, with I2 values exceeding 70% and reaching >80% for some estimates. This variability is clinically plausible given the marked differences among studies in disease spectrum and severity, patient age, ultrasound acquisition protocols, device manufacturers, reference standards, feature engineering, AI architectures, classification thresholds, and validation strategies. Accordingly, the pooled sensitivity and specificity values should not be interpreted as universally transferable performance estimates for a single renal disorder or clinical setting. Rather, they provide an overall indication of the diagnostic potential of AI-augmented renal ultrasound across a heterogeneous and predominantly developmental evidence base. The wide prediction regions further suggest that performance in a new clinical population may differ meaningfully from the pooled point estimates. Renal and systemic comorbidities were also incompletely and inconsistently reported across the included studies. Coexisting diabetic nephropathy, hypertension, glomerular disease, vascular abnormalities, previous transplantation, and other chronic renal conditions may alter renal echogenicity, vascularity, elasticity, and texture independently of the primary target condition. Residual confounding from these factors may therefore contribute to between-study variability and may partly influence the apparent performance of AI-derived imaging biomarkers. The majority of studies relied exclusively on internal validation, with only three conducting true external validation in independent cohorts, which likely leads to overoptimistic performance estimates. Risk of bias assessment using PROBAST revealed frequent concerns, particularly in the analysis domain (sample size justification, missing data handling, and overfitting). Reporting quality remained variable despite CLAIM 2024 and RQS 2.0 assessments, with inconsistent documentation of acquisition parameters and limited evaluation of clinical utility metrics such as calibration and cost-effectiveness. Formal leave-one-out and risk-of-bias-restricted sensitivity analyses were not consistently feasible because of the limited number of clinically comparable studies within several target-specific groups and the substantial methodological diversity of the included literature. Consequently, the robustness of the pooled estimates to individual influential studies remains uncertain and should be considered when interpreting the summary results. Collectively, these issues indicate that the evidence base, while promising, remains developmental rather than ready for widespread clinical deployment.

Implications for clinical practice

The accumulated evidence indicates that AI-augmented renal ultrasound is promising but remains insufficiently validated for routine clinical implementation. The most appropriate next step is prospective multicenter clinical-impact evaluation, particularly for applications such as early CKD detection and noninvasive fibrosis assessment in well-defined high-risk populations. The most valuable applications include distinguishing early fibrosis where conventional imaging is limited, noninvasive serial monitoring to reduce biopsy frequency, pre-biopsy risk stratification, and point-of-care screening in resource-limited settings. However, widespread adoption will require standardized acquisition protocols across vendors, automated segmentation pipelines, prospective validation demonstrating non-inferiority to standard care, seamless integration into clinical workflows, and targeted implementation research to address clinician trust, reimbursement, and training barriers.

Take-home messages

Taken together, several practical messages emerge from the current evidence. AI-augmented renal ultrasound demonstrates promising diagnostic performance, particularly for subtle early-stage disease and fibrosis phenotyping, with deep-learning and multimodal approaches often achieving the strongest discrimination. Nevertheless, higher discrimination should not be equated with greater clinical readiness. The current evidence remains clinically heterogeneous, predominantly internally validated, incompletely calibrated, and variably transparent, while independent external and geographic validation remains limited. Accordingly, future evaluation of renal ultrasound AI should move beyond AUC, sensitivity, and specificity and simultaneously consider calibration, external transportability, reproducibility, explainability, and clinical utility. A slightly less discriminative but well-calibrated, transparent, reproducible, and externally validated model may ultimately have greater clinical value than a higher-performing model that remains a poorly validated “black box.” At present, these technologies should therefore be considered promising clinical decision-support tools requiring further prospective validation rather than replacements for expert imaging and clinical assessment.

Future research directions

Future research should prioritize large-scale prospective multicenter validation studies with external testing across geographic regions, ethnic populations, and ultrasound platforms, alongside the development and validation of prognostic models for CKD progression and long-term outcomes. Additional priorities include standardization of radiomics methodology through consensus guidelines on acquisition, preprocessing, and quality control; integration of automated segmentation for real-time deployment; multi-omics frameworks combining imaging with genomics, proteomics, and metabolomics; pragmatic implementation trials in resource-limited settings; health economic analyses of cost-effectiveness; and regulatory science research to establish validation standards for AI diagnostic devices in nephrology.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis suggests that AI- and radiomics-based analysis of renal ultrasound can achieve promising diagnostic performance across several degenerative kidney applications. However, clinical translation is currently limited by methodological heterogeneity, predominance of internal validation, manual segmentation requirements, and lack of prospective implementation evidence. Advancing from promising laboratory performance to clinical deployment will require rigorous external validation in diverse populations and settings, automated end-to-end pipelines, and pragmatic trials demonstrating real-world effectiveness and cost-effectiveness.
