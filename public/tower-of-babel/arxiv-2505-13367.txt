Reflection phase shifts of bouncing Bogoliubov waves
Carsten Henkel

Abstract
The Bogoliubov–de Gennes equations are solved for an inhomogeneous condensate in the vicinity of a turning point, addressing the full continuous spectrum. A basis change in the space of the two Bogoliubov “particle” and “hole” amplitudes is introduced that decouples them approximately. We find a spatially extended mode that governs mainly excitations in the condensate phase, while another mode is localised to regions with density gradients. An analytical and numerical discussion of the phase shift is provided that incident matter waves suffer upon reflection at the turning point, forming standing waves. As an application, we compute eigenfrequencies in a gravitational trap, without recourse to the local density approximation. The non-condensate density at finite temperature and its quantum depletion are discussed in a companion paper.



The boundary of a many-body system provides the arena to
archetypal interference phenomena familiar from water waves.
On the microscopic scale, the surface of a dense system is often
realised as a smooth cross-over between distinct phases.
Similar to critical fluctuations in a phase transition, fluctuations
are locally enhanced, as the stabilising effect of interactions
in the high-density region is reduced.
In this paper, we provide a detailed analysis of a degenerate bosonic gas
and its boundary,
as it may be realised in the Bose-Einstein condensed phase of ultracold
atomic vapours
(
Pitaevskii and
Stringari, 2016
)
.
We consider a quasi-one-dimensional setting where the high-density phase
is characterised by a reduction of density fluctuations,
while thermal phase fluctuations destroy long-range order

(
Proukakis et al., 2013
)
.
The focus will be on the boundary region where due to a rise
in the trapping potential, the condensate density drops
so that a transition zone emerges that interfaces
a dilute, quasi-ideal Bose gas and a dense degenerate gas.
Since there is no prediction for the equation of state
that is valid across this transition
(
Kerr et al., 2024
)
,
little theoretical work
beyond the local-density approximation has been reported
when it comes to spatial density profiles and correlation functions
at finite temperature
(
Öhberg et al., 1997
;
Fedichev and Shlyapnikov, 1998
;
Fetter and Feder, 1998
;
Proukakis et al., 1998
;
Rusch and Burnett, 1999
;
Kheruntsyan et al., 2005
)
.

The shape of the condensate wave function trapped in a potential
has been studied in detail, but with an understandable focus on
its central, high-density region.
Its tails towards tunnelling regions below the confining potential
can be accurately described by a universal mathematical
function, the Painlevé II transcendent
(
DLMF, 2025
)
, provided
the trapping potential can be linearised around its crossing
with the chemical potential (i.e., the generalised eigenvalue of the
nonlinear Gross-Pitaevskii equation).
Our aim is to provide a physics-motivated analysis
of the elementary excitations around this solution,
also known as Bogoliubov modes.
In the mathematical literature, matching formulas for the Painlevé II
function that provide its asymptotic form to the left and the right
of the turning point are well known
(
Ablowitz and Segur, 1977
)
, and these
also cover the case of an oscillatory solution.
In physical problems, a particular solution of the Gross-Pitaevskii equation
is singled out by requiring that it follows the Thomas-Fermi asymptote
on the high-density side, given by
Eq. (
2
) below.
This particular case represents in a sense the separatrix
between oscillatory and diverging solutions
(
Hastings and McLeod, 1980
)
,
at least for the linear potential.
This fact may play a role when it comes to linear perturbations
around the condensate, since a naïve integration of the
differential equations from low to high densities leads to a divergence,
due to exponentially increasing solutions.
We emphasise that this happens outside the tunnelling regime and
the solutions should behave as oscillatory functions.
The divergence does not show up for trapped systems where the
oscillations cover only a finite range between two turning points
where the potential is below the chemical potential.

This paper builds on and develops further numerically stable techniques
of earlier work for the continuous spectrum
(
Diallo and Henkel, 2015
)
.
The focus is on analysing the phase shift of the oscillatory Bogoliubov modes.
In scattering theory, such phase shifts provide essential information
about the potential that is accessible from the far-field asymptotics of
scattering solutions.
They determine, for example, cross sections or the lifetime
of resonances
(
Messiah, 1995
;
Berry and Mount, 1972
)
,
and in the case of optics, an effective displacement of a reflected beam
similar to the Goos-Hänchen shift
(
Goos and Hänchen, 1947
;
Henkel et al., 1994
)
.
We find that generic shapes of the potential in the vicinity of the turning
point lead to qualitatively different phase shifts when the energy of
the reflected Bogoliubov waves is scanned.
The linear potential which has been used frequently near a turning point
has the peculiar feature that within our numerical accuracy,
its phase shift vanishes.
In other potentials like a hard wall or an exponential soft barrier,
the reflection is dispersive, i.e., the phase shift depends on energy.
These results cannot be understood
by considering only the potential barrier
because of the mean-field potential due to the condensate density.
Our analysis reveals in particular certain contributions to the
phase shift due to the spatially inhomogeneous condensate density,
i.e., contributions beyond the local density approximation.

The basic equations of Bogoliubov theory are recalled in Sec.

phase shift may be defined.
Sec.
2
outlines the main analysis of the problem and
recalls an adiabatic scheme that eliminates divergence problems approximately.
Nonadiabatic corrections are dealt with in Subsec.
2.4
,
the particular case of the linear potential in Subsec.
2.5
.
Applications are discussed in Sec.
3
,
while more technical material and further plots have been relegated
to the Appendices.
In a companion paper
(
Henkel, 2025b
)
, the Bogoliubov modes are
used to compute quantum and thermal corrections to the density profile.
A brief analysis of the zero-point energy density of the Bose gas has been
given in
Henkel (2025a)
.
We find enhanced fluctuations in the
border region around the turning point, whose dependence on temperature
is quite different compared to a homogeneous system.
This illustrates the fallacies of the common implicit assumption
behind the local density approximation.
The issue of quantum fluctuations in the atomic density is of interest
to study non-trivial quantum vacuum effects in laboratory settings,
see, e.g.,
Roberts and Pomeau (2005)
;
Edmonds et al. (2023)
.
The turning point problem is also similar to the electron density
near the surface of a conductor
(
Lang, 1969
;
Kenner et al., 1972
)

and its excitations, for example
in the form of surface plasmons
(
Feibelman, 1982
;
Apell et al., 1984
;
Schaich, 1985
;
Liebsch, 1997
)
.
This problem has recently revived some interest in hydrodynamic models
for metallic electrons
(
Forcella et al., 2014
;
Toscano et al., 2015
;
Yan, 2015
;
Benedicto et al., 2015
;
Ciracì and Della Sala, 2016
;
Yang et al., 2019
;
Mortensen, 2021
)
.

1
Model

We consider a quasi-one-dimensional Bose condensate and focus on its border,
i.e., the region where the density smoothly drops to zero.
This can be implemented with a hard or a soft barrier:
the former case is equivalent to a Dirichlet boundary condition.
The collective wave function then rises from zero to a constant density,
on the scale of the healing length
defined by the nonlinearity of the wave equation,
i.e., particle interactions at the mean-field level.
A soft barrier, on the other hand, introduces the same length scale
as the one-particle Schrödinger equation,
where, e.g., the Airy function provides a smooth connection
to the tunnelling regime below the barrier.
In the physical solution to the nonlinear wave equation
for the condensate [Eq. (
1
) below],
the nonlinearity becomes dominant on the dense side of the turning point
where it suppresses the oscillatory behaviour of the Airy function.

1.2
Basic equations

Adopt suitable units, we set
\hbar^{2}/2M=1
,
and the nonlinear Schrödinger equation for the condensate
\phi
,
also known as Gross–Pitaevskii equation
(
Pitaevskii and
Stringari, 2016
)
,
takes the form

-\frac{{\rm d}^{2}\phi}{{\rm d}z^{2}}+V(z)\,\phi+g|\phi|^{2}\phi=\mu\phi

(1)

where the constant
g
scales the mean-field potential
g|\phi|^{2}>0
.

In Fig.
1
,
numerical results for the condensate density (obtained as outlined in
Sec.
1.4
below) are shown.
They illustrate a smooth cross-over
between vanishingly small values for
z\leq 0
and the Thomas-Fermi profile

z\to\infty:\quad g|\phi_{\rm TF}|^{2}(z)=\mu-V(z)

(2)

The latter is obtained by neglecting the derivative in Eq. (
1
).
Typical atom numbers within the healing length
1/\kappa

are of the order of
\mu/(g\kappa)\sim 5\ldots 50
.
The mean-field theory behind the Gross-Pitaevskii framework is valid if
this number is not small compared to unity
(
Mora and Castin, 2003
;
Kerr et al., 2024
)
.
A more quantitative comparison in terms of “missing particles” in the
region
around the turning point
z=0
is provided in the companion paper

(
Henkel, 2025b
)
, Appendix A.1.

The linearization around the mean field,

\phi\mapsto\phi+u\,{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}Et}+v^{*}\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}Et}

leads to the (stationary) Bogoliubov–de Gennes equations
that read, in the same units,

\displaystyle{-}\frac{{\rm d}^{2}u}{{\rm d}z^{2}}+V(z)\,u+2g|\phi|^{2}\,u+g\,\phi^{2}\,v

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mu+E)\,u\,,

\displaystyle{-}\frac{{\rm d}^{2}v}{{\rm d}z^{2}}+V(z)\,v+2g|\phi|^{2}\,v+g\,\phi^{*2}\,u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mu-E)\,v\,.

(3)

We fix the phases of
\phi,u,v
to be real, choosing positive
\phi
.
We also restrict the spectrum to
E>0
, since to a pair
(u,v)
with
energy
E
corresponds another pair
(v,u)
with
-E
.

The sum and difference of the Bogoliubov amplitudes physically
represent density and phase quadratures of the condensate,
as can be seen from

u\,{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}Et}+v^{*}\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}Et}=(u+v)\cos Et-{\rm i}(u-v)\sin Et
.
Defining the family of effective one-particle Hamiltonians

H_{n}=-\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}z^{2}}+V(z)-\mu+n\,g\phi^{2}\,,\qquad(n=1,2,3)\,,

(4)

we get from Eqs. (
3
) the equation pair

\displaystyle H_{1}\varphi_{-}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Ef_{+}\,,\qquad\varphi_{-}={(u-v)}{/\sqrt{2}}\,,

\displaystyle H_{3}f_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\varphi_{-}\,,\qquad f_{+}={(u+v)}{/\sqrt{2}}\,.

(5)

The potential
V_{1}=V-\mu+g\phi^{2}
is shown in thick blue in
Fig.
1
.
It quantifies the deviation of the condensate
density from its Thomas-Fermi approximation and therefore depends
on its spatial variation;
at large distance from the turning point, deep in the condensate,

V_{1}(z)\to 0
,
while
V_{1}(z)\to V-\mu
on the dilute side.

1.3
Exact solutions

For a hard-wall potential,
V(z)=0
for
z>0
,
and a Dirichlet boundary condition
\phi(0)=0
is imposed
(also on
u
and
v
).
In this simple case, exact solutions are known:
the so-called “dark soliton” provides a condensate wave function that
goes through zero
(
Pitaevskii and
Stringari, 2016
)
.
Cutting this solution in half, the Dirichlet boundary condition case
is solved by

\phi(z)=\sqrt{\mu/g}\,\tanh(\kappa z)\,,\qquad\kappa=\sqrt{\mu/2}=\sqrt{M\mu}/\hbar\,.

(6)

The Bogoliubov mode functions can also be found explicitly
and read
(
Kovrizhin, 2001
;
Dziarmaga, 2004
;
Negretti et al., 2008
;
Walczak and Anglin, 2011
)

\displaystyle u_{k}(z)=

\displaystyle A_{k}\,k\sin(kz)\left[\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}(\kappa z)}+\alpha_{k}\right]+2A_{k}\,\alpha_{k}\kappa\cos(kz)\tanh(\kappa z)

\displaystyle v_{k}(z)=

\displaystyle A_{k}\,k\sin(kz)\left[\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}(\kappa z)}-\beta_{k}\right]-2A_{k}\,\beta_{k}\kappa\cos(kz)\tanh(\kappa z)

(7)

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha_{k}\\
\beta_{k}\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{E\pm k^{2}}{\mu}

The energy is given by the Bogoliubov dispersion relation

E=|k|\sqrt{2\mu+k^{2}}

(11)

We choose the normalisation
A_{k}

such that in the region
\kappa z\gg 1

where the condensate
\phi(z)
is constant [see Eq. (
6
)],
we have

u_{k}(z)\to\frac{\cos(\theta_{k}/2)}{\sqrt{k}}\sin(kz+\delta)\,,\qquad v_{k}(z)\to-\frac{\sin(\theta_{k}/2)}{\sqrt{k}}\sin(kz+\delta)

(12)

The relative amplitudes of
u_{k}
and
v_{k}
are set by the angle

\theta_{k}

that we read off from Eqs. (
7
,
1.3
):

\tan\theta_{k}=\frac{2\alpha_{k}\beta_{k}}{\alpha_{k}^{2}-\beta_{k}^{2}}=\frac{\mu}{E}

(13)

With this choice, the wave functions are orthogonal in the
continuous spectrum, as has been shown in
Diallo and Henkel (2015)

\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\!\frac{{\rm d}z}{\pi}\left[u_{k}(z)u_{k^{\prime}}(z)-v_{k}(z)v_{k^{\prime}}(z)\right]=\delta(E-E^{\prime})

(14)

And finally, the phase shift
\delta
is given by

\tan\delta=\frac{2\kappa}{k}

(15)

This phase shift will be the main focus of this paper.
As can be seen in Eq. (
12
),
it describes
the position of the nodes of the
u
and
v
wave functions,
relative to the reference position
z=0
set by the hard-wall boundary condition.
For an illustration, see Fig.
2
below.

Another exact solution is available in the case of the linear potential.
The Gross-Pitaevskii Eq. (
1
)
is solved by the Painlevé II transcendent
(
Ablowitz and Segur, 1977
;
Hastings and McLeod, 1980
;
Dalfovo et al., 1996
)
.
It interpolates at low densities to
the Airy function familiar from the Schrödinger equation in the linear potential,

z\to-\infty:\quad\phi^{2}(z)\to 2\,{\mathop{\rm Ai}}^{2}(-z)

(16)

(Here and in the following, we use the natural units
F=1=\ell=g
,
see Table
1
.)
The scale factor
2
is not arbitrary since we are dealing with a
nonlinear wave equation;
it is characteristic for that Painlevé II solution
that connects on the other side of the turning point to the Thomas-Fermi
approximation (
2
).
It has been shown by
Hastings and McLeod (1980)
that if the prefactor in
Eq. (
16
) is larger than
2
, then the solution
diverges for some finite value of
z>0
. If it is smaller, then
it has an oscillatory asymptote

z\to\infty:\quad\phi^{2}(z)\approx\frac{2d^{2}}{z^{1/2}}\sin^{2}\left(\textstyle\frac{2}{3}z^{3/2}-\textstyle\frac{3}{4}d^{2}\log z-c\right)

(17)

with some constants
d,c
.
If the prefactor, say
a^{2}
, in Eq. (
16
) approaches

2
,
Ablowitz and Segur (1977)
show that the constant
d
scales as

d^{2}\approx\frac{-1}{\pi}\log(1-a^{2}/2)

(18)

Coming back to the Thomas-Fermi behaviour of Eq. (
2
),
expanding
\phi(z)
around it and inserting this into the Gross-Pitaevskii
equation, finally yields the post-Thomas-Fermi series

(
Lundh et al., 1997
;
Margetis, 2000
)

z\to\infty:\quad\phi^{2}(z)=z-\frac{1}{4z^{2}}-\frac{9}{8z^{5}}+{\cal O}(z^{-8})

(19)

With the help of this expansion, we find that
the effective potential
U_{\rm ad}(z)
introduced in
Eq. (
65
) below approaches the asymptote

z\to\infty:\quad U_{\rm ad}(z)\to-\frac{E^{2}}{2z}-\frac{1}{4z^{2}}\,.

(20)

These slowly decaying terms constitute a reference potential
that is familiar from the Coulomb interaction
and centrifugal barriers, although the latter features a strange sign.
It has exact solutions right at the continuum threshold given by
ordinary Bessel functions
(
DLMF, 2025
)

j_{E}(z)=\sqrt{\pi z}\,J_{0}(E\sqrt{2z})\,,\qquad y_{E}(z)=\sqrt{\pi z}\,Y_{0}(E\sqrt{2z})

(21)

The first solution stays regular when extrapolated to
z\to 0
, while
the second one has a divergent slope there.
We have chosen a normalisation such that the Wronskian determinant
of the two solutions is
W[j_{E},y_{E}]=1
.
The Wronskian will turn out a useful tool to project a general solution
onto a pair of basis functions.
It is defined as
(
DLMF, 2025
;
Bender and Orszag, 1978
)

W[u,w]=u\frac{{\rm d}w}{{\rm d}z}-\frac{{\rm d}u}{{\rm d}z}w\,.

(22)

The actual solution
\varphi(z)
in the potential
U_{\rm ad}(z)

is, for large
z
, a linear combination of reference waves. It gives
access to the asymptotic behaviour of the Bogoliubov amplitudes
u(z)
,

v(z)
[see Eq. (
2.1
) below]
and thus defines the phase shift
\delta

z\to\infty:\qquad\varphi(z)\to j_{E}(z)\cos\delta-y_{E}(z)\sin\delta

(23)

More explicitly, a Wronskian yields
W[\varphi,j_{E}]\to A\sin\delta
.
According to the large-argument expansion of the Bessel functions
(
DLMF, 2025
)
,
we get, similar to Eq. (
12
),

\displaystyle E\sqrt{2z}\gg 1:\qquad\varphi(z)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{(2z)^{1/4}}{\sqrt{E}}\sin(E\sqrt{2z}+\pi/4+\delta)\,.

(24)

The square root in the sine argument makes the wavelength increase at large
z
,
in distinction to the constant wavelength of Eq. (
12
)
that applies to “flat-bottom” potentials.

1.4
Numerics

For numerical solutions of the Gross-Pitaevskii and Bogoliubov equations,
see, e.g.,
Xie et al. (2009)
;
Gao and Cai (2020)
;
Sadaka et al. (2024)
.
These approaches also cover two- and three-dimensional systems and are
mostly based on finite-element techniques.
In the one-dimensional system considered here,
the condensate solution can be found by minimising the Gross–Pitaevskii
energy functional (units with
\hbar^{2}/2m=1
)

{\cal F}[\phi]=\int\!{\rm d}z\left\{\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{{\rm d}\phi}{{\rm d}z}\Big)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(V-\mu)\phi^{2}+\frac{g}{4}\phi^{4}\right\}

(25)

When represented on a discrete grid, the
minimize

routine of scipy may be used

(
Virtanen et al., 2020
)
.
To improve the solution, we iterate the following recursive scheme

\delta\phi^{(n)}=H_{1}^{(n)}\phi^{(n)}\,,\quad H_{3}^{(n)}\chi^{(n+1)}=-\delta\phi^{(n)}\,,\quad\phi^{(n+1)}=\phi^{(n)}+\chi^{(n+1)}

(26)

where the mean-field potentials in
H_{1}^{(n)},H_{3}^{(n)}
are evaluated
based on
|\phi^{(n)}|^{2}
.
Convergence is achieved when the norm of the error
\delta\phi^{(n)}

in the computational domain drops below a predefined precision.

The Bogoliubov equations (
3
)
and the iterative problem (
26
)
may be solved with sparse linear algebra packages
(
Virtanen et al., 2020
)
,
using a finite-difference scheme
and suitable boundary conditions at both ends:
a Dirichlet boundary condition
at the left end (taken at
z=0
or sufficiently deep in the tunnelling
domain),
and Dirichlet or Neumann conditions on the right.
This yields a set of discrete eigenvalues
that is getting denser when the computational domain is enlarged.
To access the continuous spectrum with a smaller domain and
in particular at very low energies,
we use a Robin boundary condition, i.e.,
enforcing the logarithmic derivative

Z=(1/u)\,{\rm d}u/{\rm d}z
at the last grid point
z=L
.
This can be done by adjusting the second derivative
u_{zz}

on the grid.
With a grid spacing
{\rm d}z
, we take at
z=L

\displaystyle u_{zz}(L)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{\rm d}z}\Big[\frac{u(L-{\rm d}z)-u(L)}{{\rm d}z}+Z\,u(L)\Big]

(27)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{\rm d}z^{2}}\big[u(L-{\rm d}z)-(1-Z\,{\rm d}z)\,u(L)\big]

When a Dirichlet (or Neumann) boundary condition is applied
at
z=L
,
the number
-(1-Z\,{\rm d}z)
is replaced by
-2
(or
-1
).
(In the interior of the grid, the three-point stencil

(1,-2,1)/{\rm d}z^{2}
is used.)
The best accuracy is obtained when
Z
is
calculated with a derivative

{\rm d}u/{\rm d}z
evaluated at mid-point,
i.e., at
L+{\rm d}z/2
just outside the computational grid.
The logarithmic derivative can also implement boundary
conditions for tunnelling solutions,
computed according to the WKB approximation
(
Messiah, 1995
)
.
We have checked that the scheme is compatible with compact fourth-order
finite-difference schemes
(
Lele, 1992
;
Xie et al., 2009
)
that improve the
accuracy in
{\rm d}z
by two orders.

2
Analysis

2.1
Adiabatic basis

The eigenproblem formulation sketched in the previous section is facing
the issue that the Bogoliubov equations (
3
)
contain at least one unstable mode that grows exponentially fast.
To make analytical and numerical progress,
an adiabatic basis has been introduced by
Diallo and Henkel (2015)
.
It is given by the eigenvectors of the
2\times 2
matrix
\mathbf{M}

underlying the Bogoliubov system
[see Eq. (
60
) below].
For an intuitive understanding of the relevant transformation,
we introduce the canonical “position” and
“momentum” variables
(u,v,p={\rm d}u/{\rm d}z,q={\rm d}v/{\rm d}z)

and consider a joint rotation in the
uv
- and
pq
-planes:

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\varphi\\
f\\
\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{u}\\
{v}\\
\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\cos(\theta/2)&-\sin(\theta/2)\\
\sin(\theta/2)&\cos(\theta/2)\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{u}\\
{v}\\
\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{p}\\
\tilde{q}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{p}\\
{q}\end{array}\right)

In the special case
\theta=\pi/2
,
one recovers the difference and sum modes
\varphi_{-}
,

f_{+}
of Eq. (
5
).
Our notation conveys that
\varphi
shares many similarities
with the phase mode
\varphi_{-}
, while
f
is similar
to the the density mode
f_{+}
.
The rotation into the adiabatic basis requires, however,
a position-dependent angle
\theta(z)
.

Keeping
\theta
dependent on position,
computing the derivatives and using the Bogoliubov equations (
3
),
we find the following set of equations in this representation

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}z}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\varphi\\
f\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{p}\\
\tilde{q}\end{array}\right)+\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-\theta^{\prime}/2\\
\theta^{\prime}/2&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\varphi\\
f\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}z}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{p}\\
\tilde{q}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\varphi\\
f\end{array}\right)+\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-\theta^{\prime}/2\\
\theta^{\prime}/2&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{p}\\
\tilde{q}\end{array}\right)

The derivatives
\theta^{\prime}={\rm d}\theta/{\rm d}z

appear as off-diagonal elements.
This cross-coupling between “positions” and “momenta”
is reminiscent of the distinction between kinematic and canonical momenta
for a charged particle in two dimensions
in a magnetic field
B\propto\theta^{\prime}
.
In the second line of Eq. (
2.1
),

\mathbf{M}
is the Bogoliubov matrix

\mathbf{M}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}V_{2}(z)-E&g\phi^{2}(z)\\
g\phi^{2}(z)&V_{2}(z)+E\end{array}\right)

(60)

with
V_{2}(z)=V(z)-\mu+2g|\phi(z)|^{2}
.
It becomes diagonal when the angle
\theta
in the rotation matrix
\mathbf{R}

is chosen as

\tan\theta(z)=\frac{g\phi^{2}(z)}{E}

(61)

This is the position-dependent generalisation of
Eq. (
13
).
The eigenvalues of
\mathbf{M}
are

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{l}M_{\varphi}(z)\\
M_{f}(z)\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V_{2}(z)\mp\sqrt{E^{2}+g^{2}\phi^{4}(z)}\,.

A formulation that is slightly more explicit than Eq. (
2.1
)
is the following second-order system
for the adiabatic phase and density modes
(
Diallo and Henkel, 2015
)

\displaystyle-\frac{{\rm d}^{2}\varphi}{{\rm d}z^{2}}+U_{\rm ad}\,\varphi=\hat{L}f\,,

\displaystyle\qquad U_{\rm ad}=M_{\varphi}+(\theta^{\prime}/2)^{2}\,,

(65)

\displaystyle-\frac{{\rm d}^{2}f}{{\rm d}z^{2}}+V_{\rm ad}\,f=-\hat{L}\,\varphi\,,

\displaystyle\qquad V_{\rm ad}=M_{f}+(\theta^{\prime}/2)^{2}\,.

(66)

The derivatives of the condensate density appear in the potentials and,
via the differential operator

\hat{L}=\frac{\theta^{\prime\prime}}{2}+\theta^{\prime}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}z}\,,

(67)

they also couple the amplitudes
\varphi,f.

The decoupling of the two Bogoliubov modes has thus nearly succeeded
in the adiabatic basis:
if the variation of the condensate density is sufficiently slow or
the energy large enough,
we expect the “source terms”
\hat{L}\,f
,
-\hat{L}\,\varphi

on the rhs of Eqs. (
65
,
66
) to be small.

f_{k}

We have checked that
f_{k}
lies
below
the eigenfunctions in the potential
well
V_{\rm ad}
subject to the boundary condition at
z=0
.
Note also that the modes and their source terms scale quite differently:
the phase mode
\varphi_{k}
is much larger than
\hat{L}\,f_{k}/\mu
,
while the density mode
f_{k}
is comparable to
-\hat{L}\,\varphi_{k}/\mu
.

It is instructive to work through this formulation with the
local density approximation (LDA).
The latter amounts to replacing the chemical potential

\mu\mapsto\mu(z)=\mu-V(z)

and to use the Bogoliubov spectrum.
Inverting Eq. (
11
),
this leads to a local
k
-vector with

\mbox{LDA:}\quad k^{2}(z)=V(z)-\mu+\left[(\mu-V(z))^{2}+E^{2}\right]^{1/2}

(68)

If the Thomas-Fermi approximation is accurate, we have

V_{2}(z)\approx g\phi^{2}(z)\approx\mu-V(z)
and

k^{2}(z)\approx-M_{\varphi}(z)

from Eq. (
2.1
).
The adiabatic scheme goes beyond the LDA by including
corrections to
the Thomas-Fermi approximation and gradients in the
condensate density (derivative
\theta^{\prime}
and operator
\hat{L}
).

2.2
Adiabatic approximation

The data of Fig.
2

suggest an approximate solution to the Bogoliubov problem
that we call “adiabatic” in the following.
In a first step, we neglect the small source term
\hat{L}\,f

in Eq. (
65
) and solve

-\frac{{\rm d}^{2}\varphi^{({\rm ad})}}{{\rm d}z^{2}}+U_{\rm ad}\,\varphi^{({\rm ad})}=0

(69)

In the second step, this result is used as an approximation to the source term

\hat{L}\varphi
in Eq. (
66
)
to yield
f^{({\rm ad})}.

The potential
U_{\rm ad}
in the Schrödinger equation (
69
)
typically has a single turning point around
z\approx 0
,
as illustrated in Fig.
8
.
We re-write it in the form

U_{\rm ad}(z)=V_{1}(z)+D(z)+(\theta^{\prime}/2)^{2}

(70)

where the first term
V_{1}(z)=V(z)-\mu+g\phi^{2}(z)
was encountered
in Eq. (
4
).
The second piece

D(z)=g\phi^{2}(z)-\big[E^{2}+g^{2}\phi^{4}(z)\big]^{1/2}=-\frac{E^{2}}{g\phi^{2}(z)+\big[E^{2}+g^{2}\phi^{4}(z)\big]^{1/2}}

(71)

monotonically increases from
-E
to zero,
as the condensate density grows.
It determines the long-range behaviour of
U_{\rm ad}
:
the approach to its asymptote is slower for large
E
,
as can be seen in Fig.
8
.
Finally, the term
(\theta^{\prime}/2)^{2}=V_{\rm geo}
in
Eq. (
70
)
has been called the “geometric” potential
(
Diallo and Henkel, 2015
)
.
Similar to the geometric phase,
it arises because the adiabatic basis is position-dependent.

V_{\rm geo}
is peaking in the transition region around
z=0

where the gradient in the condensate density is particularly large
(compare thick coloured and thick gray curves in Fig.
2
,
see also Fig.
8
in the
B
).
Broadly speaking, we find the hard-wall (linear) potential to give the largest
(smallest) geometric potential, respectively, but this depends, of course,
on the specific values of the parameters (
\kappa
and
a
).
We come back in Sec.
2.5

to the linear case where we have to address
the slow spatial decay of
U_{\rm ad}(z;E)
.

Numerical solutions within the adiabatic approximation are easy to find
for any energy
E

because the exponentially growing “contamination” has been eliminated.
A simple initial value solver suffices,
starting in the tunnelling region left of the turning point.
This gives the phase mode

\varphi^{({\rm ad})}
from which we compute the source
term
-\hat{L}\varphi^{({\rm ad})}
in Eq. (
66
).
To solve the inhomogeneous Schrödinger equation for
f^{({\rm ad})}
,
we go back to a finite-difference or finite-element scheme,
using a reasonably large interval.
Since the sought solution

f^{({\rm ad})}
is below the spectrum of the
potential
V_{\rm ad}=M_{f}+V_{\rm geo}
and the source term
is spatially localised,
there is no problem with inverting the Schrödinger operator
in Eq. (
66
) on a finite grid.

In this case, one indeed expects from the definition (

)

\theta(z)

invalidating the adiabatic approximation.

2.3
Reflection phase shift

At this point, we address the main focus of this study, namely the phase
shift of the Bogoliubov waves at a generic turning point.
We define it, as illustrated in Fig.
3
,
from the oscillatory
\varphi
solution in the adiabatic basis.
At large distance from the turning point,
the density mode
f
becomes small,
and
\varphi
solves a Schrödinger equation
with a relatively simple potential.
The solutions in this potential, analytically known,
have been given in
Eqs. (
7
)
and (
21
);
they
provide reference waves, and the phase shift
\delta
is measured
relative to them.

In the flat-bottom potentials of type “hard wall”,
“quarter pipe” and “exponential”, the condensate becomes constant,

\phi^{2}(z)\to\mu/g
,
when the distance
z
is large enough compared to the scales
1/\kappa
or
1/a
.
The non-adiabatic terms proportional to
\theta^{\prime}
vanish, and
in the potential
U_{\rm ad}
[Eq. (
70
)], only the term

D(z)
survives.
It tends to the constant value

U_{\rm ad}(z)\to-\frac{E^{2}}{\mu+(E^{2}+\mu^{2})^{1/2}}=-k^{2}

(72)

which is the wavenumber
k
of the Bogoliubov dispersion (
11
), as expected from the local density approximation.
Asymptotically, the solution
\varphi(z)
is therefore
a combination of sine and cosine waves.
Similar to Eq. (
23
),
we define the phase shift
\delta
by the limit

z\to\infty:\quad\varphi(z)\to\frac{A}{\sqrt{k}}\sin(kz+\delta)=A\left[j_{k}(z)\cos\delta-y_{k}(z)\sin\delta\right]

(73)

with an amplitude
A
.
This is consistent with Eqs. (
12
) for the
standard Bogoliubov amplitudes
u
,
v
because the adiabatic angle
becomes constant,
\theta(z)\to\arctan(\mu/E)=\theta_{k}

[Eqs. (
13
,
61
)].

The pair of “reference waves”

\{j_{k},y_{k}\}=k^{-1/2}\left\{\sin(kz),-\cos(kz)\right\}

in Eq. (
73
) are normalised
such that their Wronskian
W[j_{k},y_{k}]
is unity.
The convention ensures that incident or reflected waves given by

j_{k}\mp{\rm i}y_{k}
have unit flux.
This is based on the flux of linear wave mechanics, although for the actual
particle current in a Bose gas, the interference with the condensate has
to be taken into account
(
Bouchoule and Mølmer, 2003
;
Paul et al., 2005
)
.
As mentioned around its definition in Eq. (
22
),
the Wronskian is a convenient tool to extract the phase shift
\delta(E)

of the
\varphi
solution. Indeed, from Eq. (
73
)

z\to\infty:\quad W[\varphi,j_{k}]\to A\,\sin\delta\,,\qquad W[\varphi,y_{k}]\to A\,\cos\delta

(74)

The derivative of the Wronskian is

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}z}W[\varphi,j_{k}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi\frac{{\rm d}^{2}j_{k}}{{\rm d}z^{2}}-\frac{{\rm d}^{2}\varphi}{{\rm d}z^{2}}j_{k}

(75)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\varphi\,k^{2}j_{k}+\left(-U_{\rm ad}\varphi+\hat{L}f\right)j_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\varphi\left(U_{\rm ad}+k^{2}\right)j_{k}+(\hat{L}f)\,j_{k}

The last term arises from the rhs of the inhomogeneous
Schrödinger equation for
\varphi
.
Its contribution to
\delta
is evaluated in Sec.
2.4
.
Otherwise, we find the difference between
the actual potential
U_{\rm ad}
and the constant
reference
-k^{2}
of Eq. (
72
),
a result known as the Abel identiy
(
Bender and Orszag, 1978
)
.
At large
z
, both terms in Eq. (
75
) vanish,
and
W
becomes constant,
as anticipated in Eq. (
74
).
We use this constant value to normalise the solution

\varphi
(and
f
) such that
A=1
in Eq. (
73
).

The phase shift
\delta=\delta(E)
quantifies to what extent the
inhomogeneous condensate background “drags” or “pushes” the Bogoliubov wave
into or out of the region near the turning point.
This can be understood in terms of the dip
of the effective potential
U_{\rm ad}(z)
in Fig.
8
:
the local wavelength of the
\varphi
wave decreases, and more phase
is accumulated relative to a flat potential (positive
\delta
).
The four potentials show quite some diversity in their phase shifts,
as can be seen in Fig.
4
.
The exact result in the hard-wall potential (solid line) is reproduced
by the numerical solution, while the adiabatic approximation
fails at low energies only (dashed lines).
The non-adiabatic correction discussed in Sec.
2.4

below
is also quite significant at low energies. It is represented
by the difference between the dotted and dashed lines.
This applies to all types of potentials.

The energy dependence of
\delta(E)
makes it plausible why
the level spacings in Fig.
3
slightly
differ between the three flat-bottom potentials.
For the exponential potential, the larger phase shift moves
the levels down (smaller energy spacing for a fixed right end of the
computational grid),
compared to the hard-wall potentials
whose phase shift decreases with energy.
This trend correlates with the ordering of the energy levels, e.g.,

E_{3}=0.497\,\mu>0.488\,\mu>0.481\,\mu
, when going from top left
to bottom left in Fig.
3
, even though close
to their turning points, the Bogoliubov modes are strikingly similar.

The phase shift in the quarter-pipe potential is similar to the
hard wall, with a slightly larger value for most energies
[Fig.
4
(left)].
We attribute this to a somewhat larger region (of the order of the
quarter-pipe radius
R
) where the condensate
density deviates from its constant asymptote so that
U_{\rm ad}(z)

develops a deeper dip;
compare the top panels in Fig.
1

and Fig.
10
in
B
.
In the smooth exponential potential, the phase shift starts from
the same low-energy value
\delta(0)\approx\pi/2
as for the
hard wall, but then increases monotonously with energy
[Fig.
4
(right)].
We attribute this to the “softness” of the potential
U_{\rm ad}

that provides additional “space” for the wave function
in the region
z<0
.
Indeed, the turning point
z_{*}(E)
,
the root of
U_{\rm ad}(z_{*})=0
,
moves to the left as
E
increases.
Crudely speaking, one may imagine the Bogoliubov wave to be simply
translated from the reference point
z=0
to its actual turning point

z_{*}

(
Henkel et al., 1994
)
.
This picture leads to the simple approximation
\delta=\pi/2-kz_{*}
which captures well the low-energy behaviour
[thick solid line in Fig.
4
(right)].
We have also computed within the semiclassical (WKB) approximation

(
Bender and Orszag, 1978
)

the phase of
the
\varphi^{({\rm ad})}
wave function
and found good agreement.
It is somewhat remarkable that the WKB phase correction
\pi/4

at the soft turning point in the exponential potential accounts
only for one half of the low-energy limit
\delta(0)=\pi/2
.
At low energies, of course, the semiclassical approximation breaks down
because of the rapid variation of the local momentum.

In contrast, the linear potential produces a phase shift close to zero
(note that the data in Fig.
4
, right are
magnified for better visibility).
These small values are found once the phase shift is extrapolated
from the right end point of the numerical grid to infinity:
this is discussed in more detail in Sec.
2.5
.
Before, we address the correction to
\delta(E)
arising from the
term
\hat{L}\,f
neglected so far
in the adiabatic approximation.
Including it moves the dotted curves to the dashed ones
and improves the agreement with the exact data in the range
0.25\,\mu<E<\mu

(and even for all
E
in the linear potential).

2.4
Non-adiabatic correction to phase shift

The adiabatic solution
\varphi^{({\rm ad})}
deviates from the
full one
\varphi

because it neglects the inhomogeneous term
\hat{L}\,f

in Eq. (
65
)
and in Eq. (
75
).
We use again the Abel identity to improve,
if not the phase mode, but at least its phase shift.
The technique is well known in partial wave expansions
of scattering theory
(
Messiah, 1995
)
.
Consider therefore the Wronskian
W[\varphi,\varphi^{({\rm ad})}]

between phase modes defined as in Eq. (
22
).
Computing its derivative and using the Schrödinger-type equations
for
\varphi
,
\varphi^{({\rm ad})}
at the same energy,
we find [similar to Eq. (
75
)]

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}z}W[\varphi,\varphi^{({\rm ad})}]=(\hat{L}\,f)(z)\,\varphi^{({\rm ad})}(z)

(76)

Recall from Fig.
2

that the term
\hat{L}\,f
is relatively small
and well localised.
As we move infinitely far away from the condensate border,
the expansion of
\varphi
and
\varphi^{({\rm ad})}
in reference
solutions
yields for their Wronskian (assuming for both the normalisation
A=1
)

z\to\infty:\quad W[\varphi,\varphi^{({\rm ad})}]=\sin(\delta-\delta^{({\rm ad})})

(77)

At the other end of the
z
-axis,
W
drops to zero,
since
\varphi
and
\varphi^{({\rm ad})}

vanish as physical tunnelling solutions.
By integrating Eq. (
76
), we thus get the
exact expression

\sin(\delta-\delta^{({\rm ad})})=\int\!{\rm d}z\,(\hat{L}\,f)(z)\,\varphi^{({\rm ad})}(z)=-\int\!{\rm d}z\,f(z)\,(\hat{L}\,\varphi^{({\rm ad})})(z)

(78)

The second form arises from a partial integration, using the
definition (
67
) of the differential operator
\hat{L}
.
The integrated terms vanish because the integrand is localised
around the turning point region of the condensate.
This expression can be re-arranged into (incidentally another
Wronskian)

\sin(\delta-\delta^{({\rm ad})})=\frac{1}{2}\int\!{\rm d}z\,\theta^{\prime}(z)\left[f^{\prime}(z)\,\varphi^{({\rm ad})}(z)-f(z)\,\varphi^{({\rm ad})\prime}(z)\right]

(79)

where the prime denotes spatial derivatives.
We found this form to be numerically convenient
because the integrand shows less oscillations.

At this point, it is instructive to invert
the Schrödinger operator
-{\rm d}^{2}/{\rm d}z^{2}+V_{\rm ad}(z)

with the help of the resolvent operator
\hat{G}
.
As illustrated in Fig.
2
, the potential
V_{\rm ad}(z)

is a confining well: on the left side of the turning point, it is closed
by the bare trapping potential
V(z)
, on the right side, by the repulsive
mean-field potential
V_{3}(z)
.
The resolvent
\hat{G}
provides the solution
f
of
the inhomogeneous equation (
66
),
which may be expanded as follows

f=-\hat{G}\hat{L}\,\varphi=-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\eta_{n}\frac{(\eta_{n}|\hat{L}\,\varphi)}{\epsilon_{n}}

(80)

Here, the
\epsilon_{n}
and
\eta_{n}
(
n=0,1,\ldots
) provide the discrete spectrum
in the potential
V_{\rm ad}
,
with eigenfunctions normalised according to the standard scalar product

(\eta_{n}|\eta_{m})=\int\!{\rm d}z\,\eta_{n}(z)\,\eta_{m}(z)=\delta_{nm}\,,\qquad n,m=0,1,\ldots

(81)

Since
V_{\rm ad}
is a potential well, its eigenfunctions
\eta_{n}
are localised.
In particular for small quantum numbers
n
,
the matrix elements
(\eta_{n}|\hat{L}\,\varphi)
are convergent overlap
integrals.

We insert the expansion (
80
)
into the formula (
78
)
for the non-adiabatic correction to the phase shift
and find

\sin(\delta-\delta^{({\rm ad})})=\sum_{n}\frac{(\eta_{n}|\hat{L}\,\varphi^{({\rm ad})})\,(\eta_{n}|\hat{L}\,\varphi)}{\epsilon_{n}}\approx\sum_{n}\frac{|(\eta_{n}|\hat{L}\,\varphi^{({\rm ad})})|^{2}}{\epsilon_{n}}

(82)

While the first expression is still exact, the second one is a useful
approximation. It is based on the assumption that the “back-action” of

f
on the Bogoliubov mode
\varphi
is weak, as can be
expected from Fig.
2
.
It also provides a way to correct the phase shift
of the adiabatic approximation scheme
where the density mode is

f\approx f^{({\rm ad})}=-\hat{G}\hat{L}\varphi^{({\rm ad})}
.
The results of this procedure are displayed as “adiab+” in
Figs.

,
10

The structure of Eq. (

)
is reminiscent of second-order perturbation theory where the
eigenstates
\eta_{n}
trapped in the potential
V_{\rm ad}
are coupled
to the wave function
\varphi^{({\rm ad})}
via the

\hat{L}

A common feature of Eq. (

and ground-state energy shifts is the definite sign: all terms
in the sum are positive, and
in distinction to a Feshbach resonance,
the energy denominator never crosses zero.
Still, there are oscillations in the “matrix element”
that provide relatively broad peaks.
Indeed, as the energy

is scanned,
the nodes and anti-nodes of
\varphi^{({\rm ad})}
shift across the bottom
of the potential
V_{\rm ad}(z)
where the
low-lying eigenfunctions
\eta_{n}
are localised.
These oscillations are particularly marked for the linear potential
which is the “softest” of the four,
compare dotted and dashed lines in Fig.

(right).
The turning point of
\varphi
is hence relatively free to move to
the left.

2.5
Phase shift in the linear potential

The large-
z
asymptotics in the linear potential
has to be treated in a slightly different way,
because the approach of the potential
U_{\rm ad}
to its
asymptotic form given in Eq. (
20
)
is slow.
As a consequence, when the parameters
A
,
\delta
are computed
at a finite position
z
from the asymptotic form given in Eq. (
23
),
they are still changing as
z
moves to infinity.
More explicitly, we compute
A\sin\delta
from the Wronskian
W[\varphi,j_{E}]

with the regular reference solution
j_{E}
[Eq. (
21
)].
Exactly as in Eq. (
75
),
we find that this Wronskian evolves according to

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}z}W[\varphi,j_{E}]=-\varphi\,\Big(U_{\rm ad}+\frac{E^{2}}{2z}+\frac{1}{4z^{2}}\Big)\,j_{E}+(\hat{L}f)\,j_{E}

(83)

using the natural units in the linear potential.
The last term involving
\hat{L}f

has been dealt with in the previous section and because
it is localised to small
z
, it will
be neglected in the large-distance range considered here.
The three pieces of
U_{\rm ad}=V_{1}+D+V_{\rm geo}
introduced in Eq. (
70
)
can be expanded for large
z
.
Using the post-Thomas-Fermi expansion (
19
),
we get

\displaystyle V_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4z^{2}}-\frac{9}{8z^{5}}+{\cal O}(z^{-8})

(84)

\displaystyle D(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{E^{2}}{2z}+\frac{E^{4}}{8z^{3}}-\frac{E^{2}}{8z^{4}}+{\cal O}(E^{6}z^{-5})

(85)

\displaystyle V_{\rm geo}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{E^{2}}{4z^{4}}+{\cal O}(E^{4}z^{-6})

(86)

The corrections relative to the long-range
reference potential of Eq. (
20
)
are thus

U_{\rm ad}(z)+\frac{E^{2}}{2z}+\frac{1}{4z^{2}}=\frac{E^{4}}{8z^{3}}+\frac{E^{2}}{8z^{4}}+{\cal O}(z^{-5},E^{6}z^{-5})

(87)

Equation (
83
) also provides
an equation for the phase shift
\delta
alone.
Consider amplitude
A
and phase shift
\delta
as the polar coordinates
of the tuple

(W[\varphi,y_{E}]
,
W[\varphi,j_{E}])=(A\cos\delta,A\sin\delta)
.
For the derivative of
\delta=\arctan(W[\varphi,j_{E}]/W[\varphi,y_{E}])
,
a short calculation yields

\frac{{\rm d}\delta}{{\rm d}z}\approx-\Big(\frac{E^{4}}{8z^{3}}+\frac{E^{2}}{8z^{4}}\Big)\left(j_{E}\cos\delta-y_{E}\sin\delta\right)^{2}\qquad(z\gg\ell)

(88)

At large distances, the last parenthesis can be simplified using
the asymptotic form (
24
)
of the Bessel functions.
Eq. (
88
) therefore yields an oscillatory
decrease of the phase shift,
as the position
z
moves towards infinity.
If
\delta
is already small, we have
{\rm d}\delta/{\rm d}z=0

at the nodes of the Bessel function
j_{E}(z)\propto J_{0}(E\sqrt{2z})
,
as illustrated in Fig.

.
This behaviour produces numerical artefacts when the phase shift
is computed at a finite position

They show up as a slowly
increasing, oscillatory energy dependence.

3
Applications

3.1
Discrete spectrum

As a first application of the reflection phase shifts, consider a
potential well closed by two reflecting barriers.
The Bogoliubov spectrum is then discrete, and we may expect the
phase shifts at two turning points to contribute to the total round trip
phase (or action) in the Bohr-Sommerfeld quantisation rule

S_{12}(E)=\int_{z_{1}}^{z_{2}}\!{\rm d}z\,p(z,E)=n\pi-\delta_{1}-\delta_{2}

(89)

where
p
is the momentum in the relevant potential.
Consider as a simple, but nontrivial example the “gravitational” trap
with a shallow linear turning point at the “top”
and an exponential barrier on the “bottom”

(
Wallis et al., 1992
;
Henkel et al., 1994
;
Spreeuw et al., 1995
)
:

V(z)=\mu\,\exp(-az)+Fz

(90)

For definiteness, we take
1/a\approx 130\,{\rm nm}
,
gravity for
F
,
and
\mu\approx 166\,{\rm nK}
,
with other parameters as in Table
1
.
The ratios of decay length, healing length, and characteristic length
of the linear potential are then
1/a:\xi:\ell=1:1.4:2.32
.
The resulting condensate solution is the solid black curve in the
bottom of Fig.
6
(left).

It is a challenge to identify the contribution of the phase shifts
because the length scales are not well separated, and one has to
split the total phase between the two turning points in a meaningful way
[see Eq. (
89
)].
In the adiabatic approximation with the potential
U_{\rm ad}
,
we would use the momentum
p=\sqrt{-U_{\rm ad}}

in Eq. (
89
).
We have checked that this actually reproduces (i.e., includes)
the phase shift computed in the previous sections.
The potential
V_{1}
shown in Fig.

(left)
has a narrow minimum at the left (steep) turning
point and is nearly flat when approaching the right (soft) turning

and density modes

as defined by
Eqs. (

The density modes are essentially localised in the
condensate region (gray shaded area).
For comparison, the energy-independent
potential
V_{3}
is shown in solid gray.
The density modes qualitatively behave as expected from this double-well
if the tunnelling coupling is relatively strong.
The phase modes are dominant in determining the quantised energy
levels, as shown by the comparison to the semiclassical action

3.2
Boundary correction to bulk density of states

A more direct way to explore the relevance of the phase shift
is to consider a flat-bottom potential so that for
z\to\infty
,
we expect the system to behave like a homogeneous one.
The following recipe to count states owes a lot to
Barton (1979)
.
We apply the same device as in the numerics
shown in Fig.
3
and
enclose the system in a large, finite box.
Consider the case of a Dirichlet boundary condition
at
z=L
, larger than any other scale.
(We emphasise that this boundary condition is imposed only on the Bogoliubov modes,
not on the condensate.)
From the
asymptotes (
12
) of the
u
and
v
modes,
we get for the allowed energies
E_{k}
or
k
-vectors in a flat-bottom potential
the quantisation rule

kL+\delta(k)=n\pi\,,\quad n=1,2\ldots

(91)

Consider now some quantity
Q
to be evaluated by summing
over all modes, like the mean energy in thermal equilibrium

E_{k}\langle b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}\rangle
or the ground state energy.
As
L
is large, the modes become dense and the sum turns into an integral

Q=\sum_{k}Q_{k}\approx\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\!{\rm d}k\,\rho(k)\,Q(k)

(92)

where
\rho(k)
is the density of modes in
k
-space. For the simple
reference case of a box potential between
z=0
and
z=L
, the
spacing of the discrete
k
-vectors is
\Delta k=\pi/L
so that

\rho(k)=L/\pi
. This makes
Q/L\to q
an intensive quantity
in the thermodynamic limit.

The phase shift
\delta(k)
from the left boundary
changes the mode spacing according to
[subtract Eq. (
91
) evaluated for
n+1
and
n
]

(k_{n+1}-k_{n})L+\delta(k_{n+1})-\delta(k_{n})=\pi

(93)

The mode density

1/(k_{n+1}-k_{n})=1/\Delta k
becomes for large
L
, after expanding
the phase shift

\rho(k)=\frac{1}{\Delta k\lx@nobreakspace}=\frac{L}{\pi}\left(1-\frac{1}{L}\frac{{\rm d}\delta}{{\rm d}k}\right)^{-1}\approx\frac{L}{\pi}+\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{{\rm d}\delta}{{\rm d}k}

(94)

Inserting into the mode sum (
92
) and subtracting
the extensive part
\sim L
, we get the boundary shift
\Delta Q_{B}

of the quantity
Q
relative to its extensive limit

\Delta Q_{B}=\lim\limits_{L\to\infty}\left(Q(L)-Lq\right)=\int\!\frac{{\rm d}k}{\pi}\lx@nobreakspace\frac{{\rm d}\delta}{{\rm d}k}\,Q(k)

(95)

Note that the derivative of the phase shift plays the role of
a change in mode density.
This technique can be used to compute, e.g., shifts in
zero-point energies related to the boundary conditions

(
Barton, 1979
)
.
We do not pursue this issue further here because in the
companion paper
(
Henkel, 2025b
)
, the quantities of
interest (like the ground-state energy density)
depend on the spatial profile of the modes,
a feature that is not captured by Eq. (
95
).

The same derivative as in Eq. (
95
)
appears, however, when a wave packet of Bogoliubov excitations
with a well-defined mean position approaches the turning point
and gets reflected back into the bulk.
When the trajectories of the incident and reflected wave packet are
extrapolated, they cross at
z_{B}=-{\rm d}\delta/{\rm d}k
, as if
the wave packet had bounced off at this position
(
Henkel et al., 1994
)
,
see Fig.
7
(left).
The phase shifts found in this paper for flat-bottom potentials yield
effective bounce positions shown in the right panel.
Consider a particle in two or three dimensions that
bounces at the plane
z=0

where the turning point for the condensate wave function is located.
For an oblique incidence,
the reflected trajectory is also laterally shifted,
as can be inferred from Fig.
7
(left).
This is the key idea behind the optical ray shift
of
Goos and Hänchen (1947)
.

4
Conclusion

The reflection of waves from a potential is an archetypal problem of scattering
theory with many implications for cross sections, tunnelling delay times,
or mode counting, to mention a few. We have here analysed this case
for elementary excitations of a Bose-Einstein quasi-condensate confined
by a half-open potential, covering both hard and soft barriers and the
entire continuous spectrum. Wave reflection is dispersive (with an energy-dependent
phase shift)
even for a hard wall which is mostly due to the condensate background
being inhomogeneous. The results are obtained with a combined numerical and
analytical scheme that removes divergent solutions from the Bogoliubov
equations. As applications, we have studied the energy spectrum of a trapped
system and the mirror position for a bouncing wave packet. The case of a
linear potential is special in that its phase shift is numerically relatively
small at all energies, provided the slow variations of the phase shift
in the reflected Bogoliubov wave are tracked over a sufficiently large region
into the bulk of the trapped gas.

Appendix A
WKB phase shift for the linear potential

We discuss here an approximate calculation of the phase shift in the
linear potential and
argue that the small values we find
arise from the cancellation between two large contributions.
The idea is to solve the Schrödinger equation (
69
)
with the WKB approximation
(
Messiah, 1995
)

z>z_{*}:\qquad\varphi^{({\rm WKB})}(z)=\frac{A^{\prime}}{[-U_{\rm ad}(z)]^{1/4}}\sin\bigg(\!\frac{\pi}{4}+\int_{z_{*}}^{z}\!{\rm d}z^{\prime}\,[-U_{\rm ad}(z^{\prime})]^{1/2}\bigg)

(96)

where
p(z)=[-U_{\rm ad}(z)]^{1/2}
is the semiclassical momentum
and
z_{*}
the left turning point:
p(z_{*})=0
.
We are only interested in the asymptotic regime
z\to\infty

and evaluate the action (or phase) integral approximately.
This can be done analytically
when the Thomas-Fermi approximation is used (adopting natural units
for the linear potential):

-U_{\rm ad}(z)\approx\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle E+\mu-V(z)\approx E+z\,,&\mbox{for }z\leq 0\,,\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle\sqrt{E^{2}+g^{2}|\phi(z)|^{4}}-g|\phi(z)|^{2}\approx\sqrt{E^{2}+z^{2}}-z\,,&\mbox{for }z\geq 0\,.\end{array}\right.

(97)

We thus have
z_{*}=-E
, and the interval
z^{\prime}=-E\ldots 0

contributes the phase

\int_{-E}^{0}\!{\rm d}z^{\prime}\,\sqrt{E+z^{\prime}}=\frac{2}{3}E^{3/2}

(98)

Between
z^{\prime}=0\ldots z
, the integration yields

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{z}\!{\rm d}z^{\prime}\,(\sqrt{E^{2}+z^{\prime 2}}-z^{\prime})^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2E}{3}\frac{\sqrt{E^{2}+z^{2}}+2z}{(\sqrt{E^{2}+z^{2}}+z)^{1/2}}-\frac{2}{3}E^{3/2}

(99)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\sqrt{2z}-\frac{2}{3}E^{3/2}+{\cal O}(z^{-3/2})

In the last line, we expanded for
z\gg E
to recover the
square root scaling of the phase.

Note the cancellation of the constant term
-E^{3/2}
when the
two contributions are summed. Coming back to the wave
function (
96
) and comparing to the asymptotic
form (
24
), the phase
offset
\pi/4
is matched by the WKB phase
originating from the turning point
z_{*}

(
Bender and Orszag, 1978
;
Messiah, 1995
)
.
This combination of WKB and Thomas-Fermi approximations
therefore suggests a phase shift
\delta(E)=0
,
in agreement with the trend observed
in Fig.
4
(right) for large
E
.
We note that
it is only in this regime that we expect
the WKB and the adiabatic approximations
to be accurate because the local wavelength
is small and varies slowly enough over the entire classically allowed
region.
