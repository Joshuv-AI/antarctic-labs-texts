Successful Transhepatic Lymphangiography and Embolization for Chylous Ascites following Right Nephrectomy and Inferior Vena Cava Tumor Thrombectomy
Authors: Hirokazu Ashida, Kenkichi Michimoto, Shunsuke Kisaki, Fumihiko Urabe, Takahiro Kimura, Hiroya Ojiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The case was a 71-year-old woman who had right renal cell carcinoma with inferior vena cava invasion. After the nephrectomy, chylous ascites appeared. Despite conservative treatment, chylous ascites persisted. No lymphatic leakage was identified on transpedal lymphoscintigraphy, and retrograde thoracic duct cannulation from the left subclavian vein failed. A chylous pseudoaneurysm was identified on computed tomography and embolized percutaneously; however, chylous ascites persisted. Therefore, we performed transhepatic lymphangiography and embolization before placement of a peritoneovenous shunt, and chylous ascites resolved. Transhepatic lymphatic embolization may represent a valuable treatment option for postoperative chylous ascites. This case suggests that hepatoduodenal lymphatic leakage may occur after nephrectomy involving extensive manipulation of the pancreas head or hepatoduodenal ligament, possibly related to cross-communication between hepatoduodenal and mesenteric lymphatics.

Introduction

Postoperative chylous ascites (CA) is a rare but life-threatening complication that can occur after abdominal or pelvic surgery, particularly after extensive abdominal surgical procedures for cancer therapy [1-3]. Refractory postoperative CA presents various challenges, including diagnostic difficulties, a lack of standard treatment, and poor outcomes of surgical therapy [4]. Minimally invasive lymphatic procedures, such as nodal lymphangiography and lymphatic embolization, have recently evolved primarily in the field of interventional radiology. Various case series and case reports have reported hepatic lymphorrhea treated with transhepatic lymphatic embolization (THLE) [5-8]. However, there is only one case report in which postoperative CA was treated with THLE and sclerotherapy [9]. This case involved CA after pancreaticoduodenectomy, and our case involved CA after a transabdominal right nephrectomy with extensive upper abdominal manipulation. Therefore, we report the case of a patient with CA after a non-hepatobiliary operation who was successfully treated with THLE.

Case Report

Written informed consent for publication, including clinical information and images, was obtained from the patient, excluding personal information. A 71-year-old woman presented to the hospital with edema in her bilateral lower legs. After several investigations, the patient was diagnosed with cT3b N0 M0 (Stage Ⅲ) right renal clear cell carcinoma (Figure 1) according to the Union for International Cancer Control tumor-node-metastasis classification (8th edition). Before surgical resection, neoadjuvant chemotherapy was given for four cycles of pembrolizumab and axitinib. Then, transabdominal right nephrectomy and tumor plug resection in the inferior vena cava (IVC) were performed. In detail, bilateral subcostal incisions and an upper midline abdominal incision were made, followed by ligation of the round ligament of the liver and division of the falciform ligament up to the diaphragm. The ascending colon was approached laterally and reflected medially, allowing exposure of the right kidney, IVC, and abdominal aorta. The IVC was dissected circumferentially in both the cranial and caudal directions. To expose the left kidney, the pancreas, and the ligament of Treitz were mobilized via a lesser sac approach. After mobilization of the liver, the IVC was further exposed by dividing the lumbar veins, adrenal veins, and short hepatic veins. The tumor thrombus was subsequently resected, and a Pringle maneuver was applied to the hepatoduodenal ligament for 36 minutes. No lymph node dissection or sampling was performed.

On postoperative day 5, the third day after the start of the diet, milky ascites was identified from the drainage tube placed during the surgery in the Douglas pouch and diagnosed as CA due to a triglyceride level of 118 mg/dL. Therefore, conservative treatment began with fasting and octreotide. However, CA continued, and the daily volume exceeded 1 liter. To investigate the leak point of the CA and choose the invasive treatment option, we performed non-contrast enhanced CT and technetium-99m human serum albumin-diethylenetriamine pentaacetic acid lymphoscintigraphy following bilateral pedal subcutaneous injection 10 days after the nephrectomy. However, moderate ascites with a retroperitoneal encapsulated cavity (width: 25 mm, length: 45 mm) was observed on CT, and there was no leakage on the lymphoscintigraphy (Figure 2). Initially, we attempted retrograde thoracic duct (TD) catheter cannulation and embolization during bilateral inguinal nodal Lipiodol lymphangiography 8 days after the lymphoscintigraphy. Lipiodol ascended into TD, and no extravasation was observed with this lymphangiography. However, catheter cannulation of the terminal TD failed due to its multi-channel morphology. Therefore, we embolized this retroperitoneal cavity with coils and 20% n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate (NBCA) using an 18-G Chiba needle (Sun Z needle, Tochigi Seiko, Japan) and a microcatheter (Prominent Raptor, Tokai Medical, Japan) 15 days after the CT diagnosis (Figure 3). Three days after this procedure, due to temporary poor drainage of CA, two drains were placed in the upper abdomen, and the drain in the Douglas pouch was replaced. However, the chylous leak persisted. Subsequently, a peritoneovenous shunt was considered; however, because the surgical site was in the retroperitoneum on the right side, transhepatic lymphangiography was attempted first 10 days after the retroperitoneal embolization, in line with previous literature [10]. An ultrasound-guided percutaneous trans-intercostal right hepatic lobe approach was chosen. The right anterior branch of the periportal hyperechoic area was punctured with a 21-G Chiba needle. Using a glass syringe, non-ionic iodine contrast material was injected gently using the loss-of-resistance method. After three attempts, the lymphatic duct was depicted, and contrast material progressed hepatofugally toward the hilum. The contrast advanced sufficiently, but fluoroscopy did not show extravasation. Thereafter, we performed CT, which showed contrast extravasation in the retroperitoneal space, extending into the peritoneal cavity (Figure 4). Due to the dislodgement of the Chiba needle, reinsertion was difficult. Three days later, THLE was performed using the same method, and 1.5 mL of 20% NBCA mixed with Lipiodol was injected using a 21-G needle. The dilution rate was determined based on a previous review paper and our experience [11].

After the embolization, the CA disappeared within 2 days, and the drainage tube was removed 5 days later. No complications were observed after the THLE. No recurrence of CA was subsequently observed for 4 years, and the patient was discharged 16 days after the THLE.

Discussion

The basic treatment for postoperative CA is conservative therapy, which successfully resolves most cases [2]. However, in a subset of patients, CA persists despite measures such as dietary modification, fasting, administration of medium-chain triglycerides, and somatostatin analogs. In such refractory CA cases, interventional radiological procedures become necessary. The most commonly reported image-guided treatments include lymphangiography, lymph node embolization, embolization of chylous pseudoaneurysms or cysts, retrograde TD embolization, and placement of a peritoneovenous shunt [4, 12].

Transhepatic lymphangiography is a percutaneous technique for opacifying the intrahepatic lymphatic system, which anatomically courses along Glisson's sheath in parallel with portal venous branches. Under ultrasound guidance, a fine needle is advanced into the hepatic parenchyma, and small amounts of contrast are injected while withdrawing the needle until characteristic beaded lymphatic channels are visualized. This approach is particularly useful when hepatic lymphorrhea is suspected, because conventional pedal or intranodal lymphangiography may fail to demonstrate leaks arising from the hepatic lymphatic network. Reported indications include refractory postoperative ascites or lymphorrhea after hepatobiliary or upper gastrointestinal surgery involving the hepatic hilum or hepatoduodenal ligament. Importantly, hepatic lymphatic leakage may present as non-CA, and several reports have shown that transhepatic lymphangiography can identify the leakage site and facilitate definitive treatment, most commonly NBCA-based lymphatic embolization or targeted sclerotherapy, resulting in resolution or marked reduction of persistent postoperative ascites [6, 7, 13-15].

On the other hand, reports on THLE for CA are scarce. To our knowledge, Nguyen et al. [9] have reported the only case of hepatic lymphatic embolization for postoperative CA following pancreaticoduodenectomy. In their case, the hepatoduodenal ligament was directly involved in the surgical procedure, providing a clear rationale for using transhepatic lymphangiography. In contrast, our case involved right nephrectomy and IVC tumor thrombus resection. Although the hepatoduodenal ligament was temporarily ligated using the Pringle maneuver, it was not the primary operative target. Based on the imaging findings, particularly the leakage identified on the ventral aspect of the pancreatic head on hepatic lymphangiography, it is more plausible that the lymphatic disruption occurred in association with upper abdominal surgical manipulations, including pancreatic mobilization.

Although mesenteric lymphatic injury was initially considered, and the indication for THLE was not straightforward, THLE was selected as a therapeutic option prior to peritoneovenous shunt placement.

Regarding the site of lymphatic injury and the mechanism underlying chylorrhea, CT performed after transhepatic lymphangiography demonstrated contrast extravasation at the dorsal aspect of the pancreatic head and the second portion of the duodenum (Figure 4). This location anatomically corresponded to the surgical dissection field during nephrectomy for exposure of the IVC and abdominal aorta. Previous anatomical studies have described that lymphatic vessels accompanying the inferior pancreaticoduodenal artery form a communication between the lymphatic network adjacent to the proximal superior mesenteric artery and that of the hepatoduodenal ligament [16-18]. Although this anatomical relationship could not be directly demonstrated in the present case, it is possible that chyle originating from the mesenteric lymphatic system may have entered the lymphatic network of the hepatoduodenal ligament upstream of the injury site via the lymphatic tract accompanying the inferior pancreaticoduodenal artery. Additionally, it should be noted that this outcome may have resulted from altered lymphatic flow following prior embolization of the retroperitoneal cavity due to a change in the pressure gradient caused by occlusion at one site in the presence of two injury sites.

Following the transhepatic lymphangiography, extravasation was visualized on CT despite being undetectable under fluoroscopy, which aligns with findings from previous reports emphasizing the utility of CT guidance in these procedures [6,10]. Embolization using NBCA and Lipiodol led to resolution of CA, thereby confirming the effectiveness of this approach even in anatomically and surgically atypical scenarios.

In conclusion, refractory postoperative CA following transabdominal nephrectomy with extensive upper abdominal manipulation was successfully treated with THLE. In the present case, the lymphatic injury was considered more likely to be associated with surgical manipulation around the hepatoduodenal ligament and pancreatic head. Therefore, when postoperative CA occurs after manipulation of upper abdominal organs, careful inspection of the surgical field is essential to avoid diagnostic pitfalls. When standard lymphangiographic evaluations are inconclusive, transhepatic lymphangiography and embolization may provide a valuable diagnostic and therapeutic option. This technique should be considered before resorting to more difficult-to-manage options such as peritoneovenous shunt placement [12].
