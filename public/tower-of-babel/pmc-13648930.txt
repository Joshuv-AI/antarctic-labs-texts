Linking cellular metabolism to mitochondrial outer membrane potential
Authors: Kaitlin Abrantes, Bethel G Beyene, Nina A Bautista, Wendy Fitzgerald, Tatiana K Rostovtseva, Sergey M Bezrukov, Megha Rajendran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe voltage-dependent anion channel (VDAC) of the mitochondrial outer membrane (MOM) responds to transmembrane voltage through intrinsic gating and voltage-dependent interactions with cytosolic proteins, such as α-synuclein and β-tubulin. Despite this characteristic voltage sensitivity found in vitro, the existence of a substantial MOM potential (ΔΨMOM) in vivo remains controversial. Using pH sensors targeted to the cytosol and the intermembrane space (IMS), we measured the difference in proton concentration across the MOM and calculated a ΔΨMOM of ~33 mV, positive from the IMS side in HeLa cells; in contrast, HEK-293 cells lacked substantial ΔΨMOM. Hexokinase 2 (HK2) is known to be overexpressed in cancer cells. Consistent with the previously proposed role of VDAC-HK complexation in ΔΨMOM generation, we observed lower HK2 expression in HEK-293 cells than in HeLa cells. In addition, by studying pH changes in the cytosol and IMS in response to changes in glucose and glucose-6-phosphate concentrations in HeLa cells, we established a relationship between ΔΨMOM and metabolic activity in cancer cells. Thus, our results demonstrate the metabolism-dependent generation of ΔΨMOM and provide strong evidence that VDAC regulation by voltage, observed in in vitro studies, is highly relevant to cell physiology.Keywords: Voltage-dependent anion channel, VDAC, pH sensors, Hexokinase

Introduction

Voltage-dependent anion channel (VDAC), a major β-barrel transmembrane protein in the mitochondrial outer membrane (MOM), plays a key role in maintaining and regulating ion and metabolite exchange across the MOM. In vitro, VDAC reconstituted into a planar lipid membrane characteristically gates under applied voltage of >30 mV, by switching from an anionic ATP-permeable high-conducting state to cationic calcium-favorable low-conducting states (Rostovtseva and Colombini, 1996; Tan and Colombini, 2007). Notably, as a result of voltage-dependent complexation with cytosolic proteins such as α-synuclein (Rostovtseva et al., 2015) and β-tubulin (Rostovtseva and Bezrukov, 2012), VDAC voltage sensitivity is greatly enhanced with the characteristic voltages reduced to ~10–20 mV.

Despite the pronounced VDAC voltage sensitivity found in in vitro experiments and thus its ability to be regulated by voltage, the MOM potential (ΔΨMOM) in cells has long been assumed to be essentially zero due to the high abundance of VDAC and its high permeability to small ions (see discussions in (Colombini, 2004; Lemeshko, 2006; Rostovtseva and Bezrukov, 2012). Yet measurement of ADP uptake by mitochondria in cardiomyocytes suggests that only 2% of VDAC are open in live cells (Simson et al., 2016). The mechanisms for generating ΔΨMOM were later suggested by Victor Lemeshko (2014, 2021). The model is based on the proposed VDAC complexes with hexokinase (HK) at the cytosolic side, which could act as “batteries” generating ΔΨMOM as high as 50 mV, positive at the intermembrane space (IMS) side of the MOM. In this model, the Gibbs free energy of the HK reaction with glucose is the driving force. There were two independent attempts to evaluate ΔΨMOM by measuring the proton concentrations across the MOM. Cortese et al. found a 0 to 0.5 pH difference between the IMS pH and bulk pH in isolated mitochondria using FITC-dextran as a sensor (Cortese et al., 1992). Later, Porcelli et al. estimated ΔΨMOM as ~43 mV, negative at the IMS side. They measured IMS pH using an EYFP sensor, linked to the inner mitochondrial membrane protein glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (mGPDH) in ECV304 cells (Porcelli et al., 2005). However, sensor localization can affect pH measurements. IMS is not a homogeneous compartment since cristae junctions act as a diffusion barrier for proton transport (Kondadi et al., 2020; Kowaltowski and Abdulkader, 2025). Importantly, recent studies have demonstrated significant spatial heterogeneity of pH within the cristae (pH 6.8–7.3) (Toth et al., 2020; Rieger et al., 2021), underscoring the need for more precise measurements of proton concentration differences across the MOM.

In the present study, using SypHer3s pH sensors specifically targeted to the cytosol and IMS (Ermakova et al., 2018), we calculated a ΔΨMOM of 33 ± 11 mV, positive on the IMS side, in HeLa cells. In contrast, HEK-293 cells lacked a significant pH difference across MOM. Given that HK2 is generally overexpressed in cancer cells (Farooq et al., 2023) and keeping in mind the proposed VDAC-HK complexation model of ΔΨMOM generation (Lemeshko, 2023), we studied the effect of HeLa cells exposed to various concentrations of glucose and glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) to demonstrate that the cytosol pH and IMS pH change in response to HK activity. Thus, our results establish the metabolism-dependent generation of ΔΨMOM and, as a direct consequence, point to voltage regulation of VDAC in HeLa cells.

Materials and methods

Cell culture:

HeLa cells (ATCC, CCL2) and HEK-293 cells (ATCC, CRL-1573) were grown in DMEM (Gibco, 10567) media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, 10437) at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Plasmids and cloning:

pC1-SypHer3s (Addgene plasmid # 108118 http://n2t.net/addgene:108118; RRID: Addgene_108118), pC1-SypHer3s-dmito (Addgene plasmid # 108119; http://n2t.net/addgene:108119; RRID: Addgene_108119) and pC1-SypHer3s-IMS (Addgene plasmid # 108120; http://n2t.net/addgene:108120; RRID: Addgene_108120) were a gift from Vsevolod Belousov. Cox7c-pHLuorin-sems was a gift from Karin Busch (Addgene plasmid # 107766; http://n2t.net/addgene:107766; RRID: Addgene_107766), and VV064: 1xCox8-ratiometric pHLuorin in fck was a gift from Adam Cohen (Addgene plasmid # 58502; http://n2t.net/addgene:58502; RRID: Addgene_58502).

Cox7c-RpHLuorin was subcloned by Genewiz from Azenta Life Sciences. Briefly, PCR amplified EcoRI/NotI fragment encoding ratiometric-pHLuorin was subcloned from VV0064: 1xCox8-ratiometric-pHLuorin to Cox7c-pHLuorin-sems to generate Cox7c-RpHLuorin. The plasmid sequence was confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Genewiz, Azenta Life Sciences).

Western blot:

HeLa and HEK-293 cells were seeded at 1 × 106 cells/well in 6-well plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The cells were harvested by trypsinization, washed with PBS, and centrifuged. Cell pellets were resuspended in 100 μL lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100) with cOmplete, EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma Aldrich, 4693132001), and incubated on ice for 20 min with occasional vortex. Samples were centrifuged at 14,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min, and the supernatant was transferred into a new 1.5 mL tube. Total soluble protein concentrations were measured using the Qubit™ Protein BR Assay (ThermoFisher Scientific, A50668). Samples were mixed with NuPAGE™ LDS Sample Buffer (4×) (ThermoFisher Scientific, NP0007) and heated at 95 °C for 10 min. Equal amounts of protein samples were loaded and separated using NuPAGE™ Bis-Tris Mini Protein Gels, 4–12% (ThermoFisher Scientific, NP0323BOX) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes using iBlot™ 2 Transfer Stacks (ThermoFisher Scientific, IB23001). Membranes were blocked with 3% w/v BSA prepared in TBST buffer for 30 min at room temperature, incubated with VDAC1 Antibody, clone N152B/23 (Sigma, MABN504) and hexokinase 2 antibody (Proteintech, 22029–1-AP) overnight at 4 °C, washed with TBST (three times for 10 min each), incubated with donkey anti-mouse Alexa Fluor™ Plus 800 (ThermoFisher Scientific, A32789) and donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor™ Plus 488 (ThermoFisher Scientific, A32790) for one hour at room temperature, and washed with TBST buffer (six times for 5 min each). The blots were imaged using the iBright FL1500 imaging system (ThermoFisher Scientific). The blots were analyzed with iBright analysis software (ThermoFisher Scientific, V5.4.0).

pH calibration:

Cells were transfected with pH sensors using LipofectamineTM STEM (ThermoFisher Scientific, STEM00001) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Transfected cells were seeded into μ-Slide 8 Well (Ibidi, 80806) and incubated overnight at 37 °C and 5% CO2. For pH calibration, the cells were washed and incubated in a high-potassium solution (mM: 10 glucose, 134 KCl, 2 CaCl2, 1 MgCl2) buffered with 25 mM MES (pH 5.5–6.5), HEPES (pH 7.0–7.5), Tris (pH 8.0–9.0), or CHES (pH 9–10) in the presence of 5 μM nigericin (Tocris Bioscience, 4312) and 5 μM monensin (Sigma Aldrich, M5273).

Dual-compartment pH measurement:

HeLa cells transfected with SypHer3s-IMS were treated with SNARF™−5F 5-(and-6)-Carboxylic Acid, Acetoxymethyl Ester, Acetate (ThermoFisher Scientific, S23923) to measure changes in IMS pH and cytosolic pH, respectively. Cells were incubated in HEPES buffer (pH 7.5) with 0–20 mM D-glucose (Macron, 4912) and 5 μg/mL insulin (Sigma Aldrich, I9278) or 0–20 mM glucose-6-phosphate (Sigma Aldrich, G7772).

Fluorescent imaging:

The cells were imaged with a 20×/0.75 N.A. objective on a Leica TCS SP8 microscope. SypHer3s fluorescence was sequentially excited at 405 and 488 nm, and emission was collected using a 525/25 nm band-pass filter. SNARF-5F was excited with a 552 nm laser, and emission was captured using 585/15 nm and 645/15 nm band-pass filters. Images were optimized for contrast and brightness, and fluorescence was measured in ImageJ (NIH).

Data analysis:

The normalized fluorescence ratio (F488/F405) at pH 5.5 to 10 in the presence of monensin and nigericin was fit with the Boltzmann sigmoidal equation (GraphPad Prism 11.0.0) to estimate the pKa (Schulte et al., 2006). The pH in each compartment was estimated from the fluorescence ratio in the absence of monensin and nigericin using the fit parameters from the individual calibration curve.

Statistical analysis:

Statistical analyses were performed using one-way and two-way ANOVA in GraphPad Prism 11.0.0. Three independent biological replicates were used. p-values <0.05 are reported as statistically significant.

Results

HeLa cells exhibit a significant MOM potential

To quantify the proton gradient across the MOM, we targeted the genetically encoded fluorescent pH probe, SypHer3, to the cytosol (Fig. 1A) and the IMS (Fig. 1B). The ratiometric sensor enables better control of cell-to-cell variability by normalizing measurements to cellular protein expression. Expression of the pH probes did not affect mitochondrial morphology (Supplemental Fig. S1). Sensor calibration was performed using an in situ pH titration in HeLa cells transfected with SypHer3s by measuring fluorescence at pH values from 5.5 to 10 in buffers containing the ionophores nigericin and monensin (Fig. 1C, D, and Supplemental Fig. S2A). The cytosol, IMS, and matrix proton concentrations were determined by interpolating the fluorescence ratio of SypHer3s expressed in different compartments in cells incubated in buffer without ionophores.

We calculated pH values of 7.41 ± 0.17 in the cytosol, 7.96 ± 0.08 in the IMS, and 8.01 ± 0.06 in the matrix (Fig. 1E). The lack of a significant difference in proton concentration between the IMS and matrix was concerning. However, recent studies have shown that proton concentration can vary from pH 6.9 to pH 7.3 within the cristae (Rieger et al., 2021). Since ATP production is dependent on the pH difference across the inner mitochondrial membrane, we measured the pH in the cristae. To measure cristae pH, we linked Cox7c, a subunit of complex IV in the cristae, to pHLuorin, a pH sensor with a pKa of 6.9. Cristae pH of 7.46 ± 0.08 was interpolated based on the pH calibration in cristae (Supplemental Fig. S2B). The pH difference of 0.55 across the inner membrane is consistent with previously published estimates (Davies et al., 2011; Poburko et al., 2011).

Given that VDAC is a weakly selective major channel of the MOM, a proton gradient by itself cannot generate the electric potential required to gate VDAC. However, the difference in proton concentration between the cytosol and IMS suggests the presence of a significant ΔΨMOM. MOM potential depends on HK2 metabolic activity

VDAC-HK2 complexation has been suggested to generate ΔΨMOM (Lemeshko, 2014; Lemeshko, 2021). To assess this model, we measured pH changes in IMS and cytosol in HeLa cells exposed to various concentrations of glucose and glucose-6-phosphate (G6P). HeLa cells transfected with SypHer3s-IMS were treated with SNARF-5F to simultaneously measure IMS and cytosol pH (Fig. 2A). Increasing glucose concentration decreased cytosolic pH and increased IMS pH (Fig. 2B). The opposite effect was observed with increasing G6P concentration (Fig. 2C). This suggests that ΔΨMOM increases with increasing glucose concentrations and decreases with increasing G6P concentration, thereby supporting the VDAC-HK2 complexation model.

MOM potential is cell-type dependent

To assess whether ΔΨMOM varies across cell types, we performed analogous measurements in HEK-293 cells, which express lower levels of HK2 compared to HeLa cells (Woldetsadik et al., 2017). SypHer3s was expressed in the cytosol (Fig. 3A) and IMS (Fig. 3B), and compartment-specific pH calibration was performed to estimate cytosolic (Fig. 3C), IMS (Fig. 3D), and matrix pH values (Supplemental Fig. S2C). In HEK-293 cells, we measured pH values of 7.38 ± 0.07 in the cytosol, 7.53 ± 0.12 in the IMS, and 8.11 ± 0.20 in the matrix (Fig. 3E). The pH difference between the cytosol and IMS is insignificant (0.14 ± 0.14); therefore, HEK-293 cells lack an appreciable ΔΨMOM (9 ± 8 mV). Western blot analysis confirmed that HEK-293 cells express lower levels (~50%) of HK2 compared to HeLa cells (Fig. 3F, G and Supplemental Fig. S3). These results suggest that cell-type- and metabolic activity-dependent generation and regulation of ΔΨMOM are mediated by VDAC-HK2 complexation.

Discussion

Mitochondrial inner membrane potential has been well characterized for more than 60 years (Mitchell, 1961; Nicholls, 2008). However, the presence of a membrane potential across the MOM has long been—and remains—a controversial issue. The highly negative inner membrane potential has facilitated its measurement using lipophilic cations that accumulate in the matrix, such as TPMP+, TMRM, and Rhod123 (Liberman et al., 1969; Azzone et al., 1977; Tovar-Ferrero et al., 2025), but the lack of an IMS-specific dye hinders the measurement of the electrical potential across the MOM.

Voltage-sensitive redistribution of membrane-permeable charged dyes is one of the methods used to evaluate organelle potentials (Nikolaev et al., 2023). Assuming equilibrium, differences in dye concentration between cellular compartments can be used to calculate the electric potential by the Nernst equation. In this manner, a previous study by Porcelli et al. (2005) estimated the MOM potential from the difference in proton concentration across the membrane. However, challenges in estimating membrane potentials from proton concentration measurements are not limited to the assumption of equilibrium, as local proton concentrations near charged membranes or macromolecular complexes can be significantly distorted even in equilibrium, therefore biasing estimates of the MOM potential by the Nernst equation. For example, Porcelli et al. linked EYFP to the C-terminus of mGPDH, an integral protein of the inner mitochondrial membrane (Fig. 4), and estimated an IMS pH of 6.8 (Porcelli et al., 2005). But recent studies have shown that proton concentration can vary within the IMS, with pH ranging from 6.8 to 7.2 within the cristae (Rieger et al., 2021). Therefore, given the proximity of mGPDH to the electron transport chain complexes (Orr et al., 2012), the ΔΨMOM of 43 mV, negative on the IMS side, in human ECV304 cells reported by Porcelli et al. may not reflect the MOM potential experienced by VDAC (Fig. 4).

Membrane-less compartmentalization of protons in the IMS affects the measured pH difference across the MOM. pH can vary within the IMS and cristae due to the activity of ETC complexes (Toth et al., 2020; Rieger et al., 2021). Porcelli et al. (2005) linked EYFP to mitochondrial glycerol phosphate dehydrogenase (mGPDH), which is closer to the ETC complexes in the cristae (yellow tag), and measured a cristae pH of 6.88 (pHi,1), which results in a pH difference of 0.71 compared to the cytosol (pHo), yielding a MOM potential of −43 mV. In the present work, we used SypHer3s linked to Smac/DIABLO to measure bulk IMS pH closer to MOM (green tag) and measured an IMS pH of 7.96 (pHi,2). To overcome this limitation, we used a pH sensor, SypHer3s, linked to a Smac/DIABLO fragment, which localizes in the bulk IMS rather than cristae-associated microdomains (Ermakova et al., 2018). This approach enabled a more accurate assessment of the proton concentration difference across the MOM (Fig. 4). In HeLa cells, we calculated a ΔΨMOM of +33 mV. The potential is comparable to the voltage (≥30 mV) required for gating of VDAC reconstituted in a planar lipid bilayer. Importantly, the voltage required to induce VDAC gating varies depending on the experimental conditions, such as membrane lipid composition, VDAC source, local pH (Rostovtseva et al., 2006; Teijido et al., 2014), and molecular crowding in the cell (Zimmerberg and Parsegian, 1986). Moreover, reconstituted VDAC could transition between a fully open anionic state and a partially blocked cationic state at much lower voltages of 10–20 mV in the presence of cytosolic proteins, such as the ß-tubulin dimer and α-synuclein. Both proteins have highly negatively charged, disordered C-terminal tails that can transiently enter the VDAC pore driven by the applied voltage of proper polarity (Rostovtseva et al., 2021). Indeed, the positive potential from the IMS side is critical for the negatively charged C-terminal tails of ß-tubulin and α-synuclein to enter the VDAC pore.

Interestingly, the calculated ΔΨMOM is cell-type-specific, with HEK-293 cells lacking significant potential. The model proposed by Lemeshko (2023) suggests that the VDAC-HK2 complex may act as a battery, generating a potential across the MOM. Given the differences in HK2 expression levels between HeLa cells and HEK-293 cells, we tested the effect of HK2 activity on ΔΨMOM. Consistent with theoretical calculations for the proposed VDAC-HK2 complexation model by Lemeshko (2023), we found that increasing the concentration of glucose, an HK2 substrate, increased ΔΨMOM, while increasing the concentration of G6P, an HK2 product, decreased ΔΨMOM. While these results support the metabolic association of ΔΨMOM, further studies are needed to confirm the proposed HK2-VDAC mechanism and to understand the effects of HK2 expression level, mitochondrial localization, and metabolic activity on the complex regulation of ΔΨMOM. In particular, glucose levels can affect the mitochondrial localization of HK2 (John et al., 2011), and Sun et al. demonstrated that the protective effects of HK in HEK-293 cells depended on both its mitochondrial localization and catalytic activity (Sun et al., 2008). In addition, HK overexpression increased VDAC phosphorylation via a protein kinase Cε-dependent pathway, which could also affect VDAC permeability and ΔΨMOM. Manipulating HK2 expression in different cell types will be important for assessing the mechanism of MOM potential generation via HK2-VDAC complexation.

In conclusion, this work establishes the presence of a significant, metabolism-dependent potential across the MOM in HeLa cells and suggests that the VDAC-HK2 complex may be involved in its generation. Our results substantiate the physiological role of VDAC regulation by transmembrane voltage through gating and interactions with cytosolic proteins, as demonstrated in numerous in vitro experiments. This emphasizes the possibility of dynamic control of VDAC permeability in cells depending on their metabolic state.
