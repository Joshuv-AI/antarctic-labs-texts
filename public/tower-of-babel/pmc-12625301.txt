Effect of upper-limb robot-assisted therapy combined with pneumatic gloves on upper limb function in young and middle-aged stroke patients: a pilot randomized controlled trial
Authors: Limei Wang, Wei Yuan, Hongmei Hou, Guiqiang Qi, Ge Xu, Zuying Liu, Yang Yang, Wei Gou, Qiao Yang, Jue Yu, Xing Wei, Jie Yuan, Fengqin Qin, Na Lv, Rang Li, Yuan Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the effects of an end-effector-type upper-limb robot-assisted therapy (UL-RAT) combined with pneumatic gloves (PGs) on improving upper limb function in young and middle-aged stroke patients.MethodsForty young and middle-aged stroke patients were recruited and randomized into two groups, including a UL-RAT&PG group and a control group, with 20 cases in each group. The control group received conventional upper limb occupational therapy once a day, 5 days a week, for a total of 4 weeks. The UL-RAT&PG group received the combination of UL-RAT and PGs once a day, 5 days a week, for a total of 4 weeks. To evaluate their upper limb function, the primary assessment indices were the Fugl-Meyer assessment extremities (FMA-UE) and Action Research Arm Test (ARAT), and the secondary assessment indices included the Canadian Occupational Performance Measure (COPM), modified Ashworth Scale (MAS), National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), and modified Barthel index (MBI). To assess brain plasticity, Quantitative electroencephalogram (qEEG) and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) were performed. Additionally, Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) assay were conducted to evaluate serum brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and microRNA-132 (miRNA-132) levels. Finally, the Self-rating Anxiety Scale (SAS), Self-rating Depression Scale (SDS), and Exercise Adherence Questionnaire Scale (EAQ) were used to assess patients’ emotional states and compliance with the therapy.ResultsAfter treatment, both groups showed significantly greater improvements in upper limb function. The FMA-UE, ARAT, COPM-executive ability, COPM-satisfaction, and MBI scores of the UL-RAT&PG group were 52.30 ± 1.94, 47.65 ± 7.41, 4.61 ± 1.86, 5.92 ± 1.65, and 87.10 ± 11.11, that all considerably higher than those of the control group (38.6 ± 16.97, 29.90 ± 15.39, 3.39 ± 1.66, 3.61 ± 1.67, and 69.95 ± 17.62), and their NIHSS scores were 1.75 ± 1.12, significantly lower than those of the control group (3.70 ± 1.63). DTABR scores and corticospinal tract (CST) morphology showed positive changes in both groups, indicating an improvement in brain plasticity. The serum BDNF and miRNA-132 levels of both groups were markedly higher than before treatment. In addition, SAS and SDS scores of the control group were 34.55 ± 8.48 and 45.25 ± 12.01, and = UL-RAT&PG groups were 37.85 ± 5.72 and 38.70 ± 8.99, all significantly lower than those before treatment (37.85 ± 9.92 and 49.95 ± 12.59, 42.95 ± 7.62 and 46.10 ± 11.29), indicating that their negative emotions have alleviated. However, the above results of serum BDNF, miRNA-132, SAS, and SDS had no statistical difference between the two groups. Notably, compared to the control group (49.95 ± 12.59), patients in the UL-RAT&PG group had a higher EAQ score (46.10 ± 11.29), indicating better exercise compliance.ConclusionsThe combination of the UL-RAT and PGs can significantly improve upper limb function, compliance, serum BDNF, and serum miRNA-132, and attenuate depression and anxiety in young and middle-aged stroke patients.Trial registration The study was registered on the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500097887).Keywords: Stroke, Upper-limb robot-assisted therapy, Pneumatic glove, Upper limb function, Brain plasticity

Introduction

Stroke is a severe neurological disease and a leading cause of death and disability in adults [1–3]. Approximately 85% of stroke survivors experience various degrees of motor paralysis, primarily affecting the face and the upper and lower limbs of one side of the body. Approximately 55 to 75% of these individuals suffered upper limb dysfunction, which manifests mainly as muscle weakness, spasticity, sensory loss, poor coordination, limited joint motion, and loss of use [4]. This dysfunction limits their activities of daily living (ADL), particularly for personal activities that depend on arm and hand functions, such as feeding, dressing, and grooming [5]. Only about 1/3 of patients ultimately recover some upper limb function [6]. The rehabilitation of upper limb function remains a big focus and challenge of stroke rehabilitation [7]. In recent years, the increasing incidence of stroke in young and middle-aged adults has garnered increased attention from both the medical community and society. Unlike older patients, young and middle-aged stroke patients often bear heavy responsibilities to their families and society, and urgently require a return to their families and work after rehabilitation training. However, the persistent upper limb dysfunction complicates their reintegration into society and results in shame and negative emotions, which can severely impact their quality of life. To date, conventional occupational therapy (OT) remains a fundamental rehabilitation training for stroke patients’ upper limb function. The required training equipment includes rollers, frosted boards, wooden nail boards, wands, shoulder-lifting training devices, fingerboards, etc. Training through these devices can not only improve the range and coordination function of gross upper limb movements, but also maintain joint mobility and prevent joint contracture. However, in clinical practice, many young and middle-aged stroke patients with high education levels do not strongly accept this training content. They are often more open to new concepts in training, such as video games. Therefore, introducing novel rehabilitation training methods with strong entertainment and creativity for middle-aged and young stroke patients may help promote their compliance with upper limb functional training.

In recent years, some artificial intelligence rehabilitation treatment devices have been applied in the motor rehabilitation of the upper limb for stroke patients, and have achieved positive rehabilitation treatment effects, such as upper-limb robot-assisted therapy (UL-RAT) [8], pneumatic gloves (PGs) [9], and brain computer interface technology (BCI) [10]. Previous research has demonstrated that upper-limb robot-assisted therapy (UL-RAT) can positively impact motor impairments in the paretic upper limb by significantly increasing Fugl-Meyer assessment (FMA) scores and improving sensory function [6]. UL-RAT utilizes virtual reality (VR) and robotic devices to facilitate interactive training under various movement patterns. VR provides motor- or task-oriented training in simulated real-life situations for the patient through interactive interfaces. Robotic devices can provide physical support and guide the paretic upper limb to achieve active, assisted, or passive movement during training sessions. It is worth noting that the training content is presented in the form of games, which are engaging, purposeful, and tailored to the interests of young and middle-aged stroke patients who enjoy electronic games. Some games can even serve as pre-career guidance training methods. Thus, the UL-RAT is particularly well-suited for upper limb rehabilitation in young and middle-aged stroke patients. There are two types of commercial UL-RATs used in medical institutions: end-effector and exoskeleton. The end-effector type UL-RAT contacts the hand and wrist via the end effector, and the upper limb moves to perform tasks that simulate daily life within the VR environment, following the end effector. This UL-RAT device features a simpler structure and requires less complicated control algorithms [11]. The exoskeleton type UL-RAT has a mechanical structure that directly drives the specific joint movements and trains certain muscle groups in the upper limbs. This type of UL-RAT has a more complex mechanical design and control algorithms than end-effector-based systems [12, 13]. Considering the factors of cost and venue, the end effector type UL-RAT may be more suitable for primary hospitals. Although the end-effector type UL-RAT focuses on the combined motion of the upper limb chain, its handle or fingerboard design may not provide sufficient exercise for the fingers, which can be a drawback for finger motor rehabilitation in stroke patients [14]. To address this issue, PGs can serve as a supplementary tool of end-effector type UL-RATs for finger motor training. PGs assist each finger extension by air pressure, and then the air pressure is removed to allow the finger to flex freely, achieving finger movement. For example, the pneumatic gloves developed by Connelly and colleagues (PneuGlove) can train grasp-and-release movements either with real objects or with virtual objects in a virtual reality environment [15]. At present, some commercial PGs already feature a mirror training function and a “human-machine interaction” mode for hand function training. This approach effectively combines mirror therapy (MT) with robotic assistance for hand rehabilitation. These PGs can also provide a bilateral training mode based on MT, encouraging patients to focus on coordinating the function of both hands rather than just repetitively training one hand. Notably, stroke patients can use PGs at home in addition to rehabilitation institutions.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effectiveness of the end effector type UL-RAT combined with the PGs on upper limb function in young and middle-aged stroke patients. We evaluated the rehabilitation effects on the upper limb via several methods, including the Fugl-Meyer assessment-upper extremities (FMA-UE), Action Research Arm Test (ARAT), Canadian Occupational Performance Measure (COPM), modified Ashworth scale (MAS), National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), and modified Barthel index (MBI). Additionally, we utilized quantitative electroencephalography (qEEG) and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) to assess brain plasticity in the patients. The serum levels of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and microRNA-132 (miRNA-132) were determined using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), respectively. Finally, we also investigated the emotions and compliance of the stroke patients.

Methods

Study design and participants

Participants and setting

The study recruited young and middle-aged stroke patients with upper limb hemiparesis from the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine and the Neurology Department of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Chengdu Medical College & Chengdu Pidu District People’s Hospital from July 2023 to May 2024. All patients had to meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) diagnosed with their first stroke between the ages of 18 and 58, confirmed by imaging examinations such as head CT or MRI; (2) experienced a stroke within the last 3 months; (3) had a Brunnstrom stage for the upper limb function from 1 to 4 grade, and an MAS below Grade II; (4) achieved at least level I in sitting balance grading; (5) exhibited no cognitive impairment, with a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score above 23; (6) had no unilateral neglect or hemianopia; (7) maintained stable vital signs and were conscious; and (8) provided written informed consent, with agreement from the patient and/or their relative.

Procedure

A total of 40 patients were randomized into the control group or the UL-RAT&PG group at a 1:1 ratio, with 20 cases in each group. This randomization protocol was conducted by an independent individual using computer-generated random numbers, who was not involved in the implementation or evaluation of results. Patients were informed of their treatment assignments by the unblinded members of the research team. However, the allocation remained concealed from the blinded members of the research team, and the two unblinded members of the research team did not participate in data collection or analysis of the trial results. Both groups of patients received upper limb function rehabilitation treatment, which included conventional rehabilitation therapy and acupuncture-moxibustion treatment for stroke. The control group received the conventional upper limb occupational therapy, whereas the UL-RAT&PG group received a combination of UL-RAT and PGs. The specific procedures of intervention procedures are as follows:

Conventional rehabilitation therapy and acupuncture-moxibustion treatment: (1) Hemiplegic upper extremity exercise training: This training aims to inhibit abnormal behavior patterns in hemiplegic upper limbs, induce the gradual development of voluntary movement, improve the range of motion (ROM) of the shoulder, elbow, wrist, and finger joints, and enhance upper limb muscle strength and endurance. (2) Balance function training: This training, which includes sitting balance or standing balance training, aims to improve coordination between the trunk and limbs and overall balance functionality. (3) Acupuncture-moxibustion treatment: Based on the principles of traditional Chinese medicine, specific acupoints are used primarily for acupuncture treatment. The main acupoints used include Sishencong acupoint (EX-NH1), Baihui acupoint (GV20), Hegu acupoint (LI4), Quchi acupoint (LI11), Zusanli acupoint (ST36), and Sanyinjiao acupoint (SP6). In addition, the Qihai acupoint (CV6) and Guanyuan acupoint (CV4) were used primarily for moxibustion treatment. Both groups received the above treatment once a day, 5 days a week, for 4 consecutive weeks.

Conventional upper limb occupational therapy: (1) Upper limb gross motor training includes roller operation, moving wooden blocks, wooden nail plates, frosted boards, and other activities. (2) Hand function training includes screwing screws, assembling boards, inserting wooden sticks, folding paper, gripping strength balls, picking beans, pulling bottle caps, etc. The control group received the above treatment once a day, 5 days a week, for 4 consecutive weeks (Fig. 1A–D).

UL-RAT combined with PG treatment: (1) Patients received robot-assisted motor function training via an end-effector type upper limb rehabilitation system (ArmGuider, Shanghai Zhuodao, China) for 20 min each session. The training scenes related to daily life included a “machine gun shooting game”, “catching butterflies”, “watering flowers”, “cutting fruits”, “supermarket shopping”, and other dynamic games. These games require patients to move their hemiplegic upper limb, such as elbow flexion and extension, shoulder flexion and extension, shoulder abduction and adduction, and shoulder rotation. At the same time, the wrist achieves the active or passive flexion-extension motion, following the handle of the device’s motion arm. (2) Patients received hand function training using PGs (WNK-PDO3, Shanxi Niwei Technology Co., Ltd., China) for 10 min each session. Under the guidance of a therapist, patients controlled their healthy hand and the hemiplegic-side hand to complete simultaneous grasping, stretching, single-finger flexion, extension, etc. At the same time, patients were encouraged to pay attention to the movements of both hands and imagine that the hemiplegic-side hand could perform the same actions as their healthy hand. Once patients became proficient in these movements, they practiced gripping bottles or balls. The UL-RAT&PG group received the above treatment every day, 5 days a week, for 4 consecutive weeks (Fig. 1E–H).

Outcome measurement

A series of assessments was conducted before and after treatment, which included evaluations of upper limb function, brain plasticity, serological indicator detection, and emotional and compliance status.

Upper limb function evaluation

FMA-UE

FMA-UE is a widely used scale with good validity and reliability for evaluating the degree of recovery of upper limb function in stroke patients. It has 33 items and a total score of 66 points [16]. The higher the total score, the better the upper limb function.

ARAT

ARAT consists of 4 parts and 19 tests involving grasp, grip, pinch, and gross-motion exercises to evaluate upper-limb function. Various equipment was used, including various-sized cube wooden blocks, a sharpening stone, a cricket ball, a glass and water jar, hollow tubes with varying heights and thicknesses, ball bearings, washers, and marbles. Each task is scored from 0 to 3, contributing to a total score of 57 [16]. The higher the score, the milder the degree of upper limb motor dysfunction.

COPM

COPM is a self-reported tool used to assess occupational performance in self-care, productivity, and leisure through a semi-structured interview. It is valid and reliable for stroke patients [17, 18]. The patient first identifies their expected activities and ranks each activity in importance. After attempting the five most important activities, the patient rates their ability to perform each activity and their satisfaction with the performance on a 10-point visual analogue scale (1 = not able to do it at all/not satisfied at all, 10 = performs well/very satisfied) and the total scores of execution ability and satisfaction obtained were taken as the average, respectively. Finally, the difference between the mean total scores of executive ability and satisfaction for the same activities before and after treatment was determined, respectively. The larger the difference, the better the patient’s activity or the higher the level of satisfaction.

MAS

MAS is commonly used to assess muscle tone in the upper limbs (arm and hand) of stroke patients at six levels: 0, I, I+, II, III, and IV. The higher the level, the higher the muscle tone. The biceps brachii muscle, the triceps brachii muscle, the flexor digitorum profundus muscle, and the flexor digitorum superficialis muscle were the main target muscles of MAS in this study. For the convenience of evaluating the results of MAS grading in hemiplegic arm and hand, level 0 is scored as 0 points, level I is scored as 1 point, level I + is scored as 2 points, level II is scored as 3 points, level III is scored as 4 points, and level IV is scored as 5 points, referring to the scoring for MAS grading in the research conducted by Wu et al. [19] and Cai et al. [20].

NIHSS

NIHSS is a 15-item impairment scale used to assess stroke severity, which includes the following domains: level of consciousness, eye movements, integrity of visual fields, facial movements, arm and leg muscle strength, sensation, coordination, language, speech, and neglect. The total score ranges from 0 to 42. The higher the total score, the more severe the stroke [21].

MBI

MBI is commonly used to assess the activities of daily living (ADL) in stroke patients, and consists of 10 items, including personal hygiene, bathing, feeding, using the toilet, stairs, clothes, bowel and bladder control, chair/bed transfers, and ambulation or wheelchair use, with a total score of 100 points. The higher the score, the better the ability to engage in daily activities.

Brain plasticity evaluation

qEEG

The qEEG can transform brain waves with varying amplitude over time into digital electrical signals with varying power and frequency, providing objective and quantifiable digital parameters for brain electrical activity. The qEEG with scalp columnar electrodes and a 24-lead single-stage (XE-0–24, NCC Electrophysiology, China) was conducted on patients to obtain the absolute power values of α, β, δ, and θ, and calculate the (δ + θ)/(α + β) ratio (DTABR). The electrode placement is as follows: FP1, FP2 (frontal region collection points), F3, F4 (left and right frontal lobe collection points) placed on the frontal lobe; F7, F8 (left and right anterior temporal lobe collection points), T3, T4 (left and right temporal lobe collection points), T5, T6 (left and right occipital temporal lobe collection points) placed on the temporal lobe; P3 and P4 (left and right top leaf collection points) placed on top leaves; O1 and O2 (collection points in the left and right occipital regions) placed on the occipital leaves. When patients undergo EEG examination, data is collected while their eyes are open, alternating between open and closed eyes, flashing stimulation, and hyperventilation (i.e., deep inhalation followed by deep exhalation). The EEG inspector guided patients to meditate on upper limb flexion and extension activities and fist clenching movements during the qEEG process. A filtering channel of 0.5–30 Hz, with a time constant of 0.3 s and a paper feed speed of 3 centimeters per second, was selected. The data were recorded for 30 min each time, and EEG analysis was performed using visual and spectral analysis methods (Fig. 2A, B). The lower the DTABR value, the better the brain function.

MRI acquisition

A Siemens MAGNETOM Skyra 3.0T MRI scanner (Siemens, Germany) was used for routine head MRI examinations, including diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI). Routine T1 and T2-weighted scans were performed with a diffusion weighting coefficient (b value) of 1000 s/mm2, a slice thickness of 5 mm, a 1.5 mm gap, a Matrix of 128 × 128, and an FOV of 240 × 240 mm. DTI was performed using spin-echo echo-planar imaging (SE-EP) sequence with a diffusion weighting coefficient (b value) of 1000 s/mm2, a slice thickness of 4 mm, a 1.2 mm gap, a Matrix of 128 × 128, and an FOV of 220 × 220 mm. Diffusion tensor fractional anisotropy (FA) was measured using DTI, and FA images were generated with Functool-specific software (Fig. 2C–E). In addition, the ratio of FA (rFA) was calculated by dividing the FA value on the lesion side by the FA value on the opposite side of the lesion. The rFA values range from 0 to 1, where 1 represents the maximum anisotropy. The larger the rFA value, the stronger the nerve conduction function.

Serological indicator detection

ELISA detection of serum BDNF

Peripheral blood samples were collected from all fasting patients before and after the 4-week intervention. The samples were then centrifuged to separate the serum. Serum levels of BDNF were measured using the BDNF ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s standard protocols (ZC-34227, ZCIBIO Technology Co., Ltd, China).

qRT-PCR

Peripheral blood samples were collected from all fasting patients before and after the 4-week intervention. The samples were then centrifuged to separate the serum for a qRT-PCR assay of serum miRNA-132. According to the instructions of Molpure ® Cell/Tissue Total RNA Kit (19221ES50, YEASEN, China), total serum RNA was extracted and then purified through a DNA clearance/RNA adsorption column. The primers miRNA-132-3p and U6 were synthesized by Lilaisino Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The Bulge Loop™ MiRNA qRT PCR Starter Kit (R10211-1, Ruibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China) and Hairpin itTM microRNA and U6 snRNA Normalization RT PCR Quantification Kit (GenePharma, China) were used for miRNA RT reaction. The reaction was initiated with denaturation at 95 ℃ for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95˚C for 12 s and 62 ℃ for 40 s (annealing and extension). The CT values of each test sample in the RCR process were analyzed by QuanStudioTM Design & Analysis SE Software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Relative miRNA-132 expression was defined as the ratio of miRNA-132 expression to U6 expression.

Emotional and compliance status evaluation

Self-rating anxiety scale (SAS)

Zung’s SAS is a self-report scale and is a widely used self-report tool for screening anxiety disorders. It consists of 20 items that cover various anxiety symptoms and assess patients’ experiences within the past week. Each item is scored on a 4-point scale ranging from 1 (never or rarely) to 4 (usually or always). The total score of the 20 items is multiplied by 1.25 to calculate the total SAS score. A score of 50 or higher indicates the presence of anxiety [22, 23]. The higher the SAS score, the greater the degree of anxiety.

Self-rating depression scale (SDS)

Zung’s SDS is a psychometric tool designed to assess depression and depressive states in the general population [23]. It consists of 20 self-report items that cover psychiatric disorders, somatic disorders, psychomotor disorders, and psychological disorders of depression, and assesses patients’ mood symptoms within the past week. Each item is scored on a 4-point scale ranging from 1 (none, or a little of the time) to 4 (most, or all of the time). The total score of the 20 items is multiplied by 1.25 to calculate the total SDS score. In the Chinese population, a score of 53 or higher indicates a depressive state [24]. The higher the SDS score, the greater the degree of depression.

Exercise adherence questionnaire scale (EAQ)

EAQ is an assessment tool designed to evaluate physical engagement during exercise, monitor exercise outcomes, and provide advice. It consists of 3 dimensions and 14 question items, with a total score of 56 points [25]. Each item is scored on a 4-point scale ranging from 1 (completely impossible to achieve) to 4 (completely achievable). The greater the total score, the higher the degree of exercise compliance.

Statistical analysis

The data were statistically analyzed using SPSS 27.0. All the data underwent a test of normality of the distribution using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Measurement data with means ± standard deviations were analyzed by t-tests, whereas categorical data were expressed as frequencies and percentages and were analyzed by the chi-square test. The normally distributed data in the intergroup and intragroup comparisons before and after treatment were analyzed using independent sample or paired sample t-tests. The non-normally distributed data represented by the interquartile range in the intergroup and intragroup comparisons were analyzed using Mann-Whitney tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, respectively. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Comparison of upper limb function

Primary outcomes

FMA-UE

Before treatment, the total FMA-UE scores of the control and UL-RAT&PG groups were 25.60 ± 13.10 points and 23.05 ± 11.0 points, respectively, with no statistically significant difference (P = 0.51). ARAT

Before treatment, the ARAT scores of the control and UL-RAT&PG groups were 15.40 ± 11.89 points and 21.30 ± 15.50 points, respectively, with no statistically significant difference (P = 0.18). After treatment, the ARAT score of the control group was 29.90 ± 15.39 points, which was significantly higher than that before treatment (P = 0.00). The ARAT score in the UL-RAT&PG group was 47.65 ± 7.41 points, which was significantly higher than that before treatment and in the control group (all P = 0.00) (Table 2).

Secondary outcomes

COPM

Before treatment, the mean total scores of executive ability in the control and UL-RAT&PG groups were 1.36 ± 1.00 points and 1.27 ± 1.01 points, respectively, with no statistically significant difference (P = 0.77). After treatment, the mean total score of executive ability in the control group was 3.39 ± 1.66 points, which was significantly higher than that before treatment (P = 0.00). The mean total score of executive ability in the UL-RAT&PG group was 4.61 ± 1.86 points, which was significantly higher than that before treatment and in the control group (P < 0.05) (Table 2).

Before treatment, the mean total scores of satisfaction in the control and UL-RAT&PG groups were 1.25 ± 0.95 points and 2.29 ± 1.25 points, respectively, with no statistically significant difference (P = 0.27). After treatment, the mean total score of satisfaction in the control group was 3.61 ± 1.67 points, which was significantly higher than that before treatment (P = 0.00). The mean total score of satisfaction in the UL-RAT&PG group was 5.92 ± 1.65 points, which was significantly higher than that before treatment and in the control group (all P = 0.00) (Table 2).

MAS

Before treatment, the MAS scores of the hemiplegic arm in the control and UL-RAT&PG groups were 2 (1, 2) points and 2 (1, 2.75) points, respectively, with no statistically significant difference (P = 0.88). After treatment, the MAS score in the control group was 1 (0, 1) points, significantly lower than before treatment (P = 0.00). The MAS score in the UL-RAT&PG group was 0 (0, 1) points, which was significantly lower than that before treatment and in the control group (all P < 0.05) (Table 2).

Before treatment, the MAS scores of the hemiplegic hand in the control and UL-RAT&PG groups were 2 (1, 2) points and 1.5 (1,2.75) points, respectively, with no statistically significant difference (P = 0.11). After treatment, the MAS score in the control group was 1 (0, 1) points, which was significantly lower than that before treatment (P = 0.00). The MAS score in the UL-RAT&PG group was 1(1, 1) points, which was significantly lower than that before treatment and in the control group (all P < 0.05) (Table 2).

NIHSS

Before treatment, the total NIHSS scores in the control and UL-RAT&PG groups were 6.70 ± 2.74 points and 5.30 ± 1.92 points, respectively, with no statistically significant difference (P = 0.07). After treatment, the NIHSS score in the control group was 3.70 ± 1.63 points, which was significantly lower than that before treatment (P = 0.00). The NIHSS score in the UL-RAT&PG group was 1.75 ± 1.12 points, which was significantly lower than that before treatment and in the control group (all P = 0.05) (Table 2).

MBI

Before treatment, the MBI scores of the control and UL-RAT&PG groups were 44.70 ± 21.42 points and 54.80 ± 18.92 points, respectively, with no statistically significant difference (P = 0.12). After treatment, the MBI score in the control group was 69.95 ± 17.62 points, which was significantly higher than that before treatment (P = 0.00). The MBI score in the UL-RAT&PG group was 87.10 ± 11.11 points, which was significantly higher than that before treatment and in the control group (all P = 0.00) (Table 2).

Comparison of brain plasticity

DTABR

Before treatment, the DTABR (δ + θ/а+β) values of the control and UL-RAT&PG groups were 4.11 ± 2.34 and 5.54 ± 2.24, respectively, with no statistically significant difference (P = 0.06). Before treatment, the levels of serum miRNA-132 in the control and UL-RAT&PG groups showed no statistically significant difference (P > 0.05). Discussion

In this 4-week pilot study, we assessed the upper limb function of young and middle-aged stroke patients in the UL-RAT&PG group before and after treatment. The results revealed that the primary outcomes, FMA-UE and ARAT scores, were significantly increased after treatment (all P < 0.01). The secondary outcomes, COPM and MBI scores, were significantly increased, while the MAS and NIHSS scores were significantly decreased after treatment (all P < 0.05), indicating that utilizing the UL-RAT in combination with PGs can markedly enhance the hemiplegic upper limb function and improve young and middle-aged stroke patients’ daily living activities. Notably, the primary outcomes (FMA-UE, ARAT) were significantly higher than those in the control group (all P = 0.00), indicating that the combination of UL-RAT and PGs has an advantage in the primary outcomes compared to conventional upper limb occupational therapy. These advantages may stem from the UL-RAT assists patients in completing upper limb activities such as shoulder joint flexion and extension, abduction, elbow joint flexion and extension, wrist joint flexion and extension, and ulnar radial deviation, by simulating routine activities in daily life, which contributes to improving the range of motion of various joints in the upper limb, enhancing motor control ability, and improving motor accuracy, promoting upper limb function recovery. PGs offer targeted training for individual finger joints or multiple finger joints, including finger flexion, extension, and finger alignment, which contributes to improving finger joint mobility, finger muscle strength, and joint coordination, making up for the limitations of UL-RAT. At the same time, the mirror training mode of PGs facilitates bilateral hand function training, encouraging patients to focus on the coordination of both hands. Notably, the UL-RAT combined with PGs can offer personalized scenario interaction tasks to improve hemiplegic upper limb function, focusing on muscle strength, muscle tone, and joint activity training according to individual patient needs and functional status.

Limb functional recovery in stroke patients depends on brain plasticity [26]. Therefore, assessments of brain plasticity are meaningful for evaluating and guiding the rehabilitation of hemiplegic upper limb function. qEEG can objectively reflect the connectivity between different cortical regions and changes in brain plasticity [27]. For example, qEEG-derived indices, DTABR, may be associated with the NIHSS score, providing meaningful prognostic information regarding the assessment of motor and functional recovery in stroke patients [28, 29]. In the present study, the DTABR values of patients treated with UL-RAT combined with PG were significantly lower (P < 0.05), as well as the NIHSS score, indicating an improvement in brain plasticity, which promotes the recovery of central nervous system function. Next, we observed the morphology of CST using DWI imaging. The CST originates from various cortical regions and is the thickest motor nerve conduction pathway in the pyramidal tract, innervating spinal motor neurons. Stroke-induced brain injury can compromise the integrity and conduction function of the CST, resulting in the loss of control of upper motor neurons in the spinal cord, which leads to spasticity in the contralateral limb and hinders the recovery of limb motor function in stroke patients [30, 31]. The extent of CST damage is correlated with the severity of spasticity in stroke patients [32]. Using DTI imaging can visualize the fiber routing of the CST in the brain, enabling the evaluation of the degree and extent of CST damage caused by stroke, and it can even predict hand function recovery based on the integrity of the hand fibers of the CST [33]. We found that the integrity and thickness of the CST in stroke patients treated with UL-RAT combined with PG were improved compared with those before treatment in DTI imaging. At the same time, the MAS scores of the hemiplegic arm and hand significantly declined. Similarly, Ebrahimzadeh and colleagues found that the improvements in CST integrity were associated with a significant reduction in MAS scores, resulting in decreased muscle spasticity in the upper limb and an improved range of motion in the hand of a stroke patient [34]. In addition, FA, as the most frequently used DTI-derived parameter, is an index of the integrity of neural fibers and can be used to assess the long-term outcomes in stroke patients. In particular, rFA, which reflects the severity of neural damage in the ipsilesional hemisphere relative to that in the intact contralesional hemisphere, also strongly correlates with stroke outcomes. A negative correlation between the rFA and NIHSS score in stroke patients suggests that higher rFA values are associated with better recovery of upper limb function [35, 36]. We also observed a significant increase in the DTI-rFA value and a remarkable decrease in the NIHSS score in the patients treated with the UL-RAT combined with PG in the present study, indicating that patients may experience better outcomes. These positive changes in qEEG and MRI imaging demonstrate that the UL-RAT combined with PG treatment can help restore connections between different regions of the brain, and the positive changes in CST morphology may play a role in reducing upper limb muscle spasticity and improving upper limb motor function. This improvement may be attributed to the following aspects. On the one hand, the situational interactive training of the UL-RAT offers patients various multi-sensory stimuli, including visual, auditory, cognitive, and perceptual stimuli, to activate the activity of sensory and motor-related brain regions and enhance the connectivity of various cortical regions. On the other hand, the mirror therapy of PGs stimulates the mirror neuron system through mirror visual feedback during action observation, simulated imagination, and learning, leading to activation of the primary motor cortex and promoting functional remodeling in the brain.

BDNF, a member of the neurotrophin family of growth factors, plays a crucial role in the development of the nervous system and is involved in CST axon regeneration via BDNF-TrkB signaling [37]. MiRNA-132 is a neuroprotective non-coding RNA in the nervous system that is involved in the generation, regeneration, migration, and maturation of brain synapses after injury [38]. Notably, BDNF can upregulate the miRNA-132 expression to improve the central nervous system neuronal function by the crosstalk between miRNA-132 and BDNF-TrkB signaling pathway [39–41]. In the present study, we observed that UL-RAT combined with PG treatment increased the serum BDNF and miRNA-132 levels. We suggest that this treatment method may promote CST remodeling and brain plasticity through the crosstalk of the miRNA-132/BDNF-TrkB signaling pathway, thereby promoting the recovery of central nervous system function. Of course, we cannot ignore that all patients receive conventional rehabilitation therapy and acupuncture-moxibustion treatment, which includes limb movement training and acupuncture treatment, and may also positively impact the serum levels of BDNF and miRNA-132.

The emotions and compliance of stroke patients are also important factors affecting rehabilitation outcomes. Upper limb function is crucial for daily life, so the difficulty and time of recovery will increase psychological stress, resulting in negative emotions during rehabilitation training. In the present study, we assessed the anxiety and depression levels of young and middle-aged stroke patients in the UL-RAT&PG group using SAS and SDS, respectively. We found that patients’ anxiety and depression significantly decreased after the UL-RAT combined with PG treatment, and these patients had better compliance than those who received conventional upper limb occupational therapy. We consider that the positive changes in emotions and compliance may stem from the challenging, entertaining, and personalized content of the UL-RAT combined with PG treatment, encouraging young and middle-aged stroke patients to participate in their treatment both psychologically and physically actively.

In addition, we found that conventional upper limb occupational therapy significantly increased the FMA-UE, ARAT, COPM, and MBI scores and significantly decreased the MAS, NIHSS, SAS, SDS, and DTABR scores in the control group. We also observed positive changes in the morphology of the CST. These findings indicate that conventional upper limb occupational therapy can effectively improve upper limb function, promote brain plasticity, and alleviate negative emotions in young and middle-aged stroke patients. There were significant differences in the FMA-UE, ARAT, COPM, and NIHSS scores between the control and UL-RAT&PG groups after treatment, especially the primary outcomes (FMA-UE, ARAT), indicating that the improvements in upper limb function by conventional upper limb occupational therapy were not as good as the combination of UL-RAT and PGs. We speculate that this difference may stem from the variations in training modes, conditions, and feedback mechanisms between the conventional upper limb occupational therapy and UL-RAT combined with PG treatment. The UL-RAT combined with PG treatment employs intelligent motion control, optimized parameter design, and motion data monitoring functions, allowing patients to move within a safe range and providing timely feedback, correcting abnormal motion patterns in the upper limbs, forearms, and wrists, making this method promotes the learning of correct motion control and may have advantages in upper limb function training, which are not available in conventional upper limb occupational therapy. Of course, we cannot be ignored that conventional upper limb occupational therapy can provide targeted training for patients’ finger movements, control, and hand-eye coordination abilities through training content that is closely related to daily life, such as screwing screws, assembling boards, and folding paper, which highlights the practicality of the hands and may have advantages in hand function training. We also found that the levels of serum BDNF and miRNA-132 in the control group were significantly increased after treatment, with no statistically significant difference compared to the UL-RAT&PG group, indicating that the process of improving brain plasticity through conventional upper limb occupational therapy also involves changes in the serum levels of BDNF and miRNA-132, and may involve a similar regulatory mechanism as the UL-RAT combined with PG treatment. Although patients in the UL-RAT&PG group had better compliance with functional exercise than the patients in the control group, which may lead to the UL-RAT combined with PG treatment having more benefits than conventional approaches in improving upper limb function, compliance is influenced by various factors, including level of impairment, the mechanical properties of the robot, the control strategies, the training mode of the robot, the instructions of the therapist and various other factors [42]. In this 4-week pilot study, we did not observe any significant positive changes in the morphology of the corticospinal tract (CST), serum levels of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), or miRNA-132 compared to conventional approaches after the UL-RAT combined with PG treatment. Overall, we conclude that a 4-week intervention using UL-RAT combined with PGs can significantly improve upper limb function, similar to conventional upper limb occupational therapy. As more rehabilitation facilities adopt UL-RAT and PGs, it becomes feasible to implement the combination of UL-RAT and PGs. Indeed, when performing upper limb functional rehabilitation for young and middle-aged stroke patients, it is necessary to consider the conditions of healthcare facilities, costs, and the individual characteristics of the patients (e.g., age, gender, personality, hobbies, occupation, education level, rehabilitation needs, etc.) to provide complementary various rehabilitation training modes to improve the patient’s upper limb function, promote neurological function recovery, and shorten the rehabilitation cycle.

Limitations of the study

There are some limitations to the present study that should be acknowledged. First, the sample size is small due to limited funding and research time. The intervention time is 4 weeks due to the practical limits on the length of hospital stay for patients. As a pilot study, it can provide valuable insights for future research. Second, this study utilized commonly used upper limb function assessment scales that are easy to operate in clinical practice. However, these scales may involve a certain degree of subjectivity from the assessor, which introduces a risk of bias. Third, time-consuming and expensive detection methods, including qEEG and MRI imaging, were employed. Some patients were unable to cooperate with MRI imaging throughout the research period due to funding and personnel constraints, resulting in a lack of DTI results for those individuals. In total, complete DTI data were collected for 18 cases before and after treatment. Finally, this study lacks safety outcomes and a follow-up evaluation to assess the long-term effects of the UL-RAT combined with PG treatment on stroke patients.

Conclusion

The combination of the UL-RAT and PGs can significantly improve upper limb function and compliance and attenuate depression and anxiety in young and middle-aged stroke patients. The improvement in brain plasticity after the UL-RAT combined with PG treatment may be closely related to the elevated serum BDNF and miRNA-132 levels.
