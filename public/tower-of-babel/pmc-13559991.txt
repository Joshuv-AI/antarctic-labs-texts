TMEM106B is a selective modulator of TDP‐43 pathology in Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Madison M Reeves, Anna Calliari, Tiffany W Todd, Candela Maroto Cidfuentes, Karen Jansen‐West, Mei Yue, Yuping Song, Judith Dunmore, Bailey Rawlinson, Erica Engelberg‐Cook, Michael DeTure, Gregory S Day, Neill R Graff‐Radford, Bradley F Boeve, David S Knopman, Ronald C Petersen, Casey N Cook, Melissa E Murray, Dennis W Dickson, Keith A Josephs, Leonard Petrucelli, Mercedes Prudencio

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONCo‐pathologies – including Lewy body, vascular, and TDP‐43 lesions – are common in Alzheimer's disease (AD), contributing to its clinical and pathological heterogeneity. Genetic risk factors may drive mixed pathology presentation, but their influence on the development of specific co‐pathologies remains unclear.METHODSWe evaluated the TMEM106B coding variant rs3173615 and apolipoprotein E (APOE) diplotype (rs429358, rs7412) in post mortem brains from 2604 individuals with a primary neuropathologic diagnosis of AD. Binary logistic regression models linked each genetic modifier with co‐pathology risk.RESULTSThe TMEM106B risk variant was significantly enriched in AD cases with transactive response DNA‐binding protein 43 kDa (TDP‐43) pathology – associating with increased odds of developing AD TDP‐43 subtype α – but failed to associate with Lewy body or vascular pathology. In contrast, APOE ε4 associated with increased risk for multiple co‐pathologies in AD.DISCUSSIONWe find that genetic factors individually influence AD pathological heterogeneity: TMEM106B selectively modulates TDP‐43 co‐pathology, while APOE ε4 appears broadly permissive to co‐pathology development.Keywords: AluYb8, Alzheimer's disease, apolipoprotein E, APOE, co‐pathologies, genetics, limbic‐predominant age‐related TDP‐43 encephalopathy (LATE), neurodegenerative disease, transactive response DNA‐binding protein 43 (TDP‐43), TMEM106B

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia worldwide. 1 Although defined by extracellular amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) of hyperphosphorylated tau, AD is increasingly recognized as a disorder characterized by a broad spectrum of pathological changes, including the co‐occurrence of Lewy bodies, 2 , 3 cerebrovascular lesions, 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 and transactive response DNA‐binding protein 43 kDa (TDP‐43) inclusions. 8 , 9 , 10 Such co‐pathologies are common in AD 11 , 12 , 13 and linked to worse disease outcomes: α‐synuclein Lewy bodies accelerate cognitive decline in AD, 14 , 15 , 16 while cerebrovascular lesions promote cognitive impairment independent of Aβ plaques and NFTs. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 TDP‐43 co‐pathology is present in over half of AD cases, 21 , 22 , 23 where it significantly associates with increased hippocampal atrophy, lower levels of global cognition, and more rapid cognitive decline, even after accounting for the contribution of classical AD pathology and hippocampal sclerosis. 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 Notably, the absence of TDP‐43 inclusions was identified as a key determinant of cognitive resilience in individuals of an advanced age – more so than a lower incidence of Lewy bodies or vascular lesions. 31 Indeed, a lack of TDP‐43 pathology was associated with normal cognition in a neuropathologically defined AD cohort, despite the fact that all subjects analyzed had similar Aβ and NFT burdens – arguing that TDP‐43 pathology alone may be sufficient to overpower other drivers of cognitive resilience and induce cognitive impairment in individuals that are resistant to the impact of classic AD pathology. 27 Finally, co‐pathologies may exert synergistic effects on clinical AD, as patients with multiple co‐pathologies exhibit greater dementia severity and more rapid cognitive decline. 11 , 32 , 33 Despite mounting evidence that co‐pathologies profoundly impact disease outcomes, however, few studies have sought to map the genetic underpinnings of AD mixed pathology development.

A key genetic modifier of AD risk and pathology is apolipoprotein E (APOE): the APOE ε4 allele has been linked to increased risk for late‐onset AD 34 and higher incidences of Lewy body co‐pathology, 35 , 36 TDP‐43 co‐pathology, 37 , 38 and vascular lesions like cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 in AD cohorts. But APOE is only the beginning: A recent post mortem analysis showed that many other genetic variants previously associated with clinically defined AD are not linked to Aβ plaques and NFTs but are instead risk factors for the development of co‐pathologies. 43 An interesting example is transmembrane protein 106B (TMEM106B).

TMEM106B variants represent strong risk factors for frontotemporal lobar degeneration with TDP‐43 inclusions (FTLD‐TDP), 44 AD, 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 and limbic‐predominant age‐related TDP‐43 encephalopathy (LATE). 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 Conversely, TMEM106B protective variants are enriched in cognitively healthy centenarians, 55 strongly linking TMEM106B to resilience against age‐related neuropathologies. TMEM106B risk variants reside on a linkage disequilibrium block containing several single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), one of which is coding: rs3173615 (C > G) results in a threonine‐to‐serine substitution at TMEM106B residue 185 (p.T185S). 56 The risk haplotype is also in near‐perfect linkage disequilibrium with a mobile AluYb8 insertion within the 3’ untranslated region (UTR) of TMEM106B in patients with European ancestry. 57 , 58 , 59 Notably, insoluble fibrils composed of a cleaved TMEM106B C‐terminal fragment 60 are detected in a range of conditions spanning TDP‐43 proteinopathies, tauopathies, synucleinopathies, and amyloidoses. 61 , 62 , 63 , 64 This aberrant TMEM106B fibril deposition is modulated by TMEM106B rs3173615, with carriers of the risk allele showing not only more robust TMEM106B pathology but also increased TDP‐43 pathology in FTLD‐TDP. 65 Genome‐wide association studies and targeted genotyping analyses also link TMEM106B to the presence of TDP‐43 co‐pathology in AD and dementia cohorts 38 , 43 , 50 , 66 and increased TDP‐43 burden in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). 67 Congruent with these findings, we found that the TMEM106B rs3173615 variant was significantly associated with TDP‐43 co‐pathology, independent of APOE ε4 or the presence of other co‐pathologies, in a robust cohort of 2604 neuropathologically confirmed post mortem AD cases from the Mayo Clinic Brain Bank. This association was further validated by the analysis of the TMEM106B AluYb8 insertion, which showed a similar association with TDP‐43 co‐pathology. While APOE ε4 appeared broadly conducive to the development of multiple pathologies in our cohort, TMEM106B variants were selectively linked to AD TDP‐43 pathology, especially to subtype α, positioning TMEM106B as a potent subtype‐specific modulator of vulnerability to TDP‐43 co‐pathology in AD.

METHODS

Study cohort and ethics

Autopsy brain tissue and clinical data were obtained from the Mayo Clinic Brain Bank (25‐007490), the Mayo Clinic Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (712‐98), and the Mayo Clinic Study of Aging (14‐004401). We only included individuals with primary neuropathologic diagnosis of AD; neuropathologic evaluation was performed by neuropathologists following established criteria. Written informed consent for participation in research was obtained from donors or legally authorized representatives prior to inclusion in the Brain Bank and parent studies. For each case, we abstracted, when available, age at death, sex, clinical disease duration (years from symptom onset to death), Braak NFT stage, Thal amyloid phase, TDP‐43 pathology status and subtype, Lewy body pathology status, and information on the presence of vascular lesions. Cases were considered positive for vascular lesions if pathological assessment revealed the presence of vascular dementia, CAA, infarcts, intracerebral hemorrhages, arteriovenous malformation, thrombosis, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular accident, and/or lacunae.

DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from cerebellum (preferred) or cortex using the Wizard Genomic DNA Purification kit, following the manufacturer's instructions (Promega, Catalog No.: A1620). For a subset of cases, DNA was already available from blood. DNA concentration and purity were assessed by spectrophotometry.

TMEM106B SNP genotyping

Genotyping for TMEM106B (rs3173615, C_274656458_10) SNP was performed using 40 ng of DNA in TaqMan SNP assays (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog No.: 4351376) run in a QuantStudio 7 Flex Real‐Time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) system (Applied Biosystems) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Determination of APOE status

The APOE diplotype (rs7412, rs429358) was established by evaluating an Infinium Global Screening Array version 3.0 (Illumina). These data will be made available through the database of genotypes and phenotypes (dbGaP). Paired raw probe intensity files (.idat files) were obtained for each sample (N = 2604). Conversion of.idat files to genotype call files (GTCs), and subsequently to variant call files (VCFs), was performed using Illumina's DRAGEN Array Software version 1.2. Sample VCFs were merged and processed using bcftools. 68 Additional sample‐ and variant‐level quality control (QC) was performed using PLINK2 69 (and PLINK1 for identity‐by‐descent analyses). Samples were excluded for missingness > 5%, excess heterozygosity (± 3 standard deviations, SD), relatedness (pi‐hat > 0.98), sex discordance, or non‐ancestry outlier behavior in principal component analyses. Variants were removed for call rate < 98%, Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) < 1×10− 6, or minor allele frequency < 1%. The cleaned dataset was phased using Eagle (2.4.1) 70 using the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 version 5 reference panel (GRCh37) 71 and the HapMap‐reformatted genetic map (hg19) provided by Eagle. Genotype imputation was performed using Minimac (4.1.6) 72 using the same 1000 Genomes Phase 3 version 5 reference panel for the full chromosome 19 (GRCh37). 71

Imputation accuracy for rs7412 (imputation R 2 = 0.994) was evaluated in the QC‐filtered variant calls across samples by comparing panel‐based genotyping and imputations: No discrepancies were detected, indicating high‐quality imputation. Imputation was also performed for rs429358, resulting in a similarly high imputation quality of R 2 = 0.985. The six APOE variants were assigned as follows: E2/E2 = rs7412 (T/T) and rs429358 (T/T), E2/E3 = rs7412 (C/T) and rs429358 (T/T), E2/E4 = rs7412 (C/T) and rs429358 (T/C), E3/E3 = rs7412 (C/C) and rs429358 (T/T), E3/E4 = rs7412 (C/C) and rs429358 (T/C), E4/E4 = rs7412 (C/C) and rs429358 (C/C).

TMEM106B AluYb8 element genotyping

The presence or absence of the AluYb8 element was assessed by PCR. The target region was amplified from 100 ng of genomic DNA using Apex Taq RED Master Mix (2X, 1.5 mM MgCl2; Genesse Scientific; Catalog No.: 42‐138) according to the manufacturer's instructions. PCR touchdown conditions were as follows: 95°C for 3 min, 30 cycles of 98°C for 20 s, annealing from 70°C to 60°C for 20 s, and 72°C for 30 s; followed by a final extension at 72°C for 5 min. The resulting products were resolved on 2% agarose gels run at 120 V for 30 min and visualized under a blue light transilluminator to excite the Safe DNA Gel Stain (Apex Bioresearch Products; Catalog No.: A8743). The resulting image was captured using the ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio‐Rad). The sequences of the sense and antisense primers used in this assay are respectively 5’–TGTAATGACCCGTGATGATGAG–3’ and 5’–CACAAGTTACCCAGTAAGGAC–3’.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.1) and visualized with the ggplot2 package or GraphPad Prism (version 10.2). The genotypic distribution of TMEM106B rs3173615 (p = 0.49) or the AluYb8 element (p = 0.67) did not deviate from HWE. Binary logistic regression models were applied to evaluate the association between each TMEM106B variant and the odds of exhibiting specific co‐pathologies under additive, dominant, recessive, and allelic models with respect to the effect allele of interest. Analyses were conducted using three adjustment strategies: (1) unadjusted; (2) adjusted for age at death, sex, Braak NFT stage, Thal Aβ phase, the presence of all other co‐pathologies, and either APOE ε4 (when assessing the predictive effect of TMEM106B or the AluYb8 insertion) or TMEM106B (when APOE ε4 was the predictor); and (3) adjusted as in the second model but excluding Lewy body pathology as a covariate due to reduced sample size (1522 of 2604 cases). All genetic covariates were coded under an additive model with respect to the effect allele of interest, and pathology status (present vs absent) was used as the outcome variable. For TDP‐43 subtype analyses, cases with subtype α or subtype β were compared directly with TDP‐43‐negative controls. Forest plots summarizing odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were generated from fully adjusted models (strategy 2). Linkage disequilibrium statistics (D′, r 2, and p values) were computed to assess non‐random association between TMEM106B rs3173615 and the AluYb8 insertion using the genetics package 73 in R.

RESULTS

Mixed pathology is common in AD

Our study cohort consisted of 2604 neuropathologically confirmed AD cases with a high burden of NFTs (median Braak: 6) and Aβ plaques (median Thal: 5) (Table 1). The cohort was well balanced between males (n = 1195, 46%) and females (n = 1409, 54%) and mainly consisted of White (n = 2562, 98%), non‐Hispanic (n = 2498, 96%) individuals (Table 1). Study participants had a median age at death of 81 years (range: 37 to 110) and a mean disease duration of 9 years (range: 0.1 to 36) (Table 1). In addition to canonical AD pathologies, other co‐pathologies were frequently observed: Lewy body pathology was identified in 69% (n = 1102) of cases, while cerebrovascular lesions – including macro‐ and microinfarcts, lacunes, small‐vessel disease (arteriolosclerosis), microbleeds, and/or CAA – were identified in 51% (n = 1300) of cases. TDP‐43 inclusions were present in 40% (n = 1051) of cases; among the TDP‐43‐positive cases for which subtyping data were available, 390 (44%) were AD TDP‐43 subtype α, and 492 (56%) were subtype β (Table 1).

TMEM106B is a selective modulator of TDP‐43 co‐pathology in AD, while APOE has a more global effect on mixed pathology

A coding SNP within TMEM106B (rs3173615, C > G) promotes TMEM106B fibril formation in FTLD‐TDP, 65 and congruent TMEM106B fibrils have been detected in AD brain extracts. 63 , 65 Given that this variant and the accumulation of the TMEM106B fibril core correlate with increased TDP‐43 proteinopathy in FTLD‐TDP, 65 we investigated whether the TMEM106B rs3173615 coding SNP was associated with the development of TDP‐43 or other co‐pathologies in our AD cohort, comparing its effects against the well‐established genetic modifier APOE ε4. 27 , 74 , 75

The frequency of the TMEM106B rs3173615 major (C) allele (the risk allele) was significantly more prevalent in AD cases with TDP‐43 pathology (60.4%) compared to AD cases without TDP‐43 pathology in our cohort (54.7%). Similarly, our cohort also demonstrated higher proportions of C‐containing TMEM106B genotypes, especially those homozygous for the risk allele, in AD with TDP‐43 pathology (38%) compared to TDP‐43–negative AD cases (28.2%, Figure 2A). To determine whether this TMEM106B SNP was associated with the presence of TDP‐43 co‐pathology in AD independently of other co‐pathologies, we performed binary logistic regression analysis using additive, dominant, recessive, and allelic models. Unadjusted analyses demonstrated that the presence of the risk (C) variant drove a significant increase in the odds of developing TDP‐43 pathology in AD in the additive, recessive, and allelic models, but not in the dominant model (Table 2). The association detected by these same models remained statistically significant after adjusting for age at death, sex, APOE ε4, Braak stage, Thal phase, and the presence of all other co‐pathologies (Lewy bodies, vascular lesions). In contrast, the distribution of TMEM106B genotypes was similar across cases co‐segregated by the presence or absence of Lewy bodies or vascular lesions, regardless of whether adjustments for covariates were applied (Figure 2A,B, Table 2, Table S1). Thus, the TMEM106B rs3173615 variant may selectively modulate the risk of developing TDP‐43 co‐pathology in AD while having no significant influence over the presentation of Lewy bodies or vascular lesions.

In contrast to the TMEM106B rs3173615 SNP, the distribution of APOE ε4 alleles differed greatly depending on the status of TDP‐43, Lewy body, and vascular lesions in our AD cohort. Cases with TDP‐43 co‐pathology showed a higher proportion of heterozygous (49.5%) or homozygous (16.6%) cases for the ε4 allele compared to cases without TDP‐43 inclusions (45.1% heterozygous, 14.1% homozygous; Figure 2C, Table S2). AD cases with Lewy body pathology were also enriched for the presence of the APOE ε4 allele (50.4% heterozygous, 18.3% homozygous) compared to cases that lacked this co‐pathology (42.6% heterozygous, 11.5% homozygous) (Figure 2C, Table S2), while patients positive for vascular lesions showed a higher proportion of homozygous APOE ε4 carriers (18.5%) compared to those with no vascular pathology (11.6%). Interestingly, the presence of a single allele of APOE ε4 was sufficient to drive the odds of developing TDP‐43 and Lewy body co‐pathology in our cohort, whereas vascular lesions required APOE ε4 homozygosity to show a significant effect (Figure 2C, Table S2). These effects were confirmed with binary logistic regression analysis in both unadjusted analyses and after adjusting for age at death, sex, Braak stage, Thal phase, TMEM106B rs3173615, and the presence of all other co‐pathologies (Figure 2D, Table S2).

To further validate the association between TMEM106B and the development of TDP‐43 pathology, we evaluated the influence of the insertion of the AluYb8 element in the TMEM106B 3’UTR (Figure 3A) on co‐pathology presentation in AD using a subcohort of cases for which AluYb8 testing data were available. This insertion is known to be in strong linkage disequilibrium with the TMEM106B rs3173615 risk (C) variant in populations with European ancestry. 19 , 20 Consistent with previous reports, we observed that the TMEM106B rs3173615 risk (C) SNP and the AluYb8 insertion were in high linkage disequilibrium (D’ = 0.986, r 2 = 0.968, p < 2.2 × 10−16; Figure 3B) for all cases analyzed (n = 1522). Similar to what we observed for the TMEM106B rs3173615 risk variant, the frequency of the AluYb8 insertion was higher in AD cases with TDP‐43 pathology (59.5%) than those without (55.0%), with increases driven by cases homozygous for the AluYb8 inclusion (36.5% TDP‐43‐positive cases vs 29.8% TDP‐43‐negative cases; Figure 3C). This trend was maintained after adjusting for age at death, sex, Braak stage, Thal phase, APOE ε4, and the presence of all other co‐pathologies but did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the difference in sample size between cases with TMEM106B rs3173615 genotyping data (N = 2604) and cases with AluYb8 testing data (N = 1522) (Table S3). In contrast, there was a trend toward a lower frequency of cases homozygous for the insertion in AD cases with Lewy bodies (29.2%) compared to AD cases without this pathology (36.2%), but this trend did not reach statistical significance (Table S3). The frequency distribution of the AluYb8 element did not vary between patients positive (−/− = 17.2%, ± = 48.7%, +/+ = 34.1%) or negative (−/− = 19.9%, ± = 48.4%, +/+ = 31.7%) for vascular lesions (Figure 3C, Table S3). Overall, the similarity between the influence of the AluYb8 insertion and rs3173615 SNP on mixed pathology in our cohort supports a model in which TMEM106B variants selectively modulate TDP‐43 co‐pathology in the context of AD.

TMEM106B is strongly linked to AD TDP‐43 subtype α pathology

TDP‐43 pathology in AD cases can be classified into morphological subtypes 76 using a system that draws partially from the classification method commonly used in FTLD‐TDP, 77 albeit with some key differences: AD TDP‐43 subtype α is characterized by widespread inclusion distribution throughout neuronal tissues that resembles the patterns observed in FTLD‐TDP subtype A, while AD TDP‐43 subtype β is characterized by predominantly limbic pathology and the colocalization of TDP‐43 aggregates with NFTs, resulting in inclusion morphology that largely does not resemble any FTLD‐TDP subtypes (A–E). 78 , 79 Given the selective association of TMEM106B with TDP‐43 pathology in our AD cohort, we sought to determine whether TMEM106B variants influenced the odds of developing a particular TDP‐43 pathological subtype. The TMEM106B rs3173615 risk allele and the AluYb8 insertion were more frequent in cases with AD TDP‐43 subtype α (rs3173615: 66.0%; AluYb8: 65.6%) and less frequent in cases with AD TDP‐43 subtype β (rs3173615: 55.6%; AluYb8: 50.9%) and in AD cases without TDP‐43 pathology (rs3173615: 54.7%; AluYb8: 55.0%). Further, the TMEM106B homozygous risk genotype was more frequent in AD with TDP‐43 pathology subtype α (rs3173615: 44.6%; AluYb8: 43.5%) than AD with TDP‐43 subtype β (rs3173615: 32.3%; AluYb8: 26.4%) or AD without TDP‐43 pathology (rs3173615: 28.2%; AluYb8: 29.8%) (Figure 4). Additive, dominant, recessive, and allelic binary logistic regression models revealed that both the TMEM106B rs3173615 SNP (Figure 4A, Table S4) and the TMEM106B AluYb8 insertion (Figure 4B, Table S5) selectively increase the odds of developing AD TDP‐43 subtype α. Significance was maintained in most models after adjusting for age at death, sex, Braak stage, Thal phase, APOE ε4, and the presence of all other co‐pathologies; it was only lost in analyses using the dominant model (Figure 4, Tables S4 and S5).

Altogether, these results indicate that variations at the TMEM106B locus, captured by the coding rs3173615 SNP and the 3’ UTR AluYb8 insertion, do not act as global co‐pathology modifiers like APOE ε4; rather, these variants exert a pathway‐specific influence on TDP‐43 proteinopathy in AD and are specifically linked to the morphological and distributional differences that characterize TDP‐43 subtype α pathology.

DISCUSSION

In a well‐characterized cohort of 2604 neuropathologically confirmed AD cases, we found that a coding SNP within the TMEM106B locus (rs3173615) selectively modified the risk of TDP‐43 co‐pathology in AD patients but was not connected with the co‐occurrence of Lewy bodies or cerebrovascular injury. While this connection between the TMEM106B risk haplotype and TDP‐43 pathology is supported by previous findings in FTLD‐TDP, 38 , 65 ALS, 38 , 67 and AD, 38 , 43 , 50 , 66 , 80 our study represents, to our knowledge, one of the first to explore the association between specific TMEM106B variants (rs3173615, AluYb8) and the presentation of additional co‐pathologies in AD outside of a handful of recent genome‐wide association studies. 38 , 43 , 66 Moreover, our work highlights a stark difference in the impact of TMEM106B and APOE on mixed pathology in AD: While TMEM106B is a selective modifier of TDP‐43 co‐pathology in AD, APOE was independently linked to increased odds of developing Lewy body pathology, TDP‐43 pathology, and vascular pathology in our AD cohort, suggesting a more global impact on proteostasis in the AD brain.

The global modulatory behavior of APOE on individual co‐pathologies is well supported by previous work, 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 facilitating our use of this modifier as a valuable benchmark for the behavior of TMEM106B. As APOE ε4 is also a well‐known risk factor for classical AD pathology, this genetic modifier may promote an environment that is generally permissive to pathology development in the brain, perhaps through a combination of its roles in modulating neuroinflammatory responses, lipid metabolism, endolysosomal activity, and proteostasis. 81 , 82 , 83 Indeed, APOE ε4 has been linked to the development of multiple co‐pathologies in AD. 84 , 85 , 86 , 87 , 88 Notably, the role of APOE on mixed pathology may be uniquely linked to its influence on the AD disease context: Evidence suggests that APOE ε4 is a risk factor for initial AD pathology development but not for downstream AD clinical progression, 89 , 90 and its association with Lewy body development may be contingent upon the co‐occurrence of classical AD pathology. 91 , 92 , 93

Despite the emerging link between TMEM106B and TDP‐43 pathology, a very limited number of studies have investigated the link between TMEM106B and TDP‐43 subtyping. One study reported that the TMEM106B risk allele was associated with widespread TDP‐43 inclusion distribution (potentially akin to subtype α), as opposed to a limbic‐only presentation (potentially akin to subtype β) in an AD cohort, 80 but further studies are lacking. By integrating detailed TDP‐43 subtyping data into our analyses, we provide novel insight into the link between the TMEM106B rs3173615 risk variant and the morphology and distribution of TDP‐43 inclusions, highlighting the importance of considering TDP‐43 aggregate heterogeneity in AD cohorts. Specifically, we found that individuals homozygous for the risk C allele at rs3173615 exhibited a particularly high risk of developing TDP‐43 pathology, a trend that is intensified in AD TDP‐43 subtype α cases, whereas the relative genotypic distributions between AD TDP‐43 subtype β cases and AD cases without TDP‐43 pathology were virtually equivalent. Our findings suggest two things. First, a threonine at TMEM106B residue 185 influences TDP‐43 aggregation in a dose‐dependent manner. Second, this functional effect on TDP‐43 inclusion formation selectively promotes the morphological characteristic of AD TDP‐43 subtype α. While the mechanisms underlying these observations are unknown, cryo‐electron microscopy data reveal that FTLD‐TDP subtypes A and B show distinct TDP‐43 fibril core folds 94 , 95 ; it is possible that TDP‐43 also forms distinct structures in AD TDP‐43 subtype α versus subtype β and that TMEM106B fibrils selectively potentiate the type α fold. Attempts at assessing TMEM106B pathology distribution in post mortem tissue have not detected direct colocalization between the protein and other neuropathological entities, 96 but TMEM106B fibrils likely impair lysosomal activity in neurons, which could sensitize cells to inclusion formation. Indeed, interactome analyses implicated insoluble TMEM106B fibrils in the disruption of endolysosomal pathways in FTLD‐TDP, 65 both TMEM106B 60 and TDP‐43 97 have been reported to form fibrils within lysosomes in human neurons, and lysosomal escape of the TMEM106B fibril core was predicted to facilitate TDP‐43 seeding in a recent preprint. 98 Work in cell culture models also suggests that the TMEM106B coding variant – which promotes fibril formation 65 in addition to its association with TDP‐43 pathology – could affect the expression, 99 glycosylation, 65 , 99 and function of the TMEM106B protein, 100 further contributing to lysosomal disruption in risk haplotype carriers. Finally, TMEM106B and TDP‐43 appear to have converging functions. In addition to its central role in RNA processing, 101 , 102 , 103 TDP‐43 also modulates lysosomal function. 104 , 105 , 106 , 107 , 108 And in addition to lysosomal pathways, the insoluble TMEM106B interactome was also enriched in factors involved in RNA transport and translation. 65 Given the crucial role of TDP‐43 dysfunction in TDP‐43 proteinopathies and increasing evidence selectively linking TMEM106B to TDP‐43 pathology formation, the potential interplay between TDP‐43 and TMEM106B warrants further investigation. Finally, it is worth noting that both TMEM106B and APOE are linked to higher incidences of TDP‐43 co‐pathology in both our AD cohort and others. 37 , 38 Although our work establishes that TMEM106B and APOE can exert separate, independent effects on co‐pathology development in AD, the pathway‐specific role of TMEM106B and the global influence of APOE may still converge on changes in lysosomal activity, 109 potentially leading to additive effects in dual carriers. Further studies could elucidate this possibility.

The TMEM106B risk haplotype is in strong linkage disequilibrium with an AluYb8 insertion in the TMEM106B 3’UTR, prompting our investigation of the influence of this insertion on co‐pathology presentation in AD. The insertion is suspected to negatively impact TMEM106B transcription 110 and has been reported to correlate with TMEM106B protein levels in longitudinal aging and dementia cohorts. 59 This latter study did not see a correlation between the AluYb8 insertion and TMEM106B mRNA levels, however, suggesting a potential post‐transcriptional modification effect that could be clarified by future functional studies. Nevertheless, evidence suggests a possible two‐hit mechanism at the TMEM106B locus in which the AluYb8 insertion increases TMEM106B protein levels, 59 while the risk rs3173615 variant promotes the formation of TMEM106B fibrils 65 , 100 , 111 – both of which could impact the endogenous function of TMEM106B in endolysosomal activity and proteostasis 112 and sensitize cells to TDP‐43 pathology formation. While the AluYb8 insertion in the 3’ UTR of TMEM106B did not reach a statistically significant association with TDP‐43 pathology in our study, we acknowledge that we were limited by the availability of cases with AluYb8 testing data (N = 1522 out of a potential 2604). Given the strong linkage disequilibrium we detected between the rs3173615 risk SNP and the AluYb8 insert in this cohort, which aligns with previous studies, 57 , 58 , 59 we speculate that an increased sample size, especially including additional TDP‐43 subtype α cases, would allow for a significant association between the AluYb8 insertion and TDP‐43 pathology, mirroring what we observed between rs3173615 and TDP‐43 pathology herein. Indeed, when subsetting the cohort by cases with available TDP‐43 subtyping data, both rs3173615 and AluYb8 significantly increased the odds that a patient would display AD TDP‐43 subtype α, supporting a selective, subtype‐specific relationship between TMEM106B risk variants and TDP‐43 co‐pathology in AD.

Our study has an important limitation: Our cohort was of predominantly European ancestry, which may limit generalizability, as allele frequencies and co‐pathology prevalence vary across populations. The extreme imbalance in ancestry representation precluded any meaningful ancestry‐adjusted analyses in the current study. This is an unfortunate caveat of many large AD research cohorts, emphasizing the need for greater recruitment across diverse populations to enable robust evaluation within heterogeneous populations in the future. Additionally, sample sizes differed across genotypic and pathological subgroups, resulting in unequal power to detect effects of similar magnitude. More extensive characterization of TDP‐43 subtypes and individual genetic variants in post mortem AD cohorts would help rectify this limitation. It would also be of interest to explore associations between TMEM106B, APOE, and readouts of TDP‐43 pathological burden. Quantitative measures of pathology were unavailable for many of the samples at the time of this publication but should be considered in the future.

In summary, our findings establish the TMEM106B risk haplotype as a selective, subtype‐specific modifier of TDP‐43 pathology in AD, distinguishing it from global genetic modifiers such as APOE. Recognizing how genetic modifiers individually influence co‐pathologies in AD will be critical for predicting disease progression, understanding mechanisms of cognitive resilience, and developing targeted interventions across the heterogeneous landscape of AD. Our work represents an important step in this direction that could critically inform personalized therapeutic approaches and facilitate the design of targeted clinical trials.
