Availability of Online Spanish Language Resources in Academic Hand Surgery
Authors: Carrie N Reaver, Pavithr Goli, Michael G Galvez, Charles A Goldfarb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeOnline health information enhances education and connection to care for patients seeking hand surgery in the United States but can be challenging to navigate for patients who speak languages other than English. This study sought to assess the availability and accessibility of online Spanish-language resources at academic hand surgery institutions.MethodsWe performed a cross-sectional study of 95 institutions offering hand surgery fellowships listed by the American Society for Surgery of the Hand (2024–2025). Institutional websites were evaluated for (1) patient educational content in English and Spanish, (2) language toggle features, (3) availability of language services information, and (4) the proportion of hand surgeon profiles offering Spanish. Readability of carpal tunnel syndrome resources in English and Spanish was assessed using the Fry and Gilliam-Peña-Mountain readability formulas, respectively, and differences were assessed using Student t tests. Associations between Hispanic population and language accessibility features were analyzed using point-biserial correlation.ResultsOf 93 institutional websites included in the analysis, 73.1% offered English patient resources, 18.2% provided language toggle options, 11.8% offered translated Spanish resources, 29% included a link to language assistance services, and 8.6% listed a direct phone number for interpreter access. Among 736 identified hand surgeons, 6.7% listed Spanish as an offered language. State-level Hispanic population was not significantly associated with language accessibility features. At the city level, Hispanic population was significantly associated with presence of a website language toggle (r = 0.27, P = .01) and Spanish-speaking providers (r = 0.32, P = .003). English and Spanish carpal tunnel resources (P = .45) were written above recommended reading levels, with no significant differences between languages.ConclusionsAcademic hand surgery websites demonstrate limited availability of Spanish-language patient education, language assistance information, and Spanish-speaking providers. Development of language-concordant online resources represents a cost-effective opportunity to promote equitable access to care.Clinical relevanceThis study promotes equitable patient education in hand surgery.Key words: Hand surgery, Health literacy, Language barriers, Patient education

Online health information is an important tool for education and connection to care for patients seeking hand surgery care in the United States. However, navigating online health information can be challenging for patients who speak languages other than English.1 In 2019, the United States Census Bureau reported that over 68 million people spoke a language other than English at home, with Spanish being the most common language reported (62%).2 Furthermore, the Hispanic population constitutes the most rapidly growing group in the United States, comprising nearly 20% (∼62 million) of the total population per the 2020 United States Census.3 Despite the growing Spanish-speaking population, in-person and online resources for Spanish-speaking patients remain limited within hand surgery, contributing to disparities in health literacy, surgical outcomes, and patient satisfaction.4, 5, 6

Expanding in-person language assistance services is crucial for reducing language barriers and inequities in care, but these efforts vary widely by geography and practice setting.7,8 As a result, patients often rely on nonqualified interpreters such as family members to facilitate communication. Greene et al9 reported that 80% of Spanish-speaking patients were asked to rely on nonqualified interpreters when making an hand surgery appointment. Language discordance between physicians and patients, as well as the use of ad hoc interpreters, results in reduced satisfaction and patients asking fewer questions during their appointment, negatively impacting patient education and understanding.10, 11, 12 When interpretation is suboptimal or inaccessible, readily available educational content that is concordant in both language and literacy level can help support additional opportunities for patients. By expanding access to online education, gaps in in-person communication can be mitigated.

Accordingly, this study sought to assess the availability and accessibility of Spanish resources at academic hand surgery institutions and correlate resource access by geography.

Materials and Methods

Resource availability

We performed a cross-sectional study of the 95 institutions that offer orthopedic hand surgery fellowship programs listed by the American Society for Hand Surgery for 2024–2025. Institutional websites were surveyed for (1) presence of patient educational content in English and Spanish, (2) language toggle tools or translation options, (3) availability of language assistance information, and (4) the proportion of hand surgeons who offered Spanish services on their profile. Surgeon profiles were identified using each institutional website’s built-in “Find a Surgeon” tool or related search function. Language on surgeon profiles was only confirmed if explicitly listed on the profile and was not inferred based on country of origin or training, stated racial or ethnic identity, or other biographic information. Profiles of advance practice providers, nursing staff, or clinic administrators were not included in our analysis.

Readability

We evaluated the readability (ie, reading grade level) of online hand surgery patient education provided by these institutions. Specifically, we evaluated carpal tunnel syndrome resources in English and Spanish available on each website. Carpal tunnel syndrome was selected because it is one of the most common resource topics found on these websites. Plain text from each carpal tunnel resource was evaluated using either the Fry Readability Formula (Fry) for English or its corresponding Gilliam-Peña-Mountain Readability Formula for Spanish.13,14 These readability tests were selected for their widespread use in health literacy research and availability in both English and Spanish. Both tests report US reading grade level (eg, a score of 6 corresponds to a sixth-grade reading level). All readability analyses were conducted with Oleander Readability Studio (2020).

Statistical Analysis

The availability of Spanish-language resources and language assistance information was correlated to the Hispanic population3 of the city and state where the institution resides using a point-biserial correlation (P < .05).15 The difference in readability between English and Spanish content was assessed using a Student t test (P < .05).

Results

Of the 95 institutions sampled, 93 institutions were included in the analysis (one institution lacked a patient-facing website and one institution was listed twice for separate plastic and orthopedic hand fellowships but shared the same website). In total, 68 of 93 (73.1%) of these institutions offered patient education about hand surgery on their website. Of these 68 institutions, 17 (18.2%) provided a language toggle option and 11 (11.8%) provided a translated version of English-language patient education. Variation existed across websites as some offered a prepared Spanish translation for every resource provided in English whereas others only offered one or a few prepared Spanish-language resources for patients. Twenty-seven of 93 (29.0%) websites offered a link on their hand surgery-related pages to the institution’s language assistive services information. Eight of 93 (8.6%) websites listed a readily available phone number to call for language assistive services. Fifty-five of 93 (59.1%) websites provided a readily accessible nondiscrimination notice that outlined patients’ rights to language services. Of the 93 institutions included in our search, 89 (95.7%) provided surgeon profiles accessible to patients. A total of 736 hand surgeons were identified in our search. Forty-nine (6.7%) listed Spanish as an offered language on their profile. In total, 31 of 89 (34%) institutions had at least one surgeon who listed Spanish as an offered language. These results demonstrate that across all categories assessed, the majority of institutions lack Spanish resources (Fig. 1).

Point-biserial correlations determined associations between the proportion of the Hispanic population and the presence of language accessibility features on institutional websites. At the state level, weakly positive but nonsignificant relationships were observed between Hispanic population proportion and the presence of Spanish-speaking physicians (r = 0.15, P = .16, 95% CI: −0.21 to 0.47), a website language toggle (r = 0.16, P = .13, 95% CI: −0.20 to 0.48), provision of Spanish translations for patient education resources (r = 0.13, P = .23, 95% CI: −0.23 to 0.46), and a language assistance page (r = 0.00, P = .97, 95% CI: −0.35 to 0.35). In contrast, city-level analyses revealed stronger associations, with significant positive correlations between Hispanic population proportion and both the presence of a website language toggle (r = 0.27, P = .01, 95% CI: 0.07–0.45) and Spanish-speaking providers (r = 0.32, P = .003, 95% CI: 0.12–0.49), while links to Spanish translations (r = 0.14, P = .20, 95% CI: −0.07 to 0.34) and language assistance pages (r = 0.17, P = .11, 95% CI: −0.04 to 0.37) showed nonsignificant positive trends. These findings indicate that institutions in more heavily Hispanic cities are somewhat more likely to incorporate language-concordant features, though implementation remains inconsistent. Finally, we compared the readability of carpal tunnel resources in English (n = 55) and Spanish (n = 11) provided by our sample of academic hand surgery institutions. For English resources, the average Fry score was 11.1 ± 6.9. In comparison, Spanish resources demonstrated an average Gilliam-Peña-Mountain score of 10.5 ± 8.9 (P = .45, 95% CI: −5.6 to 6.8; Fig. 3).

Discussion

There is a notable disparity in access to Spanish-language patient resources, language assistance services, and surgeons whose profiles offer Spanish language in academic hand surgery. Fewer than one in five institutions offered a Spanish-language toggle for patient materials, one in ten offered Spanish-language resources, and only one in three had at least one surgeon who advertised Spanish on their profiles. The most limited resource identified was surgeons offering Spanish language on their profiles. Moreover, there was no significant association between the size of a state’s Hispanic population and the likelihood that an institution provided Spanish language resources or interpreter information. However, at the city level, a higher Hispanic population proportion was significantly associated with the presence of a language toggle and surgeon profiles listing Spanish proficiency.

The scarcity of online Spanish resources and surgeon proficiency demonstrated by this study parallels prior workforce research in hand surgery . In contrast to the growing Hispanic population, no significant increase in hand surgery fellows who were Hispanic was observed from 2007 to 2021.16 Sobel et al17 sampled 334 surgeons who completed pediatric orthopedic fellowships and found that only 17 (5.1%) demonstrated Spanish proficiency. Additionally, several studies estimate Hispanic representation in hand surgery around 5% to 7%.18,19 Consistent with previous readability studies, much of the available online health education remains English-dominant and written above recommended reading levels for patients with limited health literacy.20, 21, 22 Despite over a decade of prior work bringing awareness to the inaccessibility of online information, little meaningful improvements in readability of online information has been reported in the literature.23,24 The convergence of a limited bilingual workforce capacity and inaccessible online resources emphasizes a critical opportunity for hand surgery institutions to strengthen language equity initiatives using a multifaceted approach.

Potential strategies for improving accessibility of resources include, first and foremost, increasing diversity within the hand surgery workforce and expanding access to professional interpreter services at every point of patient contact. While workforce diversity and online patient education are distinct domains, greater linguistic diversity within hand surgery may facilitate the recognition, prioritization, and development of language-concordant patient educational resources at the institutional level. Accordingly, encouraging surgeon proficiency in Spanish and Hispanic representation in hand surgery is important, but the linguistic and cultural diversity of the patient population has already outpaced that of the physician workforce, necessitating multifaceted approaches to mitigate disparities.25,26 Accordingly, even when surgeons or staff are not proficient in Spanish, institutions can still promote equitable care by making language assistance resources highly visible and easy to access with direct phone numbers or online scheduling links in Spanish. Federally funded providers are required to offer language services to patients with limited English proficiency; clearly stating this right and how to access free interpreter services on hand surgery websites and scheduling platforms may improve patient awareness and utilization.27

Additionally, surgeon profiles should include information about both language capabilities and interpreter availability, allowing patients and referring providers to make informed decisions about language-concordant care. Even when surgeons are not Spanish-speaking, visibility of interpreter access signals institutional commitment to equitable communication and can help Spanish-speaking patients feel more confident and comfortable seeking care. Surgeons and staff may consider reviewing strategies for effective communication through interpreter services such as speaking in short sentences, frequently checking for understanding with the patient, engaging directly with the patient rather than referring to the interpreter, and routine auditing of interpreting technology or scheduling protocols to prevent disruptions in accessing services.28,29

From a website design perspective, the following strategies could enhance accessibility: (1) providing pretranslated patient education materials that are readable and appropriate for both Spanish-speaking and bilingual audiences (including interpreters or family members); (2) ensuring language assistance information is actionable and available in Spanish directly from hand surgery department websites; and (3) including language information on surgeon profiles to facilitate language-concordant referrals. Finally, emerging advances in artificial intelligence large language models may provide scalable tools for generating accessible translations and improving the linguistic inclusivity of online health resources, though ongoing validation of their reliability and equity is essential.30,31

This study has several limitations. First, website information or surgeon profiles may not accurately reflect the in-person language assistance that is available at institutions. Furthermore, the profiles of advanced practice providers, nursing staff, or clinic administrators, if available, were not included in our search though they may provide additional language offerings not captured by surgeon profiles. Additionally, our analysis included only academic centers thus our results may not accurately reflect access to online resources or provision of language assistance across other practice settings. Finally, readability analyses are estimates and may not reflect the true understanding of material by a diverse audience, particularly for bilingual or limited English proficiency patients. Future work may aim to survey the experience of actual Spanish-speaking patients navigating online health information.

Identifying areas of disparity, such as the availability of Spanish-language online resources, is essential for promoting equitable health education and access to care in hand surgery. As the Spanish-speaking population continues to grow, efforts to address health literacy and language barriers in online health information for hand surgery are increasingly necessary. Development of language-concordant online patient education demonstrates a relatively cost-effective, timely area of intervention that promotes improved health literacy and connection to care for Spanish-speaking patients.
