Comparative efficacy of electric toothbrush technologies in plaque and gingivitis reduction: a network meta-analysis
Authors: Chenchen Luo, Nanyu Chen, Qiuyu Jing, Saidi Hu, Siran Wan, Shunhong Zhang, Guangyue Liu, Nian Li, Lin-Yong Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe comparative efficacy of different types of electric toothbrushes relative to each other and to manual toothbrushes remains a subject of clinical investigation. This network meta-analysis (NMA) aims to rank the efficacy of various electric toothbrush technologies for plaque and gingivitis reduction.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and Web of Science was conducted from inception to June 2025. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing electric (oscillating-rotating, sonic, ultrasonic, other) and manual toothbrushes were included. Two reviewers independently performed study selection, data extraction, and risk-of-bias assessment. A frequentist NMA was performed, and treatments were ranked using Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking (SUCRA) values. Consistency between direct and indirect evidence was evaluated.ResultsNineteen RCTs were included. In terms of plaque reduction, oscillating-rotating brushes were ranked first (SUCRA = 89.2%), followed by sonic (SUCRA = 72.4%), ultrasonic (SUCRA = 54.1%), other electric (SUCRA = 45.3%), and manual toothbrushes (SUCRA = 18.3%). A similar ranking was observed for gingivitis reduction. Subgroup analyses indicated superior benefits for orthodontic patients and children. The overall risk of bias was low, and no significant inconsistency was detected.ConclusionOscillating-rotating electric toothbrushes demonstrated the highest probability of being among the most effective interventions for reducing plaque and gingivitis among the compared technologies. This evidence suggests that they may be considered a preferred choice for improved oral hygiene in clinical practice, particularly in high-risk groups such as orthodontic patients and children.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00784-026-06955-z.

Introduction

Dental plaque is the primary etiological factor for prevalent oral diseases such as gingivitis and caries [1–3]. Its mechanical removal through toothbrushing constitutes the cornerstone of daily oral care. Although the manual toothbrush is the most widely used tool, its efficacy is highly dependent on user technique and compliance [4–6].

Electric toothbrushes were developed to offer a standardized brushing action, thereby addressing the limitations associated with manual brushing techniques. Systematic reviews and pairwise meta-analyses have consistently concluded that electric toothbrushes are more effective than manual ones in terms of plaque removal (Yaacob et al., 2014; Elkerbout et al., 2020; de Jager et al., 2017) [7–9]. However, these previous analyses were limited to pairwise comparisons (e.g., electric vs. manual) and did not provide a direct, quantitative ranking among different electric technologies such as oscillating-rotating, sonic, and ultrasonic brushes. Consequently, a significant gap persists in the direct comparison and ranking of various electric toothbrush technologies. For clinicians and patients to make informed decisions, it is essential to establish a hierarchical ranking of these interventions.

Network meta-analysis (NMA) is a robust statistical technique that facilitates the simultaneous comparison of multiple interventions by integrating both direct and indirect evidence [10]. Unlike traditional pairwise meta-analyses, NMA allows for the ranking of multiple treatment modalities within a unified framework, thereby directly addressing the aforementioned evidence gap [11]. Consequently, this systematic review and NMA aims to evaluate and rank the efficacy of various types of electric toothbrushes compared to manual toothbrushes in reducing plaque and gingivitis, thereby offering evidence-based guidance for clinical practice.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

This systematic review was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251273484). A comprehensive literature search was conducted across several electronic databases, including PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Web of Science, and ClinicalTrials.gov, to identify relevant studies published from the inception of these databases until June 2025. The search strategy employed a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords pertaining to “electric toothbrush,” “manual toothbrush,” “plaque,” “gingivitis,” and “randomized controlled trial,” and was limited to publications in the English language. The complete search strategy, including the exact MeSH term combinations for each database, is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Studies were included if they met the following PICOS criteria: (1) Population: Children or adults with permanent or mixed dentition. (2) Intervention: The use of an electric toothbrush, which may include oscillating-rotating, sonic, ultrasonic, or other specified types. Other electric toothbrushes refer to powered brushes that do not conform to the definitions of oscillating-rotating, sonic, or ultrasonic technologies, such as basic side-to-side motion brushes. (3) Comparator: The use of a manual toothbrush. (4) Outcomes: Changes in a validated plaque index (PI) and/or gingivitis index (GI). (5) Study Design: Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs), which encompass both parallel-group and crossover designs. The exclusion criteria for this study encompassed non-randomized studies and those lacking sufficient data for comprehensive analysis. Studies that compared various types of electric toothbrushes without a corresponding manual toothbrush control group were not automatically excluded; instead, they were included if they reported at least one outcome of interest (plaque or gingivitis index) and facilitated direct head-to-head comparisons between electric technologies (e.g., oscillating-rotating vs. sonic). This methodology enabled the network to be connected via a common comparator (manual brushing), while also integrating direct electric versus electric evidence, which is crucial for ranking interventions within the framework of network meta-analysis.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two independent reviewers screened the titles, abstracts, and full texts according to pre-defined criteria. Data were extracted using a standardized form, which captured the following information: first author, publication year, country, study design, sample size, participant characteristics, intervention and control details, follow-up duration, and outcome data, including baseline and follow-up means and standard deviations for both PI and GI. In studies reporting multiple plaque or gingival indices, a single primary index was prioritized according to the following hierarchy: (1) the index designated as primary by the study authors; (2) the most frequently reported index in the field—specifically, the Rustogi Modified Navy Plaque Index (RMNPI) for plaque and the Modified Gingival Index (MGI) for gingivitis; and (3) whole-mouth scores over site-specific scores.

The risk of bias for each included study was independently assessed by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool 2.0 (RoB 2.0). This tool evaluates five domains: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported results.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

For studies that reported multiple follow-ups, the longest follow-up period within each study was selected for analysis. Standardized Mean Differences (SMDs) with 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs) were calculated as the effect measure for continuous outcomes, specifically Plaque Index (PI) and Gingival Index (GI), due to the varying indices utilized across the studies.

For crossover and split-mouth trials, we extracted paired data whenever available. In instances where paired data were not reported, we approximated the standard deviation of the change using the method recommended by the Cochrane Handbook, assuming a correlation coefficient of 0.5 between period measurements. We assessed period effects and found them to be non-significant in all included crossover trials. Additionally, no carryover effects were reported by the original study authors.

A frequentist network meta-analysis was conducted using a random-effects model. The network geometry was visually represented, with nodes indicating interventions and edges representing direct comparisons; the thickness of the edges was scaled according to the number of studies for each comparison [12, 13]. The primary aim was to construct a connected evidence network to compare the efficacy of manual, oscillating-rotating, sonic, ultrasonic, and other electric toothbrushes in reducing plaque and gingivitis.In addition to direct comparisons against the manual toothbrush, the model quantified effects between electric technologies without direct trial evidence (e.g., sonic vs. ultrasonic) through indirect comparisons. Direct evidence between oscillating-rotating and sonic brushes was included to evaluate consistency. Both global and local inconsistency were assessed using node-splitting methods. The analysis yielded effect estimates for all pairwise comparisons and cumulative ranking probabilities, thereby addressing questions regarding the optimal technology and the differences among technologies within a unified framework.

The relative ranking of interventions for each outcome was estimated using the Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking (SUCRA) values, where a higher SUCRA percentage (ranging from 0% to 100%) indicates a greater likelihood of being the optimal treatment. Subgroup analyses were pre-specified and conducted based on population age (children, adults, elderly), specific conditions (orthodontic patients), and follow-up duration.

To address potential violations of the transitivity assumption arising from the inclusion of both short-term single-use and long-term home-use trials, we conducted a prespecified stratified NMA. Studies were categorized into two strata based on follow-up duration and study setting: (1) short-term single-use studies, involving a single brushing episode with outcomes assessed immediately post-brushing ; and (2) longer-term home-use studies, characterized by an intervention period of at least one week, reflecting habitual use and the accumulation of plaque and gingivitis over time. A separate frequentist random-effects network meta-analysis was performed for each stratum, adhering to the same analytical framework as the primary analysis. The relative rankings of interventions within each stratum were estimated using SUCRA values. To satisfy the transitivity assumption underlying valid indirect comparisons, we compared the distribution of key effect modifiers across different types of direct comparisons (manual vs. electric, and electric vs. electric). The potential modifiers examined included baseline plaque severity (mean plaque index), brushing supervision (supervised vs. unsupervised), and follow-up duration (single-use vs. ≥1 week). No substantial imbalances were observed across the comparison types, which supports the plausibility of the transitivity assumption. Detailed comparisons are reported in the "Results" section. All statistical analyses were executed using R software (version 4.5.0) with the netmeta package.

Assessment of reporting biases

Publication bias was assessed using a combination of methods suitable for a network of this size. First, comparison-adjusted funnel plots were visually inspected to identify small-study effects. Second, Egger’s regression test was conducted for the most common comparison (oscillating-rotating vs. manual toothbrush). Third, sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding smaller studies (sample size < 50) to evaluate the robustness of the rankings. The assessment of publication bias adheres to the recommendations set forth by the PRISMA-NMA guidelines [Hutton et al., 2015] and the Cochrane Handbook [Sterne et al., 2011]. We acknowledge that the statistical power to detect asymmetry is diminished when the number of studies per comparison is small (k < 10), and that the possibility of unpublished null findings cannot be entirely discounted.

We planned to assess publication bias through visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger’s regression test for the primary comparison between electric and manual toothbrushes. However, due to the limited number of studies available for each specific comparison in the network (k < 10), the statistical power to detect asymmetry was low. Therefore, the results of these analyses should be interpreted with caution.

Due to the unavailability of study-specific variance estimates across all comparisons, conventional funnel plot analysis was not feasible. Therefore, we implemented a comprehensive multi-method assessment framework that included: (1) precision-weighted regression using sample size as a proxy for study precision [14]; (2) p-curve analysis to detect selective reporting of significant results [15]; (3) comparison of prospectively registered versus published outcomes [16]; (4) network asymmetry tests [17]; and (5) sensitivity analyses that excluded small studies and incorporated data from unpublished trials [18]. This systematic review and network meta-analysis is reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses for Network Meta-Analyses (PRISMA-NMA) guidelines, and the completed checklist is provided as Supplementary File Table S5.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The initial database search yielded 1,852 records. Following the removal of duplicates and the screening of titles and abstracts, 124 full-text articles were evaluated for eligibility. Ultimately, 19 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) met the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the quantitative synthesis. Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool. Among the 19 included RCTs, 12 were classified as having a low risk of bias, while 5 exhibited some concerns—primarily due to deviations from intended interventions or missing outcome data. Additionally, 2 studies were rated as having a high risk of bias, attributed to the lack of blinding in outcome assessment and incomplete outcome data. Figure 2 summarizes the overall risk of bias, and domain-level ratings for each study can be found in Supplementary Table S2.

Network geometry

The evidence network for comparing interventions is illustrated in Fig. 3. This network comprises five nodes: Manual, Oscillating-Rotating (O-R), Sonic (S), Ultrasonic (U), and Other Electric (OE). The manual toothbrush serves as the common comparator that links all types of electric toothbrushes. Notably, direct comparisons between oscillating-rotating and sonic brushes were available from two included trials (Van der Weijden et al., 1996; Erbe et al., 2019), which contributed directly to the network and the estimation of relative effects between these two electric technologies. These direct comparisons, along with indirect evidence from the manual node, enabled the establishment of a fully connected network. Additionally, a new supplementary figure (Figure S1) illustrates the same network geometry with node sizes scaled proportionally to the number of studies per intervention, thereby providing a clearer visualization of the evidence base. The network is well-connected, which facilitates a robust NMA. To support the transitivity assumption, we compared key effect modifiers across different comparison types. The mean baseline plaque index ranged from 0.62 to 0.65 in studies comparing oscillating-rotating to manual brushing, and from 0.60 to 0.63 in studies comparing oscillating-rotating to sonic brushing. The proportion of supervised brushing was consistent across comparison types, approximately 30–40%. These findings suggest that there are no substantial imbalances that would violate the transitivity assumption.

Results of the network meta-analysis

The league table for plaque reduction is presented in Table 2. The network meta-analysis demonstrated that all types of electric toothbrushes significantly outperformed manual toothbrushes in reducing plaque, with Standardized Mean Differences (SMDs) ranging from moderate to large effect sizes. Among these, oscillating-rotating brushes exhibited the largest effect size in comparison to manual brushing (SMD [95% CI]: -1.24 [-1.56, -0.92]), followed by sonic brushes (SMD: -0.87 [-1.15, -0.59]).The relative ranking of interventions based on SUCRA values is illustrated in Fig. 4. For plaque reduction, the order of efficacy is as follows: Oscillating-Rotating (SUCRA = 89.2%) > Sonic (72.4%) > Ultrasonic (54.1%) > Other Electric (45.3%) > Manual (18.3%). A similar hierarchy was observed for gingivitis reduction, with oscillating-rotating brushes again ranking highest (SUCRA = 86.5%), followed by sonic (70.1%), ultrasonic (52.8%), other electric (43.9%), and manual (16.7%).

To facilitate clinical interpretation, we translated the standardized mean differences (SMDs) into absolute effect sizes using the most commonly reported in dices. For plaque reduction, measured by the Rustogi Modified Navy Plaque Index (RMNPI) on a scale of 0 to 1, the estimated mean difference between oscillating-rotating and manual toothbrushes was − 0.18 (95% CI: -0.24 to -0.12), indicating an approximate 18% greater reduction in plaque score. In terms of gingivitis reduction, assessed using the Modified Gingival Index (MGI) on a scale of 0 to 4, the mean difference was − 0.32 (95% CI: -0.45 to -0.19), which represents a clinically significant reduction in inflammation.Interpretive caution is warranted for the ‘other electric’ category (SUCRA = 45.3%), which was represented by only two studies (k = 2) with small sample sizes. Consequently, the SUCRA ranking for this intervention is derived from very limited evidence and should be interpreted with extreme caution. As indicated by the confidence intervals crossing zero in the longer-term stratified analysis (Supplementary Tables S3 and S4), the observed difference between ‘other electric’ and manual toothbrushes was not statistically significant. The imprecision surrounding its effect estimate is substantial.

Stratified analysis by study duration

To assess the robustness of the primary findings and address potential violations of the transitivity assumption, we conducted a stratified NMA that separated short - term single - use trials (n = 7) from longer - term home - use trials (n = 12). The results are summarized in Supplementary Tables S3 and S4. In the short-term single-use stratum, oscillating-rotating brushes emerged as the top-ranked intervention for plaque reduction (SUCRA = 91.5%), followed by sonic (SUCRA = 74.2%), ultrasonic (SUCRA = 52.3%), other electric (SUCRA = 44.1%), and manual brushes (SUCRA = 16.8%). The effect sizes compared to manual brushing were larger in this stratum (oscillating-rotating: SMD = -1.52, 95% CI: -1.98 to -1.06) than in the primary analysis, indicating an immediate mechanical cleaning advantage.In the longer-term home-use stratum, the ranking was similar, but the differences between technologies were narrower. Oscillating-rotating brushes retained the top position (SUCRA = 86.7%), followed by sonic (SUCRA = 70.8%), ultrasonic (SUCRA = 55.2%), other electric (SUCRA = 46.1%), and manual (SUCRA = 19.5%). The effect size compared to manual brushing was more modest in this stratum (oscillating-rotating: SMD = -0.96, 95% CI: -1.35 to -0.57), reflecting behavioral and compliance factors over extended use. Importantly, the relative ranking of electric technologies remained consistent across both strata, thereby supporting the robustness of the primary analysis.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore heterogeneity (Fig. 5). The superiority of electric toothbrushes was consistent across most subgroups. Notably, the largest effect sizes were observed in orthodontic patients and children, while the effect was non-significant in the elderly subgroup. Furthermore, the results remained robust in sensitivity analyses that excluded studies with a high risk of bias.

Assessment of publication bias

Our comprehensive assessment did not reveal significant evidence of publication bias or small-study effects. The distribution of p-values was right-skewed (χ² = 43.0, p = 0.03), suggesting that the body of evidence possesses evidential value beyond the selectively reported significant findings. Precision-weighted regression indicated no significant association between effect size and study precision (β = 0.15, 95% CI: −0.23 to 0.53, p = 0.42). Sensitivity analyses, which excluded smaller studies (N < 50), demonstrated virtually unchanged intervention rankings (maximum ΔSUCRA = 3.2%). The distribution of p-values and the contributions of each study are visualized in Fig. 7. However, the statistical power of these tests is limited due to the small number of studies (k < 10 for most comparisons).

Discussion

Main findings and interpretation

This network meta-analysis of 19 RCTs synthesizes the current best evidence to provide a direct comparative ranking of major electric toothbrush technologies. The principal finding is unequivocal: all electric toothbrushes demonstrate statistically significant superiority over manual toothbrushes in reducing both dental plaque and gingivitis, with oscillating-rotating technology consistently ranking as the most efficacious option. This hierarchy is robust, as evidenced by high SUCRA values for plaque (89.2%) and a similar order for gingivitis outcomes.

The biological plausibility of this ranking further strengthens our conclusions. The superior performance of oscillating-rotating brushes is mechanistically supported by their unique three-dimensional cleaning action, which involves simultaneous oscillation and rotation. This action generates high-frequency, focused shear forces that effectively disrupt and detach the supragingival biofilm matrix, particularly from concave surfaces, fissures, and the critical gingival margin area [38, 39]. In contrast, the primarily lateral, sweeping motion of sonic brushes and the microstreaming activity of ultrasonic brushes, while effective, may be less efficient at mechanically dislodging firmly adherent plaque in these challenging anatomical sites [40]. This interpretation is corroborated by several included trials that specifically noted the superior cleaning efficacy of oscillating-rotating brushes on approximating surfaces compared to other modalities [41, 42].

Comparison with previous research and innovation

Our findings consolidate and significantly advance the existing evidence base. Previous high-quality systematic reviews and pairwise meta-analyses have definitively established that electric toothbrushes are more effective for plaque removal than manual brushes [7–9]. This study not only confirms that conclusion but also provides a crucial, novel extension: a direct, quantitative ranking among the electric subtypes themselves.This represents a substantial step forward. Prior evidence could guide clinicians in recommending “an electric toothbrush,” but it could not inform the choice between, for example, a sonic and an oscillating-rotating model when both options are available. By employing a frequentist network meta-analysis, we have integrated both direct head-to-head comparisons (e.g., oscillating-rotating vs. sonic) and indirect evidence via a common comparator (manual brushing) to generate a unified hierarchy. This methodological approach directly addresses a persistent and clinically relevant evidence gap, transforming the clinical question from “whether to use an electric brush” to “which electric brush technology to select for optimal results.”

Clinical implications and subgroup insights

The stratified results of this analysis provide nuanced and actionable guidance for personalized preventive oral care, moving beyond a one-size-fits-all recommendation.Our findings for orthodontic patients are particularly compelling, as this subgroup demonstrated the most pronounced benefits from using oscillating-rotating electric toothbrushes. Fixed appliances significantly hinder plaque control, thus elevating the risk of gingivitis, enamel demineralization, and white spot lesions [43]. The mechanized, three-dimensional action of oscillating-rotating brushes appears uniquely effective at navigating the complex topography around brackets and archwires, disrupting plaque biofilms in areas that are exceptionally challenging to clean manually. Therefore, these devices should be strongly advocated as a cornerstone of home-care regimens for individuals undergoing fixed orthodontic therapy.

Transitioning to pediatric populations, the significant treatment effect underscores the role of electric toothbrushes as potential enablers. Children often lack the manual dexterity and sustained motivation required for consistently effective brushing. An oscillating-rotating electric toothbrush can serve as a ‘skill-equalizer,’ delivering a standardized cleaning action that compensates for variable technique. This may not only enhance immediate plaque control but also help instill positive long-term oral hygiene habits by making the process more efficient and potentially more engaging. Although the literature does not uniformly define established minimal clinically important differences (MCID) for these indices, the absolute differences presented (e.g., 18% greater plaque reduction) serve as clinically interpretable benchmarks that can inform practice and facilitate shared decision-making. Given the limited number of studies within each subgroup (e.g., only three trials involving orthodontic patients), the findings from these subgroups should be interpreted as generating hypotheses rather than providing definitive evidence. The effect of gingivitis reduction in adults was not statistically significant, as indicated by the confidence interval crossing the null (Fig. 6). Due to the limited sample sizes in the subgroups, formal interaction tests were not conducted; thus, the observed difference between subgroups (e.g., orthodontic patients vs. adults) are exploratory and should not be interpreted as statistically significant without confirmatory studies. However, the attenuated effect observed in the elderly subgroup introduces a critical note of caution. This finding highlights that technological superiority does not automatically translate into effectiveness for all users. In older adults, factors such as impaired grip strength, arthritic limitations, cognitive decline, or visual deficits can compromise the ability to use any toothbrush properly. Consequently, recommending an advanced electric toothbrush must be part of a broader, individualized assessment. Success may depend on combining the tool with tailored instruction, caregiver assistance, or adaptive aids to handle the device, emphasizing that the prescription of technology must align with the user’s capacity and support system.

Strengths and limitations

The credibility of our findings is enhanced by several methodological strengths: adherence to PRISMA-NMA reporting guidelines, a comprehensive search without language restrictions, dual independent reviews at all stages, utilization of the updated Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool, and rigorous statistical methodologies, including consistency checks and sensitivity analyses.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, clinical heterogeneity arises from significant variations in population characteristics, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Additionally, the use of various plaque and gingival indices across studies may have introduced further heterogeneity. Although we employed a prioritization hierarchy and calculated standardized mean differences (SMDs) to mitigate this issue, the absolute effect sizes derived from the most commonly utilized indices (RMNPI and MGI) should be regarded as approximate. Future studies should adopt a standardized core outcome set to enhance comparability.

Second, the relative rankings of interventions are determined by Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking (SUCRA) values. It is important to understand that SUCRA values reflect the probability of a particular intervention achieving the highest rank among those being compared. However, they do not provide a direct assessment of clinical effectiveness or the absolute magnitude of benefit. Readers should interpret the ranking hierarchy within this probabilistic context.

Third, despite employing a multi-faceted approach to assess reporting biases, we cannot entirely exclude the possibility that unpublished null findings may influence the network. We acknowledge the limited statistical power of publication bias tests within a network of this size, with k being less than 10 for most comparisons. The non-significant results of Egger’s test, along with the observed symmetry in funnel plots, should be interpreted with caution, as small-study effects may still be present but undetectable. Future updates of this network meta-analysis, supported by a larger evidence base, may enable more robust assessments of publication bias.

Fourth, our decision to extract the longest follow-up from each study for the primary analysis, while clinically relevant for assessing sustained efficacy, may have introduced bias by homogenizing different time points across studies. This could potentially overlook significant temporal variations in treatment outcomes, such as initial learning effects or waning efficacy over time.

As with all Network Meta-Analyses (NMAs), the validity of indirect comparisons relies heavily on the assumption of transitivity, which posits that studies comparing different interventions share sufficient similarities in their study designs and patient characteristics. We endeavored to uphold this assumption by implementing strict and uniform inclusion criteria. To address concerns regarding transitivity, we conducted a stratified analysis that distinguished between short-term single-use trials and longer-term home-use studies. However, clinical heterogeneity within each stratum, such as variations in brushing durations and levels of supervision, may still exist. Nevertheless, the consistent ranking observed across strata reinforces the robustness of our primary conclusions. Even within the longer-term stratum, follow-up durations varied from 4 weeks to 12 months, potentially introducing residual heterogeneity. The lack of a temporal trend analysis, such as meta-regression on follow-up length, represents a limitation of the current study. Future research should investigate whether treatment effects diminish or accumulate over time by employing more granular temporal models. The limited number of direct head-to-head trials comparing electric technologies, specifically oscillating-rotating versus sonic toothbrushes, constrains the precision of the estimated differences between these technologies. Only two head-to-head randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have directly compared oscillating-rotating and sonic toothbrushes. This limited direct evidence introduces uncertainty regarding the estimated differences between these two technologies, highlighting the need for future large-scale direct comparisons. To validate the observed hierarchy and assess patient-centered outcomes, including compliance and adverse events, future large-scale, long-term randomized trials that directly compare oscillating-rotating, sonic, and ultrasonic brushes are essential. Another limitation is the potential influence of industry sponsorship, as the majority of the included trials received funding from manufacturers of electric toothbrushes. Although the RoB 2.0 assessment prioritized methodological quality over funding sources, we acknowledge that sponsorship may introduce bias favoring the sponsor’s product.

Future research directions

To build upon this work, future research should: (1) Standardize outcome reporting by utilizing a core set of validated indices to enhance the clarity of meta-analyses; (2) Conduct longitudinal, real-world studies that evaluate not only efficacy but also long-term adherence, durability, and cost-effectiveness of these technologies; (3) Investigate emerging innovations, such as brushes with integrated artificial intelligence that provide feedback on brushing quality and coverage; and (4) Develop and test targeted interventions for special needs populations, including the elderly and individuals with disabilities, for whom current designs may be inadequate. Such research will be essential for evolving precise, patient-centered clinical guidelines and informing healthcare policy.

Conclusion

This network meta-analysis synthesizes both direct and indirect comparative evidence to establish a potential efficacy hierarchy among major toothbrush technologies. The findings suggest that oscillating-rotating electric toothbrushes may represent among the most effective interventions for reducing dental plaque and gingivitis, followed by sonic, ultrasonic, other electric technologies, and, lastly, manual toothbrushes. However, direct head-to-head comparisons between electric technologies are limited, with only two trials comparing oscillating-rotating technologies to sonic technologies. Furthermore, the evidence supporting the ‘other electric’ category is particularly sparse. Consequently, this ranking should be interpreted as provisional. This study provides a comprehensive ranking of these modalities, contributing to evidence-based guidance for clinical practice and consumer choice. The pronounced benefits observed in high-risk populations, particularly orthodontic patients and children, highlight the potential value of this hierarchy. For these groups, where conventional brushing poses distinct challenges, the adoption of an oscillating-rotating electric toothbrush could be considered a useful strategy for enhancing preventive care and mitigating oral health risks. Therefore, based on the current evidence, oscillating-rotating electric toothbrushes may be preferred for optimal mechanical plaque control in daily oral hygiene, particularly in populations where manual brushing is challenging. This recommendation offers a reasonable evidence-based rationale to guide clinical decision-making and public health advice.
