Donor selection for patients with HLA-homozygous haplotypes in allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation
Authors: Noriyoshi Yoshinaga, Makoto Iwasaki, Koji Kato, Fumihiko Kimura, Masahiro Hirayama, Minoru Kanaya, Satoko Morishima, Naoyuki Uchida, Noriko Doki, Takahiro Fukuda, Yoshinobu Kanda, Tetsuya Nishida, Yuta Hasegawa, Shinichi Kako, Masatsugu Tanaka, Mineo Kurokawa, Noboru Asada, Toshiro Kawakita, Keisuke Kataoka, Yukio Kondo, Kazunori Imada, Tatsuo Ichinohe, Makoto Onizuka, Yoshiko Atsuta, Junya Kanda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
HLA homozygous haplotypes occur worldwide, but outcomes after allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation using alternative donor sources remain uncertain. We retrospectively analyzed the Japanese national transplantation registry to compare outcomes after first allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in patients with HLA homozygous haplotypes. Donors were classified as homo-to-homo, defined as HLA-matched, or hetero-to-homo, defined as allele-level mismatches at HLA-A, -B, -C, and/or -DRB1 restricted to the host-versus-graft direction. The unrelated donor homo-to-homo group served as the reference. We included 691 patients: related donor homo-to-homo (n = 121), related donor hetero-to-homo (n = 76), unrelated donor homo-to-homo (n = 374), unrelated donor hetero-to-homo (n = 22), cord blood homo-to-homo (n = 40), and cord blood hetero-to-homo (n = 58). Compared with the unrelated donor homo-to-homo group, overall survival was inferior in the cord blood homo-to-homo group (adjusted hazard ratio [HR], 1.71; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.11–2.64; P = 0.015), whereas the unrelated donor hetero-to-homo group showed a nonsignificant trend toward inferior overall survival (adjusted HR, 1.77; 95% CI, 0.97–3.22; P = 0.061). In this Japanese cohort, cord blood homo-to-homo transplantation was associated with inferior overall survival, whereas related donor hetero-to-homo and cord blood hetero-to-homo transplantation were not. Introduction

Allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT) is a potentially curative treatment for hematological malignancies, including leukemia and lymphoma, as well as bone marrow failure syndromes. The use of human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-matched donors is the standard approach in HCT because HLA compatibility is a major determinant of graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), graft failure, and survival [1–4]. HLA mismatches in the graft-versus-host (GVH) direction primarily increase GVHD risk, whereas mismatches in the host-versus-graft (HVG) direction may increase the risk of graft failure [5–7]. When HLA-matched donors are unavailable, alternative donors, including haploidentical related donors, HLA-mismatched unrelated donors, or cord blood (CB), are commonly used [8–10].

HLA-homozygous haplotypes occur with varying frequencies among ethnic groups, with an estimated global prevalence of 1–4% [11–14]. In recipients with HLA-homozygous haplotypes, a haploidentical donor who carries the recipient haplotype can result in mismatches limited to the HVG direction while maintaining complete matching in the GVH direction, potentially reducing GVHD risk. Previous studies have reported comparable outcomes between HLA-matched and haploidentical related donor transplantation in this population [15].

However, donor selection remains unclear when no suitable related donor is available. Therefore, we analyzed the Japanese national transplantation registry to compare transplantation outcomes across donor sources, including unrelated donors and CB, and to inform donor selection for patients with HLA-homozygous haplotypes.

Methods

Study design and patients

Clinical data on HCT recipients were collected by the Japanese Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy (JSTCT) and the Japanese Data Center for Hematopoietic Cell Transplantation (JDCHCT) using the Transplant Registry Unified Management Program [16, 17]. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the institutional review boards of Kyoto University Hospital and the Data Management Committees of JSTCT and JDCHCT. All patients provided written informed consent to participate in this study.

We included patients aged ≥ 16 years with hematologic malignancies who were homozygous at the allele level at HLA-A, -B, -C, and -DRB1 and underwent their first bone marrow (BM), peripheral blood stem cell (PBSC), or cord blood (CB) transplantation between 2000 and 2022. Donor sources were related donors, unrelated donors, and CB. Donors were classified as homo-to-homo (matched at HLA-A, -B, -C, and -DRB1) or hetero-to-homo (allele-level mismatches at HLA-A, -B, -C, and/or -DRB1 restricted to the host-versus-graft direction). We excluded patients with missing HLA-A, -B, -C, or -DRB1 typing for either the recipient or donor.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was overall survival (OS). Secondary endpoints included relapse-free survival (RFS), cumulative incidence of relapse, non-relapse mortality (NRM), neutrophil engraftment, platelet engraftment, grade II-IV acute GVHD, grade III-IV acute GVHD, and chronic GVHD.

Definitions

OS was defined as the time from transplantation to death from any cause, with survivors censored at the last follow-up. RFS was defined as the time from transplantation to relapse or death from any cause. Neutrophil engraftment was defined as the first of 3 consecutive days with an absolute neutrophil ≥ 0.5 × 10⁹/L post-transplantation. Platelet engraftment was defined as the first of 7 consecutive days with a platelet count ≥ 20 × 10⁹/L without platelet transfusion post-transplantation. Acute and chronic GVHD were diagnosed and graded according to established criteria [18, 19]. The cumulative incidence of chronic GVHD was evaluated among patients who were alive and relapse-free at day 100 post-transplantation.

Disease risk was classified as standard or advanced according to disease type, disease status, and established prognostic criteria. Standard stage included acute leukemias in complete remission, MDS with IPSS Low or Intermediate-1 risk (or IPSS-R Very Low, Low, or Intermediate risk), CML in chronic or accelerated phase, and lymphoproliferative disorders in complete or partial remission. All other conditions were classified as advanced stage.

Conditioning regimens were classified as myeloablative (MAC) when they included total body irradiation (TBI) > 8 Gy, oral busulfan ≥ 9 mg/kg, intravenous busulfan ≥ 7.2 mg/kg, melphalan > 140 mg/m2, or thiotepa ≥ 10 mg/kg. All other regimens were classified as reduced-intensity conditioning (RIC) [20, 21]. Post-transplantation cyclophosphamide (PTCy) was defined as cyclophosphamide 40–50 mg/kg administered on days 3, 4, or 5 post-transplantation [22, 23].

HLA typing and haplotype inference

HLA-A, -B, -C, and -DRB1 typing was performed at allele-level resolution (2-field resolution) for all patients and donors. Recipients were classified as having HLA-homozygous haplotypes when they were homozygous at all four loci (HLA-A, -B, -C, and -DRB1) at the allele level. Haplotypes were inferred based on HLA allele combinations using established haplotype frequencies in the Japanese population [11].

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate, and continuous variables were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test. OS and RFS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Follow-up was calculated from transplantation to the date of last contact or death. The median follow-up time was estimated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method. The cumulative incidences of relapse, NRM, engraftment, and GVHD were estimated using cumulative incidence curves [24]. Competing events were defined as follows: relapse for NRM; death without relapse for relapse; death without engraftment for neutrophil and platelet engraftment; and death without acute GVHD for acute GVHD. For chronic GVHD (evaluated among patients alive and relapse-free at day 100), competing events were death and relapse. Group differences were assessed using the log-rank test (OS/RFS) or Gray’s test (competing-risk endpoints) [25]. Cox proportional hazards models were used for OS and RFS, and Fine–Gray models were used for competing-risk endpoints (relapse, NRM, engraftment, and GVHD) [26]. The unrelated donor (URD) homo-to-homo group served as the reference group. Candidate covariates for multivariate models included patient characteristics (age, sex, CMV serostatus, performance status, hematopoietic cell transplantation–comorbidity index, and disease risk) and transplant-related factors (year of transplantation, conditioning intensity, and GVHD prophylaxis). Hematopoietic cell transplantation–comorbidity index and performance status were retained in the final stepwise models, confirming that these covariates were adjusted for in the primary analysis.

As baseline comparisons for the subsequent combined subgroup analyses, we first evaluated OS stratified by donor source alone and by donor HLA matching status alone. We then assessed potential heterogeneity of the HLA matching effect across donor sources by including an interaction term between donor source and donor HLA homozygosity in the Cox model for OS. Because interaction tests can be underpowered, we prespecified a two-sided P value < 0.10 as suggestive of effect modification. When this criterion was met, we presented analyses stratified by donor source and by HLA matching status.

Covariates were selected using a forward stepwise selection procedure (entry and retention criterion of P < 0.05), with donor source (the main exposure) forced into all models. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 16 (StataCorp., College Station, TX, USA) and EZR software version 1.68 [27].

Results

Patient characteristics

From the registry database, we identified 38001 patients aged ≥ 16 years with hematologic malignancies who underwent their first allogeneic HCT between 2000 and 2022 and had available HLA-A, -B, -C, and -DRB1 typing information. Among these, 724 patients were identified as having HLA-homozygous haplotypes. We excluded 33 cord blood (CB) units that included GVH direction mismatches. After applying these inclusion and exclusion criteria, 691 patients were enrolled (Fig. 1). Detailed patient and transplant characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Compared with the URD homo-to-homo group, the URD hetero-to-homo and CB homo-to-homo groups included older recipients and higher proportions of patients with adverse baseline features, including higher HCT-CI and, for the CB homo-to-homo group, poorer performance status. These imbalances should be considered when interpreting subgroup differences in survival.

Regarding stem cell sources, 121 patients received grafts from related donors (RD) homo-to-homo, 76 from RD hetero-to-homo donors, 374 from URD homo-to-homo, and 22 from URD hetero-to-homo donors. Additionally, 40 and 58 patients received CB units classified as homo-to-homo and hetero-to-homo without GVH-direction mismatches, respectively. The median age at transplantation was 51 years (range, 16–76), with 26.0% of patients being older than 60 years. Underlying diseases included acute myeloid leukemia/myelodysplastic syndrome (n = 364; 52.7%), acute lymphoblastic leukemia (n = 127; 18.4%), chronic myeloid leukemia/myeloproliferative neoplasia (n = 39; 5.6%), and lymphoma/multiple myeloma (n = 161; 23.3%). A majority of patients (n = 416; 60.2%) had standard-stage disease, 225 (32.6%) had advanced-stage disease, and the disease stage was unknown in 50 patients (7.2%). Most transplants (n = 518; 74.9%) were performed in or after 2011, while 173 (25.0%) were performed between 2000 and 2010. Donor-specific antibodies (DSA) were detected in only three patients (0.4%). Regarding GVHD prophylaxis, 12 patients with RD hetero-to-homo transplants received PTCy.

Distribution of HLA mismatches in the hetero-to-homo groups

To characterize HLA mismatch patterns in the hetero-to-homo groups, we summarized the distribution of mismatches by the number of mismatched loci and by mismatched locus, stratified by donor source (Supplementary Table S1). URD hetero-to-homo transplants were predominantly single-locus mismatches (17/22, 77.2%), whereas multi-locus mismatches were more common in RD hetero-to-homo (3 loci: 19/76, 25.0%; 4 loci: 47/76, 61.8%) and CB hetero-to-homo transplants (3 loci: 32/58, 55.2%). Across donor sources, mismatches frequently involved HLA-DRB1 (RD 90.7%; URD 72.7%; CB 79.3%).

Baseline associations of donor source and HLA matching with overall survival

The median follow-up among survivors was 5.11 years (interquartile range, 2.23–9.26 years). We first evaluated outcomes according to donor source and HLA matching status as baseline comparisons for the subsequent combined subgroup analyses. The unadjusted 5-year OS rates were 50.6% (95% confidence interval (CI), 42.6%–58.0%) in the RD group, 53.7% (95% CI, 48.3%–58.7%) in the URD group and 33.8% (95% CI, 23.5%–44.4%) in the CB group (Fig. 2A).

In multivariate analysis, the CB group showed a trend toward higher mortality risk compared with the URD group (adjusted hazard ratio [HR], 1.30; 95% confidence interval CI, 0.95–1.77, P = 0.097) (Supplementary Table S2).

The unadjusted 5-year OS rates were 51.9% (95% CI, 47.3%–56.3%) in the homo-to-homo group and 44.1% (95% CI, 35.4%–52.5%) in the hetero-to-homo group (Fig. 2B). Multivariate analysis showed no significant differences in mortality risk between the groups (Supplementary Table S3).

We next asked whether HVG-direction mismatches are associated with inferior survival across donor sources. We then examined whether the combination of donor source and HLA matching category identified clinically distinct risk groups and which transplantation outcomes contributed to the observed differences in survival. In the Cox model for OS, the interaction between donor source and HLA matching category met the prespecified criterion for possible effect modification (cord blood × hetero-to-homo: HR 0.50, P = 0.077). We therefore evaluated outcomes according to combined donor source and HLA matching subgroups. The results of the univariate analyses are presented in Supplementary Table S4.

Outcomes across combined donor source and HLA matching subgroups

The unadjusted 5-year OS rates were 51.9% (95% CI, 41.7%–61.1%) in the RD homo-to-homo group, 48.7% (95% CI, 35.5%–60.6%) in the RD hetero-to-homo group, 54.3% (95% CI, 48.0%–59.0%) in the URD homo-to-homo group, 42.2% (95% CI, 19.3%–63.6%) in the URD hetero-to-homo group, 25.6% (95% CI, 11.0%–42.0%) in the CB homo-to-homo group, and 38.7% (95% CI, 25.0%–52.0%) in the CB hetero-to-homo group (Fig. 3A). Although the unadjusted OS rates in the RD hetero-to-homo and CB hetero-to-homo groups were lower than those in the URD homo-to-homo group, multivariate analysis showed no significant differences between the URD homo-to-homo group and the RD hetero-to-homo or CB hetero-to-homo groups. The CB homo-to-homo group showed a higher mortality risk compared with the URD homo-to-homo group (adjusted HR, 1.71; 95% CI, 1.11–2.64; P = 0.015). The URD hetero-to-homo group showed a trend toward inferior OS compared with the URD homo-to-homo group, although this was not statistically significant (adjusted HR, 1.77; 95% CI, 0.97–3.22; P = 0.061) (Table 2). To address potential era effects, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to transplants performed from 2011 to 2022. In this cohort, the CB homo-to-homo subgroup was not statistically significantly associated with inferior OS compared with the URD homo-to-homo group (adjusted HR, 1.38; 95% CI, 0.86–2.23; P = 0.174) (Supplementary Table S5).

Discussion

We used a Japanese registry to evaluate outcomes after allogeneic HCT in patients with HLA-homozygous haplotypes according to donor source and HLA compatibility. In multivariate analysis, OS in the RD homo-to-homo, RD hetero-to-homo, and CB hetero-to-homo groups did not differ significantly from that in the URD homo-to-homo reference group. In contrast, the CB homo-to-homo group was associated with inferior overall survival, whereas the URD hetero-to-homo group showed a trend toward inferior overall survival that did not reach statistical significance. Because this was a retrospective observational study, the findings described below should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships. Furthermore, RFS was lower in the CB homo-to-homo group than in the URD homo-to-homo group. It should be noted that the inferior overall survival observed in the CB homo-to-homo and URD hetero-to-homo groups may be partially influenced by baseline imbalances in patient characteristics. As shown in Table 1, the CB homo-to-homo group represented the oldest population (median age, 63.5 years), and both groups had higher proportions of patients with HCT-CI scores of ≥ 3 (20.0% and 31.8%, respectively) compared with the reference URD homo-to-homo group (12.3%). The URD hetero-to-homo group also had a higher median age (58.5 years) and a higher proportion of patients receiving reduced-intensity conditioning (54.5%) compared with the URD homo-to-homo group (median age 49 years; reduced-intensity conditioning, 33.8%), which may reflect a selection bias toward older or frailer patients in this subgroup.

Both HCT-CI and performance status were retained in the final stepwise models and thus directly adjusted for in the multivariate analyses. Nevertheless, residual confounding from these and other unmeasured baseline conditions cannot be excluded.

Relapse risk was higher in the RD hetero-to-homo group than in the URD homo-to-homo group. The reasons for this difference remain unclear and may reflect multiple factors, including donor and recipient characteristics and transplant strategies. In addition, our findings were unchanged in a sensitivity analysis excluding patients who received PTCy, suggesting that the observed relapse signal was not driven by PTCy use. Because only 15.7% (12/76) of patients in the RD hetero-to-homo group received PTCy, we were underpowered to evaluate the independent effect of GVHD prophylaxis on relapse in this subgroup. Although prior studies have raised the possibility of an association between PTCy-based prophylaxis and relapse, larger studies are needed to clarify whether GVHD prophylaxis contributes to relapse risk in patients with HLA-homozygous haplotypes [28, 29].

We also observed a higher relapse risk in the CB homo-to-homo group than in the URD homo-to-homo group. A previous study reported an increased relapse risk after CB transplantation with complete haplotype matching, particularly in pairs carrying the haplotype [30]. To assess whether our findings were driven by haplotype distribution, we performed a subgroup analysis restricted to pairs matched for the most frequent haplotype (A*24:02–B*52:01–C*12:02–DRB1*15:02). Relapse risk remained higher in the CB homo-to-homo group than in the URD homo-to-homo group. These findings suggest that the excess relapse observed in CB homo-to-homo transplantation is unlikely to be explained solely by a single high-risk haplotype. Several mechanisms may contribute to the higher relapse risk observed after CB homo-to-homo transplantation. CB transplantation is associated with delayed immune reconstitution, including impaired thymopoiesis and delayed functional T-cell recovery, which may limit early antileukemic immunity [31]. Under these conditions, extensive haplotype sharing may further attenuate alloreactive priming, potentially weakening graft-versus-leukemia effects despite acceptable GVHD rates. In addition, adult CBT studies suggest that transplantation outcomes depend on the balance between HLA disparity and cell dose, and that the optimal degree of HLA matching may vary across relapse risk groups [32]. Although current unit selection strategies focus on improving survival and engraftment by balancing HLA matching and cell dose, our findings, together with prior observations in haplotype-matched CBT, raise the possibility that maximal haplotype sharing may be associated with a higher risk of relapse in recipients with HLA-homozygous haplotypes, particularly in patients with intrinsically high relapse risk. The inferior OS in this group likely reflects the increased relapse burden. The distribution of HLA mismatches in the hetero-to-homo groups should also be considered when interpreting these findings (Supplementary Table S1). Mismatch burden was strongly linked to donor source, with RD, URD, and CB transplantations showing different distributions of mismatched loci. Accordingly, the present analysis could not disentangle the independent effect of specific mismatch patterns from that of donor source. This suggests that the unfavorable trend observed in the URD hetero-to-homo group may not be explained solely by the number of mismatched loci. However, because each subgroup was small, more detailed locus-specific analyses were not feasible.

Consistent with prior reports, CB transplantation was associated with lower neutrophil and platelet engraftment compared with other donor sources [33]. In our cohort, both CB homo-to-homo and CB hetero-to-homo groups showed lower engraftment compared with URD homo-to-homo, whereas we did not observe clear differences in engraftment between the two CB groups. Together, these findings suggest that, among CB transplantations without GVH direction mismatches, the presence of HVG direction mismatches may have limited impact on engraftment in patients with HLA-homozygous haplotypes. Previous studies evaluating mismatch direction in CB transplantation have reported inconsistent results. One study reported that HVG mismatches were associated with higher risks of engraftment failure and reduced survival compared with GVH-direction mismatches [5]. In contrast, another Japanese study found that isolated HVG mismatches did not affect engraftment or survival compared with bidirectional single-locus mismatches [34]. Differences in study design, cohort size, and the distribution of unidirectional versus bidirectional mismatches may partly explain these discrepancies. Our results support the observation that isolated HVG-direction mismatches do not necessarily compromise engraftment in this context.

The overall incidence of grade III–IV acute GVHD was low in this cohort of patients with HLA-homozygous haplotypes. Notably, the RD hetero-to-homo group showed a lower risk of severe acute GVHD than the URD homo-to-homo group, which may be influenced by GVHD prophylaxis strategies used in this setting, including PTCy or ATG [10, 28, 29, 35, 36]. No grade III–IV acute GVHD occurred among patients receiving PTCy or ATG in the RD hetero-to-homo group. However, the small sample size limits definitive conclusions.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective, registry-based observational study, and the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Residual confounding related to donor selection and other unmeasured clinical factors may remain. Second, transplants were performed over a long period from 2000 to 2022, during which changes in transplantation practice and supportive care may have introduced era-related confounding. Although we adjusted for year of transplantation and performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to transplants performed from 2011 to 2022, subgroup-specific estimates changed in that analysis: the signal for inferior OS in the CB homo-to-homo subgroup was attenuated, whereas the URD hetero-to-homo subgroup remained associated with inferior OS. These findings indicate that the subgroup estimates were sensitive to era restriction and should be interpreted cautiously, particularly given the limited sample size. Third, sample sizes were small in some subgroups, particularly the URD hetero-to-homo subgroup and the CB subgroups, which limited statistical precision. Fourth, generalizability may be limited because the cohort was derived from a Japanese population. Finally, interpretation of the CB transplantation findings should be limited to cases without GVH-direction mismatches, because CB units with GVH-direction mismatches were excluded.

In this registry-based observational study, RD hetero-to-homo and CB hetero-to-homo transplantation were not significantly associated with inferior OS compared with URD homo-to-homo transplantation and may be considered possible alternatives in selected patients. However, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence and require validation in contemporary prospective cohorts.
