Protocol for autofluorescence removal in microglia by photobleaching in free-floating immunofluorescent staining of mouse brain tissue
Authors: Mark N Metri, Justin You, Jeehye Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryAutofluorescence in brain tissue poses a challenge in immunofluorescent staining by obscuring antibody-labeled proteins, mainly within microglia. Here, we describe a cost-effective protocol for removing autofluorescence in mouse brain sections by photobleaching. We outline steps to collect and section brain tissue, apply a photobleaching step using a light-emitting diode (LED), and perform immunofluorescent staining with primary and secondary antibodies. This protocol enables clearer visualization of microglial markers, particularly in mouse models of neurodegenerative diseases and aging.Subject areas: genetics, immunology, microscopy, model organisms, antibody, neuroscience, molecular/chemical probes

Before you begin

The protocol below describes specific steps for immunofluorescent staining of microglia in sagittal brain sections of wildtype and a Matr3S85C/S85C mouse model of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS).1 We have focused the analysis on the cerebellum, where Matr3S85C/S85C mice exhibit Purkinje cell degeneration with an increased number of microglia as well as higher levels of autofluorescence compared to wildtype.1,2 The steps for photobleaching have been adapted from Stillman et al.3

Innovation

This protocol presents an accessible and cost-effective method for removing autofluorescence from free-floating mouse brain sections using a custom-built photobleaching apparatus. Autofluorescence, especially from lipofuscin, can confound immunofluorescent detection of microglial markers such as CLEC7A, particularly in mouse models of neurodegenerative diseases and aging. Our approach modifies existing methods by integrating a low-cost blue light-emitting diode (LED) and laboratory materials to create a stable photobleaching apparatus. This setup is simple, reproducible, and preserves tissue integrity. We optimized the protocol for OCT-embedded, free-floating sections, with demonstrated utility in validating antibody specificity using CLEC7A knockout tissue. This workflow enables clearer separation of true signal from background and improves confidence in microglial marker analysis without requiring advanced imaging infrastructure.

Expected outcomes

Removal of autofluorescence after photobleaching

To determine whether the protocol with photobleaching apparatus is effective in removing autofluorescence, we compared photobleached and non-photobleached Matr3S85C/S85C and wild-type mouse cerebellar sections, particularly the deep cerebellar nuclei (DCN) region where there is increased presence of disease-associated microglia (DAM) in the mutant mice.2 Although these sections were not further stained with antibodies, confocal images of non-photobleached sections captured under all three lasers, 488, 555 and 647 nm, shows lipofuscin-related autofluorescence (Lipo-AF; Figure 2A). However, photobleached sections show a significant decrease in autofluorescent signal compared to non-photobleached sections (Figure 2B), demonstrating effective removal of autofluorescent signal upon photobleaching.

Photobleaching removes autofluorescence in mouse deep cerebellar nuclei (DCN)

(A) Representative images and (B) quantification of lipo-AF at 488 (first column), 555 (second column), and 647 (third column) nm of unstained 60-week-old Matr3S85C/S85C and wild-type mouse DCN. Scale bar represents 100 μm. Colored bars and data points indicate samples not photobleached, while black bars and data points indicate photobleached samples. Bar heights indicate mean ± SEM. Significance was determined using ordinary two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test (∗ indicates p value ≤ 0.05).

CLEC7A (or Dectin-1), a DAM marker, colocalizes with autofluorescence (Lipo-AF) in a non-photobleached condition

Matr3S85C/S85C and wild-type mouse cerebellar sections were photobleached or non-photobleached, and stained with IBA1, a microglial marker, and CLEC7A, a DAM marker. Non-photobleached sections show that CLEC7A apparently colocalizes with autofluorescent signals, making it difficult to determine whether these are CLEC7A-specific signals or merely the presence of autofluorescent background in the same channel as CLEC7A, which were removed by photobleaching (Figure 3). This result further demonstrates that photobleaching can remove a significant amount of false-positive signal emerging from autofluorescence possibly arising from lipofuscin.

Photobleaching is required for validating the specificity of the CLEC7A antibody even in a CLEC7A knockout background

To validate the specificity of the CLEC7A antibody, Matr3S85C/S85C; Clec7a+/+ and Matr3S85C/S85C; Clec7a−/− mouse cerebellar sections were stained with IBA1 and CLEC7A. In non-photobleached conditions, the number of CLEC7A+ microglia are not significantly reduced in Matr3S85C/S85C; Clec7a−/− compared to Matr3S85C/S85C; Clec7a+/+ mice, which could suggest an incomplete knockout (Figures 4A and 4B). However, upon photobleaching, false-positive CLEC7A signals were markedly reduced in both Matr3S85C/S85C; Clec7a+/+ and Matr3S85C/S85C; Clec7a−/− mouse cerebellar sections (Figures 4A and 4B). The resulting removal of autofluorescence leads to the expected outcome (Figures 4A and 4B). Altogether, these results highlight the importance of implementing photobleaching into a standard immunohistochemistry protocol.

Limitations

We have only validated this protocol in an OCT-embedded free-floating immunostaining protocol. It is important to note that there may be multiple sources of autofluorescence in the brain, which includes but is not limited to lipofuscin. Therefore, photobleaching should only be used under specific biological contexts. We suggest implementing photobleaching when (1) detecting microglial markers, such as CLEC7A, that overlap with autofluorescence signals which may confound findings, as well as (2) validating antibody specificity through immunohistochemistry using knockout mice.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Samples dry out during photobleaching.

Potential solution

Ensure that the plate is completely sealed with parafilm to prevent evaporation (Step 18). Additionally, photobleaching can be performed in a 4°C room, which omits the requirement of fans that function to prevent the solution from getting too hot.

Problem 2

Autofluorescence remains after photobleaching.

Potential solution

Photobleaching for a longer time and ensure the plate is approximately 5–10 cm below the light (Step 19). Ensure the plate you are using is completely transparent to allow the light to pass through.
