Input-Output Data-Driven Sensor Selection for Cyber-Physical Systems footnoteinfo
Filippos Fotiadis, Kyriakos G. Vamvoudakis

Abstract
In this paper, we consider the problem of input-output data-driven sensor selection for unknown cyber-physical systems (CPS). In particular, out of a large set of sensors available for use, we choose a subset of them that maximizes a metric of observability of the CPS. The considered observability metric is related to the ℋ 2 \mathcal{H}_{2} system norm, which quantifies the average output energy of the selected sensors over a finite or an infinite horizon. However, its computation inherently requires knowledge of the unknown matrices of the system, so we draw connections from the reinforcement learning literature and design an input-output data-driven algorithm to compute it in a model-free manner. We then use the derived data-driven metric expression to choose the best sensors of the system in polynomial time, effectively obtaining a provably convergent model-free sensor selection process. Additionally, we show how the proposed data-driven approach can be exploited to select sensors that optimize volumetric measures of observability, while also noting its applicability to the dual problem of actuator selection. Simulations are performed to demonstrate the validity and effectiveness of the proposed approach.



keywords

Sensor selection, input-output data, cyber-physical systems, unknown systems.

Introduction

Cyber-physical systems (CPS) are heterogeneous systems that integrate analog and digital components, along with communication channels through which these exchange data. Among the most critical components of a CPS are its sensors and actuators, which must be carefully selected to ensure sufficient observability and controllability of the system
[
1
,
2
]
. This problem of properly choosing the CPS’ sensors (or actuators) is known as the sensor (or actuator) selection problem
[
3
,
4
]
.

The integration of heterogeneous components makes CPS widely applicable, but also challenging to control and prone to faults that can make prior models obsolete. This has led to growing interest in data-driven control strategies that rely solely on measured data, bypassing explicit models
[
5
]
. However, while data-driven control has received considerable attention, sensor and actuator selection in this setting remains largely unexplored. Most learning-based methods in this domain are heuristic, lack suitable theoretical guarantees, and are based mainly on intuition and engineering
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
. In addition, while learning-based methods for actuator selection were recently presented in
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
, those rely on the assumption of full-state feedback and do not tackle the problem of sensor selection. This gap in the literature motivates the present work: we present the first input-output data-driven sensor selection procedure for CPS that is agnostic to system models and which comes with theoretical guarantees of convergence.

Related Work:
In most cases, sensor selection concerns choosing sensors that maximize a metric of observability
[
13
]
. For example,
[
4
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
developed algorithms for the selection of sensors that optimize
\mathcal{H}_{2}
norm-related metrics of the system. The main idea was that efficient sensors should generally yield stronger signals, and this strength is captured by the
\mathcal{H}_{2}
norm. Other studies for observability-based sensor selection also regard the optimization of Gramian-related metrics
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
. However, non-observability-based sensor selection was also proposed in
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
for linear quadratic regulation, and in
[
24
,
25
]
for security.

A limitation of the above results is that they are model-based, requiring complete knowledge of the system dynamics to perform the sensor selection procedure. This can often be a restrictive requirement for CPS, which are complex by definition and thus often contain unmodeled dynamics. On the other hand, existing learning-based methods mainly concern the problem of actuator selection and assume full-state feedback
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
, are heuristic or concern static system models
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
, or use learning for purposes unrelated to having unknown system dynamics
[
26
,
27
,
28
]
. These limitations create motivation for the development of model-free sensor selection procedures with theoretical guarantees of convergence.

RL and adaptive control are frequently employed to tackle unknown dynamics in control
[
5
]
. For example,
[
29
]
surveys learning-based adaptive control procedures for the optimal control of unknown systems using input-state data, and a framework solely using input-output data was presented in
[
30
]
. The main advantage of these studies is that they are not based on heuristic arguments, however, their main aim is to design
controllers
. On the other hand, the main goal of the present work is to design data-driven algorithms for
selection

Contributions
: Inspired by the aforementioned RL-based studies for optimal control, we design the first model-free sensor selection algorithm with theoretical guarantees of convergence. More specifically, we consider the problem of choosing sensors that maximize observability metrics related to the
\mathcal{H}_{2}
norm of the system, which requires solving a set of Lyapunov equations. However, these Lyapunov equations are model-based, so we re-express them solely with respect to input-output data gathered from the system and use the resulting expression to perform guaranteed model-free sensor selection. The proposed process is also applicable to the problem of actuator selection, as well as to the problem of optimizing volumetric observability measures.

A preliminary version appeared in
[
31
]
. This paper extends
[
31
]
by providing full technical proofs, relaxing assumptions on system knowledge and the required rank of the input-output data, addressing finite-horizon and volumetric observability metrics, and discussing observability verification.

Problem Formulation

2.1
System and Preliminaries

Consider, for
t\in\mathbb{N}
, a discrete-time system of the form:

\begin{split}x(t+1)&=Ax(t)+Bu(t),\penalty\ x(0)=x_{0},\\
y(t)&=Cx(t),\end{split}

(1)

where
x(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the state with initial value
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
u(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the control input,
y(t)
is the output, and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
,
C\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
are the system matrices.
Each row of
C
represents a sensor, whereas each column of
B
represents an actuator. In particular,

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}c_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}&c_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}&\ldots&c_{p}^{\mathrm{T}}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}},\penalty\ B=\begin{bmatrix}b_{1}&b_{2}&\ldots&b_{m}\end{bmatrix},

where
c_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}
,
j=1,\ldots,p
represents a sensor, whereas
b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
i=1,\ldots,m
, represents an actuator.

In the sensor selection problem, we assume that we want to select only
p^{\prime}<p
out of the
p
sensors available for use at each time instant. That is, we seek a selection matrix

\mathbb{S}_{\gamma}=\begin{bmatrix}e_{\gamma_{1}}&e_{\gamma_{2}}&\ldots&e_{\gamma_{p^{\prime}}}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}},

(2)

with
\gamma=\{\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{p^{\prime}}\}\subset\{1,\ldots p\}
, that will choose certain rows of
C
, so that the new sensing matrix is

\tilde{C}=\mathbb{S}_{\gamma}C.

Accordingly, in the actuator selection problem, we want to select only
m^{\prime}<m
out of the
m
actuators available at each time instant through the selection matrix

\mathbb{S}_{\beta}=\begin{bmatrix}e_{\beta_{1}}&e_{\beta_{2}}&\ldots&e_{\beta_{m^{\prime}}}\end{bmatrix},

where
\beta=\{\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m^{\prime}}\}\subset\{1,\ldots m\}
. The new actuating matrix will then be

\tilde{B}=B\mathbb{S}_{\beta}.

Various studies have been carried out in the literature to solve sensor or actuator selection problems with performance guarantees
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
.
In this work, however, we consider these problems under a setup in which

1.

All system matrices
A,B,C
are unknown.

2.

Only the output provided by the selected sensors at the instant
t\in\mathbb{N}
is available for measurement, i.e.,
y^{\prime}(t)=\mathbb{S}_{\gamma}Cx(t)
.

As a minimum requirement, we still require the following observability Assumption, which implies we know a set of sensors that renders the system observable.

Assumption 1

There exists a known selection index
\hat{\gamma}=\{\hat{\gamma}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\gamma}_{r}\}\subset\{1,\ldots,p\}
,
r<p^{\prime}
, such that
(A,\penalty\ \mathbb{S}_{\hat{\gamma}}C)
is observable.

\Box

Remark 1

Assumption
1
implies we have some empirical knowledge about the system, particularly regarding its observability. This is often the case, for example, in mechanical systems, where it is well-known that measuring positions or angles makes the closed-loop observable.

Remark 2

If one has sufficient resources to concurrently utilize all sensors during the data gathering process, then
\hat{\gamma}
in Assumption
1
shall be unknown.

\Box

2.2
Observability and Controllability Indices

The next step is to define the cost function that will distinguish which set of sensors or actuators is the best to select, and metrics of observability and controllability become relevant here. Nevertheless, since all system matrices
A,B,C
are unknown, there is no unique state-space representation for the input-output behavior of (
1
). This limitation restricts us to metrics that apply to the transfer function
G
of the tuple
(A,B,C)
, instead of directly to the tuple
(A,B,C)
.

2.2.1
Infinite-Horizon Indices

One metric that quantifies controllability and observability is the
\mathcal{H}_{2}
norm of the transfer function
G

[
4
]
. This is defined as the infinite-horizon average output energy
\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\left\lVert y(t)\right\rVert^{2}]

under the condition that
x_{0}=0
and
u(0)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{m})
. The motivation behind choosing this norm as the cost function for the sensor selection problem is that more observable sensors will generally yield larger output signals and, similarly, more controllable actuators will have a larger effect on the measured state under the same actuation signal
[
4
]
. However, a limitation is that it applies only to asymptotically stable systems, owing to the infinite summation being divergent if
A
is unstable. To circumvent this issue, following
[
32
]
one can discount the output measurements in the
\mathcal{H}_{2}
norm with a parameter
a\in(0,1)
, and instead define a discounted
\mathcal{H}_{2}
norm as:

\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{2,a}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}a^{2(t-1)}\left\lVert y(t)\right\rVert^{2}\right].

(3)

If
a<\frac{1}{\rho(A)}
, similar to
[
32
]
, it is straightforward to verify that (
3
) is well-defined. In particular, it follows that
\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{2,a}^{2}=\|\tilde{G}\|^{2}_{2}
where
\tilde{G}
is the transfer function of the tuple
(aA,B,C)
. In addition, it follows from standard linear systems theory
[
33
]
that (
3
) is equivalent to

\textrm{tr}(CW_{c}C^{\mathrm{T}}),\penalty\ \textrm{tr}(B^{\mathrm{T}}W_{o}B)

(4)

where
W_{o},W_{c}
are observability and controllability Gramians for the pairs
(aA,C)
and
(aA,B)
.

2.2.2
Finite-Horizon Indices

Infinite-horizon metrics like (
3
) have the advantage that they can be computed with relatively low computations by solving time-independent ALEs. However, such metrics may not be applicable in cases where the system is deployed only for a fixed amount of time, or when a sufficiently small discount factor
a
is not a priori known. In these setups, one may instead consider the finite-horizon counterpart of the average output energy (
3
):

\left\lVert G_{T}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left\lVert y(t)\right\rVert^{2}\right],\penalty\ T\in\mathbb{N},

(5)

which, following standard systems theory, boils down to either of the following two equivalent expressions:

\textrm{tr}(CW_{c}(T)C^{\mathrm{T}}),\penalty\ \textrm{tr}(B^{\mathrm{T}}W_{o}(T)B).

(6)

Unlike (
4
), the matrices
W_{o}(T),W_{c}(T)
are the
finite-horizon
observability and controllability Gramians of
(A,C)
and
(A,B)
, whose existence is not conditional on knowing a sufficiently small discount factor
a
.

2.3
Data-Driven Sensor and Actuator Selection

In the rest of this paper, we focus on finding the sensors (or actuators) that maximize
(
4
) or (
6
). However, in doing so we will rely solely on input-output data, and not on the unknown system matrices
A,B,C
. In particular, for the sensor selection problem and depending on the metric of interest, we want to find the optimal selection matrix
\mathbb{S}_{\gamma^{\star}}\subseteq S_{p,p^{\prime}}
, where
S_{p,p^{\prime}}
is the set of selection matrices
\mathbb{S}_{\gamma}
of the form
(
2
) in which
\gamma=\{\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{p^{\prime}}\}\subset\{1,\ldots p\}
, that optimizes either one of the following two measures (as defined previously).

1.

Infinite-horizon average output energy:

\mathbb{S}_{\gamma^{\star}}=\argmax_{\mathbb{S}_{\gamma}\subseteq S_{p,p^{\prime}}}\textrm{tr}(\mathbb{S}_{\gamma}CW_{c}C^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbb{S}_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{T}}).

(7)

2.

Finite-horizon average output energy:

\mathbb{S}_{\gamma^{\star}}=\argmax_{\mathbb{S}_{\gamma}\subseteq S_{p,p^{\prime}}}\textrm{tr}(\mathbb{S}_{\gamma}CW_{c}(T)C^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbb{S}_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{T}}).

(8)

Remark 3

The problems of sensor and actuator selection are dual in nature. For this reason, in what follows, we focus only on the problem of sensor selection, while noting that the approach we present applies to the actuator selection problem with minor modifications.

\Box

Remark 4

The
\mathcal{H}_{2}
norm is a desirable metric for sensor selection as it reflects the trace of a weighted observability Gramian
[
16
,
4
]
. For actuator selection, maximizing the
\mathcal{H}_{2}
norm also improves controllability as it captures the trace of a weighted controllability Gramian. However, in the presence of input disturbances, additional considerations may be needed to mitigate their influence.

\Box

Remark 5

Instead of maximizing the
\mathcal{H}_{2}
norm, one can select sensors by optimizing volumetric observability measures
[
34
,
4
]
. We discuss this in Section
4.3
.

\Box

3
Data-Driven Estimation of the Cost Functions for Fixed Sensor Sets

The first step towards solving the sensor selection problem with unknown system matrices
A,B,C
, is being able to express the cost functions in (
7
)-(
8
) using input-output data only. In this section, we perform this task for a fixed choice of the selection matrix
\mathbb{S}_{\gamma}
, and we will subsequently use this in the next section to perform data-driven sensor selection.

Before proceeding, note that this task requires i) being able to take measurements from the sensors involved in the matrix
\tilde{C}=\mathbb{S}_{\gamma}C
(since
C
is unknown), whose cost function we want to evaluate; and ii) concurrently being able to take measurements from the sensors involved in
\hat{C}=\mathbb{S}_{\hat{\gamma}}C
, as a minimum observability requirement. This reduces the output measurements we can access to:

\begin{split}x(t+1)&=Ax(t)+Bu(t),\penalty\ x(0)=x_{0},\\
\hat{y}(t)&=\hat{C}x(t),\penalty\ \tilde{y}(t)=\tilde{C}x(t).\end{split}

(9)

3.1
Expressing the State with Input-Output Data

Apart from the matrices
A,B,C
being unknown, a major restriction of the present setup is not having access to full-state feedback, rather, only to the measured outputs available at each time. Nevertheless, directly following
[
30
]
the state

can be reconstructed using a history stack of past outputs and control inputs.

Lemma 1

[

Let Assumption
1
hold, so that

is observable. Define the matrices

\displaystyle U_{N}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}B&AB&A^{2}B&\ldots&A^{N-1}B\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle V_{N}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}(\hat{C}A^{N-1})^{\mathrm{T}}&\ldots&(\hat{C}A)^{\mathrm{T}}&\hat{C}^{\mathrm{T}}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}},

\displaystyle T_{N}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&\hat{C}B&\hat{C}AB&\ldots&\hat{C}A^{N-2}B\\
0&0&\hat{C}B&\ldots&\hat{C}A^{N-3}B\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\ldots&\ &0&\hat{C}B\\
0&0&0&0&0\end{bmatrix}.

If
N\geq K
, where

is the observability index
1
1

1

The observability index is upper-bounded as
K\leq n
.
, then

x(t)=Mz(t),\penalty\ t\geq N,

where
z(t)=[u(t-1:t-N)^{\mathrm{T}}\penalty\ \hat{y}(t-1:t-N)^{\mathrm{T}}]^{\mathrm{T}},

and
M=[M_{u}\penalty\ M_{y}]
,

M_{y}=A^{N}V_{N}^{\dagger}
,
M_{u}=U_{N}-M_{y}T_{N}
.

A clear limitation of Lemma

is that it relies on prior knowledge of the system matrices

3.2
Data-Driven Estimation of Infinite-Horizon Average Output Energy

Denote
J_{\infty}(\mathbb{S}_{\gamma})=\textrm{tr}(\mathbb{S}_{\gamma}CW_{c}C^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbb{S}_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{T}})
as the infinite-horizon average output energy (
7

\mathbb{S_{\gamma}}
using input-output data. The following Lemma re-expresses this cost function with respect to a dual ALE, which will subsequently facilitate the task of its data-driven estimation.

Lemma 2

It holds that

J_{\infty}(\mathbb{S}_{\gamma})=\mathrm{tr}(B^{\mathrm{T}}W_{o}^{\gamma}B),

(10)

where
W_{o}^{\gamma}
uniquely solves the ALE

a^{2}A^{\mathrm{T}}W_{o}^{\gamma}A-W_{o}^{\gamma}+C^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbb{S}_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbb{S}_{\gamma}C=0.

(11)

Proof 3.1
.

The proof follows similar lines to
[
33
]
.

Exploiting Lemma
2

Lemma 3.2

Consider system (

where

{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}(t)\mathrm{vech}(\bar{W}_{o}^{\gamma})+\left\lVert\tilde{y}(t)\right\rVert^{2}=0,

\begin{split}\Phi(t)=&H\Big(a^{2}((z(t+1)-E_{1}u(t))\\
&\otimes(z(t+1)-E_{1}u(t)))-z(t)\otimes z(t)\Big)\Big),\end{split}

(14)

where

was defined in Lemma
1
.

Proof 3.3
.

Multiplying both sides of equation (

a^{2}x^{\mathrm{T}}(t)A^{\mathrm{T}}W_{o}^{\gamma}Ax(t)-x^{\mathrm{T}}(t)W_{o}^{\gamma}x(t)+\left\lVert\tilde{y}(t)\right\rVert^{2}=0.

Moreover, from (

Corollary 3.6

It is important to note that Corollary

is the number of past output data,

The aforementioned detail can be problematic in practice, particularly when the fraction

4.2

The most expensive operation in Algorithm

in the infinite-horizon case, and

in the finite-horizon one, whose complexity is

5
Data-Driven Verification of Observability

A known issue with greedy selection is that it can lead to a resulting set of sensors that is not observable
[
35
]
. In that regard, modifications can be incorporated into the greedy algorithm to enforce it to converge to a fully observable set, particularly by encouraging it to increase the rank of an observability test at each iteration
[
17
]
. However, performing such an observability test becomes unclear when the system matrices are unknown and when access to only partial state measurements from the system is available.

Building on the results of the previous sections, in what follows, we provide a data-driven condition for verifying whether a given set of sensors
\tilde{C}
is observable.

Theorem 5.23
.

Let
t_{0},t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\geq Nm+n
, and consider the data matrices

Z=[z(t_{0})\penalty\ z(t_{1})\penalty\ \ldots\penalty\ z(t_{k})],\tilde{Z}=[\tilde{z}(t_{0})\penalty\ \tilde{z}(t_{1})\penalty\ \ldots\penalty\ \tilde{z}(t_{k})]
,
where
z(t)
is defined in Lemma
1
and
\tilde{z}(t)=[u(t-1:t-N)^{\mathrm{T}}\penalty\ \tilde{y}(t-1:t-N)^{\mathrm{T}}]^{\mathrm{T}}
.

1.

If
\mathrm{rank}(\tilde{Z})=Nm+n
then
(A,\tilde{C})
is observable.

2.

Let
\mathrm{rank}(Z)=Nm+n
. Then,
(A,\tilde{C})
is observable if and only if
\mathrm{rank}(\tilde{Z})=Nm+n
.

Proof 5.24
.

To prove the first item, from (
1
), the outputs from
t-1
to
t-N
can be written as
\hat{y}(t-1:t-N)=\tilde{V}_{N}x(t-N)+\tilde{T}_{N}u(t-1:t-N),

where
\tilde{V}_{N},\penalty\ \tilde{T}_{N}
are as in Lemma
1
but with
\hat{C}
substituted by
\tilde{C}
. Then, defining

\displaystyle\bar{z}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}u(t-1:t-N)\\
x(t-N)\end{bmatrix},\penalty\ \tilde{\Lambda}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{Nm}&0\\
\tilde{T}_{N}&\tilde{V}_{N}\end{bmatrix},\hskip-5.69054pt

and
\bar{Z}=[\bar{z}(t_{0})\penalty\ \ldots\penalty\ \bar{z}(t_{k})]
, it follows that
\tilde{Z}=\tilde{\Lambda}\bar{Z}
. Hence, since
\mathrm{rank}(\tilde{Z})=Nm+n
, we obtain

\mathrm{rank}(\tilde{\Lambda}\bar{Z})=Nm+n.

(49)

At the same time,
\bar{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{(Nm+n)\times k}
, and thus
\textrm{rank}(\bar{Z})\leq Nm+n
. Given this, (
49
) can hold only if

\mathrm{rank}(\tilde{\Lambda})\geq Nm+n.

(50)

Next, note that the first
Nm
columns of
\tilde{\Lambda}
are linearly independent of the last

columns. Therefore,

Nm+\textrm{rank}(\tilde{V}_{N})\geq\mathrm{rank}(\tilde{\Lambda}).

(51)

Combining (

\textrm{rank}(\tilde{V}_{N})\geq n

\textrm{rank}(\tilde{V}_{N})=n

(A,\penalty\ \tilde{C})
is observable.

To prove the second item, recall from the proof of Theorem
3.7
that
z(t)=\Lambda\bar{z}(t)
.
Hence, we have
Z=\Lambda\bar{Z}
, and since
\textrm{rank}(Z)=Nm+n

\textrm{rank}(\bar{Z})=Nm+n
. We now show necessity and sufficiency.

•

Suppose that
(A,\tilde{C})
is observable. Then,
\textrm{rank}(\tilde{\Lambda})=Nm+n

\textrm{rank}(\tilde{Z})=Nm+n

•

Suppose that

(A,\tilde{C})

.

In the presence of noise, it should be checked if the minimum singular value of

\Box

Using Theorem
5.23
, there are several ways in which observability of the selected set of sensors could be enforced in the previous section. One approach is to add heuristics to the greedy algorithm that promote full-rank observability, as in
[
17

. Another is to relax the cardinality constraint in the greedy selection algorithm, selecting additional sensors until observability of the resulting set is verified using Theorem

7
Conclusion

We studied input-output data-driven sensor selection for CPS with unknown system models. We developed a model-free algorithm to estimate metrics of observability and select sensors using input-output data.

Future work includes extending our framework to online settings, following
[
38
]
. Another open problem is that of polynomial-time data-driven sensor selection with strict observability guarantees. While details on enforcing observability were provided in Section
5
, they relied on relaxing the scope of the problem.
