A three-dimensional digital study on clinical anatomy of the acetabulum in elderly population aged 65–84 years in inner Mongolia, China
Authors: Wang Zhenyu, Hu Yihan, Gao Sheng, Liu Xingquan, Wang Haiyan, Wang Zhiqiang, Lu Jiaqi, Zhao Zhixuan, Wu Jiayu, Liu Chunhao, Jia Yao, Wang Zhi, Jia Leilei, Li Yumo, Li Xiaohe, Zhang Kai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the three-dimensional anatomical characteristics of the acetabulum in normal elderly people aged 65–84 years in Inner Mongolia, China, and provide data support for the precise design of acetabular prostheses and individualized treatment in total hip arthroplasty (THA).MethodsA total of 240 eligible elderly patients (480 hip joints in total) were selected and divided into 4 groups at 5-year intervals within the 65–84 years age range. CT scans were performed to obtain acetabular imaging data, and Mimics 21.0 software was used to reconstruct the three-dimensional model of the pelvis-acetabulum. Five acetabular parameters (superoinferior diameter, anteroposterior diameter, abduction angle, anteversion angle, depth) were measured, and statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 27.0 software.ResultsSignificant gender differences were observed in acetabular size parameters (superoinferior diameter, anteroposterior diameter, depth), with males showing larger values than females, while no significant gender differences were found in angular parameters (abduction angle, anteversion angle). There were no significant left-right differences in acetabular parameters in either gender. Among age-related variations, only the anteroposterior diameter in males and depth in females showed significant differences between age groups. Correlation analysis revealed weak negative correlations between age and partial parameters, and stronger linkage between acetabular parameters in males.ConclusionAcetabular anatomical parameters of normal elderly people in Inner Mongolia have significant gender differences, with limited clinical value of age-related changes in guiding prosthesis selection, and no left-right differences. During THA, it is necessary to optimize the size design of acetabular prostheses based on gender characteristics, without adjusting prosthesis types according to age, and left-right symmetric design is applicable to this population.

Introduction

With the acceleration of the global aging process, the incidence of hip joint-related diseases has shown a significant upward trend, among which hip osteoarthritis and acetabular fractures are particularly common [1, 2]. Total Hip Arthroplasty (THA), as an effective treatment for the aforementioned diseases, is increasingly widely used in clinical practice [3]. In THA surgery, the accurate implantation of the acetabular prosthesis is crucial to the success of the operation, and the position, orientation, and stability of the prosthesis are directly related to the patient’s postoperative functional recovery and the service life of the prosthesis [4]. Notably, healthy older adults aged 65–84 years retain considerable potential to improve muscle strength and functional performance through targeted interventions [5]. The exertion of muscle function during daily activities (e.g., walking, squatting) in older adults is highly dependent on the geometric stability provided by the acetabular implant—stable prosthesis positioning ensures effective force transmission between the hip joint and surrounding muscles, avoiding impaired functional recovery due to abnormal stress distribution. However, the anatomical structure of the acetabulum in the elderly population often changes due to age-related degeneration; in addition, osteoporosis, as an independent pathological condition, can further alter the anatomical morphology of the acetabulum. This undoubtedly increases the difficulty in judging the suitability of universal THA prostheses for elderly patients and the complexity of intraoperative operations [6, 7]. Although traditional two-dimensional imaging techniques (such as X-ray plain films) are often used for preliminary evaluation, they are limited by their dimensional characteristics and cannot fully capture the three-dimensional anatomical details of the acetabulum, which may lead to inaccurate prosthesis implantation and further cause complications such as postoperative loosening, dislocation, or early revision [8].

In THA surgeries for elderly patients, the optimized design and accurate implantation of acetabular prostheses are inseparable from precise anatomical data support. The quantitative parameters of acetabular morphology not only affect the initial stability of the prosthesis but also are closely related to the biomechanical stability of the pelvis and lower limbs [9]. Notably, orthopedic biomechanics research focuses on three core goals: enhancing functional performance, mitigating injury risks, and optimizing rehabilitation outcomes [10]. In the field of THA, the fundamental basis for achieving these goals lies in the precise anatomical characterization of the acetabulum. Recent advances in biomechanics indicate that anatomical studies driven by three-dimensional digital technology serve as a critical foundation for the application of advanced computational methods (such as artificial intelligence, AI) in biomechanical analysis [11]. This is because they can provide high-fidelity morphological data, which are essential for training and validating AI models in clinical scenarios. Therefore, the development and application of three-dimensional digital technology are of great significance for overcoming the limitations of two-dimensional evaluation, laying a data foundation for advanced computational biomechanics research, and designing total hip arthroplasty (THA) prostheses suitable for the elderly population. However, most existing studies on Chinese elderly populations focus on morphological characteristics, lack anatomical analyses across consecutive age groups, and there remains a relative scarcity of three-dimensional anatomical morphological analyses of the acetabulum in elderly individuals aged 65–84 years in Inner Mongolia, China. Thus, based on CT scanning and three-dimensional reconstruction technology, this study systematically analyzed CT data of elderly individuals aged 65–84 years in Inner Mongolia, China, and explored the variation patterns of clinical anatomical parameters of the acetabulum across different genders, sides, and age groups. This research outcome not only provides an important basis for optimizing the design of hip prostheses, addressing the compatibility issues of universally used Western-designed prostheses in Chinese elderly populations due to anatomical differences, and predicting disease risks, but also lays a foundation for formulating individualized treatment plans for the elderly population in this region.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

From January 2021 to February 2024, 540 patients who underwent lower extremity angiography were randomly selected as potential subjects from the Second Hospital of Ulanqab City, and their CT image data were subsequently subjected to preliminary imaging screening and rigorous review. Inclusion criteria: Aged 65–84 years; confirmed by CT imaging and clinical assessment to have no acetabular pathological changes, normal development, and intact anatomical structure of the hip joints and pelvis; no peripheral vascular diseases (e.g., arterial occlusive disease, thromboangiitis obliterans) or vascular-related bone metabolic abnormalities (e.g., avascular necrosis of bone). Exclusion criteria: Confirmed hip/pelvic diseases (including hip osteoarthritis); previous implantation of hip or proximal femoral prostheses; hip/pelvic dysplasia, deformities, or other anatomical abnormalities; Presence of osteoporosis and other metabolic bone diseases; poor-quality CT images (e.g., unclear details, missing key information) that failed to meet the requirements for research analysis. After strict screening, a total of 240 eligible patients were finally included, stratified by age (at 5-year intervals), gender, and side (left/right), with 30 males and 30 females in each group. The sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1 software, with the following parameters set: effect size f = 0.25, type I error probability α = 0.05, power (1-β) = 0.80, and number of age groups = 4. The minimum required sample size was 180 cases, and the 240 samples included in this study met the statistical requirements. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Ulanqab (Approval No.: YKD202002007).

Data collection

Patients were placed in a supine position for lower extremity angiography, and a 256-slice spiral CT was used for scanning. The scanning parameters were set as follows: slice thickness 0.6–1.25 mm, voltage 120 kV, current 50 mA. After scanning, the images were saved in DICOM format.

Three-dimensional model reconstruction and parameter measurement

DICOM-format images were imported into Mimics 21.0 software (Materialise Company, Leuven University, Belgium). The image processing function of the software was used to construct a three-dimensional model of the pelvis-acetabulum. The position was adjusted so that the upper edge of the pubic symphysis and the anterior superior iliac spines on both sides were aligned on the same straight line MN, thereby defining the anterior pelvic plane.Acetabular superoinferior diameter (Fig. 1-a: A1B1, A2B2): In the three-dimensional model, a straight line MN parallel to the anterior pelvic plane was drawn from the highest point of the acetabular superolateral edge to the acetabular inferomedial edge; the distance between the two endpoints of this line was the acetabular superoinferior diameter.Acetabular anteroposterior diameter (Fig. 1-a: C1D1, C2D2): On a new plane perpendicular to the anterior pelvic plane, the maximum distance between the intersection points of the anterior and posterior edges of the acetabulum was measured.Based on the anterior pelvic plane, the following parameters were measured using multiplanar reconstruction (MPR): Acetabular abduction angle (Figure.1-b: ∠F1, ∠F2): At the level of the posterior edge of the acetabular notch in the coronal plane of the reconstructed image, the angle between the line connecting the lowest points of the acetabular edges on both sides and the line connecting the outer edges of one acetabular cup was measured.Acetabular anteversion angle (Fig. 1-c: ∠H1, ∠H2): At the level of the acetabular center in the transverse plane of the reconstructed image, a straight line perpendicular to the line connecting the centers of the femoral heads on both sides was drawn; the angle between the line connecting the edges of one acetabulum and this straight line was measured.Acetabular depth (Fig. 1-d: I1J1, I2J2): At the level of the acetabular center in the transverse plane of the reconstructed image, the distance between the deepest point of the acetabular cup and the line connecting the outer edges of the acetabulum was measured [12]. The “outer edge” refers to the native cortical bone margin (excluding marginal osteophytes, commonly seen in the elderly) to avoid overestimation, with osteophytes identified and excluded by two experienced physicians via CT MPR images.

Subsequently, anatomical landmarks required for parameter measurement were independently identified and marked by two physicians with more than 5 years of experience in orthopedic image analysis; in cases of discrepancies in landmark positioning, consensus was reached through joint film review and consultation, ensuring the accuracy and reliability of landmark localization in subsequent parameter measurements.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0 software. Data conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̅±s) and 95% confidence intervals (95%CI), and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 10.0 software. An independent samples t-test was used to analyze gender differences, a paired samples t-test to compare left-right differences of acetabular parameters, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare differences among age groups—with Levene test first verifying variance homogeneity. For homogeneous variances (p > 0.05), LSD post-hoc multiple comparisons were applied; for heterogeneous variances (p ≤ 0.05), Welch test correction was used. Bonferroni correction was exclusively applied to the above multiple comparison analyses to control for Type I error accumulation caused by repeated tests, with a corrected statistical significance threshold of p ≤ 0.0009. The Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) was used to evaluate inter-rater and intra-rater reliability. For correlation analysis, Shapiro-Wilk test verified data normality: since age showed a skewed distribution, Spearman rank correlation test was used to analyze the association between age and acetabular parameters; all acetabular parameters conformed to a normal distribution (Shapiro-Wilk test, p > 0.05), so Pearson correlation test was applied to analyze the linear associations between acetabular parameters. A p-value ≤ 0.05 was adopted as the statistical significance threshold to avoid Type II errors.

Reliability verification results of measurement

The reliability verification of the 3D acetabular parameter measurement method in this study showed that the inter-rater and intra-rater reliability of all parameters reached an excellent level (ICC 0.9). Among them, the ICC values for inter-rater reliability ranged from 0.945 to 0.979, and the ICC values for intra-rater reliability ranged from 0.985 to 0.995. Comparison of acetabular parameters between different gender groups

Independent samples t-test results (Table 3, Figure3) indicated that in the 65–84 years old population, the acetabular superoinferior diameter (male: 57.89 ± 2.68 mm vs. female: 51.96 ± 2.64 mm), anteroposterior diameter (male: 52.30 ± 2.18 mm vs. female: 47.06 ± 2.05 mm), and depth (male: 27.93 ± 3.12 mm vs. female: 25.82 ± 2.77 mm) of males were significantly larger than those of females, with statistically significant differences (t = 17.238, 19.194, 5.552, all p < 0.0009); no significant gender differences were observed in the acetabular abduction angle (male: 53.47 ± 4.04° vs. female: 53.64 ± 3.93°) or anteversion angle (male: 18.88 ± 5.18° vs. female: 20.62 ± 6.45°) (t=-0.323, -2.309, all p>0.0009).

Comparison of acetabular parameters between different age and gender groups

One-way ANOVA results (Tables 6 and 7) showed that for males aged 65–84 years, only the anteroposterior diameter exhibited significant age-related differences (F = 5.391, corrected p = 0.0008 ≤ 0.0009), with the 70–74 years old group having a significantly larger value than the 80–84 years old group (53.15 ± 1.84 mm vs. 51.09 ± 1.91 mm, p = 0.0007). No significant inter-group differences were observed in the superoinferior diameter, abduction angle, anteversion angle, or depth (corrected p > 0.0009). For females in the same age range, only the depth showed significant age-related differences (Welch F = 5.646, corrected p = 0.0006 ≤ 0.0009), with the 65–69 years old group having a significantly larger value than the 75–79 years old group (27.13 ± 2.99 mm vs. 24.55 ± 1.90 mm, p = 0.0003). Notably, the depth did not follow a linear degenerative trend with age, reaching a minimum in the 75–79 years group before a slight rebound, indicating a potential cohort effect. The other four acetabular parameters (superoinferior diameter, anteroposterior diameter, abduction angle, anteversion angle) showed no significant inter-group differences (corrected p > 0.0009).

Discussion

This study systematically analyzed the acetabular anatomical parameters of elderly individuals aged 65–84 years in Inner Mongolia, China, with the core findings as follows:

There were significant gender differences in acetabular size parameters (superoinferior diameter, anteroposterior diameter, and depth). The values in males (57.89 ± 2.68 mm, 52.30 ± 2.18 mm, 27.93 ± 3.12 mm) were significantly larger than those in females (51.96 ± 2.64 mm, 47.06 ± 2.05 mm, 25.82 ± 2.77 mm; all p < 0.0009), which may be related to gender-specific differences in pelvic structure (e.g., females require a wider pelvic inlet to accommodate childbirth). No gender differences were observed in angular parameters (abduction angle and anteversion angle). For acetabular prosthesis design: male patients need appropriate enlargement of the coverage area of large-sized prostheses; female patients require optimized depth matching of small-sized prostheses to avoid insufficient stability; there is no need for gender-specific design of angular parameters.

Gender-specific correlation patterns existed among acetabular parameters (all p-values of significant correlations were < 0.05). The correlations among parameters were stronger in females: the superoinferior diameter was strongly positively correlated with the anteroposterior diameter (r = 0.739, p < 0.001) and moderately positively correlated with depth (r = 0.366, p < 0.001); the anteroposterior diameter was moderately positively correlated with depth (r = 0.346, p < 0.001), and the abduction angle was moderately positively correlated with the anteversion angle (r = 0.332, p < 0.001). This synergistic correlation suggests that the proportional coordination among various parameters should be maintained when designing small-sized prostheses for females. The correlations among parameters in males were mainly weak to moderate: the superoinferior diameter was strongly positively correlated with the anteroposterior diameter (r = 0.690, p < 0.001), and the anteroposterior diameter was moderately positively correlated with depth (r = 0.359, p < 0.001); the abduction angle was weakly negatively correlated with the anteroposterior diameter (r=-0.291, p = 0.001) and depth (r=-0.208, p = 0.022), while the superoinferior diameter was weakly positively correlated with depth (r = 0.203, p = 0.026) and the anteversion angle was weakly positively correlated with the abduction angle (r = 0.214, p = 0.019). These weak correlations (|r|<0.3) had limited clinical value, indicating that male acetabular parameters are relatively independent, facilitating individualized optimization of prostheses.

Correlation analysis between age and parameters showed that in females, age was significantly weakly negatively correlated with superoinferior diameter (r=-0.239, p = 0.009), abduction angle (r=-0.216, p = 0.018), and depth (r=-0.284, p = 0.002); in males, age was only significantly weakly negatively correlated with anteroposterior diameter (r=-0.219, p = 0.016). The absolute values of all correlation coefficients related to age were < 0.3, which lacked clinical value for guiding prosthesis selection, suggesting that the acetabular morphology of this age group remains stable. Both male and female patients can select standard-sized prostheses according to gender characteristics without adjustment based on age.

Overall, these gender-specific acetabular morphological characteristics, combined with the confirmed bilateral symmetry of acetabular parameters and age-related stability, are highly consistent with the Joint-by-Joint Training Approach (JBJTA) [13]. As emphasized by Dhahbi et al., the core of the JBJTA framework lies in the interrelatedness of the kinetic chain, where functional optimization of upstream joints (e.g., hip joint) can directly reduce the compensatory load of downstream joints (e.g., knee joint). This is highly relevant to the results of this study: female acetabular size and angular parameters showed stronger coordination (e.g., acetabular anteroposterior diameter and depth, acetabular abduction angle and anteversion angle), which may be an evolutionary kinetic chain adaptation mechanism for maintaining lower limb biomechanical balance. In contrast, the relative independence of male acetabular parameters suggests a more flexible regulatory mode of the kinetic chain. This gender-specific adaptation mode further verifies the core principle of JBJTA—that “joint functions are interrelated through the kinetic chain”—and provides a theoretical basis for gender-targeted acetabular prosthesis design. Similar to the application of kinetic chain modeling in sports performance analysis [14], the application of musculoskeletal (MSK) modeling in total hip arthroplasty (THA) can predict postoperative biomechanical outcomes by integrating anatomical data and functional dynamics, bridging the gap between kinetic chain research related to high-performance sports and elderly hip anatomical research. Acetabular morphology exhibits significant individual differences, which are jointly regulated by racial, gender, and geographical factors. This is consistent with the gender differences in acetabular parameters of elderly individuals in Inner Mongolia observed in this study and racial differences between Chinese and European-American populations. Notably, morphological changes of the acetabular fossa, which accounts for more than 50% of the acetabular surface area, are often overlooked. As the initial site of degenerative changes, structural abnormalities in this area may directly interfere with the accurate identification of key anatomical landmarks such as the acetabular margin and acetabular notch [15]. In addition, degenerative changes can distort the 2D imaging manifestations of the acetabulum (e.g., overlapping of the anterior and posterior acetabular margins), leading to increased errors in traditional X-ray assessment. Moreover, some relevant studies on European-American populations adopted 2D X-ray measurement, which has methodological differences from the 3D CT reconstruction technology used in this study, potentially exerting a latent impact on the comparison of parameters between Chinese and foreign populations. This is the key reason why this study selected 3D CT scanning combined with Mimics reconstruction technology instead of 2D imaging for landmark localization. Through the multi-planar reconstruction function of 3D models, anatomical landmarks can be displayed more clearly, reducing identification errors caused by image overlapping [15, 16]. In addition, the accurate acetabular anatomical data obtained in this study (e.g., male acetabular superoinferior diameter: 57.89 ± 2.68 mm; moderate positive correlation between female acetabular size and depth, r = 0.346) can be further correlated with the clinical synergistic function of the lumbopelvic-hip complex. Studies have shown that abnormal hip morphology can lead to increased compensatory stress on the lumbar spine, aggravating the risk of “hip pain-lower back pain” comorbidity in elderly patients; while optimizing the design of total hip arthroplasty (THA) prostheses based on gender-specific parameters can restore the force transmission function of the hip joint, providing anatomical support for breaking this cycle [17, 18]. There were differences in acetabular anatomical parameters between elderly individuals in Inner Mongolia (the subjects of this study) and foreign populations. In terms of diameter, the overall acetabular size of Chinese population was smaller than that of European-American populations; the acetabular morphology of Korean and Japanese populations was also generally smaller than that of European-American populations, suggesting that Asian populations generally have the characteristic of smaller acetabular volume [19–21]. In terms of angular parameters, the acetabular anteversion angle of Chinese females (20.62°) was larger than that of Chinese males (18.88°); relevant studies in European and American countries showed that the acetabular anteversion angle was 18.4° in females and 14.0° in males [22, 23], both generally smaller than those in Chinese population. In addition, the acetabular abduction angle of elderly individuals in Inner Mongolia, China (53.56°) was different from the average value of European-American populations (55°) [24]. These data fully indicate that prostheses designed based on the anatomical characteristics of European-American populations or other regions in China may be incompatible with the acetabular or femoral head structure of elderly patients in Inner Mongolia, and existing prostheses may have problems such as oversized dimensions and mismatched angular parameters. In terms of parameter correlation, the stronger coordination of female acetabular parameters (e.g., size and depth) suggests that the design of female prostheses should prioritize maintaining the natural proportional relationship among parameters, while the relative independence of male parameters allows for more flexible optimization of a single parameter (e.g., fine adjustment of anteversion angle) without destroying overall stability.

In terms of size impact, the acetabular superoinferior diameter of elderly males in Inner Mongolia measured in this study was 57.89 ± 2.68 mm, and the acetabular depth of elderly females was 25.82 ± 2.77 mm. Direct application of standard European-American male prostheses (diameter range: 58–62 mm) to elderly males in Inner Mongolia may reduce the acetabular coverage rate below the clinical safety threshold of 70%, affecting the geometric compatibility between the prosthesis and the acetabulum. For female prostheses, simply reducing the diameter (e.g., from 48 mm to 45 mm) without conforming to the positive correlation between anteroposterior diameter and depth of females in Inner Mongolia (r = 0.346) will damage depth compatibility and increase the risk of postoperative dislocation. In terms of angle impact, the acetabular anteversion angle of elderly females in Inner Mongolia (20.62°) was significantly higher than that of European-American females (18.4°) [22, 23]. The adoption of European-American prostheses with an anteversion angle design of 15–18° will lead to a mismatch between the static Lewinnek safe zone of the prosthesis and the dynamic hip motion trajectory of patients. During daily activities such as bending over and squatting, the risk of impingement between the acetabular component and the femoral neck increases, which may limit the postoperative hip range of motion (e.g., inability to complete 90° hip flexion to meet daily needs), accelerate the wear of the prosthesis interface, and shorten the service life of the prosthesis. Correspondingly, this study proposed targeted prosthesis optimization suggestions: for males in this region, reduce the diameter range of prostheses by 2–3 mm on the basis of European-American designs (e.g., adjusted to 55–59 mm), while maintaining an abduction angle of 53–54° to match anatomical characteristics; for females, optimize the “diameter-depth ratio”—based on the moderately positive correlation between acetabular anteroposterior diameter and depth found in this study (r = 0.346, p < 0.001), it is suggested to retain the corresponding proportion of depth while reducing the prosthesis diameter to avoid damaging acetabular anatomical compatibility, and adjust the anteversion angle to 19–21° to achieve precise adaptation between the prosthesis and the acetabular anatomical structure of this population.

The insufficient anatomical compatibility between existing European-American prostheses and elderly patients in Inner Mongolia (the subjects of this study) is an urgent problem to be solved. The Lewinnek safe zone, as a classic reference standard for acetabular cup angle in THA, was originally intended to reduce the risk of postoperative dislocation [24]. However, modern studies have clarified its limitations: it cannot predict the stability of the prosthesis in dynamic functional positions [25] and has insufficient ability to distinguish dislocation risk—approximately 37% of dislocation cases actually fall within this safe zone [26]. More notably, this static standard is significantly mismatched with the anatomical characteristics of Chinese population [27], further indicating that traditional standards based on Western population data cannot meet the individualized treatment needs of Chinese patients. Especially combined with the gender-specific parameter correlations observed in this study, the one-size-fits-all angle standard may be more unsuitable for Chinese females with synergistic changes in angles.

For example, when subtle changes occur in the acetabular anteroposterior diameter of females, the positive correlation between anteroposterior diameter and depth (r = 0.346, p < 0.001) means that simply reducing the diameter may indirectly affect depth-related stability. In such cases, 3D printed titanium alloy customized design is recommended to match both anteroposterior diameter and depth parameters simultaneously, so as to reduce the risk of postoperative complications [28, 29]. Given the significant gender differences in acetabular superoinferior diameter, anteroposterior diameter, and depth of elderly patients in Inner Mongolia, China, attention should be paid to the risk of impingement or dislocation caused by gender-specific anatomical differences during prosthesis replacement [30–32]. For instance, when adjusting the size of female prostheses, excessive reduction of depth should be avoided to maintain the natural positive correlation between size and depth; for elderly Chinese females, the positive correlation between abduction angle and anteversion angle (r = 0.332, p < 0.001) reminds clinicians to evaluate these two angles simultaneously during surgery rather than optimizing them in isolation [33, 34].Notably, while this study provides theoretical design principles for gender-targeted acetabular prosthesis optimization, clinical validation through prospective trials is necessary before applying these recommendations. Correlation-based guidelines should not be adopted clinically without further biomechanical and outcome studies, to ensure the safety and effectiveness of clinical translation.

This study has certain limitations. First, as a retrospective analysis, the subjects were limited to patients who underwent lower extremity CT angiography. This sampling method itself has selection bias, and the sample was only confined to elderly individuals in Inner Mongolia, China, resulting in limited generalizability and geographical applicability of the study results, which may not be directly extended to elderly populations in other regions of China or other Asian countries. Future verification through multi-center and multi-ethnic collaborative studies is required. Second, due to the limitations of the scope of clinical archived data and the retrospective nature of the study, this study did not systematically include key information such as patients’ body mass index (BMI), long-term activity level, occupational history, quantitative bone mineral density (BMD) measurement results, THA surgical history, and postoperative recovery evaluation, making it impossible to conduct an in-depth analysis of the correlation between these factors and core anatomical parameters. Future prospective studies should optimize data collection protocols and specifically include these variables. In addition, there are methodological differences in the comparison of acetabular parameters between Chinese and foreign populations: this study adopted CT-based 3D reconstruction technology, while some European-American studies relied on 2D imaging, which may lead to potential deviations in the results.

Based on the acetabular anatomical data provided in this study, we will collaborate with multi-center teams to construct a cross-regional database, and jointly develop 3D printed customized prostheses based on gender-specific parameters with engineers. Future research can also be extended to the development of musculoskeletal (MSK) models specific to elderly individuals in Inner Mongolia. Similar to Tajik et al. [14] who used MSK models integrating anatomical data and electromyography (EMG) to simulate muscle synergy and joint mechanical characteristics during high-speed movement, the application of this method in the THA field can realize digital simulation of postoperative gait mechanics and prosthesis impingement risk. For example, the MSK model constructed using gender-specific acetabular parameters of this study (e.g., larger acetabular depth in males, synergistic correlation among parameters in females) can accurately predict the impact of different prosthesis designs on postoperative hip force transmission efficiency, evaluate the impingement risk threshold caused by abnormal prosthesis position, and provide a more intuitive biomechanical basis for individualized prosthesis selection and surgical plan optimization. In addition, artificial intelligence technology can be integrated to develop a THA postoperative complication risk prediction model using the anatomical data of this study as training samples, further improving the accuracy of clinical decision-making.

In conclusion, the acetabular parameters of elderly individuals in Inner Mongolia, China, show significant gender differences in size and parameter correlations, and exhibit racial specificity compared with European-American populations, which provides key data support for optimizing the localized design of total hip arthroplasty prostheses and improving surgical accuracy.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated the three-dimensional anatomical characteristics of the acetabulum in 240 elderly individuals aged 65–84 years in Inner Mongolia (480 hip joints) via CT-based three-dimensional reconstruction and quantitative measurement, revealing that acetabular size parameters (superoinferior diameter, anteroposterior diameter, depth) in this population have significant gender differences (males > females) with no gender variations in angular parameters (abduction angle, anteversion angle), no significant left-right differences in either gender after Bonferroni correction, and limited clinical value of age-related changes in guiding prosthesis selection (only male anteroposterior diameter and female depth show age-group differences with weak correlations between age and partial parameters). Additionally, female acetabular parameters exhibit stronger synergistic correlations while male parameters are relatively independent, and the anatomical characteristics are racially specific compared with European-American populations. These findings provide critical data support for the precise and localized design of acetabular prostheses and individualized total hip arthroplasty for elderly individuals in this region, suggesting that prosthesis size should be optimized based on gender, left-right symmetric design is applicable, and age-based prosthesis adjustment is unnecessary.
