RBM43 promotes glioma aggressiveness and shapes the immune microenvironment through macrophage polarization
Authors: ZiLong Tan, DaPeng Wang, XiaoLong Tang, Wei He, Zhuo Chen, QiaoLi Lv, XiaoLi Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the expression pattern of RBM43 in glioma and evaluate its prognostic significance, with particular attention to its association with the glioma immune microenvironment.MethodsRBM43 expression, clinicopathological associations, and prognostic value were analyzed using RNA-seq data from the TCGA and GTEx databases. RBM43 function was evaluated in vitro using proliferation, migration, and apoptosis assays, and was further tested in an in vivo mouse model. To probe RBM43-related biology, Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) were performed to identify enriched pathways. Immune infiltration and macrophage polarization were assessed using immune-related bioinformatic analyses and flow cytometry with M1/M2 markers.ResultsElevated RBM43 expression in glioma correlated with higher WHO grade, IDH-wildtype status, and reduced overall survival (OS). Kaplan–Meier and univariable Cox analyses indicated that RBM43 was related to unfavorable outcomes. Functionally, RBM43 overexpression promoted glioma cell proliferation and migration and suppressed apoptosis, whereas RBM43 knockdown exerted the opposite effects. Immune analyses revealed that elevated RBM43 expression was closely associated with macrophage M2 polarization, suggesting its involvement in immune evasion. In vivo, RBM43 overexpression accelerated tumor progression, while RBM43 knockdown inhibited tumor growth.ConclusionRBM43 is associated with glioma aggressiveness and macrophage M2-like polarization. These findings suggest that RBM43 may serve as a prognostic biomarker and a potential therapeutic target in glioma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05135-5.

Introduction

Glioma is one of the most common primary malignant tumors in adults and remains one of the most challenging because of marked heterogeneity, diffuse infiltration, and frequent resistance to therapy [1, 2]. Epidemiological data show that gliomas represent roughly 80% of malignant brain tumors, and GBM remains the most frequent—and most aggressive—subtype [3]. Even with maximal safe resection followed by temozolomide (TMZ)–based chemoradiotherapy, outcomes are still poor [4]. The 2021 World Health Organization (WHO) classification incorporates molecular features into the diagnostic framework, integrating histopathology with key alterations such as MGMT promoter methylation, IDH mutation status, and 1p/19q codeletion to improve tumor classification and prognostic assessment [5]. Nonetheless, the molecular drivers that can be translated into effective therapeutic targets remain incompletely understood.

RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) have attracted growing attention as regulators of cancer biology because they sit at the center of post-transcriptional gene control. By shaping RNA splicing, nuclear export, stability, and translation, RBPs can influence proliferation, apoptosis, and migration [6]. Increasing evidence suggests that disrupted RBP programs contribute to tumor initiation, progression, and treatment resistance. For example, HuR, members of the IGF2BP family, and HNRNPA1 have been reported to support glioma stemness and radio-chemoresistance by stabilizing oncogenic transcripts or limiting tumor-suppressive programs [7–9]. These observations point to RBPs as biologically relevant—and potentially druggable—nodes in glioma.

RBM43 is a recently described RBP containing a canonical RNA recognition motif (RRM) and has been implicated in regulating RNA stability and translational output. Prior studies have reported abnormal RBM43 expression in several solid tumors, including esophageal squamous cell carcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma, and have linked it to increased proliferation and migration as well as poorer clinical outcomes [10–12]. However, how RBM43 is expressed in glioma, what it does functionally, and whether it carries clinical relevance remain unclear.

Here, we address these gaps by profiling RBM43 expression and prognostic associations across glioma cohorts and by testing its effects on glioma cell growth and motility in vitro and in vivo. We further investigated the relationship between RBM43 and macrophage polarization within the glioma immune microenvironment.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and transfection

U87 and U251 glioma cells, as well as THP-1 monocytes (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA), were cultured in DMEM or RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO₂. Specifically, U87 and U251 cells were maintained in DMEM, whereas THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640. U87 and U251 cells were transfected with RBM43-overexpressing lentiviral vectors or shRNA plasmids (Hanbio Biotechnology, China) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA). Stable cell populations were established by puromycin selection (2 µg/mL) for 7 days, and RBM43 overexpression or knockdown efficiency was confirmed by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) and Western blotting before subsequent experiments. THP-1 cells were maintained under the same standard culture conditions and used for macrophage differentiation. All cell lines used in this study were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling prior to the experiments. In addition, all cell lines were routinely tested and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination before use.

Macrophage differentiation and activation

To generate macrophage-like cells, THP-1 monocytes were stimulated with PMA (100 nM) for 24 h and subsequently maintained in fresh PMA-free medium for another 24 h to allow recovery [13]. Differentiation was confirmed by morphologic changes. To assess macrophage polarization, differentiated THP-1-derived macrophages were co-cultured with glioma cells with RBM43 overexpression or knockdown in a 6-well Transwell system (0.4-µm pore size) for 48 h, with macrophages seeded in the lower chamber and glioma cells seeded in the upper inserts. After co-culture, cells were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against M1 markers (CD16 and CD64) and M2 markers (CD163 and CD206), and then analyzed by flow cytometry using a BD LSRFortessa instrument (BD Biosciences, USA).

Data acquisition and prognostic modeling

Data from RNA sequencing and clinical details were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas and Genotype-Tissue Expression databases using the UCSC XENA platform [13]. The integrated dataset comprised 1,157 normal brain tissues and 689 glioma samples. For downstream analyses, patients were dichotomized by the median RBM43 expression level into high- and low-expression strata. The prognostic relevance of RBM43 for OS was assessed using Cox proportional hazards regression, including univariate screening followed by multivariable adjustment [14]. Candidate variables were selected using the Akaike information criterion (AIC). A prognostic nomogram was then constructed using the rms and survival packages in R (version 4.0.2). We assessed model performance using the C-index, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, and calibration plots [15].

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) and western blotting

Total RNA was extracted from cells or tissues using TRIzol reagent (TaKaRa, Japan). cDNA was synthesized with the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (TaKaRa, Japan), and qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Premix Ex Taq (TaKaRa, Japan) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as the internal control. The primer sequences used in this study were as follows:

For Western blotting, total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer. Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, including anti-rabbit IgG (1:5000, Cell Signaling Technology, #7074) and anti-mouse IgG (1:5000, Cell Signaling Technology, #7076). Functional and immune-related analyses

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the RBM43-high and RBM43-low groups in TCGA were identified using the limma package (|logFC| ≥ 1, p < 0.05). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were then performed on these DEGs to characterize the associated biological processes and pathways [16]. In addition, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was carried out using ranked gene lists, with the REACTOME gene set collection as the reference database.

Immune-cell infiltration was also evaluated using the CIBERSORT algorithm to estimate the relative abundance of immune-cell populations in glioma samples. Immune infiltration was first evaluated using TIMER by analyzing the correlation between RBM43 expression and the estimated abundance of infiltrating immune cells. The enrichment of 24 immune-cell signatures was further quantified by single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) implemented in the GSVA package. ESTIMATE analysis was performed to infer the relative abundance of immune and stromal components in the tumor microenvironment. Associations between RBM43 expression and representative M1/M2 macrophage markers were examined using Pearson correlation analysis in the TCGA cohort, and Pearson’s r was reported [17].

Cellular and in vivo experiments

Scratch-wound migration assay

U87 and U251 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and grown to approximately 90% confluence. A uniform wound was created using a sterile 200 µL pipette tip, and detached cells were gently removed with PBS. The medium was then replaced with DMEM containing 2% FBS to minimize the influence of cell proliferation. Images were captured at 0 and 24 h using an inverted microscope (Leica, Germany). For each well, three random fields were recorded, and each experiment was performed independently at least three times. Wound closure was calculated as (A₀ − A₂₄)/A₀ × 100%, where A₀ and A₂₄ represent the wound area at 0 and 24 h, respectively.

Colony formation and cell viability assays

For colony formation, 600 cells were seeded per well in 6-well plates and maintained for 14 days. Colonies were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Beyotime, China), photographed, and counted.

Cell viability was measured using the CellTiter 96® AQueous One Solution assay (Promega, USA). Cells (2 × 10³ per well) were seeded in 96-well plates. At 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h, OD490 was read on a Bio-Rad microplate reader (Bio-Rad, USA).

Apoptosis assay

Apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were collected, washed twice with cold PBS, and resuspended in binding buffer. The cell suspension was then stained with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Samples were analyzed immediately by flow cytometry, and the data were processed using FlowJo software.

Flow cytometry for macrophage polarization

A 48-h co-culture was set up between differentiated macrophages and glioma cells with RBM43 overexpression or knockdown at an equal cell ratio (1:1). Cells were harvested, washed with PBS, and resuspended in PBS containing 1% FBS. Samples were stained with antibodies against M1 markers (CD16-FITC, CD64-PE) or M2 markers (CD163-FITC, CD206-PE). Flow-cytometric acquisition was performed on a BD LSRFortessa cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA), with at least 10,000 events collected for each sample. Data were analyzed using FlowJo v10 (Tree Star, USA), and mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was used for quantitative comparison across groups.

Intracranial xenograft model

Four-week-old male BALB/c nude mice (Beijing Huafukang Bioscience, China) were anesthetized and stereotactically implanted with 5 × 10⁵ U87-luc cells carrying the indicated constructs (sh-NC, OE-RBM43, or SH-RBM43) into the right striatum (n = 5 per group). Tumor burden was monitored by bioluminescence imaging using an IVIS Lumina system (PerkinElmer, USA) on days 0, 7, 14, and 21. Bioluminescent signal intensity (radiance/total flux) was used as a surrogate readout of intracranial tumor growth [18]. All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethical and Welfare Committee, Nanchang Research Institute, Sun Yat-sen University.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed according to data type and distribution. For comparisons between two groups, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. Associations between RBM43 expression and clinicopathological characteristics were evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis test, Mann–Whitney U test, or chi-square test, as appropriate. Overall survival was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves, and differences between groups were compared with the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to identify prognostic factors. ROC curves were generated using the pROC package, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to assess 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS prediction [19].

For co-expression and immune checkpoint–related analyses, data processing and visualization were performed using the limma, reshape2, ggplot2, pheatmap, immunedeconv, and estimate packages [20]. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

RBM43 is highly expressed in glioma and predicts poor prognosis

The expression of RBM43 across human cancers was analyzed by combining RNA-seq data from the TCGA and GTEx projects, with batch correction applied (Fig. 1A). RBM43 transcripts were markedly elevated in multiple tumor types, including ACC, CHOL, GBM, HNSC, KIRP, LAML, LGG, LIHC, LUAD, PAAD, PCPG, SKCM, STAD, THCA, and THYM, whereas decreased expression was observed in CESC, COAD, DLBC, KICH, READ, UCEC, and UCS. Further clinical correlation analysis revealed that RBM43 expression increased progressively with higher WHO grades of glioma. In addition, patients older than 60 years exhibited significantly higher RBM43 levels, whereas markedly lower expression was detected in tumors harboring IDH mutations or 1p/19q codeletion (Fig. 1B–E). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed that patients with high RBM43 expression had significantly shorter overall survival than those with low RBM43 expression, as determined by the log-rank test (Fig. 1F). The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve showed that RBM43 expression effectively predicted patient outcomes, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.929 (Fig. 1G). Using the TCGA database, the relevant baseline data table (Table 1) was statistically analyzed. After univariable screening and multivariable adjustment, Cox proportional hazards analyses showed that age, WHO grade, IDH status, primary therapy outcome, and RBM43 expression retained independent prognostic significance in glioma (Table 2). Leveraging these covariates, we constructed a nomogram that explicitly incorporated RBM43, enabling nomogram-based risk stratification; calibration plots demonstrated good calibration fidelity for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS estimates (Fig. 1H–I). Overall, these findings indicate that RBM43 is an unfavorable prognostic biomarker for glioma and may be helpful for patient risk stratification.

RBM43 expression correlates with immune infiltration and tumor microenvironment remodeling

To explore the biological programs associated with RBM43, we compared RBM43-high and RBM43-low gliomas in the TCGA cohort and performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses, together with REACTOME-based GSEA (Tables 3 and 4). These analyses suggested that RBM43-associated genes were preferentially linked to immune-related biological processes and pathways, including immune-response activation, immunoglobulin receptor binding, and lymphocyte-mediated immunity (Fig. 2A–B).

GSEA

We next used ssGSEA to estimate the relative enrichment of 24 immune-cell signatures and found that higher RBM43 expression was positively associated with several immune-cell populations, particularly macrophages, neutrophils, eosinophils, and cytotoxic cells (Fig. 2C–F). Consistent with these observations, ESTIMATE analysis showed that the RBM43-high group had higher immune, stromal, and ESTIMATE scores, suggesting a tumor microenvironment with increased immune and stromal components (Fig. 2G–I).

RBM43 enhances glioma-cell proliferation

Stable U87 and U251 cell lines with RBM43 overexpression or knockdown were generated. RBM43 expression changes were verified by qRT-PCR (Fig. 3A–B), and the corresponding protein-level changes were confirmed by Western blotting (Supplementary Figure S1) before the functional assays were carried out. In the MTS assay, RBM43 overexpression increased cell growth, whereas RBM43 depletion suppressed proliferation in both cell lines (Fig. 3C–D). Colony-formation assays showed a similar trend, with RBM43 overexpression markedly increasing the number of colonies and RBM43 knockdown reducing clonogenic ability (Fig. 3E–H).

RBM43 silencing inhibits migration and promotes apoptosis in glioma cells

We next examined whether RBM43 affects glioma cell motility. In scratch-wound assays, RBM43 overexpression led to faster wound closure in both U87 and U251 cells, while RBM43 knockdown slowed closure relative to the negative-control (NC) group (Fig. 4A–D).

Apoptosis was quantified by flow cytometry. RBM43 suppression increased the proportion of apoptotic cells in both lines, whereas RBM43 overexpression produced little change in apoptosis (Fig. 4E–H). Overall, knocking down RBM43 reduced migration and increased apoptosis in both glioma cell models.

RBM43 is associated with macrophage infiltration and favors M2 polarization

CIBERSORT analysis of TCGA glioma data indicated a positive relationship between RBM43 expression and estimated macrophage infiltration (Fig. 5A). We further analyzed the relationship among RBM43, inhibitory immune checkpoint molecules, and immune-cell infiltration patterns. RBM43 and several checkpoint-related genes displayed distinct correlations with multiple immune-cell populations, including macrophage-related signatures (Fig. 5B).

To test these computational observations experimentally, we established a co-culture model with U87 glioma cells and macrophages. Flow cytometry showed that RBM43 overexpression lowered the M1-associated markers CD16 and CD64, whereas RBM43 knockdown increased their expression (Fig. 5C–D). In parallel, the M2 markers CD163 and CD206 rose in the RBM43-overexpression condition and fell after RBM43 silencing (Fig. 5E–F).

Overall, RBM43 perturbation in the co-culture system tracked with reciprocal changes in M1 and M2 marker expression.

RBM43 accelerates glioma growth in vivo

To evaluate RBM43 in vivo, we generated intracranial xenografts using U87 cells with stable RBM43 overexpression or knockdown. Tumor growth was followed by bioluminescence imaging in nude mice. Relative to controls, RBM43 overexpression was associated with faster tumor expansion, whereas RBM43 silencing produced smaller tumor burdens and slower progression (Fig. 6A).

Consistent with these imaging findings, mice in the sh-RBM43 group showed less pronounced weight loss than control animals (Fig. 6B–C). Although a trend toward longer survival was observed in the sh-RBM43 group, the difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 6D, log-rank test, P = 0.449). In this model, changing RBM43 levels moved tumor growth and survival in opposite directions.

Discussion

In this study, we combined bioinformatic analyses with in vitro and in vivo experiments to examine the clinical relevance and biological role of RBM43 in glioma. We found that RBM43 expression was significantly elevated in glioma tissues and increased further with advancing tumor grade. Consistent with this pattern, TCGA-based analyses linked higher RBM43 expression to worse survival. The observed AUC performance further supports the potential value of RBM43 for prognosis and motivated the construction of a prognostic model, highlighting RBM43 as a candidate biomarker in glioma.

Functional assays in stable U87 and U251 models provided complementary evidence. RBM43 overexpression enhanced proliferative and migratory phenotypes, whereas RBM43 depletion reduced these malignant behaviors and increased apoptosis. Similar functional themes have been reported for other RBM family members (e.g., RBM5 and RBM6), which have been implicated in tumor growth and metastatic potential across cancer types. In parallel, our transcriptomic analyses suggested that RBM43-associated programs are enriched for immune-related pathways [21–23], prompting us to examine whether RBM43 might intersect with the immune microenvironment.

Macrophages are central components of the glioma microenvironment, and their polarization states are closely tied to immune suppression, tumor invasion, and clinical outcome [24, 25]. In general terms, M1-like macrophages are associated with anti-tumor activity, whereas M2-like macrophages tend to support immune evasion and tumor progression [26–28]. In our co-culture system, RBM43 overexpression shifted macrophage marker profiles toward an M2-like state, with higher CD163/CD206 and lower CD16/CD64, whereas RBM43 silencing produced the opposite pattern. These findings suggest that RBM43 is associated with macrophage polarization in the glioma microenvironment. However, the molecular basis underlying this association remains to be determined. This direction is consistent with broader work in other tumor settings, including lung and breast cancers, where immune-cell polarization can shape tumor-promoting immune niches [29, 30].

The in vivo orthotopic model further supported the functional impact of RBM43. Modulating RBM43 levels produced reciprocal effects on intracranial tumor growth in mice. RBM43 overexpression was associated with faster tumor progression, whereas RBM43 depletion reduced tumor burden. Although survival curves showed a similar directional trend, the differences among groups did not reach statistical significance. This in vivo pattern mirrors the in vitro phenotypes and strengthens the case that RBM43 contributes to glioma aggressiveness.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Although our data connect RBM43 to macrophage polarization, the underlying molecular mechanism remains unresolved. While rescue experiments were not included in the current study, the reciprocal phenotypes observed in both RBM43 overexpression and knockdown models consistently supported its functional role in glioma. Additional validation may further strengthen the specificity and causal interpretation of these findings. In addition, transcriptomic profiling of RBM43-manipulated models was not performed in the current study. Such analyses would be valuable for identifying downstream targets and clarifying the molecular pathways regulated by RBM43. Macrophage polarization also represents only one dimension of the immune landscape, and further studies are needed to determine how RBM43 relates to other immune populations and cell–cell interactions, as well as whether it is linked to pathways such as PTEN or PI3K/AKT signaling in glioma [31, 32].

Looking ahead, it will be useful to map RBM43-related immune effects across multiple immune subsets (including T cells and dendritic cells) and to test whether RBM43 is linked to macrophage–T cell crosstalk in immune escape. These efforts could clarify whether RBM43 has value not only as a biomarker but also as a therapeutic entry point. If a direct, targetable dependency can be established, developing RBM43-directed inhibitors (or pathway-based interventions downstream of RBM43) may offer a complementary strategy for glioma treatment. Extending similar analyses to additional tumor types may also help define how generalizable RBM43-associated immune remodeling is across cancers.

Conclusion

In summary, our study identifies RBM43 as a glioma-associated factor linked to poor prognosis, malignant phenotypes, and macrophage M2-like polarization. These findings suggest that RBM43 may contribute to glioma progression and could serve as a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target. Further studies are needed to clarify the downstream molecular mechanisms of RBM43 and its broader role in the glioma immune microenvironment.
