IoT-based automated system for water-related disease prediction
Authors: Bhushankumar Nemade, Kiran Kishor Maharana, Vikram Kulkarni, Surajit mondal, G S Pradeep Ghantasala, Amal Al-Rasheed, Masresha Getahun, Ben Othman Soufiene
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHaving access to potable water is a fundamental right to well-being. Despite this, 3.4 million people die from diseases caused by water each year, and 1.1 billion people lack access to potable drinking water. Although industrialization, durable infrastructure, and rapid development have increased living standards, the water problem has left humanity defenseless. As different human activities have contaminated these water reserves, according to an estimate, water is the cause of 80% of ailments. As a result, it is necessary to permit enough infrastructure to ensure the security of a reliable supply of potable water. Thus, a real-time WBPCB dataset with 17 features and a proposed IoT-based system to collect data are used in this research to address the issue. The research paper provides a system for predicting diseases and forecasting long-term trends. Classification is performed using Random Forest, XGBoost, and AdaBoost, which have accuracy rates of 99.66%, 99.52%, and 99.64%, respectively. Forecasting is performed using LSTM, which has an MSE value for the pH parameter of 0.1631. The paper introduces TS-SMOTE, a novel hybridized time-series SMOTE data augmentation approach. Additionally, it offers an IoT system that uses H-ANFIS to gather data in real-time and identify attacks.Keywords: Water-borne diseases prediction, Water quality prediction, IoT-based diseases prediction, Time-series data, Forecasting diseases, TS-SMOTE, Time-series data augmentation, H-ANFIS, Long short-term memory, LSTMSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

Human life and public health depend on water. However, access to safe drinking water is not available to most people. It has evolved into a worldwide issue that is thought to impact 40% of the world’s population altogether1. In addition, it is anticipated that 3 billion people would not have access to fresh water by 2025, forcing them to live in a water-stressed environment2. This will lead to an increase in water-related illnesses and fatalities in both developed and developing nations. While the majority of instances are recorded in developing nations, wealthier nations also suffer from this3–5.

Drinking water that has been tainted by human or animal waste that contains harmful microorganisms is the main cause of waterborne illnesses. With 1.8 million fatalities and 4 billion episodes of sickness yearly, watery diarrhea is the most common infection that causes death and morbidity globally6. Drinking water is the primary source of waterborne illness transmission when an animal or human waste containing harmful microorganisms is involved. According to statistics, waterborne infections are the primary cause of death worldwide, accounting for close to 3.4 million fatalities per year and almost 4 billion episodes of sickness7. An estimated 73 million working days are expected to be lost annually owing to waterborne sickness, and an estimated 37.7 million Indians are thought to suffer from waterborne illnesses each year8,9. Moreover, it’s estimated that 1.5 million children die every year from diarrhea alone. Waterborne infections have a significant impact on a country’s healthcare costs and economic growth. Due to inadequate water supply management, particularly about drinking water and sanitation, many illnesses return every year during the warm and wet seasons10,11.

Waterborne illnesses proliferate in areas of urban slums with weak urban management, rapidly growing economies, very dense populations, and subpar housing and sanitation12. At least 30% of all illness in Mumbai’s slum regions is attributed to water-related diseases, according to one research. In rural areas, the sewage and water systems are insufficient13. Water pollution in the communities is mostly caused by infiltration, leaching, and surface runoff from fields, a lack of sewage disposal facilities, and structural leakages. Waterborne infections are often brought on by a lack of knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors (KAB) about water management, sanitation, and defecation habits, according to studies conducted in rural India14. An all-encompassing strategy is needed to address water quality and waterborne illness, including increasing access to clean drinking water, strengthening water treatment and distribution infrastructure, and decreasing pollution from industrial and agricultural sources. While considering the patterns of human water usage, particularly in India. Due to the restricted fresh water supply, rural communities are forced to share their resources with the “privileged” urban population, resulting in a fictitious shortage for them throughout the summer. Making rural residents rely on untreated rivers, lakes, ponds, wells, and other sources15,16.

The problem is evident and the convergence of IoT, machine learning, and time-series forecasting in water quality management can serve to solve this. Introduction of systems that are IoT powered built with advanced sensors for continuous data collection, automatically gathering information from water sources. Can help to develop real-time insights into critical metrics in water, such as pH, turbidity, temperature, or chemical contaminants. Continuous monitoring would be able to detect changes in water quality and limit the possibility that any contamination would make its way into the public. Machine learning algorithms, applied to these large real-time datasets, identify some of these faint patterns well enough to predict the outbreak of waterborne diseases by detecting an anomaly that traditional methods will have missed.

Further, time-series forecasting built on such predictive capacity to estimate future trends of water quality based upon past history and can serve information on taking preventive action. It will be especially useful in taking all possible preventive measures against seasonal variation risks, be it due to industrial activities or sudden changes in the environment.

Thus, the need of the hour asks for a relevant approach lie not only in real-time surveillance but also in risk prediction that enables much faster proactive responses. The management of water will then have a chance to make informed decisions that can lead to better health outcomes for the public, optimize resource use, and improve protection efforts of the environment in a scalable and efficient manner with the help of these technologies.

Related work

This review investigates state-of-the-art methods for predicting and avoiding waterborne diseases. To improve strategies for identifying and managing these diseases, researchers have used a variety of techniques, including machine learning and deep learning models. Whether through infectious disease forecasting models or innovative water quality forecasting systems, the studies provide insight on both achievements and challenges in the pursuit of precise and timely disease prevention. The significance of these advancements is based on their potential to transform public health approaches, allowing for more efficient and specific measures for addressing waterborne hazards.

Hussain et al., presented machine learning methods to predict positive cases of waterborne infections. For the years 2017–2020, these researchers have collected typhoid and malaria patient records in order to create a machine learning-based model to detect waterborne infections. They trained and tested various ML models and found that Random Forest performed the best with 77% accuracy in the prediction of positive cases of waterborne diseases. The study also identified the most important input features for disease prediction. The system’s limitations include a lack of patient symptom information. The authors proposed additional research by utilizing social media surveys and hyperparameter approaches to increase the system’s accuracy17.

The US Department of Health and Human Services developed a “Waterborne Disease Outbreak Investigation Toolkit”, which provides a comprehensive guide for investigating and controlling waterborne disease outbreaks. The toolkit covers a range of topics, including outbreak detection, sample collection, laboratory testing, case definition, data analysis, and control measures. The main advantage of this toolkit is that it provides step-by-step guidance and checklists for each stage of the investigation process, ensuring a thorough and systematic approach to outbreak investigation. However, the limitation of the toolkit is that it requires a significant number of resources and expertise and may not be accessible to all public health agencies or organizations. Overall, the toolkit is a valuable resource for public health professionals involved in investigating waterborne disease outbreaks18.

Il Won Seo et al. presented the system to forecast water quality parameters using an ANN model with stratified sampling downstream of Cheongpyeong Dam. The study utilized eight water quality attributes from a dataset collected for the duration of three years, from July 2012–August 2015. The findings indicate that, except for turbidity, which has a R squared of just 0.638, the ANN ensemble model using stratified sample data performs well for seven parameters. Five parameters in RMSE have excellent performance below 1.0. However, in the case of turbidity, it results in R squared error of 0.683 and an RMSE of 10.01, which indicates that the ANN model is not able to predict rare events. The study concludes that further research is required to lessen the influence of rare events on the prediction of water quality parameters19.

Wang M. et al. presented a deep learning-based technique for the multi-classification of infectious illnesses utilizing unstructured electronic medical records (EMR). This study used deep learning models that significantly improved the detection of infectious illnesses. The model was trained using hospital data from 20,620 occurrences of seven different types of disorders. The newly created Multiple Infectious Disease Diagnostic Model (MIDDM) by the researchers demonstrated a higher accuracy of 99.44% when compared to other models such as XGBoost, decision trees, Bayesian models, and logistic regression, which showed an accuracy of 96.19% and 90.13%, 85.19%, and 91.26%, respectively. The study aimed to aid medical professionals in the detection of infectious illnesses and the prevention of their social spread. The study had limitations since it looked just at one hospital and a small number of diseases, and it didn’t use the most precise diagnostic criteria20.

Afaq Juna et al. presented a study of water quality assessment using KNN imputers and multilayer perceptron. The given study fills in the dataset’s missing values using a nine-layer multilayer perceptron (MLP) with a KNN imputer. It has seven different machine-learning algorithms. The recommended KNN imputer model has a 99.9% accuracy rate21.

M. Wahyudi et al. presented research on the application of C4.5 and the Naive Bayes Algorithm to detect a potential increase in the case fatality rate for diarrhea. The research focused on a diarrhea epidemic from 2008 to 2014 and included data on 231 people. Algorithms such as C4.5 and Naive Bayes had accuracy rates of 82.82 and 79.85%, respectively. Its objective is to understand the factors that contributed to the pandemic, such as the population’s wealth, access to medical facilities and sanitization services, living arrangements, and family dynamics. The research addresses the problems of small sample sizes, the exclusion of classification models used to study diarrhea, and the neglect of optimum model-building parameters22.

M. K. Verma et al. presented a research study aimed at developing an underwater monitoring and alert system for the prevention and control of water-borne diseases. This study provides a method for gathering data on the temperature, pH, turbidity, and oxidation of water sources in lakes, rivers, and seas dubbed an “underwater acoustic sensor network.” In this research, the underwater surface gateway has preprocessing and rule-based engine features that send an alarm to the nearby hospital. The underwater surface gateway sends data to government healthcare institutions through the internet, and similar data is used to perform analysis using big data for the control and prevention of water-borne illnesses23.

S. Kurra et al. presented a study on water quality prediction using machine learning. The potability of the water is the subject of the study. PH, hardness, solids, chloramines, sulfate, conductivity, organic carbon, trihalomethanes, and turbidity are only a few of the nine properties of the dataset. These statistics were acquired from various locations around Andhra Pradesh. It uses the Decision Tree and KNN algorithms, with respective accuracy rates of 58.5 and 61.7%. The models performed badly because the dataset was deliberately created24.

S. Rajmohan et al. presented the T-SMOTE algorithm, which focuses on the unique temporal-oriented oversampling technique known as T-SMOTE and takes into account the peculiarities of time-series datasets that are gradually and continually developing. T-SMOTE can handle both univariate and multivariate information, and it also excels at early prediction. The thorough tests on ten datasets show that T-SMOTE outperformed all of its cutting-edge rivals by a significant margin. Moreover, T-SMOTE has been implemented in several Microsoft Azure and Microsoft 365 situations, including the prediction of disc failure and excessive latency, and has significantly enhanced the performance of the online models25.

H. He et al. presented a study on “ADASYN: Adaptive Synthetic Sampling Approach for Imbalanced Learning”, For challenges involving the categorization of unbalanced data, the unique adaptive learning method ADASYN was introduced in this study. ADASYN was adaptively generated to lessen the bias caused by the unequal data distribution, artificial data samples for the minority class are created based on the original data distribution. Further, ADASYN automatically adjusted the classifier decision boundary to concentrate on those challenging classes samples, improving learning effectiveness. To achieve these two goals, dynamic weight adjustments and an adaptive learning method that takes distributions of data into account are both applied. Results from simulations on five data sets using various assessment measures were used to demonstrate the effectiveness of the technique26.

Mushtaq Hussain et al. presented a study on “Machine learning based efficient prediction of positive cases of waterborne diseases”. Waterborne diseases have increased in Pakistan as a result of the increasing danger to water quality induced by fast population growth. A recent study at Ayub Medical Hospital from 2017 to 2020 used machine learning models to predict typhoid and malaria cases, with Random Forest demonstrating greater accuracy at 60% for malaria and 77% for typhoid. The study underlines the importance of input characteristics, highlighting age, history, and test findings as key factors in anticipating positive instances of waterborne illnesses. These findings suggest the possibility of developing tailored interventions and preventative measures based on these critical drivers, in addition to forecasting accuracy. The study highlights the value of data-driven approaches in public health by offering significant insights to inform actions to reduce the prevalence of waterborne infections in Pakistan17.

N. Khant et al. presented a study that examined into “Impact of Climate Change on Waterborne Diseases: Directions towards Sustainability”. Climate change has a significant impact on the rise of waterborne diseases (WBDs), especially in developing countries such as those in the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN). Vibrio cholerae, a common waterborne pathogen, is most common during severe weather changes. Cyclone Nargis in Myanmar (2008) and tropical cyclones in the Philippines highlight the urgency for ASEAN countries to implement policies addressing the impact of climate change on WBDs. The goals of this study are to (1) assess the health implications of climate change and identify necessary prevention policies, and (2) investigate the relationship between SDGs and climate change effects on WBDs for global sustainability. It is proposed that the SDGs be adapted for ASEAN countries in order to improve legislation on climate-related health issues. To address the multifaceted challenges posed by climate change on WBDs in ASEAN countries, effective collaboration among scientists, researchers, health professionals, and policymakers is critical27.

Mithila et al. presented a study that investigates into the economic issues posed by water-borne illnesses as a result of climate change, with an emphasis on measures for reducing disease spread and providing access to safe drinking water. The research, that was conducted in Taltali upazila, Barguna district, with 384 respondents, had notable results. Approximately 41.4% have dysentery, 22.4% have increased treatment costs for water-borne infections owing to climate change, and 37.8% have difficulty accessing safe drinking water. Tube-well water is being used by 41.7% of the population, up from 19.3% five to ten years ago. Notably, 27.4% recognize the need for water filtering in disease prevention. According to the report, susceptibility to climate change is caused by variables such as rapid population expansion, resource disparity, food insecurity, remote water collection, insufficient medical facilities, poverty, and a weak health system. These findings highlight the complicated issues posed by water-borne infections in the face of climate change28.

Singh et al. conducted a comprehensive study on “IoT-based greenhouse technologies for enhanced crop production: a comprehensive study of monitoring, control, and communication techniques”, emphasizing their potential to enhance crop production amidst varying climate conditions. The research highlights the significance of effective monitoring and control of environmental parameters, such as temperature, humidity, and soil moisture, crucial for optimal crop growth. They identify various communication techniques and sensors, offering insights into their advantages and limitations, which can guide farmers in selecting suitable technologies. The study also underscores the importance of addressing microclimate interactions within greenhouses and calls for further comparative analyses to optimize the implementation of smart greenhouse systems, thereby promoting sustainable agricultural practices29.

Data collection

A real-time dataset from which all the water quality characteristics could be determined was required because the research was based on time series forecasting. The West Bengal Pollution Control Board offers abbreviated as WBPCB an information system on water quality that includes water parameter data for all river basins that flow through Bengal along with sampling dates and times, which was a fundamental necessity for the research.

TS-SMOTE

Time-Series Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique named TS-SMOTE is a novel hybridized SMOTE developed for oversampling time-series data to solve imbalance problems in the dataset. TS-SMOTE uses the concepts of ADASYN and KNN. After labeling the data, it can be observed that the dataset contains an unequal distribution of data classes. Thus, creating the problem of an unbalanced dataset. The class having maximum number of data tuple is the class considered as majority, whereas the minority class is any other class. As represented in Table 3, the majority class can be considered the majority class, while the rest are considered the minority class.

Requirement for TS-SMOTE technique

Time series data often do not have independent and identically distributed data (IID), which is what SMOTE presumes. SMOTE has the potential to destabilize the normal correlation structure that exists between time series data and prior and subsequent data points. Time series data are usually organized chronologically34, and the synthetic data produced by SMOTE could not maintain this order, producing data points that are implausible and erroneous. By interpolating between current samples in the feature space, SMOTE creates synthetic samples. SMOTE may provide samples that are unrepresentative of the underlying process, and the feature space may not be the best place for time series data35. SMOTE may sometimes produce artificial samples that are very similar to the actual samples, which may cause overfitting and a delusion of enhanced performance36.

AdaBoost

For classification and regression problems, AdaBoost machine learning is used. It works by creating a strong learner from several weak ones. In each iteration of the method, the weak learner is trained on the data, and instances that were mistakenly categorized are given more weight to be classified correctly in the following iteration. In the final model, the weak learners are combined, and their weights are determined by how well they did throughout training. AdaBoost has been used successfully in a variety of areas, such as bioinformatics, text categorization, and face recognition. High precision and the ability to handle complex datasets are two of its most famous qualities.

The AdaBoost algorithm retains a collection of weights across training data and adapts them after each cycle of poor learning to generate a group of learners who are weak at learning. Weights for the training samples that the present poor learner incorrectly classified will rise, but weights for the examples that were correctly classified would decline38.

Evaluation metric

In forecasting research and analysis, various evaluation metrics are used to assess the accuracy and performance of forecasted values. Among the commonly used metrics are the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), which measures the average magnitude of the forecast errors, the Mean Absolute Error (MAE), which quantifies the average absolute difference between forecasted and actual values, and the R-squared (R2) value, which shows the percentage of the variance in actual values that can be explained by the forecasted values. Additionally, the Mean Forecast Error (MFE) captures the average difference between forecasted and actual values, while the Mean Percentage Error (MPE) and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) provide insight into the average percentage difference between forecasts and actual values. Lastly, the Directional Accuracy (DA) metric evaluates the proportion of correct directional forecasts.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the research paper presented a methodology and related research through which water-related diseases can be predicted and forecasted. The research paper’s methodology involved the collection of real-time data and labeling it by the permissible range of the water parameters. For the preparation of the dataset Data Wrangling techniques were performed. A novel TS-SMOTE Data Augmentation Technique was proposed to solve the problem of class imbalance in the dataset. The classification algorithms utilized were Random Forest, AdaBoost, and XGBoost, with Random Forest achieving the highest accuracy at 99.66% also it took the minimum execution time of 0.0239 s. LSTM algorithm was utilized for forecasting water quality parameters, with an MSE value for pH parameter of 0.163. The paper also discussed the IoT device used for collecting real-time data. Extending it, the paper also discussed attack detection using H-ANFIS and the Feature Reduction technique called MPCA.

Thus, the research can be used for predicting and preventing water-borne diseases. Along with it, the research can be used for other potential applications like collecting data on water quality, understanding water quality patterns, analyzing weather conditions to optimize water usage, and monitoring the water quality of river basins to identify potential sources of contamination. The research can aid the authorities and the public in cases of water-borne disease outbreaks, find high-risk areas, identify and monitor industrial contamination, monitor and regulate the temperature in aquaculture, and others.
