Same Sex Chromosomes With Independent Origins in Haplochromine Cichlids
Authors: Sophie Helen Smith, Zuzana Musilova, Astrid Böhne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElucidating theories of sex chromosome evolution requires approaches that allow fine scale delimitations of sex-determining regions within a phylogenetic context. This can address whether shared sex chromosomes across related species are due to shared ancestry, or whether genetic sex-determining regions have repeatedly evolved. Haplochromine cichlids, as one of the most successful fish lineages on Earth, have been a focal study system of sex chromosome research, both because of their rapid rate of sex chromosome turnover and the repeated emergence of certain sex chromosomes across the lineage. Here, we newly describe sex chromosomes in members of the earliest branch of the modern haplochromines, the Tropheini, based on whole-genome sequencing data, using a combination of SNP- and kmers-based methods. We show that despite the repeated co-options of ancestral chromosomes LG5 and LG7 in these species, the origins of these sex chromosomes are independent. Investigation of gene functions, allele differences, and sex-biased gene expression within the discovered sex-linked regions provides no evidence that sexual antagonism has driven the repeated evolution of a region on LG5 that overlaps between four of these species. By comparing the sex-determining regions on LG5 and LG7 across haplochromines, we show that a common origin is unlikely, and that while sex chromosomes themselves may be shared between several Haplochromini, the sex-determining genes or mechanism likely differ. This study paves the way to explore newly emerging theories of sex chromosome evolution, such as the role of chromosomal fusion or recombination patterns across the genome.

In cichlid fishes from East Africa, previous research has found that closely related species can have completely different genetic mechanisms of sex determination, but why this is the case is unclear. Here we find new sex chromosomes in a group of cichlids from Lake Tanganyika, the Tropheini, and show that while these involve the same chromosome pairs, the sex chromosomes evolved independently. We aim to address why these sex chromosomes have repeatedly evolved, both in the Tropheini and in East African cichlids in general.

Introduction

Understanding the drivers of sex chromosome evolution requires both the ability to accurately detect sex determination (SD) systems and a comparative phylogenetic framework (Saunders and Muyle 2024). Until recently, few studies fulfilled these conditions across a wider taxonomic range, limiting research on this topic (Bachtrog et al. 2014). Accordingly, the ultimate causes of sex chromosome evolution, of rapid sex chromosome turnover in some taxa and conservation of sex chromosomes in others, remain largely unexplained. However, the discovery of sex chromosomes has advanced greatly in recent years (Saunders and Muyle 2024), and fish in particular have become a key focus (Smith et al. 2023).

Drivers of sex chromosome turnover and evolution can vary in fish, such as a novel sex-determining gene (SDG) arising via gene duplication on an autosome forming a new sex chromosome (Myosho et al. 2015), repeated translocation of the SDG onto different chromosomes (Woram et al. 2003), and turnover driven by neo-sex chromosome fusion (Yoshida et al. 2014). Additionally, fish are known to display mechanisms that fall along the spectrum covering environmental and genotypic SD (Devlin and Nagahama 2002, Kratochvíl et al. 2021). In species with the same sex chromosomes, the size of the non-recombining sex-linked region (SLR) can differ between species (Darolti et al. 2019). Comparisons of sex chromosome evolution in these systems allow theories of sex chromosome evolution to be put to the test. For example, the so-called “limited options” hypothesis, that the selection of sex chromosomes across diverse vertebrate lineages is non-random due to certain genes which are repeatedly selected as SDGs (Marshall Graves and Peichel 2010), can be investigated by comparing SDGs and synteny within and between fish lineages. Sex chromosomes have been heavily studied in the hyperdiverse fish family Cichlidae, which shows an outstandingly high rate of sex chromosome turnover (El Taher et al. 2021a). In cichlids, 20 chromosomes of the 22 ancestral ones (Ozouf-Costaz et al. 2017) have been observed to play a role in SD (Behrens et al. 2024a). While most of these uniquely evolved within tribes, lineages of closely related genera, some chromosomes are repeatedly recruited as sex chromosomes across more distantly related species. In particular, across the East African Great Lakes radiations, LG5 and LG7 were convergently recruited among several species, and apparently not as a result of common ancestry, indicating that their repeated co-option as sex chromosomes may be due to their gene content (El Taher et al. 2021a, Behrens et al. 2024a).

In Lakes Malawi (LM) and Victoria (LV), the endemic cichlid radiations are composed of only one cichlid tribe, the Haplochromini (Salzburger et al. 2005, Ronco et al. 2021, Singh et al. 2022b), while the Lake Tanganyika (LT) radiation is formed of 13 endemic tribes, plus three that colonized the lake secondarily (Ronco et al. 2021). Haplochromini are also found in river systems throughout Africa. The Great Lakes’ radiations of this tribe are part of the so-called “modern haplochromines”, with the endemic LT haplochromines, called Tropheini (Poll 1986), being their earliest lacustrine radiation (Irisarri et al. 2018, Schedel et al. 2019, Singh et al. 2022b) (Fig. 1). Further haplochromine lineages are found in rivers and lakes in the Congolese drainage and other Central, Eastern, and Southern African river systems including the Pseudocrenilabrus group (sensu Schedel et al. 2019). Haplochromini are the most successful cichlid tribe, representing roughly 80% of all East African cichlids and 7% of all teleost species (Salzburger et al. 2005). While many sex chromosomes have been identified in this group, their ancestral sex chromosomes and the timing of evolution of sex chromosomes remain unclear (Behrens et al. 2024a).

In the older riverine Haplochromini, few sex chromosomes have been clearly identified (Fig. 1). Thoracochromis callichromus has an XY system on LG17 (Behrens et al. 2024a). In Pseudocrenilabrus philander, sex chromosomes differ between populations: LG7 XY in populations from Lake Chila and the Thamalakane River (Böhne et al. 2019, Behrens et al. 2024a); a potential ZW system on LG23 in Lake Mweru; and absence of sex chromosomes in other populations (Behrens et al. 2024a). Different systems have been found in other Pseudocrenilabrus: LG12 XY in P. multicolor victoriae; LG9 ZW in P. multicolor multicolor; LG5 with unclear heterogamety and LG2 XY in P. nicholsi (Behrens et al. 2024a). Lufubuchromis relictus, also part of the Pseudocrenilabrus clade, has an XY system on LG23, although in three separate regions (Behrens et al. 2024a). Still within the same clade, an LG19 XY system was identified in Orthochromis indermauri, which has a shared origin with the LG19 XY system in some Tropheus species of the LT Tropheini (see below), suggesting that this is either an old haplochromine sex chromosome or has arisen through introgression (El Taher et al. 2021a). The riverine taxon Astatoreochromis alluaudi also possesses an LG7 XY system in addition to maybe others as well (see Kocher et al. 2022), like in P. philander. Note that placement of Astatoreochromis differs between phylogenetic reconstructions as either sister to LT + LM + LV or only to LM + LV.

Taken together, several sex chromosomes have been identified outside the modern haplochromines with close to no conservation across species. This indicates rapid evolution in (older) haplochromines, suggestive of a lack of a common fixed genetic component. However, relatively few species of these groups have been comprehensively investigated.

Contrastingly, in the modern haplochromine lineage, sex chromosomes have been widely investigated, revealing extensive sex chromosome turnover (Fig. 1). Astatotilapia/Haplochromis burtoni, found in LT and adjacent rivers, has several described sex chromosomes in different laboratory strains, as opposed to natural populations or no sex chromosomes (Böhne et al. 2016, Roberts et al. 2016, Kocher et al. 2022, Lichilín et al. 2023). Astatotilapia calliptera, likely the next closest relative of the LM radiation, was originally described with an LG7 XY SLR containing gsdf (Peterson et al. 2017), a key master SDG in many fish species (Herpin and Schartl 2015). Later, a Y-linked tandem duplication of gsdf, a second sex-associated region upstream of gsdf, and a separate XY system on LG19 were identified as segregating Y alleles in A. calliptera (Munby et al. 2021).

The presumably gsdf-dependent LG7 XY system is also present in several species within the LM flock (Ser et al. 2010, Peterson et al. 2017), and the Y-linked gsdf duplication has been shown in 96 additional LM species (Munby et al. 2021), reinforcing it as a candidate ancestral sex-determining system in this radiation. However, several other systems are found in the LM cichlids: further XY systems on LG9, LG10 and LG11, and LG5 ZW (Ser et al. 2010, Blumer et al. 2025, Kumar et al. 2025). So, if ancestral, the LG7 XY system has been lost several times. The ZW system on LG5 is indeed rather widespread across LM radiation and can co-occur with the LG7 XY system (Parnell and Streelman 2013). In summary, these observations reinforce that LG7 XY may be ancestral at least to LM haplochromines, and LG5 ZW a potent derived sex-determining locus in this radiation.

While fewer sex chromosomes are known from LV, those discovered do not include the LG5 nor LG7 systems described above. An LG23 XY system was found in Haplochromis sauvagei and Pundamilia nyererei, with amh, a known SDG in other fishes, as a candidate SDG (Kocher et al. 2022). Further sex chromosome systems of LV include LG16 ZW (Haplochromis chilotes), sex-associated QTLs on LG2 (H. chilotes/H. sauvagei, Kudo et al. 2015) as well as feminizing B chromosomes in P. nyererei (Kocher et al. 2022). While the LV data are limited, they fit with the overall pattern, in that conserved sex chromosomes are more the exception than the rule.

Characterizing sex chromosomes of LT Tropheini is essential to correctly interpret sex chromosome evolution across Haplochromini. We have identified two sex chromosome systems that are shared across several LT Tropheini species: in the genus Tropheus, six species share an XY system on LG19 (El Taher et al. 2021b). In the sister group to Tropheus, an XY system on presumably fused LG5 and LG19 is found in the genera Petrochromis, Interochromis, and Pseudosimochromis. Two species-specific sex chromosomes have been identified in LT Jabarichromis pfefferi (LG11/15 XY (El Taher et al. 2021a)) and Shuja horei (LG5 XY, overlapping with the region of the 5/19 XY species mentioned above (Behrens et al. 2024b). No SDGs are known for any of the LT species. Thus, LT Tropheini illustrate conservation of sex chromosomes as well as internal sex chromosome turnover.

Determining when and how the recurrently found LG5 (XY and ZW) and LG7 (XY) arose as sex chromosomes would give significant insight into patterns of sex chromosome turnover in Haplochromini. While these two are the most frequently identified sex chromosomes, the various others found in Haplochromini make it currently unclear if these are indeed ancestral sex chromosomes that have been frequently lost, or whether they have arisen several times independently. Indeed, in P. philander, the LG7 XY system is clearly different from that of LM Haplochromini (Böhne et al. 2019), pointing to multiple origins of the same sex chromosome. The fixation of a proficient SDG, as is the case in LG7 XY species with gsdf, as well as in LG23 XY species with amh, poses the question of why either of these systems, if ancestral, would not have fixed more broadly across Haplochromini.

For LG5, a role for sexual antagonism as a driver in its emergence has been proposed (Roberts et al. 2009). While no single theory is able to comprehensively explain how sex chromosomes evolve, the so-called canonical model suggests that sex chromosomes evolve due to advantageous linkage between sexually antagonistic loci and SD loci (van Doorn and Kirkpatrick 2007, van Doorn and Kirkpatrick 2010). Across four genera of LM cichlids, a female-linked color phenotype, orange blotch OB, is thought to confer an advantage through camouflage (Roberts et al. 2009). In males, this phenotype seems to be disadvantageous as it disrupts mating coloration. Previous research has shown that an LG5 ZW SD locus and the OB locus are linked (Roberts et al. 2009), making this region act as a supergene, with an inversion ensuring the co-inheritance of both loci (Behrens et al. 2025). However, in many other fish species with known sex chromosomes and sexual dimorphism, evidence is lacking for sexual antagonism driving or maintaining a sex chromosome (Smith et al. 2023). Further, the OB phenotype is limited to a clade in the LM Haplochromini and hence cannot explain the repeated appearance of LG5 across cichlids (El Taher et al. 2021a).

In the present study, we investigated sex chromosomes of four LT Tropheini species: Petrochromis polyodon, Limnotilapia dardennii, Shuja horei (formerly Ctenochromis) and Lobochilotes labiatus. These species were of interest, firstly to contribute to our understanding of SD in Tropheini, but mainly to determine whether the LG5 or LG7 systems could be more widespread than previously thought, and what might be driving their ubiquity. Petrochromis polyodon is an extremely diverse species, with many known distinct male color morphs, while female coloration is geographically homogeneous (Konings 2016). As opposed to other members of its genus, which have the LG5-19 XY system, no sex chromosomes were identified in this rather derived species (El Taher et al. 2021a). Limnotilapia dardennii also shows strong sexual color dimorphism, as do some populations of S. horei (Tsuboi and Takahashi 2024). In S. horei, a narrow male-linked region on LG5 has been identified in specimens collected at the south-eastern lake shore (Behrens et al. 2024b), but not in specimens collected from another nearby location (El Taher et al. 2021a). Finally, L. labiatus represents a distinct lineage within Tropheini and is without apparent sexual color dimorphism. Given the different degrees of sexual dimorphism in these species, we could address how sexual antagonism may drive sex chromosome evolution in Tropheini.

We additionally included Pseudosimochromis marginatus (Northern population), in which we previously found the LG5-19 XY system (El Taher et al. 2021a), first as a confirmation that our methods find the same results as previous studies, and second to quantify the spread of the SLR across the chromosome.

Considering their pivotal placement in the evolution of Haplochromini, characterizing sex chromosome evolution in Tropheini provides a basis for estimating the likely SD mechanism of ancestral species that radiated into the prolific cichlid assemblages of Lakes Malawi and Victoria. From this, we can better understand the genetic background through which sex chromosome systems became so varied in these unique radiations, and suggest how sex chromosomes may have contributed to the enormous success of Haplochromini.

Results

Identification of Sex-Linked Regions

We successfully identified genomic regions of sex-linkage from short-read sequencing data in all species by combining intersex FST, GWAS of sex, sex-bias of SNP density, and kmers-based GWAS (Figs. 2 and 3, Figs. S1 to S7). In P. marginatus, we recovered the same LG5-19 XY system as shown previously, confirming the validity of our methodology (Fig. S1). This result was recovered from all analyses, although the intersex FST results, which showed the LG5-19 signal, also showed noise across the whole genome, likely resulting from our strict filtering parameters heavily reducing the number of SNPs analyzed in this species (see Materials and Methods). Nevertheless, the multiple methods employed allowed us to confidently define the boundaries of the SLR, based on only positions that overlapped between all methods, to one region on LG5: 24,061,516 to 38,510,000 bp and two regions on LG19: 146,386 to 4,290,001 bp and 27,909,558 to 30,690,000 bp (Table S1). These results are indicative of a fusion of the ancestral chromosomes, as previously proposed, and our results indeed show that the boundaries of the SLR extend to the chromosome ends of LG5 and LG19, suggestive of a terminal fusion of chromosomes. In this species, we also observed a weaker signal on LG14 (34,240,000 to 34,780,000 bp) in all analyses. This region could be a separate co-occurring SLR, or a rearrangement between LG14 and the SLR in P. marginatus relative to the A. calliptera reference genome. However, this region was very narrow (∼54 kb) and not comparable to the strength of the sex-linked signals from LG5 and 19, so we did not include it as a potential SLR in further analyses.

Petrochromis polyodon also returned an SLR on LG5 (Fig. S2), however much narrower and not consistently recovered in all analyses; window based intersex FST and SNP density and kmers-GWAS analyses clearly showed this result, but SNP-based GWAS did not (Table S1; Fig. S2a–d). The heterozygosity pattern within the small region identified showed continuous heterozygosity in all but two of the male samples, and none of the female samples (Fig. S2g). Note that no strong genomic association with sex was found in the kmers-based GWAS when filtering for only the most significant P-value, as this filtering method removed too much data. Relaxing filtering to only the top 0.1% of associated kmers revealed a clear sex-linked region on LG5 from 29,686,896 to 30,571,180 bp (Fig. S2e and f).

In S. horei, our sex-linkage analysis gave unclear results when combining individuals of two populations available to us, Lufubu River and Ndole Bay. A random subset of 10,000 SNPs across the whole genome clearly separated the two populations and revealed genome-wide reduced heterozygosity in the Lufubu River population (Fig. S3). We thus analyzed the two populations separately (Figs. S4 and S5). We identified a signal on LG5 corresponding to a potential male SLR in the Lufubu River population only, from 28,286,125 to 35,390,000 bp (Fig. S4; Table S1). Here again, filtering the kmers-based GWAS results based on the lowest P-value returned too few kmers to form contigs to blast, however, filtering for the top 0.1% of kmers also confirmed the results of the SNP-based methods. The Ndole Bay population showed no clear signal of a genomic region associated with sex (Fig. S5).

Next, in L. labiatus, intriguingly, LG5 and LG7 both showed signals of sex association, however, only in analyses based on 10 kb windows (FST and SNP density) (Fig. S6a–c, Table S1). A large region of roughly 5.4 Mb at the end of LG5 and another large region of over 3.6 Mb towards the end of LG7 both indicated an XY SLR. As with the results in P. marginatus, this pattern suggests fusion of these chromosomes, with the SLR spreading over the fused region. Meanwhile, neither the SNP-based GWAS nor kmersGWAS showed a strong signal of sex association from these chromosomes, or any other chromosome (Fig. S6d–g). Visualizing heterozygosity for the regions on LG5 and LG7 identified from FST and SNP density showed strong male-specific heterozygosity separating males and females into different clusters, supporting an XY-patterning of this region (Fig. 4, Fig. S6h and i).

Finally, we found for the first time an SLR in L. dardennii of roughly 7.5 Mb on LG7 (Fig. S7; Table S1). This region was clearly observed across all analyses. A heterozygosity heatmap of the identified region perfectly clustered males and females separately, with high heterozygosity in males, supporting an XY system.

Combined, our results recovered SLRs in all species, two solely on LG5 (S. horei Lufubu River, P. polyodon), one solely on LG7 (L. dardennii), one putative joint LG5-19 system (P. marginatus), and one putative joint LG5-7 system (L. labiatus). On LG5, all SLRs overlapped, while this was not the case for the two systems involving LG7 (Figs. 2 and 3). In all species, SNP heterozygosity was consistent with male-specific heterozygosity in the SLRs, indicating that all SLRs are XY systems (Fig. 4).

Potential Candidates for Sex Determination

We searched for genes with sex-related functions within each species-specific SLR. Some genes with known function in regulation of SD and differentiation in fish were located in these: wnt4 (LG 5: 33,190,301 to 33,216,716 bp) was found within the SLRs of S. horei, L. labiatus, and P. marginatus (not P. polyodon), while TGF-ß family member bmp7b (LG5: 29,111,430 to 29,120,620 bp) was also in the regions of S. horei, P. marginatus, and P. polyodon, but not L. labiatus (Table S1). Gsdf was located within the SLR of L. dardennii, however, a coverage analysis did not indicate a male-specific duplication of gsdf, nor in any other species we investigated (Fig. S8). Instead, there appears to be a duplication of gsdf in both males and females in all species. However, we did not find increased heterozygosity of this gene compared to surrounding regions, indicating that these duplications have not diverged (Fig. S9). Additionally, several TGF-ß family members were found in the L. dardennii SLR, including smad7, smad4a, bmpr1bb, bmp10l and bmp1l (Table S1).

To complement these findings, we screened the window(s) that showed the strongest association with sex (out of intersex FST per 10 kb window and SNP density), and hence could present the oldest, most differentiated SLR and thus potentially house the SDGs (Table S1). The top windows differed between each method per species, indicating some disparity between intersex FST and SNP density. When the SLR spanned two reference LGs, we used the top window from each chromosome per method (Table S1). Genes with putative SD function are reported here, for a full gene list, see Table S1. In P. polyodon, the top SNP density window contained ndrg3a, suggested to be involved in early organ development, and possibly in spermatogenesis/male meiosis (Pan et al. 2017). Ndrg3a is also part of the Wnt signaling pathway, which is involved in gonad differentiation, particularly ovary development in mammals, actively antagonizing male development (Kim et al. 2006, Lundgaard Riis et al. 2024). This gene was not located in the top FST window (Table S1) but showed male-biased expression (see below). In S. horei, the top SNP density window on LG5 contained ncoa3, encoding a member of the p160/nuclear receptor co-activator family of transcription regulators involved in hormone response (Wagner et al. 2013). Specifically, ncoa3 is involved in estrogen and androgen signaling (Sun and Xu 2020) and male gonad development. Three windows were equally highly associated with sex in intersex FST (Table S1) and contained several genes. One of these, lrrn1, a transmembrane protein (Maunsell et al. 2023), was also identified as sex-related through a QTL study in yellow catfish (Tachysurus sinensis) (Gao et al. 2020). In L. labiatus, the top SNP density window on LG7 contained lhx5, a LIM domain homeobox gene and potential transcription regulator related to forebrain and neural retinal development (Inoue et al. 2013). In mice, LIM homeodomain (LIM-HD) family genes have been shown to have sexually dimorphic gene expression and play essential roles in sexual organ and cell development (Singh et al. 2022a). Meanwhile, the top FST window contained pmfbp1, which is essential to sperm formation and has testis-specific expression in humans (Wang et al. 2022b).

Interestingly, other genes in top associated windows were involved in visual response or eye development; in S. horei, the top FST window contained cadherin-4-like, involved in tissue development and with expression on retinal ganglion cells being essential for visual stimulus response (Olguin et al. 2025); the top SNP density window on LG5 for L. labiatus contained cldn19, a claudin family transmembrane protein, which are part of tight junctions, and is related to visual impairment in humans and expressed in the retina of zebrafish (Konrad et al. 2006, Peng et al. 2011).

To capture any sex-specific SNPs related to functional differences, we ran snpEff (Cingolani et al. 2012) on the SLR of each species extended marginally on either side (Table S2). This allowed us to identify SNPs heterozygous in males and homozygous in females that highly altered gene function. No XY-patterned SNPs with high functional effects were identified in S. horei, L. dardennii, or L. labiatus. In P. polyodon, there was a male-specific high-effect SNP in the gene LOC113022893, DEP domain-containing mTOR-interacting protein, which acts in cell signaling, including the TORC1 and TORC2 pathways (ZFIN Staff 2023). These pathways regulate cell differentiation, and TORC1 has been shown to strongly impact male fertility in zebrafish (Chen et al. 2020). In P. marginatus, three genes in the LG5 SLR had high-effect male-specific SNPs: taar10b, an amine detector involved in olfactory response (Li et al. 2015); evc, also a signaling gene involved in bone development (Ruiz-Perez et al. 2007); and tafa4b, related to response to pain and inflammation (Hoeffel et al. 2021). No role in SD is known for any of these genes.

We investigated each of these genes for sex-bias in expression within each species by reanalyzing data of El Taher et al. (2021b). Neither L. dardennii nor P. marginatus were included in this dataset; we were therefore unable to investigate gene expression in these two species. We used Pseudosimochromis babaulti as a proxy for P. marginatus, as they share the same sex chromosome and are sister species. Additionally, not all genes identified in this study were annotated in the reference used in El Taher et al. (2021b), so this analysis was only performed on those available. In S. horei, we found significant female-biased expression for wnt4 and ncoa3 and male-biased expression for ADP-ribosylation factor-like protein 8b and bmp7b (Fig. S10). In P. polyodon, bmp7b, cdh4, and ndrg3a were all male-biased (Fig. S11). In L. labiatus, rplp0, cldn19, and cdh4 were all male-biased (Fig. S12). Finally, in P. babaulti (as a proxy for P. marginatus), wnt4 and evc were both female-biased (Fig. S13).

Additionally, we compared expression of gsdf across all species, as above, and found it to be male-biased in all (Fig. S14).

Independent, Repeated Emergence of a Sex-Linked Region on LG5

Since the SLR of four of the five species overlapped to some extent on LG5 (S. horei, P. marginatus, L. labiatus, P. polyodon; overlapping region = LG5: 29,850,000 to 30,470,000 bp, Fig. 3a–c), we explored this region further to shed light on the evolutionary origin of the sex-linkage and potential candidate SDGs. Haplotype-based phylogenetic reconstruction allows assembling the evolutionary history of X-haplotypes (both haplotypes of females and one haplotype of males) and Y-haplotypes (the second haplotype of males) (Dufresnes et al. 2014). Within each species, if Y haplotypes formed a separate cluster from X haplotypes, this would confirm a separate evolutionary history of this region, i.e. the evolution of a species-specific Y chromosome. Meanwhile, if Y haplotypes of all species clustered together, this would support a single origin of the Y haplotype before speciation. If there was no clustering of haplotypes within species, this would contradict sex-linkage of the region.

Our phylogenetic reconstruction supported species-specific Y chromosome origins (Fig. 5). For L. labiatus and P. marginatus, one haplotype of the males formed its own clade sister to the remaining haplotypes within either species. In P. polyodon, we observed a similar pattern for 80% of the males. In S. horei, all male haplotypes clustered together, external to the remaining haplotypes, along with a single haplotype of one female specimen. In L. dardennii, for this LG5 region, no such pattern was seen, as expected since this species had no sex association on LG5.

Gene Function Within the Co-evolved LG5 Region Shows Association to Visual Perception, but no Apparent Sexual Antagonism Over This Function

The overlapping region on LG5 contained 43 annotated genes (Fig. S15). None of the “usual candidate” SDGs were found in this region. The TGF-β family member bmp7b, as well as wnt4, were located close to the overlapping region, but wnt4 was not found within the region of P. polyodon, and bmp7b was not within the region of L. labiatus.

We aimed to identify whether any gene in the overlapping region had XY-patterned SNPs with an effect on gene function that were shared between species. Here, we used the list of XY-patterned SNPs annotated by snpEff (see above) but expanded to all impact categories: “high”, disruptive impact on protein function; “moderate”, non-disruptive impact, but may cause change to protein effectiveness; “low”, unlikely to change protein behavior; and “modifier”, non-coding variants, no evidence of impact (Cingolani et al. 2012). Overall, we found that SNPs that were likely to affect function (only one with “high” impact and 16 with “moderate” impact) were mostly species-specific (Fig. S16a and b). This was also true for SNPs with “low” impact, while “modifier” SNPs were mostly shared amongst species (Fig. S16c and d).

Amongst the 43 genes, we identified four enriched GO terms (“DNA binding”, “phototransduction”, “visual perception” and “photoreceptor outer segment”; P = 0.01), three of which were driven by three genes, involved in functions related to visual perception (Fig. S17): the opsin genes lws (red-sensitive opsin, LG5: 30,074,467 to 30,076,799 bp), sws2a (blue-sensitive opsin-like, LG5: 30,059,325 to 30,061,723 bp) and sws2b (blue-sensitive opsin, LG5: 30,066,006 to 30,069,025 bp). Opsins are involved in visual perception and detection of light stimuli, particularly in relation to color perception. Several studies found that opsins differ between closely related species of cichlids depending on habitat and ecological niche preferences (Ricci et al. 2023). Opsin alleles and expression may differ between sexes in Pundamilia cichlids (LV), although evidence is mixed (Sabbah et al. 2010, Schneider et al. 2020, Wright et al. 2020). We thus compared the sequences of the three opsin genes between haplotypes (i.e. between X and Y sequences) and among species (Table S3, Fig. S18), but found no shared XY SNP in any opsin. We again used the results of snpEff to investigate whether any XY SNP had functional impacts on the opsins. Only in P. polyodon, three of the four XY SNPs of sws2b had “low” or “modifier” impacts with no change to protein behavior predicted. Still, one SNP (position 30,067,445) had “moderate” impact, indicating a possible change in effectiveness of the protein (Cingolani et al. 2012). In this case, this was a missense variant that was heterozygous in 80% of males and not present in females.

This missense variant translates to the known opsin mutation M229V; therefore, we specifically tested a potential effect of the missense replacement of residue 235 (M229V; numbering after bovine rhodopsin) on the spectral sensitivity of the sws2b opsin. The predictions of the spectral sensitivity (λmax) showed similar expected wavelengths for both opsin alleles (430.4 nm); therefore, the mutation M229V does not seem to affect the function of the opsin protein relevant to spectral sensitivity itself.

Sex differences between alleles related to visual preference differences have only been found in lws in Pundamilia cichlids (Seehausen et al. 2008); however, these differences were not found in lws of our species. For neither of the other opsins have sex-specific alleles related to visual preference been detected (Wright et al. 2020), and all species share the “H allele” of lws, denoting greater sensitivity to red light (Seehausen et al. 2008).

Still, to identify within-species differences in opsin expression between males and females, we analyzed gene expression data from retinal tissue (Ricci et al. 2023). We found no expression differences between sexes in any of the three opsins in any of the species with the LG5 sex-linked region (Fig. S19).

Gene Expression in the Overlapping Region Shows the Convergently Evolved Sex-Linked Region has Different Expression Patterns from the Rest of the Genome

We extended our investigation of gene expression differences to the full shared region and more tissues by reanalyzing expression data from El Taher et al. (2021b). This also allowed us to include several other species possessing the LG5-19 XY system and to compare gene expression of these species to other Tropheini without this SLR.

We first tested whether species with an XY SLR on LG5 differed in sex bias of gene expression in genes in the overlapping region compared to the rest of the chromosome, both in gonads and somatic tissues. For gonad tissue, we identified 18,692 sex-biased genes (SBGs) in the whole genome. Of these, 771 were on LG5, and 20 of those were within the overlapping SLR. In gonad tissue, LG5 had more male-biased genes overall, but the SLR had the second-highest number of female-biased genes (Fig. S20a). There was a significant difference in log2 fold change (LFC, negative values indicate male-biased expression, positive values indicate female-biased expression) of expression amongst windows on LG5 (One-Way ANOVA: F = 1.82, df = 36, P = 0.002). Four windows showed significant differences in LFC, one of which was the SLR (Fig. S20b, reduced male-bias). Compared to the rest of the genome, the strength of male-bias in the SLR was much reduced (Fig. S21a). Of the 35 annotated genes in the SLR available for gene expression examination, ten had significantly male-biased LFC, while ten were significantly female-biased (Table S9). Of the ten male-biased genes, three had potential sex-related functions: ndrg3a (see above), comtb (involved in sex differences in behavior and brain morphology (Tunbridge and Harrison 2011)), and gata1a (important for germ cell differentiation (Gonçalves et al. 2022)). Three of the female-biased genes had such functions: adnpa (regulates sex-dependent neurogenesis (Shapira et al. 2025)), traip (important in female meiosis regulation (Yuan et al. 2016)), and bcl-2-like protein 1 (LOC113022892 in the A. calliptera genome annotation; regulates cell apoptosis and may play a role in sexual dimorphism (Zup et al. 2003)). Bcl-2-like protein 1 also had extremely female-biased transcripts per million (TPM) counts (see below).

In species without the LG5 SLR, for gonad tissue, we found 16,616 SBGs, 690 of which were found on LG5, with 20 within the SLR. Again, LFC differed significantly between windows (One-Way ANOVA F = 1.93, df = 36, P = 0.001), and again the SLR had significantly different LFC compared to other windows, along with four other windows (Fig. S20d). The pattern of LFC across windows was different from species with the LG5 SLR, in that several windows had similarly high counts of female-biased genes as the SLR (Fig. S20c), but overall LFC across windows was similar to species with the LG5 SLR (Fig. S20d). In these species, the SLR was also more female-biased compared to windows across the genome, showing the same pattern as species with the LG5 SLR (Fig. S21b).

To investigate whether this difference in LFC of male-biased genes between species with or without the SLR was driven by an increase in female bias or a reduction in male bias, we compared TPMs of genes in the SLR between males and females of the two species groups, and to four species of an outgroup tribe, Eretmodini, available in the dataset (Eretmodus cyanostictus, E. marksmithi, Spathodus erythrodon and Tanganicodus irsacae). We found that neither male TPM nor female TPM were significantly reduced between species groups (male TPM, One-Way ANOVA, F = 0.033, df = 2, P = 0.9676; female TPM, One-Way ANOVA, F = 0.0034, df = 2, P = 0.9967). The TPM investigation revealed four expression clusters (Fig. S22): first, genes with significantly higher TPM in females than males in all groups (two-way ANOVA on log2 transformed means, F = 13.853, df = 1, P = 0.0004); second, genes with reduced TPM overall in outgroup females only (not statistically supported; only two genes were in this cluster). Third were genes with reduced TPM in both Tropheini female groups but higher expression in outgroup species within sexes. This cluster supported a significant effect of species group (two-way ANOVA on log2 transformed means, F = 5.897, df = 2, P = 0.011). The fourth cluster contained genes with higher TPM in males than females in all groups (two-way ANOVA on log2 transformed means, F = 14.6134, df = 1, P = 0.0003).

Of the total gonad SBGs, most were shared among all species, regardless of the SLR on LG5 (Fig. 6). This was true for genes in the whole genome, on LG5 only, and within the SDR. However, the number of genes that were uniquely sex-biased in LG5 XY species was always higher than the number of genes unique to non-LG5 species. When investigating only somatic tissue, there was very little overlap between SBGs of LG5 or not-LG5 species.

Overlap of sex-biased genes. Venn diagram of sex-biased genes in gonads reveals that these were mostly shared between species with or without the LG5 sex-linked region. This was a global pattern, shared across a) the whole genome, b) chromosome LG5, and c) within the overlapping sex-linked region. Few sex-biased genes were found in somatic tissue.

Beyond Tropheini—LG5 and LG7 as Sex Chromosomes in Haplochromini

Both chromosomes identified as sex-linked in this study are also sex chromosomes in other (Haplochromini) cichlids. While large-scale synteny is conserved across cichlid genomes (Mazzuchelli et al. 2012), previous studies relied on different genome assemblies and versions to identify sex chromosomes. We have some understanding of sex chromosomes across Haplochromini, yet a detailed analysis of LG5 and LG7 and the SLRs therein is missing. In order to integrate our findings with those on other closely related species, we first analyzed synteny between the most commonly used cichlid reference genomes to infer sex-linkage (Fig. 7). This showed a broad alignment between A. calliptera (GCF_900246225.1_fAstCal1.2 used in this study), Oreochromis niloticus (GCF_001858045.1_ASM185804v2 used in El Taher et al. (2021a) and Metriaclima zebra (GCF_000238955.4_M_zebra_UMD2a used in Behrens et al. (2024b, a) and Kocher et al. (2022), with some exceptions; the orientation of whole chromosomes (LG1, LG3-9, LG13, LG14 and LG20 between M. zebra and A. calliptera and LG2, LG5, LG9-11, LG13, LG17, LG18 and LG22 between A. calliptera and O. niloticus), small rearrangements between LG3 of M. zebra with LG8 of A. calliptera and other small inversions within chromosomes. A. calliptera and M. zebra are both part of the LM Haplochromini superflock and hence expected to share extended synteny. Meanwhile, there were many small rearrangements compared to the genome of O. niloticus, a more distantly related Oreochromini, which is an outgroup to the Great Lakes' radiations and thus to the two Haplochromini genomes.

Synteny of the three predominant reference genomes used in analyses of cichlid sex chromosomes. a) Genome-wide synteny based on gene homology between Metriaclima zebra (GCF_000238955.4_M_zebra_UMD2a), A. calliptera (GCF_900246225.1_fAstCal1.2) and Oreochromis niloticus (GCF_001858045.1_ASM185804v2). Numbers refer to chromosomes, with one additional scaffold syntenic to LG9 in M. zebra. b) Detailed syntenic plots of LGs of interest in Haplochromini sex chromosome research; LG5, LG7, and LG19.

As LG5 is of specific interest in our species, we paid closer attention to the synteny of this chromosome. LG5 was inverted in A. calliptera compared to M. zebra but without rearrangements of gene order (Fig. 7b). Between O. niloticus and A. calliptera, there were several rearrangements, such that LG5 had one large region in the center of the chromosome that maintained orientation (18,581,516 to 23,179,110 bp), but two rearranged regions at the ends of the chromosome, one maintaining gene order (6,444,878 to 18,576,836 bp), and one inverted (23,190,114 to 33,388,569 bp), with the additional fusion of part of the O. niloticus LG3b with LG5 in A calliptera. Smaller rearrangements at the chromosome tips were also observed, an inverted rearrangement found at the start of the chromosome in A. calliptera that was at the end of the chromosome in O. niloticus (3,005,898 to 6,413,560 bp) and a small region that maintained order at the end of the A. calliptera chromosome found at the beginning of the O. niloticus chromosome (33,463,748 to 36,863,705 bp). Accordingly, the order of syntenic blocks was equivalent between O. niloticus and M. zebra, with an inversion at the center. Additionally, there was a region on LG5 of A. calliptera and O. niloticus that showed no synteny to M. zebra (22,803,898 to 29,462,457 bp).

LG7 was also of interest in our species, and this chromosome showed less conserved synteny between assemblies than LG5 (Fig. 7b). LG7 is the largest chromosome in the genomes of all species. Between M. zebra and A. calliptera, the chromosome was inverted, with a small central synteny block that maintained gene order (39,649,650 to 42,214,014 bp), but apart from this there were no large rearrangements between these assemblies. LG7 of O. niloticus, however, was very differently arranged. There were two large inversions near either end of the chromosome (24,888 to 5,243,756 bp; 46,474,133 to 60,741,899 bp), with several rearrangements within these, compared to the other two assemblies, while the central region (16,529,244 to 43,789,894 bp) was mostly syntenic, with some rearrangements. At one end of the chromosome was a smaller rearranged region between O. niloticus and A. calliptera in which the gene order differed but was not inverted (60,713,795 to 68,367,280 bp).

We compared the shared SLR of the LT LG5 XY species and the two regions on LG7 to other Haplochromini species for which SLRs have been found on these chromosomes, albeit using different assemblies. To this aim, we translated originally reported locations using our synteny data to A. calliptera assembly coordinates (Tables S4 to S6). This revealed that no previously identified LG5 SLR in other species overlapped with the region we identified, except for Tropheini species (El Taher et al. 2021a, Behrens et al. 2024b).

Several LV and LM species have an LG7 XY as their SD system. A 10 Mb XY region in Astatoreochromis alluaudi (Kocher et al. 2022) overlaps with both the regions we identified in L. dardennii and L. labiatus. Also, we saw a partial overlap between the LG7 region in L. labiatus and the LG7 XY system in P. philander (Böhne et al. 2019). Several LM species, including A. calliptera, were postulated to rely on a Y-specific duplication of gsdf (located at chr7: 18,089,346 to 18,092,582 bp in the A. calliptera genome) as their SD system, which is found within the SLR of L. labiatus. However, the region described as sex-associated in A. calliptera and M. mbenji, based on the O. niloticus reference genome (Peterson et al. 2017), did not overlap with L. labiatus. We suggest that this is due to the broad lack of synteny between O. niloticus and the Haplochromini genomes, and differing limitations of SLRs based on reference genome usage. However, our coverage analysis did not reveal a male-specific gsdf duplication, as shown above.

Discussion

Comparative analyses of sex chromosomes across closely related taxa allow insights into several questions of their evolutionary origin. Extending previous findings, we identified additional sex chromosomes in the cichlid tribe Haplochromini and addressed how the drivers of sex-determining regions can be shared between species, yet the evolutionary consequences follow species-specific trajectories. We identified a core SLR on LG5 across several species, seemingly independently evolved. We also found the first evidence for LG7 as a sex chromosome in the LT endemic representatives of this tribe, the Tropheini, and with it another putative case of a neo-sex chromosome emerged through chromosomal fusion. Our approach to define the SLR in each species was supported by evidence from at least two separate analyses, but in three of five species, by all analyses: intersex FST, GWAS, SNP density, and kmersGWAS. We also corroborated our results with SNP heterozygosity heatmaps that showed male-specific heterozygosity in the SLRs of all species. These results relied upon mapping to a non-self-reference genome, therefore, are limited by potential rearrangements between the reference and focal species, though this is partly alleviated by the kmersGWAS method. We employed strict filtering parameters to reduce noise and spurious signals of sex-linkage, and, combined with the multiple approaches, expect these results to be a robust identification of SLRs in all species.

Repeated, Independent Evolution of LG5 as a Sex Chromosome in Tropheini, but not Due to Sexual Antagonism

We confirmed previous results of an LG5-19 XY system in P. marginatus, further supporting a fusion between these chromosomes (El Taher et al. 2021a). Generation of long-read data or chromosome-level assembly of this species would allow comprehensive confirmation of this fusion; however, it is beyond the scope of the present study. This system has a shared origin with several members of Tropheini in the genera Pseudosimochromis, Petrochromis, and Interochromis (El Taher et al. 2021a). We show, through synteny analyses, that the regions assigned as sex-linked in our samples broadly overlap with those found previously (El Taher et al. 2021a; Behrens et al. 2024b), confirming the validity of our approach to correctly identify SLRs; accordingly, we were able to define the SLRs in P. polyodon, L. dardennii, L. labiatus, and in one population of S. horei.

Our new results allow us to place the origin of the presumably fused LG5-19 system to the node after the split of L. labiatus (Fig. 1). We suggest that this probably coincided with further chromosomal rearrangements since in L. labiatus, LG5 might be fused to LG7 whereas it is seemingly fused with LG19 in the genera Petrochromis, Interochromis, and Pseudosimochromis (Fig. 1). We further propose that the LG5-19 XY system was lost in the last common ancestor of S. horei, J. pfefferi and L. dardennii, which form a sister clade to the genus Pseudosimochromis and all have species-specific sex chromosomes (El Taher et al. 2021a, this study). Whether this was accompanied by further chromosome scale rearrangements remains to be investigated. A further loss of the XY LG5-19 seemingly occurred in Simochromis diagramma. In P. polyodon, LG5 remains sex-linked while LG19 is no longer. The LG5 region in this species overlaps with the LG5 region of all LG5-19 species, but is much smaller, and our phylogenetic reconstruction suggests that it emerged after speciation. Therefore, it is possible that LG5-19 was also lost in P. polyodon and the LG5 region reemerged independently.

While the XY LG5-19 system was likely lost at the base of the clade containing S. horei, J. pfefferi and L. dardennii, we again found a small region on LG5 to be sex-linked in one population of S. horei. Population-level differences in sex chromosomes are known from other Haplochromini (Böhne et al. 2016, Böhne et al. 2019). In the lake population of S. horei, we found no evidence of genotypic sex-linkage. Yet, in the population from Lufubu River, we identified an SLR. This population was highly homozygous, suggestive of a strong, recent bottleneck which might have facilitated fixation of the SLR. Differences in SD mechanisms and expansion of sex-linkage between lake and river populations of the same species are not uncommon (Böhne et al. 2016, Triay et al. 2022, Lichilín et al. 2023), presumably related to different ecological selection pressures (Böhne et al. 2019). In S. horei, this LG5 region might not be fully sex-linked since one female haplotype clustered with the Y haplotypes. However, a similar SLR was found in S. horei samples from the opposite shore of LT at Kalambo Falls (Behrens et al. 2024b). In both the current study and Behrens et al. (2024b), the region with the highest density of male-biased SNPs contained the gene ncoa3, involved in the regulation of transcription and response to steroids in fish (Proffitt and Smith 2025). Note that the region found by Behrens et al. is much smaller than the region we recovered, and that the region from their samples does not contain the known SDG wnt4 but does also contain bmp7b. We propose that this sex-linked region is ancestral to S. horei and was likely lost in the Ndole Bay population investigated by us. Interestingly, the Kalambo Falls population is located near the mouth of the Kalambo River. Possibly, this SLR provides some advantage in riverine populations but is not selected for in the lake environment. Alternatively, the potential bottleneck we detected for the Lufubu River population could indicate that drift rather than selection explains why this region has not been lost. Further sampling of the river and lake could establish which of these scenarios is most likely.

In L. labiatus, the identified SLR spanned LG5 and LG7 with consistent sex-linkage across all individuals and both chromosomal regions. We suggest that this indicates a chromosomal rearrangement relative to the A. calliptera reference genome used here, as in the LG5-19 system in P. marginatus and in an LG5-14 XY system in Astatotilapia burtoni (Böhne et al. 2016, Roberts et al. 2016). While we cannot unambiguously confirm fusions without long-read data or chromosome-level assembly or karyotype information, this would suggest neo-sex chromosome formation in this species and a third case for chromosome 5 to be involved in such fusions. The LG7 sex-linked portion in L. labiatus did not overlap with that found in L. dardennii, again alluding to independent origins of these SD systems. The LG5 region, however, overlaps with the LG5 region identified in other species, but our phylogenetic reconstruction and SNP analysis suggest an independent origin. We have no indication that the potential LG5-7 fusion is present in any other species. The SLR across the two chromosomes is also large (∼9 Mb) and comparable in size to the LG5-19 region from other Tropheini, further supporting a fusion. Fusion of sex chromosomes to autosomes is predicted to be the most frequent type of chromosome fusion in the genome and may be beneficial to reduce the segregation load on the sex chromosome (Charlesworth and Charlesworth 1980, Matsumoto and Kitano 2016). Additionally, neo-sex chromosome formation is thought to create a strong reproductive barrier by disrupting the inheritance of genes related to reproduction or mating (Wang et al. 2022a). Therefore, a fusion may have driven the expansion of recombination suppression in both L. labiatus and the LG5-19 species, explaining why the SLRs in these species are so large, while the LG5 sex-linked regions of P. polyodon and S. horei have not expanded. Note that S. horei has a reduced karyotype number (2n = 40) compared to other Haplochromini, but whether LG5 is fused is unknown (Ozouf-Costaz et al. 2017). Recombination at telomeres is lower than in the rest of the chromosome (Claussin and Chang 2015), also shown in cichlids (Conte et al. 2021), and low recombination rates in particular chromosomal regions are known to explain the spread of SLRs (Xue et al. 2021). Therefore, the fusion of chromosomes may propagate regions of suppressed recombination in which sex–linkage could expand. However, the lack of conclusive support for the fusion from long-read data limits this proposition to only speculative.

Whether sex-linkage first originated on LG5 in the LG5-19 species is unclear, as male-bias in SNP density and FST are even across both LG5-mapped and LG19-mapped regions. Therefore, it is not possible to conclude whether the origin of recombination suppression was on LG5 in all species in which it is a sex chromosome. However, even if this was the case, it is clear that sex-linkage on LG5 emerged independently at least four times in Tropheini, and that this chromosome harbors loci that prime it for this function.

Identification of putative SDGs on LG5 could explain why this sex chromosome is so prevalent. Two putative candidates for SDGs were found within the SLRs of three species each. Wnt4 was located within the SLR of S. horei, L. labiatus, and P. marginatus, a gene involved in sexual development in many species (Jeays-Ward et al. 2004), and essential for ovarian tissue development (Boyer et al. 2010). Located on the sex chromosomes, it has been proposed as a key SDG in the spotted knifejaw by regulating female differentiation and antagonizing male pathways (Zhao et al. 2025). Interestingly, this gene was not located within the SLR of S. horei in the samples studied by Behrens et al. (2024b). Direction of gene expression differed between species for this gene, and while it was female-biased in the proxy for P. marginatus and in S. horei, overall expression counts were low. Bmp7b, meanwhile, was also found in the regions of S. horei and P. marginatus, as well as P. polyodon. Bmp7b, a member of the TGF-ß gene family, functions in the development of many organs, including skeletal development (Nifuji and Noda 1999), cardiac regeneration (Bongiovanni et al. 2024), and eye function (Dong et al. 2022). This gene was male-biased in all three species, though not significantly in the proxy for P. marginatus. It has been proposed to play a role in sex differentiation in chicken (Hoshino et al. 2005) and mice (Ross et al. 2007) and in response to androgen hormones in medaka, regulating the development of secondary sexual characteristics (Ogino et al. 2014). The TGF-ß pathway is known to regulate SD and differentiation in many fish species and regulates expression of wnt4, though note that not all members of the TGF-ß family members are directly involved in SD. Without clear experimental evidence, the role of these genes in SD in the here studied species remains speculative.

We did not find strong putative SDGs in the most sex-biased windows of the SLRs, nor by looking at XY SNPs with highly disruptive effects. However, some of the genes identified could have potential sex-determining roles; in P. polyodon, ndrg3 and LOC113022893 DEP domain-containing mTOR-interacting protein, both have potential sex-related roles (Pan et al. 2017, Chen et al. 2020), and ndrg3 had significantly male-biased expression, in line with its putative function in male gametogenesis, but more evidence is required to determine whether either gene could be a key regulator of SD. In L. labiatus, while rplp0 has no known SD role in fish, several other ribosomal protein genes were proposed to play a role in tilapia SD (Kuang et al. 2020), and rplp0 showed male-biased expression. Likewise, in S. horei, ADP-ribosylation factor-like protein 8b was identified; ADP-ribosylation factors have known roles in female meiosis and male spermiogenesis in mice (Wang et al. 2009, Qi et al. 2013), and it was male-biased in this species. Cdh4-like was also identified; cadherins are essential for gonad structural integrity, and this cadherin is involved in both ovary maintenance and sperm survival (Piprek et al. 2020, Munakata et al. 2025). This gene was only sex-biased in P. polyodon and L. labiatus, in both cases male-biased. Finally, ncoa3 is involved in estrogen signaling as well as many female differentiation pathways, but this is mostly known from mammals (Xu et al. 2000, Gupta et al. 2016): in line with this, this gene had strongly female-biased expression. While intriguing, only ncoa3 was found within this overlapping region, and none of these genes were commonly identified between species, so it does not seem that a sole, potent SDG drove the repeated emergence of this sex chromosome. In summary, most of the putative candidate SDGs had significantly female-biased expression (8 vs. 3), including wnt4 in P. babaulti (proxy for P. marginatus) and S. horei.

No XY-patterned functionally disruptive SNPs in the overlapping SLR were shared between all species, and few were shared between two or more species. Still, genes with SNPs with modified impact were mostly shared between species. This is likely due to the few genes in this region (43) and the high chance of acquiring deleterious mutations in non-recombining regions, leading to reduced efficacy of purging. Note that the genes with overlapping functional impacts counted here do not mean the same SNP was involved in producing the impact in all species; out of 2,358 annotated impacts on genes, only ten involved the same SNP between more than one species, five between P. polyodon and P. marginatus, and five between P. polyodon and L. labiatus.

We identified three opsin genes within the region, for which there is strong evidence of a role in sexual selection in cichlids, and therefore possibly loci under sexually antagonistic selection (Seehausen et al. 2008, Wright et al. 2020). Yet, we did not find functional disruption between X and Y alleles, nor did we find differences in gene expression between males and females. Therefore, although interesting that visual perception-related genes were found in this region, it does not seem that they are under sexual antagonism. We identified other genes in the SLRs also related to visual function, cdh4-like, lhx5, and cldn19, of which only cdh4-like was within the overlapping region. Thus, while visual response could be under sexually antagonistic selection, it seems unlikely that this drove the independent emergence of the SLR.

Interestingly, in all Tropheini species, with or without the LG5 SLR, LFC of genes from gonad tissue was less male-biased in the SLR compared to the rest of LG5 and the genome. Overall, the genome was male-biased in expression, while the SLR on average was closer to neutral expression. TPM of SLR genes, meanwhile, showed that both groups of Tropheini typically shared gene expression patterns between sexes, compared to the outgroup.

Sex-biased genes themselves were overall highly shared between species with and without the LG5 region, therefore, it is most parsimonious that the gene expression pattern of this region has not changed with sex-linkage. Sex-biased genes from somatic tissue were not shared between species, in line with our previous findings in Tropheini (Lichilín et al. 2021). Overall, the comparative data suggest that the reduced male bias in gene expression in the SLR predates the region becoming sex-linked and might coincide with the origin of Tropheini. The expression pattern may support the repeated co-option of sex-linkage of this region; the X chromosome in an XY pair spends more time in females, and in such species, feminization of the X chromosome is frequently observed (Hsiung et al. 2025). By having already reduced male-biased expression, these genes may more readily become feminized, or perform female-specific functions. However, more detailed assessment of gene expression and functional analyses of these genes are required to elucidate whether this is indeed the case.

The varied degree of sexual dimorphism in the species analyzed, and the lack of coloration-related genes in the overlapping SLR, make it unlikely that sexual antagonism drove its repeated co-option. Indeed, there is overall a lack of direct evidence that sexual antagonism drives sex chromosome evolution in fish (Bergero et al. 2019, Dagilis et al. 2022), but this is often hampered by the difficulty in accurately detecting sexual antagonism (Smith et al. 2023).

Without clear evidence from gene function, another driver of sex-linkage in this region could be preexisting heterochiasmy between sexes, enabling rapid fixation of an SLR (Dufresnes et al. 2021). This is evidenced by Atlantic halibut, in which existing regions of low recombination between males and females allowed the invasion and fixation of a sex determiner (Edvardsen et al. 2022). Recombination is known to differ along chromosomes in fish, with higher recombination at centromeres in females and at telomeres in males (Sutherland et al. 2017, Sardell et al. 2018). While there is no evidence for general heterochiasmy in cichlids (Feulner et al. 2018, Conte et al. 2019, El Taher et al. 2021a), this phenomenon can differ between genera (Walter et al. 2004, Bergero et al. 2019). More studies of recombination mapping in cichlids would help to understand this dynamic. Ultimately, it is possible that the overlap of sex-linkage on LG5 is due to chance. The total size of LG5 is ∼38.7 Mb; ∼14.5 Mb is sex-linked in P. marginatus, ∼5.4 Mb in L. labiatus, ∼7.1 Mb in S. horei, and ∼1.8 Mb in P. polyodon. Of these, only 620 kb overlap. Still, the fact that these species share LG5 as a sex chromosome supports that this is not random (El Taher et al. 2021a).

Our results also maintain that male heterogamety is universal in Tropheini, though not in Haplochromini. While heterogametic transitions could result from sexually antagonistic selection (van Doorn and Kirkpatrick 2010), maintenance of heterogamety with sex chromosome turnover has several potential causes. Strong degeneration of a sex chromosome limits heterogametic transitions due to gene loss and functional degradation of the Y/W chromosome, making it unsuitable as a homogametic sex chromosome (Saunders et al. 2019, but see Veller et al. 2017). Additionally, dosage compensation, meiotic regulation, or gene expression regulation may prevent heterogametic transitions that would disrupt these mechanisms (Ellegren 2011, Mank et al. 2014). Finally, heterochiasmy between sexes limits heterogametic transitions due to the existing suppression of recombination between sex chromosomes (though ZW systems can still exist under male-specific achiasmy, (Dufresnes et al. 2021)). In Tropheini, we show that sex chromosomes are mostly homomorphic and not strongly degenerated, therefore not limited to male heterogamety by decay, nor do we find evidence for dosage compensation. Rather, maintenance of male heterogamety could substantiate the assertion that reduced recombination in males may be playing a role in sex chromosome evolution in these fish, though gene regulatory effects may also be important.

Further Evidence that LG7 XY is not the Ancestral Sex-Determining System in Modern Haplochromines

In only one of the studied species, L. dardennii, LG7 uniquely functions as an XY system. As mentioned, LG7 is also sex-linked in L. labiatus, but here together with LG5, and in a different region. LG7 is a sex chromosome in several Haplochromini, although not found in Tropheini until this study. In general, LG7 is one of the most frequently identified and independently emerged sex chromosomes of East African cichlids (Böhne et al. 2019, El Taher et al. 2021a). LG7 XY is the likely ancestral sex chromosome of LM Haplochromini (Ser et al. 2010), coupled to a male-specific gsdf duplication (Munby et al. 2021). However, this system is independent of the XY LG7 system described in P. philander (Böhne et al. 2019), therefore questioning whether this could be the ancestral sex chromosome of all modern Haplochromini (Behrens et al. 2024a). Interestingly, the LG7 XY region in P. philander spans the same SLR as in L. dardennii. L. dardennii is nested within Tropheini and is seemingly the only species of this LT tribe with LG7 XY. Still intriguing is the fact that we found gsdf to have increased coverage indicative of a duplication in L. dardennii but no difference between sexes. We show that this same apparent gene duplication is present in all other species we studied here, including those without an LG7 XY system. Furthermore, we found strong male-specific expression of this gene in all species, and no evidence of divergence between copies of gsdf, based on SNP heterozygosity. Corroborating and extending the results of Munby et al. (2021), we suggest that the duplication of gsdf might thus be an ancestral condition in (modern) Haplochromini, but that it only became male-specific in the ancestor of the LM radiation. The lack of overlap between the most prevalent LG7 XY system and the identified region in L. dardennii further alludes to this not being the ancestral sex chromosome in modern Haplochromini.

Given the lack of overlap between the sex-linked portion of LG7 in these species, we assume that sex chromosomes in L. labiatus and L. dardennii emerged independently of one another. As with the LG5 XY system previously discussed, evolution of the LG7 XY system seems unrelated to sexually antagonistic selection on sex-related traits, but rather through processes related to genomic architecture such as recombination rate and gene duplications. The ultimate drivers of these SD systems remain to be discovered.

Options of Sex Chromosomes are Limited, but not by the Same Sex-Determining Genes

LG5 and LG7 are the two most frequently evolved sex chromosomes across East African cichlids (Behrens et al. 2024a). While this would be in line with the theory that the diversity of sex chromosomes is limited by the finite pool of genes that can act as sex determiners (Marshall Graves and Peichel 2010), we did not find evidence for this. We show that species with the same sex chromosome, even when containing the same chromosomal region, do not necessarily share the same underlying SD mechanism. We show that sex-specific SNPs vary among species and rarely involve the same impact on the same genes. Further, we were able to identify many genes with possible sex-related function, and members of known sex differentiation pathways (TGF-ß, Wnt-signaling, TOR) in the SLRs but rarely shared between species. Together, this shows once more that SD is not limited to a single mechanism.

Conclusion

We were able to identify sex chromosomes in all of the species here studied, apart from the Ndole Bay population of S. horei. Prior to this study, sex chromosomes of roughly one third of species of the Lake Tanganyika cichlid radiation had been identified. We thus provide an incentive to re-analyse species that still lack sex chromosome discoveries, using a combined methodological approach and greater depth of sampling and sequencing as done here, to fully comprehend the evolution of sex chromosomes in these species. Our approach has not only been able to identify sex chromosomes, but also to delimit the spread of recombination suppression along the sex chromosomes. In the species that share the same sex chromosome, we show that the expansion of this region differs greatly, and that the origin of this region is likely independent. However, there are still many questions as to why this region has repeatedly evolved, and why it has fixed over a larger proportion of the chromosome in some species, but remains small in others. We also show how chromosomal fusions may contribute to expansion of SLRs, but lack of whole-genome sequences coupled to karyotypic information in many cichlids inhibits our ability to determine the full extent of this phenomenon. Thus, Lake Tanganyika cichlids still provide much more opportunity to uncover the dynamics of sex chromosome evolution in a highly diverse species group.

Materials and Methods

Sequence Data

We included sequence data for six males and six females of L. dardennii and five males and seven females of P. marginatus North, provided by Walter Salzburger (Souza et al. in prep, BioProject ID PRJNA1460307). These fish are part of the cichlid collection of the University of Basel, Switzerland. DNA from fin clips stored in ethanol was extracted using the Qiagen Blood and tissue kit, including RNase A treatment. Library preparation and Illumina paired-end 150 bp sequencing aiming at 10 × coverage were performed by BMK Genomics, Münster, Germany. Additionally, we included available sequencing data of L. labiatus (4 males, 5 females), P. polyodon (10 males, 7 females), and S. horei (12 males, 12 females) (BioProject IDs L. labiatus and P. polyodon PRJEB25984; S. horei PRJNA485198).

Read Quality Filtering and Mapping to Reference Genome

We assessed raw read quality using FastQC 0.11.9 (Andrews 2010) and removed adapters with Trimmomatic 0.39 (ILLUMINACLIP:TruSeq_PE_Primer_Multiplex.fasta:2:30:12:8:true MINLEN:30) (Bolger et al. 2014). No samples were removed at the QC stage. We mapped all samples to the reference genome of the Haplochromini A. calliptera (NCBI RefSeq GCF_900246225.5), an equidistant outgroup to all focal species (Schedel et al. 2019). Mapping was performed using BWA-MEM2 2.2.1 (Vasimuddin et al. 2019), resulting BAM files were sorted and indexed using samtools 1.10 (Danecek et al. 2021). We marked duplicates, added read groups, and validated with picard tools 2.22.0 (Broad Institute 2019). We assessed mapping quality and error rate with samtools. Mapping rate was 97% −99% for all samples (Table S7).

SNP Calling and Filtering

For SNP-based analyses, we produced a variant VCF file per species. Variants were called using GATKs Haplotypecaller (GATK4 1.4.1; McKenna et al. 2010), parallelized per chromosome and per individual. Individual VCFs were combined by chromosome using GATKs CombineGVCFs function, then assigned genotypes using GATKs GenotypeGVCFs, and finally merged into a genome-wide file for each species using GATK's CatVariants. We stringently filtered the resulting VCFs to reduce noise and decrease spurious signals of sex-linkage (MQ < 40; FS > 40; SOR>9.0; QRankSum < −12.5; ReadPosRankSum < −8.0; and 199 QD < 2, adding a species-specific depth filter (Table S7), using GATKs VariantFiltration and SelectVariants functions. We further filtered out low-quality genotypes (FMT/DP < 3 | FMT/GQ < 20) and retained only biallelic sites using bcftools (bcftools 1.19; Danecek et al. 2021).

We removed sites with missing data using vcftools 0.1.14 (Danecek et al. 2011); this was applied differently for each species depending on the distribution of missing data. For L. dardennii and P. marginatus, we removed sites that were missing in over 80% of samples, resulting in 1,092,488 and 144,379 SNPs in the final VCF files, respectively. Fewer resulting SNPs for P. marginatus were due to lower read number and resultantly higher quantity of missing data. For L. labiatus, P. polyodon, and S. horei, we removed sites that were missing in any sample, resulting in, 1,666,242, 2,265,175, and 1,905,490 SNPs remaining in the final VCF files for these species, respectively.

Identification of Sex-Linked Genomic Regions

To identify SLRs, we followed the workflow of the SexFindR pipeline (Grayson et al. 2022); a combination of SNP-based and kmers-based approaches to locate regions of divergence between male and female genomes. These are: 1. SNP-based methods (intersex FST, genome-wide association study (GWAS) of sex, and SNP density), and 2. kmers-based GWAS, which identifies differences in kmer presence between sexes, from kmer catalogs derived directly from read data. We did not use the coverage-based approach of SexFindR, as previous investigation of these species did not find heteromorphic sex chromosomes (El Taher et al. 2021a).

SNP-Based Methods to Identify Sex-Differentiated Regions

We ran intersex FST, both per site and per 10 kb window; GWAS between sexes; and differences in average SNP density of males and females in 10 kb windows. FST was calculated using vcftools 0.1.16. For GWAS, we generated a plink file using vcftools, used plink 1.90beta6.16 (Chang et al. 2015) to call male and female populations, and then analyzed genome-wide association with sex in gemma 0.98.3 (Zhou and Stephens 2012). SNP density was analyzed separately per individual in 10 kb windows using vcftools 0.1.16. Average SNP density of males and females was calculated per window, and further inspected in R 4.3.0 (R Core Team 2021). To identify windows with significantly higher differences in average SNP density, a permutation test was run, generating 1,000 permutations to determine expected SNP density per window, and we tested for a significant difference (P < 0.001) from the observed difference in SNP density. All results were visualized with R 4.5.1.

Kmers-Based Method to Identify Sex-Linked Regions

The kmers GWAS approach is able to identify small regions of differentiation between males and females while avoiding reference genome bias (Grayson et al. 2022). We generated lists of kmers from filtered reads per individual using kmc 3.2.1 (Kokot et al. 2017), and then used the kmersGWAS pipeline (Voichek and Weigel 2020) to build a table of sex-specific kmers. We identified kmers associated with sex using plink 1.90beta6.16 and filtered for kmers with only the most significant association with sex per species, based on the lowest P-value observed. We assembled these kmers into contigs using ABySS 2.0.2 (Jackman et al. 2017) and blasted them against the A. calliptera reference genome using blast + 2.9.0 (Camacho et al. 2009). P-values were used for filtering, and the numbers of resulting kmers and contigs are listed in Table S8. We then filtered the blast results further by length and removed any alignments with mismatches.

We defined SLRs based only on sites that were defined as sex linked based on multiple analyses. In three of the five species, all SNP-based (intersex FST, GWAS, and SNP density) and kmers-based (kmersGWAS) approaches gave clear and consistent results. However, in some species, clear results were only obtained for per-window analyses (intersex FST and SNP density), while results of the kmersGWAS and GWAS were less obvious. Therefore, for each species, we defined the boundaries of the SLR as the minimum region overlapping between intersex FST and SNP density and, where possible, GWAS and kmersGWAS. Following these analyses, putative SLRs were inspected for their heterozygosity patterns to confirm the delimitation. VCF files were filtered to the potential SLRs. The genotypes of these SNPs were assigned as either heterozygous, homozygous reference, or homozygous alternative, and plotted in R 4.5.1 using pheatmap 1.0.13 (Kolde 2025). Genes located within each of the SLRs were investigated to determine if any could play a role in SD, using the annotation available for A. calliptera.

Comparison of the Sex-Linked Regions Among Species

For L. labiatus, P. polyodon, P. marginatus and S. horei, regions of XY-patterned sex-linkage overlapped the same location of the A. calliptera reference genome. Locations of genes in this region were visualized using MG2C (Chao et al. 2021). To investigate whether this represented conservation of ancestral sex chromosomes or independent evolution of the same region between species, we reconstructed a phylogeny for this region in these species, using Paracyprichromis brieni, a member of another cichlid lineage with a different sex chromosome, as an outgroup. The phylogeny was constructed from the individual haplotypes of the overlapping SLR per species, in order to time the cessation of recombination between male and female haplotypes (i.e. X and Y chromosomes) with respect to speciation. We subset each species' VCF to the overlapping SLR, extended to the nearest 100 kb. We then phased the haplotypes using a custom Ruby script that assigned each SNP to haplotype 1 or 2 based on the heterogamety, found from the SNP-based and heterozygosity analyses described above. The resulting haplotype fasta files were then concatenated into a single fasta file and a phylogeny was reconstructed using IQTree2 2.2.2.7 with 1,000 bootstrap replicates (Minh et al. 2020).

Gene ontology (GO) terms were identified using the R package enrichplot 1.28.4 (Guangchuang 2026). Significantly enriched GO terms were plotted in R 4.5.1. Functional impacts of SNPs on genes within the SLR of each species were annotated with snpEff 3.5a (Cingolani et al. 2012) on a VCF filtered to the sex-linked region +/− 100 kb. We ran snpEff against the reference database fAstCal1.99 and filtered the resulting annotation file for SNPs that were heterozygous in males and homozygous in females in R 4.5.1.

For the three opsin genes, sws2b, sws2a, and lws, we produced haplotype phased fasta files as described above. We then inspected these sequences for XY-patterned SNPs (i.e. heterozygous in males and homozygous in females) in AliView 1.28 (Larsson 2014). To investigate the effects on spectral sensitivity of variants between X and Y sequences, we used the machine-learning tool OPTICS (Frazer and Oakley 2026) to predict the λmax value (wavelength of maximum light absorption) of the two different male haplotypes. We selected the model trained on the whole dataset and augmented by Mine-n-Match (MNM) data and used the option to specifically account for amino acid physicochemical properties (AA_prop).

We reanalyzed previously published gene expression data for retina samples (Ricci et al. 2023) by loading the published raw read count data in R 4.5.1 followed by a DESeq2 analysis (DEseq2 1.48.1, Love et al. 2014) such that we filtered the data for each species used in this study individually, and assigned sex based on Table S1 in Ricci et al. (2023). We then created a DESeq model of gene expression with sex as the explanatory variable. We retained only genes with at least five counts across at least three samples. We then tested for a significant effect of sex on gene expression by using a generalized linear mixed model with a negative binomial distribution, following the standard protocol of DESeq. This resulted in a P-value for significance and a log fold change value for each gene. We concluded that opsins were significantly differentially expressed genes if they had an adjusted P-value of less than 0.05 and an absolute log-fold change in expression of more than 1.

We also investigated differential gene expression of all genes in the overlapping SLR across multiple tissues by reanalyzing the gene expression dataset published by El Taher et al. (2021b). We extracted the counts of the DESeq object using the DESeq2 command counts() from the data provided in the Dryad repository https://doi.org/10.5061/dryad.fj6q573sj. This dataset did not comprise all our focal species, however several other Tropheini. We thus decided to compare gene expression of the overlapping SLR between species with LG5 as a sex chromosome and those with a different or unknown sex chromosome. The LG5-sex chromosome species (with sex chromosomes in brackets) were S. horei (LG5), Interochromis loocki (LG5-19), L. labiatus (LG5-7), Petrochromis famula (LG5-19), Petrochromis fasciolatus (LG5-19), P. polyodon (LG5), P. babaulti (LG5-19) and P. babaulti (South) (LG5-19). Those without LG5 as the sex chromosome were Petrochromis ephippium (NA/unknown), Simochromis diagramma (NA/unknown), Tropheus sp. “black” (LG19), J. pfefferi (LG11-15), Tropheus moorii (NA/unknown), and Petrochromis macrognathus (NA/unknown).

We separately analyzed the dataset, including species with the overlapping LG5 region and those without. For each, we created a DESeq object with only gonad tissue and one with only somatic tissue. For the gonad tissue model, species and sex were included as explanatory variables, while for the somatic model, tissue was additionally included as an explanatory variable, along with sex and species. We removed genes with fewer than five counts across fewer than three samples. We tested for significance within DESeq as above to determine contrast between sexes (ovary expression/testes expression), accounting for the other variables in our models, with a negative LFC indicating male-biased expression and a positive LFC indicating female-biased expression. Genes were considered sex-biased if they had an adjusted P-value of less than 0.05 and an absolute log-fold change in expression of more than 1. We then tested for differences in sex-biased expression between the overlapping SLR and the remaining sequence content of LG5. As El Taher et al. (2021a), used a different reference genome to us (O. niloticus GCF_001858045.1_ASM185804v2), we defined the SLR window based on the syntenic region containing homologous genes as found in our study. In O. niloticus, this matched 35 genes (see Table S9). We defined sex-biased genes as above (negative LFC for male-biased expression, positive LFC for female-biased expression), subsetted the data, respecting gene order, into windows of the same number of genes as in the SLR, with one window containing only the genes in the overlapping SLR as a comparison. We then counted the number of sex-biased genes in each window and calculated the mean LFC per window. We performed a One-Way ANOVA test on the LFC per window. We further visualized the mean LFC of the SLR against ten random windows of the same number of genes per chromosome.

We also compared TPM (transcripts per million) of genes in the SLR between species with and without the LG5 SLR, and to four members of an outgroup tribe, Eretmodini (Eretmodus cyanostictus, Eretmodus marksmithi, Spathodus erythrodon and Tanganicodus irsacae). For this, we used TPM data available in the Dryad repository above. We tested whether there was a difference in TPM per gene within each sex between species groups using a One-Way ANOVA on TPMs for each sex individually.

To compare the SLRs found in our study to those of previous studies, we compared synteny between the reference genomes used in each study, using GENESPACE 1.4 (Lovell et al. 2022) in R 4.1.2. These were A. calliptera (GCF_900246225.1, this study), Oreochromis niloticus (GCF_001858045.2, El Taher et al. 2021a), and Metriaclima zebra (GCF_000238955.4, Behrens et al. 2024b). We ran GENESPACE using default parameters, and visualized synteny using the plot_riparian function. To gain precise synteny of the SLRs, we inspected the output syntenicBlock_coordinates.csv file produced by GENESPACE.
