Lung cancer multidisciplinary care status and evidence-practice gap: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Yunfeng Xu, Zhaofei Liu, Renjian Han, Yuliu Zhang, Ronggui Sun, Junping Wei, Xinhao Fu, Maomao Han, Yue Lu, Xia Hu, Changhe Ouyang, Dong Roman Xu, Kongqi Han, Run Wang

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide, with substantial variations in incidence, stage at diagnosis and outcomes across regions. High-quality, standardised diagnosis and treatment are essential to improving survival and quality of life. Multidisciplinary care (MDC) is a cornerstone of cancer care; however, its implementation faces barriers such as limited resources, uneven policy support and inequitable access to specialised care, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. To date, no global scoping review has systematically mapped lung cancer MDC—covering implementation models, facilitators, barriers, policy frameworks, technology integration and evidence gaps. This review seeks to fill that gap and inform the optimisation of MDC models.Methods and analysisThe Joanna Briggs Institute methodological framework, underpinned by the framework of Arksey and O’Malley and enhanced by Levac and colleagues, will be used for this scoping review. Results will be reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). We will perform a comprehensive search of six electronic databases (PubMed, CINAHL, Scopus, CNKI, VIP and WanFang) and grey literature sources (ClinicalTrials.gov). In addition, we will screen the reference lists of included studies for further relevant publications. Two review authors will independently conduct the screening and data extraction processes; discrepancies will be resolved through consensus or discussion with a third review author. Findings will be presented graphically and tabularly, together with a narrative description.Ethics and disseminationWe will collect all data from published and grey literature, meaning ethical approval is not necessary. Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide.1 In 2020, an estimated 19.3 million new cancer cases and nearly 10 million cancer deaths were reported globally.1 Lung cancer accounted for approximately 2.21 million new cases (11.4% of all cancers) and 1.8 million deaths, making it the foremost contributor to cancer mortality.1 Survival outcomes are strongly stage-dependent, with the 5-year survival rate exceeding 70% for patients diagnosed at stage IA but declining to below 10% for those with stage IV disease.2 According to national registry data (2012–2015) in China, the overall 5-year survival rate for patients with lung cancer was only 19.7%.3

The management of lung cancer has become increasingly complex due to rapid advances in diagnostic modalities, staging strategies and therapeutic innovations, alongside the involvement of multiple healthcare providers.4 This complexity underscores the importance of structured and coordinated care models due to the need for timely diagnosis, multimodal treatment planning and ongoing supportive care.4 Multidisciplinary care (MDC) has therefore been established as a benchmark for high-quality oncology services, prioritising patient-centred, team-based approaches to treatment delivery.5 Within the literature, terms such as ‘MDC’, ‘multidisciplinary team (MDT) meetings’ and ‘tumour boards’ are often used interchangeably, although they represent overlapping but distinct components.6 In this review, we define MDC as a longitudinal, integrated model of care spanning the entire disease trajectory, while MDT meetings are defined as an operational mechanism in which specialists convene to discuss individual cases and make evidence-based management decisions.6–8

A typical lung cancer MDC includes professionals from oncology, thoracic surgery, respiratory medicine, radiology, pathology, palliative care, nursing and allied health disciplines.9 This collaborative framework is designed to reduce individual clinician bias, enhance adherence to clinical guidelines and tailor treatment to patient-specific needs.9 Evidence from non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) has demonstrated that multidisciplinary input can improve the appropriateness of treatment selection, enhance postoperative recovery, promote guideline-concordant care, expand access to clinical trials and increase psychosocial support.10 11 For example, a retrospective cohort study in the USA reported that the 5-year survival rate was significantly higher among patients with lung cancer managed with an MDC approach compared with conventional care (33.6% vs 23.0%; p<0.001).10 Similarly, a study in Taiwan found that MDT involvement was associated with improved survival outcomes for patients with stage III and IV NSCLC.11

Despite these advantages, MDC implementation remains heterogeneous globally.5 9 In many settings, MDC is limited to episodic MDT meetings rather than extending across the continuum of care. Furthermore, MDC is inherently resource-intensive, demanding significant time, specialised personnel and financial investment.12 These requirements pose particular challenges in resource-limited environments, where workforce shortages, insufficient diagnostic and therapeutic capacity, infrastructural limitations and gaps in policy or professional training often hinder effective delivery.5 Notably, most existing syntheses focus on high-income contexts, leaving an evidence gap regarding adaptable MDC models for low-resource environments.

From an implementation science perspective, the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) provides a lens through which to examine these challenges.13 CFIR integrates constructs related to implementation determinants into five domains: the innovation, outer setting, inner setting, individuals and implementation processes. Its comprehensive structure aligns well with the multi-level challenges of MDC implementation across diverse health systems. Applying this framework enables systematic investigation of who benefits from MDC, who does not and why.13 In this review, CFIR will guide data extraction and analysis to synthesise existing evidence, identify gaps and propose context-appropriate MDC models that are feasible and sustainable, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Objectives

The aim of this scoping review is to systematically map the global landscape of MDC for lung cancer, identifying implementation models, facilitators, barriers, policy frameworks and integration of innovations to inform the optimisation of MDC models worldwide. Specific objectives of our scoping review are to: (1) describe the existing implementation models of lung cancer MDC across different health systems and settings, (2) identify facilitators and barriers to the effective implementation of lung cancer MDC, (3) examine the extent to which emerging technologies, such as artificial intelligence, are integrated into MDC processes and (4) map existing evidence gaps to guide future research and policy development on lung cancer MDC in resource-limited settings.

Methods and analysis

To ensure the rigour and clarity of the review process, we will follow the scoping review framework outlined in the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) manual for evidence synthesis,14 which is underpinned by the framework of Arskey and O’Malley and enhanced by Levac and colleagues. The protocol was registered with Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/MVSPH). The proposed timeline and current status for the completion of this scoping review are detailed in online supplemental table 3.

Inclusion criteria

Participants

This review will include studies involving adults aged 18 years or older with a confirmed diagnosis of lung cancer, specifically small cell lung cancer (including small cell carcinoma and combined small cell carcinoma) or NSCLC (including adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma and large cell carcinoma). Studies that explore MDC or MDT processes at any stage of lung cancer management—such as diagnosis, treatment planning, postoperative care, palliation or survivorship—will be eligible, as will studies including patients suspected of lung cancer where MDT meetings are employed. Exclusion criteria comprise studies limited to other thoracic or rare malignancies (eg, lung nodules, mesothelioma, chest wall tumours, carcinoid or mediastinal tumours or cancers metastatic to the lung without primary lung cancer), studies focused solely on risk factors without a confirmed diagnosis and non-English or non-Chinese publications. In addition, studies that only acknowledge the importance of MDC without substantive discussion of its processes, barriers, facilitators or health-related and service-related outcomes will also be excluded.

Concepts

The study will define MDC as a longitudinal, integrated model of care spanning the entire disease trajectory, while MDT meetings are conceptualised as an operational mechanism in which specialists convene to discuss individual cases and make evidence-based management decisions.

Context

All settings for the individual with lung cancer will be included.

Types of evidence sources

Our scoping review will take into account a variety of sources, including any type of empirical research study (eg, quantitative study, qualitative study and mixed-method study), systematic reviews, guidelines, letters to the editor, policy papers and websites. The empirical research study will only be included if it has not been reported in an existing evidence synthesis. Articles published in languages other than English or Chinese will not be considered. Inclusion and exclusion criterion

Search strategy

Six databases will be searched: PubMed, CINAHL, Scopus, CNKI, VIP, WanFang. We will also search clinical trial.gov to locate grey literature. The search will be informed by the search strategies using the key concepts of “Lung cancer” and “MDT”. As per the JBI methodology, an initial search of PubMed will be performed to find potentially relevant keywords and terms for building the final search strategy. The search will then be tailored to each database. The preliminary search strategy for PubMed is included in online supplemental table 1. We will also examine the reference lists of all included research studies for additional resources. Key authors will be contacted with requests to provide relevant full-text papers if these are not available through the library/inter-library loan. All citations will be exported from electronic databases to Endnote to remove duplications.15 After removing duplications, all remaining citations will be imported into Covidence for the subsequent review.16

Source of evidence selection

Two researchers will independently review the titles and abstracts to determine whether they meet the review’s inclusion criterion. Full-text versions of potentially relevant publications will be obtained, and two reviewers will independently evaluate them using the study’s inclusion and exclusion criterion. If any disagreements between the reviewers occur, a third reviewer will be consulted to resolve them through discussion. Reasons for full-text paper exclusions will be documented and reported. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses Extension for Scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR) flow chart with a table of included studies will be used to report the search process (online supplemental table 2).17 The process of searching, screening and selecting studies for the scoping review may discover relevant terms and concepts; the search will then be modified and expanded.

Data extraction

The adapted JBI data extraction template draft table will be used to systematically capture study characteristics, which will include, but not be limited to: author(s), title, year of publication, population, sample size, methods, key findings. In addition, the template will document determinants and strategies relevant to the five CFIR domains: (1) innovation characteristics (eg, evidence strength of MDC, its adaptability to local contexts, complexity and perceived relative advantage), (2) inner setting (eg, organisational culture, communication networks, resource availability, leadership engagement, compatibility with existing workflows), (3) outer setting (eg, policy mandates, financing mechanisms, partnerships with other institutions, local socioeconomic or regulatory environments), (4) individual (eg, provider self-efficacy, beliefs about MDC, role clarity among team members, individual readiness to change) and (5) implementation process (eg, planning, engagement of stakeholders, audit and feedback loops, adaptation during rollout). To ensure feasibility and relevance, the draft extraction template will first be piloted independently by two reviewers on two to three included studies.18 The preliminary version of the data extraction table is provided in table 2, while the finalised version will be presented in the completed scoping review. Data extraction will be conducted by two independent reviewers, with discrepancies resolved through discussion and, if necessary, consultation with a third reviewer to ensure accuracy and consistency.

Data analysis and presentation

Data will be presented graphically and tabularly, as well as a general summary of the number, type and distribution of included studies, together with a narrative description of how they relate to the objectives of the scoping review.

Limitations

This scoping review will map the breadth and nature of available evidence rather than formally assess the methodological quality or risk of bias of included studies. In addition, heterogeneity in the definitions of MDC/MDT, study designs, healthcare settings and reported outcomes may limit comparability across studies. Although both English and Chinese publications will be included, relevant studies published in other languages may be missed.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design of this scoping review.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethics approval is not required as the scoping review will synthesise publicly available articles, and participants will not be recruited or consented. The review findings will be disseminated through open access publication and presentation at peer-reviewed academic conferences.
