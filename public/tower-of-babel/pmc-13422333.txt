Morphological and molecular evidence clarifies the taxonomic position of Indosasa solearis and reveals the hybrid origin of Sinobambusa (Poaceae: Bambusoideae: Arundinarieae)
Authors: Yu-Ling Huang, Zheng-Yang Niu, Jing-Bo Ni, Tien Chinh Vu, Jian-Quan Chen, Yi-Hua Tong, Nian-He Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe three-branched bamboos (Arundinarieae) form a taxonomically difficult group due to recent rapid radiation and frequent hybridization, with generic and species delimitations of many taxa problematic. Indosasa solearis, described from North Vietnam based on flowering materials, was later transferred to Sinobambusa. But its taxonomic position was controversial due to limited vegetative data. After a detailed morphological study of this bamboo at its type locality, we found that the overall morphology of this species is surprisingly similar to I. parvifolia and I. jinpingensis. Thus, we conducted morphological and phylogenomic analyses to ascertain the taxonomic position of I. solearis and its relationship with those two species. Morphological examination confirmed I. solearis exhibits diagnostic characters of Indosasa, like leptomorph rhizomes, diffuse culms, strongly and asymmetrically raised branch supra-nodal ridges, 4–9 foliage leaves per ultimate branch, pseudospikelets, and each floret with six stamens and three stigmas. No essential difference was found among the three Indosasa species both on macro- and micromorphology. Phylogenies based on single-copy orthologous nuclear gene datasets and single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) data strongly support I. solearis as an Indosasa member, closely related to I. parvifolia and I. jinpingensis. Based on the combined morphological and molecular evidence, we confirmed that I. solearis belongs to Indosasa, and is conspecific with I. parvifolia and I. jinpingensis. We therefore reinstate I. solearis and synonymize I. parvifolia and I. jinpingensis with it. Furthermore, our gene flow analyses by SNP data revealed the hybrid origin of the genus Sinobambusa, with genomic signatures suggesting an ancient cross between Indosasa and Pleioblastus. Introduction

The three-branched bamboos (tribe Arundinarieae) are a group of bamboos characterized by leptomorph rhizomes and a branch complement with three branches per mid-culm node, mainly including six genera: Indosasa McClure, Acidosasa B. M. Yang, Oligostachyum Z. P. Wang, Sinobambusa Makino ex Nakai, Pleioblastus Nakai and Pseudosasa Makino ex Nakai [1–4]. Due to recent evolutionary radiation and frequent hybridization, generic and species delimitation within this group has proven challenging [5–7]. While previous studies mostly focused on solving the relationships among the groups within Arundinarieae at the subtribal or generic level [1–3, 5–13], taxonomic revisions at specific rank have received less attention.

The genus Indosasa, a member of the three-branched bamboos, includes about 17 species mainly distributed in southern to eastern China, northern Vietnam and Laos. It is characterized by leptomorph rhizomes, diffuse culms, strongly raised culm and branch supra-nodal ridges, pseudospikelets, and each floret with six stamens and three stigmas [14–23]. Previous studies mainly based on morphology suggested that Indosasa has a close relationship with Sinobambusa [15, 24–26]. Since these two genera share highly similar vegetative traits, it is always difficult to distinguish them based on sterile materials. The key difference is the stamen number in each floret—Indosasa has 6 stamens per floret, whereas Sinobambusa has 3, rarely 2 or 4. Other less remarkable differences include the branch angle (nearly horizontal in Indosasa vs. with an acute angle in Sinobambusa), the morphology of supranodal ridges (strongly raised and wider than sheath scar in Indosasa vs. not strongly raised or as wide as sheath scar in Sinobambusa), the shape of the culm leaf blade (ovate-lanceolate in Indosasa vs. narrowly lanceolate or linear in Sinobambusa), etc.

Indosasa solearis was described by McClure [27] based on flowering materials (Fig. 1A) from Tam Dao, Vietnam, of which some taxonomically important vegetative characters, such as the culm leaf, are still unknown. McClure stated that this species is morphologically similar to I. crassiflora McClure, but differs in having narrower pseudospikelets, lemmas with a thinner texture and more prominent veins, and a sandal-shaped (obtuse) apex of the palea. Nguyen [28] transferred it to Sinobambusa, and made the combination Sinobambusa solearis (McClure) T. Q. Nguyen without providing any reason. Nguyen’s treatment was followed by some important bamboo works or databases, such as Vorontsova et al. [29] and “Plants of the World Online” (https://powo.science.kew.org/). However, this species has six stamens per floret according to the description in the protologue [27], which matches with the diagnostic character of Indosasa, rather Sinobambusa. Thus, Nguyen’s taxonomic treatment seems questionable, and as the information of some key vegetative characters, such as rhizome and culm leaf, are still unknown, the taxonomic position of this species needs further study.

During a field trip to Tam Dao, Vietnam—the type locality of Indosasa solearis—in May 2024, we encountered a large bamboo grove mainly composed of one species. This bamboo bore a lot of new shoots at that time, thus we collected complete specimens including rhizomes and culm leaves. After careful observations in the wild and examination of the newly collected specimens, we found that it shares the same key characters with I. solearis, such as leptomorph rhizomes, 2–3 m high culms, striated internodes, sheath scars with conspicuous remnants, strongly raised culm and branch supra-nodal ridges, a prominent groove on the branching side of the culm internodes, 4–7 foliage leaves per ultimate branch, and lanceolate to narrowly lanceolate foliage leaf blades with developed auricles and fimbriae. We also unexpectedly found that the overall morphology of this species, especially the culm leaf, was very similar to two other Indosasa species distributed on the border of China and Vietnam, namely I. parvifolia C. S. Chao & Q. H. Dai, from Pingxiang City, Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region [15], and I. jinpingensis T. P. Yi from Jinping County, Yunnan Province (some works accept I. jinpingensis as a synonym of I. parvifolia, e.g. Vorontsova et al. [29]). Thus, we conducted further morphological and phylogenomic studies to ascertain the taxonomic position of I. solearis and its relationship with I. parvifolia and I. jinpingensis.

Materials and methods

Morphological study

Most samples used in this study were collected from type locality (Table S1 & Table S2) to minimize identification error, as the long (sometimes 100–150 years) and typically semelparous life cycle of woody bamboos renders their identification using vegetative characters only somewhat challenging [8, 10, 13]. Voucher specimens were deposited in IBSC. Other related specimens or specimen photos kept in the following herbaria were also examined: GXFI, IBSC, KUN, PE, US and ZJFI. Herbarium acronyms follow Thiers [30]. Reproductive material was dissected under a stereo microscope (MZ101, Mshot, Guangzhou Micro-shot Technology Co., Ltd, Guangzhou, China) and photos were taken by the attached camera.

Micromorphology of the foliage leaf epidermis of Indosasa solearis, I. jinpingensis, and I. parvifolia was studied by scanning electron microscope (SEM). Dried foliage leaf samples were collected from the type locality. The middle portion of foliage leaves (5 × 5 mm) were treated by ultrasonication in 0.1% fatty alcohol polyoxyethylene ether(n = 25)for 2 h, followed by heating in a constant temperature for 12 h, then retreated by ultrasonication for 2 h. After the pretreatment, samples were cleared with 70% ethanol and dried at room temperature before mounting on stubs and sputtered by platinum. Observations and image acquisition were carried out using JSM-IT210 scanning electron microscope. Terminology for the epidermis appendages follows Ellis [31] and Chen et al. [32].

Taxon sampling for molecular analyses, DNA extraction and sequencing

Our taxon sampling was based on the previous phylogenetic framework of the three-branched bamboos by Guo et al. [6] and Niu et al. [4]. A total of 30 samples representing 27 species from 11 genera were chosen for further molecular phylogenetic analysis. The ingroup includes four samples of Acidosasa, six samples of Indosasa, five samples of Pleioblastus, two samples of Pseudosasa, six samples of Sinobambusa, and six samples from other representative genera in subtribe Arundinariinae. Dendrocalamus latiflorus Munro from the tribe Bambuseae was chosen as the outgroup (Table S1).

Fresh leaves were collected from the field and dried in silica gel, then were used to extract genomic DNAs by the TIANGEN Genomic DNA Extraction kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China). After sample quality controlling, a total amount of 0.2 µg DNA per sample was used as input material for the DNA library preparations and the genomic DNA sample was fragmented by Covaris LE220R-plus (Covaris, USA) to a size of 350 bp. The DNA fragments were amplified by PCR and a paired-end library was prepared for sequencing by DNBSEQ-T7 platform. The original data were filtered by Fastp software v.0.23.2 [33] to generate at least 40 Gb deep genome skimming data (DGS) of each sample.

Phylogenetic inference based on orthologous nuclear genes

To elucidate the phylogenetic position of Indosasa solearis and ensure robust phylogenetic inference, two nuclear datasets were constructed in this study. The first dataset included 737 common single-copy nuclear orthologous genes (SOGs), which were identified from protein-coding sequences of six published bamboo genomes-Dendrocalamus latiflorus Murno [34], Bonia amplexicaulis (L. C. Chia, H. L. Fung & Y. L. Yang) N. H. Xia, Guadua angustifolia Kunth, Olyra latifolia L., Raddia guianensis (Brongn.) Hitchc [35]. and Phyllostachys edulis (Carriere) J. Houz [36]. by Orthofinder v.2.5.4 [37] followed Niu et al. [4]. The putative SOGs were assembled by Hybpiper v.2.0.1 [38] and the filtered data were mapped to each SOGs by BWA mapper function in Hybpiper. To confirm the mapping quality, the gene mapping rate of each sample was calculated by Hybpiper and the sample was preserved if the rate passed 80%. Then 737 SOGs for each sample were recovered by a python script ‘retrive_sequences.py’.

Since the samples in our study are allotetraploid bamboos (except Dendrocalamus latiflorus), SOGs detected by orthofinder may have multiple copies [4, 35]. Thus, protein-coding sequences of three temperate bamboos—Hsuehochloa calcarea (C. D. Chu & C. S. Chao) D. Z. Li & Y. X. Zhang, Ampelocalamus luodianensis T. P. Yi & R. S. Wang and Phyllostachys edulis [39] were chosen to identify another SOGs dataset. Each sequence was firstly mapped to four BUSCO lineage datasets: eukaryota_odb10, embryophyta_odb10, poales_odb10 and viridiplantae_odb10 by BUSCO [40]. Then, orthologs of three reference sequences from each dataset were identified and combined together. The orthologue genes from three references were combined and inputted to Orthofinder with 940 common SOGs identified. Sample data were mapped to each SOGs and recovered by Hybpiper. To further confirm the gene number of each sample, command ‘paralog_retriever.py’ built-in Hybpiper was conducted. Four genes with multiple copies were removed. Finally, a total number of 936 SOGs was detected.

Each single-copy orthologous gene matrix based on two different datasets was aligned by MAFFT [41] and trimmed by trim Al v.1.4 [42] with default settings. The phylogenetic tree of each SOG dataset was constructed by IQ-TREE v.1.6.12 [43] with Maximum Likelihood (ML) analysis. Branch support was estimated with 1000 bootstrap replications. Then all ML trees were combined to infer the species tree by ASTRAL [44] with default settings.

SNP calling and phylogenetic inference based on SNP data

Distantly related species might affect the phylogenetic result based on single nucleotide polymorphism site (SNP), especially for those taxa under rapid radiation [45–47]. The progress of SNP calling also consumes massive computer resources and time [48, 49]. Hence, we reduced our taxon sampling to only include 23 samples representing 20 species of subtribe Arundinariinae for SNP analysis (Table S2). Indocalamus sinicus Nakai was chosen as the outgroup in this analysis.

A total of 24 chromosome-level scaffolds were filtered from the high-quality genome of the moso bamboo—Phyllostachys edulis [36] and chosen as the reference for SNP calling. An index was built based on the reference by software BWA-MEM v.0.7.17 [50] and Picard v.1.16.1 (https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). The duplicated reads generated in PCR enrichment were removed using Fastuniq v.1.1 [51]. The filtered reads were then aligned to the reference by BWA-MEM with default setting and sorted by SAMtools to BAM files. SNP calling was performed by GATK v.4.3.0.0 [52] and the SNP data were filtered by PLINK v.1.9 [53] with user-recommended settings. For the subsequent analyses, linkage disequilibrium (LD) filtering was conducted by PLINK setting as ‘--indep-pairwise 50 10 0.5’.

For the phylogenetic analyses, filtered SNP data in GVCF format was transformed into PHYLIP format by a python script ‘vcf2phylip.py’ [54] and was used to estimate a species tree by CASTER-site [55]. Branch support was estimated by a bootstrap analysis with 1000 replicates and bootstrap value of at least 95% was considered to be strongly supported.

Population structure and exploratory clustering analysis

To visualize the genetic background and clustering relationship among species, exploratory population structure and Principal Component Analyses (PCA) were conducted. It should be noted that due to the sampling strategy of one individual per species in our study, these analyses were used strictly as exploratory tools to observe genetic distances and grouping trends, rather than to infer strict population allele frequencies.

SNP data after LD filtration was converted from GVCF file to a bed file by PLINK and then was used as an input file to ADMIXTURE v.1.3.0 [56] to visually inspect the genetic background of different species, with the number of genetic clusters (K) set as 2–10. The values of cross-validation error were obtained from log files and compared, which determine the best genetic cluster (K). The plotting results for K in 3–9 were drawn by pophelper [57] and ggplot2 package [58] in R v.4.4.1. PCA based on SNP data were conducted by PLINK and then plotted by ggplot2 package.

Detection and quantification of interspecific gene flow

To distinguish hybridization from Incomplete Lineage Sorting (ILS) and quantify interspecific gene flow, allele frequency-based tests and phylogenetic network inference were employed.

Dsuite v0.5 [59] was employed for the allele frequency-based tests to further investigate interspecific gene flow. The f4-ratio was firstly calculated on the basis of the SNP coalescent species tree for all populations using the Dtrios program. The significant D-statistic and f4-ratio were visualized by scripts plot_d.rb and plot_f4ratio.rb. Then, f-branch statistic values for each phylogenetic branch were estimated by the Fbranch program. Finally, the result of f-branch statistic was plotted by a script dtools.py.

To quantify historical gene flow in Sinobambusa tootsik, PhyloNet v3.8.2 [60] was conducted for the phylogenetic network inference. Based on the result of Dsuite, six samples were chosen: Indosasa crassiflora, Indosasa jinpingensis, Sinobambusa tootsik (HB-14), Pleioblastus maculatus, Pleioblastus gramineus and Sinosasa longiligulata (outgroup). Input gene trees were inferred for 936-nuclear SOGs using IQ-TREE. InferNetwork_ML was executed to search for optimal networks containing 1 to 3 reticulations. The inheritance probability () for each hybridization event was estimated to represent the genomic contribution of each parental lineage during the hybrid speciation event. The phylogenetic networks were visualized using Dendroscope v3.5.7 [61].

Results

Morphological comparison

Examination of the type specimen of Indosasa solearis revealed that this species has six stamens per floret (Fig. 1E), supporting its placement within the genus Indosasa rather than Sinobambusa. The complete specimens with culm leaf and rhizome collected from its type locality exhibit additional morphological characters consistent with those diagnostic characters of Indosasa, including leptomorph rhizomes, branch complements with 3 branches at each node from mid-culm to the top, branches nearly horizontal, strongly raised culm and branch supra-nodal ridges, the abaxial surface of the culm leaf sheaths scattered with tufts of setae and triangular to ovate-lanceolate culm leaf blades.

Detailed morphological comparison revealed that Indosasa solearis shares the same key characters with I. jinpingensis and I. parvifolia (Table 1), such as internodes with ribs (particularly on old culms), a prominent groove on the branching side of culm internodes, infranodal region with a white powdery ring, culm leaf sheaths with white powder adaxially, culm leaf blades with developed auricles and relatively small foliage leaf blades (Fig. 1B–C) with fimbriae. Some small differences among them like the indumentum or shape of some organs are unstable, as we found that the population from Vietnam show a certain degree of variation (Fig. 2B–C). For example, the culm internodes are usually glabrous, but white-hispidulous ones also exist; the clusters of setae on the abaxial surface of culm leaf sheath can be dense or sparse, sometimes even nearly absent (Fig. 3A–D); the shape of culm leaf auricles varies from elliptic, semicircular to falcate (Fig. 3E–G); and the base of culm leaf blade is constricted or not. Thus, these differences should be recognized as population level variation.

Comparison of reproductive materials further shows that I. solearis and I. jinpingensis share nearly the same floral morphology (Fig. 1A & C). Both species have pseudospikelets with 5–13 florets, gemmiferous bracts gradually becoming larger upwards, glabrous and hollow rachilla segments, pointed lemmas and a blunt apex of the palea (Fig. 1D & F). In conclusion, there is no essential, consistent macro-morphological difference among I. solearis, I. jinpingensis and I. parvifolia.

The micromorphological analyses also reveal that I. solearis, I. jinpingensis and I. parvifolia have identical morphology of the foliage leaf epidermis (Fig. 4). Specifically, they share the same kind of silica cells, microhairs, prickles (Fig. 4D–F) and stomatal apparatus, which is covered with four to eight elongated papillae (Fig. 4G–I).

Phylogenetic inference

The ASTRAL species trees inferred from 737-nuclear SOG and 936-nuclear SOG gene trees showed that Indosasa solearis forms a clade with other Indosasa species as well as the type species of Indosasa, i.e., I. crassiflora, with strong support (PP = 1.00), but is distantly related to Sinobambusa species (Fig. 5). Indosasa solearis is clustered with I. jinpingensis and I. parvifolia, which indicates that these three species have a close relationship. However, the genus Sinobambusa was resolved as a sister group to Pleioblastus and Pseudosasa (PP = 0.64) in the species tree inferred from 737-nuclear SOG gene trees (Fig. 5, left), while it is sister to Indosasa (PP = 0.52) based on 936-nuclear SOG gene trees (Fig. 5, right).

The species tree inferred from the SNP matrix is nearly identical to the species tree based on the 936-nuclear SOG gene trees, except that Sinobambusa was resolved as paraphyletic (Fig. 6A). Population structure and clustering relationship

In the ADMIXTURE analyses (Fig. 6B), the cross-validation (CV) error decreased as the number of ancestral population (K-value) was reduced (Table S3). Hence, we present the population structure for K-values ranging from 3 to 9, which correspond with our morphological observations. When K = 3, all the Indosasa samples, including I. solearis, were assigned by a dominant genetic component (represented by the red cluster). Across all tested K-values, samples of I. solearis, I. jinpingensis and I. parvifolia consistently exhibited highly similar ancestral components. Notably, both Sinobambusa sat and S. tootsik displayed mixed components with one from Indosasa and the other from Pleioblastus under the K cluster of 3 or 4.

The exploratory Principal Component Analysis (PCA) showed that Indosasa solearis, I. jinpingensis and I. parvifolia are closely clustered and almost overlapped together in the principal component space (PC1: -0.1443 to -0.1432, PC2: 0.3108 to 0.3171), indicating a highly similar genetic background (Fig. 6C).

Gene flow analyses

Given the phylogenetic incongruence (Figs. 5 and 6A) and the distinct genetic components observed in the genus Sinobambusa (Fig. 6B), we further investigated the underlying patterns of interspecific gene flow and distinguish true introgression from incomplete lineage sorting (ILS). Patterson’s D-statistics and the f4-ratio tests across all possible species trios revealed widespread and significant signals of introgression in the three-branched bamboo (Fig. S1).

The D-statistic analyses provided robust statistical support for the different ancestral subpopulation components observed in the ADMIXTURE analysis, detecting specific gene flow events involving Sinobambusa samples (Fig. 7A). Notably, we found significant evidence of gene flow from Acidosasa glauca into both S. sat and S. tootsik (NZY171), also from Indosasa sinica into S. tootsik (NZY171). The other sample of S. tootsik (HB-14) showed introgression signals from both the Indosasa and Pleioblastus clades. This differential pattern of ancestry aligns closely with the multicomponent structure observed in the ADMIXTURE analysis.

Further inference of the phylogenetic network by Phylonet supported hybrid speciation in Sinobambusa tootsik (HB-14). The model fit, assessed by total log probability, improved as more hybridization events were allowed (Table S4). The inferred network containing one and two reticulation events suggested an asymmetrical hybrid origin of S. tootsik (HB-14), with 44.02–48.24% of the ancestral genome derived from Indosasa jinpingensis and 55.98–51.76% from Pleioblastus maculatus (Fig. 7B-C).

Discussion

Taxonomic position of Indosasa solearis and its relationship with I. jinpingensis and I. parvifolia

Recent studies suggested that Indosasa sensu lato is non-monophyletic [6, 21]. Indosasa sensu stricto (s.s.) is newly defined by the possession of leptomorph rhizomes, diffuse culms, strongly and asymmetrically raised branch supra-nodal ridges, usually 4–9 foliage leaves per ultimate branch, pseudospikelets, and each floret with six stamens and three stigmas [21, 22]. Indosasa solearis exhibits all these key diagnostic characteristics of Indosasa s.s. Combined with the phylogenomic evidence (Figs. 5 and 6A), we confirm that this bamboo belongs to Indosasa. We therefore reinstate the name Indosasa solearis McClure.

Morphological analyses reveal that Indosasa solearis, I. jinpingensis and I. parvifolia share the same morphology of culm internode, culm leaves, foliage leaves and pseudospikelets, and there is no essential difference among them. Comparison of the micromorphology of leaf epidermis also shows a consistent pattern among these three species (Fig. 4), which is type Ⅳ according to Zhang et al. [62]. Additionally, phylogenetic inferences and population analyses indicate that they share distinct genetic composition compared to other Indosasa species and mix together with a very close genetic distance (Fig. 6B–C).

In a word, the evidence from both morphology and phylogenetics confirms that Indosasa solearis, I. jinpingensis and I. parvifolia are conspecific. According to the Art. 11.4 of International Code of Nomenclature for algae, fungi, and plants (Madrid Code) [63], as the name I. solearis McClure (1942) predates I. parvifolia C. S. Chao & Q. H. Dai (1983) and I. jinpingensis T. P. Yi (2001), we treat the latter two as synonyms of I. solearis.

Potential hybrid speciation/introgression in other three-branched bamboos

Gene flow is one of the main sources for evolution, which will enhance variability within the population and facilitates novel trait combinations [64–66]. Temperate woody bamboos (tribe Arundinarieae) are allopolyploids, derived from an ancient hybridization event between two ancestral diploid species [4, 35, 67–70]. Hence, our study also identified additional homoploid speciation and introgression events, which are responsible for some phylogenetic conflicts (Figs. 5 and 6A). For instance, samples of the genus Sinobambusa were close to the Pleioblastus and Pseudosasa clade in the 737 SOG-based phylogeny but grouped with Indosasa in the 936 SOG-based tree and SNP-based tree. The gene flow tests further revealed that these individuals possess mixed genetic components from both Pleioblastus and Indosasa (Figs. 6B and 7).

Morphologically, certain Sinobambusa species, such as Sinobambusa tootsik, exhibits characteristic similar to those of Pleioblastus in having 3–5 branches per culm node, a prominent setose sheath scar usually with a corky ring, and striga at the base of the culm leaf sheath, spreading culm leaf fimbriae, each spikelet with more than 10 florets, and a white powdery lemma. It also shares some traits with Indosasa species in having protruding culm and branch nodes, ultimate branchlets with more than 5 foliage leaves, and pseudospikelets [15–17, 24–26, 71–73], which implies its hybrid origin from Pleioblastus and Indosasa.

Considering the phylogenetic incongruence and the morphological mosaicism frequently observed in the species of Sinobambusa, we propose that the genus Sinobambusa entirely may have originated through ancient hybridization (reticulate evolution) between lineages related to Pleioblastus and Indosasa. However, due to the limited sampling in this study, the evolutionary relationships among Sinobambusa, Indosasa and Pleioblastus warrant further investigation.

Taxonomic treatment

Diagnosis

Indosasa solearis is notably distinctive from other Indosasa species in its small size of foliage leaf blades, usually 6–15 × 1–1.8 cm.

Description

Rhizome leptomorph. Culm erect, diffuse, 5–10 m tall; internode with ribs, slightly white powdery, glabrous or white-hispidulous; supra-nodal ridge prominent, wider than the sheath scar, with a white powdery ring below node; branches 3 at each node of mid and upper culm, sometimes 1 or 2, branching side with a prominent groove; secondary branch node conspicuous and asymmetrically raised. Culm leaf sheath orange-yellow, 12–22 × 5–15 cm, leathery, deciduous, farinose, densely or sparsely with clusters of brown setae or subglabrous; ligule very short, slightly serrulate, sometimes ciliolate; auricles prominent, from elliptic to semicircular, or falcate with dense and curly fimbriae; blade triangular to ovate-lanceolate, green with brown margin, slightly farinose, glabrous sometimes with striga, base constricted or not, sometimes joint with auricles. Foliage leaves 4–7 per ultimate leafy branchlet; sheath glabrous; auricles ovate to oblong, with fimbriae erect or spreading; ligule truncate, ca. 0.5 mm high; blades lanceolate to narrowly lanceolate, 6–15 × 1–1.8 cm, adaxially glabrous, abaxially pubescent, apex acuminate, serrulate marginally. Pseudospikelet up to 8 cm long, sub-fusiform, base narrow, apex blunt; prophyll membranous, ca. 5 mm long, keels pubescent; bracts ca. 9, papery, glabrous, 3–16 mm long, ovate, apex sometimes with small blades or apiculate, with veins abaxially; florets 5–13 with 1–3 sterile florets at the top; glume 1 or absent, similar to lemma, but shorter; rachilla segments thick, with pith, hollow when dry, twisted, with one side notably concave in the middle, the other side convex with cilia toward apex; lemma ca. 1.2 cm long, apex obtuse or subacute, apiculate, glabrous abaxially, with many conspicuous veins, apex strigose adaxially; palea shorter than lemma, papery, keels 2, ciliate, apex obtuse; lodicules 3, sub-equal, veins prominent, lanceolate or oblong, apex acuminated or acute, ciliate at the margin; stamens 6, anthers ca. 5 mm long, linear, apex emarginate; ovary narrow, glabrous; style short, glabrous; stigmas 3, close to each other. Mature caryopsis unknown.

Distribution and habitat

This species is distributed in China (Guangxi, Yunnan) and Vietnam (Lao Cai, Vinh Phuc). It usually grows under evergreen broad-leaf forests at elevations of 800–1500 m.

Phenology

New shoots from April to May; flowering in December.

Vernacular names

Chinese name “小叶大节竹” [“xiǎo yè dà jié zhú”] or “哈竹” [“hā zhú”]. The reason for the latter name is that this bamboo is widely distributed near the houses of the Hani people in Yunnan.
