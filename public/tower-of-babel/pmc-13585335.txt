Design for Remanufacturing and Resource Circulation in Proton Exchange Membrane Water Electrolysis Systems Using Sustainability Benefit Ratio
Authors: Tomohiko Nakajima, Iwao Yamaguchi, Yuki Kinoshita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProton exchange membrane water electrolysis (PEMWE) is a promising route to low‐carbon hydrogen production, but its sustainability remains incomplete unless the electrolyzer itself is designed for circularity. Repeated stack replacement can impose substantial lifecycle burdens because PEMWE relies on resource‐intensive components such as iridium‐based anodes, fluorinated polymer membranes, and titanium‐based stack materials. Here, we investigate the significance of introducing remanufacturing into PEMWE systems from the perspective of design for remanufacturing and resource circulation. Repeated new‐manufacturing and repeated remanufacturing scenarios are compared using the Sustainability Benefit Ratio (SBR), a lifecycle‐oriented indicator that integrates greenhouse‐gas burdens, avoided burdens, and valuation‐weighted lifecycle factors into a unified circular‐economy metric. The results show that remanufacturing consistently improves the cumulative environmental–economic balance relative to repeated new manufacturing, reduces carbon‐footprint‐based burdens, and enables repeated circulation of valuable stack‐related resources while maintaining comparable hydrogen output. Cost decomposition and sensitivity analysis further reveal that the OER electrode governs the circular value of PEMWE remanufacturing and that its contribution can be translated into practical design targets in terms of catalyst cost, remanufacturing cost, recovery ratio, and electrochemical performance. These findings demonstrate that SBR can serve as a practical design‐support tool for quantifying circular‐economy value and guiding remanufacturing‐oriented PEMWE development.Keywords: circular economy, design for remanufacturing, proton exchange membrane, sustainability, water electrolysis

Introduction

Hydrogen produced by water electrolysis is increasingly recognized as an essential energy carrier for deep decarbonization, especially in sectors that are difficult to electrify directly and in energy systems with a high penetration of variable renewable power. Among water electrolysis technologies, proton exchange membrane (PEM) water electrolysis (PEMWE) has attracted particular attention because of its high current density, compactness, and excellent dynamic response under intermittent operation. These characteristics make PEMWE systems highly promising for coupling with renewable electricity sources and for enabling flexible, low‐carbon hydrogen production [1, 2, 3, 4].

At the same time, the sustainability of hydrogen technologies should not be judged solely by the absence or reduction of direct carbon emissions during operation. The device itself, including its material composition, manufacturing burden, repairability, and end‐of‐life treatment, also determines whether the overall system can be considered truly sustainable. In other words, hydrogen may be clean, but sustainability remains incomplete if the production device itself is not designed for circularity. This point is especially important for PEMWE systems because they rely on functionally intensive materials and components such as iridium‐ and platinum‐based catalysts, corrosion‐resistant metallic parts, membrane‐electrode assemblies (MEAs), porous transport layers, bipolar plates, sealing components, and fluoropolymer‐based materials. If PEMWE deployment expands without incorporating circular strategies, the resulting increase in demand for critical materials and repeated new manufacturing may intensify concerns related to resource depletion, supply risk, and lifecycle greenhouse gas emissions [5, 6].

For this reason, resource circulation should be considered not as an afterthought but as a design target from the early phase of technology development. One promising pathway is remanufacturing. In contrast to one‐way production and replacement, remanufacturing enables degraded products and modules to be recovered, disassembled, selectively repaired or rebuilt, and returned to service with restored functionality. In PEMWE systems, such an approach may create multiple benefits simultaneously. First, it may promote repeated circulation of high‐value materials and components, especially in catalyst‐ and stack‐related parts. Second, it may reduce the environmental burden associated with repeated production of new hardware. Third, it may change the lifetime design philosophy of the system itself [7, 8, 9]. This last point is particularly important. Under a conventional linear manufacturing approach, long lifetime is often treated as an unconditional design target, and every component is implicitly expected to survive for extended operation without practical intervention. However, once remanufacturing is incorporated into the system architecture and operational plan, an alternative design logic becomes possible. Instead of over‐designing all components for maximal durability, the system can be optimized to allow planned degradation, selective replacement, and periodic reconstruction of specific stack elements. Such a strategy may relax excessive lifetime requirements, simplify maintenance planning, and improve resource efficiency without sacrificing overall service performance. This is the essence of design for remanufacturing: not merely recovering value after use but shaping the initial architecture of the product so that recovery, disassembly, replacement, and rebuilding become technically and economically feasible [7].

Evaluating such circular strategies requires metrics that go beyond conventional cost analysis. The levelized cost of hydrogen (LCOH) is a widely used and indispensable indicator for assessing the economic competitiveness of hydrogen production pathways. However, LCOH alone does not fully reflect the broader circular value created by remanufacturing, such as avoided manufacturing burdens, repeated circulation of valuable materials, or the environmental significance of replacing repeated new production with selective rebuilding. A method is therefore needed that can account simultaneously for lifecycle burden, avoided burden, and valuation‐relevant factors in a manner that remains interpretable for engineering design [10, 11].

In this context, the sustainability benefit ratio (SBR) provides a useful analytical basis [12, 13]. SBR is a dimensionless lifecycle‐oriented indicator that compares the cumulative benefit and burden associated with a product system by integrating physically accounted greenhouse‐gas emissions and avoidance terms with valuation‐weighted lifecycle factors. In our previous work, SBR was applied to perovskite solar cells as a tool for evaluating circular remanufacturing strategies [12] and design‐for‐remanufacturing (DfRem) concepts [14, 15, 16] under repeated operation and recovery cycles. That study demonstrated that SBR can show sustainability benefits that are difficult to capture using conventional single‐perspective indicators alone, particularly when environmental and economic aspects must be considered simultaneously in a circular‐economy context. The value of SBR therefore lies not only in its hybrid structure but also in its ability to function as a practical design and decision‐support metric. It can indicate whether a remanufacturing‐oriented strategy creates net lifecycle advantage, how strongly this advantage depends on process cost and recovery performance, and where the dominant barriers to circular implementation lie [13].

Here, we extend this analytical perspective to PEMWE systems. By applying the SBR approach, we assess the environmental and economic effects of incorporating remanufacturing into the manufacturing and operational cycles of PEM water electrolysis systems, in comparison with conventional repeated new manufacturing scenarios. We also investigate how major cost elements associated with remanufacturing affect the practical viability and benefits. This approach is intended to clarify not only whether remanufacturing is advantageous but also the conditions under which it becomes favorable and the design requirements that must be embedded in PEM systems from the outset to unlock these benefits. In particular, we focus on the oxygen evolution electrode, a current cost bottleneck in PEMWE systems, to identify directions for material selection and performance improvement that support remanufacturing‐oriented design. Collectively, these analyses provide an initial basis for establishing electrolyzer design for remanufacturing and resource circulation as a core principle in the development of future circular hydrogen technologies.

Method

PEM Device and Remanufacturing Scenario Assumptions

In this study, the 1 MW PEMWE baseline configuration was reported by Badgett et al. is adopted as the reference system [17]. This baseline system comprises the electrolyzer stack itself as well as balance‐of‐plant (BOP) subsystems for water purification and deionization, thermal management of the stack and process streams, hydrogen–water separation, and hydrogen drying. In the reference design, the PEMWE plant is assumed to operate over a 40 year lifetime, with the electrolyzer stack replaced every 56 operating months (≈40,000 h). Based on this setup, the lifecycle scenarios considered here assume that the BOP remains in service throughout the system lifetime, while only the electrolyzer stack undergoes repeated intervention, either by replacement with a newly manufactured unit or by remanufacturing. The system boundary was defined in this way to enable a direct comparison between these two stack‐level pathways. Under this assumption, the present analysis focuses on stack‐centered circular operation by comparing a linear pathway based on repeated replacement with newly manufactured stacks and a circular pathway based on repeated stack remanufacturing. This boundary setting makes it possible to directly evaluate the practical contribution of stack remanufacturing to PEMWE system performance without conflating its effects with those of the long‐lived BOP infrastructure [3, 18].

The PEMWE stack analyzed here was defined according to the reported component architecture (Figure 1), which is broadly representative of present‐day PEM water electrolysis stacks. At the cell level, the stack consists of a catalyst‐coated membrane (CCM), in which a perfluorosulfonic acid membrane and ionomer provide proton conduction, together with an anode‐side oxygen evolution reaction (OER) catalyst layer and porous transport layer and a cathode‐side hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) catalyst layer and gas diffusion layer. These functional layers are compressed between bipolar plates and flow fields, and repeated unit cells are assembled with frames and seals into a multicell stack. Under the baseline design, the anode employs an iridium catalyst and titanium‐based transport components, while the cathode employs a platinum catalyst and carbon‐paper diffusion medium. This structure was taken as the reference stack configuration for the present remanufacturing analysis because it reflects the general material and component layout of current PEMWE technology. Based on the reported stack cost breakdown at 100 MW · yr−1, the OER electrode was treated as a focal component in the present analysis [17, 19]. Its reported manufactured cost is 85 USD · kW−1 out of a total stack manufactured cost of 183 USD · kW−1 [17]. Because Badgett et al. also note that a 50% markup should be included as a proxy for purchase price, the present study adopts a marked‐up OER electrode cost of 127.5 USD · kW−1 in the subsequent analysis.

On the basis of this stack architecture, the remanufacturing route considered here represents recycling‐assisted rebuilding of the electrodes rather than direct reuse of the degraded catalyst layers. The assumed process begins with stack disassembly and separation of the bipolar plates, transport layers, seals, and MEAs. After isolation of the CCM, the ionomer‐containing catalyst layers are liberated from the perfluorinated sulfonic acid (PFSA) membrane by mechanical decoating, ultrasonic treatment, or solvent‐assisted delamination. The anode and cathode fractions are preferably collected separately; if they become mixed, physical separation of the fine catalyst particles or subsequent hydrometallurgical separation of Pt and Ir is required. The recovered metals are then purified, converted into catalyst or catalyst‐precursor forms, supplemented with virgin material to compensate for recovery losses, and mixed with new ionomer to prepare catalyst inks. New catalyst layers are subsequently coated onto a new membrane, followed by stack reassembly using retained or renewed components and performance qualification. This process chain should be regarded as a prospective engineering route assembled from individually reported recycling operations rather than as an already established integrated industrial remanufacturing process. Although the membrane and ionomer are not assumed to be reused as functional components, their removal remains a critical part of the remanufacturing process. Residual PFSA membrane and ionomer may hinder catalyst‐layer liberation, fine‐particle separation, and purification of the Ir‐ and Pt‐containing fractions and may also affect the quality and electrochemical performance of the reformulated catalyst layers. Accordingly, even when the membrane is replaced, the technical interdependence between membrane/ionomer treatment and catalyst recovery remains a critical technical challenge.

The absolute comparison among different power‐supply cases should therefore be interpreted with this limitation, whereas the comparison between renewal and remanufacturing within each case remains internally consistent because identical operating and degradation assumptions are applied to both pathways.

A key factor in hydrogen production by water electrolysis is the selection of renewable energy sources with low environmental burdens. In this study, solar photovoltaic (PV) power, wind power, and a hybrid power supply consisting of a 1:1 mix of solar and wind were considered, assuming off‐grid operation as the electricity source for the water electrolysis system. The electricity‐source capacity factor, (F cap), was set to 0.35 for solar PV, 0.51 for land‐based wind, and 0.74 for the solar–wind hybrid case [23]. These values represent favorable, scenario‐specific operating conditions rather than general average renewable‐electricity conditions. The PV and wind values correspond to high‐resource cases used in the reported DOE assessment [20], whereas the hybrid value represents an optimized configuration with high wind–solar complementarity and renewable‐generation capacity exceeding the electrolyzer capacity. Accordingly, the selected (F cap) values should be interpreted as high‐resource or optimized benchmark cases for comparing repeated new manufacturing and remanufacturing under identical operating conditions rather than as globally representative capacity factors.

SBR

To express the environmental “return on investment (ROI)” of a remanufacturable power‐generation system as a unified, time‐dependent metric, we define the SBR as [12, 13]

In the present PEM water electrolysis analysis, the numerator of the SBR expresses the accumulated benefit generated by low‐carbon hydrogen production over time. This benefit is quantified as the amount of CO2 emissions avoided when hydrogen produced by the PEMWE system substitutes for hydrogen from a reference production route with an emission factor of EFref. Additional credits are introduced at each remanufacturing event to reflect the reduction in virgin‐material demand and the mitigation of burdens otherwise associated with end‐of‐life (EoL) processing. The denominator, in contrast, represents the total burden required to realize this benefit. These burdens include (i) emissions embodied in the initial stack manufacturing stage; (ii) emissions arising from electricity use during hydrogen production, represented by the electricity‐related emission factor EFh; and (iii) further embodied burdens associated with each remanufacturing or replacement cycle. To integrate economic aspects into the same formulation, monetary quantities such as manufacturing cost (MC), remanufacturing cost (RC), operation and maintenance cost, and EoL‐related cost are translated into CO2‐equivalent terms by means of the economic carbon‐intensity coefficient CC [24]. This formulation allows environmental loads and economically mediated burdens to be handled within a unified balance.

A central difficulty in circular economy assessment lies in treating environmental significance and economic value in a comparable manner. When analysis is based only on physical emissions, the practical attractiveness of remanufacturing may not be fully reflected. On the other hand, if the formulation becomes too dependent on detailed cost accounting, the environmental meaning of the indicator becomes less transparent. For this reason, the present study employs a compact formulation that retains physical interpretability while allowing economic effects to be reflected in a consistent way. The coefficient CC is the key element in this connection, expressing the carbon burden associated with unit monetary expenditure. Through this conversion, cost items can be directly combined with embodied emissions in the burden term, while avoided expenditures made possible by remanufacturing can also be treated as benefit‐side contributions.

This treatment is especially relevant for PEMWE systems because remanufacturing may generate value both by reducing emissions and by preserving high‐value components and alleviating disposal‐related losses [8]. In that sense, the approach can represent part of the circular value associated with scarce or costly stack materials within a single numerical index. Although more specialized methods remain necessary for detailed discussions of critical material recovery or toxicity‐related impacts, the present CC‐based approach provides a practical means of embedding such circularity‐related effects into a common CO2‐equivalent accounting basis. Since both the benefit and burden terms are expressed on the same basis, the resulting SBR is dimensionless. An SBR value greater than 1 indicates that the accumulated benefit exceeds the total burden incurred, while progressively larger values indicate stronger net advantage. Within this interpretation, a sufficiently high SBR suggests that the PEMWE system has moved beyond burden repayment and into a regime where remanufacturing‐supported operation yields sustained circular benefit. The role of SBR in this study is therefore not to replace highly detailed lifecycle or economic analysis but to provide an interpretable metric for comparing repeated new‐manufacturing and remanufacturing pathways in PEMWE systems.

Since SBR(t) is evaluated continuously over the operating lifetime, it provides a time‐evolving trajectory instead of a single static indicator. The first crossing of SBR(t) = 1 marks the point at which the accumulated benefit from PEMWE‐based hydrogen production overtakes the total burden incurred up to that time. Each remanufacturing event modifies this trajectory in a discrete manner: avoided‐material and avoided‐disposal credits increase the benefit term, whereas remanufacturing‐related emissions and cost‐derived burdens increase the burden term. As a result, the sign and magnitude of the remanufacturing effect depend on their net balance. Examining the full trajectory is therefore informative for determining both whether remanufacturing is favorable and at what stage of operation its advantage emerges. The same formulation can be applied under a wide range of assumptions, including alternative degradation rates, remanufacturing schedules, electricity carbon intensities, and cost settings, while maintaining consistency with carbon‐based comparison against other indicators such as LCOH.

For long‐term comparison, we also consider the limiting value of SBR(t) at sufficiently large t. In this limit, SBR(t) converges to a constant because the influence of one‐time initial burdens becomes negligible relative to the repeatedly accumulated operational and remanufacturing terms. For PEMWE systems, the numerator in this limit corresponds to the recurring avoided emissions associated with hydrogen production, together with remanufacturing‐related credits, whereas the denominator corresponds to the recurring operational, embodied, and monetized burdens. Assuming a fixed remanufacturing interval of T months and a long‐run average monthly hydrogen production of H¯, both terms become asymptotically proportional to time for t≫T. For the initial capital cost, the marked‐up manufactured cost was reported by Badgett et al. was not used directly as an installed capital expenditure (CAPEX) value; instead, it was corrected to approximate an installed‐cost basis that accounts for installation‐ and indirect‐cost effects [17]. They indicated that PEM electrolyzer installed costs can reach approximately 1300–1700 USD · kW−1 when installation and soft costs are included, whereas the 100 MW · yr−1 case gives a marked‐up manufactured cost of 1055 USD · kW−1. To obtain a conservative estimate, the upper end of the installed‐cost range, 1700 USD · kW−1, was adopted, and the resulting ratio, 1.61, was applied as the installed‐cost correction factor (ICC) for the initial CAPEX.

For repeated new‐manufacturing stack replacement, the same correction factor was not applied because replacement in an already‐installed system is expected to involve a smaller installation and indirect cost burden than the first‐time system deployment. The previous report also treats replacement separately from the total initial installed capital cost in its PEM electrolyzer cost assumptions [23]. Therefore, a smaller correction factor of 1.25 was used for repeated new‐manufacturing replacement (ICCreplace), representing an intermediate installed replacement cost proxy. In the SBR calculation, the cost‐related terms were converted into carbon‐equivalent burdens using the carbon‐intensity coefficient CC (kg‐CO2e · USD−1). Specifically, the initial cost‐related burden was calculated as CLinit = MC · ICC · CC, where MC is the marked‐up manufactured cost of the initial system. The repeated new‐manufacturing replacement cost term was evaluated as CLstack = MCstack · ICCreplace · CC. Thus, each economic burden was first corrected to the assumed installed cost basis and then converted into a carbon‐equivalent quantity for incorporation into the SBR. Because RC should explicitly include service‐related costs such as cleaning, disassembly, and reassembly, RC itself was treated as the RC without applying any correction factor, and its estimated value was set based on the following assumptions.

RCs for the PEM stack were estimated on the basis of the reported stack cost breakdown at a production scale of 100 MW · yr−1 [17], with particular emphasis placed on the OER electrode as the dominant stack cost component. In the remanufacturing scenario, bipolar plate substrates were assumed to be retained and recoated, while the OER and HER electrodes were reformed using recycled catalyst materials. For the OER electrode, the RC was redefined by separating the catalyst‐related recycling burden from the residual noncatalyst burden. The catalyst‐related portion was estimated from the PEMWE hydrometallurgical recycling results of Uekert et al. [7], and was interpreted as consisting of a regeneration cost and a make‐up catalyst cost associated with incomplete recovery. The remaining noncatalyst portion of the original OER electrode MC, including noncatalyst materials, process‐related cost, and fixed manufacturing overhead, was assumed to be partially retained in remanufacturing. In this study, 50% of this residual portion was assigned as the noncatalyst RC of the OER electrode. In this cost allocation, the fraction derived from Uekert et al. was used to represent catalyst recovery, purification, and the make‐up catalyst required to compensate for incomplete recovery. Downstream electrode‐rebuilding operations not explicitly included in their analysis, including catalyst reformulation, ink preparation, coating, and drying, were approximated within the residual process‐related RC described above.

It should be noted that Uekert et al. presented a process‐model‐based assessment normalized to 1 MW of PEMWE MEA, rather than an experimental demonstration of an integrated 1 MW recycling line. Their hydrometallurgical pathway was estimated to require approximately 24%–28% of the energy use and 21%–25% of the GHG emissions of virgin PEMWE manufacturing. However, their model assumes prior MEA removal and does not explicitly include the downstream reformation of catalyst layers. In the present analysis, these downstream operations were approximated through the residual process‐related cost, whereas the membrane/ionomer‐related components were assumed to be replaced with new materials, as described below. Scale‐up to stack‐level or 100 MW · yr−1 operation would further require automated disassembly, catalyst liberation and purification, fine‐particle separation, solvent and acid recovery, wastewater treatment, and catalyst reformulation. At the present stage, the adopted cost and GHG fractions should therefore be regarded as scenario‐based estimates requiring pilot‐scale validation.

Membrane/ionomer‐related components were assumed to be replaced with new materials in the remanufacturing scenario. However, because remanufacturing does not require repetition of the full first‐time fabrication process, the corresponding RC was assumed to be reduced to 90% of the original MC to account for partial savings in fixed and ancillary processing costs. Recoating of bipolar plates was assumed to require 20% of the virgin plate burden. This value was not taken from a direct experimental report but was introduced as an engineering allocation, in which 20% of the NREL bipolar‐plate and flow‐field burden was assigned to recoating and surface finishing [17], while 80% was treated as an avoided substrate‐related burden through reuse of the bulk Ti plate. Seals were assumed to be newly replaced, and stack assembly was fully reincurred during remanufacturing. Although the frames may potentially be retained, the NREL cost breakdown combines frames and seals into a single cost item, preventing a reliable allocation of the reusable frame fraction. The combined burden was therefore conservatively excluded from the avoided‐burden credit. The 50% markup adopted in the NREL model was applied consistently to the initial manufacturing, repeated new‐manufacturing, and RCs to obtain a common market‐price proxy. Detailed calculation procedures and the basis for each parameter are provided in the Supporting Information.

Results and Discussion

SBR Analysis for Remanufacturing and Renewal Replacement Operations

Figure 2 shows the time evolution of SBR(t) for remanufacturing and repeated new‐manufacturing scenarios under three electricity‐supply conditions: PV, wind, and hybrid operation. In all cases, the remanufacturing pathway yields consistently higher SBR values than the repeated renewal pathway, indicating a more favorable cumulative balance of benefit and burden. As shown in Figure 2a–c, SBR rises rapidly during the initial stage and then increases more gradually with continued hydrogen production. The periodic sawtooth‐like fluctuations reflect the repeated stack intervention events, at which both benefit and burden terms change discontinuously; nevertheless, the remanufacturing case maintains a persistent advantage over repeated renewal throughout the analyzed period.

The solid lines represent the average EFh case, while the shaded ranges indicate the variation obtained from the minimum to maximum EFh assumptions.

Among the three power‐supply conditions, the wind case shows the highest SBR values, followed by the hybrid and PV cases. This trend is mainly attributed to the larger hydrogen output driven by higher EFh and F cap, which accelerates the accumulation of avoided‐emission benefits. Because renewable‐powered electrolysis substantially reduces CO2 emissions relative to fossil‐fuel‐based gray hydrogen, the initial carbon debt is repaid relatively quickly, and net benefit begins to emerge after 56 months in all cases. In the repeated new‐manufacturing scenario, the burden associated with stack replacement is sufficiently large that SBR shows little additional increase after the first replacement event. By contrast, in the remanufacturing scenario, the lower recurring burden allows SBR to continue increasing over the 40 year operating period, gradually approaching a limiting value. This behavior indicates that the cumulative operational value of remanufacturing becomes increasingly pronounced as successive intervention events accumulate.

Figure 2d–f present the ratio SBRratio(t) (=SBRreman(t)/SBRrenew(t)), defined as the ratio of the remanufacturing case to the repeated renewal case. In all electricity‐supply conditions, the ratio remains greater than 1.0 after the first intervention event, confirming that remanufacturing is systematically more advantageous than repeated new manufacturing. This advantage is most pronounced in the wind‐power case, where the average ratio reaches approximately 1.5 in the later stage. In the solar‐PV and hybrid cases, the degree of improvement is more moderate, but a sustained enhancement is still observed. On the other hand, SBRratio(t) also depends strongly on the value of EFh. The CO2 emissions associated with hydrogen production in the water electrolysis system are related to the electricity‐related CO2 emission intensity EFe through the relationship EFh = EFe × SEC, meaning that EFh depends directly on the emission intensity of the power source. Therefore, a decrease in EFe leads to a decrease in EF t , which in turn affects the SBR value. Table 2 summarizes the currently assumed range of EFe values for each power source. When SBRratio(t) is calculated based on this range, the solar‐PV case reaches a value of approximately 1.65 at low EFh, which is nearly comparable to that of the wind‐power case. Similar tendencies are observed for the other power sources as well, indicating that the selection of a power source with low EFe is an extremely important factor in hydrogen production by water electrolysis.

To further examine the factors governing the relative advantage of remanufacturing over repeated new manufacturing, a Monte Carlo–based sensitivity analysis for PV case was conducted for SBRratio(447), evaluated at t = 447 months, that is, immediately before the final replacement event within the assumed 40 year operating period. In this analysis, 5000 parameter sets were randomly sampled within predefined ranges, and the resulting SBRratio(447) values were plotted individually against each parameter. This approach allows direct visual assessment of how strongly the benefit of remanufacturing responds to variations in degradation behavior, performance recovery, stack energy consumption, manufacturing and RCs, carbon‐intensity conversion, and hydrogen‐related emission factors. Spearman rank correlation coefficients (ρ) were additionally calculated using the same Monte Carlo samples for the PV case to quantitatively assess the trends shown in Figure 2 (Figure S2). The largest absolute coefficients were obtained for EFh (ρ = −0.553), SECstack (ρ = 0.480), and RC/MC (ρ = −0.396), quantitatively supporting the relative sensitivities observed in the scatter plots.

Figure 3 shows that the response of SBRratio(447) is not equally sensitive to all parameters. While the degradation rate δ, recovery factor r, and embodied emission terms exhibit relatively limited variation, clearer dependencies are observed for the stack energy consumption SECstack, MC, the remanufacturing‐to‐MC ratio RC/MC, the carbon‐intensity coefficient CC, and the hydrogen‐related emission factor EFh. These trends indicate that because the manufacturing burden remains large in water electrolysis systems, the importance of reducing RC increases as the number of replacement events accumulates and that, if such cost reduction can be achieved, the comparative advantage of remanufacturing becomes more pronounced. In other words, it is highly important to establish target values for the overall RC and to formulate practical strategies such as easier stack disassembly, selective component reuse, reduction of rebuilding steps, and DfRem aimed at lowering service and processing costs. Through SBR‐based evaluation, it is therefore necessary to decompose RC into its constituent cost elements and analyze what kinds of cost targets are realistically achievable. To translate these sensitivity trends into explicit engineering boundaries, an additional break‐even analysis was performed at t = 111 (Figure S3). These results indicate the break‐even points obtained by varying each parameter independently. Although appropriate safety margins from these boundary values will be required for practical implementation, the results reinforce the sensitivity‐analysis findings presented in Figure 2.

In addition to the cost‐related terms, EFh also exerts a particularly strong influence on SBRratio(447), indicating that reducing the carbon intensity on the power‐supply side is likewise an important factor. The pronounced sensitivity to MC, RC/MC, and CC shows that, in PEM water electrolysis systems, the large economic burden relative to the current assumed physical performance specifications is a central determinant of remanufacturing effectiveness. Accordingly, it should be noted that the magnitude of CC itself also affects the value of remanufacturing, and this effect may vary depending on the region of manufacturing and operation. Although the sensitivity analysis was carried out over a range of CC values covering regions relevant to PEMWE deployment (Figure S1), the subsequent calculations were performed using the global‐average value, which represents the main volume zone.

The increase in SBRratio(447) at lower EFh indicates that the environmental quality of the power source directly amplifies the benefit of circular operation in PEMWE systems. When low‐carbon electricity is supplied, the recurring benefit associated with avoided emissions is accumulated more effectively, and the burden reduction achieved by remanufacturing is reflected more clearly as an improvement in SBR.

The decrease in SBRratio(447) with increasing F cap can be interpreted as a dilution effect caused by increased hydrogen throughput. At higher F cap, avoided‐emission benefits accumulate more rapidly in both the remanufacturing and repeated new‐manufacturing scenarios, so the relative contribution of the difference in stack replacement burden becomes smaller. In this sense, the reduction of SBRratio(447) at high F cap does not imply that remanufacturing is ineffective. It suggests that under highly productive operating conditions, the overall benefit of PEMWE operation becomes dominant, while the comparative advantage of remanufacturing over repeated new manufacturing becomes less pronounced. Since the absolute SBR value itself increases with increasing F cap (Figure S4), this result should be understood as indicating that the environmental efficiency of the PEMWE system as a whole improves at higher F cap.

Impact of Remanufacturing Operation on the Carbon Footprint

Figure 5 compares cumulative hydrogen production and carbon‐footprint‐based performance metrics for the renew and remanufacturing scenarios under PV, wind, and hybrid electricity supply. Figure 5a shows cumulative hydrogen production over the full operating period. Under all three power‐supply conditions, the cumulative hydrogen production in the remanufacturing scenario is slightly lower than that in the renewal replacement scenario, approximately to the extent expected from the r (=0.95), but the overall difference remains small. The absolute hydrogen production is governed mainly by F cap, with the hybrid case showing the largest cumulative hydrogen output, followed by wind and then PV. This reduction in hydrogen output represents an explicit trade‐off associated with incomplete performance restoration after remanufacturing. In the present model, the lower hydrogen production is directly reflected in the benefit term of the SBR calculation and is therefore not neglected. Despite this penalty, the remanufacturing scenario maintains a higher SBR and also shows more favorable results for the other performance indicators discussed below than repeated new manufacturing. Thus, under the assumed recovery factor of (r = 0.95), the modest loss in cumulative hydrogen production is considered an acceptable compromise for the reduction in recurring manufacturing and replacement burdens. This interpretation is conditional on achieving a sufficiently high level of performance restoration, and substantially lower recovery factors could reduce or eliminate the advantage of remanufacturing.

The emission offset ratio (EOR), defined as the ratio of cumulative avoided emissions to cumulative emitted burdens, is shown in Figure 5b. In all electricity‐supply cases, the remanufacturing scenario exhibits a higher EOR than the renewal scenario, indicating that remanufacturing improves the balance between avoided emissions and incurred burdens. This difference becomes more pronounced in the wind and hybrid cases, where the larger hydrogen throughput increases the cumulative avoided emission benefit, while the remanufacturing pathway suppresses the burden associated with repeated replacement events. As a result, remanufacturing both improves the absolute carbon balance and shows an advantage in terms of how efficiently operational benefits offset embodied burdens.

Figure 5c shows the gross carbon footprint per unit hydrogen produced. In every power‐supply case, remanufacturing reduces the CO2 emissions per unit of hydrogen production by approximately half relative to repeated new manufacturing. This result indicates that the burden reduction achieved by avoiding repeated full‐stack replacement is directly reflected in the production‐based emission intensity. The difference becomes more evident under conditions where the carbon burden of the electricity supply itself is relatively low because the share of stack‐related embodied burdens in the total emissions per unit hydrogen becomes relatively larger. In this sense, remanufacturing is particularly effective when combined with cleaner electricity supply. Figure 5d shows the net carbon footprint per unit hydrogen, obtained by subtracting avoided emissions from emitted burdens. In all cases, the avoided‐emission term exceeds the burden term, resulting in negative values and indicating a net climate benefit relative to the reference hydrogen pathway. The remanufacturing scenario consistently gives more negative values than the renew scenario, demonstrating that remanufacturing further enhances the net decarbonization benefit of PEMWE operation. In this way, remanufacturing‐based operation contributes to reducing burdens in both economic and carbon‐emission terms. Therefore, circular operation in PEMWE systems should be evaluated both in terms of throughput and in terms of how effectively repeated stack‐related burdens can be suppressed while maintaining hydrogen production.

Cost Analysis of PEM Stack Remanufacturing

The analyses presented above show that remanufacturing‐based operation can improve the lifecycle balance of PEMWE systems. The next step is therefore to connect this system‐level advantage to practical stack design and material selection by identifying which cost elements most strongly affect the remanufacturing benefit. On this basis, the present study examines the cost breakdown of manufacturing and the assumed RC of a 1 MW PEMWE stack, focusing on the OER electrode because it most strongly influences the stack‐level cost reduction achievable through remanufacturing.

Figure 6a shows the breakdown of MC based on the estimates of Badgett et al. [17], together with the corresponding breakdown of the RC assumed in this study. The virgin MC was estimated to be 274.5 USD · kW−1, of which the OER electrode accounted for the largest share at 127.5 USD · kW−1, corresponding to 46.4% of the total. The membrane and HER electrode were the next‐largest contributors at 52.5 and 24.0 USD · kW−1, respectively. In contrast, the assumed total RC was 123.4 USD · kW−1, and the OER electrode RC was estimated to be 47.4 USD · kW−1, based on recovery, purification, and reuse of the catalyst material. Because direct functional reuse of polymeric membranes and ionomers after long‐term operation is expected to be difficult, new membrane‐related materials were assumed to be introduced, with 90% of the original membrane cost being reincurred. This corresponds to approximately 47.25 USD · kW−1, making the membrane and OER electrode nearly equal contributors to RC, each accounting for approximately 38.4% of the total. These results identify the OER electrode as a high‐priority target because it is both the largest contributor to virgin stack cost and a major critical‐material bottleneck. However, its recovery and reformation cannot be considered independently of the other stack components. In particular, membrane and ionomer residues may affect CCM delamination, catalyst‐particle liberation, separation and purification efficiency, and the quality and electrochemical performance of the reformulated electrodes. The condition and separability of the transport layers, bipolar plates, seals, frames, and other components may likewise influence the burdens associated with disassembly, recoating, replacement, and reassembly. Thus, the emphasis on the OER electrode should be interpreted as the identification of a priority design target within a broader stack‐level remanufacturing framework rather than as an assumption that remanufacturing feasibility is governed by the OER electrode alone. Because a reduction in RC/MC directly improves SBR, lowering the OER‐electrode cost remains a particularly effective strategy, but its practical benefit depends on the coordinated treatment of the surrounding stack components.

Cost breakdown and catalyst‐dependent OER‐electrode cost comparison for the PEMWE stack. (a) Estimated stack MC and RC breakdowns at 100 MW · yr−1, together with the change in the OER‐electrode cost fraction in MC for different OER catalyst compositions. (b) Catalyst material cost, (c) OER‐electrode manufacturing cost (MCOER), and (d) assumed OER‐electrode remanufacturing cost (RCOER) for representative OER catalyst materials. Here, the catalyst‐dependent comparisons isolate the effects of the assumed material and processing costs and do not include catalyst‐specific differences in stack lifetime, dissolution loss, recovery yield, recovered‐material quality, or purification complexity.

At present, the principal catalyst material used in PEMWE OER electrodes is IrO2. This is because IrO2 is a practical material capable of simultaneously providing high activity and durability under the severe anode environment of strongly acidic and high‐potential operation. Ir‐based oxides exhibit excellent corrosion resistance and relatively high reaction stability under acidic OER conditions, and they are still regarded as the de facto benchmark anode catalysts for PEMWE. By contrast, many less expensive transition‐metal oxides suffer from dissolution or structural degradation under acidic conditions and therefore still face major challenges in terms of practical long‐term operation. Indeed, recent reviews continue to regard Ir‐based catalysts as the practical standard for acidic OER. At the same time, the greatest drawback of Ir is its extreme scarcity. Iridium is one of the rarest elements in the Earth's crust, and recent reviews have pointed out that global annual production remains below roughly 10 t. In addition, Ir is supplied mainly as a byproduct of platinum‐group metal production, which imposes a structural limitation on the flexibility of supply expansion in response to increasing demand. Accordingly, for large‐scale deployment of PEMWE, Ir usage itself readily becomes a bottleneck, and this appears in concentrated form as the major cost burden of the OER electrode. It has therefore been repeatedly pointed out that large‐scale deployment will be difficult without reducing Ir usage [5, 6, 29, 30].

Against this background, one of the most promising alternatives to IrO2, or at least one of the most attractive low‐Ir candidates, is the RuO2‐based catalyst family [31, 32]. RuO2 shows very high activity for acidic OER and has been widely studied as a strong candidate for PEMWE anode catalysts because of its lower cost relative to IrO2 [33, 34]. However, stability remains a major issue. Under highly oxidizing acidic conditions, RuO2 is prone to over‐oxidation and dissolution, and its insufficient long‐term durability has been identified as a major obstacle to commercial implementation. For this reason, current research has expanded toward Ir–Ru mixed oxides and Ru‐based stabilization strategies using elements such as Mo, W, Cr, Mn, and Sb with the aim of balancing high activity and durability [35, 36, 37, 38]. Based on these developments, Figure 6a also shows how the contribution of the OER electrode to the total stack MC changes when IrO2 is replaced by RuO2. In the current IrO2‐based case, the OER catalyst cost accounts for 46.4% of the stack cost, whereas reducing the IrO2 content by 50% and adopting a RuO2–IrO2 (1:1) catalyst lowers this share to 35.9%, and complete substitution with RuO2 reduces it further to 20.6%. In other words, simply revising the catalyst composition of the OER electrode can drastically alter the overall stack cost structure, lowering the OER contribution to a level comparable to those of the membrane, bipolar plates, and balance‐of‐stack components. This result indicates that the high cost of IrO2 is the dominant driver of the total stack cost structure and that successful low‐Ir design or substitution with Ru‐based catalysts could markedly shift the relative contribution of the main burden term when remanufacturing operation is introduced.

Low‐cost OER catalyst materials intended to replace IrO2 are also being actively investigated beyond RuO2. Figure 6b–d compare the material cost, OER‐electrode MC (MCOER), and assumed RC (RCOER) for different OER catalyst materials. IrO2 is far more expensive than all other candidates already at the material‐cost level, and as a result, it also exhibits exceptionally high MCOER and RCOER. In contrast, RuO2‐based systems can achieve marked cost reduction relative to IrO2. In the present estimation, they appear as candidates capable of reducing MCOER to less than one‐third of the IrO2 case. Because Ru is highly active for acidic OER, Ru‐based catalysts remain among the most active low‐Ir or non‐Ir candidate groups under active study. However, their principal issue is again stability: RuO2 is susceptible to over‐oxidation and dissolution under high‐potential acidic conditions, making long‐term durability as a PEMWE anode problematic. For this reason, current research is focused on stabilizing Ru with other elements or on forming Ir–Ru mixed oxides, in order to reconcile activity and durability. For example, Ni‐stabilized RuO2 has been reported as a viable non‐Ir candidate [39], and reviews of Ru‐based catalysts consistently conclude that although activity is high, improvements in durability are the key requirement for implementation. Therefore, rather than viewing RuO2 as a simple one‐to‐one replacement material, it is more appropriate to regard it as a low‐cost, high‐activity platform that could be combined with recoating, partial renewal, or selective recatalyzation in a remanufacturing‐oriented setting.

At the same time, RuO2 is also generally classified as a precious‐ or critical‐metal‐based material and remains a costly raw material. Accordingly, for further cost reduction, catalyst development has also expanded toward Ru‐modified or Ru‐substituted transition‐metal oxide systems such as Ru–Mo, Ru–W, Cr–Co, CoOOH, and MnSb2O6 [40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45]. These strategies can be broadly divided into two directions. The first is a “Ru stabilization” strategy, in which Ru remains the principal active phase, while elements such as Mo, W, and Cr are used to tune the electronic structure and oxygen‐binding characteristics, thereby improving Ru stability. The second is an “earth‐abundant” route, in which more abundant and less expensive element systems, such as antimonates containing Co, Mn, and Sb, or compounds such as CoOOH, are used to construct acidic OER catalysts. The latter is highly attractive from the viewpoint of material cost but still faces serious difficulties in suppressing dissolution under acidic high‐potential conditions, maintaining conductivity, and achieving long‐term durability under realistic cell conditions. Figure 6b shows the corresponding change in material cost when IrO2 is replaced with these less‐expensive materials. Complete substitution of Ir by Ru can reduce the material cost by 92.9%.

Further cost reduction can also be achieved by partially substituting Ru with less expensive elements. For example, in RuO2:WO3 (1:1), where half of the Ru is replaced, the catalyst raw‐material cost can be reduced by 96.4% relative to the original IrO2 case. Complete substitution of the precious‐metal component with inexpensive transition‐metal‐based materials could further reduce the raw‐material burden to only a small fraction of that of the original IrO2 catalyst. Nevertheless, the comparisons presented in Figure 6b–d should be interpreted as cost‐based screening under common performance and recovery assumptions rather than as a ranking of the overall remanufacturing suitability of the candidate catalysts. In practical PEMWE operation, catalyst‐specific stack lifetime, dissolution loss, recovery yield, recovered‐material quality, and purification burden are strongly interdependent. At the remanufacturing stage, the OER catalyst layer may exhibit metal dissolution or redistribution, oxidation‐state changes, particle coarsening, detachment from the ionomer network, and changes in catalyst‐layer morphology. These end‐of‐life states determine how much catalyst remains within recoverable components and how readily the recovered material can be purified, redispersed, and regenerated. Insufficient acidic OER durability may also shorten the stack‐intervention interval and increase the required number of remanufacturing cycles. Remanufacturing‐oriented catalyst‐layer design should therefore suppress irreversible material loss, retain the catalyst within separable fractions, and enable restoration of the required composition, structure, and electrochemical activity. For Ru‐based catalysts, suppression of over‐oxidation, dissolution, and structural degradation is important not only for extending operating lifetime but also for retaining Ru in a recoverable form [46].

The introduction of stabilizing or substituting elements may improve acidic OER durability; however, multicomponent catalysts can also create more complex recycling streams. Their recovery may require selective separation, removal of dissolved or segregated phases, and restoration of the target composition before the catalyst layer can be reformed. Residual PFSA ionomer may also alter the wettability of catalyst particles and hinder their selective separation [47]. Compatibility with membrane/ionomer removal, purification, catalyst regeneration, and catalyst‐ink reformulation must therefore be considered. Candidate catalysts should consequently be evaluated using an integrated set of criteria, including acidic OER durability, retention of active material during operation, separation and purification efficiency, and the ability of the recovered material to regain the required phase, composition, morphology, and electrochemical performance after regeneration. These durability and recoverability considerations place important qualifications on the material‐cost reductions shown in Figure 6b–d. Moreover, even under the common performance and recovery assumptions adopted in the present cost‐based screening, the benefit of further reducing the catalyst raw‐material cost becomes progressively smaller when the analysis is extended to the full OER‐electrode MC, including noncatalyst materials, processing costs, and fixed overhead, as shown in Figure 6c. In particular, once the catalyst system reaches the cost level of partially substituted Ru‐based materials, further reductions in raw‐material cost have a relatively limited effect on the total electrode‐MC.

The estimated reduction of 96.4% applies only to the catalyst raw‐material cost. Ru–W mixed oxides can be prepared using precursor‐based synthesis and heat‐treatment processes similar to those generally used for IrO2; however, specific morphology‐control processes may result in additional costs, and these costs have not yet been quantified at the industrial scale. In the present model, the raw‐material savings would be offset if the Ru–W system incurred an additional Ru–W‐specific processing cost of approximately 92.8 USD · kW−1 compared with IrO2. This is a large value, corresponding to approximately three times the currently assumed MC excluding the raw‐material cost; nevertheless, in order to take advantage of the potential material‐cost benefit, it is necessary that the process cost not increase excessively.

A similar tendency is observed for RC. Substituting Ir with other elements has some beneficial effects, and conversion to RuO2 reduces RC by 38.2%. However, under the present set of assumptions, lowering the catalyst material cost beyond this point does not necessarily reduce RC any further. This is because the cost share of hydrometallurgical refining and regeneration becomes increasingly dominant, and if the cost balance between regeneration yield and the cost of supplementary material input is not favorable, an inversion can occur in which RCOER exceeds MCOER. In the present estimation, such behavior was obtained for catalyst materials in which Ru was completely replaced. In other words, once catalysts in this cost range are adopted, the rate‐limiting factor for further cost reduction is no longer material selection itself but rather the remanufacturing‐specific cost burden associated with regeneration and related processes. Reducing these costs therefore emerges as the next key issue. Accordingly, for remanufacturing‐oriented operation in the initial stage, catalyst choices involving partial substitution of Ru appear to be especially attractive candidates and may represent realistic options capable of meaningfully reducing the OER‐side cost bottleneck.

To examine whether the catalyst cost trends identified by the remanufacturing‐oriented SBR analysis also lead to improvement in a conventional plant‐level economic metric, the LCOH was evaluated for full PEMWE systems employing IrO2, IrO2–RuO2, and RuO2‐based OER catalysts. In contrast to the preceding stack‐centered discussion, the present LCOH analysis included both the electrolyzer stack and the balance‐of‐plant (BOP). Replacing IrO2 with Ru‐containing catalyst systems lowers the system‐level LCOH under all electricity‐supply conditions, and complete substitution to RuO2 decreases LCOH by approximately 0.11–0.24 USD · kg‐H2 −1 relative to the IrO2‐based case, depending on the power‐supply scenario (Figure S5). These results indicate that the cost significance identified for the OER electrode in the stack‐level analysis remains meaningful even when the full PEM plant is considered.

At the same time, remanufacturing further decreases LCOH in all catalyst systems, although the magnitude of the reduction becomes smaller as the OER catalyst cost is lowered. This trend suggests that once the catalyst‐side burden is reduced, the relative importance of other cost elements, including BOP cost and remanufacturing‐specific service or regeneration cost, becomes greater. Therefore, by further expanding the number of remanufacturable elements and by reducing the cost of the remanufacturing process itself, the contribution of circular operation to lower‐cost hydrogen production is expected to increase further. Detailed LCOH calculation procedures and input values are provided in the Supporting Information [10, 18].

Strategy for Setting Targets in PEM Stack Remanufacturing

Finally, to identify practical target values for remanufacturing‐oriented PEM water electrolysis operation, Bayesian optimization (BO) was performed using the steady‐state SBR, SBRss, as a single objective function. As described above, SBRss is defined as the asymptotic constant value of SBR and serves as a single indicator of circular‐economy value. At the same time, it incorporates both physical performance variables of the water electrolysis stack and economically converted burden terms, making it suitable as an objective function for multivariable optimization within a circular‐economy framework. In the present analysis, the optimization was carried out for the PV‐powered remanufacturing case. Five design variables were considered: degradation rate d (mV · kh−1), initial cell voltage V cell,0, OER catalyst MC MCOER, OER catalyst RC RCOER, and remanufacturing recovery ratio r. The BO started from 50 randomly generated initial samples, followed by 800 sequential iterations using a Gaussian process surrogate model and an expected improvement acquisition function. In total, 850 parameter sets were evaluated.

Figure 7a shows the parameter distributions of the top 10% of the 850 evaluated parameter sets ranked by SBRss. High‐performing solutions generally favor lower degradation rate, lower initial cell voltage, higher recovery ratio, and lower OER‐related manufacturing and RCs. However, the distributions also indicate that multiple parameter combinations can achieve similarly high SBRss, implying that the design objective should not be interpreted as a single deterministic optimum but rather as a practically accessible high‐performance design region. This point becomes clearer when the representative values are examined numerically. The median parameter set of the BO results gave SBRss = 7.870, with d = 3.792 mV · kh−1, V cell,0 = 1.666 V, r = 0.957, MCOER = 49.26 USD · kW−1, and RCOER = 35.97 USD · kW−1. This set can be interpreted as a stable central target because it represents the central tendency of the high‐performing solution pool without being biased by extreme cases.

To avoid overemphasizing unusually favorable but potentially difficult‐to‐realize outliers, representative high‐performing parameter sets are in the vicinity of the upper‐quartile region (around the 75th percentile; ranks 21–25 within the top 10% population). As examples, ranks 21–25 yielded SBRss values in the narrow range of 7.879–7.894, despite exhibiting noticeable differences in the individual parameters, as shown in Table 3. These representative results indicate that no single parameter alone determines high circular‐economy performance. Instead, comparable SBRss values can be realized through different trade‐offs among degradation resistance, stack efficiency, remanufacturing recovery, and OER‐related costs. For example, rank 21 achieves high SBRss mainly through low degradation rate and low initial cell voltage despite a relatively low recovery ratio, whereas rank 24 compensates for its higher degradation rate by combining high recovery ratio with low OER‐related costs. Rank 23, in contrast, maintains similarly high SBRss despite a relatively large MCOER, suggesting that favorable performance can still be achieved when a cost penalty is balanced by improvements in other variables. This confirms that the design problem is governed by a high‐performing feasible region rather than by a unique optimum point.

From a practical perspective, the median solution can therefore be interpreted as a stable central design target, while the rank 21–25 solutions provide examples of realistic high‐performance target candidates in the upper part of the elite solution pool. Overall, these results suggest that remanufacturing‐oriented PEMWE design should aim at lowering OER‐related cost while simultaneously improving degradation resistance, reducing initial cell voltage, and increasing the remanufacturing recovery ratio. More importantly, they show that meaningful design targets can be defined as a target region rather than a single numerical point, which is advantageous for translating the SBR‐based circular‐economy framework into practical materials and process development strategies.

An additional local sensitivity analysis was carried out around the high‐SBRss region identified by the optimization to further test the robustness of the BO results. Specifically, the top 10% of the BO solutions were first extracted, and the minimum–maximum ranges of the five design variables within this elite population were used to define a local design space. Within this BO‐derived region, 20,000 additional parameter sets were generated by random sampling, and SBRss was recalculated for each sample under the same PV‐powered remanufacturing condition. In this way, the analysis was designed to clarify both the parameter distributions obtained by optimization and the local sensitivity structure of SBRss around the high‐performance region.

The resulting parameter distributions and local sensitivity behavior are shown in Figure 7b. The scatter plots compare the sampled local population with its own top 10% subset, while the standardized linear regression coefficients quantify the relative contribution of each variable to SBRss within this region. Importantly, the top 10% solutions obtained from the local sensitivity sampling remain concentrated in nearly the same SBRss range as the top‐performing BO solutions. This indicates that the BO‐derived high‐performance region is not an isolated or accidental optimum but rather a reproducible and relatively broad design region. In other words, the local sensitivity analysis reinforces the validity of the BO results by showing that the favorable solution space remains stable even when densely resampled.

Among the five variables, the standardized regression analysis identifies V cell,0 as the most influential parameter, with a strong negative coefficient (−0.605), followed by MCOER (−0.564), r (+0.407), RCOER (−0.360), and d (−0.157). These results indicate that lowering the initial cell voltage is the most effective route to improving SBRss, reflecting the importance of reducing the initial stack SEC and thereby increasing hydrogen output and avoided emission benefit. The second‐largest contribution of MCOER shows that the economic burden of the OER catalyst remains a dominant design constraint even within the remanufacturing‐oriented framework. The positive effect of r confirms that high‐performance recovery after remanufacturing is a key strategic requirement, while the influence of RCOER indicates that simplification and cost reduction of the remanufacturing process itself are also essential. By contrast, the smaller coefficient of d suggests that degradation suppression remains beneficial, but is more compensable by favorable values of the other variables than the above factors.

These sensitivity results are consistent with and further strengthen the BO analysis. The BO median solution (SBRss = 7.87) suggested representative target values of d = 3.792 mV · kh−1, V cell,0 = 1.666 V, r = 0.957, MCOER = 49.26 USD · kW−1, and RCOER = 35.97 USD · kW−1. In addition, the representative high‐performing solutions around the upper‐quartile region of the elite population (ranks 21–25 within the top 10% BO solutions) showed similarly high SBRss values despite different parameter combinations, indicating that the design target should be interpreted as a feasible target region rather than a single deterministic point. Combined with the local sensitivity plots, these results suggest that a practical strategy for remanufacturing‐oriented PEMWE design is to prioritize: (i) reduction of V cell,0 toward the 1.6–1.7 V range, (ii) reduction of MCOER toward approximately 40–50 USD · kW−1, (iii) achievement of r >0.95, and (iv) reduction of RCOER toward approximately 33–36 USD · kW−1. Suppression of degradation remains desirable, but the present analysis suggests that it is not the primary limiting factor once the system enters the high‐SBRss design region.

From a strategic viewpoint, these results imply that remanufacturing‐oriented PEMWE development should not focus on catalyst cost reduction alone. Rather, the most effective pathway is a combined strategy in which low‐voltage/high‐efficiency stack design, lower OER catalyst cost, high remanufacturing recovery ratio, and low‐cost regeneration/recoating processes are advanced in parallel. This conclusion is important because it translates the abstract circular economy metric SBRss into concrete materials and process targets. At the same time, it shows that the circular value of PEMWE is governed by a trade‐off structure rather than by a single optimum point, which is advantageous for practical development because it allows several combinations of materials and process innovations to achieve comparably high circular economy performance.

Overall, the present study shows that the SBR approach can identify not only whether remanufacturing is beneficial but also which design variables most strongly govern its practical value and what quantitative target region should be aimed for. In this sense, SBR serves not merely as an evaluation metric but as a design‐support tool for circular implementation. Although this study focused on PEM water electrolysis, the same approach can be extended to other systems in which material recovery, component renewal, and long‐term environmental–economic trade‐offs are central issues. Thus, the present methodology provides a general route for translating circular‐economy concepts into actionable design targets across a broader range of energy and materials technologies.

Conclusion

This study examined PEM water electrolysis from the viewpoint of design for remanufacturing and resource circulation and showed that the sustainability of hydrogen production should be evaluated not only by operational emissions but also by the circularity of the production device itself. By comparing repeated new manufacturing and repeated remanufacturing scenarios, we showed that remanufacturing can provide a persistent lifecycle advantage in PEMWE systems, improving SBR, reducing carbon burdens, and sustaining the circulation of valuable stack‐related resources while maintaining nearly the same hydrogen production level. A key outcome of this work is the demonstration that its burden and corresponding circular benefit can be quantitatively evaluated within a unified SBR approach and translated into practical design strategy. In this way, the present analysis connects stack‐level burden structure to circular economy value, showing how catalyst cost, RC, performance recovery, and electrochemical efficiency together determine whether remanufacturing becomes advantageous. The results therefore shift the discussion from a qualitative recognition of cost bottlenecks to a quantitative basis for designing around them. The practical realization of these benefits, however, depends on achieving sufficient material recovery, economically viable remanufacturing, durable catalyst operation, and adequate restoration of electrochemical performance after each remanufacturing cycle. These factors therefore represent key engineering targets for translating the potential lifecycle advantages identified here into practical PEMWE remanufacturing.

The optimization and sensitivity analyses further showed that high circular‐economy performance is associated with low initial cell voltage, reduced OER‐related manufacturing and RC, and high remanufacturing recovery ratio, whereas degradation suppression, although beneficial, is not the dominant limiting factor once the design enters the high‐SBRss region. Importantly, the BO and local sensitivity analyses indicated that these advantages do not collapse to a single isolated optimum but rather emerge within a reproducible high‐performance design region. This is especially valuable for practical development because it means that circular design targets can be expressed as achievable parameter ranges rather than as a single idealized point. The present work establishes SBR as a useful framework for evaluating and designing circular hydrogen technologies. It clarifies both whether remanufacturing is beneficial and how the increase or decrease of circular‐economy value can be quantified in relation to burden and benefit and how this information can be used to formulate rational design strategies. Although shown here for PEMWE, the same methodology should be extendable to other energy and materials systems in which component renewal, resource recovery, and long‐term environmental–economic trade‐offs are central design issues.
