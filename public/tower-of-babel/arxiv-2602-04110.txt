Rate-Optimal Noise Annealing in Semi-Dual Neural Optimal Transport: Tangential Identifiability, Off-Manifold Ambiguity, and Guaranteed Recovery
Raymond Chu, Jaewoong Choi, Dohyun Kwon

Abstract
Semi-dual neural optimal transport learns a transport map via a max–min objective, yet training can converge to incorrect or degenerate maps. We fully characterize these spurious solutions in the common regime where data concentrate on low-dimensional manifold: the objective is underconstrained off the data manifold, while the on-manifold transport signal remains identifiable. Following 5 , we study additive-noise smoothing as a remedy and prove new map recovery guarantees as the noise vanishes. Our main practical contribution is a computable terminal noise level ε stat ​ ( N ) \varepsilon_{\mathrm{stat}}(N) that attains the optimal statistical rate, with scaling governed by the intrinsic dimension m m of the data. The formula arises from a theoretical unified analysis of (i) quantitative stability of optimal plans, (ii) smoothing-induced bias, and (iii) finite-sample error, yielding rates that depend on m m rather than the ambient dimension. Finally, we show that the reduced semi-dual objective becomes increasingly ill-conditioned as ε ↓ 0 \varepsilon\downarrow 0 . This provides a principled stopping rule: annealing below ε stat ​ ( N ) \varepsilon_{\mathrm{stat}}(N) can worsen optimization conditioning without improving statistical accuracy.

Introduction

Optimal transport (OT) provides a natural framework for comparing probability measures.
Neural approaches to OT have found broad use across a wide range of applications, including generative modeling
(
12
;
3
;
4
;
14
)
.
A prominent family of methods
(
7
;
20
)
is based on the
Semi-dual Neural OT
(SNOT)
, in which one learns a potential and then recovers an associated transport map via the max–min objective. Given source and target probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
and a cost function
c
, these approaches optimize

\displaystyle\sup_{V\in C(\mathcal{Y})}\inf_{T:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{L}(V,T)\quad\hbox{where}\quad\mathcal{L}(V,T):=

(1)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}c(x,T(x))-V(T(x))d\mu(x)+\int_{\mathcal{Y}}V(y)d\nu(y).

where
T
denotes a candidate transport map,
V
denotes a potential, and
C(\mathcal{Y})
is a set of continuous functions on
\mathcal{Y}
. If an optimal transport map
T^{\star}
exists, it is known that
T^{\star}
minimizes the inner objective for some potential
V^{\star}
, and
(V^{\star},T^{\star})
is an optimal solution of (
1
).

A critical challenge, however, is that
not every optimizer of the semi-dual max–min objective yields a correct transport map
. Even when (
1
) is solved accurately, the recovered
T
may fail to push
\mu
to
\nu
, producing an incorrect or degenerate mapping
(
20
;
8
;
5
)
. This challenge was referred to as the
spurious solution
problem in
5
, where examples of various failure cases were investigated. This pathology is particularly pronounced under the manifold assumption common in high-dimensional inference, where
\mu
is supported on a lower-dimensional set.

Why spurious transport maps arise

To understand this failure mode, we identify a geometric mechanism behind spurious solutions.
In (
1
), one learns a function
V
and then
recovers
a transport map
T_{V}
by minimizing the semi-dual objective over maps
T
:

\displaystyle T_{V}\in\argmin_{T:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}}\,\mathcal{L}(V,T).

(2)

Our first step is to fully characterize this recovery step (Theorem
3.1
). We show that optimal recovery is equivalent to a pointwise condition enforced only for
\mu
-a.e. inputs as in (
12
). Under the common high-dimensional assumption that
\mu
is supported on a low-dimensional manifold, this condition can
at most identify the tangential component of the optimal transport map
(Theorem
3.2
) while its behavior in
off-manifold (normal) directions remains unconstrained
. This limitation is sharp: in Example
3.3
, we exhibit infinitely many recovered maps that achieve the same objective and match the OT map tangentially, yet have arbitrary normal components. We observe the same pattern empirically: neural models can attain very small
tangential
error while exhibiting much larger
normal
error (Table
1
).

The analysis in Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
3.2
explains why spurious maps can arise under the manifold assumption, which is common in high-dimensional settings. This also motivates adding
off-manifold regularization
to the source distribution
\mu
to remove off-support degrees of freedom. Consistent with this expectation, we empirically find that smoothing reduces the normal-direction error (Table
1
).

Why smoothing works, and what it guarantees

A practical way to remove off-manifold ambiguity is to replace the singular source distribution by a full-dimensional regularization
\mu_{\varepsilon}\ll\mathcal{L}^{d}
as in
5
.
We prove that smoothing does not discard identifiable structure (Proposition
4.3
). As
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, the regularized solutions recover
the correct
on-manifold
(tangential) transport signal
, matching what is identifiable from the unregularized problem.

In the most favorable cases, where the limiting potential is differentiable, this can be strengthened to full map recovery, with the learned maps
converging (in a graphical sense) to an optimal Monge map
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
(Theorem
4.1
).

Why small noise can be hard to optimize

Empirically,
5
observe that reducing the noise level to a small positive
\varepsilon_{\min}>0
yields better stability and performance than annealing all the way to
\varepsilon=0
. We show this is driven by
conditioning
.
In settings where an exact Monge map does not exist
(a common regime for lower-dimensional supports), the
reduced
semi-dual objective (obtained by solving the inner minimization over
T
) can become increasingly
ill-conditioned as
\varepsilon\to 0
:
the Hessian with respect to network parameters can blow up near the optimizer
(Theorem
5.3
, Lemma
D.3
). This implies that gradient-based training typically needs smaller steps, more iterations, and becomes more sensitive to hyperparameters at very small noise. Figure
4
validates this: smaller
\varepsilon

requires more training iterations to converge.

The bias–stability–sample tradeoff

Noise selection is therefore a tradeoff among three effects:
(i)
stability
, which improves with larger
\varepsilon
due to better conditioning;
(ii)
bias
, which increases with larger
\varepsilon
since one solves a smoothed OT problem;
and (iii)
finite-sample estimation error
, since training uses only finitely many samples.
Our analysis makes this tradeoff explicit by bounding the expected error of the optimal plan learned from noisy samples, decomposing it into a bias term and an estimation term (Theorem
5.1
), when the target measure is sufficiently regular. Under the lower-dimensional support assumption, the estimation term depends on the
intrinsic
dimension
rather than the ambient dimension (Figure
1
), leveraging intrinsic Wasserstein estimation phenomena
(
1
)
.

A key implication of Theorem
5.1
is that for a fixed sample size
N
,
accuracy cannot improve indefinitely by taking
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.
Below a statistically meaningful scale, the finite-sample estimation term dominates, so further reducing the additive noise
\varepsilon Y
does not improve the rate. At the same time, smaller
\varepsilon
can severely deteriorate optimization conditioning (Theorem
5.3
, Lemma
D.3
).

This motivates a
terminal noise level

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{stat}}(N)
: the
largest

\varepsilon

(up to scaling constants) that still attains the statistical optimal rate; taking
\varepsilon\ll\varepsilon_{\mathrm{stat}}(N)

cannot improve statistical rate and may only worsen conditioning (see Figure
3
). Balancing bias and estimation in
Theorem
5.1
yields

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{stat}}(N)\propto\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[|Y|]}\begin{cases}N^{-1/2}&m=1,\\
(\log N/N)^{1/2}&m=2,\\
N^{-1/m}&m\geq 3.\end{cases}

(3)

Here
m
is the intrinsic dimension of the support of
\mu
.

When one uses Gaussian noise i.e.
Y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
, we have when
d\gg 1
that
\mathbb{E}|Y|\asymp\sqrt{d}
.
Without normalization, the regularization error would inherit this
ambient-scale factor and would be artificially large.

Our contributions can be
summarized as follows:

•

(Theorems
3.1
&
3.2
) We show that under manifold-supported data, the semi-dual objective underconstrains the recovered transport maps. This creates off-manifold non-identifiability that can lead to spurious recovered maps, while the tangential on-manifold signal remains identifiable.

•

(Theorem
4.1
& Proposition
4.3
)
Building on the smoothing approach of
5
, we give a geometric explanation of how smoothing removes off-support non-identifiability that leads to spurious recovery. We further strengthen prior theory by proving map-level recovery guarantees as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
: full map recovery under regularity, and tangential (on-manifold) recovery in general.

•

(Theorem
5.3
, Lemma
D.3
) We prove that as
\varepsilon\to 0
, the reduced semi-dual objective can become ill-conditioned (the Hessian with respect to the network parameters blows up), explaining the practical sensitivity of training at very small noise.

•

(Theorem
5.1
) We derive a sample-dependent terminal noise
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{stat}}(N)
as in (
3
) that balances regularization bias, optimization stability, and finite-sample error, providing a concrete stopping rule for annealing.

Preliminaries

The
optimal transport map

(
18
)
is defined as a minimizer of a prescribed cost among all maps that push the source measure
\mu
forward to the target measure
\nu
:

\mathcal{T}(\mu,\nu):=\inf_{T_{\#}\mu=\nu}\left[\int_{\mathcal{X}}c(x,T(x))d\mu(x)\right].

(4)

Here,
T_{\#}\mu
denotes the pushforward of
\mu
under
T
, and the constraint
T_{\#}\mu=\nu
means that
T
transports
\mu
to
\nu
. It is well-known that the existence of an optimal transport map is not always guaranteed, and
11
proposed the following convex relaxation of (
4
):

C(\mu,\nu):=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\left[\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}c(x,y)d\pi(x,y)\right],

(5)

We refer to the joint probability distribution
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
as the
transport plan
between
\mu
and
\nu
. Here
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is the set of probability measures with left marginal
\mu
and right marginal
\nu
(see
(
23
;
21
)
).

Semi-dual Neural OT

The Kantorovich problem (
5
) admits a semi-dual formulation
(
23
, Theorem 5.10)
,
(
21
, Proposition 1.11)
:

S(\mu,\nu):=\!\!\sup_{V}\left[\int_{\mathcal{X}}V^{c}(x)d\mu(x)\!+\!\!\int_{\mathcal{Y}}V(y)d\nu(y)\right],

(6)

where
V^{c}
denotes the
c
-transform of
V
, i.e.,

V^{c}(x)=\underset{y\in\mathcal{Y}}{\inf}\left[c(x,y)-V(y)\right].\vskip-5.0pt

(7)

Replacing
V^{c}
in (
6
) by

V^{c}(x)=\inf_{T:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}}\left[c(x,T(x))-V(T(x))\right]

(8)

yields the max–min formulation (
1
). The resulting objective has been used to learn an optimal transport map with neural parameterizations
(
20
;
7
;
15
)
. See related works in Appendix
G
.

Spurious solutions in SNOT

Although (
1
) is naturally derived from (
6
), existing SNOT approaches may admit max–min solutions that include not only the desired OT map but also spurious solutions
(
20
)
. In
5
, consistency of SNOT was established under the assumption that
\mu
does not assign positive mass to sets of Hausdorff dimension
\leq d-1
. When
\mu
is supported on a lower-dimensional set, however, the learned map
T
may fail to satisfy the desired relation
T_{\#}\mu=\nu
; see the examples in
(
5
, Section 3.2)
.

3
Geometric Origins of Spurious Solutions in Neural OT

Spurious solutions in the minimax formulation.

We study the appearance of
spurious solutions
in the minimax formulation (
1
) by fully characterizing its optimal solutions.
We say that a pair
(V^{*},T_{\mathrm{rec}})
is an optimal solution of (1) if

\displaystyle V^{*}

\displaystyle\in\argmax_{V\in C(\mathcal{Y})}\;\inf_{T:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}}\,\mathcal{L}(V,T),

(9)

\displaystyle T_{\operatorname{rec}}

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{T:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}}\,\mathcal{L}(V^{*},T).

(10)

Here we always assume that
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
are compact sets.

For later use, given a fixed potential
V
, we say that
T_{V}
is an optimal recovery map with respect to
V
if

T_{V}\in\argmin_{T:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}}\,\mathcal{L}(V,T).

(11)

We begin by characterizing what it means to optimally recover a map
T_{V}
for a fixed potential
V
.

Theorem 3.1

(Full characterization of the recovery step)
.

For any
V\in C(\mathcal{Y})
and cost
c\in C(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})
, then there exists a
T_{V}
that satisfies (
11
). Furthermore, one has that
T_{V}
satisfies (
11
)
if and only if for
\mu
-a.e.
x

c\bigl(x,T_{V}(x)\bigr)-V\bigl(T_{V}(x)\bigr)=V^{c}(x).

(12)

Theorem
3.1
explains why spurious solutions arise. Indeed, the recovery condition (
12
) is enforced only
\mu
-almost everywhere. We will see below that under the manifold assumption on
\mu
, this condition identifies only the recovered map’s tangential component along the data manifold, leaving the normal component unconstrained.

Theorem 3.2

(Tangential recovery)
.

Assume that there exists a smooth compact manifold
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\text{dim}(\mathcal{M})=m
such that
\mu\ll\mathcal{H}^{m}\llcorner\mathcal{M}
. Let
(V^{*},T_{\operatorname{rec}})
be an optimal solution of (1). Then if the cost
c(x,y)\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and an optimal transport map
T_{\mathrm{opt}}
exists, then for
\mu
-a.e.
x
,

\Pi_{T_{x}\mathcal{M}}\,\nabla_{x}c\bigl(x,T_{\operatorname{rec}}(x)\bigr)=\Pi_{T_{x}\mathcal{M}}\,\nabla_{x}c\bigl(x,T_{\mathrm{opt}}(x)\bigr).

(13)

Here,
\mathcal{H}^{m}
denotes the
m
-dimensional Hausdorff measure and

\mathcal{H}^{m}\llcorner\mathcal{M}
denotes its restriction to
\mathcal{M}
.
In addition,
T_{x}\mathcal{M}
denotes the tangent space of
\mathcal{M}
at
x
, and

\Pi_{T_{x}\mathcal{M}}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto
T_{x}\mathcal{M}
.

In the quadratic cost case, Theorem
3.2
implies for
\mu
-a.e.
x

\Pi_{T_{x}\mathcal{M}}\,T_{\operatorname{rec}}(x)=\Pi_{T_{x}\mathcal{M}}\,T_{\mathrm{opt}}(x)

(14)

meaning that the recovered map agrees with the true optimal transport

along directions tangent to the data manifold
, while discrepancies may occur
in directions normal to
\mathcal{M}
.

Practical takeaway.

Theorems
3.2
show that when
\mu
is supported on a lower-dimensional set, the semi-dual objective is underconstrained.
This underconstraint manifests in directions normal to the data manifold, rendering the recovery
step non-identifiable: there may exist multiple recovery maps that attain the same objective value
while disagreeing substantially as transport maps.
From a learning perspective, this implies that gradient-based optimization can converge to
distinct solutions that fit the objective equally well but behave arbitrarily off the data manifold, leading to unstable or spurious learned maps. Empirically, this gap is visible in Table
1
: the learned neural OT map can match the map
tangentially
while incurring much larger
normal
error.

We now illustrate that the normal component of recovered maps is non-identifiable with a simple example:

Example 3.3

(Non-identifiability in semi-dual recovery)
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be the uniform measures on

\mathrm{supp}(\mu)=[-1,1]\times\{0\},\qquad\mathrm{supp}(\nu)=\{0\}\times[-1,1].

For the quadratic cost
c(x,y)=\tfrac{1}{2}|x-y|^{2}
, any measurable map

T=(T_{1},T_{2})
with
T_{\#}\mu=\nu
is optimal. This constraint implies for
\mu
-a.e.
x

T_{1}(x)=0.

Moreover,
5
showed that any measurable

T_{\mathrm{rec}}:\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\to\mathrm{supp}(\nu)
is an optimal recovery map.
Hence the tangential component
(T_{\mathrm{rec}})_{1}=(T_{\mathrm{opt}})_{1}

\mu
-a.e. for every optimal map
T_{\mathrm{opt}}

(tangential to
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
, i.e. along the
x_{1}
direction), while the normal component
(\cdot)_{2}
may differ arbitrarily.

4
Regularization Removes Spurious Solutions and Stabilizes Learning

4.1
Off-manifold regularization

The failure mode above stems from the fact that the recovery constraint (
12
)
is enforced only
\mu
-almost everywhere, i.e., only on the data support.
A natural way to remove this ambiguity is to replace
\mu
by a full-dimensional approximation,
so that the constraint is imposed throughout ambient space.

Concretely, we smooth
\mu
by additive noise:

\mu_{\varepsilon}:=\operatorname{Law}(X+\varepsilon Y),

(15)

where
X\sim\mu
and
Y
is independent of
X
with density
\rho\geq 0
and
\int\rho=1
.
Equivalently,
\mu_{\varepsilon}=\mu*\rho_{\varepsilon}
, where

\rho_{\varepsilon}(z)=\varepsilon^{-d}\rho(z/\varepsilon)
.
For our map-level recovery analysis, we assume that
\mu_{\varepsilon}
is compactly supported (equivalently, that
\rho
is compactly supported) to enable compactness arguments
(Theorem
4.1
and Proposition
4.3
).
In contrast, the finite-sample convergence rates in Theorem
5.1
only require
\mathbb{E}[|Y|]<\infty
,
and thus apply immediately to Gaussian smoothing
Y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
as commonly used in practice.

From an optimization viewpoint, smoothing acts as an implicit regularizer.
In the unsmoothed problem, the objective constrains the recovered map only on
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
, so the loss
can be insensitive to how the map (or potential) is extended in directions normal to the data manifold.
These off-manifold degrees of freedom create non-identifiability and can lead to families of
equally optimal solutions during training.
Replacing
\mu
by
\mu_{\varepsilon}\ll\mathcal{L}^{d}
expands the set of points at which the constraint (
12
) is
enforced, which reduces this degeneracy and stabilizes both the learned potentials and the
recovered maps. Consistent with this interpretation, Table
1
shows that smoothing reduces the
normal
error.

4.2
Map consistency under regularization

The discussion above shows that smoothing resolves the non-identifiability of the recovery step by enforcing the recovery constraint (
12
) off the data manifold. In the full-dimensional setting, this mechanism uniquely identifies the transport map. Indeed, Lemma
C.1
shows that in the favorable full-dimensional regime, that Theorem
3.2
implies
the semi-dual objective is identifiable: optimizing the objective uniquely determines the transport map.

We now study the vanishing-regularization limit
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.
While
5
proved smoothing restores correct
plan-level

convergence (controlling optimal plans), this does not
establish convergence of the maps
T_{\varepsilon}
themselves, nor optimality of
any limiting map. We address this gap
by proving
map-level
guarantees: under regularity assumptions (a best-case
scenario), the recovered maps
T_{\varepsilon}
converge to the correct optimal limiting
transport map as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.

Theorem 4.1

(Map-level stability under regularization)
.

Let
c(x,y)=\tfrac{1}{2}|x-y|^{2}
. Assume that
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be compact, and let

\mu_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(K)
be such that
\mu_{\varepsilon}\ll\mathcal{L}^{d}
and
\mu_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\mu
.
Fix
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(K)
.

For each
\varepsilon>0
, let
(V_{\varepsilon},T_{\varepsilon})
be an optimal solution of
(
1
) with source measure
\mu_{\varepsilon}
and target measure
\nu
. Then
(V_{\varepsilon}^{cc},T_{\varepsilon})
is also an optimal solution of
(
1
) with the source measure
\mu_{\varepsilon}
and target measure
\nu
.

Then, if one normalizes
V_{\varepsilon}^{cc}
by setting
V_{\varepsilon}^{cc}(0)=0
, then along a subsequence,
V^{cc}_{\varepsilon}
converges uniformly on
K
.

Moreover, if the limiting potential is
V_{0}
, then:

(1)

If
V_{0}^{c}
is differentiable
\mu
-a.e., then

T_{0}(x):=x-\bigl(\nabla V_{0}^{c}(x)\bigr)

is an optimal Monge transport map from
\mu
to
\nu
.

(2)

If, in addition, each
V_{\varepsilon}^{c}
is differentiable on
K
, then for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in K

there exists a sequence
x_{\varepsilon}\to x
such that

T_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon})\to T_{0}(x).

Moreover, the pair
(V_{0},T_{0})
is an optimal solution of (

) with source measure
\mu

and target measure
\nu
.

Remark 4.2
.

Assume that
K
is connected,
\mathcal{L}^{d}(\partial K)=0
,

is Lipschitz continuous,
\nu\ll\mathcal{L}^{d}
, and

.
Then we have that

for Lebesgue a.e.

. In particular,

and

.

Without the differentiability assumption, (i) even if a limiting map exists, it may fail to even be a transport map i.e.

, and (ii) the unsmoothed problem may admit no optimal transport Monge maps (see Example

).

Theorem

characterizes a best-case regime in which smoothing yields full recovery of the optimal transport map as the noise level vanishes. We now prove an unconditional guarantee: even without such regularity, smoothing still preserves the correct on-manifold transport signal, in the sense that the tangential component along the data manifold can always be recovered.

Proposition 4.3

(Tangential recovery on manifolds as noise vanishes)
.

Using the setting and notation of Lemma
C.2
, assume in addition that there exists a smooth

\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of dimension

such that
\mu\ll\mathcal{H}^{m}\llcorner\mathcal{M}
. Let

denote the limiting potential. Then

).

and

.

\nabla_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2}-V^{c}_{0}\right)=\Pi_{T_{x}\mathcal{M}}(T_{\operatorname{opt}}(x))

is the tangential component of any recovered map.

Practical takeaway.

Adding noise during training cannot erase the identifiable on-manifold transport signal: as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
,
the learned potentials recover the correct tangential component of the optimal transport map.
Moreover, in favorable regularity regimes, the same smoothing scheme yields full recovery of the optimal transport map.

5
Bias-Stability Tradeoff for Noise Scheduling

5.1
Plan stability and quantitative rates

We next establish quantitative
plan stability
bounds, controlling how optimal OT couplings change under perturbations of the input distribution.
Unlike the weak convergence guarantees of
5
under smoothing, we provide explicit
finite-sample
rates for learning couplings when the target
\nu
is sufficiently regular.
In particular, our bounds make explicit the
bias-estimation tradeoff
induced by smoothing: the noise level
\varepsilon
introduces approximation bias, while the sample size
N
governs statistical error.
Throughout, we specialize to the quadratic cost
c(x,y)=\tfrac{1}{2}|x-y|^{2}
.

For
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, let
\pi(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
denote an optimal coupling between
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
.
We assume regularity of the target
\nu
, but allow the source
\mu
to be arbitrary (possibly supported on a lower-dimensional set), so Monge maps need not exist and we work directly with plans.

In practice,
\mu
is observed only through samples and we learn from noisy samples. Our goal is to bound the learned plan error by decomposing it into (i) a smoothing term that vanishes as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
(bias) and (ii) a sampling term that vanishes as
N\to\infty
(estimation).
Concretely, we consider

\mu^{\varepsilon}_{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{i}+\varepsilon Y_{i}},

where
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}\sim\mu
and
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{N}\sim Y
are i.i.d. and independent of each other.

Theorem 5.1

(Finite-sample stability with smoothed empirical sources)
.

Assume the target measure
\nu
satisfies the regularity conditions of Theorem
D.1
. Let
\mu
be compactly supported and suppose
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\subset\mathcal{M}
for a compact
m
-dimensional smooth manifold

\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Assume that
Y
satisfies
\mathbb{E}[|Y|]<\infty
, then for
\mu_{\varepsilon}:=\operatorname{Law}(X+\varepsilon Y)

\mathbb{E}\,W_{2}\!\big(\pi(\mu,\nu),\pi(\mu_{N}^{\varepsilon},\nu)\big)\lesssim(\mathbb{E}W_{1}(\mu,\mu^{\varepsilon}_{N}))^{\alpha(p)}

\lesssim(\mathbb{E}[|Y|]\cdot\varepsilon)^{\alpha(p)}\;+\;\begin{cases}N^{-\alpha(p)/2},&m=1,\\[2.0pt]
(\log N/N)^{\alpha(p)/2},&m=2,\\[2.0pt]
N^{-\alpha(p)/m},&m\geq 3.\end{cases}

Where
p\geq\max(4,d)
and
\alpha(p):=\frac{p}{6p+16d}

Remark 5.2
.

As

In Theorem

Takeaway.

Theorem

and Theorem

show that learning OT plans from smoothed samples is fundamentally bottlenecked by estimating the smoothed source in Wasserstein distance:
the upper bound transfers

For fixed

\varepsilon

scales with the intrinsic dimension
m
(not the ambient

5.2
Stability: small noise can degrade conditioning

Regularizing the source distribution removes the off-manifold ambiguity from Section
3
, but introduces a tradeoff:
larger noise improves identifiability and stabilizes optimization, while smaller noise reduces regularization bias. As observed in
5
, stopping the annealing at a small constant
\varepsilon_{\min}>0
can be more stable than continuing to
\varepsilon=0
. We justify this via (i) an
optimization
effect—overly small noise induces sharp maps and an ill-conditioned loss landscape, and
(ii) a
statistical
effect, there is a noise floor
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{stat}}(N)
below which finite-sample estimation error dominates. Accordingly, we set the terminal noise level to
\varepsilon_{K}=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{stat}}(N)
from (
3
).

We now explain why training should stop at a nonzero terminal noise level
\varepsilon_{K}
.
Very small noise can induce
sharp
(high-Jacobian) transport maps, which in turn create large curvature in the objective and make gradient-based training increasingly sensitive.

As a first step, Lemma
D.3
shows that if the unsmoothed problem admits no optimal Monge map, then the smoothed Monge maps
(T_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
satisfies

\limsup_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\|\nabla T_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R_{0}})}=+\infty

for some
R_{0}>0
. This is typical for manifold-supported data: smoothing restores existence but can induce sharp off-manifold extensions.

To illustrate Lemma
D.3
, Example
D.4
gives a 1D toy case where smoothing regularizes recovery: as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
,
T_{\varepsilon}
becomes steep, while larger
\varepsilon
smooths the map and reduces
\|\nabla T_{\varepsilon}\|
. Thus, larger
\varepsilon
can improve conditioning at the cost of bias.

To connect with training, we consider the reduced objective.

\mathcal{J}(\theta):=\inf_{T}\mathcal{L}(V_{\theta},T).

Even if the infimum is attained exactly, the curvature of
\mathcal{J}
in
\theta
depends on derivatives of the induced map
T_{\theta}
.

Theorem 5.3

(Curvature amplification in the reduced objective)
.

Under mild regularity and uniqueness assumptions, the Hessian
\nabla^{2}_{\theta\theta}\mathcal{J}(\theta)
contains the amplification factor

\int_{\mathcal{X}}G_{\theta}(x)^{\top}\nabla_{x}T_{\theta}(x)G_{\theta}(x)d\mu(x),

where
G_{\theta}(x)=\nabla_{x}\nabla_{\theta}V_{\theta}(T_{\theta}(x))
.

An explicit formula for
\nabla^{2}_{\theta\theta}\mathcal{J}(\theta)
is given in Lemma
D.5
.

Practical takeaway.

Lemma
D.3
and Theorem
5.3
suggest a simple mechanism:
as
\varepsilon
decreases, the transport map can become sharp (large
\|\nabla T_{\varepsilon}\|
), which can in turn make the loss landscape more ill-conditioned for gradient methods. This motivates using a nonzero terminal noise in practice, rather than pushing
\varepsilon\to 0
in a finite-sample pipeline.

7
Conclusion

This paper provides a geometric and statistical characterization of spurious solutions in semi-dual Neural Optimal Transport (SNOT). We identify that when data concentrate on a manifold, the objective is underconstrained in normal directions, though the tangential signal remains identifiable. To resolve this, we utilize additive-noise smoothing to remove off-manifold ambiguity and prove that recovered maps
T_{\epsilon}
converge to the unique Monge map as smoothing vanishes. Our quantitative analysis establishes that plan estimation error scales with the intrinsic dimension
m
rather than the ambient dimension
d
, bypassing the curse of dimensionality. We derive a principled terminal noise level
\varepsilon_{\text{stat}}(N)
that balances regularization bias against optimization stability. We validate our theory through a series of numerical experiments.

Moreover, there exists
\mu
supported on
[0,1]^{m}\times\{0\}^{d-m}
such that for
m\geq 3

\mathbb{E}\Big[W_{2}\big(\mu,\,\mu_{N}^{\varepsilon}\big)\Big]\;\geq\;\frac{1}{108m}\,N^{-1/m}-\varepsilon\mathbb{E}|Y|.

Proof of Theorem
D.2
.

We define the projection onto the first component by

\operatorname{proj}_{1}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{X},\qquad\operatorname{proj}_{1}(x,y)=x.

Observe that
\operatorname{proj}_{1}
is 1-Lipschitz continuous.

For notational simplicity, set
\pi:=\pi(\mu,\nu)
and
\pi_{N}^{\varepsilon}:=\pi(\mu_{N}^{\varepsilon},\nu)
. From the left marginal constraints on these optimal plans, we have that

(\operatorname{proj}_{1})_{\#}\pi=\mu
and

(\operatorname{proj}_{1})_{\#}\pi_{N}^{\varepsilon}=\mu_{N}^{\varepsilon}
.

Let
\gamma\in\Pi(\pi,\pi_{N}^{\varepsilon})
be any coupling between
\pi
and

\pi_{N}^{\varepsilon}
.
Then the pushforward
(\operatorname{proj}_{1},\operatorname{proj}_{1})_{\#}\gamma
is a coupling between
(\operatorname{proj}_{1})_{\#}\pi=\mu
and

(\operatorname{proj}_{1})_{\#}\pi_{N}^{\varepsilon}=\mu_{N}^{\varepsilon}
.

Using the
1
-Lipschitz property of
\operatorname{proj}_{1}
and the definition of
W_{2}
, we obtain

W_{2}(\mu,\mu_{N}^{\varepsilon})^{2}\leq\iint\|\operatorname{proj}_{1}(z)-\operatorname{proj}_{1}(z^{\prime})\|^{2}\,d\gamma(z,z^{\prime})\leq\iint|z-z^{\prime}|^{2}\cdot\,d\gamma(z,z^{\prime}).

Taking the infimum over all
\gamma\in\Pi(\pi,\pi_{N}^{\varepsilon})
yields

W_{2}(\mu,\mu_{N}^{\varepsilon})\leq W_{2}(\pi,\pi_{N}^{\varepsilon}).

Taking expectation implies the lower bound.

Now assume
\mu=\text{Unif}([0,1]^{m})\otimes\delta_{0}^{d-m}
.
Since
W_{2}\geq W_{1}
, we have

W_{2}(\mu,\mu_{N}^{\varepsilon})\geq W_{1}(\mu,\mu_{N}^{\varepsilon})\geq W_{1}(\mu,\mu_{N})-W_{1}(\mu_{N},\mu_{N}^{\varepsilon}).

To bound
W_{1}(\mu_{N},\mu_{N}^{\varepsilon})
, use the coupling

\gamma:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{(X_{i},X_{i}+\varepsilon Y_{i})},

which yields

W_{1}(\mu_{N},\mu_{N}^{\varepsilon})\leq\varepsilon\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|Y_{i}|\Big).

Taking expectation yields

\mathbb{E}\,W_{1}(\mu_{N},\mu_{N}^{\varepsilon})\leq\varepsilon\mathbb{E}|Y|

For the estimation term,
W_{1}(\mu,\mu_{N})
, we use the dual formulation of
W_{1}
.
For any
\phi\in\text{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi\,d(\mu-\mu_{N})=\int_{[0,1]^{m}}\phi(x,0)\,dx-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi(X_{i}).

Writing
X_{i}=(U_{i},0)
with
U_{i}\sim\text{Unif}([0,1]^{m})
i.i.d. and defining

\psi(u):=\phi(u,0)
, we have
\psi\in\text{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
and

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi\,d(\mu-\mu_{N})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\psi\,d(\bar{\mu}-\bar{\mu}_{N}),\quad\bar{\mu}:=\text{Unif}([0,1]^{m}),\ \bar{\mu}_{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{U_{i}}.

Hence, from the dual formulation of the
W_{1}
metric,
W_{1}(\mu,\mu_{N})=W_{1}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\mu}_{N})
. Hence,

(
2
, Theorem 2.14)
implies

\mathbb{E}\,W_{1}(\mu,\mu_{N})\geq\frac{1}{108m}\,N^{-1/m}.

Combining the above inequalities completes the proof.

∎

Lemma D.3

assume that

such that

\sup_{0<\varepsilon\leq 1}\ \|\nabla T_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R_{0}})}=+\infty.

We argue by contradiction. Then for every
R>0

D.3
A 1D toy example illustrating instability of Monge map gradients

Now we present a 1D toy example that illustrates the instability in Monge maps for small
\varepsilon>0
, while the maps become more stable as
\varepsilon
gets larger.

Example D.4

(A stability proxy in 1D)
.

In the 1D toy case with
\mu=\delta_{0}
, Gaussian smoothing gives
\mu_{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{N}(0,\varepsilon^{2})
.
Taking
\nu=\mathrm{Unif}([-1,1])
, the optimal map is

T_{\varepsilon}(x)=2\Phi(x/\varepsilon)-1,

where
\Phi
is the standard normal CDF. Differentiating yields

T_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(x)=\frac{2}{\varepsilon\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{x^{2}}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\Big),\quad\text{so}\quad\|T_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}=\frac{2}{\varepsilon\sqrt{2\pi}}.

Thus, in this example, larger
\varepsilon
produces a flatter (more stable) map, while
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
produces an increasingly sharp map.

Lemma D.5

(Derivatives of the reduced semi-dual)
.

Let
c(x,y)=\tfrac{1}{2}|x-y|^{2}
and define

\mathcal{J}(\theta):=\int V_{\theta}^{c}\,d\mu+\int V_{\theta}\,d\nu,\qquad V_{\theta}^{c}(x):=\inf_{y}\Big\{\tfrac{1}{2}|x-y|^{2}-V_{\theta}(y)\Big\}.

Assume the minimizer in the definition of
V_{\theta}^{c}
exists and is unique and write it as
T_{\theta}(x)
; set
and
G_{\theta}(x):=\nabla_{x}\nabla_{\theta}V_{\theta}(T_{\theta}(x))
.
If
V_{\theta}
is
C^{2}
and
A_{\theta}(x)
is invertible
\mu
-a.e., then

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\int\nabla_{\theta}V_{\theta}\,d\nu-\int\nabla_{\theta}V_{\theta}(T_{\theta}(x))\,d\mu(x),

and

\nabla_{\theta\theta}^{2}\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\int\nabla_{\theta\theta}^{2}V_{\theta}\,d\nu-\int\nabla_{\theta\theta}^{2}V_{\theta}(T_{\theta}(x))\,d\mu(x)-\int G_{\theta}(x)^{\top}\nabla_{x}T_{\theta}(x)G_{\theta}(x)\,d\mu(x).

Proof.

Let
T_{\theta}(x)
be the unique minimizer in the
c
-transform. Then

V_{\theta}^{c}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}|x-T_{\theta}(x)|^{2}-V_{\theta}(T_{\theta}(x)).

Observe that by differentiating the above equality we obtain

\nabla_{\theta}V_{\theta}^{c}(x)=-\nabla_{\theta}V_{\theta}(T_{\theta}(x)),

which gives the gradient formula after integrating.

For the Hessian, we first compute
A_{\theta}(x)
. By differentiating in
x

the first order optimality condition

0=T_{\theta}(x)-x-\nabla V_{\theta}(T_{\theta}(x)).

(32)

implies that

\nabla_{x}T_{\theta}=(I-\nabla_{xx}^{2}V_{\theta}(T_{\theta}))^{-1}.

Differentiating this first order optimality condition in
\theta
implies that

\partial_{\theta}T_{\theta}(x)=(\nabla_{x}T_{\theta})G_{\theta}(x).

Thus
\partial_{\theta}T_{\theta}(x)=\nabla_{x}T_{\theta}(x)G_{\theta}(x)
. Plugging this into the chain rule for

\partial_{\theta}\big(\nabla_{\theta}V_{\theta}(T_{\theta}(x))\big)
yields

\nabla_{\theta\theta}^{2}\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\int\nabla_{\theta\theta}^{2}V_{\theta}\,d\nu-\int\nabla_{\theta\theta}^{2}V_{\theta}(T_{\theta})\,d\mu-\int G_{\theta}^{\top}\nabla_{x}T_{\theta}G_{\theta}\,d\mu.

Regularization effect of Smoothing

We empirically validate the theoretical predictions established in Theorem

and Lemma

and

\mu_{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{N}(0,\varepsilon^{2}I_{10})
. In this regime, the optimal Monge map is analytically given by
T_{\varepsilon}(x)=x/\varepsilon
, which possesses a Jacobian
\nabla T_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{-1}I_{10}
that diverges as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

The results in Figure
5
confirm that the learned Neural OT map inherits this small-
\varepsilon
gradient blow-up. As the noise level
\varepsilon
vanishes, the supremum norm of the gradient
\|\nabla T_{\theta,\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}}
increases rapidly, mirroring the
\varepsilon^{-1}
singularity of the exact transport map. Because Theorem
5.3
explicitly links the Hessian of the training objective
\nabla^{2}_{\theta\theta}\mathcal{J}(\theta)
to the Jacobian
\nabla T_{\theta}
, this gradient divergence provides a clear numerical mechanism for the ill-conditioning observed in training.

Appendix G
Related Works

Neural Optimal Transport methods aim to learn the optimal transport maps between the source and target distributions using neural networks. Many approaches proposed methods based on the semi-dual formulation of the optimal transport problem
(
20
;
7
;
3
;
15
;
8
)
. These approaches have been successfully applied to generative modeling
(
20
;
8
;
3
;
4
)
, point cloud completion
(
13
)
, and inverse problems
(
9
)
.

Despite these successes, Semi-dual Neural Optimal Transport (SNOT) suffers from a fundamental limitation known as the
spurious solution problem
. The spurious solution problem refers to a failure mode of SNOT, where the solution to the max-min objective
\mathcal{L}(V,T)
(
1
) fails to recover the correct optimal transport map. Although the ground-truth Kantorovich potential and transport map
(V^{\star},T^{\star})
are a solution to the max-min formulation, the converse does not generally hold
(
20
;
8
)
. In particular, even when the max-min objecive is solved exactly, the recovered map
T_{rec}
may fail to push the source measure
\mu
to the target
\nu
, leading to incorrect transport maps.

8
showed that, when
\mu
is atomless, a stronger condition of optimality, i.e., the saddle point solution
(V_{sad},T_{sad})
of
\mathcal{L}_{V,T}
, is equivalent to the Kantorovich optimal potential and optimal transport map. Specifically, a saddle point satisfies

V_{sad}\in\arg\max_{V}\mathcal{L}(V,T_{sad}),\qquad T_{sad}\in\arg\min_{T}\mathcal{L}(V_{sad},T).

(33)

However, this recovery is not guaranteed for the generic max-min solution of
\mathcal{L}_{V,T}
.

More recently,
5
established a sufficient condition under which the max-min solution recovers the optimal transport map. Their condition requires that the source measure
\mu
does not assign positive mass to measurable sets of Hausdorff dimension at most
d-1
, where
d
denotes the ambient dimension. To enforce this condition in practice, they proposed smoothing regularization of the source measure and analyzed the resulting convergence of the learned optimal transport plan
\pi^{\star}
.

In this paper, we provide a sharper and more comprehensive theoretical analysis of the spurious solution problem within the SNOT framework. First, while
5
derived a sufficient condition to prevent spurious solutions, we characterize the degree to which the max-min solution remains accurate even when this condition is not satisfied (Theorems
3.1
&
3.2
). Second, while prior work established only weak-convergence results for the optimal transport plan
\pi^{\star}
, we prove map-level recovery guarantees for the transport map
T^{\star}
(Theorem
4.1
). Third, we derive quantitative convergence rates for
\pi^{\star}
(Theorem
5.1
), which are significantly stronger than previous qualitative results. Finally, based on these quantitative rates, we propose a principled noise annealing schedule
\varepsilon_{\textnormal{stat}}(N)
.
