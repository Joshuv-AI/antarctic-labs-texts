Molecular and Immunohistochemical Analyses of PLIN4 in the Spinal Cord and Brain During the Development of Electroacupuncture‐Induced Analgesic Tolerance in Mice
Authors: Shao‐Jia Jiang, Shi‐Ya Cao, Ming‐Fei Fan, Hao Li, Rui‐Ling Yin, Qiang‐Hui Lei, Sha Nan, Hao‐Long Wang, Zi‐Hui Cai, Ya‐Ya Jiao, Fei Huang, Yi Ding, Xiao‐Shu Zhou, Xin‐Yue Chai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundElectroacupuncture (EA) is an important non‐pharmacological approach for pain management in veterinary and human medicine. However, electroacupuncture‐induced analgesic tolerance (EAT) limits its long‐term efficacy, and the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Perilipin 4 (Plin4) is a lipid droplet‐associated protein involved in lipid storage, lipid droplet dynamics, and cellular lipid homeostasis. Emerging evidence suggests that it also plays a role in neuronal lipid metabolic changes and pain modulation.ObjectivesTo investigate the role of Plin4 in the development of EAT and explore its potential as a molecular target for attenuating this limitation.MethodsEAT was induced in male mice by daily 100 Hz EA stimulation for 7 consecutive days. Plin4 expression levels in the amygdala, periaqueductal grey, and spinal cord were examined, and immunofluorescence was used to determine cellular localization. The functional role of Plin4 was assessed by intrathecal Plin4 knockdown in mice and by Plin4 overexpression or knockdown in N2a cells. A total of 99 male mice were used in the primary experiments, with an additional 24 mice used for siRNA knockdown validation in the Supporting Information.ResultsRepeated EA induced analgesic tolerance and markedly upregulated Plin4 in the spinal cord, amygdala, and periaqueductal grey. In the spinal cord, increased Plin4 was mainly localized to spinal cord dorsal horn neurons. Intrathecal Plin4 knockdown delayed EAT onset and increased the pain threshold. In N2a cells, Plin4 expression was positively associated with lipid droplet formation.ConclusionsSpinal neuronal Plin4 contributes to the development of EA‐induced analgesic tolerance, possibly by altering neuronal lipid homeostasis. Plin4 may represent a potential molecular target for attenuating tolerance, prolonging EA‐induced analgesia, and optimizing EA‐based pain management strategies in veterinary medicine.

Introduction

Pain experienced by veterinary patients is complex and multidimensional, encompassing both sensory and affective components. In companion animals, chronic pain carries significant implications for welfare and overall quality of life; it profoundly compromises well‐being in dogs (Malkani et al. 2024) and may alter behaviour, health, quality of life, and the owner–cat bond in cats (Monteiro and Steagall 2019). Although pharmacological analgesia remains foundational to veterinary pain management, its long‐term use is limited by adverse effects, drug tolerance, and limited suitability for the long‐term management of chronic pain (Epstein et al. 2015; Gruen et al. 2022). Therefore, current guidelines emphasize multimodal analgesia integrating pharmacological and non‐pharmacological approaches, highlighting the need for safer adjunctive analgesic strategies in small animal practice (Epstein et al. 2015; Gruen et al. 2022; Monteiro et al. 2023).

Electroacupuncture (EA), combining traditional acupuncture with electrical stimulation, is considered a relatively safe adjunctive non‐pharmacological modality and an important component of multimodal pain management in companion animals (Dewey and Xie 2021; Huntingford and Petty 2022). Veterinary studies have explored the application of EA for post‐operative pain management in dogs and cats, as well as in chronic thoracolumbar pain in horses (Groppetti et al. 2011; NASCIMENTO et al. 2019; Xie et al. 2005). Mechanistically, EA is widely recognized to produce analgesic effects partly by activating the descending pain inhibitory system (Zhang et al. 2014; Lv et al. 2019). Key central structures involved in this system include the spinal cord, the periaqueductal grey (PAG), and the amygdala (AMY) (Millan 2002; Pedersen et al. 2025). The PAG is a grey matter region of the midbrain surrounding the cerebral aqueduct and serves as a key centre for descending pain modulation (Zhang et al. 2024), whereas the AMY is a limbic nuclear complex located in the temporal region of the cerebrum, composed of multiple nuclei, and involved in the emotional and affective processing of pain (Neugebauer 2020; Paul et al. 2025).

However, with prolonged or repeated sessions, the analgesic efficacy of EA often diminishes or even disappears completely, resulting in electroacupuncture analgesic tolerance (EAT). This phenomenon has been demonstrated in both rodent and goat models, where repeated 60 Hz EA induced analgesia followed by a progressive decline in pain threshold consistent with tolerance development (Han et al. 1980; Wan et al. 2018). Previous studies indicate that EAT is closely linked to dysfunction of the endogenous opioid peptide (EOP) system, particularly disruption of the dynamic balance between EOPs and anti‐opioid peptides (Han et al. 1980). Continuous or repetitive EA stimulation elevates the sustained release of EOPs (Han 2003), which in turn increases anti‐opioid peptides such as cholecystokinin octapeptide (CCK‐8) and orphanin FQ (OFQ) (Han et al. 1986; Tian et al. 1998). This negative feedback mechanism reduces the effectiveness of EA over time. Despite substantial research into the molecular mechanisms of EAT‐related bioactive substances, novel targets are still needed to mitigate this issue.

A recent transcriptomic study reported upregulation of perilipin 4 (Plin4) in a mouse model of gouty pain (Fan et al. 2022). However, that study was limited to preliminary observations of differential gene expression. It did not further investigate the functional role or underlying mechanisms of Plin4 in pain, leaving the association between Plin4 and pain development unclear. Furthermore, Plin4, a protein primarily associated with lipid droplet (LD) metabolism (Čopič et al. 2018), has also been implicated in neurodegenerative diseases and psychiatric disorders, such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (Zhu et al. 2021) and heroin addiction (Piechota et al. 2012), suggesting that it may influence neuroplasticity. Given that the development of EAT is closely associated with neuroplastic changes (Yang et al. 2023), we hypothesized that Plin4 may play a critical role in the development of this phenomenon.

Due to their well‐defined and relatively stable genetic backgrounds, mice serve as an excellent model for exploring pain pathways, EA‐induced analgesia, and molecular interventions (Casellas 2011; Wang et al. 2016). Notably, C57BL/10J (B10) mice are relatively sensitive to EA stimulation compared with other commonly used mouse strains (Wan et al. 2001). Therefore, this study used a B10 mouse EAT model combined with transcriptomic sequencing to investigate the role of Plin4 in EAT development and LD generation, with the aim of providing mechanistic insights that can help optimize EA protocols for veterinary pain management.

Materials and Methods

Animal Experimental Design and Grouping

Animals

Specific pathogen‐free male C57BL/10J mice (6–8 weeks) were sourced from Jiangsu GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China). Mice were housed under controlled conditions (temperature 23 ± 2°C, humidity 50%–65%, 12 h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum food and water. All procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Huazhong Agricultural University (HZAUMO‐2024‐0262). After a 7‐day acclimation, experiments were conducted between 08:00 and 20:00.

Establishment of EAT Model

To identify the optimal EA frequency for inducing analgesic tolerance, 18 mice were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 6/group): control, 2 Hz EA, and 100 Hz EA. Mice underwent daily EA treatment for 7 days. The procedure involved restraining the mice at 9:00 AM, shaving the hair over bilateral acupoints Zusanli (ST36) and Sanyinjiao (SP6), and disinfecting the skin with 75% ethanol. Acupuncture needles (0.16 mm × 7 mm; Beijing Zhongyan Taihe Medical Instrument Co., Ltd.) were inserted 3 mm deep at ST36 and SP6. ST36 was located approximately 3.5 mm below the fibular head on the posterolateral side of the knee joint and was needled vertically, whereas SP6 was needled from a point approximately 2.4 mm proximal to the medial malleolus at a 30° angle. The needles were connected to a WQ‐6F EA apparatus (Beijing Xin‐Donghua Electronic Instrument Co., Ltd.). A 30 min stimulation protocol with incrementally increasing intensity (10 min each at 0.5, 1.0, and maximum tolerable intensity) was applied. Control mice were restrained similarly but received no stimulation. A blinded investigator measured tail‐flick latency (TFL) using a thermal analgesia instrument (Panlab, Cornellà de Llobregat, Barcelona, Spain) before and after each session to determine pain threshold changes.

On day 7, mice were deeply anesthetized with a mixed anaesthetic consisting of 3 mg/mL xylazine and a solution of 10% urethane and 2% chloral hydrate, combined in a 3:1 ratio (v/v) (He et al. 2020), and were then euthanized before tissue collection in accordance with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (AVMA 2020). Tissues, including the L4–L6 segments of the lumbar spinal cord, AMY, and PAG, were harvested for downstream transcriptome sequencing and qRT‐PCR analysis.

Tissue Selection and Anatomical Localization

We specifically selected and collected the L4–L6 segments of the lumbar spinal cord because ST36 and SP6 are hindlimb acupoints whose sensory inputs converge directly onto this region. Also, the spinal dorsal horn within these segments serves as the primary site for the integration and processing of peripheral sensory and nociceptive information before it ascends to higher brain structures for pain perception (Todd 2010). The AMY and PAG were selected because they are key central regions involved in pain‐related emotional processing and descending pain modulation, respectively (Millan 2002; Su et al. 2023). The L4–L6 segments of the lumbar spinal cord were identified according to previously described vertebral landmarks for mouse spinal cord segment localization (Harrison et al. 2013). The AMY and PAG were identified and dissected according to atlas‐defined coronal levels and anatomical landmarks in Paxinos and Franklin's mouse brain atlas. The AMY was dissected from atlas‐defined coronal sections containing the corpus amygdaloideum (amygdaloid body). The PAG was dissected from midbrain coronal sections containing the cerebral aqueduct and corresponding to the substantia grisea centralis (central grey matter) surrounding the aqueduct (Paxinos and Franklin 2001).

Temporal Expression of Spinal Plin4

To evaluate temporal changes in spinal Plin4 during EAT development, 45 mice were randomly divided into 5 groups: control (Day 1) and 100 Hz EA treatment at Days 1, 3, 5, and 7 (n = 9/group). We selected days 1, 3, 5, and 7 as tracking intervals to monitor spinal Plin4 expression during the early, middle, and late phases of EAT development. This 7‐day window was chosen based on previous work showing that repeated EA stimulation becomes largely ineffective by Day 7 due to the formation of tolerance (Cui et al. 2016). Sampling at these specific time points allowed us to capture the dynamic molecular shifts leading up to EAT establishment while minimizing animal use in accordance with the 3Rs principle.

Tissues from the L4–L6 segments of the lumbar spinal cord were collected and processed for RNA/protein extraction and histological analysis. This time‐course experiment focused on the spinal cord because it is an important site for EA‐related analgesic modulation (Zhao 2008; Todd 2010; Fan et al. 2024). In addition, our preliminary transcriptomic analysis of the spinal cord, AMY, and PAG showed that Plin4 exhibited marked differential expression in the spinal cord. Based on these considerations, this experiment was designed to analyze the temporal dynamics of spinal Plin4, and the AMY and PAG were not included in this specific time‐course analysis.

Plin4 Knockdown

For functional studies, 36 mice were allocated to four groups: Con+NC, Con+si, EA+NC and EA+si (n = 9/group). Plin4 siRNA or negative control (NC) was injected intrathecally on Days 0, 2, 4, and 6. The EA groups received 100 Hz EA treatment, while the control groups were restrained. Pain thresholds were assessed, and tissues were collected on Day 7 for further analysis.

Tail‐Flick Thermal Pain Assay

Pain sensitivity was measured using a thermal tail‐flick test. Animals were adapted to daily handling for one week before testing. During measurement, mice were gently restrained using cotton gloves, allowing the midsection of the tail to lie naturally on the heating platform with a focused spot set at a focal distance of 45 units. A halogen lamp was used to irradiate the middle portion of the tail, and the TFL was recorded as the duration from irradiation onset to tail withdrawal. Irradiation was limited to a maximum of 10 s to prevent tissue injury. Each measurement was repeated three times at intervals of ≥5 min, and the mean TFL was calculated. The percentage change in pain threshold was calculated using the formula: [(TFL post‐treatment—TFL pre‐treatment)/TFL pre‐treatment] × 100%.

Transcriptome Sequencing and Differential Gene Analysis

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). RNA purity and integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and gel electrophoresis. High‐quality RNA samples were subjected to library construction and Illumina high‐throughput sequencing by BGI (Shenzhen, China). Following quality control, reads were aligned to the mouse genome using HISAT2. FeatureCounts was used for gene quantification, and Pearson correlation analysis was conducted. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using R software, with differentially expressed genes defined by fold change ≥1.5 and Q < 0.05. Heatmaps and volcano plots illustrated expression patterns of differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

Reverse Transcription and qRT‐PCR

Total RNA was extracted from the L4–L6 segments of the lumbar spinal cord, AMY, and PAG tissues using TRIzol reagent. cDNA synthesis was performed using ABScript III RT Master Mix (ABclonal, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. qRT‐PCR was conducted using 2×SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (ABclonal) on a Bio‐Rad CFX‐384 (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Reaction systems and protocols strictly followed the kit instructions. Primers targeting mouse genes were synthesized by Wuhan Genecreate Biological Technology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). GAPDH served as the internal control, validated for stable expression. Primer sequences for differentially expressed mouse genes are listed in Table S1. Relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔC t method.

Western Blot Analysis

Proteins were isolated from the organic phase of TRIzol extracts by chloroform partitioning and precipitated with isopropanol. The protein pellet was washed three times with 0.3 M guanidine hydrochloride‐95% ethanol, dried, and dissolved in 1% SDS solution at 50°C. Proteins (40 µg) underwent electrophoresis on 7.5% SDS‐PAGE gels (Shanghai Yazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 200 V for approximately 30 min, then transferred onto PVDF membranes (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) at 110 V for Plin4 (∼150 min) or β‐actin (∼42 min). Membranes were blocked using SuperKine rapid blocking buffer (Abbkine, Wuhan, China), incubated overnight with primary antibodies (Plin4 antibody, Novus Biologicals, Centennial, CO, USA, 1:275; β‐actin, ABclonal Technology, 1:100,000), followed by secondary antibody incubation (HRP‐conjugated donkey anti‐rabbit IgG, ABclonal Technology, 1:50,000). Chemiluminescence was visualized using SuperKine ultrasensitive ECL reagent (Abbkine) and a CCD camera. Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software, normalizing target protein to β‐actin expression.

Immunofluorescence Colocalization and Time‐Course Staining

The L4–L6 segments of the lumbar spinal cord were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, paraffin‐embedded, and sectioned at 5 µm. The spinal dorsal horn was selected as the region of interest for colocalization analysis because it is a major site for nociceptive afferent input and spinal pain processing, where dorsal horn neurons process sensory information before transmitting it to supraspinal pain‐related regions; in dogs and cats, nociceptive signals also pass through the spinal dorsal horn and ascend via spinal pathways to the somatosensory cortex (Todd 2010; Pedersen et al. 2025). Sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol to water, and subjected to heat‐mediated antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) by microwave heating for 20 min. After blocking with 5% BSA for 30 min at room temperature, sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with rabbit anti‐Plin4 (1:275) together with one of the following markers: mouse anti‐NeuN (1:300; Wuhan Sanying Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China), mouse anti‐Iba1 (1:300; Zhejiang Oasis Biofarm Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China), and mouse anti‐GFAP (1:300; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA). Following three PBS washes, sections were incubated for 45 min at 37°C with secondary antibodies: Cy3‐conjugated donkey anti‐rabbit IgG (1:400; Servicebio, Wuhan, China) to detect Plin4, and FITC‐conjugated donkey anti‐mouse IgG (1:500; Servicebio) to detect mouse NeuN/GFAP/Iba1, followed by three additional PBS washes. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:3000; Servicebio) for 5 min at room temperature, washed three times with PBS, and mounted with an anti‐fade medium. Images were acquired using an EVOS FL Auto Imaging System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For the time‐course analysis, the L4–L6 segments of the lumbar spinal cord collected on EA Days 1, 3, 5, and 7 (D1, D3, D5, D7) were processed identically to compare Plin4 colocalization with cellular markers and its temporal expression dynamics.

Intrathecal siRNA Injection in Conscious Mice

The junction between a 31G insulin needle and its plastic hub was briefly softened over an alcohol flame and the metal needle removed with forceps. The needle was coupled to a 10 µL Hamilton (Reno, NV, USA) microsyringe via a sterile flexible tube, and airtightness was confirmed by repeated aspiration/expulsion of 0.9% NaCl. siRNA complexes were prepared with animal siRNA transfection reagent (Engreen, Beijing, China): 1 µg siRNA + 0.5 µL reagent topped with 0.9% NaCl to 5 µL, incubated at room temperature for 10 min. Mice were restrained with a cotton cloth; the lumbar area was shaved and alcohol‐sterilized, and a 15 mL tube was placed under the abdomen–pelvis to gently flex the spine. Using the midpoint between the anterior iliac crests to locate L5–L6, the needle was inserted ∼20° to the spinal axis through tissue between the spinous and transverse processes, adjusted to ∼10°, and advanced slowly until a sudden tail flick reflex indicated subarachnoid entry. Then 5 µL of complex was injected slowly, with a 15 s dwell to prevent reflux. After 5–10 min observation without abnormalities, mice were returned to cages. siRNA sequences (GeneCreate) are provided in Table S2.

Plin4 Knockdown and Overexpression in N2a Cells

For knockdown, following lentiviral packaging and validation by shRNA screening (methods detailed in Supplement materials 1.3), the sequence with the highest knockdown efficiency was selected for subsequent knockdown experiments. For overexpression, the pcDNA3.1‐Plin4‐Linker‐EGFP plasmid (constructed by Suzhou Institute of Cell Therapy, Suzhou, China) was transiently transfected into N2a cells using Neofect (Beijing, China) at 2.0–2.5 µg per well in six‐well plates. Cells were harvested 48–72 h post‐transfection for analysis. An empty vector control was included, and each experiment was performed in at least three biological replicates.

LDs Staining in N2a Cells (Oil Red O and Nile Red)

Oil Red O and Nile Red staining were used as complementary methods to evaluate LD accumulation in N2a cells: Oil Red O enabled brightfield visualization of neutral lipid deposition, whereas Nile Red provided fluorescence‐based detection of intracellular lipid droplets, allowing cross‐validation of LD changes after Plin4 manipulation (Greenspan et al. 1985; Mehlem et al. 2013). Culture medium was removed, cells were rinsed twice with PBS, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature. Following two washes with PBS (5 min each), cells were stained with freshly prepared Oil Red O working solution (stock solution: double distilled water = 3:2, Solarbio, Beijing, China), filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane and used within 2 h. After a brief rinse with 60% isopropanol, cells were briefly air‐dried, stained for 1–3 min under microscope observation to avoid overstaining, rinsed twice with PBS, and imaged under brightfield microscopy while kept moist with PBS. For Nile Red staining, cells were incubated with Nile Red working solution (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) for 5–10 min at room temperature in the dark, washed twice with PBS (5 min each), and mounted with antifade reagent for fluorescence imaging. Staining conditions and incubation durations were consistent within experiments, and appropriate controls were included.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism v10.0.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Behavioural data involving repeated measurements over time were analyzed by two‐way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni's post hoc test. Other multiple‐group comparisons were performed using one‐way ANOVA followed by appropriate post hoc tests. Data are presented as mean ± SD, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Repeated High‐Frequency EA Induces Analgesic Tolerance in Mice

To investigate the effects of high‐frequency and low‐frequency EA on the development of EAT, mice received EA stimulation at different frequencies (2 and 100 Hz) for 7 consecutive days (Figure 1A). Both frequencies significantly reduced pain thresholds by Day 7 compared with Day 1 (p < 0.001, Figure 1B). In the 2 Hz group, the pain threshold dropped most on Day 2, then gradually recovered, though it remained higher than the control group (p < 0.05). In contrast, the 100 Hz group showed a significant decline in pain thresholds, even reaching negative values on Days 6 and 7, indicating the onset of hyperalgesia. On Day 5, the pain threshold changes in the 100 Hz group showed no significant difference from controls (p > 0.05), while the 2 Hz group still showed significantly higher thresholds (p < 0.05). Thus, the 100 Hz frequency was selected for subsequent experiments.

Transcriptome Sequencing Reveals Plin4 Upregulation in Key Pain‐Regulating Regions of EAT Mice

To identify key genes involved in EAT, transcriptome sequencing was conducted on spinal cord, AMY, and PAG tissues from mice subjected to EAT and control groups (Figure 1A). After applying stringent criteria (fold change ≥ 1.5, Q < 0.05), 178 DEGs (122 upregulated, 56 downregulated) in spinal cord, 126 DEGs (82 upregulated, 44 downregulated) in AMY, and 143 DEGs (104 upregulated, 39 downregulated) in PAG were identified (Figure 1C–E). Further analysis revealed 59 overlapping DEGs among the three tissues, and Z‐score heatmap clustering indicated consistent expression patterns across regions (Figure 1F). Notably, Plin4 was the most significantly upregulated gene (log2 Fold Change: spinal cord, 3.47; PAG, 3.77; AMY, 2.76).

Increased Spinal Plin4 Expression in EAT Mice

To confirm the changes in Plin4 expression, qRT‐PCR and Western blot (WB) were employed. As shown in Figure 2A–C, Plin4 mRNA levels were significantly elevated in the spinal cord (Figure 2A), AMY (Figure 2B), and PAG (Figure 2C) tissues of the EAT group compared with the control group. Given that the spinal cord is the primary integration centre for nociceptive signals and the direct target for subsequent intrathecal interventions, further experiments were performed using the spinal cord. WB analysis confirmed a significant increase in Plin4 protein levels in the spinal cord of the EAT group compared with controls (p < 0.01; Figure 2D,E). These results align with the transcriptomic data, confirming the upregulation of Plin4 in EAT development.

Plin4 Protein in the Spinal Cord Dorsal Horn Is Primarily Localized in Neurons

To clarify the cellular localization of Plin4 protein, immunofluorescence (IF) staining was performed, revealing significant colocalization of Plin4 (red fluorescence) with the neuronal marker NeuN (green fluorescence) in the spinal cord dorsal horn (white arrows, Figure 2F). In contrast, no apparent colocalization was observed with astrocytes (GFAP‐positive) or microglia (Iba1‐positive). These findings suggest that increased Plin4 expression in the spinal cord dorsal horn in the EAT model occurs primarily in neurons, potentially contributing to neuronal mechanisms underlying EAT.

Session‐Dependent Upregulation of Plin4 During EAT Development

To investigate the dynamic expression of Plin4 during the development of EAT, we examined tissue samples on Days 1, 3, 5, and 7 (D1, D3, D5, D7) following EA stimulation. qRT‐PCR analysis showed significantly elevated Plin4 mRNA expression at all EA time points compared with controls (p < 0.05), with the highest elevation at D1, indicating a transient transcriptional response of Plin4 to EA stimulation. Compared with D1, Plin4 transcription levels in the spinal cord of mice showed a declining trend from D3 onwards but still remained significantly above control levels (Figure 3A). WB results confirmed a progressive increase in spinal Plin4 protein expression during the development of EAT, with significant differences at D5 and D7 compared with controls (p < 0.05) (Figure 3B,C). This suggests that the upregulation of Plin4 transcription leads to a cumulative increase in its protein expression, a pattern that aligns with the observed decline in pain thresholds. Given the pivotal role of the spinal cord dorsal horn in nociceptive processing, we next assessed Plin4 expression in this region via IF. IF staining indicated a time‐dependent increase in Plin4 fluorescence intensity in the spinal cord dorsal horn during EAT development. Specifically, there was no significant difference in Plin4 fluorescence intensity between the D1 and control groups, whereas significant increases were observed at D3, D5, and D7 compared with controls (p < 0.05) (Figure 3D,E). Taken together, these findings indicate that repeated daily EA stimulation first induces Plin4 transcription, and this effect accumulates over time, leading to a gradual rise in Plin4 protein levels, particularly within the spinal cord dorsal horn, during the formation of EAT, suggesting a potential role for this protein in nociceptive signal transmission.

Intrathecal Plin4 Knockdown Delays Development of EAT

To elucidate the role of Plin4 in EAT, Plin4 expression was knocked down intrathecally in mice, and changes in pain thresholds were monitored. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by qRT‐PCR and WB (Figure 4A–C). As illustrated in Figure 4D, pain thresholds remained stable in both control groups throughout the experimental period. The EA+NC group exhibited a rapid decline in pain threshold beginning at D1, with thresholds nearing baseline by D7, indicating typical analgesic tolerance development. Although the EA+si group also showed a downward trend, pain thresholds at D1 and D3 were significantly higher than those of the EA+NC group (p < 0.05), suggesting that Plin4 knockdown enhanced analgesic effects and delayed tolerance onset, thus supporting Plin4's role in promoting EAT development.

Plin4 Regulates LD Formation in N2a Cells

Given Plin4's importance in lipid metabolism, its relationship with neuronal LDs was investigated. Plin4 knockdown (Figure 5A,B) and overexpression (Figure 5C,D) in N2a cells were validated by WB. Nile Red fluorescence staining revealed significantly reduced numbers and areas of LDs following Plin4 knockdown (Figure 5E,F). Conversely, Plin4 overexpression significantly increased LD density and area, as demonstrated by both Nile Red (Figure 5G,H) and Oil Red O staining (Figure 5I,J). These findings suggest that Plin4 positively regulates LD formation and may contribute to EAT progression through lipid accumulation.

Discussion

EA is widely used for pain management and has also been incorporated as an adjunctive non‐pharmacological modality in multimodal pain management for dogs and cats (Gruen et al. 2022; Dewey and Xie 2021). Clinical studies in dogs and cats suggest that the analgesic efficacy of acupuncture or EA is influenced by the pain condition and stimulation protocol (Groppetti et al. 2011; NASCIMENTO et al. 2019; Baker‐Meuten et al. 2020; Iwe et al. 2025). However, the analgesic effect of EA may decrease after repeated stimulation, a phenomenon known as EAT (Han et al. 1980). In the present study, we successfully established an EAT model using 100 Hz high‐frequency EA and confirmed that high‐frequency stimulation induces tolerance more rapidly. This result is consistent with previous studies showing that repeated or continuous EA stimulation can induce tolerance in mice, rats, and goats (Huang et al. 2007; Cui et al. 2016; Wan et al. 2018). As mentioned in the Introduction, the development of EAT is considered a negative feedback process. Previous studies have shown that inhibiting CCK or OFQ not only potentiates the analgesic effect of EA but also significantly delays the onset of tolerance (Tian et al. 1998; Tang et al. 1997). Recent studies using rat models further indicate that thymosin beta 4 and microRNA let‐7b‐5p are also involved in EAT development (Wan et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2020).

EA analgesia is closely related to the descending pain inhibitory system, which activates brain regions such as the PAG and AMY to modulate pain (Lv et al. 2019; Price 2000; Gao et al. 2004). Animal studies in rats and goats further support that EA can suppress hyperalgesia or visceral hypersensitivity by engaging descending pain modulatory pathways (Li et al. 2007; Wan et al. 2017). From a clinical veterinary perspective, EA has also been investigated in dogs with thoracolumbar intervertebral disc disease and spinal cord injury, supporting its relevance to small animal spinal pain and neurological conditions (Hayashi et al. 2007; Joaquim et al. 2010; Dragomir et al. 2021). In addition, animal evidence suggests that EA can influence pain‐related emotional behaviours by modulating the basolateral amygdala (Wu et al. 2022). Huang et al. (2019) further revealed a specific neural pathway, namely the basolateral amygdala–prefrontal cortex–PAG–spinal cord pathway. This pathway reduces pain signals and has a causal relationship with both the sensory and affective dimensions of pain (Huang et al. 2019). In summary, based on the critical roles of the spinal cord, PAG, and AMY in the pain modulation network, we selected these three regions for transcriptome sequencing analysis in the present study.

The glucocorticoid (GC) signalling pathway is crucial for coping with sustained stimuli and for maintaining energetic homeostasis (Stavreva et al. 2015). Previous rat studies have shown that EA modulates GC/HPA‐axis‐related responses and glucose metabolism under chronic stress conditions, while GR participates in EA‐induced analgesia in CFA‐induced inflammatory pain through the regulation of Nav1.7 expression (Ma et al. 2020; Luo et al. 2022). Notably, in our EAT model, numerous genes associated with GC signalling were markedly upregulated, including those related to energy metabolism such as Slc2a1, Pdk4, and Plin4 (Carter et al. 2012; Olson and Pessin 1996; de Guia 2020). Although the precise functions of these GC‐related genes remain to be elucidated, their expression profiles parallel observations in models of chronic drug administration (Piechota et al. 2010), providing new insights into the interplay between stress‐energy metabolism pathways and EAT.

Bioinformatic analyses have shown that Plin4 expression increases in mouse models of chronic inflammatory pain and gouty pain (Fan et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2025). Our study provides direct evidence that Plin4 was not only significantly upregulated but also displayed the largest fold change in expression, suggesting that it may play a key role in EAT. However, previous research on Plin4 in the field of pain has been largely limited to bioinformatic analyses of pain models, and direct functional evidence for its role in a physiological model has been lacking. To fill this gap, for the first time, our study provides direct evidence from an EAT model that the phenotype of reduced pain thresholds and even hyperalgesia in mice is positively correlated with spinal Plin4 expression levels. Moreover, in vivo knockdown experiments further demonstrated that inhibiting spinal Plin4 expression can effectively delay the development of tolerance and increase pain thresholds, identifying Plin4 as a critical regulator of EAT formation.

Plin4, with its amphipathic helical structure, can bind directly to the surface of LDs, playing a key role in LD biogenesis, lipolysis, and cellular lipid homeostasis (Čopič et al. 2018; Giménez‐Andrés et al. 2021). However, when Plin4 is abnormally expressed, this homeostatic regulator may become a pathological driver. A study on Parkinson's disease showed that a core Plin4/LD/mitophagy axis is associated with the pathophysiology of the disease (Han et al. 2018). Specifically, within this axis, overexpressed Plin4 promotes the excessive accumulation of intraneuronal LDs and impairs mitophagy, further leading to neurodegenerative damage (Han et al. 2018). Furthermore, another study demonstrated that the formation of a complex between SH2B1 and HSC70 can promote Plin4 degradation, thereby suppressing LD accumulation and oxidative stress and ultimately antagonizing neurodegeneration in Parkinson's disease (Han et al. 2022). Using mouse N2a neuroblastoma cells, we confirmed that Plin4 modulates neuronal LD biogenesis: Plin4 silencing significantly decreased LD number and area, whereas Plin4 overexpression produced the opposite effect. Considering the known relationship among Plin4, LDs, and neural plasticity (Zhang et al. 2025), we propose that Plin4 may contribute to EAT development by promoting LD accumulation, thereby reducing the analgesic efficacy of EA. Future studies should elucidate the interplay among Plin4, mitochondrial function, and lipid‐derived metabolites, thereby providing new mechanistic insights and potential therapeutic targets for EAT.

Despite these findings, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the EAT model was established in C57BL/10J mice. Although this model is useful for elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying electroacupuncture analgesic tolerance, the findings need to be further validated in other animal models, particularly clinically relevant small animal species, such as dogs and cats. In addition, pain sensitivity and responses to electroacupuncture may differ between male and female mice (Smeester et al. 2012). Future studies should include female animals to determine whether the effects observed in this study are sex‐independent. Second, pain sensitivity was assessed only using the tail‐flick test, which primarily reflects spinal reflex responses. Future studies should incorporate additional behavioural assays, such as the hot‐plate, von Frey, and Hargreaves tests, to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of analgesic responses. Third, although 2 and 100 Hz EA were evaluated initially, other stimulation frequencies were not systematically compared. Therefore, the possibility that other frequencies may produce different biological responses cannot be excluded. Finally, although N2a cells can serve as a neuron‐like cell model for preliminary mechanistic exploration, they cannot fully recapitulate the complex microenvironment of spinal dorsal horn neurons and glial cells in vivo. Moreover, although the findings suggest associations among Plin4 expression, EAT development, and lipid droplet accumulation, the current data do not establish a direct causal relationship. Further studies using specific genetic or pharmacological approaches are needed to clarify the causal role of Plin4 in EAT development.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that Plin4 is an important molecular regulator involved in the development of electroacupuncture‐induced analgesic tolerance. In the mouse EAT model, Plin4 was significantly upregulated in pain‐related regions, and intrathecal Plin4 knockdown delayed the onset of EAT and increased pain thresholds. In N2a cells, Plin4 expression was positively associated with lipid droplet formation, suggesting a potential link between neuronal lipid homeostasis and EAT development. These findings provide new mechanistic insight into the reduced analgesic efficacy of repeated EA stimulation, which is clinically relevant to long‐term and multimodal pain management in veterinary practice, particularly in small animal clinical settings. Plin4 may represent a potential molecular target for delaying or attenuating EA‐induced analgesic tolerance, prolonging EA analgesia, and optimizing EA‐based pain management strategies in veterinary medicine.
