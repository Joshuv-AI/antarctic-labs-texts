Association between delayed transcatheter aortic valve replacement beyond 6 months and clinical outcomes in severe aortic stenosis: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Ayumi Omuro, Shinichi Okuda, Yosuke Miyazaki, Masaki Tamitani, Junya Nawata, Yusuke Nakashima, Miho Fujita, Kaori Murakawa, Masamune Uchida, Yuki Furushimo, Hiroshi Kurazumi, Ryo Suzuki, Akihito Mikamo, Takayuki Okamura, Kimikazu Hamano, Motoaki Sano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveEarly intervention for asymptomatic severe aortic stenosis (AS) has shown prognostic benefits; however, some patients undergo transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) after extended observation owing to mild symptoms or comorbidities. Although guidelines recommend surveillance of 6–12 months, the clinical implications of waiting beyond 6 months remain unclear. We assessed whether TAVR performed >6 months after the diagnosis of severe AS was associated with adverse outcomes.MethodsWe retrospectively analysed 149 patients who underwent TAVR for severe AS between January 2021 and December 2023. Patients were classified into early (≤6 months; n=100) and delayed (>6 months; n=49) groups. The primary endpoint was heart failure (HF) hospitalisation or all-cause mortality.ResultsBaseline characteristics were similar between the groups. During a median follow-up of 729 days, the primary endpoint occurred more frequently in the delayed group than in the early group (33% vs 16%, p=0.0134). This excess risk was largely attributable to non-cardiac mortality. In multivariable Cox regression, delayed intervention was independently associated with the primary endpoint (HR 3.47, 95% CI 1.61 to 7.47; p=0.0014); higher Clinical Frailty Scale scores were also associated with this endpoint (HR 1.62, 95% CI 1.07 to 2.42; p=0.0195).ConclusionsTAVR performed 6 months after severe AS diagnosis was associated with a higher risk of HF hospitalisation or all-cause mortality, with the excess risk predominantly attributable to non-cardiac mortality. These findings emphasise the need for careful clinical surveillance and may support more frequent reassessment in selected high-risk patients.Keywords: Aortic Valve Stenosis, Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement, Heart Failure

Introduction

Aortic stenosis (AS) is becoming increasingly prevalent in ageing societies, and the number of patients presenting with AS-related heart failure (HF) continues to rise. Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has been established as an effective therapeutic option for improving survival in patients with AS, as demonstrated in landmark randomised trials.1 2 Despite these advances, approximately 25% of patients experience rehospitalisation for HF after TAVR, highlighting the need to optimise patient selection and the timing of intervention.3

However, substantial barriers to timely intervention persist in real-world practice. International registry data indicate that up to 40% of patients with severe AS do not receive guideline-directed intervention over a 4-year period, which severely compromises long-term survival.4 Furthermore, delays in aortic valve intervention in real-world practice have been shown to be associated with adverse outcomes,5 underscoring the need for optimisation of healthcare systems.

Recent randomised controlled trials have shown the clinical benefits of early intervention in asymptomatic severe AS.6–8 However, real-world practice often differs from these findings. In clinical settings, some patients do not undergo immediate treatment despite meeting the diagnostic criteria for severe AS owing to the absence of symptoms, patient preference, frailty or other clinical considerations. Delays in aortic valve intervention have been reported in contemporary cohorts, and such delays may contribute to adverse outcomes.9 Current guidelines for the management of valvular heart disease recommend clinical and echocardiographic surveillance at intervals of 6–12 months for patients with severe AS10 11; however, the safety of waiting beyond the lower end of this recommended surveillance interval has not been directly evaluated in real-world cohorts. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate whether TAVR performed more than 6 months after the diagnosis of severe AS was associated with adverse clinical outcomes in real-world practice.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective study included consecutive patients who underwent TAVR at Yamaguchi University Hospital between 1 January 2021 and 31 December 2023. Patients with low-flow, low-gradient AS (LF-LG) were excluded. The final study cohort comprised patients who met the diagnostic criteria for severe AS, defined as an aortic valve area <1.0 cm² and either a peak aortic jet velocity ≥4.0 m/s or a mean transvalvular pressure gradient ≥40 mm Hg, based on the current American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) and European Society of Cardiology/European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery (ESC/EACTS) guidelines for the diagnosis of severe AS.10 11 Most patients in this cohort met guideline-based indications for TAVR (Class I or IIa) per ACC/AHA and ESC/EACTS recommendations. This study included only patients who ultimately underwent TAVR. Outcome data for patients who died, deteriorated or became ineligible before TAVR were not systematically captured in our institutional registry.

Data collection

Clinical characteristics, laboratory data, echocardiographic measurements, frailty indices, cardiac damage staging (stage 0–4),12 procedural data and clinical outcomes were retrospectively obtained from electronic medical records. Surgical risk was quantified using the logistic EuroSCORE, EuroSCORE II13 and the Society of Thoracic Surgeons Predicted Risk of Mortality score.14 Frailty status was assessed using gait speed over 5 m, maximal handgrip strength, lower-limb muscle strength, skeletal muscle mass index and the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS).15 Symptom status (asymptomatic or symptomatic) was determined retrospectively based on chart documentation and New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class. Because this was a retrospective study, symptom assessment relied primarily on patient self-report recorded in clinical notes and standardised exercise stress testing was not uniformly performed.

Group classification

Patients were categorised into two groups according to the interval between the diagnosis of severe AS and TAVR. The 6-month threshold was selected a priori based on current guideline recommendations: both the 2020 ACC/11AHA and 2021 ESC/EACTS guidelines recommend clinical and echocardiographic surveillance at intervals of 6–12 months for patients with severe AS.10 11 Therefore, the groups were defined as follows: the early intervention group (E group) comprised patients who underwent TAVR within 6 months of the diagnosis of severe AS, corresponding to the lower end of the commonly recommended surveillance interval; and the delayed intervention group (D group) comprised patients who underwent TAVR >6 months after diagnosis, exceeding the minimum recommended surveillance interval.

This classification was designed to evaluate whether waiting beyond the guideline-recommended minimum surveillance interval was associated with adverse clinical outcomes in real-world practice settings.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was a composite of hospitalisation for HF or all-cause mortality. Clinical outcomes were compared between the two groups using Kaplan-Meier survival curves.

Echocardiography

A comprehensive transthoracic echocardiographic examination was performed in accordance with the recommendations of the American Society of Echocardiography16–19 using commercially available ultrasonography systems. Standard parameters, including the aortic valve area calculated using the continuity equation, peak aortic jet velocity and mean transvalvular pressure gradient, were obtained using Doppler echocardiography. Peak aortic jet velocity was assessed using multiple acoustic windows, including the parasternal, apical and right parasternal approaches, to ensure the highest-velocity recording. Echocardiographic assessments were performed by experienced sonographers and board-certified cardiologists, with review by board-certified cardiologists.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using JMP Student Edition V.18 software (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are presented as the mean±SD, whereas non-normally distributed variables are expressed as the median with IQR. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages.

For between-group comparisons, Student’s t-test was applied to normally distributed continuous variables with equal variances, as confirmed using Levene’s test. When equal variances were not assumed, Welch’s t-test was used. The Mann-Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test when the expected cell counts were <5.

Event-free survival was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between groups were evaluated using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards models were used to identify factors associated with clinical endpoints. The multivariable model included the primary exposure variable, delayed intervention >6 months, variables repeatedly reported as prognostic factors (age, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF)),20–24 and variables with p<0.10 in univariable analyses. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. Baseline clinical variables had less than 5% missing values. For echocardiographic parameters, heavy mitral annular calcification resulted in missing septal e′ and E/e′ values in approximately 15% of patients, and tricuspid regurgitation pressure gradient was not obtained in 13% of patients because no tricuspid regurgitation was present. Given this pattern and extent of missingness, complete-case analysis was applied. Given the limited number of events, multivariable Cox models were selected to avoid overfitting associated with propensity score methods in small datasets.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Information about the study was disclosed on the hospital website, and an opt-out approach was implemented to allow patients to decline participation.

Results

Baseline characteristics

During the study period, 174 patients underwent TAVR. After excluding 25 patients with LF-LG AS, 149 patients were included in the final analysis. The mean age was 85.8±4.4 years, and 36% were male. Of the total, 100 were assigned to the early intervention group (E group) and 49 to the delayed intervention group (D group). Reasons for delayed intervention in the D group included initial refusal of surgery (n=16), asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic status (n=11), prioritisation of treatment for other diseases (n=9) and clinical observation based on the physician’s judgement (n=13). The median observation period in the D group was 13 months (IQR 8–34; maximum, 82 months). When stratified by diagnostic context, patients previously diagnosed with moderate or less-than-moderate AS had significantly longer observation periods before TAVR than those newly diagnosed with severe AS (median 17.5 vs 9 months, p=0.0450).

Baseline characteristics, including age, sex, body mass index, most comorbidities, frailty indices and echocardiographic parameters, were largely similar between the groups, with a few notable exceptions (tables 1 and 2 and online supplemental table S1). A history of stroke was significantly more prevalent in the D group than in the E group (33% vs 16%, p=0.0200). The prevalence of chronic kidney disease was numerically higher in the D group, although the difference was not statistically significant (80% vs 65%, p=0.0684). Similarly, the distribution of cardiac damage stages differed between the groups, but the difference did not reach statistical significance (p=0.0560). Regarding frailty, the D group tended to have slower preprocedural 5 m gait speed (0.65 vs 0.75 m/s, p=0.0604) and had significantly lower maximum lower-limb muscle strength (175.3 vs 197.1 N, p=0.0467). No significant differences were observed in age, sex, body size, CFS categories, other comorbidities or echocardiographic parameters. Discharge medications at hospital discharge were similar between groups, as summarised in online supplemental table S2.

Clinical outcomes during follow-up

During a median follow-up of 729 days (IQR 387–883.5 days), the incidence of the primary composite endpoint, HF hospitalisation or all-cause mortality, was significantly higher in the D group than in the E group (33% vs 16%, log-rank p=0.0134) (figure 1). The composite of HF hospitalisation or cardiac death did not differ significantly between the groups (log-rank p=0.9719) (figure 2). Causes of death differed between the groups. In the E group, deaths were due to cardiac causes (n=5), pneumonia (n=2), trauma (n=1) and unknown causes (n=1). In contrast, the D group had fewer cardiac deaths (n=2) but more non-cardiac deaths, including pneumonia (n=4), malignancy (n=2), age-related deterioration (n=1) and unknown causes (n=4). In exploratory subgroup analyses stratified by aortic valve peak velocity (<4.5 m/s vs ≥4.5 m/s), the early intervention group showed a consistently lower incidence of the composite endpoint across both strata. In the ≥4.5 m/s subgroup, the difference reached statistical significance (log-rank p=0.0460), whereas the <4.5 m/s subgroup did not show a significant difference (log-rank p=0.1909) (online supplemental figures S1 and S2).

Echocardiographic and laboratory changes 1 year after TAVR

At 1 year after TAVR, no significant differences in B-type natriuretic peptide levels, change in LVEF, or left ventricular mass index were observed between the E and D groups. Additionally, the severity of postoperative aortic regurgitation did not differ significantly between the two groups (online supplemental table S3).

Multivariable cox regression analysis for predictors of HF hospitalisation or all-cause mortality

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis, adjusting for established prognostic factors (age, eGFR and LVEF) and variables with p<0.10 in the univariable analysis, identified delayed intervention (TAVR performed >6 months after diagnosis; HR 3.47, 95% CI 1.61 to 7.47; p=0.0014), higher CFS score (HR 1.62 per 1-grade increase, 95% CI 1.07 to 2.42; p=0.0195), higher B-type natriuretic peptide level (HR 1.07 per 100 pg/mL increase, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.12; p=0.0098) and lower LVEF (HR 1.04 per 1% decrease, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.09; p=0.0354) as independent predictors of the primary endpoint (table 3).

Discussion

Significance and clinical impact

The primary significance of this study is the observation that prolonged delays after the diagnosis of severe AS were associated with a higher risk of HF hospitalisation or all-cause mortality in real-world practice. In clinical settings, treatment is often deferred because of minimal symptoms, patient preference and competing comorbidities. Our findings suggest that when the interval between diagnosis and intervention exceeds 6 months, the observed risk of the primary endpoint is higher, with this difference largely related to non-cardiac mortality. These results highlight the importance of timely reassessment of patients with severe AS and may help inform clinical decision-making regarding the appropriate timing of intervention.

Real-world delays in treatment after the diagnosis of severe AS

Multiple randomised controlled trials have established the clinical efficacy of TAVR across the full spectrum of surgical risk, demonstrating its non-inferiority or even superiority to surgical aortic valve replacement in high-risk, intermediate-risk and low-risk patients.1 2 25–27 Given this robust evidence base, the timing of intervention has become a critical clinical consideration. Recent randomised trials have shown that early intervention for asymptomatic AS may improve clinical outcomes.6–8 However, the definition of ‘asymptomatic’ in these trials differs substantially from that in our real-world cohort. In these studies, asymptomatic status was confirmed using standardised exercise stress testing and objective markers of disease progression, whereas symptom assessment in our cohort relied primarily on chart documentation and NYHA class.

Regardless of whether deferral stems from truly absent symptoms, unrecognised mild symptoms, patient preference or frailty, treatment is frequently deferred in real-world practice, with many patients undergoing periodic clinical follow-up over several months.10 11 In our cohort, 49 of 149 patients (32.9%) experienced a delay of more than 6 months between the diagnosis of severe AS and TAVR. Importantly, patients previously diagnosed with moderate or less severe AS had significantly longer observation periods than those newly diagnosed with severe AS, suggesting that an earlier-stage diagnosis may influence the timing of subsequent intervention.

These findings are consistent with prior reports showing that older patients often defer intervention because of frailty, multimorbidity, fear of procedural risks or family concerns.28–30 In the OCEAN-TAVI registry, even a single refusal of TAVR was associated with significantly worse outcomes,9 highlighting the potential impact of delays in decision-making. A persistent gap remains between the evidence supporting early intervention and the complex, multifactorial decision-making encountered in everyday practice. Understanding these factors is essential for developing patient-centred strategies that support timely evaluation while respecting individual preferences. This study was designed to evaluate the prognostic impact of real-world delays in treatment decision-making rather than to assess the efficacy of waiting in strictly asymptomatic patients as defined in randomised clinical trials.

Predictors of long-term outcomes after TAVR and the impact of delayed intervention

Long-term outcomes after TAVR have consistently been associated with advanced age, impaired renal function, reduced left ventricular systolic performance and periprocedural complications.20–24 Additional comorbidities, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and atrial fibrillation, have also been identified as important prognostic factors in large-scale studies.31 32 Recently, frailty has emerged as a central determinant of outcomes in older TAVR candidates, with studies such as FRAILTY-AVR and OCEAN-TAVI showing strong associations between frailty assessed using validated tools, such as the CFS, and mortality, functional decline and procedural complications.33 34 Consistent with these findings, our study identified higher CFS scores as an independent predictor of the primary composite endpoint.

A key observation of this study is that delayed intervention, defined as TAVR performed more than 6 months after the diagnosis of severe AS, was independently associated with a higher risk (HR 3.47) of all-cause mortality or HF hospitalisation, even after adjustment for established prognostic factors. Notably, the excess risk in the delayed-intervention group was largely explained by non-cardiac mortality. In our cohort, the delayed group exhibited a higher proportion of deaths due to pneumonia, malignancy and age-related decline, whereas cardiac deaths were less frequent. This pattern suggests that prolonged observation may coincide with a period of accumulating systemic vulnerability in elderly patients, rather than progression of valvular disease alone. These findings support the hypothesis that the guideline-recommended surveillance interval of 6–12 months may represent a clinically meaningful period during which frailty and non-cardiac risks may become increasingly relevant.

Frailty is a dynamic condition, with transitions from non-frail to pre-frail or frail states occurring over relatively short periods in community-dwelling older adults.35 36 In patients with severe AS, chronic pressure overload, reduced physical activity and systemic inflammation may accelerate the decline in physiological reserve. In our cohort, although the distribution of CFS categories was similar between the groups, the delayed-intervention group exhibited poorer objective performance measures, including slower gait speed and reduced lower-limb muscle strength. This discrepancy suggests that subtle declines in physical performance may precede overt changes in global frailty scores, reflecting an early phase of physiological vulnerability. Thus, a period of watchful waiting, although clinically reasonable in asymptomatic patients, may coincide with a vulnerable phase during which functional deterioration occurs. Consequently, prolonged observation after the diagnosis of severe AS may disproportionately affect patients with high baseline vulnerability, increasing their susceptibility to subsequent non-cardiac complications. Furthermore, haemodynamic severity may also modulate this risk. Exploratory subgroup analyses stratified by aortic valve peak velocity (<4.5 m/s vs ≥4.5 m/s) showed a trend toward worse outcomes with delayed intervention in both strata. In the ≥4.5 m/s subgroup, this difference reached statistical significance (log-rank p=0.0460), whereas the <4.5 m/s subgroup did not show a significant difference. These exploratory findings suggest that the adverse impact of delayed intervention may be more pronounced in patients with higher echocardiographic severity, although the limited sample size warrants cautious interpretation. Taken together, these findings suggest that the association between delayed intervention and adverse outcomes likely reflects a combination of haemodynamic severity and underlying systemic vulnerability, including frailty and non-cardiac comorbidities. Accordingly, these results should be interpreted with caution and not as definitive evidence that earlier TAVR would necessarily have prevented the excess non-cardiac mortality observed in the delayed group. In a sensitivity analysis treating waiting time as a continuous variable, a longer waiting period was consistently associated with a higher risk of the primary endpoint (HR 1.03 per additional month, p=0.0175; online supplemental table S4). Furthermore, exploratory correlation analyses showed only weak and non-significant associations between waiting time and baseline parameters, including B-type natriuretic peptide, cardiac damage stage, LVEF and frailty indices, suggesting that baseline biological vulnerability alone was unlikely to fully account for the observed differences in outcomes.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-centre, retrospective observational study, which may limit its generalisability and introduce potential selection bias. In addition, this study included only patients who ultimately underwent TAVR. Individuals who died, deteriorated or became ineligible during the waiting period were not captured in our institutional registry. Recent reports have shown that waiting-list mortality in severe AS is not negligible, with most deaths occurring within the first 3 months. Therefore, our findings reflect post-TAVR outcomes among patients who survived to intervention rather than the full clinical consequences of prolonged waiting.

Second, the timing of TAVR was not randomised but was influenced by real-world clinical factors. Although baseline demographics, comorbidities and echocardiographic parameters were generally well balanced between the groups, this inherent selection process introduced the possibility of unmeasured confounding. Notably, the delayed-intervention group exhibited a trend towards greater frailty at the time of TAVR, as indicated by slower gait speed and lower-limb muscle strength. However, our cross-sectional assessment at baseline cannot distinguish whether these frailty differences reflect pre-existing vulnerability that may have contributed to delayed decision-making, or progressive functional decline during the waiting period itself. This temporal ambiguity, namely whether frailty is a cause or consequence of delayed intervention, represents an inherent limitation of observational studies. Additionally, symptom status was assessed retrospectively from chart documentation, and standardised exercise stress testing was not uniformly performed. Consequently, patients classified as asymptomatic or NYHA I in our cohort may not correspond to truly asymptomatic individuals.37 Therefore, the applicability of our findings to strictly asymptomatic severe AS is limited. In addition, because the decision to defer intervention was influenced by patient preference, perceived procedural risk and physician judgement, residual confounding related to these clinical factors cannot be fully excluded. Such factors may have contributed both to the likelihood of prolonged observation and to subsequent non-cardiac outcomes, representing a potential source of bias inherent to real-world observational studies. Third, the number of clinical events was relatively small, limiting the statistical power and the number of variables that could be included in the multivariable analyses. Fourth, the observation period varied widely, especially in the delayed group, making it difficult to uniformly assess the impact of the waiting time on outcomes. Future multicentre prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and better define the optimal timing of intervention after the diagnosis of severe AS. Finally, the impact of the timing of initial AS diagnosis, such as diagnosis at the moderate stage, was not evaluated in detail in this study because this represents a distinct clinical question and is being investigated in a separate analysis. Therefore, the present study specifically focused on the waiting period after the diagnosis of severe AS.

Conclusions

In patients with severe AS, TAVR performed more than 6 months after diagnosis was independently associated with a higher risk of HF hospitalisation or all-cause mortality, with non-cardiac mortality contributing substantially to this excess risk. This association may reflect the underlying systemic vulnerability of patients who experience prolonged delays before intervention. These findings suggest that the timing of intervention after the diagnosis of severe AS warrants careful clinical consideration, even in minimally symptomatic patients.
