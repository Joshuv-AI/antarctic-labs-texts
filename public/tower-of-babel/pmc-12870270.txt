Seroprevalence and associated factors of Helicobacter pylori infection among young adults in the West Bank, Palestine
Authors: Nour Aljouda, Aya Amer, Ameera Dawoud, Farah Lahlabat, Zahra Qadah, Tala Amleh, Zaher Nazzal, Mohammad Qadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAffecting an estimated 4.4 billion people globally, Helicobacter pylori is a major human pathogen and the leading cause of chronic gastritis and peptic ulcer disease. This study aimed to determine the seroprevalence of H. pylori and its associated factors among young adults in Palestine. A cross-sectional study was conducted involving a total of 415 university students. Data was collected from the recruited participants using a validated self-administered questionnaire and through serological testing for anti-H. pylori IgG antibodies via an Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay. The overall seroprevalence of H. pylori was 51.1% (95%CI: 46.2–56.0%). Although univariate analysis revealed significant associations between seropositivity and self-reported stomachache (P = 0.006) and weight loss (P = 0.030), a subsequent multivariate logistic regression model identified two independent associated factors. The analysis showed that infrequent handwashing before meals was significantly associated with a higher risk of infection (Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR): 1.9, 95% CI: 1.1–3.7; P = 0.047), as was the habit of eating unpeeled fruits (AOR: 1.9, 95% CI: 1.1–3.4; P = 0.031). These findings indicate a high seroprevalence of H. pylori among young adult in Palestine and underscore the critical role of personal hygiene and food safety in its transmission. Introduction

Helicobacter pylori is a Gram-negative bacterium that infects the gastric mucosa and represents one of the most widespread bacterial infections globally, affecting an estimated 4.4 billion people [1, 2]. Chronic H. pylori infection is a major cause of several serious gastrointestinal conditions, including chronic gastritis, peptic ulcer disease and is a significant risk factor for the development of gastric cancer [3, 4]. H. pylori infection can disrupt normal gastric physiology, causing symptoms such as, epigastric pain, bloating, and dyspepsia, which often overlap with functional gastrointestinal disorders [5]. Another study also reported that H. pylori-induced chronic inflammation and metabolic alterations may have systemic effects, influencing cardiovascular risk factors such as blood pressure and lipid profiles [6]. In today’s post-pandemic environment, the interaction between chronic infections such as H. pylori with emerging health challenges, including COVID-19 requires consideration. Some studies have suggested that the pro-inflammatory state triggered by H. pylori could influence the severity of COVID-19 gastrointestinal symptoms [7, 8]. Furthermore, recent studies are exploring if H. pylori infection, with its gastric inflammation and microbiome changes, could impact people’s response to COVID-19 vaccines [9, 10]. Therefore, current seroprevalence of H. pylori is an essential initial step in establishing a population baseline for future studies on the complex interaction between this highly prevalent chronic pathogen and global vaccination efforts.

Beyond its gastrointestinal manifestations, H. pylori infection imposes a significant public health burden, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Its association with gastric cancer and peptic ulcer disease contributes substantially to global morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs worldwide. Moreover, socioeconomic inequalities, including limited healthcare access, poor sanitation, and household crowding, continue to perpetuate transmission cycles in endemic regions [11–14]. These issues are particularly acute in the West Bank, Palestine, where specific constraints related to population density and socioeconomic variability contribute to maintaining high-transmission environments.

The primary modes of H. pylori transmission are believed to be oral-oral and fecal-oral, with a strong emphasis on environmental factors and hygiene. Studies have shown that inadequate sanitation, living conditions, and family size can heavily influence the degree of contamination and transmission risk [15, 16].

Recent global studies have highlighted that the prevalence of H. pylori is declining in many developed nations, likely due to improvements in hygiene and socioeconomic status; conversely, it remains high in developing countries, reflecting disparities in access to healthcare and sanitation [1, 17].

Furthermore, young adults, a group typically aged between 18 and 25 years, represent a unique population in transition between adolescence and full adulthood, often characterized by lifestyle and dietary changes that may influence their risk of infection [13, 18].Additionally, behavioral factors such as smoking, coffee consumption, and frequent dining outside the home have been suggested as potential contributors to transmission in this age group [19, 20]. This age group is highly relevant for epidemiological study, as university students represent a mobile segment of the population that acts as a key link between households and community settings, potentially serving as silent reservoirs for intrafamilial and community-based transmission.

Despite some regional studies, there remains a need for updated data on the seroprevalence of H. pylori and its associated factors within specific populations in Palestine. Previous studies in the region have generally focused on adult populations or symptomatic patients presenting with gastrointestinal symptoms; consequently, data on the prevalence of H. pylori among younger, apparently healthy adults are lacking. This highlights a significant gap and underscores the need for targeted research among university‑age cohorts who may act as silent reservoirs for transmission within households and communities.

Understanding the epidemiological pattern of H. pylori among young adults in Palestine will help in guiding national prevention programs, particularly school- and university-based screening or awareness initiatives. Such data are essential for shaping early intervention strategies before chronic infection becomes clinically apparent later in life [21].

Therefore, the aim of this study was to determine the seroprevalence of H. pylori among university students, and to describe the association between various potential factors and infection status. The findings from this research will provide a clearer picture of how widespread this bacterium is within this specific community, which is crucial for informing future prevention strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted at An-Najah National University (ANNU) in Nablus, Palestine, between October 3 and December 12, 2022. The study aimed to estimate the seroprevalence of Helicobacter pylori infection and identify potential associated factors among young adults in a university setting.

Study population and sampling

Undergraduate students from all nine faculties located on the main campuses were invited to participate, including Medicine and Health Sciences, Fine Arts, Law, Science, Engineering and Information Technology, Islamic Law, Economics and Social Sciences, Educational Sciences, and Humanities. From each faculty, we randomly selected a proportional number of students, ensuring that the final sample reflected the actual distribution of students across the campuses. From a total student population of approximately 24,000, the minimum required sample size was calculated using Epi Info software, assuming a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and an expected prevalence of 50%. After applying the finite population correction and adjusting for an anticipated 8% non-response rate, the target sample size was set at 415. To achieve this target while maintaining proportional representation across faculties, a total of 453 students were approached.

All currently enrolled undergraduate students at ANNU were eligible to participate in the study. Inclusion criteria were limited to being an active, currently enrolled undergraduate student and providing written informed consent. Students were excluded if they had a known diagnosis of gastrointestinal malignancy or if they refused to provide a blood sample. The exclusion of individuals with known gastrointestinal malignancy was predetermined to ensure that the seroprevalence and associated factors reflect the typical pattern of infection among apparently healthy young adults and to prevent potential confounding effects on the serological profile being measured. (It is important to note that no participants were excluded from the study based on a known diagnosis of gastrointestinal malignancy.) Data collection and blood sampling were performed by trained students and licensed laboratory technician; respectively, from the Department of Medical Laboratory Sciences under direct faculty supervision. Participation was voluntary, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. Anonymity and confidentiality were strictly maintained throughout the study.

Data collection

A validated, Arabic-language questionnaire developed in this study, adapted from previously published studies [22–24] was used to collect data on potential factors associated with H. pylori infection. Prior to full distribution, the questionnaire was reviewed by faculty experts at ANNU specializing in community medicine and microbiology. They provided feedback and assessment on the instrument’s content validity, clarity of wording, relevance to the study aims, cultural appropriateness, and overall structure. Following experts review, the questionnaire was pilot-tested among 15 students to ensure clarity, comprehensibility, and ease of use. Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. The finalized questionnaire captured information across four key domains: (A) demographic and socioeconomic characteristics (including age, gender, academic faculty, blood group, marital status, type of residency [camp, village, or city], household size, toilet-sharing practices, number of water closets at home, and monthly family income); (B) personal hygiene and lifestyle habits (such as smoking status, categorized as active, passive, or non-smoker, and hand-washing frequency and duration before meals); (C) dietary behaviors (including consumption of red meat, spicy foods, fruits, peeled fruits, garlic, and types of drinking water [tap, filtered tap, or mineral]); and (D) medical history (encompassing self-reported gastrointestinal disorders, specific symptoms, such as bloating, diarrhea, constipation, indigestion, stomach ulcers, abdominal pain, heartburn, nausea, vomiting, appetite or weight loss, and bloody stools, history of H. pylori infection, vitamin B12 testing and results, anemia, chronic diseases including diabetes, current medication use, and family history of cancer, further classified as gastrointestinal or non-gastrointestinal), See supplementary data: an English-translated copy of the questionnaire.

Blood sample collection

Following the completion of the questionnaire, a 4–5 mL venous blood sample was collected from each consenting participant, blood sampling was performed by licensed laboratory technician from the Department of Medical Laboratory Sciences under direct faculty supervision. Blood samples were placed in plain tubes, allowed to clot at room temperature, and then centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 min. The resulting serum was transferred to an Eppendorf tube and stored at -20 °C until laboratory analysis.

Laboratory analysis

Anti-H. pylori (IgG) antibodies were detected in the participant serum using an Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) kit (NovaTec Immundiagnostica GmbH, Dietzenbach, Germany; Product Number: HELG0220). The kit utilizes a solid phase coated with specific H. pylori antigens, source of the antigens: H. pylori isolate of ATCC strains 43,504 and 43,629 that contain proteins associated with CagA genes and VacA genes, as well as urease and other H. pylori antigens. According to the manufacturer’s validation data, the assay has a reported Diagnostic Sensitivity of 98.08% (95% confidence interval: 93.23% − 99.77%) and a Diagnostic Specificity of 93.0% (95% confidence interval: 86.11% − 97.14%). A positive result for H. pylori infection was defined by an antibody concentration of > 20 NTU/mL, and concentrations < 15 NTU/mL were classified as negative. Samples yielding equivocal results (15–20 NTU/mL) were repeated immediately in duplicate to rule out technical error; those that remained equivocal upon re-testing were ultimately classified as negative for the purpose of defining seroprevalence, as follow-up testing per the manufacturer’s recommendation was outside the scope of this single-time-point study. The analysis was performed strictly according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Statistical analysis

All data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using SPSS software (v.22.0). Descriptive statistics, including numbers and percentages, were used to summarize the data. The chi-square test was employed to determine the strength of the association between H. pylori seroprevalence and the potential associated factors in univariate analysis. A binary logistic regression model was then utilized to determine the independent factors associated with H. pylori infection, controlling for confounders. Variables with a p-value < 0.10 in the univariate analysis and those deemed clinically relevant based on existing literature were included in the model. Multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF); all values were within the acceptable range (VIF < 2), indicating no substantial effect on the estimates. A p-value of less than 0.05 (p < 0.05) was considered statistically significant.

Ethical consideration and consent to participate

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at An-Najah National University (Ref: Med. September 2022/1) before the research began. The privacy and confidentiality of all participants were strictly maintained. All students were provided with a research overview and gave their informed consent to participate, understanding that their involvement was voluntary and they could withdraw at any time. The research followed all relevant ethical guidelines and regulations and comes in compliance with Helsinki declaration.

Discussion

This cross-sectional investigation aims to determine the seroprevalence of H. pylori among young adults at An-Najah National University and to identify associated factors.

The overall seroprevalence was 51.1%, similar to high rates seen in nearby countries, such as Lebanon (52%), Egypt (40.9%), and Saudi Arabia (65.9%) [1]. This rate is substantially higher than the global seroprevalence (44.3%), especially when compared to the lower prevalence of 34.7% reported in developed countries [2]. Our observed H. pylori seroprevalence of 51.1% is highly consistent with rates reported in similar regional populations, such as, 55.8% reported among medical students in Iraq [25], and 55.0% observed in a study of male students in Saudi Arabia [26]. The consistency among young adults suggests that environmental and socio-economic factors in developing countries drive H. pylori infection more than local differences. Unlike students from developed countries, who have much lower H. pylori infection rate, often below 20% [1, 27]. Globally, low socioeconomic status, poorer sanitation, and older age are linked to a higher infection rate [1, 2], so the prevalence among the general Palestinian population is probably higher, but remains to be verified.

Univariate analysis identified a significant association between H. pylori seropositivity and self-reported stomachache and weight loss. Although a definitive causal link cannot be established due to the cross-sectional design, this finding suggests that, H. pylori colonization is frequently accompanied by notable gastrointestinal symptoms and aligns with broad regional and international literature [28–30], though this association in a university cohort needs further investigation. This result aligns with a recent local study that showed that not having GI symptoms was linked to lower H. pylori infection rate in a smaller population using a different methodology [21]. Other self-reported medical conditions, such as family history of cancer and chronic diseases like hypertension and diabetes, were not significantly associated with infection, likely reflecting the low prevalence in our young population.

Many demographic and socioeconomic factors failed to show a significant association with H. pylori status in the univariate analysis. Specifically, age, gender, faculty of study, marital status, and basic hygiene proxies like water closet sharing and family income showed no statistical correlation with seroprevalence. This contrast with international findings that typically show prevalence increases with age [1, 31] or greater prevalence in males or married individuals [32, 33]. However, the results of no gender association is consistent with another regional study [34], suggesting that exposure risk is similar for both genders. Furthermore, our data found no significant relationship between blood group and seropositivity, which is supported by some national and international studies [21, 35], but differs from others that associate blood type O to increased sensitivity [36]. The lack of significant demographic association suggests that risk in this cohort is driven more by shared environmental or behavioral factors than by individual traits.

The multivariate analysis provided a more focused and powerful insight by identifying the independent behavioral predictors of H. pylori seropositivity, strongly supporting environmental and hygiene-related transmission routes. The most critical finding related to hygiene was that students who reported washing their hands “often” rather than “always” before meals had a significantly higher risk of seropositivity. After controlling for confounding variables, this result supports fecal-oral route of transmission. This is consistent with a regional study in Jordan, and global literature emphasizing the role of personal hygiene in preventing H. pylori acquisition, particularly in developing countries [30, 37]. This finding highlights the need for targeted public health campaigns focused on improving hand-washing compliance in Palestine.

Similarly, the multivariate analysis identified consumption of unpeeled fruits as an independent risk factor for infection. This suggests that contaminated produce serves as a significant vehicle for transmission. This result is consistent with a study from different regions [38–40] that also found an association between fruits and vegetables peels and elevated infection risk. Given that our univariate analysis of water sources and socioeconomic factors was not significant, the contamination pathway appears to be less about family-level sanitation (e.g., shared water closets) and more about the individual behavior of consuming improperly cleaned raw foods.

The primary strength of this study lies in its contribution to understanding H. pylori seroprevalence and associated factors within a specific, young adults in Palestine.

Acknowledging its limits, the cross-sectional design prevents us from establishing direct causality, particularly for the observed associations with symptoms like stomachache and weight loss. There is also potential for residual confounding, as unmeasured factors—such as additional dietary or hygiene behavior that may have influenced the observed associations. Moreover, while it is less invasive and easy method, the IgG serology test cannot distinguish between current and past H. pylori infection. Finally, potential for false positives due to cross-reactivity with other bacterial antigens. This limits how accurately the prevalence in our study reflects current infection rates. Furthermore, the reliance on self-reported data introduces the potential for recall bias regarding hygiene and dietary habits. Although we used a stratified random sampling approach, the potential for selection bias remains a limitation. Some of the students declined to participate, and participants may differ from non-participants in their motivations, workload, or interest in the topic. These differences may affect the results of the findings. Despite these limitations, the results of this study offer valuable, localized insights into the dominant transmission pathways, notably poor hygiene and unpeeled fruit consumption—critical for informing future public health interventions in the region.

Future research should build upon these findings by employing longitudinal study designs to assess the incidence of new H. pylori infections in this cohort and to track seroconversion. This approach is essential for establishing genuine causality and fully understanding the dynamics of transmission in young adults. Furthermore, it is recommended that subsequent follow-up studies utilize more sensitive and specific diagnostic methods, such as the Urea Breath Test (UBT) or stool antigen tests, to definitively distinguish between active and past infection.

In conclusion, the data presented here confirm a significant seroprevalence of H. pylori in the young adult’s population of Palestine. The multivariate analysis was conclusive: poor hand hygiene and the intake of unpeeled fruits are independent and have significant associations with infection. This evidence points directly to the critical importance of the fecal-oral and foodborne transmission pathways in this group and highlights the urgent need to develop targeted public health educational programs focused on improving hygiene and food safety practices among the young adults population and the broader community.
