Yang Li, Anna Maria Feit

Abstract
. Intelligent text entry (ITE) methods, such as word suggestions, are widely used in mobile typing, yet improving ITE systems is challenging because the cognitive mechanisms behind suggestion use remain poorly understood, and evaluating new systems often requires long-term user studies to account for behavioral adaptation. We present WSTypist, a reinforcement learning–based model that simulates how typists integrate word suggestions into typing. It builds on recent hierarchical control models of typing, but focuses on the cognitive mechanisms that underlie the high-level decision-making for effectively integrating word suggestions into manual typing: assessing efficiency gains, considering orthographic uncertainties, and including personal reliance on AI support. Our evaluations show that WSTypist simulates diverse human-like suggestion-use strategies, reproduces individual differences, and generalizes across different systems. Importantly, we demonstrate on four design cases how computational rationality models can be used to inform what-if analyses during the design process, by simulating how users might adapt to changes in the UI or in the algorithmic support, reducing the need for long-term user studies.



1.
Introduction

Modern touchscreen devices typically incorporate intelligent text entry systems (ITEs), such as word suggestions, to reduce typing effort and increase efficiency
(
59
)
. Word suggestions rely on language models to predict likely continuations or corrections of a word. While prior work
(
1
;
43
;
17
;
37
;
63
)
has shown that suggestions can reduce manual keystrokes and are often perceived as helpful, they also introduce additional visual and cognitive costs. Even without word suggestions, mobile typists must divide their visual attention between the keyboard and input field
(
28
;
29
)
to proofread typed text while guiding finger movements. The word suggestion list introduces an additional target for visual attention and information processing during typing and thus creates a tension between potential efficiency gains and interruptions caused by shifting gaze, checking suggestions, and selecting words.

Empirical research shows substantial individual differences in suggestion use. Some users leverage suggestions for completion, error correction, or anticipation of upcoming words, whereas others, particularly faster typists, tend to ignore them
(
43
;
59
)
. When using suggestions, typists check the suggestion list more frequently than they select from it
(
47
)
. This highlights that suggestion usage depends not only on system quality but also on individual behavioral policies for allocating visual and cognitive resources. Understanding how such policies emerge and how they are shaped by alternative system designs is critical for advancing intelligent text entry. However, collecting empirical data on these policies is costly, especially when including eye-tracking data
(
27
)
, and considering that users need time to effectively adapt their behavior to a new keyboard design
(
30
)
. Computational simulation models
(
54
)
can offer an effective alternative to costly user studies by providing a flexible framework for collecting (simulated) user behavior. However, prior models of mobile typing fail to handle the cognitive mechanisms that affect how users integrate word suggestion systems
(
29
;
71
)
or rely on manually specified abstractions of gaze and motor behavior rather than modeling them as part of the decision process
(
37
)
.

To address this challenge, we propose WSTypist, a simulation model that predicts how users balance the visual and cognitive demands of suggestion use with manual typing and proofreading. WSTypist builds on previous hierarchical control models of typing within the computational rationality framework
(
29
;
71
)
. However, rather than focusing on low-level vision and motor processes, our goal is to simulate high-level decisions that govern when and how typists attend to, evaluate, and act on word suggestions during regular typing.
Therefore, we implement three mechanisms that allow the agent to
learn
individual strategies for deciding when the benefits of suggestions outweigh their cognitive and motor costs: (1)
Efficiency assessment
, where to-be-typed words are evaluated against potential keystroke savings and proofreading demands while considering working memory constraints; (2)
Linguistic properties
, which determine whether suggestions help reduce orthographic uncertainty and spelling errors; and (3)
Personal reliance and preference
, which captures systematic individual differences in suggestion use, such as a tendency to offload cognitive effort, satisfaction when system behavior meets expectations, or a preference for uninterrupted manual typing. These factors enable the supervisory agent to learn when suggestions are beneficial and when manual typing is preferable, and mirror the diverse ways human typists balance typing effort, accuracy, and personal preference
(
47
;
43
)
. To train and evaluate our model, we also introduce a custom suggestion system and empirically grounded benchmarking metrics that capture not only typing speed and error rates but also checking behavior and suggestion usage patterns.
Our evaluations show that WSTypist replicates human-like typing realistically across multiple metrics (e.g., word suggestion usage, gaze allocation, typing speed) and user groups (e.g., users with very high or very low typing speeds), that it generalizes to systems without suggestions or auto-correction, and that it can reproduce individual usage strategies (completing a word, correcting a typo, capitalizing the first letter, selecting a similar word and completing it manually).

We then demonstrate, for the first time, how computational rationality models can be operationalized to support system design by simulating how users adapt their behavior under alternative design choices. Concretely, we apply WSTypist in four use cases that enable what-if analyses of alternative system designs by simulating user behavioral adaptation: (1) we explore how the accuracy of the suggestion algorithm influences typing behavior and find higher accuracy encourages more suggestion use but can also induce overreliance and higher failure rates, with accuracies in the 60–70% range offering the best trade-off; (2) we test the effectiveness of prioritizing longer words by the suggestion algorithm, which yields measurable gains in typing efficiency; (3) we examine how tailoring suggestion algorithms to personal strategies might affect suggestion usage and find that prioritizing capitalized words could benefit users who frequently type proper nouns or prefer capitalized input; and (4) we evaluate an alternative UI design that places the top suggestion directly in the input field and makes it accessible through a keyboard shortcut and find that this could improve suggestion efficiency and overall typing performance.

Together, these results demonstrate that WSTypist achieves ecological validity in reproducing human typing behavior and show how computational rationality models can directly inform the evaluation and design of intelligent text entry systems.
To summarize, this paper makes three main contributions:

•

We introduce WSTypist, a computational model that simulates how users integrate word suggestions into their typing process by learning an optimal control policy under cognitive and motor constraints. Unlike prior models that focus mainly on speed–accuracy trade-offs in unsupported typing, WSTypist focuses on the cognitive mechanisms underlying the high-level decisions for word suggestion use, one of the core behaviors in modern mobile text entry.

•

We introduce empirically grounded quantitative and qualitative benchmarks tailored to word suggestion use, providing a more comprehensive basis for assessing the realism and generalizability of typing simulations.

•

We demonstrate the practical value of computational rationality models for system design, by showing how WSTypist enables what-if analyses by simulating how users might adapt their typing behavior to changes in keyboard algorithms or user interface design. Observing such behavioral adaptations would otherwise require costly and time-consuming long-term user studies.

We open-source the model, making it readily available for the research community to use in both evaluation and system design

Related Work

In this section, we review prior work on typing behavior with word suggestions, suggestion system design, and simulation-based modeling of text entry. We examine empirical findings on how users allocate visual attention and interact with suggestion interfaces, as well as modeling approaches that aim to capture typing behavior, and identify gaps in the joint modeling of gaze, motor actions, and decision-making when using word suggestions.

2.1.
Understanding typing behavior with word suggestions and suggestion system design

To support efficient text entry on touchscreen devices, numerous Intelligent Text Entry (ITE) methods have been developed
(
38
)
. Gesture input
(
45
;
40
)
, for instance, allows users to slide their fingers across the keyboard, while auto-correction automatically fixes typos or applies adjustments such as capitalization
(
5
;
1
)
.
Another widely adopted approach is word suggestions, originally developed for augmentative and alternative communication devices
(
12
;
19
)
, which early systems showed can partially automate text input to increase communication speed and ease, particularly for people with physical disabilities. Today, word suggestions are a standard feature of mobile keyboards
(
44
;
59
)
, typically displayed above the keyboard and updated in real time based on the user’s ongoing input.

While suggestion lists can reduce keystrokes, prior studies show they may also introduce overhead
(
1
;
43
;
17
;
37
;
59
;
63
;
2
)
. This is due to additional visual attention shifts among the keyboard, input field, and suggestion list, as well as the cognitive effort required to evaluate options
(
63
;
43
;
47
;
34
)
. Despite these costs, users often perceive suggestions as easier and less effortful
(
59
;
63
)
. To better understand usage patterns, Lehmann et al.
(
43
)
identified eight distinct use cases, ranging from Completion and Correction to adjustments like Capitalization and Contraction. More recently, gaze-based analyses
(
47
)
revealed inefficiencies such as frequent failed checks, unnecessary manual typing despite available suggestions, and delays due to attentional shifts.

Beyond understanding usage patterns and modeling typing processes, another strand of research has focused on how suggestion systems themselves should be designed. Interface variations include displaying predictions inline with the input field (e.g., Gmail Smart Compose
(
11
)
) or across device types
(
66
)
. The choice of ranking strategy also matters: early systems relied on statistical models, while recent work leverages neural and federated approaches for more personalized and context-aware predictions
(
39
;
20
;
18
;
23
;
75
)
. These advances highlight key design trade-offs between efficiency, error risk, and cognitive effort, with users often preferring designs they perceive as less demanding even if objective efficiency gains are small
(
59
;
63
)
.

However, most prior evaluations focus on isolated metrics such as typing speed or prediction accuracy, overlooking the combined impact of visual attention, cognitive effort, and motor actions. By integrating gaze and finger movement modeling, our approach provides a unified way to quantify these costs and benefits, enabling a more comprehensive assessment of suggestion system designs. This perspective not only helps identify more effective interfaces but also informs design decisions that balance efficiency, usability, and user experience in real-world text entry.

2.2.
Simulation-based modeling of typing

Simulation and model-based evaluation have long contributed to HCI by providing predictive accounts of user behavior, improving performance, safety, or accessibility, and reducing reliance on costly user studies (see
(
54
)
for a recent overview). Specifically in text entry research, models have been used for decades to simulate user input, assess system performance, explore the impact of design changes under controlled conditions, or optimize system designs
(
16
;
25
;
37
;
30
;
53
)
. In the context of word suggestion usage, early works by Koester and Levine
(
36
;
35
;
33
)
modeled user interaction with word prediction systems using keystroke-level models that incorporated motor actions as well as simplified perceptual and cognitive components involved in typing and prediction selection. Their simulations demonstrated that the effectiveness of word prediction is highly sensitive to factors such as prediction accuracy, list organization, and user decision costs, challenging the assumption that prediction universally improves typing performance.
However, similar to other rule-based models, such as ACT-R
(
3
)
and EPIC
(
32
)
, which simulate eye and finger movements step by step, these approaches require manually specified heuristics for decision-making and attention allocation.
In contrast, more recent models have been developed within the computational rationality framework, which is grounded in supervisory optimal control
(
57
;
10
)
. It allows agents to learn optimal strategies for allocating attention and planning typing actions with respect to cognitive and environmental constraints. Previous models focused on predicting eye and finger movements during typing, offering insights into proofreading and error correction behavior
(
29
)
. Extensions of this work have improved generalization by incorporating pixel-level visual input, expanding benchmarking capabilities
(
71
)
, and simulating different error sources
(
70
)
. Yet, these models still do not account for intelligent text entry methods such as word suggestions, limiting their applicability to everyday mobile typing.

Building on this foundation, we introduce a simulation model that extends these computational rationality models with the cognitive mechanisms that determine how a user assesses the trade-offs involved in deciding whether to rely on word suggestions. Thus, instead of focusing on low-level motor planning or proofreading, we focus on high-level decision-making in integrating suggestions into typing.
We also demonstrate, for the first time, how computational rationality models enable efficient what-if analyses that facilitate ITE design by quantifying trade-offs between suggestion use and manual typing under different system designs, and identify new opportunities for more effective intelligent text entry systems.

3.
WSTypist: Modeling mobile typing with Word Suggestions

The goal of our work is to facilitate the design of ITE methods by developing a simulation model that allows designers to assess how different users might adapt their use of word suggestions (e.g., how often they pick suggestions, the suggestion strategies they use
(
43
)
, etc.) and their checking behavior (e.g., when they start checking the suggestion list, how many failed checks they make
(
47
)
, etc.) in response to changes in the system’s design, such as the suggestion algorithm or user interface.

To achieve this, we use a computational rationality approach
(
57
)
, in which typing is formulated as a bounded optimality problem in a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP)
(
31
)
.
Within this framework, we model typing with word suggestions as a hierarchical supervisory control problem in which a supervisory agent learns, via reinforcement learning (RL), an optimal policy for distributing visual attention across the keyboard, input field, and word suggestion list, enabling effective checking and selection of suggestions while managing the cognitive constraints of manual typing and proofreading. Specifically, we build on a previously proposed architecture
(
29
;
71
)
where at each time step, the agent gathers partial observations through eye movements, processes them via a working memory component with limited cognitive capacity and noise, and selects subsequent gaze and finger actions based on this internal state.

While prior work
(
71
)
focused on low-level motor and visual control, particularly the speed–accuracy trade-off during typing, our work targets higher-level cognitive decisions that determine whether and when typists interrupt manual typing to attend to the intelligent support provided by the interface, i.e., check the word suggestion list.
Efficient use of suggestions involves strategic choices, such as when to start checking (trading off potential keystroke savings against the probability that the target appears), how often to check, when to stop, and whether to use suggestions for completion, error correction, or spelling confirmation. These decisions vary widely across users
(
47
)
and reflect individual strategies that go beyond word completion
(
43
)
, such as strategically omitting capitalization or apostrophes and relying on suggestions to insert them. They are shaped not only by perceptual and motor constraints, but also by linguistic skills and personal preferences for relying on external support.

In the following, we first identify the mechanisms underlying these decisions of word suggestion usage from typists, and then describe how they are implemented within our hierarchical computational rationality model.

3.1.
Mechanisms underlying Suggestion Usage

The decisions involved in using suggestions require weighing the associated costs and benefits. These depend on the to-be-typed word, the performance of the suggestion algorithm and design of the typing interface, and, critically, on the cognitive limitations of the typist. For each to-be-typed word and for each keystroke, the agent must decide whether to check the suggestion list and pick a suggested word or to press the keys manually. On the one hand, picking a suggestion can be worthwhile if it meaningfully reduces effort
(
47
)
. For example, correcting an error that would otherwise require multiple backspaces or completing a word to save several keystrokes. On the other hand, checking the suggestion list demands additional visual resources, which cannot be used for proofreading or guiding finger movements, and interrupts the typing flow
(
43
)
. Picking a suggestion also requires moving the finger away from the keyboard to the suggestion list.
However, independent of any performance gains, previous studies have found that typists also use suggestions even if they do not contribute to faster typing speed
(
43
;
59
;
63
)
. This could be attributed to reduced cognitive effort related to a user’s linguistic knowledge
(
62
)
, personal preferences or habits of relying on suggestions for specific words.

To enable the supervisory agent to learn an optimal allocation of visual and finger resources during typing with word suggestions, it is thus not enough to simply expand the action space to account for gaze shifts toward the suggestion list (Vision) and picking suggested words (Finger). We have to include additional cognitive mechanisms that allow the supervisory agent to learn what affects the cost–benefit trade-off of using word suggestions, including the benefits unrelated to typing speed and accuracy.

gives an overview of the components we newly introduce in this paper. Specifically, they include:

Efficiency assessment

We assume that one goal of using suggestions is to improve typing efficiency. In the case of word suggestions, this is closely tied to keystroke savings
(
66
)
. Recent work
(
47
)
shows that selecting a suggestion can cost up to three times more than a regular keystroke. This limits efficiency gains to cases where the time required to enter the remaining keystrokes (including error corrections) exceeds the cost of checking and selecting from the suggestion list. We extend the observation space to allow the supervisory agent to decide whether to consider the suggestion list both at the word level and after each keystroke. At the word level, information about the length and frequency of the to-be-typed word influences a person’s decision to consult suggestions
(
43
;
47
)
(
W_{L}
and
W_{F}
in
Figure 1
) and can also help predict whether a word will be correctly suggested by the keyboard algorithm. Decisions at the keystroke level are based on information about the current
Completeness
and
Correctness
of the word. These are derived by the agent through proofreading but can be noisy due to working memory limitations. While Correctness has been considered in previous models
(
71
)
, we add a
Completeness
assessment to the Working Memory component (see
Figure 1
).

Linguistic properties

Linguistic properties of the target word, such as word length and frequency, influence typing processes
(
15
;
62
)
and specifically word suggestion usage
(
47
)
. In addition, the typist’s linguistic skill, particularly orthographic knowledge, influences both general typing
(
62
)
and the utility of using word suggestions. When typists are uncertain about a word’s spelling, they may derive greater benefit from attending to the suggestion list as a spelling reference
(
47
)
. We thus extend the Observation space to include the target word’s
Length
(
W_{L}
) and
Frequency
(
W_{F}
), and define a
Linguistic Knowledge Parameter
(
P_{K}
) incorporated into the error simulation to model the likelihood of producing spelling errors. Weaker linguistic knowledge leads to a higher error rate, increasing reliance on the suggestion list. In addition, when the agent gazes at the suggestion list, it not only gathers information about the provided suggestions (
Sugg
in
Figure 1
) but also gains certainty about the words’ spelling, reducing errors related to linguistic uncertainty.

Personal reliance and preference

Typists differ in their individual reliance on word suggestions
(
59
)
, independent of typing expertise. Some users rely heavily on suggestions to reduce workload, while others prefer typing manually to maintain control or avoid interruptions
(
47
)
. This variation could come from a person’s readiness to embrace new technologies
(
60
)
, prior experiences with similar systems, or an individual tendency for cognitive offloading
(
65
)
. What is more, users whose expectations of a system’s behavior are met tend to be more satisfied and continue using it
(
7
)
. In the case of word suggestions, this could mean that users decide to continue checking the suggestion list until the desired word appears to confirm that the system will deliver their expected support. We therefore assume that a reward mechanism is involved: a user might feel satisfied if the system proposes a correct suggestion, even if it requires multiple (costly) checks and the word is nearly complete. To capture this, we introduce a
Suggestion Reliance Parameter
(
P_{S}
) in the reward function, which reflects individual preferences for using the word suggestion system and captures a user’s perceived reward when selecting a suggestion that matches the target word, independent of any performance gains.

3.2.
The WSTypist Model

The implementation of these mechanisms within the hierarchical supervisory control architecture is illustrated in
Figure 1
. In the following, we describe each component of the model and its implementation in detail.

3.2.1.
Supervisor Agent

Similar to previous architecture
(
29
;
71
)
, the supervisor agent collects partial observations of the internal and external environment, which are processed by the working memory module, and makes decisions on how to allocate visual and motor resources to act on the external environment. Each action is rewarded through mechanisms in the internal and external environment. To enable the agent to learn boundedly optimal policies that are similar to empirically observed strategies and adapted to the limitations of the internal (cognitive) and external (word-related) environment, we provide the supervisory agent with the following components, allowing it to better evaluate the cost–benefit trade-offs of suggestion use.

Observation space

The agent receives a partial observation of the full environment state. We adopt the core items from previous architecture
(
71
)
and extend the space with additional suggestion-related items (highlighted in bold). This includes the
Length
and
Frequency
of the target word (
W_{L}
,
W_{F}
), the current gaze position, and whether the target word is in the suggestion list (
Sugg
), the outputs of the three working memory components (Certainty, Correctness, and
Completeness
), and the cognitive parameters (
P_{M}
for Memory ability,
P_{F}
for
Finger speed
,
P_{K}
for
linguistic Knowledge
, and
P_{S}
for
Suggestion reliance and preference
. For further details, see
subsubsection 3.2.2
).

Action space

The action space consists of two separate components: one for gaze movement and one for finger movement. Similar to the action given by supervisor agent from the previous architecture
(
29
;
71
)
, our Gaze action determines where attention is on the three discrete UI elements: the keyboard, the
suggestion list
, or the input field. The Finger action can either type the next character, press backspace,
pick a suggestion
, or perform a NoAct (no action). The NoAct option allows pauses in finger movement and enables gaze shifts between keystrokes.

Reward function

Prior work
(
29
;
71
;
70
)
has primarily focused on the balance between speed and accuracy. However, we argue that users also optimize their behavior to meet personal preferences or subjectively perceived benefits when typing with ITE support. Therefore, these factors should be incorporated into the reward function (See Section
7.1
and Appendix
B
for more discussion). We adopt the basic structure of the reward function from previous work
(
71
;
70
)
, with an additional component capturing
suggestion usage
(the third part). The full reward function is defined as follows.

(1)

R=\underbrace{(1-UER^{\beta})}_{\text{accuracy}}-\underbrace{(\gamma\times\tfrac{T}{WL})}_{\text{speed}}+\underbrace{(P_{S}\times\mathbb{I}_{\text{Pick}})}_{\text{suggestion use}}

Here,
UER
denotes the final uncorrected error rate, computed by comparing the agent’s final input with the target word. The term
\beta
is a scaling factor that adjusts the penalty applied for typing errors. The term
\gamma
is the weight to adjust the influence of typing speed.
\frac{T}{WL}
represents the normalized time cost, where
T
is the total time taken for the episode and
WL
is the length of the target word.
P_{S}
is the Suggestion reliance parameter introduced below, reflecting individual differences in reliance on suggestions.
\mathbb{I}_{\text{Pick}}
is an indicator function that equals 1 if a suggestion is picked and 0 otherwise, providing a suggestion-related bonus when applicable.
\beta
,
\gamma
and
P_{S}
are all positive values. The agent receives this reward only at the end of an episode (i.e., after completing the typing of a word). Additionally, we include intermediate rewards (e.g., penalties for meaningless backspaces) after each step to facilitate faster and more stable training. They are used only during the episode to support early-stage training. Once the agent exhibits reasonable behavior, they can be removed, and we exclude them from the final reward formulation for simplicity.

Cognitive parameters

We define three cognitive parameters to capture different cognitive abilities, enabling the simulation of individual differences. These parameters are used by other components within the internal environment and in the reward function. The Memory Parameter (
P_{M}
) is adopted from previous work
(
71
)
and described in Appendix
A
. In addition, we define:

•

Linguistic Knowledge Parameter (
P_{K}
):
This parameter quantifies the typist’s linguistic knowledge. A higher value indicates better orthographic knowledge, meaning the typist is more familiar with correct word spellings. It affects the error rate during typing: typists with lower knowledge are more likely to make spelling-related errors. However, if a word appears in the suggestion list and is gazed at, the agent obtains certainty about its spelling and avoids orthographic mistakes ((c) in
Figure 2
).

•

Suggestion Reliance Parameter (
P_{S}
):
This parameter captures the typist’s intrinsic reliance on word suggestions and affects the final reward the agent receives when picking a correct suggestion (See
Reward Function
above and also (d) in
Figure 2
). A high value indicates strong reliance, reflecting a preference for or dependence on suggestions.

We evaluated the influence of the two newly introduced parameters (
P_{K}
and
P_{S}
) on the model’s ability to produce human-like behavior for suggestion usage in Appendix
B
, showing the necessity of such mechanism components and their alignment with expected behavior.

Working memory

The Working Memory
(
4
)
component in our model captures key aspects of human cognitive limitations, which are essential for simulating realistic typing behavior. It receives input from the external environment via the Vision and Finger modules, including time cost and current typing status, and outputs the following three metrics (among other states) to the agent.
Certainty
(how confident the typist is about the currently
typed content) and
Correctness
(quantifies the accuracy of the current input)) have been proposed by previous work
(
71
)
and are described in Appendix
A
. We additionally incorporate a critical mechanism that helps the agent to assess the number of characters left to type in a word:
Completeness
. This reflects how complete the agent assumes the currently typed word is (See (a)-(c) in
Figure 2
). It is critical for determining the utility of using a suggestion. Importantly, information about completeness is subject to memory limitations. It is computed based on the current input length (
IL
) divided by the length of the target word (
WL
) and multiplied by the agent’s
Certainty
:
Completeness=\frac{IL}{WL}\times Certainty
.

Vision and Finger modules

Compared to previous models
(
71
;
70
)
, we simplify the Vision and Finger modules to operate at the level of UI elements rather than pixels to reduce model complexity and facilitate training. Since our modeling focus lies on high-level decisions related to word suggestion usage, this simplification is not expected to substantially affect our results. Moreover, by adjusting modeling parameters related to movement speed or linguistic knowledge, we can still simulate how error prevalence or typing speed influences suggestion usage (see Section
5.2
). We discuss this modeling choice further in
subsection 7.3
. Here we describe the specific implementation of each module:

The Vision module retrieves information from the external environment based on the most recent gaze action and updates Working Memory with information about gaze location and suggestion status. We assume that each action provides Vision with partial information about the specific location being gazed at. For example, when gazing at the input field, Vision can access the current input content, effectively performing proofreading, and updates the Certainty in Working Memory to 1. When gazing at the
suggestion list
, Vision can observe whether the target word is currently suggested ((a)-(c) in
Figure 2
). If the gaze shifts to other fields, the suggestion status becomes unknown. When the gaze is on the keyboard, Vision guides typing to reduce the error rate (described below). Gaze shifts between interface elements contribute to the overall time cost, sampled from a normal distribution (M = 200 ms, SD = 100 ms) to capture natural variability in visual attention shifts, similar to previous work
(
71
)
.

The Finger module executes typing-related actions in the external environment, including typing the next letter,
picking a suggestion
((d) in
Figure 2
) from the suggestion list, using backspace to correct errors, or performing no action (NoAct). We define an additional parameter,
P_{F}
, to represent finger movement speed, analogous to the inter-keystroke interval (IKI). It determines how each keystroke action contributes to the overall time in the final reward. Agents are trained on a range of movement speeds to reflect variations in human typing speeds, obtained from empirical data
(
47
)
(see Section
3.3
). For picking a suggestion, the time cost is sampled from a normal distribution (M = 760 ms, SD = 150 ms
(
47
)
) to reflect the stochasticity and timing variability of suggestion selection.

Typing errors are intentionally included to simulate real human behavior. In our simulation, error rates are derived from empirical data
(
47
)
: an average of 5% when the gaze is on the keyboard, 6% when on the suggestion list, and 10% when on the input field. Furthermore, the
Linguistic Knowledge Parameter
modulates error rates, with lower knowledge increasing the likelihood of mistakes.

3.2.3.
External environment

The external environment consists of the typing interface, which provides raw information about the current typing state, enabling the agent to perceive context and make informed decisions. Based on the agent’s Gaze and Finger actions, the environment updates the current input text, modifies available word suggestion information, and provides feedback according to the gaze location.

To train the agent efficiently in word suggestion usage, we developed a custom suggestion system capable of generating word suggestions, optionally including an auto-correction feature. Existing open-source systems are either too slow (e.g., GPT-based language models
(
64
)
) to support the high-volume data collection required during training, or require substantial setup before use (e.g., Presage). Our suggestion system first identifies all words matching the current prefix and ranks them using a weighted score combining word length and frequency, based on the first 5,000 words from Wiktionary:Frequency lists 2006
2
2

2

https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Wiktionary:Frequency_lists/PG/2006/04/1-10000
. By default, suggestions are generated using a weighted combination following a method similar to
(
63
)
. To improve realism, we adjust the weights to achieve a comparable suggestion accuracy based on observations of commercial mobile suggestion systems
(
43
;
47
)
. When a typing error is detected, the system applies fuzzy matching using Levenshtein distance
(
46
)
to include close alternatives. Since word suggestions are typically used together with auto-correction, we also incorporate auto-correction into the model and suggestion system. Auto-correction is simulated such that if the agent stops typing and the typed word is within an edit distance of less than two from the target word, the error is corrected automatically with 80% accuracy, reflecting realistic performance
(
71
)
. To validate the system, we extracted all words appearing at least three times in the WS-Gaze dataset
(
47
)
, yielding approximately 1,080 distinct English words. Our suggestion system correctly provides suggestions for 65% of these words (
Accuracy
3
3

3

Here, accuracy refers to the likelihood that a correct suggestion is provided before a word is completed. Commercial system accuracy varies widely, e.g., 45%–80%, depending on source and context.
), with suggestions appearing on average after 54% of the word length, similar to commercial systems
(
47
)
. The system is fast and highly tunable, allowing adjustments to accuracy, suggestion appearance rates, and patterns.

3.3.
Implementation and training details

The training dataset consists of 1,080 unique words from the WS-Gaze dataset
(
47
)
. In each episode, the environment randomly selects a word and a starting gaze position and initiates interaction with the agent. An episode terminates when one of the following occurs: 30 steps have been taken, a suggestion is successfully picked, or the agent types one character beyond the target word length (representing a space input). The 30-step limit prevents infinite loops and ensures efficient training
(
72
)
. At each step, two potential suggestions are presented, similar to standard systems, allowing the agent to observe whether the correct suggestion is included. Note that the leftmost suggestion in
Figure 2
always mirrors the currently typed content, similar to standard mobile typing systems.

We employ the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm
(
68
)
with an LSTM-based architecture
(
26
)
to capture temporal dependencies in sequential typing actions. Two-thumb and one-finger typing are simulated using different cognitive parameters, although the agent issues only one finger action per step. The agent is trained over 5,000 episodes to learn effective word suggestion use, with varying cognitive parameters to balance exploration and exploitation. Preliminary experiments showed most models converged to specific parameter ranges. To improve efficiency, we restricted each parameter to a narrower range while still permitting sufficient exploration
4
4

4

The parameter ranges are:
P_{M}\in[0.0,0.2]
,
P_{F}\in[0.15,0.35]
,
P_{K}\in[0.3,0.5]
, and
P_{S}\in[0.5,0.7]
. Please note that some of them may not be needed for certain evaluations or simulations.
.

We apply Bayesian Optimization
(
74
)
to fit the four cognitive parameters by minimizing the Jensen–Shannon divergence
(
48
)
, following the method used in previous work
(
71
;
70
)
, and identify the best parameter set across all four parameters (
P_{M}
,
P_{F}
,
P_{K}
and
P_{S}
) that aligns with the target metrics characterizing the corresponding human group behavior. Early in training, the agent often selected suggestions arbitrarily, picking words dissimilar to the target and earning no reward. To address this, we employed curriculum learning
(
6
)
, gradually expanding the allowable picking distance as the agent’s proficiency improves. Initially, the agent can only select suggestions exceeding a similarity threshold, which is relaxed later. This encourages incremental exploration of suggestion strategies (e.g., Select and Modify
(
43
)
) while avoiding premature convergence on suboptimal behaviors. Over training, the agent refines its strategy, picking suggestions efficiently and only when the suggested word is sufficiently similar to the target.

4.
Evaluating Word Suggestion Models

We introduce an extended benchmark for assessing word suggestion models that defines relevant modeling tasks and appropriate performance metrics. The benchmark emphasizes alignment with human performance on core metrics, coverage of diverse typing strategies, sensitivity to individual differences across user groups, and generalization across different systems. We therefore extend the benchmark proposed by
71
, with a set of new metrics that characterize typing behavior with word suggestions and propose a different set of modeling tasks. We evaluate our model using this benchmark and rely on two human datasets as reference, which contain both keystroke and gaze data. The WS-Gaze dataset
(
47
)
is the only available dataset of typing with word suggestions that includes gaze data; the How-we-type-mobile dataset
(
28
)
captures standard typing without suggestions and is used to evaluate the generalizability of the model to typing without ITE methods.

4.1.
Quantitative evaluation metrics

We adapt four metrics from prior work
(
71
;
15
;
47
)
to evaluate basic typing performance, independent of word suggestion behavior:

•

Words per minute (WPM):
Primary measure of typing speed, calculated as the number of characters in the final input, divided by the time spent (in minutes), assuming an average word length of five characters
(
71
;
15
)
.

•

Gaze ratio on keyboard (Gaze Kbd):
Proportion of time spent looking at the keyboard, calculated by dividing total fixation duration on the keyboard by overall fixation duration
(
71
;
47
)
.

•

Backspaces per character (BPC):
A measure of editing behavior, calculated by dividing the total number of backspace presses by the number of characters in the final input
(
47
)
. Note that this varies from the previous benchmark
(
71
)
, which used the number of backspaces per sentence, which is sensitive to sentence length.

•

Uncorrected error rate (UER):
Main indicator of typing accuracy. Computed as the Levenshtein distance between final input and ground truth, divided by the length of the longer string
(
71
;
47
)
.

In addition, we introduce five new metrics that specifically capture word-suggestion–related performance:

•

Picked suggestions (Picked):
Percentage of typed words for which the suggestion list was fixated and for which the user picked a suggestion
(
47
)
.

•

Failed suggestions (Failed):
Percentage of typed words during which the suggestion list was fixated, but no suggestion was picked
(
47
)
.

•

Start checking character (Start):
Percentage of typed characters within a word before the user fixates the suggestion list for the first time. Computed for each word individually, for which the suggestion list is fixated at least once
(
47
)
.

•

Gaze ratio on suggestion list (Gaze Sugg):
Proportion of time spent looking at the suggestion list, calculated by dividing total fixation duration on the suggestion list by overall fixation duration across the interface
(
47
)
.

•

Keystroke savings (KS):
Key metric for assessing the benefit of suggestion usage, calculated as 1 minus the ratio of total keystrokes to the number of characters in the final input
(
47
;
43
)
.

We regard these metrics as essential when evaluating typing models that simulate suggestion usage. However, if gaze behavior is not modeled explicitly,
Failed
and
Start
could be dropped. Additional metrics might be needed for models that simulate behavior with other ITE systems or input devices (e.g., typing on a tablet or laptop
(
66
)
).

4.2.
Modeling tasks

Similar to the Benchmark proposed by
71
, we consider three categories of modeling tasks. However, all except the first category in Generalizability entail new tasks relevant for evaluating simulation models of typing with word suggestions.

Accuracy in simulating human-like behaviors

We propose that the agent should exhibit human-like behavior across the key metrics above, which we identify as the most important indicators of typing performance with word suggestions. Following prior work
(
71
)
, we evaluate this against both two-thumb (
2-T
) and one-finger (
1-F
) typing, which is also represented in the WS-Gaze dataset
(
47
)
. In addition, we evaluate against the average (
Avg
) behavior of all users across all conditions in the WS-Gaze dataset
(
47
)
.

Simulating differences in suggestion usage due to typing speed

This task evaluates whether the model captures how typing speed influences suggestion usage. Empirical studies showed that fast typists use fewer word suggestions compared to slow typists
(
59
;
47
)
. Therefore, we aggregate human data from two user groups of the WS-Gaze dataset
(
47
)
: the highest- and lowest-typing-speed groups (
H-WPM
and
L-WPM
), each consisting of four participants. By comparing simulations to each of these groups, we test a model’s ability to replicate speed-related behavioral differences in suggestion usage.

Generalization to different ITE methods

In contrast to the existing benchmark
(
71
)
, we focus on the generalizability of our model to simulate typing with different ITE methods (and disregard generalizability to different keyboard designs). Specifically, we evaluate the ability to simulate typing behavior without ITE support, with word suggestions only, and with auto-correction and word suggestions. In the first case, in addition to comparing to human data from
28
, we also compare simulations of our model to those from previous work
(
71
)
that generate typing behavior without ITE support.

Replication of behavioral strategies

This task evaluates whether the model reproduces not only statistical-level behavior but also cognitively plausible strategies. Previous empirical work has identified six main strategies that account for the majority of human strategies for suggestion usage: Completion, Correction, Prediction, Contraction, Capitalization, and Select and Modify
(
43
)
. The model should simulate these strategies. In addition, it should capture finer-grained behavioral adaptations, such as adjusting suggestion usage based on word length, as observed in human users
(
47
)
.

We also examine generalization to systems without suggestions or auto-correction. ) with reported values being means and standard deviations across all words and agents.

6.
Applications: Using simulations to inform system design

In this section, we demonstrate the applicability of our model to system design. Designing word suggestion systems or other ITE methods involves particular challenges. For example, balancing word length against frequency or prioritizing certain word types can have unintended consequences, often only revealed through lengthy user testing. Similarly, evaluating novel interface designs, such as displaying suggestions directly in the input field or enabling shortcut-based selection, typically requires costly and time-consuming studies. Typing is an activity in which most people are highly trained and have developed idiosyncratic strategies
(
15
;
43
)
. Adapting to new system designs or algorithms can take many hours, and user studies often span multiple sessions or days
(
52
;
58
;
76
;
14
)
. A/B testing new versions in the wild is risky: users dislike adapting to a new keyboard unless the benefit is clear, and they may abandon the app. In other cases, developing the new keyboard version itself is costly, and evidence of benefit is needed first. Our simulation model offers a way to explore design trade-offs and test prototypes efficiently, generating realistic simulation data to guide development. Importantly, it models how users
adapt
to a new system, i.e., what happens when an update is released. This is particularly relevant for typing applications, which people may use for several hours daily
(
59
)
, and where interaction strategies are often highly specialized.

We illustrate this in four design cases. In each, we assume the role of a developer working on a smartphone keyboard app tasked with improving the suggestion system. From data logs or prior research, we derive
observations
about user behavior and preferences, from which we formulate
design hypotheses
. For each hypothesis, we mock up a prototype and use the WSTypist model to
simulate
typing behavior after adaptation. By observing simulated users, we then make a
recommendation
on whether and how to proceed with development.

To simulate behavior with each new system, we took four previously trained agents that were fitted to the average behavior of participants in the WS-Gaze Dataset (i.e.,
Avg
in
Table 1
). We then restarted training in each new environment (see cases below), fine-tuning the agent’s behavior with a tenfold smaller learning rate to enable gradual adaptation to the new system, and applied early stopping once the typing behavior (i.e., reward) stabilized. This simulates the behavior of users who have extensive experience with one system and then switch to another one (e.g., a keyboard with a new suggestion algorithm), to which they adapt their interaction strategy over time.

This procedure demonstrates how we envision simulation models to integrate into practitioners’ workflows in the future. Specifically, we apply this in four examples: to explore how suggestion algorithm accuracy affects user behavior, whether prioritizing longer or capitalized words increases suggestion use, and how the placement of suggestions and shortcut-based selection influence typing performance. These cases highlight our model’s flexibility in adapting to diverse input environments and its potential to inform the broader design space for future suggestion systems.

Accuracy-based system

Observation

Empirical data
(
47

show that users experience varying success rates with word suggestions, with some checking the suggestion list without choosing more often than others. It is unclear whether this comes from difficulty predicting when a word appears in the list or from personal strategies (e.g., using the list as a spell-check reference). A key design question is whether higher algorithm accuracy reduces failed checks and whether investing in more accurate algorithms is worthwhile.

We hypothesize that suggestion accuracy strongly influences usage and that higher accuracy increases typing speed

6.3.
Capitalization-priority system

Observation

Capitalization strategies reveal noteworthy behaviors. Some users intentionally skip capital letters, relying on the suggestion list to correct them, while others type capitals manually or omit them entirely, accepting more errors in exchange for faster input.

Hypothesis

We hypothesize that increasing the accuracy of capitalized suggestions may encourage users to rely on the system for correcting capitalization, while decreasing accuracy may discourage the use of this strategy, requiring them to type capital letters manually.

Simulation

In contrast to all other cases, where we could use previously trained agents, in this case, we had to first modify our model. In the agent’s observation space, we added a binary indicator to signal whether the next character should be capitalized. The "Type" action inputs the capital letter if the indicator is 1 (doubling the normal typing time) and behaves normally otherwise. The "NoAct" action allows the agent to skip the capital letter (typing a lowercase letter) or skip a regular character with no time cost. We then manipulated how capitalized words appeared in the suggestion system:

•

High Priority:
The capitalized form was presented as soon as it appeared within the top five suggestions.

•

Baseline:
The standard suggestion system used in previous experiments.

•

Low Priority:
60% of capitalized words that could have been suggested correctly were instead shown in lowercase.

These configurations resulted in accuracies of 94% (High), 58% (Baseline), and 38% (Low) for capitalized words. To emphasize this usage strategy, we trained agents on a dataset containing 216 normal words and 187 capitalized words, raising the proportion of capitalized words to nearly half. Four agents were first trained from scratch on the Baseline system (with auto-correction disabled and stricter error tolerance) to establish a stable baseline. For the High and Low systems, we then fine-tuned these agents with a tenfold smaller learning rate, applying early stopping once performance stabilized.

6.4.
Adaptation to new interfaces

Observation

Users typically shift their gaze across three areas while typing: the keyboard, the suggestion list, and the input field. Each suggestion selection can require up to three times the effort of a normal keystroke, making the process costly. This suggests that interface design, specifically the placement of suggested words, plays a key role in shaping suggestion efficiency.

Hypothesis

We hypothesize that placing the word suggestion directly in the input field and introducing a shortcut for selecting it from the keyboard will increase suggestion usage and improve typing efficiency.

Simulation

We simulated agents’ behavior using two modified systems to test this hypothesis. Gazing at the input field provides information about the suggestion status (i.e., whether the target word is suggested), while looking elsewhere leaves this information unknown. The action and state spaces were kept unchanged. In one system, we reduced the picking time to match a regular keystroke, simulating a keyboard shortcut for selecting the suggestion displayed in the input field.

7.
Discussion

In this paper, we presented and evaluated a new computational model of typing behavior that simulates how humans type with word suggestions. This model serves as a tool for testing potential typing systems and for gaining insights into human cognitive behavior through the analysis of agent behaviors. We discuss the design implications and limitations of our approach in this section.

7.1.
Understanding diversity in human behavior

Empirical research has shown that human typing behavior with word suggestions is far from uniform. Our simulations likewise reveal substantial individual variations in strategy, efficiency, and reliance on assistance features. For instance, high-WPM users tend to minimize interaction with the suggestion list, relying primarily on their own typing fluency, while low-WPM users exhibit more frequent gaze shifts toward suggestions. However, typing speed is only one axis along which behaviors diverge. Cognitive factors such as linguistic knowledge, working memory, and willingness to explore suggestions also play important roles.

By explicitly parameterizing these cognitive traits and personal preferences, our model provides a systematic way to capture and reproduce these behavioral differences. This parameter space allows us to generate a wide spectrum of user profiles, from “lazy” typists who frequently check and select suggestions to expert typists who rarely divert attention away from the keyboard. Importantly, the model does not enforce a single
optimal
interaction pattern but instead supports a range of strategies that emerge naturally from the underlying cognitive and motor constraints.
Although the precise preferences that drive suggestion use, and the internal reward structure governing action decisions, are not yet fully understood, our ablation studies (Appendix
B
) indicate that incorporating
P_{S}
into the reward function aligns with observed empirical behavior. Future work should thus further explore which cognitive or psychological aspects contribute to users’ typing behavior beyond aiming for speed and accuracy.

Recognizing the diversity in behaviors has two implications. First, evaluation of typing systems should not rely solely on aggregated averages, as these may mask important subgroup differences. Second, system designs that aim for broad adoption should account for multiple usage styles, explicitly accommodating them through personalization that suits varying levels of skill and reliance on assistance.

7.2.
Using simulations to inform system design

We consider the personalized typing systems that allow users to tailor their experience to their own needs. While personalized dictionaries are common in commercial keyboards, the hyperparameters of suggestion algorithms or UI designs are rarely personalized. For instance, some users may rely on suggestions for longer words or favor capitalized suggestions. Such customization could enhance efficiency by aligning system behavior with individual goals and habits, although it remains unclear how aware users are of their own preferences regarding suggestion use. Beyond manual customization, a more advanced approach involves systems that learn from users’ behavior over time. This shifts the interaction paradigm: rather than users adapting to a fixed system, the system adapts to users, fostering greater control and satisfaction. Future systems could be optimized for different user profiles. For example, by de-prioritizing Failed suggestions (using eye gaze data), supporting users with limited linguistic knowledge, or introducing gaze-based adaptive features, such as highlighting suggestions when fixated or offering personalized gaze-based shortcuts for selection. By continuously monitoring and analyzing typing patterns, the system could learn preferred words, common phrases, or stylistic tendencies and dynamically update suggestions.

Simulation models can play a key role in guiding such designs. With our model, agents can be parameterized with specific cognitive profiles while system parameters are varied, allowing us to identify which designs best support different behavioral patterns. For instance, our investigation of capitalization strategies was motivated by the idea that users who frequently type capitalized words might benefit from tailored support, whereas those who rarely use capitalization might not value such prioritization, or could even be hindered by it. More broadly, users with different cognitive profiles may require different forms of support. For example, individuals with strong linguistic knowledge might gain more from prioritizing longer words, as this maximizes keystroke savings, whereas those with weaker linguistic knowledge could benefit more from higher-frequency words that support spelling accuracy. Exploring such personalized adaptations offers a promising direction for future research. These patterns can be interpreted as representing groups of real users.

7.3.
Limitations and future work

By focusing on modeling higher-level typing behavior and decision making in relation to suggestion usage, we simplified low-level aspects such as finger movements or pixel-level vision
(
71
)
. This decision follows the modeling principle of parsimony, i.e., choosing the simplest model that describes the data well
(
55
)
. Rule-based simulations, such as the EMMA model
(
67
)
for visual fixation time or the Weighted Homographic model
(
22
)
for typing errors based on distance and speed, can provide more detail for specific use cases. However, these approaches depend on fixed screen setups, are driven by cognitive parameters, and are tuned to best-case scenarios. As a result, they do not necessarily improve realism with respect to word suggestion usage behavior; rather, they increase detail in areas outside our main focus. Therefore, we simplified these modules and incorporated parameters derived from real-world data. As demonstrated in the results, our model still produces realistic simulations, making it both practical and valuable for simulation-based UI testing. Nevertheless, our hierarchical supervisory control architecture allows for future integration of more generalizable Vision and Finger modules. These can be useful for detailed pixel-level behavior analysis. For example, to simulate typing on different screen sizes not covered by current datasets, or to simulate interaction with fundamentally different UI layouts.

Currently, we model reading behavior by assuming that once the agent gazes at the suggestion list, it can immediately recognize whether the target word appears. Future work could thus extend the vision module to explicitly simulate reading behavior, for example, by training a low-level gaze-scanning model. This would allow us to better simulate behavior adaptations due to phrase suggestions or longer suggestion lists with multiple items displayed simultaneously
(
9
)
. We also plan to incorporate noise in the recognition of suggestions displayed on the interface by linking gaze duration to certainty about the displayed suggestions. For simulating typing errors, we plan to incorporate multiple causes, including slips, lapses, and mistakes, following the approach of newly released models
(
70
)
. More realistic error simulations might affect strategies for word suggestion usage to correct typing errors. This might also allow comparisons of error correction strategies across backspaces, word suggestions, and auto-corrections.

Previous work
(
47
)
showed that the high failure rate, that is, words where users look at the suggestion list but do not pick a suggestion, was attributable not only to algorithm performance but also to users’ behavior, their limited ability to anticipate the algorithm’s actions, but also their conscious choice of typing a word manually even if they fixated it in the suggestion list. By tuning the model to be as close as possible to specific human behavior, we can better identify whether such unexpected and at first sight suboptimal behavior is related to poor algorithm performance, inefficient user strategies, or users’ individual preferences.

A large part of our evaluations in Section
4
and simulations in Section
6
are based on the WS-Gaze dataset
(
47
)
, which is the only available dataset of typing with word suggestions that includes gaze data. The data was collected on participants’ personal phones with a range of display sizes and keyboards. This diversity allows us to simulate different user groups, illustrating the range of behaviors it can exhibit and its potential to adapt to future datasets. By adjusting the cognitive parameters and hyperparameters (e.g., picking speed, gaze shift speed,
P_{S}
,
P_{K}
, etc.) to the specific data, we can produce behavior that is similar to specific users on a specific device or to averages over the diversity of devices used in the study. Nevertheless, the dataset is limited in its size, covering the behavior of only 27 users who are mostly young adults, and more data will be needed to simulate realistic user behavior of specific user groups (e.g., elderly people or people with tremor) or interaction on different devices (e.g., suggestion usage on tablets or laptops).

Looking ahead, an important research direction is to make such simulation tools more accessible to practitioners. Our demonstrations relied on expert fine-tuning of agents and environments, but for simulations to inform design at scale, tools must be usable by designers and developers without deep expertise in reinforcement learning. Recent work has proposed tools for quickly parameterizing models and inspecting simulated behavior
(
70
)
, but more work is needed to develop and evaluate tools that can integrate into real-world development processes of practitioners. At the same time, we recognize that simulation-based testing will not entirely replace user evaluations. Human studies will still be necessary to verify whether objective metrics translate into user satisfaction and increase user experience, and whether simulations align with real-world data. However, studying adaptation requires multi-session experiments where users interact with different keyboards over time, making empirical evaluation slow and resource-intensive. Simulation-based evaluations are an efficient means to speed up hypothesis testing and identify promising design candidates in early phases of design.

Finally, future work could further explore how linguistic knowledge and automatic control mechanisms
(
50
)
influence word suggestion usage, and potentially enhance the hierarchical supervisory control framework with an intermediate stage representing linguistic processing of the to-be-written content using LLM-driven models
(
69
;
8
;
56
)
. Additionally, psychological factors such as motivation, trust, and emotions
(
41
;
42
;
77
)
, combined with hierarchical reinforcement learning techniques
(
61
)
, may increase the realism of typing simulations. For example, by capturing users’ frustration with prediction or auto-correction errors
(
1
;
24
)
, which could affect their reliance on these tools
(
73
)
. As LLM-based writing and typing tools
(
49
;
21
)
, including personalized systems, continue to grow in use, it will be crucial to study how users adapt and to design systems that more closely align with human needs.

8.
Conclusion

We present WSTypist, a cognitively informed reinforcement learning model that simulates human typing with word suggestions. The model captures key human-like behaviors, including gaze distribution, typing speed, and suggestion usage, and generalizes across different user groups and system settings. Across four use cases, we show how the simulation can inform the design of word suggestion algorithms and user interfaces. WSTypist combines fidelity to human performance with the flexibility to explore novel system configurations, making it a valuable tool for advancing our understanding of typing behavior and guiding the design of intelligent text entry systems.

Appendix A
Components from Previous Work

To keep the main content focused and readable, we provide relevant components from previous work
(
71
)
here for convenient reference.

Memory Parameter (
P_{M}
)

This parameter represents the strength of the typist’s memory, where a value of 0 indicates perfect memory, i.e., the typist knows exactly what has been typed so far. It influences the capacity of Working Memory and, regarding suggestion usage, affects how uncertain the agent is about the number of characters left to type in a word.

Certainty

This metric models how confident the typist is about the currently typed content:
Certainty=e^{-P_{M}\times T}
. Certainty decays exponentially over time, governed by the memory parameter (
P_{M}
). When the text is proofread (i.e., gaze on the input field), time is reset to 0, making Certainty equal to 1.

Correctness

This metric quantifies the accuracy of the current input:
Correctness=1-CER(Typed+Left,Target)^{\lambda}
, where
CER
is the Character Error Rate and
\lambda
controls its influence.
CER
is computed by comparing the
Target
word with the concatenation of the already typed text (
Typed
) and the remaining text (
Left
). The agent’s
Certainty
indicates confidence in the correctness of the
Typed
portion.
