Optimizing adult nutrition and mating duration to improve mass rearing of Chrysoperla zastrowi sillemi
Authors: KT Shivakumara, SS Udikeri, T. Venkatesan, T. Sudha, MC Keerthi, M. Mohan, C. Manjunatha, K. Ashok, GS Guruprasad, SG. Rayar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chrysoperla zastrowi sillemi (CZS) is an important predatory lacewing used in biological pest control across protected and open-field systems due to its high predation efficiency and insecticide tolerance. However, current mass-rearing practices are insufficient to meet commercial needs, primarily because of poor-quality production and low fecundity. This study integrates the functional response of different CZS larval instars with an evaluation of adult diet formulations and male exposure durations. By assessing both larval predation efficiency and adult reproductive performance, it provides insights to improve and optimize mass-rearing strategies of CZS. Larval functional response assays (n = 30) showed that sterilized Corcyra cephalonica eggs supported the highest consumption, with third instars feeding on 370.67 ± 49.53 eggs (t = 42.96**, p < 0.01). Adult diet trials (n = 20, triplicate) demonstrated that a mixture of water, 50% honey, protinex 20% and castor pollen produced the highest fecundity (426.60 ± 2.64 eggs/female, p < 0.0001). Mating duration experiments (n = 20, triplicate) revealed that a male paired with the female for five days exposure maximized fecundity (678.87 ± 2.66 eggs/female, p < 0.0001) while maintaining female longevity. Continuous male presence reduced both longevity and egg production, whereas virgins lived longest (54.73 days) but laid few eggs (36.53 ± 1.46 eggs/female, p < 0.0001). This study provides the first integrated assessment of diet composition and male exposure duration in CZS mass production, offering practical baselines for improving CZS mass-rearing protocols.

Introduction

Chrysoperla zastrowi sillemi (CZS) (Esben-Petersen) (Neuroptera: Chrysopidae), commonly known as the aphid lion, is an important predatory lacewing used in augmentative biological control across various agro-climatic zones of India1. While its general biology and wide prey range are well documented2–5, systematic studies evaluating the combined effects of larval diet quality and adult male exposure duration on reproductive performance and mass-rearing efficiency are lacking. Larvae can be maintained on factitious hosts such as Corcyra cephalonica, and adults feed on nectar, pollen, and sugary plant secretions6,7. However, optimized protocols integrating larval feeding efficiency, adult nutrition, and mating management have not been established, limiting commercial-scale production of high-quality CZS.

Reproductive fitness in CZS is influenced by both male availability and adult nutrition. Studies have shown that nutrients and water from ejaculates provide fecundity benefits8. In adult insects, reproductive energy is derived from foraging behavior and larval reserves. Multiple mating in polyandrous females can replenish sperm, enhance fertilization, and improve fecundity, with males often providing additional fitness benefits. Accordingly, Den Hollander and Gwynne9 found that repeatedly mated C. maculatus females produced more eggs and showed higher reproductive success. Despite the widespread occurrence of multiple mating in female insects, the evolutionary advantages, physiological costs, and ecological implications of these mating strategies are still not fully elucidated10. Multiple matings can enhance reproductive fitness and progeny performance, but excessive male encounters may negatively impact female longevity11. The costs and benefits of multiple matings are shaped by environmental conditions, including population density, which influences female mating strategies12. At low male densities, females expend more energy searching for mates, whereas higher male densities may reduce female re-mating interest but increase male harassment risks, potentially leading to physical harm and decreased lifespan9. Despite extensive research on CZS mass production, the impact of environmental conditions on balancing the costs and benefits of male exposure remains unclear. Additionally, variation in male density and its effect on female fitness have yet to be systematically studied13.

Nutrition critically influences arthropod reproductive behaviour, with protein being particularly essential; deficiency can reduce mate selectivity and fecundity. Other nutrients, including lipids, carbohydrates, vitamins, and minerals, also support reproductive performance, physiological health, and overall fitness14,15. Current mass-rearing protocols for CZS generally provide honey solutions as adult diets and maintain continuous male exposure, but the combined effects of optimized adult diet composition and controlled male exposure duration on fecundity, longevity, and reproductive efficiency have not been systematically evaluated.

For large-scale applications, efficient mass-rearing of CZS under laboratory conditions is essential. Larvae are typically reared on factitious hosts such as C. cephalonica eggs, whereas adults are maintained on sugar or honey-based liquid diets. However, successful mass-rearing depends not only on high larval survival but also on optimal adult reproductive performance. Adult nutrition, particularly protein content, as well as mating frequency or male availability, are known to strongly influence female fecundity and lifetime egg production. Despite the ecological and commercial significance of CZS, the interactive effects of adult diet quality and male exposure on reproductive output remain insufficiently explored. Therefore, the present study examines the combined effects of adult nutritional enrichment and controlled male exposure on fecundity, egg viability, and female longevity. Understanding these interactions will help optimize reproductive efficiency and refine mass-rearing protocols for biological control programmes.

Materials and methods

Study site

The reproductive performance and feeding potential of CZS were evaluated at the Division of Genomic Resources, ICAR-National Bureau of Agricultural Insect Resources, Bengaluru, India. Experiments were conducted under laboratory conditions maintained at 25 ± 1 °C and 75 ± 5% relative humidity. The study was carried out over a two-year period from 2024 to 2025.

Culture ofCorcyra cephalonica

C. cephalonica was reared on a fortified maize-based diet (maize, groundnut powder, yeast, and sulfur) following standard protocols16. Emerging adult moths were collected, and their eggs were UV-sterilized (15 W, 1 h) to inactivate embryos, providing a controlled, standardized prey source for evaluating the feeding potential and reproductive performance of CZS. This approach ensures consistent prey quality and reproducibility, enhancing the reliability of predator mass-rearing studies.

Culture ofChrysoperla zastrowi sillemi

A laboratory population of CZS has been maintained at NBAIR for 12 years, spanning 135 generations without insecticide exposure. UV-treated C. cephalonica eggs were used as prey. Newly emerged first-instar CZS larvae were reared individually in sterile glass vials (Ø 2.5 × 5.5 cm) to prevent cannibalism and fed daily with sterilized eggs until pupation. Adults were sexed and maintained individually in PVC boxes (20 × 16 cm) covered with perforated brown cardboard paper for oviposition and provided water-soaked cotton, 50% honey solution, protein mixture, and castor pollen. Eggs were collected every two days for hatching. Rearing conditions were maintained at 25 ± 1 °C, 75 ± 5% RH, with a 14:10 h light: dark photoperiod, following Venkatesan et al.16.

Experimental setup for feeding potential ofChrysoperla zastrowi sillemi

To assess the feeding potential of CZS larvae, a completely randomized design was used with individual larvae as the statistical unit. Different densities of sterilized and unsterilized C. cephalonica eggs were provided. Newly emerged larvae were transferred from the hatching chamber using a fine camel hair brush into glass vials (5 × 2.5 cm) sealed with cotton. Based on previous studies, 100, 300, and 500 eggs were offered to first, second, and third instars, respectively (n = 30 per treatment). Prey consumption was recorded at 24 h intervals, and total consumption over the larval period was calculated. Each experiment was replicated three times to ensure accuracy, and data were used to evaluate larval feeding efficiency across instars and prey densities. The study was conducted under controlled conditions with a temperature of 25 ± 1 °C, relative humidity of 75 ± 5%, and a 14 h light/10 h dark photoperiod.

Effect of different diets on reproductive performance

Reproductive performance of CZS was assessed using 20 individuals per treatment, with each of the seven adult nutritional diets tested in triplicate. Protinex® (Danone Asia Pacific Holdings Pte Ltd) is a commercially available, protein-rich nutritional supplement widely used in insect rearing because it provides a balanced blend of proteins, essential amino acids, lipids, vitamins, and minerals that support insect growth, development, and reproduction in predatory insects. Honey (Dabur India Ltd) was diluted in a 1:1 (v/v) ratio with distilled water, and Protinex® was prepared in a 1:4 (w/v) ratio (20% concentration). Using these components, the study has formulated an optimized artificial diet for laboratory rearing of adult CZS, incorporating Protinex® as the primary protein source. The final mixture consisted of 50% honey, 20% Protinex®, distilled water, and selected pollen types to provide a balanced supply of carbohydrates, proteins, and micronutrients. All components were evenly distributed within the rearing containers to ensure uniform nutrient availability. The diets included: T1 (Water + 50% honey), T2 (Water + 50% honey + protinex), T3 (Water + 50% honey + castor pollen), T4 (Water + 50% honey + maize pollen), T5 (Water + 50% honey + protinex + castor pollen), T6 (Water + 50% honey + protinex + maize pollen), and T7 (Water + castor pollen). All diets were properly prepared and administered to adults using cotton wool. Newly emerged CZS adults (< 12 h old) were randomly selected from plastic containers and the males and females were then sexed based on morphological characteristics [16]. Each male-female pair was placed in a plastic container (10 cm height × 6 cm width) with the assigned adult diet. Diets were replaced daily, and adults were maintained on the same diet until both sexes perished. Each diet was tested on fifteen healthy females. To promote oviposition, the top of the container was covered with perforated brown paper. Biological parameters, including daily fecundity and adult longevity, were recorded for all treatment pairs, and the total fecundity of each female was calculated.

Experiment protocol of male availability on reproductive fitness

The availability of a male and duration of male availability plays a crucial role in achieving optimal fecundity for future generations. Different male exposure durations were evaluated in a 10 × 6 cm mating chamber using 20 individuals per treatment, with each of the seven adult diets replicated three times, to determine effects on CZS fecundity. The male exposure conditions included: (1) virgin females with no males, (2) male paired with the female for one day, (3) male paired with the female for three days, (4) male paired with the female for five days, (5) male paired with the female for one week, (6) male paired with the female for two weeks, and (7) male paired with the female for their entire lifespan. After the designated period, the males were carefully removed from the containers to assess the females’ reproductive potential. Each male was used only once to prevent cross-contamination between treatments. A diet consisting of water, 50% honey, protinex, and castor pollen was found to enhance fecundity. This adult diet was consistently provided across all treatments. Each plastic container was inspected daily, and the adult diet was replaced accordingly. Egg production per female (i.e., the number of eggs laid on the paper lining the container’s interior) was recorded daily throughout their lifespan. Additionally, pre-oviposition period, oviposition period, post-oviposition period, female longevity, and fecundity were monitored every 24 h until death.

Statistical analysis

A two-sample t-test was conducted (n = 30, in triplicate) using WASP-Web Agri Stat Package 2.0 (version 2.0; ICAR-CCARI, Goa, India; https://ccari.res.in/wasp2.0/index.php). The test assumes independence of observations, approximate normality of data within each group, homogeneity of variances between groups, and a continuous dependent variable. All assumptions were checked prior to analysis to ensure the validity of the results. Life table analysis was conducted using TWOSEX-MSChart (version v3, 2022-12-26; 10.5281/zenodo.7484085)17 following the age-stage, two-sex life table approach18–20. Mean values and standard errors for longevity, fecundity, reproductive periods, and population growth parameters were estimated using the Bootstrap technique with 100,000 resampling. Pairwise comparisons of means were performed using the paired Bootstrap test based on confidence intervals (p < 0.0001)21,22. Results

Feeding potential and prey preference ofC. zastrowi sillemi

The predatory efficiency of the CZS grub was assessed by counting the total number of Corcyra eggs consumed at each larval instar stage. As shown in Table 1, the predation rate of CZS gradually increased from the first to the third instar, regardless of the Corcyra host type. First-instar grubs consumed significantly more sterilized eggs (82.13 ± 9.29) than unsterilized C. cephalonica eggs (65.76 ± 13.97), as evidenced by a highly significant difference (t = 39.82**, p < 0.01). The third-instar grubs exhibited the highest predation rate among all larval stages. They consumed significantly more sterilized C. cephalonica eggs (370.67 ± 49.53) compared to unsterilized eggs (286.90 ± 32.63; t = 42.96**, p < 0.01). In contrast, the second-instar grubs showed moderate predation, consuming more sterilized eggs (223.03 ± 33.62) than unsterilized eggs (198.03 ± 28.11; t = 49.10**, p < 0.01). The increased predation observed in the third instar is likely due to its larger body size, higher mobility, and increased nutritional demands required for successful pupal metamorphosis. Overall, the results indicate that among the available hosts, sterilized C. cephalonica eggs were the most preferred, while unsterilized C. cephalonica eggs were the least favored by the CZS predator.

Impact of adult feeding regimes on the reproductive biology

The reproductive performance of female insects is largely influenced by their adult diet (Table 2). The highest fecundity was recorded in females provided with the diet comprising water + 50% honey + protinex + castor pollen, which resulted in an average of 426.60 ± 2.64 eggs per female (df = 6, F cal = 375.53, p < 0.0001). This was followed by females fed water + 50% honey + castor pollen, which produced 302.00 ± 2.25 eggs per female. The highly significant statistical difference underscores the substantial improvement in reproductive output achieved by supplementing the adult diet with a protein source (Protinex) in combination with pollen. These two diets significantly outperformed all others (p < 0.0001). In contrast, females fed maize pollen produced only 195.13 ± 2.29 eggs per female, indicating notably lower fecundity compared to castor pollen-based diets. Females provided with water + 50% honey+protinex laid an average of 237.87 ± 2.26 eggs per female. The results further indicate that diets consisting solely of honey or protinex may not fulfill the nutritional needs of adults, leading to reduced fecundity. Therefore, selecting suitable pollen sources is essential for maximizing egg production. Notably, the water + 50% honey diet resulted in the lowest fecundity (91.20 ± 6.26 eggs per female). Among all tested diets, the water + 50% honey+protinex+castor pollen combination proved to be the most effective in promoting maximum fecundity in CZS (Table 2).

Influence of male availability on reproductive fitness

To assess the impact of male exposure on CZS fecundity, newly emerged males were exposed to females for varying durations. Males exposed to females for different durations produced between 301.40 ± 3.35 and 678.87 ± 2.66 eggs per female, whereas virgin females produced only 36.53 ± 1.46 eggs. The highest fecundity was recorded when females were paired with males for five days, producing 678.87 ± 2.66 eggs per female (df = 6, F cal = 5072.48, p < 0.0001). This was followed by females paired for three days, which laid 614.13 ± 3.90 eggs per female (p < 0.0001). However, males continuously exposed to females throughout their lifetime exhibited lower fecundity (412.33 ± 2.33 eggs/female) (Table 3). Virgin females experienced the longest pre-oviposition, oviposition, and post-oviposition periods (3.47 ± 0.13, 43.20 ± 0.42, and 8.07 ± 0.23 days, respectively). In contrast, Male paired with the female for five days had significantly shorter pre-oviposition and post-oviposition periods (2.07 ± 0.16 and 3.27 ± 0.18 days, respectively), resulting in the highest oviposition duration (p < 0.0001). The daily fecundity rate was notably higher in Male paired with the female for five days (28 eggs/female/day) compared to those exposed for three days (27.50 eggs/female/day). Virgin females laid between 0.27 and 2.22 eggs per day. Across all exposure durations, female egg production showed a steady increase (Fig. 1), peaking between the 10th and 20th days of the oviposition period before gradually declining after 20th day.

Influence of mating frequency on longevity and demographic fitness

Female lifespan decreased as mating frequency or duration of male’s exposure increased. Virgin females exhibited significantly greater longevity (54.73 ± 0.50 days; df = 6, F cal = 104.38, p < 0.0001) compared to males that were continuously exposed to females. In contrast, males that remained with females throughout their lifespan showed the shortest adult longevity (40.33 ± 0.57 days; p < 0.0001; Fig. 2). These findings indicate a substantial cost of continuous mating interactions on male survival in CZS. Females exposed to males for only five days exhibited higher net and gross reproductive rate (316.88 ± 5.7 and 381.27 ± 5.8 eggs/female, p < 0.0001), whereas males that were exposed to females continuously had lower net reproductive and gross reproductive value (159.86 ± 4.1 and 189.68 ± 4.5 eggs/female; p < 0.0001) (Table 4). Female survival was strongly influenced by the extent of male female interaction, with virgin females exhibiting higher survival rates (p < 0.0001). In males, prolonged association with females markedly reduced longevity, indicating a substantial survival cost of continuous mating activity. Overall, mating interactions increased adult mortality, whereas virgin females and those with minimal exposure to males showed notably higher survival (Fig. 3). The hatchability of eggs in CZS was largely unaffected by the duration of male female pairing. Females paired with males for one day exhibited 82.33 ± 2.52% hatchability, while those paired for five days showed 81.67 ± 1.44%. Continuous male presence throughout the female’s lifespan resulted in 84.17 ± 1.44% hatchability. Statistical analysis indicated no significant difference among these treatments (df = 5, F cal = 0.91, p = 0.504), suggesting that once mating occurs, even a brief exposure is sufficient to ensure normal fertilization and embryo development. Notably, eggs laid by virgin females did not hatch, confirming that CZS requires mating for successful reproduction (Fig. 4). The incubation period of CZS eggs was similar across all mating treatments, ranging from 2.70 ± 0.13 to 2.95 ± 0.11 days, with no significant differences observed (df = 5, F cal = 0.63, p = 0.68).

Discussion

Insects typically engage in reproductive behaviours that enhance offspring success in future generations25. During mating, females acquire sperm essential for progeny development26, but excessive mating or prolonged male contact can reduce reproductive fitness in many insects by increasing energetic costs, disrupting feeding, or causing physiological strain27. Although some studies have examined male-female interactions under different environmental conditions, including adult nutrition and water availability28, information specific to Chrysoperla zastrowi sillemi (CZS) remains limited. The present study integrates three key components of CZS biology namely larval feeding efficiency, adult dietary requirements, and optimal male exposure to identify mechanistic drivers of reproductive output and to refine strategies for large scale commercial production. By evaluating how prey quality, nutrient balance, and controlled mating duration shape fecundity and survival, the study provides practical recommendations for enhancing mass-rearing efficiency while reducing biological and operational costs.

Effective mating began around three days post emergence, consistent with previous work on Chrysoperla spp. Females mated at three days exhibited the highest fecundity and hatchability, comparable to those paired with five-day-old males and females. These findings corroborate Chakraborty et al.29, who reported that males generally mate between two- and three-days post-emergence and re-mate every two to four days, and Jones et al.30, who found that males typically begin mating at three to four days old. Frequent mating can impose physiological and behavioural costs in insects31, and male female interactions may reduce female fitness due to repeated mating attempts or energetic stress32. The present study found that differential male exposure affected female fitness by reducing oviposition duration and fecundity. Virgin females exhibited longer life spans than mated females, suggesting that mating and subsequent oviposition are energetically demanding. Male exposure can also be costly due to frequent mating attempts, feeding disruptions, toxicity of seminal fluid molecules, transmission of sexually transmitted diseases, and mating-induced damage to the female reproductive system33. Interestingly, the continued presence of males led to increased female lifespan and fecundity. However, minimal male exposure resulted in enhanced egg production. These findings align with Li34 who reported that unmated females had the highest survival rates compared to mate females. Females paired with males for five days laid 678.87 eggs/female, an increase of 64% compared to continuous exposure (412.33 eggs/female) and far exceeding virgin females, which produced very few unfertilized eggs. These results confirm earlier findings that a single or limited number of matings can support lifetime reproduction in chrysopids2.

Egg hatchability remained consistent across treatments (82–84%), demonstrating that even short male exposure ensures complete sperm transfer. In the present study, differential male exposure clearly influenced female fitness. Virgin females lived longer than mated females, indicating that mating and subsequent egg production are energetically demanding. Similar trends have been reported in C. agilis35. However, continuous male presence did not enhance fecundity; instead, minimal but sufficient male exposure (three to five days) resulted in markedly higher egg production. Demographic parameters further support the advantage of controlled male exposure36. Females exposed to males for five days achieved the highest net and gross reproductive rates, nearly double those of females continuously paired with males. Virgin females exhibited the longest lifespan but produced no viable eggs, reaffirming the reproductive cost of mating. Taken together, the results indicate that restricted male exposure maximizes fecundity while reducing adult mortality an important consideration for commercial rearing. This observation is congruent with observations from other chrysopids, including Chrysopa oculata, Chrysopa perla, and C. carnea37.

Cannibalism was observed in CZS, involving predation on conspecific larvae, and has also been reported in other chrysopids, particularly under laboratory conditions with low-quality diets. The progressive increase in predation from the first to third instar aligns with general chrysopid developmental physiology, as metabolic demands escalate when larvae approach pupation. Across all instars, CZS larvae displayed higher predatory efficiency on sterilized C. cephalonica eggs than on unsterilized eggs, consistent with the findings38,39. Third-instar larvae consumed the most eggs, reflecting their heightened energy requirements before pupation. The preference for sterilized eggs suggests that surface sterilization may enhance prey acceptability, possibly by reducing microbial contamination or altering egg surface chemistry to facilitate handling and ingestion. Quantitatively, sterilized eggs increased prey consumption by 15–25% across instars, representing a substantial improvement for mass-rearing programs where feed conversion efficiency and larval development rate directly affect production output.

Adult diet composition significantly influenced reproductive performance. The combination of water + 50% honey + protinex + castor pollen yielded the highest fecundity (426.60 eggs/female), representing a 368% increase relative to honey alone (91.20 eggs/female) and a 44% improvement over the second-best diet (honey + castor pollen). These results align with earlier observations that castor pollen enhances fecundity in C. carnea38 and that adult chrysopids require both carbohydrate and protein sources for optimal reproductive output40,41. Castor pollen likely provides essential sterols and amino acids absent in maize pollen, explaining the substantially lower fecundity observed with maize-based diets42. Our findings also clarify contradictions reported by Satapathy and Mandal43, who suggested that a honey–protinex mixture alone maximizes reproduction. Instead, the present results demonstrate that both pollen and protein are necessary components, and that castor pollen is a critical ingredient for maximizing egg production.

The combined results highlight clear mechanistic drivers of reproductive efficiency: larval performance improves when fed sterilized eggs, likely due to increased palatability and reduced microbial load. Adult fecundity depends on a balanced nutrient supply, especially sterol and protein-rich pollen. Mating success is optimized by providing a limited male exposure period (three to five days), reducing unnecessary energetic expenditure. Together, these factors can significantly enhance mass-rearing efficiency by lowering adult mortality, reducing diet waste, and maximizing egg yield per female. This study was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions, which may not fully represent field or semi-field environments. Field validation of optimal diets, male exposure schedules, and prey preferences would increase the practical relevance of the findings. Additionally, a cost benefit analysis comparing different diets particularly the economic viability of castor pollen and protein supplements would strengthen recommendations for commercial insectaries. Although mating behaviour was noted, detailed behavioural quantification (e.g., rate of male approaches, duration of copulation) was not undertaken; future work integrating ethological methods could clarify whether behavioural interference contributes to reduced fecundity under continuous male exposure. Overall, the study provides mechanistic and applied insights that directly enhance the mass-rearing efficiency of CZS. Optimal larval prey type, balanced adult diet composition, and controlled male exposure can dramatically increase egg production while minimizing mortality and resource expenditure key improvements for large-scale biological control programs.

Conclusion

This study provides practical guidance for enhancing the mass-rearing efficiency of CZS for augmentative biological control. Sterilized C. cephalonica eggs were identified as the most suitable larval diet, and an adult diet comprising water, 50% honey, Protinex (20%), and castor pollen (1:1:1:1 ratio) significantly improved fecundity, providing actionable strategies to increase colony productivity. Additionally, a five-day male exposure period was sufficient to maximize egg production while maintaining female longevity, offering insights for optimizing mating management in rearing systems. These findings were obtained under controlled laboratory conditions; factors such as genetic variability, environmental fluctuations in commercial insectaries, and the long-term effects of sterilized eggs or enriched adult diets were not examined and may influence large-scale application. Future research should focus on nutritional biochemistry of diets, semi-field validation of rearing protocols, and cost-benefit analyses to determine the feasibility of implementing these strategies in commercial biological control programs.
