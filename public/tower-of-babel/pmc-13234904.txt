Comprehensive Metabolomic Analysis of Saliva Using SWATH–DIA Reveals Systemic Metabolic Adaptations to Exercise
Authors: Maroof Athar Hashmi, Diksha Gogia, Nirpendra Singh, Arvind Ramanathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Saliva is an accessible, noninvasive biofluid for real-time monitoring of physiological and metabolic changes. However, its potential to capture physical-exciton-induced biochemical responses has been limited by the metabolite identification depth and reproducibility of conventional metabolomics tools. In this study, we established a Sequential Window Acquisition of All Theoretical Fragment-Ion Spectra (SWATH–DIA)–based untargeted LC–MS metabolomics workflow for comprehensive profiling and relative quantitation of the salivary metabolome before and after physical exercise. Saliva samples were collected from 27 recreational runners before and immediately after a standardized 5 km run to investigate acute metabolic fluctuations in participants. The Zeno SWATH–DIA method enabled the simultaneous acquisition of precursor and fragment ion spectra across the full m/z range (50–800 Da) in positive and negative mode of electrospray ionization (ESI), resulting in detection and validation metabolites spanning lipids, amino acids, organic acids, carbohydrates, and short-chain carnitines. Compared with traditional data-dependent acquisition (DDA) approaches, Zeno SWATH–DIA provided enhanced metabolite coverage, improved reproducibility, and reduced precursor selection bias (a statement of quantitation of how much more). Multivariate analyses (PCA, OPLS-DA) revealed clear separation between pre- and postexercise samples, highlighting metabolic shifts involving carbohydrate metabolism (lactate, pyruvate), fatty acid oxidation (acylcarnitines, glycerol), amino acid turnover (BCAAs, arginine, ornithine), and nitrogen metabolism (urea, spermidine). Collectively, these findings establish Zeno SWATH–DIA saliva metabolomics as a robust, high-coverage analytical approach for noninvasive assessment of acute metabolic responses to the exercise-induced physiological changes. The workflow provides a methodological foundation for future integrative studies linking saliva-based metabolomics with performance, fatigue, and metabolic health monitoring.

Introduction

Saliva is a noninvasive, easily accessible, and repeatable biofluid that reflects both host-derived and exogenous metabolites. , Its metabolomic profile has increasingly been leveraged for diagnostic and prognostic applications, including the identification of biomarkers associated with oral diseases, diabetes, cardiovascular dysfunction, and neurodegenerative disorders. − Despite these advances, the potential of saliva to capture dynamic physiological responses in exercise remains underexplored. Previously reported studies employing Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectroscopy in soccer athletes revealed distinct clustering of salivary metabolites associated with fatigue, performance, and recovery. Subsequent studies using Liquid Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry (LC–MS) and quasi-targeted metabolomics approaches in competitive athletes reported correlations between salivary metabolitessuch as lactate, creatinine, acylcarnitines, and amino acidsand established plasma biomarkers of metabolic stress. ,

Comprehensive systemic metabolomics studies have frequently reported that aerobic and high-intensity exercise induces substantial increases in lactate, pyruvate, tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediates, fatty acids, ketone bodies, and acylcarnitines. , A recent systematic review examining 27 human metabolomics studies identified 196 metabolites that significantly changed within the first 24 h after exercise, underscoring the extent of metabolic reprogramming during physiological adaptation. Despite these systemic findings, the potential of saliva to capture physiologically relevant metabolic changes using untargeted, high-coverage metabolomic workflows remains to be thoroughly evaluated.

Prior saliva metabolomics studies have achieved broad metabolite coverage by combining multiple analytical platforms, but the coverage from LC–MS/MS workflows has remained limited to few metabolic classes. This underscores the need to evaluate high-coverage, untargeted LC–MS/MS strategies for comprehensive salivary metabolomic profiling. Sequential Window Acquisition of All Theoretical Fragment-Ion Spectra (SWATH–DIA) represents a major advancement in untargeted metabolomics by enabling unbiased and reproducible data acquisition. Unlike data-dependent approaches, which preferentially fragment the most abundant ions, SWATH–DIA fragments all detectable precursor ions within predefined m/z windows across the chromatographic run, thereby minimizing precursor selection bias and enhancing analytical reproducibility across samples. This data-independent strategy not only enhances metabolite coverage by 30–55% compared to DDA-based workflows but also enables retrospective data interrogation, thereby increasing confidence in metabolite annotation and quantification. , Moreover, validated SWATH–DIA workflows have demonstrated high specificity (>96%) and notably low false discovery rates (<4%) across large spectral libraries consisting of more than 1200 compounds, establishing their suitability for comprehensive small-molecule analysis.

In this study, we applied a SWATH–DIA-based LC–MS workflow to perform untargeted metabolomic profiling of saliva samples collected before and immediately after a 5 km run in recreational runners. Our primary objectives were to (i) develop a LC–MS method to achieve broad and reproducible coverage of saliva metabolites across multiple classes, (ii) quantify and statistically identify acute metabolic alterations associated with exercise-induced physiological adaptation, and (iii) evaluate the feasibility of saliva as a noninvasive matrix to access physiological adaptations. Through this approach, we demonstrate that the untargeted SWATH–DIA LC–MS/MS workflow achieved enhanced analytical coverage compared with previously reported salivary metabolomics approaches with the MS/MS validation and quantification of 274 metabolites from saliva (compared to previously reported 73 metabolites). Through this approach, we demonstrate that SWATH–DIA enables detailed characterization of exercise-related metabolic shifts in saliva, which may reflect physiologically relevant changes associated with systemic energy metabolism and recovery and is therefore investigated here as a potential, rather than established, surrogate of systemic metabolic responses.

Materials and Methods

Cohort and Sampling

Twenty-seven healthy amateur runners (ages 20–45, balanced sex distribution) participated in a timed 5K running event, along with four age- and sex-matched nonrunner controls who provided resting saliva samples. Saliva samples were collected with unstimulated, passive drooling in a sterile 15 mL microfuge tube at 7AM IST. The run included 31 participants, comprising 27 runners and 4 nonrunners, who served as controls. The information related to run time and corresponding pace of the participants are present in Table S3. Running pace was calculated as the total time taken to complete the run divided by the running distance (5 km) and expressed as minutes per kilometer (min·km–1).

Ethical approvals and informed consent were obtained prior to participation. Participants abstained from eating, drinking (except water), and oral hygiene practices for at least 1 h prior to sample collection. Before the collection of baseline samples, subjects were asked to rinse their mouths with lukewarm water to rinse off residual food particles or medication. The samples were stored at −80 °C until metabolite extraction was performed.

Metabolite Extraction Protocol

For metabolomics, a 45 μL saliva sample was neutralized with extraction solvent (450 μL of 1:2:2 water: methanol: ACN containing 1% formic acid and 2 μL of QRESS metabolite mix as labeled internal standards). The samples were briefly vortexed and then centrifuged at 16,000g, 4 °C for 20 min. The supernatant was then transferred to a fresh 1.5 mL tube, dried, and then stored at −80 °C until the day of metabolomics data acquisition. The samples were resuspended in 90 μL of 50% methanol in water and then transferred to autosampler vials.

Untargeted Metabolomic Analysis of the SWATH-MS Data

The LC method was modified from previously published Vorkas et al. (2015). UPLC separation was conducted by using the Shimadzu UPLC System. Acquity UPLC BEH HILIC 2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm, column (Waters Corp, USA) was used. Column temperature was set at 35 °C. Mobile phase A consisted of acetonitrile (ACN)/water (95:5) and mobile phase B consisted of ACN/water (50:50). Both solvents had ammonium acetate of 10 mM and formic acid of 0.1%. The elution gradient was set as follows: 99% A (0.0–2.0 min; 0.4 mL/min), 99–45% A (2.0–8.0 min; 0.4 mL/min), 45–1% A (8.0–9.0 min; 0.4 mL/min), 1% A (9.0–9.1 min; 0.4–0.8 mL/min), 1% A (9.1–10.0 min; 0.8 mL/min), 1–99% A (10.0–10.1 min; 0.8 mL/min), 99% A (10.1–11.0 min; 0.8 mL/min), 99% A (11.0–14.1 min; 0.8–0.4 mL/min), 99% A (14.1–15.0 min; 0.4 mL/min). An injection volume of 2 μL was used for both positive (ESI+) and negative (ESI−) ionization polarity modes. The autosampler rack temperature was set to 5 °C.

Mass spectrometry was performed using a Sciex 7600 Zeno-TOF with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source and collision-induced dissociation (CID). The parameters for IDA-MS acquisition were as follows: Source temperature 550 (°C), curtain gas 30, CAD gas 7, source gas 1:50 psi, source gas 2:60 psi, spray voltage: 5500 V (for positive ionization) and −4500 V (for negative ionization), declustering potential (V): ±80, collision energy (V): ±10 for MS1 and ±35 (spread ±15) for MSMS. Accumulation time for the IDA-MS method was 0.25 for MS1 and 0.04 for MS/MS, and for SWATH-DIA method, it was 0.1 for MS1 and 0.02 for MS/MS; Zeno pulsing was on for 25 ms for SWATH acquisition.

For the SWATH-DIA method, variable window calculation was performed by the SWATH Variable Window Calculator_V1.1 (available at the vendor Web site). The peak intensity and mass table generated from pooled samples using the IDA-MS method was used to calculate SWATH 40 variable windows spanning the mass range between 50 and 800 with 1 Da overlap.

The data file generated from the pooled sample in “wiff” file format was used in the SciexOS software for a library search using the NIST metabolite library. The NIST library consists of a 13571 unique metabolite spectral database used for MS/MS validation. Flagging rules for MS/MS and MS only identification was a mass error (ppm) < 10, % difference isotope ratios <20, RT width of 0.4 s, and a library fit score above 70 and above. Library spectral match is reported with the fit score. The ID, name of the metabolite, m/z, and RT (min) were used for quality checks in SciexOS software and peak integration checks. An alternate method of manual metabolite validation is mentioned in the following Metabolite Manual Validation section. Metabolites with data in ≥70% samples were considered for analysis.

Metabolite Manual Validation

Peak and intensity tables from the SWATH-DIA MSMS spectrum of pooled samples were used for manual validation. Peak annotation was performed in MetaboAnalyst with a library search with exact mass (Precursor Mass Tolerance: 10 ppm) and MSMS spectrum (Fragment Mass Tolerance: 20 ppm). All libraries were used for annotation, which included HMDB, MS-Dial, MassBank, MoNA, GNPS, and LipidBlast and were assigned as Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) Level 2 (putatively annotated compounds). Similarity method used was dot-product.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis of the metabolomics data was performed using “MetaboAnalyst”. A nonparametric t-test was performed on samples with the assumption of unequal variance and Benjamini–Hochberg correction. For all analyses, p < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant (Tables S4–S7). For multivariate PCA and OPLS-DA analysis, preprocessing (in MetaboAnalyst) of data involves normalization by median; data was transformed with log transformation (base 10); data scaling with mean centering (mean-centered only). The pathway analysis method uses a hypergeometric test (enrichment analysis) with the human reference metabolome. Pathway impact is based on the enrichment analysis procedures, identifying the most relevant metabolic pathways via pathway impact and adjusted p. Pathway impact represents a combination of the centrality and pathway enrichment results; higher impact values represent the relative importance of the pathway; the size of the circle indicates the impact of the pathway, while the color represents the significance (the more intense the red color, the lower the p). Results and Discussion

Comprehensive Saliva Metabolomic Analysis with SWATH-DIA

We performed untargeted metabolomics using SWATH-DIA from saliva samples to develop a fast-sampling and comprehensive analysis method for observing metabolic changes during exercise. For the study, saliva samples were collected from subjects participating in a 5 km run. Our cohort consisted of 27 runner subjects (with 4 control nonrunners), where the sampling was done before the start of the run, collected as the baseline samples (BS), and after the completion of the run, collected as the follow-up samples (FUP).

From the saliva metabolomic analysis, we detected a greater number of MS1 and MSMS features with the SWATH-DIA method (both in ESI+ and ESI−) compared to the IDA MS method (Figures and C,D). In ESI + mode, at the MS1 level, SWATH–DIA detected 1774 features, compared with 1256 features using DDA, representing an approximately 41% increase in feature coverage and at the MS/MS level, SWATH–DIA detected 860 features, compared with 600 features using DDA, corresponding to an approximately 43% increase. In ESI– mode, at the MS1 level, SWATH–DIA detected 814 features, compared with 656 features using DDA (∼24% increase), and at the MS/MS level, SWATH–DIA detected 526 features, compared with 423 features using DDA (∼24% increase). The batch variation among the samples in ESI+ and ESI– were in the acceptable range, that is, %CV of m/z and RT < 25 (Figures S1 and S2). This allows for validation and reporting of a greater number of metabolites from the saliva MSMS data. Through metabolomic analysis of saliva samples, we detected and quantified 274 validated metabolites. Prior comprehensive saliva metabolomics study have identified 246 and quantified 201 unique or nonoverlapping metabolites or metabolite species across multiple analytical platforms (including DFI/LC–MS/MS, NMR and GC–MS), with the combined DFI/LC–MS/MS method only detecting 73 compounds or compound species. In comparison, our SWATH–DIA-based workflow enabled the detection and MS/MS validation of 274 metabolites from saliva. From this analysis, we quantify 61 of 111 previously reported disease-associated saliva biomarkers (Figure B). In comparison to the existing metabolomics methods, our SWATH-DIA method provides better coverage for the comprehensive analysis of different classes of compounds.

We detected different classes of metabolites from saliva metabolomics data. These were broadly classified into different classes such as lipids (n = 65, 28.5%); exposome, dietary, and drug metabolites (n = 52, 22.8%); amino acids, peptides, and amino acid derivatives (n = 40, 17.5%); carbohydrates (n = 27, 11.8%); organic acids (n = 22, 9.56%); steroids and derivatives (n = 10, 4.4%); nucleotides and derivatives (n = 6, 2.6%); and short-chain carnitines (n = 6, 2.6%) (Figure A). Metabolic Shift in the Physiological Response to the Exercise Adaptation

Exercise-induced metabolic response has been widely studied from the plasma. Metabolic profiling of plasma indicates pathways such as lipid metabolism, the TCA cycle, glycolysis, amino acid metabolism, carnitine metabolism, and purine metabolism were affected. , In order to analyze these changes, we performed pairwise analysis of the baseline and follow-up metabolites, which reveals acute exercise-induced metabolic changes (Supporting Information). Among the metabolites that were significantly different in pairwise analysis belong to glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, pyruvate, amino acid metabolism, starch and sucrose, galactose metabolism, and butanoate metabolism pathways (Figure A, Table S2). Metabolites associated with adaptation of the body to the energy requirements, such as lactic acid, ketone bodies (BHB), creatinine, organic acids (acetic acid and propionic acid), short-chain carnitines like acetyl and glutaryl carnitines, and nitrogenous waste products like uric acid and urea, were increased (Figure C). , Along with the increase in the levels of short- and medium-chain acylcarnitine, we also observed a significant increase in levels of trimethyl lysine, further suggesting upregulated carnitine biosynthesis, a pathway critical for transporting fatty acids into mitochondria (Table S1). Collectively, these observations indicate changes in the saliva metabolite are consistent with the well-established exercise physiological changes and systemic responses rather than indicating a direct causal shift in substrate utilization. ,,

Multivariate Analysis of Baseline vs Follow-Up Samples

Saliva metabolites quantified from baseline and follow-up samples were analyzed with unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) (Figure A) and supervised orthogonal partial least-squares–discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) (Figure B) in MetaboAnalyst 6.0. The PCA was employed as an exploratory tool to visualize the overall variance in the data set. The first principal component accounted for 31.3% and 34.7% of the variance in positive and negative ionization modes, respectively, and suggested a trend toward separation between baseline and follow-up samples. PERMANOVA revealed significant multivariate differences between groups (ESI+ (F = 4.72, R 2 = 0.073, p = 0.016); ESI– (F = 7.01, R 2 = 0.115, p = 0.001)), suggesting that group status explains a moderate proportion of the overall variance.

Permutation testing indicated that the OPLS-DA models captured nonrandom structure in both ionization modes. In ESI– mode, the model consistently outperformed all permuted models (empirical p < 0.01 for both R 2Y and Q 2), providing strong support that the observed separation was unlikely to arise by chance. In ESI + mode, model performance also exceeded that of permuted data sets (empirical p < 0.05 for both R 2Y and Q 2), though with comparatively lower robustness. Taken together, these findings suggest the presence of structured group-level differences while remaining consistent with the exploratory scope of the study. The observed multivariate patterns suggest that exercise may be associated with coordinated changes across multiple metabolite classes, including carnitines, organic acids, amino acids, saccharides, bile acids, and nitrogenous waste metabolites, several of which showed higher relative abundance in follow-up samples (Figure C).

Multivariate approaches such as PCA and OPLS-DA are useful for visualizing complex metabolic patterns that may not be apparent at the level of individual metabolites, particularly in high-dimensional data sets. In the present study, these analyses were applied to support the identification of candidate metabolic pathways responsive to exercise and to guide subsequent univariate and feature-selection analyses. Importantly, we do not interpret these results as evidence of definitive metabolic discrimination or broad generalizability. Instead, they provide an initial framework for understanding exercise-associated salivary metabolomic trends that warrant validation in larger, independent cohorts. Using this multivariate analysis approach, we can visualize complex metabolic shift in the exercise. This will lead to a generalized interpretation across different studies, as interpretation from a single metabolite level may be influenced by various factors, especially when we consider saliva sampling. It also helps to model physiological changes to improve the reliability of saliva as a biofluid for monitoring health and athletic performance.

Comparison with Systemic Exercise Metabolomics

Our findings align closely with the systemic metabolic changes previously reported in plasma and urine metabolomics studies investigating exercise-induced physiological adaptations. Several plasma-based analyses have demonstrated increased concentrations of lactate, β-hydroxybutyrate (BHB), acylcarnitines, ketone bodies, and branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) during physical exercise (Figure B). ,

The initial energy demand during exercise is primarily supported by carbohydrate metabolism, consistent with the pronounced increase in lactate and pyruvate concentrations observed in saliva. As exercise intensity and duration increase, lipid metabolism progressively contributes to meeting the elevated energy requirements. , The accumulation of acylcarnitines observed in our study indicates a shift toward enhanced fatty acid oxidation, reflecting mitochondrial substrate switching, a well-documented hallmark of endurance adaptation (Table S1). ,,, In parallel, we detected increased glycerol levels, a known marker of adipose tissue lipolysis, further supporting lipid catabolism in play later during exercise.

We also observed elevated concentrations of BCAAs such as valine, isoleucine, and leucine. These amino acids, together with acylcarnitines, have been proposed as biomarkers of metabolic fitness and mitochondrial efficiency.

During prolonged or high-intensity exercise, skeletal muscle is known to accumulate ammonia due to increased amino acid catabolism. Experimental evidence suggests that arginine and ornithine supplementation can delay fatigue by enhancing ammonia clearance via the urea cycle. Correspondingly, our data show increased concentrations of arginine, ornithine, and urea in postexercise saliva samples, potentially reflecting the activation of nitrogen detoxification pathways during late-stage during exertion. This observation supports the hypothesis that the arginine–ornithine–urea axis contributes to mitigating exercise-induced fatigue and may serve as a mechanistic marker of metabolic resilience.

In addition, we observed elevated spermidine levelsa downstream metabolite of argininewhich have been associated with systemic responses to exercise and cellular stress resistance. Mechanistically, the interplay between amino acid metabolism and neurotransmitter biosynthesis is also noteworthy. Increased levels of phenylalanine and tyrosine may enhance catecholamine synthesis, influencing cardiovascular regulation and energy mobilization during exercise. Collectively, these findings underscore the potential of saliva metabolomics to capture integrated neuro-metabolic and physiological responses to exercise, highlighting its value as a noninvasive tool for monitoring systemic adaptation and fatigue dynamics.

Future research should aim to integrate saliva metabolomics with complementary physiological and molecular markers to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of exercise adaptation. Combining metabolomic profiling with hormonal assaysincluding cortisol, testosterone, and other stress- and recovery-related hormonescould help delineate the interplay between metabolic, endocrine, and inflammatory responses to physical exertion. Parallel monitoring of inflammatory and oxidative stress markers would further enhance the interpretive power of metabolomic data, providing multidimensional insights into athlete readiness, recovery status, and stress resilience.

Furthermore, the application of machine learning-based multivariate models to integrated metabolomic and performance data sets holds considerable promise for identifying predictive biomarkers of fatigue, adaptation, and overtraining. Such models can discern subtle, multivariate patterns that may be overlooked through univariate analyses, thereby improving the robustness of the physiological interpretation. Incorporating time-resolved or longitudinal sampling designs would additionally allow the modeling of metabolic fluctuations across exercise, recovery, and adaptation phases, offering a dynamic perspective of individual metabolic flexibility.

Overall, the present findings establish a methodological and analytical foundation for saliva-based metabolomic monitoring using SWATH-DIA, highlighting its potential for noninvasive, high-throughput assessment of acute exercise-induced metabolic changes. Future studies that combine this approach with integrative omics and computational modeling frameworks could advance precision monitoring in sports physiology and personalized health assessment.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the utility of SWATH–DIA-based untargeted metabolomics to achieve comprehensive and reproducible coverage of the salivary metabolome during exercise. The method enabled the detection of diverse metabolite classes and revealed shifts in carbohydrate, carnitine, lipid, and amino acid metabolism associated with acute exercise. These patterns qualitatively align with exercise-related metabolic changes previously reported in plasma and urine in the literature. While systemic measurements for plasma and saliva metabolites were compared in the present study, this literature-based, qualitative comparison suggests that saliva may capture physiologically relevant aspects of exercise-induced metabolic adaptation and supports its investigation as a potential, noninvasive matrix for monitoring dynamic metabolic responses. With further validation in a larger cohort and the combination of data matrices from different studies for exercise performance, saliva metabolomics could become a standard tool in sports science, rehabilitation, and general health monitoring, supporting individualized exercise prescriptions and early detection of metabolic imbalances. Together, these findings establish a robust analytical framework for high-throughput assessment of exercise-induced metabolic changes and pave the way for future integrative studies linking saliva metabolomics with performance, recovery, and personalized health monitoring.
