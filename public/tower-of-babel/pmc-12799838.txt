Posttraumatic stress disorder and associated factors among stroke survivors in Northwest Ethiopia
Authors: Gashaw Mehiret, Tilahun Belete, Yordanos Semegnew
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPosttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) can occur after life-threatening medical conditions; however, there is little consensus on its exact prevalence. Suffering from PTSD among stroke Survivors not only decreases Stroke Survivors’ quality of life but also relates to non-adherence to treatment. Evidence shows that there is limited research on PTSD in stroke survivors in low-income countries.ObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the prevalence and associated factors of PTSD among stroke survivors in Northwest Ethiopia in 2024.MethodsAn institutional-based cross-sectional study was conducted from October 1 to December 30, 2024, among 326 adult stroke survivors (aged ≥ 18 years) with a confirmed clinical diagnosis of stroke (ischemic or hemorrhagic) who were at least one month post-stroke and able to communicate or respond to the questionnaire were included. Data were collected using validated and structured questionnaires, including the Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Checklist for DSM-5, the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale, the Modified Rankin Scale, the Medication Adherence Report Scale-5, the Brief COPE Inventory, and the Oslo 3-Item Social Support Scale. Data were analysed using SPSS version 27. Descriptive statistics such as frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations were computed to summarise sociodemographic, clinical, and psychosocial characteristics of the participants. Bivariate and multivariable logistic regressions were employed to identify associated factors, with significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe prevalence of PTSD symptoms was 37.1% (95% CI 31.2–42.6%). Factors significantly associated with PTSD included being female [AOR = 3.92, 95% CI 2.05–7.47], having no formal education [AOR = 2.91, 95% CI 1.27–6.61], stroke duration of 1–5 years [AOR = 2.24, 95% CI 1.12–4.45] and > 5 years [AOR = 5.12, 95% CI 1.95–13.45], recurrent stroke [AOR = 2.01, 95% CI 1.04–3.89], poor functional status [AOR = 3.83, 95% CI 1.98–7.41], anxiety symptoms [AOR = 2.41, 95% CI 1.31–4.81], and maladaptive coping style [AOR = 3.41, 95% CI 1.76–6.59].ConclusionsOver one-third of stroke survivors experienced PTSD symptoms. Female gender, low educational status, recurrent and long-standing stroke, poor functional status, anxiety symptoms, and maladaptive coping styles were key predictors. The findings underscore the need for routine PTSD screening and integrated psychosocial support within stroke rehabilitation programs to enhance recovery and mental well-being.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44192-025-00358-4.Keywords: Post-traumatic stress disorders, Stroke, Stroke survivors, Ethiopia

Introduction

Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a severe mental health condition triggered by exposure to traumatic experiences involving actual or perceived threats to one’s life or physical integrity [1]. A diagnosis is established when symptoms persist beyond 30 days and include recurrent intrusive recollections of the trauma, avoidance of trauma-related cues, negative alterations in cognition and mood, and heightened arousal or reactivity [2–6]. The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) classifies PTSD under trauma- and stressor-related disorders, characterised by four symptom domains: intrusion, avoidance, negative alterations in cognition and mood, and alterations in arousal and reactivity [7, 8].

Traditionally, PTSD has been associated with external traumatic events such as interpersonal violence, accidents, or natural disasters [7, 9–11]. However, growing evidence indicates that internal medical crises, including acute life-threatening conditions like stroke, can also precipitate PTSD [12, 13]. The enduring somatic threat (EST) model posits that internal somatic reminders of ongoing risk for recurrent medical events contribute to the onset and persistence of PTSD symptoms [14, 15]. Consequently, PTSD following acute medical events is marked by the perception of being unable to escape the threat of future harm due to the somatic origin of the trauma [15]. PTSD has been documented in connection with other serious medical illnesses such as HIV [16], breast cancer [17, 18], acute coronary syndromes [12, 14, 19], and COVID-19 [20]. Nonetheless, research specifically addressing stroke-related PTSD remains limited [2, 21].

Stroke is a sudden, life-threatening neurological event that can meet the diagnostic criteria for PTSD among survivors [8, 22]. Beyond physical disability and functional impairment, stroke survivors frequently experience psychological distress, including anxiety and depression, which may precipitate or maintain PTSD symptoms [8]. Emerging data highlight PTSD as a serious neuropsychiatric consequence of stroke that warrants greater clinical and research attention [3].

Globally, PTSD is recognised as a major contributor to disability and diminished quality of life [23, 24]. Despite the high burden of stroke in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), research on PTSD following stroke is scarce. Studies from North America, Europe, and Asia report prevalence estimates ranging from 6.5% to 40% among stroke survivors [24–29], with meta-analytic findings indicating that approximately one in four individuals may develop clinically significant PTSD symptoms within the first year after stroke [30].

In SSA, including Ethiopia, no published study has systematically examined PTSD among stroke survivors. Most African research on post-stroke mental health has focused on depression and anxiety [31–33], leaving PTSD under-recognised and undocumented. This evidence gap is concerning, as SSA countries face unique psychosocial and health system challenges such as limited access to rehabilitation services, weak mental health infrastructure, pervasive stigma, and reliance on traditional explanatory models of illness that may heighten both the risk and persistence of PTSD symptoms [34–36].

PTSD following stroke is associated with profound reductions in quality of life, economic productivity, and social participation. It imposes an additional psychological burden on survivors, complicating rehabilitation and recovery [37, 38]. The presence of PTSD symptoms may reduce medication adherence, especially when treatment serves as a reminder of the stroke, and increase the likelihood of missed follow-up appointments and recurrent events [39, 40]. These symptoms may also impede neurorehabilitation by consuming cognitive resources and diminishing engagement in therapeutic programmes [41, 42].

These considerations underscore the need for systematic screening of PTSD throughout the stroke recovery continuum, given its implications for functional outcomes and overall quality of life [4, 43–45]. However, there remains a dearth of intervention studies and clinical guidelines tailored to PTSD in the context of cerebrovascular disease [22]. Despite its clinical importance, stroke-related PTSD is substantially under-researched globally, particularly in low-resource settings such as Ethiopia [33, 46]. Based on the researcher’s clinical experience in the neurology unit of Tibebe Ghion Comprehensive Specialised Hospital (TGCSH), a considerable number of stroke survivors present with unrecognised psychological difficulties, signalling an urgent need for context-specific evidence [47].

This study therefore aims to assess the prevalence and associated factors of PTSD symptoms among stroke survivors in Northwest Ethiopia. The working hypothesis is that comorbid medical conditions, prior mental illness, low functional status, non-adherence to medication, and maladaptive coping strategies increase the likelihood of developing PTSD symptoms following stroke, even after controlling for sociodemographic and socioeconomic variables.

Methods and materials

Study setting

The study was conducted across three comprehensive specialised hospitals in northwest Ethiopia: Tibebe Ghion Comprehensive Specialised Hospital (TGCSH) and Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialised Hospital (FHCSH) in Bahir Dar, and Gondar University Comprehensive Specialised Hospital (GUCSH) in north Gondar. TGCSH and FHCSH are teaching hospitals affiliated with Bahir Dar University and serve over 14 million and 7 million people, respectively [48–50]. GUCSH is a teaching hospital affiliated with Gondar University and provides tertiary care to the population of more than 5–7 million, receiving referrals from various district hospitals [51–53]. All selected hospitals have a neurology department, including inpatient, intensive care, and outpatient services with comparable infrastructure, human resources, and service delivery systems.

Study design and period

The institutional-based cross-sectional study design was carried out from October 1 to December 30, 2024.

Source and study population

Source population

The source population consists of all stroke survivors attending neurology follow-up clinics at comprehensive specialised Hospitals in Northwest Ethiopia.

Study population

The study populations were stroke survivors attending neurology follow-up clinics at the selected Hospitals in Northwest Ethiopia during the study period.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

All Stroke Survivors of stroke, aged 18 years and above were included in the study.

Exclusion criteria

Clients having less than one month of diagnosis, already diagnosed with dementia, who developed Aphasia, had a hearing impairment (chart) and were critically ill levelled by the physician, were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

Sample size determination

In this study, the sample size was determined by using a single population proportion formula. As there were no similar studies related to PTSD among stroke survivors in Ethiopia, the calculations were based on the assumption that the probability of having PTSD symptoms among stroke survivors was 50.0%, with a 95% confidence interval, and a limit of precision of 5%

The n = minimum sample size required for the study.

Za/2 = standard score (Z = 1.96 with two tails) with a confidence interval of (CI) 95% and level of significance α = 5% (0.05) critical point to reject the null hypothesis (Ho) or to accept the alternative hypothesis (H1).

d = Absolute precision or tolerable margin of error (d) = 5% ( 0.05).

Since the total population was less than 10,000 (N = 1286), we used the correction formula.

For the non-response rate taking a contingency of 10% was given, which gave a final sample size of 326.

Sampling technique and procedure

A multistage sampling technique was employed to select participants for the study. In the first stage, three comprehensive specialised hospitals, Tibebe Ghion Comprehensive Specialised Hospital (TGCSH), Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialised Hospital (FHCSH), and Gondar University Comprehensive Specialised Hospital (GUCSH)were selected using a simple random sampling (SRS) method from a larger pool of potential hospitals. Before data collection, the total number of stroke survivors attending follow-up clinics over three months (October–December 2023) was obtained from each hospital’s registry: 503 from TGCSH, 294 from FHCSH, and 489 from GUCSH, totalling 1,286 stroke survivors. The total sample size of 326 was proportionally allocated to each hospital to ensure representativeness (Fig. 1).

Schematic presentation of sampling procedure of patients with stroke survivors in Northwest Ethiopia, 2024

Within each hospital, a systematic random sampling technique was used to select participants from the patient registry. The sampling interval (k) was calculated by dividing the total number of stroke survivors in each hospital by the allocated sample size (k = N/n). The first participant was selected using a simple random method (lottery), and then every kth patient was included until the required sample size for that hospital was achieved. For example, if 503 stroke survivors were listed at TGCSH and 127 participants were allocated, the sampling interval was k = 503/127 ≈ 4, meaning every 4th patient was selected after a random start. This procedure ensured that all eligible stroke survivors attending the follow-up clinics had an equal chance of being included in the study.

Variables of the study

Dependent variable

Post-traumatic stress disorder (Yes/No).

Independent variables

Independent variables were selected based on prior literature on PTSD following medical illness and stroke [3, 8, 21, 25, 54], theoretical insights from the Enduring Somatic Threat model [14], and clinical reasoning regarding factors pertinent to post-stroke psychological outcomes in the Ethiopian context.

Socio-demographic characteristics age, gender, marital status, religion, educational level, occupational status, residency, ethnicity, and income level.

Clinical Factors Type of stroke (Ischemic, Hemorrhagic), time since stroke onset, location of stroke, functional status (disability), adherence to medication regimen, family history of mental illness, previous history of mental illness.

Psychosocial Variables Prior trauma exposure other than stroke, coping strategies, social support, comorbid depression, and anxiety.

Substance use Alcohol consumption, Cigarette smoking, and Khat chewing.

Operational definition and terms

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) A study participant is said to have PTSD symptoms if the score is ≥ 33 from the range of 0–80 on the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5)[55].

Co-morbidity is defined as the coincidental presence of two or more disorders in the same person without inferring a causal relation [56, 57].

Functional status evaluated by the modified Rankin Scale (MRS), a six-point ordinal scale of disability and 1 for death [30, 58]. It was categorised into good (mRS < 3) and poor (mRS ≥ 3) functional recovery [59–61].

Adherence was measured using the Medication Adherence Reporting Scale-5 (MARS-5), with a total possible score ranging from 5 to 25. Categorised as poor or good if the total score was less than 20 and greater than or equal to the mean score, respectively [62].

The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) was used to measure anxiety and depressive symptoms among patients with stroke Stroke Survivors. The tool has 14 items and two subscales. Anxiety subscale (HADS-A) and depression subscale (HADS-D). Patients who scored ≥ 8 for both anxiety and depression symptoms will be considered as having depression and anxiety symptoms, respectively [63].

Adaptive coping was assessed by using the Brief-COPE scale. The scale has 28 items and a score of 65 and above [64, 65].

Social support The social support level of study participants was assessed by using the Oslo 3 Social Support Scale (OSSS-3. It has a total sum score ranging from 3–14 points. Based on the total sum score, the level of social support was categorised as poor, moderate, and high social support with sum scores of 3–8,9–11, and 12–14, respectively [66].

Current substance use using at least one of a specific substance for non-medical purposes within the last three months (alcohol, khat, tobacco, and others like cannabis) [67].

Ever use of a substance using at least one of any specific substance for a non-medical purpose at least once in a lifetime (alcohol, khat, and tobacco, others like cannabis) [67].

Data collection tools, procedures, and quality control

Data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire composed of socio-demographic, clinical, psychosocial, and substance use sections. The instruments included the Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5), the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), the Modified Rankin Scale (mRS), the Medication Adherence Report Scale (MARS-5), the Brief COPE Inventory, and the Oslo 3-Item Social Support Scale (OSSS-3 The instruments were translated into Amharic following the WHO recommended steps for cross-cultural adaptation, which included forward translation, expert panel review, backward translation, and pretesting among 16 stroke survivors. The PCL-5, although not yet formally validated among Ethiopian stroke survivors, has been semantically validated and used in studies of PTSD related to war, road traffic accidents, and interpersonal violence in Ethiopia. Internal consistency coefficients in the current study demonstrated good reliability. Four BSc nurses, one health officer, and two psychiatry nurses were trained for this task and collected the data using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire using Kobo Collect version 2024.1.3 (because most of the respondents were unable to read and write). The data collection was supervised by One General practitioner (FHCSH), one neurology resident (GUCSH), and one BSC nurse (TGCSH).

Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms were measured using the Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5), comprising the 20 PTSD symptoms (criteria B, C, and D) to measure PTSD. The total score is calculated by summing 20 items, and the possible scores range from 0 to 80 on the Likert scale of 5 points (0 = Not at all, 1 = A little bit, 2 = moderately, 3 = Quite a bit, 4 = extremely) with a cutoff point of ≥ 33. With a sensitivity of 74.5% and a specificity of 70.6%, the validity and reliability of the PCL-5 have been tested and proven on external factors of PTSD, like displaced people and refugees in several countries, and this tool was used in a study done in Ethiopia previously [68]. The reliability of the PCL-5 was tested in our study, which was 89.9% (α = 0.899).

Functional status evaluated by the modified Rankin Scale (MRS), a six-point ordinal scale of disability and 1 for death [30, 58]: 0 = no symptom at all; 1 = no significant disability despite symptoms, able to carry out all usual duties and activities; 2 = slight disability, unable to carry out all previous activities but able to look after own affairs without assistance; 3 = moderate disability, requires some help but able to walk without assistance; 4 = moderately severe disability, unable to walk without assistance and unable to attend to own bodily needs without assistance; 5 = severe disability, bedridden, incontinent, and requiring constant nursing care and attention; and 6 = dead and measures the degree of disability or dependence in everyday life, including instrumental and basic activities of daily living. MRS6 will not be considered in this analysis because the present study focuses on the disability among stroke survivors. It was categorised into good (mRS < 3) and poor (mRS ≥ 3) functional recovery [59–61].

For medication adherence (MA) assessment, we used the medication adherence reporting scale-5 (MARS-5), which is a validated tool that has been used in Ethiopia to assess medication adherence for different chronic diseases, including diabetes mellitus [69]. The MARS-5 has demonstrated acceptable internal consistency with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.77 for measuring medication adherence [70]. It contains 5 items and each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (always) to 5 (never). The range of the MARS-5 tool’s total score is between 5 and 25. Categorised as poor or good if the total score was less than 20 and greater than or equal to the mean score, respectively [62].In this study, its internal validity was good, with its reliability statistics being Cronbach’s α = 0.701.

The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) Anxiety and depressive symptoms were measured using HADS, which comprises 14 items divided into two subscales: HADS-A (anxiety, 7 items) and HADS-D (depression, 7 items). Items are rated on a four-point Likert scale (0 = Not at all, 3 = Most of the time), with subscale scores ranging from 0 to 21. Scores ≥ 8 on either subscale indicate the presence of anxiety or depressive symptoms. The scale was administered by trained interviewers to ensure comprehension among participants with limited literacy, with supervision by senior clinical staff. Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.78 for HADS-A, 0.76 for HADS-D, and 0.87 for the total scale, indicating good reliability [63].

The coping strategy was/were assessed by using the Brief-COPE scale. The scale has 28 items that assess the degree to which a participant utilises a specific coping strategy. The 28 items are categorised into 14 coping strategies. Participants use the inventory score themselves from 1 to 4, with 1 being “I have not been doing this at all” and 4 being “I have been doing this a lot”. A higher score applies when the participant gives a response of 3 or 4 for the given statement, and the individual is considered as strongly using the specific coping strategy as one of his or her core coping strategies. If an individual scores low, with a 1 or 2, then we could not consider it as one of his or her core coping strategies [71]. In validation studies, the Brief COPE Scale was found to have reasonable reliability and validity. It was used to assess coping styles for mental illness in our country. In this study, the alpha value was 0.857. The Brief COPE scores scale was computed with no reversals of coding, and it includes self-distraction, active coping, denial, substance use, use of emotional support, use of instrumental support, behavioural disengagement, venting, positive reframing, planning, humour, acceptance, religion, and self-blame coping strategies [64, 65]. The reliability of the PCL-5 was tested in our study, which was 86.5% (α = 0.865).

Social support The social support level of study participants was assessed by using the Oslo 3 Social Support Scale (OSSS-3). The tool has three-item questions collectively used to measure the accessibility of support patients receive from their family, friends, and neighbours if needed. It has a total sum score ranging from 3–14 points. Based on the total sum score, the level of social support was categorised as poor, moderate, and high social support with sum scores of 3–8,9–11, and 12–14, respectively, with the internal consistency could be regarded as acceptable with α = 0.640 [66]. The reliability of the OSSS-3 had been tested in our study, which was 64.9% (α = 0.649).

Data processing and analysis methods

The data were exported from the Kobo Collect V2024.1.3 software package to IBM SPSS Statistics version 27 software for analysis.

The descriptive analyses of data were indicated using numerical summary measures, and the normal distribution of the studied variables was checked, showing that they were normally distributed. The data were presented as frequency tables, figures, and graphs.

The full model was significant (χ2 [8] = 109.27; P < 0.001, the null hypothesis is rejected (p < 0.05)), The model fitness was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (p = 0.906), the model has also good fit since the test could not reject the hypothesis of model appropriateness indicating that the model distinguishes successfully between patients with and without PTSD symptoms. The model explained between 39.6% (Cox and Snell R2) and 54.1% (Nagelkerke R2) in the variance of PTSD symptoms occurrence and correctly classified 75.8% of cases, and multicollinearity was evaluated for its absence or minimal presence, and it satisfied our assumptions (Tolerance = 1/VIF > 10%).

A binary logistic regression model was used to see the association between the explanatory and outcome variables. Henceforth, multivariable logistic regression analysis was employed by selecting only variables with a P value < 0.25 in the bivariate analysis. The odds ratio with 95% CI was used to measure the strength of association between dependent and independent variables at a P-value < 0.05 to determine the level of statistical significance [72].

Results

Socio‑demographic characteristics of participants

A total of 310 stroke survivors were included in the study. The mean age of participants was 60.6 ± 14.9 years. More than half (57.4%) were men, and the majority (71.6%) identified as Orthodox Christian. About two-thirds (67.1%) of the participants were married, and 55.8% had no formal education. Regarding occupation, one-third (33.9%) were farmers, followed by merchants (30.3%) and government employees (20.0%). Over half (57.1%) resided in urban areas, and nearly all (90.0%) were of Amhara ethnicity. These findings indicate that the majority of stroke survivors in this study were older adults with limited formal education and predominantly from urban areas (Table 1).

The prevalence of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms among stroke survivors

The prevalence of post-traumatic stress disorder among participants was 37.1% (n = 115; 95% CI = 31.2–42.6%), the mean score of PCL-5 was 29.75 ± 13.28.with an estimated prevalence of 36.5% (n = 42), and 63.5% ( n = 73) for women, and men respectively.

Factors associated with PTSD symptoms among stroke survivors

In the bivariate analysis, factors associated with the outcome included gender, marital status, level of education, stroke duration, stroke location, stroke-related functional status, recurrent stroke, medication adherence, anxiety symptoms, coping strategy, and social support. Those variables, significantly associated with the outcome variable in the bivariate analysis, were included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis. Multiple binary logistic regression analyses, the following variables, namely, women’s gender, no formal education, long duration of a living stroke, poor functional status, recurrent stroke, having anxiety Symptoms, and poor coping strategy, remained statistically associated with PTSD symptoms. After controlling for the effect of the other confounding variables, women have 3.92 times higher odds of the outcome compared to men [AOR = 3.92 (95% CI 2.05–7.47, P = 0.000)]. Regarding the level of education, participants who have no formal education have 2.91 times the odds of outcome variables compared to college, and above an educational level ([AOR = 2.91 (95% CI 1.27–6.63, p = 0.011)].

Duration of stroke, after controlling the effect of other confounding variables, and taking less than 1 year as reference group–5 years, and greater than 5 years, have 2.24 times, and 5.13 times higher odds of the outcome variables compared to less than 1 years [ (AOR = 2.24, (95% CI 1.12–4.45, P = 0.022), and (AOR = 5.12, (95% CI 1.95–13.45, P = 0.001)] respectively. The functional status (disability) of participants, after adjusting for other confounding variables, showed that those with poor functional status are 3.83 times more likely to experience the outcome compared to good functioning respondents [AOR = 3.83 (95% CI 1.98–7.41), P = 0.000].In recurrent stroke, after controlling for the effect of the confounding factors, participants who have a recurrent stroke have 2.01 times higher odds of the outcome compared to no recurrence [AOR = 2.01 (95% CI 1.04–3.89) P = 0.040].

Participants who have anxiety symptoms have 2.51 times the likelihood of the outcome (PTSD) compared to those with no anxiety symptoms [AOR = 2.51 (95% CI 1.31–4.81) P = 0.006].

Discussions

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study in Ethiopia to investigate the prevalence and associated factors of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms among stroke survivors. The study, conducted from October to December 2024 in Northwest Ethiopia, found a PTSD symptom prevalence of 37.1% (95% CI 31.2–42.6%). This rate is more than ten times higher than the general population lifetime prevalence of 3.9% reported for PTSD from other traumatic causes [34, 73]. The finding aligns with studies from the University of South Florida, USA (35%) [74], and the University of Oldenburg, Germany (40%) [28], but exceeds the pooled global prevalence of 16.5% reported in meta-analytic reviews [75], and the other individual studies range from 6.5%–30% were identified in [3, 14, 21, 25, 30, 38, 43, 76]. Variations across studies may reflect differences in methodology, assessment tools, sample characteristics, and sociocultural contexts [3]. The elevated prevalence in Northwest Ethiopia may reflect contextual factors, including limited access to post-stroke rehabilitation, socioeconomic stressors, and weaker psychosocial support systems [31].

After controlling for other variables, gender, educational level, duration of stroke, functional status, stroke recurrence, anxiety symptoms, and stress coping strategy (COPE_M) remained statistically associated with PTSD symptoms.

Women were nearly four times more likely to experience PTSD symptoms than men, even after controlling for the effect of other covariates. This pattern is consistent with findings from studies in New York, USA [21], China [26], and other global evidence [58–60]. Biological factors (e.g., hormonal modulation of stress reactivity) [61, 77], social factors (e.g., higher exposure to interpersonal trauma) [78], and psychological tendencies toward emotion-focused coping may explain this vulnerability. Emotion-focused strategies such as denial or avoidance offer short-term relief but may impede long-term recovery, increasing PTSD risk [79].

Stroke survivors with no formal education had nearly threefold higher odds of PTSD symptoms compared to those with college-level education or higher. This finding echoes evidence from New York [21]. Lower educational attainment often correlates with low income, limited access to healthcare, and poor health literacy, which collectively increase vulnerability to psychological distress [80–82]. Moreover, individuals with limited education may rely on maladaptive coping styles and negative cognitive appraisals, further heightening PTSD risk [3, 81].

Stroke survivors who experienced recurrent strokes were two times more likely to develop posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms compared to those with a first-time stroke. This finding aligns with studies conducted in New York, USA [21, 83]. The heightened risk may stem from the cumulative psychological impact of repeated cerebrovascular events, which intensify feelings of fear, helplessness, and uncertainty. Each recurrent stroke not only reinforces traumatic memories but also exacerbates physical and cognitive impairments, prolonging recovery and diminishing a sense of control. These factors collectively contribute to persistent stress, hypervigilance, and intrusive recollections, core features of PTSD [54].

Stroke survivors with poor functional status were nearly four times more likely to experience posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms compared to those with better functional outcomes [43], New York, USA [21, 27], and in China [26]. Stroke survivors with greater functional impairment are more vulnerable to PTSD due to multiple interrelated mechanisms. Physical disability often entails loss of independence and autonomy, fostering feelings of helplessness and diminished control that heighten psychological distress. Moreover, functional limitations serve as constant reminders of the traumatic event, preventing emotional detachment and reinforcing trauma-related memories. Persistent challenges in mobility, self-care, and social or occupational functioning contribute to ongoing frustration and chronic stress, which may perpetuate PTSD symptoms [3, 21, 84, 85].

Stroke survivors with longer durations since the initial event showed a significantly higher likelihood of developing PTSD symptoms. Specifically, those who had lived with a stroke for one to five years were about two times more likely to exhibit PTSD symptoms, while those with a duration exceeding five years were approximately five times more likely to do so compared to individuals whose stroke duration was less than one year. This finding contrasts with a study conducted in New York, USA [25]. Which did not find a significant association between stroke duration and PTSD symptoms.

The higher odds observed in the present study may be explained by the chronic psychological burden associated with long-term physical disability and functional impairment. Prolonged exposure to such limitations can lead to sustained stress, maladaptive coping, and heightened psychological vulnerability. Furthermore, extended post-stroke periods often involve recurrent medical complications, fear of recurrence, and ongoing social or emotional difficulties, all of which may cumulatively intensify PTSD symptomatology. Previous reviews have also suggested a bidirectional relationship between PTSD and stroke, indicating that traumatic stress may both contribute to and result from cerebrovascular events [75, 84].

Another significant factor associated with the development of PTSD symptoms among stroke survivors was the presence of anxiety symptoms. Stroke survivors who experienced anxiety were more than twice as likely to exhibit PTSD symptoms compared to those without anxiety, even after controlling for potential confounding variables. This finding aligns with prior studies conducted among the German population [28, 76], in New York, USA [21], and at Columbia University, New York [38]. Similarly, a pilot study in the Chinese population [26] reported a significant association between anxiety and PTSD symptoms.

This relationship may be explained by the shared neurobiological and psychological mechanisms underlying both disorders. PTSD and anxiety disorders commonly involve dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and hyperactivity of the amygdala, which heighten physiological arousal and fear responses, predisposing individuals with anxiety to posttraumatic stress symptoms [27, 76, 83]. Furthermore, emotional vulnerability, characterised by increased sensitivity to stress and difficulty with emotional regulation, can amplify trauma-related distress. Cognitive distortions, such as catastrophizing and persistent negative appraisals, may further reinforce intrusive memories and hyperarousal. The coexistence of comorbid conditions can also worsen symptom persistence and functional impairment [86].

Participants exhibiting maladaptive stress coping styles (COPE_M) had approximately four times higher odds of developing PTSD symptoms compared to those employing adaptive coping strategies, after adjusting for potential confounders. This finding aligns with studies conducted at the University of Oldenburg, Germany [28], and Friedrich-Alexander University Erlangen-Nuremberg, Germany [76], which indicate that maladaptive coping mechanisms significantly contribute to the progression from stroke to PTSD. Specifically, strategies such as denial, substance use, and behavioural disengagement may intensify stress responses and impede adaptive emotional processing, thereby elevating the risk of PTSD among stroke survivors [87]. In contrast, adaptive coping strategies, including problem-solving and the utilisation of social support, appear to enhance effective stress regulation and emotional resilience, thereby mitigating susceptibility to PTSD in this population [88].

Strengths and limitations of the study

Strengths of the study

This study employed a relatively large sample size and used validated, standardised tools to assess PTSD symptoms and related factors among stroke survivors. The inclusion of multiple psychosocial and clinical variables allowed for comprehensive analysis. The findings provide valuable evidence for clinical and rehabilitation planning in the Ethiopian context.

Limitations of the study

This study has some limitations. The cross-sectional design restricts the ability to establish causal relationships between the identified factors and PTSD symptoms. The reliance on self-reported data introduces the possibility of recall or social desirability biases, particularly in sensitive areas such as mental health and substance use. Furthermore, the exclusion of individuals with communication difficulties or severe cognitive impairments may have omitted a subgroup with a potentially higher risk of PTSD, limiting the generalizability of the findings.

Future directions

Future research should explore longitudinal trajectories of PTSD among stroke survivors to determine causal and temporal relationships between stroke recovery and psychological outcomes. Additionally, intervention studies assessing the effectiveness of targeted psychosocial and behavioral therapies in resource-limited settings are warranted. Such research could inform integrated rehabilitation models that address both neurological and mental health needs of stroke survivors in low- and middle-income countries.

Conclusions

Based on the findings, approximately one in three stroke survivors experienced PTSD symptoms, a prevalence notably higher than that observed in populations exposed to external traumatic events. Several factors were significantly associated with PTSD symptoms, including female sex, lack of formal education, longer stroke duration, poor functional status patient with stroke, stroke recurrence, comorbid anxiety symptoms, and maladaptive coping strategies.

Female stroke survivors were found to be at greater risk of developing PTSD symptoms than their male counterparts. This may be attributed to biological vulnerability (e.g., hormonal and neuroendocrine differences influencing stress response), higher prevalence of internalising coping styles, and greater exposure to psychosocial stressors.

The study underscores the need for clinicians in neurology and rehabilitation settings to routinely screen for PTSD and anxiety symptoms, with particular attention to high-risk groups such as women, those with recurrent or long-standing strokes, and those with poor functional recovery. Strengthening social support systems, promoting adaptive coping mechanisms, and providing early psychiatric referrals are critical to improving mental health and overall quality of life among stroke survivors.
