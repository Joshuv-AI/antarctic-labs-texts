When Sufficient is not Enough: Utilizing the Rashomon Effect for Complete Evidence Extraction
Katharina Beckh, Stefan Rüping

Abstract
Feature attribution methods typically provide minimal sufficient evidence justifying a model decision. However, in many applications this is inadequate. For compliance and cataloging, the full set of contributing features must be identified – complete evidence. We perform a case study on a medical dataset which contains human-annotated complete evidence. We show that individual models typically recover only subsets of complete evidence and that aggregating evidence from several models improves evidence recall from ∼ \sim 0.60 (single best model) to ∼ \sim 0.86 (ensemble). We analyze the recall-precision trade-off, the role of training with evidence, dynamic ensembles with certainty thresholds, and discuss implications.



1
Introduction

Evidence extraction identifies input features that justify a model prediction. In most prior work, the focus is on minimal
sufficient
justifications
10
;
18
;
11
. However, in certain settings, such as regulatory compliance or billing, stakeholders need
complete
evidence which covers all supporting text spans.
This especially applies to scenarios with long texts, where relevant information is distributed across the document.

Completeness is essential for compliance and cataloging.
For example, standards and specifications may undergo revisions when new technologies emerge which are not captured yet by the rules in the existing standard document
16
;
1
. It is therefore necessary to identify all information to be modified. This also applies to compliance scenarios where texts must be revised to ensure conformity with new regulations.
Moreover, completeness matters when the amount of evidence influences decision-making.
For instance, in psychiatric care, the number and severity of indicators, such as self-harm and aggression, determine the need for hospitalization and impact billing
13
.

Figure 1:
Illustration of complete evidence extraction on the medical coding task. Model 1 identifies two relevant evidence spans in the input document, Model 2 one. Aggregating the evidence of several models leads to higher recall and, thus, more complete evidence.

Achieving completeness is challenging in practice because its goal diverges from the standard classification objective. For the latter, the goal is to find the determining factors to distinguish between different classes. Models latch on a set of discriminative features, recovering only a subset of relevant evidence.
Hence, a single model will not suffice to provide complete evidence.
The Rashomon effect implies that different models achieve similar performance while relying on distinct solution strategies
3
;
15
;
12
.
We use the effect to our advantage: if different models attend to different valid cues, then aggregating their evidence may increase coverage.

In this work, we empirically investigate complete evidence extraction using a medical dataset with human-annotated evidence (Fig.
1
).
We compare the evidence of single models to ensembles formed by aggregating evidence from multiple models. We examine the trade-off between recall and precision, the role of evidence-guided training, and whether limiting ensemble members by certainty helps in reducing precision loss.

2
Sufficient and Complete Evidence

Here, evidence refers to input tokens that support a model prediction.
Evidence does not necessarily provide insight into how a prediction was reached
17
which is why we use the more specific term evidence instead of explanation.

We adopt the distinction by
4
:
sufficient
evidence is any subset of text that, on its own, justifies a model prediction, whereas
complete
evidence is the exhaustive set of all text in the document that supports that prediction.
While the term completeness suggests a binary nature, we view completeness as a spectrum as to how much evidence is covered.
Most related work, especially in the field of explainable AI, focuses on sufficiency
10
;
11
;
18
. While the notion of
comprehensiveness

5
is similar, completeness does not assume that evidence removal leads to a reduction in model confidence.

3
Case Study: Evidence Extraction in Medical Coding

Due to the Rashomon effect, we expect that a single model only covers parts of all evidence.
In a case study, we confirm this phenomenon and empirically show that aggregating evidence from several models leads to more complete evidence.

We use a dataset from the medical domain which contains complete evidence annotated by human experts.
The setting is a classification task and evidence is extracted via a feature attribution method.
Completeness is seen as the set of all tokens in the input that justify the label and we use recall as the main evaluation measure.

We compare recall values of single models and ensembles.
It is explored whether using evidence only from models with high certainty leads to similar or higher recall.
To assess the impact of different training regimes, a comparison between regular and evidence-guided training is performed.

3.1
Task and Dataset

We consider the classification task of assigning medical codes to free-text clinical notes as the basis task and focus our analysis on the evidence extraction task, realized by retrieving text snippets that support a specific code.
MDACE
4
is a medical dataset based on MIMIC-III
7
;
8
. It contains electronic health records in the English language with diagnosis and procedure codes. Here, ICD-9 codes are utilized. For each code, evidence is provided in the form of annotated text spans in the clinical note. For example, code 416.8 (other pulmonary heart disease) has ‘pulmonary hypertension’ as one evidence span.

MDACE contains documents which are annotated in a sufficient style, and also a complete style, annotating
all
text that is relevant for a code. The complete subset has roughly three times more evidence compared to the sufficient subset.
Following established set-ups, discharge summaries serve as data basis and the sufficient evidence is used for train and validation purposes.
We follow the dataset split by
4
of 181 train, 60 validation, 61 test documents.

Where our work differs from most prior work is that we utilize the complete subset for test purposes. We use the subset that contains both sufficient and complete annotated evidence, with the same codes, which presents 44 test cases.
Since we are interested in cases with more than one evidence span, a filtering step is performed, yielding 17 final test cases.
Due to the small amount of data, we additionally use the whole test set, i.e., with sufficient evidence, to analyze model agreement as a rough estimate how reliable the results are.

3.2
Models

We rely on training approaches and existing model weights from

6
. The underlying architecture is a transformer, encoder-based architecture, trained on medical text and fine-tuned on MDACE. We use models with two different training regimes. In an unsupervised approach, no evidence annotations are used in the training, but input gradient regularization (IGR) with the aim to reduce the importance of irrelevant tokens.
In a supervised approach, human-annotated evidence spans were used in the training process to steer model attention to that evidence.
To avoid confusion with the more frequent meaning of supervised in a classification task, we use Evidence-Guided Training (EGT) to refer to this training approach.
For each approach 10 seeds from random initializations are available, leading to a total of 20 models.

Single models

The best model per approach, for IGR and EGT, is determined by computing per-sample winners.
Further, we report the maximum value score (max value) as upper limit to what a single model can retrieve.

Model ensembles

For each training approach, we build ensembles out of the 10 respective models.
As the standard ensemble, we are aggregating the evidence of all models, i.e., the union of extracted tokens.
To investigate the role of certainty, we create a dynamic ensemble only using evidence from models that have a probability above a certain threshold and manipulate the threshold values to observe the effect on the evaluation metrics.

3.3
Evidence Extraction with Feature Attribution

To retrieve evidence, a feature attribution method is employed. The method assigns each input feature a numerical value representing its importance with respect to the model prediction. We used AttInGrad which showed the highest faithfulness and plausibility metrics in prior work
6
;
2
.
AttInGrad is a combination of Attention and Input
\times
Grad where the attention weights are multiplied by scores derived from the L2 norm of the Input
\times
Grad attributions. With a decision threshold that is set based on a validation set, a list of tokens (or rather position-dependent token IDs) is obtained as model evidence.

3.4
Metrics

We assume the human-annotated evidence as ground truth and compute recall by using model evidence and human-annotated evidence.
Recall is the proportion of human-annotated tokens that appear in the model’s predicted tokens. Formally,

\text{recall}=\frac{\left|\text{set}(gt\_ids)\cap\text{set}(pred\_ids)\right|}{\left|\text{set}(gt\_ids)\right|}

where
gt\_ids
refers to the human annotated token IDs and
pred\_ids
to the predicted token IDs from the model.
To quantify the effect of adding models on the number of additional unwanted evidence tokens, we compute precision by dividing with the predicted token IDs.

\text{precision}=\frac{\left|\text{set}(gt\_ids)\cap\text{set}(pred\_ids)\right|}{\left|\text{set}(pred\_ids)\right|}

We measure agreement of models in an ensemble in the following way:

\text{agreement}=\frac{\frac{\sum_{t}c_{t}^{2}}{T}-1}{M-1}

where
c_{t}
refers to the occurrence count of a token ID
t
,
T
is the total number of token
-
occurrences across the ensemble and
M
the number of models.

Qualitative insights

When looking at specific data points, we find that EGT models focus more on clinically relevant tokens, whereas IGR models more frequently highlight spurious features, e.g., function words and punctuation.
In one case of low recall (0.18), the human-annotated evidence ‘glomus vagale tumor’ of code 237.3 (neoplasm of uncertain behavior of paraganglia) could not be reliably retrieved.
Although nearly all models identified semantically meaningful tokens ‘paragangli’, ‘vagus’, and subword fragment ‘omus’, none extracted the token ‘tumor’.
This suggests that the models did not attend to descriptive tokes, likely because ‘tumor’ occurs in many contexts and is not a discriminative feature.

We also observed under-annotation in the human-annotated spans: in several cases, the ensemble discovered valid supporting spans that were not present in the annotation, suggesting that the ground truth is imperfect and that ensemble recall may be underestimated (also see
9
). The loss in precision can be partly explained by missing annotations in the ground truth.

4
Conclusion

We investigated completeness in evidence extraction with the goal to recover all input features that support a model prediction. Our analysis demonstrates how the Rashomon effect can benefit complete evidence extraction. Aggregating evidence across model ensembles substantially increases evidence recall: one model alone cannot provide the same coverage. However, this gain comes with decreased precision. Evidence-guided training partially mitigates precision loss by reducing spurious correlations. Applying a certainty threshold may raise precision but reduces recall, suggesting that relevant evidence can appear in model predictions even when model confidence is not high.
A qualitative analysis showed that ensembles often retrieve semantically meaningful text that single models miss. These results imply that when exhaustive coverage is required, e.g. in compliance or cataloging, ensembles are a useful strategy to improve completeness, but systems must handle the resulting increase in false positives.
There is a need for more datasets with complete annotations to better evaluate completeness. The models used here derive from different random initializations; to better exploit predictive multiplicity, increasing model diversity during training is a promising direction
14
. Future work can apply diversity training to directly optimize for completeness.

Limitations

Our analysis is limited to a small sample size and includes one feature attribution method. There is a lack of data with complete evidence annotations, which limits the generalizability of the results.
By using feature attribution methods, we acknowledge that the extracted evidence may not completely represent model mechanisms, since these methods only approximate model behavior. For this reason, we selected a feature attribution method with high faithfulness scores.
From a resource perspective, the ensemble approach leads to additional compute requirements.
In our analysis, the models are small and do not require substantial GPU resources. For other model types, it should be carefully considered whether the need for complete evidence warrants the additional resources.
The term completeness can be misleading and we caution against treating it in a literal sense. What counts as evidence is dependent on the use case, context, and subjective preferences. In this analysis, we adopted the term from prior work to ensure consistency, but for other tasks, another wording may be more appropriate.
