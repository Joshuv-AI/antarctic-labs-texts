Reliable fairness auditing with semi-supervised inference
Jianhui Gao, Jessica Gronsbell

Abstract
Machine learning (ML) models often exhibit bias that can exacerbate inequities in biomedical applications. Fairness auditing, the process of evaluating a model’s performance across subpopulations, is critical for identifying and mitigating these biases. However, such audits typically rely on large volumes of labeled data, which are costly and labor-intensive to obtain. To address this challenge, we introduce Infairness , a unified framework for auditing a wide range of fairness criteria using semi-supervised inference. Our approach combines a small labeled dataset with a large unlabeled dataset by imputing missing outcomes via regression with carefully selected nonlinear basis functions. We show that our proposed estimator is (i) consistent regardless of whether the ML or imputation models are correctly specified and (ii) more efficient than standard supervised estimation with the labeled data when the imputation model is correctly specified. Through extensive simulations, we also demonstrate that Infairness consistently achieves higher precision than supervised estimation. In a real-world application of phenotyping depression from electronic health records data, Infairness reduces variance by up to 64% compared to supervised estimation, underscoring its value for reliable fairness auditing with limited labeled data.



Keywords:
Group fairness; Machine learning; Missing data; Semi-supervised inference

1
Introduction

Machine learning (ML) models are used across healthcare and biomedical research to improve patient outcomes, enhance diagnostic accuracy, and optimize treatment effectiveness
(
Rajpurkar et al. 2022
)
. For example, state-of-the-art computer vision models have achieved radiologist-level performance in identifying pneumonia from chest X-rays, demonstrating potential to support diagnosis in resource-limited settings
(
Rajpurkar et al. 2017
;
Khanna et al. 2021
;
Hipolito Canario et al. 2022
)
. Similarly, large language models applied to electronic health record (EHR) data are accelerating recruitment for clinical trials, making the process more efficient and improving the generalizability of findings to real-world populations
(
Yan et al. 2024
;
Jin et al. 2024
)
.

Progress notwithstanding, mounting evidence indicates that ML models can be
unfair
by making more accurate predictions for particular individuals or groups. A systematic review of computer vision models across diverse imaging modalities showed that nearly all studies published between 2019 and 2023 that evaluated model performance across groups defined by protected attributes, such as age, sex, and race, reported performance disparities
(
Xu et al. 2024
)
. Likewise, a benchmarking study of widely used EHR phenotyping algorithms for pneumonia and sepsis identified significant variability in diagnostic performance across subpopulations defined by gender, race, and ethnicity
(
Ding et al. 2024
)
. This phenomenon poses a serious threat to ML-based biomedical applications as gaps in model fairness can lead to the reinforcement of structural inequity without proper foresight
(
Gao et al. 2024
)
.

In the context of decision-making, unfairness is more formally defined as the presence “of any prejudice or favoritism toward an individual or group based on their inherent or acquired characteristics”
(
Mehrabi et al. 2021
)
. Concepts of fairness have been studied across disciplines such as philosophy, economics, law, and, more recently, ML
(
Dwork et al. 2012
;
Hutchinson and Mitchell 2019
)
. While numerous frameworks have been introduced to quantify fairness,
group fairness
is most widely used in biomedical applications
(
Rajkomar et al. 2018
;
Gao et al. 2024
)
. Group fairness requires that an ML model performs similarly in terms of accuracy, discrimination, or calibration across groups defined by protected attributes
(
Hardt et al. 2016
)
. Researchers generally audit group fairness at various stages of the model development pipeline as part of broader efforts to identify and mitigate disparate performance
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
.

However, a significant practical barrier in auditing group fairness is obtaining sufficient gold-standard labeled data to evaluate subgroup performance
(
Humbert-Droz et al. 2022
;
Gangwal et al. 2024
)
. Outcome labeling is especially time-intensive and costly in biomedical applications as it requires specialized expertise
(
Velupillai et al. 2018
;
Xiao et al. 2018
)
. For example, it can take a trained annotator between 2 and 15 minutes to label a medical image, whereas commonplace images, such as those within ImageNet, can be labeled at a rate of two images per second through crowdsourcing
(
Li 2010
;
Do et al. 2020
;
Bangert et al. 2021
)
. In the analysis of EHR data, labeling a single discharge summary can take up to 10 minutes and often involves multiple clinicians to ensure consensus
(
Gehrmann et al. 2018
)
. The labeling burden results in small gold-standard datasets for fairness auditing, which limits estimation precision and increases the risk of missing important fairness gaps.

To improve estimation efficiency without increasing labeling demands, semi-supervised (SS) inference has emerged as a powerful framework to accommodate such settings where labeled data is difficult to obtain, but unlabeled data is plentiful
(
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Chakrabortty et al. 2022
;
Zhang and Bradic 2022
;
Song et al. 2023
;
Cai et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. While SS methods have been extensively studied for prediction tasks since the 1970s, a particularly under-explored area is the use of SS inference to improve model performance evaluation
(
van Engelen and Hoos 2020
;
Xu et al. 2024
)
.
Gronsbell and Cai 2018
introduced the first method for SS estimation of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and later proposed a method for estimation of the misclassification rate and model calibration
(
Gronsbell et al. 2022
)
. Similar approaches for overall model performance evaluation have also been proposed in the related areas of transfer learning and weakly-supervised learning
(
Gao et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Kiyasseh et al. 2024
)
. Within the context of fairness auditing, however, only one SS method currently exists.
Ji et al. 2020
proposed a Bayesian approach to estimate a small number of discrimination-based group fairness metrics, such as the difference in true positive rates across groups. Nonetheless, the validity of this method relies on strong parametric assumptions that rarely hold in practice and that, when violated, can lead to bias and variance inflation relative to standard supervised estimation using only labeled data. Outside of the SS literature,
Miller et al. 2021
introduced a model-based approach that improves subgroup estimates of various accuracy measures by pooling information across the entire sample. Later,
van Breugel et al. 2023
used deep generative models to simulate validation data in order to stabilize subgroup performance estimates. While these methods address the related challenge of limited subgroup data, they do not make use of abundant unlabeled data, which naturally arises in many biomedical applications.

To fill the current methodological gap in SS methods for fairness auditing, we introduce a unified framework, which we coin
Infairness
, that enables precise estimation of a wide variety of commonly used group fairness metrics. We propose to impute the missing outcomes in the unlabeled data based on a regression model with carefully selected nonlinear basis functions. The imputation model is trained with the labeled data and the imputed outcomes are then used to evaluate subgroup performance for fairness auditing. Importantly, we show that the Infairness estimator is (i) consistent regardless of the specification of the ML or imputation model and (ii) guaranteed to achieve lower variance than supervised estimation with the labeled data when the imputation model is correctly specified. We also introduce asymptotic inference procedures and evaluate our method through extensive simulation studies and a real-world fairness audit using EHR data.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. Section
2
formally introduces the problem set-up and the parameters of interest. Section
3
details the Infairness method. Section
4
presents our theoretical results, including the asymptotic distribution of our proposed estimator. Section
5
includes simulation studies that demonstrate our framework’s efficiency across a broad range of settings. Section
6
includes an application of our method to fairness auditing of an EHR-based phenotyping model for depression. We conclude in Section
7
with a discussion of the implications of our work and future research directions.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Notation and problem set-up

We consider an ML model for predicting a binary outcome, denoted as
Y
. As our focus is on group fairness, we aim to evaluate whether the performance of the model differs across groups defined by a categorical protected attribute, denoted as
A
. The ML model is trained on the basis of a set of features,
\mathbf{X}
, which may or may not include
A
.
\mathbf{X}
need not be a vector and may be an image, text, tensor, or other type of data. During validation, a vector of auxiliary covariates,
\mathbf{W}=(W_{1},\dots,W_{p})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}
, may also be available for analysis. For example, in medical imaging or EHR applications,
Y
may be a disease of interest,
\mathbf{X}
a chest X-ray or discharge summary, and
\mathbf{W}
a set of additional protected attributes or clinical variables not utilized during model training. We will utilize
\mathbf{W}
to improve the efficiency of group fairness auditing in Section
3.2
.

With a slight abuse of notation, the validation data therefore consists of a
labeled
dataset with
n
independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples,
\{(Y_{i},\mathbf{X}_{i}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}},\mathbf{W}_{i}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}},A_{i})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\mid i=1,...,n\}
, together with an independent
unlabeled
dataset with
N
i.i.d. realizations of

\{(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}},\mathbf{W}_{i}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}},A_{i})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\mid i=n+1,...,n+N\}
. We focus on the classical SS setting in which it is assumed that (i) the labeled and unlabeled data arise from the same underlying distribution (or equivalently that
Y
is missing completely at random) and (ii)
n/N\to 0
as
n\to\infty

(
Chakrabortty and Cai 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
. This setting occurs when an investigator has access to a large set of unlabeled data and a limited budget is available to select samples uniformly at random from the underlying data for labeling, such as in the medical imaging or EHR examples from Section
1
.

To ensure that group-specific performance metrics can be estimated, we further assume that there is sufficient labeled and unlabeled data within each of the groups defined by
A
. To this end, let
n_{a}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}I(A_{i}=a)
and
N_{a}=\sum_{i=n+1}^{n+N}I(A_{i}=a)
denote the number of labeled and unlabeled observations in the group with
A=a
, respectively. We assume that
n_{a}/n\to\rho_{a}\in(0,1)
as
n\to\infty
and
N_{a}/N\to\rho_{a}\in(0,1)
as
N\to\infty
for each
a
. These assumptions, together with assumption (ii), imply that
n_{a}/N_{a}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

We make no assumptions on the ML model, denoted as
\hat{f}
, other than that it is trained on a dataset,
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{train}}
, that is independent of the validation dataset. The validity of our method does not require the ML model to approximate the underlying regression function (i.e., the model is correctly specified for
\mathbb{E}(Y\mid\mathbf{X})
). In practice, models are commonly trained with weakly-supervised or semi-supervised methods that accommodate scenarios with scarce labeled data or simply use pre-trained models, such as large language models or computer vision algorithms
(
Yu et al. 2018
;
Seyyed-Kalantari et al. 2021b
, e.g.,)
. The predictions of
Y
are denoted as
S=\hat{f}(\mathbf{X})
and are available for all
(n+N)
observations in the validation data. We obtain the final classification for
Y
as
D=I(S\geq c)
for some pre-specified threshold
c\in(0,1)
.

2.2
Parameter of interest

To quantify the performance of the trained ML model in predicting
Y
within groups defined by
A
, we consider a wide range of group fairness metrics based on accuracy, discrimination, and calibration. Table
1
summarizes common group-specific metrics that measure model performance within each group with
A=a
, denoted generally as
\mathcal{M}_{a}
. For example, the group-specific true positive rate (TPR) is defined as

\textrm{TPR}_{a}\coloneq{P}\left(D=1\mid Y=1,A=a\right).

Note that the probability is taken over the distribution of
(Y,\mathbf{X}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}},A)^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}
conditioning on
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{train}}
. To simplify notation, we omit explicit conditioning on
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{train}}
, with the understanding that all target parameters and corresponding inference procedures are defined conditionally on the training data. To further unify notation for the various group-specific metrics, we define

\mu_{a}^{Z}=\mathbb{E}(Z\mid A=a)

for a random variable
Z
. For example, the group-specific TPR can be expressed as

\textrm{TPR}_{a}=\frac{\mu_{a}^{DY}}{\mu_{a}^{Y}}.

Existing work within the fairness literature primarily focuses on comparing group-specific metrics across two groups defined by a binary protected attribute
(
Mehrabi et al. 2021
;
Gao et al. 2024
)
. We similarly focus on the two-group setting and discuss extensions to the multi-group setting in Section
7
. In the two-group setting, group fairness criteria are most often defined in terms of the difference between the group-specific metrics, denoted as
\Delta_{\mathcal{M}}=\mathcal{M}_{0}-\mathcal{M}_{1}
and the goal is to make inference on
\Delta_{\mathcal{M}}
. Table
2
summarizes common group fairness criteria
(
Hardt et al. 2016
)
. Again using the TPR as an example,
\Delta_{\textrm{TPR}}=\textrm{TPR}_{0}-\textrm{TPR}_{1}
, and when
\Delta_{\textrm{TPR}}=0
the model is said to satisfy the
equal opportunity
criterion. For exposition, we use equal opportunity as a running example in introducing our proposal in Section
3
.

3
Methods

3.1
Review of supervised inference for group fairness auditing

To motivate our estimation procedure, we begin by reviewing the standard approach to evaluating group fairness using only the labeled data. We refer to this approach as supervised inference, since the unlabeled data is not utilized for estimation. Constructing supervised estimators for the metrics in Table
1
requires estimating
\mu_{a}^{Z}
for
Z\in\{Y,D,S^{2},SY,DY\}
within the labeled data as
n_{a}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}I(A_{i}=a)
. Supervised estimators for
\mathcal{M}_{a}
and
\Delta_{\mathcal{M}}
are then obtained by substituting the estimator of
\mu_{a}^{Z}
into the formulas provided in Table
1
. For example, the supervised estimator of
\Delta_{\textrm{TPR}}
is given by

\widehat{\Delta}^{\text{\tiny{SUP}}}_{\textrm{TPR}}\coloneq\widehat{\textrm{TPR}}^{\text{\tiny{SUP}}}_{0}-\widehat{\textrm{TPR}}^{\text{\tiny{SUP}}}_{1}=\frac{n_{0}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}Y_{i}I(A_{i}=0)}{n_{0}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}I(A_{i}=0)}-\frac{n_{1}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}Y_{i}I(A_{i}=1)}{n_{1}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}I(A_{i}=1)}.

To make inference on
\Delta_{\textrm{TPR}}
, large sample confidence intervals can be constructed based on the asymptotic normality of
\widehat{\Delta}^{\text{\tiny{SUP}}}_{\textrm{TPR}}
using variance estimates derived from the influence functions provided in Theorem S1 in the Supplementary Material.

3.2
Infairness: Proposed SS inference method for group fairness auditing

The key distinction between the SS and supervised approaches to inference is that the SS approach uses the large unlabeled dataset alongside the small labeled dataset to improve estimation efficiency. Intuitively, the efficiency gain arises from exploiting the abundant unlabeled data within each group defined by
A
, which provides near-complete information on the joint distribution of the features,
\mathbf{X}
, and the auxiliary covariates,
\mathbf{W}
. We begin by providing an overview of the general framework of our proposed SS method, which we call
Infairness
, building on this core intuition.

As in the supervised setting, constructing SS estimators for the group fairness metrics in Table
1
requires estimation of
\mu_{a}^{Z}
for
Z\in\{Y,D,S^{2},SY,DY\}
. The Infairness estimators for
\mathcal{M}_{a}
and
\Delta_{\mathcal{M}}
are obtained by substituting the SS estimators of
\mu_{a}^{Z}
into the formulas provided in Table
1
. To this end, we consider two cases. First, consider
\mu_{a}^{Z}
for
Z\in\{Y,SY,DY\}
as these functionals depend on
Y
, which is only available in the labeled data. To illustrate the potential utility of the unlabeled data in estimation, note that
\mu_{a}^{Z}\coloneq\mathbb{E}(Z\mid A=a)
can be rewritten as

\mu_{a}^{Z}=\mathbb{E}\{\phi(Z)\mathbb{E}(Y\mid S,\mathbf{W},A=a)\mid A=a\}\quad\mbox{where}\quad\phi(Z)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }Z=Y\\
S&\text{if }Z=SY\\
D&\text{if }Z=DY.\end{cases}

(1)

The expression in (
1
) highlights that
\mu_{a}^{Z}
inherently depends on the distribution of
(S,\mathbf{W}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\mid A=a
and hence estimation may be improved by making careful use of the unlabeled data. Moreover, a natural SS estimator can be obtained by constructing the empirical analogue of (
1
). Specifically, we propose to estimate
\mu_{a}^{Z}
by (i) imputing the missing
Y
with an estimate of
\mathbb{E}(Y\mid S,\mathbf{W},A=a)
learned from the labeled data and (ii) averaging the resulting imputations over the unlabeled data in the group with
A=a
. The potential efficiency gain relative to supervised estimation hinges on the quality of the imputation in step (i) and is driven by step (ii), which leverages the abundant unlabeled data. We detail our imputation strategy in the subsequent sections.

In contrast, for the second case with
Z\in\{D,S^{2}\}
,
\mu_{a}^{Z}
does not depend on
Y
and therefore estimation does not require imputation. We can simply obtain estimators for
\mu_{a}^{Z}
with the corresponding empirical averages in the unlabeled data. Similar to the imputation-based SS estimators, these estimators offer improved precision relative to their supervised counterparts due to averaging over a much larger sample size.

3.2.1
Imputation strategy

In the context of estimating overall (rather than group-specific) performance metrics in the SS setting without auxiliary covariates,
Gronsbell and Cai 2018
proposed a nonparametric imputation approach using kernel smoothing over
S
within the labeled dataset. Smoothing over
S
, rather than relying directly on its observed values, yields estimators that are consistent for the target performance metric even under ML model misspecification (i.e., when
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X})
does not approximate
\mathbb{E}(Y\mid\mathbf{X})
). However, in the current setting, smoothing is not a viable option due to (i) the potentially limited size of group-specific labeled samples and (ii) the objective of incorporating auxiliary covariates
\mathbf{W}
into the imputation model. Including
\mathbf{W}
can more effectively harness the unlabeled data as
\mathbf{W}
may contain additional information about
Y
beyond what is captured by
S
. Moreover, auxiliary covariates are often readily available in biomedical applications. For instance, pre-trained models are increasingly used to generate disease predictions from medical images and free-text EHR data, and fairness audits often span multiple protected attributes that are not utilized for training
(
Seyyed-Kalantari et al. 2021a
;
Miller et al. 2021
;
Bedi et al. 2024
)
. Protected attributes not under investigation in a given audit provide natural candidates for
\mathbf{W}
and enable the model to adjust for potential differences across subpopulations to further improve the quality of the imputation for
Y
.

More specifically, we propose to impute
Y
by fitting a
working
regression model

\mathbb{E}(Y\mid S,\mathbf{W},A=a)=g\left(\bm{\theta}_{a}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\mathcal{B}_{a}\right)

(2)

where
\mathcal{B}_{a}=\mathcal{B}_{a}(S,\mathbf{W})
is a finite set of basis functions of fixed dimension that includes an intercept, and
g(\cdot):\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
is a specified smooth monotone function such as the expit function. We detail the choice of basis in Section
3.2.2
. We propose to estimate
\bm{\theta}_{a}
from the labeled data with
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{a}
, which is the solution to

n_{a}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{B}_{a,i}\left\{Y_{i}-g\left(\bm{\theta}_{a}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\mathcal{B}_{a,i}\right)\right\}I(A_{i}=a)-\lambda_{n_{a}}\bm{\theta}_{a}=\bm{0}

(3)

and
\lambda_{n_{a}}=o\left(n_{a}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)
is a penalty term used to stabilize model fitting. We show in Supplementary Section S.2.3.1 that
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{a}
is a consistent estimator of
\overline{\bm{\theta}}_{a}
, defined as the solution to
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{B}_{a}\{Y-g(\bm{\theta}_{a}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\mathcal{B}_{a})\}I(A=a)]=\bm{0}
.

The imputations are computed as
\widehat{m}_{a}=g(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{a}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\mathcal{B}_{a})
within the unlabeled data and used to evaluate the fairness criteria. More specifically, using our running example of equal opportunity, the Infairness estimator of
\Delta_{\textrm{TPR}}
is:

\widehat{\Delta}^{\text{\tiny{SS}}}_{\textrm{TPR}}=\widehat{\textrm{TPR}}^{\text{\tiny{SS}}}_{0}-\widehat{\textrm{TPR}}^{\text{\tiny{SS}}}_{1}=\frac{N_{0}^{-1}\sum_{i=n+1}^{n+N}D_{i}\widehat{m}_{0,i}I(A_{i}=0)}{N_{0}^{-1}\sum_{i=n+1}^{n+N}\widehat{m}_{0,i}I(A_{i}=0)}-\frac{N_{1}^{-1}\sum_{i=n+1}^{n+N}D_{i}\widehat{m}_{1,i}I(A_{i}=1)}{N_{1}^{-1}\sum_{i=n+1}^{n+N}\widehat{m}_{1,i}I(A_{i}=1)}.

Similar to supervised estimation, large sample confidence intervals for
\Delta_{\textrm{TPR}}
can be constructed based on the asymptotic normality of
\widehat{\Delta}^{\text{\tiny{SS}}}_{\textrm{TPR}}
using variance estimates derived from the asymptotic linear expansion provided in Theorem
1
.

3.2.2
Basis construction

The basis utilized for the imputation model in equation (
2
) must be carefully constructed to ensure that the resulting estimator is both consistent for the given fairness metric and more efficient than its supervised counterpart. With respect to the former, a sufficient condition for the consistency of the fairness metrics is that the SS estimators of
\mu_{a}^{Z}
are consistent for their targets. For
Z\in\{Y,SY,DY\}
,
\mu_{a}^{Z}
depends on
Y
and the corresponding SS estimators are based on the imputations for
Y
. We therefore require that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{(1,D,S)^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}(Y-\widehat{m}_{a})I(A=a)\mid\widehat{m}_{a}\}\overset{p}{\to}{\bf{0}}\mbox{ as }n_{a}\to\infty.

(4)

While this condition is satisfied if the working model in equation (
2
) is correctly specified, the functional form of
\mathbb{E}(Y\mid S,\mathbf{W},A=a)
is essentially always misspecified in practice. We can, however, ensure that the condition in equation (
4
) is satisfied by simply including an intercept,
S
, and
D
in the basis as
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{a}
is derived from the estimating equation in (
3
).

With respect to efficiency, the goal in selecting the basis is to closely approximate
\mathbb{E}(Y\mid S,\mathbf{W},A=a)
in order to glean as much information as possible from the unlabeled data. In the context of regression modeling in the SS setting,
Song et al. 2023
showed that it is infeasible to
find the theoretically optimal basis in practice. The authors instead propose to utilize a polynomial basis with the order selected by optimizing an extension of the generalized Bayesian information criterion
(
Lv and Liu 2014
)
. This approach works well in our empirical studies and we provide technical details in Supplementary Section S1.

To generate

6
Real data analysis

We next apply our method to audit a phenotyping model developed with EHR data. Identifying patient phenotypes (e.g., diseases) is a fundamental step in EHR-based research as phenotypes define cohorts for a range of downstream applications, including epidemiological studies, comparative effectiveness research, and disease risk prediction
(
Yang et al. 2023
)
. Because these cohorts inform clinical insights and decision-making, it is essential that they adequately represent the underlying patient population, irrespective of protected attributes. However, phenotyping model performance is rarely comprehensively evaluated (e.g., only overall PPV is assessed). This gap is largely due to the scarcity of labeled data, as gold-standard phenotype labels require manual chart review, which is both costly and time-consuming
(
Swerdel et al. 2022
;
Gao et al. 2024
)
. Consequently, fairness auditing of phenotyping models is just now beginning, with best practices starting to take shape in the literature
(
Sun et al. 2023
)
.

Here we focus on phenotyping depression based on the information in discharge summaries from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III (MIMIC-III), a task previously considered by
Gehrmann et al. 2018
. MIMIC-III is a publicly available database of de-identified EHR data for over 40,000 patients admitted to critical care units of the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2001 and 2012
(
Johnson et al. 2016
)
. The underlying population consists of patients who are not “frequent-flyers”, meaning that they have had less than 3 admissions within a calendar year. We consider each patient’s first admission in our analysis. The gold-standard depression label was derived from chart review and is only available for
n=416
patients while
N=26,925
patients comprise the unlabeled dataset.

We used a weakly supervised approach to train the ML model without labeled data. The training process is described in detail in Section S4.1. While the choice of fairness criteria is crucial in practice as multiple criteria can generally not be satisfied simultaneously, we evaluate all metrics for the purposes of illustration
(
Barocas et al. 2023
)
. We evaluated fairness across four attributes: race (White: 73% vs non-White: 27%), gender (Male: 56.2% vs Female: 43.8%), marital status (Married: 44.5% vs non-Married: 55.5%), and insurance type (Medicare: 54.3% vs non-Medicare: 45.7%). Although some of these protected attributes originally included more than two categories, they were binarized for the purpose of our analysis. The auxiliary covariates,
\mathbf{W}
, included the protected attributes not used for a given fairness audit. For example, when evaluating fairness across gender,
\mathbf{W}
included age, race, marital status, and insurance type, but excluded gender. We present results for the supervised method and the Infairness method using either
S
or both
S
and
\mathbf{W}
to construct the basis for the imputation model.

7
Discussion

Motivated by ML in biomedical applications, we proposed a SS method, called Infairness, to audit group fairness when labeled data is limited, but unlabeled data is abundant. Our method uses a carefully constructed model to impute missing outcomes to enable evaluation of fairness criteria using both the labeled and unlabeled data. Our theoretical and empirical results demonstrate that the Infairness method yields more efficient inference than its supervised counterpart without requiring additional labeled data. While our proposal has the potential to be widely used in practice, we close by commenting on several practical issues and potential extensions.

First, although our primary focus is on inference for difference-based disparity metrics as they are most commonly used in practice, Infairness is also applicable to ratio-based metrics (e.g.,
\frac{\mathcal{M}_{0}}{\mathcal{M}_{1}}
) using inference procedures derived from Theorem
1

(
Yeh et al. 2024
)
. In addition, Infairness supports hypothesis testing for
\epsilon
-fairness, a relaxed fairness criterion allowing group-specific disparities to fall within a small threshold,
\epsilon

(
Denis et al. 2023
)
. Second, many protected attributes define more than two groups, and methods for evaluating multi-group fairness remain an active area of research. Early approaches relied on visualizations of model performance across groups, using tools such as AI Fairness 360 and Aequitas
(
Bellamy et al. 2018
;
Saleiro et al. 2019
)
. More recent work has proposed meta-metrics, such as the max-min difference, max absolute difference, and variance of performance metrics, as quantitative summaries. However,
Lum et al. 2022
showed that supervised estimators for these meta-metrics are biased. Recent contributions by
Cherian and Candès 2024
have framed multi-group fairness as a multiple hypothesis testing problem, introducing a bootstrap-based method to jointly bound disparities across groups. Developing SS approaches for multi-group settings is a focus of our ongoing work.

Third, while our emphasis has been on group fairness, individual fairness and causal fairness are emerging within biomedical applications
(
Xue et al. 2020
;
Makhlouf et al. 2024
)
. Individual fairness requires that similar individuals receive similar predictions, while causal fairness seeks to uncover causal relationships between protected attributes and model outputs
(
Castiglione et al. 2022
;
Cornacchia et al. 2023
;
Byun et al. 2024
)
. Extending our SS framework to support these fairness notions is an avenue of future research. Fourth, while we consider incorporating auxiliary covariates into the imputation step, including features used for training the ML model may further improve efficiency, particularly when the ML model is misspecified and the number of features is not large. However, care must be taken to avoid overfitting and a resulting loss in the efficiency gain of the SS estimator when the group-specific sample sizes are not large. Finally, Infairness relies on the assumption that labeled data are a simple random sample from the underlying pool of data. In many biomedical contexts, labeled data may be collected using non-random sampling strategies (e.g., stratified sampling) to ensure adequate subgroup representation. Ongoing work in SS inference has begun to address such designs and incorporating these advances into our current framework is a promising direction (e.g.,
Gronsbell et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2023
).
