Mpox surveillance in endemic regions: a scoping review of trends, challenges, and recommendations
Authors: Fortune Benjamin Effiong, Emmanuel Ebuka Elebesunu, Tolulope Joseph Ogunniyi, Dimeji Abdulsobur Olawuyi, Emmanuel Ekpor, Monica Ahiadorme, Archibong Edem Bassey, Ibrahim Adebayo Hassan, Emmanuella Benjamin Effiong, Oluwaseun Adeolu Ogundijo, Ayomide Timilehin Kayode, Leonard Ighodalo Uzairue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMpox continues to pose a significant public health threat in endemic regions of Africa, particularly with the emergence of new viral clades and continuous human-to-human transmission. This scoping review assesses existing Mpox surveillance systems in endemic parts of Africa, highlighting key trends, challenges, and opportunities for improvement.MethodsA scoping review was conducted following the PRISMA-ScR guidelines. Five electronic databases (PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library) were searched in September 2025. Studies conducted in the WHO African Region that described, evaluated, or implemented Mpox laboratory surveillance systems were included. Eligible articles underwent screening and data extraction using the Covidence software. Data extracted from the reviewed studies were charted according to surveillance structures, diagnostic methods, reporting strategies, and implementation challenges.Results24 studies were included for review, predominantly from Central and West Africa, particularly the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), Nigeria, Cameroon, and the Central African Republic (CAR). Surveillance systems varied in scope and design, ranging from passive and active national frameworks to cross-border monitoring. Diagnostic approaches commonly relied on polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing, though limitations in laboratory infrastructure and delayed specimen transport hindered timely case confirmation. Key challenges identified include deficits in diagnostic capacity, misclassification of disease based on symptoms, and limited follow-up investigations. Structural barriers such as poor infrastructure, healthcare worker shortages, and conflict-related disruptions further weakened Mpox surveillance. Stigma and lack of community awareness contributed to underreporting. Additionally, the circulation of multiple Mpox viral clades and frequent cross-border transmission complicated outbreak detection and containment.ConclusionWhile Mpox surveillance capacity has improved modestly, major structural and systemic challenges persist. Strengthening Mpox surveillance in endemic regions requires a multi-level strategy to ensure timely detection and containment of future outbreaks.Clinical trial numberNot applicableSupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12175-9.Keywords: Mpox, Infectious disease, Outbreak, Surveillance, Public health

Introduction

Mpox (formerly known as Monkeypox), a disease caused by the monkeypox virus, a member of the Orthopoxvirus genus, remains a persistent and evolving threat today, necessitating robust surveillance systems to protect global and public health security [1]. Research has shown the virus’s capability, particularly in its evolution from previously known ‘animal to human transmission’ to sustained ‘human to human transmission,’ as seen in the emergence of distinct viral clades. These clades are clade I (with subclades Ia and Ib) and clade II (with subclades IIa and IIb), with the former historically found in Central Africa, and the latter circulating in West Africa [2]. As a result of these changes, the patterns of disease transmission have been altered, posing challenges to existing outbreak surveillance systems.

Dating back to the first reported human case of mpox in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) in 1970, mpox has largely remained endemic to Central and West African countries. This is up until 2003, when imported rodents from Ghana resulted in 47 confirmed cases in the United States, making this the first documented outbreak outside of Africa [1]. Subsequently, there were no reported travel-related cases until 2018, when there was a re-emergence of the disease in Nigeria, resulting in 11 travel-related mpox cases recorded across the United States, Israel, Singapore, and the United Kingdom between 2018 and 2021 [1]. This continued until May 2022, when a novel Claude IIb lineage (B.1) appeared globally, causing the World Health Organization (WHO) to declare Mpox, a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) as of 23rd July, 2022. This declaration marked a crucial moment in surveillance, as mpox cases now appeared in non-endemic countries across the world [3].

However, the pattern of the global response revealed stark disparities. While cases declined substantially in the Global North by early 2023, leading the WHO to lift the PHEIC declaration in May 2023 [4], African countries experienced a contrasting reality. The WHO African region reported a dramatic 78.5% increase in suspected cases between 2022 and 2023, with DRC contributing the highest numbers of laboratory-confirmed cases. [5]. The situation deteriorated further in 2023 when the DRC reported its first cases of clade I mpox among bisexual, gay, and other men who have sex with men, marking a concerning shift toward sustained human-to-human transmission [6, 7]. This epidemiological transition was followed by outbreaks within heterosexual networks and among female sex workers, attributed to a newly characterized clade 1b variant. By July 2024, the evolving threat had spread beyond country boundaries. Clade Ib Mpox reached previously unaffected countries, including Burundi, Kenya, Rwanda, and Uganda, while clade IIb re-emerged in South Africa, resulting in high morbidity and mortality rates, particularly among individuals with advanced HIV disease [5]. The gravity of these developments led the Africa Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (Africa CDC) to declare Mpox a public Health Emergency of Continental Concern, followed by the WHO’s reissuance of a PHEIC declaration on 13th and 14th of August 2024, respectively [8, 9].

The continued spread and evolution of Mpox in Africa, particularly its transition from zoonotic to human-to-human transmission, underscores the urgent need to strengthen laboratory surveillance systems. Reliable tracking and reporting mechanisms are crucial to monitor viral spread and to inform targeted interventions, particularly in hotspots like DRC and areas with limited access to healthcare. Yet, underreporting and inadequate laboratory capacity persist, creating significant gaps in case reporting. Addressing these gaps requires a proactive approach that includes scaling up PCR testing, deploying mobile laboratories, and developing point-of-care tests, particularly for remote and conflict-affected regions [5].

Over five decades after the first documented human Mpox case in DRC in 1970, critical research gaps remain in understanding the laboratory surveillance systems for the Mpox virus in Africa [10]. This scoping review aims to assess and map existing evidence on Mpox surveillance systems, detection strategies, and reporting mechanisms in endemic regions, identifying key challenges and gaps to inform improvements in outbreak detection and response.

Methods

The review was conducted following the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA), with specific adherence to the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [11].

Literature search

Five electronic databases (PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library) were systematically searched, and this was complemented by citation tracking to capture any citations missed by electronic searches. Our search was initially conducted in October 2024 and was updated in September 2025 to ensure the inclusion of the most recent evidence. The search terms were blended using the following concepts: (i) Mpox virus, (ii) laboratory, (iii) surveillance, and (iv) countries in Africa. The full electronic search strategy is available as an attached supplementary file (Appendix 1).

Eligibility criteria

In terms of eligibility, all types of observational studies (cohort, case-control, cross-sectional), experimental studies, including randomised controlled trials (RCTs), quasi-experimental studies, and government reports that provide valuable insights into laboratory surveillance systems were included in the review. All included studies had accessible full texts, were published in English, and were conducted in the WHO African Region. The selected studies described, evaluated, or implemented laboratory surveillance systems for Mpox, along with information on the challenges and limitations in existing surveillance systems. Any studies that did not meet these criteria were excluded from the review. All papers found from the database search were imported into the Covidence software, and their titles and abstracts were screened for eligibility. The eligible articles were subjected to full-text screening, and only articles that met the inclusion criteria were finally included for review. Accordingly, a PRISMA flow chart was created to depict the systematic literature search and selection process (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flow diagram for literature search

Data extraction

Data required to answer the research question of this scoping review were extracted from the eligible articles using a standardized extraction sheet. The data extracted include: author, study population, study design, specimen collection strategies (e.g., types of samples collected, algorithms for testing), laboratory testing methods (e.g., PCR, viral isolation), data reporting and analysis procedures, challenges and limitations with existing surveillance systems, recommendations for improving existing surveillance systems. The team conducted a pilot survey on the final selected articles to determine if the extraction form was in line with the scoping review question [12], and the data extraction form was adjusted accordingly before the main data extraction was performed using Covidence. Disagreements were handled through consensus, and when two authors could not reach an agreement, a third reviewer helped resolve it.

Results

Study characteristics

This scoping review analysed findings from 24 studies conducted in regions endemic to Mpox (MPX), with a predominant focus on Central and West Africa (Fig. 1). The studies primarily targeted countries such as the DRC, Nigeria, Cameroon, and the Central African Republic (CAR). These areas represent key hotspots for Mpox outbreaks, given their proximity to zoonotic reservoirs and vulnerable populations. The studies collectively spanned from 2010 to 2024, with most focusing on epidemic periods, re-emergence trends, and factors influencing disease dynamics.

The studies spanned diverse designs and timelines. Nine observational studies explored Mpox outbreak trends, clinical characteristics, and demographic factors, often highlighting variations in incidence over time. Field-based investigations focused on zoonotic transmission routes and environmental drivers, including the role of the bushmeat trade. Six surveillance reports assessed the efficacy of existing systems, exposing gaps in outbreak detection and reporting processes. The time periods covered ranged from short-term, outbreak-focused studies spanning a few months to longitudinal efforts over several years. Across these studies, the predominant aim was to assess the re-emergence of Mpox following the waning smallpox immunity and evolving human-wildlife interactions (Table 1).

Surveillance strategies reported in the reviewed studies

Surveillance system structure

The surveillance systems assessed in the studies were primarily national-level frameworks, with some integrating regional or cross-border components. Surveillance efforts ranged from short-term outbreak investigations to sustained systems operating over several years. Shorter surveillance systems faced limitations in capturing long-term patterns. Surveillance often targeted high-risk populations, such as bushmeat handlers and hunters [13]. However, rural communities were generally underrepresented in passive surveillance systems. Both passive systems and active case-finding systems were employed. Some studies utilised mixed systems incorporating both active and passive surveillance [14–18]. Active systems showed improved case detection and geographic coverage compared to passive reporting systems. While the majority of studies focused on regional surveillance, a few extended to cross-border monitoring to track the spread of disease. Some systems operated at regional or local levels, while others covered national or cross-border outbreaks, reflecting varying capacities and priorities (Table 1).

Laboratory analysis and data collection

Primary data sources included hospitals, community health centres, laboratories, field investigations, and outbreak investigation teams. Some studies relied on field teams to collect data during outbreaks in remote villages, demonstrating how integrating data from local health centers helped to improve outbreak mapping. Collected specimens included lesion swabs, blood samples, and crusts. Studies such as Yinda et al. [19] and Whitehouse et al. [20] collected a range of specimens, ensuring comprehensive diagnostic coverage. Additionally, Malembi et al. [18] reported the collection of vaginal swabs and placental tissue for the diagnosis of Mpox. Some studies employed quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) for Mpox confirmation, while others used serology for retrospective analysis. Molecular diagnostics, especially PCR, were the gold standard in most studies (Table 1).

Challenges with existing surveillance systems

Diagnostic limitations and laboratory capacity

A major challenge across multiple studies is the limited diagnostic capacity and chronic underreporting of cases, particularly in remote and conflict-affected areas. During the 2017 Mpox outbreak in Nigeria, the absence of an in-country laboratory capable of confirming cases led to significant delays in containment efforts, increasing anxiety among healthcare workers (HCWs) and the public [23]. In DRC, poor transportation infrastructure with tremendous logistical challenges in northern and western DRC further compounded these delays, with some samples taking over two months to reach testing centers, limiting timely case confirmation [14, 18].

Beyond logistical challenges, external disruptions such as civil unrest and global health crises have severely impacted Mpox surveillance systems. In CAR, passive surveillance efforts have failed to capture the full burden of disease, as most reported cases come from regions with better healthcare access, leaving many undiagnosed in harder-to-reach areas [31]. Kalthan et al. [25] similarly attribute underreporting in CAR’s conflict zones to limited diagnostic resources and ongoing instability. Besombes et al. [15] highlight that civil unrest (2013–2014) and the COVID-19 pandemic (2020) further weakened case detection, particularly in rural areas.

Structural and logistical weaknesses continue to hinder effective surveillance. In Cameroon, the lack of a computerized database creates inefficiencies in real-time case monitoring and reporting, delaying response efforts [32]. Similarly, in the Kivus region of the DRC, ongoing conflict has disrupted surveillance networks, making it difficult to conduct epidemiological investigations [27]. Across multiple studies, delays in case detection and reporting remain a critical concern, with significant gaps observed between initial identification, sample collection, and laboratory confirmation [33].

Geographic inaccessibility further restricts specimen collection and patient follow-up. Studies have highlighted how difficult terrain and poor infrastructure severely limit surveillance efforts [15, 31]. While these studies focus on physical barriers, Kalthan et al. [25] underscore the role of sociopolitical instability, particularly in CAR’s conflict zones, as a major factor in disrupting disease surveillance (Table 2).

Mpox case detection and case identification challenges

Limited laboratory capacity in many regions has led to a heavy reliance on symptom-based surveillance, often resulting in misdiagnosis. Many healthcare facilities in endemic areas lack access to rapid molecular diagnostic tools, delaying case confirmation and increasing the risk of underreporting [32]. Overreliance on clinical presentation leads to frequent misdiagnosis, particularly when differentiating Mpox from varicella-zoster virus (VZV), and other overlapping diseases like measles [13, 22, 29]. Underreporting of confirmed cases due to limited sample collection from suspected cases also occurs with overreliance [34]. Without sufficient laboratory integration, differentiating Mpox from other febrile rash illnesses remains a significant challenge.

Misclassification is a recurring issue across studies. A study in the DRC found that 12.1% of suspected Mpox cases were actually coinfections with VZV, highlighting the difficulty of distinguishing between the two infections [22]. Similarly, in Bas-Uélé, DRC, a significant proportion of suspected Mpox cases were later confirmed as chickenpox, exposing the inaccuracy of symptom-based case definitions [28].

Broad case definitions further contribute to inaccurate surveillance. A study in the DRC found that overly generalized definitions resulted in a high number of false positives, misclassifying other rash illnesses as Mpox [24]. This overestimation of cases complicates response efforts and diverts resources away from actual Mpox outbreaks.

Underreporting remains a major challenge. In Tshuapa Province, DRC (2011–2015), fewer than half of suspected Mpox cases were fully investigated due to logistical constraints such as impassable roads and inadequate public health resources [20]. Also, in DRC between 2010 and 2023, reliance on suspected cases reported through passive surveillance resulted in only 12.2% of suspected cases having samples tested, and 57.1% of those tested were positive, leading to underreporting of Mpox cases [35]. In Rivers State, Nigeria (2017–2022), only 46.7% of suspected cases were confirmed through RT-PCR testing, pointing to inefficiencies in case identification [30]. Additionally, inconsistent follow-up investigations have weakened surveillance accuracy; in the DRC, suspected Mpox cases were rarely reassessed to determine their final diagnosis, contributing to incomplete case identification [24] (Table 2).

Healthcare system gaps and stigma in Mpox outbreak response

A critical challenge in the Mpox outbreak response has been the lack of dedicated isolation facilities. During the 2017 outbreak in Nigeria, the Niger Delta University Teaching Hospital (NDUTH) in Bayelsa State had no designated isolation unit, forcing the conversion of a general ward to accommodate Mpox patients [23]. This lack of preparedness increased the risk of nosocomial transmission and placed additional strain on hospital resources.

Healthcare workers (HCWs) also expressed reluctance to participate in outbreak response efforts due to fear of infection. In Bayelsa, some HCWs refused to manage Mpox patients unless financial incentives were provided [23]. These concerns were compounded by inadequate infection prevention and control (IPC) measures, including shortages of personal protective equipment (PPE) and poor waste disposal systems, further heightening the risk of transmission within healthcare settings [23].

Surveillance efforts have also been hindered by a shortage of trained personnel capable of accurately identifying and reporting Mpox cases. In Cameroon, inadequate training of HCWs has resulted in inconsistencies in case detection and data reporting, weakening surveillance reliability [27]. Similar challenges have been observed in the Kivus region of the DRC, where HCWs struggled to recognize Mpox symptoms, partly due to a lack of prior smallpox vaccination, which could have provided indirect familiarity with orthopoxvirus infections [23]. Compounding this issue, low community awareness has led to delays in reporting suspected cases, increasing the potential for further disease spread [32].

Beyond structural and technical barriers, stigma surrounding Mpox has discouraged individuals from seeking medical care. During the 2017 outbreak in Nigeria, patients and their families faced discrimination, leading to reluctance in reporting cases [23]. In one extreme case, the psychological distress associated with the disease contributed to a confirmed Mpox patient’s suicide [19]. This stigma not only affects case reporting but also undermines public health efforts to contain outbreaks and educate communities about the disease (Table 2).

Circulation of multiple virus strains and Cross-Border transmission

Several studies highlight the complexity of Mpox epidemiology due to the presence of multiple co-circulating viral strains. In Cameroon, both Clade I and Clade II of the Mpox virus have been detected, creating a unique challenge for surveillance and response efforts [33]. In the Republic of the Congo, genetic sequencing revealed multiple distinct clusters within Clade I, indicating multiple independent transmission events rather than a single-source outbreak [16]. The presence of multiple strains complicates outbreak prediction and containment strategies. Additionally, cross-border transmission has been a recurring issue, with cases frequently moving between the DRC and neighboring countries, exacerbating surveillance challenges (Table 2) [19].

Discussion

Surveillance infrastructure and diagnostic capacity

Our review highlights pervasive delays in case confirmation due to centralized laboratory networks and inadequate transport logistics [23, 32]. These findings mirror those of Reynolds and Damon [37], who noted that the cessation of smallpox vaccination significantly contributed to the underreporting of Mpox cases by removing both population immunity and familiarity of healthcare workers with orthopoxviruses. Similarly, the systematic review by Bunge et al. [38] found that fragmented diagnostic systems in Central and West Africa consistently prolong the interval between symptom onset and laboratory confirmation, thus impeding outbreak containment.

To enhance diagnostic capacity for Mpox, it is important to establish decentralized molecular diagnostic centers in endemic regions, such as regional GeneXpert platforms or mobile PCR units [23]. This would dramatically shorten turnaround times and reduce delays in case confirmation, as demonstrated in pilot studies in Uganda [39]. The GeneXpert system has demonstrated a sensitivity of 98.8% and a specificity of 100% for Mpox, making it a promising field-based diagnostic tool [21]. Furthermore, shifting from a sequential testing approach to simultaneous testing for Mpox and VZV could improve diagnostic accuracy [22]. Investments in cold-chain logistics and standardized sample-referral networks are important to translate laboratory capacity into timely surveillance [21, 40].

In terms of strengthening disease surveillance, governments and health policymakers should invest in national digital surveillance systems that enable real-time case tracking. For instance, in Cameroon, the establishment of a centralized database would significantly enhance the efficiency of case detection and reporting [32]. In conflict-prone areas such as the Kivus, integrating mobile health technologies could facilitate remote case reporting and ensure continued disease monitoring despite instability [27].

Mpox case Detection/Identification

The development and deployment of rapid molecular diagnostic tests are crucial for improving the accuracy of Mpox case identification. In Bas-Uélé, DRC, symptom-based surveillance alone has proven inadequate. Integrating laboratory-based confirmatory testing into routine surveillance activities could significantly enhance disease monitoring [28]. Refining Mpox case definitions is another key strategy for reducing false positives. Incorporating specific clinical criteria – such as fever preceding rash, lymphadenopathy, and deep-seated, firm lesions- could enhance diagnostic accuracy [24]. Furthermore, there is a need to refine diagnostic protocols in order to reduce confusion between Mpox and VZV [13, 22].

Healthcare workforce constraints

The reluctance of HCWs to engage in Mpox case management was a notable barrier, which is largely due to insufficient PPE, lack of incentives, and limited clinical training [23, 27]. This is consistent with observations from the 2003 U.S. outbreak, where non-specialist clinicians misclassified Mpox as Varicella despite clear epidemiological links [41]. Training deficiencies not only reduce diagnostic accuracy but also compromise IPC measures in healthcare settings, which elevates nosocomial risks. It is important to establish structured training modules covering clinical recognition, IPC protocols, and biosafety, which should be integrated into routine continuous professional development (CPD). In addition, financial incentives (like hazard allowances) and psychosocial support proved effective during Ebola outbreaks, and could similarly be applied in this context to encourage the participation of HCWs in outbreak response efforts [42].

Community engagement and stigma

Our findings depict how stigma and misinformation can impede care-seeking and suppress case reporting. The discrimination against Mpox patients reported in Bayelsa State, Nigeria, shows a deeper mistrust of health authorities and limited public awareness [23]. This is further supported by qualitative research findings in Nigeria and the DRC, indicating that fear of social ostracization leads many patients to seek traditional healers instead of formal healthcare [43].

Proper education and trust-building are important in outbreak management, and it is necessary to implement activities that will improve awareness and trust among community indigenes, especially in politically unstable regions, to encourage case reporting and cooperation for contact tracing [25]. Public health campaigns should inform communities about Mpox symptoms, transmission, and treatment options, as this will help reduce stigma and improve public trust in surveillance and control measures [23]. Local leaders, religious figures, and faith-based organizations should also be carried along in outreach efforts to enhance the acceptance of public health interventions [15]. Furthermore, mental health support could also be included in Mpox case management to address the psychological impact of the disease, as recommended by WHO guidelines [44].

Genomic surveillance and Cross-Border dynamics

Genomic sequencing should be integrated into routine surveillance efforts to track the emergence of new viral strains and mutations. The co-circulation of Clade I and Clade II viruses, and the identification of multiple Clade I clusters in the DRC, illustrates the genetic complexity of Mpox in endemic regions [19, 33]. These observations validate earlier phylogenetic analyses that demonstrated separate Central and West African lineages with differing case-fatality rates [26, 41]. In Cameroon, where both Clade I and Clade II circulate, molecular surveillance should be prioritized to determine whether viral evolution is influencing transmission patterns [33]. To this effect, it is important to incorporate routine whole-genome sequencing into national surveillance platforms to enable real-time tracking of viral evolution and transmission chains.

Cross-border transmission, particularly between the DRC and neighbouring nations, further complicates Mpox containment due to differences in reporting frameworks and population movements [19]. This resonates with the regional spread witnessed during the 2017–18 Nigerian outbreak, where porous borders facilitated Mpox exportation to Cameroon and Chad [14]. To help resolve this, it is important to establish regional data-sharing agreements, which can be modelled after the Global Influenza Surveillance and Response System (GISRS), to harmonize reporting and expedite cross-border interventions [26].

Recommendation

To address the challenges of Mpox surveillance in endemic regions, we developed a framework to strengthen response strategies, focusing on diagnostic capacity, workforce development, data and digital systems, community education and risk communication, genomic surveillance, and cross-border collaboration. Strengths, limitations, and future directions

A strength of this review is its comprehensive mapping of surveillance modalities across diverse epidemiological and geopolitical contexts. However, limitations include using only published articles without considering extending the search to grey literature, which might pose a potential bias in examining documents and reports relevant to this study. Potential publication bias, as studies in conflict zones may remain unpublished, and heterogeneous study designs that prevent quantitative synthesis, also served as a limitation of this study. In terms of future research, a potential area of focus is to employ a one-health approach (given the zoonotic origin of Mpox) by implementing multidisciplinary surveillance to link wildlife monitoring with human health data, which could help anticipate spill-over events. Also, cost-effectiveness studies could be implemented to compare decentralized versus centralized diagnostic networks, which will inform policy decisions on the allocation of resources.

Conclusion

This scoping review has highlighted the current trends in Mpox surveillance across endemic regions, especially in Central and West Africa. While there have been improvements in Mpox case detection and laboratory diagnostics, the overall surveillance landscape remains laden with challenges such as limited diagnostic capacity, inadequate health workforce training, unreliable data systems, and community stigma, all of which impede timely outbreak detection and response. The circulation of multiple viral strains and cross-border transmission is also a concern, requiring the development of effective genomic and regional surveillance frameworks. To strengthen Mpox surveillance and control efforts, it is important to establish a comprehensive, multi-level strategy that involves decentralized diagnostics for Mpox, workforce development, digital surveillance tools, community engagement, and one health collaboration. These approaches will not only improve outbreak preparedness and response but also contribute to global health security in the face of re-emerging zoonotic threats.
