Comprehensive Analysis of Health Service Utilization for Febrile Illness and Malaria in Malaria-Endemic States in India: LLINs as a Pillar of Control Strategies
Authors: Vani H Chalageri, Shrinivasa B Marinaik, Richa Singhal, Sanjeev K Gupta, Sujith Nath Nathan, Alex Eapen, Chander P Yadav, Piyoosh K Singh, Praveen K Tripathi, Waseem A Malla, Suresh C Pradhan, Diamond P Sinha, Shri P Singh, Kuldeep Singh, Pranab J Bhuyan, Manju Rahi, Tanu Jain, Rinku Sharma, Anupkumar R Anvikar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMalaria elimination remains a global public health priority, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. India has set a target of eliminating malaria by 2030, making it essential to understand gaps in healthcare access, utilization, and preventive practices in endemic areas to guide focused interventions to achieve national and global malaria elimination targets.MethodsA subnational, cross-sectional study surveying 37,849 households in ten malaria-endemic states in India, which covered 177,644 individuals, was carried out to estimate the health service and long-lasting insecticidal net (LLIN) utilization for febrile illness and malaria.ResultsThe study found that the accessibility of a government healthcare facility was within 2.5 km (interquartile range [IQR] 1–5 km), with 57.4% of febrile individuals visiting a government healthcare center or approaching a government-designated healthcare provider for fever within 24–48 h. Adequate knowledge and behavior regarding prevention were observed in 89.2% households, while 73.6% households reported using LLINs regularly.ConclusionsLack of formal education and belonging to the tribal population were found to be high risk factors for malaria. Healthcare-seeking behavior played a crucial role in malaria burden. This study highlights the gap between accessibility of health facility and its utilization, as well as LLIN ownership and its regular utilization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-026-01359-8.Keywords: Malaria, Behavioral gap, LLINs, Malaria control intervention coverage, Febrile-like illness, Health-seeking behavior

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Malaria elimination depends not only on the implementation of interventions but also on the availability of their effective distribution, accessibility, and utilization. However, evidence regarding gaps in health service access, healthcare-seeking behavior, and long-lasting insecticidal net (LLIN) usage across malaria-endemic areas remains limited.Therefore, this study across ten malaria-endemic states in India assessed the health services and LLINs in terms of their distribution, availability, accessibility, and utilization, and identified inequities across sociodemographic groups to inform targeted malaria elimination strategies. What was learned from the study? This subnational, cross-sectional survey found that accessibility to government health facilities was generally adequate; however, the timely utilization of services for febrile-like illness and the use of LLINs can be improved. Gaps were most pronounced among the populations with lower educational qualifications and those from tribal communities, indicating persistent inequities in malaria control implementation.Findings suggest that improving healthcare-seeking behavior, effective and LLIN usage, and addressing social barriers are essential for malaria elimination in India and may also apply to other malaria-endemic low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).

Introduction

The World Malaria Report 2024 indicates that India accounted for nearly half of the estimated malaria cases in the South-East Asia Region in 2023 [1], highlighting the country’s substantial share of the regional burden. Through concerted efforts, India has achieved an overall decline in malaria incidence and mortality with 255,500 cases and 86 deaths reported in 2024 [2]. This progress reflects a multi-pronged approach in detection, treatment, and vector control, including indoor residual spraying (IRS), long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs), larval source management (LSM) using bacterial cultures, larvivorous fishes, and larvicides, and malaria detection by rapid diagnostic tests (RDT) and/or quality blood slide preparation by healthcare staff trained at village level. Treatment outcomes improved with national drug policies using artemisinin-based combination therapy for Plasmodium falciparum and chloroquine/primaquine for Plasmodium vivax [3].

The rising resistance to antimalarial drugs and insecticides, along with rapid urbanization, increased tourism, and endemic pockets in hard-to-reach and tribal areas pose a challenge to the malaria elimination efforts in India [4]. These challenges mirror those in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where health system limitations and social determinants limit malaria control. Interventions such as the National Framework for Malaria Elimination (NFME), Intensified Malaria Control Program (IMCP), National Strategic Plan for Malaria Elimination 2023–2027 (NSP) [5], and Intensified Malaria Elimination Project (IMEP), as well as sustained support from Global Fund to fight AIDS, Tuberculosis and Malaria (GFATM), aim to address these challenges [6, 7].

Against this backdrop, the present subnational survey assessed existing program-based operational service delivery across selected malaria-endemic states of India, with focus on availability, accessibility, and utilization of health services for febrile-like illness and malaria, and special emphasis on LLINs and their role in malaria control. The findings identify implementation gaps that limit program effectiveness. Beyond India, the results offer valuable insights for other malaria-endemic LMICs pursuing elimination, highlighting the importance of converting coverage into effective utilization. Reinvigorating malaria elimination efforts, reinforcing the need to reignite, reimagine and reinvest, remains essential to ensure malaria ends with us.

Methods

Results of the study have been reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [8].

Ethical Approval

The study was approved by the National Institute of Malaria Research (ICMR) ethics committee via letter no. NIMR/ECM/2023/Feb/14 dated 24 April 2023.

Study Design and Setting

This survey was carried out in ten malaria-endemic states in India, covering 47 districts and 375 villages (Supplementary Table S1). The study collected household-level data from all these districts.

Sample Size and Sampling Procedure

A total of 37,976 households were to be surveyed using multistage sampling technique. Sample size calculation and sampling techniques, along with a list of study sites, have been published as a protocol paper [6]. The sample size calculation was carried out on the basis of lowest estimated prevalence (35% for healthcare seeking for fever) to ensure comprehensive coverage, which yielded a target of 178 households with febrile illness per state. With 10% fever prevalence, a design effect of 1.5 for multistage sampling, and a 20% nonresponse rate, the per-state target was 3210 households (totaling 35,310 across ten study states). The population-proportion-to-size method selected 44 districts, with additional districts from smaller states added for representation, resulting in a total of 47 districts (808 households per district).

A multistage sampling approach was used within selected districts, based on malaria burden. Two primary health centers (PHCs) under each community health center (CHC; two) per district were selected using annual parasite incidence (API) load, followed by two villages per PHC/CHC chosen on the basis of past malaria positivity, maintaining a 7:1 rural–urban household ratio.

Participants

A respondent was any adult household member (≥ 18 years) present at the time and willing to provide the requisite information after granting written consent (regional language). If the respondent was unable to read and write, thumb impression was obtained on the consent form, in the presence of a witness. Only households with a minimum residence period of 6 months were included.

Survey Instrument and Variables

Household data were collected through a structured, pretested, and validated questionnaire administered through face-to-face interviews by field investigators, conducted in the regional language. It consisted of availability, accessibility, and utilization of healthcare facilities; healthcare-seeking behavior of the households on febrile-like illnesses and malaria; LLIN utilization; and sociodemographic variables comprising age, gender, education, nature of the household, social category, access to electricity, etc. Healthcare facility included the nearest government health facility and type, and its distance from the household. Data related to LLINs included household availability and utilization (regular i.e., ≥ 4 times per week and previous night) per individual (with age and gender breakup) and their reasons. Data on prevalence of febrile-like illness and malaria cases in the past 12 months, along with the latest three febrile-like illnesses in the household during the past month, were also collected.

Febrile-like illness was defined as self-reported fever in the last year for any family member; medical records were reviewed whenever available.

Data Collection and Management

Data were collected using an Open Data Kit (ODK) platform, enabling real-time monitoring through geographic coordinates. All datasets were anonymized by removing any personal information of the households and respondents. The de-identified data were shared with the analysis team in a cleaned comma-separated values (CSV) format and analyzed using SPSS-29 software.

Bias

As inherent to cross-sectional surveys, recall and reporting bias were expected. These were minimized through standardized training of field investigators, neutral probing, nonleading questions, and use of simple, specific questions. Self-reported information was cross-checked with observable indicators, i.e., LLIN presence and condition, treatment records, etc.

Statistical Analysis

Discrete variables are presented as frequency (percentage), while continuous data are presented as median (interquartile range [IQR]). Logistic regression analysis with malaria incidence as the dependent variable and variables such as education of the household manager, type of house, tribal category household, regular healthcare worker visits, healthcare-seeking behavior among households, and LLIN availability with regular utilization used as independent variables were carried out to be predictors. Adjusted and crude odds ratios are presented with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The analysis has been carried out using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 29.0. The quadrant analysis was constructed across ten states on MS Excel, with median values used as cutoff points for a proportion of households. Maps were prepared using QGIS (https://www.qgis.org/).

Results

Out of the calculated sample size of 37,976 households, data from 37,849 (99.6%) households were collected. Calculating one respondent per household, a total of 37,849 respondents provided information accounting for a total population of 177,644.

Surveyed Households and Population Demographics

The majority of the respondents were male (20,597; 54.4%), with a median age of 39 years (IQR: 30–50). Overall, 13,228 (34.9%) respondents reported being unable to read and write, while 13,180 (34.8%) had primary-level education. Most households, 23,781 (62.8%), were kuccha (dwelling constructed of temporary or nondurable materials), while 35,754 (94.5%) households had access to electricity. The (self-reported) median annual income was Indian rupees (Rs.) 50,000 (IQR: 30,000, 65,000). Children under 5 years constituted 9.9% (17,524) of the population, and 1014 pregnant women were covered (Table 1).

Healthcare-Seeking Behavior

Healthcare-seeking behavior of the surveyed population was generally adequate. Government health centers were the preferred choice for febrile illness for 67.2% (25,426) of the households, followed by seeking advice from a healthcare worker (17.8%, 6744 households).

Among 5463 households reporting fever cases in the family, details related to healthcare seeking were elicited for the last three cases, yielding 5360 febrile cases. A total of 3077 (57.4%) reported seeking care within 24–48 h of fever onset.

Overall, 31,525/37,849 (83.3%) households reported seeking advice for malaria-like illness in the past year. Among these, 20,838 (66.1%) underwent laboratory investigation for malaria, and 3598 (17.26%) were confirmed positive. Out of the 37,849 households visited, 3598 (9.5%) had confirmed malaria cases in the “past year,” of whom 2659 (73.9%) reported adherence to prescribed treatment (Table 2).

Assessment of Availability and Utilization of LLINs

LLIN coverage was high, with 85.2% (32,238) of households having at least one LLIN. Among 29,270 households (that had received at least one LLIN and were present in the household [HH] on the previous night), the proportion of the population that slept under an LLIN on the previous night was 119,543/140,822 (84.9%). The proportion of children (< 5 years) was 12,583/13,513 (93.1%), and for pregnant women this was 636/682 (93.3%). Out of 32,238 households having at least one LLIN, 90.2% (29,092) reported using them regularly. Out of 818 pregnant women residing in households having at least one LLIN, 63.2% (517) reported using them regularly. The proportion of children regularly using LLIN was 69.1% (10,331). Predominant reasons for not using LLIN regularly were reported as: inadequate availability of nets (55.1%), heat/humidity (21.7%), lesser number of mosquitoes (23.1%), and inappropriate size (16.6%) (Table 3).

Knowledge of the Community Regarding Prevention and Control of Malaria

Knowledge regarding prevention and control of malaria at the community level was found to be adequate at 89.2% (33,750 households). Among respondents, 89.6% were aware of malaria transmission. Among the 29,270 households having at least one LLIN and where family members were present during the “previous night” of the survey, 74.7% (21,876 households) reported sleeping under an LLIN, which is indicative of adequate prevention practices being followed by the community (Table 3).

Factors Associated with Incidence of Malaria

Multivariable logistic regression analysis of the factors associated with the odds of members of households being malaria-positive in the last year was performed. The factors considered in the model were education level of the manager of the household, type of house, hard-to-reach areas, tribal community, visits of healthcare workers, healthcare-seeking behavior of the household, availability of an LLIN in the household, and regular utilization of an LLIN. Multivariate analysis revealed that the odds of being malaria-positive were 1.196 (95% CI: 1.113, 1.286), if the manager of the household had no formal education (p < 0.001). Similarly, the odds of members of the household being diagnosed as malaria-positive were 1.457 times higher (95% CI: 1.330, 1.597) if a healthcare worker never inquired about malaria-like illness when visiting the household (p < 0.001). The chances of suffering from malaria were 2.702 times higher (95% CI: 2.486, 2.936) in those households where people did not exhibit adequate healthcare-seeking behavior (p < 0.001) (Table 4).

Discussion

The findings signify considerable progress in malaria prevention and control across India, with nuanced inter- and intra-state variations in LLIN coverage, utilization, and health-seeking behavior, as well as accessibility. These variations highlight the importance of localized programmatic gaps that require state- and district-specific strategies.

The study shows that overall LLIN distribution (85.2%) and regular use (73.6%) were high (Table 3), underscoring India’s vector control efforts. Maintaining high LLIN coverage and use is challenging, for instance, by 2022, sub-Saharan Africa reported 70% household coverage, but only 49% of the covered population used it the previous night [9], compared with 85.2% and 84.9%, respectively, in this study.

State-specific disparities were evident in states such as Madhya Pradesh and Jharkhand reporting lower accessibility to the “nearest health facility” (Fig. 2), increasing the risk of delayed detection, diagnosis, and treatment. Large village size and scattered settlements may explain this pattern in Madhya Pradesh. Nonetheless, the presence of functional subcenters can aid in early diagnosis and complete treatment (EDCT) of isolated malaria cases. Since the accessibility to health facility is low, utilization is also low in these states. Additional measures of deploying health workers in the field need to be considered to improve accessibility. Studies showed access to nearby health centers is crucial in malaria prevention and could mitigate malaria risk by 33.4%, reinforcing impact of physical access in malaria prevention [10].

The persistent behavioral gap between LLIN ownership and consistent use was observed in several states such as Arunachal Pradesh, where LLIN availability was high but with comparatively lower usage (Fig. 3). This gap may be partly explained by the drastic decline in malaria cases, which resulted in decreased perceived threat, leading to complacency in preventive behavior. Thus, interventions providing awareness and health education at the village level, in the local language and through village leaders, as well as community gatherings, especially before the distribution phase of LLINs, are needed [11].

Similarly, although Madhya Pradesh records low malaria incidence, LLIN coverage and its utilization rates need improvement, indicating vulnerability to resurgence. Data from 2017 to 2018 discussing similar behavioral gaps have shown that awareness campaigns can increase LLIN acceptance rate by 90% [12]. A 2019 study also showed that routine monitoring and supervision intervention could raise “regular” LLIN usage to 47% (statistical significance of p < 0.01) [13]. A similar behavioral gap exists in the state of Assam (estimated: low-to-moderate malaria-receptive state) as well, which has extended monsoons. Moreover, studies in the region have shown that this behavioral gap also exists among malaria-infected populations [14].

In contrast, Chhattisgarh, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Odisha indicate remarkable progress, with high LLIN ownership and utilization. Studies from 2020 to 2022 highlight government interventions in reduced malaria burden in these regions [5, 15], indicating that such practices could be adapted and scaled in other endemic regions. Sustaining these state vector-borne disease control (VBDC) programs is essential to achieve malaria-free status for these states.

Three of the top ten districts with the highest malaria burden “in the last year” were in Odisha. The Durgama Anchalare Malaria Nirakarana (DAMaN) program implemented in Odisha emphasized large-scale LLIN distribution as a key intervention, reduced malaria incidence by over 80% [16, 17]. A recent study showed that poverty, significant tribal populations, and large forested areas [18] warrant immediate reappraisal of the malaria situation and targeted follow-up interventions.

Despite distribution of 414,197 LLINs in Mizoram districts with village API > 1 during 2020, high malaria burden persisted in Lawngtlai and Lunglei, likely reflecting cross-border transmission from Chittagong Hill Tracts of Bangladesh, where an estimated 85% of malaria cases in that country are located [19, 20]. Therefore, targeted interventions must consider border and sociodemographic profile.

The current comprehensive analysis suggests that LLINs should be re-evaluated not just as a distribution measure but also as an integrated, behaviorally aligned intervention embedded within a broader health service delivery system. Strengthening community participation, supported by strong political will, is essential to bring about the behavior change. Effective malaria-preventive approaches demonstrated in states such as Chhattisgarh could be adapted and scaled nationally, while states such as Tripura may benefit from strengthened surveillance and grassroots-level interventions. Bridging the behavioral gaps between LLIN ownership and regular usage will require community-sensitive strategies and reinforced continued engagement of local health staff in screening, monitoring, and timely notification. The results of the present study pave way to re-ignite, re-imagine, and re-invest our funds in addressing the behavioral issues of health service and LLIN utilization to ensure malaria ends with us.

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this study is its coverage of multiple remote villages across malaria-endemic districts of India. The study employed a statistically sound design and sample size to address the LLIN-related interventions. Additionally, locally trained staff were hired to avoid cultural and language bias/barriers.

The study has a few limitations. First, the number of malaria and febrile-like illness cases is based on respondents’ self-reports and confirmation from local health staff. Second, delays between the estimated and actual LLIN distribution may have impacted the population density estimates and the number of LLINs needed per household, leading to perceptions of inadequate LLIN availability. Finally, respondent bias may occur when reporting on government-sponsored interventions such as LLINs.

Conclusions

The inter- and intra-state variability of health-seeking behavior for febrile illness and LLIN utilization is due to the population practices and geographical location of the selected study sites in India. Findings of this study are more encouraging in terms of reaching unreached and marginalized communities, including hard-to-reach and tribal populations affected by malaria. The study highlights the near target of reaching saturation in the availability of LLINs and emphasizes the gap between ownership and the behavioral issue of regular LLIN usage.
