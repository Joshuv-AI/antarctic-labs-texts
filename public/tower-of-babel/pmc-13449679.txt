Human pressure and protection gradients reshape diel activity patterns of terrestrial mammals
Authors: Dol Raj Thanet, Saneer Lamichhane, Peggy A. Bevan, Guilherme Braga Ferreira, Santosh Rayamajhi, Thakur Silwal, Kate E. Jones
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHuman activities increasingly reshape wildlife diel activity patterns, yet species-specific adjustments across land-use gradients with varying levels of human pressure and protection remain poorly understood. Nepal’s 2020 COVID-19 lockdown created a quasi-experimental reduction in human mobility, allowing clearer attribution of temporal responses to human pressure.MethodsUsing camera trap data from matched 30-day windows (March 24–April 24) in 2019 (pre-lockdown) and 2020 (lockdown) across the Bardia complex, representing a mosaic of human pressure and protection gradients, including Bardia National Park (NP), its buffer zone (BZ), and adjacent human-dominated areas outside of buffer zone (OBZ). NP-2020 treated as the baseline and quantified species-specific activity patterns with kernel densities, coefficients of overlap (Δ, 95% CIs), circular mean peak-time shifts (hours, 95% CIs), and a nocturnality index (% detections between 19:00–05:00 h, 95% bootstrap CIs). We analyzed four contrasts and reported overlap and peak-time shifts within species, contrasting NP-2020 with the same species in BZ and OBZ during survey periods 2019 and 2020.ResultsRelative to the NP-2020, herbivores —especially chital, barking deer, hog deer, and wild boar shifted activity peaks by ~ 6–11 h toward night and showed reduced overlap within-species in BZ and OBZ, with several near anti-phase schedules in OBZ. Sambar remained comparatively stable and crepuscular, while tigers exhibited low NP-BZ overlap and near 12 h offsets in their activity peaks. Lockdown conditions increased overlap and attenuated nocturnal displacement for several species (notably chital and sambar), evidencing rapid behavioral relaxation.ConclusionThe dominant pattern is a gradient of increasing nocturnality and declining overlap in activity patterns as human pressure increases from NP to BZ to OBZ, with persistent nocturnality in hog deer and wild boar indicating chronic constraints. We emphasize integrating temporal refugia into management by limiting high-intensity human activities during crepuscular periods and reducing nighttime noise and lighting at forest-agriculture interfaces, so wildlife need not rely on extreme nocturnality to coexist with humans. Long-term monitoring that couples activity metrics (overlap, peak-time shifts) with environmental factors and physiological indicators is needed to evaluate demographic and fitness consequences and to inform temporal ecology in multi-use landscapes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12862-026-02538-4.

Introduction

Human activities are profoundly reshaping ecosystems globally, influencing wildlife distributions, movements, and diel activity patterns across a range of systems, with detectable effects even inside protected areas (PAs) where fear responses can disrupt natural rhythms [13, 17, 23, 47, 53, 57, 58]. Beyond habitat loss and fragmentation, increasing human footprint, agricultural expansion, and rural settlement intensification escalate threats to biodiversity [20, 24]. Animals often avoid people and show heightened vigilance as risk-avoidance strategies in human-dominated settings [22]. Even within PAs, human presence can trigger avoidance behaviors, modify diel timing, and provoke strong fear responses that subsequently propagate ecological consequences [14, 52, 53], with nature-based recreation and other disturbances further adding complexity to conservation action [3, 29, 55].

The COVID-19 pandemic created a unique global “anthropause,” which markedly reduced human mobility through lockdowns beginning in early March 2020 [4, 27, 47]. Measures such as travel bans, diminished road traffic, and the closure of recreation sites, including PAs, rapidly decreased human pressure [46]. These fluctuations revealed both opportunities and risks: some wildlife species exhibited increased activity and ventured into previously avoided areas [5, 11, 31, 51], highlighting how human presence shapes a “landscape of fear” [53, 60]. In contrast, weakened enforcement in certain locations increased illegal activities that threatened vulnerable species [5, 9, 50]. These contrasts underscore the need to understand how changes in human activity alter wildlife’s temporal behavior, particularly within ecosystems characterized by high biodiversity and significant human influence.

Most evidence on the temporal interactions between humans and wildlife still relies on observations, where human presence is confounded with habitat changes, limiting causal inference [36, 38, 44]. The COVID-19 lockdown provided a rare quasi-experimental scenario in which habitats remained largely unchanged and human activity decreased, enabling clearer attribution of behavioral responses of wildlife to human presence [4, 13, 47]. However, South Asian landscapes—where high biodiversity coexist with dense human populations—remain rarely studied in tests of how wildlife temporal niches shift along human pressure gradients [54].

Nepal’s nationwide lockdown (March 24–June 21, 2020) created a natural experiment by suspending domestic and international travel, limiting gatherings, and closing PAs to nature-based recreation [27, 35, 50]. In the Terai Arc Landscape of Nepal, the Bardia Complex presents pronounced land-use regimes spanning across Bardia National Park (NP), its buffer zone (BZ), and adjacent human-dominated areas outside the buffer zone (OBZ): a mosaic of protection and human pressure gradients [6, 10, 19, 34]. This gradient effect enables a direct test of how wildlife temporal niches track human disturbance from strictly protected NP cores to increasingly anthropogenic peripheries (BZ and OBZ). Camera trapping during matched 30-day windows (March 24–April 24 in 2019 and 2020) offers a robust framework to quantify species-specific temporal responses to fluctuating human activity across distinct protection gradients. Wildlife responses to human disturbance are not uniformly negative; the direction and magnitude of behavioral changes are mediated by species-specific traits, including body mass, diet breadth, and prior exposure to disturbance, that together produce the heterogeneity commonly observed across taxa [11, 28, 48, 54].

Against this backdrop, our objective is to quantify species-specific diel activity patterns and its change across the NP, BZ, and OBZ using camera trap data of matched temporal windows (2019: pre-lockdown and 2020: lockdown). We treat NP-2020 as the baseline because the strict lockdown effectively eliminated human entry into the National Park, creating a true low-human reference condition, while human activity in BZ and OBZ remained high or even increased. This allows us to estimate species-specific temporal overlap (Δ, 95% CIs) and circular mean peak-time shifts (hours, 95% CIs) relative to a contemporaneous low-human baseline, rather than reflecting within-zone changes in BZ or OBZ.

We test three linked hypotheses: (i) lockdown effect: reduced human activity in 2020 increases species diurnality and elevates overlap with NP relative to 2019; (ii) human-pressure gradient: species activity becomes more nocturnal and overlap with NP declines from NP to BZ to OBZ, yielding larger mean peak-time shifts outside the core; and (iii) species specificity: responses are heterogeneous, with broadly distributed or human-tolerant taxa showing stronger and more detectable temporal plasticity than specialist species. By empirically evaluating these hypotheses, we provide evidence from South Asia to the global discourse on anthropause and advance understanding of how human activity shapes wildlife temporal ecology in multi-use landscapes [4, 47].

Materials and methods

Study area

The study area encompasses the core area of Bardia National Park (BNP), its buffer zone (BZ), and adjacent human-dominated areas characterized by fragmented forests and agricultural land, including human settlements (hereafter, outside of the buffer zone, OBZ; Fig. 1), a part of Nepal’s Terai Arc Landscape representing a gradient of human pressure and protection intervention. BNP (968 km²) is strictly protected; entry is limited to permit holders, and daily anti-poaching patrols are conducted by the park authority with Nepal Army units [6]. Natural vegetation is dominated by Sal forest (Shorea robusta), interspersed with grassland and riverine forest patches, providing prime habitat for several globally threatened wildlife species such as Asian elephant (Elephas maximus), greater one-horned rhinoceros (Rhinoceros unicornis), tiger (Panthera tigris), leopard (Panthera pardus), swamp deer (Rucervus duvaucelii), sambar (Rusa unicolor), chital (Axis axis), barking deer (Muntiacus vaginalis), four-horned antelope (Tetracerus quadricornis), hog deer (Axis porcinus), wild boar (Sus scrofa) and many more [6, 34]. The surrounding buffer zone (507 km²) retains approximately 60% of its natural vegetation, with the remainder comprising subsistence farming and settlements; community forest user groups manage forest resources (timber, firewood, fodder, and grazing) under operational plans approved by the park authority [6, 10]. In OBZ, natural vegetation is highly fragmented, with substantial areas under agriculture and rural settlements [34].

Data collection

Camera trap survey and species labelling

We implemented a systematic camera trap survey on a 2 × 2 km grid cell across three land-use regimes: core area of Bardia National Park (NP), its buffer zone (BZ), and areas outside the buffer zone (OBZ) (Fig. 1) in both 2019 and 2020 spring seasons (February - May). Each regime had 50 camera sites, and these sites were distributed to maintain a narrow elevation range across the study area. At each grid cell, a single Browning Dark Ops HD Pro (black flash) camera unit was installed close to the grid centroid. Unbaited camera units mounted on trees or posts 50 cm above ground, set up to capture a single picture per trigger with a 1-second delay, and operated continuously over 24 h [19]. During camera installation, any vegetation obstructing the field camera view was removed, and the cameras were routinely inspected to retrieve data, change batteries, and ensure unobstructed views. Cameras were deployed at the same georeferenced locations in both survey periods and remained operational for ~ 10 weeks per field season. For this study, to ensure comparability, analyses were restricted to an identical 30-day period (24 March–24 April) across both survey periods, which coincided with Nepal’s nationwide lockdown (24 March–24 April, 2020). Both sampling periods (24 March – 24 April 2019 and 24 March – 24 April 2020) fall within the late dry season at Bardia. Weather records from the Chisapani (Karnali) Meteorological Station (Station Index no. 0405; [16]) confirm similar weather conditions across both sampling periods: mean daily air temperatures were 27.14 °C (2019) and 26.62 °C (2020) respectively, with no significant inter-annual difference (paired two samples for mean: t31 = 1.67, p = 0.11), and average rainfall during each sampling periods (four rainfall events in each survey window) were 1.03 mm and 0.65 mm, respectively (paired two samples for mean: t31 = 0.39, p = 0.70). No anomalous weather events were recorded in either year that would differentially affect wildlife activity patterns. Camera stations affected by theft, malfunction, or fire were omitted, resulting in 138 operational camera stations per season for the analyses. The 873,757 raw images obtained from 8,334 camera days were thinned to a 1-minute interval between consecutive photographs captured at the same camera station [19], resulting in 89,437 images for annotation. Images were annotated using the Visual Object Tagging Tool (VoTT v2.1.0, Microsoft): each visible object category (wildlife species, humans, vehicles) received a bounding box. Wildlife species were classified following Baral and Shah [2]. Similarly, blank images were labeled “blank,” and uncertain cases due to poor image quality were labeled “unidentified.” Altogether, 27 species of wild mammals were identified (Supplementary Information - S1).

Statistical analysis

Diel activity patterns

Diel activity patterns were analyzed using a paired-station, matched-temporal window design: only stations active in both seasons within the same 30-day window (24 March–24 April) were retained to ensure temporal comparability across land-use gradients (NP, BZ, OBZ). Independent records were defined as detections separated by ≥ 5 min at the same camera station, consistent with independence thresholds commonly used in wildlife camera-trap studies [5, 25, 39]. To verify robustness, we repeated all analyses using 15- and 30-min independence thresholds (Supplementary Information - S1); results were qualitatively unchanged (Supplementary Information – S2). Because the field season 2020 coincided with a nationwide lockdown, we treated NP-2020 as a quasi-undisturbed temporal baseline and made four pre-specified within-species contrasts: NP-2020 vs. BZ-2020, NP-2020 vs. OBZ-2020, NP-2020 vs. BZ-2019, and NP-2020 vs. OBZ-2019. We evaluated H1 (lockdown effect) using NP-2020 as the baseline and BZ-2020/OBZ-2020 as comparisons. Next, we tested H2 (human-pressure gradient) with NP-2020 contrasted to BZ-2019/OBZ-2019, and finally, we examined H3 (species specificity: species-specific nocturnality (% detections between 19:00–05:00 h) and nocturnality change (comparison − NP-2020, percentage points) with 95% bootstrap CIs).

Species were included in a contrast when each land-use gradient–year survey site had ≥ 20 independent detections. Timestamps were converted into time of day and then to radians for circular analyses. For each species contrast, we estimated kernel activity densities and the coefficient of overlap (Δ) using the R package Overlap [45]. Following best practice, we used D-hat 1 (Δ1) when either sample contained < 50 detections and D-hat 4 (Δ4) otherwise [33, 45], and quantified uncertainty with 2,000 bootstrap resamples to obtain 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Directional activity peak-time shifts were computed as the shortest signed circular difference between regimes’ circular means, converted to hours on [− 12, + 12]; by convention, positive values indicate the comparison regime peaks later than NP-2020, and negative values indicate earlier peaks. We obtained 95% CIs for peak shifts by bootstrapping detections within each regime 2,000 times [8, 33]. Because circular means can lie between modes for crepuscular species and shifts near ± 12 h arise from wrap-around, such values are interpreted as near opposite daily schedules (anti-phase) rather than precise clock differences. To distinguish “more nocturnal” from “more diurnal” patterns, we also report a species nocturnality index defined as the percent of detections occurring between 19:00 and 05:00 h, with 95% bootstrap CIs; this interval was chosen because astronomical twilight at Bardia (~ 28°N) ends at approximately 19:00 h and begins at approximately 05:00 h (full darkness) during our survey period; species nocturnality change is expressed as a percentage point difference (comparison − NP‑2020), so negative values indicate the comparison regime is less nocturnal than the baseline. To test whether activity distributions differed significantly between paired zones and years, we applied Watson’s two-sample test for circular data [59], with significance thresholds following Mardia and Jupp [32].

Results

Diel activity patterns within-species were analyzed for taxa with a minimum of ≥ 20 independent detections per land-use regime, irrespective of survey period within the matched 30-day window (24 March–24 April). Per species detection counts by regime and year are summarized in Supplementary Information - S1; brief totals referenced below provide context for sample size of some taxa of lockdown period (e.g., NP: chital 2,787; sambar 132; wild boar 75; BZ: chital 364; barking deer 108; wild boar 152; OBZ: chital 42–46; wild boar 39–55). Similarly, human detections declined in NP (forced lockdown in the NP core area) during the lockdown (93 to 26) but remained high or increased in BZ (2,970 to 3,387) and OBZ (3,373 to 6,124), respectively; we therefore interpret lockdown effects, acknowledging non-uniform changes in human activity across land-use regimes. The results were structured by hypotheses, and plots show species-specific kernel densities for each contrast (black = NP-2020 baseline; blue = comparison), vertical dashed lines at circular means, and panel annotations for overlap (95% CI) and peak-time shift (95% CI); species lacking sufficient data are omitted (Figs. 2, 3, 4 and 5). Species-specific nocturnality (% detections between 19:00–05:00) and nocturnality change (comparison − NP-2020; percentage points) with 95% bootstrap CIs are summarized in Table 1. Watson’s two-sample test statistics (U² and significance level) for each pairwise comparison are reported in the corresponding figure panels alongside the overlap coefficient (Δ) and peak-time shift.

H1 [lockdown effect (NP-2020 vs. BZ-2020; NP-2020 vs. OBZ-2020)]: Several species exhibited quick behavioral relaxation when human activity was reduced: the magnitude of nocturnal displacement decreased relative to pre-lockdown, and within-species overlap with NP-2020 increased (Figs. 2 and 3). For chital, overlap was higher for NP-2020 vs. BZ-2020 (~ 0.81) than for NP-2020 vs. BZ-2019 (~ 0.61), with smaller peak shifts (Fig. 2), indicating a partial return toward baseline-like crepuscular timing during lockdown. Sambar exhibited near-zero peak shift and high overlap between NP and BZ during the lockdown, which is consistent with a stable crepuscular activity (Fig. 2). Where lockdown effects were weak or absent (e.g., species with persistently nocturnal activity outside NP or insufficient OBZ detections), we note this explicitly in H3 below.

H2 [Human-pressure gradient (NP-2020 vs. BZ-2019; NP-2020 vs. OBZ-2019)]: Across herbivores, activity became more nocturnal (Table 1), and within-species, overlap with NP-2020 declined progressively from NP to BZ to OBZ (Figs. 3, 4 and 5). Chital exhibited a large peak-time shift (− 8.4 h) and increased nocturnality relative to NP-2020 (Table 1) in BZ-2019 (Δ ≈ 0.61; Fig. 4) and ~ 6–7 h earlier in OBZ-2019 (Δ ≈ 0.61–0.63; Fig. 5) relative to NP-2020, demonstrating stronger nocturnality and less overlap at higher human pressure. Barking deer showed a peak-time shift of ~ − 7.1 h and increased nocturnality relative to NP-2020 (Table 1) in BZ-2019 (Δ ≈ 0.71; Fig. 4). Hog deer in OBZ-2019 was near anti-phase to NP-2020 (shift ~ + 11.3 h; Δ ≈ 0.39; Fig. 5), and wild boar showed large near anti-phase differences outside NP (shifts ~ − 10 to − 11.4 h; Δ ≈ 0.33–0.53; Figs. 3, 4 and 5; Table 1). Shifts near ± 10–12 h are interpreted as opposite daily schedules (anti-phase) rather than precise clock differences.

H3 [ species-specific patterns]: Responses were heterogeneous across taxa. Chital and barking deer—typically crepuscular in low-disturbance settings—became more nocturnal outside NP and showed reduced overlap with the NP-2020 (Figs. 2, 3, 4 and 5; Table 1). Hog deer diverged strongly: BZ-2020 was much earlier than NP-2020 ( ~ − 9.5 h; Δ ≈ 0.38; Fig. 2), whereas OBZ-2019/-2020 were near anti-phase (Figs. 3 and 5; Table 1). Wild boar remained consistently nocturnal in BZ and OBZ across years with low to moderate overlap (Figs. 3, 4 and 5; Table 1). In contrast, sambar remained comparatively stable and crepuscular with high overlap in NP–BZ 2020 (Δ ≈ 0.82; shift < 1 h; Fig. 2) and only moderate differences in 2019 (Fig. 4). Tigers in 2020 showed low NP–BZ overlap (Δ ≈ 0.31) and near 12 h offsets (Fig. 2); sample sizes outside NP were limited, so precision is cautious.

Discussion

This study quantified within-species diel responses across the land-use gradients (NP–BZ–OBZ) using NP-2020 as the temporal baseline and three a priori hypotheses. First (H1, lockdown effect), several species—notably chital and sambar—became less nocturnal and more similar to the NP-2020 baseline during 2020 than 2019 (higher within-species overlap, smaller peak-time shifts), indicating rapid behavioral relaxation under reduced human activity. Comparable anthropause relaxations have been reported elsewhere, with increased overlap or re-alignment toward diurnal/crepuscular activity during lockdowns in multiple systems [13, 15, 31, 56, 58, 60]. In Nepal, Koju et al., [27] documented freer wildlife movement within the national park during the lockdown, consistent with the patterns we detect temporally in the NP core area. Second (H2, human-pressure gradient), as human pressure increased from NP to BZ to OBZ, herbivores were more nocturnal and exhibited less activity overlap with the NP-2020 (lower overlap), with some taxa approaching near anti-phase schedules outside the NP core—consistent with a landscape-of-fear mechanism [21, 23, 30]. Third (H3, species specificity), species responses were heterogeneous: chital, barking deer, hog deer, and wild boar shifted strongly, whereas sambar remained relatively stable and crepuscular; tiger exhibited low NP–BZ overlap but had limited detections outside NP. We interpret ± 10–12 h peak-time shifts as opposite daily schedules (anti-phase) rather than precise clock differences, and read “more/less nocturnal”. Here, “more nocturnal” refers to a higher percent (%) night (19:00–05:00 h) relative to NP-2020 (Table 1), interpreted alongside overlap in activity pattern and peak-time shifts (Figs. 2, 3, 4 and 5). For context, human detections declined in the NP during the lockdown but remained high or increased in BZ and OBZ, so H1 is interpreted acknowledging non-uniform changes in human activity across land-use regimes (Supplementary Information - S1).

Quantitatively, the gradient signal is clear, and the lockdown attenuation is evident in several taxa. Chital showed higher overlap and smaller peak-time shifts under lockdown (NP-2020 vs. BZ-2020 Δ ≈ 0.81) compared with pre-lockdown (NP-2020 vs. BZ-2019 Δ ≈ 0.61), while OBZ comparisons showed earlier peaks by ~ 6–7 h with moderate overlap (Figs. 2, 3, 4 and 5). Barking deer advanced ~ 7.1–8.9 h in BZ (Δ ≈ 0.71–0.55). Hog deer in outside NP was nearly anti-phase compared to the NP-2020 (OBZ shifts ~ + 9.6 to + 11.3 h; Δ ≈ 0.22–0.39), and wild boar in BZ/OBZ was persistently near anti-phase ( ~ − 10 to − 11.4 h; Δ ≈ 0.33–0.53). In contrast, sambar remained comparatively stable and crepuscular in NP–BZ 2020 (Δ ≈ 0.82; shift < 1 h), with only moderate differences in 2019, while tigers in 2020 showed low NP–BZ overlap (Δ ≈ 0.31) and ~ 12 h offsets (Figs. 2, 3, 4 and 5). Together, these within-species contrasts to NP-2020 demonstrate more nocturnal and less overlapping activity as human pressure increases from NP to BZ to OBZ, and partial attenuation of these displacements during lockdown, where human activity truly declined.

Species-specific mechanisms further clarify why responses diverged among taxa. Chital and barking deer, which are typically crepuscular in low-disturbance areas, became more nocturnal outside of NP. This pattern is consistent with their risk-avoidance behavior near settlements, roads, and agriculture-forest edges. These shifts can reduce direct human encounters, but they may also increase temporal overlap with predators and alter trophic interactions ([12, 21, 23, 43] vêque et al., [49]). Hog deer exhibited pronounced divergence activity from the NP baseline, with very low overlap and nearly anti-phase timing in OBZ, indicating high sensitivity to disturbance regimes and edge conditions; similar nocturnal displacement has been associated with increased human activity, hunting pressure, and habitat degradation in other systems [18, 26, 42]. Likewise, wild boar maintained persistently nocturnal schedules in BZ/OBZ, aligning with responses to disturbance and exploitation risk [18, 23, 26, 42]. In contrast, sambar remained comparatively stable and crepuscular across NP and BZ, although chronic pressures (e.g., poaching) can modulate behavior in some contexts [1, 37]. Tigers exhibited low NP–BZ overlap with ~ 12 h offsets, consistent with temporal segregation from human activity and prey-linked adjustments; long-term telemetry study in the region and elsewhere shows large carnivores can shift toward nocturnality under human pressure [13, 40, 41]. Taken together with evidence that non-exploitative human presence can sometimes provide a temporal “human shield” for prey [28], these species-specific results highlight that both avoidance and shield dynamics may operate, and their expression depends on disturbance context and species’ life histories. These species-specific patterns are consistent with global reports that COVID-19 lockdowns relaxed human pressure enough to shift temporal niches and movements [58, 60] and with Nepal-specific evidence of freer wildlife movement during lockdown [27].

Behavioral flexibility enables wildlife to persist in multi-use landscapes; however, it does not inherently ensure ecological resilience. Sustained displacement toward night can raise energetic costs, reduce foraging efficiency, and affect reproductive fitness [7], especially in taxa that show near anti-phase schedules and low within-species overlap outside NP (e.g., hog deer and wild boar with Δ ≈ 0.22–0.53 and shifts ~ ± 10–11 h; Figs. 3, 4 and 5). This partial reversion toward diurnal activity observed during lockdown suggests that even brief reductions in human disturbances can foster behavioral recovery. Management should incorporate temporal refugia alongside spatial protection: regulate tourism and high-intensity human activities during crepuscular periods, especially in buffer zones and forest-agriculture interfaces where shifts were strongest; reduce nighttime noise and lighting near community forests; and tailor enforcement to nights and edges to maintain low-disturbance envelopes and natural diel patterns. In OBZ planning, integrate spatial-temporal risk into corridor design and community agreements to limit nocturnal disturbance during peak wildlife activity. While our paired, matched-temporal window design and NP-2020 baseline strengthen inference, sample sizes for certain species (e.g., hog deer, tiger) remain limited. Future research should integrate continuous monitoring, physiological stress indicators (e.g., glucocorticoids), and environmental covariates (moon phase, temperature, vegetation cover) to refine the mechanism linking human disturbance to temporal shifts. Where available, human/livestock/vehicle detections can serve as activity proxies to quantify relationships between human pressure and nocturnality. Methodological choices (Δ1/Δ4 rules and bootstrap CIs) were appropriate for the observed sample sizes; near anti-phase shifts (± 10–12 h) should be interpreted as opposite schedules rather than precise hour differences. We recognize that NP, BZ, and OBZ differ in habitat quality, vegetation cover, prey availability, and land use, and these differences persist across both years. Although the COVID-19 lockdown created a true low-human baseline within NP, comparisons across zones inevitably reflect both human pressure and these underlying habitat differences. Separating the effect of human activity from long-standing habitat variation would require replicated within-zone designs where the same sites experience different levels of human disturbance.

Collectively, our within-species contrasts to the NP-2020 baseline show that the NP, BZ, and OBZ gradients elicit strong, species-specific temporal plasticity under human disturbance (lower overlap, larger peak-time shifts—including near ± 10–12 h anti-phase), with partial diurnal/crepuscular recovery under lockdown conditions for some taxa (notably chital and sambar) and persistent nocturnality for others (e.g., hog deer, wild boar) where human pressure remains high. These concurrent temporal responses, together with previously documented spatial patterns in the same system, indicate that mammals respond strongly to the protection gradient, underscoring the central role of the NP core as a spatial refuge [19]. This synthesis supports integrating temporal refugia into management—limiting high-intensity human activities at dawn/dusk and reducing nighttime disturbance at forest–agriculture edges—while pairing activity overlap and peak-time shifts with human/livestock/vehicle detections to monitor effectiveness and guide adaptive, multi-use conservation initiatives. We note that NP-2020 represents a contemporaneous low-human baseline due to strict lockdown-related park closure, whereas human pressure in BZ and OBZ remained high. Thus, contrasts with NP-2020 primarily capture spatial differences in human disturbance rather than within-zone lockdown effects. The attenuation signal of lockdown is therefore inferred across years, where higher overlap in NP2020 vs. BZ2020 than NP2020 vs. BZ2019 indicates behavioral relaxation toward a low-disturbance state.

Conclusion and policy implications

This study demonstrates that terrestrial mammals show strong, species-specific temporal adjustments along a gradient of human pressures in the multi-use Bardia Complex of Nepal’s Terai Arc Landscape. As human pressure increases from the core area of Bardia National Park (NP) to the buffer zone (BZ) and to areas outside the buffer zone (OBZ), species diel activity patterns shift toward greater nocturnality and decreased temporal overlap with the NP baseline, with several species approaching near anti-phase schedules outside the NP. Together with previously documented spatial responses in the same system, our findings show that mammals respond strongly to the protection gradients, highlighting the crucial role of Bardia National Park as a spatial refuge in this multi-use landscape [19]. Chital, barking deer, hog deer, and wild boar exhibit pronounced temporal responses that are consistent with a human-induced landscape of fear, with temporal shifts ranging from ~ 6–11 h. In contrast, the partial reduction of these shifts during the COVID-19 lockdown shows quick behavioral relaxation and highlights how human activity directly affects temporal niches. Although larger species such as sambar maintained relatively stable crepuscular patterns, widespread nocturnality among other herbivores and tigers suggests ongoing anthropogenic pressures that may have potential consequences for predator–prey interactions, foraging efficiency, and energy budgets. All these findings support the notion that temporal responses are sensitive indicators of disturbance regimes and should be considered when planning for multi-use landscape conservation.

Management actions should explicitly target when and where human pressures are highest in accordance with peak wildlife activity. First, incorporate temporally defined refugia into BZ and OBZ by restricting high-intensity human activities during crepuscular periods (e.g., limiting night driving/heavy vehicle movement at night, regulating tourism timing) and reducing nighttime lighting and noise near community forests and edges of forest-agriculture. Second, incorporate spatial-temporal risk into OBZ planning by focusing disturbance away from peak wildlife activity, prioritizing quiet corridors that connect habitat fragments, and limiting nighttime disturbance through co-designing community agreements. Third, monitor and adapt: combine camera-trap activity metrics (overlap, peak-time shifts, percent night) with human, livestock, and vehicle detections as well as environmental variables (e.g., moon phase, temperature, vegetation cover, etc.) to track compliance and efficacy. Finally, long-term programs that integrate camera-trap data with physiological stress indicators (e.g., glucocorticoids) are needed to evaluate whether temporal shifts translate into demographic or fitness effects. Managing both the spatial and temporal dimensions of human-wildlife interactions will be essential to reduce behavioral displacement and sustain ecological processes in this multi-use human-dominated landscape.
