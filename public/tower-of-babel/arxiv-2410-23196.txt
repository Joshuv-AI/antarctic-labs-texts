Abstract
. Any semigroup 𝒮 \mathcal{S} of stochastic matrices induces a semigroup majorization relation ≺ 𝒮 \prec^{\mathcal{S}} on the set Δ n − 1 \Delta_{n-1} of probability n n -vectors. Pick X , Y X,Y at random in Δ n − 1 \Delta_{n-1} : what is the probability that X X and Y Y are comparable under ≺ 𝒮 \prec^{\mathcal{S}} ? We review recent asymptotic ( n → ∞ n\to\infty ) results and conjectures in the case of majorization relation (when 𝒮 \mathcal{S} is the set of doubly stochastic matrices), discuss natural generalisations, and prove a new asymptotic result in the case of majorization, and new exact finite- n n formulae in the case of UT-majorization relation, i.e. when 𝒮 \mathcal{S} is the set of upper-triangular stochastic matrices.



1.
Introduction

The subject of combinatorial and algebraic aspects of partially ordered
sets has received much attention in recent decades in quantum physics, with an explosion
of works in the field, since the (re)discovery of the interplay between certain resource theories
and order relations, most notably between entanglement theory and majorization
[
NV01
]
. See
[
DBG15
,
CG16
,
SAP17
,
dOJCŻK22
]
and references therein.
The basic idea of a (quantum) resource theory
is to study information processing under a restricted set of operations, called
free
.
Thus, a resource theory leads to a partial ordering of states, ranking them from harder to easier to prepare by means of free operations.
This notion of partial order, introduced in physics by Uhlmann
[
AU82
]
, has deep mathematical roots dating back to Schur, Hardy, Littlewood, Pólya, and others.

Consider a finite system whose states can be described by
n
-dimensional probability vectors
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
,
x_{i}\geq 0
,
\sum_{i}x_{i}=1
. These states belong to the probability simplex
\Delta_{n-1}
referred to as the
state space
. Stochastic matrices represent the most general linear transformations between states in
\Delta_{n-1}
. Formally, a resource theory
[
CG19
]
can be constructed by declaring that the set of
free operations
between states in
\Delta_{n-1}
is a given proper semigroup
\mathcal{S}
of stochastic matrices. The set of free operations
\mathcal{S}
leads to an order relation in the state space: we write
x\prec^{\mathcal{S}}y
whenever
x=yP
for some
P\in\mathcal{S}
, that is if the state
y
can be converted into
x
by means of a free operation. In this paper, we adopt the right (row) stochastic matrix convention, where stochastic matrices act on the right on row probability vectors. This is standard in probability theory, where stochastic matrices are also called Markov matrices (see Section
2
). The standard majorization relation
[
MOA79
]
is obtained by choosing
\mathcal{S}
as the semigroup of doubly stochastic matrices. An equivalent characterization of majorization (and other semigroup majorization) is in terms of monotonicity of family of functions that deserve the name of ‘generalised entropies’
[
Rén61
]
. In some situations the family of generalised entropies can be generated by nonnegative linear combinations of a finite family of ‘monotone’ functions.

Given two states
x,y
, for any free operations
P\in\mathcal{S}
, one can certainly decompose
yP
, as a convex combination
px+(1-p)z
for some
z\in\Delta_{n-1}
(with
p
that depends on
P
). The trivial case
p=1
corresponds the case
yP=x
, i.e.,
x\prec^{\mathcal{S}}y
. For general
x,y
it is natural to ask what is the largest value of
p
, denoted with
\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}(x,y)
, that can be achieved in a transformation
yP=px+(1-p)z
, as
P
runs over all free transformations. In the context of resource theories
\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}
can be thought of as the
maximal success probability of conversion
of
y
into
x

[
Vid99
,
DBG15
,
ZMC+17
,
OB19
]
. This happens when a quantum measurement is used in the conversion protocol, which allows one to extract
x
from the convex combination
px+(1-p)z
conditionally on certain outcomes of the measurement, whose occurrence probability sum to
p
. However, it is worth mentioning that measurements may not be free operations in a given quantum resource theory
[
AOP16
]
.

It is clear that in a resource theory, a state
y
is ‘useful’ (or ‘more useful than others’) if from
y
it is possible to reach ‘many’ other states by free operations. For example, in entanglement theory maximally entangled states of a bipartite system can be converted to
every
other state using local operations and classical communication only, and this is why such special states (like Bell states), sometimes referred to as ‘golden state’, are key ingredients in applications. But such golden
states are very special.

We therefore address the following question:
given a fixed resource theory, how
likely is it that two ‘generic’ states can be connected via a free operation in
\mathcal{S}
? More precisely, pick
X
and
Y
at random according to a (natural) probability measure on the state space
\Delta_{n-1}
: what is the probability that
X\prec^{\mathcal{S}}Y
? Our initial intuition is that in the limit of large dimensionality
n\to\infty
, typical pairs of states are so uncorrelated, that they cannot be converted to each other via free operations and hence are incomparable. This
‘comparability is atypical’
statement was first put forward by Nielsen
[
Nie99
]
in quantum theory and was only recently proved in
[
CFFG21
]
and in
[
JKM24
]
for the resource theories of quantum coherence and quantum entanglement, respectively. Given that exact conversion between generic states is typically impossible to achieve by means of free operations, one can consider transformations that succeed with some probability
p
(not necessarily equal to
1
) at transforming the initial state into the desired final state. If we allow nondeterministic transformations (i.e., some probability of failure), what is the typical maximal probability of successful transformation
\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}(X,Y)
for random states
X
and
Y
?

1.1.
Majorization

Let
\prec
be the majorization relation. This is the semigroup majorization corresponding to the set of doubly stochastic matrices (precise definitions are given later). Let also
\Pi(x,y)
be the largest value
p\in[0,1]
such that
px+(1-p)z\prec y
for some
z\in\Delta_{n-1}
.

Theorem A
.

Pick
X,Y
independently and uniformly at random in
\Delta_{n-1}
. Then,

(1.1)

\lim_{n\to\infty}P\left(X\prec Y\right)=0,

and
\Pi(X,Y)
converges in probability of
1
: for all
\epsilon>0
,

(1.2)

\lim_{n\to\infty}P(\Pi(X,Y)<1-\epsilon)=0.

The limit (
1.1
) is a restatement of a claim proved in
[
CFFG21
]
, while the convergence (
1.2
) is a new result (Theorem
4.2
below).
Here is an informal rephrasing: the proportion of pairs of states
(x,y)
such that
x
is majorised by
y
tends to
0
for large
n
; nevertheless in any
\epsilon
-neighbourhood of
x
, if
n
is large, there is a vector
x_{\epsilon}
that is majorized by
y
. The result may, at first, feel paradoxical. A way to
reconcile these facts is to observe that (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) show that
exchanging limits does not work here: for fixed
\epsilon>0
, the
n\to\infty
limit yields
1
, but if
one first takes
\epsilon\to 0
and then
n\to\infty
, then the probability becomes zero.

1.2.
Upper-triangular majorization

Let
\prec^{\mathrm{UT}}
be the semigroup majorization relation corresponding to the semigroup
\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{UT}}
of upper-triangular stochastic matrices (UT-majorization). Let also
\Pi_{\mathrm{UT}}(x,y)
be the largest value
p\in[0,1]
such that
px+(1-p)z\prec^{UT}y
for some
z\in\Delta_{n-1}
.
In physics, the semigroup
\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{UT}}
arises in the zero-temperature limit of thermal operations
[
NG15
]
.

Theorem B
.

Pick
X,Y
independently and uniformly at random in
\Delta_{n-1}
. Then,

(1.3)

P\left(X\prec^{\mathrm{UT}}Y\right)=\frac{1}{n}.

Moreover, the distribution function of
\Pi_{\mathrm{UT}}(X,Y)
is

(1.4)

P(\Pi_{\mathrm{UT}}(X,Y)\leq t)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $t\leq 0$,}\\
\left(1-\dfrac{1}{n}\right)t&\text{if $0<t<1$,}\\
1&\text{if $t\geq 1$}.\end{cases}

A more precise statement of (
1.3
) (for more general measures
\mu_{n}
on
\Delta_{n-1}
) and (
1.4
) is given in Theorems
3.3
and
4.4
later on. Eq. (
1.3
) implies the claimed ‘comparability is atypical’ statement: the probability that two random states can be connected by a upper-triangular stochastic matrix vanishes in the limit
n\to\infty
. Eq. (
1.4
) addresses the question of ‘nondeterministic convertibility’.

We stress that Theorem
B
is an exact finite-
n
result, and in this sense the model of comparability of random vectors with respect to UT-majorization can be considered ‘exactly solvable’: explicit calculations are possible and might give insights to understand the asymptotics of other, non-solvable models.

1.3.
Outline of the paper

In the next section, we give some background on partial orders, semigroup majorization, and orderings induced by function sets. In Section
3
, we formulate the problem, recall known asymptotic results for majorization (Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
) and state the first main theorem on UT-majorization, Theorem
3.3
, which gives an exact universal formula for the probability that two random states
X
and
Y
are comparable with respect to UT-majorization. In Section
4
we consider the maximal success probability of conversion of random states
X
and

. In the case of majorization and UT-majorization relations, we first present and prove an explicit formula for
\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}(\cdot,\cdot)
involving ratios of monotone functions (Propositions
4.1
and
4.3
). In the majorization relation, one expects the existence of a limit in distribution of the random variable
\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}(X,Y)
. We prove that this is indeed the case and we find the limit distribution to be concentrated at
1
(Theorem
4.2
). For UT-majorization, we find the exact finite-
n
distribution function (Theorem

We included some technical results in the appendices
A

2.
Background

We begin by recalling some terminology. The reader is referred to the monograph of Marshall, Olkin, and Arnold
[
MOA79
]
for further details.

2.1.
Order relations

A
partial order
is a pair
\left(\mathcal{A},\sqsubseteq\right)
, where
\mathcal{A}
is a set and
\sqsubseteq
a binary relation on
\mathcal{A}
satisfying

(1)

reflexivity:
for all
x\in\mathcal{A}
,
x\sqsubseteq x
.

(2)

transitivity:
for all
x,y,z\in\mathcal{A}
, if
x\sqsubseteq y
and
y\sqsubseteq z
, then
x\sqsubseteq z
.

(3)

antisymmetry:
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
, if
x\sqsubseteq y
and
y\sqsubseteq x
, then
x=y
.

A
preorder
is a pair
\left(\mathcal{A},\sqsubseteq\right)
satisfying the first two conditions. If
x\sqsubseteq y
we say that ‘
x
is less than
y
’.
If it exists, a
bottom element
of a partial order, is an element
\bot\in\mathcal{A}
satisfying
\bot\sqsubseteq x
for all
x\in\mathcal{A}
. If it exists, a
top element
of a partial order, an element
\top\in\mathcal{A}
satisfying
x\sqsubseteq\top
for all
x\in\mathcal{A}
. If neither
x\sqsubseteq y
nor
y\sqsubseteq x
, we say that
x
and
y
are
incomparable
.
A
total order
is a partial order
\left(\mathcal{A},\sqsubseteq\right)
in which for every pair of elements
x,y
either
x\sqsubseteq y
or
y\sqsubseteq x
holds. The
dual
(or converse) relation
\left(\mathcal{A},\sqsubseteq^{*}\right)
, is defined by setting
x\sqsubseteq^{*}y
if and only if
y\sqsubseteq x
.

For a given
y\in\mathcal{A}
, the set of all
antecedents
of
y
in the relation
\sqsubseteq
is denoted
by
\gamma(y,\sqsubseteq)
. Thus

(2.1)

\gamma(y,\sqsubseteq)=\{x\in\mathcal{A}\colon x\sqsubseteq y\}.

The
graph
of the relation
\sqsubseteq
on
\mathcal{A}
will be denoted by
\Gamma(\mathcal{A},\sqsubseteq)
. Thus

(2.2)

\Gamma(\mathcal{A},\sqsubseteq)=\{(x,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\colon x\in\gamma(y,\sqsubseteq)\}=\{(x,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\colon x\sqsubseteq y\}.

We will simply write
\Gamma
when
\left(\mathcal{A},\sqsubseteq\right)
is clear from the context.

2.2.
Semigroup majorization

For
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we denote by
x^{\downarrow}
the permutation of
x
with the components arranged in nonincreasing order, i.e.,
x_{1}^{\downarrow}\geq x_{2}^{\downarrow}\geq\cdots\geq x_{n}^{\downarrow}
. For
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we say that
x
is
majorized
by
y
(or
y
majorizes
x
) and write
x\prec y
if

(2.3)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}x^{\downarrow}_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}y^{\downarrow}_{i},&\text{for $k=1,\ldots,n-1$}\\
\\
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}x^{\downarrow}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}y^{\downarrow}_{i}.\end{cases}

If the equality in (
2.3
) is replaced by a corresponding inequality, we say that
x
is
weakly (sub)majorized
by
y
and write
x\prec_{w}y
, i.e., for
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
:

(2.4)

x\prec_{w}y\quad\text{if}\quad\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}x^{\downarrow}_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}y^{\downarrow}_{i},\quad\text{$k=1,\ldots,n$}.

It can be seen
[
Mal05
]
that
x\prec y
if and only if, for
j=1,\ldots,n
,

(2.5)

\operatorname{conv}\{x_{i_{1}}+\cdots+x_{i_{j}}\colon 1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{j}\leq n\}\subset\operatorname{conv}\{y_{i_{1}}+\cdots+y_{i_{j}}\colon 1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{j}\leq n\},

where
\operatorname{conv}(A)
denotes the convex hull of a set
A
(the smallest convex set containing
A
).

By a celebrated result by Hardy, Polya and Littlewood
[
HLP52
]
,
x\prec y
if and only if
x=yD
for some doubly stochastic matrix
D
. This relation is a preorder because the set of
n\times n
doubly stochastic matrices contains the identity and is closed under multiplication. Other preorders are obtained if the set of doubly stochastic matrices are replaced by another semigroup
\mathcal{S}
of matrices with identity
[
MOA79
]
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and let
\mathcal{S}
be a semigroup of linear transformations (matrices) mapping
\mathcal{A}
to
\mathcal{A}
. A vector
x\in\mathcal{A}
is said to be
semigroup majorized
by
y
, written
x\prec^{\mathcal{S}}y
, if
x=yS
for some
S\in\mathcal{S}
. From the semigroup property of
\mathcal{S}
, it follows that
\left(\mathcal{A},\prec^{\mathcal{S}}\right)
is a preorder, but not a partial order in general. See
[
Joe90
,
vED22
,
vEM24
]
for further details.

Recall that a square matrix
P
is row (sub)stochastic if all entries are nonnegative and the row sums are (less than or) equal to
1
. If we denote the row vector
e=(1,\ldots,1)
, we have that a square matrix
P
with nonnegative entries is row (sub)stochastic if
Pe^{\prime}=e^{\prime}
(
Pe^{\prime}\leq e^{\prime}
), where
e^{\prime}
denotes the transposed vector of
e
and
\leq
is to be understood component-wise.

We define the following types of matrices with nonnegative entries (mapping
\mathcal{A}={\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{n}
to itself):

•

Doubly stochastic matrices (DSM)
:

row stochastic matrices
D
with column sums equal to
1
, i.e.,
eD=e
.

•

Weakly doubly stochastic matrices (wDSM)
:

row substochastic matrices
D
with column sums at most
1
, i.e.,
eD\leq e
.

•

Upper triangular stochastic matrices (UTSM)
:

row stochastic matrices
S=(s_{ij})
with
s_{ij}=0
whenever
i>j
.

•

Weakly upper triangular stochastic matrices (wUTSM)
:

row substochastic matrices
S=(s_{ij})
with
s_{ij}=0
whenever
i>j
.

The sets of
n\times n
DSMs, wDSM, UTSMs, and wUTSMs, are all semigroups with identity.
We denote the semigroup majorization of DSMs, wDSM, UTSMs, and wUTSMs by

\prec
,
\prec_{w}
,
\prec^{\mathrm{UT}}
,
\prec^{\mathrm{UT}}_{w}
for short, and we say that
x
is majorized, weakly majorized, UT-majorized, weakly UT-majorized by
y
, whenever
x\prec y
,
x\prec_{w}y
,
x\prec^{\mathrm{UT}}y
, or
x\prec^{\mathrm{UT}}_{w}y
, respectively.

Birkhoff’s theorem
[
Bir46
]
states that the set of doubly stochastic matrices is the convex hull of all permutations matrices. As a consequence,
x\prec y
if and only if
x
lies in the convex hull of the
n!
permutations of
y

(this is a restatement of (
2.5
)).
The set of
n\times n
doubly substochastic matrices is the convex hull of the set of
n\times n
matrices that have at most one unit in each row and each column, and all other entries are zero
[
Mir59
]
.
Therefore
x\prec_{w}y
if and only if
x
is in the convex hull of the points
(\eta_{1}y_{\pi(1)},\ldots,\eta_{n}y_{\pi(n)})
, where
\pi
is a permutation and each
\eta_{i}
is
0
or
1
.

Let
\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{UT}}
and
\mathcal{S}_{w}^{\mathrm{UT}}
be the sets of UTSMs and wUTSMs, respectively. (all entries are nonnegative and the row sums are equal to
1
). It is easy to verify that both
\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{UT}}
and
\mathcal{S}_{w}^{\mathrm{UT}}
are convex sets. The extremal points of
\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{UT}}
are upper-triangular matrices with a single entry
1
in each row and all other entries zero. The extremal points of
\mathcal{S}_{w}^{\mathrm{UT}}
are upper-triangular matrices with at most one entry
1
in each row and all other entries zero.

To summarise, the antecedents of
y\in\Delta_{n-1}
in
\mathcal{A}={\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{n}
for the preorders
\prec
,
\prec_{w}
,
\prec^{\mathrm{UT}}
and
\prec_{w}^{\mathrm{UT}}
are

(2.6)

\gamma(y,\prec)=\operatorname{conv}\{\left(y_{\pi(1)},\ldots,y_{\pi(n)}\right)\colon\pi\in S_{n}\},

(2.7)

\gamma(y,\prec_{w})=\operatorname{conv}\{\left(\eta_{1}y_{\pi(1)},\ldots,\eta_{n}y_{\pi(n)}\right)\colon\pi\in S_{n},\eta_{i}\in\{0,1\}\},

(2.8)

\gamma(y,\prec^{\mathrm{UT}})=\operatorname{conv}\left\{\left(\sum_{j_{1}\in f^{-1}(1)}y_{j_{1}},\ldots,\sum_{j_{n}\in f^{-1}(n)}y_{j_{n}}\right)\colon f\colon[n]\to[n]\,\,\text{s.t. $f(i)\geq i$ for all $i$ }\right\}.

(2.9)

\gamma(y,\prec_{w}^{\mathrm{UT}})=\operatorname{conv}\Biggl\{\left(\sum_{j_{1}\in f^{-1}(1)}\eta_{j_{1}}y_{j_{1}},\ldots,\sum_{j_{n}\in f^{-1}(n)}\eta_{j_{n}}y_{j_{n}}\right)\colon\\
f\colon[n]\to[n]\,\,\text{s.t. $f(i)\geq i$ for all $i$, and }\eta\in\{0,1\}^{n}\Biggr\}.

Example 2.1
.

For
n=3
, let
y=(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})
. Then,

(2.10)

\gamma(y,\prec)=\operatorname{conv}\{\left(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}\right),\left(y_{2},y_{1},y_{3}\right),\left(y_{2},y_{3},y_{1}\right),\left(y_{1},y_{3},y_{2}\right),\left(y_{3},y_{1},y_{2}\right),\left(y_{3},y_{2},y_{1}\right)\},

\left(0,y_{2},y_{1}+y_{3}\right),\left(0,y_{1},y_{2}+y_{3}\right),\left(0,0,y_{1}+y_{2}+y_{3}\right)\}.

Remark 2

(Physics)
.

Another prominent example of semigroup majorization is obtained from the semigroup
\mathcal{S}^{q}
of
q
-stochastic matrices
defined as follows.
Let
q
be a nonnegative vector. A row stochastic matrix
S
is said to be
q
-stochastic if

.

x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
we say that

is

, and write

e_{k}
is a top element if and only if
q_{k}
is minimal in
q
. See
[
vED22
, Theorem 3]
. For
q=e
we get the set of DSMs.
Notice that
\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{UT}}
is a proper subsemigroup of
\mathcal{S}^{e_{n}}
,
e_{n}=(0,\ldots,0,1)
.

If we write
q=\left(e^{-\beta E_{1}},\ldots,e^{-\beta E_{n}}\right)
for some sequence of energy levels
E_{1}>\cdots>E_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and inverse temperature
\beta>0
, then
q
-majorization is equivalent to
thermomajorization

[
HO13
,
Shi20
]
, as
\mathcal{S}^{q}
is the semigroup that fixes the ‘Gibbs state’
q
. At high temperature
\beta\to 0
, thermomajorization reduces to standard majorization; at zero temperature
\beta\to\infty
, thermomajorization relation collapses to UT-majorization. Note that in physics it is customary to order the energy levels in increasing order
E_{1}<\cdots<E_{n}
. To make a correspondence with the existing literature
[
HO13
,
NG15
]
it is enough, by taking the transpose, to consider column-stochastic (rather than row-stochastic) matrices acting on column probability vectors. Note that transposing an upper-triangular matrix yields a lower-triangular matrix.

Remark 3

(Combinatorics)
.

In the realm of discrete combinatorics, the counterpart of majorization is called
dominance order
for integer partitions or integer compositions. There exists a natural extension due to Narayana
[
Nar59
]
that can be used in the continuous setting to provide yet another generalisation of majorization. Let
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
. For
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the vector
x
is said to be
s
-dominated
by
y
, and we write
x\prec^{s}y
if

(2.13)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}x^{\downarrow}_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}y^{\downarrow}_{i}\leq s,\text{for $k=1,\ldots,n$}.

When
s=0
, the
s
-dominance order is the weak majorization order.

2.3.
Orderings induced by function sets

Here is another convenient description of partial orders induced by functions that can be thought of as generalised entropies.
Let
\Phi
be a set of real-valued functions
\phi
defined on a subset
\mathcal{A}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. For
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
we write

(2.14)

x\prec_{\Phi}y\quad\text{ iff }\quad\phi(x)\leq\phi(y),\,\text{ for all }\phi\in\Phi.

If (
2.14
) holds, then it holds also for all linear combinations of functions in
\Phi
with positive coefficients. These linear combinations form a cone that is said to be generated by
\Phi
.

For instance, let
\Phi:=\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n+1}\}
, where

(2.15)

\phi_{k}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}^{\downarrow},\quad k=1,\dots,n,\qquad\phi_{n+1}(x)=-\phi_{n}(x).

Then
\prec_{\Phi}
is equivalent to
\prec
according to definition (
2.3
).
The cone generated by
\Phi_{w}:=\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n}\}
, with
\phi_{k}
in (
2.15
) induces the weak majorization preorder on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
.

Similarly, the sets
\Phi^{\mathrm{UT}}_{w}:=\{\phi_{1}^{\mathrm{UT}},\dots,\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{UT}}\}
, and
\Phi^{\mathrm{UT}}:=\Phi^{\mathrm{UT}}_{w}\cup\{\phi_{n+1}^{\mathrm{UT}}\},

where

(2.16)

\phi_{k}^{\mathrm{UT}}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i},\quad k=1,\dots,n,\qquad\phi_{n+1}^{\mathrm{UT}}(x)=-\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{UT}}(x),

generate the cones inducing weak UT-majorization and UT majorization, respectively.

3.
Probability that generic states are comparable

Let
(\mathcal{A},\sqsubseteq)
be a preorder with
\mathcal{A}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
and let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\mathcal{A}
. If
\Gamma
is measurable, we define the
probability
of
\Gamma
as

(3.1)

P(\Gamma)=\mu{\otimes}\mu\left(\Gamma\right)=\mu{\otimes}\mu\left(\{(x,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\colon x\sqsubseteq y\}\right).

The above quantity can be interpreted as follows. Pick
X,Y
independently at random according to the probability measure
\mu
. Then
P(\Gamma)=P(X\sqsubseteq Y)
is the probability that
X
is less than
Y
. By symmetry, this is also equal to the probability
P(Y\sqsubseteq X)
, since
X,Y
are independent and identically distributed.

In this paper we consider the case of partial orders on the simplex of discrete probability vectors.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and consider the
(n-1)
-dimensional simplex

(3.2)

\Delta_{n-1}:=\left\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\colon x_{i}\geq 0,\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=1\right\}=\operatorname{conv}\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\},

where
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
stand for the unit vectors of the
standard orthonormal basis of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

With this notation, consider the poset
\left(\mathcal{A},\sqsubseteq\right)=\left(\Delta_{n-1},\prec\right)
, with

\prec
the preorder of majorization.
Note that the bottom element is
\bot=\frac{1}{n}(e_{1}+\cdots+e_{n})=(1/n,\ldots,1/n)
, while the top elements are
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
.
In
[
CFFG21
]
, Cunden, Facchi, Florio and Gramegna, proved the following asymptotic result.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
CFFG21
]
)
.

Let
\Gamma_{n}=\Gamma\left(\Delta_{n-1},\prec\right)
, and
\mu_{n}
the uniform probability measure on the simplex
\Delta_{n-1}
. Write
P(\Gamma_{n})=\mu_{n}\otimes\mu_{n}(\Gamma_{n})
. Then,

(3.3)

\lim_{n\to\infty}P(\Gamma_{n})=0.

This statement is Eq. (
1.1
) of Theorem
A
. The proof of Theorem
3.1
in
[
CFFG21
]
uses Kolmogorov’s 0-1 law and hence it is fundamentally
non-quantitative.
Recently, Jain, Kwan and Michelen
[
JKM24
]
proved the following quantitative (though presumably not optimal) estimate.

Theorem 3.2

(
[
JKM24
]
)
.

With the same notation of Theorem
3.1
, there is a constant
C>0
such that

(3.4)

P(\Gamma_{n})\leq C\left(\log n/n\right)^{1/16}.

The same authors
[
JKM24
]
, proved the analogue of Theorem
3.1
when the measure
\mu_{n}
on
\Delta_{n-1}
comes from the fixed-trace Wishart measure of random matrix theory, thus settling a conjecture put forward by Nielsen
[
Nie99
]
and numerically investigated in
[
CFFG20
]
in the context of entanglement theory.

Consider now the poset
\left(\mathcal{A},\sqsubseteq\right)=\left(\Delta_{n-1},\prec^{\mathrm{UT}}\right)
; in this case the bottom element is
\bot=e_{n}=(0,0,\ldots,1)
, while the top element is
\top=e_{1}=(1,0,\ldots,0)
.
We state the following simple theorem on the exact probability of
\Gamma_{n}
for UT-majorization.

Theorem 3.3
.

Consider the graph
\Gamma_{n}=\Gamma\left(\Delta_{n-1},\prec^{{\mathrm{UT}}}\right)
and let
\mu_{n}
be an absolutely continuous and permutation symmetric probability measure on
\Delta_{n-1}
. Then, for all
n\geq 2
,

(3.5)

P(\Gamma_{n})=\frac{1}{n}.

When
\mu_{n}
is the uniform measure on the simplex, Theorem (
3.3
) provides Eq. (
1.3
) of Theorem
B
.

4.
Maximal success probability of conversion

Note that for
x,y\in\Delta_{n-1}
,
x\prec y
if and only if
x\prec_{w}y
, since the normalization condition automatically guarantees the equality constraint in Eq. (
2.3
).

For
x,y\in\Delta_{n-1}
, it can happen that
x
is not majorized by
y
, but it is always possible to find
p\in[0,1]
and
z\in\Delta_{n-1}
such that

(4.1)

px+(1-p)z\prec y.

Let

(4.2)

\Pi:\Delta_{n-1}\times\Delta_{n-1}\to[0,1],

with
\Pi(x,y)
defined as the largest
p\in[0,1]
such that (
4.1
) holds for some
z\in\Delta_{n-1}
. Equivalently,
\Pi(x,y)
is the largest
p
such that
yD=px+(1-p)z
for some DSM
D
and some vector
z\in\Delta_{n-1}
.

The function
\Pi
can be expressed in terms of the monotones
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n}
as defined in (
2.15
).

Proposition 4.1
.

(4.3)

\Pi(x,y)=\min_{1\leq k\leq n}\frac{\phi_{k}(y)}{\phi_{k}(x)}.

where
\phi_{k}
are defined in (
2.15
).

Proof.

Let
p^{*}=\min_{1\leq k\leq n}\frac{\phi_{k}(y)}{\phi_{k}(x)}
.
We first show that

(4.4)

p^{*}=\max\{p\in[0,1]:px\prec_{w}y\}.

Let
k^{*}\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
p^{*}=\frac{\phi_{k^{*}}(y)}{\phi_{k^{*}}(x)}
. Then,

\phi_{k}(p^{*}x)=p^{*}\phi_{k}(x)=\left(\frac{\phi_{k^{*}}(y)}{\phi_{k^{*}}(x)}\right)\phi_{k}(x)\leq\left(\frac{\phi_{k}(y)}{\phi_{k}(x)}\right)\phi_{k}(x)=\phi_{k}(y),\,\text{for all $k=1,\ldots,n$}.

Hence
p^{*}x\prec_{w}y
. Now suppose that there is
p^{\prime}>p^{*}
such that
p^{\prime}x\prec_{w}y
. Then
\phi_{k}(p^{\prime}x)>\phi_{k}(p^{*}x)
for all
k=1,\ldots,n
. Choosing
k=k^{*}
,

\phi_{k^{*}}(p^{\prime}x)>\phi_{k^{*}}(p^{*}x)=p^{*}\phi_{k^{*}}(x)=\phi_{k^{*}}(y).

we get a contradiction. Hence
p^{*}
is the largest value
p\in[0,1]
such that
px\prec_{w}y
, i.e., such that

yP=px

for some wDSM
P
.

We can now prove the claim (
4.3
). The case
p^{*}=1
is trivial. Assume
p^{*}<1
. Reasoning as above, we first show that
\Pi(x,y)\leq p^{*}
. Suppose by contradiction that there is
p^{\prime}>p^{*}
such that
p^{\prime}x+(1-p^{\prime})z\prec y
, for some
z\in\Delta_{n-1}
. Then,
\phi_{k}(y)\geq\phi_{k}(p^{\prime}x+(1-p^{\prime})z)\geq\phi_{k}(p^{\prime}x)>\phi_{k}(p^{*}x)
for all
k=1,\ldots,n
and, choosing
k=k^{*}
, we get a contradiction.

It remains to show that there exists a DSM
D
such that
yD=p^{*}x+(1-p^{*})z
with
z\in\Delta_{n-1}
. By (
4.4
) we know that
yP=p^{*}x
with
P
wDSM. A theorem attributed to von Neumann (see
[
MOA79
, P. 37]
) ensures that there exists a DSM
D
such that
P_{ij}\leq D_{ij}
for all
i,j=1,\ldots,n
. Note that
P^{\prime}:=D-P
is also a wDSM. Then,

(4.5)

yD=yP+y(D-P)=p^{*}x+(1-p^{*})z,

where
z=\frac{1}{1-p^{*}}y(D-P)\in\Delta_{n-1}
.
∎

Note that

(4.6)

1/n\leq\Pi(x,y)\leq 1.

The minimum is achieved for
x=e_{1}=(1,0,\dots,0)
and
y=(1/n,\ldots,1/n)
.
On the other hand,
\Pi(x,y)=1
if and only if
x\prec y
, and so

(4.7)

\Gamma(\Delta_{n-1},\prec)=\{(x,y)\in\Delta_{n-1}\times\Delta_{n-1}\colon\Pi(x,y)=1\}.

Define

(4.8)

P(\Pi(X,Y)\leq t)=\mu\otimes\mu\left(\left\{(x,y)\in\Delta_{n-1}\times\Delta_{n-1}\colon\Pi(x,y)\leq t\right\}\right).

The next result is a restatement of Eq. (
1.2
) of Theorem
A
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\mu_{n}
be the uniform probability measure on the simplex
\Delta_{n-1}
. Then,

(4.9)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pi(X,Y)=1,

in probability.

The proof is based on formula (
4.3
). The idea is that if
X
is uniform in the simplex, then for each fixed
\epsilon>0
,
\phi_{k}(X)
is essentially equal to
(1\pm\epsilon)
times its mean, aside from an event with probability that is small even when we sum over
k=1,\ldots,n
. This then shows that
\phi_{k}(X)/\phi_{k}(Y)=(1\pm\epsilon)
for all
k
with probability tending to
1
. Since this holds for each
\epsilon>0
, it shows
\Pi(X,Y)
converges to
1
in probability.
The rate of convergence (i.e. how small
\epsilon
can be taken it we allow it to decrease to
0
as

) seems quite slow; from the proof is appears that
\epsilon
must be at least
1/\mathrm{poly}(\log n)
. It is therefore tricky to see evidence of the convergence (
4.9
) in a simulation without taking
n
to be fairly large. See Figure

\Pi_{\mathrm{UT}}:\Delta_{n-1}\times\Delta_{n-1}\to[0,1],

\Pi_{\mathrm{UT}}(x,y)=\min_{1\leq k\leq n}\frac{\phi_{k}^{\mathrm{UT}}(y)}{\phi_{k}^{\mathrm{UT}}(x)}.

.

Let

Remark 5
.

Equation (

5.
Proofs

In this section we will use some standard notation and terminology. For two random variables
X
and
Y
, we write
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Y
to denote
identity in distribution
. We say that
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are
independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.)
random variables, if they are jointly independent and
X_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}X_{j}
for all
i,j
. We say that
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are
exchangeable
, if their joint law is invariant under permutations,
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(X_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,X_{\sigma(n)})
, for all
\sigma\in S_{n}
. Finally,
Z
is an
exponential
random variable
with rate
1
, if it has density
f_{Z}(z)=e^{-z}
for
z\geq 0
, and zero otherwise.

5.1.
Proof of Theorem
3.3

Pick
X,Y
independently according to
\mu
. Since UT-majorization is equivalent to unordered majorization, we want to compute

(5.1)

P\left(X\leq^{\mathrm{UT}}Y\right)=P\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(Y_{i}-X_{i})\geq 0,\text{for all $k=1,\ldots n$}\right).

Let
Z:=Y-X
and define

(5.2)

S_{0}=0,\quad S_{k}=S_{k-1}+Z_{k},\quad\text{for $k=1,\ldots,n$}.

Note that
(S_{k})_{0\leq k\leq n}
is a random bridge starting and ending at
0
, i.e.,
S_{0}=S_{n}=0
.
Hence, the probability (
5.1
) can be seen as the
persistence probability
above level
0
of the random bridge
(S_{k})_{k}
:

(5.3)

P\left(X\leq^{\mathrm{UT}}Y\right)=P\left(S_{k}\geq 0,\text{for all $k=1,\ldots,n$}\right).

The steps
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
are exchangeable and we have
P\left(S_{k}=0\right)=0
for all
k=1,\ldots n-1
, since
\mu
is absolutely continuous. Hence, by Sparre-Andersen theorem
[
And53
, Theorem 2]
, denoting by
N_{n}
the number of sums
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}
which are
>0
, we have

P\left(N_{n}=m\right)=\frac{1}{n},

for all
m=1,\ldots,n
. In particular
P\left(N_{n}=n\right)=P\left(S_{k}\geq 0,\text{for all $k=1,\ldots,n$}\right)=1/n
.

5.2.
Preparatory propositions

The starting point
is that if
X
is uniformly distributed on the simplex, then its components and the decreasing rearrangement
of its components have a closed form description in terms of independent exponential random variables (see, for instance,
[
Dev86
]
or
[
JKM24
, Theorems 3.1 and 3.2]
). These classical results provide convenient representations of the monotones
\phi_{k}(X)
and
\phi_{k}^{\mathrm{UT}}(X)
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
be a uniform point on the simplex
\Delta_{n-1}
, and
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
be i.i.d. exponential random variables with rate
1
.

(1)

Denote
\phi_{k}(X):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}X^{\downarrow}_{i}
for
k=1,\ldots,n
. Then,

(5.4)

\left(\phi_{1}(X),\phi_{2}(X),\ldots,\phi_{n}(X)\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\frac{1}{V_{n}}\left(V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{n}\right),

where
V_{k}=a_{k,1}Z_{1}+a_{k,2}Z_{2}+\cdots+a_{k,n}Z_{n}
, with

(5.5)

a_{k,i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{for $i\leq k$}\\
\frac{k}{i}&\text{for $i>k$}\end{cases}.

(2)

Denote
\phi_{k}^{\mathrm{UT}}(X):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}
for
k=1,\ldots,n
. Then,

(5.6)

\left(\phi_{1}^{\mathrm{UT}}(X),\phi_{2}^{\mathrm{UT}}(X),\ldots,\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{UT}}(X)\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\frac{1}{W_{n}}\left(W_{1},W_{2},\ldots,W_{n}\right),

where
W_{k}=b_{k,1}Z_{1}+b_{k,2}Z_{2}+\cdots+b_{k,n}Z_{n}
, with

(5.7)

b_{k,i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{for $i\leq k$}\\
0&\text{for $i>k$}\end{cases}.

A more convenient representation for the monotones
\phi_{k}^{\mathrm{UT}}(X)
is provided by a connection between partial sums of a uniform point on the simplex and the order statistics of independent uniform random variables. If
U_{1},\dots,U_{n-1}
are
{n-1}
independent random variables, we denote the corresponding order statistics with
U_{({n-1},1)},\ldots U_{({n-1},{n-1})}
, where
U_{({n-1},i)}
is the
i-
th smallest among the
U_{j}
’s, that is the
{n-1}
random variables are rearranged in nondecreasing order:
U_{({n-1},1)}\leq\cdots\leq U_{({n-1},{n-1})}
.

Then, using classical representation theorems for order statistics (see, e.g.,
[
HR54
,
ABN08
]
) we have the following.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
be a uniform point on the simplex
\Delta_{n-1}
, and
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n-1}
be i.i.d. uniform random variables on the interval
[0,1]
. Denote
\phi_{k}^{\mathrm{UT}}(X):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}
for
k=1,\ldots,n
. Then,

(5.8)

\left(\phi_{1}^{\mathrm{UT}}(X),\phi_{2}^{\mathrm{UT}}(X),\ldots,\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{UT}}(X)\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\left(U_{({n-1},1)},\dots,U_{({n-1},{n-1})},1\right).

Since
X_{j}=\phi_{j}^{\mathrm{UT}}(X)-\phi_{j-1}^{\mathrm{UT}}(X)
(with
\phi_{0}^{\mathrm{UT}}(X):=0
), this is also equivalent to say that
X
has the same distribution of the sequence of spacings between order statistics of
{n-1}
uniform random variables.

Remark 6
.

If
U_{1},\dots,U_{n-1}
are i.i.d. uniform random variables on
[0,1]
,

(U_{(n-1,1)},\dots,U_{(n-1,n-1)})
is a uniform point over the simplex
\mathcal{E}_{n-1}:=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}:0\leq u_{1}\leq u_{2}\leq\cdots\leq u_{n-1}\leq 1\}
.

5.3.
Proof of Theorem
4.2

The technical ingredient to prove Theorem
4.2
is the following bound.

Proposition 5.3

(Bernstein’s inequality (see, for example,
[
Ver18
, Theorem 2.8.2]
))
.

Let
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
be i.i.d. exponential random variables with rate
1
, and let
V=a_{1}Z_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}Z_{n}
for some coefficients
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. There is an absolute constant
c>0
such that

(5.9)

P\left(|V-EV|\geq t\right)\leq\exp\left(-c\min\left(\frac{t^{2}}{a_{1}^{2}+\cdots+a_{n}^{2}},\frac{t}{\max|a_{i}|}\right)\right),

for every
t\geq 0
.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
V_{k}
defined as in (
5.4
)-(
5.5
). Then,
for all
k\leq n
,

(5.10)

P\left(|V_{k}-EV_{k}|\geq\epsilon EV_{k}\right)\leq\exp\left(-c\min\left(\epsilon^{2}{A_{k}},\epsilon B_{k}\right)\right),

where

(5.11)

A_{k}:=\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{k,i}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{k,i}^{2}},\quad B_{k}:=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{k,i}}{\max_{1\leq i\leq n}a_{k,i}}.

We denote by
H_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{i}
the
n
-th harmonic number, and
H_{n,m}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{i^{m}}
the
n
-th harmonic number of order
m
.

Lemma 5.5
.

For all
k=1,\ldots,n
,

(5.12)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{k,i}=k\left(1+H_{n}-H_{k}\right),\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{k,i}^{2}=k\left(1+k(H_{n,2}-H_{k,2})\right),\quad\max_{1\leq i\leq n}a_{k,i}=1.

Lemma 5.6
.

We have

(5.13)

\displaystyle A_{k}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\log\frac{n}{2}\right)^{2}+\alpha_{n}(k-1),

(5.14)

\displaystyle B_{k}

\displaystyle\geq\log\frac{n}{2}+\beta_{n}(k-1)

where

(5.15)

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}=\frac{n}{n-1}\left(1+\frac{\left(1+\log\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\right)^{2}}{2n-1}\right),\qquad\beta_{n}=1-\frac{\log\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)}{n-1}.

Proof.

We start from the following bounds,

(5.16)

\displaystyle H_{n}-H_{k}=\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}\frac{1}{i}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{k+1}^{n+1}\frac{dx}{x}=\log\frac{n+1}{k+1},

(5.17)

\displaystyle H_{n,2}-H_{k,2}=\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}\frac{1}{i^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{k}^{n}\frac{dx}{x^{2}}=\frac{n-k}{nk}.

Therefore,

(5.18)

\displaystyle A_{k}=\frac{k\left(1+H_{n}-H_{k}\right)^{2}}{\left(1+k(H_{n,2}-H_{k,2})\right)}\geq\frac{nk}{(2n-k)}\left(1+\log\frac{n+1}{k+1}\right)^{2}.

The function
x\mapsto g(x):=\frac{nx}{(2n-x)}\left(1+\log\frac{n+1}{x+1}\right)^{2}
is concave for
x\in[1,n]
. Hence, it can be bounded from below by the linear interpolation
g(1)+\frac{g(n)-g(1)}{n-1}(x-1)
. This gives

(5.19)

\displaystyle A_{k}\geq\frac{n\left(1+\log\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\right)^{2}}{2n-1}+\frac{\left(n+\frac{n\left(1+\log\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\right)^{2}}{2n-1}\right)}{n-1}(k-1),

and hence (
5.13
). The proof of (
5.14
) for
B_{k}
is similar. From (
5.16
),

(5.20)

\displaystyle B_{k}=k\left(1+H_{n}-H_{k}\right)\geq k\left(1+\log\frac{n+1}{k+1}\right).

The map
x\mapsto h(x):=x\left(1+\log\frac{n+1}{x+1}\right)
is concave for
x\in[1,n]
. We can therefore write

(5.21)

\displaystyle B_{k}\geq\log\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)+1+\frac{\left(n-\log\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)-1\right)}{n-1}(k-1),

and hence (
5.14
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

We want to show that

P\left(\Pi(X,Y)<1-\epsilon\right)\to 0,
as
n\to\infty
.
Let
\epsilon\in(0,1)
.
We have

P\left(\Pi(X,Y)<1-\epsilon\right)=P\left(\min_{1\leq k\leq n}\frac{\phi_{k}(Y)}{\phi_{k}(X)}<1-\epsilon\right)\\
=P\left(\frac{\phi_{k}(Y)}{\phi_{k}(X)}<1-\epsilon,\,\,\text{for some $k\leq n$}\right)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}P\left(\frac{\phi_{k}(Y)}{\phi_{k}(X)}<1-\epsilon\right),

by the union bound.
Using the representation of Proposition
5.1
, we want to bound

(5.22)

P\left(\frac{\phi_{k}(Y)}{\phi_{k}(X)}<1-\epsilon\right)=P\left(\frac{V_{k}(Y)}{V_{n}(Y)}<(1-\epsilon)\frac{V_{k}(X)}{V_{n}(X)}\right)\leq\delta_{n,k},

so that,

(5.23)

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\delta_{n,k}\to 0,\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$}.

By Corollary
5.4
,

\left|V_{n}-n\right|\leq n\epsilon
with probability at least
1-e^{-c\epsilon^{2}n}
.
Hence,

\displaystyle P\left(\frac{V_{k}(Y)}{V_{n}(Y)}<(1-\epsilon)\frac{V_{k}(X)}{V_{n}(X)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq P\left(V_{k}(Y)<(1-\epsilon)\frac{V_{n}(Y)}{V_{n}(X)}V_{k}(X),\quad(V_{n}(X),V_{n}(Y))\in\left((1-\epsilon)n,(1+\epsilon)n\right)^{2}\right)+2e^{-c\epsilon^{2}n}

\displaystyle\leq P\left(V_{k}(Y)<(1+\epsilon)V_{k}(X)\right)+2e^{-c\epsilon^{2}n}

\displaystyle\leq P\left(V_{k}(Y)<(1+\epsilon)V_{k}(X),\quad V_{k}(X)\in\left((1-\epsilon)EV_{k}(X),(1+\epsilon)EV_{k}(X)\right)\right)

\displaystyle+P\left(V_{k}(Y)<(1+\epsilon)V_{k}(X),\quad V_{k}(X)\notin\left((1-\epsilon)EV_{k}(X),(1+\epsilon)EV_{k}(X)\right)\right)+2e^{-c\epsilon^{2}n}

\displaystyle\leq P\left(V_{k}(Y)<(1+\epsilon)^{2}EV_{k}(X)\right)+e^{-c\min\left(\epsilon^{2}A_{k},\epsilon B_{k}\right)}+2e^{-c\epsilon^{2}n}

\displaystyle\leq P\left(|V_{k}(Y)-EV_{k}(Y)|<3\epsilon EV_{k}(Y)\right)+e^{-c\min\left(\epsilon^{2}A_{k},\epsilon B_{k}\right)}+2e^{-c\epsilon^{2}n}

\displaystyle{\leq 4e^{-c\min\left(\epsilon^{2}A_{k},\epsilon B_{k}\right)},}

where in the last line we used
A_{k}\leqslant n
.

Therefore we have the bound (
5.22
) with

(5.24)

\delta_{n,k}:=4e^{-c\epsilon^{2}A_{k}}+4e^{-c\epsilon B_{k}}.

It remains to show (
5.23
).
For this we use Lemma
5.6
:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}e^{-c\epsilon^{2}A_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-c(\epsilon\log(n/2))^{2}/2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}e^{-c\epsilon^{2}\alpha_{n}k}=e^{-c(\epsilon\log(n/2))^{2}/2}\frac{1-e^{-c\epsilon^{2}n\alpha_{n}}}{1-e^{-c\epsilon^{2}\alpha_{n}}}\to 0,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}e^{-c\epsilon B_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-c\epsilon\log(n/2)}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}e^{-c\epsilon\beta_{n}k}=e^{-c\epsilon\log(n/2)}\frac{1-e^{-c\epsilon n\beta_{n}}}{1-e^{-c\epsilon\beta_{n}}}\to 0,

as
n\to\infty
.
∎

5.4.
Proof of Theorem
4.4
and Proposition
4.5

The connection with order statistics given in Proposition
5.2
is useful for our purposes since a large body of literature has been devoted to the study of order statistics of independent random variables (see, e.g.,
[
DLP03
,
DN04
,
ABN08
,
SW09
]
).
Of special interest for us are the probabilities

(5.25)

P\left(a_{j}\leq U_{(m,j)}\leq b_{j}\text{ for all }1\leq j\leq m\right),

where
0\leq a_{1}\leq\dots\leq a_{m}\leq 1
and
0\leq b_{1}\leq\dots\leq b_{m}\leq 1
.

From now on, we specialize (
5.25
) to the case
b_{j}=1
for all
1\leq j\leq m
,
and we use the notation

(5.26)

P_{m}(a_{1},\dots,a_{m}):=P(U_{(m,j)}>a_{j}\text{ for all }1\leq j\leq m).

The key ingredient to prove Theorem
4.4
is a recursive relation for (
5.26
) discovered by L. N. Bolshev shortly before his death and later published in
[
KC83
]
.

Proposition 5.7

(Bolshev’s recursion,
[
SW09
]
§9.3)
.

Let
0\leq a_{1}\leq\dots\leq a_{m}\leq 1
. Then:

(5.27)

P_{m}(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})=1-\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m}{k}~a_{k}^{k}P_{{m}-k}(a_{k+1},\dots,a_{m}),

with
P_{0}\equiv 1
.

A proof of this proposition can be found in
[
KC83
]
and
[
SW09
]
in terms of empirical distribution functions.
For the reader’s convenience, we include a self-contained proof in Appendix
A
.

Proof of Theorem
4.4
.

Let
F_{n}(t):=P(\Pi_{\mathrm{UT}}(X,Y)\leq t)
be the distribution function of
\Pi_{\mathrm{UT}}(X,Y)
. By the normalization of
X,Y\in\Delta_{n}
,

(5.28)

0\leq\Pi_{\mathrm{UT}}(X,Y)\leq\frac{Y_{1}+\dots+Y_{n}}{X_{1}+\dots+X_{n}}=1,

almost surely, which implies
F_{n}(t)=0
for
t<0
, and
F_{n}(t)=1
for
t\geq 1
. Let
0\leq t<1
, and denote with
S_{k}:=X_{1}+\dots+X_{k}
and
T_{k}:=Y_{1}+\dots+Y_{k}
for
k=1,\dots,n-1
. Then,

(5.29)

F_{n}(t)=1-P(\Pi_{\mathrm{UT}}(X,Y)>t)=1-P\left(\frac{T_{1}}{S_{1}}>t,\dots,\frac{T_{n-1}}{S_{n-1}}>t\right),

where we dropped the inequality
T_{n}/S_{n}>t
, since it is redundant for
t<1
.
Using Proposition
5.2
and the notation (
5.26
) we can write

(5.30)

\displaystyle 1-F_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=P\left(T_{1}>tS_{1},\dots,T_{n-1}>tS_{n-1}\right)=E_{S}[P_{n-1}(tS_{1},\dots,tS_{n-1})],

where
E_{S}
denotes the average with respect to
S_{1},S_{2},\ldots,S_{n-1}
.
Inserting Bolshev’s recursion (
5.27
) into (
5.30
), one has

(5.31)

F_{n}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\binom{{n-1}}{k}t^{k}E_{S}\left[S_{k}^{k}P_{{n-1}-k}(tS_{k+1},\dots,tS_{n-1})\right].

Isolating the first term in (
5.31
) and relabeling the summation index on the remaining terms one obtains

(5.32)

F_{n}(t)=(n-1)tE_{S}\left[S_{1}P_{n-2}(tS_{2},\dots,tS_{n-1})\right]\\
+\sum_{k=1}^{n-2}\binom{n-1}{k+1}t^{k+1}E_{S}\left[S_{k+1}^{k+1}P_{n-k}(tS_{k+2},\dots,tS_{n-1})\right],

We now use Bolshev’s recursion again on the first term of (
5.32
), obtaining:

(5.33)

F_{n}(t)=(n-1)tE_{S}S_{1}-(n-1)t\sum_{k=1}^{n-2}\binom{n-2}{k}t^{k}E_{S}\left[S_{1}S_{k+1}^{k}P_{n-2-k}(tS_{k+2},\dots,tS_{n-1})\right]\\
+\sum_{k=1}^{n-2}\binom{n-1}{k+1}t^{k+1}E_{S}\left[S_{k+1}^{k+1}P_{n-k-2}(tS_{k+2},\dots,tS_{n-1})\right]\\
=(n-1)tE_{S}S_{1}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-2}\binom{n-1}{k+1}t^{k+1}E_{S}\left[(S_{k+1}-(k+1)S_{1})S_{k+1}^{k}P_{n-k-2}(tS_{k+2},\dots,tS_{n-1})\right].

The average in the
k
-th term of the summation is given by

(5.34)

E_{S}\left[(S_{k+1}-(k+1)S_{1})S_{k+1}^{k}P_{n-k-2}(tS_{k+2},\dots,tS_{n-1})\right]\\
=n!\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}s_{n-1}\int_{0}^{s_{n-1}}\mathrm{d}s_{n-2}\dots\int_{0}^{s_{2}}\mathrm{d}s_{1}[s_{k+1}-(k+1)s_{1}]s_{k+1}^{k}P_{n-k-2}(ts_{k+2},\ldots,ts_{n-1}),

where the integration over
s_{1},\dots s_{k+1}
can be carried out explicitly, yielding

\int_{0}^{s_{k+2}}\mathrm{d}s_{k+1}\dots\int_{0}^{s_{2}}\mathrm{d}s_{1}[s_{k+1}-(k+1)s_{1}]s_{k+1}^{k}=\int_{0}^{s_{k+2}}\mathrm{d}s_{k+1}\left(s_{k+1}\frac{s_{k+1}^{k}}{k!}-(k+1)\frac{s_{k+1}^{k+1}}{(k+1)!}\right)=0.

Then, all terms of the summation in (
5.33
) vanish.
Finally, noting that
E_{S}S_{1}=(n-1)!/n!=1/n
, we conclude the proof

F_{n}(t)=(n-1)tE_{S}S_{1}=\frac{n-1}{n}t=\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)t.

∎

Proof of Proposition
4.5
.

Let
\alpha
be a positive integer.
As a special case of the stick-breaking representation in Proposition
B.1
, we have that if
U_{1}\ldots,U_{n\alpha-1}
are independent random variables uniformly distributed over the interval
[0,1]
, then
the vector
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
with components

\displaystyle X_{1}

\displaystyle=U_{(n\alpha-1,\alpha)}

\displaystyle X_{2}

\displaystyle=U_{(n\alpha-1,2\alpha)}-U_{(n\alpha-1,\alpha)}

\displaystyle X_{n-1}

\displaystyle=U_{(n\alpha-1,(n-1)\alpha)}-U_{(n\alpha-1,(n-2)\alpha)}

\displaystyle X_{n}

\displaystyle=1-U_{(n\alpha-1,(n-1)\alpha)},

is distributed according to
\operatorname{Dir}(\alpha,\ldots,\alpha)
.
Therefore, using the same notation as in the proof of Theorem
4.4
,

1-F_{n}(t)=P\left(T_{1}>tS_{1},T_{2}>tS_{2},\dots,T_{n-1}>tS_{n-1}\right)\\
=P\left(V_{(n\alpha-1,\alpha)}\geq tU_{(n\alpha-1,\alpha)},V_{(n\alpha-1,2\alpha)}\geq tU_{(n\alpha-1,2\alpha)},\ldots,V_{(n\alpha-1,(n-1)\alpha)}\geq tU_{(n\alpha-1,(n-1)\alpha)}\right),

where
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n\alpha-1},V_{1},\ldots,V_{n\alpha-1}
are independent uniform random variables in the interval
[0,1]
. We can easily upper bound the latter probability

(5.35)

P\left(V_{(n\alpha-1,j)}\geq tU_{(n\alpha-1,j)},\text{for all $j\in\{\alpha,2\alpha,\ldots,(n-1)\alpha\}$}\right)\\
\geq P\left(V_{(n\alpha-1,j)}\geq tU_{(n\alpha-1,j)},\text{for all $j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\alpha-1\}$}\right)=1-\frac{n\alpha-1}{n\alpha}t,

where the last equality follows from Theorem
4.4
applied to pairs of uniform random points in
\Delta_{\alpha n-1}
.
∎

Appendix A
Proof of Bolshev’s recursion

We denote by

(A.1)

G_{m}(t):=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\bm{1}_{(-\infty,t]}(U_{k}),

the empirical distribution function of
m
independent random variables
U_{1},\dots,U_{m}
uniformly distributed in
[0,1]
.

The probability of the event
\{U_{(m,1)}>a_{1},\dots,U_{(m,m)}>a_{m}\}
can be found by partitioning the sample space into the following disjoint union of events:

(A.2)

\{U_{(m,1)}>a_{1},\dots,U_{(m,m)}>a_{m}\}\cup\bigcup_{k=1}^{n}\{U_{(m,k)}\leq a_{k},\ U_{(m,j)}>a_{j}\text{ for all }j>k\}.

Informally, we first isolate the event
\{U_{(m,1)}\leq a_{1}\}
, then we partition the complementary event according to the position of
U_{(m,2)}
into
\{U_{(m,1)}>a_{1},U_{(m,2)}\leq a_{2}\}
and
\{U_{(m,1)}>a_{1},U_{(m,2)}>a_{2}\}
, and we iterate the procedure up to
m
. From (
A.2
) we obtain

\displaystyle P_{m}(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})=1-\sum_{k=1}^{m}P\left(\{U_{(m,k)}\leq a_{k}\}\cap\{U_{(m,j)}>a_{j}\text{ for all }j>k\}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=1-\sum_{k=1}^{m}P(mG_{m}(a_{k})=k)P(mG_{m}(a_{j})\leq j-1\text{ for all }j>k|mG_{m}(a_{k})=k),

where in the last equality we used the empirical spectral distribution (
A.1
) and the fact that
\{U_{(m,k)}\leq a_{k}\}\cap\{U_{(m,k+1)}>a_{k+1}\}
with
a_{k+1}>a_{k}
selects the event where exactly
k
among the
m
random variables
(U_{1},\dots,U_{m})
fall within
[0,a_{k}]
. The corresponding probability is given by the binomial distribution
\operatorname{Bin}(m,a_{k})
, so that we have:

(A.3)

P_{m}(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})=1-\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m}{k}a_{k}^{k}(1-a_{k})^{m-k}P(mG_{m}(a_{j})\leq j-1\text{ for all }j>k|mG_{m}(a_{k})=k)

Now notice that

\displaystyle P(mG_{m}(a_{j})\leq j-1\text{ for all }j>k|mG_{m}(a_{k})=k)

(A.4)

\displaystyle\qquad=P((m-k)G_{m-k}(a_{j})\leq j-1-k\text{ for all }j>k|(m-k)G_{m-k}(a_{k})=0),

that is, the locations of the largest
(m-k)
among
(U_{1},\dots,U_{m})
given that
k
of them fall below
a_{k}
are identically distributed
to the locations of the order statistics of
(m-k)
uniform random variables
(V_{1},\dots,V_{m-k})
given that
none
of them falls below
a_{k}
.
Note also that
(1-a_{k})^{m-k}
corresponds to the probability that among
m-k
uniform random variables in
[0,1]
none of them falls in
[0,a_{k}]
. Therefore:

\displaystyle(1-a_{k})^{m-k}P(mG_{m}(a_{j})\leq j-1\text{ for all }j>k|mG_{m}(a_{k})=k)

\displaystyle\qquad=P((m-k)G_{m-k}(a_{j})\leq j-1-k\text{ for all }j>k)

\displaystyle\qquad=P(V_{(m-k,1)}>a_{k+1},\dots,V_{(m-k,n-k)}>a_{m})

(A.5)

\displaystyle\qquad=P_{m-k}(a_{k+1},\dots,a_{m}),

Substitution of (
A.5
) into (
A.3
) yields the claimed recursive relation.

Appendix B
Dirichlet distribution and stick-breaking representation

The Dirichlet measure with parameters
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}>0
, denoted
\operatorname{Dir}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
, is the probability measure on the unit simplex
\Delta_{n-1}
with density

(B.1)

f_{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}}(x)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{1})\cdots\Gamma(\alpha_{n})}\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\alpha_{i}-1}\bm{1}_{\Delta_{n}}(x).

For
n>2
, the Dirichlet distribution is a natural multidimensional generalisation of the Beta distribution.

When
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
are positive integers, a generalisation of Proposition
5.2
gives a nice and useful representation of a Dirichlet distributed random vector in terms of spacings of order statistics of independent uniform random variables in the interval
[0,1]
.

Proposition B.1

(Stick-breaking representation of the Dirichlet distribution)
.

Let
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
be positive integers. Denote
m_{1}=\alpha_{1}
,
m_{2}=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
, …,
m_{n-1}=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n-1}
, and
m=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}
. Let
U_{1},\ldots,U_{m-1}
be independent random variables that are uniformly distributed over the interval
[0,1]

and
U_{(m-1,1)}\leq\cdots\leq U_{(m-1,m-1)}

the corresponding order statistics.
Then, the vector
\left(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}\right)
of the
n
spacings

\displaystyle X_{1}

\displaystyle=U_{(m-1,m_{1})}

\displaystyle X_{2}

\displaystyle=U_{(m-1,m_{2})}-U_{(m-1,m_{1})}

\displaystyle X_{n-1}

\displaystyle=U_{(m-1,m_{n-1})}-U_{(m-1,m_{n-2})}

\displaystyle X_{n}

\displaystyle=1-U_{(m-1,m_{n-1})}

is distributed according to
\operatorname{Dir}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
.
