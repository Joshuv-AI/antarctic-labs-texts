Safe cystoperitoneal shunting of thoracic meningocele in a patient with neurofibromatosis type 1
Authors: Shio Kinami, Yasuo Sasagawa, Takuya Nakamura, Daiki Kakita, Mitsutoshi Nakada
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Thoracic meningocele is a rare complication of neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1), and only rarely do symptomatic lesions require surgery. Here, a 55-year-old female with NF1 and an 80 mm T4 thoracic meningocele was successfully managed with the placement of a thoracic cystoperitoneal (CP) shunt.Case Description: A 55-year-old female with NF1 presented with progressive dyspnea and cough. The magnetic resonance (MR)-documented left lung compression due to a T4 80 mm thoracic meningocele. Due to the anticipated high morbidity of an open surgery, the patient underwent CP shunt placement. The cyst puncture route was determined based upon both computed tomography (CT) and MR studies and supplemented with intraoperative ultrasound. To minimize intrathoracic pressure fluctuations, one lung was collapsed while the other was routinely ventilated. Once the shunt catheter was safely and accurately placed, the patient’s respiratory symptoms improved, and she remained symptom-free 6 months later.Conclusion: Utilizing preoperative CT/MR and intraoperative ultrasound guidance, a 55-year-old female underwent safe/effective CP shunting of a T4 80 mm thoracic meningocele attributed to underlying NF1.

INTRODUCTION

Intrathoracic meningoceles occur in approximately 60–85% of patients with neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1); most of these are small and asymptomatic.[4,7] However, when the meningoceles enlarge and significantly compress adjacent tissues, surgical treatment often becomes necessary. Surgical options include posterior approaches (laminectomy), anterior or lateral thoracotomy, thoracoscopic resection with/without the management of attendant spinal deformities, and any/all may warrant dural repair.[3] Here, a giant T4 80 mm thoracic meningocele encountered in a 55-year-old female with NF1 was successfully managed with the percutaneous placement of a cystoperitoneal (CP) shunt.[9,10]

CASE PRESENTATION

A 45-year-old female presented with a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), documenting a cystic lesion/meningocele located on the left side at the T4 level. Originally, she just underwent a spinal fusion, as the cyst was asymptomatic, and therefore, conservatively managed [Figure 1a]. Now, at age 54, she newly presented with a cough and worsening exertional dyspnea. The chest computed tomography (CT) revealed an 80 mm cystic lesion within the left thoracic cavity at the T4 level, arising from the apex of her prior spinal deformity; there was also marked left-sided scoliosis [Figures 1b and c]. The CT also documented multiple asymptomatic neurofibromas scattered throughout the trunk. The MRI further confirmed that the lesion was a cyst/meningocele (i.e., lesion was isointense to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) on all sequences, and was continuous with the subarachnoid space) [Figure 1d]. Cistern scintigraphy (i.e., using intrathecal administration of 111In-diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid, repeated at 24 h) showed radiotracer accumulation within the cyst, thus further confirming its communication with the subarachnoid space [Figure 1e].

Surgery

Over the next year, the patient became increasingly symptomatic (i.e., cough and dyspnea), and the plan was to place a CP shunt under CT navigation. At surgery, a catheter was placed by collapsing the left lung, while ventilating the right (i.e., to avoid respiratory fluctuation and avoid abrupt intrathoracic pressure changes) [Figure 2a]. Intraoperative ultrasound monitored respiratory movement and the distance from the pleura in real time. A lumboperitoneal (LP) shunt system was then successfully inserted. After confirming CSF outflow through test puncture with a spinal needle (opening pressure: 18 cmH2O), the catheter was placed into the cyst. The valve was positioned in the left abdominal wall, and the programmable shunt valve was appropriately adjusted to a relatively high-pressure setting. Notably, a small amount of CSF was drained intraoperatively (i.e., to avoid abrupt lung re-expansion). The postoperative CT confirmed proper placement of the shunt catheter within the cyst [Figures 2b and c]. Two months later, the cyst had decreased in size, and both cough and dyspnea symptoms had improved; 6 months later, the patient was asymptomatic and continues to be monitored [Figure 2d].

DISCUSSION

Thoracic spinal meningoceles associated with NF1 are rare. They likely arise from dysplasia of the spinal dura mater and enlargement of the intervertebral foramina. If the dural sac herniates into the thoracic cavity, the resultant cyst may cause lung compression.[4,7] Often, these meningoceles form at the apex of a spinal curvature, particularly with scoliosis and kyphosis. Notably, instability is correlated with disease progression.[1] Table 1 summarizes the previously reported cases of NF1-associated thoracic meningoceles, including their symptoms, surgical strategies, and outcomes.[1–10] Surgical options include direct surgical repair with significant perioperative/postoperative morbidity (i.e., transvertebral or transthoracic cyst resection and duraplasty through thoracotomy or thoracoscopy, potentially causing infection, neurological injury, and CSF leakage), or shunt placement.[2,3] Shunts, including ventriculoperitoneal, LP, and cyst peritoneal (CP) options, pose less invasive alternatives. Although they reduce CSF pressure within the cyst and alleviate its compressive symptoms, cysts may recur; thus, these patients require both short and long-term postoperative monitoring.[8-10] As documented in this case, preoperative and intraoperative CT guidance ensures safe and accurate shunt catheter-peritoneal cavity placement within these thoracic meningocele.[5,6] Postoperatively, CT-guided stepwise adjustment of intracyst shunt pressures should correlate with progressive reductions in cyst size.

CONCLUSION

CP shunting was an effective treatment for this 55-year-old female with NF1 and a thoracic meningocele causing shortness of breath and dyspnea. She successfully underwent CP shunting under CT guidance, without attendant perioperative or postoperative morbidity.
