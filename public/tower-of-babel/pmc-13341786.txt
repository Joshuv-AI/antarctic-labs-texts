Physics-informed neural networks for predicting laser-tissue interaction in maxillofacial reconstruction surgery
Authors: Mohamed E Yahia, Alireza Abdikian, Hasti Abdolrasuli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study employs Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) to simulate the thermal dynamics of biological tissue under laser irradiation by embedding the heat transfer and radiative transport equations into the training process. The PINN architecture, comprising three hidden layers with 50 neurons each and Tanh activation, accurately predicts temperature distributions, achieving close agreement with the analytical solutions (final MSE ). The thermal penetration depths, calculated under uniform conditions of P = 5 W and beam diameter = 1 mm, were CO (0.11 mm), Nd:YAG (0.077 mm), Er:YAG (0.063 mm) and Diode (0.055 mm), lasers. These values are strongly dependent on laser power density and absorption coefficients, and thus represent reference conditions rather than generalizable results. CO lasers concentrated energy at the surface, enabling precise incisions; Nd:YAG achieved deeper subsurface heating suited for coagulation; Er:YAG provided highly efficient superficial ablation; and Diode lasers offered balanced heating for minimally invasive procedures. The analysis further showed that laser-tissue interactions are strongly influenced by fluence, power, and pulse duration. Higher power leads to more superficial heating, while lower power favors deeper diffusion. Simulations in age groups revealed that elderly tissue, with lower absorption coefficients, exhibits greater penetration, whereas adolescent tissue shows more superficial confinement, underscoring the importance of patient-specific parameter adjustment. Critical thermal thresholds for coagulation, vaporization, and irreversible cellular damage were identified, providing clinically relevant safety margins. These findings demonstrate that PINNs provide a robust, physics-based framework for predicting laser-tissue interactions, showing close agreement with analytical benchmarks and offering a computationally efficient alternative to traditional solvers, although the current model is one-dimensional and still not experimentally validated, which will be the focus of future work. Beyond clinical optimization, this work establishes a computational foundation for future extensions, such as incorporating Arrhenius damage integrals, heterogeneous tissue layers, nonlinear optical effects, and real-time feedback modalities.Keywords: Physics-informed neural networks, Laser-tissue interaction, Thermal penetration depth, Maxillofacial surgery, Laser parameter optimizationSubject terms: Applied physics, Biophysics, Medical research

Introduction

CO lasers with a wavelength of 10.6 m were first introduced in the 1960s as a groundbreaking medical technology1. Initially applied to skin lesions and general surgery2, they soon became integral to maxillofacial procedures owing to their precision, thermal control, and reduced collateral damage. Clinical use expanded in the late 1970s and early 1980s, driven by the need for accurate cutting with minimal bleeding. A key advantage of CO lasers is their strong absorption by tissue water3, enabling simultaneous incision and coagulation and replacing mechanical tools in complex surgeries4,5. Applications in bone reconstruction, correction of maxillofacial deformities, and soft tissue tumor removal confirm their superiority in hemostasis and accuracy. Recent innovations allow fine control of power, pulse duration, and beam geometry, enabling individualized surgical planning and improved recovery6–8. Integration with robotic platforms has further advanced CO laser surgery, combining real-time thermal monitoring and automated beam control to ensure consistent incision quality9–11. Pacheco et al.12 demonstrated precise coagulation and vascular sealing by coupling automated focusing with infrared thermography, validated across multiple tissue types with tracking errors below 1C. These developments highlight the growing role of robotics and intelligent feedback in achieving reproducible outcomes despite tissue variability. In maxillofacial surgery, critical clinical questions remain: Which laser type and power settings best balance safety and efficacy? How can penetration depth be optimized to avoid damage to mimetic muscles and neurovascular structures?13,14. Facial soft tissue anatomy provides key guidance: eyelid skin is 0.5 mm thick, facial skin 0.5–1.5 mm, and palms/soles 1.5–4 mm15. Beneath the skin lie subcutaneous fat, SMAS, mimetic muscles, and deep fascia16, each requiring controlled laser interaction. For example, preserving SMAS (0.5-1 mm) is essential for facial stability, while deeper structures (2-3 mm) demand strict thermal limits to prevent nerve and vessel injury17. Maintaining safe penetration depth thus requires balancing laser power, exposure time, and cooling strategies, principles central to reducing collateral damage and optimizing outcomes18,19. Recent progress in robotic and feedback-assisted systems enhances precision, safety, and patient-specific tailoring20,21. Among surgical lasers, CO, Nd:YAG, Er:YAG, and diode systems dominate practice, each with wavelength-dependent advantages. Tables 1 and 3 summarize their characteristics, showing that CO lasers remain preferred for soft tissues, Nd:YAG for deeper targets, Er:YAG for hard tissues, and diode lasers for selected adjunctive roles22–25. The optical properties used in this study, including absorption coefficients, scattering coefficients (), anisotropy factor (g), and refractive index (n) for CO, Er:YAG, Nd:YAG, and Diode lasers, were adapted from established literature27,28 are presented in Table 1. We note that while Mie theory predicts a monotonic decrease in scattering with wavelength, reported biological tissue data do not always follow this trend due to factors such as water absorption bands, experimental variability, and tissue-specific composition. For example, at mid-infrared wavelengths (2,940 nm and 10,600 nm), water strongly absorbs and dominates tissue optical behavior, which can lead to anomalously higher effective scattering coefficients reported in certain studies.

Artificial neural networks (ANNs), inspired by the communication pathways of biological neurons, have been a major focus of artificial intelligence research since the pioneering work of Rumelhart et al., in the 1980s26. Over the past decades, ANNs have become indispensable in fields ranging from speech recognition and image processing to autonomous systems8. Among their recent innovations, Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) offer a transformative framework for modeling physical processes by embedding governing differential equations directly into the learning architecture29–31. PINNs have shown strong potential in laser-tissue research. Bowman et al.32 used PINNs as surrogate solvers for simulating laser-tissue interactions under different boundary conditions, while Farmer et al.33 extended their application to bio-effects using sparse training data and uncertainty quantification. These studies demonstrate the ability of PINNs to deliver high-fidelity predictions of laser-tissue dynamics with reduced computational cost.

We employ a PINN model to predict the biological effects of CO laser radiation on human skin, with emphasis on maxillofacial reconstructive surgery. This framework advances clinical understanding of radiation effects while also informing burn assessment, laser safety standards, and defense medicine. Given that inaccuracies in high-risk applications may have serious consequences, reliability and robustness are prioritized over average model performance34. Our approach integrates heat transfer and radiative transfer equations into the PINN architecture to simulate temperature distributions and identify optimal laser parameters for safe and effective surgical use. This reduces reliance on large experimental datasets and computationally expensive solvers, while improving predictive accuracy for delicate interventions. In addition, we show how ambient temperature and laser power influence PINN performance, offering practical insights for clinical translation.

The paper is organized as follows: Sect. 2 introduces the governing equations and the PINN methodology; Sect. 3 presents the key findings and their clinical implications; finally, Sect. 4 concludes the paper.

Methods and fundamental equations

To optimize the performance of lasers in maxillofacial surgeries and accurately evaluate their effects on biological tissues, advanced theoretical models are essential. These models must simulate both the thermal and optical processes governing laser-tissue interactions35. PINNs, consisting of an input layer, hidden layers, and an output layer, enable efficient solution of these coupled equations. Their optimal implementation requires careful selection of hyperparameters such as neurons per layer, activation functions, and interlayer connectivity. In PINNs, physical equations are embedded directly into the loss function, guiding solutions toward compliance with both empirical data and governing laws. This hybrid approach leverages deep learning flexibility while constraining solutions with physics, ensuring reliable predictions in layered tissues such as skin29,30. To simplify the modeling framework, several key assumptions were adopted. Specifically, the present model is one-dimensional, with biological tissue represented as homogeneous and characterized by uniform optical and thermal properties. Thermal conductivity was assumed constant during laser exposure. A flat, single-layer geometry was employed, which omits anatomical curvature and multilayered structures inherent to real maxillofacial tissues. While these simplifications limit direct clinical translation, they provide a tractable baseline framework for demonstrating the feasibility of PINNs in predicting laser–tissue interactions. Future work will focus on extending the model to higher dimensions and heterogeneous, layered structures to more accurately represent the anatomical complexity of maxillofacial regions. In the present study, the optical parameters (absorption coefficient and scattering coefficient ) were assumed spatially uniform and temperature-independent; therefore, temperature-dependent variations of and were not accounted for and are identified as limitations to be addressed in future work. Consequently, nonlinear optical phenomena, including multiphoton absorption and plasma formation, were not incorporated32,33. These simplifications provided a controlled environment for applying PINNs while focusing on the fundamental interactions between laser energy and biological tissue. We emphasize that these assumptions were not intended to diminish the complexity of real laser-tissue interactions but rather to establish a baseline framework for validating PINN predictions against analytical benchmarks. In clinical settings, dynamic tissue responses such as variations in absorption, scattering, hydration, coagulation, and ablation thresholds govern laser-tissue effects36,37. Future iterations of the model will explicitly incorporate these dynamic responses to improve clinical fidelity.

To analyze the thermal behavior of the laser systems, modeling was performed using PINNs, with the governing equations being the heat transfer and radiative transport equations. Numerical parameters were chosen for direct comparison with established PINN-based studies32,33,45, providing consistency and validation of the present framework. To partially address the absence of direct experimental validation using phantoms or ex vivo tissue of the present study, we conducted systematic literature-based validation by comparing PINN predictions against well-established phantom and tissue datasets (Refs.27,36,37). The observed agreement in penetration depths, thermal thresholds, and heating patterns provides confidence in the model’s predictive capability. Future work will focus on expanding this validation through direct phantom experiments and in vivo measurements to further confirm clinical applicability. Results

Several established approaches exist to couple coagulation and ablation with thermal transport. Arrhenius-type kinetic models and damage integrals have been widely employed to quantify irreversible thermal injury and to update thermophysical properties following coagulation43. Similarly, ablation and vaporization are commonly modeled by introducing latent heat/enthalpy terms and incorporating phase-change dynamics into the energy balance. Both experimental and computational studies have demonstrated the necessity of moving-boundary or mass-loss source formulations to accurately reproduce ablation depth progression44. Within the present work, such mechanisms are not directly implemented; however, we outline them as a roadmap for future PINN extensions. Specifically, integration of (a) additional physics residuals, (b) temperature-dependent properties, and (c) hybrid coupling with explicit solvers for stiff chemistry or phase-change is envisioned. The PINN framework is well suited for this multimodal integration since it can simultaneously enforce multiple coupled physical residuals during training. To strengthen validation, the simulated thermal penetration depths and biological thresholds were systematically compared with established experimental values across laser systems (Table 6). The calculated penetration depths for CO (0.11 mm), Nd:YAG (0.077 mm), Er:YAG (0.063 mm), and Diode lasers (0.055 mm) fall within reported ranges from classical optical–thermal studies. Similarly, the simulated thresholds for protein coagulation (60 °C) and tissue vaporization (100 °C) align closely with benchmark Arrhenius-based injury data. These comparisons confirm that, despite the simplified one-dimensional framework, the PINN simulations reproduce key physiological thresholds with high fidelity, providing confidence in their applicability for surgical parameter optimization.

In addition, the predicted thermal thresholds were benchmarked against biological limits reported in classical tissue optics studies. Protein coagulation was reproduced at 60 °C, consistent with the experimental range of 58–62 °C61,62. Tissue vaporization was observed at 100 °C, aligning with values of 95–105 °C reported by Vogel and Venugopalan36 and Anderson and Parrish28. The Arrhenius damage integral (), indicating a 63% probability of irreversible injury, was reproduced for exposure times of 10–100 ms at 60–70 °C, in close agreement with experimental data (10-200 ms depending on tissue type)37,57. These results underscore that the simulated outcomes fall within well-established biological thresholds, reinforcing the physiological validity of the PINN-based framework. The PINN-generated subsurface thermal distributions (Figs. 7 and 8) were further compared with published experimental and computational datasets. For CO irradiation at m and powers between 3–10 W, the model predicted peak surface temperatures of 85–105 °C at 0.1 s exposure, followed by exponential decay within the first 0.5–1.0 mm of tissue. These results are consistent with Vogel and Venugopalan36, who reported surface heating above 90 °C under similar conditions, and with Welch and van Gemert37, where penetration depths of 0.1 mm were observed in water-rich tissues. Recent phantom-based measurements by Farmer et al.33 documented subsurface temperatures of 40–60 °C at 0.5 mm, which correspond closely to our PINN predictions (48–55 °C). The secondary peaks observed in Fig. 7 at 1.5-2.0 mm depth are consistent with stratified absorption phenomena in layered tissues, as previously described in Kaya et al.18 and Bowman et al.32.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) can accurately predict the thermal response of biological tissue under laser irradiation, achieving high fidelity with analytical solutions (final MSE ). By embedding the heat transfer and radiative transport equations into the training process, the framework quantified laser-specific penetration depths: 0.11 mm (CO), 0.077 mm (Nd:YAG), 0.063 mm (Er:YAG), and 0.055 mm (Diode). These results clarify distinct clinical roles: CO lasers enable precise surface incisions, Nd:YAG supports deeper coagulation, Er:YAG provides highly efficient superficial ablation for delicate microsurgery, and Diode lasers offer versatile, balanced heating suitable for minimally invasive applications. The analysis further revealed that thermal diffusion is strongly modulated by power, pulse duration, fluence, and patient-specific optical properties. Higher power concentrated heating at the surface, whereas lower power promoted deeper subsurface diffusion. Age-dependent absorption differences significantly influenced penetration, with elderly tissues exhibiting greater thermal spread and adolescent tissues more superficial confinement. Biological thresholds such as coagulation and vaporization points were acknowledged conceptually, but these were not explicitly modeled. Instead, the present framework establishes a baseline for incorporating Arrhenius damage integrals in future work. These findings highlight how tailoring parameters such as pulse duration to the thermal relaxation time of tissue can minimize lateral diffusion while preserving surgical precision. A key outcome of this work is the demonstration that PINNs provide a promising predictive framework for tailoring laser dosimetry by embedding physical laws directly into the training process. The novelty lies in showing that a PINN framework can reproduce clinically established thermal roles under varying parameter settings, thereby linking physics-informed modeling with known surgical outcomes. However, the current implementation is constrained by its one-dimensional, homogeneous, single-layer tissue assumption, which diverges from the anatomical complexity of maxillofacial regions. This limits immediate clinical applicability but establishes a foundational proof-of-concept. Future extensions will incorporate multilayered structures, tissue curvature, and heterogeneity to enhance surgical relevance. In addition, the present model does not account for nonlinear processes such as coagulation and ablation and has not yet been experimentally validated. While it demonstrates computational efficiency compared to traditional numerical solvers, future work will expand the framework to multidimensional geometries, integrate nonlinear thermal damage dynamics, and benchmark predictions against experimental measurements and finite-element simulations. Future developments will focus on integrating Arrhenius-based injury models, beam quality parameters, nonlinear optical effects, and ablation dynamics, as well as experimental validation through phantom studies and benchmarking against COMSOL and ZEMAX simulations. Coupling the PINN framework with real-time intraoperative feedback modalities (e.g., optoacoustic monitoring, OCT, ultrasound) represents a promising pathway toward adaptive, patient-specific laser surgery with enhanced safety, precision, and clinical reliability.
