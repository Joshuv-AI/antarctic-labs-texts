Equity in healthcare financing and access to care among vulnerable populations in Africa: a systematic review
Authors: Glory Olalekan Adebajo, Gbenga Joseph Alabi, Ifeoluwa Ruth Atobatele, Damilola Jeremiah Ayowole
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAchieving equitable access to healthcare remains a major challenge across African health systems, largely influenced by inequitable financing mechanisms. While Universal Health Coverage (UHC) emphasizes financial risk protection and equitable service access, evidence on how different financing models affect vulnerable populations in Africa remains fragmented. This study systematically reviews the relationship between healthcare financing strategies and equity in access to care among vulnerable populations in Africa.MethodsA systematic review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines and registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251137174). Literature searches were performed in PubMed, Embase, Scopus, African Journals Online, and Google Scholar for studies published between January 2015 and August 2025. Eligible studies examined healthcare financing mechanisms and their impact on access to care among vulnerable populations in African countries. Study quality was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale, CASP, and MMAT, while certainty of evidence was evaluated using GRADE and GRADE-CERQual. A narrative synthesis was undertaken.ResultsEighteen studies were included. Insurance-based mechanisms, including national, social, and community-based health insurance, were consistently associated with increased healthcare utilization. However, persistent inequities were observed, particularly among low-income and rural populations. Financial barriers such as informal payments and out-of-pocket costs remained significant despite insurance coverage. Structural constraints, including geographic inaccessibility and health system limitations, further limited equitable access. Evidence certainty ranged from low to moderate for quantitative outcomes and high confidence for qualitative findings.ConclusionWhile healthcare financing reforms in Africa have improved service utilization, they have not fully achieved equity in access. Addressing persistent disparities requires integrated financing strategies alongside structural health system strengthening to advance equitable UHC for vulnerable populations.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15159-w.Keywords: Healthcare financing, Universal health coverage, Health insurance

Background

Africa is the second-largest continent by population, accounting for approximately 18.8% of the global population with an estimated population of 1.5 billion people [1]. The health of this large population needs to be catered for through the provision of equitable and affordable healthcare to ensure the attainment and maintenance of a healthy lifestyle. Even though there have been improvements in global health service delivery, yet equitable access to healthcare remains a major challenge in many African countries [2, 3]. The inequity largely affects vulnerable individuals compared to the general population. Vulnerable populations include low-income households, rural communities, women, children, older adults, persons with disabilities, and people living with chronic illnesses [4]. Some other individuals such as Lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, and queer are socially marginalized groups, causing them to experience disproportionately poorer access to healthcare services [5].

Thus, vulnerability is an interplay of interaction of socioeconomic disadvantage, geographic isolation, discrimination, poor health conditions, and limited access to financial resources. In Africa, about 57% of the population is concentrated in rural areas, where there are shortages of standard healthcare facilities, healthcare workers, transport, and essential medicines [6–8]. Poverty levels are high in Africa with over 40% of the population living below the international poverty line of US$3.00 per day, which predisposes to financial hardship due to healthcare expenditure [9, 10].

Improving healthcare access among vulnerable populations is central to achieving Universal Health Coverage (UHC), a key target of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). According to the World Health Organization (WHO), UHC means that all individuals and communities receive the health services they need without suffering financial hardship [11]. Progress toward UHC is commonly assessed using the UHC Service Coverage Index (SCI), which is a composite indicator based on 14 tracer indicators on a scale of 0 to 100 [12]. UHC SCI covers all aspects of maternal, reproductive, newborn and child health, infectious diseases, and non-communicable diseases. It also covers healthcare service capacity and access [12]. UHC SCI directly aligns with SDG indicator 3.8.1.

According to the WHO, global UHC SCI increased from 45 in 2000 to 68 in 2015, which is a substantial improvement in coverage of essential health services [11]. However, there has only been a slight increase from 68 in 2015 to 71 in 2023 [13]. The African UHC SCI currently sits at 48, far below the global average of 71 [13]. The slower pace of progress after 2015 has been attributed to persistent underinvestment in health systems, shortages and maldistribution of healthcare workers, rising healthcare costs, population growth, increasing burden of non-communicable diseases, humanitarian crises and conflicts, as well as disruptions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic, all of which have strained health systems and widened existing inequities in access to care [14–16].

The concept of UHC and equity are closely related, yet there are some differences. UHC focuses on ensuring access to essential health services and protection from financial hardship at the population level, whereas equity is the absence of systematic, avoidable, and unfair differences in healthcare access, utilization, quality of care, and financial protection across different sets of a population [3]. Thus, a health system may achieve improvements in overall service coverage while substantial marginalization exists between advantaged and disadvantaged populations [3, 17]. Equity is increasingly recognized as a fundamental health system objective and an essential dimension of progress toward UHC because improvements in average coverage may conceal persistent inequalities affecting vulnerable groups.

The means of financing healthcare costs is an important determinant of equitable healthcare access. It determines how healthcare resources are generated, pooled, allocated, and utilized, thereby shaping access to healthcare services and financial risk protection. Healthcare financing in Africa is characterized by a mix of financing mechanisms, including general taxation, national and social health insurance schemes, community-based health insurance programmes, donor-supported initiatives, private health insurance, and direct out-of-pocket payments [18]. In Africa, healthcare is financed primarily through out-of-pocket (OOP) because of limited insurance coverage, large informal sectors that complicate contributory insurance schemes, inadequate government health expenditure, and fragmented risk-pooling arrangements [2, 19–21]. Furthermore, donor funding remains an important source of healthcare financing in several African countries, particularly for HIV, tuberculosis, malaria, and maternal and child health programmes, although concerns remain regarding sustainability and dependence on external resources [22].

Despite efforts to expand prepayment and risk-pooling mechanisms through health insurance reforms and government-funded programmes, out-of-pocket expenditure remains the dominant financing mechanism in many African countries. This is partly because a large proportion of the population works in the informal sector and remains outside contributory insurance schemes, while many existing insurance programmes provide incomplete coverage, resulting in continued direct payments at the point of care even among insured populations [23, 24]. The consequences of low availability of alternative healthcare funding are poor financial protection and inequities as reflected in catastrophic health expenditure (CHE) [25]. CHE occurs when healthcare spending consumes more than 10% of household resources and compromises a household’s ability to meet other basic needs [25, 26]. Recent evidence indicates that approximately 97 million people in Africa incur catastrophic health expenditure annually, while an estimated 133 million people experience impoverishing health spending or face increased risk of poverty due to out-of-pocket healthcare payments [27]. Rising healthcare costs, population ageing, the growing burden of chronic diseases requiring long-term treatment, and continued reliance on OOP payments have been identified as important contributors to this trend. In addition, inadequate public spending on health and gaps in financial risk protection mechanisms have limited the ability of many health systems to shield households from catastrophic and impoverishing health expenditures [19, 26]. Vulnerable persons are disproportionately affected by CHE because they often have lesser financial reserves and face additional barriers to healthcare access [28, 29].

Although numerous studies have examined health insurance schemes, healthcare financing reforms, and financial protection in African countries, much of the existing literature has focused on UHC, insurance coverage, healthcare financing and vulnerability of disadvantaged populations. There is a paucity of intersecting studies on these subjects in Africa. Considering the growing discussion and policies on UHC and reducing health inequalities across Africa, a comprehensive study is needed to understand how healthcare financing arrangements influence healthcare utilization, affordability, financial protection, and equitable access among vulnerable populations.

Thus, this study aims to systematically explore the relationship between healthcare financing strategies and equity in healthcare access within African health systems, with a focus on their impact on vulnerable populations. The rationale for our study is grounded in the broader context of achieving Universal Health Coverage (UHC), especially in the post-SDG era which emphasises equitable access to quality health services without financial hardship. Thus, we aim to make recommendations for policy and practice based on our findings to increase UHC in Africa.

Conceptual framework

This review was guided by a conceptual framework integrating theories of health equity, healthcare financing, and healthcare access [32–36]. Healthcare financing mechanisms, including tax-based financing, social health insurance, community-based health insurance, private insurance, and out-of-pocket payments, were considered the primary exposure. These mechanisms influence the extent to which individuals can obtain needed healthcare services without experiencing financial hardship, a key objective of Universal Health Coverage (UHC) [35].

Equity was defined as the absence of systematic, avoidable, and unfair differences in healthcare access and financial protection across population groups.[32,33} In this review, equity was assessed through indicators such as health insurance coverage, healthcare utilization, affordability of care, unmet healthcare needs, catastrophic health expenditure, and financial hardship resulting from healthcare spending [32, 33, 35].

Vulnerable populations were defined as groups experiencing increased barriers to healthcare access due to socioeconomic, demographic, geographic, or health-related disadvantages. These included low-income households, rural populations, women, children, older adults, persons living with disabilities, individuals with chronic illnesses, and other marginalized populations identified in the included studies [34].

The framework assumes that healthcare financing mechanisms influence equity through pathways such as risk pooling, financial protection, affordability, and resource allocation [35]. Access to healthcare is conceptualized as the opportunity to identify healthcare needs, seek care, reach services, obtain appropriate care, and benefit from healthcare interventions [36]. Contextual factors, including socioeconomic status, education, gender, place of residence, and health system characteristics, may modify these relationships.

Information sources

A comprehensive literature search of studies published in English between January 1, 2015, and August 10, 2025 was conducted in PubMed, Embase (via Ovid), and Scopus. Additional searches were conducted in African Journals Online (AJOL) and Google Scholar to identify relevant studies. Reference lists of included articles were also screened to identify additional eligible studies. The start date of 2015 was selected to capture contemporary evidence generated in the post–Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) era and to reflect recent healthcare financing reforms and universal health coverage initiatives across African countries.

Study selection

All 3116 records retrieved from the database search were exported into Mendeley reference management software and duplicates were removed. Titles and abstracts were screened independently by two reviewers to identify potentially relevant studies. Full texts of eligible articles were then assessed against the predefined inclusion criteria. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Data extraction

Data extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers using a standardized data extraction form. An initial data extraction table was designed including included study characteristics (author, year, country, and study design), characteristics of the vulnerable population studied, type of healthcare financing mechanism, outcome measures related to healthcare access and equity, and key study findings. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion between reviewers.

The data extraction form was piloted on four including studies representing different countries, study designs, vulnerable populations, and healthcare financing mechanisms. The pilot demonstrated that the original outcome measures field encompassed diverse constructs related to equity, including insurance coverage, affordability, financial protection, and healthcare access. To improve consistency and facilitate synthesis, an additional equity domain field was incorporated to categorize outcomes according to the aspect of equity being assessed (e.g., insurance coverage, financial protection, affordability, healthcare utilization, and access to care). The revised extraction form was subsequently used for full data extraction.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias of included studies was assessed according to study design. Quantitative observational studies were appraised using the adapted Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cross-sectional studies, which evaluates three domains: Selection (maximum five stars), Comparability (maximum two stars), and Outcome (maximum three stars), giving a maximum score of ten stars [37]. Studies scoring 8–10 stars were classified as low risk of bias (high methodological quality), those scoring 5–7 stars as moderate risk of bias, and those scoring 0–4 stars as high risk of bias.

Qualitative studies were assessed using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) qualitative checklist, which examines the clarity of the research aims, appropriateness of the methodology and study design, recruitment strategy, data collection methods, consideration of researcher–participant relationships, ethical issues, rigour of data analysis, clarity of findings, and the value of the research [38]. Consistent with CASP guideline, qualitative studies were not assigned numerical scores. Instead, an overall risk-of-bias judgement was made based on the extent to which studies satisfied the appraisal criteria. Studies meeting most criteria with no major methodological concerns were classified as low risk of bias, those with some methodological limitations as moderate risk of bias, and those with substantial methodological concerns as high risk of bias.

Mixed-methods studies were appraised using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) which evaluates the appropriateness of the mixed-methods design, the methodological quality of the qualitative and quantitative components, integration of findings, interpretation of results, and consideration of inconsistencies between data sources [39]. Consistent with MMAT recommendations, overall numerical scores were not calculated. Instead, studies were assigned an overall judgement of low, moderate, or high risk of bias according to the extent to which they fulfilled the MMAT quality criteria and the likely influence of any methodological limitations on the credibility of the findings.

To enhance the reliability of quality assessment, two reviewers independently appraised each study using the relevant tool (adapted NOS, CASP, or MMAT). Following independent assessment, the reviewers compared their ratings and discussed any disagreements regarding individual appraisal domains or overall quality classifications. Discrepancies were resolved through iterative discussion and re-examination of the study methods and reported findings against the relevant appraisal criteria. Where consensus could not initially be reached, a third reviewer independently reviewed the study and adjudicated the final quality judgement. Final risk-of-bias assessments were therefore based on consensus among the review team.

Certainty of evidence assessment

The certainty of evidence for quantitative outcomes was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach. Each outcome was assessed across five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias [40]. Since the quantitative evidence was derived from observational studies, certainty was initially rated as low. Downgrading was applied where there were serious or very serious concerns relating to study limitations, unexplained heterogeneity, limited applicability to the review question, uncertainty around effect estimates, or possible selective publication. Upgrading was considered where there was a large magnitude of effect, evidence of a dose-response relationship, or where plausible residual confounding was unlikely to explain the observed association. Final certainty ratings were classified as high, moderate, low, or very low.

For qualitative and mixed-methods evidence, confidence in review findings was assessed using GRADE-CERQual [41]. The four CERQual domains were methodological limitations, coherence, adequacy of data, and relevance. For mixed-methods studies, only qualitative data contributing to the review finding were included in the CERQual assessment. Confidence was initially considered high and downgraded where there were concerns about study conduct, weak coherence across studies, thin or limited data, or reduced applicability to the review question. Final confidence ratings were classified as high, moderate, low, or very low.

Data synthesis

A narrative synthesis was conducted to summarize findings across the included studies due to anticipated heterogeneity in study designs, healthcare financing mechanisms, populations, and outcome measures. Findings were grouped and compared thematically according to key domains, including types of healthcare financing mechanisms, equity in healthcare access, and vulnerable population groups studied. A meta-analysis was planned; however, substantial heterogeneity in study designs, populations, interventions, and outcome measures precluded statistical pooling of results. Quantitative findings were synthesized narratively by extracting and comparing reported effect estimates, proportions, odds ratios, coverage rates, and measures of financial protection across studies, with emphasis placed on the direction and magnitude of observed associations.

Results

Search results

The electronic searches yielded a total of 3116, 2537 duplicates were identified and resolved to leave 579 articles for the first stage of screening, and 359 articles were excluded after the first screening based on our exclusion criteria. Full texts were retrieved for 220 studies. Of these, 204 studies were excluded during the assessment of the full text. 16 articles were eligible at the end of the final screening. Two additional studies were identified from manually checking the selected studies, bringing the total number of eligible studies to 18.

A total of 200 disagreements between reviewer GJA and IRA occurred primarily during the title/abstract screening and full-text review stages, particularly regarding: (1) whether studies explicitly evaluated healthcare financing mechanisms as opposed to broader health system factors (2) the inclusion of populations classified as “vulnerable,” especially in studies with mixed populations lacking subgroup analysis (e.g., low-income groups, rural populations, women, children, and the elderly); and (3) the relevance of reported outcomes to equity in healthcare access. These are also summarised on the PRISMA flowchart in Fig. 1 as reasons for exclusion.

These discrepancies were initially addressed through discussion between the two reviewers (GJA and IRA), with reference to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. A third reviewer independently assessed the study (GOA) and made the final determination when the GJA and IRA couldn’t reach a consensus.

A range of healthcare financing mechanisms were examined, including national health insurance schemes (NHIS), the National Hospital Insurance Fund (NHIF), community-based health insurance (CBHI), social health insurance (SHI), premium exemption policies, government and donor financing, and out-of-pocket payment systems were found in the selected studies. The vulnerable populations studied include older adults, women, persons with disabilities, people living with HIV/AIDS, HIV-positive pregnant women, rural households, injured patients, and socioeconomically disadvantaged communities across seven African countries. Of all the healthcare financing mechanisms, insurance-based financing was consistently associated with improved healthcare utilization and access to services compared with non-insured individuals. For instance, insured individuals in Ghana had significantly higher odds of utilizing outpatient (OR = 2.51, 95% CI: 2.3–2.8) and inpatient services (OR = 2.78, 95% CI: 2.2–3.6), while NHIF enrolment in Kenya increased institutional delivery by 12.5% and skilled birth attendance by 19% among HIV-positive pregnant women. Summary of included studies

Even though there was an increase in healthcare utilization, substantial inequities in healthcare access were noted. Several studies reported continued OOP expenditures among insured populations, with 41.9% of Ghanaian women fully bearing healthcare-related OOP costs and 37.9% of insured women still experiencing such expenditures. Socioeconomic inequalities were also prevalent, as poorer households continued to experience lower access to healthcare services and financial cushion despite being on insurance schemes. Structural barriers including transportation difficulties, workforce shortages, limited financial capacity, and the concentration of services in urban areas further limited equitable access. In South Africa, rehabilitation services were predominantly concentrated in urban tertiary facilities, while studies from Ghana, Ethiopia, and other settings highlighted the combined influence of financial hardship, geographic barriers, and health system limitations on healthcare access. In summary, these studies suggests that although healthcare financing reforms, especially insurance-based mechanisms, improve healthcare utilization among vulnerable persons, achieving equity in access requires complementary interventions and financing mechanisms that address persistent financial, socioeconomic, and health system barriers.

Financing mechanisms studied

The included studies investigated a diverse range of healthcare financing mechanisms and their relationship with equity in healthcare access among vulnerable populations across Africa as shown in Fig. 1. The most frequently examined financing mechanism was the NHIS and related national insurance programmes. Six studies evaluated the impact of NHIS on healthcare access, utilization, enrolment, and financial protection among vulnerable populations, particularly in Ghana. These studies assessed outcomes among older adults, women, rural households, and other disadvantaged groups and generally reported improved healthcare utilization and insurance coverage among insured populations, although financial and geographic barriers persisted. [24, 47, 49, 52, 55,57] Community-based health insurance (CBHI) was the second most frequently studied financing mechanism. Three studies examined CBHI schemes in Kenya and Rwanda, focusing on their effects on healthcare utilization, financial protection, and equity among low-income and rural populations [45, 46, 53]. While CBHI schemes were generally associated with increased healthcare utilization and improved financial risk protection, evidence suggested that they did not eliminate disparities between poorer and wealthier households, indicating persistent socioeconomic inequities despite insurance coverage.

Two studies explored social health insurance (SHI) and broader insurance-based financing arrangements in Kenya and Ghana [55, 56]. These studies demonstrated that insurance coverage improved affordability and healthcare utilization; however, beneficiaries frequently reported informal payments, uncovered services, and limited understanding of benefit packages, which reduced the effectiveness of financial protection mechanisms. Additional studies examined health insurance as part of broader healthcare financing systems among injured patients, people living with HIV/AIDS, and caregivers of young children, highlighting the importance of insurance coverage in facilitating access to care while also identifying continuing barriers related to transportation costs, service availability, and affordability [51, 54, 58].

Government financing, donor funding, and out-of-pocket (OOP) payment systems were also examined. In Tanzania, a study assessed the equity and efficiency of government spending, donor Health Basket Funds, community health funds, and OOP expenditures, finding that donor funding was progressive while community-based contributions and OOP payments disproportionately burdened poorer populations [48]. Similarly, studies among persons with disabilities and rural populations demonstrated that OOP expenditures for healthcare, transportation, assistive devices, and related services substantially increased financial vulnerability and limited healthcare access [43, 58]. Public health financing and resource allocation were further explored in studies conducted in South Africa and Ghana, where inadequate resources, workforce shortages, and unequal distribution of healthcare services constrained equitable access despite existing financing mechanisms [42, 44].

Even though many of the included studies focused on a single financing mechanism, several examined multiple financing approaches simultaneously, comparing insurance-based schemes with out-of-pocket payments, government funding, donor financing, or broader health system financing arrangements [48, 56, 58]. These studies provided important insights into how different financing models interact with socioeconomic, geographic, and health system factors to influence equity in healthcare access among vulnerable populations.

Vulnerable populations identified

The included studies examined a diverse range of vulnerable populations across African healthcare systems. Rural and socioeconomically disadvantaged populations were among the most frequently represented groups. Several studies focused on rural households and underserved communities, investigating how geographic location, poverty, and limited healthcare infrastructure influenced healthcare utilization, financial protection, and access to health insurance schemes [51, 53, 57]. Women also featured prominently in the evidence base, particularly in studies examining maternal healthcare utilization, health insurance coverage, and access to reproductive health services among pregnant women and HIV-positive pregnant women [24, 52, 55].

The elderly constituted were another important vulnerable group, with one study specifically evaluating the impact of NHIS premium exemption policies on healthcare access among elderly populations in Ghana [47]. Other studies focused on populations experiencing health-related or social vulnerabilities, including persons with disabilities, people living with HIV/AIDS, HIV orphans, injured patients requiring trauma care, and caregivers of children under five years of age [43, 50, 51, 54, 58]. Across these populations, financial hardship, transportation costs, stigma, inadequate insurance coverage, and limited-service availability emerged as common barriers to healthcare access.

Several studies also explored broader socioeconomic vulnerabilities by comparing healthcare access and financial protection across income groups, insurance status, and regional economic strata [48, 49, 53]. The evidence consistently demonstrated that poorer households and socially disadvantaged populations experienced lower levels of healthcare access and financial protection despite the presence of insurance schemes and public financing mechanisms. Overall, the included studies highlight that vulnerable populations in Africa frequently face intersecting financial, geographic, social, and health system barriers, underscoring the need for healthcare financing reforms that address both economic and structural determinants of equity in access to care.

Geographical distribution of included studies

The included studies were conducted across multiple African countries and multi-country settings, as shown in Fig. 2. Ghana contributed the largest number of studies (n = 6), primarily examining the effects of the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) on healthcare access, utilization, and financial protection among vulnerable populations. Kenya was the second most represented country (n = 4), with studies focusing on community-based health insurance, social health insurance, and the National Hospital Insurance Fund (NHIF). South Africa contributed two studies examining healthcare access among persons with disabilities and the availability of rehabilitation services, while single-country studies were conducted in Tanzania (n = 1), Rwanda (n = 1), Ethiopia (n = 1), and Nigeria (n = 1). In addition, two studies employed multi-country designs, examining healthcare access and equity across multiple sub-Saharan African settings, including Ghana, Rwanda, and South Africa, and a broader analysis of socioeconomic inequalities in healthcare access across several African countries.

Thematic analysis

Insurance financing improved healthcare utilization and service access

Across the included studies, insurance-based financing mechanisms were consistently associated with increased healthcare utilization and improved access to health services among vulnerable populations. In rural Ghana, enrolment in the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) significantly increased the likelihood of utilizing outpatient services (OR = 2.51, 95% CI: 2.3–2.8) and inpatient services (OR = 2.78, 95% CI: 2.2–3.6) compared with uninsured individuals [57]. Similarly, among HIV-positive pregnant women in Kenya, National Hospital Insurance Fund (NHIF) enrolment increased institutional delivery by 12.5% points and skilled birth attendance by 19% points, with greater benefits observed among women with severe HIV disease [55]. Community-based health insurance schemes in Kenya and Rwanda also improved healthcare utilization, quality of care, and financial protection among enrolled populations [45, 53]. A study examining social health insurance in Ghana and Kenya further demonstrated that insured individuals sought care more frequently and reported greater affordability of healthcare services [56]. Collectively, these findings suggest that risk-pooling mechanisms can improve healthcare utilization and reduce financial barriers to care among vulnerable populations.

Persistent financial hardship despite insurance coverage

Although insurance schemes improved access, financial hardship remained a recurring challenge across studies. Several studies reported that insured individuals continued to incur substantial out-of-pocket expenditures for medications, transportation, diagnostic services, and services excluded from insurance benefit packages. In Ghana, 41.9% (95% CI: 39.4–44.4) of women incurred out-of-pocket healthcare expenditures, while 37.9% of insured women continued to experience out-of-pocket spending despite NHIS enrolment [24]. Similarly, patients enrolled in social health insurance schemes in Ghana and Kenya reported inappropriate charges and uncertainty regarding covered benefits [56]. Persons with disabilities in South Africa experienced considerable disability-related expenditures for transportation, assistive devices, caregiving, and healthcare services that increased economic vulnerability [43]. Injured patients in Ghana, Rwanda, and South Africa similarly identified inadequate insurance coverage and financial scarcity as major barriers to accessing quality care [58]. In Rwanda, although community-based health insurance reduced catastrophic health expenditure, significant inequalities in financial protection between poor and non-poor households persisted [53]. These findings indicate that insurance coverage alone may be insufficient to guarantee effective financial protection, particularly for vulnerable populations with complex healthcare needs as shown in Fig. 3.

A schematic diagram of healthcare financing and equity in Africa

Socioeconomic inequalities continued to shape healthcare access

Socioeconomic status remained an important determinant of healthcare access across the included studies. Poorer households were consistently less likely to enrol in insurance schemes, access healthcare services, or experience adequate financial protection. In Rwanda, community-based health insurance increased healthcare utilization and reduced catastrophic health spending, yet substantial disparities between poor and non-poor households persisted [53]. Similar concerns were identified in Tanzania, where community health fund contributions (KI = − 0.152) and out-of-pocket expenditures (KI = − 0.187) disproportionately burdened poorer regions [48]. In Nigeria, maternal healthcare utilization varied significantly according to socioeconomic characteristics such as income, education, and place of residence, while the financing system for maternal healthcare was found to be regressive [52]. Evidence from Ghana also demonstrated that despite NHIS coverage exceeding 95% among vulnerable populations, 18.5% of respondents reported being unable to access healthcare in the preceding year, with inadequate financial resources accounting for 69.7% of reported barriers [49]. These findings suggest that healthcare financing reforms alone may be insufficient to eliminate entrenched socioeconomic inequities in healthcare access.

Structural health system barriers limited the equity effects of financing reforms

Several studies highlighted that financial reforms were constrained by broader health system weaknesses. Geographic barriers, workforce shortages, transportation challenges, and inadequate service availability frequently limited healthcare access, particularly among rural populations. In South Africa, rehabilitation services were concentrated in urban tertiary facilities, restricting access for rural populations requiring rehabilitation care [42]. Studies conducted in Ghana identified resource constraints, staffing shortages, long waiting times, transportation difficulties, and geographical barriers as major impediments to patient-centred care [44]. Caregivers in rural Ghana also reported that long travel distances, transportation challenges, and limited facility operating hours contributed to delays in seeking child healthcare [51]. These findings indicate that financing reforms must be accompanied by investments in healthcare infrastructure, workforce development, and service availability to achieve equitable access.

Multiple and intersecting vulnerabilities influenced healthcare access

The studies demonstrated that vulnerable populations often faced multiple and overlapping disadvantages that influenced healthcare access. Older adults, persons with disabilities, women, children, rural populations, HIV-positive individuals, and injury patients experienced combinations of financial, social, geographic, and institutional barriers. In Ghana, adults aged ≥ 70 years were 2.7 times more likely to enrol in NHIS and those aged 60–69 years were 1.7 times more likely to enrol compared with younger adults, suggesting that premium exemption policies improved coverage among older adults [47]. In Ethiopia, access to HIV treatment was shaped by income constraints, food insecurity, stigma, transportation costs, and health system factors [54]. HIV orphans in Kenya encountered administrative and legal barriers to obtaining health insurance coverage for cancer treatment [50]. Likewise, injury patients across Ghana, Rwanda, and South Africa experienced intersecting challenges involving transportation, health literacy, financial hardship, and service availability [58]. These findings underscore the importance of adopting intersectional approaches to healthcare financing and policy development that account for multiple dimensions of vulnerability.

Health financing sources demonstrated different equity profiles

Evidence from Tanzania demonstrated that the equity effects of financing mechanisms varied considerably according to funding source. Government health expenditure was proportional to regional economic status (KI = − 0.047; p = 0.348), whereas donor Health Basket Fund allocations were progressive and favoured poorer regions (KI = − 0.195; p < 0.001) [48]. In contrast, community health fund contributions and out-of-pocket expenditures disproportionately burdened poorer populations. The study also reported an average regional efficiency score of 90%, indicating that approximately 10% of health financing resources were underutilized. Similarly, studies from Kenya showed that the equity impact of community-based health insurance depended heavily on financing structures, contribution arrangements, strategic purchasing, and government stewardship [45, 46]. These findings highlight important differences in the equity implications of various healthcare financing mechanisms and suggest that financing design is critical to achieving equitable health outcomes.

Risk of bias assessment

The results of the risk-of-bias assessment are presented in Appendix II and III. Overall, the methodological quality of the 18 included studies was judged to be moderate to high, based on study design, appropriateness of sampling, measurement of outcomes, control of confounding, depth of analysis, integration of findings, and transferability of results.

Among the 11 quantitative studies, methodological quality was generally moderate to high. Using the adapted Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cross-sectional studies, six studies achieved 8–10 stars and were therefore classified as low risk of bias, while five studies scored 5–7 stars and were classified as moderate risk of bias. Dalinjong et al. (2017), Ekholuenetale et al. (2024), Liu et al. (2019), Were et al. (2020), and Binyaruka et al. (2024) were judged to be at low risk of bias because they employed large or nationally representative datasets, appropriate sampling strategies, adjusted statistical analyses, and clearly defined measures of healthcare utilisation, financial protection, or equity [24, 48, 53, 55, 57]. However, the principal methodological limitations among quantitative studies related to study design, residual confounding, and outcome measurement. Most studies employed cross-sectional designs, limiting causal inference. Several also relied on self-reported measures of healthcare utilisation, access, or expenditure, increasing the possibility of recall and reporting bias. Residual confounding remained possible where insurance enrolment was influenced by socioeconomic status, health status, or ability to pay. Consequently, studies with large samples, robust analytical approaches, and clearly reported outcome measures achieved higher NOS ratings, whereas smaller facility-based or scheme-level cross-sectional studies were generally classified as moderate risk of bias.

Among the five qualitative studies, methodological quality was generally high. Hanass-Hancock et al. (2017), Yakob and Ncama (2016), Nyande et al. (2025), and Kennedy et al. (2024) were judged to be at low risk of bias because they clearly stated their research aims, employed appropriate qualitative methodologies, recruited participants relevant to the research questions, and conducted rigorous thematic analyses supported by rich contextual data [43, 51, 54, 58]. Nevertheless, several methodological limitations were identified, particularly regarding transferability, reflexivity, and potential reporting bias. Hanass-Hancock et al. (2017) focused exclusively on persons with disabilities in South Africa, Yakob and Ncama (2016) examined HIV care within a single zone in Ethiopia, and Nyande et al. (2025) was conducted in one rural district of Ghana [43, 51, 54]. While these studies provided rich contextual insights, their findings may not be readily transferable to other settings. Kennedy et al. (2024) was strengthened by its multicountry design, although data collection during the COVID-19 pandemic and restriction to patients with musculoskeletal injuries may have limited the broader applicability of its findings [58]. Langat et al. (2020), a case report, was judged to have moderate risk of bias because, although it provided valuable illustrative evidence regarding health insurance barriers among HIV orphans, it involved a very small sample, lacked a comparator, and had limited generalisability [50].

The two mixed-methods studies, Mazibuko et al. (2024) and Suchman et al. (2020), were classified as moderate risk of bias following appraisal with the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT). Both studies appropriately combined qualitative and quantitative approaches to address the research questions and generated complementary evidence on healthcare financing and access [42, 56], However, their overall methodological quality was reduced by limited integration of qualitative and quantitative findings, while the quantitative components were primarily descriptive and did not always support strong explanatory inferences.

In summary, methodological judgements were based on the strengths and limitations identified within each appraisal tool rather than numerical comparisons across study designs. Across all included studies, the most common methodological issues related to the inability of cross-sectional studies to establish causality, residual confounding, reliance on self-reported outcome measures, limited transferability of qualitative findings, and incomplete integration of qualitative and quantitative evidence in mixed-methods research. Despite these limitations, the overall body of evidence was considered sufficiently robust to support the findings of this review.

Evidence certainty and confidence assessment summary

Tables summarising the results are shown in Appendix IV and V. The certainty of evidence for quantitative outcomes ranged from very low to moderate. Evidence examining the effect of insurance coverage on healthcare utilization and access was rated as moderate certainty. Studies by Dalinjong et al. (2017), Were et al. (2020), Liu et al. (2019), and Duku et al. (2015) consistently demonstrated positive associations between insurance enrolment and healthcare utilization, including increased outpatient and inpatient service use, improved institutional delivery, and higher insurance coverage among older adults [47, 53, 55, 57]. Although these studies were predominantly observational and therefore subject to residual confounding, certainty was strengthened by the consistency of findings across different countries and vulnerable populations. Similarly, evidence relating to access to care among vulnerable populations despite insurance coverage was judged as moderate certainty, as studies by Acquah-Hagan et al. (2021), Dalinjong et al. (2017), and Duku et al. (2015) consistently showed that insurance improved access but did not eliminate financial and geographic barriers [47, 49, 57].

Evidence relating to equity in maternal and women’s healthcare access was rated as low certainty. While Were et al. (2020) reported increased institutional delivery and skilled birth attendance among insured HIV-positive pregnant women in Kenya, studies by Oyewale and Mavundla (2015) and Ekholuenetale et al. (2024) demonstrated persistent socioeconomic inequalities and out-of-pocket expenditures among women despite insurance coverage [24, 52, 55]. Certainty was downgraded because of methodological limitations associated with observational designs and variability in the outcomes assessed across studies. Evidence concerning financial protection and out-of-pocket expenditure was judged as very low certainty. Although Ekholuenetale et al. (2024), Liu et al. (2019), Binyaruka et al. (2024), and Gitahi and Njuguna (2018) consistently highlighted persistent financial burdens despite insurance coverage, the evidence was downgraded because of inconsistency in financing mechanisms, reliance on self-reported expenditure measures, and imprecision arising from heterogeneous outcome measures [24, 46, 48, 53]. Similarly, evidence relating to health financing distribution and regional equity (Binyaruka et al., 2024) and the equity effects of community-based health insurance schemes (Gitahi & Njuguna, 2018; Gitahi & Okech, 2018; Liu et al., 2019) was rated as very low certainty because it was derived from a limited number of studies, included indirect measures of equity, and showed variability in findings across settings [45 ,46, 53].

Confidence in the qualitative review findings ranged from moderate to high. Findings relating to persistent financial barriers despite insurance coverage were assessed as high confidence, supported by consistent evidence from Hanass-Hancock et al. (2017), Suchman et al. (2020), Kennedy et al. (2024), and Nyande et al. (2025), all of which identified transportation costs, uncovered services, informal payments, and affordability challenges as barriers to healthcare access [43, 51, 56, 58]. Evidence concerning structural health system barriers and the influence of rurality and transportation costs on access was also judged as high confidence, with strong and coherent findings reported across studies by Mazibuko et al. (2024), Kodom and Netangaheni (2024), Yakob and Ncama (2016), Kennedy et al. (2024), and Nyande et al. (2025). These studies consistently highlighted workforce shortages, service unavailability, long travel distances, and weak referral systems as important constraints to equitable healthcare access [42, 44, 51, 54, 58].

Findings describing overlapping vulnerabilities among marginalized populations and the role of insurance in improving affordability without fully achieving equity were assessed as moderate confidence. Evidence from Hanass-Hancock et al. (2017), Kennedy et al. (2024), Langat et al. (2020), Nyande et al. (2025), and Yakob and Ncama (2016) demonstrated that financial hardship, disability, HIV status, orphanhood, and rural residence often interacted to create multiple barriers to care. Confidence was reduced because some evidence originated from highly context-specific populations and a small number of studies [43, 50, 51, 54, 58]. Likewise, the finding that stigma, acceptability, and patient–provider interactions influenced healthcare access was rated as moderate confidence, based on evidence from Yakob and Ncama (2016), Kodom and Netangaheni (2024), Kennedy et al. (2024), and Acquah-Hagan et al. (2021) [44, 49, 54, 58]. Although these studies consistently identified social and interpersonal barriers to healthcare utilization, fewer studies directly examined these factors, and their relationship to healthcare financing was less explicit. Overall, the qualitative evidence strongly supported the quantitative findings by demonstrating how financial, structural, and social barriers continue to limit equitable healthcare access among vulnerable populations despite ongoing healthcare financing reforms.

Discussion

This systematic review examined healthcare financing mechanisms and their implications for equitable access to healthcare among vulnerable populations in Africa. Four major findings emanated from this study. First, the financing landscape across the included studies was heterogeneous, including NHIS, SHI, CBHI, tax- and government-funded systems, donor-supported financing, and OOP system. Secondly, although insurance-based mechanisms generally improved healthcare utilization. Coverage and benefit were not evenly distributed across population groups and settings. Third, vulnerable populations continued to face persistent inequities in access to care, even where formal financing reforms were in place. Lastly, expected benefits of financing reforms were strongly hampered by structural health system constraints, including geography, service availability, workforce limitations, and provider practices.

Although risk of bias and certainty of evidence were formally assessed, these assessments did not substantially influence the interpretation of the findings because most studies were of low risk of bias and moderate-to-high methodological quality, and the evidence largely showed overlapping findings.

Healthcare financing models and the transition towards UHC

The range of healthcare financing mechanisms identified in this review reflects the transitional and pluralistic nature of healthcare financing across African health systems. At one end, several studies evaluated national insurance models, especially in Ghana, while others assessed CBHI schemes in Kenya and Rwanda [45, 46, 47, 49, 53, 57]. Kenya’s NHIF is a social health insurance system with implications for maternal and reproductive health service use [55, 56]. Other studies moved beyond insurance schemes and examined government funding, donor participation, and out-of-pocket payment models, especially in Tanzania and South Africa [42, 43, 48]. Recent evidence from Ghana further highlights the persistence of out-of-pocket expenditure despite the existence of national insurance schemes. This study found that 41.9% of Ghanaian women reported out-of-pocket expenditure for healthcare services, while 37.9% of insured women still incurred direct payments. The study further demonstrated socioeconomic and rural–urban disparities in financial protection, suggesting that insurance enrolment alone may not fully shield vulnerable populations from healthcare costs [24].

This pattern is important from a global health perspective because UHC framework for health systems financing emphasizes three core functions including revenue raising, pooling, and purchasing as essential tools for actualization of UHC [59] Many of the studies in this review revealed partial progress in one or more of these domains but also showed fragmentation in financing mechanisms in Africa. For example, Dalinjong et al. (2017) demonstrated that insured individuals had 2.51 times higher odds of outpatient healthcare utilization and 2.78 times higher odds of inpatient healthcare utilization than uninsured individuals, illustrating the positive effect of NHIS on healthcare use in rural Ghana [57]. A Kenyan study showed that strategic purchasing within CBHI may improve equity, yet another similar study in Kenya found that the prevailing cost-sharing diversification remained insufficient to ensure equitable access for low-income households [45, 46]. Similarly, a Tazanian study showed that government expenditure was proportional to regional economic status (Kakwani Index = − 0.047), health basket funding was progressively distributed (Kakwani Index = − 0.195; p < 0.001), whereas out-of-pocket payments remained regressive, disproportionately affecting poorer populations [48].

These findings align with broader evidence from previous studies in low- and middle-income countries showing that expansion of formal insurance schemes alone does not necessarily translate into equity, reduced out-of-pocket payments, or protection from catastrophic health expenditure among poor and vulnerable populations [60, 61]. This is especially relevant to SDG Target 3.8, which emphasizes access to quality essential services and financial risk protection regardless of socioeconomic status [11].

Prevalence and distribution of financing mechanisms across settings

The findings of this study show that insurance-based mechanisms dominate the policy and research space in Africa, particularly in West and East Africa. Ghana was the most represented country in the review, with multiple studies focused on NHIS and its relationship to utilization, enrollment, exemptions, and access among vulnerable populations [24, 44, 47, 49, 51, 56–58]. These studies covered diverse populations including 55,992 household members in rural northern Ghana, 9,396 Ghanaian women and 710 vulnerable individuals in Kumasi providing extensive evidence on healthcare financing and equity [24, 49, 57]. These studies showed that women continue to make substantial direct payments for healthcare services, with higher burdens among rural residents and socioeconomically disadvantaged groups despite being on NHIS. Another study found that challenges with NHIS ownership, renewal processes, transportation costs, and long distances to health facilities continued to impede access to child healthcare services in rural Ghana [51]. Kenya and Rwanda similarly contributed evidence on SHI and CBHI, particularly around maternal care, utilization, and inequality [45, 46, 53, 55]. This concentration is consistent with the broader African policy landscape, where Ghana, Rwanda, and Kenya are often presented as leading examples of innovations toward UHC through prepayment and pooling reforms [62–64].

However, this review also reveals an important imbalance: there is comparatively less evidence from Central and North Africa. A multicountry analysis of WHO data in the North African region showed that OOP payment was the main source of funding healthcare costs and poorer households were more likely to incur catastrophic health expenditure [65]. In Cameroon, a study found that there was a slight positive redistributive effect from OOP payments where payment was largely stratified by income [66]. Still, there were persistent inequities with poorer households expending a higher proportion of their income on healthcare costs. Only a few studies captured in our review examined tax-based financing and the role of domestic public expenditure as the primary pathway to equitable coverage. This observation is important to consider critically as global trends are increasingly shifting toward mixed financing models because single financing approaches have often proven insufficient. In Thailand, for instance, a predominantly tax-funded model achieved high service and financial protection coverage when linked with strong primary care and public investment, while countries in Latin America have followed mixed insurance and public financing systems with variable equity effects [67, 68].

Equity of access to healthcare insurance among vulnerable populations

A major contribution of this review is the finding that insurance availability does not necessarily translate into equitable access or coverage for vulnerable populations. Multiple studies documented socioeconomic inequities in insurance enrolment and healthcare use. A Ghanaian study showed that although more than 95% of vulnerable participants were enrolled under the NHIS, 18.5% still reported being unable to access healthcare during the previous 12 months because of financial constraints, distance, and provider-related barriers [49]. This finding demonstrates that high insurance coverage does not necessarily guarantee equitable access to healthcare.

Similarly, in Rwanda, CBHI improved utilization and reduced catastrophic spending but did not significantly eliminate inequality between poor and non-poor groups [53]. Duku et al. (2015) reported that premium exemptions substantially improved enrolment among older adults in Ghana, with individuals aged 70 years and above being 2.7 times more likely to enrol and those aged 60–69 years being 1.7 times more likely to enrol than younger adults, suggesting that targeted exemption policies can enhance inclusion [47]. However, this was not sufficient to eliminate all barriers, especially where awareness and trust remained low. In Kenya, NHIF coverage increased institutional delivery by 12.5% points and skilled birth attendance by 19% points among HIV-positive pregnant women, with an even larger improvement of 20% points among women with more advanced HIV disease [55]. Yet, Suchman et al. (2020), drawing on evidence from 106 qualitative interviews and 4,042 client exit interviews conducted in Ghana and Kenya, documented poor understanding of benefit packages together with reports of inappropriate charges despite insurance coverage [56].

Similar concerns emerged in a more recent Ghanaian studies among women which reported that caregivers of children under five experienced challenges with health insurance registration and membership renewal, perceived gaps in benefit coverage, and continued out-of-pocket payments for services not covered by the NHIS [51]. These barriers often delayed care-seeking and disproportionately affected rural households. Evidence from Kenya further illustrates how administrative and legal barriers may exclude highly vulnerable populations from insurance benefits. Langat et al. (2020) described the experiences of HIV orphans with Burkitt lymphoma whose access to health insurance depended on obtaining legal guardianship, a process that was costly, complex, and poorly understood by families and healthcare providers [50]. The study demonstrates that vulnerable populations may remain effectively uninsured even where insurance mechanisms formally exist.

Non-insurance-focused studies in South Africa demonstrated that persons with disabilities face financing inequities that are not fully captured by formal insurance coverage alone [42, 43]. Mazibuko et al. (2024) further showed that rehabilitation services were available in 78% of tertiary-level urban facilities compared with only 45% of rural settings, illustrating persistent geographical inequities in service availability [42]. Disability-related expenses, transport costs, and concentration of services in urban facilities further reduced the effective accessibility of care. Similarly, among people living with HIV in Ethiopia, access to treatment depended not only on financing and service availability but also on stigma, food insecurity, and social support [54]. A multicountry African study further showed that injured patients faced combined barriers related to cost, insurance gaps, transportation, and service availability [58]. Likewise, Oyewale and Mavundla (2015) found that socioeconomic characteristics including education, income, residence, and insurance status significantly influenced utilisation of maternal healthcare services in Nigeria [52]. Their findings suggest that health insurance interacts with broader social determinants and may be insufficient to ensure equitable access when structural socioeconomic barriers persist.

Looking at the bigger picture, these studies suggest that equity in access to healthcare insurance should not be measured by enrolment figures alone. Availability, uptake, continuity, benefit realization, and the ability to use covered services without social or structural exclusion should also be considered. This position aligns closely with WHO’s emphasis on effective coverage rather than nominal coverage as the true marker of UHC progress [11].

Effect of financing alternatives on access, utilization and financial protection

Some of the included studies showed that non-out-of-pocket (OOP) financing alternatives improve healthcare utilization among vulnerable individuals, although financial protection and equity remained persistently low. A study conducted among poor insured individuals in rural northern Ghana showed that insured participants had 2.51-fold higher odds of outpatient utilization (95% CI: 2.3–2.8) and 2.78-fold higher odds of inpatient utilization (95% CI: 2.2–3.6) compared with uninsured persons [57]. Similarly, a Kenyan study found that NHIF enrolment increased institutional delivery by 12.5% points and skilled birth attendance by 19% points among HIV-positive pregnant women, with the largest gains observed among women with advanced HIV disease [55]. In Ghana, positive utilization effects from premium exemptions under the NHIS were also reported [47].

However, evidence also showed that increased utilization does not always translate into reductions in long-standing inequalities. In Rwanda, CBHI improved care utilization and reduced catastrophic expenditure, yet did not significantly reduce poverty-related inequalities [53]. A study further showed that poorer children across 19 sub-Saharan African countries were less likely to access health facilities and malaria diagnostic testing. Access also varied by facility type, with poorer children obtaining relatively better access in public primary healthcare facilities than in private hospitals [69].

Similarly, in Ghana and Kenya, SHI improved affordability and increased care-seeking behaviour, but clients still reported informal charges and uncertainty regarding benefit packages. Suchman et al. (2020), drawing on evidence from 4,042 client exit interviews and 106 qualitative interviews, found that clients continued to report inappropriate charges and poor understanding of insurance benefit packages despite being enrolled in SHI schemes, highlighting persistent implementation gaps. Out-of-pocket expenses substantially undermined financial protection among persons with disabilities in South Africa [43]. In Nigeria, similarity was seen in a report that found that financing system for maternal healthcare was regressive, with poorer households spending a disproportionately larger share of their income on healthcare than wealthier households [52]. At the health system level, Binyaruka et al. (2024) demonstrated that out-of-pocket financing remained regressive (Kakwani Index = − 0.187), indicating that poorer populations continued to bear a disproportionately greater financial burden than wealthier groups in Tazania [48]. These findings reinforce broader global evidence that health financing systems remain inequitable when prepayment mechanisms coexist with substantial residual out-of-pocket burdens.

Implications for global health policy

The findings of this review have implications that extend beyond Africa. As a blueprint for global healthcare cost financing systems, we provided evidence that UHC is not simply about introducing insurance. Rather, healthcare financing systems should be designed to achieve redistributive, equitable, and connected quality service delivery. The implication of this is that the WHO recommendations on financial protection and service coverage should be interpreted through an equity lens, especially for vulnerable populations facing multiple disadvantages.

We also put forward evidence that progress toward SDG 3.8 should be measured not only by aggregate service utilization or insurance enrollment, but also by whether poorer, rural, disabled, and other marginalized populations are benefiting optimally. Policymakers must prioritise pro-poor subsidies, exemption policies, strategic purchasing, integration of donor and domestic financing, and strong primary healthcare systems.

Strengths, limitations and future directions

This systematic review was thorough methodologically through adherence to established reporting guidelines and prospective protocol registration, enhancing transparency and reproducibility. A comprehensive multi-database search strategy and the inclusion of diverse study designs enabled a broad and contextually rich synthesis of evidence on healthcare financing and equity in Africa. The application of standardized quality appraisal tools and evidence grading frameworks further strengthens the credibility of our findings. Importantly, the focus on vulnerable populations increases the policy relevance of this review in advancing Universal Health Coverage.

However, there are several limitations which require due acknowledgement. The predominance of observational and cross-sectional studies limits causal inference. Substantial heterogeneity across study designs, populations, and outcome measures precluded meta-analysis and necessitated narrative synthesis. The geographical distribution concentration of included studies in West and East Africa, particularly Ghana and Kenya were a major limitation. The exclusion of non-English studies introduces potential language bias which may have caused exclusion of studies from francophone African countries Additionally, reliance on self-reported measures and the possibility of residual confounding and publication bias may affect the robustness of our findings. The non-availability of studies on socially disadvantaged vulnerable population such as sex workers and LGBTQ + is noteworthy.

Future research should prioritize longitudinal and quasi-experimental designs to strengthen causal evidence and expand geographic representation, particularly in under-studied regions such as Central and North Africa. There is a need to examine how financing mechanisms interact with structural health system factors, including service delivery platforms, geography, and social determinants. Further studies should explore mixed financing models and adopt standardized equity metrics to improve comparability. Implementation-focused and qualitative research will also be critical to understanding real-world barriers, including informal payments and access gaps, and to inform context-specific policy interventions aimed at achieving equitable Universal Health Coverage.

Conclusion

This systematic review demonstrates that a range of healthcare financing mechanisms have been evaluated in relation to equitable healthcare access among vulnerable populations in Africa, with national health insurance schemes, community-based health insurance, social health insurance, government and donor financing, and out-of-pocket payment systems being the most frequently studied. However, the evidence base was dominated by insurance-based financing mechanisms, particularly from Ghana and Kenya, with comparatively limited studies on tax-funded and mixed financing models and from several North and Central Africa.

We found that alternative financing mechanisms generally performed better than out-of-pocket payment in improving healthcare utilization, affordability, and financial protection among vulnerable populations. National health insurance, community-based health insurance, and social health insurance were consistently associated with increased healthcare utilization Nevertheless, these financing mechanisms did not consistently achieve equitable access, as many insured populations continued to experience out-of-pocket expenditure, financial hardship, and disparities in healthcare utilization.

This review further shows that equitable healthcare access is shaped not only by the type of financing mechanism but also by broader contextual factors. Financial constraints, transportation costs, geographic location, workforce shortages, uneven distribution of healthcare services, administrative barriers, limited awareness of insurance benefits, and socioeconomic disadvantage repeatedly emerged as barriers to effective access, while premium exemption policies, risk pooling, and reduced direct healthcare costs facilitated healthcare utilization among vulnerable populations.

In a nutshell, the findings suggest that healthcare financing reforms should be evaluated not only by insurance coverage or healthcare utilization but also by their ability to reduce persistent financial, geographic, and socioeconomic inequities in access to care. Future research should address the substantial geographical gaps identified in this review, particularly in Central and North Africa, and generate comparative evidence on non-insurance financing approaches, including tax-funded and mixed financing systems, to better inform equitable progress towards universal health coverage across diverse African health system contexts.
