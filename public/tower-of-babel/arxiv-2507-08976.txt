Nilpotent BCK-algebras
C. Matthew Evans

Abstract
. We recall the derived subalgebra of a BCK-algebra, and use this to define the derived ideal . Using the derived ideal, we show that the category of commutative BCK-algebras is a reflective subcategory of the category of BCK-algebras. After this, we introduce central series and define a notion of nilpotence for BCK-algebras and prove some properties of nilpotence. In particular, for any variety of BCK-algebras, the sub-class of nilpotent algebras is a sub-pseudovariety, though in general not a variety. We also show that the class of BCK-algebras of nilpotence class at most c c is a sub-quasivariety of all BCK-algbras, and is a variety if and only if c = 1 c=1 . We close by showing that every finite BCK-algebra is nilpotent.

1.
Introduction and preliminaries

BCK-algebras were introduced in 1966 by Imai and Iséki in
[
8
]
as the algebraic semantics for a non-classical logic having only implication. These algebras are named in reference to the earlier combinatory logic, specifically the combinators B, C, and K. For more on the connection between the axioms for BCK-algebras and these combinators, see
[
2
]
.

Researchers have found many connections between BCK-algebras and other algebraic structures. Bounded commutative BCK-algebras are term-equivalent to MV-algebras
[
12
]
while bounded implicative BCK-algebras are Boolean algebras
[
11
]
. Cornish showed in
[
3
]
that certain classes of rings admit an implicative BCK-structure, while BCK-algebras satisfying something called
condition (S)
are commutative semigroups with respect to a derived operation
[
9
]
.

Given these connections, particularly with Boolean algebras and the other algebras of logic, one may wonder “how classical” a BCK-algebra is. For example, one expects that “
x\wedge y=y\wedge x
” in any classical setting, but this kind of commutativity is not true in BCK-algebras in general. In the author’s pre-print
[
6
]
, the commuting degree of finite BCK-algebras is studied; that is, the probability that two randomly selected elements commute. This gives one measure of “how commutative” a
finite
BCK-algebra is, and thus a “measure of classicality.” Probabilities for other equations that are true in the classical setting were studied in finite BCK-algebras in
[
5
]
. But it is not obvious how to generalize this probabilistic approach to infinite BCK-algebras.

In this paper, mimicing ideas from group theory, we define a notion of
nilpotence
for BCK-algebras, and this gives us a measure of commutativity (and hence a measure of classicality) for the whole class of BCK-algebras.

The paper is structured as follows: in the remaining paragraphs of the introduction, we remind the reader of some important definitions involving BCK-algebras. In Section
2
, we introduce pseudo-commutators and the derived ideal. We go on to show that
\mathtt{cBCK}
, the category of commutative BCK-algebras, is a reflective subcategory of
\mathtt{BCK}
. In Section
3
, we define central series for BCK-algebras and use them to define nilpotence. We show that nilpotence can be characterized by the behavior of both an upper central series and a lower central series, similar to groups. Some examples are provided. Finally, in Section
4
, we discuss the algebraic properties of nilpotent BCK-algebras. In particular, we show that, for any variety
\mathtt{V}
of BCK-algebras, the subclass
\mathtt{V}_{\mathrm{nil}}
of nilpotent members is a pseudovariety, though not generally a variety. We also show that
\mathtt{BCK}_{c}
, the class of BCK-algebras of nilpotence class at most
c
, is a sub-quasivariety of
\mathtt{BCK}
, and that
\mathtt{BCK}_{c}
is a variety if and only if
c=1
. We end the paper by showing that all finite BCK-algebras are nilpotent.

Definition 1.1
.

A
BCK-algebra
is an algebra
\mathbf{A}=\langle A;\boldsymbol{\cdot},0\rangle
of type
(2,0)
such that

(BCK1)

\bigl[(x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)\boldsymbol{\cdot}(x\boldsymbol{\cdot}z)\bigr]\boldsymbol{\cdot}(z\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)=0

(BCK2)

\bigl[x\boldsymbol{\cdot}(x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)\bigr]\boldsymbol{\cdot}y=0

(BCK3)

x\boldsymbol{\cdot}x=0

(BCK4)

0\boldsymbol{\cdot}x=0

(BCK5)

x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y=0
and
y\boldsymbol{\cdot}x=0
imply
x=y
.

for all
x,y,z\in A
.

These algebras are partially ordered by:
x\leq y
if and only if
x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y=0
. Note that 0 is the least element by (BCK4). One can also show that

(6)

\displaystyle x\boldsymbol{\cdot}0=x

for all
x\in\mathbf{A}
and that

(7)

\displaystyle x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y\leq x

for all
x,y\in\mathbf{A}
. For more elementary properties of BCK-algebras, we refer the reader to
[
11
]
.

Define a term operation
\wedge
by
x\wedge y:=y\boldsymbol{\cdot}(y\boldsymbol{\cdot}x)
. The element
x\wedge y
is a lower bound for
x
and
y
, but in general is not the greatest lower bound of
x
and
y
. One can show that
x\wedge y=\glb\{x,y\}
if and only if
x\wedge y=y\wedge x
, and in this case we say that
x
and
y

commute
. If all elements of
\mathbf{A}
commute, we say
\mathbf{A}
is
commutative
.

Definition 1.2
.

A subset
I\subseteq\mathbf{A}
is an
ideal
of
\mathbf{A}
if
0\in I
and the following implication is satisfied:

if
x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y\in I
and
y\in I
, then
x\in I
.

We note that ideals are both downsets and subalgebras. We will write
\mathbf{B}\leq\mathbf{A}
to indicate that
\mathbf{B}
is a subalgebra of
\mathbf{A}
, and
I\unlhd\mathbf{A}
to indicate that
I
is an ideal of
\mathbf{A}
. Given a subset
S\subseteq\mathbf{A}
, the notations
\langle S\rangle
and
(S]
refer to the subalgebra generated by
S
and the ideal generated by
S
, respectively. It is a result of Iséki and Tanaka
[
10
]
that

(8)

\displaystyle(S]=\{\,x\in\mathbf{A}\,\mid\,(\cdots((x\boldsymbol{\cdot}s_{1})\boldsymbol{\cdot}s_{2})\cdots)\boldsymbol{\cdot}s_{n}=0\text{ for some $s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\in S$}\,\}\,.

Give
I\unlhd\mathbf{A}
, we define an equivalence relation
\sim_{I}
on
\mathbf{A}
by

x\sim_{I}y\text{ if and only if }x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y,y\boldsymbol{\cdot}x\in I\,.

Let
C_{x}
denote the equivalence class of
x\in\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{A}/I
the set of equivalence classes. Then
\mathbf{A}/I
is a BCK-algebra via
C_{x}\boldsymbol{\cdot}C_{y}=C_{x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y}

[
11
]
. (The
C_{x}
notation is perhaps unusual, but relatively standard in the literature on BCK-algebras.)

2.
Pseudo-commutators and the derived ideal

Definition 2.1
.

The
pseudo-commutator
of
x
and
y
is

[x,y]:=(x\wedge y)\boldsymbol{\cdot}(y\wedge x)\,.

Informally, this element represents a “measure” how close
x
and
y
are to commuting, as we see in the next lemma, the proof of which is straightforward.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a BCK-algebra and
x,y\in\mathbf{A}
.

(1)

If
[x,y]=0
, then
x\wedge y\leq y\wedge x
.

(2)

If
x\wedge y=y\wedge x
, then
[x,y]=0
.

(3)

If
[x,y]=[y,x]
, then
x\wedge y=y\wedge x
.

(4)

If
x\leq y
, then
[x,y]=0
.

(5)

[x,y]\boldsymbol{\cdot}x=0
and
[x,y]\boldsymbol{\cdot}y=0
.

(6)

[x,0]=[0,x]=[x,x]=0
.

Unfortunately, the inequality in Lemma
2.2
(1) cannot be upgraded to equality: looking ahead at Example
2.6
, we have
[2,3]=0
, but
2\wedge 3=0<2=3\wedge 2
. This explains the term “
pseudo
-commutator.”

Given subsets
S,T\subseteq\mathbf{A}
, we define
[S,T]\subseteq\mathbf{A}
by

[S,T]:=\Bigl\langle\,[s,t]\,\mid\,s\in S,t\in T\,\Bigr\rangle\,,

the subalgebra generated by all pseudo-commutators of elements of
S
with elements of
T
. We will call the set of pseudo-commutators
\{\,[s,t]\,\mid\,s\in S,t\in T\,\}
a
generating set
for
[S,T]
, and each pseudo-commutator
[s,t]
a
generator
of
[S,T]
. Then
[S,T]
simply consists of finite products of generators.

We collect some properties that will be used later.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
S,U\subseteq\mathbf{A}
and
T,V\subseteq\mathbf{B}
. Then

[S\times T,U\times V]_{\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}}=[S,U]_{\mathbf{A}}\times[T,V]_{\mathbf{B}}\,.

Proof.

Take
(s,t)\in S\times T
and
(u,v)\in U\times V
. Then

\displaystyle\bigl[\,(s,t)\,,\,(u,v)\,\bigr]

\displaystyle=\bigl(\,(s,t)\wedge(u,v)\,\bigr)\boldsymbol{\cdot}\bigl(\,(u,v)\wedge(s,t)\,\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bigl(s\wedge u\,,\,t\wedge v\bigr)\boldsymbol{\cdot}\bigl(u\wedge s\,,\,v\wedge t\bigr)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\,(s\wedge u)\boldsymbol{\cdot}_{\mathbf{A}}(u\wedge s)\,,\,(t\wedge v)\boldsymbol{\cdot}_{\mathbf{B}}(v\wedge t)\,\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\,[s,u]_{\mathbf{A}}\,,\,[t,v]_{\mathbf{B}}\,\Bigr)\,.

This implies that subalgebras
[S\times T,U\times V]
and
[S,U]\times[T,V]
have the same sets of generators, and thus they are equal.

∎

Lemma 2.4
.

If
S\subseteq T\subseteq\mathbf{A}
, then
[S,\mathbf{A}]\leq[T,\mathbf{A}]
.

Proof.

Any generator
[s,a]
of
[S,\mathbf{A}]
is also a generator of
[T,\mathbf{A}]
since
S\subseteq T
.

∎

The
derived subalgebra
of
\mathbf{A}
is
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}:=\mathbf{A}^{(1)}:=[\mathbf{A},\mathbf{A}]
. From there we define the
derived series
of
\mathbf{A}
:

\mathbf{A}^{(2)}=[\mathbf{A}^{\prime},\mathbf{A}^{\prime}]\text{ and }\mathbf{A}^{(k+1)}=[\mathbf{A}^{(k)},\mathbf{A}^{(k)}]

for
k>2
. It is easy to see that
\mathbf{A}^{(k+1)}
is a subalgebra of
\mathbf{A}^{(k)}
, so this is a descending series of subalgebras:

\mathbf{A}\geq\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\geq\mathbf{A}^{(2)}\geq\cdots\geq\mathbf{A}^{(k)}\geq\cdots\,.

We say
\mathbf{A}
is
solvable
if
\mathbf{A}^{(n)}=\{0\}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Najafi proved in
[
13
]
that
\mathbf{A}
is commutative if and only if
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}=\{0\}
, so any commutative BCK-algebra is solvable. In the subsequent paper
[
14
]
, Najafi and Saied proved that a quotient
\mathbf{A}/I
is commutative if and only if
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\leq I
. The proof of this follows from the fact that
[C_{x},C_{y}]_{\mathbf{A}/I}=C_{[x,y]}
.

In general, the derived subalgebra
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
need not be an ideal. To remedy this, we introduce the following:

Definition 2.5
.

The
derived ideal
of
\mathbf{A}
is the ideal generated by
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
. Symbolically,

\DI(\mathbf{A}):=\bigl(\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\bigr]=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\subseteq I\\
I\unlhd\mathbf{A}\end{subarray}}I\,.

From (8), it follows that

\DI(\mathbf{A})=\{\,x\in\mathbf{A}\,\mid\,(\cdots((x\boldsymbol{\cdot}a_{1})\boldsymbol{\cdot}a_{2})\cdots)\boldsymbol{\cdot}a_{n}=0\text{ for some $a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathbf{A}^{\prime}$}\,\}\,.

Notice that
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\leq\DI(\mathbf{A})
and thus the quotient
\mathbf{A}/\DI(\mathbf{A})
is commutative.

Definition 2.7
.

The
commutativization
of
\mathbf{A}
is the quotient algebra

\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{comm}}:=\mathbf{A}/\DI(\mathbf{A})\,.

Let
\mathtt{BCK}
and
\mathtt{cBCK}
denote the categories of BCK-algebras and commutative BCK-algebras, respectively, with BCK-homomorphisms. The commutativization enjoys the following universal property:

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathtt{BCK}
. Given any homomorphism
\varphi\colon\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{C}
with
\mathbf{C}\in\mathtt{cBCK}
, there exists a unique homomorphism
\psi\colon\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{comm}}\to\mathbf{C}
such that
\varphi=\psi\circ\pi
, where
\pi
is the natural projection
\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{comm}}
.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{A}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{comm}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\exists!\,\psi}

Proof.

Since
\varphi
is a homomorphism and
\mathbf{C}
is commutative, an easy computation shows

\varphi\bigl([x,y]_{\mathbf{A}}\bigr)=\bigl[\varphi(x),\varphi(y)\bigr]_{\mathbf{C}}=0_{\mathbf{C}}

and thus
[x,y]\in\ker(\varphi)
for all
x,y\in\mathbf{A}
. This means
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\subseteq\ker(\varphi)
, but since
\ker(\varphi)
is an ideal we get
\ker(\pi)=\DI(\mathbf{A})\subseteq\ker(f)
as well. By Theorem 15 of
[
11
]
, there exists a unique homomorphism
\psi\colon\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{comm}}\to\mathbf{C}
such that
\varphi=\psi\circ\pi
.

∎

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
\varphi\colon\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{B}
be a homomorphism of BCK-algebras. Then:

(1)

\varphi\bigl(\DI(\mathbf{A})\bigr)\subseteq\DI(\mathbf{B})
, and

(2)

\varphi
induces a homomorphism
\Phi\colon\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{comm}}\to\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{comm}}
by

\Phi(C_{x})=C_{\varphi(x)}\,.

Proof.

(1) We first show that
\varphi(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})\subseteq\mathbf{B}^{\prime}
. Take
y\in\varphi(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})
. Then there exists
x\in\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
with
\varphi(x)=y
, and since
x\in\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
we can write
x=\prod_{i=1}^{n}[a_{i},c_{i}]
for some
a_{i},c_{i}\in\mathbf{A}
. But then

y=\varphi(x)=\varphi\Bigl(\prod[a_{i},c_{i}]\Bigr)=\prod\varphi\bigl([a_{i},c_{i}]\bigr)=\prod\bigl[\varphi(a_{i}),\varphi(c_{i})\bigr]\in\mathbf{B}^{\prime}\,.

Hence,
\varphi(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})\subseteq\mathbf{B}^{\prime}
.

Now take
y\in\varphi\bigl(\DI(\mathbf{A})\bigr)
. Then there is
x\in\DI(\mathbf{A})
with
\varphi(x)=y
, and since
x\in\DI(\mathbf{A})
there exist
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
such that

(\cdots((x\boldsymbol{\cdot}a_{1})\boldsymbol{\cdot}a_{2})\cdots)\boldsymbol{\cdot}a_{n}=0_{\mathbf{A}}\,.

Applying
\varphi
to both sides gives

y\boldsymbol{\cdot}\varphi(a_{1})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\varphi(a_{2})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\cdots\boldsymbol{\cdot}\varphi(a_{n})=0_{\mathbf{B}}\,,

where the product is left-associated. Since each
\varphi(a_{i})\in\mathbf{B}^{\prime}
, we have
y\in DI(\mathbf{B})
. Thus,
\varphi\bigl(\DI(\mathbf{A})\bigr)\subseteq\DI(\mathbf{B})
.

(2) We show that
\Phi
is well-defined. Suppose
C_{x}=C_{y}
for some
x,y\in\mathbf{A}
. This means that
x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y,y\boldsymbol{\cdot}x\in\DI(\mathbf{A})
. But then by (1) above, we have
\varphi(x)\boldsymbol{\cdot}\varphi(y),\varphi(y)\boldsymbol{\cdot}\varphi(x)\in\DI(\mathbf{B})
. Hence,
C_{\varphi(x)}=C_{\varphi(y)}
.

An easy computation now shows that
\Phi
is a BCK-homomorphism.

∎

From this it is straightforward to see that
(-)^{\mathrm{comm}}
is a covariant functor
\mathtt{BCK}\to\mathtt{cBCK}
which assigns to each BCK-algebra
\mathbf{A}
its commutativization
\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{comm}}
and to each homomorphism
\varphi\colon\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{B}

. From Theorem

we see that this functor is left adjoint to the forgetful functor
\mathsf{U}\colon\mathtt{cBCK}\to\mathtt{BCK}
, and hence
\mathtt{cBCK}
is a reflective subcategory of
\mathtt{BCK}
.

3.
Central series and nilpotence

In this section, we introduce the concept of central series for BCK-algebras and use them to define a notion of nilpotence. As mentioned in the introduction, we can view nilpotence as a measure of “how commutative” a BCK-algebra is, in the same way that nilpotence for groups is a measure of how close a group is to being Abelian.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a BCK-algebra.

(1)

An
ascending central series
of
\mathbf{A}
is a sequence of subalgebras

\{0\}=\mathbf{B}_{0}\leq\mathbf{B}_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\mathbf{B}_{n-1}\leq\mathbf{B}_{n}\leq\cdots\leq\mathbf{A}

such that
[\mathbf{B}_{i+1},\mathbf{A}]\leq\mathbf{B}_{i}
.

(2)

A
descending central series
of
\mathbf{A}
is a sequence of subalgebras

\{0\}\leq\cdots\leq\mathbf{B}_{n}\leq\mathbf{B}_{n-1}\leq\cdots\leq\mathbf{B}_{1}\leq\mathbf{B}_{0}=\mathbf{A}

such that
[\mathbf{B}_{i},\mathbf{A}]\leq\mathbf{B}_{i+1}
.

(3)

We say
\mathbf{A}
is
nilpotent
if it has a central series of finite length. The length of a finite central series is the
nilpotence class
of
\mathbf{A}
.

Just as in group theory, there are other equivalent definitions of nilpotence. First, for
c\geq 3
, the
(left-normed) pseudo-commutator of weight
c
is defined by

[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{c}]=\bigl[\,[x_{1},\ldots,x_{c-1}]\,,\,x_{c}\,\bigr]\,.

Using these higher order pseudo-commutators, we define the
upper central series
of
\mathbf{A}
by
Z_{0}(\mathbf{A})=\{0\}
,

Z_{1}(\mathbf{A})=\langle\,x\in\mathbf{A}\,\mid\,[x,y]=0\text{ for all $y\in\mathbf{A}$}\,\rangle\,,

and for
n>1
,

Z_{n}(\mathbf{A})=\langle\,x\in\mathbf{A}\,\mid\,[x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}]=0\text{ for all $y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\in\mathbf{A}$}\,\rangle\,.

When the underlying algebra is understood, we will simply write
Z_{k}
for
Z_{k}(\mathbf{A})
.

Lemma 3.2
.

The upper central series is an ascending central series.

Proof.

It is straightforward to show that this series of subalgebras is ascending.

We show this series is central; that is,
[Z_{k},\mathbf{A}]\leq Z_{k-1}
. Take a generator
z\in[Z_{k},\mathbf{A}]
so that
z=[x,y]
where
x\in Z_{k}
and
y\in\mathbf{A}
. Now take
y_{1},\ldots,y_{k-1}\in\mathbf{A}
. Then we have

[z,y_{1},\ldots,y_{k-1}]=\bigl[[x,y],y_{1},\ldots,y_{k-1}\bigr]=[x,y,y_{1},\ldots,y_{k-1}]=0

since
x\in Z_{k}
, and this equality tells us
z\in Z_{k-1}
. Since all the generators of
[Z_{k},\mathbf{A}]
are elements of
Z_{k-1}
, we have
[Z_{k},\mathbf{A}]\leq Z_{k-1}
.

∎

We call
Z_{1}(\mathbf{A})
the
psuedo-center
of
\mathbf{A}
, as the elements of
Z_{1}
need not be central, as the next example shows.

\mathbf{A}_{k+1}
is a subalgebra of
\mathbf{A}_{k}
, and it is a central series by definition.

We will show that an algebra
\mathbf{A}
is nilpotent if and only if the lower central series is finite if and only if the upper central series is finite, and that the two series will have the same length.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose
\{\mathbf{B}_{n}\}
is a descending central series of
\mathbf{A}
. Then
\mathbf{A}_{k}\leq\mathbf{B}_{k}
for all
k
.

Proof.

Note that
\mathbf{A}_{1}=[\mathbf{A},\mathbf{A}]=[\mathbf{B}_{0},\mathbf{A}]\leq\mathbf{B}_{1}
, giving our base case. Suppose that
\mathbf{A}_{k-1}\leq\mathbf{B}_{k-1}
for some
k\geq 2
. Then by Lemma
2.4
we have

\mathbf{A}_{k}=[\mathbf{A}_{k-1},\mathbf{A}]\leq[\mathbf{B}_{k-1},\mathbf{A}]\leq\mathbf{B}_{k}

and the result follows by induction.

∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Suppose
\{\mathbf{B}_{n}\}
is an ascending central series of
\mathbf{A}
. Then
\mathbf{B}_{k}\leq Z_{k}(\mathbf{A})
for all
k
.

Proof.

By definition we have
\mathbf{B}_{0}=\{0\}=Z_{0}
. Suppose
\mathbf{B}_{k-1}\leq Z_{k-1}
for some
k\geq 1
. Take
x\in\mathbf{B}_{k}
and
y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{k}\in\mathbf{A}
. Then

[x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}]=\bigl[[x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{k-1}],y_{k}\bigr]=[0,y_{k}]=0\,,

which means
x\in Z_{k}
. Thus
\mathbf{B}_{k}\leq Z_{k}
and the result follows by induction.

∎

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a BCK-algebra. Then
Z_{n}(\mathbf{A})=\mathbf{A}
if and only if
\mathbf{A}_{n}=\{0\}
for some
n
, in which case the least
n
’s for which these occur are the same.

Proof.

Suppose
Z_{n}=\mathbf{A}
. By reversing indices, we can view the upper central series as a descending central series: set
\mathbf{B}_{k}=Z_{n-k}
for
k=0,1,\ldots,n
. By Lemma
3.4
, we get
\mathbf{A}_{k}\leq\mathbf{B}_{k}=Z_{n-k}
, which means
\mathbf{A}_{n}=\{0\}
.

The converse is analogous using Lemma
3.5
.

∎

Corollary 3.7
.

A BCK-algebra
\mathbf{A}
is nilpotent if and only if the upper central series terminates at
\mathbf{A}
in finitely steps if and only if the lower central series terminates at
\{0\}
in finitely many steps.

Corollary 3.8
.

A BCK-algebra
\mathbf{A}
is commutative if and only if
Z_{1}(\mathbf{A})=\mathbf{A}
. That is, the commutative BCK-algebras are precisely those that are nilpotent of class 1.

Example 3.9
.

Recall the algebra
\mathbf{B}
from Example
3.3
. We saw previously that
Z_{1}(\mathbf{B})=\{0,1,2,4,5,6\}
. We further compute

\mathbf{B}_{1}=\{0,a\}\subset\{\,x\in\mathbf{B}\,\mid\,[x,y]=0\text{ for all $y\in\mathbf{B}$}\,\}\subset Z_{1}(\mathbf{B})

from which it follows that
Z_{2}(\mathbf{B})=\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{B}_{2}=\{0\}
. Thus,
\mathbf{B}
is nilpotent of class 2, using any definition. Note that
\mathbf{B}
is not commutative.

So we see that any commutative BCK-algebra is nilpotent, but not every nilpotent BCK-algebra is commutative. We can therefore view the nilpotence class as a measure of “how commutative” a BCK-algebra is, with a lower nilpotence class indicating an algebra that is “closer” to being commutative.

Proposition 3.12
.

If
\mathbf{A}
is a nilpotent BCK-algebra, then it is also solvable.

Proof.

We first show by induction that
\mathbf{A}^{(n)}\leq\mathbf{A}_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The base case is obvious since
\mathbf{A}^{(1)}:=\mathbf{A}^{\prime}:=\mathbf{A}_{1}
.

Suppose
\mathbf{A}^{(k)}\leq\mathbf{A}_{k}
for some
k>1
. By definition we have

\mathbf{A}^{(k+1)}=[\mathbf{A}^{(k)},\mathbf{A}^{(k)}]\text{ and }\mathbf{A}_{k+1}=[\mathbf{A}_{k},\mathbf{A}]\,.

Let
[x,y]\in\mathbf{A}^{(k+1)}
be a generator. Then
x\in\mathbf{A}^{(k)}\leq\mathbf{A}_{k}
by the inductive hypothesis and
y\in\mathbf{A}^{(k)}\leq\mathbf{A}
. Thus,
[x,y]
is a generator of
\mathbf{A}_{k+1}
. Hence, every generator of
\mathbf{A}^{(k+1)}
is also a generator of
\mathbf{A}_{k+1}
, which implies that
\mathbf{A}^{(k+1)}\leq\mathbf{A}_{k+1}
. By induction, we see
\mathbf{A}^{(n)}\leq\mathbf{A}_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

From this it is immediate that, if
\mathbf{A}
is nilpotent, then
\mathbf{A}
is solvable.

∎

Thus, we have the following containments

\text{Commutative BCK }\subset\text{ Nilpotent BCK }\subseteq\text{ Solvable BCK }\subset\text{ BCK }\,.

We have not yet found an example of a solvable BCK-algebra that is not nilpotent, though we suspect such examples exist. Any such example must be infinite (see Theorem
4.11
below) and non-commutative.

4.
Properties of nilpotent algebras

We collect here some properties of nilpotent BCK-algebras, many of which should not be surprising.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
\mathbf{B}\leq\mathbf{A}
, then
\mathbf{B}_{n}\leq\mathbf{A}_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

It is clear that
\mathbf{B}^{\prime}\leq\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
since any generator of
\mathbf{B}^{\prime}
is a generator of
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
.

Suppose
\mathbf{B}_{k-1}\leq\mathbf{A}_{k-1}
for some
k>1
. Take a generator
z\in\mathbf{B}_{k}
. Then we can write
z=[x,y]
for some
x\in\mathbf{B}_{k-1}
and
y\in\mathbf{B}
. By the inductive hypothesis,
x\in\mathbf{A}_{k-1}
and of course
y\in\mathbf{A}
. Thus,
z
is a generator of
\mathbf{A}_{k}=[\mathbf{A}_{k-1},\mathbf{A}]
. Since all generators of
\mathbf{B}_{k}
are generators of
\mathbf{A}_{k}
, it follows that
\mathbf{B}_{k}\leq\mathbf{A}_{k}
.

The result follows by induction.

∎

Corollary 4.2
.

Subalgebras of nilpotent BCK-algebras are nilpotent.

Lemma 4.3
.

If
\varphi\colon\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{B}
is a surjective BCK-homomorphism, then
\varphi(\mathbf{A}_{n})=\mathbf{B}_{n}
.

Proof.

We saw in the proof of Lemma
2.9
that
\varphi(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})\subseteq\mathbf{B}^{\prime}
. For ther other inclusion, take
b\in\mathbf{B}^{\prime}
. Then we can write
b=\prod[y_{i},z_{i}]
for some finite collections
y_{i},z_{i}\in\mathbf{B}
. Since
\varphi
is surjective, there exist
a_{i},c_{i}\in\mathbf{A}
such that
y_{i}=\varphi(a_{i})
and
z_{i}=\varphi(c_{i})
, and we have

b=\prod[y_{i},z_{i}]=\prod\bigl[\varphi(a_{i}),\varphi(c_{i})\bigr]=\prod\varphi\bigl([a_{i},c_{i}]\bigr)=\varphi\Bigl(\prod[a_{i},c_{i}]\Bigr)\in\varphi(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})\,.

Thus,
\mathbf{B}^{\prime}\subseteq\varphi(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})
and this gives
\mathbf{B}^{\prime}=\varphi(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})
.

The rest of the argument proceeds by an induction.

∎

Corollary 4.4
.

If
\mathbf{A}
is nilpotent and
\varphi\colon\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{B}
a surjective homomorphism, then
\mathbf{B}
is nilpotent.

Some caution is needed here. The class
\mathtt{BCK}
is not a variety: Wroński showed in
[
15
]
that
\mathtt{BCK}
is not closed under homomorphic images. However, Blok and Raftery showed in
[
1
]
that the lattice of ideals of a BCK-algebra is isomorphic to the lattice of BCK-congruences (that is, congruences
\theta
of
\mathbf{A}
such that the quotient
\mathbf{A}/\theta
is again a BCK-algebra). Hence, by Corollary
4.4
, if
\mathbf{A}
is nilpotent then so is
\mathbf{A}/I
for any ideal of
\mathbf{A}
. From this discussion, we see that for any variety of BCK-algebras
\mathtt{V}
, the subclass
\mathtt{V}_{\mathrm{nil}}
of nilpotent members of
\mathtt{V}
is closed under homomorphic images.

Corollary 4.5
.

Finite direct products of nilpotent BCK-algebras are nilpotent. If
\mathbf{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{A}_{k}
are nilpotent of classes
c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}
, respectively, then
\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{A}_{i}
is nilpotent of class
\max\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\}
.

Proof.

For two BCK-algebras
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
, we apply Lemma
2.3
above to argue inductively that
(\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B})_{n}=\mathbf{A}_{n}\times\mathbf{B}_{n}
. From this it follows that if
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
are both nilpotent, then so is
\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}
. We extend this to more factors by another induction.

The final claim follows immediately.

∎

Example 4.6
.

On the other hand, an infinite product of nilpotent BCK-algebras need not be nilpotent. Recall the algebras
\mathbf{M}_{n}
defined in Example
3.10
and consider
\mathbf{D}=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{M}_{i}
. We label a sequence of elements in
\mathbf{D}
:

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=(3,4,5,6\ldots)

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle=(2,3,4,5\ldots)

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}

\displaystyle=(1,2,3,4\ldots)

\displaystyle\alpha_{3}

\displaystyle=(0,1,2,3\ldots)

\displaystyle\alpha_{4}

\displaystyle=(0,0,1,2,\ldots)

One computes

\displaystyle[\alpha,\alpha_{1}]

\displaystyle=(\alpha\wedge\alpha_{1})\boldsymbol{\cdot}(\alpha_{1}\wedge\alpha)=\alpha_{1}\in\mathbf{D}_{1}

\displaystyle[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{1}\wedge\alpha_{2})\boldsymbol{\cdot}(\alpha_{2}\wedge\alpha_{1})=\alpha_{2}\in\mathbf{D}_{2}

and in general

\displaystyle[\alpha_{k},\alpha_{k+1}]

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{k}\wedge\alpha_{k+1})\boldsymbol{\cdot}(\alpha_{k+1}\wedge\alpha_{k})=\alpha_{k}\in\mathbf{D}_{k}\,.

Thus,
\mathbf{D}_{n}\neq\{0_{\mathbf{D}}\}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, meaning
\mathbf{D}
is not nilpotent.

From Corollary
4.4
, Corollary
4.5
, and the discussion following Corollary
4.2
, we see that
\mathtt{V}_{\mathrm{nil}}
is a pseudovariety for any variety
\mathtt{V}
of BCK-algebras. Example
4.6
shows that
\mathtt{V}_{\mathrm{nil}}
need not be a variety in general. In particular, any variety
\mathtt{V}
of BCK-algebras that contains the algebra
\mathbf{I}
of Example
3.11
will contain the algebra
\mathbf{D}
from Example
4.6
, and thus
\mathtt{V}_{\mathrm{nil}}
will not be a variety.

Let
\mathtt{BCK}_{c}
denote the class of nilpotent BCK-algebras with nilpotence class at most
c
.

Lemma 4.7
.

\mathbf{A}\in\mathtt{BCK}_{c}
if and only if
[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{c}]=0
for all choices of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{c}\in\mathbf{A}
.

Proof.

Fix
c\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose
[a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{c}]=0
for all
a_{1},\ldots,a_{c}\in\mathbf{A}
. This immediately implies that
Z_{c}(\mathbf{A})=\mathbf{A}
and thus
\mathbf{A}\in\mathtt{BCK}_{c}

Conversely, suppose
[a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{c}]\neq 0
for some
a_{1},\ldots,a_{c}\in\mathbf{A}
. This tells us
\mathbf{A}_{c}\neq\{0\}
. If
[a_{1},\ldots,a_{i}]=0
for any
1\leq i\leq c
, then we would have

[a_{1},\ldots,a_{c}]=\bigl[[a_{1},\ldots,a_{i}],a_{i+1},\ldots,a_{c}\bigr]=[0,a_{i+1},\ldots,a_{c}]=0\,,

a contradiction. So
[a_{1},\ldots,a_{i}]\neq 0
for each
1\leq i\leq c
, which means
\mathbf{A}_{i}\neq\{0\}
for each
1\leq i\leq c
. Thus
\mathbf{A}\notin\mathtt{BCK}_{c}
.

∎

From this we see that
\mathtt{BCK}_{c}
is a sub-quasivariety of
\mathtt{BCK}
with quasi-equational basis given by (BCK1)-(BCK5) together with
[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{c}]=0
. On the other hand,

In

, it was observed that all BCK-algebras up to order 5 are solvable, and the authors conjectured that all finite BCK-algebras are solvable. Dudek resolved this conjecture in the affirmative in
[

. We show the same is true for nilpotency; that is, every finite BCK-algebra is nilpotent, and our proof follows a similar tack to Dudek’s result.

Lemma 4.9

(
[
4
]
)
.

If
x\neq 0
, then
[x,y]<x
for any
y\in\mathbf{A}
.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a non-trivial BCK-algebra and let
M
denote the subset of maximal elements. Then
\mathbf{A}\setminus M
is a subalgebra of
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\leq\mathbf{A}\setminus M
.

Proof.

For the first claim, if
\mathbf{A}\setminus M=\{0\}
, we are done. So suppose
\mathbf{A}\setminus M\neq\{0\}
. Take
u,v\in\mathbf{A}\setminus M
. Then
u\boldsymbol{\cdot}v\leq u
which shows
u\boldsymbol{\cdot}v
is not a maximal element. Hence
u\boldsymbol{\cdot}v\in\mathbf{A}\setminus M
.

To show
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\leq\mathbf{A}\setminus M
, we will show that
M
contains no pseudo-commutators. To this end, first notice that
[0,y]=0\notin M
for all
y\in\mathbf{A}
since
\mathbf{A}
is non-trivial. Then, for
x\neq 0
, we have
[x,y]<x
for any
y\in\mathbf{A}
by Lemma
4.9
which implies
[x,y]
cannot be a maximal element. Thus,
[x,y]\notin M
for any
x,y\in\mathbf{A}
, meaning
\mathbf{A}\setminus M
contains all the pseudo-commutators and therefore
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\leq\mathbf{A}\setminus M
.

∎

Theorem 4.11
.

Any finite BCK-algebra is nilpotent.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathbf{A}
is a finite BCK-algebra. Then
M\neq\varnothing
. If
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}=\{0\}
then
\mathbf{A}
is nilpotent and we are done.

Assume
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\neq\{0\}
. Let
M^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
denote the maximal elements of
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
. We claim that

\bigl\{\,[x,y]\,\mid\,x\in\mathbf{A}^{\prime},y\in\mathbf{A}\,\bigr\}\subseteq\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\setminus M^{\prime}\,.

Since
\mathbf{A}
is finite and
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\neq\{0\}
, we have
M^{\prime}\neq\varnothing
and
0\notin M^{\prime}
. This implies
[0,y]=0\notin M^{\prime}
for any
y\in\mathbf{A}
. Now take
x\neq 0
in
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
. By Lemma
4.9
we have
[x,y]<x
and so
[x,y]
cannot be maximal in
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
for any
y\in\mathbf{A}
. This proves the claim which now implies that
\mathbf{A}_{2}=[\mathbf{A}^{\prime},\mathbf{A}]\leq\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\setminus M^{\prime}
. Because
M^{\prime}\neq\varnothing
we have
|\mathbf{A}_{2}|<|\mathbf{A}^{\prime}|
, and by an induction we have

|\mathbf{A}^{\prime}|>|\mathbf{A}_{2}|>|\mathbf{A}_{3}|>\cdots>|\mathbf{A}_{k}|=1

for some
k
since
\mathbf{A}
is finite. So
\mathbf{A}
is nilpotent.

∎
