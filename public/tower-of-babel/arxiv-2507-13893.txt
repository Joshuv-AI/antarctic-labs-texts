The spin-orbit alignment hypothesis in millisecond pulsars
A. Lorange, J. Pétri, M. Sautron

Abstract
Context. Millisecond pulsars (MSPs) are spun up during their accretion phase in a binary system. The exchange of angular momentum between the accretion disk and the star tends to align the spin and orbital angular momenta on a very short time scale compared to the accretion stage. Aims. In this work, we study a subset of γ \gamma -ray MSPs in binaries for which the orbital inclination i i has been accurately constrained thanks to the Shapiro delay measurements. Our goal is to constrain the observer viewing angle ζ \zeta and to check whether it agrees with the orbital inclination i i , thus ζ ≈ i \zeta\approx i . Methods. We use a Bayesian inference technique to fit the MSP γ \gamma -ray light-curves based on the third γ \gamma -ray pulsar catalogue (3PC). The emission model relies on the striped wind model deduced from force-free neutron star magnetosphere simulations. Results. We found good agreement between those two angles ( i i and ζ \zeta ) for a significant fraction of our sample, between 1/2 and 2/3, confirming the spin-orbit alignment scenario during the accretion stage. However about one third of our sample deviates significantly from this alignment. The reasons are manifold: either the γ \gamma -ray fit is not reliable or some precession and external torque avoid an almost perfect alignment.



1
Introduction

Millisecond pulsars (MSPs) represent a special class of neutron stars rotating at periods around several milliseconds, close to their rotational break up limit. They are spun up during the accretion phase in the binary system they are formed. The accretion efficiency depends on the timescale of this phase, on the type of binary and on nature of the companion star. MSPs spend their life either as isolated pulsars or as accreting X-ray pulsars
(
Lorimer 2008
)
. These two classes are linked by the recently discovered transitional pulsars. Isolated MSPs are mostly detected as radio-loud
\gamma
-ray pulsars as reported in the third pulsar catalogue by
Smith et al. 2023
whereas accreting X-ray pulsars show pulsations in the X-ray band without any counterpart in radio or
\gamma
-rays. In some binary systems with favourable orbital inclination
i
, that is the angle between the orbital angular momentum of the binary and the line-of-sight, the Shapiro effect is detected and used to accurately estimate this angle
i
. Moreover, if the observer line of sight angle
\zeta
, that is the angle between the rotation axis and the line-of-sight, is assumed to coincide with
i
, then powerful tools exist to determine the magnetic obliquity
\textstyle\,\chi
from multi-wavelength pulse profile modelling, the obliquity being the angle between the rotation and magnetic axis.
This idea has been successfully applied to isolated
\gamma
-ray pulsars, like in
Benli et al. 2021
for MSPs and in
Pétri & Mitra 2021
for young pulsars for which good radio polarisation data even better constrain the geometric angles
\zeta
and
\textstyle\,\chi
. If we set
\zeta=i
, as expected in a perfect spin-orbit alignment scenario, then the constrain on the obliquity
\textstyle\,\chi
will be tighter.
Pulse profile modelling (PPM) in X-ray binary pulsars also furnishes an interesting mean to check for stellar spin and orbital angular momentum alignment in those systems, as demonstrated by
Laycock et al. 2025
. Thermal X-ray PPM has also proven to be an efficient tool to deduce the hot spot geometry onto the neutron star surface and to extract the line of sight inclination
\zeta
, see for instance
Riley et al. 2019
;
Salmi et al. 2024
;
Choudhury et al. 2024
using the Neutron star Interior Composition ExploreR (NICER) data.

During the accretion phase in binary systems, the concomitant stellar spin axis and the magnetic obliquity evolution involve a complex interaction between the accretion disk and the neutron star magnetosphere, including electromagnetic torques and precession of a possibly deformed star, deviating from a perfect sphere. The impact on the magnetic inclination angle has been described by
Biryukov & Abolmasov 2021
. A recent review on the spin evolution of neutron star taking into account the different regimes of accretor, propeller and ejector is given by
Abolmasov et al. 2024
. Also
Yang & Li 2023
investigated the evolution of the magnetic obliquity
\textstyle\,\chi
based on the model of
Biryukov & Abolmasov 2021
.

From an observational point of view,
Guillemot & Tauris 2014
used constrains from binary evolution scenarios to fit MSP
\gamma
-rays light curves. However only the outer gap and slot gap models were considered. They claimed that the non detection in
\gamma
-rays is due to the unfavourable geometry. However a flux too low for the Fermi/LAT sensitivity could also explain the lack of detection. Therefore it is desirable to extend their work by increasing the sample of pulsars seen in
\gamma
-ray and for which Shapiro delays are measured. As the striped wind is now the paradigm for high-energy emission
(
Kirk et al. 2002
;
Pétri 2011
)
, their results must be updated considering this emission model. Among all possible target pulsars, spiders are an interesting sub-class of MSPs for which
Blanchard et al. 2025
published a complete census in our galaxy as observed with the Nançay radio telescope (NRT). Many of them show eclipses
(
Clark et al. 2023
;
Polzin et al. 2020
)
and are also detected in
\gamma
-rays
(
Hui & Li 2019
)
.

In this paper we check the hypothesis of spin-orbit alignment in some MSPs for which the inclination angle
i
is accurately constrained by the Shapiro delay or at least constrained with reasonable accuracy. The line-of-sight inclination will be estimated by the
\gamma
-ray light-curve fittings.
Sec.
2
briefly recalls the underlying model used for the magnetosphere and the associated emission models for radio and
\gamma
-rays. Our Bayesian inference approach and light-curve interpolation by discrete Fourier transform is outlined in Sec.
3
. Detailed results for individual pulsars are discussed in Sec.
4
. Possible deviations from perfect alignment are discussed in Sec.
5
. Finally conclusions are drawn in Sec.
6
.

2
Emission model and samples

The multi-wavelength emission model relies on force-free simulations of a dipolar magnetosphere based on
Pétri 2012
and updated in
Pétri 2024
where all the details are given. For completeness, we recall the ingredients in this section, starting with the magnetosphere model, following with the radio and
\gamma
-ray emission sites. We close this section by choosing relevant targets for our samples of MSPs with measured Shapiro delay.

2.1
Magnetosphere model

The pulsar magnetosphere, filled with plasmas, is computed in the force-free regime to deduce its magnetic field structure. In this approximation, the fluid characteristics of the plasma are completely ignored, and only its electromagnetic properties are considered. The size of the closed magnetosphere is
r_{\rm L}={c}/{\Omega}
with
\Omega
the angular velocity of the pulsar and
c
the speed of light. Due to computational resource limitations, the ratio between the neutron star radius
R
and the light-cylinder radius is set to
R/r_{\rm L}=0.1
which corresponds to a 2 ms pulsar.

2.2
Radio emission

The radio photons originate from the polar caps, defined as the pulsar surface to which are rooted the open magnetic field lines. There are two symmetric polar caps, situated at the magnetic poles of the pulsar. The radio photons are produced through curvature radiation. Because of the high magnetic field, the gyration radius of particles inside the magnetosphere is very small, and it is as if they followed exactly the magnetic field lines. Due to the curved trajectory they adopt, they will emit
\gamma
-ray photons, which cannot escape the magnetosphere. Instead, they produce
e^{-}e^{+}
pairs, that emit again
\gamma
-ray photons through annihilation with another pair, and so on: particles are produced in cascade. An
e^{-}e^{+}
plasma is progressively created, which emits in the radio waveband in the form of a beam.

2.3
Gamma emission

The discontinuity of the magnetic field outside the light cylinder is at the origin of a current sheet. Due to the rotation of the pulsar, this current sheet will propagate like a ”ballerina skirt”. The
\gamma
-ray emission emanates from this current sheet and produces a pulsed emission. The number of pulses detected per period depends on the line-of-sight inclination. If the observer looks directly through the current sheet, two pulses per period are observed; if it looks at the edge of the layer, one pulse is seen and if the line-of-sight is outside the current sheet, no pulsation is detected. The emission starts directly at the light cylinder radius, and continues up to several times this radius, with a decaying emissivity. It is assumed that the particles propagate radially at a speed close to the speed of light, thus they will present a focalised relativistic emission. The cone of emission of the particles will depend on the Lorentz factor
\Gamma
associated to their velocity. We fix
\Gamma=10
for concreteness.

2.4
Pulsar geometry

We define three angles linked to the pulsar geometry in the binary. First we introduce the angle
\textstyle\,\chi
, which is the angle between the rotation axis and the magnetic axis, also called the obliquity. Next the viewing angle
\zeta
represents the angle between the rotation axis and the line-of-sight. Finally, we define the orbital inclination angle
i
, which is the angle between the line-of-sight and the binary orbital angular momentum. To observe the pulsar shining in
\gamma
-rays, radio, or both, some conditions need to be satisfied. First, for
\gamma
-ray emission, the condition reads
|\zeta-\frac{\pi}{2}|\leq{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\,\chi$}}}
. This ensures that the observer is looking through the current sheet of the pulsar. For radio emission, the ideal configuration corresponds to an observer looking directly at one magnetic pole at least. However, due to the opening of the polar cap, there is a tolerance on the discrepancy between
\zeta
and
\textstyle\,\chi
. In general, the condition to see the radio emission is
|\zeta-{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\,\chi$}}}|\leq\rho
, with
\rho
the half-opening angle of the radio beam cone. By symmetry, the condition for the other pole is
|\pi-\zeta-{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\,\chi$}}}|\leq\rho
.

3
Fitting of the light curves

Because light-curves are periodic functions of the rotational phase
\varphi
, in a first stage we use Fourier transforms to interpolate the predicted
\gamma
-ray profiles at any phase
\varphi
to high accuracy. In a second stage, to fit our model, we use Bayesian inference to extract the maximum likelihood value and get an estimate of the error in the fitted angles
({\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\,\chi$}}},\zeta)
and phase shift
\phi
.

3.1
Interpolation using discrete Fourier transform

The predicted light curves are computed on a discrete set of phases
\varphi
that are not necessarily sampled at the values as the observations. The idea therefore is to interpolate the model on the observed phases before comparing both curves. To this end, since the signal is periodic, discrete Fourier transform can be applied in order to replace the discrete set of our model by an analytical function evaluable at any phase. Thus, we first perform a fast Fourier transform on the predicted light curve, giving the expansion coefficients of the Fourier transform. Then, we re-compute the light curve using those coefficients, at any phase associated to the Fermi data, by performing an inverse discrete Fourier transform. We make use of the Clenshaw algorithm for the computation of the sine and cosine sums, in order to reduce the execution time of the program
(
Press et al. 2007
)
.

3.2
Bayesian inference

Finding the model that best fits the observed light curve is a typical Bayesian inference problem. The parameters of the fitting are the angles
\textstyle\,\chi
and
\zeta
, as well as a phase shift
\phi
. We look for the posterior
p({\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\,\chi$}}},\zeta,\phi|\{t_{i}\},\{y_{i}\},\{\sigma_{i}\})
of the parameters and maximise it, in order to obtain the best fit parameters. In this expression,
\{t_{i}\}
corresponds to the time data points,
\{y_{i}\}
corresponds to the data points of normalised intensity of the signal, and
\{\sigma_{i}\}
is the error made on those measurements. This uncertainty is divided by the intensity of the signal plus one, in order to give more weight to the peaks in the signal. We compute this distribution thanks to Bayes theorem

p({\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\,\chi$}}},\zeta,\phi|\{t_{i}\},\{y_{i}\},\{\sigma_{i}\})=\frac{p(\{y_{i}\}|{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\,\chi$}}},\zeta,\phi,\{t_{i}\},\{\sigma_{i}\})\,p({\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\,\chi$}}},\zeta,\phi)}{p(\{y_{i}\}|\{t_{i}\},\{\sigma_{i}\})},

(1)

with the right hand side top left term being the likelihood, the top right term being the prior, and the bottom term being the evidence. The prior is taken to be uniform for the phase parameter, with
\phi\in[-0.2,0.2]
. The prior for the
\zeta
and
\textstyle\,\chi
angles is also taken to be uniform in
[0,90]^{\circ}
, however with a constraint on the two angles expressed by :
|\zeta-\frac{\pi}{2}|\leq{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\,\chi$}}}
, which guarantees that the pulsar can be seen in the
\gamma
domain, Sec.
2.4
. The evidence is a normalisation factor that we discard for the maximisation of the posterior. Consequently, maximising the posterior is equivalent to maximise the likelihood, or in the same way its logarithm. By taking a Gaussian distribution for the likelihood, we recover the least square method. In practice, the computation is made using the Python library Bilby, which is a Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling algorithm, originally implemented for the analysis of gravitational waves from merging compact objects
(
Ashton et al. 2019
)
.
After performing the fitting of all the pulsars light curves from our sample, we compare the best
\zeta
fit to the inclination angle
i
provided by Shapiro delay measurements. To do so we perform a second fitting of all the light curves, but now imposing a perfect alignment, meaning
\zeta=i
. The prior for the
\zeta
angle is now taken to be a Gaussian of mean
i
and variance
0.01
, and the prior for
\textstyle\,\chi
and
\phi
are the same as previously.

3.3
Test of the quality of the fitting

In order to assess the quality of the fitting, a Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test is performed on the distribution of the residuals
\{R_{n}\}
(
n
being the number of data points), which are defined as
(
Andrae et al. 2010
)
:
R_{n}={(y_{{\rm data},n}-y_{{\rm model},n})}/{\sigma_{n}}
, with
y_{{\rm data},n}
the data points,
y_{{\rm model},n}
the points predicted by the model after the fit, and
\sigma_{n}
the uncertainty on the data points. If the fit is accurate, the distribution of the residuals should follow a Gaussian of mean
\mu=0
and variance
\sigma^{2}=1
: this is the null hypothesis. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test compares the distribution of the residuals to the Gaussian in order to determine by how much they differ. If the result of this test, which is named the p-value, is larger than
0.05
(meaning that the null hypothesis can not be rejected at a 5 percent confidence level), then the two distributions are close enough to consider that we have an accurate fitting.

4.1.1
J0218+4232

This pulsar of period
P=2.32

\mathrm{ms}
shows one wide
\gamma
peak on almost the whole period, and one radio peak with a complex shape, possibly characteristic of multipolar magnetic field components. The light curve fitting provides
{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\,\chi$}}}=66.5[+0.4,-0.4]^{\circ}
,
\zeta=17.5[+0.3,-0.4]^{\circ}
and
\phi=-0.1
. Due to the peculiar shape of the
\gamma
peak, spread over the whole period, the fitting is inaccurate, with a p-value of
0.005
, since this kind of peak is not reproduced by our model. Indeed rather than one wide peak, we obtain two unresolved pulses. However the radio fit is rather good, considering the complex shape of the radio pulse. The fitted
\zeta
angle is also far from what has been obtained with Shapiro delay, with
i=85.2^{\circ}

(
Tan et al. 2024
)
. When doing a second fit imposing a perfect alignment, the curve deviates even further from the observed light curve, with a p-value of
0.002
. The
\gamma
light curve modelled presents a lot of noise, and the radio pulse profile follows poorly the observed one in terms of amplitude.

4.1.2
J0437-4715

This pulsar of period
P=5.76

\mathrm{ms}
shows one
\gamma
peak and one radio peak. The fitting of the light curve is accurate, with a p-value of
0.24
, and gives back
{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\,\chi$}}}=42.5[+0.4,-0.5]^{\circ}
,
\zeta=43.5[+0.4,-0.4]^{\circ}
and
\phi=0.04
. This is consistent with the inclination from Shapiro delay, with
i=42.42^{\circ}
, assuming the complementary angle
\pi-i
which gives the same light-curves
(
Reardon et al. 2016
)
. When a second fit imposing a perfect alignment is done, we indeed recover a very similar fitting, with a p-value of
0.24
.

4.1.3
J0737-3039A

This pulsar has the largest period of the sample, with
P=22.7

\mathrm{ms}
. It belongs to a very peculiar system, the only double pulsar system known so far. It shows two
\gamma
peaks and a radio peak with an interpulse, hinting for an almost perpendicular rotator. The fitting of the
\gamma
light curve is accurate, with a p-value of
0.85
, providing
{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\,\chi$}}}=88.8[+0.9,-2.6]^{\circ}
,
\zeta=89.5[+0.4,-0.4]^{\circ}
and
\phi=0.06
. This is coherent with the Shapiro delay, with
i=89^{\circ}

(
Hotan et al. 2005
)
. The resulting fitting of the radio pulse profile gives radio peaks with an amplitude and a shape that is not exactly the one obtained through observations, but they are well centred on the observed peaks which is what we are looking for with this model. When a second fit imposing a perfect alignment is done, we can see that we recover a very similar fitting, with a p-value of
0.58
.

4.1.4
J0740+6620

This pulsar of period
P=2.89

\mathrm{ms}
shows double
\gamma
peaks and a radio peak with an interpulse. The fitting of the
\gamma
light curve is accurate, with a p-value of
0.48
, however the radio fit is unable to recover the radio interpulse due to inherent limitations of our model, that we will discuss below. The fitting gives back
{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\,\chi$}}}=74.2[+0.9,-1.7]^{\circ}
,
\zeta=69.8[+1.62,-0.6]^{\circ}
, and
\phi=0.01
. The fitted
\zeta
angle is rather different from the inclination given by Shapiro delay, with
i=87.38^{\circ}

(
Cromartie et al. 2020
)
.

The model assumes that the polar caps, from which are emitted the radio photons, are antipodal, but recent observations from the NICER mission
(
Gendreau et al. 2012
)
tend to show that this is not necessarily true. If this is the case, radio photons for an aligned rotator could be observed by looking at its equator, or could miss an interpulse that should be observed for an orthogonal rotator. This effect is particularly important for MSPs, for which radio and
\gamma
emission occurs close to the stellar surface. Since this feature is not taken into account by the model used, this could explain why we can miss an interpulse in a radio fit resulting from an accurate
\gamma
fit.

When a second fitting is done imposing a perfect alignment, we recover the radio interpulse. However the main radio peak is wider than previously. The
\gamma
light curve obtained is similar to the previous one, even if both peaks seem less centred on the observed ones, leading to a p-value of
0.06
.

4.1.5
J0955-6150

This pulsar of period
P=1.99

\mathrm{ms}
is the fastest pulsar of the sample. It shows one strong and one weak
\gamma
peak, as well as a radio peak with a complex shape. The fitting gives back
{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\,\chi$}}}=88.5[+0.4,-0.3]^{\circ}
,
\zeta=24.6[+0.5,-0.5]^{\circ}
and
\phi=0.35
. For this pulsar, a time shift in the interval
[-0.2,0.2]
was not enough and we had to take a prior allowing for larger values of this parameter. Due to the noise and the large delay between the two
\gamma
peaks, the fitting was hard to perform, only the right part of the first
\gamma
peak is recovered, and the amplitude of the second modelled
\gamma
peak is slightly too large compared to what is observed. The radio fit predicts a radio interpulse that is not observed, and overestimates the signal between the peaks. The fit is still accurate, with a p-value of
0.06
.

The best fit
\zeta
angle is not compatible with what is given by Shapiro delay measurements, with
i=83.2^{\circ}

(
Serylak et al. 2022
)
. The fit imposing perfect alignment poorly reproduces the first
\gamma
peak, with a modelled peak that is too thin and has an amplitude too large compared to what is observed, however the second
\gamma
peak seems closer to the observed one compared to the previous fit. The resulting p-value obtained is
0.13
. The fit of the radio pulse profile is slightly more accurate than for the previous fit, with a smaller interpulse and less signal between the peaks.

4.1.6
J1012-4235

This pulsar of period
P=3.1

\mathrm{ms}
shows double
\gamma
peaks and one radio peak. The fitting of the
\gamma
light curve is accurate, with a p-value of
0.34
, even though the second
\gamma
peak has an amplitude slightly too high compared to what is observed. The fitting of the radio pulse profile shows an interpulse that is not observed. The reason for this is the same as for the non-fitted interpulse of J0740+6620. The fitting gives back

4.2
Discussion

Histograms shown in figure
2
summarise the best fitting parameters
({\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\,\chi$}}},\zeta,\phi)
. Considering the unconstrained fits, the distribution of
\zeta
seems to peak towards values close to
90^{\circ}
, whose origin is a selection bias in our MSP sample. Indeed, one important criteria for their selection was to look for existing Shapiro delay measurements to determine their inclination angle
i
. This Shapiro effect is particularly strong for edge-on binaries, which explains this kind of distribution for
\zeta
. Thus this effect is even more pronounced for the distribution of
i
angles.

Figure 2:
Histogram of best fitting parameters
\textstyle\,\chi
(on the left),
\zeta
(in the middle),
\phi
(on the right) for the first sample of MSPs, in green in the general case and in orange for the fitting imposing
i=\zeta
.

The distribution for
\textstyle\,\chi
for the same unconstrained fits peaks towards values close to
90^{\circ}
, which is inconsistent with an isotropic distribution of
\textstyle\,\chi
, a results also found by
Guillemot & Tauris 2014
. On the other hand the distribution of
\textstyle\,\chi
for the constrained fit imposing
i=\zeta
seems to tend more to values close to
25^{\circ}
. However the sample of MSPs we considered is rather small, so those claims should be taken caution.
Considering the distribution of the phase shift
\phi
, it actually has a mean value of
0.09
, meaning that in general the pulses arrived earlier than expected by the model. A possible explanation could be that the pulsar wind does not propagate perfectly radially, as assumed by the model.

Figure
3
gives a summary of the best fitting parameters, on the left, in the
\textstyle\,\chi
-
\zeta
plane showing the couple
\textstyle\,\chi
and
\zeta
, and on the right, in the
\zeta
-
i
plane. Giving the time scale of alignment during the accretion phase of the binary, we expect those two angles to be equal, as it is represented by the blue dotted line.

Figure 3:
On the left,
\textstyle\,\chi
-
\zeta
plane showing the best fitting couple of angles
\textstyle\,\chi
and
\zeta
for the first sample of MSPs ; on the right,
\zeta
-
i
plane showing the best fitting angle
\zeta
for the inclination
i
imposed by Shapiro delay measurements, with the blue dotted line corresponding to the
i=\zeta
line.

The majority of the pulsars are well aligned on the
\zeta=i
line, representing
63
% of the sample. Among the misaligned pulsar, three of them (J0218
+
4232, J1640
+
2224, J1713
+
0747) correspond to light curves that are not well explained by the model. However for the three others (J0740
+
6620, J0955
-
6150, J1857
+
0943), the result is rather unexpected since the fitting of the light curves is satisfactory and that this kind of curve is well predicted by the model. This could indicate that the pulsar is unaligned with its binary. If this is the case, a possible explanation for this misalignment could be a movement of precession induced by additional torques acting during the accretion phase, not taken into account by the current model or simply a too long alignment timescale.

In order to verify if the
63
% of aligned pulsars could have been obtained by chance, we performed a reduced
{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\,\chi$}}}^{2}
test for the ratio between the viewing angle
\zeta
and inclination angle
i
following the idea of
(
Laycock et al. 2025
)
. The quantity to test is
R={\zeta}/{i}
, which in theory is equal to one, according to our understanding of the evolution of a pulsar in a binary. The reduced
{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\,\chi$}}}^{2}
associated is defined by

{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\,\chi$}}}^{2}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(R_{k}-1)^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}

(2)

n
being the number of pulsars in our sample (here
n=16
),
d
the number of degrees of freedom (
d=n-1
), and
\sigma_{k}
the error on the ratio
R
, obtained from the propagation of the errors made on the estimation of
\zeta
. Its value must be compared to a critical value associated to a probability to find by chance a result above this critical value. The latter is found in a
{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\,\chi$}}}^{2}
table, and for a confidence level of
99.9
% (meaning having a probability of
0.001
to find a higher value), the critical value is
{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\,\chi$}}}_{\rm crit}\approx 2.45
. However the value for our sample is
{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\,\chi$}}}^{2}=1171.10\gg{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\,\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\,\chi$}}}_{\rm crit}
, far above the critical value, and thus excluding the possibility that this result has been obtained by chance.

5
Discussion about possible misalignment

The spin-orbit alignment arises due to the accretion of matter and therefore angular momentum from an accretion disk whose angular momentum vector is orthogonal to the orbital plane that overlaps with the accretion disk plane. The accreted angular momentum
\Delta L
must be comparable to the pulsar spin angular momentum
L
in order to expect significant evolution towards alignment, thus
\Delta L\approx L=I\,\Omega
, with
I=\frac{2}{5}MR^{2}
the moment of inertia of the pulsar (
M
and
R
being respectively the mass and radius of the pulsar), and
\Omega
the angular velocity. The evolution of the pulsar angular momentum is then given by

\frac{dL}{dt}=I\,\frac{d\Omega}{dt}+\frac{dI\,}{dt}\Omega=N

(3)

with
N
the torque acting on the pulsar due to the accretion of matter from the companion. A simple estimate assuming a constant accretion rate of
\dot{m}
at a radius
r_{\rm acc}
shows that
N=\dot{m}\,\sqrt{G\,M\,r_{\rm acc}}
with the internal limit of the accretion disk designates as the radius
r_{\rm acc}
. Moreover, as explained in details in
Abolmasov et al. 2024
, in order for the pulsar to be in an accretion regime, this radius must be smaller than the corotation radius
R_{\rm co}
and the gravitational capture radius
R_{G}
.

Taking a pulsar of initial mass
M_{0}
and increasing with time as
M(t)=M_{0}+\dot{m}t
, equation (
3
) is rewritten as

\frac{dL}{dt}=I\,\dot{\Omega}+I\,\frac{\dot{m}}{M}\Omega=\dot{m}\sqrt{GMr_{acc}}

(4)

using the fact that
\frac{\dot{I}}{I}=\frac{\dot{m}}{M}
. Its integration yields the general expression for the stellar angular velocity as

\Omega(t)=\frac{\Omega_{0}}{1+\frac{\dot{m}}{M_{0}}t}+\frac{5}{3R^{2}}\sqrt{G\,M_{0}\,r_{\rm acc}}\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{\dot{m}}{M_{0}}t}-\frac{1}{1+\frac{\dot{m}}{M_{0}}t}\right)

(5)

with
\Omega_{0}
corresponding to the initial angular velocity of the pulsar, taken to be
\Omega_{0}=\frac{2\pi}{P_{0}}
with
P_{0}
its initial rotation period. The associated general expression for the angular momentum is

L(t)=I(t)\Omega(t)=L_{0}+\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{GM_{0}^{3}\,r_{\rm acc}}\left[\left(1+\frac{\dot{m}}{M_{0}}t\right)^{3/2}-1\right]

(6)

with
L_{0}=I_{0}\,\Omega_{0}=\frac{2}{5}M_{0}\,R^{2}\,\Omega_{0}
the initial angular momentum. The typical alignment time scale
\tau_{\rm align}
for which we would have a variation of the angular momentum of the order
\Delta L\approx L_{0}
can then be estimated by

\frac{\Delta L}{L_{0}}=\frac{5\,P_{0}\,\dot{m}}{4\,\pi\,R^{2}}\,\tau_{\rm align}\,\sqrt{\frac{G\,r_{\rm acc}}{M_{0}}\left(1+\frac{\dot{m}}{M_{0}}\,\tau_{\rm align}\right)}\approx 1\ .

(7)

Assuming a spherical accretion, we take for the inner radius of the accretion disk the expression (see
Biryukov & Abolmasov 2021
)

r_{\rm acc}=0.5\left(\frac{\mu^{4}}{2\,G\,M_{0}\,\dot{m}^{2}}\right)^{1/7}

(8)

where
\mu
is the stellar magnetic moment. The latest can be estimated with the formula
\mu^{2}=4\pi\frac{B^{2}R^{6}}{\mu_{0}}
, where
B
is the intensity of the dipolar magnetic field. This way the previous equation can be recast into

\displaystyle\tau_{\rm align}=3.8\cdot 10^{6}\left(\frac{P_{0}}{1ms}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{R}{10km}\right)^{8/7}\left(\frac{M_{0}}{1.4M_{\odot}}\right)^{4/7}

(9)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\dot{m}}{\dot{M}_{Edd}}\right)^{-6/7}\left(\frac{B}{10^{6}T}\right)^{-2/7}yr

with
\dot{M}_{Edd}
the Eddington accretion rate. To obtain this form, we assumed that
\frac{\dot{m}\,\tau_{\rm align}}{M_{0}}\ll 1
. Indeed, in a first approximation we can write
\dot{m}\approx\frac{\Delta M}{\tau_{\rm align}}
, with
\Delta M
the variation of mass of the pulsar, meaning that
\frac{\dot{m}\,\tau_{\rm align}}{M_{0}}\approx\frac{\Delta M}{M_{0}}
. As explained in
Tauris et al. 2017
, the total mass accreted by the pulsar during the accretion phase represents only a small fraction of the total mass of the pulsar, which is why we can assume
\frac{\Delta M}{M_{0}}\ll 1
. The formula we obtained gives us an estimate of the typical time needed to reach alignment, which is of the order of
3.8\text{\times}{10}^{6}\text{\,}\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}
using the orders of magnitude indicated in the formula.

In most cases, when the accretion phase starts, the period of the pulsar will be much larger than
P_{0}=1

\mathrm{ms}
as given in the former formula, meaning that the previous result rather corresponds to an upper limit of this alignment time. By taking a more realistic value for the initial period, that is
P_{0}=5

\mathrm{s}
, we now obtain a new value for the alignment time of the order of
1
kyr. An accretion rate well below the Eddington limit could also drastically increase the alignment time scale by a factor 10 to 100.

To assess whether a pulsar had enough time to reach alignment during the accretion phase, we need an estimate of the total duration of that phase. For that purpose, we simulated a population of millisecond pulsars (MSPs) born in binaries with main-sequence companions, modelling their evolution using the SEVN code
(
Spera & Mapelli 2017
;
Iorio et al. 2023
)
to track both stellar and binary processes. The spin and magnetic evolution of pulsars was treated following the prescription of
Biryukov & Abolmasov 2021
. To identify detectable systems, we applied a detection pipeline closely following
Sautron et al. 2024
, accounting for selection effects in both radio (at 1.4 GHz) and
\gamma
-ray bands, based on Fermi/LAT sensitivity. Full details of the simulation and detection methodology will be presented in Sautron et al. (in prep). One of the outcomes of the simulation, which involved 580 000 binaries and led to 519 detections (either in radio or
\gamma
-ray, or both) that matched the observations in the
P-\dot{P}
diagram, with only 240 pulsars remaining in binaries, is shown in figure
7
. This figure shows the number of pulsars obtained at the end of the simulation depending on the angle between the rotation axis and the orbital angular momentum of the binary (the angle
\alpha
), indicated on the horizontal axis, and the duration of the accretion phase, indicated on the vertical axis. For the large majority of pulsars, the duration of the accretion phase is about
10^{8-9}
yr, which is much larger than the orders of magnitude we found for the alignment time in the previous calculation. For this reason we expect most of the pulsars to reach alignment during the accretion phase. Figure
7
also confirms that point, since the majority of the pulsars obtained at the end of the simulation that undergone a phase of accretion of
10^{8-9}
yr ended with an angle
\alpha
situated between
0^{\circ}
and
10^{\circ}
. However, 20 % of the simulated pulsars finished their accretion phase with a residual angle
\alpha
superior to
10^{\circ}
. If we exclude the pulsars for which the misalignment we found most probably comes from some limitations of our
\gamma
-ray light-curve model, it seems indeed possible that several MSPs are misaligned in our sample.

6
Conclusion

MSPs are recycled pulsars that are spun up by accretion of matter from their companion in a binary system. This spin up is accompanied by a torque tending to align the stellar spin with the orbital angular momentum vector on a rather short time scale. Being old pulsars with ages of around a billion year, they had enough time to align their spin with the orbit. This hypothesis has been check in our work by computing the pulsar viewing angle from
\gamma
-ray light curve fitting. By using two samples, one with accurate measurements of the orbital inclination angle
i
and another with some constraints on the possible intervals, we found that between 1/2 and 2/3 of the MSPs indeed show spin-orbit alignment within several degrees. However a small subsample cannot be accommodated with this hypothesis, thus either our
\gamma
-ray light-curve modelling is inaccurate or they indeed are largely misaligned. This possible misalignment has been found in our MSP population synthesis simulation where a small fraction of pulsars still have a discrepancy greater than
10\degr
between the rotation axis and normal to the orbital plane. The origin of this misalignment must be accounted for, by for instance the accretion history and by the initial condition before the accretion regime. However such detailed analysis of the binary evolution and outcome will be the focus of a forthcoming investigation based on MSP population synthesis.
