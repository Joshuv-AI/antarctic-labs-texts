Do STEM graduates fare better at times of Crises? Evidence from COVID 19 pandemic in India
Jheelum Sarkar

Abstract




Abstract

I study whether and to what extent STEM college degrees offer labor market resilience during the COVID 19 shock. Both the pandemic and its nationwide lockdown affected occupations unevenly. While some jobs could adapt by switching to remote work or surging demand, others could not. Using the nationally representative high-frequency labor force survey data (2017-2023), I used a difference-in-difference strategy to compare changes in employment participation and monthly earnings between STEM and non-STEM graduates during and after the pandemic. The results suggest that individuals with a STEM college degree were more likely to be employed compared to their non-STEM counterparts during and after the pandemic period. Although STEM graduates appeared to experience higher wage growth compared to their non-STEM peers, the difference was statistically insignificant. Additional evidence on mechanisms suggests that the results are driven by the type of industries with higher share of STEM graduates, their geographical location and their career stages. The main findings were robust across alternative specifications and falsification tests.

1
Introduction

Science, Technology, Engineering and Mathematics (STEM) education is widely known to generate higher employability and earning capacity. As global labor markets are rapidly shifting towards digitalization, the demand for workers who can adapt to rapidly changing technological environment and solve complex problems has also increased
[
2
,
18
,
23
]
. Such transformations have potential to increase labor market inequalities between individuals with and without STEM college degrees.

In emerging economies like India, expansion of higher education has outstripped job growth which raises concerns about job mismatch
[
12
,
14
]
. Persistent gaps between participation and returns to employment are likely to cause inefficient human capital allocation, rising economic inequalities and receding confidence in college degrees. Understanding how educational backgrounds affect employment opportunities can inform research in both education and labor sectors. While STEM education is perceived to secure jobs in high demand sectors
[
4
,
10
]
, there is a lack of empirical evidence on whether STEM education offers resilient labor market conditions during large negative shocks. Moreover, studies have shown that STEM jobs change quickly over time
[
7
]
.

The recent COVID 19 pandemic presents an exclusive context to explore whether STEM college degrees could protect individuals from job loss. The containment measures associated with the pandemic caused global economic crisis which jeopardized the jobs and incomes of especially vulnerable workers
[
6
,
11
]
. The situation was particularly grim in developing world. In this paper, I examine whether STEM degree premium sustains after the arrival of COVID 19. Using various rounds of nationally representative periodic labor force survey, I compare employment participation and wage growth between STEM degree holders and their non-STEM peers after the COVID 19 shock. Using dynamic difference-in-difference method, pre-trend analysis shows that the disparities in labor market outcomes between STEM and non-STEM college degree holders are statistically insignificant before the advent of the pandemic. Results from difference-in-difference estimation show that STEM graduates were more likely to be employed than their non-STEM counterparts during and after the pandemic. Though it was apparent that STEM graduates earned more than their non-STEM peers, the wage differential was statistically insignificant during and post-COVID 19 shock.

I have explored three key mechanisms behind this result. First, STEM graduates were overrepresented in industries which were positively affected by the COVID 19 lockdown. For instance, pharmaceutical industry saw an increase in demand for their products and the information & technology sector was highly adaptable to changes during pandemic. Second, majority of the STEM graduate workers were in urban areas where remote work was more feasible and STEM-related jobs were predominant. Third, STEM graduate employees were more concentrated in early career and mid-career stages. While young professionals have higher adaptation capacity to exogenous changes (e.g., increasing digital skills during pandemic), mid-career cohorts are less likely to be displaced as they have more experiences and stable working relationships.

One potential concern with the baseline findings is that the binary indicator for STEM college degree may be too coarse since it assumes that all STEM jobs share same extent of exposure to the pandemic and so do the non-STEM jobs. However, there exists noteworthy variations within these two broad fields of study and hence their degree of COVID 19 exposure. For instance, both IT engineers and lab technicians fall under STEM but their exposure to pandemic differs. While IT engineers have higher adaptability to perform remote work, lab technicians cannot do so and may be susceptible to job loss if pandemic driven lockdown hindered their commuting. Similarly, individuals with, for example, law and liberal arts background come from non-STEM field but have varying levels of exposure to adverse shock like the COVID 19. To account for this, I complement the binary classification with a continuous variable which assigns scores to various occupations based on their remote work feasibility. The results are consistent with the main findings. Besides, I also conduct a falsification test using individuals without college degrees. It suggests that technical education alone is not sufficient for insuring jobs but rather the college or higher-level STEM training could protect one’s employment relative to the corresponding non-STEM counterparts.

While existing literature on the economic effects of the pandemic has largely focused on overall employment patterns, general educational outcomes, this study is first to utilize nationally representative data to explore whether specialized education can mitigate adverse effects of negative shocks like the COVID 19. The findings have important implications for both education and labor market policies: if STEM fields present better labor market resilience, as shown in this study, promoting equitable access to STEM education becomes policy priority for absorbing negative shocks in developing countries.

2
The Theoretical Framework

In labor economics, human capital is viewed as sets of skills and characteristics which increase labor productivity. According to Becker
[
3
]
, human capital is represented by stock of skills which is a direct input of the production function. STEM education improves technical and quantitative skills which are often valued in innovation driven and technologically intensive high-skilled jobs, where earning potential is quite high.

STEM education also conforms to the Schultz/Nelson-Phelps view
[
20
,
16
]
in which human capital is measured as adaptation capacity to changing environment. The ongoing fourth industrial revolution (4IR) has increased demand for skills which can contribute to the production environment with minimum manual human labor
[
19
,
24
]
. This calls for up-to-date knowledge in emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence and machine learning which are fed by STEM education
[
13
]
. In fact, STEM jobs are characterized by high rates of skill changes
[
5
,
7
]
which have the potential to increase their resilience at times of major shocks or shifts.

Segmented labor market approach, as the name suggests, contends that labor markets are segmented which give rise to unequal income distribution and unemployment issues. Though this approach argues that human capital is less relevant to individual earnings in secondary sectors, it has agreed that human capital has some applications in primary sector where rewards are higher and employment ia more stable
[
21
,
22
]
.

The recent COVID 19 pandemic presents an opportunity to empirically test these theoretical arguments. That is, whether and to what extent individuals with STEM college degrees are secured in labor markets compared to non-STEM college degrees during and post the crisis can provide evidence on two grounds: (a) if STEM education (a form of human capital) can generate more rewards in labor market than other college degrees; (b) if labor market inequalities in terms of employment and wages exist between STEM-intensive and non-STEM intensive segments.

3
Data and Method

3.1
Data

Periodic Labor Force Survey (PLFS):

I utilize unit level data from PLFS for six annual rounds of July 2017—June 2018, July 2018—June 2019, July 2019—June 2020, July 2020—June 2021, July 2021—June 2022 and July 2022—June 2023. PLFS is nationally representative survey conducted by the National Statistical Office (NSO) of India. It provides detailed information on key labor market indicators, including labor force participation, type of occupation and earnings in each quarter of the corresponding annual round. The worker-level data also includes demographic and socioeconomic information such as age, gender, education, marital status, religion, social group, monthly consumption spending, household size, location. Despite the richness of the data, PLFS did not start collecting information on migration until July 2020—June 2021.

Industry Exposure to Pandemic:

I utilize the list of lockdown exemptions from the Ministry of Home Affairs, Government of India
[
9
]
and the 2-digit National Industry Classification (NIC 2008) to assess exposure of each type of industry to the pandemic. I broadly classify the exposure as: (a)
positively affected
, (b)
negatively affected
, and (c)
not affected
. For example, manufacturing textiles (NIC 13), are grouped as negatively affected while telecommunications (NIC 61) and computer programming (NIC 62) are classified as positively affected by the shock. This classification of indsutries is helpful to understand whether the type of industries influences the disparity in labor market outcomes between STEM and non-STEM graduates. The detailed list of industry and their exposure is shown in Table
A.2
.

Remote work feasibility scores:

I assign scores using the methodology developed by
[
8
]
that captures the extent to which a job can be performed remotely. Because many STEM-related jobs are remotely feasible as shown in the figure
A.1
, so I use this continuous measurement as an alternative to my primary binary treatment variable for robustness check. I compared O*NET classifications with India’s National Classification of Occupation (NCO-2004) before assigning the scores since the scores are assigned based on O*NET job categories. The complete table of scores by occupation is in Table
A.3
.

Sample Restrictions:

I restrict my analysis to all individuals with college education and have received some form of technical training within the age group of 16-65 years old. STEM graduates are defined as those with graduate level or higher technical degrees in agriculture, engineering/technology and medicine. In contrast, non-STEM graduates are individuals without technical degrees. However, this classification has a limitation: it does not explicitly account for degrees in natural sciences which are usually considered as part of STEM. This arises due to data constraints. The dataset does not separately mention any category of overall or specific science subjects in survey responses or questions specific to education levels of individuals surveyed. Hence, this definition may result in conservative estimates. I define 2017-2019 as the pre-COVID period and 2020-2023 as during and post-COVID period. India faced a nationwide lockdown during March 2020 which led to job losses, income shocks and structural changes in nature of work and employment patterns throughout the year 2020 and thereafter.

3.3
Empirical Strategy

To estimate how the COVID 19 pandemic influences the college education premium between men and women, I utilize a double difference (DD) strategy using the following specification:

y_{ist}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}({COVID}_{t}\times{STEM}_{is})+\beta_{2}{COVID}_{t}+\beta_{3}{STEM}_{is}+\mathbf{X}^{\prime}_{ist}\beta+\tau_{s}+\tau_{t}+\varepsilon_{ist}

(1)

In the above empirical specification,
y
measures labor market outcome for individual-
i
located in
s
at time period-
t
.
COVID
is a time dummy which takes value 1 for years 2020-2023 and 0 for 2017-2019.
{STEM}_{is}
takes value 1 if individual-
i
located in region-
s
has STEM education in college and 0 for those with non-STEM college education.
\mathbf{X}^{\prime}_{ist}
is vector of individual level characteristics which may or may not vary by time. These include age, years of formal education, marital status, religion and social group.
\tau_{s}
refers to region-fixed effects and
\tau_{t}
stands for quarter fixed effects.
\varepsilon
are robust standard errors.

I estimate a two-part model to separately estimate the extensive and intensive margins of paid labor outcomes. The extensive margin captures probability of being employed. It is defined as a binary variable which takes value 1 if an individual is employed (i.e., with positive earnings), and 0 otherwise. The intensive margin represents wage premium which is measured by log of monthly earnings conditional on employment.

4
Results

4.1
Parallel Trend

To validate the difference-in-difference estimates as causal, it is important to test the presence of pre-event trends between treatment and control groups. If there exist no statistically significant differences in pre-event trends, it supports the validity of attributing post-event differences in outcomes to the COVID 19 shock.

I test this assumption using dynamic difference-in-difference approach using the following specification:

y_{it}=\gamma_{0}+\sum_{k}\delta_{-k}({STEM}_{is}\times T(\text{Event}=-k))+\sum_{k}\delta_{+k}({STEM}_{is}\times T(\text{Event}=+k))+\mathbf{X}_{it}\gamma+\eta_{s}+\eta_{t}+\varepsilon_{it}

(2)

where labor market outcome for individual-
i
in period-
t
.
STEM
is a dummy that takes value 1 for individuals with STEM college degree and 0 if non-STEM college degree.
T(\text{Event}=k)
is a dummy that takes value 1 if the observation is
k
years before or after 2020. Here, the base period is 2019, that is the year before the pandemic.
\mathbf{X}^{\prime}_{it}
is vector of individual level characteristics.
\eta_{s}
and
\eta_{t}
are respectively region and quarter fixed effects.
\varepsilon
is the robust standard errors. The estimates are shown in Table
A.1.
in the Appendix.

Figure
1
shows the results for extensive margin or employment participation. It is clear from the diagram that the coefficients corresponding to the years of 2017 and 2019 are not statistically significant. This indicates that there was no substantial variation in employment participation among STEM graduates and non-STEM graduates before the pandemic. Thus, the assumption of parallel trend holds. The probability of being employed for STEM graduates increases significantly after the pandemic (i.e., year 2020) compared to their non-STEM counterparts. This implies that the likelihood of being employed increases for those with STEM degrees after the COVID 19 hit.

Another channel which can influence the differences in paid labor market outcomes between STEM and non-STEM graduates during and post COVID 19 shock is location of dwelling. In India, STEM jobs are disproportionately concentrated in urban areas where industries such as information technology, telecommunications, finance companies, pharmaceuticals are located. Moreover, remote work adoption was more feasible in urban areas compared to rural areas because accessibility to high-speed internet and job opportunities that require such digital skills are mainly available in urban areas.To examine the role of location of dwelling, I estimate eq. (
1

Panel B presents the results for logarithm of monthly earnings (i.e., intensive margin). While the monthly wage is higher for STEM graduates relative to their non-STEM counterparts during and post-COVID 19 shock, the effects are statistically insignificant in both rural and urban areas (Column 1 and 2, Panel B, Table

This could happen because STEM graduates who continued to be in the workforce are likely to concentrate in occupations where wages are based on fixed pay scales such as government or public sector, Information & Technology.

7
Conclusion

This study is the first to show how STEM education buffers workers from negative exogenous shocks in developing countries. Key results show that STEM graduates are more likely to be employed relative to their non-STEM counterparts during and after the pandemic. However, the positive wage differences between STEM and non-STEM graduates are not statistically significant in the post-COVID period. This implies that employment security provided by STEM college degrees does not necessarily translate into higher income, at least in the short run after COVID 19 crisis. This could be attributable to the structure of jobs. In India, majority of stable and/or high skilled jobs are based on fixed base monthly salary. So, the effect of the pandemic was not significant on monthly wage gap between STEM and non-STEM graduates. Alternative reason could be weaker bargaining power of workers during economic crises if near substitute job opportunities are less compared to number of candidates applying for them.

There are possible mechanisms which can explain these results. STEM-related jobs are highly concentrated in industries which were positively affected by the pandemic. For instance, the tremendous health crises caused by COVID 19 also increased need for medical staff like doctors and nurses in hospitals
[
17
]
. Thus, STEM graduates are more likely to be employed than their non-STEM counterparts. Geographical location also plays an important role. Majority of STEM-related jobs are concentrated in urban areas due to better infrastructure facilities which explains why the disparity in labor outcomes between STEM and their non-STEM peers are pronounced in urban areas compared to rural areas. Furthermore, their position in career lifecycle also matters. STEM graduates are overrepresented in early-career and mid-career stages. More specifically, early-career cohorts are highly adaptable to any shock-induced changes in labor market while mid-career cohorts enjoy relatively more stable employment positions.

It is important to note that this study has some drawbacks. Firstly, the definition of STEM used here excludes education degrees in natural sciences due to data constraints. This can cause conservative estimates of the true effect of STEM education. Secondly, this study does not capture cases of temporary layoffs, and/or job shifts which usually occur in labor markets during a crisis. Irrespective of these limitations, this study indicates that investing in STEM education could increase individual safety from adverse economic shocks. However, accessibility to STEM education is highly unequal in emerging economies such as India
[
15
]
. This disparity comes by gender, caste and wealth
[
1
,
15
]
. Hence, any action to boost STEM education should be accompanied by inclusiveness and reduction in entry barriers.
