MRI-based radiomics models for the preoperative prediction of massive intraoperative blood loss in spinal metastases patients
Authors: Heng Deng, Jiayang Yan, Jiayi Zhang, Fukai Li, Guangwen Duan, Wenhao Jia, Xiang Wang, Shiyuan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo develop and validate MRI-based radiomics models for predicting intraoperative massive blood loss (MBL) in patients with spinal metastases.Materials and methodsA total of 507 patients diagnosed with spinal metastases were enrolled in this study, who were classified as MBL and non-MBL group, with the 2500 ml as the threshold. Radiomic features were extracted from T2WI and CET1 sequences and dimensionality reduction was performed by LASSO regression analysis. Radiomics models were developed using radiomics features, yielding a radiomics signature from the best model. Clinical variables were analyzed to create a clinical model. The combined model incorporated both clinical variables and Rad-signature. The predictive performance was assessed through the area under the curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and F1 score. Calibration curves and decision curve analyses (DCA) were generated to evaluate the model’s accuracy and clinical utility. Finally, a nomogram was developed to visualize the optimal model.ResultsTumor vascularity, preoperative embolization, tumor location, and surgical segments were independent risk factors for MBL. Among 11 machine learning models evaluated, the AdaBoost model demonstrated optimal predictive performance (AUC = 0.775) and was selected as the radiomics model. The combined radiomics-clinical model achieved the highest discriminative ability (AUC = 0.818; 95% CI: 0.7191–0.9165), outperforming both the radiomics model (AUC = 0.768) and the clinical model (AUC = 0.583). The nomogram showed good calibration and provided the greatest net clinical benefit.ConclusionsRadiomics-based prediction models enable preoperative assessment of MBL risk in surgery and support personalized perioperative management strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12880-026-02541-7.Keywords: Massive blood loss, Spinal metastasis, Radiomics, Machine learning, Prediction model

Introduction

Spinal metastases occur in 30–70% of cancer patients and usually lead to symptoms such as thoraco-lumbar back pain and neurological dysfunction, which significantly reduce patients’ quality of life [1, 2]. Due to the global trend of population aging, increased life expectancy, and advancements in cancer therapies, the prevalence of spinal metastases has been steadily rising. Surgical treatment combined with external beam radiotherapy has become a cornerstone for managing spinal metastases [2, 3]. Surgery often aims to alleviate pain, restore neurological function, stabilize the spine, and provide a foundation for adjunctive treatments such as chemotherapy and radiation, thereby improving patients’ quality of life [4]. However, massive blood loss (MBL) remains a significant challenge in surgical intervention for spinal metastases, due to the rich vascular supply of vertebral tumors. MBL is associated with increased postoperative complications, prolonged hospital stays, and a higher risk of early mortality, highlighting the need for improved strategies to mitigate this complication and enhance the safety of surgical management for spinal metastases [5–7].

Managing bleeding perioperatively has become crucial in treating spinal and extraspinal metastases due to significant blood loss (averaging 1200 ml, up to 6000 ml), especially when minimally invasive methods can’t adequately address issues like spinal alignment from vertebral collapse and hyperkyphosis [8]. Therefore, accurate prediction of intraoperative MBL is an essential component of preoperative planning [9]. Existing studies highlights that intraoperative blood loss (IBL) in patients with spinal metastases varies significantly and is influenced by factors such as surgical approach, tumor characteristics, and preoperative embolization [7, 8, 10]. In clinical practice, surgeons often estimate IBL based on personal experience rather than organized data, which may lead to potential errors. Notably, underestimating IBL can result in inadequate preparation for significant blood loss, risking surgical failure or complications, while overestimating can waste medical resources. However, despite continuous efforts, managing MBL remains challenging for surgeons due to the vascular nature of vertebral tumors, which underscores the need for improved predictive methods.

Currently, preoperative imaging evaluation plays an indispensable role in preoperative planning for patients with spinal metastases, which can guide fluid management, intraoperative blood transfusion strategies, and preoperative embolization, thereby mitigating potential complications. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), as a bone marrow-sensitive modality, is widely regarded as the first-choice imaging technique for spinal tumors [11, 12]. Conventional MRI sequences, including T1-weighted imaging (T1WI) and T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), have demonstrated excellent sensitivity in detecting vertebral tumors, providing critical insights into tumor size, location, and invasion. Spinal tumor vascularity, evaluated by MRI, CT and digital subtraction angiography, is also an important factor affecting IBL [13]. However, most existing studies on predicting IBL rely heavily on the clinical experience of the radiologist and surgeons. This subjectivity may limit the reproducibility and accuracy of such predictions [14]. Furthermore, conventional MRI signs for assessing tumor vascularity are subject to considerable controversy with reported definitions remaining vague and interobserver agreement suboptimal [13]. There is an urgent need for novel and reliable methods to effectively predict intraoperative MBL and guide patient management.

Radiomics, a high-throughput method for analyzing radiographic imaging phenotypes, offers valuable individualized risk assessments [15]. Our past studies have shown its effectiveness in differential diagnosis for spinal metastasis [11, 16]. We hypothesized that radiomics could offer a more accurate prediction of MBL compared to traditional radiology by extracting detailed features from preoperative imaging. This approach potentially provides enhanced insights into tumor vascularity, heterogeneity, and the bleeding potential during surgery. The aim of this study is to develop MRI-based radiomics models for predicting intraoperative MBL in patients with spinal metastasis and to evaluate its effectiveness. Specifically, we developed models using only clinical features, only MRI radiomic features, and fusion features that combines both. This study is an attempt to provide a comprehensive and accurate prediction of MBL, assisting clinicians in perioperative individualized management for spinal metastasis patients.

Materials and methods

Patients enrollment

Between January 2021 and December 2023, a total of 507 patients diagnosed with spinal metastases who underwent surgical treatment at our center were enrolled. Clinical data collected from each patient included demographics (age, sex, height, weight, BMI, and ASA classification I–IV), tumor radiological characteristics (tumor location and radiomics features extracted from preoperative MRI as detailed in the Methods), pathological and oncological variables (tumor hypervascularity), surgical variables (approach, number of segments, and preoperative embolization), and laboratory findings (RBC, HGB, HCT, MCV, MCH, MCHC, RDW, Plt, MPV, PT, INR, APTT, TT, Fbg, and D-dimer). Surgical segments were defined as the number of spinal vertebral levels requiring instrumentation during the procedure, as documented in the operative record. All patients underwent X-ray and MRI to identify lesions, and blood samples were collected for routine preoperative testing.

The inclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (a) patients underwent contrast-enhanced spinal MRI prior to surgery, comprised of T2WI and CET1 sequences, (b) had complete clinical, pathological, and imaging data available, and (c) pathologically confirmed diagnosis of spinal metastases. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (a) had incomplete clinical, imaging, or pathological data, (b) poor image quality of MRI, or (c) History of surgery, biopsy, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy for spinal lesions prior to MRI examination. Cases from January 2021 to June 2023 were randomly split into a training set (n = 294) and an internal validation set (n = 126) in a 7:3 ratio, with the remaining cases used as a test set (n = 87) for model development and validation (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of the patient selection procedure

The intraoperative blood loss was determined using a formula that accounted for theoretical total blood loss and allogeneic blood transfusion [17, 18]. The primary outcome of this study was MBL, which was define as the volume of intraoperative blood loss over 2500 ml [19]. According the threshold, patients were assigned into two groups: MBL group and non-MBL group.

In the formula for total blood volume, , K2, and K3 are sex-specific constants derived from the Nadler equation. For male patients, K1 = 0.3669, K2 = 0.03219, and K3 = 0.6041, while for female patients, K1 = 0.3561, K2 = 0.03308, and K3 = 0.1833 [19]. HCTpre and HCTpost refer to hematocrit values measured preoperatively and on postoperative day 1, respectively, and HCTave was defined as their average.

Ethical approval for this study was obtained, and the requirement for informed consent from patients was waived because of the retrospective analysis in this study. This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. An overview of the study design is presented in Fig. 2.

Equipment and imaging parameters

MRI examinations were performed for all patients using three types of scanners: GE Signa 1.5T (Milwaukee, USA), Siemens Avanto 1.5T (Shenzhen, China), and Philips Achieva 3.0T (Veenpluis 4–6, 5684 PC Best, the Netherlands). The imaging protocols were standardized across all devices, employing contrast-enhanced T1-weighted imaging (CET1) and T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) sequences. The parameters for CET1 sequences included a repetition time (TR) of 500–650 ms and an echo time (TE) of 12–14 ms. For T2WI sequences, the TR was set at 2500–4500 ms, and the TE ranged from 94 to 120 ms. Both imaging modalities utilized a slice thickness of 3–6 mm, a matrix resolution between 320 × 160 and 512 × 512, and a field of view ranging from 240 × 240 mm² to 380 × 380 mm². A gadolinium-based contrast agent (GD-DTPA, Consun Pharma, Guangzhou) was intravenously administered at a dosage of 0.2 ml/kg body weight. Sagittal, axial, and coronal plane images were acquired and subsequently exported in DICOM format for further analysis.

Tumor segmentation, radiomics features extraction and data preprocessing

For each case, tumor regions were manually delineated on axial T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) and contrast-enhanced T1-weighted imaging (CET1) sequences. Initial segmentation was performed by a musculoskeletal radiologist with 8 years of clinical experience using MITK software (version 2017.07; available at http://www.mitk.org/). To ensure the accuracy of tumor delineation, all initial segmentations were independently reviewed and validated by a second musculoskeletal radiologist with 10 years of expertise.

To assess inter-observer agreement, a third musculoskeletal radiologist with comparable experience—who was blinded to the initial segmentations and all clinical information of the patients—independently re-segmented a randomly selected subset of 30 cases (accounting for approximately 20% of the total cohort). Intra-observer reproducibility was evaluated by having the original annotators re-contour the same 30 cases at an interval of at least four weeks after the initial segmentation, to minimize recall bias. Both inter- and intra-observer agreements were quantified using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), with the following interpretation criteria: ICC < 0.50 indicating poor agreement; 0.50–0.75 indicating moderate agreement; 0.75–0.90 indicating good agreement; and ICC > 0.90 indicating excellent agreement.

Radiomic features were extracted using PyRadiomics (version 3.0.1; https://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io/), an open-source software package compliant with the Image Biomarker Standardisation Initiative (IBSI) guidelines, to ensure the reproducibility and comparability of the results. All extracted features were categorized into three primary groups based on IBSI terminology: (1) Geometric (shape) features, which characterize the three-dimensional structural properties of the tumor; (2) Intensity (first-order statistical) features, which describe the distribution of voxel intensities within the tumor region; and (3) Texture features, which capture spatial patterns and higher-order relationships of voxel intensities, thereby providing insights into intratumoral heterogeneity. Texture features were extracted using multiple established computational matrices in line with IBSI recommendations, including: (1) Gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM), which evaluates the frequency of voxel intensity pairings at specific spatial distances and directions; (2) Gray-level run-length matrix (GLRLM), which quantifies the length and distribution of consecutive voxels with the same intensity; (3) Gray-level size zone matrix (GLSZM), which measures the size and distribution of homogeneous zones with identical gray-level intensities; and (4) Neighborhood gray-tone difference matrix (NGTDM), which assesses the intensity differences between a given voxel and its surrounding neighborhood. These comprehensive texture analysis methods enabled detailed characterization of tumor morphology and internal heterogeneity, laying a solid foundation for subsequent statistical analysis.

Feature selection and radiomics models building

A total of 2394 radiomic features were initially extracted from T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) and contrast-enhanced T1-weighted imaging (CET1) MRI sequences. These features were classified into three categories, including shape, intensity, and texture-based features, all derived from the region of interest (ROI) manually segmented by experienced radiologists. To ensure the reliability and reproducibility of ROI segmentation, inter-rater consistency was evaluated in 30 randomly selected patients using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Only radiomic features with an ICC ≥ 0.75 (indicating good inter-rater agreement) were retained for subsequent analysis to eliminate features affected by segmentation variability.

Given the high dimensionality of the remaining features, overfitting represented a critical challenge that might compromise the generalization ability of the predictive models. To address this issue, a rigorous and stepwise feature selection pipeline was implemented to reduce dimensionality, remove redundancy, and retain the most predictive features. First, univariate analysis was performed to screen features associated with the outcome: the Student’s t-test was applied to normally distributed features, while the Mann-Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed features. Only radiomic features with a p-value < 0.05 (statistically significant association with the outcome) were retained for further processing.

Subsequently, Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated to assess the inter-feature correlation, and features with a correlation coefficient > 0.9 were excluded to reduce feature redundancy and avoid multicollinearity. Thereafter, the minimum redundancy maximum relevance (mRMR) algorithm was employed to prioritize features that had the highest relevance to the outcome while maintaining minimal redundancy with each other, and the top 15 most informative features were selected. Finally, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression with 10-fold cross-validation was performed on these 15 mRMR-selected features. By adjusting the regularization parameter λ (determined by 10-fold cross-validation with the minimum cross-validation error as the criterion), LASSO regression shrinks the regression coefficients of irrelevant features to zero, thereby further filtering the features. A total of 13 radiomic features with non-zero coefficients were ultimately retained, which served as the input variables for subsequent machine learning model construction (Fig. 3). These features reflect the tumor morphology and internal heterogeneity from multiple scales and perspectives.

LASSO least absolute shrinkage and selection operator

To evaluate the predictive efficiency of the selected 13 radiomic features, eleven machine learning models were constructed, including Logistic Regression (LR), Naïve Bayes, Support Vector Machine (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Random Forest (RF), Extra Trees, XGBoost, LightGBM, Gradient Boosting, AdaBoost, and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP). Five-fold cross-validation was used to optimize the hyperparameters of each model and validate its predictive performance, ensuring the robustness of the model. The model with the highest Area Under the Curve (AUC) in the test set was identified as the optimal radiomics model, which was the AdaBoost model. Importantly, the radiomics signature was explicitly defined as the predictive probability (or predictive score) output by this optimal AdaBoost model. In summary, the 13 LASSO-selected radiomic features served as input variables, the AdaBoost model acted as the calculation framework, and the predictive output of this optimal model was designated as the radiomics signature, which was used for subsequent predictive analysis. Additional SHAP analysis results are provided in the supplementary materials, which enhance the interpretability of the model (Figure S1).

Clinical feature selection and stepwise logistic regression model

Univariate analysis was performed on all clinical features to identify potential prognostic factors. Variables with a p-value < 0.2 were included in the multivariate stepwise logistic regression model to evaluate their independent predictive value. The results of the multivariate analysis were subsequently integrated into the combined model construction.

Combined model construction

To enhance predictive accuracy, a combined model was developed by integrating the Rad Signature from the optimal radiomics model with independent clinical risk factors identified through logistic regression analysis. A nomogram was constructed to provide a user-friendly visualization of the combined model’s predictive capacity. The nomogram’s performance was systematically evaluated using data from both the internal validation and test sets.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and proportions, while continuous variables were summarized using median and interquartile range. Differences between groups were assessed using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables. Model performance was assessed using metrics such as accuracy (ACC), sensitivity (SEN), specificity (SPE), AUC, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). The predictive performance and clinical utility of the models were further evaluated through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, decision curve analysis (DCA), and calibration plots. Pairwise comparisons of model performance were conducted using the DeLong test and net reclassification improvement (NRI) analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 3.4.1) with a two-sided significance level of p < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 507 patients with spinal metastases were included, divided into a training set (294 patients), an internal validation set (126 patients), and a test set (87 patients). The demographic and clinical characteristics of the three sets are provided in Table 1. The results demonstrated the training set, internal validation set, and test set showed balanced distributions in demographic characteristics, tumor characteristics, and most hematological parameters (P > 0.05). However, certain coagulation function parameters (e.g., prothrombin time, thrombin time) and hematological indicators (e.g., mean hemoglobin concentration) exhibited significant differences in the test set (P < 0.05), suggesting the need for further evaluation of their potential impact on predictive outcomes during model validation.

Feature selection and predictive variable analysis

Use rigorous feature selection procedures involving LASSO regression and 10-fold cross-validation (Fig. 3a, b), 13 stable radiomic features were identified from an initial pool of 2,394 features derived from ROIs of T2WI and CET1 sequences. These included seven T2WI features and six CET1 features for constructing the radiomics model. The combined-sequence performed better than single-sequence and the result of DeLong’s test is presented in Figure S2. The intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) of these 13 features are presented in Table S2. Detailed characteristics of the selected features are illustrated in Fig. 3c. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses of factors influencing massive intraoperative blood loss (Table 2) revealed tumor vascularity, preoperative embolization, tumor location, and surgical levels as independent risk factors for MBL. Notably, preoperative embolization exhibited a protective effect (OR = 0.23).

Predictive performance of all models on the training and validation sets

Overall, all models performed well on the training set but generally exhibited performance degradation after being transferred to the validation set. There were significant differences in the indicator balance among the models: some focused on Sensitivity or Specificity, while a few could balance generalization ability and indicator balance, providing a basis for selecting the optimal model.

The performance on the training set showed obvious stratification: the KNN model performed the best (AUC = 0.976, Accuracy = 97.3%, Specificity = 1.000) with extremely strong fitting ability; the Gradient Boosting model ranked second (AUC = 0.862, Accuracy = 75.2%) with good indicator balance. AdaBoost formed the second echelon with ExtraTrees, MLP, and XGBoost (AUC ranging from 0.821 to 0.835), among which AdaBoost achieved a Sensitivity of 87.2%, demonstrating outstanding positive sample recognition capability. The Naive Bayes model showed moderate performance on the training set (AUC = 0.686, Accuracy = 72.4%), while the LightGBM model performed the worst (AUC = 0.612, Accuracy = 37.8%) with extremely low Specificity and severe class bias. Performance on the validation set better reflects generalization ability. All models experienced degradation, but the extent varied significantly: KNN (AUC decreased to 0.646, Accuracy = 43.7%) and Gradient Boosting (AUC decreased to 0.603) suffered from severe overfitting and poor generalization ability. The Naive Bayes model exhibited particularly significant differences between the training set and validation set: although its AUC on the validation set increased to 0.779, the Accuracy was only 78.6%, showing large indicator fluctuations and poor generalization stability. In contrast, AdaBoost had superior generalization stability, with an AUC of 0.775 on the validation set (only a decrease of 0.06 from the training set) and an improved Accuracy of 84.1% and Specificity of 87.3%. Despite a decrease in Sensitivity to 62.5%, its overall indicators were balanced with small fluctuations.

After comprehensive evaluation, the AdaBoost model was identified as the optimal model. Its core advantage lies in strong generalization stability (only a 0.06 decrease in AUC with small indicator fluctuations) and excellent performance on the validation set (Accuracy = 84.1%, Specificity = 87.3%), with better indicator balance than most competitors. Although the Naive Bayes model had acceptable AUC on the validation set, it showed large differences between the training and validation sets and insufficient stability, making it unsuitable for practical applications. Other models either suffered from severe overfitting or poor stability, failing to balance fitting effect and practical value. Thus, AdaBoost is the optimal choice.

A combined model (rad-signature + clinical) was constructed by integrating four significant clinical predictors with the radiomic signature. ROC curves for the rad-signature, clinical, and combined models are shown in Fig. 4. Diagnostic performance comparisons (Table 4) demonstrated that the combined radiomics-clinical model achieved the highest discriminative ability, with an AUC of 0.818 (95% CI: 0.7191–0.9165), outperforming both the radiomics model (AUC = 0.768; 95% CI: 0.6613–0.8739) and clinical model (AUC = 0.583; 95% CI: 0.3939–0.7716). DeLong’s test revealed a statistically significant difference between the two AUCs, indicating that the performance of the two models is not equivalent (Figure S3).

Comparison of diagnostic performance of the radiomics model, clinical model, and combined model in the training, validation and test sets

To verify the stability of the model and mimic true prospective deployment, we also conducted subgroup analyses based on time. Specifically, we divided all patients according to their enrollment period: 231 cases from January 2020 to December 2021 and 276 cases from January 2022 to December 2023 as independent temporal sets. The results show that radiomic model performance slightly decreased from an AUC above 0.75 to approximately 0.71 in the temporal test set. This reduction is reasonable and expected, as the original test set was randomly sampled within the same period and thus shared a highly consistent distribution with the training set. In contrast, the temporally defined test set reflects a more realistic prospective scenario with mild distribution shifts over time. Notably, all models remained stable and maintained acceptable discriminative ability (AUC > 0.70) across different time periods, indicating good temporal generalizability. This additional analysis confirms that our findings are not overly affected by temporal bias and supports the reliability of our model (Figure S4).

Discussion

This study developed and validated MRI-based radiomics models to preoperatively predict MBL in patients undergoing spinal metastasis surgery. By integrating clinical variables and quantitative radiomic features derived from CET1 and T2WI sequences, we constructed a combined model that achieved the best predictive performance across the training set (AUC = 0.864), internal validation set (AUC = 0.806), and test set (AUC = 0.818), outperforming models based solely on either clinical data or radiomic features. A nomogram visualizing our model provided a reliable tool to quantify the risk of MBL. This predictive framework may assist surgeons in identifying high-risk patients before surgery, enabling individualized perioperative strategies such as timely embolization, blood product preparation, and optimized surgical planning, ultimately reducing the risk of complications and improving patient outcomes.

Despite the established clinical benefits of surgical intervention for spinal metastases, intraoperative blood loss remains a significant challenge. Moreover, current tools for predicting MBL in spinal metastases surgeries remain limited. Previous studies have reported substantial variability in MBL among spinal metastasis, with mean blood loss volumes ranging from 870 mL to 2,180 mL [10, 20, 21]. The reasons for the discrepancy may lay in that on the one hand, there existed differences of surgical approaches, patient populations, and measurement methodologies among different studies; on the other hand, MBL prediction is extremely complex and no comprehensive and standardized prediction system is available. Prior studies have identified various clinical factors influencing intraoperative blood loss based on traditional statistical methods, including smoking status, ECOG score, type of primary tumor, sites of spinal tumors, level of instrumentation, level of vertebrectomy, resection method, preoperative platelet count, and so on [7, 10].

Our multivariate analysis also identified several significant independent predictors. The present results show that tumor hypervascularity substantially increases the risk of MBL, and importantly, preoperative embolization effectively mitigates this risk, aligning with prior finding [22, 23]. Furthermore, tumor location critically influences intraoperative hemorrhage. As widely recognized, regions like the thoracolumbar and sacral areas are anatomically complex and richly vascularized, posing heightened risks of vascular injury during tumor resection, irrespective of tumor type. In addition, surgical segment counts also served as a significant determinant of hemorrhage. Procedures involving more segments inherently expand the operative field, thereby escalating the likelihood of tissue and vascular damage, consequently increasing blood loss. Crucially, traditional univariate and multivariate regression analyses are inherently limited to evaluating linearly correlated factors. However, clinical practice in spinal metastatic carcinoma often involves complex, non-linear interdependencies among various factors influencing MBL. The inherent complexities significantly constrain the ability of traditional analytical methods to comprehensively and reliably quantify MBL. Consequently, there is an urgent need for more advanced and robust assessment frameworks to effectively capture these intricate relationships.

Radiomics technology has demonstrated considerable potential in various oncological fields, including spinal oncology. Distinct from previous studies that primarily relied on non-contrast sequences [19], our research comprehensively analyzed texture features extracted from both contrast-enhanced CET1 and T2WI sequences. The contrast-enhanced sequences improve tissue contrast for tumor delineation and provide more details on tumor heterogeneity [24]. T2WI sequences are also crucial, exhibiting high sensitivity in assessing osseous signal abnormalities [25]. Importantly, our study revealed that radiomics models exhibited superior predictive efficacy compared to models built solely on clinical characteristics. This finding further corroborates the significant value of radiomics technology in the field of spinal oncology, extending its proven utility to the prediction of MBL. Moreover, the integration of clinical and radiomic features into a combined fusion model achieved the optimal predictive performance. This underscores that, in the era of precision medicine, combining comprehensive clinical insights with high-dimensional imaging information allows for more accurate patient stratification and provides robust support for individualized therapeutic decision-making in spinal metastasis surgery. This framework holds promise for guiding preventive measures, such as preoperative blood transfusion planning.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center retrospective study with a limited sample size, which may restrict generalizability. Future multi-center prospective studies are needed to externally validate the model. Second, tumor segmentation was performed manually, which is time-consuming and may introduce inter-observer variability. In future work, semi- or fully automated segmentation using AI techniques should be explored to enhance reproducibility and efficiency. Third, due to mandatory data de-identification requirements from our institutional review board, all imaging device metadata were permanently removed and could not be recovered, even after consulting with the radiology and PACS administration teams. Due to the lack of device information, this study cannot exclude scanner batch effects, which limits the generalizability of the conclusions. Future prospective studies must retain device metadata to enable reproducibility analysis and batch effect correction. Fourth, extremely inflated odds ratios for some surgical approaches in Table 2 reflect statistical instability, likely due to quasi-complete separation from small subgroups and sparse outcome events. Though alternative methods suit sparse data, we retained original results for analytical consistency, with a note cautioning interpretation. Larger samples in future studies are needed for reliable assessment of surgical approach associations.

In conclusion, we developed and validated a radiomics-clinical fusion model that achieved favorable predictive performance for identifying patients at high risk of MBL during spinal metastasis surgery. This model may support more accurate risk stratification and help clinicians tailor perioperative strategies. For high-risk patients, early embolization and sufficient blood product preparation should be prioritized. In contrast, for low-risk patients, excessive blood preparation may be avoided to reduce unnecessary resource utilization. This risk-adapted approach may ultimately improve surgical safety and facilitate more personalized management in patients with spinal metastases.

Conclusion

This study developed and validated clinical and multi-sequence MRI-based radiomic prediction models. The combined T2WI and CET1WI radiomics model demonstrates significant preoperative predictive value for intraoperative blood loss risk in spinal metastasis patients.
