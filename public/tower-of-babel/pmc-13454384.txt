CD4 Count and Clinical Outcomes of Mpox in People Living With HIV: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Kesaobaka Batisani, David Chisompola, Tolulope Joseph Ogunniyi, Godsave Binlak Wazhi, Ronald Simusokwe, Misheck Chileshe, Dike Ujunwa Precious, Mahlale Maenetja, Rosemary Mosweu, Hezron Lubemba, Stephen Ebuka Ukaobasi, Nandi Mwangilwa, Lungowe Mulope, Roseline Dzekem Dine, Charles Saleji, Eustarckio Kazonga, Omolola Onifade, Abdulmajeed Opeyemi Agboola, Panji Kwanga, Chipo Zidana, Martin Chakulya, Sepiso Kenias Masenga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHIV causes immunosuppression by targeting CD4+ T lymphocytes, which may influence susceptibility and clinical outcomes of coinfections such as the Mpox virus (MPXV). Despite conflicting evidence, understanding the relationship between CD4 counts and MPXV severity in people living with HIV (PLWH) remains critical. This study systematically reviews and meta‐analyzes available evidence to elucidate this association.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, a comprehensive search from a variety of databases was conducted for studies published between January 2014 and August 2025. Inclusion criteria encompassed observational studies reporting on HIV and MPXV co‐infection with data on CD4 counts and clinical outcomes. Six studies, including 1254 participants, were included. Data extraction focused on immune parameters, disease severity, and clinical manifestations. The Newcastle–Ottawa Scale assessed study quality, and meta‐analyses evaluated pooled associations, heterogeneity, and publication bias.ResultsMeta‐analysis revealed no statistically significant association between CD4 counts and MPXV disease severity (p = 0.1072). The pooled mean CD4 count was 692.38 cells/mm3 (95% CI: 647.73 to 737.03). Most HIV‐positive patients, particularly those on ART, experienced mild disease courses with favorable outcomes, with no mortality reported. Although there was a trend indicating that lower CD4 counts (< 200 or 500 cells/mm3) might be associated with increased complications, these differences were not statistically significant.ConclusionCD4 count alone was not found to be a statistically significant factor associated with MPXV severity among HIV‐coinfected individuals on ART. Effective viral suppression and immune reconstitution through ART appear protective, emphasizing the need to prioritize ART adherence over reliance on CD4 metrics and validation in well‐designed prospective studies.

Introduction

The 2022 global outbreak of Mpox, driven by the Mpox virus (MPXV), prompted the World Health Organization (WHO) to declare a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC), highlighting the virus's potential for rapid spread beyond its traditional endemic regions [1]. A defining feature of this outbreak was its disproportionate impact on men who have sex with men, a population that also carries a high burden of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection [2]. As a result, a considerable proportion of reported MPXV cases have been identified among people living with HIV (PLWH), raising important questions regarding the clinical course and outcomes of this coinfection.

HIV remains a significant public health challenge, with approximately 40.8 million individuals living with HIV worldwide as of the end of 2024 and 1.3 million new infections reported that year [3]. The virus primarily targets CD4+ T lymphocytes, leading to gradual progression through three distinct phases: the acute stage, chronic HIV infection, and ultimately, acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) [4, 5]. Without Anti‐Retroviral Therapy (ART), this progression results in severe immunosuppression and increases susceptibility to opportunistic infections and malignancies [6]. The mechanisms of HIV immune evasion, including high mutation rates, downregulation of major histocompatibility complex molecules, and targeting CD4 + T cells, are crucial for understanding infection dynamics and therapeutic implications [7].

While ART has dramatically improved clinical outcomes by suppressing viral replication and restoring immune function [8, 9, 10], its influence on susceptibility to other viral infections, including MPXV, remains insufficiently characterized. Clinically, MPXV infection typically presents with fever, headache, myalgia, and lymphadenopathy, followed by a characteristic rash that begins on the face and spreads to other body regions [11]. The impact of HIV on the clinical manifestations and severity of MPXV infection has been debated. Some studies suggest no significant differences in disease presentation or outcomes between PLWH and HIV‐negative individuals [12]. In contrast, other reports indicate that immunosuppression associated with HIV coinfection may exacerbate MPXV pathogenesis, leading to more persistent infections, worsened clinical manifestations, including pneumonia, proctitis and secondary bacterial infections, and higher rates of hospitalization, morbidity and mortality [13, 14].

Furthermore, HIV's integration into host genomes and resulting immune dysregulation may increase vulnerability to secondary infections like MPXV [15]. In particular, individuals with uncontrolled HIV or advanced immunosuppression may experience worse outcomes. These conflicting findings highlight the need for a systematic investigation into how immune status, particularly CD4 count, influences MPXV disease severity in co‐infected populations.

While several recent reviews have examined HIV‐Mpox coinfection broadly [16, 17, 18], the evidence remains limited regarding the independent impact of CD4 cell count on Mpox disease severity using meta‐analytic methods. Prior work has focused predominantly on overall clinical outcomes, hospitalization rates, or epidemiological patterns without systematically assessing the prognostic value of immune status as measured by CD4 counts. Critically, the clinical assumption that lower CD4 counts directly translate to more severe Mpox disease remains untested in a pooled evidence synthesis. This review addresses this gap by conducting a systematic review and meta‐analysis specifically designed to evaluate whether CD4 count independently predicts Mpox disease severity in PLWH, thereby providing evidence‐based guidance for clinical prognostication.

Despite growing evidence on HIV and Mpox coinfection, a critical knowledge gap remains regarding whether CD4 count independently predicts disease severity in PLWH. Existing studies report conflicting findings: some outbreak cohorts found broadly similar clinical presentations in people with and without HIV, particularly among those receiving effective ART, whereas other studies have shown that advanced HIV disease, low CD4 count, or poor engagement in care may increase the risk of hospitalization and severe outcomes [19, 20, 21].

We hypothesize that although reduced CD4 counts may biologically predispose individuals to more severe Mpox through impaired cellular immunity and reduced viral clearance, this effect is likely modified by ART‐mediated immune reconstitution and virologic control. This hypothesis is supported by recent clinical and immunologic studies suggesting that well‐controlled HIV infection is often associated with favorable Mpox outcomes, while poorer HIV control may contribute to more complicated disease courses [12, 18]. Clarifying this relationship is both necessary and timely.

Therefore, this study was undertaken to systematically review and meta‐analyze the available evidence on the association between CD4 count and Mpox disease severity in PLWH, and to assess whether CD4 count alone is clinically informative for prognostication in the current ART era.

Materials and Methods

Protocol and Registration

This systematic review and meta‐analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Supporting Information S1) [22]. The protocol was prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO CRD42024580873) [23].

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

This systematic review and meta‐analysis included peer‐reviewed observational studies (cross‐sectional, cohort, case‐control, and case series with ≥ 5 participants) published in English between January 2014 and August 2025, which investigated people living with HIV with confirmed MPXV infection. Eligible studies were required to report CD4 counts, either as categorical tiers (< 200, 200–500, > 500 cells/mm3) or as continuous data, and to document at least one clinical outcome of MPXV infection, such as disease severity (e.g., lesion extent, systemic complications), hospitalization, or specific complications (e.g., pneumonia, proctitis, bacterial superinfection). Exclusions applied to studies lacking PLWH or CD4 data, those without MPXV outcome reporting, non‐primary literature (reviews, editorials, commentaries, conference abstracts), single case reports (n < 5), and non‐English publications; no randomized controlled trials were identified in the search.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

A comprehensive search was performed across multiple databases, including PubMed, Google Scholar, Scopus, Web of Science, EMBASE, and Cochrane. The search was conducted between January and August 2025. The search terms were developed using a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and keywords related to HIV, CD4 count, and Mpox. The following Boolean search strategy was used for PubMed and adapted for other databases: (HIV [MeSH] OR HIV Infections [MeSH] OR human immunodeficiency virus [tiab] OR HIV‐positive[tiab]) AND (CD4 [MeSH] OR CD4 lymphocyte count [MeSH] OR CD4 count [tiab] OR CD4 + T cells [tiab] OR CD4 cell count [tiab]) AND (Mpox [MeSH] OR monkeypox [MeSH] OR MPXV [tiab] OR monkeypox virus [tiab] OR Mpox virus [tiab]) AND (coinfection [MeSH] OR co‐infection [tiab] OR coinfection [tiab] OR cooccurrence [tiab]). The search strategy is detailed in Supporting Information S2.

Study Selection

The study selection process was conducted systematically following PRISMA guidelines. Following an initial removal of duplicates using EndNote X9, the remaining records were uploaded to Covidence for screening. Two independent reviewers (K.B. and D.C.) screened all titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria. Inter‐rater agreement was calculated using Cohen's kappa (κ = 0.89), indicating near‐perfect agreement. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or by a third reviewer (T.J.O.) when consensus could not be reached. Studies deemed potentially relevant subsequently underwent a full‐text review by the same two independent reviewers against the predetermined eligibility criteria. The reasons for exclusion at the full‐text stage were documented and are presented in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1). Data extraction was performed systematically using the standardized online tool Covidence to ensure consistency. The primary focus of the extraction was on clinical outcomes associated with HIV‐MPXV coinfection, with particular attention to its effects on disease severity, clinical progression, and patient outcomes. The extracted data were then synthesized and organized into structured tables and charts to facilitate analysis.

PRISMA flow diagram illustrating the study selection process for the systematic review on HIV and MPXV co‐infection.

Study Outcomes

The primary outcomes of this systematic review and meta‐analysis were to determine the association between immune status, as measured by CD4 count, and the clinical severity of MPXV infection in PLWH. The secondary outcomes focused on elucidating the risk of specific clinical complications and understanding the broader clinical course of the co‐infection. These included quantifying the relative risks for complications such as pneumonia, proctitis, bacterial superinfection, and hospitalization.

Quality Assessment

Two reviewers independently assessed the risk of bias of each included study using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale [24] (Supporting Information S3), a validated tool designed for evaluating the quality of non‐randomized studies, including cohort and case‐control studies. In the event of any discrepancies among the evaluators, a third author intervened to address and resolve them. This tool appraised studies across three domains: (1) the selection of study groups, (2) the comparability of groups, and (3) the determination of the outcome or exposure. Based on the total stars awarded, studies were categorized as high (7–9), moderate (4–6), or low (0–3) quality to inform the subsequent sensitivity analysis and evidence synthesis [25].

Data Collection Process and Data Items

Data extraction was performed systematically by two independent reviewers using a pre‐piloted, standardized online data collection form in Covidence. The following key data items were extracted from each included study: first author, year of publication, country of origin, and study design. Population characteristics, including the total sample size and the stratification into HIV‐positive and HIV‐negative cohorts, were recorded. Clinical data encompassed reported signs and symptoms, indicators of disease severity (e.g., extensive lesions, systemic complications, hospitalization requirements), and investigated factors associated with disease severity, such as CD4 count. Mortality rates were also extracted where available. Any discrepancies in the extracted data were resolved through consensus, and a third researcher verified the final dataset to ensure accuracy and consistency prior to analysis.

As this study is a systematic review and meta‐analysis of published observational studies, no priori sample size calculation or power analysis was conducted by the authors. Accordingly, technical replicates were not applicable. The relevant sample size measures reported in this review were the number of included studies, the total number of participants, and the number of studies contributing to each pooled analysis. Additionally, no experimental control conditions (e.g., vehicle controls) were applicable. Instead, comparisons were based on study‐defined groups within the included literature. These included stratification by immune status (e.g., CD4 count thresholds such as < 200 cells/mm3) and, where available, comparisons between people living with HIV and HIV‐negative individuals. Pooled estimates were derived using reported effect measures (e.g., relative risks) from individual studies.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines using R software (version 4.5.1). A random‐effects meta‐analysis model was employed to account for between‐study variability when pooling effect estimates. For continuous outcomes (e.g., CD4 count), pooled mean estimates with 95% confidence intervals were calculated, while for categorical outcomes (e.g., complications), pooled relative risks (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals were derived.

Between‐study heterogeneity was assessed using the I 2 statistic with values of 25%, 50%, and 75% representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plot asymmetry. Sensitivity analysis was performed using a leave‐one‐out approach to assess the robustness of pooled estimates.

No formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied, as analyses were limited to a small number of predefined outcomes. All reported p‐values are two‐sided and presented as exact values where applicable, with statistical significance defined at p < 0.05.

Results

Study Selection and Characteristics

The initial search yielded 2782 studies from databases and gray literature. After removing duplicates and screening titles and abstracts, 1556 articles were removed (Figure 1). Of these, 1110 records were excluded based on title and abstract review as they did not address HIV‐Mpox coinfection, lacked CD4 count data, or were not original research. The remaining 309 full‐text articles were assessed for eligibility. Following full‐text review, 303 articles were excluded due to missing outcome variable of interest (no CD4 count data reported). Six studies met all inclusion criteria [26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31] (Supporting Information S4), encompassing diverse regions (Spain, Poland, Germany, China). This study included observational cohorts (prospective or retrospective), published between 2022 and 2024, focusing on CD4 counts and clinical outcomes in MPVX‐HIV coinfected individuals. The combined sample size was 1254; PLWH participants ranged from 14 to 849 per study. The selection process is visually summarized in Figure 1 using a PRISMA flow chart.

Summary of Study Findings

The clinical presentation of MPXV in coinfected individuals was broadly consistent across studies, with common symptoms including skin lesions, fever, lymphadenopathy, and systemic manifestations such as headache and malaise [27, 28]. Several studies have observed that PLWH, particularly those with lower CD4 counts (CD4 < 200 cells/mm3) (Supporting Information S4), exhibit tendencies toward more extensive skin lesions, prolonged disease duration, and higher hospitalization rates [29, 31]. However, these differences did not reach statistical significance in the pooled analysis (p = 0.1072). Most studies stratified participants based on immune status, highlighting that individuals with profound immunosuppression (CD4 < 200 cells/mm3) experienced a higher prevalence of complications, such as secondary bacterial infections and systemic involvement. Nonetheless, the overall correlation between low CD4 counts and increased disease severity remains inconclusive, suggesting multifactorial influences on clinical outcomes. Across the included studies, clinical outcomes were compared using study‐specific reference groups, typically defined by CD4 count thresholds or HIV status, with effect estimates standardized and pooled using a random‐effects model.

Meta‐Analysis of CD4 Counts and Clinical Outcomes

Our key focus was to evaluate the immune status of PLWH, primarily through the measurement of CD4 count, and to explore how this correlates with MPXV disease severity. The pooled analysis of CD4 count and clinical outcomes included data from six studies comprising 1254 participants. The aggregated data highlighted moderate heterogeneity (I 2 = 44.7%). The pooled analysis revealed no statistically significant difference in clinical manifestations based on CD4 count (p = 0.1072) (Figure 2). While individual studies indicated that patients with lower CD4 counts (CD4 < 200 cells/mm3) tended to experience more complications, these trends did not reach statistical significance in the combined analysis.

Clinically, the outcomes observed in individuals co‐infected with HIV and MPXV predominantly demonstrated mild to moderate disease courses, especially among patients on ART. Common presentations include skin lesions, fever, lymphadenopathy, headache, malaise, and systemic symptoms such as myalgia and fatigue. While these features were consistent, variations in disease severity appeared to correlate with immune status, particularly the CD4 count. Patients with lower CD4 counts tended to experience more extensive skin lesions, prolonged disease duration, and higher rates of complications, such as secondary bacterial infections and systemic involvement.

Some studies have reported increased hospitalization rates among severely immunosuppressed individuals, indicating a potential trend toward more severe disease in this subgroup [26, 28, 29, 30, 31]. However, despite these clinical observations, the pooled analysis did not find statistically significant differences in disease severity solely based on CD4+ T cell counts, suggesting that immune status, while influential, may not be the sole determinant of disease outcome.

Notably, most PLWH, particularly those receiving effective ART with viral suppression, experienced favorable clinical courses with minimal complications and no mortality. These findings imply that immune reconstitution through ART plays a crucial role in mitigating disease severity, even in the context of low CD4 counts. Regarding complications, secondary bacterial infections and systemic manifestations, such as lymphadenopathy and extensive skin involvement, were more frequently observed in individuals with profound immunosuppression. Nonetheless, the absence of significant associations in the meta‐analysis highlights the importance of a comprehensive clinical assessment beyond CD4 metrics alone.

Heterogeneity and Limitations

Moderate heterogeneity (I 2 = 44.7%) was observed among the included studies, attributable to differences in geographic regions, patient immune status, and clinical management. Despite this variability, the overall evidence indicates no significant association between CD4 count and MPXV disease severity. The limitations of the current evidence include the observational nature of the studies, potential reporting biases, and limited data on ART adherence, viral suppression, and long‐term outcomes. Future research should focus on larger, more diverse cohorts with standardized reporting to clarify the complex interactions between immunosuppression and MPXV pathogenesis.

Assessment of Publication Bias

To evaluate the potential for publication bias among the included studies, a funnel plot was created as an assessment tool (Figure 3). One study was positioned directly on the central reference line, two studies were inside the funnel boundaries, and three studies appeared outside the boundaries, indicating some asymmetry. This suggests the potential for publication bias or heterogeneity, although the overall distribution remained relatively balanced. Given the moderate heterogeneity (I 2 = 44.7%), these findings should be interpreted with caution, and further assessment of the bias sources is advisable.

Sensitivity Analysis

To assess the robustness of our meta‐analytic findings, a leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis was conducted, whereby each study was sequentially omitted to evaluate its influence on pooled estimates and heterogeneity. The results showed that the overall effect size and heterogeneity measures remained stable across the analyses. Notably, Núñez et al. [32], contributed substantially to heterogeneity, with the I 2 statistic increasing from 44.7% to 98.4% upon its exclusion. This indicates that Núñez et al. significantly influenced the variability of the included studies.

Given its disproportionate impact, this study was excluded from a post hoc sensitivity analysis to evaluate its effect on the pooled results. However, because this exclusion was conducted post hoc based on its influence on heterogeneity, the results should be interpreted with caution. The persistent high heterogeneity initially observed indicates variability among studies, likely due to differences in sample size, geographical area, ART adherence, and immune status. Overall, the sensitivity analyses supported the stability of our main conclusions, but they also highlighted the need for further subgroup analysis to explore the sources of heterogeneity.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta‐analysis investigated the relationship between CD4 counts and clinical outcomes in individuals coinfected with HIV and MPXV. Unlike prior studies and reviews that have broadly examined HIV‐Mpox co‐infection, our analysis specifically evaluates CD4 count as an independent factor of disease severity. Our key findings indicate that CD4 count alone is not a statistically significant factor associated with Mpox severity, thereby challenging conventional assumptions that immunosuppression, as measured solely by CD4 levels, directly translates to worse clinical outcomes [14, 33]. This study suggests that CD4 count may not be an independent factor of Mpox disease severity in people living with HIV, based on pooled observational evidence. Furthermore, patients with lower CD4 counts did not consistently experience more severe clinical manifestations or higher complication rates. Most individuals, particularly those on ART, exhibited mild disease courses with favorable outcomes and no mortality reported across the included studies.

While low CD4 counts are conventionally regarded as markers of immunosuppression and a higher risk of infectious complications, our findings suggest that CD4 alone may not be a definitive independent factor of MPXV disease severity in PLWH. Notably, the majority of patients in the included studies were on ART, which likely contributed to immune reconstitution and improved clinical outcomes. This highlights the importance of effective HIV management, as viral suppression and immune recovery can potentially offset the risks posed by low CD4 counts. Moreover, although some studies observed a trend toward increased complications, such as secondary bacterial infections in severely immunosuppressed individuals, these differences did not reach statistical significance in the pooled analysis. This finding aligns with emerging evidence suggesting that immune recovery through ART may mitigate the severity of co‐infections, including MPXV [34].

Despite the lack of a statistically significant association between CD4 count and MPXV severity in our pooled analysis, the pathophysiological interplay between HIV and MPXV warrants further consideration. We propose a conceptual model (Figure 4) illustrating the potential mechanism by which advanced HIV immunosuppression may predispose individuals to more severe MPXV disease. This model is hypothesis‐generating and is based on existing immunological knowledge rather than direct experimental validation within the included studies. The model posits that PLWH, particularly when characterized by a low CD4 count and an unsuppressed viral load, leads to impaired cellular and humoral immunity [35]. This compromised immune state potentially facilitates robust MPXV replication. Enhanced viral replication can, in turn, manifest clinically as more pronounced symptoms, such as extensive skin lesions, prolonged fever and malaise, systemic complications, and lymphadenopathy, ultimately increasing the likelihood of hospitalization. This model highlights the importance of maintaining immune function through ART, which may interrupt the proposed pathway and explain the generally favorable outcomes observed in well‐managed PLWH in our analysis.

MPXV pathogenesis among PLWH with low CD4.

While reduced CD4 counts are biologically linked to impaired immune function, the extent to which this directly drives Mpox severity remains uncertain and is likely influenced by additional factors such as viral suppression, ART adherence, and host immune responses.

Our results are consistent with recent reports indicating that well‐managed HIV infection does not necessarily predispose individuals to severe MPXV disease [12, 36]. Several case series and observational studies have documented mild clinical courses in PLWH receiving ART, even with low CD4 counts [12, 27]. Conversely, other studies have highlighted that severely immunocompromised individuals, especially those with uncontrolled viremia and advanced AIDS, may be at higher risk for complications [18, 37]. The heterogeneity observed in our analysis reflects these conflicting findings, emphasizing the need to consider additional factors such as ART adherence, viral load, and overall health status.

This study is, to our knowledge, among the first to systematically evaluate and meta‐analyze the role of CD4 count as an independent factor of Mpox severity in PLWH. By highlighting the limited clinical utility of CD4 count alone, our findings shift the clinical paradigm toward a more integrated assessment that includes ART status, viral suppression, and overall immune recovery. However, it is important to note that the findings of this meta‐analysis reflect associations derived from observational studies and should not be interpreted as evidence of causality.

Strengths and Limitations

A notable strength of this study is its comprehensive approach, which incorporates data from diverse geographic regions and utilizes rigorous systematic review and meta‐analytic methods. This enhances the generalizability of our findings and provides a clearer understanding of the complex interplay between immune status and MPXV disease.

This study has several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. Methodologically, all included studies were observational in design, which limits the ability to establish causal relationships and introduces potential biases such as confounding and selection bias. The relatively small number of included studies (n = 6) and variability in study‐specific sample sizes may have reduced the statistical power to detect modest associations, particularly in subgroup and complication‐specific analyses. Additionally, heterogeneity in study populations, geographic settings, clinical management, and definitions of disease severity may have influenced pooled estimates. From a mechanistic perspective, although this study explores the role of CD4 count in Mpox disease severity, the included studies did not directly assess immunological pathways, viral dynamics, or host–pathogen interactions. Therefore, the proposed biological mechanisms should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating rather than experimentally validated. From a translational standpoint, the applicability of these findings to broader clinical settings may be limited by incomplete reporting of key variables such as ART adherence, viral load suppression, comorbidities, and long‐term outcomes. Furthermore, the evolving epidemiology of Mpox and differences in healthcare systems may affect the generalizability of the results. Future research using larger, prospective, and mechanistically informed studies is needed to validate these findings and refine clinical risk stratification strategies.

Our findings suggest that clinicians may wish to exercise caution in relying solely on CD4 count when assessing the risk of severe MPXV disease in PLWH. Instead, a comprehensive evaluation, including ART status, viral suppression, and overall immune health, is crucial. Ensuring optimal ART adherence and early initiation is paramount for reducing complications and improving outcomes. From a public health perspective, strategies should focus on promoting HIV testing, treatment access, and education to prevent severe coinfections. Given the evolving epidemiology of MPXV, ongoing surveillance and targeted interventions are necessary to mitigate potential outbreaks, particularly among immunocompromised populations.

Further studies are needed to elucidate the roles of viral load, immune recovery markers, and other host factors in influencing MPXV disease severity in PLWH. Longitudinal research assessing long‐term outcomes and the impact of ART adherence and viral suppression will provide deeper insights into this issue. Additionally, exploring the immunological mechanisms underlying the observed clinical patterns can inform vaccine development and therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

In this pooled analysis of observational studies, CD4 count was not found to be a statistically significant factor associated with MPXV disease severity among PLWH, particularly among those receiving ART. Although lower CD4 counts were associated with trends toward increased clinical complications in some studies, these associations were not statistically significant in pooled analyses, suggesting that CD4 count in isolation may have limited prognostic utility. Most PLWH individuals, especially those on ART with preserved immune function, experience mild MPXV disease courses with favorable outcomes and no reported mortality. These findings highlight the critical importance of considering broader clinical and immunological factors, including ART adherence, viral suppression, and overall immune recovery, when assessing the risk of severe Mpox outcomes in PLWH. However, given the observational nature of the included studies, the relatively small number of available studies, and heterogeneity in study populations and clinical reporting, these results should be interpreted with caution and not as evidence of causality. Maintaining ART adherence and viral suppression appears to mitigate the potential severity of coinfection, emphasizing that immune recovery, rather than CD4 count alone, should guide clinical management and public health strategies. Future research should focus on large, prospective, and mechanistically informed studies to better define factors associated with Mpox disease severity in PLWH and to develop clinically applicable risk prediction models. Such efforts will be essential to inform evidence‐based clinical management and guide public health interventions in populations affected by both HIV and Mpox.
