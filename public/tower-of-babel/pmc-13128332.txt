Double-balloon (CANDIS) Retrograde Transvenous Obliteration Improves Hepatic Functional Reserve Compared with the Conventional Method
Authors: Tomoko Aoki, Masakatsu Tsurusaki, Naoshi Nishida, Masahiro Morita, Hirokazu Chishina, Masahiro Takita, Satoru Hagiwara, Yasunori Minami, Kazuomi Ueshima, Masatoshi Kudo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose: Conventional balloon-occluded retrograde transvenous obliteration often fails to achieve complete occlusion in cases with complex collateral drainage, leading to variable outcomes. The cooperative anti-reflux and double interruption system, a coaxial dual-balloon device, was developed to achieve more stable venous occlusion. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical and hepatic functional benefits of double-balloon balloon-occluded retrograde transvenous obliteration compared with the conventional single-balloon method for gastric varices.Material and Methods: This single-center retrospective study included 48 patients with gastric varices who underwent balloon-occluded retrograde transvenous obliteration between February 2008 and March 2020. Eighteen patients received the single-balloon method, and 30 underwent the double-balloon procedure. Clinical and technical success rates, complications, and hepatic function parameters (Child-Pugh and albumin-bilirubin scores) were compared up to 12 months after balloon-occluded retrograde transvenous obliteration.Results: The clinical success rate was significantly higher with cooperative anti-reflux and double interruption system than with the single-balloon method (90.0% vs. 50.0%, p = 0.004), whereas technical success rates were high in both groups (100% vs. 88.9%). In the cooperative anti-reflux and double interruption system group, hepatic functional reserve improved markedly, with significant decreases in albumin-bilirubin (Δalbumin-bilirubin: −0.283 vs. +0.001, p < 0.05) and Child-Pugh scores (ΔChild-Pugh: −0.19 vs. +1.09, p < 0.05) during 5-12 months of follow-up. Complication rates were comparable between groups.Conclusions: Double-balloon balloon-occluded retrograde transvenous obliteration using the cooperative anti-reflux and double interruption system significantly improves clinical success and long-term hepatic function compared with the conventional method, providing a safe and effective treatment for gastric varices. This dual-balloon approach ensures stable occlusion and uniform sclerosant distribution even in cases with complex collateral drainage, leading to more reliable treatment outcomes and enhanced hepatic functional recovery.Keywords: balloon-occluded retrograde transvenous obliteration, gastric varices, gastrointestinal hemorrhage, hepatic functional reserve, portal hypertension

Introduction

The treatment of gastric and esophageal varices due to portal hypertension in patients with cirrhosis has become a vital part of daily clinical practice. Rupture of gastric varices (GVs) is often reported to be highly lethal because their drainage via gastro-renal (GR) shunts often carries a higher blood flow and pressure than esophageal varices [1]. Treatment of portal hypertension includes surgical, endoscopic, and catheter-based interventional radiology approaches. Surgical procedures, such as esophageal transection, Hassab's procedure, and endoscopic treatment, mainly target esophageal varices, not GVs. GVs are usually located in the fornix, a site that makes hemostatic procedures and, consequently, endoscopic treatments challenging in GVs. Therefore, interventional therapies such as transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS) have become the mainstay of minimally invasive treatment [2-4].

However, TIPS has been less commonly performed in Japan because of limited insurance coverage, procedural complexity, and the potential for hepatic encephalopathy. Consequently, balloon-occluded retrograde transvenous obliteration (B-RTO) [5-7], developed mainly in Japan, Korea, and the United States, has become the standard of care for GVs in East Asia [5, 6]. In clinical practice, endoscopic injection sclerotherapy [8] and partial splenic artery embolization [9] are occasionally used as adjunctive options, but TIPS and B-RTO remain the two major interventional approaches for managing GVs.

In B-RTO, major draining veins such as the GR shunt or the inferior phrenic vein are occluded using a balloon catheter, and a sclerosing agent is injected to induce thrombosis while interrupting hepatofugal flow. It is critical to achieve complete balloon occlusion of the shunt to evaluate collateral portal hemodynamics and to perform effective embolization of the varices [10]. Special attention is required when performing B-RTO in cases with a large left gastric vein (≥3.5 mm in diameter), as exacerbation of esophageal varices due to altered portal flow is occasionally observed after the procedure [11]. Nevertheless, conventional single-balloon B-RTO sometimes fails to achieve sufficient occlusion in patients with complex or tortuous draining veins, resulting in incomplete thrombosis. The Cooperative Anti reflux and Double Interruption System (CANDISⓇ; Medikit, Tokyo, Japan; Figure 1) was developed to address these limitations by allowing more effective and stable occlusion, even in cases with thick, long, or tortuous shunts [12].

For example, single-balloon occlusion is frequently unstable in cases with long or tortuous GR shunts, high-flow drainage routes, or multiple large collateral veins. In such anatomy, the balloon may not seal the entire shunt lumen, leading to persistent hepatofugal flow, rapid washout of sclerosant, or inability to opacify the variceal complex [13]. These limitations highlight the technical vulnerability of the conventional approach in anatomically challenging situations. The CANDIS system was specifically designed to overcome these issues. Its coaxial structure and dual-balloon configuration provide two-point anchoring within the shunt, improving catheter stability and enabling more complete occlusion even in thick, long, or highly curved vessels. By reducing collateral inflow and maintaining stable retrograde pressure, CANDIS allows sclerosing agents to remain in the variceal complex for a longer duration, thereby facilitating more effective thrombosis in anatomically complex cases [14, 15].

Kumamoto et al. [16] proposed the concept of “portal systemic shunt syndrome,” in which patients with large spontaneous portosystemic shunts may develop metabolic disturbances, hormonal imbalances, and hepatic dysfunction. The prognosis and hepatic function of patients with large splenorenal shunts are poorer than those of patients without shunts [16, 17]. Given that B-RTO occludes such shunts and redirects portal flow back to the liver, it can theoretically ameliorate these metabolic and functional impairments. Importantly, B-RTO not only achieves hemostasis but also has been reported to improve hepatic functional reserve and increase liver volume after shunt occlusion [17-19].

Although this physiological benefit has been demonstrated with the conventional single-balloon method, evidence regarding whether a double-balloon system (CANDIS) provides additional clinical or hepatic advantages remains scarce. The present study retrospectively compared the treatment outcomes and long-term effects of conventional and double-balloon (CANDIS) B-RTO on hepatic functional reserve in patients with GVs.

Material and Methods

Patients

This single-center, retrospective cohort study included patients with GVs who underwent B-RTO between February 2008 and March 2020. A total of 65 patients were treated during this period, and 48 patients who were available for follow-up were included in the final analysis. Among them, 18 patients underwent B-RTO using a single balloon, consistent with the conventional method (single-balloon group), whereas the remaining 30 patients were treated with the double-balloon system (CANDIS; Medikit, Tokyo, Japan) (double-balloon group) (Figure 2).

To ensure procedural transparency, it should be noted that single-balloon B-RTO was performed between 2008 and 2016, whereas CANDIS-assisted double-balloon B-RTO was performed between 2012 and 2020. During this period, the primary operator changed from Operator A (Y.Y., with approximately 17 years of interventional radiology experience at the time) to Operator B (M.T., with approximately 25 years of experience). All B-RTO procedures were performed by two attending interventional radiologists at our institution. Although both operators possessed equivalent technical expertise, it is plausible that operator skill influenced procedural success rates. No changes in angiographic equipment or sclerosant formulation occurred during the study period. Device selection was not based on anatomical or clinical characteristics but was determined by institutional protocol changes. After the introduction of CANDIS in 2012, the double-balloon method gradually replaced the conventional technique, and both methods were used concurrently during the transition period (2012-2016), depending on device availability. During the study period, the primary operator transitioned from Operator A (2008-2012) to Operator B (2013-2020). Operator A performed the conventional method (using a single-balloon system) in all cases. Operator B primarily used the double-balloon system (CANDIS) but also performed the conventional method in some cases. This temporal transition represents an inherent “era effect,” which we acknowledge as a potential source of selection bias. Therefore, we acknowledge that differences in operator skill level, not just device performance, may have introduced bias into the technical outcomes, although operator-related variability cannot be completely excluded.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0 or 1; (2) liver cirrhosis with GVs of Form 2-- and/or the presence of a red color sign with a GR shunt; and/or (3) emergent or previously ruptured GVs. All included patients were followed for more than one month after B-RTO.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: severe hepatic dysfunction (total bilirubin ≥4.0 mg/dL and/or Child-Pugh score ≥13), severe renal impairment (estimated glomerular filtration rate <30 mL/min/1.73 m2), the presence of other serious comorbidities with poor prognosis, absence of a portosystemic shunt amenable to a retrograde approach, or a history of allergic reaction to ethanol.

Evaluation

We examined indicators of hepatic functional reserve, including serum total bilirubin, serum albumin, prothrombin time, Child-Pugh score, and albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) score [20, 21], at 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 12 months after B-RTO. Treatment response was evaluated by CT, MRI, or esophagogastroduodenoscopy performed at least one month after the procedure.

The primary endpoints were improvement in hepatic functional reserve six months after B-RTO, clinical success rate, technical success rate, and incidence of complications. Technical success was defined as complete embolization of the targeted GVs confirmed by fluoroscopy or contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) immediately after the procedure.

Clinical success was assessed using CECT when available, or non-contrast CT in patients who underwent only plain imaging. On CECT, “disappearance” was defined as the complete loss of opacification of the collateral outflow tract. In both CECT and non-contrast CT, “reduction” was defined as a ≥30% decrease in the maximal diameter of the collateral vein, corresponding to an approximate 50% reduction in vascular volume. Findings below this threshold were classified as “residual.” Endoscopic evaluation was performed according to the Hashizume-Kitano classification [22]. A ≥1-grade reduction in variceal form (e.g., F3→F2/F1) and/or complete disappearance of red color signs was defined as endoscopic improvement. Endoscopic findings were used to complement CT-based assessment when contrast images were not available.

Additional evaluation parameters included total fluoroscopy time, total dose of ethanolamine oleate mixed with iopamidol (EOI), length of hospital stay, and procedure-related complications such as pulmonary edema, hemothorax, hematuria, disseminated intravascular coagulation, coil migration, portal vein thrombosis, minor vascular injuries, rupture of GVs or esophageal varices, cardiogenic shock associated with EOI administration, and allergic reactions to ethanol.

B-RTO procedure

All patients were preoperatively evaluated for the configuration, size, and hemodynamics of GVs and draining veins using CECT and/or transarterial angiography. B-RTO was performed according to an established technique described previously [8-10, 14], which can be summarized as follows: (1) insertion of a balloon catheter into the GR shunt via the left renal vein, (2) occlusion of the shunt to interrupt hepatofugal flow, and (3) retrograde injection of sclerosant into the variceal complex under balloon occlusion.

In the conventional (single-balloon) group, an 8-Fr long shepherd hook-shaped sheath (ASATO; Medikit, Tokyo, Japan) was inserted through the right femoral vein. A 6-Fr catheter with a 20-mm diameter balloon or a 5.2-Fr catheter with a 9-mm diameter balloon (Selecon MP Catheter; Terumo Clinical Supply Co., Gifu, Japan) was then advanced retrogradely through the sheath into the GR shunt. In contrast, in the CANDIS (double-balloon) group, balloon catheters were introduced via the right femoral vein using a 9.5-Fr sheath, as illustrated in Figure 3. The 9-Fr guide catheter was shaped into a customized hook or L-configuration to facilitate selective insertion into the GR shunt. The 9-Fr balloon was positioned within the main trunk of the GR shunt to achieve stable anchoring and complete interruption of hepatofugal flow. The 5-Fr balloon was then used to fine-tune the occlusion level closer to the variceal inflow, allowing two-point fixation within the shunt. This proximal-distal balloon configuration ensured stable dual occlusion, reduced catheter instability, and facilitated uniform retention of the sclerosant within the variceal complex.

After catheter placement, balloon-occluded retrograde transvenous venography (B-RTV) was performed to delineate the shunt anatomy and collateral outflow. In the double-balloon group, the proximal 9-Fr guiding balloon was positioned within the GR shunt to achieve stable anchoring, whereas the distal 5-Fr coaxial balloon was selectively advanced to the distal shunt segment to optimize retrograde pressure control.

Superselective catheterization using a microcatheter (Sirabe; Piolax Medical Devices) was performed for collateral veins identified on B-RTV, particularly the inferior phrenic or pericardiac veins. Collateral embolization with microcoils was indicated when persistent outflow was observed that prevented adequate opacification of the GVs.

When selective embolization was not technically feasible or collateral flow remained complex, the injection strategy was shifted from selective to distal injection using a stepwise downgrading technique [16, 17] under temporary balloon occlusion, aiming to simplify shunt anatomy to Hirota classification Grade 1 or 2 [8]. In the double-balloon (CANDIS) group, the 5-Fr balloon catheter was advanced to the proximal segment of the GR shunt, near the inflow to the GVs. This position was selected to enable selective balloon-occluded retrograde venography of the variceal complex and to optimize sclerosant delivery after collateral flow had been controlled. For sclerotherapy, 10% ethanolamine oleate (Oldamin; Takeda Pharmaceutical, Osaka, Japan) mixed in equal volume with the nonionic contrast medium iopamidol (Iopamiron 300; Bayer Healthcare, Osaka, Japan) was used, resulting in a 5% EOI solution.

This sclerosant was injected under balloon occlusion after retrograde opacification of GVs under fluoroscopy. The microcatheter was used almost exclusively for selective venography and embolization of collateral veins. Sclerosant injection was not performed through the microcatheter. In most cases, EOI was injected exclusively through the balloon catheter after sufficient retrograde opacification of the GVs had been confirmed under balloon occlusion.

To prevent hemolysis-related renal dysfunction, 2,000-4,000 units of haptoglobin were administered intravenously before the procedure. The balloon catheter was kept inflated within the draining vein overnight or for at least six hours to ensure complete thrombosis and to prevent pulmonary infarction caused by unstable thrombus migration. Patients were then returned to the interventional suite the following day. Manual angiography was performed to confirm complete occlusion of the variceal vein before withdrawing the catheter and sheath under fluoroscopic guidance. After catheter removal, manual compression was applied to the right femoral puncture site, and vital signs were continuously monitored until the procedure was completed.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or as median with range, as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between groups were performed using Fisher's exact test for categorical data and the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous data. Changes in hepatic functional reserve parameters before and after B-RTO were analyzed using paired t-tests or one-way repeated-measures ANOVA, depending on data distribution. To confirm statistical robustness, parallel analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) in addition to SPSS (version 28; IBM, Tokyo, Japan).

All tests were two-tailed, and a p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Given the retrospective design and limited sample size, results were interpreted with caution, and trends near the threshold of significance (0.05 ≤ p < 0.10) were reported descriptively.

Ethics

All patients provided informed consent for treatment after their physicians explained the advantages and risks of the procedure. The study protocol was conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013) and was approved by the Ethics Committee of our institution (approval No. 25-118).

The clinical success rate of B-RTO was significantly higher in the double-balloon group than in the single-balloon group (90.0% vs. 50.0%, p = 0.004) (Table 2). The technical success rate was also higher in the double-balloon group than in the single-balloon group (100.0% vs. 88.9%, p = 0.136), although this difference did not reach statistical significance.

Among patients with Hirota Grade 2-4, the technical success rate of the conventional single-balloon method was only 66.7%. In contrast, in the same Grade 2-4 cohort, the CANDIS achieved a technical success rate of 100%. Downgrading to Hirota Grade 1 was achieved in 22 of 30 patients, all of whom demonstrated complete obliteration of GVs. Of the remaining eight patients downgraded to Hirota Grade 2, six showed complete disappearance of GVs on CT/MRI, whereas two exhibited no radiological reduction.

There were no significant differences between the groups in total fluoroscopy time, total dose of EOI, or length of hospital stay. Details of the treatment outcomes are summarized in Table 2. Discussion

Our study demonstrated that double-balloon B-RTO using the CANDIS markedly improved the success rate, even in refractory or anatomically complex cases of GVs. Importantly, this improvement was achieved without increasing complications, fluoroscopy time, or the total amount of sclerosant, indicating that the CANDIS-assisted procedure is both safe and patient-friendly.

Although the technical success rate of the conventional single-balloon method in our cohort appeared lower than that reported in earlier series, several important contextual factors should be considered. In our institution, although the clinical success rate of conventional single-balloon B-RTO was limited to 50.0%, the technical success rate nevertheless reached 88.9%, indicating that the procedure itself was technically feasible in a substantial proportion of cases. This discrepancy suggests that, in anatomically complex situations, technical embolization alone does not always translate into sustained clinical success when adequate flow control or sclerosant stasis cannot be achieved. Previous reports describing high technical success rates of conventional B-RTO (generally 90%-100%) primarily defined success as procedural completion or immediate variceal thrombosis and rarely distinguished technical success from sustained clinical success assessed by cross-sectional imaging or endoscopy [23]. In many published cohorts, the proportion of cases requiring embolization of outflow routes was less than 50% [5, 6, 24], suggesting that earlier reports may have contained a higher proportion of anatomically simple GVs. In contrast, as a tertiary referral center, our institution treated a substantially higher proportion of anatomically complex cases, including more than half classified as Hirota Grade 3-5 and several cases consistent with the gastric-inferior vena cava (IVC) shunt configuration (“Type 2” in the classification by Kanagawa et al. [5]). Notably, when analysis was limited to patients with Hirota Grade 2-4, the technical success rate of the conventional single-balloon method decreased to only 66.7%, underscoring the technical limitations of this approach in anatomically complex GVs. These anatomies are well known to be challenging for conventional single-balloon B-RTO due to incomplete balloon occlusion and poor sclerosant stasis. Moreover, a change in operators occurred during the study period, introducing an unavoidable era effect, although both operators had comparable experience. Together, these factors likely contributed to the lower technical success rate observed in the single-balloon group and provide the necessary context for interpreting our results. However, when analysis was restricted to patients with Hirota Grade 2-4, the technical success rate of the single-balloon method decreased to 66.7%, highlighting the limitations of single-point occlusion in anatomically complex cases.

In Hirota Grade 1 GVs, sclerosant can be injected easily because collateral vessels are minimally developed. In contrast, Hirota Grade 2-4 cases often involve multiple collateral channels that require targeted management to ensure effective sclerotherapy. Recently, various adjunctive strategies (such as the downgrading technique, stepwise injection, superselective embolization, and dual B-RTO) have been developed to address these complex drainage patterns [25-27]. The downgrading technique involves advancing the balloon catheter beyond a collateral branch; stepwise injection eliminates collateral flow by gradually introducing small amounts of sclerosant; superselective embolization targets thick collateral veins with microcoils; and dual B-RTO uses an additional balloon catheter inserted from the IVC to occlude the drainage vein. Alternative approaches, including plug-assisted retrograde transvenous obliteration (PARTO) [28, 29] and coil-assisted retrograde transvenous obliteration [30] techniques, have also been described.

Our findings suggest that the coaxial dual-balloon design of the CANDIS achieves the same goal of complete and stable occlusion more efficiently, providing consistent results across different venous anatomies. Notably, few studies have directly compared hepatic functional improvement between the double-balloon and conventional single-balloon methods, underscoring the novelty of our work. The hemodynamics of GVs associated with GR shunts are unfavorable because the diversion of portal blood flow reduces intrahepatic perfusion [31]. Consequently, intestinal toxins such as ammonia bypass hepatic detoxification and contribute to hepatic encephalopathy. After shunt occlusion with B-RTO, the redirection of splanchnic blood flow into the liver increases portal perfusion and may enhance hepatic amino-acid metabolism, leading to improved hepatic reserve. Consistent with previous reports, our study confirmed significant post-procedural improvement in hepatic function indicators, including ALBI and Child-Pugh scores [10, 18, 32].

It is worth emphasizing that therapeutic strategies for GVs differ substantially between Asia and Western countries [33, 34]. A major issue lies in determining the most appropriate intervention―TIPS, which decompresses the portal circulation; B-RTO, which achieves variceal obliteration; or a combination of both―according to each patient's clinical condition [10]. Comparative studies have shown that B-RTO provides superior hemostasis and a lower rebleeding rate than TIPS in cases of ruptured GVs [35]. Furthermore, B-RTO demonstrates similar long-term outcomes and complication rates to TIPS but with a significantly lower incidence of hepatic encephalopathy, supporting its role as a preferred therapy in East Asia [36, 37]. Although combined TIPS plus B-RTO may be technically advantageous in selected cases, excessive antegrade decompression can paradoxically promote new collateral formation [38]. Thus, B-RTO is considered one of the most effective first-line treatment options for portal-systemic shunt encephalopathy and gastric variceal bleeding. These findings suggest that improved hemodynamic control, rather than procedural completion alone, may be a key determinant of post-B-RTO hepatic functional recovery.

As a limitation, this was a single-center retrospective study with a relatively small sample size, which may introduce selection bias. In addition, because the conventional single-balloon and double-balloon procedures were performed during partially different time periods, an unavoidable era effect and operator transition existed. Although both operators had comparable experience, operator-related variability cannot be completely excluded and represents a limitation inherent to the retrospective study design. Another limitation is that anatomical complexity was primarily assessed using the Hirota classification. Quantitative anatomical metrics―such as shunt diameter, tortuosity, and the number of collateral veins―were not uniformly available across all patients, limiting our ability to fully adjust for anatomic difficulty between groups. Furthermore, imaging-based evaluation of treatment response was not completely homogeneous: CECT was used in most cases, whereas non-contrast CT was used in a subset of patients. This variability may have introduced some heterogeneity in assessing variceal reduction.

Future multicenter prospective trials with randomized comparison between the two methods are warranted to validate these findings. Moreover, considering the widespread use of TIPS worldwide, comparative analyses evaluating hepatic functional recovery between double-balloon B-RTO and TIPS would further clarify their relative therapeutic value.

Conclusion

Double-balloon B-RTO using the CANDIS was associated with greater improvement in hepatic functional reserve, particularly the Child-Pugh score, from five to 12 months after treatment compared with the conventional single-balloon method. Given its high technical and clinical success rates, even in anatomically complex cases, and its comparable safety profile, the double-balloon B-RTO may represent a safe, effective, and promising therapeutic option for patients with GVs.
