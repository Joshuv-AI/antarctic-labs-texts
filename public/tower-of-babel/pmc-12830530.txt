Research Progress of Lipid Metabolism-Mediated Neuroinflammation in Alzheimer’s Disease
Authors: Yuzhen Shen, Xiaoran Wang, Xinyu Liu, Guoying Wang, Xueqin Hou, Xiaoming Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disease closely associated with age. The main clinical manifestations include cognitive impairment, including visuospatial ability, memory, language, and behavioral disorders. These manifestations considerably impair the patients’ ability to perform daily activities. Although the pathogenesis of AD remains unclear, many studies have confirmed the essential role of abnormal lipid metabolism and inflammatory response in AD occurrence and progression. In this review, based on the relationship between lipid metabolism disorders and neuroinflammation, the regulatory mechanism of lipid mediators, and the role of microglia, we systematically discuss how lipid metabolism affects the pathological process of AD by regulating the inflammatory response.Keywords: Lipid metabolism, Neuroinflammation, Microglia, Alzheimer's disease

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a neurological disease characterized by progressive neuronal degeneration. It is the primary subtype of dementia and one of the most serious public health concerns in the 21 st century(Scheltens et al. 2021). Currently, epidemiological data suggest that the number of patients with AD worldwide has exceeded 55 million, with a doubling every 5 years(Janoutová et al. 2021). Notably, the prevalence of AD is significantly and positively correlated with age, with the incidence increasing as the population ages. As the number of patients with AD is increasing, current research must focus on elucidating the underlying pathological mechanisms and developing effective treatment strategies. The pathogenesis of AD is complex and involves various pathological processes, including senile plaques formed by β-amyloid (Aβ) deposition, pathological phosphorylation of Tau protein, central nervous system inflammation, cholinergic system dysfunction, and oxidative stress injury(Mondragón-Rodríguez et al. 2013; Liu et al. 2019; Breijyeh and Karaman 2020; Yadollahikhales and Rojas 2023). The hallmarks of AD pathology are plaques comprising aggregated Aβ and twisted lines of tangled tau protein(Feng et al. 2024). Studies have revealed that various pathogeneses of AD interact and affect each other, jointly promoting AD occurrence and development. As an important form of energy storage and metabolic substrate, lipids play an important role in regulating cell signal transduction. Lipids are abundant in the central nervous system, accounting for up to 50% of the dry weight of the brain tissue, second only to adipose tissue, ranking second in all human tissues. Brain lipids primarily comprise phospholipids (approximately 50%), glycolipids (< 40%), cholesterol and its esters (approximately 10%), and trace triglycerides(Hornemann 2021).

Increasing evidence suggests that the imbalance of lipid metabolism homeostasis is closely associated with the pathogenesis of various neurodegenerative diseases such as AD(Yoon et al. 2022; Nie et al. 2024). Specifically, lipid species such as free fatty acids, their bioactive derivatives lipid mediators, sphingolipids, and apolipoprotein E (ApoE) participate in AD neuroinflammation through multiple signaling pathways that regulate Aβ dynamics and control microglial phenotype switching(Tong et al. 2024; Pawar et al. 2025). Research indicates that sphingolipids promote the metabolism of amyloid precursor protein (APP) and the generation of Aβ by modulating multiple pathways such as apoptosis, calcium homeostasis, Tau phosphorylation, acetylcholine biosynthesis, and the amyloidogenic pathway(Varma et al. 2018). ApoE mediates the internalization of Aβ by binding to receptors such as the LDL receptor-related protein (LRP), thereby influencing its clearance and promoting aggregation (Kao et al. 2020). Studies comparing the metabolomes of 3xTg-AD and wild-type mice demonstrate that the gut microbiota aggravates AD pathology and cognitive impairment through polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) -associated neuroinflammation (Chen et al. 2022). Furthermore, a large-scale meta-analysis reveals that a higher intake of n-3 PUFAs is associated with a 14% reduced in the risk of mild cognitive impairment (MCI)(Zhu et al. 2021). A comprehensive analysis of the dynamic changes of lipid metabolism and its regulatory network will provide new ideas for elucidating the pathological mechanism of neurodegenerative diseases. In this article, we systematically reviewed the current research progress of lipid metabolism affecting the pathological process of AD by regulating neuroinflammation. (Fig. 1)

Dysregulated lipid metabolism exacerbates neuroinflammation and subsequently induces multiple pathogenic mechanisms, collectively promoting the initiation and progression of AD

Lipid Metabolism and Neuroinflammation in AD

The pathological process of AD is closely associated with neuroinflammation, with lipid metabolism disorder playing a key role in this process. Abnormal lipid metabolism can exacerbate neuroinflammation via different mechanisms, including changes in lipid raft composition in the brain promoting Aβ aggregation and enhancing its neurotoxicity, disruption of lipid droplet homeostasis leading to energy metabolism disorders, and aging-related changes in lipid profile directly activating microglia(Hamilton et al. 2015; Grassi et al. 2020; Díaz et al. 2023). The crucial and potential initiating role of lipid metabolism in AD is further supported by the strong relationship between key AD risk factors and various aspects of lipid metabolism (Fig. 2). Simultaneously, neuroinflammation further disrupts lipid metabolism, including proinflammatory factors interfering with the activity of lipid synthetase and abnormal lipid metabolites activating inflammasomes. This bidirectional “lipid–inflammation” regulation collectively aggravates AD symptoms(Cerasuolo et al. 2024). The inflammatory response of the central nervous system involves the coordinated participation of various glial cells, primarily including microglia, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and myeloid cells. Microglia, as the primary immune effector cells in the central nervous system, play a central role in AD-related neuroinflammation(Nolte et al. 1996). Microglia have dual functions, potentially exhibiting beneficial or harmful effects. In the healthy state, microglia play an important neuroprotective role, contributing to the health and stability of the brain environment via efficient phagocytosis and the release of neurotrophic factors. However, when the central nervous system is stimulated by disease, inflammation, or the abnormal accumulation of lipid metabolites (such as oxidized lipids and cholesterol), microglia are activated, and proinflammatory factors (such as IL-1α, IL-1β, and TNF-β) and active mediators (such as H2O2 and NO) are released in large amounts. These inflammatory mediators work together and trigger a strong proinflammatory response, aggravating brain tissue damage(Flynn et al. 2003). Furthermore, activated microglia produce various proteolytic enzymes, including cathepsin B, which can destroy the extracellular matrix and cause neuronal dysfunction.

Lipid metabolism dysregulation and neuroinflammation form a vicious cycle, jointly promoting the pathogenesis of AD

Compared with other AD risk factors, neuroinflammation does not directly cause AD. However, as an important driver of the disease process, it promotes AD occurrence and development by aggravating lipid metabolism disorders and abnormal microglial activation. Considering its central role in various neurological diseases, the regulation of neuroinflammation has become a hot research area in AD treatment, providing an important direction for developing new therapeutic strategies.

Molecular Pathways of Abnormal Lipid Metabolism-Induced Neuroinflammation

Contribution of Obesity-Related Metabolic Dysregulation to Neuroinflammation

A long-term high-fat diet can induce systemic lipid metabolism disorders, thereby promoting obesity. Recent studies have revealed that obesity is a chronic inflammatory state characterized by the abnormal secretion of proinflammatory adipokines such as IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and various chemokines by the adipose tissue; these factors have biological characteristics similar to immune regulatory molecules(Xu et al. 2003). This peripheral inflammatory environment can affect the central nervous system via different mechanisms and is significantly associated with AD pathogenesis. A high-fat diet is a significant factor contributing to the loss of blood-brain barrier integrity. Research has shown that it disrupts the blood-brain barrier by reducing the expression of tight junction proteins, allowing peripheral pro-inflammatory cytokines and circulating fatty acids to cross the barrier. This process further exacerbates CNS inflammation(Klockars et al. 2019). Moreover, obesity-related inflammatory mediators can activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, triggering an excessive response that leads to hypercortisolemia and ultimately results in neuronal apoptosis and other forms of CNS damage(Ruiz et al. 2018). Peripheral metabolic stress signals can also directly activate immune cells within the CNS. For instance, saturated fatty acids activate microglia and astrocytes via a Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-dependent, nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB)-mediated inflammatory response(Ly et al. 2023), thereby amplifying inflammation within the CNS.

At the molecular level, central neuroinflammation is primarily characterized by the abnormal activation of microglia and astrocytes. When activated, these glial cells not only produce neurotoxic substances such as reactive oxygen species and reactive nitrogen species but also upregulate the expression of proinflammatory cytokines and cause phagocytosis dysfunction; this eventually results in irreversible neuronal damage. Notably, insulin resistance, a core feature of type 2 diabetes, also participates in this pathological process. When peripheral insulin resistance occurs, elevated insulin levels can penetrate the BBB, stimulating the overexpression of inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β and IL-6 in the central nervous system(Chen et al. 1993 ; Zhu et al. 2018). More importantly, a positive feedback regulation exists between neuroinflammation and Aβ deposition, which significantly accelerates neurodegeneration.

Regulation of Neuroinflammation by the Microbiota–Gut–Brain Axis

Recent studies have revealed that the gut microbiota and the brain form a complex two-way communication system via the microbiota–gut–brain axis. This system plays a key regulatory role in the occurrence and development of neurodegenerative diseases such as AD by interacting with neural, endocrine, and immune pathways. In particular, an imbalance of the intestinal flora can affect the AD process by promoting peripheral and central inflammatory responses; this alters the permeability of the BBB and regulates neurotransmitter metabolism(Ma et al. 2019). Studies have revealed that changes in the structure of specific intestinal flora are significantly associated with the degree of Aβ deposition in the brain, and the presence of healthy intestinal flora can decrease Aβ deposition, thereby delaying AD development(Zha et al. 2025). This association may be achieved via the regulatory effects of microbiota metabolites on neuroinflammation(Mou et al. 2022). More importantly, the continuous interplay between gut microbiota and the innate immune system can lead to a chronic inflammatory state; this systemic inflammatory microenvironment may further exacerbate the characteristic pathological changes of AD, forming a vicious cycle.

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are key gut microbiota-derived metabolites that mediate the regulation of neuroinflammatory processes. SCFAs, including butyrate, propionate, and acetate, are primarily produced by gut microbiota such as Bacteroidetes through the fermentation of dietary fibers(Huang et al. 2024). These compounds can cross the blood-brain barrier via monocarboxylate transporters on endothelial cells and help maintain blood-brain barrier integrity by inhibiting nonspecific inflammatory pathways associated with microbial infections(Marizzoni et al. 2020). Regarding their mechanisms of action, SCFAs function as endogenous ligands for G-protein-coupled receptors, activating receptors including GPR41 and GPR43, while also modulating gene expression and cellular functions through inhibition of histone deacetylase activity(Dalile et al. 2019). In experimental models of AD, acetate exerts anti-inflammatory effects by upregulating GPR41 and suppressing the ERK/JNK/NF-κB pathway(Liu et al. 2020), whereas sodium butyrate enhances PI3K/AKT/CREB/BDNF pathway activity to improve synaptic plasticity and memory function(Saw et al. 2020). In addition, SCFAs directly regulate immune functions in microglia by reducing antigen-presenting capacity and decreasing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-12 and TNF-α, thereby suppressing neuroinflammation(Corrêa-Oliveira et al. 2016).

In transgenic animal models of AD, germ-free mice exhibited decreased Aβ, which was reversed by microbiota reconstitution; this confirms that gut microbiota directly affects Aβ metabolism(Harach et al. 2017). Clinical analysis has revealed that patients with AD exhibit characteristic changes in the composition of intestinal flora, with increased proinflammatory bacteria and decreased anti-inflammatory bacteria, accompanied by decreased levels of SCFAs(Zhang et al. 2017). This flora dysbiosis can impair intestinal barrier function, downregulate P-glycoprotein expression, and then trigger systemic inflammatory response and damage the integrity of the BBB(Haran et al. 2019). Furthermore, intestinal microbial metabolites such as those derived from tryptophan can also regulate neuroendocrine functions via the brain-gut axis (Wu et al. 2022). Notably, infection with specific pathogens such as herpes simplex virus type 1 can exacerbate AD pathology by upregulating enzymes associated with cholesterol metabolism(Papassotiropoulos et al. 2005; Wozniak et al. 2009). In contrast, antibiotic intervention may improve Aβ deposition(Minter et al. 2016). These findings validate the close relationship between gut microbiota and neuroinflammation via the brain–gut axis. Additional research on this complex interaction mechanism will uncover new research directions for the early diagnosis, prevention, and treatment of AD. Therapeutic strategies based on regulating intestinal flora, including probiotic intervention, diet regulation, and targeted antibiotic application, have exhibited broad clinical application prospects, providing new ideas and a theoretical foundation for preventing and treating AD.

Imbalance of Fatty Acid Metabolism and Neuroinflammatory Mechanisms in AD

Fatty acids, as key structural components of the neuronal membrane, are primarily present in the central nervous system in the form of phospholipids and cholesteryl esters. The liver primarily synthesizes these lipid molecules and then transports them to the brain via the blood circulation. While the specific mechanism by which they cross the BBB remains unclear, they may be completed via passive diffusion or mediated by specific transporters(Mitchell et al. 2011; Mitchell and Hatch 2011; Cutuli 2017). From the perspective of molecular structural characteristics, fatty acids can be divided into saturated fatty acids, monounsaturated fatty acids, and PUFAs based on the number of double bonds(Miles et al. 2021). In the nervous system, PUFA accounts for approximately 50% of the neuronal membrane and 70% of the myelin sheath and maintains neural functions via various mechanisms, including regulating cell membrane fluidity and integrity, participating in signal transduction and gene expression regulation, and affecting synaptic plasticity(Mesa-Herrera et al. 2019). In particular, the balance of n-3 and n-6 series PUFAs is essential for neuroinflammatory regulation. n-6 PUFAs (such as arachidonic acid(AA) are the precursors of proinflammatory mediators, whereas n-3 PUFAs (such as ocosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) primarily produce anti-inflammatory mediators(Schönfeld and Reiser 2013). The disruption of this balance plays a key role in AD pathogenesis.

Studies have revealed a significant decrease in DHA levels in the brains of patients with AD, accompanied by n-3/n-6 ratio imbalance; this change is closely associated with cognitive impairment(Astarita et al. 2010; Astarita and Piomelli 2011; Thomas et al. 2015). In vitro experiments indicate that various PUFAs, such as linoleic acid, AA, α-linolenic acid, DHA, EPA, and oleic acid, can directly interact with both Aβ40 and Aβ42, effectively inhibiting their fibrillation process, among which oleic acid and DHA demonstrate the most potent anti-aggregation activity(El Shatshat et al. 2019). Furthermore, fatty acids directly participate in regulating the immunophenotype and inflammatory response of microglia: n-3 PUFAs, including DHA and EPA, promote microglial polarization toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype and downregulate pro-inflammatory gene expression, thereby attenuating demyelination and improving neurological function in models of neurodegeneration such as multiple sclerosis(Chen et al. 2014).

However, the relationship between fatty acids and AD remains controversial. Some studies have reported no significant differences in DHA content in erythrocytes or specific brain regions between AD patients and healthy controls(Kröger et al. 2009; Fraser et al. 2010). These discrepancies may result from factors such as the type of biological sample (e.g., whole brain, specific brain regions, erythrocyte membranes), the stage of AD, genetic background (e.g., ApoE genotype), and the presence of comorbid metabolic disorders. Relevant research has observed a relative increase in DHA levels in AD-vulnerable regions such as the middle frontal gyrus and inferior temporal gyrus(Snowden et al. 2017), which may represent a compensatory response during early disease stages. Therefore, the association between DHA and AD must be evaluated within specific disease stages and biological contexts, as its changes likely reflect a dynamic process rather than a simple static deficiency.

The roles of oleic acid and AA in AD pathology are also complex. Experimental studies have shown that exogenous oleic acid supplementation suppresses Aβ production in cellular and animal models, potentially by downregulating prolyl endopeptidase activity; however, other evidence indicates that oleic acid can enhance γ-secretase activity, thereby promoting Aβ generation(Park et al. 2006; Amtul et al. 2011). Regarding AA, although its biological role as a precursor to pro-inflammatory eicosanoids suggests that high dietary intake may increase AD risk, epidemiological findings remain inconsistent, with some studies reporting no significant correlation(Naghshi et al. 2021; Yin 2023). Thus, the neuromodulatory functions of individual fatty acids are not unidirectional or constant but are co-regulated by multiple factors, including their metabolic conversion pathways, the local tissue microenvironment, and individual genetic background and overall dietary pattern. Future studies are warranted to further clarify the exact mechanisms of different fatty acids in AD and to explore precise treatment strategies based on the regulation of fatty acid metabolism. In general, intervening in the neuroinflammatory process by modulating fatty acid metabolism provides new ideas and directions for preventing and treating AD.

The Central Role of Lipid Mediators in Regulating Neuroinflammation in AD

Lipid mediators, a class of key signaling molecules derived from PUFA metabolism, play critical roles in regulating the initiation, amplification, and resolution of neuroinflammation in AD. AA, the most significant n-6 PUFA, is released from membrane phospholipids by cytosolic phospholipase A2 (cPLA₂) and subsequently metabolized primarily via the cyclooxygenase (COX) and lipoxygenase (LOX) pathways into pro-inflammatory eicosanoid mediators. In the cerebral cortex and hippocampus of AD patients, COX-2 expression is significantly upregulated(Thomas et al. 2016). Prostaglandin E2 (PGE₂), a key pro-inflammatory mediator produced via the COX pathway, has been shown to mediate complex inflammatory regulatory networks through different receptors: deletion of the PGE₂ EP2 receptor suppresses microglial toxic responses and promotes Aβ clearance(Johansson et al. 2015), whereas inhibition of its EP4 receptor exacerbates neuroinflammation(Woodling et al. 2014). Furthermore, the lipoxygenase pathway is similarly activated. 5-LOX and its metabolites exacerbate Tau protein phosphorylation and Aβ aberrant aggregation in AD models. Additionally, 12/15-LOX and its products, 12(S)-HETE and 15(S)-HETE, are elevated in the brain tissue and cerebrospinal fluid of AD patients, and genetic deletion of 12/15-LOX in AD model mice improves cognition and reduces both Aβ deposition and Tau phosphorylation(Giannopoulos et al. 2013). Collectively, this evidence indicates that AA-derived pro-inflammatory lipid mediators play a pivotal role in AD-associated neuroinflammation.

Regarding anti-inflammatory mechanisms, n-3 PUFAs, such as DHA and EPA, exert neuroprotective effects through multiple pathways. As precursors of anti-inflammatory mediators, n-3 PUFAs can be converted into active molecules including specialized pro-resolving mediators (SPMs) and neuroprotectins(Serhan and Levy 2018; Chiang and Serhan 2020). Among these, Neuroprotectin D1 (NPD1) and the Resolvin D series (RvDs), both derived from DHA, are particularly important. In cellular models, NPD1 demonstrates the ability to downregulate β-secretase (BACE1) and activate α-secretase, thereby directly inhibiting Aβ production. More importantly, DHA and its derivatives, NPD1 and RvDs, significantly suppress the pro-inflammatory phenotype of microglia and enhance their phagocytic capacity for Aβ(Baker et al. 2016). Their mechanisms of action involve inhibiting the activation of key pro-inflammatory transcription factors such as nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) and activator protein 1 (AP-1), consequently reducing the expression of pro-inflammatory factors including inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α)(Zgórzyńska et al. 2021).

In summary, during AD progression, the overactivation of AA metabolic pathways coupled with a relative deficiency of n-3 PUFA-derived pro-resolving mediators collectively contributes to a chronic neuroinflammatory environment. The balance between these two classes of lipid mediators directly regulates the activation status and function of microglia and astrocytes, representing a central hub connecting disordered lipid metabolism with AD neuroinflammatory pathology. Therefore, targeting lipid mediator metabolic pathways to restore their balance has emerged as a highly promising direction for AD intervention strategies.

Regulation of Neuroinflammation by Apolipoprotein E (ApoE) Gene Polymorphisms

ApoE is a glycoprotein with a molecular weight of approximately 34 kDa. It is the primary cholesterol carrier in the central nervous system and plays a key role in lipid metabolism and neurodegeneration. The human ApoE gene has three common allelic variants ε2, ε3, and ε4, which encode three isoforms (E2, E3, and E4, respectively)(Lanfranco et al. 2020). Many studies have confirmed that ApoE4 is the strongest genetic risk factor for AD. Carrying an ε4 allele increases the risk of AD 3 to 4 fold, whereas homozygous carriers increase the risk of AD 9 to 15 fold(Farrer et al. 1997; Genin et al. 2011; Neu et al. 2017). ApoE4 can significantly promote Aβ deposition, which is reflected by increasing neuroinflammatory plaque burden(Schmechel et al. 1993; Polvikoski et al. 1995; Kok et al. 2009); this promotes the formation of neurotoxic Aβ oligomers and aggravates the severity of cerebral amyloid angiopathy(Koffie et al. 2012; Rannikmäe et al. 2014; Wang et al. 2022).In contrast, ApoE2 exhibits neuroprotective effects(Mahley 2017; Yamazaki et al. 2019).

In the central nervous system, ApoE is primarily synthesized by astrocytes. Microglia, oligodendrocytes, and damaged neurons can also produce ApoE to some degree(Huang et al. 2004; Xu et al. 2006; Polazzi et al. 2015). ApoE participates in the formation of high-density lipoprotein (HDL) like particles via the cholesterol transport pathway mediated by the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter family members ABCA1 and ABCG1(Horiuchi et al. 2019). This process is important for maintaining lipid homeostasis in the brain, and ABCA1 overexpression can decrease amyloid deposition and improve Aβ clearance(Wahrle et al. 2004, 2008). In contrast, ABCA1 deletion decreases ApoE levels and increases Aβ deposition(Hirsch-Reinshagen et al. 2004; Koldamova et al. 2005; Wahrle et al. 2005). Clinical studies have revealed that ABCA1-mediated cholesterol transport capacity is decreased by approximately 30% in patients with AD and mild cognitive impairment compared with healthy people(Yassine et al. 2016). Accumulating evidence suggests that this functional decline primarily stems from a primary disruption in lipid metabolism triggered by the ApoE4 genotype. In ApoE4 mouse models, brain levels of ABCA1 protein are significantly lower than in ApoE3 mice, even in the absence of overt neuroinflammation. More importantly, studies using the ABCA1-specific agonist CS-6253 to enhance its activity have successfully reversed ApoE4-related lipidation deficits, subsequently ameliorating Aβ deposition, Tau pathology, and cognitive impairment(Boehm-Cagan et al. 2016). Thus, ABCA1 dysfunction acts as an upstream driver rather than merely a downstream consequence in the ApoE4 pathogenic cascade.

In addition to regulating lipid metabolism, ApoE isoforms also affect the AD pathological process via different mechanisms(Table 1). In terms of neuroinflammation, recent studies have revealed that ApoE4 can specifically activate the calcium-dependent cytoplasmic phospholipase A2 (cPLA2) signaling system, which plays a role in inflammatory signal transduction and is increased in AD brain plaques; the activation effect of ApoE4 on cPLA2 is higher than that of ApoE3(Parhizkar and Holtzman 2022). The specific activation of the cPLA2 signaling system by ApoE4 is likely strongly linked to its distinct lipidation status. Studies indicate that the ApoE4 protein itself tends to form poorly lipidated lipoprotein particles, whereas ApoE2 shows the opposite tendency(Hu et al. 2015). This lipidation defect may alter the distribution, stability, or receptor interactions of ApoE4 within the membrane microenvironment, thereby specifically enhancing its activation of membrane‑associated phospholipases such as cPLA2. However, whether ApoE4‑mediated cPLA2 activation directly depends on its lipidation state requires further experimental validation. Moreover, chronic low-grade inflammation in the periphery of ApoE4 carriers may increase AD risk(Shi et al. 2017). ApoE also participates in neuronal growth and ApoE synaptic plasticity, membrane repair, and remodeling processes, and is closely associated with tau-mediated neurodegeneration, glucose metabolism, and other processes(Hickman et al. 2008). Of particular note, the lipidation status of ApoE lipoproteins in the brain may exert neuroprotective effects by maintaining BBB integrity and cerebrovascular function independent of Aβ metabolism. In summary, ApoE gene polymorphisms play a role in AD pathogenesis by regulating neuroinflammation and Aβ metabolism. Targeting APOE-related pathways can be a potential therapeutic strategy for AD.

Regulatory Role of Microglia in Neuroinflammation in AD

Microglia are resident immune cells in the central nervous system. They play a complex and vital role in the pathological process of AD. The latest single-cell sequencing technology reveals that the phenotype of microglia dynamically changes during AD progression, which is closely associated with the disease stage and brain region specificity. In previous studies, activated microglia were generally divided into the M1 and M2 phenotypes, with M1 being a proinflammatory phenotype and M2 being an anti-inflammatory phenotype (Fig. 3). In the early stage of AD, activated microglia play a neuroprotective role via mechanisms such as engulfing Aβ plaques, removing cellular debris, and forming physical barriers. They can decrease Aβ toxicity to neurons via alternative activation states(Jimenez et al. 2008; Condello et al. 2015; Hickman et al. 2018). However, with disease progression, persistent Aβ deposition and tau pathology impair microglia function, shifting the phenotype from the protective M2 anti-inflammatory to an M1 proinflammatory phenotype; this is characterized by decreased phagocytosis and an excessive release of proinflammatory cytokines(Alshaebi et al. 2025), with subsequent toxic effects on peripheral neurons and synapses. This functional transformation reflects the “double-edged sword” characteristics of microglia in AD: in the physiological state, they maintain neural homeostasis via immune surveillance, synaptic pruning, and other functions(Norden and Godbout 2013). However, in the pathological state, continuous abnormal activation may aggravate neurodegeneration via proinflammatory responses(Spittau 2017; Zheng et al. 2017). Based on these findings, the current treatment strategies for AD primarily focus on two modalities: enhancing the Aβ clearance ability of microglia and regulating their inflammatory response. These interventions are being evaluated in several clinical trials, providing new possibilities for AD treatment.

Diverse stimuli drive microglial phenotypic polarization, promoting neuroinflammatory responses that ultimately exacerbate AD pathology

Lipid Metabolism Affects the Phenotype of Microglia

Recent studies have revealed that lipid metabolism disorders play a key role in AD pathogenesis by affecting the phenotypic transformation of microglia. Triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2), a surface receptor of microglia, regulates microglial chemotaxis, phagocytosis, and survival by binding to DAP12 to form a signaling complex(Poliani et al. 2015; Yeh et al. 2016). Furthermore, TREM2 can recognize various lipid-related ligands, including phospholipids, lipoproteins (LDL and HDL), and apolipoproteins (ApoE and clusterin/apoJ)(Krasemann et al. 2017; Fernandez et al. 2019; Lanfranco et al. 2021). These interactions are essential for maintaining lipid homeostasis in the brain. Notably, lipoprotein lipase (LPL), a key enzyme in triglyceride metabolism, is regulated by diet, with high-carbohydrate and high-fat diets increasing its expression in microglia(Gao et al. 2017). Transcriptomic analyses have demonstrated that in AD, LPL expression exhibits regional specificity, being significantly upregulated in microglia within brain regions with high Aβ pathological burden, such as the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, which is closely associated with the activation of the disease-associated microglia (DAM) phenotype(Keren-Shaul et al. 2017; Zhou et al. 2020; Sun et al. 2023). Functionally, downregulation of LPL in microglia impairs lipid uptake and utilization, leading to mitochondrial abnormalities, reduced ATP synthesis, and compromised phagocytic capacity. Conversely, LPL upregulation promotes fatty acid oxidation (FAO), attenuates the inflammatory response of microglia, and enhances their ability to clear amyloid-β, thereby exerting neuroprotective effects(Ulland et al. 2017). However, the neuroprotective function of LPL is notably modulated by APOE genotype. APOE4 disrupts overall lipid homeostasis in microglia and suppresses the activation of protective microglial states, such as the microglial neurodegenerative phenotype (MGnD), which is driven by the TREM2-APOE signaling axis, thereby compromising the beneficial metabolic reprogramming mediated by LPL(Yin et al. 2023). Consequently, in APOE4 carriers, LPL upregulation may fail to effectively enhance phagocytosis or suppress inflammation and could even exacerbate microglial dysfunction due to dysregulated lipid handling(Haney et al. 2024).

Mechanistically, TREM2 affects microglial transformation to a disease-associated microglia phenotype by regulating the metabolic reprogramming(Kobayashi et al. 2016) and inflammatory signaling(Keren-Shaul et al. 2017) of microglia(Nugent et al. 2020). TREM2 dysfunction not only impairs cholesterol and myelin metabolism in the brain(Cantoni et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2016) but also significantly impedes the phagocytic and clearance ability of microglia to Aβ–lipoprotein complexes(Heneka et al. 2013). This dysfunction drives microglial polarity toward a proinflammatory phenotype; this results in the massive release of proinflammatory factors such as IL-1β and TNF-α by activating the TLR/NLRP3 inflammasome pathway(Wang et al. 2015). This disorder of lipid metabolism and microglial activation forms a vicious circle: on the one hand, lipid accumulation exacerbates neuroinflammation by activating inflammatory pathways; on the other hand, inflammation further disrupts lipid homeostasis and accelerates the pathological progression of AD.

Mechanisms Underlying the Interactions Between Glial Cells and Neurons

There is a complex interaction between glial cells and neurons in neuroinflammation. In particular, Aβ can promote the excessive secretion of complement C3 by activating the NFκB signaling pathway in astrocytes(Lian et al. 2015). Secreted C3 directly damages neuronal function by binding to its receptor C3aR and activating microglia(Lian et al. 2016). Notably, activated microglia can secrete proinflammatory factors such as IL-1α, C1q, and TNF, inducing the transformation of the neurotoxic astrocytic phenotype(Liddelow et al. 2017); this results in a positive feedback regulatory loop between glial cells, leading to the continuous amplification of inflammatory responses. In the pathological process of AD, the homeostatic regulatory network between neurons and glial cells is significantly impaired. Studies have revealed that two pairs of key signaling molecular systems, namely, CD200-CD200R and CX3CL1-CX3CR1, play a key role in maintaining microglial homeostasis(Simon et al. 2019). However, the expression of CD200, CD200R, and CX3CR1 in the brain tissues of patients with AD is significantly downregulated(Walker et al. 2009; Holtman et al. 2015); this directly destroys the normal communication between neurons and microglia and ultimately aggravates the pathological process of neuroinflammation.

Summary

Lipid metabolism plays an essential role in AD pathogenesis by regulating neuroinflammation. Studies have revealed that lipid metabolism disorders can accumulate proinflammatory lipid mediators and decrease anti-inflammatory lipids, activating microglia and astrocytes and triggering chronic neuroinflammatory responses. This inflammatory microenvironment directly damages neurons and further disrupts lipid metabolic homeostasis, forming a vicious cycle. Lipid metabolism-based anti-inflammatory therapies have become a key focus in the development of drugs for AD, with major strategies involving n-3 fatty acid supplementation and targeting key lipid-metabolizing enzymes. Despite epidemiological studies showing a certain degree of protective effect of n-3 PUFAs, large-scale clinical trials (RCTs) have failed to demonstrate significant cognitive improvements in AD patients. Subgroup analyses suggest that n-3 PUFAs may be more beneficial for non-ApoE4 carriers or early-stage AD patients, highlighting the importance of patient stratification. Furthermore, statins, which are commonly used to lower blood lipids, may also exert anti-neuroinflammatory effects. However, conclusive evidence for their cognitive improvement benefits in AD patients remains lacking.

In addition to existing intervention strategies, several emerging targets have attracted attention in recent years. TREM2, as a lipid sensor, is implicated in AD pathology, where mutations prevent microglia from effectively clearing myelin debris and lipids, thereby triggering chronic inflammation. Based on this mechanism, antibody therapies aimed at enhancing TREM2 function have entered preclinical research and are emerging as promising anti-inflammatory and repair-promoting targets. Moreover, specialized SPMs, such as Resolvin D1, are significantly reduced in the brains of AD patients. Exogenous administration of these mediators has shown potential to alleviate neuroinflammation and amyloid-beta pathology, presenting a novel treatment concept focused on “inflammation resolution” rather than traditional “anti-inflammatory” approaches. These findings have propelled the application of lipid metabolism regulation in AD treatment. Current clinical evidence indicates that the therapeutic effects of these strategies are significantly influenced by disease stage (e.g., mild cognitive impairment or preclinical stages), ApoE genotype, and individual metabolic conditions. Therefore, future research should focus on early interventions, incorporating genetic and biomarker-based precision stratification. Additionally, the development of targeted drugs with fewer side effects and enhanced ability to cross the blood-brain barrier will provide a solid theoretical foundation for the innovation of AD treatment strategies.
