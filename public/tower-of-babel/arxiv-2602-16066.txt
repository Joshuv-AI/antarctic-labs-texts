Improving Interactive In-Context Learning from Natural Language Feedback
Martin Klissarov, Jonathan Cook, Diego Antognini, Hao Sun, Jingling Li, Natasha Jaques, Claudiu Musat, Edward Grefenstette

Abstract
Adapting one’s thought process based on corrective feedback is an essential ability in human learning, particularly in collaborative settings. In contrast, the current large language model training paradigm relies heavily on modeling vast, static corpora. While effective for knowledge acquisition, it overlooks the interactive feedback loops essential for models to adapt dynamically to their context. In this work, we propose a framework that treats this interactive in-context learning ability not as an emergent property, but as a distinct, trainable skill. We introduce a scalable method that transforms single-turn verifiable tasks into multi-turn didactic interactions driven by information asymmetry. We first show that current flagship models struggle to integrate corrective feedback on hard reasoning tasks. We then demonstrate that models trained with our approach dramatically improve the ability to interactively learn from language feedback. More specifically, the multi-turn performance of a smaller model nearly reaches that of a model an order of magnitude larger. We also observe robust out-of-distribution generalization: interactive training on math problems transfers to diverse domains like coding, puzzles and maze navigation. Our qualitative analysis suggests that this improvement is due to an enhanced in-context plasticity. Finally, we show that this paradigm offers a unified path to self-improvement. By training the model to predict the teacher’s critiques—effectively modeling the feedback environment—we convert this external signal into an internal capability, allowing the model to self-correct even without a teacher.

How do we impart capabilities?

Natural language is central to human progress, allowing us to both distribute information via static sources, like scientific articles, as well as provide real-time feedback to adapt collectively to unpredictable situations.
While Large Language Models (LLMs) excel at processing large amounts of static knowledge, they struggle to learn from real-time interaction.
Consequently, because models do not readily adapt on the fly, AI researchers must typically intervene when capabilities are missing by manually curating and adding datapoints, or tasks, to the training mixture.
Alternatively, researchers, and users alike, rely on complex prompt engineering to steer models toward desired outcomes.
While effective, these strategies create a bottleneck, placing the burden entirely on researchers to monitor failures and adjust task distributions.
As the tasks users assign to LLMs grow in specificity,
maintaining this level of oversight becomes prohibitively difficult.

Yet, for many tasks of interest, the knowledge required to resolve these tail-end failures likely already exists, distributed across the model’s user base.
In such cases, the bottleneck is not the availability of knowledge, but the model’s capacity to effectively acquire it through interactions.
However, guiding current LLMs via interaction is brittle, often requiring users to engineer complex initial prompts to elicit desired capabilities
(
35
)
. In Figure

, we present this through a simple evocative example between two leading closed-source models, where one of them misses the obvious hint.
To make it worthwhile for users to teach LLMs, models must adeptly interpret, reason, and adjust their behavior dynamically within the context of a conversation.
Doing so robustly could lead to exponentially more efficient learning
(
37
)
.
However, this presents a significant challenge: unlike standard Reinforcement Learning (RL) where scalar rewards offer a clear gradient for optimization, language feedback does not readily lend itself to such direct optimization.

In this work, we propose a framework to address this problem by treating interactive in-context learning from natural language feedback as a distinct, trainable capability.
We introduce a simple and scalable method that reformulates the vast amounts of available single-turn verifiable reasoning problems into multi-turn didactic dialogues. As shown in Figure
2
, we simulate interactions between a student and a teacher, where the student must iteratively refine its solution by integrating the teacher’s guidance.
This setup serves as an effective approximation to the real-world corrective feedback between users and models.
A key insight in our work is that
generating high-quality feedback on verifiable domains does not require a superior model, but rather information asymmetry
.
The teacher can utilize the same base model as the student but is conditioned on privileged information, such as reference solutions or unit test outputs.
By prompting the teacher to provide guidance without revealing the answer (experiments show this happens in less than
1\%
of cases), we can fine-tune the student model to improve its ability to effectively adapt in-context to natural language feedback.

We demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach through a comprehensive series of experiments across diverse domains.
We first establish that off-the-shelf flagship models struggle to integrate language feedback during complex reasoning tasks.

We then study how our didactic training paradigm
improves an agent’s interactive in-context learning ability
and
empowers it to actively leverage teacher feedback, turning initial errors into successes through multi-turn dialogue.
Remarkably, a smaller model (Gemini 2.5 Flash) fine-tuned via our method nearly matches the performance of a significantly larger model (Gemini 2.5 Pro).
Furthermore, we verify the strong generalization of these capabilities: models trained exclusively on verifiable math interactions show marked improvements in distinct domains, such as coding and logic puzzles (e.g., ARC-AGI).
This transfer learning even extends to multi-turn tasks unrelated to the didactic training setup, including Poker and Wordle.
Investigating the mechanism behind these improvements, we find that fine-tuned models exhibit greater in-context plasticity, whereas baselines tend to merely repeat their initial answers.

Finally, we demonstrate that this approach creates
a unified pathway to self-improvement
. Rather than training solely on the student’s turns, we train on the full interactive dialogues, including the teacher’s critiques. Considering the teacher as the environment, providing new observations between student turns, this process is analogous to having an auxiliary world modeling objective. Importantly, we train the model to predict the teacher’s turns as if they were unconditioned on privileged information, requiring the model to infer the logic of the critique solely from the context of the error. This allows a single model to internalize the whole feedback loop: it learns the student’s ability to correct reasoning and the teacher’s ability to spot flaws. Consequently, the external guidance provided during training is transformed into an internal capability, allowing the model to self-correct by playing the role of its own teacher.

2
Learning from Language Feedback

We now detail our methodology for enhancing the interactive in-context learning capabilities of LLMs. Our design prioritizes verifiable tasks, where performance is measured strictly by the model’s ability to achieve the ground-truth solution. This focus allows us to isolate methods that robustly drive improvement across multiple interaction turns, thereby avoiding the evaluation ambiguity and subjectivity inherent in non-verifiable tasks.

Interactive learning via language feedback can be viewed through the lens of social learning
(
2
;
17
;
23
)
—the process of acquiring behaviors by observing, imitating and interacting with others. Our algorithm builds on one of the simplest yet most powerful social learning settings: the didactic interaction between a teacher and a student.
As illustrated in Figure
2
, our mechanism pairs a student model, which attempts to solve a problem, with a teacher model that provides targeted language guidance.
The student is conditioned on the problem statement; if the automatic verifier deems its answer incorrect, the teacher intervenes with guidance, prompting the student to attempt the problem again.
Unlike common student-teacher frameworks such as distillation
(
15
;
1
)
, where the teacher is instantiated as a larger, more capable model, our approach relies solely on information asymmetry. The teacher leverages privileged information (such as ground truth labels) to generate corrective feedback. Consequently, the teacher and student can be instantiated using the exact same model. This cooperative self-play dynamic, optimized via RL, will be shown to yield significant performance improvements after fine-tuning. Our setup is related to
22
, who introduce a framework of social learning in LLMs where models exchange knowledge via natural language to maintain data privacy. Overall, our teacher-student setup is a particular instantiation of the concepts of language games and boundless socratic learning
(
29
)
.

2.1
Formal Definition

Learning from language feedback can be modeled as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP)
\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{O},T,R\rangle
. The student model, an agent with parameters
\theta_{S}
, learns a policy
\pi_{S}
, whereas the teacher model is parametrize with
\theta_{T}
and learns a policy
\pi_{T}
. In our experiments, the student and the teacher actions
a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
are natural language utterances
u_{t}
, however, in the general case, this could include tool calls. The observation at time
t
is modeled as the conversation history
o_{t}=(u_{1}^{T},u_{1}^{S},\dots,u_{t}^{T},u_{t}^{S})
, where
u_{t}^{T}
and
u_{t}^{S}
are teacher and student utterances at time
t
.
A state at time
t
is then defined as the tuple consisting of the conversation history
o_{t}
and the task’s privileged information
k
, written as
s_{t}=(k_{t},o_{t})=(k_{t},u_{1}^{T},u_{1}^{S},\dots,u_{t}^{T},u_{t}^{S})\in\mathcal{S}
. This privileged information can take the form of a ground-truth solution or output of private unit tests.

At each turn
t
, the teacher observes the conversation history and the task privileged information
k
, and takes an action
u_{t+1}^{T}\sim\pi_{T}(\cdot|s_{t},k)
.
After the teacher’s utterance, the student responds with
u_{t+1}^{S}\sim\pi_{S}(\cdot|u_{t+1}^{T},o_{t})
. In our implementation, the teacher and the student policies define the transition function
T
. In the general case, these policies could be acting on an environment (such as computer use) with a separate transition function.
This interaction loop continues until the end of an episode, defined through a maximum number of turns
T
, or when the student outputs the correct solution.
The reward function then measures the student’s output through a sparse function
R(s_{t},a_{t})
focusing on conversation-level outcomes like final answer correctness. We use verifiable domains like math and code, where objective performance measures exist.

Our algorithm leverages the model’s in-context learning ability to integrate natural language feedback during conversations. After each such trajectory, weight optimization is done through gradient updates. In other words, we are improving the model’s ability of
learning how to learn from interactions
, i.e. its meta-learning ability
(
30
;
33
)
.
The standard approach in meta learning, recently generalized by recent works
12
;
14
through the lens of bi-level optimization, typically requires gradient updates in the inner loop, leading to meta-gradients.
We avoid this by relying on, and improving, the existing in-context learning abilities of LLMs.

This approach is similar to black box meta-learning (e.g., RL
2
)
(
34
;
10
)
and first-order meta-learning
(
24
)
. In particular, since during training language feedback is only observed following turns that do not achieve the verifiable reward, the presence of language feedback can be interpreted as an augmented reward observation. The LLM can thus be viewed as implementing an in-context RL algorithm with weights that are in turn optimized via RL. We name our method
Reinforcement Learning with Language Feedback (RL
2
F)
to indicate the use of these two learning signals and to highlight the connection with RL
2
.

Experiments

We perform an extensive study designed to measure the efficacy and generalizability of our proposed method for improving the interactive in-context learning abilities of LLMs. We structure our investigation into three distinct areas:

1.

We first analyze the interactive in-context learning abilities of state-of-the-art models, revealing
significant headroom in the frontier models’ multi-turn reasoning abilities
across interactions on hard reasoning tasks.

2.

We conduct fine-tuning experiments on a non-thinking model, Gemma 3 12b, as well as the thinking flagship Gemini 2.5 Flash. Experiments focus on measuring the improvements in terms of multi-turn in-context adaptation, where
RL
2
F

leads to significant in-distribution gains and strong out-of-distribution generalization
. We study the reasons behind such improvements through qualitative analysis, which points to an
increased in-context plasticity
.

3.

We demonstrate a
pathway to self-improvement
by training on multi-turn attempts to internalize the feedback loop. By treating the teacher’s turns as an auxiliary world-modeling objective, the model can internalize the feedback loop, thereby developing the capability to self-correct.

Does the teacher leak the ground truth solution?
An important question that we need to address in the current evaluation setup relates to whether we can trust that the student legitimately solves the problem, or has the solution been revealed by the teacher. We investigate this question by having a verification pipeline of two detection mechanisms: (1) a string matching function applied on each teacher output and (2) an LLM judge that, conditioned on the question, ground truth solution and teacher output, evaluates whether the solution has been revealed. We report that in an average of
0.3%
of cases our verification pipeline signals that the teacher has revealed the solution, indicating a strong enough instruction-following ability of the Gemini and GPT models. The noise that might come from such false positives is far below the gap in performance between the models that we have evaluated.

In this section, we answer in the affirmative, demonstrating that internalizing the feedback patterns from didactic interactions is key to self-improvement and becoming autodidact.
Concretely, we adopt a world-modeling view by having the student predict the teacher’s feedback.
At inference, we then let the model interact with itself, generating a loop of self-critiques and self-refinements.
We do not condition the self-critique mechanism on privileged information: the agent gives itself feedback solely by observing its past attempts.

We hypothesize that this external signal prevents the degenerate loops often caused by biased self-feedback, providing the high-quality data necessary for robust learning.

5
Related Work

A growing body of research explores how agents can learn from natural language feedback. One line of work focuses on the theoretical underpinnings of such learning. For instance,
36
formalize the Learning from Language Feedback (LLF) problem, where an agent observes textual feedback instead of a latent reward signal. They propose that for an agent to learn effectively with no regret, the feedback must be unbiased, the agent must be able to interpret it, and the agent’s hypothesis space must include the environment’s true hypothesis.

Another research direction aims to integrate language more deeply into the reinforcement learning paradigm.
11
introduce Natural Language Reinforcement Learning (NLRL), a framework that redefines core reinforcement learning concepts, such as value functions and policies, in natural language. This approach seeks to improve the interpretability and sample efficiency of RL agents by using language as the medium for representing and reasoning about the task.

The concept of leveraging a more knowledgeable teacher to provide feedback is explored by
4
. This work highlights the importance of information asymmetry between a teacher and a student model. The teacher is provided with privileged information, such as the exact location of an object in a navigation task, which is not available to the student. This allows the teacher to provide more effective feedback on the student’s failed trajectories. The student then learns from this feedback through imitation learning, where the teacher’s correct actions and rationales replace the student’s incorrect ones. Experiments have shown that this can lead to the student outperforming the teacher, and that a student can even improve by receiving feedback from a privileged version of itself.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we have introduced a method for learning from language feedback through didactic interactions and have shown it to be an effective way to improve the ability of LLMs to interactively in-context learn from language feedback.
Our results show that this approach not only allows smaller models to rival the performance of flagship models on verifiable reasoning tasks but also induces a fundamental increase in in-context plasticity that generalizes to diverse, out-of-distribution domains.
Furthermore, we showed that by internalizing the feedback loop from didactic interactions, models can bootstrap their own capability for self-improvement and become autodidacts.

In our setup, the teacher has not played a role in the selection of the problems the student faces. However, human teachers typically craft careful
curricula
for their students, in part based on observed mistakes during interactions. Exploring this idea further would be a promising avenue, with many connections to be made to curriculum learning
(
19
;
31
)
such as unsupervised environment design
(
8
;
18
)
.

Our goal was to train a model to better integrate feedback in-context, which led to increased adaptability. However, this also raises an important question: could this same mechanism incentivize undesirable behaviors, such as sycophancy? Further studies into the potential
safety
issues, such as misuse and misalignment
(
32
)
, that these new capabilities raise is of great importance.

Furthermore, while our investigation focused on a cooperative setting between a teacher and a student, the scope and complexity of such social learning settings extend far beyond such settings. Many real-world scenarios involve agents operating with partial information and conflicting goals
(
6
)
. Consequently, a promising avenue for future research lies in exploring
mixed-motive settings
, such as debate or negotiation, which could potentially yield distinct cognitive benefits.

Finally, we believe this work represents a significant step toward robust
continual learning
, yet a critical piece of the puzzle remains. We have successfully improved the model’s interactive in-context learning, but we have not yet addressed the integration of these transient signals into permanent capabilities. Developing a mechanism to consolidate this enhanced short-term adaptation into long-term knowledge remains a crucial frontier.
