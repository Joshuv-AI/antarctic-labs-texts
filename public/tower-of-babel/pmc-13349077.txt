High-performance and regenerable poly(2-aminothiazole)-modified montmorillonite for textile dye removal
Authors: Leila Mouacher, Ouidad Saiah, Aicha Brassi, Bendoukha Abdelkrim Reguig, Ahmed Yahiaoui, Haroun Houicha, Mohammed El Amine Monir, M Fatmi, Mustafa Jaipallah Abdelmageed Abualreish, Murat Yaylacı, Aseel Smerat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdsorption is a cost-effective and environmentally friendly method for removing organic pollutants from water. In this study, cetyltrimethylammonium bromide–modified montmorillonite (OMt) and a poly(2-aminothiazole) nanocomposite (OMt|PAT) were synthesized and evaluated for removing Bemacid Blue dye from aqueous solutions. BET analysis showed that PAT incorporation restructured the clay, reducing the microporous surface area from 3.41 to 0.51 m2 g− 1, indicating that the polymer chains effectively occupied the original micropores. Structural modifications were confirmed after BB-Dye uptake via FTIR, XRD, SEM, and EDX. Batch experiments showed that optimal adsorption occurs at pH 5.5–6.0. Kinetic studies revealed that OMt followed a pseudo-second-order model, while OMt|PAt followed a pseudo-first-order model, achieving equilibrium within 60–90 min. Isotherm modeling indicated that OMt|PAT follows the Langmuir model (qm = 18.86 mg g− 1, R2 ≈ 0.994), while OMt adsorption is better described by the Hill de Boer model (R2 ≈ 0.989), suggesting cooperative interactions. Thermodynamic analysis confirmed a spontaneous (ΔG° < 0) and endothermic adsorption process. The enthalpy changes (ΔH° = 15.83 and 12.48 kJ mol− 1 for OMt and OMt|PAT, respectively) indicate a physisorption mechanism, while positive entropy values (ΔS° = 140.59 and 132.03 35 J.mol− 1.K− 1) reflect an entropy-driven process for both adsorbents. Regeneration studies using acetic acid demonstrated > 90% desorption, with the nanocomposite maintaining > 80% efficiency after four cycles. These findings highlight OMt|PAT as a robust, high-affinity, and recyclable material for sustainable dye removal.Keywords: Bemacid blue, Adsorption, Polymer–clay nanocomposite, poly(2-aminothiazole), Montmorillonite

Introduction

Ensuring access to clean water has increasingly become a major global challenge in the last several decades. Rapid industrialization, population growth, and evolving agricultural practices have significantly increased the presence of chemical and biological contaminants in water resources. Recent studies emphasize the need to remove not only synthetic dyes but also other persistent pollutants, including complex pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals, and heavy metals [1–4]. Advanced nanostructured materials, including biochar-supported and polymer-based composites, have shown promising performance for the remediation of these contaminants [5, 6]. Synthetic dyes are widely applied in industries such as textiles, leather, paper, cosmetics, and pharmaceuticals because of their remarkable chemical and photolytic stability [7–9]. Depending on the charges of their chromophoric groups, dyes are typically categorized as nonionic, cationic, or anionic [10]. Anionic dyes, such as Bemacid Blue (BB), an anthraquinone-based compound widely used for dyeing wool and nylon fibers, possess complex aromatic structures that ensure high solubility and chemical stability [11–13]. Under oxidative or photochemical conditions, these dyes may release toxic, carcinogenic, and mutagenic by-products [14, 15]. Their persistence also reduces light penetration and dissolved oxygen, potentially causing hypoxic conditions and threatening human health [15, 16]. Although various chemical and biological strategies, including solvent extraction, chemical precipitation, photocatalytic degradation, membrane separation, ion exchange, and electrochemical processes, have been investigated for dye removal [17–23], their limited practical applicability and the risk of secondary pollution—particularly for dyes with complex aromatic structures have directed attention toward adsorption using simple, cost-effective, and regenerable materials [24]. Thiazole-based compounds have attracted considerable attention in environmental remediation due to their heterocyclic structure containing nitrogen and sulfur atoms, which can interact strongly with a variety of pollutants. Recent studies have shown that embellishing thiazole-modified surfaces with nanoparticles creates highly efficient heterocatalysts for the degradation of complex pharmaceutical effluents [25]. Poly(2-aminothiazole) (PAT), a polymeric thiazole derivative, possesses a nitrogen- and sulfur-rich backbone that facilitates binding with metal ions such as Hg(II), Ag(I), and Cr(VI) [26–28]. These heteroatoms also enable electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and surface complexation with dye molecules. Unlike chitosan, which is prone to acid-induced swelling and structural leaching, PAT remains insoluble and rigid even at low pH, preserving the structural integrity of the adsorbent. Compared to nitrogen‑only polymers such as polyaniline (PANI), the thiazole ring contains both sulfur and nitrogen heteroatoms in a conjugated aromatic system, which enhances electron density and facilitates multipoint interactions (e.g., coordination, π‑π stacking and heteroatom–metal interactions) with target molecules [29]. This dual‑heteroatom configuration has been shown to promote strong adsorption and selective capture of metal ions and organic pollutants due to increased electronic delocalization and specific binding sites associated with the thiazole ring structure. However, the modest specific surface area and limited porosity of PAT can restrict its adsorption capacity. To overcome this limitation, PAT can be combined with clay minerals, particularly montmorillonite, which offers a large accessible surface area, elevated cation-exchange capacity, excellent mechanical and thermal stability, and notable adsorptive characteristics, making it an ideal support for polymer-based nanocomposites [30–32]. To date, montmorillonite-based adsorbents for dye removal have primarily been modified with polyaniline, polypyrrole, or surfactants, focusing on enhancing surface area and electrostatic interactions. However, no studies have reported the use of PAT-functionalized montmorillonite for the adsorption of organic dyes. This work fills this gap by leveraging the unique nitrogen-sulfur (N-S) heterocyclic synergy of PAT, which provides a different set of chemical interactions—such as π‑π stacking and specific coordination—that are absent in traditional clay-based systems. Montmorillonite, a hydrous aluminum silicate of the 2:1 layered type, is distinguished by a nanolayered structure with single layers approximately 1 nm thick and lateral sizes between 200 and 300 nm [33]. Its expandable interlayer spaces accommodate exchangeable cations; however, the hydrophilic nature of this clay limits its affinity for hydrophobic dyes. Surface modification with organic cations such as cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) produces organo-modified montmorillonite (OMt) with increased interlayer spacing, enhanced hydrophobicity, and improved compatibility with polymer matrices [34–37]. The incorporation of PAT into OMt leads to nanocomposites that synergistically combine the structural advantages of organoclay and the polymer’s functional groups, which together lead to enhanced adsorption efficiency toward anionic dyes compared with OMt alone [29]. In this study, montmorillonite was employed as a support for the in-situ polymerization of PAT to fabricate an OMt|PAT nanocomposite. The synthesized OMt|PAT was evaluated alongside OMt for the adsorption of Bemacid Blue (BB-Dye). Prior to and following adsorption, the samples were characterized using FTIR, XRD, SEM, EDX, and BET surface area analysis. The influence of adsorbent dosage, solution pH, contact time, initial dye concentration, and temperature on adsorption was systematically assessed. Adsorption kinetics were analyzed through pseudo-first-order (PFO) and pseudo-second-order (PSO) models, while equilibrium adsorption data were interpreted using multiple isotherm models, including Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin, Elovich, Dubinin–Radushkevich, Kiselev, Fowler–Guggenheim, and Hill–de Boer. Thermodynamic parameters were also determined, and desorption–regeneration studies evaluated the recyclability and economic viability of the adsorbents.

Materials and methods

Material and reagents

2-Aminothiazole (2-AT, 98%), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), ammonium persulfate (APS), and cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (high-purity reagent) and utilized as supplied, without further processing. Deionized water served as the medium for all procedures. Prior to experiments, the initial pH of the solutions was regulated using dilute HCl or NaOH. Montmorillonite was procured from the northwestern zone of Algeria (Maghnia deposit) and supplied by ENOF Chemical Ltd. The natural clay was purified by dispersion in DDW (10% w/v) and repeated sedimentation for 24 h, then dried at 80 °C, crushed, and sieved to a particle size of < 50 µm. To enhance its cation exchange capacity, the purified clay was converted into its sodium form (Na⁺-Mt) by mixing with a 10 g of clay with 200 mL of a 1 M NaCl solution under constant stirring (500 rpm) for 24 h. The suspension was then centrifuged (4000 rpm for 15 min), followed by thorough washing with DDW to eliminate residual chloride, confirmed by the AgNO3 test. The final product was dried at 105 °C for 12 h and subsequently pulverized, consistent with protocols reported in previous studies [38, 39]. The resulting Na⁺-Mt sample exhibited a cation exchange capacity (CEC) of 42 meq/100 g, as measured according to the ASTM C837-99 standard. Bemacid Blue (BB) (Fig. 1), an anionic anthraquinone dye supplied from the Soitex–Tlemcen textile company (Algeria) and used without further purification. Its molecular formula is C₃₉H₄₁O₇SN₄Na, with a molar mass of 732.82 g mol− 1.

Preparation of OMt

To prepare OMt, 10 mmol of CTAB were dissolved in 5 mL of 1 N HCl and then diluted with DDW to a total volume of 100 mL. The mixture was maintained under constant stirring (500 rpm) at ambient temperature for 2 h to obtain a homogeneous solution. Then, 10 g of purified Na⁺-Montmorllonite was incorporated into the surfactant solution, followed by continuous stirring for 24 h to permit maximum cation exchange. Following vacuum filtration, the recovered clay was carefully rinsed several times with pre-heated (40 °C) DDW until no foam formation was observed, ensuring the complete removal of excess surfactant and residual Cl− ions. Finally, the organo-modified clay (OMt) was dried at 105 °C for 24 h, ground into a fine powder using an agate mortar, and stored in airtight conditions for further use [36].

Synthesis of OMt|PAT nanocomposite

The synthesis of OMt|PAT nanocomposite was carried out via in situ oxidative polymerization. Briefly, 0.25 g of OMt was suspended in 100 mL of an aqueous solution containing 15 mmol of 2-aminothiazole and stirred vigorously (600 rpm) at 70 °C for 30 min to ensure complete dispersion. Polymerization was initiated by the dropwise addition of 25 mL of an aqueous solution containing 7.5 mmol of ammonium persulfate (APS) as an oxidant (monomer/oxidant molar ratio of 2:1). The addition was performed over 30 min, and the reaction was maintained for 24 h under reflux while being vigorously stirred at 70 °C. A progressive color shift from light orange to dark brown indicated the progression of the polymerization and the formation of PAT. Following vacuum filtration, the obtained solid was extensively washed with lukewarm DDW (at ~ 40 °C) and air-dried for 48 h to remove residual reagents. This method is consistent with previously reported protocols [36].

Determination of the point of zero charge (pHPZC)

The pHPZC of OMt and OMt|PAT was determined using the pH-drift method. Briefly, 50 mg of each adsorbent was added to 50 mL of a 0.01 M NaCl solution to maintain a constant ionic strength. The initial pH (pHi) values were adjusted between 2.0 and 12.0 using 0.1 M solutions of HCl or NaOH solutions. After 24 h of equilibrium under constant stirring at room temperature, the final pH (pHf) was measured. The pHPZC was identified graphically by plotting ΔpH (pHf-pHi) against pHi. The point where the curve intersects the horizontal axis (ΔpH = 0) represents the pHPZC of the material.

Characterization techniques

The structural, morphological, and surface properties of the prepared materials were characterized using complementary techniques. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded on a PerkinElmer spectrometer using KBr pellets in the range 4000–400 cm− 1 to identify functional groups. The crystalline organization and interlayer spacing of montmorillonite before and after modification were analyzed with a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer supplied with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). The diffraction patterns were collected over 2θ range of 5°–80° with a step size of 0.02° and a scan rate of 2° min− 1. The basal spacing (d₀₀₁) was calculated according to Bragg’s Law (n λ = 2d sin θ), where n is the reflection order (n = 1), λ is the X-ray wavelength, d is the interplanar distance, and θ is the diffraction angle. Surface morphology was analyzed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using a JEOL JSM-6700F after gold sputtering. UV–visible absorption spectra of BB-Dye aqueous solutions were recorded with a Shimadzu UV-1800 spectrophotometer at 605 nm. Textural properties, including specific surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution, were determined by nitrogen adsorption–desorption at 77 K using a Quantachrome NOVA 2200e analyser; samples were degassed at 120 °C for 12 h, and the data were analyzed using the BET and BJH methods.

Results and discussions

Characterization

The adsorption process primarily occurs on the external surfaces of solid adsorbents. Consequently, the morphology and characteristics of the surface play a key role in controlling the adsorption efficiency. Table 1 summarize The BET and pore structure analysis of OMt and OMt|PAT. The pristine OMt exhibits à moderate surface area of 16.26 m2 g− 1 and a typical pore volume of 0.18 cm3 g− 1, characteristic of CTAB-modified layered silicates where surfactant chains partially restrict access to interlayer pace [40, 41]. Upon incorporation of the PAT matrix, both the specific surface area and total pore volume decrease (11.74 m2 g− 1 and 0.08 cm3 g− 1, respectively). This reduction confirms that PAT chains successfully intercalated and occupied a significant fraction of the accessible mesopores and interlayer galleries. In contrast to the total surface area, the analysis reveals a significant reduction in microporous surface area (3.41 → 0.51 m2 g− 1) and micropore volume (1.35 × 10–2 → 0.19 × 10–3 cm3 g− 1). This indicates that the polymer growth effectively filled the smallest cavities and confined voids. The slight increase in mean pore diameter (28.15 → 30.34 nm) and average mesopore size (23.54 → 23.80 nm) further suggests that the remaining accessible network consists of larger mesopores, as the smaller pores were preferentially blocked by the polymer framework. Overall, the BET results confirm that PAT insertion restructures the clay, creating a hybrid framework where dye adsorption is driven by chemical affinity with the polymer rather than simple microporous entrapment.

The distinctive IR bands of the samples are presented in Table 2. The spectrum of OMt confirms its organo-clay structure, with Si–O–Mg vibrational signals around 1005 cm− 1, together with O–H related vibrations observed near 3655 and 1638 cm− 1, which reflect the layered silicate framework and interlayer water molecules [42, 43]. The fundamental clay vibrations remain unchanged, while intensity enhancements in multiple bands, particularly aromatic C = C and C = N vibrations, confirm effective dye incorporation into the clay interlayers and on its surface. The FTIR spectrum of OMt|PAT prior to adsorption displays characteristic PAT vibrations: N–H stretching at 3450 cm− 1, C = N vibrational mode of the thiazole heterocycle at 1625 cm− 1, aromatic C = C at 1495 cm− 1, C–N and C–S vibrations are observed at 1250 cm− 1 and 1221 cm− 1, respectively, confirming successful polymer incorporation [28, 49]. The structural integrity of montmorillonite is maintained, as seen in the 1005 cm− 1 and 3655–3670 cm− 1 bands, while the C–H region (2815–2905 cm− 1) indicates the presence of both CTA+ and PAT. Following BB-Dye adsorption, the N–H stretching band broadens markedly, indicating strong hydrogen bonding, while the C–H region increases in intensity. New peaks appear between 1200 and 1600 cm− 1, corresponding to characteristic BB-Dye vibrations. The clay framework remains intact, confirming that dye incorporation occurs primarily through non-covalent interactions without structural disruption.

The XRD patterns in Fig. 4 were analyzed to assess the crystalline structure and potential modifications of OMt and OMt|PAT nanocomposite, as well as possible structural modifications induced by BB-Dye adsorption. Pristine OMt exhibits its characteristic (d001) basal reflection at 2θ ≈ 5.1°, indicating an interlayer distance (d001) of 17.3 Å. This indicates successful CTA⁺ intercalation, interlayer expansion, reduced hydration, and a shift from hydrophilic to hydrophobic surface properties [36, 54]. After BB-Dye uptake, the position and shape of this (d001) reflection remain essentially unchanged, while variations in peak intensities suggest improved stacking or ordering of the clay platelets. These findings indicate that BB-Dye primarily interacts with the clay surface and accessible interlayer regions occupied by CTA+ ions, without causing structural disruption or exfoliation of the layered framework. In the case of the OMt|PAT nanocomposite, the (d001) basal reflection is not clearly discernible even prior to BB-Dye interaction, with only a broad low-angle signal around 2θ ≈ 4° (001 ≈ 22.1 Å). This reflects a disordered intercalated or partially exfoliated structure and a loss of long-range order due to the in-situ polymerization of PAT within the clay galleries. Following BB-Dye adsorption, the diffraction peaks corresponding to the higher-order planes of the 2:1 layered montmorillonite, namely (020, 110) at 2θ ≈ 19.8°, (003) at 2θ ≈ 27.5°, (130, 200) at 2θ ≈ 35.8°, (004) at 2θ ≈ 44°, (006) at 2θ ≈ 68.2° [55, 56], have broadened and decreased in intensity, forming low-intensity humps. Furthermore, the stability of the (060) reflection at 2θ ≈ 61.8° (noted in all samples) confirms that the dioctahedral layered structure of the clay remains intact despite the organic modifications. The substantial loss of crystalline order in the OMt|PAT after dye uptake suggests that BB-Dye incorporation into the polymer–clay network is the primary driver of the structural reorganization, rendering the nanocomposite predominantly amorphous. This behavior, corroborated by the spherical aggregates observed in SEM, highlights the enhanced adsorption affinity and the synergistic effect between the polymer matrix and BB-Dye entities. Overall, the XRD results demonstrate that while the structure of OMt remains largely intact following BB-Dye uptake, the OMt|PAT nanocomposite undergoes significant structural transformation, highlighting its superior adsorption efficiency.

Adsorption behavior

Adsorbent dose effect

Figure 5a depicts the influence of adsorbent dose on the removal efficiency of BB-Dye by OMt and OMt|PAT, using an initial dye concentration of 10 mg L− 1, pH 7, temperature of 25 °C, and a fixed contact time of 60 min. OMt reached a maximum removal of 75.29% at 0.01 g. Beyond this optimal mass, the removal rate decreased, likely due to particle agglomeration and the 'screening effect', where an excess of adsorbent particles leads to the overlapping of active sites, thereby limiting dye diffusion [57, 58]. In contrast, OMt|PAT exhibited an enhanced performance with a characteristic “increase–decrease–increase” profile. After an initial rise, a transient decline was observed at 0.03 g, attributed to inter-particle aggregation that temporarily reduces the accessible surface area. However, at 0.05 g, the removal efficiency reached its global maximum of 89.01%. It is important to note that while the adsorption capacity (qe) is theoretically higher at lower dosages like 0.02 g due to a higher dye-to-adsorbent ratio, 0.05 g was selected as the optimized dose. This choice prioritizes achieving the highest possible decontamination level (maximum R%), which is the primary criterion for practical wastewater treatment.

Evaluation of operating parameters for BB-Dye adsorption onto OMt and OMt|PAT: (a) effect of adsorbent dosage, (b) effect of contact time, (c) influence of initial dye concentration for OMt and OMt|PAT respectively, and (d) effect of temperature

At adsorbent masses above 0.05 g, a final decline in efficiency was observed, confirming that excessive particle clustering diminishes the quantity of available active sites. Accordingly, 0.01 g of OMt and 0.05 g of OMt|PAT were chosen as the ideal masses for subsequent adsorption tests.

pH effect and pHPZC study

The pH dependence of BB-Dye uptake was examined over the range of 2–10 at a fixed initial dye concentration (10 mg L− 1), temperature (25 °C), and using the optimal adsorbent masses established above (0.01 g for OMt and 0.05 g for OMt|PAT). Figure 6a demonstrates that the adsorption of BB-Dye on OMt and OMt|PAT is strongly pH-dependent, with both adsorbents exhibiting similar behavior. The ideal pH for the adsorption of BB-Dye was found to be 5.5 and 6.0 for OMt and OMt|PAT, respectively. This behavior is closely related to the pHPZC, which was determined to be 6.9 for OMt and 6.3 for OMt|PAT as shown in Fig. 6b. In acidic to near-neutral conditions (pH < pHPZC), the amino groups of the PAT matrix undergo protonation to form -NH3+ cations, enhancing the electrostatic attraction with the anionic sulfonate groups (–SO3−) of the dye, which remains negatively charged due to its low pKa < 2. In addition, the quaternary ammonium groups (–N(CH3)3+) from CTA+ provide a permanent positive charge, further promoting electrostatic adsorption [59]. Beyond electrostatic interactions, π–π stacking occurs between the electron-rich thiazole rings of PAT and the aromatic anthraquinone rings of the dye, while hydrogen bonding between PAT nitrogen/sulfur atoms and dye functional groups stabilizes the adsorbent-dye complex, collectively explaining OMt|PAT’s slightly higher adsorption capacity compared to OMt. In highly acidic environments (pH 2), H⁺ ions compete with dye anions for adsorption sites, reducing uptake. As the pH increases towards the pHPZC (6.3 for OMt|PAT), the surface positive charge density reaches an optimal balance with the dye speciation, explaining the maximum removal at pH 6.0. However, at alkaline pH (pH > 6.3), deprotonation leads to a net negative surface charge. In this region, the negatively charged clay layers and deprotonated PAT functional groups dominate, leading to strong electrostatic repulsion with the the anionic dye, causing a sharp decrease in adsorption [60–62]. OMt|PAT shows a marginally greater adsorption capacity across the studied pH range, reflecting the enhanced surface activity provided by the polymer grafting.

Contact time effect

As illustrated in Fig. 5b, the time-dependent adsorption of BB‑Dye onto OMt and OMt|PAT under the previously determined optimal conditions exhibits a rapid initial uptake within the first 10 min, particularly for OMt, due to the abundance of available active sites on its surface [63]. This fast adsorption is facilitated by minimal competition among dye ions at low concentrations; as the concentration increases, competition for accessible sites intensifies, resulting in a gradual decrease in the adsorption rate [64]. Following this initial stage, the adsorption rate slows, reflecting intraparticle diffusion influenced by structural factors such as particle aggregation, interlayer spacing, and the accessibility of internal binding sites [65]. At extended times, OMt stabilizes at a plateau, indicating that adsorption–desorption equilibrium is reached and that accessible sites are fully occupied. In contrast, OMt|PAT reaches a higher maximum removal (approximately 60 min), suggesting that PAT intercalation provides additional internal adsorption sites that enhance overall capacity. Beyond these maxima, a noticeable decrease in adsorption capacity, which can be attributed to the partial, desorption of weakly bound dye molecules under continuous mechanical stirring [66]. The agitation likely overcomes weak electrostatic interactions, leading to a dynamic adsorption–desorption process rather than a conventional stable plateau. Consequently, 60 min represents the optimal contact time for maximum removal in OMt|PAT, rather than a strictly reached steady-state equilibrium.

Initial dye concentration effect

Figure 5c illustrate the adsorption of BB‑Dye on OMt and OMt|PAT under the previously determined optimal conditions, with dye concentrations varying from 20 to 120 mg L− 1. The results demonstrate that increasing the initial BB-Dye concentration leads to an overall decrease in the removal percentage for OMt|PAT, falling from 94.50% to 63.84%. This trend is primarily due to the saturation of available active sites on the adsorbent surface as the ratio of dye molecules to adsorbent mass increases. For OMt, the removal efficiency shows a relatively stable profile with a slight increase at higher loadings, reflecting the progressive filling and eventual depletion of its relatively homogeneous high-affinity sites, consistent with a Langmuir-type monolayer regime [67–69]. By contrast, OMt|PAT displays a more intricate two-stage response: after a significant initial drop in efficiency at 50 mg L− 1 (56.86%), the removal percentage rises again at 100 mg L− 1 (76.12%) before reaching a second equilibrium. This sequential trend is characteristic of systems where multilayer formation, occupation of heterogeneous or lower-affinity sites, or concentration-dependent structural rearrangements become significant. At elevated dye loadings, additional contributions from dye aggregation or subtle reorganization within the PAT-modified framework may further promote access to secondary sorption domains. Taken together, these results indicates that while the overall R% may fluctuate with concentration, PAT functionalization enhances the number of accessible binding sites while simultaneously broadening the energetic heterogeneity of the surface, giving rise to more complex adsorption pathways than those observed for pristine OMt.

Temperature effect

Adsorption behavior of BB‑Dye on OMt and OMt|PAT was studied under the previously determined optimal conditions while varying the temperature from 25 to 65 °C. As depicted in Fig. 5d, both materials exhibit enhanced adsorption with increasing temperature, although OMt is more thermally sensitive, reaching a sharp increase in removal efficiency up to ~ 330 K (95%), whereas OMt|PAT displays a more gradual and controlled enhancement. This behavior suggests that CTAB modification of OMt forms closely packed aggregates with hydrophobic surfaces, promoting adsorption through electrostatic and nonpolar interactions, while the dense structure and increased interlayer spacing may slow diffusion, requiring higher temperature or stirring [69]. The slight decline observed at 338 K for OMt indicates an upper thermal limit, beyond which adsorbed species may become destabilized. By contrast, OMt|PAT maintains a continuous improvement without thermal destabilization, demonstrating that PAT functionalization moderates surface energetic heterogeneity and stabilizes adsorption sites against thermal desorption.

Desorption and recyclability

The regeneration and reuse of adsorbents are key determinants of their industrial viability. As shown in Fig. 10b, BB-Dye desorption was tested using various eluents, among which acetic acid (CH₃COOH) delivered the highest efficiency (> 90%) for both adsorbents, surpassing HCl and HNO₃. This performance can be attributed to the mild complexation ability of CH₃COOH and its capacity to protonate the adsorbed dye without significantly altering the adsorbent structure, unlike strong mineral acids that may induce surface degradation. OMt|PAT consistently outperformed pristine OMt, demonstrating that PAT functional groups enhance reversible dye interactions. The reusability of OMt and OMt|PAT was evaluated over five cycles as shown in Fig. 10a. Both materials maintained high removal efficiencies (> 80%) during the first four cycles, followed by a decline in the fifth cycle (44% for OMt and 34% for OMt|PAT). This performance drop is primarily due to the cumulative saturation of active sites; the strong electrostatic interactions between the dye’s sulfonate groups and the adsorbent’s quaternary ammonium/protonated amino groups hinder complete desorption [78, 79]. Additionally, repeated exposure to acidic eluents and mechanical stirring may induce minor structural fatigue and partial site occupancy by the regenerating agent [80–82]. Despite this late-cycle decline, OMt|PAT offers a superior techno-economic alternative to commercial activated carbon (AC). While AC typically requires energy-intensive thermal reactivation near 800 °C, leading to high costs and 5–10% mass loss per cycle [83], OMt|PAT is efficiently restored via a mild acidic wash at room temperature. Although alternative chemical or vacuum methods exist for carbonaceous materials, they often involve complex setups [84]. In contrast, the cold chemical desorption of OMt|PAT preserves the structural integrity of the polymer–clay matrix and reduces the energy footprint, making it a more sustainable and cost-effective solution for large-scale wastewater treatment.

Comparative performance synthesis

A comparative assessment shows that raw OMt benefits from a faster adsorption rate (k2 = 0.04 g·mg− 1·min− 1), yet it is limited by lower removal efficiency and thermal instability above 338 K. In contrast, the OMt|PAT nanocomposite achieves a higher removal efficiency (≈89%) and greater thermodynamic stability. Although the modification introduces steric hindrance evidenced by slower, diffusion-controlled kinetics (k2 = 0.01 g·mg− 1·min− 1), it facilitates a more predictable Langmuir monolayer adsorption. Contrary to initial assumptions, both materials follow an entropy-driven process, but the lower enthalpy of OMt|PAT (ΔH° = 12.48 kJ mol− 1) indicates a more energetically optimized physisorption. Ultimately, OMt|PAT offers robust adsorption through synergistic π–π stacking and electrostatic interactions, representing a more reliable and sustainable choice for industrial applications due to its consistent performance and recyclability.

Conclusion

OMt and OMt|PAT were synthesized and evaluated as adsorbents for Bemacid Blue (BB-Dye). BET analysis revealed that PAT incorporation reduced the total surface area (16.26 → 11.74 m2 g− 1) and significantly decreased the microporous surface area (3.41 → 0.51 m2 g− 1). This trend indicates that the PAT polymer chains successfully occupied the micropores and interlaminar spaces of the clay. Structural, compositional, and morphological characterization before and after BB-Dye uptake was performed via FTIR, EDX, SEM, and XRD. FTIR confirmed the integrity of the clay framework, successful PAT incorporation, and the presence of BB-Dye characteristic bands. EDX revealed elemental changes (↑C, N, S; ↓Al, Si, Mg), reflecting effective dye immobilization. XRD and SEM showed that OMt retained its layered crystalline structure, whereas OMt|PAT transformed into a predominantly amorphous structure exhibiting spherical aggregates and interstitial voids. Adsorption studies demonstrated that OMt|PAT consistently outperformed OMt, achieving optimal removal at pH 5.5 for OMt|PAT in 60 min and at pH 6.0 for OMt in 40 min. Higher initial dye concentrations enhanced adsorption by increasing the driving force and accessibility of active sites. Kinetic studies revealed that adsorption followed a PSO model for OMt and a PFO model for OMt|PAt, reflecting a shift in the rate-controlling mechanism and equilibrium behavior after surface modification. Among the evaluated isotherms, the Hill–de Boer model best described OMt, while the Langmuir model was most appropriate for OMt|PAT, based on the highest correlation coefficients (R2 ≈ 0.989 for OMt and 0.994 for OMt|PAT). BB-Dye uptake increased with temperature, and negative ΔG° values confirmed the process's spontaneity and feasibility. For both OMt and OMt|PAT, adsorption was endothermic and entropy-driven. The enthalpy values (< 20 kJ mol− 1) indicate that the dye is removed through physisorption, primarily governed by electrostatic interactions and increased randomness at the solid–liquid interface. Regeneration tests showed > 90% desorption using acetic acid and > 80% efficiency retention over four cycles. Overall, OMt|PAT is an efficient, high-affinity, and recyclable adsorbent, highlighting the potential of polymer–clay hybrids for scalable and effective wastewater treatment.
