On the analytical behavior of the k k – ω \omega turbulence model in buoyant-driven thermal convection
Da-Sol Joo

Abstract
The representation of buoyancy-driven turbulence in Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes models remains unresolved, with no widely accepted standard formulation. A key difficulty is the lack of analytical guidance for incorporating buoyant effects, particularly under unstable stratification. This study derives an analytical solution of the standard k k – ω \omega model for Rayleigh–Bénard convection in an infinite layer, where turbulent kinetic energy is generated solely by buoyancy. The solution provides explicit scaling relations among the Rayleigh ( 𝑅𝑎 \mathit{Ra} ), Prandtl ( 𝑃𝑟 \mathit{Pr} ), and Nusselt ( 𝑁𝑢 \mathit{Nu} ) numbers that capture the simulation trends: 𝑁𝑢 ∼ 𝑅𝑎 1 / 3 ​ 𝑃𝑟 1 / 3 \mathit{Nu}\sim\mathit{Ra}^{1/3}\mathit{Pr}^{1/3} for 𝑃𝑟 ≪ 1 \mathit{Pr}\ll 1 and 𝑁𝑢 ∼ 𝑅𝑎 1 / 3 ​ 𝑃𝑟 − 0.415 \mathit{Nu}\sim\mathit{Ra}^{1/3}\mathit{Pr}^{-0.415} for 𝑃𝑟 ≫ 1 \mathit{Pr}\gg 1 . This framework quantifies the discrepancies in the conventional buoyancy treatment and clarifies their origin. Informed by this analysis, the buoyancy-related modelling terms are reformulated to recover the measured 𝑁𝑢 \mathit{Nu} – 𝑅𝑎 \mathit{Ra} – 𝑃𝑟 \mathit{Pr} trends. Only two dimensionless algebraic functions are introduced, which vanish in the absence of buoyancy, ensuring full compatibility with the standard closure. The corrected model is validated across a range of buoyancy-driven flows, including two-dimensional Rayleigh–Bénard convection, internally heated convection in two configurations, unstably stratified Couette flow, and vertically heated natural convection with varying aspect ratios. Across all cases, it provides highly accurate predictions.

Introduction

Buoyancy-driven turbulence is common in nature, spanning the oceans and atmosphere, and central to engineering, including indoor ventilation and nuclear safety. For such flows, Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) closures remain widely used in engineering applications owing to their favourable accuracy-to-cost ratio, with two-equation models such as the standard
k
–
\varepsilon
and
k
–
\omega
formulations being particularly common.

References and review works on buoyancy-related RANS modelling can be found in
37
;
47
;
48
;
25
;
8
;
34
;
14
;
22
;
15
;
23
.
In natural convection, temperature acts as an active scalar that modifies the momentum field through buoyancy. The exact transport equation for turbulent kinetic energy (
k
) contains an explicit buoyancy-production term proportional to the inner product between the turbulent heat flux and gravity; the term is negative in stable stratification and positive in unstable stratification. Because the modelled
k
equation in both
k
–
\varepsilon
and
k
–
\omega
frameworks is derived from this exact equation, it is standard to include buoyant production in
k
.

The central controversy concerns the scale-determining equation—namely, the dissipation (
\varepsilon
) or dissipation-per-unit-
k
(
\omega
) equation—which is usually the sole source of buoyancy-related modelling terms in two-equation RANS closures.
As summarised in reference works
(
8
;
22
)
and articles
(
26
;
44
;
24
;
23
)
, there is no universally accepted prescription for representing buoyancy at this level.
Broadly, two lines of work can be distinguished.
One omits any buoyant contribution from the scale-determining equation.
The rationale is that shear production there can be motivated by canonical shear-flow analyses, whereas an analogous justification for a buoyant counterpart in
\varepsilon
or
\omega
is lacking
(
38
)
.
The other line of work adds a buoyancy term to the scale-determining equation, modelling it by analogy with shear production and using the same functional form as the shear-production term in the
\varepsilon
- or
\omega
-equation.
As described by
24
, in flows such as Rayleigh–Bénard convection, where shear production is nearly zero but buoyant production dominates, omitting the buoyant-production term from the
\varepsilon
equation can render
\varepsilon
a pure sink; when combined with finite buoyant production in
k
, this may lead to unphysical growth of
k
.
A number of empirical blends have also been proposed for specific regimes
(
47
;
48
;
26
;
24
;
44
)
.
A frequently cited guideline is to suppress buoyant contributions under stable stratification while adding them under unstable stratification, using the same functional form as the shear-production term
(
48
)
.
Each of these studies explicitly acknowledged the empirical nature of their proposed treatments, and the proper representation of buoyancy in two-equation RANS closures remains an open issue.

The lack of a unified criterion arises from the absence of theoretical analyses that establish how standard two-equation closures behave in canonical, buoyancy-dominated flows. In contrast, in shear-driven canonical flows, the analytical behaviour of standard RANS models is well established: they reproduce classical results—the logarithmic velocity law with the von Kármán constant, boundary-layer scaling, spreading rates of free-shear flows, and self-similarity in homogeneous shear and decaying turbulence.
Such benchmarks have served both to justify the formulation of the models and to calibrate their coefficients
(
52
;
46
;
61
)
.

Specifically, to the author’s knowledge, analytical criteria are available for modelling stably stratified flows, whereas corresponding criteria for unstable stratification remain scarce.
In the stable regime, linear-stability analyses of stably stratified shear flows provide a clear physical measure of flow suppression, commonly formulated in terms of the flux Richardson number.
This quantity, defined locally as the ratio of buoyant to shear production, attains a critical value of
\mathit{Ri}_{f}=0.25
, beyond which stratification begins to stabilise the shear flow.
Owing to this well-established threshold,
\mathit{Ri}_{f}
has long served as a practical modelling reference
(
17
;
14
)
,
guiding the selection of model constants in
k
–
\varepsilon
closures
(
7
)

and informing explicit algebraic closures for stable stratification
(
35
)
.

By contrast, in unstably stratified buoyant flows, an appropriate model should account for the rate at which potential energy associated with density variations is converted into the kinetic energy of thermal plumes—a mechanism fundamentally different from shear production.
In homogeneous shear flow, the dynamics are primarily governed by the shear rate
S
,
k
, and
\varepsilon
, yielding the local similarity relation
\varepsilon/k\sim S
, which is widely accepted as a standard modelling benchmark
(
46
;
61
)
. For buoyant effects, however, evidence for such purely local self-similarity is scarce.
This scarcity is often cited as an indirect justification for omitting a buoyancy term from the scale-determining equation, as in
38
.
Instead, similarity in buoyant flows is typically expressed through global power-law relations among the Nusselt (
\mathit{Nu}
), Rayleigh (
\mathit{Ra}
), and Prandtl (
\mathit{Pr}
) numbers—for example, the relation
\mathit{Nu}\sim\mathit{Ra}^{0.31}
reported over
10^{6}\lesssim\mathit{Ra}\lesssim 10^{17}
for
\mathit{Pr}\approx 0.7

(
40
)
.
Because these relations depend on global temperature differences and a domain-scale length, the assessment of buoyancy effects in two-equation closures is more appropriately undertaken through domain-integrated analyses than through purely local arguments.

Against this background, the present study adopts one-dimensional Rayleigh–Bénard convection as a canonical test bed for analysing buoyancy effects in the
61

k
–
\omega
model. In this configuration, the mean flow vanishes and the turbulent kinetic energy budget is governed solely by buoyant production, thereby isolating the role of the buoyancy term in the
\omega
equation. This case corresponds to Rayleigh–Bénard convection in a horizontally periodic plane layer, for which direct numerical simulation (DNS) budgets clearly show buoyant production dominating the
k
balance
(
55
)
.

The choice of the
k
–
\omega
model is motivated by its near-wall formulation with a prescribed
\omega
distribution, which makes analytical treatment more tractable than in the
k
–
\varepsilon
model. In earlier work with a similar motivation
(
28
;
29
)
, the role of the buoyancy term in the
\varepsilon
equation is examined within the
k
–
\varepsilon
framework. However, in the standard
k
–
\varepsilon
model, the boundary conditions of the
\varepsilon
equation depend on the spatial gradients of
k
and
\varepsilon
, and as a result a unique steady-state solution is not strictly assured; in a closed Rayleigh–Bénard convection configuration, the predicted
k
and
\varepsilon

(
28
;
29
)
.

This difficulty does not arise in the
k
–
\omega

k
–
\omega

\omega

(
61
)
. Consequently, even in a Rayleigh–Bénard convection configuration, the steady-state
\omega

k
–
\omega
model is therefore one of the central objectives of the present study. Owing to the distinct mathematical structures of the two models, the analytical approach introduced here for the
k
–
\omega
model is almost entirely independent of the prior analysis for the
k
–
\varepsilon

The main contributions of this study are threefold:
(i) an analytical framework that clarifies how the scale-determining equation governs buoyancy-dominated turbulence and its simulation behaviour;
(ii) a closed-form analytical solution for one-dimensional Rayleigh–Bénard convection under the standard
k
–
\omega
closure, yielding explicit
\mathit{Nu}
–
\mathit{Ra}
–
\mathit{Pr}
scalings; and
(iii) corrections to the buoyancy-related model terms, intended to bring the RANS closure into closer agreement with established experimental trends across broad
\mathit{Ra}
and
\mathit{Pr}
ranges.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows.
§

presents the
k
–
\omega
model of
61

§
3
derives an analytical solution for one-dimensional Rayleigh–Bénard convection, yielding an explicit relation for
\mathit{Nu}

\mathit{Ra}
and
\mathit{Pr}
.
§
4
proposes a new buoyancy–correction methodology based on the established analytical solution.
The buoyancy term in the scale equation is modified, and a buoyancy-wall model is introduced into the turbulent thermal diffusivity.
The resulting corrected

§
5
further validates the proposed model beyond the one-dimensional Rayleigh–Bénard convection test case, through numerical simulations of two types of internally heated convection, unstably stratified Couette flow, Rayleigh–Bénard convection in a two-dimensional enclosure, and vertically heated natural convection with varying aspect ratios.
Finally, §

2.1
Turbulence closure model

This study considers incompressible flow in which buoyancy is represented by the Boussinesq approximation.
The Reynolds-averaged momentum and temperature transport equations are

\frac{\partial U_{i}}{\partial t}+U_{j}\frac{\partial U_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}=-\frac{\partial P}{\partial x_{i}}-g_{i}b(T-T_{0})+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\left(\nu\frac{\partial U_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}-\overline{u_{i}u_{j}}\right),

(1)

\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}+U_{i}\frac{\partial T}{\partial x_{i}}=Q+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\left(a\frac{\partial T}{\partial x_{i}}-\overline{\theta u_{i}}\right),

(2)

where
U_{i}
and
u_{i}
are the mean and fluctuating velocity components,
T
and
\theta
are the mean and fluctuating temperature,
x_{i}
denotes the Cartesian coordinates, and
t
is time.

P
is the mean kinematic pressure,
g_{i}
is the gravitational acceleration,
b
is the thermal expansion coefficient,
T_{0}
is a reference temperature,
\nu
is the kinematic viscosity,
Q
is the volumetric heat source per unit heat capacity, and
a
is the thermal diffusivity.
When a lower wall is present, the value of
T_{0}
merely shifts the hydrostatic reference state and does not affect the fluid motion; for simplicity,
T_{0}
is set to zero.
The overbar denotes Reynolds averaging, and
\overline{u_{i}u_{j}}
and
\overline{\theta u_{i}}
are the Reynolds stress tensor and the turbulent heat flux vector, respectively, modelled using the turbulent-viscosity and gradient-diffusion hypotheses as

\overline{u_{i}u_{j}}=\frac{2}{3}k\delta_{ij}-2\nu_{T}S_{ij},\qquad\overline{\theta u_{i}}=-a_{T}\frac{\partial T}{\partial x_{i}},

(3)

where

S_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial U_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial U_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}\right),\qquad a_{T}=\frac{\nu_{T}}{\mathit{Pr}_{T}}.

(4)

Here
S_{ij}
is the mean strain-rate tensor,
\nu_{T}
is the turbulent viscosity,
a_{T}
is the turbulent thermal diffusivity, and the turbulent Prandtl number is set to
\mathit{Pr}_{T}=0.89
.

This study analyses the
61

–
\omega

The notation for the model variables and constants follows that reference exactly, with only the buoyant terms added.
The full set of model equations, including the transport equations for

\nu_{T}=\frac{k}{\tilde{\omega}},\qquad\tilde{\omega}=\max\left\{\omega,C_{lim}\sqrt{\frac{2S_{ij}S_{ij}}{\beta^{\ast}}}\right\},

(5)

\frac{\partial k}{\partial t}+U_{j}\frac{\partial k}{\partial x_{j}}=\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{P}_{b}-\beta^{\ast}k\omega+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\left[\left(\nu+\sigma^{\ast}\frac{k}{\omega}\right)\frac{\partial k}{\partial x_{j}}\right],

(6)

\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial t}+U_{j}\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial x_{j}}=\alpha\frac{\omega}{k}\left(\mathcal{P}+C_{\omega b}\mathcal{P}_{b}\right)-\beta_{0}f_{\beta}\omega^{2}+\frac{\sigma_{d}}{\omega}\frac{\partial k}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\left[\left(\nu+\sigma\frac{k}{\omega}\right)\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial x_{j}}\right],

(7)

where the turbulent kinetic energy production terms due to the mean velocity gradient and buoyancy are

\mathcal{P}=-\overline{u_{i}u_{j}}\frac{\partial U_{i}}{\partial x_{j}},\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\mathcal{P}_{b}=-g_{i}b\overline{\theta u_{i}}.

(8)

The auxiliary relations are

\sigma_{d}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\frac{\partial k}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial x_{j}}\leq 0\\
\sigma_{do},&\frac{\partial k}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial x_{j}}>0,\end{array}\right.

(9)

f_{\beta}=\frac{1+85\chi_{\omega}}{1+100\chi_{\omega}},\qquad\chi_{\omega}=\left|\frac{\Omega_{ij}\Omega_{jk}S_{ki}}{(\beta^{\ast}\omega)^{3}}\right|

(10)

with

\Omega_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial U_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}-\frac{\partial U_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}\right).

(11)

The closure coefficients are

C_{lim}=0.875
,

\beta^{\ast}=0.09
,

\sigma^{\ast}=0.6
,

\alpha=0.52
,

\beta_{0}=0.0708
,

\sigma=0.5
, and

\sigma_{do}=0.125

In the
\omega
-equation, the model constant associated with buoyancy effects is
C_{\omega b}
.

At no slip wall, boundary conditions are

k=0,\qquad\omega=\frac{6\nu}{\beta_{0}n^{2}}

(12)

where, for a cell-centred discretisation of

3
Analytical solution in Rayleigh–Bénard convection

The aim of this analysis is to establish how the standard
k
–
\omega
model predicts the Nusselt number as a function of the Rayleigh and Prandtl numbers in a buoyancy-driven flow.
As mentioned in §
1
, a one-dimensional Rayleigh–Bénard configuration is selected as a testbed for the analysis, in which all mean quantities depend solely on the vertical coordinate
z
and the mean velocity is zero.
In this configuration, the turbulent kinetic energy budget is governed solely by buoyant production, thereby isolating the role of the buoyancy term in the
\omega

3.1
Distribution of
\omega

The distribution of
\omega
is first established.
Owing to the symmetry of the domain, only the half region
0\leq z\leq L/2
is considered.
For the analytical derivation, the governing equation is simplified uniformly under the condition
\nu\gg\nu_{T}
in the region
z\leq\lambda_{\nu}
, whereas in the region
z\geq\lambda_{\nu}
it is simplified under the opposite condition
\nu\ll\nu_{T}
. Under these assumptions, the resulting
\omega
distributions are continuous at the interface
z=\lambda_{\nu}
, although their spatial gradients are discontinuous.

Close to the wall (
z\leq\lambda_{\nu}
), the distribution of
\omega
is fixed by the boundary condition of the model
(
61
)
:

\omega=\frac{6\nu}{\beta_{0}z^{2}}.

(20)

This expression can also be obtained from the
\omega
-equation by balancing the sink term with the viscous diffusion term.

In the bulk region,
\lambda_{\nu}\leq z\leq L/2
, the distribution of
\omega
is determined under the approximation
\nu_{T}\gg\nu
.
In the
k
-equation for the bulk, it is assumed that the production of
k
is balanced with dissipation as
\mathcal{P}_{b}=\beta^{\ast}k\omega
.
The heat balance (Eq.
19
) gives

\mathcal{P}_{b}=gb\overline{\theta u_{z}}=-gba_{T}\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}z}=\frac{qbq}{1+a\mathit{Pr}_{T}/\nu_{T}},

(21)

and
\mathcal{P}_{b}=\beta^{\ast}k\omega
reduces to

\frac{qbq}{1+a\mathit{Pr}_{T}/\nu_{T}}=\beta^{\ast}k\omega.

(22)

Using
\nu_{T}=k/\omega
, this can be written as

k=\frac{gbq}{\beta^{\ast}\omega}-\mathit{Pr}_{T}a\omega.

(23)

Since production and dissipation balance in the
k
-equation but not in the
\omega
-equation, diffusion must be retained in the latter.
The
\omega
-equation in the bulk then becomes

0=\alpha\frac{\omega}{k}C_{\omega b}\frac{qbq}{1+a\mathit{Pr}_{T}/\nu_{T}}-\beta_{0}\omega^{2}+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}\left(\sigma\frac{k}{\omega}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{\mathrm{d}z}\right).

(24)

Substituting
k
from Eq. (
23
), this leads to

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}\left[\left(\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}-\frac{\beta^{\ast}\mathit{Pr}_{T}a}{gbq}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{\mathrm{d}z}\right]=\frac{\beta^{\ast}}{\sigma gbq}\left(\beta_{0}-\alpha\beta^{\ast}C_{\omega b}\right)\omega^{2}.

(25)

The value of
\omega
is largest at the wall and decreases toward the domain centre.
Hence the right-hand side must be positive, which imposes a constraint on
C_{\omega b}
:

C_{\omega b}<\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha\beta^{\ast}}=1.52\quad\text{for}\quad\mathcal{P}_{b}>0.

(26)

The
\omega
-equation can be written in non-dimensional form as

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\tau}{\mathrm{d}{z^{\ast}}^{2}}-K\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}{z^{\ast}}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\right)=-\frac{A}{\tau^{2}},

(27)

where

\tau=\frac{\nu}{L^{2}\omega},\qquad z^{\ast}=\frac{z}{L},

(28)

A=\frac{\beta^{\ast}}{\sigma}\left(\beta_{0}-\alpha\beta^{\ast}C_{\omega b}\right)\mathit{Pr}^{2}\mathit{Nu}^{-1}\mathit{Ra}^{-1},

(29)

K=\mathit{Pr}_{T}\beta^{\ast}\mathit{Nu}^{-1}\mathit{Ra}^{-1}\mathit{Pr}.

(30)

The constant
K
arises from the
a\mathit{Pr}_{T}/\nu_{T}
term in the bulk expression of
\mathcal{P}_{b}
(Eq.
21
).
In the asymptotic limit
z\gg\lambda_{a}
, where
q\approx\overline{\theta u_{z}}
, this contribution vanishes and hence
K\approx 0
.

The corresponding boundary conditions are

\tau=\tau_{\nu}=\frac{\beta_{0}}{6}\left(\frac{\lambda_{\nu}}{L}\right)^{2}\quad\mathrm{at}\quad z^{\ast}=\frac{\lambda_{\nu}}{L},

(31)

\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{\mathrm{d}z^{\ast}}=0\quad\mathrm{at}\quad z^{\ast}=\frac{1}{2}.

(32)

where
\tau_{\nu}
denotes the value of
\tau
at
z^{\ast}=\lambda_{\nu}/L
.

Equation (
27
) is then solved to obtain an analytical solution.
When
K=0
, the equation admits a simple closed-form solution, whereas for
K\neq 0
the expression becomes considerably more complex.
Since the aim of this study is to gain structural insight into the model through a tractable analytical formulation, the analysis is restricted to the case
K=0
.
From Eq. (
30
),
K\approx 0
is satisfied when
\mathit{Ra}
is sufficiently large and
\mathit{Pr}
is not excessive.

Multiplying the differential equation for
\tau
(Eq.
27
) by
\mathrm{d}\tau/\mathrm{d}z^{\ast}
and integrating under the boundary conditions gives

\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{\mathrm{d}z^{\ast}}\right)^{2}=2A\left(\frac{1}{\tau}-\frac{1}{\tau_{c}}\right),

(33)

where
\tau_{c}
denotes the value of
\tau
at the centre of the domain (
z^{\ast}=1/2
).

Integration of this relation gives

z^{\ast}=\frac{1}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{\tau_{c}^{3}}{2A}}\left[\arccos\left(\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{\tau_{c}}}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{\tau_{c}}\left(1-\frac{\tau}{\tau_{c}}\right)}\right],

(34)

which satisfies
z^{\ast}=1/2
at
\tau=\tau_{c}
.

The value of
\tau_{c}
follows from substituting
\tau=\tau_{\nu}
at
z^{\ast}=\lambda_{\nu}/L
.
When the boundary layer is thin,
\omega
is very large near the wall and much smaller in the core, so that
\tau_{\nu}/\tau_{c}\ll 1
.
Expanding the
\arccos
and square-root terms in a Taylor series then gives

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{\nu}}{L}=\frac{1}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{\tau_{c}^{3}}{2A}}\left[\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{2}{3}\left(\frac{\tau_{\nu}}{\tau_{c}}\right)^{3/2}+\cdots\right],

(35)

\displaystyle\tau_{c}=\left(\frac{2A}{\pi^{2}}\right)^{1/3}\left[1-\frac{4}{3}\frac{\lambda_{\nu}}{L}+\frac{4}{9}\sqrt{\frac{2}{A}}\tau_{\nu}^{3/2}+\cdots\right].

(36)

Thus,
\omega
attains its minimum value at
z=L/2
, given by
\nu L^{-2}\tau_{c}^{-1}
.

3.2
Distribution of
k

In the bulk region (
\lambda_{\nu}\leq z\leq L/2
), the distribution of
k
follows directly from Eq. (
23
) by substituting the previously obtained distribution of
\omega
(Eq.
34
).
In the near-wall region (
z\leq\lambda_{\nu}
), similar to the
\omega
distribution, the
k
distribution is determined by the intrinsic behaviour of the
k
–
\omega
model
(
61
)
.
The
k
-equation reduces to a balance between dissipation and diffusion:

\beta^{\ast}k\omega=\nu\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}k}{\mathrm{d}z^{2}},

(37)

and substitution of
\omega=6\nu/(\beta_{0}z^{2})
gives

\frac{6\beta^{\ast}}{\beta_{0}}\frac{k}{z^{2}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}k}{\mathrm{d}z^{2}}.

(38)

Assuming a power-law form
k\propto z^{p}
yields

p=\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{6\beta^{\ast}}{\beta_{0}}+\frac{1}{4}}\approx 3.307.

(39)

Thus, for
z\leq\lambda_{\nu}
, the near-wall distribution of
k
is approximated as

k=k_{\nu}\left(\frac{z}{\lambda_{\nu}}\right)^{p},

(40)

where
k_{\nu}
denotes the value of
k
at
z=\lambda_{\nu}
.

k_{\nu}
is obtained by enforcing continuity between the near-wall and bulk distributions at
z=\lambda_{\nu}
, which gives

k_{\nu}=\frac{gbq}{\beta^{\ast}}\frac{\beta_{0}\lambda_{\nu}^{2}}{6\nu}.

(41)

3.3
Boundary-layer thicknesses

By substituting the values of
k
and
\omega
for
z\leq\lambda_{\nu}
obtained from
Eqs. (
20
), (
39
), and (
41
), the turbulent viscosity
\nu_{T}=k/\omega
for
z\leq\lambda_{\nu}
is

\nu_{T}=\frac{gbq}{\beta^{\ast}}\left(\frac{6\nu}{\beta_{0}z^{2}}\right)^{-2}.

(42)

Applying the defined condition
\nu=\nu_{T}
at
z=\lambda_{\nu}
gives the estimate

\frac{\lambda_{\nu}}{L}=\left(\frac{36\beta^{\ast}}{\beta_{0}^{2}}\right)^{1/4}\mathit{Nu}^{-1/4}\mathit{Ra}^{-1/4}\mathit{Pr}^{1/2},

(43)

and hence

\tau_{\nu}={\beta^{\ast}}^{1/2}\mathit{Nu}^{-1/2}\mathit{Ra}^{-1/2}\mathit{Pr},

(44)

which represents the value of
\omega=\nu/(L^{2}\tau_{\nu})
at
z=\lambda_{\nu}
.

When
\mathit{Pr}\geq\mathit{Pr}_{T}
, and hence
\lambda_{a}\leq\lambda_{\nu}
, the thermal boundary-layer thickness is estimated from
a=a_{T}
at
z=\lambda_{a}
, using the near-wall distribution of
\nu_{T}/\nu
:

\frac{a_{T}}{a}=\frac{\mathit{Pr}}{\mathit{Pr}_{T}}\frac{\nu_{T}}{\nu}=\frac{\mathit{Pr}}{\mathit{Pr}_{T}}\left(\frac{\lambda_{a}}{\lambda_{\nu}}\right)^{p+2}=1,

(45)

where
p=3.307
.
It then follows that

\frac{\lambda_{a}}{L}=\left(\frac{36\beta^{\ast}}{\beta_{0}^{2}}\right)^{1/4}\mathit{Pr}_{T}^{1/(p+2)}\mathit{Nu}^{-1/4}\mathit{Ra}^{-1/4}\mathit{Pr}^{p/(2p+4)}.

(46)

On the other hand, when
\mathit{Pr}\leq\mathit{Pr}_{T}
, so that
\lambda_{a}\geq\lambda_{\nu}
,
\lambda_{a}
should be derived from the bulk distribution of
\omega
.
The condition
a=a_{T}
, with
\nu_{T}=k/\omega\approx gbq{\beta^{\ast}}^{-1}(L^{2}\tau_{a}/\nu)^{2}
, yields

\tau_{a}={\beta^{\ast}}^{1/2}\mathit{Pr}_{T}^{1/2}\mathit{Nu}^{-1/2}\mathit{Ra}^{-1/2}\mathit{Pr}^{1/2},

(47)

where
\tau_{a}
is the value of
\tau
at
z=\lambda_{a}
.
Assuming
\tau_{a}\ll\tau_{c}
, a Taylor expansion of the
\tau
–
z^{\ast}
relation (Eqs.
34
and
35
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{a}}{L}\approx\frac{1}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{\tau_{c}^{3}}{2A}}\left[\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{2}{3}\left(\frac{\tau_{a}}{\tau_{c}}\right)^{3/2}+\cdots\right],

(48)

and
\lambda_{a}
for
\mathit{Pr}\leq\mathit{Pr}_{T}
is obtained as

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{a}-\lambda_{\nu}}{L}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{\frac{\tau_{c}^{3}}{2A}}\left[\left(\frac{\tau_{a}}{\tau_{c}}\right)^{3/2}-\left(\frac{\tau_{\nu}}{\tau_{c}}\right)^{3/2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}\frac{\sigma^{1/2}}{\alpha^{1/2}{\beta^{\ast}}^{1/4}}\left(\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha\beta^{\ast}}-C_{\omega b}\right)^{-1/2}\left(\mathit{Pr}_{T}^{3/4}-\mathit{Pr}^{3/4}\right)\mathit{Nu}^{-1/4}\mathit{Ra}^{-1/4}\mathit{Pr}^{-1/4}.

By substituting the expressions for the boundary-layer thicknesses derived above, the distributions of
\omega
and
k
can be expressed as functions of
\mathit{Ra}
,
\mathit{Pr}
, and
\mathit{Nu}
.

3.4
Temperature profile

The temperature profile is analysed separately in the near-wall region (
z\leq\lambda_{\nu}
) and in the bulk region (
\lambda_{\nu}\leq z\leq L/2
), providing the basis for establishing the relation among
\mathit{Nu}
,
\mathit{Ra}
, and
\mathit{Pr}
discussed in §
3.5
. The associated heat balance is governed by the thermal boundary-layer thickness
\lambda_{a}
. For
z>\lambda_{a}
, the heat flux can be approximated by the turbulent relation
q\approx\overline{\theta u_{z}}
, whereas for
z<\lambda_{a}
the effect of molecular thermal diffusivity
a
must also be taken into account.

3.4.1
Near-wall contribution

The near-wall temperature rise across the kinetic boundary-layer thickness,
\Delta_{BL}
, is first evaluated as

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta_{BL}}{\Delta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\Delta}\int_{0}^{\lambda_{\nu}}\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}z}\mathrm{d}z=\mathit{Nu}\left(\frac{\lambda_{\nu}}{L}\right)\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{1+a_{T}/a}\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}

(50)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{36\beta^{\ast}}{\beta_{0}^{2}}\right)^{1/4}\mathit{Nu}^{3/4}\mathit{Ra}^{-1/4}\mathit{Pr}^{1/2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{1+(\mathit{Pr}/\mathit{Pr}_{T}){z^{\prime}}^{p^{\prime}}}\mathrm{d}z^{\prime},

where
p^{\prime}=p+2=5.307
,
a_{T}/a=(\mathit{Pr}/\mathit{Pr}_{T})(z/\lambda_{\nu})^{p+2}
(Eqs.
4
and
42
), and
z^{\prime}=z/\lambda_{\nu}
.
The integral in the second line approaches unity when
\mathit{Pr}/\mathit{Pr}_{T}\ll 1
.

For
\mathit{Pr}/\mathit{Pr}_{T}\gg 1
, the asymptotic value of the integral can be obtained by applying the variable transformation
\eta=(\mathit{Pr}/\mathit{Pr}_{T}){z^{\prime}}^{p^{\prime}}
, yielding
(
4
)
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{1+(\mathit{Pr}/\mathit{Pr}_{T}){z^{\prime}}^{p^{\prime}}}\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\mathit{Pr}}{\mathit{Pr}_{T}}\right)^{-1/p^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\eta^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}-1}}{1+\eta}\mathrm{d}\eta

(51)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{p^{\prime}\sin(\pi/p^{\prime})}\left(\frac{\mathit{Pr}}{\mathit{Pr}_{T}}\right)^{-1/p^{\prime}}.

On the basis of the asymptotic behaviour described above, the integral can be approximated by a simple algebraic function,

\left[1+0.7535(\mathit{Pr}/\mathit{Pr}_{T})^{0.9338}\right]^{-1/(0.9338p^{\prime})},

which satisfies the asymptotic limits for both
\mathit{Pr}/\mathit{Pr}_{T}\ll 1
and
\mathit{Pr}/\mathit{Pr}_{T}\gg 1
.
This approximation yields a maximum relative error of 0.2% over the range
10^{-4}\leq(\mathit{Pr}/\mathit{Pr}_{T})\leq 10^{4}
.

Combining the above results, the near-wall temperature rise is expressed as

\frac{\Delta_{BL}}{\Delta}=4.506\mathit{Nu}^{3/4}\mathit{Ra}^{-1/4}\mathit{Pr}^{1/2}\left[1+0.7535\left(\frac{\mathit{Pr}}{\mathit{Pr}_{T}}\right)^{0.9338}\right]^{-1/(0.9338p^{\prime})}.

(52)

3.4.2
Bulk contribution for
\mathit{Pr}\geq\mathit{Pr}_{T}

The bulk contribution,
\Delta_{bulk}
, is now considered.
For
\mathit{Pr}\geq\mathit{Pr}_{T}
, the approximation
\overline{\theta u_{z}}\approx q
holds throughout the bulk region, where
z\geq\lambda_{\nu}\geq\lambda_{a}
.
From the heat balance equation, the temperature gradient is expressed as

\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}z}\approx-\frac{q}{\nu_{T}/\mathit{Pr}_{T}}=-\frac{\Delta}{L}\beta^{\ast}\mathit{Pr}_{T}\mathit{Ra}^{-1}\mathit{Pr}\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}.

(53)

Integration between
z=\lambda_{\nu}
and
z=L/2
then gives the bulk temperature rise:

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta_{bulk}}{\Delta}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta}\int_{\lambda_{\nu}}^{L/2}\frac{q}{\nu_{T}/\mathit{Pr}_{T}}\mathrm{d}z=\beta^{\ast}\mathit{Pr}_{T}\mathit{Ra}^{-1}\mathit{Pr}\int_{\lambda_{\nu}/L}^{1/2}\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\mathrm{d}z^{\ast}

(54)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta^{\ast}\mathit{Pr}_{T}\mathit{Ra}^{-1}\mathit{Pr}\int_{\tau_{\nu}}^{\tau_{c}}\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\left[2A\left(\frac{1}{\tau}-\frac{1}{\tau_{c}}\right)\right]^{-1/2}\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2}\sigma^{1/2}{\beta^{\ast}}^{1/4}}{\left(\beta_{0}-\alpha\beta^{\ast}C_{\omega b}\right)^{1/2}}\left(1-\frac{\tau_{\nu}}{\tau_{c}}\right)^{1/2}\mathit{Nu}^{3/4}\mathit{Ra}^{-1/4}\mathit{Pr}^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 2.532\left(\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha\beta^{\ast}}-C_{\omega b}\right)^{-1/2}\mathit{Pr}_{T}\mathit{Nu}^{3/4}\mathit{Ra}^{-1/4}\mathit{Pr}^{-1/2}.

The final expression is obtained by applying the assumption
\tau_{\nu}/\tau_{c}\ll 1
.

3.4.3
Bulk contribution for
\mathit{Pr}\leq\mathit{Pr}_{T}

For
\mathit{Pr}\leq\mathit{Pr}_{T}
, the ordering
\lambda_{\nu}\leq\lambda_{a}
holds.
In the region
\lambda_{\nu}\leq z\leq\lambda_{a}
, the total thermal diffusivity
a+\nu_{T}/\mathit{Pr}_{T}
is less than
2a
, so that the approximation
\nu_{T}/\mathit{Pr}_{T}\approx 0
is adopted, whereas for
z>\lambda_{a}
it is assumed that
\nu_{T}/\mathit{Pr}_{T}\gg a
.
This approximation modifies only the prefactor of the power law while leaving the exponent unchanged, thereby justifying its use in deriving analytical scaling laws.

The bulk temperature rise is then expressed as

\frac{\Delta_{bulk}}{\Delta}\approx\frac{1}{\Delta}\int_{\lambda_{\nu}}^{\lambda_{a}}\frac{q}{a}\mathrm{d}z+\frac{1}{\Delta}\int_{\lambda_{a}}^{L/2}\frac{q}{\nu_{T}/\mathit{Pr}_{T}}\mathrm{d}z.

(55)

Using the expressions for
\tau_{a}
and
\lambda_{a}
in Eqs. (
47
) and (
3.3
), it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta}\int_{\lambda_{\nu}}^{\lambda_{a}}\frac{q}{a}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathit{Nu}\left(\frac{\lambda_{a}-\lambda_{\nu}}{L}\right)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}\frac{\sigma^{1/2}}{\alpha^{1/2}{\beta^{\ast}}^{1/4}}\left(\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha\beta^{\ast}}-C_{\omega b}\right)^{-1/2}\left(\mathit{Pr}_{T}^{3/4}-\mathit{Pr}^{3/4}\right)\mathit{Nu}^{3/4}\mathit{Ra}^{-1/4}\mathit{Pr}^{-1/4},

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta}\int_{\lambda_{a}}^{L/2}\frac{q}{\nu_{T}/\mathit{Pr}_{T}}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta^{\ast}\mathit{Pr}_{T}\mathit{Ra}^{-1}\mathit{Pr}\left(\frac{2}{A\tau_{a}}\right)^{1/2}\left(1-\frac{\tau_{a}}{\tau_{c}}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2}\sigma^{1/2}}{{\beta^{\ast}}^{1/4}\alpha^{1/2}}\mathit{Pr}_{T}^{3/4}\left(\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha\beta^{\ast}}-C_{\omega b}\right)^{-1/2}\mathit{Nu}^{3/4}\mathit{Ra}^{-1/4}\mathit{Pr}^{-1/4}.

By summing the two contributions obtained above, the total bulk temperature rise becomes

\frac{\Delta_{bulk}}{\Delta}\approx 2.532\left(\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha\beta^{\ast}}-C_{\omega b}\right)^{-1/2}\left(\frac{4}{3}\mathit{Pr}_{T}^{3/4}-\frac{1}{3}\mathit{Pr}^{3/4}\right)\mathit{Nu}^{3/4}\mathit{Ra}^{-1/4}\mathit{Pr}^{-1/4}.

(58)

3.5
Dependence of
\mathit{Nu}
on
\mathit{Ra}
and
\mathit{Pr}

The total temperature rise across the half-domain satisfies
\Delta/2=\Delta_{BL}+\Delta_{bulk}
.
By combining the near-wall and bulk contributions obtained in Eqs. (
52
), (
54
), and (
58
),
the resulting relation can be expressed as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 4.506\mathit{Nu}^{3/4}\mathit{Ra}^{-1/4}\mathit{Pr}^{1/2}\left[1+0.7535\left(\frac{\mathit{Pr}}{\mathit{Pr}_{T}}\right)^{0.9338}\right]^{-1/(0.9338p^{\prime})}

(59)

\displaystyle\mbox{}+2.532{C_{\omega b}^{\prime}}^{-1/2}\mathit{Nu}^{3/4}\mathit{Ra}^{-1/4}\mathit{Pr}_{T}^{1/2}f\left(\frac{\mathit{Pr}}{\mathit{Pr}_{T}}\right),

with

C_{\omega b}^{\prime}=\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha\beta^{\ast}}-C_{\omega b}>0,

(60)

and

f(s)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{4}{3}s^{-1/4}-\frac{1}{3}s^{1/2},&0<s<1\\[2.0pt]
s^{-1/2},&s\geq 1.\end{array}\right.

(61)

The bridging function
f(s)
is continuous and differentiable at
s=1
, ensuring a smooth crossover between the regimes.

From Eq. (
59
), the asymptotic dependence of
\mathit{Nu}
on
\mathit{Ra}
and
\mathit{Pr}
is

\mathit{Nu}\sim\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathit{Ra}^{1/3}\mathit{Pr}^{1/3},&\mathit{Pr}\ll 1\\[2.0pt]
\mathit{Ra}^{1/3}\mathit{Pr}^{-2/3+4/(3p^{\prime})},&\mathit{Pr}\gg 1.\end{array}\right.

(62)

Here, for
\mathit{Pr}\gg 1
, the exponent of
\mathit{Pr}
evaluates to
-0.4154
, with
p^{\prime}=5.307
.

The resulting
\mathit{Nu}
dependence on
\mathit{Ra}
and
\mathit{Pr}
predicted by the standard model exhibits a quantitative deviation from experimental observations.
A detailed comparison with experiments, along with a new correction proposed to address this discrepancy, is presented in §
4
.

4
Formulation and calibration of buoyant corrections

The analytical relation for
\mathit{Nu}
(Eq.
59
) depends not only on the control parameters
\mathit{Ra}
and
\mathit{Pr}
, but also on the model constants.
If
\mathit{Pr}_{T}
and
C_{\omega b}
are treated as fixed, the model cannot alter the asymptotic
\mathit{Nu}
–
\mathit{Ra}
–
\mathit{Pr}
relations given by Eq. (
62
).
To enable calibration, these quantities must therefore be expressed as dimensionless functions, analogous to the auxiliary functions employed in the standard
k
–
\omega
formulations.

The calibration draws upon complementary datasets that collectively span wide ranges of
\mathit{Ra}
and
\mathit{Pr}
.
The experiments of
40
established
\mathit{Nu}\sim\mathit{Ra}^{0.31}
over
10^{6}\leq\mathit{Ra}\leq 10^{17}
for
\mathit{Pr}\approx 0.7
, providing a benchmark
\mathit{Ra}
-scaling.
Three-dimensional DNS studies revealed
\mathit{Pr}
dependences of
\mathit{Nu}\sim\mathit{Pr}^{0.14}
at
\mathit{Ra}=6\times 10^{5}
for
0.022\leq\mathit{Pr}\leq 0.7

(
58
)
, and
\mathit{Nu}\sim\mathit{Pr}^{0.18}
at
\mathit{Ra}=10^{6}
for
0.001\leq\mathit{Pr}\leq 1

(

)
.
At higher
\mathit{Pr}
, the simulations of
36

\mathit{Pr}
-independent behaviour,
\mathit{Nu}\sim\mathit{Pr}^{-0.02}
, for
11.7\leq\mathit{Pr}\leq 650.7
and
6.0\times 10^{8}\leq\mathit{Ra}\leq 3.0\times 10^{10}
.
These findings are broadly consistent with the theoretical prediction of
21
, which yields
\mathit{Nu}\sim\mathit{Pr}^{1/8}
for
\mathit{Pr}\ll 1
and
\mathit{Nu}\sim\mathit{Pr}^{0}
for
\mathit{Pr}\gg 1

Together, these datasets delineate the relevant parameter space for calibration.

From a strict standpoint, these scaling laws depend on the ranges of
\mathit{Ra}
and
\mathit{Pr}
, as well as on geometry, aspect ratio, wall roughness, and internal structure, and therefore cannot be represented by a single universal scaling law.
Within the Reynolds-averaged framework, however, it is neither practical nor necessary to account for such dependencies explicitly.
In engineering applications, where RANS models are primarily employed, the main objective is to reproduce the correct mean Nusselt number rather than the detailed flow organisation.
As noted by
1
, parameters such as the aspect ratio and large-scale circulation exert only a weak influence on the mean
\mathit{Nu}
.
Hence, the adopted scaling laws are considered sufficient for calibration over the practically relevant range
10^{-3}\leq\mathit{Pr}\leq 10^{3}
and
\mathit{Ra}\leq 10^{10}
.

The standard model exhibits its largest discrepancy from experiments in the
\mathit{Pr}>O(1)
regime, where the predicted
\mathit{Nu}\sim\mathit{Pr}^{-0.4154}
at fixed
\mathit{Ra}
contrasts with the nearly
\mathit{Pr}
-independent behaviour observed.
For
\mathit{Pr}<O(1)
, the prediction
\mathit{Nu}\sim\mathit{Pr}^{1/3}
moderately departs from the experimental scaling
\mathit{Nu}\sim\mathit{Pr}^{0.14}
to
\mathit{Pr}^{0.18}
observed in experiments.
In terms of
\mathit{Ra}
scaling, the observed
\mathit{Nu}\sim\mathit{Ra}^{0.31}
is marginally lower than the model prediction
\mathit{Nu}\sim\mathit{Ra}^{1/3}

Overall, the standard model exaggerates the
\mathit{Pr}
-dependence, producing exponents of larger magnitude in both limits, which motivates the introduction of the present buoyant corrections.

4.1
Definition of the correction parameters

The first step is to identify the parameters available for constructing the correction term.
In the present formulation, the locally definable independent variables (Galilean invariants) reduce to six:

k,\quad\omega,\quad\nu,\quad a,\quad gb,\quad\frac{dT}{dz},

where
g
and
b
appear as a product because gravity acts only through buoyancy.

These six dimensional variables, expressed in terms of temperature, time, and length scales, can be reduced to three nondimensional parameters:

\mathit{Re}_{T}=\frac{\nu_{T}}{\nu},\quad\mathit{Pr},\quad\frac{\mathcal{P}_{b}}{\varepsilon},

(63)

where
\mathit{Re}_{T}
is the turbulent Reynolds number, defined in the same manner as in
61
, and the final term represents the ratio of buoyant production to the dissipation rate of
k
, with
\varepsilon=\beta^{\ast}k\omega
.

In the near-wall region (
z\leq\lambda_{\nu}
), the analytical distributions of
\mathit{Re}_{T}
and
\mathcal{P}_{b}/\varepsilon
are

\mathit{Re}_{T}=\left(\frac{z}{\lambda_{\nu}}\right)^{p+2},

(64)

\frac{\mathcal{P}_{b}}{\varepsilon}=\left(\frac{z}{\lambda_{\nu}}\right)^{2-p}\frac{a_{T}/a}{1+a_{T}/a}=\frac{\mathit{Pr}}{\mathit{Pr}_{T}}\left(\frac{z}{\lambda_{\nu}}\right)^{4}\left[1+\frac{\mathit{Pr}}{\mathit{Pr}_{T}}\left(\frac{z}{\lambda_{\nu}}\right)^{p+2}\right]^{-1},

(65)

with
p=3.307
.
For
\mathit{Pr}>\mathit{Pr}_{T}
,
\mathcal{P}_{b}/\varepsilon
attains a maximum proportional to
(\mathit{Pr}/\mathit{Pr}_{T})^{(p-2)/(p+2)}
, located at
z<\lambda_{\nu}
.

The turbulent Reynolds number reaches its maximum at
z=L/2
:

\displaystyle\mathit{Re}_{T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{gbq}{\beta^{\ast}\nu}\left(\frac{L^{2}\tau_{c}}{\nu}\right)^{2}

(66)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta^{\ast}}\left[\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}\frac{\beta^{\ast}}{\sigma}\left(\beta_{0}-\alpha\beta^{\ast}C_{\omega b}\right)\right]^{2/3}\mathit{Ra}^{1/3}\mathit{Nu}^{1/3}\mathit{Pr}^{-2/3}.

Under the present one-dimensional Rayleigh–Bénard configuration, the bulk value of
\mathcal{P}_{b}/\varepsilon
is nearly uniform and close to unity.
In contrast, when shear production is present, the dissipation
\varepsilon
balances the total production
(\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{P}_{b})
, so
\mathcal{P}_{b}/\varepsilon
need not remain equal to unity.

In summary, any nondimensional buoyant correction function can be expressed in terms of the three parameters defined here, with its spatial variation inferred
a priori
from the present analytical framework.

4.2
Correction of
\mathit{Pr}
dependence

The standard model shows its largest discrepancy from experiments in the
\mathit{Nu}
–
\mathit{Pr}
relation at fixed
\mathit{Ra}
, since the
\mathit{Pr}
dependence of buoyancy effects has not been explicitly addressed in previous formulations.
This subsection corrects that dependence within the analytical framework established in §
3
.

Including the model constants associated with buoyant thermal convection, the asymptotic form of Eq. (
59
) becomes

\mathit{Nu}\sim\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathit{Ra}^{1/3}\left(\mathit{Pr}^{1/2}+{C_{\omega b}^{\prime}}^{-1/2}\mathit{Pr}_{T}^{3/4}\mathit{Pr}^{-1/4}\right)^{-4/3},&\mathit{Pr}\ll\mathit{Pr}_{T}\\
\mathit{Ra}^{1/3}\left(\mathit{Pr}_{T}^{0.1884}\mathit{Pr}^{0.3116}+{C_{\omega b}^{\prime}}^{-1/2}\mathit{Pr}_{T}\mathit{Pr}^{-1/2}\right)^{-4/3},&\mathit{Pr}\gg\mathit{Pr}_{T}.\end{array}\right.

(67)

Equation (
67
) indicates that the
\mathit{Pr}
dependence of
\mathit{Nu}
can be controlled through
C_{\omega b}^{\prime}
and
\mathit{Pr}_{T}
.
The terms
{C_{\omega b}^{\prime}}^{-1/2}\mathit{Pr}_{T}^{3/4}\mathit{Pr}^{-1/4}
and
\mathit{Pr}_{T}^{0.1884}\mathit{Pr}^{0.3116}
dominate in the low- and high-
\mathit{Pr}
limits, respectively, corresponding to the contributions of
\Delta_{bulk}
and
\Delta_{BL}
identified in §
3.5
.
Accordingly, two complementary corrections naturally emerge: a bulk correction to
C_{\omega b}^{\prime}
for
\mathit{Pr}\ll 1
, and a near-wall modification of
a_{T}
for
\mathit{Pr}\gg 1
.

Given that the uncorrected model predicts
\mathit{Nu}\sim\mathit{Pr}^{1/3}
in the low-
\mathit{Pr}
regime, adopting a smaller asymptotic scaling exponent is effective for reproducing the observed behaviour
\mathit{Nu}\sim\mathit{Pr}^{0.14}
to
\mathit{Pr}^{0.18}
over
0.001\leq\mathit{Pr}\leq 1
. Motivated by this consideration, the model targets the theoretical scaling
\mathit{Nu}\sim\mathit{Pr}^{1/8}
for
\mathit{Pr}\ll 1
proposed by
21
. To meet this target, the model must satisfy

\left({C_{\omega b}^{\prime}}^{-1/2}\mathit{Pr}_{T}^{3/4}\mathit{Pr}^{-1/4}\right)^{-4/3}\sim\mathit{Pr}^{1/8},

(68)

then,

C_{\omega b}^{\prime}\sim\mathit{Pr}^{-5/16}\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\mathit{Pr}\ll 1.

(69)

Here, the standard value
\mathit{Pr}_{T}=0.89
is retained to avoid introducing unnecessary modifications to the model.

The correction to
C_{\omega b}^{\prime}
is applied uniformly throughout the domain and is expressed as

C_{\omega b}^{\prime}=c_{1}+c_{2}\mathit{Pr}^{-5/16}.

(70)

Here, the coefficients
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
determine both the Prandtl number at which the model enters the low-
\mathit{Pr}
regime and the corresponding level of
\mathit{Nu}
in that regime.

To recover
\mathit{Nu}\sim\mathit{Pr}^{0}
for
\mathit{Pr}\gg 1

\Delta_{BL}/\Delta\sim\mathit{Pr}^{0}
, the near-wall integral term in Eq. (
50

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{1+a_{T}/a}\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}\sim\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\mathit{Pr}\ll 1\\[2.0pt]
\mathit{Pr}^{-1/2},&\mathit{Pr}\gg 1,\end{array}\right.

(71)

where
z^{\prime}=z/\lambda_{\nu}
. The modelling objective is thus to design the near-wall
a_{T}
distribution so that this integral reproduces the target scaling.
Following the approach in §
3.4.1
, the wall integral is approximated as

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{1+a_{T}/a}\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}\approx\left(1+d_{1}\mathit{Pr}^{d_{2}/2}\right)^{-1/d_{2}},

(72)

Analogously to

and

(iv)

(74)

The corresponding near-wall distribution of

4.3
C_{\omega b}
for stable stratification

After calibrating
C_{\omega b}
for unstable stratification (
\mathcal{P}_{b}>0
), the analysis is extended to the stably stratified regime (
\mathcal{P}_{b}<0
) to complete the formulation of the
\omega
equation.
As summarised in §
2.2
,
48
recommended the empirical setting
C_{\varepsilon b}=0
for
\mathcal{P}_{b}<0
, an approach that has been widely adopted.
Through the correspondence between the
k
–
\varepsilon
and
k
–
\omega
formulations (Eq.
18
), this prescription corresponds to
C_{\omega b}\approx-2
.
As an alternative to empirical calibration, the present study examines an analytical procedure for determining
C_{\omega b}
for
\mathcal{P}_{b}<0
.

This analytical approach is guided by the canonical case of homogeneous stably stratified shear flow, which provides a well-defined stability threshold for model consistency.
As discussed in §
1
, models for
\mathcal{P}_{b}<0
are typically constrained by the behaviour of stably stratified shear flows, where linear stability analyses predict flow stabilization once the flux Richardson number
\mathit{Ri}_{f}=-\mathcal{P}_{b}/\mathcal{P}
exceeds approximately
0.25

(
17
;
14
)
.
Following this benchmark,
7
;
8
analysed the
k
–
\varepsilon
model from a dynamical-systems perspective to determine the appropriate form of
C_{\varepsilon b}
for stably stratified shear flows.
This framework treats the
k
–
\varepsilon
model as a set of ordinary differential equations for
k(t)
and
\varepsilon(t)
, whose trajectories in the
(k,\varepsilon)
phase space are analysed to determine the stabilization conditions.
For further discussions on dynamical-systems analyses of RANS models, see
45
;
49
;
50
.

In the same framework, the appropriate
C_{\omega b}
for
\mathcal{P}_{b}<0
can be determined analytically.
The idealized configuration corresponds to a homogeneous stably stratified shear flow in which
k
and
\omega
vary only with time.
Under these conditions, the production terms become

\mathcal{P}=\nu_{T}S^{2}=S^{2}\frac{k}{\omega},\qquad\mathcal{P}_{b}=-g\beta\frac{\nu_{T}}{\mathit{Pr}_{T}}\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}z}=-\left(\frac{g\beta}{\mathit{Pr}_{T}}\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}z}\right)\frac{k}{\omega}=-\mathit{Ri}_{f}\mathcal{P},

where
S^{2}=S_{ij}S_{ij}
and
\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}z>0
, so that
\mathcal{P}>0
and
\mathcal{P}_{b}<0
with constant
S
and
\mathit{Ri}_{f}>0
.

Under this configuration, the
k
–
\omega
equations reduce to

\frac{\mathrm{d}k}{\mathrm{d}t}=k\!\left[\frac{(1-\mathit{Ri}_{f})S^{2}}{\omega}-\beta^{\ast}\omega\right],

(78)

\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{\mathrm{d}t}=\alpha S^{2}(1-\mathit{Ri}_{f}C_{\omega b})-\beta_{0}\omega^{2}.

(79)

Because
\omega
evolves independently of
k
, its temporal behaviour is examined first.
By definition,
\omega(t)
must remain positive for any
\mathit{Ri}_{f}
, implying that the source term must stay non-negative even in the limit of large
\mathit{Ri}_{f}
.
This constraint implies

C_{\omega b}\leq 0\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\mathcal{P}_{b}<0,

(80)

as a positive
C_{\omega b}
would drive
\mathrm{d}\omega/\mathrm{d}t<0
near
\omega\to 0
, yielding unphysical negative values of
\omega
.

For
C_{\omega b}\leq 0
,
\omega
asymptotically approaches a steady state (
t\to\infty
) given by

\omega_{\infty}=\left[\frac{\alpha S^{2}}{\beta_{0}}(1-\mathit{Ri}_{f}C_{\omega b})\right]^{1/2},

(81)

which is readily seen to be a stable equilibrium, as
\mathrm{d}\omega/\mathrm{d}t<0
for
\omega>\omega_{\infty}
and
\mathrm{d}\omega/\mathrm{d}t>0
for
\omega<\omega_{\infty}
.

Substituting
\omega_{\infty}
into the
k
equation gives

\frac{\mathrm{d}k}{\mathrm{d}t}=k\!\left[\frac{(1-\mathit{Ri}_{f})S^{2}}{\omega_{\infty}}-\beta^{\ast}\omega_{\infty}\right].

(82)

For the turbulent kinetic energy to decay with time, the right-hand side must be negative:

\frac{(1-\mathit{Ri}_{f})S^{2}}{\omega_{\infty}}-\beta^{\ast}\omega_{\infty}<0.

Rearranging yields the stability condition

\mathit{Ri}_{f}>\left(1-\frac{\alpha\beta^{\ast}}{\beta_{0}}\right)\!\left(1-\frac{\alpha\beta^{\ast}}{\beta_{0}}C_{\omega b}\right)^{-1}.

(83)

Hence, the value of
C_{\omega b}
that reproduces the conventional stabilization threshold of
\mathit{Ri}_{f}\geq 0.25
is

C_{\omega b}=\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha\beta^{\ast}}-\frac{1}{0.25}\left(\frac{\beta_{0}}{\alpha\beta^{\ast}}-1\right)=-0.5385\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\mathcal{P}_{b}<0.

(84)

By comparison, the earlier empirical recommendation
C_{\omega b}\approx-2
would predict stabilization at
\mathit{Ri}_{f}\geq 0.146
, lower than the theoretical benchmark.

Combining Eqs. (
76
) and (
84
) and substituting them into (
7
) gives the complete
\omega
equation:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial t}+U_{j}\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial x_{j}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha\frac{\omega}{k}\left[\mathcal{P}+C_{\omega b}^{+}\max(\mathcal{P}_{b},0)+C_{\omega b}^{-}\min(\mathcal{P}_{b},0)\right]

(85)

\displaystyle{}-\beta_{0}f_{\beta}\omega^{2}+\frac{\sigma_{d}}{\omega}\frac{\partial k}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\!\left[\!\left(\nu+\sigma\frac{k}{\omega}\right)\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial x_{j}}\!\right],

where the buoyancy coefficients are

C_{\omega b}^{+}=-0.9752-0.2988\mathit{Pr}^{-5/16},\qquad C_{\omega b}^{-}=-0.5385.

(86)

As a further quantitative validation, a stably stratified plane channel flow is considered.
The upper hot wall (
T=\Delta
at
z=h
) and the lower cold wall (
T=0
at
z=-h
) are both isothermal and no-slip.
Gravity acts in the vertically downward (
-z
) direction, while the flow is driven horizontally.
The dimensionless control parameters are the friction Reynolds number,
\mathit{Re}_{\tau}=U_{\tau}h/\nu
, where
U_{\tau}
(

\omega

\mathit{Re}_{b}=1.03\times 10^{4}

5
Model evaluation

This section assesses the performance of the buoyancy-corrected
k
–
\omega
model across a range of buoyancy-driven flows, extending beyond the one-dimensional Rayleigh–Bénard configuration.
The corrected formulation preserves the structure of the standard model and differs only through the buoyancy-related modifications introduced in Eqs. (
77
,
85
,
86
).
For evaluating the uncorrected standard model, the conventional choices
C_{\omega b}=1
for
\mathcal{P}_{b}>0
and
C_{\omega b}=-2
for
\mathcal{P}_{b}<0
are adopted.
The simulation procedure is identical to that described in §
3.6
, and steady solutions are obtained by neglecting the temporal derivatives and iterating until convergence.

Previous RANS studies of buoyancy-driven turbulence have largely focused on a limited set of canonical configurations.
Vertically heated natural convection in enclosed cavities has been the most widely explored, typically within the ranges
\mathit{Pr}=0.7
–
7
and
10^{8}\leq\mathit{Ra}\leq 10^{15}

(
27
;
26
;
44
;
13
;
30
;
11
)
.
Rayleigh–Bénard convection has also been examined, although generally for
\mathit{Ra}\leq 10^{9}
at fixed
\mathit{Pr}=0.7

(
31
;
9
)
.
Vertical natural convection in channels has received comparatively less attention and is usually restricted to
\mathit{Ra}=10^{5}
–
10^{7}
at
\mathit{Pr}=0.7

(
12
;
11
)
.

Despite spanning several orders of magnitude in Rayleigh number, these investigations cover only a small number of geometries and share common limitations.
Systematic evaluation of Prandtl-number effects is rare, and many buoyancy-correction approaches rely on ad hoc functional forms whose validity has been demonstrated only within restricted flow regimes.
As a result, the broader applicability of these corrections remains uncertain.

The present study examines a wider variety of buoyancy-driven flows:
(i) two internally heated convection configurations;
(ii) unstably stratified Couette flow (mixed convection);
(iii) Rayleigh–Bénard convection in a two-dimensional square cavity; and
(iv) vertically heated natural convection in two-dimensional rectangular cavities with aspect ratios of 1 and 4.

Vertical channel flow is not included.
Although buoyancy formally drives the flow in this configuration, the imposed temperature gradient is strictly perpendicular to gravity.
Under the gradient-diffusion hypothesis used for turbulent heat-flux modelling, this alignment yields
\mathcal{P}_{b}=0
throughout the domain, and the buoyancy-correction terms introduced here therefore have no effect.

6
Conclusions

This study establishes an analytical framework for representing buoyancy effects in the standard
k
–
\omega
model, addressing the long-standing lack of a consistent formulation for two-equation closures.

To that end, an explicit analytical solution of the
k
–
\omega
model in one-dimensional Rayleigh–Bénard convection is derived.
This solution reveals how the conventional buoyancy modelling determines the heat-transport scaling in natural convection and clarifies the origin of the model’s deviations from established
\mathit{Nu}
–
\mathit{Ra}
–
\mathit{Pr}
trends.
Guided by this analysis, the closed-form correction functions emerge naturally rather than from empirical tuning, allowing the model to recover the observed scaling behaviour.
Only two algebraic, dimensionless functions are introduced, and the formulation reduces to the original model in the absence of buoyancy, ensuring seamless compatibility and negligible computational overhead.
The associated modelling coefficients are calibrated through a transparent and reproducible process using literature DNS and experimental datasets.

The corrected model is evaluated across a wide range of buoyancy-driven configurations—including Rayleigh–Bénard convection in one- and two-dimensional settings, two internally heated convection systems, unstably stratified Couette flow, and vertically heated natural convection with varying aspect ratios.
Across all cases, the corrected formulation substantially improves quantitative accuracy relative to the baseline model while maintaining robustness and numerical stability.

In summary, the proposed framework provides a systematic treatment of buoyancy effects within two-equation RANS closures and yields consistently accurate predictions of turbulent heat transport across a broad range of buoyancy-driven configurations and parameter regimes.

Despite these strengths, several limitations remain outside the present scope.
Although the model reproduces the turbulent heat flux distribution accurately, its reliance on the gradient–diffusion hypothesis means that the temperature field may exhibit modest discrepancies in situations where the turbulent heat flux is not well aligned with the mean temperature gradient.
The calibration dataset could also be expanded, particularly at high
\mathit{Ra}
and low
\mathit{Pr}
, where reference data remain scarce.
Finally, other physical effects that can alter natural-convection dynamics, such as system rotation, are not considered here.
These limitations indicate some directions for further refinement and for extending the present approach to more complex buoyancy-affected flows.
