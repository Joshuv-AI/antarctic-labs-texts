EraseLoRA: MLLM-Driven Foreground Exclusion and Background Subtype Aggregation for Dataset-Free Object Removal
\name Sanghyun Jo *, † \dagger \email shjo.april@gmail.com, \name Donghwan Lee * \email tdr.lee@snu.ac.kr, \name Eunji Jung * \email eunji220728@snu.ac.kr, \name Seong Je Oh \email seongje.oh@snu.ac.kr, \name Kyungsu Kim † \dagger \email kyskim@snu.ac.kr

Abstract
Object removal differs from common inpainting, since it must prevent the masked target from reappearing and reconstruct the occluded background with structural and contextual fidelity, rather than merely filling a hole plausibly. Recent dataset-free approaches that redirect self-attention inside the mask fail in two ways: non-target foregrounds are often misinterpreted as background, which regenerates unwanted objects, and direct attention manipulation disrupts fine details and hinders coherent integration of background cues. We propose EraseLoRA, a novel dataset-free framework that replaces attention surgery with background-aware reasoning and test-time adaptation. First, Background-aware Foreground Exclusion (BFE), uses a multimodal large-language models to separate target foreground, non-target foregrounds, and clean background from a single image-mask pair without paired supervision, producing reliable background cues while excluding distractors. Second, Background-aware Reconstruction with Subtype Aggregation (BRSA), performs test-time optimization that treats inferred background subtypes as complementary pieces and enforces their consistent integration through reconstruction and alignment objectives, preserving local detail and global structure without explicit attention intervention. We validate EraseLoRA as a plug-in to pretrained diffusion models and across benchmarks for object removal, demonstrating consistent improvements over dataset-free baselines and competitive results against dataset-driven methods. The code will be made available upon publication.

1
Introduction

Image inpainting methods based on GANs
(
Goodfellow et al. 2021
;
Suvorov et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2022
)
and text-to-image diffusion models
(
Ho et al. 2020
;
Rombach et al. 2022
;
Podell et al. 2023
;
Esser et al. 2024
)
have advanced the ability to synthesize visually plausible content in missing regions. However, these approaches primarily aim to generate realistic textures rather than restore the underlying background structure. As a result, they often hallucinate plausible objects instead of faithfully reconstructing what lies behind the removed target.

Object removal, in contrast, requires both eliminating the target and recovering the occluded background with structural and contextual consistency. Recent dataset-free diffusion methods
(
Jia et al. 2025
;
Sun et al. 2025
)
attempt to achieve this by redirecting or blocking self-attention within the masked region so that the model focuses on unmasked context. While effective in simple cases, these approaches share two inherent limitations. First, they treat the masked region as the only foreground and often misinterpret non-target foregrounds outside the mask as background, causing unintended regeneration of objects (see Fig.
1
). This reflects a lack of background-aware reasoning. Second, directly constraining attention compromises local detail preservation and fails to coherently integrate multiple background subtypes, leading to blurred or structurally inconsistent textures such as brick walls or wooden fences (see Fig.
2
). This limitation arises because attention suppression does not preserve the diversity of background cues for reconstruction.

(
Jia et al. 2025
;
Sun et al. 2025
)
directly modify attention inside the mask, leading to blurred or distorted background textures, whereas EraseLoRA aggregates background subtypes without attention blocking and preserves sharp, coherent structures.

We introduce EraseLoRA, a dataset-free framework that addresses these issues through background-aware reasoning and test-time adaptation. The first stage, Background-aware Foreground Exclusion (BFE), leverages a multimodal large-language model (MLLM)
(
Bai et al. 2025
;
Zhu et al. 2025a
)
to produce clean background cues by separating target foregrounds, non-target foregrounds, and background from a single image–mask pair. The second stage, Background-aware Reconstruction with Subtype Aggregation (BRSA), performs test-time optimization with Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
to aggregate multiple inferred background subtypes into a coherent reconstruction without explicit attention blocking.
EraseLoRA is validated as a plug-in across diverse pretrained diffusion backbones
(
Esser et al. 2024
;
Podell et al. 2023
)
and standard object-removal benchmarks
(
Kuznetsova et al. 2020
;
Sagong et al. 2022
)
, demonstrating consistent gains over dataset-free baselines and competitive performance with dataset-driven methods. Beyond performance, it establishes a practical and extensible formulation of dataset-free object removal that combines the reasoning capability of MLLMs with the generative fidelity of diffusion models, enabling faithful background reconstruction without additional data or retraining.

Our key contributions are as follows:

•

We identify a fundamental failure mode in object removal: non-target foregrounds are frequently misinterpreted as background, causing their unintended regeneration across recent dataset-free methods.

•

We propose EraseLoRA, a background-aware, dataset-free object-removal framework that combines MLLM-guided separation of target and non-target foregrounds from background with a multi-background-aware test-time adaptation scheme, preventing foreground regeneration while maintaining contextual coherence.

•

We release ground-truth masks that explicitly annotate non-target foregrounds and introduce two metrics tailored to unpaired object-removal settings.

•

EraseLoRA improves background similarity by at least 23% over previous dataset-free methods and retains its advantage when all pretrained diffusion and MLLM components are replaced by alternatives, demonstrating strong model-agnostic behavior.

2.1
Image Inpainting with Generative Models

Image inpainting aims to complete missing regions using the visible context. Early approaches
(
Zhao et al. 2021
;
Zuo et al. 2023
;
Sargsyan et al. 2023
)
based on GANs have been surpassed by diffusion-based methods
(
Xie et al. 2022
;
Yang et al. 2022
)
, which produce more stable, detailed, and high-fidelity completions. Building on text-to-image diffusion models
(
Rombach et al. 2022
;
Podell et al. 2023
;
Esser et al. 2024
)
, methods such as HDPainter
(
Manukyan et al. 2023
)
and SmartBrush
(
Xie et al. 2022
)
further fine-tune these diffusion backbones on paired inpainting datasets so that the model can exploit text prompts while learning to fill masked regions. However, these models are still optimized for context-consistent completion and tend to hallucinate plausible new objects, rather than faithfully reconstructing the original background occluded by the target.

2.2
Diffusion Models for Object Removal

Object removal is a specialized form of inpainting that must not only erase the masked target but also restore the occluded background with structural and contextual fidelity.
Existing methods fall into two categories: dataset-driven approaches
(
Ekin et al. 2024
;
Zhuang et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2025
;
Jiang et al. 2025
)
, which train on additional dataset, mostly on paired before/after removal images, and dataset-free approaches
(
Chen et al. 2024
;
Jia et al. 2025
;
Sun et al. 2025
)
, which operate directly on pretrained text-to-image diffusion models
(
Rombach et al. 2022
;
Podell et al. 2023
;
Esser et al. 2024
)
without additional paired data. For object removal, however, constructing paired examples is expensive and often unrealistic, since most pairs must be synthesized or extracted from video frames, leading to limited diversity and redundant supervision. As a result, recent work has increasingly focused on dataset-free methods that control the diffusion process at inference time.

Recent state-of-the-art dataset-free approaches
(
Jia et al. 2025
;
Sun et al. 2025
)
redirect or suppress self-attention within the masked region to guide the model toward unmasked context. While this attention manipulation reduces unwanted regeneration to some extent, it introduces two fundamental limitations. First, these methods remain background-unaware: by treating only the masked area as foreground, they often misinterpret non-target foregrounds as background and regenerate them, as shown in
fig.

1
. Second, blocking or altering attention disrupts fine textures and prevents consistent integration of multiple background cues, leading to blurry or structurally inconsistent results, as illustrated in
fig.

2
.

To address these limitations, we introduce a background-aware, dataset-free framework that leverages the reasoning capability of multimodal large-language models (MLLMs) to identify and exclude non-target foregrounds and to produce clean background cues for reconstruction. We further aggregate multiple inferred background subtypes coherently through test-time adaptation, enabling structurally consistent background restoration without explicit attention blocking. Our method is plug-in and model-agnostic, and it can be applied across diverse diffusion backbones and MLLMs. Table
1
summarizes this design in a conceptual comparison, highlighting how EraseLoRA differs from previous state-of-the-art methods
(
Zhu et al. 2025c
;
Jiang et al. 2025
;
Jia et al. 2025
;
Sun et al. 2025
)
.

2.3
MLLMs for Visual Reasoning

Multimodal large-language models (MLLMs)
(
Liu et al. 2023
;
Bai et al. 2025
;
Team 2025
;
Zhu et al. 2025a
)
have rapidly gained traction in vision–language tasks due to their strong ability to interpret visual scenes and reason about object relations. Recent editing methods
(
Kim et al. 2025a
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
leverage this capability to extract semantic descriptions, generate editing instructions, or guide global scene manipulation. In inpainting, some works
(
Fanelli et al. 2025
;
Tianyidan et al. 2025
;
Zhou et al. 2025
)
use MLLMs to analyze the visible context and propose content to fill masked regions. However, these approaches primarily rely on visible context and aim to generate new objects, rather than inferring the occluded background behind a removed target. In this work, we utilize MLLMs in a different role, as background-aware reasoners for object removal. Instead of generating new foreground content, we use MLLMs to identify and exclude non-target foregrounds that cause unintended regeneration and to infer background cues that lie behind the removed target but are not visible in the input image, as illustrated in Fig.
3
.

3
Method

Our proposed EraseLoRA is a dataset-free object removal framework that leverages MLLM-guided background reasoning and test-time adaptation to achieve coherent background reconstruction. It is composed of two stages: Background-aware Foreground Exclusion (BFE; Sec.
3.1
) and Background-aware Reconstruction with Subtype Aggregation (BRSA; Sec.
3.2
). The overall pipeline is illustrated in Fig.
4
, and we provide diffusion and attention preliminaries in Appendix
A
.

Figure 5
:
Identification of non-target foregrounds.
Prior methods
(
Chen et al. 2024
;
Sun et al. 2025
)
treat the entire unmasked region as background, which causes regeneration of non-target foregrounds. In contrast, EraseLoRA explicitly identifies non-target foregrounds within the mask and excludes them, producing clean background.

3.1
Background-aware Foreground Exclusion

The first stage, BFE, prevents unintended object regeneration by explicitly excluding non-target foregrounds from reference regions and extracting clean background cues for contextually coherent reconstruction.
Given an input image
I
and a mask, we leverage the background-aware reasoning of MLLMs
(
Bai et al. 2025
;
Zhu et al. 2025a
)
to partition target foreground, non-target foregrounds, and background.
The MLLM first identifies all semantic tags in the image and classifies the masked object as the target foreground, visible objects that may cause regeneration as non-target foregrounds, and occluded objects or scene components behind the target as background cues.
For each MLLM-driven tag, we simply use Tag2Mask models (
e.g.
, Grounding DINO
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
and SAM2
(
Ravi et al. 2025
)
) to localize its corresponding region. The union of these localized regions defines the non-target foreground mask, while pixels outside both the target and non-target foreground regions constitute the clean background.

We define a region label function
\ell(p)
on each spatial index
p
in the latent space:

\ell(p)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }p\text{ belongs to the target foreground},\\
1&\text{if }p\text{ belongs to non-target foregrounds},\\
2&\text{if }p\text{ belongs to clean background}.\end{cases}

(1)

This three-label formulation explicitly distinguishes non-target foreground objects from the unmasked background region, allowing us to isolate distractors that previous dataset-free methods
(
Sun et al. 2025
;
Jia et al. 2025
)
mistakenly treat as background (see Fig.
5
), which in turn yields cleaner background supervision for subsequent reconstruction.

Figure 6

Effect of the background puzzle loss.
We visualize how each background subtype (spectator, advertisement, grass) is represented inside the mask. The background puzzle loss ensures structurally coherent integration of background subtypes within the mask, unlike the weak integration without it.

3.2
Background-aware Reconstruction with Subtype Aggregation

Based on the clean background cues obtained in BFE, BRSA performs test-time optimization with Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
to effectively aggregate multiple background subtypes and reconstruct the masked region with structural and contextual consistency.
To achieve this, BRSA jointly optimizes two complementary objectives: the Background Reconstruction Loss (
\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}
) and the Background Puzzle Loss (
\mathcal{L}_{\text{puzzle}}
).
The overall objective is formulated as

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}+\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{puzzle}}
, where
\lambda
balances background reconstruction fidelity and subtype aggregation.
Together, these losses guide how background information is integrated into the masked region, softly regulating attention flow without the hard attention-blocking used in prior methods
(
Podell et al. 2023
;
Sun et al. 2025
)
.

Background Reconstruction Loss.
To preserve regions that are confidently identified as clean background by BFE (
section

3.1
), we impose a reconstruction loss only on those locations in the latent space. Let
z=\mathrm{Enc}(I)
be the latent representation of the input image
I
, and
\hat{z}
be the reconstructed latent after denoising. The background
reconstruction loss is defined as

\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}=\frac{1}{|\{p\mid\ell(p)=2\}|}\sum_{p:\ell(p)=2}\big\|\hat{z}[p]-z[p]\big\|_{2}^{2},

(2)

where
p
denotes spatial indices in the latent feature map and
\ell(p)
is the region label defined in
eq.

1
, with label
2
corresponding to clean background. By anchoring
\hat{z}
to
z
on these background locations,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}
provides a reliable background reference that promotes globally coherent reconstruction.

Background Puzzle Loss.
While the reconstruction loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}
preserves high-fidelity background appearance, it does not explicitly control how different background subtypes are integrated, often leading to structurally inconsistent or partially missing patterns (
e.g.
, misaligned background context in Fig.
6
). To address this, we introduce a background puzzle loss that treats each background subtype as a distinct puzzle piece that must contribute coherently to reconstructing the masked region (see Fig.
6
):

\mathcal{L}_{\text{puzzle}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{div}},

(3)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{div}}
jointly
encourage spatial alignment and balanced contribution from background subtypes.

The first term,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}
, enforces that background attention is concentrated only on valid regions (target foreground or clean background), preventing attention distraction toward non-target foregrounds and improving spatial consistency:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}=1-\mathrm{Dice}\!\left(A^{\mathrm{dom}},\mathbf{1}_{\{\ell(p)\in\{0,2\}\}}\right),

(4)

where
\mathrm{Dice}(\cdot,\cdot)
measures the spatial agreement between attention activation and valid regions (see details in Appendix
B
). Here,
A^{\mathrm{dom}}[p]=\max_{b\in\mathcal{B}}A_{b}[p]
denotes the dominant attention response at location
p
, and each
A_{b}[p]
is the normalized cross-attention map for background subtype tag
b
.

The second term,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{div}}
, prevents any single background subtype from dominating the reconstruction and encourages all subtypes to contribute within the masked region:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{div}}=1-\min_{b\in\mathcal{B}}S_{b},

(5)

where
S_{b}=\max_{\ell(p)=0}A_{b}[p]
is the maximum activation of subtype
b
in the masked (target foreground) region defined by the label function
\ell(p)
in
eq.

1
.

Together,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{div}}
enable EraseLoRA to achieve structurally coherent integration of background subtypes within the mask, resulting in faithful and contextually consistent background reconstruction, as shown in
fig.

6
.

4.1
Experimental Setup

Implementation details.
We implement EraseLoRA on three text-to-image diffusion backbones
(
Podell et al. 2023
;
Esser et al. 2024
;
Labs 2023
)
. For a fair comparison, we strictly follow the official inference configurations (scheduler, guidance scale, resolution, and the number of sampling steps) and apply EraseLoRA in a purely dataset-free, test-time manner. During test-time adaptation (TTA), we freeze all backbone parameters and optimize only the inserted LoRA adapters
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
with respect to the final loss defined in
section

3.2
across all backbones and benchmarks. Additional details, such as TTA iterations, are provided in Appendix
B
.

Benchmarks.
We evaluate EraseLoRA on two benchmarks: 200 samples from OpenImages V7
(
Kuznetsova et al. 2020
)
and 343 frames from RORD
(
Sagong et al. 2022
)
. In both datasets, the original annotations do not distinguish non-target foregrounds from background, so we annotate them with three-label (target / non-target foreground / background) ground-truth masks that explicitly capture distractor regions. These refined annotations are released and form the basis of the evaluation metrics described below.

Evaluation metrics.

For unpaired object removal, we use DINO similarity
(
Caron et al. 2021
)
, following recent image generation and editing works
(
Li et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
. Foreground Similarity (FG Sim.) and Background Similarity (BG Sim.) measure, respectively, how much the reconstructed region stays similar to the foreground (lower is better) and how well it aligns with the background (higher is better; see Fig.
7
). We additionally report Background Preservation (BG Pres.), computed via SSIM
(
Wang et al. 2004
)
on unmasked regions, following the evaluation protocol of recent editing methods
(
Zhu et al. 2025b
;
Kim et al. 2025c
)
, to assess fidelity outside the masked area. Further implementation details are provided in Appendix
B
.

4.3
Discussion

To understand EraseLoRA’s performance gains, we conduct component-wise ablations on OpenImages V7
(
Kuznetsova et al. 2020
)
, measuring the effect of each stage and loss term.

Effect of BFE with prior dataset-free methods.
To test whether our foreground exclusion module (BFE;
section

3.1
) is useful beyond EraseLoRA itself, we plug into prior dataset-free object-removal methods
(
Jia et al. 2025
;
Sun et al. 2025
)
. By explicitly excluding non-target foregrounds from their reference regions, background similarity improves by up to 6.6%, while foreground similarity decreases by 8.6% (see
table

3
), indicating stronger suppression of unwanted foreground regeneration. These results, together with the qualitative examples in
fig.

9
, show that BFE effectively alleviates the re-generation limitation of existing dataset-free approaches
(
Jia et al. 2025
;
Sun et al. 2025
)
in a fully model-agnostic way and enables more complete background reconstruction.

Limitations.
While EraseLoRA achieves strong performance in object removal, several limitations remain. First, the use of MLLMs and test-time optimization with LoRA adapters introduces additional computational overhead. Although no paired data are required, iterative optimization and MLLM queries increase latency and memory usage at inference time. Second, the MLLM-guided background definition can be imperfect in complex scenes with subtle semantic boundaries or heavy occlusion, which may lead to incomplete or inaccurate removal. A more detailed analysis is provided in Appendix
E
.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce EraseLoRA, a dataset-free object removal framework that leverages MLLM-guided, background-aware reasoning and test-time adaptation to enable faithful background restoration. Building on an analysis of key failure modes in previous state-of-the-art dataset-free methods, EraseLoRA explicitly separates target, non-target foreground, and background, filters out distractors to prevent their regeneration, and integrates multi-background subtypes. As a plug-and-play, model-agnostic module, EraseLoRA consistently produces cleaner and more coherent background reconstructions across diffusion backbones and benchmarks without requiring any paired data.

Appendix A
Preliminaries

Diffusion Models.
Diffusion models generate images by learning a reverse denoising process that gradually transforms noise into image
(
Podell et al. 2023
;
Esser et al. 2024
)
. In a typical text-to-image implementation, an input image
I
is first mapped into a latent representation
z_{0}=\text{Enc}(I)
through a VAE encoder. A denoising network
\epsilon_{\theta}
then iteratively predicts and removes the noise from
z_{t}
, optionally conditioned on a text embedding
c
. Finally, the denoised latent
\hat{z}_{0}
is decoded back into the image space by a VAE decoder, yielding
\hat{I}=\text{Dec}(\hat{z}_{0})
. This framework allows controllable generation by conditioning on text prompts or other external signals.

Attention Mechanisms in Diffusion Models.
In latent diffusion models
(
Rombach et al. 2022
)
, attention blocks regulate how information flows during denoising. There are two types of attention: self-attention captures dependencies among latent tokens, while cross-attention establishes interactions between latent tokens and external condition tokens such as text prompts. For a latent
z_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{HW\times d}
at step
t
, self-attention computes a weight matrix
A^{\text{self}}=\mathrm{softmax}(QK^{\top}/\sqrt{d})\in[0,1]^{HW\times HW}
, where each row sums to
1
, which represents how strongly one latent token attends to all other tokens. Cross-attention follows the same principle but aligns latent queries with condition tokens
C\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}
, yielding
A^{\text{cross}}\in[0,1]^{HW\times L}
. Here each row of
A^{\text{cross}}
sums to
1
, corresponding to
\sum_{i=1}^{L}A^{\text{cross}}_{i}[p]=1
for each spatial index
p
, where
A^{\text{cross}}_{i}\in[0,1]^{HW}
denotes the cross-attention matrix for condition token
i
. This indicates how strongly each latent token relates to the
L
condition tokens. In practice, the way attention is computed has evolved with the design of
modern diffusion backbones. Earlier text-to-image diffusion models
(
Rombach et al. 2022
;
Podell et al. 2023
)
compute attention directly at the latent level on feature maps. However, recent powerful text-to-image diffusion models
(
Esser et al. 2024
;
Labs 2023
)
group multiple latent tokens into larger patch tokens (
latent patchify
) and compute attention at the patch level to enable more efficient and scalable processing of high-resolution images.

Dice Score.

The Dice coefficient between two binary maps
X
and
Y
is computed as

\mathrm{Dice}(X,Y)=\frac{2\sum_{p}X[p]Y[p]}{\sum_{p}X[p]+\sum_{p}Y[p]+\epsilon},

where
\epsilon
is a small constant added for numerical stability.

Normalized Cross-Attention.

Here,
\tilde{A}_{b}[p]
denotes the raw cross-attention value before applying our tag-wise normalization.
The per-tag cross-attention at spatial index
p

A_{b}[p]

A_{b}[p]=\frac{\exp(\tau\tilde{A}_{b}[p])}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\exp(\tau\tilde{A}_{i}[p])},

where
\tau
denotes the temperature parameter controlling the sharpness of attention, and we set
\tau=100

B.1.2
Metric Details

To evaluate object-removal performance on unpaired datasets which do not have ground-truth images after removal, we use two metrics based on DINO-based cosine similarity
(
Caron et al. 2021
)
(introduced in
section

4
).
We reuse the region label function
\ell(p)
defined in Eq.
1
,
which partitions each spatial index
p
into target foreground (
\ell(p)=0
),
non-target foreground (
\ell(p)=1
), and background (
\ell(p)=2
).
We manually curate the corresponding masks to obtain reliable sets for metric computation and will release them publicly.

BG Sim.

Background Similarity (BG Sim.) measures how well the reconstructed masked
region aligns with the true background (higher is better). We compute it as the cosine similarity
between DINOv3
(
Siméoni et al. 2025
)
features extracted from the background
region in the input image and the reconstructed region in the output:

\mathrm{BG\text{ }Sim.}=\frac{f(I[\mathcal{B}])\cdot f(\hat{I}[\mathcal{R}])}{\|f(I[\mathcal{B}])\|\,\|f(\hat{I}[\mathcal{R}])\|}

(6)

where

\mathcal{B}=\{p\mid\ell(p)=2\}
is the background region,

\mathcal{R}=\{p\mid\ell(p)=0\}
is the reconstructed region,
and
f(\cdot)
denotes DINOv3 feature extraction.

FG Sim.

Foreground Similarity (FG Sim.) measures how much the reconstructed masked region
incorrectly refer to foreground (lower is better). It is computed as the cosine similarity between DINOv3
(
Siméoni et al. 2025
)
features extracted from the foreground region in the input image and the reconstructed region in the output. To discourage background-inconsistent restoration, we weight this score by

(1-\mathrm{BG\text{ }Sim})
:

\mathrm{FG\text{ }Sim.}=(1-\mathrm{BG\text{ }Sim.})\cdot\frac{f(I[\mathcal{F}])\cdot f(\hat{I}[\mathcal{R}])}{\|f(I[\mathcal{F}])\|\,\|f(\hat{I}[\mathcal{R}])\|}

(7)

where
\mathcal{F}=\{p\mid\ell(p)\in\{0,1\}\}
denotes the all foreground region (target and non-target).

BG Pres.

Background Preservation (BG Pres.) evaluates how well the unmasked region is maintained after object removal. It is computed as SSIM
(
Wang et al. 2004
)
between the input image
I
and reconstructed image
\hat{I}
over the unmasked region
\mathcal{U}=\{p\mid\ell(p)\neq 0\}
, following the protocol of recent editing works
(
Zhu et al. 2025b
;
Kim et al. 2025c
)
.

B.2
Design Rationale

B.2.1
Limitations of Attention Surgery

Previous state-of-the-art dataset-free methods
(
Chen et al. 2024
;
Jia et al. 2025
;
Sun et al. 2025
)
redirect or block self-attention inside the mask so that the model focuses on unmasked context. Specifically, they block any interaction within the masked region itself by setting those attention values to zero (see Fig.
13
(b)).
However, these methods are unstable and often produce blur or structural artifacts (see Fig.
13
(c)). They unintentionally disrupt short-range activations of self-attention, referring to the local interactions where latent tokens mainly attend to their nearby neighbors. These activations are crucial for preserving fine-grained details, and their disruption often leads to blurred textures. Furthermore, when applied to recent text-to-image diffusion models
(
Esser et al. 2024
;
Labs 2023
)
that compute attention at the patch level, blocking attention inside the mask amplifies instability, resulting in patch-wise artifacts such as checkerboard patterns. These failures show that prior self-attention control methods
(
Chen et al. 2024
;
Jia et al. 2025
;
Sun et al. 2025
)
are neither detail-preserving nor robust across modern text-to-image diffusion architectures
(
Esser et al. 2024
;
Labs 2023
)
.

B.2.2
Effect of Test-Time Adaptation

Instead of explicitly blocking self-attention, BRSA (Sec.
3.2
) performs test-time adaptation with LoRA adapters
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
to steer the model toward referencing background context. Guided by the background cues inferred in BFE (Sec.
3.1
), BRSA reconstructs the masked region using the background reconstruction loss (Eq.
2
) and the puzzle loss (Eq.
3
). By avoiding direct manipulation of attention maps, BRSA preserves short-range activations and yields sharp, artifact-free background reconstructions across diverse text-to-image diffusion backbones.

We vary two key factors in test-time optimization (BRSA;

LoRA rank.

From experiments with ranks {16, 32, 64, 128}, rank 32 yields the best trade-off, achieving the strongest foreground suppression and the most consistent background reconstruction (see Fig.

TTA iterations.

Although longer optimization generally improves reconstruction performance, the marginal gains diminish relative to the additional time cost. Hence, we adopt 500 iterations as a practical balance between quality and efficiency (see Fig.

D.3
Discussion

Concern on object hallucination of MLLMs.

Recent works
(
Zhao et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2025
)
have reported hallucination issues in multi-modal large language models (MLLMs), where models describe objects that do not exist in the image, and proposed methods to mitigate such behavior. To examine whether such hallucination affects object removal quality, we include both a standard MLLM (LLaVA
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
) and its hallucination-mitigated variant (MARINE
(
Zhao et al. 2024
)
) in our ablation study and observe only trivial differences between them (see Tab.
6
).

This result arises because our framework naturally mitigates object hallucination through the Tag2Mask models
(
Ren et al. 2024
)
. Even if the MLLM outputs a tag that does not exist in the image, such a tag cannot be spatially localized and is therefore automatically discarded, having no influence on the object removal process. In contrast, misclassifying existing foreground objects as background subtypes has a much stronger impact on quality, since these improper tags can guide model to regenerate unintended object traces. Consequently, models with stronger background-aware reasoning
(
Zhu et al. 2025a
)
) provide more reliable cues and outperform smaller or even hallucination-mitigated models (see Tab.
6
and Fig
19
).

Multiple objects removal.

When the mask contains multiple objects to erase, EraseLoRA removes all targets jointly and reconstructs each region with coherent background subtypes. Because the adaptation operates per background rather than per instance, its performance remains stable regardless of the type or number of masked objects, requiring no modification to the framework (see Fig.
23
(b)).

Appendix E
Limitations and Future Works

E.1
Limitations

Sensitivity to image–text alignment capacity.

EraseLoRA restores true background by aggregating background subtypes, guided by the puzzle loss (Eq.
3
), which regulates image–text alignment (
section

3.2
). Thus, its effectiveness depends on how accurately the model aligns textual tags with visual features. Earlier text-to-image diffusion models
(
Rombach et al. 2022
;
Podell et al. 2023
)
exhibit weaker image–text alignment, often resulting in inaccurate subtype aggregation and unintended object-like artifacts (see Fig.
21
).

Figure 22
:
Failure cases of EraseLoRA.
Our method fails in two cases: (1) object traces by misclassified background tags and (2) presence of object effects.

Figure 23
:
Efficiency of video extension.
For video frames sharing similar background context, one-shot optimization on a single frame can be reused across the sequence, achieving performance comparable to per-frame optimization while reducing adaptation cost by the number of frames. Moreover, EraseLoRA remains robust for multiple object removal without additional optimization.

Misclassification of background subtypes.
While our method is robust to hallucinated tags, its quality can degrade when true foreground tags are mistakenly classified as background subtypes. In such cases, BRSA (
section

3.2
) follows incorrect cues and regenerates residual object traces (see Fig.
22
). Strong background-aware reasoning from large MLLMs
(
Bai et al. 2025
;
Zhu et al. 2025a
)
alleviates this issue, but fully preventing semantic misclassification remains an open challenge.

Presence of object effects.
EraseLoRA removes the target object and synthesizes plausible background texture, but does not explicitly handle physical effects caused by the object outside the mask (
e.g.
, shadows, lighting distortion, reflections). Thus, if these effects are not masked, some subtle traces may remain after removal (see Fig.
22
).

Additional computational overhead.

EraseLoRA requires background-aware reasoning from MLLMs
(
Liu et al. 2023
;
Zhu et al. 2025a
;
Bai et al. 2025
)
and test-time optimization
(
Wang et al. 2020
)
with LoRA adapters
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
, introducing extra computation compared to training-free object removal methods
(
Esser et al. 2024
;
Jia et al. 2025
;
Sun et al. 2025
)
. Since the optimization is performed per background context, the cost scales with the number of different backgrounds encountered.

E.2
Future Works

Although EraseLoRA incurs additional computational overhead, its optimization is performed per background rather than per image.
In video sequences where many frames share similar backgrounds, this allows the optimization cost to be reused across frames, making video object removal a promising next step.

To validate this potential, we apply test-time optimization
(
Wang et al. 2020
)
only to a single representative frame and reuse the adapted model for the remaining frames. As shown in Fig.
23
, the outputs remain comparable to per-frame optimization, where the model is independently adapted for every frame. This demonstrates that leveraging shared background context makes video object removal an efficient extension of EraseLoRA (see Fig.
23
(a)).
