Contrasting Effects of Clozapine and Risperidone on Cholesterol Metabolism, Synaptic Proteins, and Transcriptional Regulation in Human LUHMES Neurons
Authors: Piotr Rybczyński, Radoslaw Cacala, Zuzanna Cepil, Ewelina Fic, Weronika Romanska, Lukasz Marczak, Katarzyna Pustelny, Sylwia Kedracka-Krok
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Antipsychotic drugs elicit complex cellular responses in neurons, yet the molecular mechanisms underlying their actions remain incompletely understood. Here, we employed deep proteomic profiling (DDA/DIA-MS) in LUHMES-derived dopaminergic neurons to explore the distinct proteomic profile induced by clozapine and risperidone. Pathway analysis revealed that clozapine robustly upregulated lipid metabolic pathways, particularly those involved in cholesterol and fatty acid biosynthesis. Conversely, risperidone primarily enhanced oxidative phosphorylation and modulated synaptic architecture and protein metabolic-pathways. Functional enrichment further indicated that clozapine promotes sterol metabolism, neuronal viability, and neurite outgrowth—hallmarks of neurotrophic activity – while simultaneously suppressing neurotransmission. Importantly, quantitative analysis of the nuclear subproteome enabled us to map these opposing regulatory hubs with high precision. Mechanistically, clozapine induced a transcriptionally repressive state, marked by downregulation of chromatin-opening factors CHD5 and DPF3 (BAF complex components) and HMGN1. In parallel, upregulation of the transcriptional repressor CIC reinforced this repressive signature, suggesting diminished chromatin accessibility. Furthermore, downregulation of activity-dependent regulators CRTC3 and JUNB pointed to an attenuation of excitability-driven gene expression. In contrast, risperidone upregulated TCF4, TCF7L1, FOS, HMGN1, and coactivators RREB1 and NCOA3, promoting Wnt signaling, chromatin accessibility and synaptic plasticity. Taken together, our data unveil distinct proteomic, transcriptional and epigenetic programs orchestrated by clozapine and risperidone, providing novel molecular insights into their divergent clinical actions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12035-025-05515-y.

Introduction

Differences in the clinical effectiveness and risk profiles of clozapine and risperidone underscore the importance of understanding how they mediate therapeutic and adverse effects through complex interactions with multiple neurotransmitter receptors and downstream intracellular signaling in human neurons.

Risperidone, the first atypical antipsychotic approved as a first-line agent, remains one of the most prescribed worldwide due to its effectiveness and favorable tolerability in the treatment of schizophrenia [1, 2]. It also demonstrates efficacy in the management of bipolar disorder, and irritability associated with autism spectrum disorder.

Its mechanism of action entails potent and balanced antagonism of D2/5-HT2A receptors enabling it to address both positive and negative symptoms of schizophrenia. A significant advantage of risperidone is its low risk of extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS) at lower doses, making it a safer option compared to many first-generation antipsychotics. However, risperidone is associated with dose-dependent side effects. These include weight gain, metabolic changes, elevated prolactin with endocrine effects dose-dependent EPS, and in elderly dementia patients, increased cerebrovascular risk and mortality [2]. Risperidone is also available in multiple formulations, including long-acting injectables, improving treatment adherence [3].

Clozapine remains the sole evidence-based pharmacological intervention for treatment-resistant schizophrenia (TRS), a condition affecting approximately 30% of patients [4]. Clozapine is the only FDA-approved antipsychotic for reducing suicide risk in schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder, as demonstrated by the InterSePT trial [5]—crucial finding given that approximately 5% of patients die by suicide [6]. Importantly, evidence shows that delayed initiation reduces response rates, underscoring the need for early use and challenging its restriction to treatment-resistant cases alone [4, 7]. In addition, real-world studies consistently show that clozapine is the most effective antipsychotic for preventing relapse, outperforming other agents in routine clinical practice. While randomized clinical trials (RCTs) confirm its high efficacy, real-world data emphasize its outstanding performance under everyday treatment conditions [8]. Recent head-to-head RCTs comparing clozapine and risperidone further demonstrate clozapine’s greater efficacy on the Clinical Global Impression–Severity scale and lower discontinuation rates due to inefficacy. Real-world evidence corroborates these findings, highlighting clozapine’s pronounced advantage over risperidone in relapse prevention [9].

Despite extensive clinical application of clozapine, the precise mechanisms underlying its superior efficacy are not yet fully elucidated. Clozapine has a broader receptor binding profile than risperidone. Preclinical and pharmacogenomic investigations suggest that clozapine’s efficacy stems from a confluence of multiple, overlapping mechanisms [10]. These include its distinct affinity for serotonin 5-HT2, dopamine D1, α2C-adrenergic, and muscarinic acetylcholine receptors; its engagement with receptor dimers; and potential biased agonism. Clozapine’s receptor profile also encompasses unique interactions with D4 and M1 receptors and its characteristic “loose” binding to D2 receptors [11], which is thought to underpin both its superior efficacy in treatment-resistant schizophrenia (TRS) and its distinct side-effect profile.

Continued investigation of these drugs—particularly using human-derived neuronal models—is imperative for further elucidation of their precise molecular mechanisms of action, thereby facilitating the development of more targeted and personalized therapeutic strategies for the treatment of psychotic disorders. Furthermore, clozapine’s lipophilic and alkaline physicochemical properties are thought to contribute to its unique therapeutic profile. At the synaptic level, clozapine modulates gene transcription and protein expression, thereby potentially influencing synaptic architecture [12–14]. A comprehensive understanding of these multifaceted mechanisms is pivotal for the development of novel therapeutic strategies for TRS [10].

The fetal human mesencephalic cell line, LUHMES (Lund Human Mesencephalic cells, MESC 2.10) derives its conditional immortality from the controlled expression of the v-myc oncogene. Repression of this oncogen permits efficient differentiation of LUHMES cells into mature dopaminergic neurons [15–17]. This model system presents significant advantages over primary human neurons. Notably, LUHMES cells offer a virtually unlimited and homogenous cell supply, starkly contrasting with the scarcity, ethical complexities, and frequently heterogeneous characteristics of primary human neurons. Such scalability and consistency ensure high experimental reproducibility and facilitate large-scale investigations, including drug screening [18, 19], which are challenging to conduct with primary cultures. Moreover, LUHMES cells are generally more amenable to in-vitro culture and genetic manipulation than their more fragile primary counterparts [20]. Consequently, their human origin, combined with these practical benefits, established LUHMES cells as an invaluable tool for neurobiological research, particularly for modelling neurodegenerative disorders, such as Parkinson’s disease [21], thereby circumventing many limitations inherent to primary human neuronal cultures.

LUHMES-derived neurons develop long, well-defined neurites and exhibit a pronounced increase in neuronal marker expression over a 7‐day differentiation period, a timeframe notably shorter than that required for SH-SH5Y cells [22]. Functionally, LUHMES cells, in both their undifferentiated and differentiated states, demonstrate high sensitivity to neurotoxic agents. This feature may be attributed to diminished expression of genes integral to cell survival and apoptosis resistance. LUHMES-derived neurons possess distinct morphological characteristics and functional responsiveness that differentiate them from other in vitro models under investigation [22].

This study employed LUHMES-derived neurons, serving as a human neuronal model to investigate the molecular mechanism underlying clozapine’s action in comparison to risperidone. A comprehensive proteomic approach was utilized, incorporating two distinct data acquisition strategies: data-dependent acquisition (DDA) and data-independent acquisition (DIA).

Methods

Luhmes Differentiation

Lund human mesencephalic (LUHMES) neuronal cells were used as an experimental model for human neurons. Differentiation was carried out according to the previous protocol for dopaminergic neurons [23]. After proliferation in medium with N2 supplement, 2 mM L-glutamine and 40 ng/ml basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), LUHMES cells differentiated into neurons by exposure to 1 μg/mL tetracycline together with 0,2 mM ascorbic acid, 1 mM cAMP and mixture of growth factors (GDNF 20 ng/ml, BDNF 20 ng/ml, TGFβ III 20 ng/ml, LIF 10 ng/ml). After 13 days of differentiation with the addition of growth factors, mature neurons were obtained. Neurons were harvested after 72 h of stimulation with 10 mM clozapine, 3 mM risperidone, or 0,1% DMSO (solvent, control group).

Neuron Nuclei Isolation

Nuclei isolation from neurons was performed according to a modified Qin et al. protocol [24]. After washing three times with PBS, neurons adhered to the plate were incubated with hypotonic buffer (10 mM TrisHCl pH = 7,5, 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2) at 4 °C for 15 min. Next, Nonidet P-40 was added to a final concentration of 0.5% and after 3 min, neuronal nuclei were mechanically dissociated by gently pipetting the solution. Subsequently nuclei were centrifuged at 600 g for 10 min and washed with sucrose solution (230 mM sucrose, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM KH2PO4), followed by centrifugation at 600 g for 10 min and careful removal of supernatant.

Purity of the Nuclear Fraction Assessed by Western Blot

SDS-PAGE was performed using the Laemmli system (4–12% acrylamide/bis-acrylamide, 0.1% SDS gel; running buffer: 25 mM Tris, 200 mM glycine, 0.1% SDS). Electrophoresis was carried out for 20 min at 80 V, followed by 50 min at 150 V. Proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes using CAPS transfer buffer (10 mM, pH 11, 10% methanol). Membranes were then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin in TBS-T (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween-20), incubated with primary antibodies, and subsequently with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies, both for 1 h in room temperature. Antibodies used, along with their dilutions, are listed in supplementary Table S1. Chemiluminescent detection was performed using ImmunoCruz™ Western Blotting Luminol Reagent (SC-2048), and signals were recorded on an Azure 600 imaging system (exposure times: 15 s and 30 s).

Immunocytochemistry

LUHMES cells, seeded on coverslips coated with poly-L-ornithine and fibronectin, were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, then washed twice with PBS. Next, cells were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min, washed with PBS, and then, nonspecific binding was blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1.5 h at room temperature (RT). Cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with 20 μl of mixed mouse and rabbit primary antibody solution (suspended in 1% BSA) per coverslip. To visualize specific protein markers, after washing with PBS, cells were stained with 20 μl of a mixture of Alexa Fluor 546- and 488-conjugated secondary antibodies (suspended in 3% BSA) per coverslip, for 2 h at RT in the dark. Table S1 lists primary and secondary antibodies and the dilution used. To visualize the DNA, after two washes with PBS, cells were treated with 2 μg/ml Hoechst for 10 min. Imaging was performed using a Leica DMI6000B microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany), equipped with a DFC360FX CCD camera and a HCX PL APO 40/1.25 OIL immersion objective. Exposure times and other parameters were manually adjusted and preserved for each protein marker.

LC–MS/MS Data Analysis

Initial Preparation of Samples

For the extraction of total proteins from whole cells and nuclei, samples were lysed in a lysis buffer. This buffer comprised 2% SDS (Fluka Chemie), 0.1 M dithiothreitol (DTT) (Merck), in 0.1 M Tris–HCl (BioShop) at pH 7.5, freshly supplemented with 1 mM sodium orthovanadate (Sigma-Aldrich), 1% cOmplete™ EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche), and 1% phosphatase inhibitor cocktail 3 (Sigma-Aldrich). Lysates were sonicated twice for 10 min (30 s on/off, 320 W) using a Bioruptor UCD-200 (Diagenode), then heated at 95 °C for 5 min and centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 15 min at 20 °C. Supernatants were collected and stored at −80 °C for further use.

Sample Preparation for LC–MS/MS Measurements

For proteomic analysis, samples from both lysates and nuclear fractions, were prepared using a modified SP3 protocol [25]. Proteins were reduced with DTT, alkylated with iodoacetamide (IAA), and bound to magnetic beads in 70% acetonitrile (ACN). After washing, they were digested overnight with a trypsin/LysC mixture (protein-to-enzyme ratio 20:1), and then peptides were recovered by elution in 2% DMSO with shaking and sonication. Peptides were acidified with TFA to a final concentration of 0.1% and desalted on C18-StageTips.

Analysis Using Exploris 480 in DDA Mode

Peptides were analyzed with liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) using Exploris 480 Quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides were separated on a reverse phase Acclaim PepMap RSLC nanoViper C18 column (75 µm × 25 cm, 2 µm granulation) with a gradient 4–60% ACN in 0.1% formic acid (FA) for 190 min.

The spectrometer was operated in data-dependent MS/MS mode with survey scans acquired at a resolution of 60,000 at m/z 200 in MS mode, and 15,000 at m/z 200 in MS2 mode. Spectra were recorded in the scanning range of 350–1200 m/z in the positive ion mode. Higher energy collision dissociation (HCD) ion fragmentation was performed with normalized collision energies set to 28%.

The raw LC–MS/MS files were analyzed using MaxQuant software with search against the SwissProt database with Homo sapiens taxonomy restriction (20 404 sequences). Statistical analysis was done using Perseus software, and Gene Ontology analysis was performed using STRING 12.0 tool.

Analysis Using OT-ASTRAL in DIA Mode

Peptides were analyzed on a Thermo Orbitrap Astral with a Vanquish Neo UHPLC (Thermo Scientific) in trap-and-elute mode using a 50 cm EASY-Spray PepMap Neo column (75 µm × 500 mm, 2 µm particle size; Thermo Scientific). Separation was performed over a 45-min or 27-min gradient (4–45% ACN) for lysate or nuclei, respectively, using 0.1% FA in water (A) and 80% ACN with 0.1% FA (B) as mobile phases. Data were acquired in DIA mode (m/z 380–980), with MS1 scans at 240,000 resolution (3 ms injection time) and DIA scans performed using a 4 Th window (4 ms injection time). DIA raw data were processed in Spectronaut v19 (Biognosys) using directDIA with default settings and a human FASTA file. Common protein contaminants were removed from the set of identified proteins [26].

The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited in the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the MassIVE partner repository with the dataset identifier [ID: MSV000099217].

Clozapine Significantly Upregulates Proteins Involved in Cholesterol and Lipid Metabolism

Analysis of differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) identified by both DDA and DIA analyses (Table 2), revealed that clozapine robustly activated key pathways linked to cholesterol biosynthesis, including the production of necessary precursors within LUHMES-derived neurons. Specifically, clozapine treatment, compared to untreated control cells, resulted in an increased abundance of proteins crucial for fatty acid precursor supply (FASN, ACACA, FABP3), the mevalonate pathway (HMGCS1), and subsequent sterol synthesis (FDFT1, LSS, DHCR7). In comparison to risperidone, clozapine further upregulated additional enzymes associated with lipogenesis (ACLY, ACSL4) and cholesterol biosynthesis CYP51A1 as well as DHCR24, which participates in the terminal stage of cholesterol biosynthesis and ACAT2 involved in the esterification of cholesterol for storage. Additionally clozapine upregulated FADS2, a desaturase critical for poly-unsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) synthesis. Consequently, clozapine downregulated ECHS1, a key mitochondrial enzyme in β-oxidation.

This observed proteomic signature suggests that clozapine promotes anabolic lipid and cholesterol synthesis in LUHMES-derived neurons, concurrently suppressing lipid catabolism. Such metabolic remodeling has the potential to modulate membrane fluidity, vesicle trafficking, and synaptic signaling pathways.

Conversely, risperidone treatment markedly reduced HMGCS1, FDFT1, and CYP51A1 levels relative to control, suggesting an inhibitory effect on cholesterol biosynthesis. These findings are of particular significance given that dysregulation of cholesterol metabolism has been implicated in the pathophysiology of various neuropsychiatric disorders [29].

Furthermore, clozapine treatment led to the upregulation of prosaposin (PSAP), a pivotal protein that serves as the precursor of saposins A–D. These saposins are small lysosomal cofactors indispensable for the degradation of sphingolipids. In addition to its role in lysosomal function, PSAP is also recognized as a secreted neurotrophic factor. Within neuronal context, PSAP contributes to oxidative stress resistance.

Beyond the upregulation of PSAP, clozapine treatment also resulted in significant increase in the abundance of several other key proteins including FAR1, involved in lipid synthesis, ERG28 implicated in cholesterol biosynthesis, MPV17L2 associated with mitochondrial function, and NCOA4, a critical component of ferritinophagy pathway (Fig. 4). These observed alterations are hypothesized to further contribute to membrane remodelling, enhance oxidative stress defense mechanism, and promote neuronal metabolic stability.

Risperidone Alters the Expression of Proteins Linked to Synaptic Plasticity and Mitochondrial Function

Relative to control conditions, clozapine treatment, leads to an increased abundance of several proteins associated with synaptic function and the neuronal stress response (Table 2). Notably, GABA(A) receptor-associated protein like 2 (GABARAPL2) and purinergic receptor P2X3 (P2RX3) are implicated in inhibitory and purinergic signaling, respectively, thereby modulating synaptic activity. Furthermore, Ras homology family member A (RHOA) and carboxypeptidase E (CPE) are known to regulate dendritic spine architecture and neuropeptide processing. Additionally, proSAAS (PCSK1N) and ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1) contribute to neuropeptide maturation and defense against oxidative stress, while ribonuclease inhibitor 1 (RNH1) supports cellular defense mechanisms under stress conditions. Moreover, when compared directly with risperidone, clozapine treatment resulted in elevated levels of peroxiredoxin-like 2 A (PRXL2A), a redox-regulating protein integral to cellular stress defense. Collectively, these clozapine-induced proteomic alterations suggest a promotion of synaptic stability, enhanced neuropeptide modulation, and augmented protection against cellular stress.

In contrast to clozapine, risperidone treatment, when compared to control, modulated the expression of several proteins involved in synaptic signaling, neurotrophic support, and protein homeostasis. Prominently among these, risperidone led to a notable upregulation of the neuropeptide precursor VGF (VGF nerve growth factor inducible) and insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 5 (IGFBP5).

Risperidone treatment resulted in decreased levels of Ras-related protein Rab-5B (RAB5B) and casein kinase 2 subunit alpha1 (CSNK2A1), both of which are implicated in receptor trafficking and synaptic signaling. Additionally, risperidone downregulated eukaryotic translation initiation factor 3 subunit M (EIF3M) and ubiquilin 1 (UBQLN1), proteins integral to mRNA translation and protein quality control, respectively. These alterations could potentially impact synaptic protein turnover and neuronal adaptive capacity.

These preliminary findings, derived from a curated subset of the most reliably identified proteins, suggest a potential interplay where risperidone may enhance neurotrophic signaling, while concurrently dismissing synaptic and proteostatic support mechanisms. A more comprehensive interpretation, encompassing the full spectrum of differentially expressed proteins, will be detailed in subsequent sections of the publication.

Notably, among the proteins most significantly upregulated by risperidone were coiled-coil-helix-coiled-coil-helix domain-containing protein 2 (CHCHD2) and CXXC motif-containing protein 4 (CMC4) (Fig. 4), both of which are known for their roles in mitochondrial respiration and redox homeostasis. Observed upregulation of these proteins may contribute to the maintenance of neuronal energy balance and enhance mitochondrial stability.

These analyses provide insights into the underlying cellular mechanisms and potential functional consequences of the observed protein alterations. A key distinction emerged from the pathway analysis: clozapine treatment robustly stimulates cholesterol and fatty acid biosynthesis pathways, while risperidone predominantly enhances oxidative phosphorylation and modulates biochemical processes associated with synaptic function and protein metabolism (Fig. 6A). Further examination of regulated molecular functions corroborated clozapine’s profound and consistent upregulation of metabolic processes involving sterols, fatty acids, and their derivatives. Additionally, IPA predicts a positive influence of clozapine on neuronal viability, overall neuronal function, and neurite outgrowth, collectively suggesting a potential beneficial impact on synaptic density and plasticity. However, counterbalancing these effects, the analysis also indicated an inhibitory influence of clozapine on neurotransmission (Fig. 6B).

This observed configuration may reflect a cellular strategy to enhance endogenous cholesterol synthesis, preliminary through SREBP1 activation, while concurrently attempting to mitigate excessive cholesterol accumulation via a feedback inhibition mechanism. Furthermore, the predicted inhibition of PPARGC1A, a pivotal coactivator implicated in mitochondrial biogenesis and lipid oxidation, could further bias cellular metabolism toward lipid storage and membrane remodeling, at the expense of catabolic processes.

Collectively, the IPA mechanistic network indicates that clozapine induces a significant reorganization of cholesterol regulatory networks. This may culminate in neuron-specific increases in cholesterol biosynthesis, albeit operating within a constrained or potentially compensatory regulatory framework.

In contrast, risperidone may enhance neurosecretory and growth factor signaling through upregulation of VGF and IGFBP5. Compared to control and risperidone, clozapine further modulates neuronal excitability by enhancing inhibitory signaling via GABBR2 and KCNQ2, while simultaneously increasing excitatory AMPA receptor expression (GRIA1/2) and reducing modulatory glutamate signaling (GRM2, SLC17A6), suggesting a potential balancing effect on neuronal activity. Inspection of the biochemical pathways that most strongly differentiate the mechanisms of action of clozapine and risperidone in LUHMES-derived neuron nuclei suggests that clozapine exerts a predominantly positive effect on mature neurons compared to risperidone. It enhances synaptic plasticity, neuronal survival, and metabolic support through upregulation of SREBP-mediated lipid and cholesterol biosynthesis, ERK5 signaling, and inositol phosphate pathways, while simultaneously downregulating the pro-apoptotic HIPPO pathway. However, the concurrent downregulation of ERK/MAPK signaling relative to risperidone may somewhat limit adaptive synaptic responses, suggesting a potentially weaker effect on certain aspects of neuronal function.

Discussion

Obtained results confirm the dopaminergic identity of LUHMES-derived neurons and highlight the presence of multiple proteins supporting dopaminergic signaling. The volcano plots further show that, although a relatively large number of proteins reach the significance threshold (q-value), the fold changes remain modest(not exceeding 50%), indicating a rather subtle drugs effect.

The present study demonstrates that in human LUHMES-derived neurons, clozapine robustly activates pathways integral to cholesterol and fatty acid biosynthesis, in conjunction with molecular functions linked to sterol metabolism.

Beyond its impact on general lipid metabolism, clozapine appears to modulate specific neuroprotective proteins, which may have important implications for neuronal health and psychiatric outcomes. One such protein is PSAP, whose deficiencies are associated with the development of lysosomal storage disorders [30, 31]. Moreover, genetic variants in PSAP gene have been linked to increased risk of Parkinson’s disease, underscoring its crucial involvement in maintaining lipid homeostasis, particularly within dopaminergic neurons [32]. Notably, diminished PSAP levels have been observed in postmortem brain tissues from individuals with schizophrenia. Supporting this, preclinical studies in mice have demonstrated that neuronal-specific knockdown of PSAP in the cingulate cortex induced behavioral alterations relevant to schizophrenia, such as increased anxiety-like behavior and impaired prepulse inhibition, while not significantly impacting locomotion, working memory, or depressive-like behaviors [33].

One of the most strongly upregulated proteins by clozapine compared to controls and risperidone is NCOA4 (Table 3, Table S3_DEPs NUCLEI), which plays an important role in ferritinophagy, a process that regulates iron storage and mobilization. Ferritin stores iron in a redox-inactive form, thereby preventing iron-induced oxidative stress and maintaining cellular iron homeostasis [34]. Importantly, disturbances in cortical iron homeostasis have been reported in schizophrenia and correlate with the onset of disease, with increased iron and reduced ferritin levels detected in the prefrontal cortex compared to healthy controls [35]. In our study, both FTL (ferritin light chain) and FTH1 (ferritin heavy chain) were upregulated following clozapine treatment, but not after risperidone (Table 2, Table S3_DEPs NUCLEI). The simultaneous upregulation of NCOA4 and ferritin subunits (FTL and FTH1) by clozapine may facilitate dynamic iron regulation in schizophrenia, promoting safe storage of excess iron while allowing controlled mobilization for metabolic needs, potentially providing neuroprotective benefits. Indeed, NCOA4 regulates ferritinophagy in dopaminergic neurons and its modulation can alleviate neuronal degeneration in Parkinson’s disease [36].

Clozapine also enhanced neuronal viability, and neurite outgrowth. These findings suggest a potential neurotrophic role for clozapine and may indicate its influence on synaptic plasticity. Concurrently, clozapine was observed to exert an inhibitory effect on neurotransmission, potentially contributing to the dampening of excessive neuronal activity. In stark contrast, risperidone treatment enhanced oxidative phosphorylation and modulated biochemical processes related to synaptic function and protein metabolism, indicative of increased neuronal activity, augmented synaptic signaling, and possibly the engagement of adaptive plasticity mechanisms.

Among the proteins most prominently affected by risperidone, VGF and IGFBP5 emerged as key regulators of neurotrophic signaling and synaptic plasticity, highlighting potential mechanisms through which risperidone may influence neuronal adaptation and affective processes. Notably, VGF is an activity-dependent neuropeptide, whose expression is induced by neurotrophic factors such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), nerve growth factor (NGF), and neurotrophin-3 (NT-3). It fulfils a critical role in promoting synaptic plasticity, long-term potentiation, neurogenesis, and mood regulation. The VGF/BDNF/TrkB autoregulatory feedback loop has been implicated in neuronal plasticity associated with depression-like behavior, memory consolidation, and the rapid antidepressant effects of ketamine [37]. Notably, diminished VGF expression has been documented in major depressive disorder and in schizophrenia [38–40]. Insulin-like growth factor-binding (IGFBP5) is among the most abundant IGFBPs within the central nervous system, where it modulates the bioavailability and activity of insulin growth factor 1 (IGF-1). IGF-1, in turn, supports crucial neuronal processes including survival, dendritic arborization, and synaptogenesis. Consequently, elevated IGFBP5 levels may potentiate local IGF-1 signaling in neurons [37], thereby contributing to enhanced neuronal resilience and structural integrity of synaptic networks. While consistent reductions in IGF1 and IGFBP2 have been reported in postmortem brain tissue from individuals with schizophrenia, IGFBP5 levels have not exhibited such a reproducible alteration in this disorder [41].

Clozapine- and Risperidone-Induced Transcriptional and Epigenetic Alterations in LUHMES Nuclei

The observed alterations in chromatin remodelers, transcription factors, and various transcriptional activators and repressors likely contribute to the elucidation of molecular mechanisms underlying these drug-induced proteomic and functional changes.

Chromodomain helicase DNA binding protein 5 (CHD5) and family PDH finger protein 3 (DPF3) are integral components of the Brg1/BRM-associated factor (BAF; also known as SWI/SNF) chromatin remodeling complex, which facilitates transcription by enhancing chromatin accessibility. CHD5 operates as an ATP-dependent remodeler, mediating nucleosome repositioning, a process critical for activating neuronal differentiation programs [42]. DPF3, a neuron-specific subunit of the BAF complex, functions as a histone reader, directing the complex to acetylated chromatin regions, thereby enabling the transcription of genes pivotal for neuronal development and function [43]. In contrast, Capicua transcriptional repressor (CIC) functions independently of the BAF complex [44]. CIC exerts its repressive effects by binding to specific DNA sequences and recruiting corepressors complexes, including ATXN1 and the NCoR/SMRT, which leads to histone deacetylation and chromatin condensation. Consequently, CIC suppresses the expression of its target genes, encompassing those involved in neuronal signaling and plasticity. Collectively, although CIC, CHD5, and DPF3 have antagonistic roles in chromatin regulation, the observed upregulation of CIC alongside the downregulation of CHD5 and DPF3 under clozapine treatment suggests a coherent shift toward reduced chromatin accessibility. This imbalance is hypothesized to result in diminished BAF-mediated chromatin accessibility and augmented transcriptional repression, potentially transitioning LUHMES-derived neurons toward a less transcriptionally dynamic state.

Interestingly, during neuronal differentiation, CIC has been demonstrated to transcriptionally repress VGF, with CIC deficiency resulting in a significant elevation of VGF expression. Such elevated VGF levels have been shown to impede neuronal differentiation by inhibiting the exit of neural progenitor cells from the cell cycle and their transition into a post-mitotic state [44]. In the context of 13-day differentiated LUHMES neurons, our findings reveal that CIC was exclusively upregulated by clozapine, whereas VGF expression was specifically increased following risperidone treatment. Although these differentiated neurons are predominantly post-mitotic, this divergent regulation of CIC and VGF by clozapine and risperidone may signify distinct modulatory effects of these antipsychotics on neuronal plasticity, stress response pathways, or broader transcriptional reprogramming within mature neuronal populations.

The observed downregulation of CREB-regulated transcription coactivator 3 (CRTC3) and JunB proto-oncogene (JUNB) after clozapine treatment suggests an attenuation of activity-dependent transcriptional programs within LUHMES-derived neurons. CRTC3 functions as a coactivator for the transcription factor cAMP responsive element binding protein (CREB), a master regulator of a diverse array of genes integral to synaptic plasticity, memory formation, and neuronal survival [45]. Notably, FOS proto-oncogene (FOS), an immediate early gene (IEG) rapidly induced upon neuronal stimulation, is a well-established downstream target of CREB. Similarly, JUNB, a component of the activator protein 1 (AP-1) transcription factor complex, is also rapidly induced by neuronal activity and contributes to the regulation of IEGs, including FOS. Consequently, the diminished expression of JUNB further supports the hypothesis of suppressed activity-responsive transcription following clozapine exposure. While it is plausible that the downregulation of CRTC3 and JUNB could dampen downstream plasticity-related cellular processes—potentially contributing to clozapine’s therapeutic capacity to stabilize neuronal activity and mitigate network hyperexcitability—the precise neuronal function of CRTC3 remains to be elucidated. Although previous studies have underscored a key role of CRTC3 in systemic metabolic regulation [46], recent findings have demonstrated that mice with either systemic or astrocyte-specific CRTC3 deficiency exhibit altered social dominance hierarchies and diminished prefrontal-parietal functional connectivity [47].

The observed downregulation of SKI proto-oncogene (SKI) and SKI-like proto-oncogene (SKIL), coupled with the upregulation of SMAD family member 5 (SMAD5), strongly indicates an enhanced activation of the bone morphogenetic protein (BMP)-dependent SMAD signaling pathway within LUHMES-derived neurons. BMPs are secreted, signaling molecules, belonging to the transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) superfamily. Both SKI and SKIL are well-established negative regulators of SMAD-mediated transcription. They exert this repression by forming complexes with receptor-regulated SMADs (R-SMADs), thereby inhibiting their transcriptional activity [48, 49]. Consequently, a reduction in SKI and SKIL expression removes this inhibitory constraint, permitting SMAD5 to more effectively execute its transcriptional functions. Notably, specific BMPs, such as BMP5, have been demonstrated to potently promote the differentiation of human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) and neural stem cells into midbrain dopaminergic neurons [50]. Therefore, the observed upregulation of SMAD5, together with diminished SKI/SKIL-mediated repression, likely supports neuronal maturation and facilitates the transition of progenitor cells towards a post-mitotic, terminally differentiated state.

The clozapine-induced upregulation of Ras responsive element binding protein1 (RREB1) and nuclear receptor coactivator 3 (NCOA3, also designated as SRC-3 [51]) suggests a modulatory impact of this antipsychotic on neuron morphology and cellular metabolism. RREB1 expression is suggested to be regulated by SREBP-1c [52], thereby providing a potential link between lipid metabolism and the transcriptional control of neuronal gene expression. Furthermore, RREB1 is implicated in the regulation of neuronal proteostasis, autophagy, microtubule dynamics, as well as the microtubule and network dendritic branching [53].

NCOA3 (SRC-3), a member of the p160 steroid receptor coactivator family, functions as a transcriptional coactivator that potentiates nuclear receptor activity and governs the expression of genes integral to glucose and lipid metabolism. Deficiency in NCOA3 has been shown to impair lipid synthesis, disrupt metabolic homeostasis, and potentially contribute to insulin resistance by diminishing cellular responsiveness to nuclear receptor signaling [54]. Although NCOA2 (SRC-2) has been demonstrated to directly coactivate SREBP1, thereby promoting lipogenesis [55], the involvement of NCOA3 in SREBP1 regulation within neurons has, to date, only been suggested [56].

In rat hippocampal neurons, NCOA3 has been reported to regulate dendritic spine morphology and baseline excitatory synaptic activity via miRNA-dependent mechanisms [57]. Specifically, knockdown of NCOA3 results in a significant reduction in average dendritic spine volume, concurrently with an increase in dendritic complexity, highlighting its role in modulating synapse development and plasticity [57]. Furthermore, NCOA3 deficiency has been linked to impairments in hippocampus-dependent cognitive functions, including deficits in short-term and spatial memory [58].

The simultaneous upregulation of TCF4, TCF7L1, FOS, RREB1, NCOA3 and HMGN1 after risperidone treatment suggests the activation of distinct transcriptional programs related to neuronal plasticity and chromatin remodeling. TCF4, a member of the basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) family, is an activity-dependent transcription factor modulated by synaptic stimulation and intracellular calcium signaling. Phosphorylation of TCF4 by protein kinase A (PKA) is a prerequisite for its optimal transcriptional activity and is essential for cortical neuron development. Furthermore, TCF4 exerts a significant influence on neuronal excitability and dendritic morphology. Experimental knockdown of TCF4 has been shown to promote dendritic growth and alter ion channel gene expression, whereas its overexpression leads to a reduction in dendritic complexity [59]. Notably, genetic polymorphism within the TCF4 gene has been associated with an increased risk of schizophrenia [60].

Transcriptional factor 7 like 1 (TCF7L1) belongs to the TCF/LEF (T-cell factor/lymphoid enhancer factor) family of transcription factors, which are key mediators of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling cascade. This pathway is fundamental for neurodevelopment processes, including dendritic arborization and synaptic organization [61]. Within the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, TCF7L1 predominantly functions as a transcriptional repressor [61].

The observed upregulation of FOS, a canonical immediate early gene and a well-established marker of neuronal activation, indicates an increased cellular responsiveness to synaptic stimuli following risperidone treatment.

High mobility group nucleosomal binding domain 1 (HMGN1) is a chromatin-associated protein that acts by antagonizing the linker histone H1. This action decondenses chromatin structure, thereby promoting a more open chromatin state conducive to rapid gene activation. In neuronal context, HMGN1 is thought to support transcriptional flexibility and contribute to synaptic plasticity [62].

Collectively, the risperidone-induced upregulation of these nuclear proteins—TCF, TCF7L1, FOS and HMGN1 may reflect enhancement of neuronal adaptability. This could manifest as improved transcriptional responsiveness to stimuli, facilitation of synaptic remodeling, and an augmented capacity for activity-dependent plasticity.

Drug-Specific Modulation of the Nuclear Subproteome by Clozapine and Risperidone

To gain a more comprehensive insight into the differences between the effects of clozapine and risperidone, the most statistically significant biochemical pathways were identified from all DEPs in the CLO/RIS comparison within the nuclear subproteome. A strong correlation between the results for neuronal lysates and neuronal nuclei is evident (Fig. 10). Beyond these pathways, analysis of the nuclear subproteome revealed a drug-specific effect on the Hippo signaling. This pathwayis critically involved in neuroinflammation, neuronal differentiation, and apoptosis [68]. Hyperactivation of Hippo signaling promotes aberrant apoptosis, which directly contributes to neurodegeneration [69]. Genome-wide mRNA expression (GSEA) analysis of NT2-derived neurons treated with clozapine (10 µM) or risperidone (0.1 µM) for 24 h showed strong downregulation of Hippo pathway genes with clozapine, whereas risperidone treatment showed only a non-significant trend toward downregulation [70]. Consistent with these findings, our results confirm that only clozapine, and not risperidone, downregulated the Hippo pathway activity in the nuclear subproteome of LUHMES-derived neurons.

Our findings indicate that clozapine exerts a specific impact on cholesterol and lipid metabolism as well as on chromatin structure. Importantly, as highlighted in the Introduction, clozapine shows the greatest efficacy when introduced early in the course of treatment, whereas delayed initation is often associated with reduced therapeutic benefit [4]. This observation suggests that the cellular pathways modulated by clozapine are influenced by disease progression and may become increasingly resistant to modulation over time. Accordingly, understanding the unique molecular action of clozapine compared with other antipsychotic drugs carries significant translational relevance.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrates that clozapine and risperidone elicit distinct proteomic alterations in human LUHMES-derived neurons (Fig. 11). Specifically, clozapine robustly activated pathways integral to cholesterol and fatty acid biosynthesis and augmented molecular functions associated with sterol metabolism, enhanced neuronal viability, and neurite outgrowth, while concurrently exerting an inhibitory influence on neurotransmission. In contrast, risperidone treatment resulted in upregulation of proteins implicated in oxidative phosphorylation, synaptic signaling, and protein metabolism, indicative of increased neuronal activity and the promotion of adaptive plasticity. These divergent proteomic signatures were paralleled by drug-specific modulation of chromatin remodelers and transcription factors, notably the clozapine-mediated repression of regulators governing chromatin accessibility and the risperidone-induced activation of genes associated with neuronal plasticity.
