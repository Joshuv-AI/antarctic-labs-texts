Epidemiology, risk factors and clinical outcomes of multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa colonization in bronchiectasis: a territory-wide study in Hong Kong
Authors: Zhiling Yuan, Long Kiu Tsang, James Chung Man Ho, Ivan Fan Ngai Hung, Wang Chun Kwok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMultidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (MDR-PA) is a common colonizer in bronchiectasis patients, associated with worse clinical outcomes. This study aimed to investigate the epidemiology, risk factors, and clinical impacts of MDR-PA colonization among bronchiectasis patients in Hong Kong.MethodsThis territory-wide retrospective cohort study included adult bronchiectasis patients colonized with Pseudomonas aeruginosa from 2014 to 2023 using the Clinical Data Analysis and Reporting System (CDARS) of the Hospital Authority, Hong Kong. MDR-PA prevalence and associated factors were analyzed. Negative binomial regression with the log of follow-up time as an offset was used to assess the association between MDR-PA colonization and hospitalization-required exacerbations.ResultsAmong 5,765 bronchiectasis patients colonized with Pseudomonas aeruginosa, 220 patients had MDR-PA colonization, corresponding to an overall prevalence of 3.82%. In the multivariable logistic regression model, MDR-PA colonization was associated with proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use [adjusted odds ratio (aOR) =1.74, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.26–2.39], inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) use (aOR =1.41, 95% CI: 1.01–1.98), macrolide therapy (aOR =2.12, 95% CI: 1.56–2.88), and hospitalized exacerbation episodes (aOR =1.09, 95% CI: 1.07–1.11). In the negative binomial regression model, MDR-PA colonization was independently associated with a higher frequency of hospitalization-required exacerbations (adjusted incidence rate ratio =2.07, 95% CI: 1.69–2.52, P<0.001). This association remained consistent when hospitalization-required exacerbation was analyzed as a binary outcome (aOR =2.34, 95% CI: 1.64–3.36, P<0.001).ConclusionsMDR-PA colonization was uncommon but clinically important among bronchiectasis patients colonized with Pseudomonas aeruginosa in Hong Kong. Medication exposure, particularly PPI, ICS and macrolide use, were associated with MDR-PA colonization. MDR-PA colonization was strongly associated with hospitalization-required exacerbations, supporting the need for early identification of high-risk patients and timely antimicrobial management.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Among 5,765 bronchiectasis patients colonized with Pseudomonas aeruginosa in Hong Kong, 3.82% had multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (MDR-PA) colonization.

• Proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use, inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) use, macrolide therapy, and a greater number of previous hospitalized exacerbations were associated with MDR-PA colonization.

• MDR-PA colonization was independently associated with a higher frequency and likelihood of hospitalization-required exacerbations.

What is known and what is new?

• MDR-PA colonization is clinically relevant in bronchiectasis, but territory-wide data regarding its prevalence, associated factors, and clinical impact in Asian populations remain limited.

• This territory-wide study provides real-world epidemiological evidence from Hong Kong and identifies medication exposure and previous hospitalized exacerbations as factors associated with MDR-PA colonization. It further demonstrates a strong association between MDR-PA colonization and subsequent hospitalization-required exacerbations.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Bronchiectasis patients with repeated hospitalized exacerbations or substantial exposure to PPIs, ICSs, and macrolides may warrant closer microbiological surveillance. Early identification of MDR-PA and careful antimicrobial stewardship may help inform risk stratification and clinical management, although the observational findings should not be interpreted as establishing causality.

Introduction

Bronchiectasis is an irreversible chronic airway disease clinically characterized by persistent cough and sputum production and is associated with significant morbidity and mortality (1). Bronchiectasis patients colonized with potentially pathogenic microorganisms are prone to accelerated disease progression (2). Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA), a gram-negative bacterial pathogen frequently implicated in nosocomial infections, particularly among patients with structural lung disease, demonstrates remarkable propensity for both resistance selection and subsequent transmission in vivo (3-5). The increasing prevalence of multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (MDR-PA), occurring in conjunction with both interpatient transmission of resistant strains and prior antimicrobial exposure, constitutes a significant public health challenge, particularly in healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) and hospitalized patient populations (6). According to standardized international terminology, multidrug resistance (MDR) is defined as non-susceptibility to ≥1 agent in ≥3 antimicrobial categories (7).

The prevalence of MDR-PA varies across regions: 28.47% in a Spanish study (8), 14.5% in a Canadian ward surveillance study (9), and 24.7% among pneumonia patients in a U.S. study (10). Notably, 13.7% of PA isolates exhibited resistance to ≥3 antimicrobial groups in a European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) study (11). Previous studies have established that PA infection is associated with persistent and heightened lower airway inflammation, which increases exacerbation risk and mortality in bronchiectasis (5,12-15).

However, evidence on MDR-PA colonization in bronchiectasis, particularly its risk factors and impact on severe exacerbations, remains limited. Identifying high-risk patients may help guide microbiological surveillance and timely antimicrobial management. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the epidemiology and factors associated with MDR-PA colonization in bronchiectasis patients in Hong Kong and to evaluate its association with exacerbations requiring hospitalization. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1445/rc).

Methods

Patient selection

A territory-wide retrospective cohort study was conducted on patients with bronchiectasis and confirmed PA colonization from all public hospitals and clinics under the Hong Kong Hospital Authority (HKHA) between 2014 and 2023. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of The University of Hong Kong/Hospital Authority Hong Kong West Cluster (HKU/HKWC IRB) (No. UW 24-157). Informed consent was waived because this was a non-interventional retrospective study using anonymized routinely collected healthcare data with no direct patient contact. Patients were identified through the Clinical Data Analysis and Reporting System (CDARS) with the diagnostic code 494 in International Classification of Diseases (ICD) Nineth Revision (16,17). Patients with co-existing asthma and/or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), identified by the respective ICD-9 codes (493 for asthma and 496 for COPD), were excluded. Additionally, patients were excluded from the analysis if PA was not isolated from sputum or bronchoscopic cultures during the study period. Furthermore, patients with bronchiectasis and PA isolation were also excluded if susceptibility results were available for fewer than three antimicrobial categories.

Clinical data retrieval

Age, sex, clinical details [baseline blood lymphocyte count, baseline blood neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR], bassline systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), serum albumin level, platelet count) and comorbidities (gastroesophageal reflux disease, history of tuberculosis, diabetes mellitus, connective tissue disease, malignancies, chronic kidney disease, etc.) and prior number of hospitalized bronchiectasis exacerbation were retrieved from the CDARS. The dates of PA/MDR-PA isolation and hospitalization-required bronchiectasis exacerbations were collected. Comorbidity burden was quantified using the updated Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) using ICD-9 code of each corresponding comorbidity (categorized into 0, 1, 2, ≥3) (18). The medical treatments considered in the present study included use of inhaled corticosteroid (ICS), use of long-term macrolide (>14 days), use of inhaled aminoglycoside, use of proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) for >30 days. The sputum microbiology reports including bacteria species and antibiotics sensitivity also were retrieved from the CDARS.

Microbiologic evaluation

MDR- PA was defined as non-susceptibility to at least one antimicrobial agent in three or more anti-pseudomonal antimicrobial categories (7). The anti-pseudomonal categories considered in this definition included anti-pseudomonal β-lactams, carbapenems, monobactams, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, and polymyxins, as shown in Table S1. For each antimicrobial category, resistance status was determined based on the antimicrobial susceptibility testing interpretation recorded in the microbiology database. An isolate was considered non-susceptible to a given antimicrobial category if non-susceptibility or resistance was recorded for at least one antimicrobial agent within that category. MIC-based variables were interpreted according to the susceptibility interpretation already recorded in the microbiology database. If no susceptibility result was available for all agents within a category, the resistance status of that category was considered not assessable rather than susceptible. PA colonization was defined as PA being repeatedly (≥3 times) isolated from a sputum or bronchoscopic culture during the study period (2). Non MDR-PA was defined as patients with PA colonization who do not fulfill the criteria for MRD-PA. We categorized patients into “PA group” in case PA was isolated at the initial circumstances and with sufficient drug susceptibility test.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Baseline characteristics were compared between MDR-PA and non-MDR PA groups using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, as appropriate.

MDR-PA prevalence was calculated as the proportion of MDR-PA cases among all bronchiectasis patients colonized with PA. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with MDR-PA colonization, and results were reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

The main outcome was hospitalization-required bronchiectasis exacerbation. Since the number of hospitalization-required exacerbations was a count variable with overdispersion, negative binomial regression was used as the primary model. The logarithm of follow-up time was included as an offset to account for different follow-up durations. Results were reported as adjusted incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% CIs. A sensitivity analysis was performed by treating hospitalization-required exacerbation as a binary outcome, defined as at least one hospitalization-required exacerbation during follow-up, using multivariable logistic regression. Significance level is P<0.05. All analyses were performed using R (Version 4.2.2).

Results

As shown in Figure 1 and Table 1, from CDARS, 13,064 patients with bronchiectasis were identified between 2014 and 2023. A total of 6,450 bronchiectasis patients having PA isolated in respiratory tract specimens, 5,765 of them have sufficient drug susceptibility tests and 220 bronchiectasis patients having MDR-PA were confirmed. Figure 2 shows that resistance was most frequently observed among fluoroquinolones (n=540), followed by beta-lactams (n=489) and aminoglycosides (n=153). Sulfonamides were also included in the database; however, these agents are not routinely used as antipseudomonal antibiotics and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

This territory-wide retrospective cohort study investigated the epidemiology of MDR-PA colonization, its associated factors, and its clinical impact among bronchiectasis patients colonized with PA in Hong Kong. Among 5,765 PA-colonized bronchiectasis patients, 220 had MDR-PA colonization, corresponding to an overall prevalence of 3.82%. PPI use for more than 30 days, ICS use, long-term macrolide therapy for more than 14 days, and hospitalization-required exacerbation episodes were associated with MDR-PA colonization. Importantly, MDR-PA colonization was independently associated with a higher rate of hospitalization-required bronchiectasis exacerbations after adjustment for demographic characteristics, comorbidities, medication exposure, albumin level, inflammatory markers, and follow-up time incorporated as an offset. These findings suggest that MDR-PA colonization is not only a microbiological finding, but also a clinically relevant marker of exacerbation burden among patients with bronchiectasis.

In recent years, the prevalence of MDR and XDR PA strains has been rising, with rates ranging from 15% to 30% in certain regions (8-10). In Europe, most countries reported resistance rates exceeding 10% across all antimicrobial groups under surveillance (11). Previous studies have also shown that MDR organisms are not uncommon during bronchiectasis exacerbations. A European cohort study of bronchiectasis patients reported that MDR bacteria were isolated in 20% of exacerbation episodes, with PA being one of the most frequently identified pathogens (19). Chang et al. reported 14.8% of the MDRS organisms being MDR-PA (20). However, evidence on MDR-PA colonization in among bronchiectasis patients in Asia remains limited. In the present study, the number of MDR-PA cases among PA-colonized bronchiectasis patients increased during the study period. Because the annual number of MDR-PA cases was relatively small, the study period was grouped into two intervals, 2014–2018 and 2019–2023, with 100 and 120 MDR-PA cases identified, respectively. Compared with rates reported in previous studies conducted in different clinical setting, the prevalence of MDR-PA colonization in our cohort was relatively low. There are several possible explanations. First, our study included a broad real-world population of bronchiectasis patients colonized with PA, rather than being restricted to patients admitted for severe infection or acute exacerbation. Second, MDR-PA identification depended on routinely collected sputum culture and antimicrobial susceptibility testing records. Not all bronchiectasis patients underwent sputum culture, and not all PA isolates had complete susceptibility data available. Therefore, some potential MDR-PA cases may not have been captured, which may have led to an underestimation of the true prevalence of MDR-PA. Third, differences in MDR definitions, antibiotic exposure patterns, microbiological testing practices, and healthcare systems may also contribute to variation across studies.

Several factors have been reported to be independently associated with MDR bacterial isolation, including prior MDR isolation, hospitalization within the previous year, and chronic kidney disease (21). In the present study, PPI use, ICS use, macrolide therapy, and hospitalization-required exacerbation episodes were associated with MDR-PA colonization.

The association between PPI use and MDR-PA may be partially explained by the drug’s impact on gastric pH. Previous studies have suggested that PPIs may increase susceptibility to respiratory infections by promoting bacterial colonization in the upper gastrointestinal, which may predispose patients to pulmonary infection through microaspiration and subsequent airway colonization (22,23). However, this association should be interpreted with caution, as PPI use may also serve as a marker of greater comorbidity burden, frailty, or more frequent healthcare contact. Therefore, confounding by indication cannot be exclude in the observational study.

ICS use was also associated with MDR-PA colonization. Previous studies have reported an increased risk of pneumonia and potentially resistant organisms in patients receiving corticosteroids therapy (24,25). In COPD populations, chronic ICS use has been associated with a higher incidence of PA infection (26). Sibila et al. further reported that ICS is significantly associated with pneumonia risk in COPD patients, potentially due to its local immunosuppressive effects (27). In bronchiectasis, ICS may similarly alter airway host defense and microbiome composition, thereby potentially influencing bacterial colonization patterns. Nevertheless, this association should also be interpreted cautiously, as ICS is more likely to be prescribed to patients with more severe airway disease, asthma, COPD overlap, or frequent symptoms. Therefore, the observed association may partly reflect underlying disease severity rather than a direct causal effect.

Long-term macrolide therapy was another important factor associated with MDR-PA colonization. Macrolides are widely used in bronchiectasis to reduce exacerbation frequency; however, prolonged exposure may increase antimicrobial selection pressure. Previous studies have linked long-term macrolide use to increased minimum inhibitory concentrations and increased antimicrobial resistance, particularly among patients receiving chronic therapy (21,28,29). Therefore, although macrolide therapy remains an effective strategy for selected bronchiectasis patients with bronchiectasis, the potential risk of resistance emergence and MDR-PA colonization should be considered. Regular microbiological surveillance and antimicrobial stewardship are warranted in patients receiving long-term macrolide treatment.

Hospitalization-required exacerbation episodes were also associated with MDR-PA colonization. This is clinically plausible because patients with frequent severe exacerbations are more likely to experience repeated healthcare exposure, receive broad-spectrum antibiotics, and undergo recurrent microbiological testing. Prior hospitalization for exacerbation has been recognized as a risk factor for MDR pathogens (30). In our cohort, MDR-PA status was defined before the assessment of subsequent exacerbation outcomes. One possible chronological pathway is that patients with more severe bronchiectasis experience recurrent exacerbations requiring hospitalization and repeated antibiotic exposure, which may facilitate the acquisition of MDR-PA. Once established, MDR-PA may contribute to persistent airway infection and increase the risk of subsequent severe exacerbations, creating a potentially self-perpetuating cycle. Nevertheless, because of the observational nature of this study, reverse causation cannot be excluded. Patients with more advanced disease may be inherently predisposed to both MDR-PA acquisition and frequent exacerbations. Therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of causality.

The clinical relevance of MDR-PA colonization was further supported by its association with hospitalization-required bronchiectasis exacerbations. In the negative binomial regression model with follow-up time incorporated as an offset, MDR-PA colonization was associated with an approximately two-fold higher rate of hospitalization-required exacerbations. This association remained consistent when hospitalization-required exacerbation was analyzed as a binary outcome. These findings suggest that MDR-PA colonization is not only a microbiological finding, but also a clinically relevant marker of exacerbation burden. Resistant PA strains may reduce the effectiveness of empirical antibiotic therapy, delay appropriate treatment, and contribute to persistent airway infection and inflammation. This may amplify the infection–inflammation cycle described in bronchiectasis, in which chronic infection, airway inflammation, impaired mucociliary clearance, and structural airway damage interact to promote disease progression and recurrent exacerbations (31).

In the context of our findings, this process may operate through a bidirectional pathway. Hospitalization-required exacerbations may increase the likelihood of MDR-PA acquisition through healthcare exposure and antibiotic selection pressure. Once established, MDR-PA colonization may subsequently increase the risk of future severe exacerbations through persistent infection, greater inflammatory burden, and reduced treatment effectiveness. This supports the clinical concept that MDR-PA may serve as a marker of a high-risk bronchiectasis phenotype requiring closer follow-up, repeated sputum surveillance, and individualized antimicrobial strategies. However, because of the observational nature of this study, the temporal direction of these associations cannot be definitively established.

Other factors associated with hospitalization-required exacerbations included TB history, lower baseline albumin level, PPI use, ICS use, and macrolide therapy. A history of tuberculosis may reflect post-infectious structural lung damage and a more severe bronchiectasis phenotype. A low baseline albumin level may indicate poor nutritional status, systemic inflammation, or greater disease severity, all of which may increase the risk to severe exacerbations. The observed associations of PPI, ICS, and macrolide use with exacerbations requiring hospitalization should be interpreted in the context of confounding by indication, as these medications are more likely to be prescribed to patients with more severe symptoms, comorbid airway disease, or recurrent exacerbations.

Several studies have indicated that the prevalence of MDR-PA is increasing globally (6), and varies across different airway diseases (32). A retrospective international study including data from the United States, France, Germany, Italy, and Spain reported that MDR-PA was found in approximately 30% of pneumonia patients infected with PA (33). In contrast, a Swiss study reported a prevalence of 11% of MDR-PA among COPD patients infected with PA (34). An Australian study of cystic fibrosis reported MDR-PA colonization rates of 31% in infants and 35% in adults (35). However, studies specifically examining MDR-PA colonization and its clinical outcomes in bronchiectasis remain limited. A European study reported that MDR pathogens were detected in 20.1% of bronchiectasis patients experiencing exacerbations, with MDR-PA accounting for 48.5% of these MDR isolates (19). Another Chinese study conducted at Zhengzhou University in China identified MDR-PA colonization in 34 of 147 bronchiectasis patients infected with PA (36). Compared with these studies, the prevalence of MDR-PA observed in our cohort was lower. This may be because our study included a broad territory-wide real-world population rather than selected cohorts of hospitalized patients or patients experiencing exacerbations. In addition, MDR-PA detection depended on the availability of sputum culture and antimicrobial susceptibility testing data collected during routine clinical practice.

This study has several limitations. First, although data were obtained from all public hospitals and clinics under the Hong Kong Hospital Authority, patients managed exclusively in private healthcare settings may not have been captured. Second, this was a retrospective observational study based on routinely collected clinical data; therefore, residual confounding, missing information, and coding inaccuracies cannot be excluded. Third, MDR-PA identification depended on sputum culture and antimicrobial susceptibility testing performed in routine practice. Patients without sputum cultures or complete susceptibility testing may have been missed, potentially leading to an underestimation of MDR-PA prevalence. In a supplementary comparison (Table S2), patients excluded because of insufficient susceptibility testing were broadly similar to those included in the final analysis, although differences were observed in several comorbidities, inflammatory markers, long-term macrolide use, and exacerbation burden. These differences suggest that some degree of selection bias may remain and should be considered when interpreting the prevalence estimate. Fourth, in the non-MDR PA group, we did not further stratify patients according to full susceptibility status, such as drug-sensitive PA and non-MDR drug-resistant PA. Previous studies comparing PA-S and PA-R suggest that prior antibiotic exposure and frequent exacerbations may be associated with PA-R infection (36). Fifth, several clinically relevant variables were unavailable in the CDARS database, including bronchiectasis severity scores, lung function parameters, radiological extent of disease, and detailed information on previous antibiotic exposure. These factors are important determinants of both MDR-PA acquisition and exacerbation risk. Although we adjusted for a broad range of demographic characteristics, comorbidities, laboratory parameters, and medication use, the absence of these variables may have resulted in residual confounding. Finally, although multivariable models were used to adjust for measured confounders, treatment selection bias and confounding by indication may remain, particularly for associations involving PPI, ICSs, and macrolides. Future prospective studies with standardized microbiological testing and more detailed treatment information are warranted to validate these findings.

Conclusions

In this territory-wide retrospective cohort study including 5,765 bronchiectasis patients colonized with PA in Hong Kong between 2014 and 2023, MDR-PA colonization was identified in 220 patients, corresponding to a prevalence of 3.82%. PPI use, ICS use, long-term macrolide therapy, and hospitalization-required exacerbation episodes were associated with MDR-PA colonization. MDR-PA colonization was independently associated with a higher rate of hospitalization-required bronchiectasis exacerbations. These findings suggest that MDR-PA colonization is an important clinical marker of exacerbation burden among patients with bronchiectasis and highlight the need for early identification of high-risk patients, regular microbiological surveillance, and appropriate antimicrobial stewardship to support risk stratification and clinical management.
