Marginal and internal fit and fracture resistance of lithium disilicate laminate veneers for teeth with proximal cavities: an in-vitro comparative study
Authors: Mehmet Ozan Taş, Ayşegül Güleryüz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe management of proximal cavities under porcelain laminate veneers remains a clinical challenge. Conventional strategies—such as placing an intermediate composite restoration or extending the preparation—require additional steps or sacrifice healthy tooth structure. While the literature reports conflicting findings on whether composite substrates affect veneer longevity, a comprehensive biomechanical evaluation of a monolithic veneer design that directly incorporates the proximal cavity has not been reported. This in-vitro study compared the marginal fit, internal adaptation, and fracture resistance of lithium disilicate laminate veneers fabricated for three substrate conditions: an intact tooth, a tooth with a proximal composite restoration, and a monolithic veneer with an integrated proximal cavity.MethodsThirty-six maxillary central incisor typodont teeth were divided into three groups (n = 12): control (intact tooth), composite restoration, and integrated cavity. Standardized proximal cavities were prepared in the latter two groups; the composite restoration group received a composite restoration prior to veneer fabrication, while the integrated cavity group had the cavity incorporated directly into the veneer design. Lithium disilicate laminate veneers were fabricated using a CAD/CAM system. Marginal and internal fit were evaluated at 19 points per specimen using a silicone replica technique examined under stereomicroscopy. Fracture resistance was assessed in a universal testing machine with a load applied at a 135° angle. Data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests (α = 0.05).ResultsThe integrated cavity group exhibited a statistically significantly larger marginal gap at the proximal margin compared to the control and composite restoration groups (p<0.001). A corresponding significant increase in cement gap thickness was observed in the mesial internal region of the integrated cavity group. No significant differences were detected at any other marginal or internal measurement points. Fracture resistance did not differ significantly among the three groups (p = 0.516), and the integrated cavity group recorded the highest mean fracture resistance value.ConclusionsIncorporating a proximal cavity directly into a monolithic laminate veneer design compromised marginal and internal fit in the cavity region but did not negatively affect overall fracture resistance. This preliminary proof-of-concept is promising; however, validation through thermomechanical aging and clinical trials is essential before routine application.Keywords: Dental veneers, Marginal adaptation, Fracture resistance, Lithium disilicate, Computer-aided design

Background

Historically, full crowns were often used for the treatment of anterior teeth. However, with the development of ceramic systems and adhesive agents, porcelain laminate veneers, a more conservative option, have become the preferred treatment in appropriate situations [1]. Porcelain laminate veneers are a minimally invasive treatment option for teeth with discoloration, malformations, positional anomalies, fractures, and loss of tooth structure [2, 3]. Although porcelain laminate veneers are generally accepted as a reliable and effective esthetic solution, potential complications include fracture and marginal defects [4]. Specifically, marginal integrity and fracture resistance are the most critical factors that directly determine the long-term clinical success of the veneers.

The clinical success of porcelain laminate veneers depends on the nature of the underlying substrate. Situations where restorations are applied over exposed dentin or existing composite restorations, rather than sound enamel, have been reported in the literature as a significant variable affecting performance. A systematic review and several clinical observations have reported higher failure rates, increased microleakage, and marginal defects when porcelain laminate veneers are bonded to existing composite restorations rather than sound enamel [5–8]. These clinical findings are supported by biomechanical analyses and long-term follow-ups of up to ten years, which recommend extending preparation margins away from the composite to mitigate complications such as discoloration and caries recurrence [9–15]. However, literature on this topic remains controversial [16]. In contrast to the aforementioned studies, several long-term prospective clinical trials have revealed that the presence of a composite restoration under a laminate veneer does not significantly compromise its overall success [17–19]. To overcome potential bonding issues, some researchers advocate for strengthening the composite-resin cement interface using various surface conditioning treatments [20, 21]. Consequently, clinicians facing teeth with existing restorations or caries typically rely on management-based solutions: either extending preparation margins—which sacrifices healthy tooth structure [9, 13–15]—or employing additional surface treatments that increase clinical steps and costs [20, 21].

However, while all these approaches preserve the composite restoration as an intermediate layer, this study investigated a more fundamental alternative to the problem: directly including the existing cavity in a monolithic laminate veneer design. Mechanically, this approach aims to reduce the number of adhesive interfaces within the restorative complex. By eliminating the composite-to-resin cement interface—a potential weak link in conventional techniques—this monolithic configuration is theoretically expected to provide superior structural integrity and a more homogeneous stress distribution under functional loading. Yet, a comprehensive study comparing the combined effects of this integrated cavity design on marginal fit, internal fit, and particularly fracture resistance with traditional methods is lacking in the literature.

Therefore, the purpose of this in-vitro study was to comparatively evaluate the marginal and internal fit and the fracture resistance of lithium disilicate laminate veneers prepared in three groups simulating different clinical situations: an intact tooth, a tooth with a proximal composite restoration, and a tooth in which the proximal cavity was incorporated into the laminate veneer. The null hypothesis (H0) of this study was that there would be no statistically significant difference in marginal and internal fit and fracture resistance among the three tested groups.

Methods

This in-vitro, experimental study was planned to evaluate the effects of three different substrate types on the marginal fit, cement thickness, and fracture resistance of lithium disilicate laminate veneers. The sample size for each group was determined based on a power analysis conducted prior to the experimental phase, using data from a previous study investigating the marginal fit of lithium disilicate restorations across different margin designs [22]. Based on the reported mean marginal gap values (78 ± 25 μm and 104 ± 18 μm), an effect size (f) of 0.55 was calculated. With an alpha level of 0.05 and a target power of 0.80, the analysis indicated that a minimum of 11 specimens per group would be sufficient to detect significant differences. To enhance statistical reliability and account for potential specimen loss, 12 specimens per group (N = 36) were included in the current study. A total of 36 acrylic typodont teeth (Nissin Dental Products Inc.; Kyoto, Japan) were used in the study [1, 23, 24]. The teeth were divided into three test groups with 12 specimens in each group: the Control group (intact tooth) (K), the composite restoration group (CR), and the cavity-containing group (CAV) (Fig. 1).

Schematic representation of the experimental groups used in the study: A an intact tooth with a laminate veneer preparation (Control Group - K), (B) a prepared tooth with a proximal composite restoration (CR Group), (C) a prepared tooth in which the proximal cavity was directly incorporated into the laminate veneer design (CAV Group). The standardized proximal cavity dimensions are 3.5 mm in cervico-incisal length, 2.78 mm in mesiodistal width, and 2.4 mm in depth

A maxillary left central incisor was selected for the laminate veneers. Before preparation, the typodont tooth was scanned with an intraoral scanner (Trios, 3Shape A/S; Copenhagen, Denmark) to replicate the tooth anatomy and to be used as a biocopy for designing the laminate veneers. A silicone index (Zetaplus, Zhermack SpA; Badia Polesine, Italy) was fabricated to ensure consistent and adequate preparation depth [25, 26]. The scan data was transferred to a design software. In the software, the mesiodistal width of the tooth was marked to be divided into 3 equal parts using the contour points as references. The same procedure was repeated for the cervico-incisal width of the tooth, and the resulting rectangle in the mesial middle third of the tooth was designated as the cavity margin for the CR and CAV groups. A guide plate was designed (Blender, v4.1, Blender Foundation, Amsterdam, Netherlands) with an opening on the buccal surface of the tooth, leaving the cavity borders exposed (Fig. 2), and was fabricated from 3D printer (Anycubic UV Tough Resin, Anycubic, Shenzhen, China) (Bene 5, Nova3D, Shenzhen, China). The fabricated guide plate was placed on the teeth of the CR and CAV groups, the cavity margins were marked on the teeth, and cavities with a depth of 2.4 mm were prepared using a depth-controlled diamond rotary instrument (828TR, Hager & Meisinger GmbH; Neuss, Germany). In the CR group teeth, an adhesive (Universal Bond, Tokuyama Dental Corp.; Tokyo, Japan) was applied to the cavity, air-thinned, and polymerized with an LED light-curing unit (VALO Cordless, Ultradent Products Inc.; Utah, USA). Subsequently, a composite restoration (Omnichroma Blocker, Tokuyama Dental Corp.; Tokyo, Japan) was applied using a layering technique. All specimens were embedded in a 2 cm diameter cylindrical mold with cold-curing acrylic resin (Meliodent, Kulzer GmbH; Hanau, Germany) using a parallelometer (PR 242, Eleksan Otomasyon Teknolojileri San.Tic.Ltd.Şti.; Istanbul, Turkey) (Fig. 3). Thus, all specimens were made ready for laminate veneer preparation.

The laminate veneer preparation was planned with a thickness of 0.5 mm, an incisal reduction of 1 mm, a cervical margin 0.5 mm above the cementoenamel junction, and proximal margins just anterior to the contact areas. On the buccal surface of the preparation, grooves were created in two planes with a 0.5 mm depth-controlled, three-wheeled diamond rotary instrument (834 − 021, Hager & Meisinger GmbH; Neuss, Germany). For the incisal reduction, grooves were created with a 1 mm depth-controlled diamond rotary instrument (828TW, Hager & Meisinger GmbH; Neuss, Germany). The tooth was then prepared for a butt-joint laminate veneer design using a Laminate Veneer Preparation diamond kit (Laminate Veneer Kit 2413, Hager & Meisinger GmbH; Neuss, Germany) (Fig. 4) [27]. Two silicone indices were fabricated; one was sectioned vertically (labio-palatally) and the other horizontally. These were used to ensure uniformity and depth control during preparation [25, 26].

Laminate veneer preparation and the final design. A The laminate veneer preparation diamond kit. B The prepared typodont tooth demonstrating the final veneer preparation design

The laminate veneers were fabricated in three steps using a CAD-CAM system. The prepared typodont teeth were scanned using the same intraoral scanner (Trios, 3Shape A/S; Copenhagen, Denmark). A dental design software program (ExoCad, ExoCad GmbH; Darmstadt, Germany) was used to design the veneers. During the design phase, pre- and post-preparation scans were superimposed to restore the original tooth morphology. The cement space value was set to 30 microns, and the production process was initiated.

The veneers were fabricated from lithium disilicate ceramic blocks (IPS e.max HT C14 A1, Ivoclar Vivadent Inc.; Schaan, Liechtenstein) using a wet milling method with a 4-axis milling machine (Redon r-one, Redon Technology A.Ş.; Istanbul, Turkey). The fit of the fabricated veneers was verified using 3x magnification loupes. A glaze paste (IPS e.max CAD Crystall/Glaze Paste & Liquid, Ivoclar Vivadent Inc.; Schaan, Liechtenstein) was applied to the veneers, and a combined crystallization and glaze heat treatment of 24 min was performed in a furnace according to the manufacturer’s recommendations to provide mechanical and esthetic properties.

The silicone replica technique was used for marginal and internal fit measurements [28, 29]. A light-body vinyl polysiloxane impression material (Zhermack Elite HD+ Light Body, Zhermack SpA; Badia Polesine, Italy) was applied to the intaglio surface of each veneer using the manufacturer’s dispenser, placed on the tooth with finger pressure, and allowed to polymerize. To ensure clinical relevance and maintain consistency, finger pressure was applied following literature indicating that seating forces exceeding 5 kg do not significantly influence cement film thickness [30] and that average clinical finger pressure is approximately 8 ± 1.3 kg [31]. To minimize operator-related variability, all silicone replica and cementation procedures were performed by a single experienced prosthodontist applying a consistent and controlled static force. After polymerization, another light-body vinyl polysiloxane of a contrasting color (Panasil Initial Contact X-Light, Kettenbach GmbH & Co. KG; Eschenburg, Germany) was applied over the polymerized light-body on the veneer, followed by a heavy-body putty material (Zetaplus, Zhermack SpA; Badia Polesine, Italy) for support. The obtained replicas were sectioned vertically and horizontally through the center, examined under a stereomicroscope (Stereo Microscope, Leica Microsystems; Wetzlar, Germany), and the resulting images were transferred to the stereomicroscope’s measurement software (Leica Application Suite, Leica Microsystems; Wetzlar, Germany). On the software, cervical, incisal, distal, and mesial margin points were measured for each specimen. For internal fit, measurements were taken from 3 equidistant points in the cervical, middle, incisal, distal, and mesial regions (Fig. 5). A total of 19 points were measured for each specimen: 4 marginal points and 15 internal fit points [23]. Measurements at all points were repeated 3 times, and their averages were recorded.

Digital measurement of marginal and internal fit on a sectioned silicone replica

The adhesive cementation of the veneers was performed with a dual-cure resin cement (Variolink N Professional Kit, Ivoclar Vivadent Inc.; Schaan, Liechtenstein). The intaglio surfaces of the lithium disilicate veneers were etched with 5% hydrofluoric acid for 20 s, thoroughly rinsed with water, and dried. Silane was applied to the veneers, and the dual-cure resin cement was applied. The veneers were seated on the teeth with static finger pressure, following the aforementioned standardized protocol, and excess cement was cleaned with an applicator. The veneers were polymerized from all surfaces for 20 s with an LED light-curing unit (VALO Cordless, Ultradent Products Inc.; Utah, USA). Following polymerization, a glycerin gel (Liquid Strip, Ivoclar Vivadent Inc.; Schaan, Liechtenstein) was applied to all veneer margins.

For the fracture resistance test, a custom holder apparatus was prepared to position the teeth at a 135° angle to the vertical plane [32, 33]. The teeth were mounted on the holder with cold-curing acrylic supports, secured, and placed in a universal testing machine (Universal Test Machine, Instron; Massachusetts, USA) (Fig. 6). A force was applied at an angle of 135° to the vertical plane at a speed of 1 mm/min [32, 34, 35] until the veneers fractured or debonded, and the force value was recorded. After the fracture resistance test, the failure modes of the specimens were visually inspected and categorized as either ‘adhesive failure’ (complete debonding of the veneer from the tooth) or ‘fracture’ (cohesive failure of the ceramic material).

The fracture resistance test setup, showing the specimen angulated at 135° for load application

The normality of the marginal adaptation, internal adaptation, fracture resistance and failure modes data was evaluated with the Shapiro-Wilk test, while the homogeneity of variance between groups was assessed with the Levene test. When both assumptions were met, one-way ANOVA was used for group comparisons; otherwise, the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test was applied. When a statistically significant difference was detected between groups, pairwise comparisons were made using the Mann-Whitney U test. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS software (v27.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and the significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Results

The mean values for the marginal and internal fit of the test groups are presented in Tables 1 and 2, respectively. No statistically significant difference was observed among the groups in the gingival (p = 0.730), incisal (p = 0.811), and distal (p = 0.734) margins. However, the marginal gap of the CAV group was found to be statistically significantly larger than that of both the K and CR groups (p < 0.001). Similarly, no significant difference was detected among the groups in the internal fit at the cervical (p = 0.805), middle (p = 0.844), incisal (p = 0.871), and distal (p = 0.594) regions. In contrast, the cement thickness in the mesial internal region of the CAV group was found to be statistically significantly greater than that of the K and CR groups (p < 0.001).

Discussion

The primary purpose of this in-vitro study was to compare the effects of different restorative approaches in the presence of a proximal cavity on the fit and fracture resistance of lithium disilicate laminate veneers. The most significant finding of the study is that the fit of the group in which the cavity was directly incorporated into the veneer (CAV) was statistically poorer in the mesial region than the other groups, but this did not adversely affect the fracture resistance of the restoration. In light of these results, the null hypothesis of the study was rejected for mesial fit, while it was accepted for fracture resistance and fit in other regions.

In the present study, the finding that the mesial marginal and internal fit in the CAV group, where the cavity was incorporated into the veneer, was statistically significantly poorer than in the other groups reveals a potential challenge of this technique. This situation can be attributed to the complex internal surface geometry created by the cavity compared with a standard veneer preparation. Indeed, studies by Baig et al. [22] have also indicated that preparation designs with more complex geometries, such as a palatal chamfer, can adversely affect scanning and milling processes, leading to larger marginal gaps. The similar geometric challenge created by the integrated cavity design in the present study may explain this finding. In addition, as emphasized in the systematic review by Baig et al. [22], the 4-axis CAD/CAM systems used in the present study (reporting values in the 53–315 μm range) tend to produce higher marginal gap values compared with 5-axis systems (reporting values in the 71–84 μm range). This technological limitation offers another strong explanation for the increased gap values in the CAV group.

When the findings are compared with specific values in the literature, the marginal fit values obtained in the K and CR groups (~ 100–110 μm) are consistent with similar studies for CAD/CAM-fabricated lithium disilicate veneers, such as the 106.5 μm reported by Hanoon et al. [36], 104 μm reported by Baig et al. [22], and 99.7 μm reported by Kang et al. [29]. Although the higher value in the CAV group (~ 200 μm) may seem high at first glance, similar or even higher values have been reported in the literature. Indeed, Al-Dwairi et al. [37], in their study comparing different digital workflows, reported absolute marginal gaps of up to 289 μm, particularly in the incisal region. Therefore, the present finding remains within the wide range (53–315 μm) reported for 4-axis systems in the systematic review by Baig et al. [27].

When the clinical significance of this statistical difference is evaluated, it is seen that different acceptability limits are proposed in the literature. While some studies, such as Hanoon et al. [36] and Baig et al. [22], reference the 100–150 μm range, one of the most frequently cited studies by McLean and von Fraunhofer [38] specified the limit as 120 μm. However, it is clear that even the highest mean value obtained in the present study falls within the overall range (22–315 μm) reported for clinically used veneers in the systematic review by Baig et al. [27]. Therefore, it can be considered that this statistically detected difference may not translate to a clinically unacceptable misfit, and these values could likely be improved, particularly with a 5-axis fabrication technology.

Parallel to the marginal fit findings, no general difference in internal fit was detected among the groups in the present study, except for a statistically significantly higher cement gap observed only in the mesial region of the CAV group. The underlying reasons for this can be considered multifactorial. In addition to the complex geometry created by the cavity and the known limitations of 4-axis CAD/CAM systems, the lithium disilicate material itself may have contributed to this result. Indeed, Elbasty et al. [23] and Rizk et al. [39] have emphasized that lithium disilicate undergoes some shrinkage during the post-milling crystallization heat treatment, and this dimensional change can lead to an increased internal gap, especially in complex geometries. The mean internal fit values obtained in the K and CR groups in the present study (~ 150–160 μm) are positioned among similar studies in the literature. Although the present findings are slightly higher than the 106–114 μm range reported by Baig et al. [22] and the 121–134 μm range reported by Rizk et al. [39], they are consistent with the 195.5 μm value found by Yuce et al. [28] for the same material. The higher value in CAV group (~ 250 μm) can be considered a plausible outcome for such a technique, given the high value of 340 μm reported by Aboushelib et al. [26] and the wide range (68–340 μm) specified for 4-axis systems in the systematic review by Baig et al. [27].

The clinical significance of these values is a more complex issue. In the literature, the ideal cement gap recommended for the optimal performance of resin cements by Kusaba et al. [40] and Molin et al. [41] is generally 50–100 μm, and it is clear that the values in the present study are above this ideal range. It is known that increased cement thickness can increase the stress on the veneer itself, as stated by Ghasemi et al. [42], and may reduce the fracture resistance of the restoration, as expressed in the review by Baig et al. [27]. However, even though the ideal range was exceeded, the present findings remain within the broader ranges defined as “clinically acceptable” in the literature. Elbasty et al. [23] specified a range of 91–308 μm for partial ceramic restorations, while the systematic review by Baig et al. [27] reported a range of 61–208 μm for veneers. Therefore, it can be concluded that while the integrated cavity technique results in a thicker cement gap than ideal, this may still be within clinically acceptable limits.

One of the most important findings of the present study is that despite the apparent differences observed among the groups in terms of marginal and internal fit, no statistically significant difference was found in terms of fracture resistance (p = 0.516). This main result of the present study directly aligns with the findings of Sadighpour et al. [43], who also reported no significant difference in fracture resistance between veneers bonded to intact teeth and those bonded to teeth with Class III and Class IV composite restorations. The fact that the CAV group exhibited the highest mean fracture resistance despite having the poorest fit values requires careful examination of the underlying mechanisms. The most plausible explanation for this is the increased ceramic thickness in that region due to the veneer being designed to fill the cavity. This hypothesis is strongly supported by the study of Yıldız et al. [44], who showed that the fracture resistance of lithium disilicate veneers increased significantly when the thickness was increased from 0.5 mm to 0.7 mm. Furthermore, it is likely that the butt-joint incisal preparation design used in the present study also contributed to the overall high resistance values. A meta-analysis by da Costa et al. [45] revealed that the butt-joint preparation is the design that best preserves the original resistance of the tooth and leads to fewer ceramic fractures compared with other designs.

In terms of clinical significance, the lowest fracture resistance value recorded in present study, 337 N, is consistent with similar in-vitro studies in the literature and is even in the upper range of these values. For example, Tuğcu et al. [46] reported a mean of 322.86 N for lithium disilicate veneers with a 0.5 mm preparation depth, Alghazzawi et al. [33] reported a range of 263–306 N for a similar glass-ceramic material, and Gresnigt et al. [32] reported a fracture resistance of 264 N for conventional veneers. Most importantly, the values obtained in all groups are well above the physiological chewing forces in the anterior teeth. Gibbs et al. [47] reported that the mean occlusal force during mastication of hard foods reaches up to ~ 348 N (78.3 lbs). Similarly, Ferrario et al. [48] found that the maximal bite force in the incisor region of healthy young adults averaged between 139 and 146 N. The fact that even the lowest value in the present study is higher than the force required to chew hard indicates that all three tested approaches may provide clinically sufficient fracture resistance under normal functional forces.

Furthermore, the failure mode analysis revealed that the majority of failures in all groups were fractures rather than simple debonding. The lack of significant difference in failure patterns suggests that incorporating the proximal cavity directly into the veneer design (CAV group) does not lead to more catastrophic failure modes compared to conventional restorative strategies.

From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that for anterior teeth with existing cavities or restorations, a monolithic laminate veneer may represent an alternative restorative pathway. This single-unit approach simplifies the clinical workflow by eliminating the need for a separate intermediate restoration, thereby removing the composite-to-cement bond interface inherent in conventional techniques. Clinically, extending the veneer preparation into a proximal cavity inevitably involves greater dentin exposure compared to conventional veneer preparations that are primarily limited to enamel. Since bonding to dentin presents distinct biomechanical challenges, clinicians must pay meticulous attention to adhesive protocols, such as immediate dentin sealing, to ensure the long-term success and optimal bond strength of the integrated monolithic restoration. However, in terms of clinical relevance, this approach should currently be viewed as promising but preliminary. While it introduces a potentially useful concept, the current evidence is not yet strong enough to change routine clinical practice without further in-vitro aging studies and long-term clinical trials.

When interpreting the findings of this in-vitro study, certain limitations must be considered. First, the in-vitro nature of the study cannot fully replicate the complex biological and mechanical factors of the oral environment, such as saliva, pH changes, and multidirectional chewing forces. Similarly, only a single ceramic material (lithium disilicate) and a single fabrication technique utilizing a 4-axis milling system were tested in the study; it should not be overlooked that different materials or more advanced fabrication technologies could yield different results and improve the generalizability of these findings. Additionally, from an esthetic perspective, the increased ceramic thickness in the integrated cavity region may alter the light transmission of the lithium disilicate, potentially complicating the final shade match of the monolithic restoration. Furthermore, the anatomical model was restricted to a single tooth type, and the cavity design was limited to a standard-sized Class III cavity in a single location; how cavities of different sizes, locations, or varying tooth morphologies might affect the results is beyond the scope of this study. Finally a major limitations of the present study is the absence of artificial aging protocols, such as thermocycling or mechanical fatigue loading. As this research was designed as a proof-of-concept study to evaluate the immediate biomechanical influence and the fitting accuracy of the integrated cavity design, static loading was utilized to establish a baseline for structural integrity. While static tests provide essential initial data regarding the design’s geometry and immediate behavior, further studies incorporating long-term thermomechanical aging simulations are necessary to fully understand the performance and durability of this monolithic configuration under complex clinical conditions.

Although the findings of this study suggest that the integrated cavity technique could be a potential alternative, future studies are needed to elucidate the topic more comprehensively. First, it is essential that the results of this in-vitro study are validated by long-term, randomized controlled clinical trials that include all the variables of the oral environment. Furthermore, this study only examined lithium disilicate material and a 4-axis CAD/CAM system. A comparative evaluation of the success of this integrated cavity design with different materials and more precise fabrication technologies would offer clinicians a broader perspective.

Conclusions

This in-vitro study demonstrated that directly incorporating a proximal cavity into a monolithic laminate veneer design compromised marginal and internal fit specifically in the mesial region, but did not negatively affect the fracture resistance of the restoration. Notably, the integrated cavity group recorded the highest mean fracture resistance values, likely attributable to the increased ceramic thickness in the cavity region. However, it must be emphasized that these are preliminary laboratory findings based on an in-vitro model using acrylic teeth without thermomechanical aging. While the integrated cavity design introduces a promising concept, it is not yet a clinically proven option. Further in-vitro aging studies and long-term clinical trials are strictly required to validate these initial results before recommending this technique for routine clinical practice.
