Mechanistic insights into c-Met rs368750834 mutation and a bifunctional CAR-T strategy for serous ovarian carcinoma
Authors: Yanqiu Li, Haipeng Li, Yanjun Zhang, Jixuan Ye, Shuai Chen, Jingting Min, Luyuan Chang, Zhenghong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe c-Met gene encodes a protein known as the hepatocyte growth factor receptor (HGFR), which is frequently called the c-Met receptor. This gene and its encoded receptor play pivotal roles in normal physiological processes as well as in the initiation, progression, and metastasis of various diseases, particularly cancer. However, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying the missense mutation at residue 110 (valine to isoleucine) within this gene remain unexplored. This mutation occurs hypothesized to impact protein stability; yet, how it alters the three-dimensional structure, dynamic behavior, and functional properties of c-Met is unclear. In this study, we innovatively integrate high-precision structure prediction, protein model-based molecular dynamics simulations, and AI-driven thermodynamic stability analyses to systematically elucidate the molecular mechanism by which the residue 110 mutation disrupts protein structural integrity, modulates conformational dynamics, and ultimately impairs receptor function. Utilizing c-Met as a tumor-associated antigen, we developed a PD-1 antibody-secreting c-Met-targeted CAR-T cell and evaluated its cytotoxic efficacy against the serous ovarian cancer cell line SKOV-3 both in vitro and in vivo. This study reveals that mutation at position 110 of c-Met induces protein conformational reprogramming, transforming it from a stable, compact structural state into a more loosely organised and dynamically flexible conformation. Experimental evidence confirms that this structural alteration leads to significantly elevated protein expression levels of the V110I mutant in ovarian cancer cells compared to the wild-type, functionally demonstrating the substantive impact of altered conformational stability on protein metabolism. Building upon the identification of c-Met protein as a potential therapeutic target in ovarian cancer, we developed c-Met CAR-T cells capable of secreting anti-PD-1 antibodies. These cells exhibited markedly enhanced cytotoxicity against tumour cells overexpressing c-Met, accompanied by increased release of key effector cytokines such as interferon-γ. Introduction

Ovarian cancer is the eighth most prevalent malignancy among women globally and is one of the most lethal diseases affecting females, with over 70% of patients identified at advanced stages experiencing irreversible results [1, 2]. In 2020, ovarian cancer represented roughly 3.7% of all cancer diagnoses and 4.7% of cancer-related fatalities globally [3]. The most efficacious cancer prevention technique entails the preventive excision of the target organ before tumor formation. Bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (BSO) is the usual prophylactic intervention for women with germline pathogenic variations in BRCA genes, diminishing the risk of ovarian cancer by 80–90% and breast cancer by 50%[4]. However, in premenopausal women — especially younger individuals — BSO induces medically-induced premature menopause, resulting in significant endocrine disruption with deleterious effects on quality of life, sexual function, bone density homeostasis, and cardiovascular health [5]. Against this backdrop, revolutionary advances in chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy have offered transformative treatment paradigms for solid malignancies. Following the landmark success of CD19-targeted CAR-T in hematologic malignancies in 2017, advancements like the phase II clinical trial targeting CLDN18.2 in 2025 have validated the therapeutic potential of CAR-T in solid tumors [6]. This milestone holds strategic significance for the prevention and treatment of solid tumors, particularly ovarian cancer. Therefore, developing novel CAR-T strategies targeting serous ovarian carcinoma to improve patient prognosis is of urgent clinical importance.

The MET gene, located on chromosome 7q31.2, encodes the transmembrane receptor c-Met. The binding of its ligand, hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), triggers c-Met phosphorylation and activates downstream signaling pathways that facilitate cell proliferation, motility, and angiogenesis [7, 8]. Similar to many growth-regulating kinases, c-Met is present in around 70% of human ovarian cancer cases and is overexpressed in more than 30% of patients and cell lines [9, 10]. Of particular interest is the missense mutation at residue 110 of c-Met, substituting valine (Val) with isoleucine (Ile), which is hypothesized to affect protein stability, thereby impacting normal cell proliferation and angiogenesis, and potentially driving aberrant cell growth. Despite extensive research exploring c-Met’s role in cancer, mechanistic insights into how the residue 110 mutation specifically alters protein structure and function remain limited. Current studies primarily focus on genotype-phenotype correlations and associations between c-Met mutations and solid tumor progression [11–13], with insufficient investigation into the molecular effects on protein stability, three-dimensional conformation, folding dynamics, and functional modulation.

This study is significant in that it employs an integrative approach combining AlphaFold3 structure prediction, protein dynamics simulations (PDS), and Thermodynamic Mutant Selection Neural Network(ThermoMPNN) thermodynamic stability assessments to provide the first comprehensive analysis of the c-Met residue 110 mutation. By coupling molecular dynamics simulations with AI-based tools, we reveal how this mutation destabilizes c-Met. Following elucidation of the mutation’s mechanistic impact, we engineered a novel CAR-T therapy secreting PD-1 antibodies and targeting c-Met, which potently kills SKOV-3 cells and exhibits enhanced therapeutic efficacy and safety in vivo, underscoring its profound clinical potential.

Methods

Identification of missense mutation site in protein sequence

To precisely determine the impact of the mutation on the c-Met protein sequence, detailed queries were performed using the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Gene (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene) and Ensembl databases (https://www.ensembl.org/index.html?redirect=no). These platforms provide comprehensive gene annotations including gene function, sequence information, expression profiles, and disease associations, commonly utilized in clinical research.

Determine the mutation frequency of the c-Met protein

To determine the mutation frequency of the c-Met protein, we utilised the cBioPortal database (https://www.cbioportal.org/). By extracting TCGA (The Cancer Genome Atlas) data and selecting serous ovarian cancer datasets, we established its mutation frequency. The cBioPortal database is an international public repository integrating cancer genomic and clinical data from large-scale tumour research projects such as TCGA and ICGC (International Cancer Genome Consortium).

Retrieval of wild-type c-Met structure and amino acid sequence

Detailed information on the c-Met gene was obtained from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/), which offers insights into gene function, disease relevance, and expression patterns. Using this, the Ensembl transcript ENSP00000317272.6 of c-Met was identified. Subsequently, the corresponding amino acid sequence of the first 208 residues was downloaded from UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/) using the UniProt ID: P08581, specifying Homo sapiens as the species.

AI-Based modeling of c-Met mutant using AlphaFold3

AlphaFold3, the latest deep learning model for protein structure prediction, was employed to predict the three-dimensional structure of the c-Met mutant protein. The mutant amino acid sequence was submitted to the AlphaFold3 online server (https://alphafoldserver.com/), which automatically generated the predicted structure [14]. The resulting CIF (Crystallographic Information File) was downloaded and converted to PDB (Protein Data Bank) format using PyMOL 3.2 (http://www.pymol.org/pymol) Educational Edition for subsequent molecular simulations and analyses.To ensure a fair and accurate comparison of structural differences between the wild-type and mutant forms, we must employ identical computational methods for their construction. However, as no experimental structure for the V110I mutant exists in the PDB, AlphaFold3 simulations were utilised for both the wild-type and mutant forms of c-Met.

Molecular dynamics simulation of c-Met protein

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed with GROMACS version 2024.5 to investigate the in vivo behavior of the c-Met protein. The AMBER14SB force field was selected for protein parameterization due to its accuracy in modeling amino acid interactions. TIP3P was used as the solvent water model. Protein topology files were generated using pdb2gmx, and the protein was centered in a cubic simulation box maintaining a minimum distance of 1.0 nm from the box borders using editconf. The system was hydrated with water molecules via the solvate command. Energy minimization was executed via the steepest descent algorithm for 1000 steps with a maximum step size of 0.01 nm to eliminate steric clashes. Equilibration was conducted under Constant Number of particles, Volume, and Temperature (NVT) conditions for 100 ps followed by Constant Number of particles, Pressure, and Temperature (NPT) equilibration for 100 ps at 300 K and 1 atm. The production run comprised 100 ns under NPT conditions with a 2 femtosecond timestep, utilizing the SHAKE algorithm to limit hydrogen atom bonds. The simulation trajectories were used for downstream analyses and visualization. Throughout the simulations, RMSD, RMSF, radius of gyration (Rg), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) and Gibbs free energy were computed with the GROMACS utilities g_rmsd, g_rmsf, g_gyrate and g_sasa, respectively.

Prediction of free energy changes (ΔΔG°) of c-Met mutants using thermoMPNN

To further assess the thermal stability impact of c-Met mutations, ThermoMPNN (Thermodynamic Mutant Selection Neural Network), a deep learning-based predictor, was utilized. ThermoMPNN integrates extensive protein structural and mutational thermodynamic datasets to accurately predict the effects of amino acid substitutions on protein stability [15]. The model was run on the Google Colab platform with GPU support. After environment setup and dependencies installation, mutant c-Met PDB files were uploaded and analyzed using default ThermoMPNN parameters. Predictions included melting temperature shifts, thermal stability scores, and potential structural impacts of mutations.

Validation of c-Met and PD-L1 expression using gene expression profiling interactive analysis (GEPIA) database

Expression levels of c-Met and PD-L1 (CD274) in ovarian cancer were analyzed via the GEPIA database (https://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/), an interactive web tool based on TCGA and Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) datasets [16]. Transcript per million (TPM) boxplots were generated to visualize expression differences in ovarian serous carcinoma.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

To assess protein expression of c-Met and PD-L1 in ovarian serous carcinoma, 32 surgically resected ovarian cancer tissue samples and 20 matched adjacent normal tissues (> 5 cm from tumor margin) were obtained from the First Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical College. Samples were preserved in formalin for 24 h, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Sections were dewaxed in xylene, rehydrated using graded ethanol, and underwent heat-induced antigen retrieval in citrate buffer for 20 min. Following permeabilization with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min and subsequent blocking with 3% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) for 30 min at ambient temperature, primary and secondary antibodies were applied per manufacturer protocols. Diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen was developed under microscopic monitoring until brown staining appeared, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining, graded ethanol dehydration, xylene clearing, and neutral resin mounting. Two pathologists independently scored staining intensity (0–3) and positive cell percentage (0–3) based on membrane staining. Scores were combined (intensity × positivity) to yield final scores categorized as negative (0–1), ± (2), + (3–5), ++ (6–8), +++ (> 8). Discrepancies were resolved by a gynecologic pathologist.

Western blot (WB)

To establish a cellular model overexpressing the c-Met(V110I) mutant, the pcDNA3.1-c-Met plasmid was transfected into SKOV-3 cells using Neofect™ transfection reagent. Twenty-four hours prior to transfection, viable SKOV-3 cells were seeded into 6-well plates. For transfection, 1 µg of plasmid DNA was resuspended in 50 µL of medium, gently pipetted to mix, then 1 µL of Neofect™ transfection reagent was added. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 15 min to form a complex. A total of 100 µL of the complex was then added dropwise to the cell culture medium. Cells were harvested 48–72 h post-transfection for subsequent protein expression validation. Total proteins were extracted from normal ovarian epithelial cells, SKOV-3 cells, and mutant SKOV-3 cells. Following protein concentration measurement via the Bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay, equal volumes of protein samples were subjected to Sodium Dodecyl-Sulfate Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis(SDS-PAGE) gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. Following blocking at room temperature, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against c-Met and GAPDH (internal control). Subsequent incubation with Horseradish Peroxidase (HRP-labelled) secondary antibody at room temperature was performed, followed by visualisation using ECL ultra-sensitive chemiluminescent reagent. Band intensity was quantified via ImageJ software analysis.

Flow cytometry analysis of c-Met and PD-L1 expression in ovarian cancer cells

Expression of c-Met and PD-L1 proteins on human serous ovarian cancer cell lines SKOV-3 and A2780 was analyzed by flow cytometry. Cells (1 × 10^6) were harvested, rinsed with Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS), and resuspended in PBS supplemented with 1% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS). Cells were divided into three aliquots and incubated with FITC-conjugated anti-human c-Met, PE-conjugated anti-human PD-L1 antibodies, or no antibody (control) at 4 °C in the dark for 30 min. Following washing, cells were resuspended in PBS and subsequently examined using a flow cytometer. Data were analyzed via FlowJo software.

Lentiviral vector construction and packaging

The c-Met CAR construct comprised a c-Met-specific single-chain variable fragment (scFv), CD8α hinge and transmembrane domains, 4-1BB co-stimulatory domain, and CD3ζ signaling domain. A PD-1 scFv secreting cassette was introduced via a porcine teschovirus-1 2 A (P2A) self-cleaving peptide to generate a dual-function c-Met/PD-1 CAR. Both CAR sequences were cloned into the lentiviral transfer vector GV401 (GeneChem, containing EF-1α promoter and WPRE enhancer). Lentivirus was produced by co-transfection of 293 T cells with transfer plasmid, VSVG, and packaging plasmid (PSPAX2) plasmids at a 4:3:2 ratio using Lipofectamine 2000. Viral supernatant was harvested 72 h post-transfection and concentrated by ultracentrifugation. Viral titers > 1 × 10^8 TU/mL were confirmed by quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR), and CAR expression validated by flow cytometry. All procedures were performed by GeneChem.

Generation of PD-1/c-Met CAR-T and c-Met CAR-T cells

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were extracted from healthy adult volunteers and grown in RPMI-1640 complete media supplemented with Interleukin 2 (IL-2). T cells were stimulated on CD3/CD28 antibody-coated 48-well plates for 24 h. Plates pre-coated with Retronectin were incubated overnight at 4 °C. Activated T cells (1 × 10^6 cells/mL) were seeded and transduced at Multiplicity of Infection (MOI) = 5 with lentivirus encoding either c-Met-targeted CAR or PD-1 antibody-secreting c-Met-targeted CAR constructs in the presence of 8 µg/mL polybrene. Following a 6-hour incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, additional medium with IL-2 was added. Transduction efficiency was preliminarily assessed by Green Fluorescent Protein (GFP) fluorescence under microscopy at 48 h. Cells were expanded when confluence reached 80%.

CAR-T positivity and subtype analysis

On day 4 post-transduction, CAR-T cells (1 × 10^6) were collected and resuspended in PBS. GFP expression was detected by flow cytometry FITC channel to assess CAR positivity. For T cell subtype analysis, cells were stained with CD3 - APC, CD4 - PE, and CD8 - PerCP5.5 antibodies. Single-color controls were included. After incubation at 4 °C for 30 min in the dark and washing, cells were analyzed by flow cytometry to determine the proportion of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ CAR-T subsets.

In vitro proliferation assay of CAR-T cells (CCK-8)

The impact of PD-1/c-Met CAR-T cells on tumor cell proliferation was evaluated by CCK-8 assay. SKOV-3 and A2780 were inoculated at a density of 1 × 10^5 cells/mL in 96-well plates (100 µL/well) and incubated for 24 h. Cells were rinsed with PBS and co-cultured with T cells, c-Met CAR-T cells, or PD-1/c-Met CAR-T cells at 2 × 10^5 cells/mL (200 µL/well) for 24, 48, and 72 h. Control wells without effector cells or without washing were included. After incubation, 20 µL of CCK-8 reagent was introduced and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 1 h. Optical density was assessed at 450 nm. Proliferation index was calculated as (OD of wells without washing − OD of washed wells)/OD at 0 h. Total effector cell count was derived accordingly.

Cytokine secretion assay (ELISA)

Effector cytokine secretion (IL-2, TNF-α, IFN-γ) upon target cell stimulation was quantified by ELISA. SKOV-3 and A2780 cells were adjusted to 1 × 10^6 cells/mL; T cells and CAR-T cells were adjusted to 1 × 10^5 cells/mL. Equal volumes (100 µL each) of target and effector cells were co-cultured in 96 - well round-bottom plates for a duration of 24 h. Supernatants were collected and analyzed using ELISA kits (ELISA Bio, China) according to manufacturer instructions. Cytokine concentrations were determined by OD measurements.

In vitro cytotoxicity assay (LDH Release)

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release was used to assess cytotoxicity. Effector and target cells (SKOV-3, A2780, T cells, c-Met CAR-T, PD-1/c-Met CAR-T) were co-cultured at effector-to-target (E: T) ratios of 5 : 1, 10 : 1, and 20 : 1 in U-bottom 96-well plates (200 µL total volume). After 7 h, lysis buffer (20 µL) was added to maximum release control wells. At 8 h, plates were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min. Supernatants were mixed with LDH substrate (1 : 2), incubated in in darkness for 1 h, and absorbance assessed at 490 nm. Cytotoxicity (%) was calculated based on corrected OD values as per established formula.

In vivo CAR-T cytotoxicity and safety evaluation

SKOV-3 cells were transduced with Red Fluorescent Protein-lentivirus (RFP-lentivirus) at MOI = 10 and selected with 0.5 µg/mL puromycin to establish SKOV3-luc cells. Cells were resuspended in Matrigel and PBS, and subcutaneously injected into the right inguinal region of female mice (2.0 × 10^6 cells/mouse). Upon tumors reaching ~ 100 mm³, Select 20 mice were randomized into 4 groups (n = 5). Starting on day 21, tumor volumes and body weights were measured every 3 days. On days 21 and 28, groups received intratumoral injections of T cells, c-Met CAR-T cells, PD-1/c-Met CAR-T cells (1 × 10^8 cells/mL, 100 µL), or PBS control. On day 35, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and tumor bioluminescence imaged using an In Vivo Imaging System (IVIS) Spectrum2 system. Major organs and tumors were harvested, fixed, and processed for hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining and immunohistochemistry (c-Met, PD-L1, Ki67, Caspase-3; Sevier, China).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted utilizing R 4.3.1, SPSS 24.0, and GraphPad Prism 9. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). Intergroup comparisons were conducted using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All experiments were independently repeated three times.

Results

Confidence analysis of structural modeling results

To systematically define the genomic profile of c-Met in ovarian cancer, we aggregated and analyzed data from cBioPortal database. Results indicate that the MET gene is prone to mutation in serous ovarian cancer, with a mutation rate of 5%. (Fig. 1A, B). Our analysis encompassed copy number variation, somatic mutations, and structural variants, establishing a comprehensive genetic background for this study. We subsequently employed AlphaFold3 to model the three-dimensional structure of the c-Met domain, the critical region harboring the V110I mutation. The predicted model exhibits high confidence, with a predicted template modeling (pTM) score of 0.77 and a joint pTM (jpTM) score further supporting the model’s accuracy (Fig. 1C). The per-residue confidence metric (pLDDT) indicates that the core of the domain, including the V110 site, is modeled with high to very high confidence (pLDDT > 70). The proportion of pLDDT < 70 accounts for 27%. The V110 residue itself is situated within a well-folded, confident region of the protein, providing a reliable structural context for investigating the mechanistic impact of the valine-to-isoleucine substitution. This structural model served as the foundation for our subsequent molecular dynamics simulations.

Analysis of molecular dynamics simulation results

In molecular dynamics simulations, we employed RMSD, RMSF, SASA and Rg to compare the kinetic stability of wild-type c-Met and mutant c-Met, and conducted repeated experiments (Figure S1, S2). To ensure direct comparability of RMSD values between wild-type and mutant simulations, the RMSD analysis for wild-type simulations used the predicted wild-type structure as the reference. For mutant simulations, models were constructed by replacing the mutation site in the wild-type structure, followed by molecular dynamics simulations. Results indicate that mutant c-Met exhibits higher overall RMSD values and greater fluctuations compared to the wild-type, suggesting reduced overall conformational stability. RMSF analysis further demonstrated significantly enhanced fluctuations in residues at the mutation site and its neighbouring regions, reflecting increased local flexibility. SASA results indicated that mutant c-Met possessed a larger solvent-accessible surface area than the wild-type, suggesting a more open conformation with increased exposed regions. Concurrently, the mutant exhibited a larger radius of gyration (Rg) with greater fluctuation amplitude, implying a tendency towards looser overall folding. The mutation induces a shift in c-Met from its original stable, compact conformation towards a more unstable, loosely folded, and flexible structural profile. This conformational alteration may impair its binding efficiency with ligands or inhibitors, potentially compromising subsequent signal transduction functions (Fig. 2A-D).

The missense mutation at residue 110, substituting valine with isoleucine, was analyzed for its impact on protein stability. Structural mapping highlights residue 110 in red, indicating a mutation-sensitive region where alterations are likely to significantly affect protein function. Correspondingly, the heatmap on the right shows the mutation to isoleucine at position 110 also marked in red, with an increased ΔΔG value, signifying a rise in free energy. Calculations indicate that the free energy change resulting from the V110I mutation is ΔΔG = + 0.25 kcal/mol. This increase reflects reduced structural stability of the mutant c-Met protein, potentially compromising its native conformational dynamics and functional integrity. Consequently, the mutation may impair c-Met’s physiological activity and contribute to functional deficits (Figs. 3A, B, Table S1).

Immunohistochemical analysis of c-Met and PD-L1 expression in ovarian cancer tissues

Immunohistochemistry was performed to assess c-Met and PD-L1 expression in 32 ovarian cancer tissue samples and 20 adjacent normal tissues located > 5 cm from tumor margins. Positive staining was defined as the presence of brown-yellow granules on the cell membrane and/or in the cytoplasm (Fig. 5), independently evaluated in a double-blinded manner by two experienced pathologists. PD-L1 positivity was observed in 90.6% (29/32) of cancer tissues, markedly exceeding that in adjacent normal tissues (1/20, 5%) (P < 0.001) (Table 2). These results demonstrate that both c-Met and PD-L1 are highly expressed in serous ovarian carcinoma tissues, whereas their expression is low or absent in adjacent normal tissues.

Comparison of CAR-T cell subsets by flow cytometry

Following lentiviral transduction, the ratio of CD8⁺ to CD4⁺T cells underwent significant changes (Figur 8C). Using CD3⁺gating, flow cytometric analysis revealed that the CD8⁺/CD4⁺ ratio in PD-1 antibody-secreting c-Met-targeted CAR-T cells was 1.17 ± 0.03, significantly higher than that in c-Met-targeted CAR-T cells (0.81 ± 0.02) and untransduced T cells (0.19 ± 0.01). The elevated CD8⁺/CD4⁺ ratio observed in PD-1 antibody-secreting c-Met-targeted CAR-T cells was statistically significant compared to all other groups (P < 0.001) (Fig. 8D).

Proliferation, cytotoxicity, and cytokine secretion of CAR-T cells following target cell stimulation

The proliferative responses of untransduced T cells, c-Met CAR-T cells, PD-1 antibody-secreting c-Met-targeted CAR-T cells were evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. Effector cells from each cohort were co-cultured with the ovarian cancer cell lines SKOV-3 and A2780, and proliferation activity was measured. Upon stimulation by both SKOV-3 and A2780 cells, PD-1 antibody-secreting c-Met-targeted CAR-T cells exhibited significantly increased proliferation compared to c-Met-targeted CAR-T cells and untransduced T cells (P < 0.01) (Figs. 9A, B). Cytotoxic activity was assessed by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay following an 8-hour co-culture of effector and target cells. The killing efficiency of PD-1 antibody-secreting c-Met-targeted CAR-T cells was markedly higher than that of c-Met CAR-T cells and untransduced T cells, particularly at an effector-to-target (E: T) ratio of 20:1, where cytotoxicity reached 63.4 ± 3.74% and 28.67 ± 2.52%, respectively (P < 0.01) (Figs. 9C, D). Furthermore, cytokine secretion was quantified by Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) after 24 h of co-culture. PD-1 antibody-secreting c-Met-targeted CAR-T cells produced significantly higher levels of key cytokines, including IL-2, TNF-α, and IFN-γ, compared to both c-Met CAR-T cells and untransduced T cells (P < 0.01) (Fig. 9E-J).

In vivo antitumor efficacy of PD-1/c-Met CAR-T cells in a severe immunodeficient mouse model of ovarian cancer

Three weeks after subcutaneous inoculation of SKOV-3-luc ovarian cancer cells, mice bearing tumors with volumes reaching 100 mm³ were selected and randomly assigned to receive intratumoral injections of c-Met-targeted CAR-T cells, PD-1 antibody-secreting c-Met-targeted CAR-T cells, activated T cells, or PBS control. Treatments were administered once weekly for two consecutive weeks (schematic workflow shown in Fig. 10A). Mouse body weight and tumor volume were monitored every three days and plotted as growth curves (Fig. 10B, C). At the end of treatment, in vivo bioluminescence imaging revealed markedly reduced tumor fluorescence intensity in mice treated with PD-1 antibody-secreting c-Met-targeted CAR-T cells compared to those receiving c-Met-targeted CAR-T cells, activated T cells, or PBS. The c-Met-targeted CAR-T cells group showed lower fluorescence intensity than the activated T cell and PBS groups, while no significant difference was observed between the activated T cell and PBS groups (Fig. 10D). Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation according to institutional animal care guidelines and laid flat on absorbent paper for subsequent tissue collection(Fig. 10E). Ex vivo tumor size analysis (Fig. 10F) further confirmed that tumors in mice treated with PD-1 antibody-secreting c-Met-targeted CAR-T cells were significantly smaller than those in mice treated with c-Met-targeted CAR-T cells, activated T cells, or PBS (p < 0.01). No significant difference in tumor size was observed between the activated T cell and PBS groups throughout the treatment period.

Histopathological and immunohistochemical analysis of major organs and tumor tissues in NOD Scid gamma (NSG) mice

In the SKOV3-luc subcutaneous tumor model established in NSG mice, animals were sacrificed on day 35, and major organs including heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney were harvested for HE staining. Histological examination revealed that myocardial tissues in all groups displayed regular striated architecture with tightly arranged cardiomyocytes containing centrally located oval nuclei. Minimal interstitial spaces were observed, with no evident inflammatory cell infiltration or fibrosis. Liver sections showed intact hepatic lobule structures with hepatocytes arranged radially around the central vein, exhibiting typical polygonal morphology, centrally placed nuclei, and clear cytoplasm, without signs of necrosis or inflammation. The spleen demonstrated clear demarcation between white and red pulp; lymphocytes in the white pulp were densely packed, while the red pulp contained abundant blood cells, with no pathological alterations detected. Pulmonary tissues exhibited intact alveolar structures with thin alveolar walls rich in capillaries, and well-organized epithelial cells. There was no evidence of fibrosis, alveolar damage, or congestion. Renal histology showed preserved glomerular architecture without proliferation or sclerosis, and renal tubules maintained normal structure and cellular organization without degeneration or necrosis (Fig. 11). IHC analysis of tumor xenografts demonstrated a significant reduction in c-Met expression in tumors from mice treated with both PD-1 antibody-secreting c-Met-targeted CAR-T cells and c-Met-targeted CAR-T cells, compared to PBS and activated T cell controls, with the group receiving PD-1 antibody-secreting c-Met-targeted CAR-T cells exhibiting the lowest staining intensity. PD-L1 expression levels did not differ significantly among groups. Ki67 proliferation marker staining showed a marked decrease in tumor cell proliferation in the PD-1 antibody-secreting c-Met-targeted CAR-T cells group relative to other groups, indicating effective tumor growth suppression. Furthermore, Caspase-3, an apoptosis marker, was significantly upregulated in tumors from the PD-1 antibody-secreting c-Met-targeted CAR-T cells group compared to c-Met-targeted CAR-T cells group, activated T cells, and PBS groups, demonstrating that CAR-T cells exert antitumor effects in part via induction of tumor cell apoptosis (Fig. 12).

Discussion

Molecular dynamics simulations revealed that the mutant c-Met exhibited higher RMSD and Rg values compared to the wild-type, suggesting a loosening of its overall structure. Concurrently, increased RMSF and SASA values indicated that the mutation enhanced local flexibility and increased the protein’s exposure to the solvent. This mutation-induced conformational instability may have impacted c-Met’s conformational regulation and degradation pathways. Previous studies have demonstrated that spatial conformational changes in receptor tyrosine kinases may diminish their binding efficiency with molecules involved in ubiquitination or lysosomal degradation, thereby prolonging their half-life. Corroborating our Western blot results, the expression levels of the mutated c-Met were markedly elevated. This is hypothesised to stem from the mutation either reducing the protein degradation rate or enhancing post-translational stability, leading to c-Met accumulation within cells. Thus, this mutation not only alters the structural stability of c-Met but may also lead to its elevated expression within cells by regulating protein degradation and homeostasis mechanisms [7]. It should be emphasised that the results obtained through simulation and prediction are for reference only. The analysis of structural stability in this study relies on computational predictions, and this conclusion requires direct validation through biophysical experiments such as differential scanning calorimetry in future work.

Biologically, this study originated from a computational-biology-driven hypothesis: the missense mutation at residue 110 of c-Met (Val→Ile) could alter the conformational stability of its SEMA domain and thereby modulate protein function and expression. To test this, we first performed molecular-dynamics simulations that predicted a local destabilization of the c-Met structure induced by the mutation. Western-blot and flow-cytometry analyses subsequently confirmed that the mutation indeed up-regulates c-Met expression in an ovarian-cancer model, and ovarian-cancer patients express significantly higher c-Met levels than healthy controls, establishing c-Met as an experimentally validated therapeutic target. Building on these in-silico predictions and experimental findings, and considering the immune-suppressive microenvironment of ovarian cancer, we reasoned that a bifunctional CAR-T cell simultaneously targeting c-Met while locally reversing immunosuppression might elicit synergistic anti-tumor activity. Ultimately, in-vitro and in-vivo experiments demonstrated that c-Met-directed CAR-T cells engineered to secrete PD-1 antibodies confer markedly enhanced therapeutic efficacy in ovarian-cancer models, thereby closing the logical loop from mechanistic prediction to therapeutic validation.

MD simulations and thermal stability analyses further revealed that this mutation markedly increases global conformational flexibility while reducing thermodynamic stability, changes that may compromise HGF-binding affinity and perturb receptor dimerization, thereby impairing precise regulation of downstream Rat Sarcoma/Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase (PI3K/AKT), RAS/MAPK, and Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (STAT3) pathways. Prior research has shown that conformational perturbations in c-Met al.ter the phosphorylation patterns of intracellular tyrosine residues, modulating tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and immune regulatory functions [8]. Here, we provide direct MD-based evidence for this phenomenon, establishing a mechanistic rationale for deploying high-affinity targeted immunotherapies in the context of this specific mutation. It should be noted that while this study identified a potential association between the V110I mutation and altered protein function, the specific mechanism of action within downstream signaling pathways remains incompletely elucidated. The proposed mechanism based on existing literature constitutes scientific speculation, and experimental validation of this hypothesis will serve as a key focus of our subsequent research. The core objective of the current work is to validate the efficacy of a dual-functional CAR-T therapeutic strategy targeting this mutant. A systematic analysis of the signaling pathways affected by the mutation will require dedicated future studies.

Immunohistochemistry and flow cytometry analyses further demonstrated a pronounced co-overexpression of c-Met and PD-L1 in both ovarian cancer tissues and cell lines, which was closely associated with immune cell infiltration. This molecular co-expression pattern suggests the formation of a “dual immune barrier”: on one hand, c-Met accelerates tumor progression by promoting cellular proliferation, invasion, and angiogenesis; on the other hand, PD-L1 binds to PD-1 on T cells, inducing effector T-cell exhaustion and establishing an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Previous studies have shown that c-Met signaling can transcriptionally upregulate PD-L1 expression through the Janus Kinase/Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription (JAK/STAT) and Nuclear Factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) pathways [17–19], implying a potential positive feedback loop between these two molecules. Based on this mechanistic insight, we rationally engineered a PD-1 antibody–secreting c-Met CAR-T cell designed to simultaneously block c-Met–driven tumor growth signals and PD-1/PD-L1–mediated immunosuppression within the tumor microenvironment.

Compared with existing literature, this study demonstrates innovation in both target selection and immunoregulatory strategy. Previous investigations have examined various loci within the MET gene. For example, a functional promoter single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) in MET (rs1858830) has been linked to autism spectrum disorder (ASD) in Caucasian and Japanese populations [20], while SNP rs38845 shows association with ASD in Chinese populations [21]. The MET-T1010I SNP has been implicated in breast cancer progression [22]. In contrast to these earlier studies, our work not only identifies a specific pathogenic mutation site but also, for the first time, systematically investigates the structural and functional consequences of the c-Met residue 110 missense mutation by integrating AlphaFold3 structure prediction, molecular dynamics simulations, and ThermoMPNN-based thermodynamic stability modeling. Using AlphaFold3, we generated a high-confidence c-Met structural model and subsequently assessed its stability via molecular dynamics simulations. RMSD and RMSF analyses revealed that the mutation induces global destabilization and increased local flexibility, thereby disrupting the stability of critical functional domains. Comparative dynamic evolution between the wild-type and mutant proteins provided realistic simulation data that further corroborated the link between structural instability and functional impairment. Thermodynamic analysis using the ThermoMPNN model predicted a significantly elevated ΔΔG° value for the mutant, indicating a substantial reduction in structural stability and a concomitant loss of functional integrity.

Historically, clinical strategies targeting c-Met have predominantly focused on small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitors (e.g., crizotinib, savolitinib) and monoclonal antibodies (e.g., onartuzumab). However, in ovarian cancer, their objective response rates have generally remained below 20%, with high incidences of acquired resistance [23–25]. In the field of CAR-T therapy, preliminary efforts have explored c-Met as a target for solid tumors, yet efficacy has often been hindered by the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [26, 27]. More recently, systemic PD-1 blockade has been combined with CAR-T therapy to overcome such immune suppression [28], which can enhance antitumor activity but is frequently accompanied by a markedly increased incidence of immune-related adverse events. In this study, we engineered PD-1/c-Met CAR-T cells capable of sustained, localized release of PD-1 antibodies within the tumor site, thereby achieving spatially and temporally precise immunomodulation. This design effectively augmented antitumor efficacy while minimizing systemic immune toxicity. Furthermore, by elucidating the mechanistic impact of the c-Met mutation prior to CAR-T design, we ensured a higher degree of target specificity and optimized immune engagement. It should be noted that the evaluation of treatment safety primarily relied on histopathological examination, which serves as a gold standard for detecting morphological alterations in tissues. However, we recognize that the lack of serum biochemical analysis represents a limitation. Histopathology identifies structural damage, whereas biochemical parameters reflect the functional consequences of such damage. Incorporating serum chemistry in future studies would be invaluable for correlating microscopic tissue changes with systemic functional metrics, such as liver enzyme levels or renal function markers, to obtain a more holistic understanding of the treatment’s physiological impact.

The functional validation further confirmed the therapeutic effectiveness of this technique. PD-1/c-Met CAR-T cells displayed a markedly bigger proportion of CD8⁺ T cells, increased in vitro cytotoxicity, expanded proliferative capability, and heightened cytokine secretion relative to conventional c-Met CAR-T cells, and exhibited superior tumor suppression in vivo. Elevated levels of IL-2 and IFN-γ not only sustained prolonged T-cell activation but also likely modulated the tumor chemokine milieu to promote recruitment of endogenous immune effector populations, thereby triggering a broader antitumor immune response. Notably, PD-1/c-Met CAR-T cells maintained robust proliferative activity for up to 72 h post-stimulation, a feature of particular relevance for ovarian cancer patients who require durable immune surveillance to prevent relapse.

Despite the clear translational potential of our findings, several limitations warrant consideration. First, this study relies primarily on AI-based modeling and computational simulations, lacking direct experimental validation. Future work should incorporate protein expression and functional assays to corroborate these predictions. Although the ThermoMPNN model demonstrates high accuracy in thermodynamic stability estimation, its predictive power for multi-site mutations or synergistic effects remains limited; integrating broader experimental datasets and complementary computational frameworks may enhance predictive precision.

Second, while locally secreted PD-1 antibodies may reduce systemic toxicity, their long-term safety and the in vivo persistence of CAR-T cells require further evaluation in large-animal models and early-phase clinical trials. Currently, our CAR-T cells were generated using viral vector–based manufacturing; however, non-viral approaches are increasingly being explored. Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats/CRISPR-associated protein 9-mediated T-cell receptor alpha constant (CRISPR/Cas9-mediated TRAC) locus knockout enables the production of universal CAR-T cells that bypass autologous constraints and have demonstrated enhanced antitumor activity [29]. Similarly, “armored” CAR-T cells engineered to secrete cytokines—such as IL-15—have shown remarkable tumor infiltration and cytotoxicity in xenograft models of lung cancer, neuroblastoma, and glioblastoma [30, 31]. Incorporating such modifications could further potentiate therapeutic efficacy.

Third, tumor heterogeneity and antigen escape may limit the coverage of single-target CAR-T therapy, underscoring the potential of dual- or multi-target CAR designs to broaden applicability. Finally, the immunosuppressive network in ovarian cancer is multifaceted; in addition to the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, molecules such as Transforming Growth Factor-beta (TGF-β), T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM-3), and Lymphocyte-activation gene 3 (LAG-3) also contribute to T-cell dysfunction [32, 33]. Comprehensive mechanistic dissection of these pathways will be essential for next-generation CAR-T optimization.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we identify a c-Met mutation could drive mechanism that promotes ovarian cancer progression and immune evasion, and exploit this knowledge to design a bifunctional CAR-T cell capable of local PD-1 blockade. Structural modelling, molecular dynamics and thermodynamic prediction reveal that the c-Met variant destabilizes the protein. Functional assays show that PD-1/c-Met CAR-T cells enhance cytotoxicity, proliferation and cytokine release, and remodel the tumour immune microenvironment more effectively than conventional c-Met CAR-T cells. This approach overcomes microenvironment-mediated resistance while limiting systemic toxicity, offering a strategy with translational potential for c-Met–overexpressing solid tumours. However, it should be emphasized that the in vivo findings are limited to mouse xenograft models. Caution is therefore warranted in extrapolating these results to the clinical setting, and further validation in more clinically relevant models and ultimately in clinical trials will be essential.
