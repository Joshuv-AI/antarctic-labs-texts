Metal-Organic Framework-Based and Metal-Organic Framework-Derived Nanomaterials for Cancer Theranostics and Antibacterial Applications: Advances, Challenges, and Perspectives
Authors: Yiming Wang, Jie Zhang, Can Zhang, Xinyi Tian, Liudi Gu, Xue Liang, Nan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetal-organic frameworks (MOFs), constructed through coordination-driven self-assembly of metal ions/clusters and organic linkers, have emerged as a uniquely versatile class of porous nanomaterials with broad biomedical potential. Despite substantial clinical progress, both oncological treatment and antimicrobial intervention remain constrained by inadequate tumor-targeting selectivity, multidrug resistance, immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments, and the global proliferation of antibiotic-resistant pathogens, limitations that conventional nanocarrier platforms have addressed only in part. MOF-based and MOF-derived nanomaterials, distinguished by tunable pore architecture, structurally and compositionally adaptable metal nodes, high surface areas, and stimulus-responsive degradability, offer a rational framework for overcoming these barriers. This review systematically examines the synthetic strategies underlying MOF-based and MOF-derived nanomaterials, including pyrolysis, chemical etching, composite modification, and functional group introduction, and their structural determinants of performance. In cancer theranostics, we critically evaluate their roles as multimodal imaging contrast agents, stimulus-responsive drug delivery carriers, and platforms for combination therapies encompassing photodynamic, photothermal, chemodynamic, and immunomodulatory modalities. In antibacterial applications, we analyze the mechanistic basis of MOF-based and MOF-derived activity, including physical membrane disruption, reactive oxygen species-mediated oxidative stress, and sustained metal ion release, alongside strategies targeting biofilm formation and antibiotic resistance. Multifunctional platforms that concurrently integrate cancer theranostic and antibacterial capabilities are further discussed. This review also addresses the principal barriers to clinical translation, encompassing large-scale manufacturing, long-term biosafety, and regulatory approval, and proposes future directions incorporating artificial intelligence-assisted design and materials genomics, underscoring the transformative potential of MOF-based and MOF-derived nanomaterials as next-generation precision nanomedicines. This review establishes a unified mechanistic framework grounded in the intrinsic physicochemical properties of MOF-derived nanomaterials, systematically integrating their applications in cancer theranostics and antibacterial therapy. Critically, it bridges fundamental advances with translational reality by incorporating a rigorous assessment of regulatory pathways, scalable manufacturing constraints, and clinical implementation barriers, and offers a comprehensive, practice-oriented reference for the rational design and responsible translation of MOF-based and MOF-derived nanomaterials.

Introduction

In recent decades, cancer has remained one of the foremost threats to global human health.1,2 Despite significant advances in surgical techniques, cytotoxic chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immune checkpoint blockade, the five-year survival rates for many solid tumors, including lung, pancreatic, and esophageal cancers, indicating the persistent gap between therapeutic innovation and clinical outcomes.2,3 Compounding this challenge, cancer patients face a disproportionately elevated risk of bacterial infection due to treatment-induced immunosuppression, mucosal barrier disruption, and the increasing use of indwelling medical devices.4,5 Bacterial infections have emerged as a parallel global health crisis: antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is projected to claim 10 million lives annually by 2050,6 driven by the indiscriminate overuse of conventional antibiotics and the alarming proliferation of multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens.5,7 These make the co-occurrence of malignancy and infection a clinically urgent and underserved therapeutic challenge.4

Conventional cancer therapies continue to suffer from critical limitations that substantially compromise clinical outcomes. Systemic chemotherapeutic agents lack tumor selectivity, resulting in widespread off-target cytotoxicity, dose-limiting systemic adverse effects, and a narrow therapeutic window.3,8 The absence of spatiotemporal control over drug release leads to subtherapeutic intratumoral concentrations alongside toxicity in healthy tissues.8 Equally problematic is MDR, driven by drug efflux pump overexpression, enhanced DNA damage repair, and dysregulation of apoptotic pathways.4,9 Beyond pharmacological resistance, the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME)-characterized by hypoxia, elevated glutathione (GSH), weak acidity (pH 5.5–6.5), and immunosuppressive cellular components including M2-polarized tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and regulatory T cells—further undermines both cytotoxic and immune-based strategies.10–13 In the antibacterial domain, conventional antibiotics increasingly fail against biofilm-embedded and MDR pathogens,4,5,7 while the development pipeline for novel antibiotics has stagnated.5,6 Traditional nanocarriers, including liposomes and polymeric micelles, partially address bioavailability issues but still face challenges of premature drug leakage, insufficient tumor penetration, and limited functional programmability.8,14 These converging limitations collectively underscore the urgent need for a new generation of intelligent, multifunctional nanomedicines.

Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), constructed through the coordination-driven self-assembly of metal ions or clusters with bridging organic linkers, have emerged as a uniquely versatile class of porous materials with compelling potential to address these unmet needs.15–17 MOFs exhibit one-, two-, or three-dimensional infinite network architectures with exceptional surface areas, tunable porosity, compositionally diverse metal nodes, and readily modifiable organic linkers—properties unmatched by any single conventional nanocarrier class.15,16,18,19 Their high surface area and adjustable pore geometry enable high-payload drug encapsulation,17,18 while intrinsic metal-node reactivity—particularly iron, copper, zinc, and hafnium nodes—allows the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and engagement with cell death pathways including ferroptosis and cuproptosis.20–23 Crucially, MOF nanoparticles can be engineered with stimulus-responsive release mechanisms triggered by TME-specific cues such as acidic pH, elevated GSH, or hypoxia, enabling precise spatiotemporal control over therapeutic payload delivery.24,25 Surface functionalization with targeting ligands, biomimetic membranes, or stimuli-responsive gatekeepers further confers tumor-selective accumulation.14,25 Beyond cancer, the inherent antibacterial activity of metal ions released during MOF degradation, combined with ROS-generating catalytic activity and physical membrane disruption by MOF nanostructures, positions MOF-based and MOF-derived materials as compelling alternatives to conventional antibiotics.26–29 MOF-based nanomaterials refer to intact MOF nanostructures used directly as drug carriers, imaging agents, or antibacterial platforms. MOF-derived nanomaterials refer to materials obtained through post-synthetic transformation of MOF precursors, including pyrolysis, chemical etching, and composite modification.

MOF-based and MOF-derived nanomaterials, by virtue of their structural tunability and compositional diversity, can be rationally engineered to simultaneously overcome the biological barriers imposed by the TME and the resistance mechanisms of MDR pathogens. Specifically, the structural merits of large surface area and tunable pore geometry underpin efficient drug loading and TME-responsive controlled release;17,24 surface engineering with targeting ligands or biomimetic membranes further ensures tumor-selective accumulation with minimal off-target toxicity;14,25 and crucially, the intrinsic reactivity of metal nodes drives ROS generation, oxidative stress amplification, and synergistic activation of immunogenic cell death pathways,20,21,23 collectively remodeling the immunosuppressive TME and potentiating systemic antitumor immunity.12,13

Despite their unprecedented structural modularity, exceeding that of clinically validated nanomedicine platforms such as liposomal doxorubicin and polymeric micelles, MOFs face substantial pharmacological and manufacturing barriers that critically impede clinical translation. Whereas lipid-based nanocarriers benefit from well-established, Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-compliant manufacturing protocols with demonstrated scalability, the synthesis of therapeutic MOFs, especially multi-component composites or surface-engineered architectures, commonly exhibits significant batch-to-batch variability.24 Scaling up these complex, multi-step coordination reactions without compromising crystalline integrity, pore size, or particle uniformity presents a formidable engineering challenge that currently limits industrial viability. Furthermore, the in vivo biological fate of MOFs introduces a vastly more complex toxicological profile than that of traditional organic nanocarriers.27 Established platforms typically degrade into easily metabolized lipids or biocompatible polymers. In contrast, the physiological degradation of MOF platforms releases synthetic organic linkers and high local concentrations of metal ions into systemic circulation. While these metallic nodes are strategically leveraged for ROS generation and catalytic therapy, their long-term pharmacokinetic behavior, potential for chronic immunotoxicity, and propensity for non-specific accumulation in the liver and spleen via rapid mononuclear phagocyte system clearance remain poorly characterized.29 Additionally, balancing the structural stability of MOFs in complex physiological fluids with their desired TME-responsive degradability remains a delicate equilibrium. Premature framework collapse or payload leakage in the bloodstream frequently undermines their theoretical targeting efficiency. Until these scale-up inconsistencies and long-term bio-safety concerns are systematically addressed through standardized toxicological screening, MOF-based platforms will struggle to bridge the gap between elegant benchtop chemistry and authorized clinical practice.

In this review, as illustrated in Figure 1, the synthetic strategies and key structural determinants of MOF-based and MOF-derived nanomaterials are systematically presented, encompassing pyrolysis, chemical etching, composite modification, and functional group introduction;16,19 this structural overview serves as the foundation for a comprehensive evaluation of their cancer theranostic applications, including multimodal imaging, stimulus-responsive drug delivery, and combination therapy platforms,2,18 as well as an in-depth discussion of their antibacterial mechanisms, performance-enhancement strategies, and representative applications.28,29 Multifunctional platforms concurrently integrating cancer theranostic and antibacterial capacities are subsequently addressed. The review concludes with a critical assessment of the principal barriers to clinical translation and a prospective outlook on emerging research directions, notably artificial intelligence-assisted MOF design and materials genomics, with the overarching aim of guiding the rational development of next-generation precision nanomedicines.1,3 Figure 1Schematic illustration of the biomedical applications of MOF-derived nanomaterials.A diagram of MOFs showing pyrolysis, chemical etching, composite modification and functional group introduction.

Synthetic Strategies and Structural Features of MOF-Derived Nanomaterials

MOFs as Precursors

Common types of MOFs include Fe-based, Zr-based, and Cu-based MOFs.30 Fe-based MOFs, such as PCN-600(Fe), have been demonstrated as effective peroxidase mimics. PCN-600 exhibits a one-dimensional channel as large as 3.1 nm and shows high stability in aqueous solutions with pH values ranging from 2–11 (Figure 2A).31 Zr-based MOFs are known for their rich structure types, outstanding stability, and intriguing properties. They have been widely studied for applications in catalysis, molecule adsorption and separation, drug delivery, and fluorescence sensing.32 Cu-based MOFs, like HKUST-1, also have unique structures and properties and have been explored in various fields. MOF nanocrystals function as self-sacrificial templates and chemical precursors to generate hollow nanostructures. A wide range of MOF-derived compositions has been reported, encompassing hollow metal hydroxides, sulfides, oxides, phosphides, and carbonaceous frameworks.33,37–39 Zeolitic imidazolate frameworks ZIF-8 (Zn) and ZIF-67 (Co) were employed as self-sacrificial templates and precursors to generate M-Co-LDH (M = Mg, Co, Ni) hollow nanocages (Figure 2B).33 In situ TEM directly captured the transformation of ZIF-8 nanocubes and rhombic dodecahedra into LDH nanocages, as well as the evolution of ZIF-67@ZIF-8 core-shell particles into shell-in-shell LDH nanocages (Figure 2C).34 Capitalizing on their propensity to undergo oxidative pyrolysis in air, MOF templates readily convert to metal oxides, making them ideal sacrificial precursors for a variety of hollow metal-oxide architectures. Guo et al used a Ni-Co-BTC MOF to obtain multilayered CuO@NiO hollow spheres (Figure 2D),35 while Guan et al reported a practical, general strategy to produce multicomponent metal oxides—including Co-Ni, Co-Mn, Mn-Ni, Zn-Mn, and Co-Mn-Ni frameworks.40 Rational incorporation of secondary species into MOFs, followed by templated pyrolysis, affords a versatile route to compositionally programmable hollow materials. Wu et al reported a MOF-assisted synthesis that confines molybdenum carbide nanoparticles within porous-carbon hollow octahedra (Figure 2E).36 This paradigm has since been extended to hollow metal sulfides, selenides, and phosphides by exploiting MOF nanocrystals as sacrificial templates and precursors.

The structure stability and functionality selection of MOFs have significant impacts on the properties of their derivatives. The stability of the MOF precursor affects the integrity of the derived nanostructures during the transformation process. A more stable MOF precursor may lead to derivatives with better-preserved structures and more consistent properties. In terms of functionality, the choice of metal ions and organic ligands in the MOF precursor can endow the derivatives with specific catalytic, adsorption, or sensing properties. The presence of certain metal ions in the MOF can confer unique redox or coordination capabilities to the derived nanomaterials, which are beneficial for applications such as catalysis and drug delivery.19

Derivation Approaches

Pyrolysis

Pyrolysis is a common method for deriving nanomaterials from MOFs. During pyrolysis, MOFs are heated in an inert atmosphere, which leads to the decomposition of the organic ligands and the formation of carbon-based materials with embedded metal or metal oxide nanoparticles.30 The pyrolysis of MOF-nanoparticle composites can form highly porous N- or P-doped graphitized MOF-derived nanomaterials, which are increasingly used as efficient catalysts, especially in electro- and photocatalysis. The temperature and duration of pyrolysis are crucial parameters. High-temperature pyrolysis can increase the graphitization degree of the carbon matrix, improving the electrical conductivity of the derived materials. However, excessive temperature may also cause the aggregation of metal nanoparticles, reducing their dispersion and active surface area. Liu et al pyrolyzed ZIF-8@ZIF-67 core-shell composites under nitrogen and, after selective etching, obtained CNT-anchored, double-shelled NC@Co-NGC nanocages (Figure 3A).41 The hollow architecture is attributed to high-temperature, contraction-induced surface instability of the ZIF-8@ZIF-67 core-shell nanocrystals. More broadly, a variety of hollow carbons42,43 and hollow carbon-supported, metal-based materials, including metals,44 metal oxides and sulfides,45 metal phosphides,46 and metal selenides, can be readily produced via MOF@secondary-component templating followed by appropriate pyrolysis. Chemical Etching

Chemical etching can be used to reshape nanocrystals via atomic addition and subtraction.49 In the context of MOF-derived nanomaterials, chemical etching can be employed to modify the surface or structure of the derived materials. Selective chemical etching can create honeycomb-like structures on the outside surface of capsules derived from MOFs, enhancing the transportation of reactants and dispersion of active sites. The choice of etchant and the etching conditions (such as concentration, temperature, and time) determine the extent and selectivity of the etching process. Different etchants may react preferentially with certain components of the MOF-derived materials, enabling the precise control of their morphology and structure.

Composite and Doping Modification

Composite and doping modification methods can introduce new functionalities to MOF-derived nanomaterials. Doping can tailor the electrical and optoelectronic properties of semiconductors (Figure 3B).47 In MOF-derived materials, doping with heteroatoms such as N, P, or S can change the electronic structure, enhancing their catalytic or sensing performance. Composite formation, on the other hand, can combine the advantages of different materials. The combination of MOFs with metal nanoparticles can result in materials with enhanced antibacterial or catalytic activity, as the metal nanoparticles can provide additional active sites while the MOF matrix can support and disperse them effectively (Figure 3C).48

Functional Group Introduction

Functional group introduction is an important strategy to modify the surface properties of MOF-derived nanomaterials. By introducing specific functional groups, such as amino, carboxyl, or hydroxyl groups, the biocompatibility, solubility, and targeting ability of the materials can be improved.8 The introduction of amino groups can enhance the interaction between the nanomaterials and biological molecules, facilitating drug delivery and biosensing applications. The methods for functional group introduction include covalent bonding, ligand exchange, and surface grafting. These methods need to be carefully selected according to the nature of the MOF-derived materials and the desired functionalization effect.

Key Factors Affecting Nanomaterials Properties

Specific Surface Area and Pore Size Distribution

The specific surface area and pore size distribution of MOF-derived nanomaterials significantly influence their performance. A large specific surface area provides more active sites for adsorption, catalysis, and drug loading. Mesoporous MOFs with high specific surface areas can adsorb a large amount of drugs, increasing the drug-loading capacity50 (Figure 4A). The pore size distribution also plays a crucial role. Different applications require different pore sizes. In gas separation, small-pore MOFs can selectively adsorb specific gas molecules based on their molecular sizes. In drug delivery, mesoporous materials with appropriate pore sizes can facilitate the release of drugs in a controlled manner. The synthesis conditions, such as the choice of precursors, reaction temperature, and time, can be adjusted to control the specific surface area and pore size distribution of the derived nanomaterials. Crystal Structure Control

Controlling the crystal structure of MOF-derived nanomaterials is essential for achieving desired properties. The crystal structure affects the electronic, optical, and mechanical properties of the materials. In noble metal nanomaterials, the crystal phase can influence their catalytic activity. The synthesis of palladium-boron (Pd-B) nanoparticles with precisely controllable crystal-phases and highly ordered mesostructures has been achieved by interstitially inserting metallic B atoms into face-centered cubic mesoporous Pd (fcc-mesoPd) confined in a mesoporous silica template51 (Figure 4B). These crystal-phase control strategies have been adopted and adapted in the design of MOF-derived nanomaterials. The engineering of crystal-phase structures at the atomic level can tune the catalytic performance of the nanomaterials. Computational methods can be used to predict and design the crystal structures, guiding the synthesis process.

Control of Metal Ion Activity and Dispersion During the Derivation Process

The activity and dispersion of metal ions in MOF-derived nanomaterials are critical for their catalytic and antibacterial properties. Well-dispersed metal ions with high activity can provide more effective catalytic sites or antibacterial agents. In the synthesis of supported metal oxide nanoparticles derived from MOF composites, a strategy has been developed to achieve well-dispersed and size-controlled supported metal oxides through the manageable growth of a metal organic framework (Cu-BTC) on TiO2 followed by pyrolysis52 (Figure 4C). The choice of MOF precursor, the pyrolysis conditions, and the presence of surfactants or ligands can all affect the activity and dispersion of metal ions. A proper balance between metal ion dispersion and stability is needed to ensure the long-term performance of the derived nanomaterials.

MOF-Derived Nanomaterials for Cancer Theranostics

Cancer Diagnosis and Imaging

MOF-derived nanomaterials can serve as contrast agents in multiple imaging modalities. In MRI, some MOF-derived materials with paramagnetic metal ions can enhance the contrast of tumor tissues. Gadolinium-containing MOFs can shorten the relaxation time of water protons, resulting in brighter signals in T1-weighted images.18 In CT imaging, MOF-derived nanomaterials with high X-ray attenuation coefficients, such as those containing heavy metals like barium or iodine, can improve the visualization of tumors. Compared with other platforms for cancer therapy and imaging, the framework architecture of core-shell MOFs affords a stimuli-responsive interface, a high-capacity drug-loading core, and enhanced per-metal-ion relaxivity. Gd/Tm-based MOFs operate as pH-responsive delivery vehicles and bimodal imaging contrast agents.53 Engineering metal-ion-based MOFs with a uniform shell and targeting motifs enables programmable loading and stimulus-triggered release. In addition, several formulations of magnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (IONPs), including ferumoxsil, ferrixan, and ferumoxide, have been used clinically as biocompatible T2-weighted MRI contrast agents. More recently, multifunctional core-shell IONPs have been engineered for cancer diagnosis and therapy.54,55 Shen et al developed TiO2-coated magnetite nanoparticles (Fe3O4@TiO2) to enable targeted drug delivery and CT; the TiO2 shell functions both as a sonosensitizer for sonodynamic therapy (SDT) and as a loading platform for chemotherapeutics (Figure 5A). Moreover, Chowdhuri et al56 engineered a core-shell MOF platform comprising RITC-labeled iron oxide encapsulated in porous IRMOF-3 nanoparticles with folic-acid targeting (RITC-IO@IRMOF-3/FA NPs). In fluorescence imaging, MOF-derived nanomaterials can be designed to emit fluorescence upon excitation. Some MOFs with fluorescent ligands or doped with fluorescent ions can be used as fluorescent probes. The fluorescence can be quenched or enhanced in the presence of specific biomarkers, enabling the detection of tumors. For photoacoustic imaging, MOF-derived nanomaterials with strong light absorption in the near-infrared region can generate acoustic waves upon laser irradiation. The absorbed light energy is converted into heat, causing thermo-elastic expansion and generating photoacoustic signals, which can be detected to image the tumor.62 To target cancer cells, nanoparticle (NP) surfaces are widely conjugated with functional ligands—folic acid (FA), antibodies, and aptamers (Apts)—to enable receptor-mediated uptake.63 Alijani et al57 reported a magnetic Fe3O4@MOF-DOX-carbon dot (CD)-aptamer nanocomposite (≈16 nm) for imaging and delivery: doxorubicin (DOX) was loaded into Fe3O4@MOF, bright CDs were integrated, and AS1411 (nucleolin-targeting) was used to cap the construct. The Fe3O4@MOF-DOX-CD-Apt platform enabled selective, pH-responsive release to nucleolin-overexpressing tumors and improved therapy against MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells; additionally, CD-derived fluorescence supported biodistribution monitoring57 (Figure 5B). Carbon quantum dots (CQDs) exhibit chemiluminescent potential arising from their donor-acceptor electronic structure, supporting broad prospects for chemiluminescence-guided cancer therapy.64 Building on this, He et al58 fabricated ~110 nm CQD@ZIF-8 nanocomposites via encapsulation and demonstrated simultaneous imaging and drug delivery (Figure 5C).

Strategies and Case Analyses for Improving the Selective Targeting Performance of Tumor Tissues

To improve the selective targeting performance of tumor tissues, several strategies can be employed. One common strategy is to conjugate targeting ligands to the surface of MOF-derived nanomaterials. The conjugation of cyclic RGD peptide (cRGD) to the surface of nanoparticles can target αvβ3 integrin overexpressed on tumor cells. A study reported a peptide-doped bio-MOF system (ZFH-DGR) that can target ανβ3 integrin overexpressed on liver hepatocellular carcinoma (HepG2) cells. Doxorubicin-loaded ZFH-DGR (ZFH-DGR/Dox) demonstrated high cell selectivity between HepG2 cells and normal liver (L02) cells.65 Zhang et al59 engineered a peptide-targeted core–shell nanoplatform by encapsulating polymer-coated gold nanostars within MIL-101-NH2(Fe), yielding AuNS@MIL-101-NH2(Fe)-ZD2 (225 nm). Prepared via a four-cycle, stepwise coating, the construct provides T1-weighted MRI contrast and enables selective photothermal ablation of MDA-MB-231 cells, underscoring its potential for image-guided breast-cancer theranostics59 (Figure 5D).

Another strategy is to utilize the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect. MOF-derived nanomaterials with appropriate sizes can accumulate preferentially in tumor tissues due to the leaky vasculature and poor lymphatic drainage in tumors. EPR-mediated tumor uptake of MOF nanostructures increases at sizes of 20–150 nm.66 Yet balancing imaging performance with the EPR advantage remains non-trivial, as further downsizing can compromise MOF-enabled tumor imaging. Addressing this, Shang et al engineered 89 nm Au@MIL-88 core-shell nanoparticles that delivered trimodal imaging in U87 tumors and exhibited high passive accumulation via the EPR effect60 (Figure 5E). Leveraging the EPR effect and favorable MRI performance, Fe3O4@UiO-66 core-shell nanostructures have been reported as candidates for in vivo delivery of doxorubicin to HeLa tumors61 (Figure 5F). Likewise, camptothecin-loaded AuNR@MOF nanostructures accumulate in solid tumors via the EPR effect and enhance therapy through NIR-triggered drug release, exemplifying an exogenous stimulus-responsive DDS.67 However, the EPR effect alone may not be sufficient for precise targeting, and a combination of active targeting ligands can further improve the selectivity.

Drug Delivery Carrier: Regulation Mechanisms of Drug Loading and Release

MOF-derived nanomaterials can act as efficient drug delivery carriers. The high porosity of MOFs allows for the encapsulation of drugs through physical adsorption or chemical bonding. The drug-loading capacity depends on factors such as the pore size, specific surface area, and chemical nature of the MOF-derived materials. Mesoporous MOFs can accommodate a large amount of hydrophobic drugs within their pores. The regulation of drug release can be achieved through various mechanisms. pH-responsive drug release is a common strategy. In the acidic environment of tumor tissues, the coordination bonds in MOFs can be cleaved, leading to the release of encapsulated drugs. For instance, some MOFs with acid-labile linkages can release drugs in the endosomes or lysosomes of tumor cells.8 Gao et al68 reported a UiO-66-NH2–H2O2/PB platform that couples pH-responsive drug delivery and NIR-induced photothermal therapy with H2O2-catalytic activity, thereby overcoming hypoxic stress in cancer cells (Figure 6A). Another mechanism is enzyme-responsive release. Enzymes overexpressed in tumor tissues can degrade specific components of the MOF-derived carriers, triggering drug release. Because MIL-88 (enzyme-mimetic) adapts its pore size via breathing to match drug geometry and ZIF-8 degrades under acidic conditions, the resulting MIL-88-ICG@ZIF-8-DOX core-shell platform affords synergistic PTT/PDT/chemotherapy against tumors69 (Figure 6B). Mechanisms of Action and Research Progress of MOF-Derived Nanomaterials in Photothermal and Photodynamic Therapy

In photothermal therapy (PTT), MOF-derived nanomaterials can absorb near-infrared (NIR) light and convert it into heat, leading to the thermal ablation of tumor cells. Some MOF-derived materials, such as those containing noble metals or carbon-based components, have strong NIR absorption capabilities. A photoactive MOF-derived cobalt-silver bimetallic nanocomposite (Ag@CoMOF) exhibits a strong photothermal conversion effect of Ag nanoparticles, accompanied by a rapid temperature increase under NIR irradiation.73 Chien et al70 reported MCP-1/GNR@MIL-100(Fe), a core-shell system designed to tune cellular uptake and cytotoxicity. In tumor-bearing mice, NIR-mediated photothermal treatment administered over 43 days inhibited tumor progression (Figure 6C). Corroborated by tissue histology, proliferation readouts, and bioluminescence/fluorescence imaging, the MCP-1/GNR@MIL-100(Fe) platform shows strong promise for image-guided photothermal therapy. Exhibiting mesoporous characteristics, AuNR@MOF core-shell nanoparticles display NIR-triggered 1O2 activation (conferred by the porphyrinic MOF shell) and robust plasmonic heat conversion (inherent to Au nanorods)71 (Figure 6D). The integration of PTT and PDT thus yields a potent cancer-treatment platform. Accordingly, directing nanoparticle crystallographic orientation within core-shell designs emerges as a forward-looking strategy for synergistic phototherapy.

In photodynamic therapy (PDT), MOF-derived nanomaterials can generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) upon light irradiation. Photosensitizers incorporated in MOFs can be excited by light, transferring energy to oxygen molecules to produce ROS, such as singlet oxygen. These ROS can damage the cell membrane, proteins, and DNA of tumor cells, leading to cell death. Research has focused on improving the efficiency of ROS generation, the light-penetration depth, and the targeting ability of MOF-derived PDT agents. The design of multifunctional MOF-based nanoplatforms that can simultaneously enhance light absorption, ROS generation, and tumor targeting has been an active area of research.2 Beyond generating 1O2, PDT efficacy hinges on alleviating tumor hypoxia by supplying O2. Thus, catalase-like nanozymes—platinum,74 MnO275 gold,76–79 and iron nanoparticles80—are often integrated with MOF nanoplatforms to elevate local oxygen levels.

Chemotherapy Sensitization and Synergistic Therapy Effects

MOF-derived nanomaterials can enhance the efficacy of chemotherapy through chemotherapy sensitization. They can increase the intracellular uptake of chemotherapeutic drugs, overcome the drug-resistance of tumor cells, and enhance the synergistic effect with other therapies. Some MOF-derived nanomaterials can disrupt the tumor microenvironment, making tumor cells more sensitive to chemotherapy drugs. In synergistic chemotherapy, MOF-derived nanomaterials can be used to co-deliver multiple chemotherapeutic drugs or combine chemotherapy with other therapies such as phototherapy or immunotherapy. CSD-MOFs afford high-capacity loading of ART with pH-responsive release in the acidic TME, delivering synergistic PTT-chemotherapy both in vitro and in vivo.81 Notably, even without targeting moieties, their biocompatibility and limited side effects support safe use as theranostic agents. In line with this concept, PB/ZIF-8-based CSD-MOFs@DOX have been applied to dual-modal imaging and combined PTT-chemotherapy.81 Li et al reported AZGL, a liposomal core-shell AuNS@NMOF platform decorated with gambogic acid, which couples PTT, PDT, and chemotherapy for breast-cancer treatment72 (Figure 6E). Tian et al82 reported a straightforward one-pot strategy to integrate ZIF-8 (drug reservoir) with GQDs (photothermal module), yielding a multifunctional chemo-PTT system. DOX-ZIF-8/GQD nanoparticles displayed marked cytotoxicity against 4T1 cells under NIR, attributable to chemo-photothermal synergy, positioning ZIF-8/GQD nanocarriers as promising agents for breast-cancer combination therapy.

Beyond functioning solely as passive drug carriers, MOF-derived nanoplatforms are now deliberately engineered to harness their intrinsic physicochemical properties, enabling direct induction of non-apoptotic, regulated cell death. A leading strategy leverages the redox-active metal nodes of MOFs to target intracellular metabolic vulnerabilities. Iron-based frameworks undergo stimulus-responsive degradation in the mildly acidic tumor microenvironment, thereby amplifying intracellular Fenton reactions. This localized ROS burst depletes glutathione, inactivates GPX4, and ultimately triggers lethal lipid peroxidation—culminating in ferroptosis.83

Building upon the success of metal-driven cytotoxicity, the recent formalization of cuproptosis has catalyzed a paradigm shift toward copper-containing MOF platforms. These nanomaterials act as targeted vehicles that selectively release copper ions into the cytoplasm. The subsequent binding and aggregation of lipoylated proteins within the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle induce severe proteotoxic stress, offering a distinct and highly effective mechanism for bypassing conventional apoptosis resistance.84

Concurrently, the structural modularity of MOFs permits the integration of photoactive organic linkers to synergize these metabolic interventions with phototherapy. Upon irradiation, combination PTT/PDT generates acute thermal and oxidative damage that directly triggers immunogenic cell death (ICD). Crucially, the resulting emission of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) transforms localized physical ablation into a systemic immunological event. This process drives dendritic cell maturation and elicits robust, systemic antitumor immunity, effectively bridging primary tumor clearance with long-term immunosurveillance against metastasis.

In immunotherapy, MOF-derived nanomaterials can be designed to modulate the immune response. They can release immunomodulatory agents or activate immune cells to enhance the anti-tumor immune response. Some MOF-based nanoplatforms can be used to deliver antigens or cytokines to dendritic cells, promoting the activation of T cells and enhancing the anti-tumor immunity. The combination of chemotherapy and immunotherapy using MOF-derived nanomaterials has shown promising results in preclinical studies, with enhanced anti-tumor effects compared to single-therapy approaches.2 By steering the in vivo biodistribution of MOFs, one can regulate their pharmacokinetic behaviour, toxicity profiles, and immunological responses. Continued efforts along these lines are expected to yield biocompatible platforms suitable for safe translation to the pharmaceutical marketplace in the near term.

However, despite these promising advances, the field confronts critical translational bottlenecks that impede near-term pharmaceutical commercialization. Although researchers strive to engineer biocompatible MOFs by modulating their in vivo biodistribution, precise control over this parameter remains exceptionally challenging. Systemic administration commonly triggers rapid clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system, leading to non-specific accumulation predominantly in the liver and spleen. Moreover, fundamental knowledge gaps persist regarding the long-term pharmacokinetic profiles of MOFs, the metabolic fate of constituent metal nodes, and the potential for chronic immunotoxicity. To enable safe clinical translation, future research must pivot from documenting broad immunomodulatory effects toward mechanistically dissecting the specific molecular and cellular interactions occurring at the bio–nano interface. Addressing these core pharmacological and toxicological challenges is an indispensable prerequisite for advancing MOF-based platforms from preclinical development to clinical application.

Challenges and Solutions in Cancer Theranostics

Biocompatibility, Degradability, and Targeting Optimization

Improving the biocompatibility of MOF-derived nanomaterials is crucial for their clinical translation. Some studies have focused on surface modification to reduce the toxicity of MOFs. Coating MOF-derived nanoparticles with biocompatible polymers such as polyethylene glycol (PEG) can improve their blood circulation time and reduce non-specific interactions with normal tissues.8 Regarding degradability, efforts have been made to design MOFs that can degrade in the body into non-toxic products. Some MOFs with hydrolyzable or enzymatically cleavable linkages can be broken down in the physiological environment. The choice of synthesis method enables rational tuning of the physicochemical characteristics of biopolymer-modified nanostructures.85 As a case in point, chitosan-coated Zn-MOF@GO microspheres carrying 5-FU were reported for targeted delivery to human breast cancer cells. The formulation combined robust colloidal stability and spherical morphology (20–40 µm) with high payload capacity (45%), marked swelling at acidic pH (260.5%), favorable biocompatibility, augmented cellular uptake, and strong tumor drug-delivery capability.

In terms of targeting optimization, in addition to the strategies mentioned above, the use of cell-membrane-camouflaged MOF-derived nanoparticles has shown promise. These nanoparticles can inherit the targeting ability of the cell membrane, such as red-blood-cell-membrane-coated nanoparticles that can evade the immune system and target tumor tissues.14

Clinical Translation

One of the main bottlenecks in clinical translation is the lack of large-scale production methods with consistent quality. The current synthesis methods of MOF-derived nanomaterials are often complex and difficult to scale up. To address this, research is needed to develop more efficient and reproducible synthesis techniques. Another challenge is the high cost of production, which may limit their widespread use. Strategies to reduce costs include the use of inexpensive precursors and simplifying the synthesis process.

The regulatory approval process is also a significant hurdle. There is a lack of clear guidelines for the evaluation of MOF-derived nanomaterials in terms of their safety and efficacy. To overcome this, more preclinical and clinical studies are required to provide comprehensive data on their biocompatibility, toxicity, and therapeutic effects. Additionally, collaboration between academia, industry, and regulatory agencies is essential to establish appropriate regulatory frameworks for the clinical translation of MOF-derived nanomaterials.3

MOF-Derived Nanomaterials for Antibacterial Applications

MOF-derived nanomaterials possess multiple antibacterial mechanisms. Physical interaction is one of the mechanisms. The sharp edges or rough surfaces of some MOF-derived nanoparticles can physically damage the bacterial cell membrane, leading to cell leakage and death. Graphene-based MOF composites may have sharp edges that can disrupt the cell membrane of bacteria.86 A growing class of 2D-morphology MOFs has demonstrated remarkable antimicrobial activity. Among them, ZIF-L reported by Yuan et al87 possesses a nano-daggered surface unlike the dodecahedral ZIF-8, affording >7-log10 antibacterial reductions against E. coli and S. aureus, recyclable killing over ≥4 cycles. The authors ascribe the activity to physical contact-killing by the nano-dagger surface (Figure 7A). Using additive-directed synthesis with TEA and acetic acid, Wang et al88 generated four morphology-distinct yet isostructural copper CPPs—rhombic layered, rhombic disk-like, rhombic block-like, and bread-like—by tuning linker deprotonation kinetics. Antibacterial testing revealed weak activity for the bread-like and rhombic disk-like forms (MIC >50 µg/mL), but robust activity for the rhombic block-like and rhombic layered CPPs against B. subtilis, P. vulgaris, S. aureus, P. aeruginosa, and S. enteritidis (MIC <25 µg/mL). Oxidative stress is another important mechanism. MOF-derived nanomaterials can generate ROS such as superoxide anions, hydroxyl radicals, and hydrogen peroxide. These ROS can oxidize bacterial proteins, lipids, and DNA, causing damage to the bacteria. Some metal-containing MOFs can catalyze the generation of ROS through Fenton-like reactions. For instance, iron-based MOFs can react with hydrogen peroxide to produce hydroxyl radicals.28 Natural enzymes can efficiently generate ROS from low H2O2 levels, leading to irreversible damage of bacterial envelopes and critical biomolecules (DNA, proteins, polysaccharides, nucleic acids).92 Practical translation is limited by cost, purification complexity, instability, and poor reusability, creating an urgent need for robust enzyme-mimicking catalysts. Recently, MOF-based catalytic systems that mimic glucose oxidase (GOx/GOD), peroxidase (POD), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT) have been developed, revealing that MOFs can emulate enzymatic activity to generate ROS or their precursors for antibacterial action.93–97 Burachaloo et al98 demonstrated that disruption of an Fe-based MOF (MIL-88B) releases Fe2+, driving intracellular Fenton-like generation of ·OH. In parallel, Hao et al89 showed that in GSH-rich infectious microenvironments, Cu2+ in a Cu-MOF is reduced to Cu+, which catalyzes H2O2 into ·OH, thereby eradicating bacteria (Figure 7B).

Ion release is also a common antibacterial mechanism. MOF-derived nanomaterials can release metal ions such as silver, copper, or zinc. These metal ions can interact with bacterial proteins, enzymes, or DNA, inhibiting bacterial growth and metabolism. Silver ions can bind to the thiol groups of bacterial proteins, disrupting their normal function.73 A variety of antibacterial MOFs have been obtained by embedding the aforementioned metal ions into the lattice; in each case, gradual ion release emerged as the key driver of antimicrobial efficacy.87,88,99–101 Aguado et al102 reported ZIF-67 and Co-SIM-1, cobalt MOFs derived from 2-methylimidazole and 4-methyl-5-imidazolecarboxaldehyde. In the 5–10 mg/L range, both materials suppressed S. cerevisiae, P. putida, and E. coli by more than 50%, an effect ascribed to the persistent trace release of Co2+. To delineate bactericidal pathways arising from ion release, Taheri et al90 studied ZIF-8 degradation under physiological conditions. Data show that ZIF-8 immersed in PBS first yields Zn2+, which swiftly converts to Zn3(PO4)2—an agent with broad-spectrum activity (Figure 7C). Thus, ZIF-8’s strong antibacterial performance is attributed to in situ Zn3(PO4)2 formation in physiological environments. While slow metal-ion release from MOFs underpins robust and persistent antimicrobial activity, the in vivo metabolism of these ions remains obscure and mechanistic interpretations diverge. Some studies ascribe bactericidal effects to the ions themselves, whereas others attribute them to ion-driven formation of organic–inorganic antimicrobial agents.90

Antibacterial Activity in Different Types of Bacteria

The antibacterial activity of MOF-derived nanomaterials may vary against different types of bacteria. Gram-positive bacteria have a thick peptidoglycan layer in their cell wall, while gram-negative bacteria have an outer membrane in addition to the peptidoglycan layer. Some MOF-derived nanomaterials may have different interactions with these two types of bacteria. Palladium (Pd) nanocrystals exhibit facet-dependent oxidase and peroxidase-like activities that endow them with excellent antibacterial properties via generation of reactive oxygen species. The antibacterial efficiency of Pd nanocrystals against Gram-positive bacteria is consistent with the extent of their enzyme-like activity, while a reverse trend is observed against gram-negative bacteria. Pd octahedrons display stronger penetration into the outer membrane of gram-negative bacteria than Pd nanocubes, resulting in higher antibacterial activity against gram-negative bacteria91 (Figure 7D). Bacterial killing via membrane disruption and ROS-mediated mechanisms is achieved through the MOF-derived composition of the platform.

Strategies to Enhance Antibacterial Performance

Surface functionalization of MOF-derived nanomaterials can enhance their antibacterial performance. The introduction of cationic groups on the surface can increase the electrostatic interaction between the nanomaterials and the negatively charged bacterial cell membrane, improving the binding and antibacterial activity. Chitosan-functionalized MOF-derived nanomaterials can enhance the antibacterial effect due to the antibacterial property of chitosan itself and the improved interaction with bacteria.103

Composite material formation is also an effective strategy. Combining MOF-derived nanomaterials with other antibacterial agents such as antibiotics, metal nanoparticles, or natural antibacterial agents can result in synergistic antibacterial effects. For instance, the combination of MOFs with silver nanoparticles can enhance the antibacterial activity, as the silver nanoparticles can provide additional antibacterial action through ion release and ROS generation, while the MOF matrix can support and disperse the silver nanoparticles.73 Deng et al104 synthesized ZIF-8-coated gold nanoflowers (ZIF-8@Au NFs) and, via tannic-acid-mediated selective etching, obtained yolk-shell architectures. The ZIF-8 evolved into an ultrathin shell with enlarged void space between shell and Au core, yielding a higher drug-loading capacity than the original core-shell ZIF-8@Au NFs. The Au nanoparticles also exhibit a strong LSPR at ~530 nm, enhancing visible-light absorption; together these effects promote electron transport and charge-carrier separation. The combination of MOF-derived nanomaterials with antibiotics can overcome the problem of antibiotic resistance. MOFs can improve the solubility and delivery of antibiotics to bacteria, increasing their intracellular concentration. Some MOF-based nanocomposites can encapsulate antibiotics and release them in a controlled manner near the bacteria, enhancing the antibacterial effect. Lin et al107 reported a one-pot encapsulation of VAN into MOF-53(Fe) driven by strong electrostatic interactions, affording 20 wt% loading and good biocompatibility. The system maintained durable, high-level antibacterial performance (99.3%) in inflammatory, bacteria-rich milieus due to the slow liberation of VAN and Fe3+ (Figure 8C). In addition, combining with natural antibacterial agents such as essential oils or plant extracts can also enhance the antibacterial performance. These natural agents often have multiple antibacterial mechanisms, and their combination with MOF-derived nanomaterials can lead to a more comprehensive antibacterial effect. Research has shown that the combination of MOF-derived nanomaterials with natural antibacterial agents can effectively inhibit the growth of drug-resistant bacteria4 (Figure 8D).

Antibacterial Applications and Case Studies

Medical device coatings are an important application field for MOF-derived nanomaterials. Bacterial infections on medical devices can lead to serious complications. Coating medical devices with MOF-derived nanomaterials can prevent bacterial adhesion and growth. Urinary catheters coated with MOF-based materials can reduce the formation of biofilms and the incidence of catheter-associated urinary tract infections. The antibacterial mechanism may involve the release of metal ions or the generation of ROS from the MOF-derived coating.5

Biofilm control is another significant application. Biofilms are complex communities of bacteria embedded in an extracellular matrix, which are difficult to eradicate. MOF-derived nanomaterials can penetrate the biofilm matrix and kill the bacteria inside. Some MOF-based nanocomposites can disrupt the biofilm structure by releasing antibacterial agents or generating ROS, effectively controlling biofilm formation and growth6 (Figure 9A). Cancer patients undergoing systemic therapy or harboring indwelling medical devices exhibit markedly elevated susceptibility to bacterial co-infections; critically, MDR pathogens and treatment-refractory tumors converge mechanistically, through shared adaptations including oxidative stress resilience, biofilm formation, and TME-driven immune suppression. MOF-derived nanomaterials uniquely bridge these dual therapeutic challenges via a single, rationally engineered platform. Their redox-active metal nodes enable programmable ROS modulation, their crystalline porosity permits precise payload loading and stimulus-responsive release, and their surface chemistry supports synergistic immunomodulation.

Representative Case Analyses

A study developed a photoactive MOF-derived cobalt-silver bimetallic nanocomposite (Ag@Co MOF) for antibacterial applications. This nanocomposite was used to treat bacterial infections in a liquid culture environment. It showed superior antibacterial activities against E. coli and B. subtilis, with a 22.1-fold and 18.3-fold enhanced inhibition of bacterial growth compared to traditional antibiotics. The nanocomposite continuously releases antibacterial metal ions (Ag and Co ions) in the aqueous phase and exhibits a strong photothermal conversion effect of Ag nanoparticles under near-infrared (NIR) irradiation, which synergistically enhances the antibacterial ability.73

By impregnating ZIF-8 with AgNO3 followed by in situ pyrolysis, Cui et al108 produced Ag/ZnO hybrid cages that retain a well-defined polyhedral architecture and display mesoporous interiors. The hybrids show strong antibacterial performance (MIC 6.25 μg mL−1), comparable to streptomycin and ciprofloxacin. Their heterojunction enables electrons to reduce O2 to ·O2−, while valence-band holes participate in oxidation or generate ·OH from H2O, collectively effecting bacterial inactivation (Figure 9B).

Hatami et al109 developed GO/Co-PTA, a cobalt MOF-derived carbon composite exhibiting up to 99% inhibition against E. coli and S. aureus. The antibacterial activity is ascribed to cooperative mechanisms—Co2+ release coupled with GO’s physical interactions with bacterial cells (Figure 9C).

Another case is the use of MOF-based coatings on orthopedic implants. These coatings can prevent the adhesion and growth of bacteria on the implant surface, reducing the risk of implant-related infections. The MOF-based coatings can release antibacterial agents in a controlled manner, providing long-term protection against bacteria.6

Limitations and Prospective Improvements

Antibacterial Activity Persistence and Stability Issues

One of the limitations of MOF-derived nanomaterials is the persistence and stability of their antibacterial activity. Over time, the release of antibacterial agents such as metal ions may decrease, or the generation of ROS may be inhibited. To improve the persistence of antibacterial activity, strategies such as encapsulating antibacterial agents in a slow-release matrix or using stimuli-responsive systems can be employed. Designing MOF-derived nanomaterials with pH-responsive or enzyme-responsive release mechanisms can ensure the continuous release of antibacterial agents in the bacterial microenvironment. Regarding stability, improving the chemical stability of MOF-derived nanomaterials in different environments is crucial. Surface modification or the use of more stable MOF precursors can enhance their stability.110

Resistance and Biosafety Assessment

The development of bacterial resistance to MOF-derived nanomaterials is a concern. Although the antibacterial mechanisms of MOF-derived nanomaterials are different from traditional antibiotics, long-term exposure may still lead to the emergence of resistant bacteria. To address this, the combination of multiple antibacterial mechanisms or the use of rotation therapy with different antibacterial agents can be considered.

Biosafety assessment is also essential. MOF-derived nanomaterials may have potential toxicity to human cells and the environment. Comprehensive in vitro and in vivo studies are needed to evaluate their biosafety. Studies on the cytotoxicity, genotoxicity, and immunotoxicity of MOF-derived nanomaterials should be conducted. In addition, the environmental impact of MOF-derived nanomaterials, such as their fate in water and soil, needs to be investigated.111

Multifunctional MOF-Derived Platforms for Simultaneous Cancer Theranostics and Antibacterials

Multifunctional MOF-derived platforms can integrate both cancer theranostics and antibacterial functions. The design of these platforms often involves the incorporation of multiple functional components into the MOF-derived nanostructures. A MOF-based nanoplatform can be designed to encapsulate anticancer drugs for cancer therapy, while also having antibacterial agents or properties to prevent bacterial infections that may occur during cancer treatment. The advantages of such multifunctional systems are significant. They can simplify the treatment process by providing both diagnostic and therapeutic functions for cancer, as well as antibacterial protection. This can reduce the number of different materials or treatments required, potentially improving patient compliance. Moreover, the synergistic effects between different functions can enhance the overall efficacy. The antibacterial function can prevent secondary infections, which may otherwise compromise the effectiveness of cancer treatment, and the cancer-targeting and imaging functions can help in the accurate diagnosis and treatment of cancer.2

Some studies have reported MOF-derived nanomaterials that can simultaneously perform cancer theranostics and antibacterial functions. A certain MOF-based nanocomposite was designed to carry a photosensitizer for photodynamic therapy of cancer and also had antibacterial properties through the release of metal ions. In the tumor microenvironment, the nanocomposite can generate ROS under light irradiation for cancer cell killing, while the released metal ions can inhibit the growth of bacteria in the surrounding area. The mechanism behind this is that the MOF matrix can encapsulate and protect the photosensitizer and metal ions, and release them in a controlled manner. The photosensitizer can be excited by light to produce ROS for cancer therapy, and the metal ions can interact with the bacterial cell membrane and proteins, inhibiting bacterial growth.2 Hu et al reported DMTH nanoparticles formed by loading DOX into MIL-100(Fe), then installing a TA coordination layer and an HA shell. Designed for PAI-guided combination therapy (CDT + chemotherapy + PTT), these NPs show selective tumor targeting, minimal systemic toxicity, and robust therapeutic benefit in breast-cancer models112 (Figure 10). Another example is a MOF-based system that can target cancer cells for imaging and drug delivery, and at the same time, its surface functionalization can endow it with antibacterial activity. The targeting ligands on the surface of the MOF-derived nanoparticles can specifically bind to cancer cells, enabling accurate cancer diagnosis and therapy, while the antibacterial functional groups can prevent the growth of bacteria in the tumor microenvironment. Challenges and Future Perspectives

The studies reviewed here collectively establish a compelling mechanistic case for MOF-derived nanomaterials as next-generation biomedical platforms, yet a sober appraisal of the field reveals that clinical translation remains constrained by problems that mechanistic elegance alone cannot resolve.1,3 Four of these problems deserve particular scrutiny, not because they are novel observations but because the field has largely been content to acknowledge them in passing rather than confront them with the analytical depth their importance demands.

The most consequential unresolved question concerns the biological fate of metal nodes after MOF framework degradation in vivo. Current safety reporting is dominated by short-term acute toxicity assessments, typically spanning four to eight weeks, which are statistically incapable of detecting the chronic organ accumulation, endogenous metal homeostasis disruption, and cumulative immunogenicity that prolonged exposure to non-physiological elements may produce.113 This concern is most acute for hafnium, ruthenium, and indium, which lack established physiological roles and whose tissue clearance kinetics, speciation under physiological conditions, and interactions with endogenous metalloprotein networks remain almost entirely uncharacterized in the published literature.20 The mechanistic basis for concern is not speculative: hafnium accumulation in reticuloendothelial organs has been documented following nanoparticle administration,20 ruthenium compounds are known to intercalate DNA and inhibit mitochondrial respiration at sub-cytotoxic concentrations, and indium has recognized nephrotoxic potential in occupational exposure contexts. None of these observations constitutes a definitive contraindication, but they collectively define a preclinical safety program that responsible clinical translation requires and that the field has not yet conducted. Quantitative multi-organ biodistribution by ICP-MS at serial time points extending to at least six months, hematological and histopathological assessment across the same period, and species-specific toxicokinetic studies in large animals for elements without established safety precedent are not optional refinements but minimum standards for any platform seriously intended for human evaluation.

Equally fundamental, and less frequently stated with the directness it merits, is the question of whether passive tumor accumulation through the enhanced permeability and retention effect operates with sufficient magnitude and consistency in human solid tumors to serve as a viable therapeutic strategy.66 The preclinical evidence base for MOF nanomedicine is constructed almost entirely on subcutaneous xenograft models, tumor architectures characterized by high vascular permeability, low interstitial pressure, and minimal stromal density that bear limited resemblance to the tumors most relevant to the therapeutic claims being made. Desmoplastic malignancies, including pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, and triple-negative breast cancer, present a categorically different delivery landscape: dense collagen matrices with interstitial pressures that actively oppose convective nanoparticle transport, irregular and compressed vasculature that reduces the vascular surface area available for extravasation, and abundant stromal cell populations that intercept nanoparticles before they reach tumor parenchyma.25 Clinical pharmacokinetic data from the handful of nanomedicines that have progressed to human studies suggest that tumor drug concentrations achieved through passive targeting are, in many cases, far lower than preclinical models predicted. Whether active targeting ligands can compensate for this shortfall in the context of these physical barriers, or whether fundamentally different delivery strategies are required for stromal-rich tumor types, represents a genuine open question that the field cannot answer from subcutaneous model data alone. Addressing it demands orthotopic implantation models with physiologically faithful tumor-organ microenvironments, patient-derived xenograft systems that preserve inter-patient stromal heterogeneity, and ultimately early-phase clinical studies incorporating quantitative tumor biopsy pharmacokinetics as primary endpoints.

A third problem that warrants more rigorous examination is the actual selectivity of stimulus-responsive release under physiological conditions. The pH gradients and glutathione concentrations exploited as tumor-specific triggers are not binary switches but continuous variables that exist, to varying degrees, in a range of non-target physiological contexts: inflamed tissues, ischemic regions, hepatic sinusoids, and sites of active infection all present microenvironmental parameters that partially overlap with the tumor microenvironment in ways that current release models do not adequately account for.23,24 Whether dual-stimuli or triple-stimuli gating achieves tumor-to-normal-tissue selectivity ratios sufficient for clinical safety has not been directly demonstrated in any study reviewed here; the evidence typically presented consists of comparative in vitro release curves at different pH values, supplemented by fluorescence imaging that tracks nanoparticle distribution but not drug concentration at the molecular level in target versus off-target compartments. Resolving this question requires pharmacodynamic modeling grounded in the full complexity of in vivo conditions, validated by quantitative microdialysis or mass spectrometry-based drug exposure measurements in both tumor and representative normal tissues over time. Until this standard of evidence is routinely achieved, claims of stimulus-responsive tumor selectivity remain mechanistically plausible but pharmacologically unsubstantiated.

Underlying all three of the above challenges is a design tension that the field has not yet resolved with sufficient honesty: the most mechanistically sophisticated MOF platforms are, almost without exception, the most difficult to manufacture reproducibly at clinical scale.114 Multi-step fabrication sequences involving biomimetic membrane coating, post-synthetic ligand modification, and multi-component therapeutic co-loading produce materials whose structural complexity is a genuine asset for demonstrating mechanistic novelty in a publication context but a substantial liability when batch reproducibility, shelf stability, and GMP compliance become the operative criteria. This is not purely an engineering problem amenable to incremental process optimization; it reflects a deeper question about whether the degree of structural complexity currently being pursued is actually necessary for clinical benefit, or whether it is in part an artifact of publication incentives that reward elaboration. A productive reorientation for the field would involve asking, at the outset of platform design rather than as an afterthought, what the minimum structural complexity required for the desired therapeutic outcome actually is. Continuous-flow synthesis approaches offer improved reproducibility and scalability for platforms of moderate complexity; machine learning-assisted compositional optimization, trained on existing structure-property datasets, can identify simpler formulations that retain key functional attributes.115 Whether the field is willing to accept that a clinically translatable platform may be mechanistically less impressive than a proof-of-concept study is, ultimately, a question of priorities rather than capability.

Highly elaborate architectures, such as multi-component metal-semiconductor heterojunctions or intricate yolk-shell nanocomposites, currently remain strictly in the proof-of-concept stage. Although these systems demonstrate superior in vitro catalytic activity and synergistic therapeutic effects, their complex, multi-step synthesis hinders scalable, reproducible manufacturing under current GMP guidelines. Moreover, the poorly characterized in vivo degradation kinetics and unresolved long-term toxicity profiles of multi-metal formulations present substantial regulatory challenges for clinical translation. Conversely, platforms with the greatest near-term clinical translatability emphasize architectural simplicity and compositional biocompatibility. Minimalist frameworks constructed from endogenous or clinically validated metals, such as iron-based MIL-100/101 or zinc-based ZIF-8, functionalized with regulatory-approved stealth excipients or natural biopolymers, exhibit the highest translational readiness. These streamlined systems enable reproducible, scalable synthesis under controlled conditions; possess well-characterized hydrolytic degradation pathways; and display predictable, dose-dependent pharmacokinetics in preclinical models. Accordingly, accelerating clinical advancement necessitates shifting focus away from increasingly complex hybrid composites toward systematic pharmacological and toxicological qualification of these simpler, chemically robust MOF platforms.

The integration of cancer theranostics and antibacterial functionality within a single MOF platform represents a strategically significant therapeutic frontier. The primary advantage lies in exploiting shared mechanistic vulnerabilities: both multidrug-resistant biofilms and solid tumors exhibit dense extracellular matrices and oxidative stress resilience. MOFs uniquely address these dual clinical challenges through spatiotemporally controllable ROS generation and precisely regulated metal-ion release. This dual-action capability holds strong translational promise: by concurrently ablating tumor tissue and eradicating biofilm-associated pathogens, such platforms may mitigate infection-related complications in immunocompromised oncology patients, thereby enabling safer, more effective anticancer therapy. However, uncontrolled release of potent antibacterial metal ions and nonspecific ROS generation carry substantial risks, including systemic toxicity, off-target tissue injury, and dysbiosis of the host microbiota. Realizing the clinical utility of such dual-function MOF platforms thus hinges on resolving the inherent tension between localized antimicrobial efficacy and the stringent biocompatibility requirements essential for safe systemic administration in oncology patients.

Conclusion

MOF-derived nanomaterials have established themselves as a genuinely distinctive class of biomedical platforms. What sets them apart from existing nanocarriers is not incremental improvement in delivery efficiency but access to therapeutic mechanisms that molecular drugs cannot engage: the direct induction of ferroptosis, cuproptosis, and immunogenic cell death through intrinsic metal-node reactivity; the integration of multimodal imaging with stimulus-responsive drug delivery within architecturally unified constructs; and the capacity to address cancer cytotoxicity and antibacterial function within a single platform, reflecting the clinical reality that malignancy and infection frequently co-occur. Across the broad spectrum of cancer types and therapeutic modalities reviewed here, these capabilities rest on a compelling scientific foundation. MOF-derived platforms present a superior alternative to conventional antibiotics by utilizing synchronized, multi-modal mechanisms that actively circumvent multidrug resistance. Unlike single-target therapies, MOFs eradicate pathogens through the strategic convergence of nanozyme-catalyzed ROS oxidative stress and metal-ion-dependent membrane disruption. This combinatorial synergy is particularly decisive in anti-biofilm strategies, where MOFs efficiently penetrate and dismantle protective extracellular matrices while unleashing localized biocidal redox cascades. By consolidating these diverse, resistance-evading mechanisms into a single tunable architecture, MOFs offer a highly robust paradigm for managing recalcitrant clinical infections. Yet the distance between that foundation and clinical practice remains substantial. Closing it will require a frank reorientation of research priorities: from mechanistic novelty toward pharmacokinetic rigor; from subcutaneous convenience models toward orthotopic, patient-derived, and organoid systems that faithfully recapitulate human disease biology; and from laboratory-scale synthesis toward GMP-compliant manufacturing with demonstrated batch reproducibility. New mechanisms are not what the field currently lacks. What is needed is the translational commitment to validate existing ones with the pharmacological depth and safety evidence that regulatory review and, more fundamentally, patient benefit require. With that reorientation, and with the accelerating contribution of artificial intelligence and materials genomics to rational platform design, MOF-derived nanomedicines hold genuine promise as a transformative force in precision oncology and the integrated management of infection-associated malignancy.
