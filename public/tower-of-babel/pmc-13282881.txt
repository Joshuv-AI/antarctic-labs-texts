TRPML1 agonists synergize with enzyme replacement therapy in fibroblasts from Pompe disease patients
Authors: Carla Damiano, Antonietta Tarallo, Sandra Strollo, Nadia Minopoli, Anna Valanzano, Antonia Assunto, Maria Rosaria Tuzzi, Nicola Brunetti-Pierri, Andrea Ballabio, Nicolina Cristina Sorrentino, Giancarlo Parenti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePompe disease is a severe and progressive metabolic myopathy caused by pathogenic variants of the GAA gene, deficiency of acid alpha-glucosidase (GAA), and lysosomal glycogen storage. The current standard of treatment for PD is enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) with recombinant human GAA (rhGAA). Despite significant success of ERT in correcting some disease manifestations, limitations of its efficacy have emerged, due to several factors. Poor expression or abnormal intracellular distribution of the cation-independent mannose-6-phosphate receptor (M6PR) at the plasma membrane of specific cells has been identified as one of these factors. Here, we investigated whether activation of Transient Receptor Potential Mucolipin 1 (TRPML1) synergizes with ERT. TRPML1 is a lysosomal ion channel that has been shown to induce multiple effects, including regulation of calcium homeostasis, stimulation of autophagy, activation of lysosomal biogenesis and exocytosis, enhancement of vesicle and membrane trafficking.MethodsWe studied the effects of two TRPML1 agonists in cultured fibroblasts from Pompe disease patients. Specifically, we analyzed M6PR availability at the plasma membrane of control and mutant cells, level of correction of GAA activity by rhGAA, processing and lysosomal trafficking of the recombinant enzyme.ResultsTreatment with two TRPML1 agonist drugs increased M6PR total amounts and its availability at the plasma membrane and improved M6PR intracellular recycling. The improvements in M6PR distribution translated into better correction of GAA activity in cells incubated with rhGAA and in improved lysosomal trafficking and processing of the recombinant enzyme.ConclusionThese data provide in vitro proof-of-concept evidence supporting the combination of ERT with pharmacological manipulation of secondarily altered M6PR distribution as a strategy to obtain better exposure of cells to therapeutic enzymes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08343-3.

Introduction

Pompe disease (PD; OMIM #232300) is a severe and progressive metabolic myopathy caused by pathogenic variants of the GAA gene and by deficiency of acid alpha-glucosidase (GAA), an acidic hydrolase involved in the lysosomal breakdown of glycogen. GAA deficiency results in generalized intra-lysosomal glycogen storage [1, 2] and secondary dysregulation of cellular pathways and functions, including impairment of autophagy [3, 4], mitochondrial dysfunction [5, 6], oxidative stress [7], impaired trafficking of membrane-associated proteins such as LAMP-1 [8] and the cation-independent mannose-6-phosphate receptor (M6PR) [9].

GAA deficiency results in a broad range of clinical manifestations, mostly related to heart and skeletal muscle pathology [2]. The classical infantile-onset form of the disease is characterized by severe hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, hypotonia, respiratory infections and feeding difficulties. In attenuated forms, overall referred to as late-onset PD, skeletal muscle involvement leads to progressive decline of motor and respiratory functions, while heart involvement is generally absent. All clinical forms are highly debilitating, significantly impact patients’ quality of life, and are often associated with premature death.

The current standard of treatment for PD is enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) with recombinant human GAA (rhGAA) [10] in association with supportive or palliative interventions. The enzyme preparation used so far, since its approval in 2006, is alglucosidase alpha (Myozyme). Despite significant success of alglucosidase in prolonging patient survival and improving heart and motor function, limitations of its efficacy have emerged. The response to ERT varies considerably among PD patients and in some of them resumption of disease progression after a few years of therapy has been observed [11, 12]. In infantile-onset patients treated with ERT a residual phenotype has been observed [13], due to insufficient response of some muscles that appear to be refractory to treatment and to central nervous system involvement.

Some of the factors responsible for these limitations have been identified and extensively characterized, such as the high immunogenicity of the recombinant enzyme preparation used for the treatment of patients, the cross-reactive immunologic material (CRIM) status of patients, the presence of advanced and irreversible tissue pathology. Strategies to address these factors have been developed and translated with success into clinical applications, for example immune-modulating protocols in CRIM-negative patients [14] and newborn screening programs to allow early start of treatment [15–17].

Other factors are less well understood. Among them, the dysregulation of cellular pathways and functions induced by storage is a critical aspect that is common to other lysosomal storage diseases and is emerging as a major player in the pathophysiology of these disorders [18]. Several lines of evidence indicate that secondary cellular abnormalities impact the effect of therapeutic agents in specific target tissues.

It has been shown that a substantial fraction of the therapeutic recombinant enzyme provided for ERT is unable to reach lysosomes because it is diverted to areas of autophagic build-up, thus being ineffective [4, 19, 20]. In a previous work we showed that oxidative stress affects the efficacy of ERT [7].

Poor expression or abnormal intracellular distribution of M6PR at the plasma membrane of specific cells is another interesting factor [9, 21]. Interactions between the M6PR and the recombinant enzymes used for ERT in lysosomal storage diseases (including PD) are critical for the uptake and lysosomal delivery of therapeutic enzymes. Thus, approaches aimed to enhance these interactions have been considered as a potential adjunctive therapy. Second-generation recombinant GAA enriched in mannose-6-phosphate (M6P) and with improved muscle-targeting properties have been developed and approved, as monotherapy [22] or in combination with a small-molecule stabilizer [23]. Beta-2-agonists have been proposed as agents to enhance the availability of the M6PR in muscles and tested and used in pre-clinical and clinical studies [24, 25].

In the present study we have tested the synergistic effects between Transient Receptor Potential Mucolipin 1 (TRPML1) pharmacological modulation and ERT with rhGAA in PD fibroblasts. TRPML1, encoded by the MCOLN1 gene, is a lysosomal ion channel that has been shown to induce multiple effects, including regulation of calcium homeostasis [26, 27]; activation of autophagy [28, 29]; lysosomal biogenesis and exocytosis [27]; vesicle, membrane and endolysosomal trafficking [26, 30]. The effects of TRPML1 on vesicle and membrane-trafficking are potentially of interest as they may result into improved M6PR intracellular distribution.

To evaluate this approach, in a proof-of-concept in vitro study we tested two TRPML1 agonists in fibroblasts from PD patients for their ability to restore M6PR intracellular localization and availability at the plasma membrane, facilitate rhGAA lysosomal trafficking, processing, and correction of GAA activity.

Materials and methods

Patients’ cells

Primary human fibroblasts and myoblast cell lines from patients with PD were obtained from the cell bank of the Department of Translational Medical Sciences (DISMET), Section of Pediatrics, Federico II University, Naples.

Fibroblasts were cultured at 37°C with 5% CO2 in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Invitrogen, NY, USA) and 20% fetal bovine serum (Invitrogen, NY, USA), supplemented with 2 mM/L-glutamine, and antibiotics (Invitrogen, NY, USA). Myoblasts were expanded in proliferation medium consisting of high-Glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (Invitrogen, NY, USA), supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (Invitrogen, NY, USA), 10 ng/mL EGF (R&D Systems, MN, USA), 2 ng/mL β-FGF (R&D Systems, MN, USA), 10 μg/mL insulin (Roche Applied Science, IN, USA) and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin-Glutamine solution (Invitrogen, NY, USA). All cell lines were grown in a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere at 37 °C. Both fibroblast and myoblast cell lines were obtained for diagnostic purposes. Patients or their legal guardians provided their informed consent to the storage of cells and to their use for future research purposes.

The experiments on correction of GAA activity by rhGAA were performed in all cell lines. All other experiments (immunofluorescence, oxidative stress assay, and evaluation of M6PR availability and trafficking) were performed in three of the cell lines, PD1, PD2 and PD3, representative of the severe classic infantile and of the intermediate clinical forms.

GAA activity assay

GAA activity was measured using the fluorogenic substrate 4-methylumbelliferyl-α-D glucopyranoside (4MU) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), as previously described [7]. Cells were harvested by trypsinization and lysed by five cycles of freezing and thawing. Cell homogenates (10 μg of total protein) were incubated in a final volume of 20 μL containing 2 mM substrate in 0.2 M acetate buffer (pH 4.0) for 1 hour at 37 °C. Reactions were stopped by adding 1 mL of 0.5 M glycine carbonate buffer (pH 10.7). Fluorescence was read at 365 nm (excitation) and 450 nm (emission) using a Modulus 9200 fluorometer (Turner Biosystems).

Western blot analysis

After trypsinization, cells were lysed in water (for GAA detection) or RIPA buffer (for LC3 detection). Total protein concentration was determined using the Lowry method. Equal amounts of protein (20 μg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. After blocking with 5% milk, membranes were washed with Tris-buffer saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBS-T), incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, anti-GAA (PRIMM, Milan, Italy) and anti-LC3 (Novus Biologicals, Littleton, CO, USA). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-coupled secondary antibodies (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) for 1 hour at room temperature. Immunoreactive proteins were detected by chemiluminescence (BioRad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA).

Images were acquired using a ChemiDocTM XRS+ imaging system and analyzed with Image LabTM Software (BioRad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Band intensities were quantified by densitometry and normalized either to endogenous reference protein or to total protein using stain-free technology. For stain-free normalization, total lane signal was quantified under UV illumination.

Oxidative stress assays

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein (DCFDA, Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA), as described before [7]. After 48 hours of treatment, cells were washed twice with PBS plus (1 mM CaCl2, 0.5 mM MgCl2 and 30 mM glucose), harvested, and resuspended at 1 × 105 cells/mL. Cells were then incubated with 25 μM H2-DCFDA for 30 minutes at 37 °C. Fluorescence (Ex/Em:488/525 nm) using a Tecan microplate reader. ROS levels were expressed as the percentage of DCF fluorescence relative to untreated controls.

Lipid peroxidation was assessed using thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) assay. After incubation, 5 × 104 cells were collected and resuspended in 0.67% thiobarbituric acid (TBA) and 20% trichloroacetic acid (TCA). Samples were heated at 95 °C for 30 minutes, incubated in ice for 10 minutes and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. Absorbance was measured at 532 nm. Lipid peroxidation levels were expressed as percentage of absorbance relative to untreated controls.

Total glutathione (GSH) content was determined by 5,5’-dithiobis-2-nitrobenzoic acid (DTNB; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Protein extracts (50 μg) were incubated with 3 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and 144 μM DTNB (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) in 30 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.2) and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 5 minutes, absorbance was measured at 412 nm using a microplate reader (Epoch Biotek Winooski, USA). GSH levels were calculated from a standard curve (1–500 μM) and expressed relative to untreated controls.

All assays were performed in triplicate and repeated in at least three independent experiments.

FACS analysis

Surface M6PR expression was quantified by flow cytometry. Following treatment, fibroblasts were detached and resuspended in blocking buffer (PBS containing 1% FBS). After blocking, cells were incubated for 30 minutes on ice with a mouse anti-M6PR antibody (Novus Biologicals, Littleton, CO, USA). Cells were washed and incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibody (Invitrogen Corporation, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for 30 minutes on ice. After washing, cells were filtered through 70 μm cell strainers (BD Biosciences Pharmingen, San Jose, CA, USA) and analyzed using a BD Biosciences FACS Aria III (BD Biosciences Pharmingen, San Jose, CA, USA). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software. At least 10,000 events were acquired per samples after single discrimination by FSC/SSC gating. Surface M6PR expression was expressed as mean fluorescence intensity.

Immunofluorescence analysis

The intracellular distribution of GAA/LAMP2 was examined in human fibroblasts grown on coverslips fixed with methanol, permeabilized with PBS containing 0,1% Triton X-100 and blocked with 0.05% saponin and 1% BSA diluted in PBS (blocking solution) at room temperature for 1 hour. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-GAA rabbit polyclonal antiserum (PRIMM, Milan, Italy), and with an anti-LAMP2 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, USA). The following day, cells were incubated for 1 hour at room temperature with anti-rabbit IgG and anti-mouse IgG secondary antibodies in blocking solution (Invitrogen Corporation, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Finally, samples were mounted in Vectashield mounting medium containing DAPI (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, USA).

To assess autophagic activity and lysosomal function, immunofluorescence staining for LC3 and LAMP2 was performed. Cells were fixed with methanol for 5 minutes at −20 °C and after blocking with 10% FBS and 0.1% saponin at room temperature for 1 hour, were incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-LC3 rabbit polyclonal antiserum (Novus Biologicals, Littleton, CO, USA), and with an anti-LAMP2 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, USA). After washing, cells were incubated with anti-rabbit IgG and anti-mouse IgG secondary antibodies in blocking solution at room temperature for 1 hour and then mounted in Vectashield with DAPI.

To investigate the distribution of M6PR, fibroblasts grown on coverslips were fixed using 4% para- formaldehyde (PFA) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), permeabilized with 0.05% saponin and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA), in PBS with the addition of 50 mM NH4Cl, 0.02% NaN3 for 1 hour. Cells were incubated with the primary anti-M6PR antibody (Novus Biologicals, Littleton, CO, USA) overnight at 4 °C; and were incubated with an anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (Invitrogen Corporation, Carlsbad, CA, USA) 1 hour at room temperature. Samples were then washed, mounted with DAPI (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA).

Images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM700 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany) integrated with the AxioCam MR camera and 63x oil objective. Digital images were captured by using Zeiss AxioVision software and maximum intensity projections were generated using Fiji (ImageJ, NIH, USA) software.

M6PR plasma membrane recycling assay

To assess M6PR recycling from the plasma membrane, PD and control fibroblasts were pre-treated with WR54-006 or WR7-003 (0.3 μM) for 48 hours. Cells were incubated with an anti-M6PR antibody (Novus Biologicals, Littleton, CO, USA) at 16 °C for 1 hour to induce surface binding and to synchronize receptor trafficking while preventing internalization, then transferred to 37 °C to promote receptor internalization and recycling and fixed at the indicated time points (0 and 30 min). After fixation using 4% PFA, cells were permeabilized and co-stained with an antibody against the trans-Golgi network marker TGN46 (Bio-Techne, Minneapolis, MN, USA). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM700 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany) integrated with the AxioCam MR camera and 63x oil objective.

RNA isolation and quantitative real time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cultured fibroblasts using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, MD, USA) according to the manufacturer′s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed spectrophotometrically. cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the High-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (Thermo Scientific, MA, USA). qRT-PCR was performed with TaqMan gene expression assay kit, (Applied Biosystems; Grand Island, NY, USA) on a LightCycler® 480 System (Roche Diagnostics, Risch-Rotkreuz, Switzerland). TRPML1 (MCOLN1) (TaqMan Assay ID: Hs01100653_m1) expression levels were normalized to GAPDH (TaqMan Assay ID: Hs02786624_g1) as endogenous control. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.1.1 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. For multiple group comparisons, one-way ANOVA followed by Šídák’s multiple comparison test was applied, as specified in the corresponding figure legends. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance was defined as follows: ****p < 0.0001, ***p < 0.001, **p < 0.01, *p < 0.05.

We first tested whether the two agonist compounds elicit the known TRPML1 effects on diverse cellular functions in cultured PD fibroblasts. To this purpose we incubated PD cells either with 0.3 μM WR54-006 or with 0.3 μM WR7-003 for 48 hours. Both compounds increased TRPML1 expression (Supplementary Figure S2) analyzed by qRT-PCR.

To verify the effects of enhanced TRPML1 expression in fibroblasts, we analyzed the amounts and localization of Microtubule-associated protein 1A/1B-light chain 3 (LC3) and of the Lysosome-associated membrane protein 2 (LAMP2). LC3-I and LC3-II are key protein markers used to monitor autophagy. Cytosolic LC3-I is converted into the lipidated, membrane-bound LC3-II during autophagosome formation. The ratio of LC3-II to LC3-I is a reliable marker for autophagy activity. LAMP2 mediates the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes and is a marker of the late-endosomal/lysosomal compartment. Enhanced TRPML1 expression resulted in an increased LC3-II/LC3-I ratio (Fig. 1C, D) by western blot analysis, increased LC3 signal by immunofluorescence analysis, and improved co-localization of LC3 with LAMP2 (Fig. 1E, F, Supplementary Figure S3), indicating enhanced autophagosome-lysosome fusions.

In addition, incubation of PD fibroblasts with WR54-006 and WR7-003 significantly decreased oxidative stress levels with reduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and of lipid peroxidation, and increase of GSH levels (Fig. 1G), as a measure of improved function of the autophagic-lysosomal pathway and enhanced removal of oxidized materials and organelles [31].

Overall, these results indicate that WR54-006 and WR7-003 are active in PD fibroblasts and induce the expected effects of TRPML1 activation, and that PD fibroblasts are a suitable in vitro model to study a possible synergy with rhGAA.

TRPML1 agonists improve M6PR cellular distribution

We next tested whether TRPML1 agonists impact on M6PR amounts and subcellular localization. Immunofluorescence staining with anti-M6PR antibody and quantitative analysis of M6PR, showed reduced amounts of the receptor in PD cells compared to controls (Fig. 2A, B). Total cellular M6PR levels were reduced by 41% in PD fibroblasts relative to control cells. These data are consistent with previous findings obtained in our laboratory [7, 9]. We also studied the availability of the receptor at the plasma membrane, a critical element for efficient uptake and lysosomal delivery of recombinant enzymes, in PD cell lines in comparison with control fibroblasts by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis. Cells were kept in ice-cold medium to prevent internalization of the M6PR and to label with the anti-M6PR antibody only the fraction of receptor exposed at the plasma membrane. The amounts of M6PR available at the plasma membrane were reduced in PD cell lines (Fig. 2C, D). On average, PD fibroblasts showed a 29% reduction in plasma membrane M6PR availability compared with controls cells, again in keeping with previous results obtained by our laboratory [7, 9]. Histogram overlays (Fig. 2C) showed a uniform leftward shift in PD fibroblasts, consistent with reduced plasma membrane M6PR levels throughout the cell population.

We then studied the effects of treatment with TRPML1 agonists on M6PR distribution. When cells were treated with both compounds and analyzed by FACS, as indicated above, the amounts of receptor exposed at the plasma membrane increased both in control and in PD fibroblasts (Fig. 3A, B). In PD fibroblasts, treatment with WR54-006 and WR7-003 increased surface M6PR levels by 48 and 29%, respectively, compared with untreated PD cells. To gather further information on the mechanism underlying this effect, we analyzed the receptor recycling from the plasma membrane. After pre-treatment with TRPML1 agonists, M6PR trafficking was synchronized in PD and control cells at low temperature (16°C) (Fig. 3C). Cells were then warmed at 37 °C and the receptor co-localization with the trans-Golgi network marker TGN46 was analyzed by immunofluorescence at different time points. In untreated PD fibroblasts the M6PR signal did not show the typical juxta-nuclear localization and appeared diffuse at 30 minutes with a poor co-localization with TGN46 (Fig. 3D). In cells treated with WR54-006 and WR7-003 the M6PR signal was more condensed (white arrows), with a pattern similar to that seen in control cells, and colocalized with TGN46 (Fig. 3 Dand inset). A quantitative analysis of the M6PR signal colocalizing with the TGN46-defined areas showed significant increases in cells treated with WR54-006 and WR7-003 (Fig. 3E).

TRPML1 agonists enhance correction of GAA activity by alglucosidase alpha and rhGAA lysosomal trafficking

The effect of TRPML1 agonists on M6PR distribution and recycling is expected to be advantageous for the uptake and lysosomal trafficking of the rhGAA used for ERT. Therefore, we tested the effects of these compounds on the level of correction of GAA activity by rhGAA, on the maturation of the recombinant enzyme and on its lysosomal trafficking.

PD fibroblasts were incubated with WR54-006 and WR7-003 concentrations of 0.3 μM for 48 hours. During the last 24 hours of incubation 50 μM rhGAA was added to the medium (Fig. 4A). In all cell lines, co-incubation with rhGAA and TRPML1 activators resulted in enhanced GAA activity correction (Fig. 4B). To better evaluate the overall effect, data were averaged and normalized to rhGAA-treated samples (set to 100%), showing a significant increase in GAA activity, with an average fold increase of 2.05 for WR54-006 and of 2.60 for WR7-003 (Fig. 4C). This enhancing effect on GAA activity correction was abolished by 30 μM ML-SI1, a TRPML1 inhibitor (Fig. 4D, Supplementary Figure S5), confirming that the effect of WR54-006 and WR7-003 was mediated by TRPML1 activation. We also studied the effects of WR54-006 and WR7-003 in two cultured myoblast cell lines from PD patients (Supplementary Figure 4A). The enhancing effect of TRPML1 agonists was also detectable in these cells but varied among the different lines depending on TRPML1 agonists concentrations (Supplementary Figure 4B).

We also evaluated the effect of TRPML1 agonists on the processing of the recombinant enzyme used for ERT in PD. Alglucosidase alpha (rhGAA) is produced as 110 KDa precursor that, after cellular uptake, is processed into mature active 70–76 KDa isoforms during lysosomal trafficking. In two PD cell lines co-treated with rhGAA and TRPML1 agonists, both the amount of internalized enzyme and its processing into mature active isoforms were significantly increased (Fig. 4E, F). The effect of TRPML1 agonists on endogenous residual GAA was negligible and not significant (Supplementary Figure S6).

The improved processing of rhGAA by western blot analysis pointed to an improved lysosomal trafficking of the recombinant enzyme. To obtain further information on rhGAA lysosomal delivery we looked at rhGAA co-localization with the late endosome/lysosomal marker LAMP2 by immunofluorescence analysis in three cell lines from infantile onset/intermediate PD patients (PD1, PD2, PD3). While poor rhGAA signal and colocalization were seen in cells treated with rhGAA alone, significant improvements in rhGAA/LAMP2 colocalization were observed in the cell lines pre-treated with both WR54-006 and WR7-003 (Fig. 4G, H, Supplementary Figure S7).

Discussion

The scope of this study is to provide in vitro proof-of-concept evidence supporting the hypothesis that TRPML1 activation synergizes with ERT for PD. Dysregulation of multiple cellular pathways and functions is emerging as an important player in the pathophysiology of lysosomal storage diseases. Although the primary and indispensable goal of therapies is to correct enzyme deficiencies, adjunctive therapies that are based on different approaches or target the secondary abnormalities triggered by storage may be warranted and are being supported by growing evidence.

Co-dosing of ERT with other therapeutic agents to enhance its efficacy has already been evaluated in PD. For example, antioxidants [7] to modify stress and enhance correction of GAA activity, or small molecule chaperones to stabilize recombinant enzymes while they are trafficking through unfavorable compartments [23, 32] have been tested in preclinical and clinical studies. Substrate reduction therapy has been shown to result into better glycogen clearance if used in combination with ERT [33]. Beta-2-agonists that enhance M6PR availability in muscles have been evaluated in pre-clinical and clinical studies [24, 25]. Other approaches have been developed to modulate immune response against recombinant enzymes [14, 34].

Our study, for the first time, shows the results of the combination of ERT with small molecule TRPML1 agonists and investigates the molecular mechanisms of this synergy.

TRPML1 activates Ca2 + -dependent pathways involved in multiple cellular functions, such as induction of calcineurin-mediated nuclear translocation of transcription factor EB (TFEB) [28, 35] and autophagic vesicle biogenesis by a non-transcriptional, TFEB-independent mechanism based on activation of calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase kinase β (CaMKKβ), AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), induction of the Beclin1/VPS34 autophagic complex, and generation of phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate (PI3P) [27].

Previous work has shown that autophagy is impaired in PD [3], with secondary accumulation of autophagic material, particularly in muscles. In our work we documented that TRPML1 overexpression has detectable effects on autophagy and vesicle trafficking in PD fibroblasts by demonstrating the increased amounts of the lipidated LC3-II form, increased co-localization of LC3 with LAMP2, and improved clearance of ROS as a measure of autophagy function. TPRML1-mediated autophagy is a potential therapeutic target, and its modulation may provide clues for developing innovative strategies for the treatment of lysosomal storage diseases. For example, it has been reported that activation of TFEB increases lysosomal proteostasis and enhances clearance of lysosomal storage, including cholesterol accumulation, in Niemann–Pick disease type C (NPC) cells [36].

TRPML1 also promotes the trafficking of vesicles, membranes, and membrane-associated proteins, and promotes lysosomal exocytosis [30, 37], thus driving the transport of intracellular vesicles containing CI-M6PR to the plasma membrane. These effects may be relevant for the availability of M6PR at the plasma membrane [38].

Interactions between the M6P moieties of recombinant enzymes and the M6PR are well recognized requisites for the success of ERT [39]. Limitations in the efficacy of alglucosidase alpha in PD patients can in part be ascribed to the relative low amounts of M6P in this enzyme preparation [40]. To address this issue, research in PD has recently been centered on the development of second-generation recombinant GAA enzymes enriched in M6P to facilitate interactions between the enzyme and the M6PR, and to obtain improved muscle targeting.

However, the availability of M6PR levels at the plasma membrane may represent an additional, critical and limiting factor affecting uptake of lysosomal enzymes, including the new-generation preparations enriched in M6P. In principle, it may be expected that optimal results and higher tissue exposure to therapeutic enzymes would be obtained by combining both the use of M6P-enriched recombinant enzymes and an increased availability of M6PR in cells. The combination of drugs increasing M6PR availability and new-generation enzyme is an attractive strategy that should be tested as a follow up of our research. Thus, the approach proposed by us is complementary to ERT and aimed at modulating secondarily affected cellular pathways, rather than replacing existing therapies.

Although not formally proven, our results support the hypothesis that the enhancement of ERT is dependent on the cellular distribution of the M6PR at the plasma membrane. We showed that the two compounds tested improve M6PR total amounts and availability at the plasma membrane. The improvements in M6PR distribution were associated with efficient alglucosidase alpha processing, lysosomal delivery and improved correction of GAA activity in cells incubated with this recombinant enzyme.

To test whether TRPML1 activation is a feasible approach to enhance ERT we chose to use cultured PD fibroblasts, as already performed in several of our previous studies on PD. Fibroblasts have the advantage that can be obtained from patients with minimally invasive procedures, can be easily manipulated in culture, and can be exposed in vitro to a variety of pharmacological agents or experimental conditions. In previous work we showed that fibroblasts are suitable to study the uptake, trafficking and processing of rhGAA [32, 41] and to study or modulate secondary derangement of cellular pathways, such as oxidative stress [7].

Although the two drugs used in our studies have not been tested for in vivo use, our data provide the bases for pharmacological manipulation of secondarily altered cellular pathways as a strategy to obtain better exposure of tissues to therapeutic enzymes and, possibly, better outcome of patients.

To understand whether this translates into better substrate clearance will require in vivo studies evaluating both glycogen clearance, and possible tissue-specific responses. In principle, the efficacy of each of the drug may vary in different tissues, depending on factors such as their metabolism, pharmacokinetic properties, absorption. Future in vivo studies will also have the potential to assess functional outcomes such as muscle strength and respiratory function.
