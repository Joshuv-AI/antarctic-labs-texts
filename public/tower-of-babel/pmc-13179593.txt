Twenty‐year results of the randomized European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer trial 22922/10925 evaluating internal mammary chain and medial supraclavicular lymph node irradiation in stage I–III breast cancer
Authors: Orit Kaidar‐Person, Caroline G Weltens, Catherine Fortpied, Luc J E E Scheijmans, Carine Y Kirkove, Volker Budach, Karine Peignaux‐Casasnovas, Mariacarla Valli, Max Peters, Femke van der Leij, Sofia Rivera, Nicola Weidner, Desirée H J G van den Bongard, Claudia Linsenmeier, Roxolyana Abdah‐Bortnyak, Shaymaa Hosni, Eveline Koiter, Antoine M Engelen, Adinda Baten, Lydia Champezou, Alain Fourquet, Harry G M M Bartelink, Henk Struikmans, Philip M P Poortmans, for the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Radiation Oncology and Breast Cancer Groups
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEuropean Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer trial EORTC 22922/10925 evaluated internal mammary and medial supraclavicular (IM‐MS) lymph node irradiation (IM‐MS‐RT) in patients with stage I–III breast cancer. Eligible patients had involved axillary nodes and/or centrally/medially located tumors regardless of nodal involvement. The primary end point was overall survival, secondary end points were disease‐free survival, distant metastases‐free survival, breast cancer mortality, and any breast recurrence. Between 1996 and 2004, 4004 patients were randomized. The median patient age was 54 years. At a median follow‐up of 22.2 years, 1550 (38.7%) patients died, of whom 796 (51.4%) died from breast cancer. At 20 years, the overall survival rate was 61.8% in the control group versus 61.0% in the IM‐MS‐RT group (hazard ratio [HR], 1.00; p = .967); the disease‐free survival rate was 49.0% versus 48.2%, respectively (HR, 0.97; p = .515); and the distant metastases‐free survival rate was 59.8% versus 58.9%, respectively (HR, 0.97; p = .578). The breast cancer mortality rate was 22.4% in the control group and 18.6% in the IM‐MS‐RT group (HR, 0.82; p = .006), whereas the rate of deaths not from breast cancer or from unknown causes was 15.8% versus 20.4%, respectively (HR, 1.26; p = .002). Lung fibrosis, cardiac fibrosis, and cardiac diseases were more frequent after IM‐MS‐RT versus no IM‐MS‐RT (6.3% vs. 3.2%, 2.7% vs. 1.7%. and 15.2% vs. 11.7%, respectively); and the rates of severe cardiac and lung morbidities (scores of 3 or 4) were 1.9% versus 1.7% and 0.3% versus 0.0%, respectively. Breast cancer mortality at 20 years was statistically significantly lower after IM‐MS‐RT, but deaths not from breast cancer increased after 15 years, resulting in no long‐term benefit of IM‐MS‐RT on overall survival. Therefore, the authors strongly call for very long‐term follow‐up of treatments for prognostically favorable cancers such as breast cancer.Keywords: breast cancer, European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC), internal mammary, lymph nodes, radiotherapy, randomized trial

INTRODUCTION

The predictive, sequential pattern of breast cancer spread from the primary tumor to the regional lymph nodes and thereafter at distant organs is a fundamental principle that has governed clinical breast cancer management for nearly a century, starting from Halsted's radical mastectomy first applied in 1889. 1 , 2 Fisher and colleagues' systemic hypothesis, supported by randomized controlled trials—according to which breast cancer distant spread occurs early in the course of the disease—led to the adoption of less radical surgery for breast cancer. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 This, however, does not contradict the specific hierarchy of breast cancer spread primarily to the lower axillary levels (levels 1 and 2) in laterocranial and internal mammary (IM) nodes in a medial direction, from which it only subsequently spreads to the apex of the axilla and possibly to the medial supraclavicular (MS) lymph nodes (axilla levels 3 and 4), with its likelihood and extension depending on tumor burden and tumor location within the breast. Up to today, the spectrum hypothesis, in which Hellman merged the hypotheses from Halsted and Fisher et al., is the foundation of regional nodal radiation therapy (RT) management in breast cancer. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Irregular distribution and skipping nodal metastasis are unusual in patients who have breast cancer without predisposing events, 9 supporting quite straightforward RT indications and target volumes. Nonetheless, for many years, IM‐RT remained controversial because data were conflicting for breast cancer outcomes versus RT‐related morbidity and mortality. 12 , 13 Older studies demonstrated that inclusion of the IM nodes at least doubled the heart dose and increased the lung dose, explaining the increased risks of mainly cardiovascular and lung morbidity and mortality. 14 , 15 Also, match‐line fibrosis caused by a local overdosage of RT at the junction zone between the IM and breast/chest wall fields in the two‐dimensional RT planning era became of greater importance after the introduction of breast‐conserving therapy in the 1980s. The need to re‐evaluate the role of IM and MS (levels 3 and 4) nodal RT (IM‐MS‐RT) in patients with breast cancer, integrating a thorough RT quality‐assurance program, formed the rationale for the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) 22922‐10925 IM‐MS‐RT trial in patients with stage I–III breast cancer (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT0000285). Because one of the key assumptions of the trial was that the effect of IM‐MS‐RT on survival, when balancing its benefits and side effects, will not appear before 10 years after treatment and may be delayed until 20 years, the trial was planned for its last analysis to be performed at 20 years' follow‐up (see Supporting Information S1).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and participants

EORTC trial 22922/10925 is a prospective multicenter, randomized, phase 3 trial that recruited patients between 1996 and 2004. The study design has been previously reported. 16 In summary, eligibility criteria included women aged 75 years and younger; unilateral, histologically confirmed breast adenocarcinoma of any type; and stage I–III breast cancer with a primary tumor located centrally or in the medial quadrants of the breast, irrespective of axillary involvement, or a primary tumor located in any breast quadrant combined with axillary lymph node involvement. Surgery consisted of mastectomy or breast‐conserving surgery and axillary lymph node dissection (ALND). For the last years of the trial, the protocol was amended to include sentinel lymph node biopsy, followed by an ALND in case of involved sentinel lymph nodes.

Systemic therapy was applied after surgery (postoperative) and according to physician's preference and institutional or national guidelines. At the time of the trial, tamoxifen was recommended in postmenopausal women who had estrogen receptor (ER)‐positive tumors, whereas women who had ER‐negative tumors and/or were premenopausal (regardless of ER status) with more advanced‐stage disease were treated with postoperative chemotherapy. 17 Tamoxifen was not applied for premenopausal women with ER‐positive tumors. 17

Randomization and masking

After signing written informed consent forms, patients were centrally randomized at the EORTC headquarters to receive either RT or no RT of the IM‐MS lymph nodes. A minimization algorithm with a 1:1 ratio was used for the randomization considering the following stratification factors: treatment center, menopausal status, site of the primary tumor within the breast, type of breast surgery, type of axillary dissection (as stated by the surgeon), pathologic tumor (T) classification, and pathologic nodal (N) status. Neither patients nor investigators were masked to treatment allocation.

Procedures

RT was planned to start within 8 weeks of the last surgery, not exceeding 11 weeks from randomization. If chemotherapy was received after surgery, RT was applied within 6 weeks of completing the last cycle, not exceeding 8 months after surgery. The prescribed RT dose was 50 grays (Gy) in 25 fractions of 2 Gy, once daily, 5 days a week, over 5 weeks to the breast/chest wall with or without the IM‐MS lymph nodes, including the undissected part of the axilla. The trial allowed the exclusion of patients with inner upper quadrant tumors after breast‐conserving surgery, in which case RT of the breast might inevitably include part of the IM.

The RT protocol was set for IM‐MS‐RT and not for the primary site (breast or chest wall), and RT of the primary was according to physician preference and institutional guidelines. Therefore, an additional tumor bed boost after whole‐breast RT and chest wall RT postmastectomy were left to the discretion of the treating physician. Axillary RT (axillary lymph node levels 1–2; operated axilla), according to the protocol, was allowed only in patients who underwent microscopic or macroscopic incomplete ALND. If such a patient was randomized to the control group (no IM‐MS‐RT), an MS field was not allowed in case of axillary RT.

RT was subjected to centralized quality assurance, which was predefined and done by a centralized multidisciplinary RT quality‐assurance (RTQA) team. It included an evaluation of the ability of the trial sites to adhere to the RT protocol and a preplanned quality‐assurance program. Three IM‐MS‐RT techniques were allowed. 18 The RTQA evaluations for EORTC trial 22922/10925 were published at early phases of the trial. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 Adjacent to the end of accrual, the RTQA team distributed a case report form containing questions concerning the Statement on IM‐MS Chain Irradiation Policy, including information on individual localization of the IM nodes, the RT technique used for IM‐MS‐RT, and whether this was changed during the trial. This information was used to verify and update the information obtained with the RTQA procedures. 17

After completion of treatment, patients were seen yearly for their disease status and survival for the first 5 years after randomization and then every 2 years or upon recurrence or death. Apart from performance status, the presence of side effects, both longitudinally at every follow‐up and cross‐sectionally at 5‐year intervals, was scored on dedicated case report forms (see Supporting Information S2: Supporting Materials 2 and 3).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was overall survival (OS). Secondary end points were disease‐free survival (DFS), distant metastases‐free survival (DMFS), breast cancer mortality (BCM), and the time to any breast recurrence (BR). All end points, which were defined according to the DATECAN (Definition for the Assessment of Time‐to‐Event Endpoints in Cancer) guidelines, 23 were calculated from randomization to the date of the first event of interest (see Supporting Information S2: Table S1). Death from causes other than breast cancer was considered as a competing risk for BCM and BR; contralateral breast cancer and second nonbreast cancer were additional competing risks for BR. Patients without any event of interest or competing risk at the cutoff date for the analysis were censored at the date of last follow‐up. The trial was preplanned for 10‐year, 16 15‐year, 24 and 20‐year analyses of these outcomes. The trial was planned to capture and report treatment‐related side effects in addition to breast cancer outcomes. 25 , 26

Statistical analysis

Time‐to‐event curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method were and compared based on a two‐sided log‐rank test. The cumulative incidences of BCM and BR were compared using the Gray test. Hazard ratios (HRs) and IM‐MS‐RT versus no IM‐MS‐RT were estimated by using a unadjusted Cox model (or Fine and Gray models for end points with competing risks). An exploratory analysis of predictive factors for OS was conducted using forest plots. All analyses were performed according to the intention‐to‐treat principle. All tests were conducted at the two‐sided 5% significance level. Statistical analyses were conducted at the clinical cutoff date of June 2, 2025.

RESULTS

Between July 1996 and January 2004, 46 radiation oncology departments from 13 countries accrued a total of 4004 patients. The CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) flow diagram is displayed as Supporting Information S2: Figure S1. Thirty‐two of the 4004 randomized patients (0.8%) were clinically ineligible, mainly because of concurrent diseases and inadequate tumor stage. Another 106 patients (2.6%) were not treated according to randomization or protocol. Patient‐related, tumor‐related, and treatment‐related characteristics of the patients were well balanced between the control and intervention groups (Table 1): The median age was 54 years (range, 19–75 years), 59.0% of patients were postmenopausal, 95.8% had a primary tumor size of up to 5 cm, and 87.5% had no or up to three involved axillary lymph nodes. The majority (n = 3049; 76.1%) underwent breast‐conserving surgery followed by whole‐breast RT, which, in 85.1%, was followed by a boost to the primary tumor bed. In total, 955 patients (23.9%) underwent mastectomy, and chest wall RT was received by 73.4% of patients in both arms. Most patients underwent ALND (n = 2197; 54.9%), 38.0% underwent partial axillary dissection, and 7.1% had a negative sentinel lymph node biopsy without further axillary surgery. Axillary RT was received by 8.3% and 7.4% of patients who did or did not receive IM‐MS‐RT, respectively. Nearly all node‐positive (99.0%) and 66.3% of node‐negative patients received postoperative systemic treatment. IM‐MS‐RT received by 1944 patients (97.1%) who were randomized to receive it and by 41 (2.0%) who were randomized not to receive it.

The median follow‐up, calculated by censoring deaths and patients who were lost to follow‐up, is 22.2 years (interquartile range, 20.2–24.5 years). The median follow‐up duration was very similar in both treatment arms (22.2 and 22.3 years in the groups without and with IM‐MS‐RT [the control and intervention groups], respectively). Overall, 612 patients (15.3%) were lost to follow‐up. In total, 1842 patients (46.0%) were alive at their last follow‐up contact, including 918 (45.9%) and 924 (46.2%) patients in the control and intervention groups, respectively. In both groups, the main cause of death was breast cancer, which occurred in 796 patients (51.4% of all deaths). Causes of death are listed in Table 2 and in Supporting Information S2: Table S2. The OS rate at 20 years was 61.8% (95% confidence interval [CI], 59.4%–64.1%) and 61.0% (95% CI, 58.6%–63.4%) in the control and intervention groups, respectively (HR, 1.00; 95% CI. 0.90–1.10; p = .9671; Figure 1A). After adjustment for the stratification factors (except institution), the analysis with 3979 patients, excluding the patients for whom information for the final pathologic tumor and nodal stage was missing, also indicated no significant treatment effect of IM‐MS‐RT on OS (HR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.89–1.09; p = .743). No statistically significant heterogeneity was identified in any subgroup analysis defined by the stratification factors or other selected factors. Only the number of lymph nodes examined resulted in a borderline significant interaction (p = .061), which suggests that the IM‐MS‐RT benefit may be more favorable in the subgroup of patients who have <10 axillary lymph nodes examined compared with those who have ≥10 lymph nodes examined (Figure 2).

At the time of this final analysis, 942 patients (47.1%) in the control group and 942 patients (47.1%) in the intervention group experienced a recurrence of breast cancer, a second breast cancer, or died from any cause as an event for DFS. The DFS rate at 20 years was 49.0% (95% CI, 46.4%–51.5%) and 48.2% (95% CI, 45.6%–50.7%) in the control and intervention groups, respectively (HR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.89–1.06; p = .5148; Figure 1B). Distant metastases were the most frequent first event for DFS, occurring in 381 (40.4%) and 344 (36.5%) of patients in the control and intervention groups, respectively, followed by death from causes other than breast cancer in 206 (21.9%) and 261 (27.7%) patients, respectively.

The 20‐year DMFS rate was 59.8% (95% CI, 57.3%–62.2%) and 58.9% (95% CI, 56.4%–61.4%) in the control and intervention groups, respectively (HR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.88–1.08; p = .5777; Figure 1C).

Breast cancer was the cause of death for 431 and 365 patients in the control and intervention groups, respectively, representing a relative proportion of 56.3% versus 46.6% of all deaths in each arm and 21.5% versus 18.2% for all 4004 patients, respectively. The BCM rate at 20 years was 22.4% (95% CI, 20.5%–24.3%) and 18.6% (95% CI, 16.8%–20.4%) in the control and intervention groups, respectively (HR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.72–0.95; p = .006; Figure 1D). Nonbreast cancer death occurred in 297 (38.8%) and 367 (46.8%) patients in the control and intervention groups, respectively, and cause of death was unknown for 38 (5.0%) and 51 (6.5%) patients, respectively. Cumulative incidence curves of death from causes other than breast cancer and from unknown causes indicate a late separation: the cumulative incidence rate was similar at 15 years (approximately 10%); whereas, at 20 years, it was 15.8% (95% CI, 14.1%–17.7%) and 20.4% (95% CI, 18.4%–22.4%) in the control and intervention groups, respectively (HR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.09–1.46; p = .0016; Figure 3). At the time of analysis, the observed number of patients who had any type of breast cancer recurrence recorded at any time during follow‐up was 567 (28.3%) and 514 (25.7%) in the control and intervention groups, respectively. The cumulative incidence rate of any type of breast cancer recurrence at 20 years was 30.2% (95% CI, 28.1%–32.4%) and 27.3% (95% CI, 25.2%–29.4%) in the control and intervention groups, respectively (HR, 0.88; 95% CI, 0.78–0.99; p = .0369; see Supporting Information S2: Figure S2). An overview of all primary and secondary end points for the three primary analyses, at 10, 15, and 20 years, is summarized in Supporting Information S2: Table S4. Ipsilateral regional lymph node recurrence occurred in a total of 187 (4.7%) patients, including 110 (5.5%) and 77 (3.8%) in the control and intervention groups, respectively. The most frequent reported site in the absence of other sites of the first regional lymph node recurrences (a patient may have had several sites involved at first recurrence) was axillary levels (1–2) for both groups: 43 of 110 (39.1%) and 33 of 77 (42.9%) patients in the control and intervention groups, respectively, followed by MS RT in 39 of 110 (35.5%) and 29 of 77 (37.7%) patients, respectively. The IM lymph node chain as the site of first recurrence was more than double in the control group (11 of 110 patients; 10.0%) compared with four of 77 patients (5.2%) in the intervention group. Local recurrence occurred in a total of 324 of 4004 patients (8.1%), including 168 (8.4%) and 156 (7.8%) in the control and intervention groups, respectively. The most frequent site of local recurrence was in breast tissue outside the primary tumor bed after breast‐conserving therapy in 138 of 3049 patients (4.5%). Distant recurrence occurred in a total of 889 of 4004 patients (22.2%), with bone the most frequent site of metastases in both groups (n = 298; 33.5%). Details of the sites of regional, local, and distant metastases observed as the first event for the end point of breast cancer recurrence are provided in Supporting Information S2: Table S5.

Side effects recorded at every follow‐up concerned cardiac fibrosis (1.7% vs. 2.7%) and cardiac disease (11.7% vs. 15.2%) in the control and intervention groups, respectively. The rates of cardiac fibrosis and cardiac disease for right breast cancer were 1.2% and 10.0% versus 2.8% and 14.2% in the control and intervention groups, respectively; and those for left breast cancer were 2.2% and 13.4% versus 2.5% and 16.1%, respectively. Lung fibrosis of any grade was identified in 3.2% and 6.3% of patients in the control and intervention groups, respectively. At the clinical cutoff date (June 2, 2025), in total, 7528 late‐effect forms collected at 5‐year intervals were available with more detailed data on heart, lung, esophageal, or other late RT side effects. At 20 years, the completion rate was 810 of 1444 patients (56.1%) at risk. To take into account the appropriate number of patients at risk over time, these late effects were analyzed using cumulative incidence curves. The 20‐year incidence of heart RT side effects were 5.2% and 6.4% in the control and intervention groups, respectively; and, when considering severe side effects (score, 3–4), the rates were 1.7% and 1.9%, respectively. Regarding lung RT side effects, the rates were 3.0% and 4.7% in the control and intervention groups, respectively; and, when considering severe lung side effects (score, 3–4), the rates were 0.0% and 0.3%, respectively. The incidence of esophageal side effects was 1.1% and 1.2%, and scores ≥3 in 0% and 0.2%, respectively, in the control and intervention groups, respectively (see Supporting Information S2: Figure S3). The cumulative incidence rate of second breast cancers, either ipsilateral or contralateral, in the control and intervention groups, was similar at 9.9% (95% CI, 8.4%–11.4%) versus 10.4% (95% CI, 8.9%–12.0%), respectively (HR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.84–1.27; p = .781); and the cumulative incidence rate of any second cancer was 22.8% (95% CI, 20.7%–24.9%) and 23.0% (95% CI, 20.9%–25.2%), respectively (HR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.88–1.15; p = .9935; see Supporting Information S2: Figure S4). The cumulative incidence rate of second breast cancers, either ipsilateral or contralateral, including ductal carcinoma in situ, was 10.4% (95% CI, 8.9%–12.0%) and 9.9% (95% CI, 8.4%–11.4%) in the control and intervention groups, respectively (HR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.84–1.27; p = .7808; see Supporting Information S2: Figure S5). An explanatory analysis intended to identify subgroups that might benefit more from IM‐MS‐RT, according to clinical risk factors that were available at the time of the trial (T classification, N status, ER and progesterone receptor status, and tumor location within the breast), did illustrate the interplay of locoregional treatment delivery with risk factors, with the least benefit observed in the low‐risk and high‐risk subgroups (see Supporting Information S2: Figure S6).

DISCUSSION

The objective of EORTC trial 22922/10925 was to assess outcomes after IM‐MS‐RT in patients with stage I–III breast cancer at three time points—10, 15, and 20 years—hypothesizing that the effect of IM‐MS‐RT on survival outcomes would not appear before 10 years from treatment and could be delayed until 20 years, whereas the evaluation of treatment‐related morbidity and mortality, mainly from lung, cardiac, and secondary malignancies, mandates very long‐term follow‐up. 27 At 20 years, no differences were observed for OS, DFS, or DMFS rates. Although the BCM rate was significantly improved in the IM‐MS‐RT group, the rate of death from causes other than breast cancer or from unknown causes was increased starting after 15 years of follow‐up. Side effects were more frequent after IM‐MS‐RT, but mostly were low‐grade.

EORTC trial 22922/10925 is the largest prospective, randomized trial evaluating IM‐MS‐RT and—up to now—the only RT trial providing 20 years of follow‐up in patients who received IM‐MS‐RT. 28 The outcomes at the predefined follow‐up times (10 and 15 years) were published in two pivotal publications. 16 , 24 The 10‐year results demonstrated a marginally improved, but not statistically significant, benefit in OS and a significant improvement in DFS, DMFS, and BCM. 16 The second analysis, at a median follow‐up of 15.7 years, 24 no longer indicated any significant differences in OS, DFS, or DMFS, whereas BCM and any breast cancer recurrence remained statistically better after IM‐MS‐RT. The currently presented analysis at 22.2 years, similar to the analysis at 15.7 years, did not demonstrate a benefit of IM‐MS‐RT for OS, DFS, or DMFS. Nonetheless, BCM and any breast cancer recurrence remained significantly improved.

These differences in survival outcomes (OS, DFS, and DMFS vs. BCM) are a result of using the DATECAN guidelines (see Supporting Information S2: Table S1) which include all causes of death for OS, DFS, and DMFS end points and noninvasive breast cancer as well as contralateral breast cancer for DFS. This dilutes the effect of IM‐MS RT on pure breast cancer outcomes. Although invasive breast cancer–free survival may be considered as the preferred oncological outcome for evaluating regional therapy for breast cancer, 29 the aim of EORTC trial 22922/10925 was also to capture morbidity and mortality from IM‐MS‐RT. Therefore, OS, DFS, and DMFS are end points that capture all other causes of mortality, whereas BCM as secondary end point allows the trial to capture the direct gain of IM‐MS‐RT. 28

The relative risk reduction of 31% for OS that was noted at the 15‐year timepoint with IM‐MS‐RT among patients who had <10 nodes examined was reduced to 17% and was no longer significant at 20 years, but it did demonstrate a borderline‐significant interaction (p = .06), suggesting that locoregional therapy and, in this specific case, IM‐MS‐RT, may be more favorable in the subgroup of patients who undergo less extensive axillary surgical intervention. We previously reported an unplanned subset analysis demonstrating that patients within EORTC trial 22922/10925 who underwent more extensive axillary surgeries (complete ALND) compared with partial ALND were more likely to be premenopausal and had a more advanced T classification and/or N status; and patients who received RT to the dissected part of the axilla (level 1–2) had a more advanced T classification and/or N status. 17 Therefore, the observation of the greater benefit of IM‐MS‐RT when a lower number of axillary lymph nodes was examined is thought‐provoking. This challenges the absolute concept of Fisher and colleagues' theory that breast cancer is a systemic disease from diagnosis, and it emphasizes the importance of optimized, comprehensive locoregional therapy in conjunction with systemic therapies, which fits Hellman's spectrum hypothesis. Nonetheless, the current validity of these findings is unclear because the treatment approach for breast cancer has changed significantly. These patients are now diagnosed with better imaging, and patients with high nodal burden and larger node‐negative tumors with an aggressive molecular profile are often referred for primary systemic therapy, whereas the extent of axillary surgery is also tailored according to the treatment response. Still, this point is highly relevant for contemporary breast cancer management because, for a few decades, the notion for de‐escalation of axillary therapy has prevailed, although most leading prospective, randomized trials only superficially evaluated the interaction between the extent of axillary surgery and of regional node RT, providing scarce data about the intentional and/or incidental dose to the axillary nodal levels. 30 , 31 , 32 The lack of RT dosimetric information to assist in interpreting the outcomes of numerous trials (surgical, RT, and systemic therapy) was raised as a topic of concern and led the publication of a set of minimal requirements for reporting such data. 33 This contributed much to the current controversy among experts about the indications and volumes for locoregional RT in the upfront surgery setting and is even more complicated in the postprimary systemic therapy setting. 34 , 35

The benefit from comprehensive locoregional therapy on breast cancer outcomes is supported a recent analysis of the Early Breast Cancer Trialists' Collaborative Group 36 and the very contemporary publication of the Danish Breast Cancer Group DBCG IMN‐2 (internal mammary node radiation‐2) trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT06549920). 37 Our observation that not only side effects but also recurrences, including regional and distant, can occur up to more than 2 decades after initial diagnosis merits careful treatment planning and also long follow‐up of future trials. At the same time, it calls for very careful adoption into clinical practice after reporting of treatments with a limited follow‐up. This is underscored by the DBCG IMN‐2 trial, 37 which includes more recent diagnostic tests and more advanced systemic (such as anti‐HER2, endocrine therapy for premenopausal women) and locoregional therapies, nonetheless confirming the significant gain from IM chain RT with an absolute OS benefit at 15 years of 4.2% (adjusted HR, 0.85; 95% CI, 0.76–0.94; p = .0016), a significant benefit in BCM, and reduced risks for distant metastasis.

Disturbingly, our results confirm loss of the initial OS survival benefit attained from IM‐MS‐RT over time, as first suggested at the 15‐years analysis. BCM indicates an early but slow separation of curves, increasing from around 10 years and stable after 15 years, with a nearly 20% relative reduction in breast cancer‐related mortality. However, the excess in nonbreast cancer–related mortality increased from 0.1% at 10 years, to 0.8% at 15 years, and to 4.5% at 20 years, resulting in an OS indicating that, after the initial benefit from IM‐MS‐RT, a there is a crossing over of the curves between 15 and 20 years, with slightly worse OS at the final analysis because of a late increase in nonbreast cancer–related mortality. These results may suggest that patients in the control arm tended to die earlier from breast cancer, whereas patients in the IM‐MS‐RT arm tended to die later from other causes, which may be associated with the limitations of the RT techniques that were available 25 years ago. This dynamic evolution over time of benefits in BCM and losses related to other causes of death illustrate the importance of adhering strictly to predefined guidelines for recording and reporting of outcomes, of discussing the time pattern with patients, and, most important, to optimize factors related to benefits while minimizing factors associated with losses. Because data collection in the era that the trial was launched precludes a deeper analysis of the side effects and the causes of death and their possible association with IM‐MS‐RT, a review of any free text on the case report forms that may provide further context to this is being considered in a future exploratory, qualitative analysis. The long‐term safety analyses conducted in the per‐protocol population demonstrated an overall higher incidence of lung and cardiac side effects after IM‐MS‐RT. However, as reported previously, there was no difference between left‐sided and right‐sided treatments, and the absolute rates and differences were very low. 25 Especially because cardiovascular mortality and morbidity were of great concern when planning the trial, patients with documented pre‐existing serious cardiac disease were excluded from the trial, as were patients older than 75 years. Also, in view of the effect on cosmesis of matchline fibrosis between abutting fields in the parasternal region, the trial was subjected to a full set of preplanned quality‐assurance procedures. Nevertheless, a major limitation of our trial is the lack of precise information, according to contemporary standards, about the dose distribution to organs of interest, mainly heart and lungs, and to the target volumes, especially the IM‐MS region, which is expected to have both an influence on the intended effect and the side effects, and possibly on cardiovascular mortality. In addition, the trial case report form did not allow space to sufficiently record details about cardiac morbidity, thus limiting the true ability to assess cardiac morbidity that possibly may be related to mortality. To better understand these outcomes, we initiated two unplanned subset analyses: one analyzing the outcomes according to the three RT techniques allowed in the trial and the other reconstructing the three RT techniques according to the trial's protocol using modern planning systems to more accurately estimate the dose distribution, including the dose to organs of interest (e.g., heart, lung). 38 The latter analysis revealed significant differences in the dose distributions between RT techniques, both for target volumes (IM‐MS) and for organs of interest. IM‐MS‐RT (for all techniques used) significantly increased mean heart doses compared with the mean heart dose of 2.5 Gy for breast‐only RT. In the standard and modified standard IM‐MS‐RT techniques, the mean heart dose was 18.3 and 16.6 Gy, respectively. This was much higher compared with the individualized IM‐MS‐RT technique (9.5‐Gy mean heart dose) introduced by some of the trial sites as an advanced RT technique. This individualized technique was considered an important step toward current RT planning that is adjusted to the patient's body habitus based on more advanced imaging (such as computed tomography simulation). 18 In the other subset analysis of the impact of techniques based on the trial data outcomes from 15 years of follow‐up, 18 we demonstrated that the use of more individualized RT techniques was associated with higher rates of oncological improvements without increased risks for late side effects. However, this benefit was lost at the current 20‐year update of this subset analysis, as shown in Supporting Information S2: Table S6. Our assumption is that the 15‐year benefit of the individualized technique was lost because of the still high mean heart dose, which was approximately 4‐fold to 8‐fold of the mean heart dose in IM‐MS RT using current RT planning and techniques, including deep inspiration breath hold. Recently, mean heart doses with IM‐MS‐RT as low as 1.2 Gy for right‐sided breast cancer and 2.3 Gy for left‐sided breast cancer have been reported. 39

A linear relation between the mean heart dose and the rate of major coronary events was previously suggested by Darby et al. 15 without a safe dose threshold, suggesting that the currently used lower heart doses will probably lead to greater long‐term benefits. This claim is supported by observations from the Early Breast Cancer Trialists' Collaborative Group's meta‐analysis 36 on the differences in 15‐year outcomes, demonstrating that, only in trials initiated after 1989 (compared with older trials), regional nodal RT significantly reduced recurrence rates and BCM, with no significant effect on nonbreast cancer–related mortality, resulting in a significantly reduced all‐cause mortality likely associated with better RT planning and quality assurance. 36

Because our trial focused on the primary question regarding the contribution of IM‐MS‐RT, it pragmatically allowed for physicians' preferences in aspects of treatment that were outside of the scope of the trial's question. Correspondingly, tamoxifen (the endocrine therapy available that time) was applied for postmenopausal women with ER‐positive tumors, whereas chemotherapy was reserved for more advanced disease, ER‐negative tumors, and premenopausal women. 17 Therefore, these treatments were influenced by breast cancer risk factors. Thanks to the stratification factors used during the randomization procedure and the large sample size, the trial arms (interventional and control) were very well balanced for the different treatment components, 17 minimizing the impact of confounding factors on the estimated treatment effects.

From our data, we could not clearly identify a patient subgroup that benefits most from elective nodal RT based on combinations of patient‐related, tumor‐related, and other treatment‐related factors. In the multivariate analysis, only <10 axillary lymph nodes examined had a possible impact for improved OS with IM‐MS‐RT. This hypothesis requires formal validation before its implementation in clinical practice. In addition, as indicated above, it is unclear how to translate this observation into action in current clinical settings because many patients with positive lymph nodes currently receive primary systemic therapy, including tailored and less extensive surgery and RT, according to tumor response. In patients with limited axillary involvement based on sentinel lymph node biopsy, axillary RT is acceptable for replacing ALND. 30 , 40 , 41

As strengths of our work, we mention the upfront planning of three analyses concerning the primary end points—after 10, 15, and 20 years of follow‐up—including optimized data collection from participating patients, resulting in a median follow up of 22.2 years with a narrow interquartile range of 20.2–24.5 years. Our patient population is very representative of current practice, with a median age at randomization of 54.0 years, and most patients had stage I and II disease. The overall 20‐year survival rate, as high as 61.4% and estimated at approximately 50% at 25 years, is in line with current expectations and also allows for a meaningful evaluation for the long‐term impact on nonbreast cancer–related events. Especially after recent publications, elective regional lymph node RT remains a topic of interest, 32 , 42 and the EORTC phase 3 trial is by far the largest one accruing patients in a reasonable modern era with such a long follow‐up. It is also important to note the extensive quality‐assurance program, which should not be taken for granted and greatly assisted in performing the explanatory analysis about the impact of treatment techniques on outcomes, providing a plausible explanation for relating the results at 22.2 years to a combination of suboptimal coverage of the target volumes and excessive doses to organs of interest.

As limitations of our study, we recognize the progress made in all fields of medicine, from prevention and screening overdiagnosis, to treatment, and after care. Our trial, which ran 25 years ago, used mainly field‐based, two‐dimensional RT techniques, whereas contemporary volume‐based techniques facilitate treatment individualization with improving dose coverage of target volumes while limiting doses to organs of interest. Our evaluation of the impact of treatment techniques on outcomes supports the importance of optimizing all steps of breast cancer RT, from target volume selection and contouring overtreatment planning to delivery, to ensure optimal coverage of the target volumes while minimizing doses to organs of interest. With improvements of systemic therapy, doubts rise about the importance of locoregional treatments, including regional nodal surgery and RT. Because the survival outcomes of our patients are aligned with more recent data, and in view of results from the DBCG IMN‐2 trial, it is likely that the benefits also remain in the presence of more effective systemic therapies and may even become more evident thanks to a decrease in the risk of distant metastases, as suggested previously, with an increasing importance of optimizing locoregional control for patients who have decreasing risks for distant metastases after early detection and improved systemic therapies. 43 This interplay of locoregional treatment delivery with risk factors is further supported by our explanatory analysis to identify subgroups according to clinical risk factors that benefit more from IM‐MS‐RT.

We see the outcomes of our trial as an opportunity to convince the oncological community to extend the follow‐up for clinical trials in breast cancer, and also for other slowly proliferating or good‐prognoses diseases, up to decades after initial treatment. Such an extension will allow us to become informed not only about short‐term benefits but also about possible very‐long‐term drawbacks, not only after RT but also after surgery and systemic treatments that may have an impact on late organ functioning, including the immune system. Unfortunately, because of changing regulations on privacy, attitudes on collecting and submitting very‐long‐term data, and increasing costs related to data management, it is currently extremely cumbersome to present such data for large numbers of patients. However, we need to recognize that this is extremely useful, (1) to confirm the initial benefits on disease outcomes and (2) to record and present the possible occurrence of a very late increase in side effects, which may allow us to define preventive measures and facilitate early detection. In contrast, much of the current research in breast cancer focuses especially on high‐risk patients, often leading to practice‐changing initiatives after as little as 3 years and based on DFS instead of OS. Whether the outcomes will be reproducible in real‐life practice and will translate into favorable long‐time outcomes remains to be demonstrated. Because this drains a lot of resources from research that may affect a larger patient population at much lower costs, we propose a debate about investing smarter in future developments, to shift from short‐term gains for few to long‐term benefits for many, by redistributing decreasing resources among a growing population with an increasing life expectancy.

SUMMARY

EORTC trial 22922/10925 evaluated elective regional RT of the IM‐MS lymph nodes in 4004 patients with stage I–III breast cancer. Previous evaluations at a median follow‐up of 10.9 and 15.7 years demonstrated that elective nodal RT lowered breast cancer recurrences and breast cancer‐related mortality. Whereas the occurrence and severity of side effects was limited, and no increased treatment‐related mortality was seen, this did not translate into an improved overall survival. Here, we present the last of three scheduled primary analyses, at a median follow‐up of 22.2 years, demonstrating that the benefit on breast cancer–related mortality was maintained but that the overall results were jeopardized by increased nonbreast cancer–related mortality, confirming a trend that could be suspected at 15.7 years but not confirmed in view of missing data. We hereby also clearly demonstrate that, even after an extremely long follow‐up of 22.2 years posttreatment for early breast cancer, breast cancer events continue to occur, with distant metastases comprising 60% of breast cancer–related events. This suggests that breast cancer is a very long‐standing threat and that appropriate education, tailored follow‐up, and risk‐adapted treatments should be made available for these patients.
