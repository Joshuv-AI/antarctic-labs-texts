Environmental impact of total hip replacements: a life cycle assessment study
Authors: Anna Roe Rasmussen, Stig Brorson, Katarzyna Dudka, Michael Zwicky Hauschild
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesHealthcare contributes considerably to greenhouse gas emissions, particularly from operating theatres. The global demand for total hip replacements (THR) is rising, highlighting the need to understand its impact on the environment. The study aims to assess the environmental impact of THRs using life cycle assessment (LCA) and to identify key contributors.DesignA process-based LCA focusing on the surgical procedure was conducted in accordance with global standards (ISO 14040:2006 and ISO 14044:2006). Eleven sensitivity scenarios were performed to assess the robustness of the results.SettingA Danish University Hospital.ParticipantsThe empirical data involved the quantity and type of surgical equipment, material composition, energy usage and clinical infrastructure. There were no study participants.InterventionsNo study interventions were performed.Primary and secondary outcome measures18 environmental impact scores, such as global warming, human toxicity and water consumption, were assessed.ResultsThe carbon footprint of a THR was 62.0 kg CO2e, with major contributors being single-use disposable utensils (54 %), implants (22 %), and sterilisation of non-disposable utensils (18 %). Operating theatre energy usage, non-disposable utensils and clinical infrastructure contributed less, at 3 %, 2 % and 1 % each. Although the results of the other 17 environmental impact scores varied, they were predominantly influenced by the same factors as the carbon footprint. The sensitivity analysis showed that the overall carbon footprint varied by no more than 6 % unless the energy system shifted to less renewable energy, potentially increasing the footprint by 47 %.ConclusionTHRs impose a substantial environmental burden, and sustainable solutions should focus on the primary drivers of this impact: disposable utensils, implants and the sterilisation process. In contrast, clinical infrastructure and non-disposable utensils appear to have a relatively minimal environmental impact.

Introduction

Total hip replacement (THR) for painful osteoarthritis is a common and effective procedure,1 with operations projected to increase from 1.8 million in 2015 to 2.8 million by 2050 in OECD countries.2 Despite its frequency, the environmental impact of THRs has, to the best of our knowledge, not yet been quantified in accordance with global life cycle assessment standards.

According to the WHO, climate change is the greatest threat to human health in the 21st century.3 The role of healthcare in the transition to a greener society is often overlooked, despite it being estimated to cause 4.6% of global carbon emissions.4 Most emissions originate from the healthcare supply chain, with operating theatres being substantial contributors.58

Despite the established link between healthcare and the emission of greenhouse gases, more empirical data is needed to estimate the environmental impact of orthopaedic procedures covering a broader range of environmental impacts than climate change.9 10 We consider THRs a good case study, as the procedure is often carried out using comparable implants and surgical technique,11 which may strengthen the external validity of the findings.

Life cycle assessment (LCA) is a globally recognised methodology for quantifying the environmental impact of processes or products across their entire life cycle. It follows a four-phase iterative framework defined by global standards (ISO 14040:2006/14044:2006),12 13 which is explained elsewhere.14 The life cycle perspective and the assessment of several environmental impact scores, such as global warming, air pollution and ecotoxicity, help ensure that the solution to one environmental problem does not worsen the others.15

The environmental impact of surgical procedures has been reported in at least 17 studies,51618 of which five report to follow the global LCA standards.171922 Only four of the 17 studies considered other environmental impacts than climate change171921; the remaining were carbon footprint studies. To effectively reduce the environmental impacts of surgical procedures, such as THRs, we need more knowledge supported by studies complying with global LCA standards. The objective of this study is to estimate the environmental impact of a THR using LCA and to identify substantial environmental contributors to this impact.

Methods

The study was conducted at Zealand University Hospital in Køge, Denmark, from 2022 to 2023, with inventory data, assumptions and clinical practices reflected in the analysis corresponding to this period. At the study site, primary THR, with osteoarthritis as an indication, is routinely performed with metal-based and polyethylene-based implants, using a hybrid technique for fixation of the femoral stem.

The study is process-based and follows the global LCA standards (ISO 14040:2006/14044:2006)12 13 using the ecoinvent 3.7.1 database and SimaPro 9.4.0.2 software. The methodological approach is summarised here, and further details are provided in the Supplementary Material.

For the scope definition, the system boundaries were defined as a cradle-to-grave LCA. The functional unit was the surgical treatment of one patient undergoing a THR because of osteoarthritis at Zealand University Hospital (incision to wound closure). Preoperative and postoperative treatment and anaesthesia were outside the scope of the LCA. The study was attributional, and the product system is described in figure 1.

The inventory data were categorised into six main process categories to identify environmental hotspots: disposables, non-disposables, implants, sterilisation, energy use and hospital infrastructure, figure 1. The weight, material composition, volume, area, energy consumption and ecoinvent inventory datasets used for the six process categories, including transportation and packaging, are listed in online supplemental tables 1.1 to 6.3. The main assumptions are summarised below, and further details are provided in the online supplemental material.

The study examined the surgical utensils used for THR, which are included in the local operating theatre checklist of items that must be prepared before surgery begins. Before data collection began, a convenience sample of ten surgical procedures was observed to ensure that no additional utensils were used. Since the number and type of utensils may differ depending on the surgical site and possible surgical complications, a sensitivity analysis was also performed. This analysis considered variations of 10% more or fewer utensils, online supplemental table 7.1 to 7.2.

The process categories for disposables and non-disposables contain either single-use or multiple-use surgical utensils. The material composition was identified using manufacturer information when available from the packaging; otherwise, assumptions were made based on similar materials/products in ecoinvent, other LCA studies or by judgement from a senior engineer, online supplemental table 1.1 to 1.5. Non-disposable surgical utensils were assumed to have a lifespan of 300 uses,23 which we considered a conservative estimate. In the sensitivity analysis, it was examined how many times the disposables would need to be used for their overall environmental impact to drop below 1 %, online supplemental table 7.4. All the surgical waste is treated as residual waste at Zealand University Hospital, which was tested in the sensitivity analysis by modifying the waste scenario to landfill, which could apply in other countries, online supplemental table 7.3. The district heating and electricity obtained through waste incineration were credited for online supplemental table 1.4.

The processes of sterilising non-disposables, which include disinfection and autoclave, are combined into one process category, figure 1. Detailed information about the materials and manufacturing stage of the equipment used for wash and disinfection was provided by the manufacturer, online supplemental table 2.1 to 2.5. The electricity used for one cycle of disinfection (10.2 kWh) and autoclaving (23.0 kWh) was also provided by the manufacturer. Electricity generation was modelled as the average Danish grid mix.

The implant process category includes the implant components. Material composition and manufacturing location information was identified from their packaging. Assumptions about the manufacturing process (e.g. energy usage) were based on an LCA study of a knee implant consisting of the same implant material,24 online supplemental table 3.1. The sensitivity analysis tested the implant size, which may vary by patient, as shown in online supplemental table 7.5 and 7.6.

The energy use process category includes the energy consumption (electricity and district heating) of the operating theatre. Due to a lack of specific operating theatre data, the hospital’s overall electricity consumption (118.6 kWh/m2/year) and district heating (122.4 kWh/m2/year) from 2021 were used. It was assumed that the energy use in the operating theatre was six times higher than the hospital’s average based on the findings of another study with access to this specific data in three countries (online supplemental table 4.1).25 The average surgery time (incision to wound closure) was assessed to be 75 min by an orthopaedic consultant. To assess the robustness of this assumption, the average surgery time was analysed in the sensitivity analysis, ranging from 45 to 120 min, online supplemental table 7.7 to 7.8.

The last process category, hospital infrastructure, includes the hospital building and the permanently installed surgical equipment in the operating theatre. Ecoinvent datasets for multi-storey buildings, lamps, computers, tables etc., which represent generic averages for such facilities, were used to model hospital infrastructure. Online supplemental table 6.1 to 6.3. The carbon footprint of the ecoinvent dataset for a multi-storey building is half the maximum value reported elsewhere for the construction of a Danish hospital (7.24 kg CO2e/m2/year).26 The modelled environmental impact of a multistorey building was therefore doubled. The surgical schedule was reviewed over 4 weeks to determine how the environmental footprint of these facilities can be distributed per procedure performed in the specific operating theatre, online supplemental table 6.2.

For the life cycle impact assessment, the ReCiPe (H) method was used, which contains 18 environmental impact scores referred to as midpoint scores.27 Specific data were used for foreground processes, while average data from ecoinvent were applied for the background (figure 1). The midpoint score for global warming, referred to here as the carbon footprint, is described in more detail to compare with the results of previous surgical carbon footprint studies.

The last phase of the LCA method, the interpretation, was performed iteratively throughout the LCA, conducting 11 sensitivity analyses to reveal where changes in the main assumptions could affect the overall environmental impact or the identification of hotspots, including examining how the results varied when using the average energy grid mix for Europe, the USA and China. We operated with a cut-off limit of 1% as a threshold below which the contributions of a process or products to the overall environmental impact can be considered negligible. The cut-off limit was established to determine when it was unnecessary to further improve the inventory data of a product or process.

Ethics

The data collected focused on the number and type of surgical utensils, the weight and material composition, energy consumption and surgical infrastructure. No patients or patient data were involved in the study.

Patient and public involvement

There was no patient and public involvement in the study.

Results

The carbon footprint was 62.0 kg CO2e per THR. In 2025, more than 2.1 million THRs are expected to be performed in the OECD countries,2 which can result in a carbon footprint of ~130,000,000 kg CO2e. This is equivalent to the average yearly carbon footprint of ~15 000 citizens living in the OECD countries.28 The largest contributors to the carbon footprint were disposables (33.8 kg CO2e, 54 %), implants (13.4 kg CO2e, 22 %) and the sterilisation process (10.9 kg CO2e, 18 %), figures2 3.

Among the disposables, surgical drapes (12 %), blue wrap (6 %), the pulsed lavage system (5 %), surgical gowns (4 %) and the gauze sponges (3 %) were the main contributors. The remaining disposables each contributed <3% to the total carbon footprint. Most disposables were plastic-based, with heavier products, such as synthetic textiles (drapes, blue wrap and gowns), leading to a higher carbon footprint; see online supplemental table 1.1. Transportation and packaging each accounted for 6% of the total carbon footprint. The end-of-life of the disposables leads to a 2% net reduction in carbon footprint because residual waste is incinerated and turned into district heating and electricity in Denmark, causing crediting with the avoided environmental impact from the replaced energy sources.

The sterilisation process contributed to 7% (wash and disinfection) and 11% (autoclave) of the overall carbon footprint, predominantly due to electricity usage. The materials/manufacturing stage, packaging, transportation and end-of-life of the equipment used for autoclaving contributed 3% to the overall carbon footprint, while the wash and disinfection equipment accounted for <1%.

Among the implants, the acetabular cup contributed 8%, the femoral head 6% and the bone cement used for implant fixation 4% to the overall carbon footprint, while the other implant components contributed <3% each. The materials for the acetabular cup (titanium) and femoral head (cobalt-chrome) caused a higher carbon footprint than the femoral stem (stainless steel). The process categories contributing the least to the carbon footprint were energy use in the operating theatre (3 %), non-disposables CO2e (2 %) and hospital infrastructure (1 %).

The results for the other 17 environmental impacts (midpoint scores) showed variable distributions across the six process categories, figures2 3. Similar to the carbon footprint, the total impact for the 17 midpoint scores was primarily influenced by the same process categories (disposables, implants and sterilisation). Disposables were responsible for >50% of the total impact in 9 of the 17 midpoint scores, and the remaining three process categories (hospital infrastructure, energy use and non-disposables) generally contributed less than 10% of the total impact (figures2 3). The implants were responsible for >50% of the environmental impact of mineral resource scarcity (76 %), mainly caused by the production of cobalt used in the femoral head. The sterilisation process was responsible for >50% of the environmental impact for freshwater eutrophication (68 %), predominately caused by the use of reverse osmosis water in the wash and disinfection equipment.

Sensitivity analysis

For the sensitivity analysis, eleven scenarios were examined to test the robustness of the environmental impact results, online supplemental tables 7.1 to 8.1. Different material compositions for disposables, such as assuming polypropylene instead of polyethylene, did not change the overall carbon footprint. When the number of disposables was decreased or increased by 10 %, which may be the case for other hospitals, the overall carbon footprint decreased or increased by 6%. Changing the waste treatment of disposables from incineration to landfill, which prevails in some countries, increased the overall carbon footprint by 3% because energy obtained from incineration is no longer credited. Increasing the number of uses of the non-disposables from 300 to 500 changed their carbon footprint from 3% to <1% (cut-off limit). Changing the weight of the implants by 10%, as the size of the implants may vary depending on the patient, decreased and increased the overall carbon footprint by 2%. The assumed duration (75 min) of the surgical procedure was varied, ranging from 45 to 120 min, which decreased and increased the overall carbon footprint by 1% and 2%.

Using different ecoinvent datasets for electricity grid mixes from Europe (average), the USA and China, on the other hand, increased the carbon footprint by 10 %, 17% and 47 %, online supplemental tables 7.1 to 8.1. Given that the hospital building, including permanently installed surgical equipment, only contributed by 1% (the cut-off limit), sensitivity testing was not considered relevant.

Discussion

The growing number of THRs performed globally has a substantial environmental impact. Disposables, implants and the sterilisation process are the main contributors. This LCA study, covering the full life cycle of the surgical procedure itself, is, to our knowledge, the first to include hospital infrastructure and the material and manufacturing stage of the equipment used for washing and disinfecting non-disposables. The overall environmental impact of these facilities is less than our established cut-off limit of 1%, suggesting that future studies of surgical procedures may not need to focus on modelling these complex facilities in detail. The ecoinvent datasets and assumptions used are furthermore reported in the Supplementary Material for transparency, allowing others to build on this LCA model, which has not been the case in the seventeen surgical studies reported previously.51618

The carbon footprint of these 17 surgical studies ranged from 6 kg CO2e for cataract surgery in India to 814 CO2e for a robotic hysterectomy in the US.51618 This variation is evidently influenced by the type of surgery and by the CO2 intensity of the national energy system. A UK study found the carbon footprint of a THR to be higher (104.9 kg CO2e) than in our study (62.0 kg CO2e).18 The UK study does not report to comply with the global LCA standards and only assesses the carbon footprint, leaving out other environmental impacts. This limits the possibilities of comparing their goal, scope and findings with ours. While the UK study reports to cover preoperative and postoperative treatment and anaesthesia, it excludes implants and anaesthetic drugs due to data limitations. The scope of the UK study is broader in some respects, which results in a higher number of disposable items and thereby a larger carbon footprint compared with our study. Our results were sensitive to changes in the Danish energy grid mix, which may also explain the differences between the studies. No sensitivity analysis was conducted in the UK study.

Our finding that disposables had the greatest impact on the carbon footprint of THRs aligns with systematic reviews.5 16 Synthetic textiles, particularly drapes, were major contributors among disposables. Drapes accounted for the largest proportion of disposables by weight, highlighting their importance for surgical hygiene for joint replacement procedures. A UK study found drapes among the top three contributors in four out of five common surgeries, suggesting that this finding may extend to other surgeries.29 Our sensitivity analysis indicated that the environmental impact from the disposables, primarily of plastics, showed little sensitivity to the specific type of plastic used. The weight of disposable products may, therefore, serve as a simple indicator of their carbon footprint when product-specific data is unavailable.

The energy-intensive materials used to produce the implants, such as titanium and cobalt-chrome, had a greater influence on the carbon footprint than the weight of the individual implants. In Sweden, recycling metals from cremation is required,30 while Danish law allows for the recycling of large pieces that cannot fit into the urn. Recycling these implants after cremation would reduce their overall environmental impact.

The carbon footprint of the sterilisation process is dominated by energy usage. The ecoinvent dataset for Denmark’s electricity is based on 2017 statistics. The Danish Energy Agency projects a near-total transition to renewable energy by 2035 based on current energy policies. This is expected to result in a 99% reduction in national carbon emissions from the energy sector compared with 1990.31 Additionally, crediting of waste incineration of disposables with the avoided carbon footprint from heat and electricity production will amount to close to zero in Denmark in the future. This means that the environmental benefits of using non-disposables over disposables must be expected to increase in the future for countries with similar policies for renewable energy transition.

There are some limitations to this study. First, the hospital’s technical department could not provide specific data for the energy use of individual operating theatres. We assumed the energy consumption was six times higher than the hospital average based on a study with access to this specific data in three countries.6 It would be beneficial if more studies could support the use of this assumption.

Second, in our study, anaesthesia was not part of the defined goal and scope, potentially affecting the result. We focused on the surgical procedure rather than the type of anaesthesia, which may vary depending on the patient and local anaesthesiologic guidelines. An Australian study found that the carbon footprint of general, spinal and combined anaesthesia for total knee replacements ranged between 9.9 kg CO2e (European energy data) and 18.5 kg CO2e (Chinese energy data).32 This indicates that anaesthesia might be a substantial contributor had it been included. Preoperative and postoperative treatment, which could have included patient/staff transportation, was also not deemed to be part of the LCA scope definition because the goal was to assess the environmental impact specific to the surgical procedure itself. The overall environmental impact will be greater if preoperative and postoperative treatment is included. Consequently, the results should be interpreted as procedure-specific, and the full environmental impact would be greater if the entire surgical care pathway had been included. Future studies should work towards incorporating anaesthesia and perioperative care as standardised inventories and as greater methodological consensus regarding their system boundaries becomes available.33

To assess the applicability of our findings in other hospitals conducting THRs, it is important for others to review the sensitivity analysis results (online supplemental tables 7.1 to 8.1). This analysis showed that the overall carbon footprint could increase by up to 47% when the electricity grid mix shifts to countries with less renewable energy than the Danish average, such as China. The process categories, sterilisation, as well as energy use in the operating theatre, will have a more dominating contribution to the overall carbon footprint in countries with less renewable energy. Notably, none of the other tested scenarios changed the overall carbon footprint by more than 6 %, suggesting that the main contributors to the overall environmental impact (disposables, implants and the sterilisation process) remain consistent across sites.

Patients will continue to rely on THRs as an effective treatment for painful osteoarthritis.1 As the number of procedures increases globally, it is important to explore ways to enhance their environmental impact. Future research should identify additional strategies to address the key contributors to the environmental impact while maintaining treatment quality and safety, as this was beyond the scope of our study.

Conclusion

The environmental impact of THRs is substantial, with disposables, implants and the sterilisation process being the main contributors. Clinical infrastructure and non-disposable utensils seem to have a relatively minimal contribution to the overall environmental impact. As the global demand for this procedure is rising, it is important to explore environmental improvements that maintain patient safety and quality. Sustainable solutions should focus on disposable utensils, while the use of renewable energy could reduce the impact of the sterilisation of non-disposables. Renewable energy in the production of surgical implants and recycling after cremation may also reduce the environmental impact.
