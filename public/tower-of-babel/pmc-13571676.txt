Identifying Key Intervention Attributes Driving Acceptability of Spouse/Partner-Centered HIV Interventions in Vietnam Using Conjoint Analysis
Authors: Thi Huong Dang, Thi Thanh Thuy Dinh, Lynn T Nguyen, Truong Giang Nguyen, Le Minh Giang, Sung-Jae Lee, Chunqing Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeHIV care increasingly addresses the psychosocial challenges faced by people living with HIV. Spouse/partner-centered interventions have shown benefits for women living with HIV (WLWH) globally. However, evidence from Vietnam - a strongly family-oriented context - remains limited. Conjoint analysis (CA) is a method for assessing intervention acceptability by examining how specific intervention attributes influence respondents’ willingness to engage. This study uses CA to identify key attributes driving acceptability of spouse/partner-centered interventions among Vietnamese WLWH and their partners, and to explore associations between participant characteristics and individual-level attribute impact scores.MethodsFifty WLWH and thirty male partners completed an anonymous REDCap survey rating six hypothetical spouse/partner-centered intervention scenarios varying in key attributes (including format, duration, spouse/partner involvement, session type, facilitator, and targeted outcomes). Willingness to participate in each intervention was rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Mixed-effects ordered logistic regression was applied to assess the likelihood of participation. Exploratory Spearman correlation analyses explored associations between participant characteristics and individual-level attribute impact scores.ResultsIn the full-sample analysis, session type had the strongest impact on the likelihood of participation (impact score = −1.65; p = 0.029), followed by spouse/partner involvement (impact score = 0.99; p = 0.022), intervention duration (impact score = 0.94; p = 0.003), and facilitator type (impact score = 0.89; p = 0.039). Exploratory analyses found no significant associations between participant characteristics and attribute impact scores (all p > 0.05).ConclusionCA identified key intervention attributes influencing willingness to participate in spouse/partner-centered interventions. Session type, spouse/partner involvement, intervention duration, and facilitator type should be considered when developing culturally appropriate interventions for WLWH in Vietnam and similar settings. The absence of significant associations in the exploratory analyses highlights the value of larger studies with more choice scenarios to better identify factors associated with individual variation in intervention preferences.Keywords: conjoint analysis, spouse/partner-centered intervention, HIV, women, Vietnam, implementation science

Introduction

The advent of antiretroviral therapy (ART) has transformed HIV into a manageable chronic condition.1 In the current era, HIV care needs to extend beyond clinical treatment to include social and psychosocial support. Women living with HIV (WLWH), in particular, face intersecting social, structural, and health-related challenges, including stigma, gender inequality, caregiving responsibilities, and elevated mental stressors, underscoring the need for psychosocial support to help address these barriers. In Vietnam, family structures and partner relationships remain central to healthcare decision-making, particularly in the treatment of chronic conditions.2,3 Family members, especially husbands or partners, directly influence WLWH’s well-being as they can provide critical support or, conversely, contribute to stigma, health disparities, and violence.4,5

Previous literature in international settings reported that family- and partner-based interventions can improve ART adherence and healthcare access among WLWH,6,7 enhance mental health outcomes,8,9 reduce HIV-related stigma, promote active coping strategies,10 and decrease sexual intimate partner violence while strengthening family relationships.11,12 Given the central role of family in WLWH’s daily lives and care experiences, interventions that engage both WLWH and their husbands/partners may be particularly relevant in Vietnam. However, evidence on family-involved interventions in this context remains limited. Additionally, implementing such interventions within a gendered cultural context may present significant challenges, as expectations around gender roles, caregiving responsibilities, communication patterns, and authority within the household may shape participant acceptability of interventions.13,14

To achieve effective implementation of spouse/partner-centered interventions, especially in family-oriented settings in Vietnam, it is crucial to carefully consider participant acceptability, as it directly influences intervention uptake, adherence, and sustainability.15 However, assessments of acceptability for HIV behavioral interventions were often conducted post-implementation rather than informing intervention design beforehand.16 For spouse and partner-involved interventions, evaluating pre-implementation acceptability is particularly important as participants may differ in power dynamics and HIV status, leading to diverse preferences for specific intervention features. To address these challenges, conjoint analysis (CA) emerges as a promising approach, allowing researchers to examine how individual attributes of an intervention influence participants’ willingness to engage, rather than assessing acceptability only as a post hoc outcome. Initially utilized in market research,17 this method has been widely used in health economics and service design.18 By presenting respondents with hypothetical intervention profiles that vary across predefined attributes, CA enables the estimation of attribute-level utilities and identification of configurations that maximize acceptability. In the context of family-centered HIV interventions, CA offers a valuable tool to uncover potentially divergent preferences between WLWH and their husbands/partners, therefore informing the development of interventions that are both contextually relevant and implementation-ready. This study aims to examine the acceptability of various attributes of spouse/partner-centered interventions among Vietnamese WLWH and their partners. We also explored associations between participant characteristics and individual-level attribute impact scores. Findings are expected to make a significant contribution to the development of interventions that are highly valued and widely accepted by stakeholders.

Methods

Study Design

This study used a cross-sectional survey with a CA approach to examine preferences among WLWH and their partners/husbands regarding key attributes of six spouse/partner-oriented intervention scenarios. Among stated-preference methods, discrete choice experiments (DCEs), and best-worst scaling (BWS) can also be used to quantify preferences for healthcare interventions and services. DCEs typically require respondents to choose between two or more competing alternatives, whereas BWS asks respondents to identify the most and least preferred options within a presented set. In this study, we selected a rating-based CA because our primary objective was to assess participants’ willingness to participate in individual hypothetical intervention profiles and to estimate the contribution of specific intervention attributes to acceptability, rather than to predict choices between competing alternatives. Rating one intervention profile at a time also provided a relatively straightforward task for participants and was considered appropriate for minimizing respondent burden in this exploratory study.18–23

Participants

A total of 80 participants were enrolled between May and July 2025, including 50 WLWH and 30 partners/husbands. WLWH were recruited through HIV outpatient clinics (OPCs) in Hanoi and through existing chat groups on Zalo (a widely used social media platform in Vietnam) established during our previous study, which was a group-based intervention that exclusively enrolled WLWH and did not involve their spouses or partners.24 At the OPCs, potentially eligible WLWH were referred by healthcare providers to research staff, while those in Zalo groups were approached directly by research staff. Partners/husbands were recruited either through OPCs or word-of-mouth invitations from their spouses/partners. Trained research staff provided interested participants with verbal and written information about the study and assessed their eligibility. Eligible WLWH were 18 years or older and were married to or living with a male partner (with no restriction on the partner’s HIV status). Eligible partners/husbands were 18 years or older, married to or living with a WLWH, and aware of their wife’s or partner’s HIV status. Based on participation patterns, the sample comprised 26 couples, 24 WLWH who participated without their partner/husband, and 4 partners/husbands who participated without their WLWH partner.

Data Collection

The conjoint survey was pilot tested with two WLWH and two male partners before data collection. After providing written informed consent, participants completed a REDCap survey administered by trained research staff. WLWH and their spouses/partners completed the survey separately in private settings. Each survey interview lasted 30 to 60 minutes, depending on the participant’s and/or their partner’s HIV status. Participants received 400,000 Vietnam dong (approximately 15 USD) as compensation after completing the survey. All interviews were conducted in person, with participants’ responses entered directly into REDCap online. The survey did not collect any identifying information. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol and materials were reviewed and approved by Institutional Review Boards at University of California, Los Angeles (IRB-24-5707) and Hanoi Medical University (approval No. 1729/GCN-HMUIRB issued on December 19, 2024).

Intervention Scenarios

Based on findings from a scoping review of effective spouse/partner-based interventions for WLWH25 and from Community Advisory Board meetings evaluating evidence-based spouse/partner-centered interventions for WLWH (Dang et al, in submission), we identified six attributes that might influence decisions to participate in the intervention models. These attributes included: (1) format, (2) duration, (3) spouse/partner involvement, (4) session type, (5) facilitator, and (6) targeted outcomes, including social empowerment (defined broadly as improvements in confidence, coping, social support, and ability to address social challenges) and health outcomes (defined as improvement in physical and mental well-being and engagement in HIV care). Two levels were assigned to four attributes (format, duration, spouse/partner involvement, and facilitator), while three levels were assigned to the remaining two attributes (session type and targeted outcomes). Attribute levels were adapted to better reflect the preferences and needs of WLWH in Vietnam. Therefore, the intervention scenarios were not intended to replicate previously identified spouse/partner-based interventions in the scoping review. Theoretically, the six attributes and their associated levels might generate 24 x 32 = 144 possible scenarios. To minimize respondent burden, we used a fractional factorial orthogonal design to reduce the number of scenarios to six.26 Table 1 presents the specific combinations of the attributes and levels among the six generated scenarios. Scenario 1, for example, represented an intervention conducted in-person over one month, in which WLWH would participate jointly with their partner/husband in a large group with other couples; and this intervention would be facilitated by professional providers and focus on improving family relationships.

In the REDCap survey, the order of scenarios was randomized to minimize order bias. Participants were asked to rate each scenario one by one with a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (very unlikely to participate) to 5 (very likely to participate). In the survey platform, scenarios were programmed to be presented in randomized order to minimize order effects and fatigue effects on participants’ responses. After completing the final scenario, participants were given the opportunity to adjust their ratings for any previously evaluated scenarios if they wished.

Other Measures

The survey also collected a range of additional measures that may influence participants’ evaluations of the intervention scenarios. Demographic information included age, education level, employment status, and individual annual income. Family background characteristics included number of children, spouse/partner’s annual income, and average annual household income. HIV-related information for both participants and their spouse/partner covered the number of years living with HIV and the route of HIV transmission. Access to home internet was determined by whether participants had broadband (Wi-Fi) available at home.

Satisfaction with spousal/partner relationships was assessed by the Relationship Assessment Scale (RAS), a 7-item self-report measurement on a 5-point Likert scale.27 Example items include “In general, how satisfied are you with your relationship?” and “To what extent has your relationship met your original expectations?”. Each item was scored from 1 (low satisfaction) to 5 (high satisfaction), generating a total score ranging from 7 to 35. Two items (item 4 – “How often do you wish you hadn’t gotten in this relationship?” and item 7 – “How many problems are there in your relationship?”) were reverse-coded before summation. Higher summation scores indicated greater relationship satisfaction. In our dataset, the RAS showed excellent internal consistency, with a standardized Cronbach’s alpha of 0.9152.

HIV status openness was assessed using a measure adapted from previous research on community HIV disclosure.28 The measure was expanded to capture openness across both family and community contexts. Participants were asked how widely their own HIV status (if applicable) and their spouse/partner’s HIV status (if applicable) was known across eight groups: family members (eg, parent or children), extended family/relatives, friends, co-workers, commune health workers and/or service providers in local clinics/hospitals, community/village leaders, people in the community/village, and others (specified if applicable). Responses were rated on a 3-point scale from 0 (None of them), 1 (Some of them), to 2 (All of them), with higher aggregate scores indicating wider openness. Internal consistency was high for both participants’ openness (standardized Cronbach’s alpha = 0.8223, n = 73) and spouse/partner’s openness (standardized Cronbach’s alpha = 0.8494, n = 63).

Data Analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted for all study variables. Continuous variables were summarized using means with standard deviations (SD) and interquartile ranges (IQR), while binary and categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests and Fisher’s exact tests were used to examine differences in characteristics between WLWH and their partners/husbands. To assess participants’ likelihood of participating in the six intervention scenarios, we applied a mixed-effects ordered logistic regression model with a cumulative logit link for the overall sample and separately for WLWH and partners/husbands. The overall analysis included 480 observations (6 scenarios per respondent x 80 respondents). Random intercepts were included at the respondent level to account for repeated scenario ratings within respondents. In the overall model, a couple-level random intercept was also included to account for clustering within couples, with unpaired respondents treated as singleton clusters. The outcome, likelihood of participation, was treated as an ordinal variable ranging from 1 (very unlikely) to 5 (very likely). All six attributes were included as predictors, with each attribute’s regression coefficient representing its individual impact on the likelihood of participation. Given the observed difference in the direction of the format coefficients between WLWH and partners/husbands, a post hoc interaction analysis was conducted to formally assess whether the effect of intervention format differed between the two groups. In addition, individual-level part-worth utilities were estimated by fitting a separate multiple regression model for each respondent, with the likelihood rating as the dependent variable and five of the six scenario attributes entered as independent variables. One attribute included in the hierarchical model - targeted outcome - was omitted from the individual-level regression models due to the limited number of observations (six scenarios) per respondent, which prevented estimation of all regression coefficients while including a model constant. The resulting attribute coefficients represented each respondent’s impact scores, which were then correlated with participant characteristics using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients. Correlations were calculated separately for each pair of variables using all available data. These correlation analyses were considered exploratory; thus, the p-values were not adjusted for multiple comparisons. Data were analyzed using STATA 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Table 2 presents characteristics of the 50 WLWH and 30 spouses/partners in the study. Participants in both groups were generally middle-aged, with WLWH being younger (42.3 ± 5.6 years) than spouses/partners (45.4 ± 6.5 years; p = 0.003). Most participants in both groups were employed full-time and had one or two children, while spouses/partners reported somewhat higher income levels than WLWH. Among the 30 spouses/partners, 23 (76.7%) were living with HIV, while 33 WLWH (66.0%) reported having a spouse/partner living with HIV. Among participants living with HIV, the mean duration of living with HIV was 16.0 years for WLWH and 18.5 years for spouses/partners. HIV status openness was generally similar between groups, with spouses/partners reporting mean scores of 4.5 ± 3.2 for their own HIV status compared with 3.7 ± 2.7 among WLWH, and identical spouse/partner HIV status openness scores in both groups (3.5 ± 2.6). Home broadband access was common in both groups (90.0% of WLWH and 96.7% of spouses/partners). WLWH reported lower relationship satisfaction than spouses/partners (mean RAS score: 25.0 ± 6.3 vs 28.3 ± 3.3; p = 0.005).

As shown in Table 1, scenarios 2 and 3 were evaluated as the most preferred options, with mean likelihood of participation scores of 3.5 and 3.7, respectively. Both scenarios featured a 3-month intervention duration and facilitation by professional providers. Scenarios 4, 5, and 6 shared the same mean score (3.4), similar SDs (1.1–1.2), and all were designed to be facilitated by peers. Scenario 1 received the lowest likelihood of participation score (3.3).

Table 3 presents the regression coefficients, representing the impact scores of the six attributes on the likelihood of participation across the six intervention scenarios for WLWH, partners/husbands, and all participants combined. When WLWH or partner/husbands were examined separately, only intervention duration had a significant effect on participation likelihood (impact scores = 0.91 and 1.03, respectively; p < 0.05). In the full sample, session type had the strongest impact (impact score = −1.65; p = 0.029), indicating a substantial reduction in the likelihood of participating in intervention scenarios involving individual sessions. Spouse/partner involvement ranked second in its impact (impact score = 0.99; p = 0.022), followed by intervention duration (0.93; p = 0.003), and facilitator type (0.89; p = 0.039). The individual couple level of session type and the targeted outcome attribute were omitted from the model due to perfect collinearity across the six conjoint scenarios. Although the format coefficients were not statistically significant within either subgroup, they showed opposite directions between WLWH (impact score = 0.47; p = 0.217) and partners/husbands (impact score = −0.40; p = 0.418). Based on this observed difference, a separate post hoc interaction analysis was conducted and showed that the effect of intervention format differed significantly between WLWH and partners/husbands (interaction coefficient = −1.33; p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study used rating-based conjoint analysis to examine the acceptability of key attributes of spouse/partner-centered HIV interventions among WLWH and their partners in Vietnam. Overall, intervention duration, session type, spouse/partner involvement, and facilitator type were important determinants of participants’ willingness to participate. Intervention duration was the only attribute significantly associated with participation likelihood in both WLWH and partners when analyzed separately, while session type showed the strongest impact in the full sample. These findings highlight the importance of considering not only intervention content, but also how spouse/partner-centered interventions are structured and delivered when designing acceptable programs for WLWH and their partners.

Our conjoint design followed established good research practices for the application of CA in health care research to ensure methodological rigor and feasibility.18,29 Several key principles guided our decisions, including identifying attributes through literature review and stakeholder consultation, limiting each attribute to three levels to reduce cognitive burden, and conducting pilot testing to assess clarity, relevance, and survey feasibility. Although good research practice recommends approximately 8–12 tasks in healthcare conjoint studies, our study included six tasks, each requiring participants to rate one intervention profile or scenario.29 The decision to limit the instrument to six tasks was made after careful consideration of respondent burden among WLWH and their partners. Given the sensitive context and the cognitive demands of rating hypothetical scenarios, prioritizing participant feasibility was essential to maintaining response quality while still capturing meaningful preference data.

Study findings indicate that intervention duration was the only attribute significantly associated with participants’ likelihood to participate across each group. Although most WLWH and partners were engaged in full-time employment and had at least one child (Table 2), they expressed a stronger preference for a 3-month intervention over a shorter one-month program. This suggests that participants did not simply prioritize minimizing time commitment; rather, they valued a duration perceived as sufficient to generate meaningful and sustained benefits, even when balancing competing work and family responsibilities. Importantly, this preference for a 3-month intervention is also consistent with intervention durations reported in the existing literature. A meta-analysis examining psychosocial interventions for mental health among people living with HIV reported a people living with HIV mean intervention duration of 12.20 weeks, although duration varied considerably across studies.30 In addition, the meta-analysis found that interventions totaling 12–18 hours produced the largest effect sizes compared with both shorter and longer programs. These findings may help inform the design of our future intervention. For example, a 3-month intervention could include approximately 12 weekly sessions, with each session lasting about 1–1.5 hours depending on the delivery format (online or in person), resulting in a total intervention dose of approximately 12–18 hours. This structure could provide a practical starting point for intervention development and adaptation, while session length and frequency should be further refined based on feasibility and acceptability.

In the full-sample analysis, in addition to intervention duration, the likelihood of participating spouse/partner-centered interventions was significantly influenced by session type, spouse/partner involvement, and facilitator type. Session type had the strongest impact, particularly the preference for joining a group with other couples. This finding was consistent with previous research showing that group-based formats are widely utilized in HIV interventions, as they help to reduce feelings of shame and isolation, normalize HIV-related challenges, and build meaningful peer connections.31–33 Moreover, the acceptability of delivering intervention sessions separately to spouses or partners suggests that participants value having a safe and private space to discuss sensitive issues among same-sex peers, rather than engaging in joint sessions. Considering session type together with spouse/partner involvement, a partner-based intervention delivered in sex-specific groups may best align with participants’ preferences. To date, no evidence exists from Vietnam evaluating the effectiveness of this intervention design. However, our research team has previously implemented a hybrid intervention that included group sessions exclusively for WLWH, and the results demonstrated effectiveness in reducing stigma, promoting active coping, and improving care engagement.19 Although this intervention did not include spouses or partners, its findings provide preliminary support for the potential benefits of peer-based group sessions among WLWH in the Vietnamese context. Notably, the interaction analysis showed that the effect of intervention format differed significantly between WLWH and partners/husbands (Table 3). This finding highlights the importance of considering the preferences of both WLWH and their partners when designing a couple-based intervention, as a single delivery format may not equally accommodate both groups. Providing flexibility in delivery, such as applying a hybrid approach, may therefore help address these potentially differing preferences and improve intervention acceptability. However, because of the post hoc nature of this analysis, the finding should be interpreted cautiously and examined in future studies. In the study sample, we found limited evidence that the participant characteristics examined were associated with individual variation in intervention preferences. The exploratory nature of this analysis and the limited number of scenarios used to estimate individual-level impact scores may have limited the ability to detect such associations. Further research with larger samples and more choice scenarios is needed to better understand factors that could contribute to individual differences in intervention preferences.

Similar to other studies, our research had several limitations. First, WLWH and their male partners were recruited in Hanoi by a non-random sampling method. Therefore, the generalizability of the findings to other regions of Vietnam may be limited. Second, although the sample size of 80 participants is acceptable for a rating-based CA with an exploratory purpose,34 assessing impact scores among small subgroups (50 WLWH and 30 partners) may have limited statistical power to detect significant effects within each group. For studies aiming to compare respondent groups and identify statistically significant differences, adequate sample sizes should be carefully considered for each stated-preference research design.34 Third, our CA design included six attributes and six scenarios per respondent. Consequently, one attribute (targeted outcome) had to be excluded from the individual-level regression models because there were insufficient observations to estimate all six attribute coefficients together with an intercept. Even after excluding this attribute, the individual-level models included five predictors plus an intercept based on only six observations, leaving no residual degrees of freedom and potentially resulting in unstable part-worth estimates. These estimates were therefore interpreted with caution. Future studies should include a larger number of scenarios per respondent to allow more reliable estimation of all attributes’ effects.

Conclusions

Conjoint analysis proved to be a useful approach for quantifying the preferences of WLWH and their partners regarding the willingness to participate in spouse/partner-centered interventions. These findings suggest the importance of structuring spouse/partner-based interventions with appropriate duration, opportunities for couple and group engagement, and credible professional facilitation to enhance acceptability and participation. The differing effects of intervention format between WLWH and their partners further underscore the value of flexibility in intervention delivery to accommodate the preferences of both groups. In exploratory analyses, no participant characteristics were significantly associated with attribute impact scores. Future studies with larger samples and a greater number of choice scenarios may help identify factors that contribute to individual variation in intervention preferences.
