Dementia incidence across countries: a sex-specific ecological analysis of cancer incidence
Authors: Wenpeng You, Hui-Chen (Rita) Chang, Brendon Coventry, Ashlyn Sahay, Maciej Henneberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDementia incidence is rising globally with population aging and improved survival from chronic diseases. Cancer incidence may reflect longevity, diagnostic capacity, socioeconomic development, and demographic transition, but sex-specific population-level evidence on its association with dementia incidence remains limited.MethodsCountry-level associations between cancer incidence in 2019 and dementia incidence in 2023 were examined separately for females and males across 204 countries. Analyses included bivariate correlations, principal component analysis, partial correlations, and forced-entry regression models adjusted for gross domestic product per capita adjusted for purchasing power parity, urbanization, reduced selection opportunity, and sex-specific life expectancy at age 60.ResultsCancer incidence was positively associated with dementia incidence in both sexes. Globally, cancer incidence correlated strongly with dementia incidence among females (r = 0.765, p < 0.001) and males (r = 0.781, p < 0.001). In adjusted models, cancer incidence remained associated with dementia incidence in females (β = 0.496, p < 0.001; adjusted R2 = 0.693) and males (β = 0.526, p < 0.001; adjusted R2 = 0.709). Incremental explained variance was larger among males.ConclusionsCancer incidence is a sex-specific country-level correlate of dementia incidence. Findings indicate population-level co-variation, not individual-level risk or causality in aging populations worldwide.Keywords: Dementia incidence, cancer incidence, population aging, sex differences, global epidemiology, socioeconomic development, life expectancy, ecological analysis

Background

Dementia is a major and growing global public health challenge, placing substantial strain on healthcare systems, social care infrastructure, and economic sustainability [1–3]. As populations age and life expectancy increases worldwide, dementia incidence continues to rise, particularly in countries undergoing demographic transition [4]. Despite advances in prevention and care, dementia remains largely incurable, making the identification of population-level determinants essential for effective prevention strategies and health policy planning [4,5].

Cancer is another leading contributor to global disease burden and mortality, with incidence increasing sharply with age [6,7]. Cancer patterns are strongly shaped by longevity, healthcare access, diagnostic capacity, and socioeconomic development. Improvements in early detection and treatment have led to longer survival in many regions, resulting in a growing number of cancer survivors reaching older ages. This demographic shift raises important questions about how cancer burden and survival intersect with other age-related conditions, including dementia [8,9].

At the individual level, the relationship between cancer and dementia remains debated [10,11]. Some studies report inverse associations, suggesting possible biological tradeoffs between neurodegeneration and carcinogenesis [12,13], while others identify positive or null relationships depending on cancer type, timing, and methodology [14,15]. However, most evidence comes from cohort or clinical studies in high-income countries, limiting global generalizability [16]. Moreover, individual-level associations do not necessarily reflect population-level patterns, where broader demographic and structural factors play a dominant role [17,18].

From a population health perspective, cancer incidence often reflects cumulative survival, healthcare development, and demographic maturity rather than disease burden alone [19,20]. Countries with higher cancer incidence typically exhibit longer life expectancy, stronger health systems, widespread screening, better diagnostic ability, better data collection/analysis and reduced mortality from infectious and cardiovascular diseases [19,20]. These same conditions facilitate the emergence and detection of dementia, which predominantly occurs later in life [4,21]. As such, cancer incidence may function as an aggregate indicator of population aging and survivorship, both of which shape dementia incidence at the national level.

Socioeconomic development further complicates this relationship. Higher economic affluence, urbanization, and healthcare capacity are consistently associated with increased reported incidence of both cancer and dementia [6,19]. Consequently, observed associations between the two may be driven by shared demographic and socioeconomic determinants rather than a direct link [22,23]. Social isolation and loneliness may also contribute to physical and neurocognitive health in older adults [24]. These psychosocial factors have been associated with poorer mental health, Alzheimer’s disease, cardiovascular disease, immune dysfunction, and increased healthcare use. Therefore, social isolation may represent an additional contextual factor that could shape vulnerability to both dementia and chronic disease in later life. Although comparable country-level measures of social isolation were not available for inclusion in the present ecological models, its potential role should be considered when interpreting population-level associations between cancer incidence and dementia incidence. In addition, evolutionary-demographic processes, such as reduced selection opportunity across the life course, may permit the accumulation of late-onset diseases within populations [25]. These processes are increasingly recognized as relevant for explaining cross-national variation in chronic disease incidence, including neurodegenerative disorders [26].

Sex differences add another important dimension. Women generally experience higher dementia prevalence due to longer life expectancy, whereas men exhibit higher incidence rates for many cancers and different exposure profiles across the lifespan [6,27]. Biological, behavioral, and healthcare-related factors differ by sex and may influence both cancer and dementia risk [6,28,29]. Whether cancer incidence shows comparable population-level associations with dementia incidence among females and males remains unclear, as few cross-national studies have conducted explicit sex-specific comparisons.

Older adults with cancer and neurocognitive disorders may also differ in clinical and psychosocial profiles, including comorbidity burden, stroke history, metastatic disease, medication use, functional status, and depressive symptomatology. Prior evidence suggests that neurocognitive deficits are more common among older adults with cancer and may be shaped by multiple clinical factors, including comorbidity and disease severity. Depressive symptoms are also relevant in older populations, particularly among women, as they may overlap with or exacerbate cognitive symptoms and influence healthcare use and disease detection. These factors were not directly modeled in the present ecological analysis due to the lack of comparable country-level data, but they provide important context for interpreting sex-specific population-level associations between cancer incidence and dementia incidence.

Despite these considerations, population-level evidence on the relationship between cancer and dementia remains limited. To the best of our knowledge, only one ecological study has reported a direct association between the incidence of these two conditions; however, it did not examine sex-specific effects or differentiate outcomes by sex [30]. Global dementia research typically focuses on aging, cardiovascular risk, or socioeconomic factors, while cancer burden is rarely examined as a potential correlate [27,31]. Conversely, global cancer studies seldom consider downstream implications for neurodegenerative disease burden [6,32]. Integrating these perspectives is essential for understanding how changing survival and chronic disease patterns may jointly influence future dementia trends [33].

To address these gaps, the present study builds on previous ecological evidence by providing an explicitly sex-disaggregated analysis of the country-level association between cancer incidence and dementia incidence [30]. Rather than proposing a mechanistic or causal relationship, this study examines whether the population-level association differs between females and males, and whether sex-specific cancer incidence contributes differently to dementia incidence after adjustment for demographic and socioeconomic indicators [17,22].

Using sex-specific data across countries, the analysis incorporates bivariate correlations, principal component analysis, stratified comparisons across economic and regional groupings, partial correlations, and multivariable regression models. This approach allows assessment of the consistency of observed associations, consideration of shared macro-level determinants, and direct comparison between female and male patterns [17,22,23].

By situating cancer incidence within a broader framework of life expectancy at age 60, economic development, demographic transition, and healthcare diagnostic capacity, this study contributes additional population-level evidence relevant to global dementia surveillance and health system planning [20,23,34]. Cancer incidence is conceptualized as a country-level indicator of survivorship, diagnostic intensity, and accumulated chronic disease burden, rather than as a causal risk factor for dementia [19,20].

Material and method

Data sources

Country-level cancer and dementia incidence data were obtained from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study, curated by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME), University of Washington, USA.

Dementia incidence estimates were derived from a synthesis of population-based cohort studies, health surveys, hospital and outpatient records, claims databases, and cause-of-death data. Case definitions followed International Classification of Diseases (ICD) criteria for Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias [34,35]. Bayesian meta-regression modeling (DisMod-MR) was used to integrate heterogeneous data sources, ensure internal consistency across epidemiological parameters, and generate age- and sex-specific incidence estimates comparable across countries and over time.

Cancer incidence rates (per 100,000 population) were obtained from GBD cancer estimates based on population-based cancer registries, health surveys, vital registration systems, and hospital data, synthesized using standard GBD modeling approaches [34,35].

All analyses used age-standardized incidence rates (ASIRs) generated by direct age standardization to the GBD world standard population, enabling valid cross-national comparisons by accounting for differences in age structure. Sex-specific ASIRs were applied consistently throughout the analyses. GBD estimates account for under-ascertainment through model-based correction and are accompanied by 95% uncertainty intervals; however, the present analyses focus on central point estimates. Because the present analyses used central point estimates, uncertainty intervals were not incorporated into the correlation, partial correlation, or regression models. This limitation is acknowledged in the Discussion and Strengths and limitations sections.

Socioeconomic and demographic indicators were compiled from established international sources. Economic affluence was measured using gross domestic product (GDP) per capita adjusted for purchasing power parity (PPP; constant 2018 US dollars) [25], and urbanization was defined as the percentage of the population residing in urban areas; both indicators were obtained from the World Bank World Development Indicators [36,37]. Life expectancy at age 60 (life e60; years) was retrieved from the World Health Organization (WHO) Global Health Observatory [23].

Reduced selection opportunity, an index reflecting the opportunity for natural selection in human populations, was derived from earlier publications [25]. Reduced selection opportunity was included as a secondary population-level demographic indicator derived from previously published life-table-based estimates. It reflects variation in mortality and fertility patterns that may influence the opportunity for natural selection across populations. In the present study, this variable was not treated as a causal mechanism for dementia incidence but as a contextual demographic covariate relevant to population aging and epidemiological transition.

Countries were classified using multiple international frameworks to enable stratified and comparative analyses. These included World Bank income categories (low, lower-middle, upper-middle, and high income), United Nations development status (developed vs. developing), and the six WHO regions (AFRO, AMRO, EMRO, EURO, SEARO, WPRO). Additional geopolitical and cultural groupings (e.g., APEC, Arab World, European Union, OECD, Latin America and the Caribbean, SADC, SCO, and English-speaking countries) were applied to capture broader contextual variation. Analyses were based on data from up to 204 countries, with sample sizes for particular calculations limited to countries with complete information for all study variables.

Study design and variable specification

This study employed an ecological, cross-national design. Cancer incidence was specified as the explanatory indicator and dementia incidence as the outcome for analytical purposes. However, this specification should not be interpreted as evidence of temporal precedence, causality, or individual-level disease progression. Although cancer incidence data from 2019 and dementia incidence data from 2023 were used, both variables are modeled country-level estimates rather than measurements from longitudinal individual-level follow-up. Therefore, the four-year difference between reference years does not establish causal direction, particularly given the long preclinical development of dementia. Findings are interpreted as population-level co-variation between cancer incidence and dementia incidence within broader demographic, socioeconomic, and healthcare-system contexts.

Data preparation

Data from multiple international repositories were harmonized to construct a country-level dataset. Cancer and dementia incidence rates were aligned with socioeconomic and demographic indicators, including GDP PPP, urbanization, life expectancy at age 60, and reduced selection opportunity. All variables were expressed using standardized population-based units: incidence rates per 100,000 population, percentages for urbanization, years for life expectancy, and constant US dollars for GDP.

Countries with missing data on any study variable were excluded using listwise deletion, resulting in consistent analytic samples across correlation, principal component, partial correlation, and regression analyses. Extreme cancer incidence values greater than 1,000 per 100,000 were retained to preserve global variability, with their influence assessed through sensitivity analyses rather than exclusion at the outset.

Threshold-based sensitivity analyses

To assess robustness to extreme cancer incidence values, threshold-based sensitivity analyses were performed. Countries with cancer incidence exceeding 1,000 per 100,000 were excluded, and multivariable regression models were re-estimated separately for female and male dementia incidence. Model stability was evaluated by comparing standardized regression coefficients, adjusted R2 values, and influence diagnostics (Cook’s distance, leverage, and DFBETAs) between the full and restricted samples.

In addition, cancer and dementia incidence rates were log10-transformed, and correlation and regression analyses were repeated to assess robustness to right-skewed distributions.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize cancer incidence, dementia incidence, and covariates across countries. Associations among variables were examined using Pearson’s correlation coefficients for linear relationships and Spearman’s rho for monotonic relationships. Differences in correlation strength across income levels, development status, and regional groupings were tested using Fisher’s r-to-z transformations.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted separately for female and male indicators as a descriptive dimension-reduction analysis. The purpose was to examine whether cancer incidence clustered with broader country-level indicators of socioeconomic development and population aging, including GDP PPP, urbanization, reduced selection opportunity, and life expectancy at age 60. Components were retained using eigenvalues greater than 1, supported by inspection of the eigenvalue pattern and percentage of variance explained. Only one dominant component was retained in both female and male analyses; therefore, rotation was not applied because a rotated solution is not meaningful when a single component is extracted. PCA was used only to describe shared macro-level patterning among variables and was not used to infer causality or a distinct cancer–dementia mechanism.

Partial correlations were used to examine whether the association between cancer incidence and dementia incidence remained after sequential adjustment for socioeconomic and demographic factors. Multiple linear regression models were then fitted to examine associations with dementia incidence. Forced-entry models were used as the primary multivariable analyses because predictors were selected a priori based on conceptual relevance. Stepwise regression was used only as a supplementary exploratory analysis to assess whether the pattern of retained predictors was broadly consistent with the primary forced-entry models. Findings from stepwise models were not used as the primary basis for inference.

Standardized beta coefficients (β), adjusted R2 values, and F-statistics were reported. Multicollinearity was assessed using tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF) statistics. To improve transparency, tolerance and VIF values for the primary forced-entry regression models including cancer incidence are reported in Supplementary Table S1. Given the conceptual overlap between cancer incidence, population aging, socioeconomic development, and healthcare diagnostic capacity, regression coefficients were interpreted cautiously as indicators of country-level association rather than evidence of independent biological effects.

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 using two-tailed tests. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 31 (IBM Corp., 2023). All findings are interpreted within an ecological framework and do not imply causal relationships or individual-level risks.

Results

The Results are presented using complementary analytical approaches, including correlation analyses, dimension reduction, and regression modeling, to examine population-level associations between cancer incidence and dementia incidence.

Visual and sensitivity evidence of sex-specific associations

Figure 1 presents sex-specific scatter plots showing the country-level association between cancer incidence in 2019 and dementia incidence in 2023. In both females and males, higher cancer incidence was generally associated with higher dementia incidence. The overall direction of association was similar across sexes, although a small number of countries had very high cancer incidence values above 1,000 per 100,000, particularly at the upper end of the distribution. Therefore, the fitted curves should be interpreted as descriptive visual aids rather than formal nonlinear models.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted after excluding countries with cancer incidence values above 1,000 per 100,000. The results remained consistent. Among females (n = 170), cancer incidence remained strongly associated with dementia incidence (β = 0.87, p < 0.001), with high model fit (adjusted R2 = 0.85). A similar pattern was observed among males (n = 170), where cancer incidence also remained the strongest associated variable (β = 0.85, p < 0.001; adjusted R2 = 0.86). Life expectancy at age 60 remained independently associated with dementia incidence in the restricted models, whereas urbanization and selection opportunity were not statistically significant. Influence diagnostics indicated that the restricted models were not unduly affected by high-leverage observations.

Bivariate correlations

Table 1 summarizes the global bivariate associations among cancer incidence, dementia incidence, and key socioeconomic and demographic indicators. At the global level, female and male dementia incidence were highly correlated (r = 0.972, p < 0.001), indicating concordant sex-specific patterns across countries. Female and male cancer incidence were also highly correlated (r = 0.988, p < 0.001). Cancer incidence showed strong positive associations with dementia incidence in both females (r = 0.765, p < 0.001) and males (r = 0.781, p < 0.001). Cancer incidence was also correlated with GDP PPP, life expectancy at age 60, urbanization, and reduced selection opportunity, indicating that it overlapped with broader indicators of socioeconomic development, population aging, and healthcare system capacity. These correlations support cautious interpretation of regression findings within an ecological framework.

Across subgroup analyses (Table S2), positive associations were consistently observed, although their magnitudes varied. Correlations were weakest in low-income countries and strongest in upper-middle-income and developing regions. Partial correlations between cancer incidence and dementia incidence

Table 3 presents the partial correlation analyses examining whether the association between cancer incidence and dementia incidence remained after sequential adjustment for economic affluence, urbanization, selection opportunity, and life expectancy at age 60. At the zero-order level, cancer incidence was moderately to strongly associated with dementia incidence in both females (r = 0.607, r2 = 0.369, p < 0.001) and males (r = 0.634, r2 = 0.402, p < 0.001). These associations remained statistically significant across all adjusted models, indicating that the cancer–dementia relationship was not fully explained by the included socioeconomic and demographic indicators.

Adjustment for economic affluence (GDP PPP) alone (Model 1) and in combination with urbanization (Model 2) resulted in minimal attenuation of the associations for either sex. Further controlling for selection opportunity (Model 3) similarly yielded stable correlation coefficients, with r values ranging from 0.604 to 0.607 in females and 0.633 to 0.639 in males.

Multiple regression models examining associations with dementia incidence by sex (ENTER)

Table 4 presents the primary ENTER regression models examining associations with female and male dementia incidence. In Model 1, which included socioeconomic and demographic variables only, life expectancy at age 60 showed the strongest association with dementia incidence in both females (β = 0.591, p < 0.001) and males (β = 0.399, p < 0.001). Economic affluence and urbanization were not statistically significant in either sex, while reduced selection opportunity was significant only in males (β = 0.218, p = 0.002). All models were estimated using listwise deletion, yielding a consistent analytic sample of 174 countries. Model 1 explained 55.4% of the variance in female dementia incidence and 53.6% of the variance in male dementia incidence.

In Model 2, inclusion of sex-specific cancer incidence substantially improved model performance. Cancer incidence was strongly associated with dementia incidence in both females (β = 0.496, p < 0.001) and males (β = 0.526, p < 0.001), while the effect of life expectancy at age 60 was attenuated but remained statistically significant in both sexes. Adjusted R2 increased to 0.693 for females and 0.709 for males, corresponding to incremental gains of 0.139 and 0.173, respectively. All models were statistically significant (p < 0.001), with a larger incremental contribution of cancer incidence observed in males.

Multicollinearity diagnostics for the primary ENTER regression models are presented in Supplementary Table S1. In the female model including cancer incidence, tolerance values ranged from 0.282 to 0.557 and VIF values ranged from 1.795 to 3.548. In the male model including cancer incidence, tolerance values ranged from 0.367 to 0.616 and VIF values ranged from 1.623 to 2.721. These values were within conventional thresholds, indicating no severe multicollinearity based on tolerance and VIF criteria. Nevertheless, because the predictors represent related macro-level development, aging, and healthcare-system indicators, the regression coefficients were interpreted cautiously.

Discussion

This global ecological analysis extends existing research on the cancer–dementia relationship by demonstrating a consistent population-level association across sexes, income strata, and geographic regions, thereby supplementing and expanding upon previous findings that did not stratify analyses by sex [9,30,33]. Unlike most prior studies, which have focused on individual-level cohorts and clinical samples [38], this study adopts a macro-level perspective that captures cross-national variation in disease incidence. The observed positive associations suggest that cancer incidence functions as a population-level marker of broader processes shaping dementia burden, including demographic aging, cumulative exposure to chronic disease risk factors, and healthcare system development. Importantly, these findings do not imply individual-level co-occurrence or biological causation, but instead highlight shared patterns of disease burden operating at the population level.

Cancer incidence should therefore be interpreted as contributing additional statistical information within the fitted ecological models, rather than as a fully independent biological exposure separated from aging, development, and healthcare diagnostic capacity.

The findings should be interpreted strictly within an ecological and population-level framework. The observed associations do not indicate that cancer causes dementia, nor do they imply that individuals with cancer are at increased risk of dementia. Rather, the results show that countries with higher reported cancer incidence also tend to report higher dementia incidence. This pattern is likely to reflect shared macro-level determinants, including population aging, longer survival into older age, socioeconomic development, diagnostic capacity, healthcare access, and chronic disease surveillance. Therefore, cancer incidence is interpreted here as a population-level marker of demographic and epidemiological transition, rather than as an individual-level causal exposure.

Reconciling ecological findings with individual-level evidence

Much of the individual-level literature, particularly from high-income settings, has reported inverse or null associations between cancer history and subsequent dementia risk, often interpreted through hypotheses of biological antagonism [38–40]. These interpretations have emphasized cellular mechanisms such as apoptosis, DNA repair, cell-cycle regulation, and protein aggregation [40–42]. However, growing evidence indicates substantial heterogeneity in cognitive outcomes among cancer survivors, varying by cancer type, treatment modality, age at diagnosis, comorbidity burden, and survival duration [43,44]. The present findings complement this literature by shifting attention from individual survival trajectories to population-level disease burden, where survival bias is reduced and broader structural determinants of health become more apparent [22,33,45].

Cancer incidence as an indicator of demographic and epidemiological transition

The strong alignment observed between cancer incidence, dementia incidence, economic affluence, and life expectancy at age 60 is consistent with established theories of demographic and epidemiological transition [20,46]. As countries progress economically, declining infectious disease mortality and improved healthcare lead to longer life expectancy with increased opportunity and thereby prevalence of age-related chronic conditions [20,46,47]. The principal component analysis should be interpreted cautiously. It primarily indicates that cancer incidence is patterned alongside broader development- and aging-related indicators, including life expectancy, economic affluence, urbanization, and reduced selection opportunity. This clustering is expected in global epidemiological data and supports interpretation of cancer incidence as a population-level marker of demographic and epidemiological transition, rather than as evidence of a distinct biological relationship with dementia. Within this context, cancer incidence may serve as a sentinel indicator of cumulative biological stress and long-term exposure to environmental and lifestyle risk factors associated with modernization [19,48].

Independence from life expectancy and affluence

The persistence of the cancer–dementia association after adjustment for GDP, urbanization, and life expectancy at age 60 indicates that this relationship is not solely a by-product of population aging or economic development [17,22]. Although adjustment for life expectancy attenuated effect sizes, a substantial independent association remained, suggesting partial overlap but not redundancy between cancer incidence and life expectancy at age 60 [17]. At the population level, cancer incidence reflects additional dimensions of health, including long-term exposure to carcinogenic environments, metabolic and inflammatory stressors, and the intensity of medical surveillance [48,49]. While such factors have also been implicated in neurodegenerative processes, they are discussed here only as contextual considerations rather than explanatory mechanisms [17,50]. Although the models adjusted for selected macro-level indicators, the observed associations may still be influenced by unmeasured behavioral, environmental, healthcare-system, and diagnostic factors. Therefore, the regression results should be interpreted as ecological associations rather than evidence of an independent disease-specific relationship [17,27]. Although highly relevant at the individual level, these variables are strongly patterned by national development and life expectancy at age 60 and may introduce multicollinearity when included simultaneously in ecological analyses [17,23,33].

Sex differences in the cancer–dementia associations

The consistently stronger associations observed among males merit particular attention. Sex differences in cancer epidemiology are well documented, with men exhibiting higher incidence rates for several cancers and often presenting at later disease stages [6,19,51]. Treatment patterns also differ by sex, with evidence suggesting greater exposure to aggressive therapies among men, potentially increasing vulnerability to long-term cognitive sequelae [52,53]. The present findings extend this finding by demonstrating that, at the ecological level, cancer incidence contributes a larger incremental share of explained variance to male dementia incidence than in females. This pattern underscores the importance of sex-disaggregated analyses in global dementia research and suggests distinct population-level trajectories of cancer burden and survivorship [4,23]. Additionally, well described hormone-related protective effects exist with a range of chronic diseases, notably cardiovascular diseases, in females [54]. These cytoprotective and microvascular effects might explain some of the reduced dementia incidence in females over male sex cohorts [55,56]. Sex differences in prevalences of a range of chronic inflammatory conditions are also well described, and this too might extend to dementia development as a possible explanation of our findings [57,58].

Regional variation and non-linear patterns

Substantial variation across income groups and regions was observed, consistent with disparities in disease detection, reporting, and survival [4,59]. Weaker and less consistent associations in low-income countries likely reflect under-ascertainment of both cancer and dementia, compounded by limited diagnostic infrastructure and high competing (often earlier) mortality [4,27]. In contrast, stronger associations in middle-income and developing regions may reflect rapid population aging combined with expanding access to cancer diagnosis and treatment, resulting in increased survivorship and cumulative disease burden [19,20,33].

The curvilinear relationships observed between cancer and dementia incidence align with previous ecological studies showing non-linear associations between chronic disease burden and development indicators [17,30,46]. Dementia incidence appears to rise disproportionately once populations surpass critical thresholds of life expectancy at age 60, healthcare exposure, and chronic disease survival. Sensitivity analyses excluding countries with extremely high cancer incidence confirmed that these patterns were not driven by outliers, supporting the robustness of the findings [60,61].

Implications for dementia epidemiology

These findings should be interpreted cautiously within a population-level ecological framework. The observed association between cancer incidence and dementia incidence does not support individual-level risk prediction, causal inference, or direct integration of cancer surveillance into dementia prevention strategies. Instead, cancer incidence may provide contextual information about broader demographic and epidemiological transition, including population aging, survivorship, diagnostic capacity, and chronic disease reporting.

From a health system perspective, countries experiencing rising cancer detection and survival may also need to prepare for increasing demand for dementia diagnosis, long-term cognitive care, and aging-related health services. This is particularly relevant for middle-income and developing settings undergoing rapid demographic transition. However, these implications relate to population-level planning and surveillance, not to individual-level prevention or clinical decision-making.

Although cancer and dementia incidence estimates were drawn from different Global Burden of Disease reference years, this should not be interpreted as longitudinal evidence or temporal precedence. Both outcomes reflect long-term, slowly evolving population-level burdens, and Global Burden of Disease estimates are temporally modeled and smoothed. The findings should therefore be interpreted as country-level co-variation rather than short-term temporal or causal relationships.

Future research directions

The mechanisms discussed above are presented solely as potential avenues for future research and are not intended to explain the observed ecological associations. Evaluating proposed individual-level pathways, such as viral involvement or cancer treatment-related neurotoxicity, requires longitudinal individual-level data and lies beyond the scope of this study. Future research integrating clinical, biological, and population-based approaches may help clarify whether and how such pathways contribute to dementia risk.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It draws on standardized international datasets from the Global Burden of Disease Study, World Bank, and World Health Organization, enabling cross-national analysis across a large number of countries. The use of sex-disaggregated incidence data allowed direct comparison of female and male patterns, addressing an important gap in global dementia epidemiology. The analytical approach combined bivariate correlations, stratified analyses, principal component analysis, partial correlations, pre-specified forced-entry regression models, and sensitivity analyses. This helped assess the consistency of the observed population-level associations across complementary methods. Sensitivity analyses excluding countries with extreme cancer incidence values further indicated that the findings were not driven solely by outlying observations.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the ecological design precludes causal inference and does not permit conclusions at the individual level. Country-level associations cannot be extrapolated to individuals, and the possibility of ecological fallacy must be recognized. The study therefore provides evidence of population-level co-variation between cancer incidence and dementia incidence, not evidence that cancer causes dementia or that individuals with cancer have increased dementia risk.

Second, residual confounding remains possible. Although the models adjusted for selected macro-level indicators, including economic affluence, urbanization, reduced selection opportunity, and life expectancy at age 60, several factors that may influence both cancer and dementia incidence were not included. These include smoking, obesity, alcohol use, education, environmental pollution, physical inactivity, healthcare expenditure, cardiovascular disease burden, genetic predisposition, diagnostic infrastructure, treatment modalities, and survival duration. These variables were not incorporated because comparable, complete, and consistently measured country-level data were not available across all included countries, and many of these indicators are closely related to broader development and healthcare-system capacity. Therefore, the observed associations may partly reflect shared healthcare access, surveillance intensity, diagnostic capacity, and chronic disease reporting rather than a disease-specific relationship between cancer and dementia.

Third, reliance on modeled Global Burden of Disease estimates is an important limitation. Although the Global Burden of Disease framework provides standardized and internationally comparable estimates, these estimates are derived from heterogeneous data sources, modeling assumptions, imputation procedures, and temporal smoothing. The present analyses used central point estimates and did not incorporate 95% uncertainty intervals into the correlation or regression models. As a result, the precision of country-level estimates may be overstated, particularly in low-income settings where dementia diagnosis, cancer registration, and health system reporting may be less complete.

Fourth, the inclusion of reduced selection opportunity should be interpreted cautiously. This indicator is not routinely used in mainstream epidemiological modeling and may be viewed as conceptually distinct from conventional public health determinants. In this study, it was included as an exploratory demographic covariate rather than as evidence of an evolutionary mechanism underlying dementia incidence. The main findings should therefore be interpreted primarily in relation to country-level aging, socioeconomic development, healthcare capacity, and chronic disease reporting.

Finally, the different reference years for cancer incidence and dementia incidence should not be interpreted as evidence of temporal precedence. Both outcomes are modeled country-level estimates reflecting long-term population-level patterns. The findings should therefore be interpreted as broad ecological co-variation rather than as longitudinal evidence, causal direction, or individual-level disease linkage. Future research using more comprehensive country-level datasets and longitudinal individual-level data is needed to assess potential behavioral, clinical, healthcare-system, and biological pathways more directly.

Public health implications

The findings of this global ecological analysis have important implications for public health policy, surveillance, and planning in aging societies. The consistent association between cancer incidence and dementia incidence across sexes, regions, and income groups suggests that dementia risk should be situated within a broader chronic disease and aging framework rather than considered solely as a neurological outcome. At the population level, cancer incidence appears to capture cumulative biological stress, healthcare exposure, and survival into older age, factors that are increasingly relevant as global life expectancy rises.

The stronger and more stable associations observed among males highlight the need for sex-sensitive public health strategies. Men may experience distinct trajectories of cancer burden, treatment intensity, and survivorship that translate into greater vulnerability to later life cognitive impairment. Integrating cognitive screening, monitoring, and preventive interventions into cancer survivorship programs, particularly for male populations, may help mitigate future dementia burden.

The pronounced associations identified in middle-income and developing regions underscore the challenges facing countries undergoing rapid demographic and epidemiological transition. As cancer detection and survival improve in these settings, reported dementia incidence may also rise as more people survive into older age and health systems become better able to identify chronic and neurocognitive conditions. This pattern should be interpreted as reflecting shared population-level processes rather than evidence of a direct causal relationship between cancer and dementia.

These findings suggest that health systems in transitioning countries may need to prepare for increasing burdens of both cancer and dementia through strengthened diagnostic capacity, workforce training, integrated chronic disease surveillance, and long-term cognitive care planning. However, the present ecological analysis cannot determine whether clinical, biological, treatment-related, or behavioral pathways contribute to dementia risk among individual cancer survivors. Future research using longitudinal individual-level and clinical data is required to examine these potential pathways.

Overall, these findings support more integrated public health approaches that link cancer control/prevention, aging policy, and dementia prevention. Coordinated strategies targeting shared upstream determinants, such as environmental exposures, lifestyle risk factors, and access to healthcare, may yield broader and more sustainable benefits for population health as societies continue to age.

Conclusion

This global ecological study found that cancer incidence was positively associated with dementia incidence at the country level in both females and males. Across correlation, stratified, partial correlation, and multivariable regression analyses, the association remained evident after adjustment for selected demographic and socioeconomic indicators, including economic affluence, urbanization, reduced selection opportunity, and life expectancy at age 60.

The association appeared somewhat stronger among males, with cancer incidence contributing a larger incremental gain in explained variance in male models than in female models. This finding suggests that sex-specific population-level patterns may be relevant when interpreting global dementia incidence, particularly in relation to aging, survivorship, diagnostic capacity, and chronic disease reporting.

These findings should be interpreted strictly within an ecological framework. They do not indicate that cancer causes dementia, nor do they imply that individuals with cancer have increased dementia risk. Rather, cancer incidence may serve as a contextual population-level marker of demographic and epidemiological transition. The findings may inform health system planning for aging populations, but they do not justify individual-level causal inference, clinical risk prediction, or direct dementia prevention recommendations. Future research using longitudinal individual-level and clinical data is needed to examine potential behavioral, healthcare-related, treatment-related, and biological pathways.
