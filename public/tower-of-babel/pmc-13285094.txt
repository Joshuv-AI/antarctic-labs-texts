Proteomic study of high-altitude pulmonary hypertension in the Xinjiang Pamir highlanders
Authors: Zi-Yan Sun, Henry S. Lynn, Ainiwaier Tuerxun, Maierhaba Xuereti, Kawsaray Memetimin, Dilinuer Maimaitiyiming
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigh-altitude pulmonary hypertension (HAPH), classified as Group 3 pulmonary hypertension, is a significant threat to the health of high-altitude populations. The scarcity of studies in diverse populations has become a research bottleneck, limiting diagnostic and therapeutic advances.MethodsIn this first proteomic study focusing on the eastern Pamir Plateau (Kizilsu Kyrgyz Autonomous Prefecture, Xinjiang), plasma samples were analyzed using data-independent acquisition (DIA) mass spectrometry. Differential expression analysis in parallel with weighted gene co-expression network analysis was performed to identify core pathways and hub proteins, and gene set enrichment analysis was used for quality assessment. Integrative analysis of the two methods was used to select candidates for validation by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) in an independent cohort.ResultsAmong > 1400 detected proteins, 123 were differentially expressed and 45 were identified as hub proteins significantly associated with HAPH. Extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling- and angiogenesis-related proteins were upregulated, whereas proteins related to enzyme activity, iron metabolism, and inflammatory responses were downregulated. Integrative analysis identified 23 core proteins, with ECM-receptor interaction and TGF-β/Smad signaling identified as key pathways. ELISA confirmed that plasma levels of THBS2, LOXL1, and POSTN were significantly elevated in patients with HAPH (P < 0.05). Among these, THBS2 and LOXL1 levels were positively correlated with mPAP (THBS2: r = 0.389, 95% CI: 0.034–0.657, P = 0.033; LOXL1: r = 0.457, 95% CI: 0.115–0.701, P = 0.011).ConclusionsECM remodeling is closely associated with HAPH in this indigenous high-altitude population. THBS2, LOXL1, and POSTN show potential as biomarkers and therapeutic targets.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04325-9.

Introduction

High-altitude pulmonary hypertension (HAPH) falls under Group 3 of pulmonary hypertension (PH), specifically categorized as hypoxia-related pulmonary hypertension [1]. Hypoxia-induced pulmonary hypertension affects an estimated 5% or more of people living above 2500 m, and the low early diagnosis rate and the lack of effective treatment methods make it a public health issue in high-altitude areas [2]. Although chronic hypoxia is widely recognized as a primary contributor to HAPH, not all residents living at high altitudes develop this condition, and the prevalence of HAPH varies across different geographic regions [3, 4]. As early as 2007, Penaloza attributed this phenomenon to adaptive mechanisms occurring at successive stages of the oxygen transport cascade, with genetic factors and environmental conditions (altitude, geographical factors) playing significant roles and influencing the comparability of research findings [5]. This point has been supported by many subsequent investigations [6]. Studying diverse populations enhances our understanding of HAPH pathogenesis and provides insights into human adaptation to chronic hypoxia. However, an important gap in the research on high-altitude illnesses including HAPH is the limited number of different populations studied to date [1, 7].

Previous studies have shown that key pathways such as energy metabolism, oxidative stress, and extracellular matrix remodeling are involved in HAPH [8–10]. However, most of these studies have focused on native populations living at altitudes above 4000 m on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, the Andean Plateau, the Kyrgyz Plateau, and the Ethiopian Highlands. The Pamir region of Xinjiang remains understudied. The eastern Pamir Plateau differs climatically from the relatively humid eastern Tibetan Plateau, with sparse precipitation and harsh, arid winters [11]. Located in the westernmost part of China, the Kizilsu Kyrgyz Autonomous Prefecture borders several countries, including Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan [12]. This geographical position has fostered longstanding cultural exchanges among communities inhabiting this transboundary region. The indigenous people mainly lead a nomadic lifestyle. Their eating habits differ from those of inhabitants of the mainly agricultural Andean Plateau and the semi-agricultural and semi-pastoral indigenous populations of the Tibetan Plateau. Consequently, investigating HAPH in this population not only enhances understanding of disease mechanisms but also provides a foundation for personalized therapeutic strategies.

This study represents the first proteomic investigation of HAPH patients and healthy indigenous controls living in the eastern Pamir Plateau (Xinjiang) at an altitude of 3000–3800. The goal was to explore biomarkers and therapeutic targets for HAPH, thereby providing new perspectives for future research.

Materials and methods

Study population and sample collection

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University (approval numbers: K202306-06 and K202506-42). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. The study participants were indigenous residents of Bulungkol Township and Muji Township in Akto County, Xinjiang. All participants were of Kyrgyz ethnicity. A total of 27 participants were recruited for proteomic analysis, comprising 14 HAPH patients and 13 healthy controls. The validation cohort consisted of 30 age-, sex-, and altitude-matched participants for ELISA validation, including 15 HAPH patients and 15 healthy controls (Fig. 1).

Portable ultrasound machines were employed for diagnosis. The diagnosis of HAPH was established according to the “Qinghai Criteria” [2], which was defined as a mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) > 30 mmHg at rest. Participants with excessive erythrocytosis (female: haemoglobin > 190 g/L; male: haemoglobin > 210 g/L), left heart disease, or pulmonary hypertension secondary to severe pulmonary disorders were excluded. In this study, mPAP was estimated from the pulmonary regurgitation velocity using the modified Bernoulli equation with right atrial pressure assumed at 8 mmHg per ASE guidelines; right atrial minor dimension (RA minor) was measured in the apical four-chamber view at end-systole; the right ventricular end-diastolic dimension (RVEDD) was measured in the parasternal long-axis view at end-diastole; and the Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was calculated using the biplane Simpson’s method [13, 14]. All echocardiographic parameters were acquired by an experienced echocardiographer and independently reviewed by a second specialist (Fig. 1).

Plasma was obtained from peripheral blood by centrifugation at 3500 rpm for 15 min and stored at -80 °C until analysis.

Proteomics analysis

Preparation of protein samples

Low-abundance proteins were enriched using the QLBIO MagicOmics-DMB8X kit. Briefly, 100 µL of sample was mixed with an equal volume of dilution buffer and incubated with pre-conditioned magnetic beads at 37 °C for 30 min with shaking at 1000 rpm. After magnetic separation, the beads were washed three times with dilution buffer. Captured proteins were eluted with 30 µL of lysis buffer at 95 °C for 10 min. After cooling to room temperature, the eluate was digested with 100 µL of digestion buffer and 2 µL of trypsin at 37 °C for 4 h with shaking at 1000 rpm. The digestion was terminated by adding 5 µL of termination buffer, and the resulting samples were lyophilized.

HPLC-MS/MS analysis

HPLC-MS/MS analysis was performed on a RIGOL L-3000 HPLC system (Beijing Rigol Precision Instrument Co., Ltd.). Mobile phase A consisted of 100% water with 0.1% formic acid, and mobile phase B was 80% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. The lyophilized powder was reconstituted in 10 µL of mobile phase A, centrifuged at 14,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C, and 400 ng of the supernatant was injected. Chromatographic separation was achieved with the following gradient: 3.5% B at start, increased to 32% B at 17 min, then to 95% B at 18 min, and held at 95% B until 20 min. Mass spectrometry was conducted on a timsTOF_HT mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics) equipped with a Captive Spray ion source. Data were acquired in data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode over a mass range of 300–1500 m/z, with an MS1 resolution of 60,000 (at m/z 1222), an accumulation time of 50 ms in the TIMS tunnel, a capillary voltage of 1.5 kV, an ion mobility range of 0.70–1.30 cm²/(V·s), and a total cycle time of 1.23 s.

Database retrieval and protein quantification

Proteomics raw data files were searched against the reviewed Homo sapiens database from UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/) using DIA-NN software (v1.8). Up to two missed cleavages were allowed. Carbamidomethylation on cysteine, oxidation of methionine, and N-terminal acetylation were set as variable modifications. The precursor ion mass tolerance was set to ± 15 ppm. The default false discovery rate (FDR) threshold was set at 1% at both the peptide and protein levels.

Data preprocessing

All bioinformatics analyses were conducted in the R software environment (v4.5.1). Variance Stabilizing Normalization (VSN) was performed using the VSN function to correct for the impact of technical differences on quantitative data. The K-Nearest Neighbors algorithm was applied to impute missing values in the quality-controlled proteomics data.

Data processing of differentially expressed proteins

Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) between the HAPH and control groups were identified using the “DEP” package. Differential expression analysis (DEP) was performed using a linear model combined with empirical Bayes moderation. Significant DEPs were defined as those with P < 0.05 and |log2 fold change (log2FC)| > 0.58.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was conducted on peptide abundance data of all samples using the “WGCNA” package [15]. A scale-free network and topological overlap matrix (TOM) were first constructed based on a Pearson correlation matrix and a chosen soft threshold (β). Hierarchical clustering of the TOM followed, with co-expression modules identified via the dynamic tree cutting method. Modules most strongly correlated with HAPH were then selected by calculating Spearman’s correlation coefficients between module eigengenes and HAPH for further analysis.

Functional enrichment analysis

The “clusterProfile” R package [16] was used to investigate the biological functions of proteins. Gene Ontology (GO) [17], Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [18], and Reactome [19] pathway enrichment analyses were performed on proteins obtained from WGCNA and DEP analyses. P < 0.05 and FDR < 0.1 were used for the statistical significance analysis.

Gene set enrichment analysis

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed on all identified proteins, which were mapped to their corresponding gene symbols and ranked in descending order based on log2FC values calculated from the HAPH versus control comparison. The pre-ranked list was tested against MSigDB Hallmark gene sets (v2025.2) as reference. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 and FDR < 0.25 according to GSEA guidelines [20].

Statistical analysis

To compare continuous variables between the HAPH and control groups, the Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to assess normality, and Levene’s test was applied to evaluate homogeneity of variance. Based on the study design, independent-samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests were applied to the discovery cohort, whereas paired-samples t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used for the validation cohort. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages), and between-group comparisons were performed using Fisher’s exact test. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the relationship between plasma protein expression levels and mPAP. The significance threshold was set at α = 0.05, with a two-tailed P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

ELISA analysis

The ELISA kits of Thrombospondin-2 (THBS2, JL29340), Lysyl oxidase homolog 1 (LOXL1, JL18354), Insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 7 (IGFBP-7, JL14206), Fibrillin-1 (FBN1, JL15417), and Periostin (POSTN, JL19141) were purchased from Shanghai Jianglai Biotech Co., Ltd. Formal experiments were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance of each sample at 450 nm was obtained using a Bio-Tek microplate reader model ELX800.

Results

Participants and clinical characteristics

The HAPH and control groups were matched for gender, age, and body mass index (BMI). No significant differences were observed between the two groups in oxygen saturation, blood pressure, heart rate, red blood cell count, hemoglobin, or lipid profiles. However, compared to the control group, the HAPH group had significantly larger RA minor (p < 0.05), RVEDD (p < 0.05) and higher mPAP (p < 0.001), which were also in line with the pathological manifestation of HAPH (Table 1).

Differential expression and functional enrichment analysis

To elucidate the molecular regulatory characteristics underlying HAPH, we performed differentially expressed protein analysis between the HAPH and control groups. Using the thresholds of P < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 0.58, 77 significantly upregulated proteins and 46 downregulated proteins were identified. The distribution and expression patterns of the DEPs are visualized in the volcano plot (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Material 1).

Subsequently, GO, KEGG, and Reactome enrichment analyses were performed on the DEPs. GO analysis revealed that the upregulated DEPs were significantly enriched in extracellular matrix (ECM) organization, collagen fiber organization, cell-matrix adhesion, and the TGF-β receptor signaling pathway (Biological Process, BP); collagen-containing ECM, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) lumen, and basement membrane (Cellular Component, CC); and ECM structural constituent and glycosaminoglycan binding (Molecular Function, MF) (Fig. 3B). In contrast, the downregulated DEPs were enriched in inflammatory response and wound healing (BP); secretory granule lumen, vesicle lumen, and platelet microparticle (CC); and endopeptidase activity (primarily serine-type), protease binding, vitamin binding, and antioxidant activity (MF) (Fig. 3C).

KEGG pathway analysis indicated that the upregulated DEPs were enriched in ECM-receptor interaction, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, the TGF-β signaling pathway, and the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway (Fig. 3D). In contrast, the downregulated DEPs were enriched in the proteasome, nucleotide sugar biosynthesis, and ferroptosis pathways (Fig. 3E).

Reactome analysis showed that the upregulated DEPs were enriched in pathways related to ECM organization and degradation, while the downregulated DEPs were enriched in platelet activation, aggregation, release, and signaling, response to elevated platelet cytosolic calcium ions, and neutrophil degranulation (Fig. 3F). Additionally, both groups were co-enriched in pathways involved in the regulation of insulin-like growth factor (IGF) transport and uptake by insulin-like growth factor binding proteins (IGFBPs) and post-translational protein phosphorylation (Fig. 3G).

Identifying disease-associated protein modules by WGCNA

Differential expression analysis identifies individual proteins with altered abundance, yet it does not fully capture the complex, interconnected nature of biological systems. To overcome this limitation and identify coordinated protein networks associated with HAPH, we employed WGCNA. Sample expression profiles were examined using hierarchical clustering and the dynamic tree-cut algorithm, and four outlier samples were removed to ensure analytical reliability (Supplementary Material 2). A soft-thresholding power (β = 5) was selected, which achieved a scale-free topology fit index of R2 = 0.85 and ensured the construction of a scale-free co-expression network (Supplementary Material 3). In total, 13 distinct co-expression modules were identified and labeled with different colors (Fig. 4A-B).

According to the correlation analysis between module eigengenes and HAPH (Fig. 4B), the red module exhibited a strong negative correlation with HAPH (r = -0.64, P = 0.001). This finding was further substantiated by the Module Membership–Gene Significance (MM-GS) correlation analysis (r = 0.56, P < 0.001; Fig. 4D). In contrast, the brown module displayed a positive correlation with HAPH that did not reach statistical significance (r = 0.4, P = 0.06). Nevertheless, MM-GS analysis revealed a significant correlation between module membership and gene significance in this module (r = 0.32, P < 0.001; Fig. 4C), indicating that proteins occupying more central positions within the brown module exhibited stronger associations with HAPH, thereby reinforcing its biological relevance. Based on these findings, we applied a stringent dual-threshold screening strategy (MM > 0.8 and GS > 0.4), which resulted in the identification of 45 core proteins, including 34 from the red module and 11 from the brown module (Fig. 4C-D; Supplementary Material 4).

Functional enrichment analysis of core proteins in the module

We next performed GO, KEGG, and Reactome enrichment analyses on the 45 core proteins identified in the red and brown modules. GO analysis revealed enrichment in immune responses and complement-related pathways (BP); collagen-containing ECM, secretory granule lumen, and endoplasmic reticulum membranes (CC); as well as ECM structural constituents, glycosaminoglycan binding, and peptidase activity (MF) (Fig. 5A). KEGG pathway analysis indicated that these proteins were enriched in the complement and coagulation cascades, ferroptosis, cytoskeleton organization in muscle cells, ECM–receptor interaction, and TGF-β signaling pathway (Fig. 5B). Reactome analysis showed enrichment in the complement cascade, regulation of IGF transport and uptake by IGFBPs, and post-translational protein phosphorylation (Fig. 5C).

GSEA analysis

To ensure the biological reliability of the dataset underlying the preceding analyses, we further employed GSEA as an orthogonal validation approach. By examining the enrichment significance of known HAPH-related pathways, the capacity of the dataset to capture disease-related characteristics was validated. The results showed that, compared with the control group, the angiogenesis and epithelial-mesenchymal transition pathways were significantly upregulated in the HAPH group, whereas oxidative phosphorylation, heme metabolism, and protein secretion pathways were significantly downregulated (Fig. 6A-B), consistent with the pathological process of HAPH, thereby validating the biological reliability of the dataset.

Selection of candidate protein biomarkers and validation

Differential expression analysis provided information on changes at the single-protein level, whereas WGCNA highlighted network hub proteins with high connectivity. To identify the most reliable candidate molecules for clinical validation, we integrated the results from DEP analysis and WGCNA and identified 23 overlapping proteins (Fig. 7A; Table 2). Pathway annotation of these core proteins using the KEGG and Reactome databases revealed that THBS2, THBS3, TF, FBN1, and CP were mapped to critical pathways, including ECM-receptor interaction, PI3K-Akt signaling, HIF-1 signaling, TGF-β signaling, and ferroptosis, where they served as hubs connecting different pathways. AP1B1 was annotated to the lysosomal pathway, suggesting its involvement in organelle homeostasis (KEGG) (Fig. 7B; Supplementary Material 5). Additionally, LOXL1, IGFBP7, FBN1, CP, and TF were implicated in biological processes such as IGF transport and IGFBPs uptake, post-translational protein phosphorylation, iron uptake and transport, and elastic fiber formation. THBS2 and THBS3 were involved in the PDGF signaling pathway, whereas AP1B1 was associated with Nef-mediated downregulation of MHC class I complex cell surface expression (Reactome) (Fig. 7C; Supplementary Material 6).

Based on the biological function annotation results and a comprehensive literature review, THBS2, LOXL1, IGFBP7, POSTN, and FBN1 were ultimately selected for validation in an independent cohort. The ELISA validation results demonstrated significant differences in the expression levels of THBS2 (P = 0.003), LOXL1 (P = 0.013), and POSTN (P = 0.027) between the HAPH group and the control group (Fig. 7D-H; Supplementary Material 7).

To further evaluate the clinical relevance of these biomarkers, we analyzed the associations between plasma levels of the three proteins and mPAP. The results showed that both THBS2 (r = 0.389, 95% CI: 0.034–0.657, P = 0.033; Fig. 7I) and LOXL1 (r = 0.457, 95% CI: 0.115–0.701, P = 0.011; Fig. 7J) were significantly positively correlated with mPAP. POSTN exhibited a positive correlation trend but did not reach statistical significance (r = 0.335, 95% CI: -0.028-0.621, P = 0.070; Fig. 7K). These findings suggest that THBS2 and LOXL1 may serve as potential biomarkers that reflect pulmonary hemodynamic burden in HAPH.

Discussion

This study investigated indigenous individuals residing at elevations ranging from 2800 to 3800 m in the Pamir Plateau (Kizilsu, Xinjiang). Of the > 1400 proteins detected, 123 exhibited significant differences between the HAPH group and the control group (DEP); 45 hub proteins were identified as significantly associated with HAPH (WGCNA). The majority of proteins with increased abundance in HAPH were associated with ECM remodeling and angiogenesis. In contrast, those with decreased abundance were involved in reduced enzyme activity, iron metabolism, and inflammatory responses. By integrating dual screening criteria from DEP analysis and WGCNA, we identified 23 overlapping proteins. These proteins participate in pathways such as ECM-receptor interaction, PI3K-Akt, HIF-1, TGF-β signaling, and ferroptosis. Subsequently, we selected five of these proteins for further validation. ELISA validation in an independent cohort demonstrated significant upregulation of THBS2, LOXL1, and POSTN in HAPH patient plasma (P < 0.05). Notably, plasma levels of THBS2 and LOXL1 correlated positively with mPAP. This finding suggests their potential as biomarkers that reflect pulmonary hemodynamic load in HAPH.

Detection of known mechanistic changes

We observed significant downregulation of oxidative phosphorylation-related proteins and upregulation of angiogenesis- and epithelial-mesenchymal transition-associated proteins in the HAPH group, consistent with the pathological mechanisms reported for HAPH in other high-altitude populations. Specifically, hypoxia-induced inhibition of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation leads to energy imbalance and abnormal proliferation and apoptosis of pulmonary artery endothelial cells (PAECs), and consequently elevates pulmonary arterial pressure [6]. Notably, proteins involved in ECM remodeling, including those participating in ECM organization, degradation, collagen fibril cross-linking, and elastic fiber formation, were significantly upregulated. Both ECM-receptor interaction and TGF-β/Smad signaling pathways were identified as critical pathways associated with HAPH. These pathways represent important topics in plateau medicine [6]. Under hypoxic conditions, hypoxia-inducible factor-α (HIF-α) serves as a core transcription factor that regulates multiple signaling pathways, including ECM-receptor interaction, TGF-β/Smad, and PI3K/Akt. This results in ECM accumulation, suppressed degradation, and inflammatory responses, ultimately driving pulmonary vascular remodeling [21]. The involvement of TGF-β signaling in HAPH pathogenesis has been demonstrated in numerous animal studies [22, 23]. Although interspecies differences may limit translational applicability [24], studies demonstrating that Halofuginone ameliorates HAPH symptoms in rats via TGF-β1/Smad pathway inhibition suggest that this pathway represents a promising therapeutic target [25].

Novel findings

We observed elevated plasma THBS2 levels in HAPH patients and further validated this finding using plasma ELISA in an independent cohort. THBS2 is a secreted glycoprotein belonging to the ECM protein family, primarily involved in connective tissue development, collagen fibril formation, and vascular injury repair [26]. THBS2 has been shown to directly enhance fibroblast proliferation and migration [27], and its mRNA upregulation has been reported in the pulmonary vasculature of hypoxia-induced rats [28]. Mechanistic studies further demonstrated that THBS2 activates the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 signaling axis, thereby regulating ECM-receptor interactions and resulting in abnormal collagen deposition that promotes fibrogenesis [26]. In a study of 196 PH patients, Dittrich et al. reported elevated plasma THBS2 levels that correlated with right ventricular dysfunction and hemodynamic deterioration; THBS2 also demonstrated superior prognostic ability compared to NT-proBNP [29]. In contrast, Harbaum et al. reached an opposing conclusion, finding that THBS2 (OR, 0.83; 95% CI, 0.74–0.94) exhibited a marked protective effect in Group 1 PH patients [30]. Unlike the established cardiotoxicity of THBS1, evidence suggests that impaired THBS2 activity may disrupt cardiovascular homeostasis [26], as THBS2 knockout mice display enhanced angiogenic capacity [31]. Although our findings appear more consistent with Dittrich et al., given the bidirectional regulatory nature of THBS2, whether its elevation represents a protective compensatory response or a pathogenic injury signal in HAPH remains to be elucidated.

To our knowledge, this study represents the first report of elevated plasma LOXL1 levels in HAPH patients. LOXL1 belongs to the lysyl oxidase family and is directly involved in elastic fiber formation and collagen cross-linking, representing one of the key mechanisms underlying pulmonary vascular remodeling [32]. Nave et al. demonstrated that LOXL1 is hypoxia-inducible in pulmonary artery adventitial fibroblasts, whereas no similar upregulation was observed in pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs) [33], indicating cell type-specific expression. In the same study, LOX expression was upregulated in lung tissues of hypoxia-induced PH mice, whereas LOXL1 levels remained unchanged. In contrast, Xia et al. reported that both LOX and LOXL1 mRNA and protein levels were elevated in hypoxia-induced rat pulmonary artery tissues, with increased enzymatic activity, suggesting that LOXL1 can be induced by hypoxia in this species [34]. These discrepancies may be attributable to model selection. Under identical hypobaric hypoxic conditions, the same genes exhibit differential expression patterns between mouse and rat lung tissues, with rats displaying more pronounced pulmonary vascular remodeling responses to hypoxia [24]. Although the specific regulatory mechanisms of LOXL1 in pulmonary vessels remain incompletely understood, evidence suggests that inhibition of lysyl oxidase activity may exert beneficial effects during hypoxia-induced pulmonary vascular remodeling [35]. Given their pharmacological tractability, lysyl oxidases represent attractive targets for mechanistic investigation and therapeutic development in pulmonary vascular diseases.

Increased plasma POSTN levels are of note given its multiple synergistic interactions and feedback mechanisms with hypoxia, particularly involving HIF1α and tropomyosin receptor kinase B (TrkB) [36]. In PAECs, hypoxia upregulates POSTN and TrkB expression while promoting brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) production. This enhances HIF1α expression and establishes a positive feedback loop centered on HIF1α. This process further upregulates the vasoactive peptide ET-1 and the angiogenic factor vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), driving vascular remodeling. Elevated POSTN levels have been observed not only in lung tissue homogenates, pulmonary arteries, and PAECs from patients with Group 3 PH due to hypoxic lung disease [37], but also in rat PASMCs exposed to hypoxia [38]. Nie et al. identified POSTN as both a marker and mediator of pulmonary vascular remodeling in hypoxic lung disease [37], while Fang et al., through in vivo and in vitro studies, established POSTN as a crucial gene associated with hypoxic pulmonary hypertension [39]. Our finding of elevated plasma POSTN levels in HAPH patients further supports its utility as a biomarker for Group 3 PH. Although POSTN expression is also detected in the neointimal layer of remodeled pulmonary arteries in Group 1 PH patients [40], researchers have proposed that the anatomical localization of POSTN expression may be associated with PH etiology, as POSTN is most abundantly expressed in the distal vascular endothelium in Group 3 PH [36]. Currently, novel pharmacological tools targeting POSTN are under development [39, 41], and its potential utility as a biomarker for HAPH warrants further investigation in prospective cohorts.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the study cohort comprised HAPH patients exclusively from Kizilsu; the single-population design and relatively small sample size constitute inherent limitations. Second, some participants had received medication before enrollment, which may have influenced protein expression levels. Additionally, due to data collection constraints, comprehensive right ventricular parameters were not systematically assessed, limiting our ability to fully elucidate the relationship between candidate biomarkers and right heart function.

Nevertheless, several circulating proteins and pathways identified here have been previously implicated in the HAPH pathogenesis, supporting the validity of our findings. However, the specific expression of THBS2, LOXL1, and POSTN has not been observed in proteomic studies of HAPH patients from the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau [9] or in non-indigenous HAPH patients from the Pamir Plateau [42]. This discrepancy may reflect differences in population genetics, altitude gradients [43], and protein selection criteria [44]. Notably, several key proteins identified in this study are also highly expressed in other pulmonary hypertension subtypes; importantly, targeted interventions against these molecules have been shown to significantly ameliorate pathological phenotypes. These observations suggest that despite distinct initiating factors, common therapeutic targets may exist across PH subtypes. Accordingly, the findings from this HAPH study hold translational potential for other forms of PH. However, given the heterogeneity of the disease microenvironment, the clinical generalizability of these findings warrants further validation in larger-scale cohorts. Future studies will aim to evaluate the robustness of these candidate biomarkers in expanded cohorts and enhance echocardiographic assessment to establish correlations between circulating protein levels and right ventricular structure and function.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates a significant association between ECM remodeling and HAPH in the indigenous population of Kizilsu. The interactions between ECM and its receptors, along with the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway, play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of HAPH. Notably, ECM remodeling-related proteins THBS2, LOXL1, and POSTN have the potential as biomarkers and therapeutic targets for HAPH.
