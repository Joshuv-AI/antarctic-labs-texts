A discrete element model for rock pressure sensitivity and stress-driven contact stiffness evolution
Authors: Cong Wang, Yong Zhao, Yongquan Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With the rapid development of deep buried traffic tunnels, underground space utilization, and global energy resource extraction, the scale of deep underground engineering continues to expand. These trends place increasing demands on understanding the mechanical behavior of rock under high in-situ stress conditions. However, traditional discrete element models, constrained by constant stiffness assumptions, often over-linearize the compaction stage and have difficulty capturing tangent modulus evolution across confining pressures. To address this limitation, this study integrated triaxial test data for multiple lithologies, including granite, slate, and sandstone, and identified a robust exponential relationship between the tangent modulus in the compaction stage and mean stress. Building on this observation, a variable stiffness parallel bond model was developed. The model established a real-time mapping between contact stiffness and contact stress and incorporated a three-state judgment mechanism that enables adaptive stiffness updating. Furthermore, based on the theoretical correlation of stress states, a multi-confining-pressure calibration strategy was introduced. By adopting a method of parameter selection from multiple benchmark conditions combined with interpolation verification, this approach effectively resolves the non-uniqueness issue inherent in micro–macro parameter mapping. Validation using triaxial compression tests on carbonaceous slate over a wide confining pressure range demonstrated that the proposed model can accurately reproduce the morphology of full stress–strain curves. The relative error in peak strength remains within a small margin. The model also systematically captured key pressure-sensitive responses, including the shortening of the compaction stage, peak strength enhancement, and the brittle-ductile transition. Overall, the proposed model provides a high-fidelity numerical tool for predicting rock behavior in deep environments.

Introduction

In deep underground engineering under high in-situ stress, excavation and support activities cause substantial stress redistribution. This redistribution significantly alters the mechanical environment of the surrounding rock. Understanding how the stress state regulates the responses of rock masses is a focal point in stability analysis1. A systematic characterization of the pressure sensitivity and nonlinear mechanical behavior of rock is essential for predicting engineering hazards, such as rockbursts and large deformations. It also establishes a foundation for developing high-fidelity numerical models.

Classical studies2 have shown that intact rock under uniaxial loading exhibits a “plastic-elastic–plastic” evolution prior to reaching its peak strength. The resulting stress–strain curve features a typical S-shape. During the compaction stage, the tangent elastic modulus (hereafter “tangent modulus”) strengthens markedly with increasing axial stress σ12. Under triaxial conditions, the confining pressure σ3 influences both deformation and failure behavior3. As σ3 increases, elastic parameters increase nonlinearly4. Failure mode transitions from tension-shear mixed failure to shear-dominated failure5. Residual strength and dilation angle can be described by logarithmic or exponential functions, respectively6. Cohesion and friction angle exhibit logarithmic increase and linear decrease with σ37. However, the mechanical behavior of deep rock masses is governed by the coupled action of σ1 and σ3. Existing studies are largely conducted under constant confining pressure, and nonlinear responses in the compaction stage are often overly linearized. A quantitative model that captures the evolution of the tangent modulus with the stress state remains lacking. This gap limits accurate characterization of rock deformation and failure under high confining pressures.

The discrete element method (DEM), with its ability to resolve mesoscale mechanisms, serves as a key multiscale simulation tool in rock and soil mechanics8. The conventional parallel-bond model (PB, Linear Parallel-Bond Model) typically assumes constant contact stiffness9,10. Moreover, the mapping between macroscopic properties and microscopic parameters is not unique. Parameters calibrated under a single confining pressure—commonly under uniaxial conditions—cannot be reliably generalized to other stress states11. Accurate reproduction of full stress–strain curves across confining pressures remains challenging.

Although improved DEM formulations have been developed to introduce nonlinear stiffness, existing approaches still exhibit major limitations. First, geometry-based nonlinear contact models12,13, such as the Hertz-Mindlin model, are primarily used for unbonded granular materials (e.g., sands or granular materials). Their normal stiffness varies nonlinearly with contact deformation. However, the linkage between micro-parameters and macro-mechanical behavior is indirect. As a result, calibration requires extensive trial-and-error. Moreover, the approach is not well-suited for bonded rock materials. Second, some approaches based on mesoscale geometric reconstruction14,15 incorporate explicit fractures via CT scanning or statistical generation, altering initial particle arrangements and contact states to simulate compaction behavior. However, fracture geometry parameters (density, orientation, aperture) are difficult to measure accurately and impose heavy calibration burdens. Model responses also exhibit high sensitivity to these fracture parameters. Consequently, the applicability of such methods across confining pressures and their practical scalability in engineering contexts still requires thorough validation.

To address these limitations, this study proposed the Variable Stiffness Parallel-Bond Model (VSPB) and a multi-confining-pressure joint calibration framework. First, triaxial test data for multiple lithologies were synthesized to establish a functional relationship between the tangent modulus and stress state. Second, this macroscopic relationship was mapped to a mesoscale equation linking contact stiffness to contact stress. A staged contact-parameter updating mechanism was developed to quantify pressure-sensitive behavior. Third, a multi-pressure calibration strategy was designed. “Curve similarity, peak strength, and peak strain” served as multi-objective criteria. A strategy of “parameter selection from multiple benchmark conditions + interpolation to other confining pressures within the range” was adopted to assess generalization capability, thereby mitigating the non-uniqueness limitation of micro–macro parameter mapping.

The results of triaxial compression tests on carbonaceous slate demonstrated that the VSPB model accurately reproduces nonlinear deformation in the compaction stage and pressure-sensitive responses across confining pressures. The model provides a high-precision numerical tool for stability analysis of surrounding rock in deep engineering environments.

Analysis of rock pressure-sensitive characteristics

Constant confining pressure

As shown in Fig. 1, the deformation and failure of rocks of the same lithology under constant confining pressure (σ3 = const) are progressive and mutually dependent23. During this process, the accumulation of microcracks and pores ultimately leads to macroscopic failure. The mechanical responses of each stage are summarized below:

Stage I (Compaction): The curve exhibits a concave shape. With continued axial loading, microcracks close and material density increases, manifested as an increase in curve slope23. The overall curvature of this stage is positively correlated with initial porosity and microcrack density. Sandstone samples under uniaxial compression achieve a maximum closure strain εc of 0.70% (Fig. 3), significantly higher than the 0.35% observed for granite.

Stage II (Linear Elasticity): The curve becomes nearly linear. Under the same confining pressure, the ranking of elastic modulus is: Granite > Slate ≈ Phyllite > Sandstone.

Stage III (Pre-Peak Plasticity): The curve becomes convex, and the rate of stress increase gradually declines. At the peak, both the number and propagation length of microcracks elevate sharply, eventually forming an explicit failure plane. Under identical confining pressure, the ranking of peak strength follows: Granite > Slate ≈ Phyllite > Sandstone. Slate samples show considerable scatter in peak strength, likely due to variations in the inclination and density of discontinuous interfaces.

Stage IV (Post-Peak Plasticity): The curve exhibits a continuous decline. Macroscopic failure surfaces develop, leading to a loss of load-bearing capacity. However, the specimen retains partial structural integrity.

Stage V (Residual Stage): The curve approaches a plateau. Despite the presence of through-going fractures, the specimen can still sustain load. Under the same confining pressure σ3, granite shows markedly lower residual strength compared with the other lithologies.

Varying confining pressures

As illustrated in Fig. 2, confining pressure significantly influences the mechanical responses across all stages, and the degree of sensitivity varies by stage. At higher confining pressures, the stress–strain curves collectively envelop those obtained under lower confining pressures. The elastic modulus, peak strength, peak strain, and residual strength all increase with confining pressure σ3.

Stage I (Compaction): The increased σ3 promotes the closure of microcracks and initial pores, substantially reducing the compaction-to-pre-peak strain ratio Rcp. The overall curvature is negatively correlated with σ3. Among the samples examined, sandstone-Ⅰ exhibits the largest reduction in Rcp within 0–10 MPa (about 62.1%), markedly exceeding the reductions in granite (about 26.2%) and slate (about 39.3%). This reflects the higher porosity and more pervasive fissure networks in sandstone, which lead to its high sensitivity to confining pressure. Once σ3 reaches a critical threshold, initial pores are largely closed. Macroscopic deformation transitions from nonlinear to linear behavior. Under high confining pressure, dense rocks may even bypass the compaction stage entirely (e.g., Slate-Ⅲ).

Stage II (Linear Elasticity): The slope of the stress–strain curve shows a positive correlation with σ3. However, the rate of stiffening diminishes with increasing σ3. This indicates a “saturation” effect in the enhancement of elastic modulus at high confining pressures.

Stages III-IV (Plastic Deformation): Confining pressure suppresses crack propagation, enabling greater plastic deformation prior to macroscopic failure. The plastic strain range (εres-εe) expands with increasing σ3, while the crack propagation rate diminishes. As shown in Fig. 4, both peak strength and peak strain increase with σ3 across all lithologies. For instance, the peak strength of granite-Ⅰ increases from 89 MPa at σ3 = 0 MPa to 308 MPa at σ3 = 40 MPa, an increase of approximately 246%.

Stage V (Residual Stage): Experimental results demonstrate that the residual strength (σres) and residual strength ratio (Rrep) exhibit a pronounced upward trend with σ3 (Fig. 5). This behavior arises from the geometric confinement imposed by σ3 and the consequent enhancement of frictional resistance along the rupture plane, allowing the rock to maintain substantial load-bearing capacity post-failure. Between 0 and 10 MPa, Rrep increases significantly in all nine samples examined (granite-Ⅰ shows an increase of up to 538.3%). Beyond this interval, the increase slows down. Under low confining pressures, friction along the rupture surface is insufficient to sustain load, and residual strength may approach zero (e.g., Sandstone-Ⅱ). When σ3 exceeds a critical value, a characteristic brittle-ductile transition emerges24. The critical confining pressure varies across lithologies.

In summary, this study defines rock stress sensitivity as the systematic variation in the staged mechanical responses under different stress conditions (confining pressure σ3 and axial stress σ1). These responses include nonlinear compaction deformation (Stage I), confining-pressure-enhanced elastic modulus (Stage II), increased plastic deformation capacity (Stages III–IV), and the confining-pressure dependence of residual strength (Stage V).

Stress dependence of the tangent modulus and its quantitative model

As illustrated in Fig. 6, elastic parameters are characterized by the secant modulus (Es), average modulus (Eav), and tangent modulus (Et). Es reflects the overall deformation from initial loading to a specified stress state. Eav is typically used to estimate the slope of the quasi-linear segment of the curve, suitable for rapid engineering evaluations. However, it is insufficient for capturing instantaneous nonlinear behavior. Et describes the instantaneous stiffness at a given point on the curve, representing the local stiffness evolution within both the compaction and pre-peak nonlinear stages. Accordingly, Et is adopted as the primary indicator in the subsequent analysis.Fig. 6Characterization of the three moduli on the full stress–strain curve23.

Construction of the variable-stiffness parallel bond (VSPB) model

Conventional parallel bond (PB) model and its limitations

In PFC3D-based granular discrete element models (DEMs), the macroscopic mechanical behavior of materials is indirectly represented by the contact mechanics between particles. The PB model characterizes the complex mechanical responses between particles through a dual-spring system (Fig. 8). This model can effectively capture tensile failure, shear slip, and bending-moment transfer within granular systems. It operates under two distinct transmission mechanisms: first, the composite load-transfer mechanism in the bonded state (Fig. 8b). Normal and shear loads are jointly carried by the linear springs (knl, ksl) and the bond springs (knb, ksb). The bonded components provide bending stiffness. Second, the linear load-transfer mechanism after bond breakage (Fig. 8c). The bond springs cease to transmit forces (knb = ksb = 0). Principle and development workflow of the VSPB model

Mapping equation between contact stiffness and contact stress

Building on the macroscopic observation that the tangent modulus Et increases nonlinearly with the mean stress σm, this relationship is transferred to the mesoscale contact level by establishing a mapping between the contact stiffness and the local contact stress σc. In granular DEMs, the macroscopic tangent modulus Et is approximately linearly related to the mesoscopic contact stiffness (assuming a fixed particle size distribution and structural configuration). Accordingly, it is assumed that the mesoscopic contact stiffness follows an exponential strengthening relationship with respect to contact stress. Admittedly, the actual physical mechanism driving this macroscopic stiffening is the progressive closure of initial microcracks and pores. However, explicitly reconstructing the complex spatial geometry of all multi-scale micro-defects within a DEM assembly requires a prohibitively massive number of particles, which is computationally unacceptable for large-scale engineering applications. Therefore, the VSPB model is proposed as a practical equivalent homogenization approach. Rather than explicitly meshing these flaws, we apply a stress-driven hardening rule at the contact level to phenomenologically mimic the macroscopic densification induced by microcrack closure. This localized phenomenological strategy ensures high computational efficiency while effectively capturing the pressure-sensitive behavior of the rock matrix, laying a foundation for future multi-scale rock mass simulations coupled with macroscopic Discrete Fracture Networks. To reflect particle contact effects, the stiffness parameter updating mechanism is implemented via a callback function as follows:

First, contact state traversal: iterate over all particle contacts, collecting relevant parameters such as bond status, current contact stress, and contact surface geometry.

Second, state grouping: classify contacts into State I-III according to the criteria in Table 2.

Third, coordinated parameter updating: based on the contact state and stress environment, update the four stiffness parameters in real time according to the equations and Table 2. If σc ≤ 0 (tensile stress or contact opening), the stiffness of the bond spring is set to zero, avoiding nonphysical reverse softening. Transition from State II to State III is triggered when the local contact stress drops below the threshold (σc ≤ γσcpre). Although implemented as an abrupt numerical transition (by applying the degradation coefficient ξ), this mechanism possesses rigorous justifications at both macroscopic and microscopic scales. Macroscopically, as demonstrated by our extensive investigation of triaxial tests across various lithologies, intact rocks exhibit highly variable strain domains for the pre-peak strain-hardening and post-peak strain-softening stages. These discrepancies in strain ranges become particularly pronounced under high confining pressures. The introduction of this abrupt state transition—governed by the parameters γ and ξ—provides the indispensable algorithmic degrees of freedom required to independently regulate the lengths of these macroscopic strain stages, thereby accurately capturing complex pressure-dependent behaviors. Microscopically, the failure of quasi-brittle rock is characterized by discrete, sudden micro-ruptures rather than continuous yielding. When a mesoscopic bond breaks and the local stress unloads (State II to State III), the interlocking asperities on the newly formed rough microcrack surfaces rapidly degrade, shear off, or slip. This instantaneous loss of structural interlocking and the associated sudden release of strain energy (frequently recorded as acoustic emissions) necessitate an abrupt reduction in local load-bearing stiffness. Therefore, this mathematically engineered jump ensures both macroscopic phenomenological fidelity and microscopic physical consistency.

Fourth, update frequency optimization: Define a timestep parameter freq. Given the gradual stress evolution in quasi-static loading, updating stiffness at every numerical step is computationally superfluous. Reducing freq appropriately can yield smoother stress–strain curves.

Response characteristics and innovations of the VSPB model

The VSPB model achieves a refined representation of rock stress-sensitive behavior through two technical advancements:

First, stress-driven stiffness mapping: the macroscopic “tangent modulus-mean stress” relationship is transferred to the mesoscopic contact level. By introducing the stiffness ratio coefficient η and the linear-bond stiffness ratio λ, exponential functional relationships (Eq. (5)) are established between the four contact stiffness components (knl, ksl, knb, ksb) and contact stress σc. Second, three-state parameter updating: based on the bond condition and stress evolution, particle contacts are classified into three states (State I-III), with differentiated stiffness updating strategies applied (Table 2).

Case verification

To ensure the accuracy of the DEM and contact parameter selection, a standardized workflow includes: (1) Contact model selection: the microscale constitutive model is chosen according to material characteristics. (2) Parameter calibration: contact parameters are iteratively adjusted via a trial-and-error procedure. (3) Experimental validation: the simulated mechanical response is compared with laboratory test data to verify consistency.

Triaxial compression tests

Following the International Society for Rock Mechanics (ISRM) recommended procedures25, standard specimens were prepared from carbonaceous slate collected at the tunnel site. Specimen preparation included cutting, coring, and end-face grinding. Specimens without any defects were preferentially selected to minimize the influence of structural heterogeneity on deformation and failure mechanisms. First, under stress-controlled conditions, mean pressure was applied at a rate of 0.1 MPa/s until the target confining pressure was reached. Then, the confining pressure was maintained constant, and axial loading was applied under displacement control at 0.02 mm/s until failure. Six compression tests were conducted with confining pressures ranging from 0 to 50 MPa at an increment (Δσ3) of 10 MPa. A 3D DEM with a high aspect ratio (height/diameter = 2:1) was constructed using PFC3D. Mineralogical crystallography or cracks were not explicitly reconstructed; instead, an equivalent particle assembly was employed to represent the bulk material response. The loading path mirrored that of the laboratory tests.

Analysis of mesoscopic mechanisms and macroscopic response features

Based on the comparison of stress–strain curves, this section analyzes how the VSPB model reproduces the nonlinear deformation and stress-sensitive characteristics of rock from a mesoscopic perspective. According to the stress–strain features illustrated in Fig. 2, the deformation process is divided into three stages: compaction and elastic deformation, crack initiation and propagation, and crack coalescence and failure.

Compaction and elastic deformation (Stages I and II)

At the mesoscopic level, during the initial loading, almost all contacts within the specimen are in State I. The bonds are intact, and linear and cemented springs share the load. The contact stress σc increases with loading, triggering the stiffness update rules in Eq. (5). As a result, the four stiffness components (knl, ksl, knb, ksb) evolve synergistically toward their respective upper limits. Under higher confining pressures, the initial contact stress is larger, and the initial contact stiffness increases more rapidly toward its theoretical upper bound, reducing the available space for stiffness growth.

At the macroscopic level, the collective nonlinear increase of contact stiffness causes the tangent modulus Et to increase with σm, producing concavity in the stress–strain curve. At higher confining pressures, a large number of contacts simultaneously reach high stiffness, elevating the initial tangent modulus and its growth rate, which reduces the concavity in the compaction segment. Under very high confining pressures, the initial contact stiffness is already close to its upper limit, and the compaction stage almost disappears.

Crack initiation and propagation (Stages III and IV)

Micromechanically, when individual contacts reach the strength criterion (controlled by\bar c

and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{\phi}$$\end{document}), they transition from State I to State II. Bonds break, and microcracks form. The load is then transferred to the linear springs, and the total stiffness experiences its first reduction. When the contact stress σc decreases to γσcpre, the contacts enter State III. During this stage, linear springs begin to soften, resulting in an additional drop in total stiffness. Under high confining pressures, the stiffness evolution path of individual contacts shifts upward. The increased normal stress on the cemented springs raises their failure threshold. The stiffness of linear springs at bond failure becomes larger, which slows the rate of total stiffness decay.

Macroscopically, numerous contacts in the stress concentration zones successively transition to States II and III, forming a distributed microcrack network. This results in a continuous reduction of Et prior to the peak and a reduction in load-bearing capacity after the peak. Under low confining pressures, contacts quickly reach State III, and knl |fail is relatively small. Consequently, total stiffness rapidly drops, demonstrating brittle failure. Under high confining pressures, the micromechanical mechanisms described above moderate stiffness loss. Even with many contacts in State III, the rate of total stiffness reduction remains low. This leads to a gradual post-peak stress decline and a long-lasting stress plateau, indicative of ductile failure.

Crack coalescence and failure (Stage V)

At the microscale, microcracks continue to propagate and eventually form through-going fracture surfaces. Bonded contacts completely fail and transition to pure frictional load transfer. The residual strength is governed by the normal stiffness of the linear springs and the friction coefficient μ.

At the macroscale, a large number of contacts within the fracture zones lose their bonding and can only sustain load through friction. Stress decreases to a residual plateau with minor fluctuations. The specimen undergoes overall failure and enters a sliding-dominated deformation mode. As confining pressure elevates, the normal stress on the fracture surface also increases, leading to a notable enhancement of residual strength.

The VSPB model, through the evolution of contact states (State I → II → III) and adaptive stiffness regulation, ensures real-time updating of contact stiffness with σc and smooth transitions between stage-specific stiffness update strategies. As shown in Fig. 10(b), using the physically meaningful parameter set { knb(0), knb(max), β, η, λ, γ, ξ}, the model systematically reproduces confining pressure-dependent features, such as modulus enhancement, strength increase, compaction-stage attenuation, and brittle-to-ductile transition.

Model applicability and prospects

Regarding practical engineering applicability, the VSPB modifications fundamentally influence the mechanical behavior far beyond the initial pre-yield stage. Because rock deformation is strictly sequential, accurately simulating the nonlinear compaction establishes a structurally realistic internal stress state and contact fabric. This preceding densification is the physical prerequisite for capturing the critical confining-pressure dependency of the subsequent macroscopic elastic modulus. Furthermore, integrated with the state-transition criteria γ and ξ, the model continuously adapts both deformation and post-peak strength parameters in response to localized stress states. In the analysis of deep-buried tunnels—where surrounding rocks are subjected to severe spatial stress gradients (from zero confinement at the boundary to extreme in-situ stresses)—the VSPB model provides the indispensable analytical capability to objectively predict stress-dependent macroscopic displacements and progressive localized failure zones.

While demonstrating satisfying applicability and robust interpolation capability within the calibrated confining pressure range, the current VSPB model setup requires further rigorous validation for true extrapolation—that is, predicting geomechanical responses under extreme conditions strictly beyond the calibration range (e.g., highly confined states where σ1 exceeds the maximum baseline pressure, or macroscopic tensile conditions where σ 1< 0).Additionally, the current model framework does not explicitly incorporate multi-field physical interactions, such as temperature gradients and pore fluid pressure.

Furthermore, regarding spatial scales, it should be acknowledged that the VSPB model fundamentally relies on a mathematical homogenization strategy for the initial microstructures. While this macro-to-micro equivalent approach is highly efficient for capturing the macroscopic tangent modulus evolution and pre-peak compaction behavior, it inherently homogenizes localized microstructural heterogeneities, which limits its ability to explicitly represent complex post-peak morphological mechanisms governed by localized micro-defects at the specimen scale. However, from the perspective of rock engineering, “intactness” is strictly relative to the observation scale. In large-scale rock mass environments, explicitly reconstructing tens of thousands of microscopic flaws using grain-scale approaches leads to computationally prohibitive bottlenecks. The VSPB model is specifically conceptualized as an efficient “relatively intact rock matrix.” Generally, the post-peak macroscopic failure and deformability of jointed rock masses are structurally controlled by large-scale discontinuities rather than the micro-cracks within the rock blocks. Therefore, a highly promising avenue for future improvement is to couple the VSPB contact model with explicit macro-scale Discrete Fracture Networks (DFN). By utilizing the VSPB model to efficiently reproduce the nonlinear response of the rock matrix and employing DFN to explicitly capture the geometric interactions of structural planes, this hybrid approach will organically bridge the gap between matrix deformation and structurally controlled post-peak failure, thereby providing a computationally feasible tool for large-scale engineering applications.

The VSPB model was developed. The model realized an exponential mapping between contact stiffness and contact stress. Using a three-state contact criterion (State I-III), four stiffness components were adaptively updated in a coordinated manner. A “multi-baseline parameter acquisition + generalization verification within the confining-pressure interval” strategy was proposed. It effectively mitigated the non-uniqueness problem in micromechanical-to-macroscopic parameter mapping.

For carbonaceous slate over a wide confining pressure range (0–50 MPa), numerical simulations accurately reproduced the compaction-stage curve shape, with peak strength prediction errors below 5.27%. The model successfully reproduced confining pressure-dependent characteristics, such as modulus enhancement, strength increase, compaction-stage attenuation, and the brittle-to-ductile transition.

It should be noted that the VSPB model is primarily applicable to rock types characterized by significant initial porosity, micro-fracture development, or a distinct nonlinear compaction stage, such as slate, sandstone, and certain phyllites. For extremely dense hard rocks or intact rocks that exhibit purely linear-elastic characteristics and negligible pressure sensitivity, the traditional constant-stiffness PB model remains a more computationally efficient and suitable choice. Therefore, the necessity of adopting the VSPB model should be evaluated with caution based on the specific lithological characteristics and the significance of the nonlinear deformation stage in the target engineering environment.
