Exploring the association of poor social support and loneliness with cardiovascular history among lebanese residents: insights into myocardial infarction and stroke: a cross-sectional approach
Authors: Fatima Sharaf, Reem Khairallah, Jad Osta, Jad Ali Hassan, Iffat Ayoub, Hiba Deek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular diseases (CVDs), including myocardial infarction (MI) and stroke, remain among the most prevalent causes of global morbidity and mortality. Evidence suggests that psychosocial variables, especially loneliness and social support, play an important role in cardiovascular health. However, this relationship has not been studied in Lebanon.ObjectiveThis study aims to assess the association of insufficient social support and loneliness with cardiovascular history specifically, history of MI and stroke.MethodsA cross-sectional study that involved a self-administered questionnaire from community participants. Data were collected from people aged 50 and above with a mean age of 60.15 (SD = 9.8) years. Data collection forms included sociodemographic characteristics, Charlson’s comorbidity index including cardiovascular history, The Medical Outcome Study Social Support Survey including its five domains and the Arabic translated version of the University of California Los Angeles Loneliness Scale.ResultsOut of the sample of 499 participants, the analysis showed that 89.6% of participants experienced high levels of loneliness, and poor levels of social support across all of the five domains of the scale. History of cardiovascular events was significantly associated with age (p < 0.001), gender (0.026), employment (p < 0.001) and marital status (p = 0.039) in addition to the living region. History of cardiovascular events was also associated with social (p < 0.001) and medical history (p < 0.001) of the participants. Loneliness was found to be significantly associated with acute cardiovascular history in the multivariate analysis (OR: 1.051, 95% CI: 1.021, 1.081, p = 0.001).ConclusionsLoneliness and low social support are strongly associated with poor cardiovascular outcomes, highlighting the need to address psychosocial factors in heart health care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-06025-y.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are of the most common causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide and represent an influential factor in the global health burden [1]. Based on this report, CVDs cause about 31% of all deaths worldwide primarily due to insufficient health care access and lifestyle-related risk factors (e.g., smoking, unhealthy diet and increased physical inactivity). In Lebanon as population grew older and with multiple comorbidities, the prevalence of CVDs has steadily increased over the last few years, especially among adults aged 50 years and older [2]. From this perspective, given the ageing of population, older people are at high risk for MI and stroke. However, a significant gap remains in the understanding of psychosocial determinants of these health outcomes in Lebanon, particularly for rapidly ageing populations.

Although the exact mechanisms are not well understood, a growing body of evidence indicates that psychosocial variables - loneliness and poor social support in particular - significantly influence cardiovascular health [3]. These in conjunction with the traditional risk factors primarily physiological (e.g. hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia) have been implicated in the development of CVD [4]. Psychosocial variables cannot be observed in every clinical encounter, but there is evidence to suggest they affect biological mechanisms directly related to heart disease, including inflammation, blood pressure, and stress reactivity [5]. Loneliness, defined as the subjective state of social isolation, has recently been recognized as an independent risk factor for MI and stroke [6]. Conversely, developing social support, defined as emotional and practical support that friends, family, and the community provide, has been shown to be associated with great improvement in mental and physical health outcome and even improved recovery after cardiovascular events [7]. In recent years many research studies on loneliness, social support and cardiovascular health have received greater attention. New studies have identified the association between low social support and high levels of loneliness with increased risk of heart disease and stroke [8]. People in an unsocialized state tend to have higher chronic stress levels and consequently increased blood pressure, increased cortisol and a dysfunctional immune response, all risk factors associated with cardiovascular events [9]. It has been observed that the acute effects of lonely people on cardiovascular events include elevated heart rate and inflammatory systemic conditions that may worsen pre-existing cardiovascular disease, to the risk for acute events such as myocardial infarction or stroke [10]. On the other hand, social support has been shown to mitigate stress by facilitating health-promoting behaviors, improving mental and physical health outcomes [11]. Higher perceived levels of social support were associated with lower cardiovascular-related mortality. Despite this increasing body of evidence, there have been very few studies investigating the relationship between loneliness and social support on cardiovascular health in Lebanon, including among older age groups that are prone to these psychosocial determinants of health [12]. Lebanese people are known for their current mix of individualism and collectivism based on their geographical location and their cultural background [13]. However, the factors of loneliness and social support were never studied with all the sociopolitical and economical changes that have been occurring in the country and challenging its people for the past five years [14]. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the association of poor social support and loneliness with history of acute cardiovascular events specifically of MI and stroke, in adults aged 50 years and older in Lebanon.

Methods

Design

This is a cross-sectional study that involved self-administered questionnaire of participants in the community. Data collection was conducted between January 27th and March 3rd, 2025 through convenience sampling of participants in the community and on social media platforms. Community participants were approached in public places of the community throughout the data collection period. Potential participants were notified about the study and invited to participate through completing the link provided through a QR code leading to the link with the consent and questionnaire. The same QR code was disseminated through social media platforms through pages related to the community and the research team acquaintances. There was no response rate calculated as it was not known who the link reached from the community.

Eligibility criteria and setting

Inclusion was based on age and living region. Those aged 50 years and above, irrespective of their nationality or gender, were included. Participants were resident of the Lebanese community.

Procedure

Ethical approval was secured from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Beirut Arab University (BAU) with the following approval code: 2024-H-218-HSC-M-0666. The ethics board reviews studies and grants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The data collection form was generated using Google Forms and a QR code with the tool link was distributed through social media platforms. A consent form preceded the data collection form where participants were informed about their freedom to participate or withdraw from the study at any point and their privacy and confidentiality of their data. Participants had the choice of continuing to the data collection form or terminate the survey at that point through an accept or decline options. The consent and the questionnaire were in the simple Arabic language that is understood by 5th graders. Sections A & B of the below form were developed for the current study while sections C & D were taken from the literature as indicated below (supplementary file A).

Sociodemographic data

This section gathered information on participants’ age, gender, nationality, occupation, education level, marital status, employment status, income, and social habits, including smoking and alcohol consumption.

Patient health information

Participants were asked to provide details about their health, including their past medical history, past surgical history, familial history, and home medications, if any were prescribed or consumed over the counter. Past medical history was gathered using the Charlson’s comorbidity index which has previously been shown to be a comprehensive summary of the comorbidities. The index provides a sum of the weight of each condition which can be evaluated against other variables [15]. Through this section, participants were categorized as having history of acute cardiovascular events (MI and stroke) or otherwise no CV history. Additionally, history of CVD was compared to the CV surgeries to rule out those that had undergone cardiovascular surgeries not related to cardiology such as correction of varicose veins. Additionally, participants that had undergone diagnostic studies such cardiac catheterization and showed normal results were categorized as those with non-CVD history. Therefore. The history of CVD versus no-CVD history was validated against the surgical history.

The Medical Outcome Study Social Support Survey (MOS-SSS)

This survey evaluated the perceived level of social support among participants. Developed as part of the Medical Outcomes Study, the MOS-SSS is designed to evaluate perceived social support across four subscales. These subscales are the emotional and informational support (8 items), which evaluate the perceived ability of empathy and the availability of advice, guidance, and information. Tangible support (4 items), shows the availability of material help or assistance with daily tasks. Affectionate support (3 items), shows the availability of love, closeness, and affection. Finally, Positive Social Interaction (4 items), shows availability of companionship for leisure activities. This 19-item scale allows individuals to assess their perceptions of available support, helping to elucidate how social networks influence feelings of loneliness [16]. The items are scored on a five-point Likert scale ranging between 0 “I find no one” and 4 “I find them always” with total scores ranging between 0 and 76 with highest scores indicating better social support. As for the detailed measures, scores of the emotional and informational support subscales range between 0 and 32 with scores below 12 indicating low level of support, scores between 13 and 28 indicating average levels and scores above 28 indicating high levels. In terms of tangible support and social interaction, scores lower than 6 indicate low levels of support, scores between 7 and 13 indicate average and scores higher than 14 indicate high levels of support. Finally, for the affectionate support subscale, scores up to 4 indicate low levels of support, scores ranging between 5 and 10 show average levels and scores above 11 indicate higher levels of support [17]. The psychometric properties of the scale demonstrated strong reliability with a Cronbach alpha of over 0.9 [16]. The scale had previously been translated to Arabic and validated in the Arabic setting in Sudan and showed excellent psychometric properties. Cronbach’s alpha for most subscales ranged from 0.86 to 0.94, indicating high internal consistency. The Affectionate Support subscale had a lower alpha of 0.65 but this was still considered acceptable for research purposes. These results confirm the Arabic version as a reliable tool for assessing social support in Arabic-speaking populations [18].

University of California Los Angeles Loneliness Scale (UCLA Loneliness Scale)

Originally developed in 1978 at the University of California, Los Angeles, the scale includes 20 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale: 1 = Never; 2 = Rarely; 3 = Sometimes; 4 = often in positively worded items and reversed in negatively worded items. The positively worded items are 1, 5, 6, 9, 10, 15, 16, 19 and 20. And the negatively worded items are 2, 3, 4, 7, 8, 11, 12, 13, 14, 17 and 18. Positive items are reverse-scored before summing to have all items in the same direction. The total then score ranges between 20 and 80 with higher scores indicating higher levels of loneliness. To categorize participants into being lonely versus not lonely, a cut-off score above 44 was adopted for the lonely category [19, 20]. The UCLA Loneliness Scale, known for its high reliability and validity, is widely used in psychology, healthcare, and social sciences to assess the association between loneliness and well-being [21]. The Arabic version of the scale had been evaluated for psychometric properties and was found to have good validity and reliability [22].

Sample size

The target sample size was 500 participants for the current study. This was calculated based on the estimated prevalence of health-related issues to a conservative scenario of 50% with a CI of 95% and a 5% error margin. This is also considering a 25% of drop out or incomplete data rate.

Data analysis

Univariate data were presented as means and standard deviations for continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Group comparison was done using the chi-square test for categorical variables and t-test for continuous variables. Significance level was set at less than 0.05 based on two-tailed tests and were unadjusted for multiplicity. All data were entered and analyzed using version 24 of the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS). Logistic regression was adopted to examine factors related with the history of acute cardiovascular events. The dependent variable was presence vs. absence of self-reported acute cardiovascular events, specifically, myocardial infarction and stroke. Independent variables considered for inclusion in the model included sociodemographic characteristics, medical profile and psychosocial scales (UCLA Loneliness score and MOS-SSS subscale). Multivariable logistic regression model included the variables that were statistically significant in the bivariate analyses (p < 0.05) or considered clinically relevant based on previous literature. Data from the literature included hypertension and diabetes in addition to the social factors such as social support and loneliness while the those yielded from the results of the current study included age, gender, employment and marital status in addition to medical history of CVA, heart failure, diabetes and depression. A number of models were carried out based on the accepted number of variables per model for the given sample size; which was reported to be a ratio of 1:45 [23]. The final model was established according to the figures that were yielded in the analysis with four variables.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics and medical profile of the study participants

A total of 512 participants completed the survey; however, after data cleaning and the removal of those who did not meet the inclusion criteria, 499 participants were included in the final analysis. The mean age of the sample was 60.15 (SD = 9.8) years with almost equal gender distribution. In terms of educational and occupational status, 40.7% held a university degree, 47.1% were employed with 175 participants (35.1%) receiving a minimal salary of less than $400. More than half of the participants were Lebanese (52.1%), resided in the capital city, Beirut (51.3%) and more than two-thirds were married (69.1%).

Regarding health behaviors, 238 participants (47.7%) were smokers, and 64 participants (12.8%) consumed alcohol. The primary caregiver for half the sample was the spouse. In terms of medical history, 87 participants (17.4%) had a history of surgery, while 71 participants (14.2%) had undergone cardiac surgery. Additionally, 227 participants (45.5%) had a family history of chronic diseases. A total of 385 participants (77.2%) were on daily medications, with 362 (72.5%) taking medications for physical illnesses and 21 (4.2%) on psychological medications. The medical profile revealed that 21 participants (4.2%) had cerebrovascular disease, 39 (7.8%) had congestive heart failure, and 45 (9.0%) had COPD or asthma. Hypertension was the most prevalent condition among half the sample (50.5%). The Charlson’s comorbidity index score was 2.19 (SD = 11.17). Social isolation findings among the study participants

The total MOS-SSS score was 30.82 (SD = 19.66) indicating low to moderate levels of social support. When looking at the different sub-scale scores, it was noted that the emotional, tangible, affectionate and interaction scores were 13.46 (SD = 8.77), 6.21 (SD = 4.28), 4.62 (SD = 3.54) and 6.51 (SD = 4.78) respectively, as indicated in Table 1. The individual scores of the subscales and the total score were significantly different among those having CV history and those not (Mean difference: -14.23, 95% CI: -18.58, -9.89; p < 0.001). The difference between the groups was also significant at every level of the subscales; low, average and high and every item of the scale, as indicated in Table 2.

Loneliness findings among the study participants

The mean loneliness score was 46.41 (SD = 10.05) indicating moderate scores (range 20–80) and high percentage (68.9%) of those feeling lonely, considering the cut-off score of 44. In terms of comparison between those with CVD history and those without, it was noted that the former had significantly higher scores on the loneliness scale when compared to the later as indicated in Table 1. Considering the association of psychological medication intake with the feeling of loneliness [24], a sensitivity analysis was conducted excluding the 4.2% population on these medications. The relation between loneliness and acute cardiovascular illness event remained statistically significant (p < 0.001) suggesting that the findings are robust. Additionally, when looking at each item of the loneliness scale, it was noted that those with CVD history significantly showed higher levels of loneliness when compared to their counterparts in all of the items except in items 3 and 4 which are negatively worded items. Details about the loneliness scale scores are presented in Table 3.

Significant associates of acute cardiovascular history

Older age, male gender and employment status were significantly associated with CV history. It was also noted that those living in the capital city of Lebanon, Beirut and those who were married were significantly less likely to have CVD history. Social habits such as smoking and alcohol consumption were less associated with CVD history when consumed. Surgical history and medication use were significantly more associated with history of acute cardiovascular events as were comorbidities such as congestive heart failure, renal insufficiency and dementia. However, those who were categorized to have no history of acute cardiovascular events had significantly higher rates of diabetes, depression, hypertension and family history of CVD as indicated in Table 1.

Logistic regression analysis was performed to outline the variables that were significantly associated with acute cardiovascular history in 83.2% of the whole sample at a multivariate level. The final model included age, gender, tangible score of the MOS-SSS and the UCLA scale. The model had a good fit to the data as indicated by the non-significant findings of the Homer- Lemeshow goodness of fit (x2 = 7.985, df = 8, p = 0.435) with a significant chi-square analysis (x2 = 73.827, df = 4, p < 0.001). The model explained a moderate variance of 0.226 indicating that the model is explaining 22% of the likelihood of having cardiovascular diseases with increased age, male gender, higher tangible scores on the MOSS and higher levels of loneliness. These variables showed low variance inflation factors reflecting no effect of collinearity on the regression estimates. Details of the logistic regression analysis are presented in Table 4.

Discussion

The purpose of the present study was to investigate the association of low social support and loneliness with cardiovascular outcomes in adult participants from urban areas of Lebanon. The main finding of the study shows that almost 90% of the study participants experiencing loneliness with a mean UCLA score of 54. 38 (SD = 11. 17). This score is higher than the established cut-off of 44 by the UCLA Loneliness Scale for assessment of the high level of loneliness [19, 20]. Loneliness among the adults with CVD aged more than 55 was also found to be significant in another study when compared to their younger counterparts. This was found in a recent study conducted in the United States [25]. This same finding was consistent with another study conducted also in the US which showed the significant correlation between CVD and loneliness even in middle aged adults with a mean of 42.39 years. This was found even after controlling for possible confounding factors such as age and marital status [26]. Marital status was found to be associated with history of acute cardiovascular events where those who were married where significantly less likely to have the history of these events. However, the analysis was inconclusive in terms of significance which contradicts findings of the literature [27]. These findings warrant further analysis in future studies that look at the impact of each variable on the main study outcome through prospective longitudinal designs.

The mean total score of the MOS-SSS was 30. 82 (SD = 19. 66), suggesting a low to moderate level of perceived social support to participants. These results share findings from other studies which have investigated the relationship between social support and health outcomes, and more particularly among individuals with chronic conditions such as cardiovascular diseases [28]. Moderate levels of social support have been shown to serve as a protective factor against stress, and also to promote treatment adherence, decrease depression levels, and better overall physical and mental health outcomes [16]. These results support the growing recognition of social support as a critical component in managing chronic illnesses and improving quality of life. Strengthening support networks and integrating psychosocial care into health services may contribute to improved patient outcomes and overall well-being.

The current study used the scoring scale presented by Zanini, Peixoto, Nakano [17] with a total of 76. When comparing these numbers to the literature, the scores had to be calculated based on the scoring system that was provided by other studies. Examples on these were those presented by Wang, Lau, Chow, Thompson, He [29] and Berard [30] who showed a total score of 100. In both, the scores where somehow comparable in terms of informational and emotional scores and in positive social interactions. However, those two studies differed in the tangible subscale and the affectionate subscale where the tangible scores showed much higher rates in the Chinese context when compared to the Canadian context. The other difference was the affectionate which scored 58.5 in the Chinese study in relation to 80 in the Canadian studied. The differences there can be understood from the different studied contexts and the different time periods 2014 vs. 2020 [29, 30]. When comparing to the current study, however, all scores of the current study were significantly lower for falling in the low category as indicated in the Tables. These striking differences suggest the need for addressing the social factors that influence outcomes in the Lebanese society. This is true especially in the established findings of the positive relationship between loneliness and poor perceived social support with increased risk of morbidity and mortality in cardiovascular disease [31]. The above findings could be explained by the possibility of the COVID-19 era changes on people’s social context post-COVID-19. This is because people’s understanding of relationships and their importance changed during lockdown conditions and periods of limited mobility [32]. However, future studies are warranted to better understand these variables.

In terms of CV history versus none, it was noted that those with depression and diabetes were significantly less likely to have history of acute cardiovascular events. This finding is puzzling since the association between diabetes and cardiovascular diseases has long been established [33]. This should be addressed in future studies on this particular population in prospective approaches.

Limitations

Some limitations were noted in this study in terms of design and setting. With the multiple changes in the country including those related to social, political, and public health conditions of Lebanon, it is difficult to verify causality between loneliness, social support, and cardiovascular health at a single point in time with a cross-sectional design. For example, the effects of post-pandemic recovery, economic instability, or displacement could affect individuals’ perceptions of loneliness and support and therefore might have different results if conducted longitudinally. Such factors can be studied in future research to outline the specific associations between the factors. Furthermore, the sample used in this study may not reflect the whole Lebanese population because many of the respondents were residents of the metropolitan area, confined geographically to the city itself. Additionally, the use of social media platforms for recruitment in addition to those available in the general places of the community resulted in a convenience sampling which limited the validity and the generalizability of the findings to those who had access to social media or were out in the community at the time of data collection. However, the results are drawn from a collectivist society that has been subjected to many challenges on many levels. The findings could be applicable to other countries with similar challenges and cultures as that of the Lebanese. Self-report measures such as the UCLA Loneliness Scale and the MOS-SSS are prone to answer choice bias; this could result from participants using the two measures in ways that are not necessarily in accordance with their social desirability or what they consider to be their own private interpretations of the items. Future prospective studies conducted under a longitudinal design with more representative and diverse sampling can address in greater detail the relationship between social support and loneliness and cardiovascular health in Lebanon. Moreover, recall and selection biases may be introduced due to the recruitment via social media and reliance on self-reported medical history.

Conclusion

This study explored the association between loneliness and poor social support and cardiovascular health, focusing on myocardial infarction (MI) and stroke among adults aged 50 and above. The findings revealed a high prevalence of loneliness and moderate levels of social support among participants, with significant associations between low social support, increased loneliness, and history of acute cardiovascular events. Participants with CVD consistently reported lower scores across emotional, tangible, affectionate, and interactive support domains, alongside higher loneliness scores, reinforcing existing literature on the psychosocial dimensions of heart health. These results highlight the pressing need for integrating psychosocial evaluations into routine cardiovascular care, especially in culturally specific contexts such as Lebanon and those of similar cultures.
