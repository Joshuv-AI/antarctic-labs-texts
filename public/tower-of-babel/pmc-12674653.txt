Fabry Disease Nephropathy: Compendium of “in vitro” and “in vivo” Renal Effects of Globotriaosylsphingosine
Authors: Sebastián Pedro Antonio Jaurretche, María Victoria del Rosal, María Luana Brajkovic, Juan Ignacio Isaac, Graciela Venera, Germán Pérez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRenal damage in Fabry disease (FD) is a consequence of pathological and progressive glycosphingolipids accumulation, which occurs in different magnitudes among FD phenotypes, but is a constant renal tissue phenomenon in all patients with renal involvement. A significant correlation between plasma Lyso-Gb3 (Gb3 analog) and disease severity has been described but, there is lack of information from clinical studies regarding the correlation between Lyso-Gb3 and renal events in humans and, the few results have certain discrepancies. In clinical practice, it is necessary to have disease-specific biomarkers, non-invasive and useful in diagnosis, prognosis and therapeutic response. Evidence relating to the prognostic value of lyso-GL3 is mixed.SummaryWe reviewed the evidence on renal effect of plasma and/or urinary Lyso-Gb3 in FD patients and the usefulness as a biomarker of diagnostic and therapeutic response. Additionally, the in vitro renal effects of Lyso-Gb3 were reviewed. The literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane, and Google academic. Search terms were (Fabry disease + [Lyso-Gb3 or Lyso-GL3] + [“renal” or “kidney”]). Inclusion criteria: (i) “in vitro” studies in which a direct effect of Lyso-Gb3 on kidney tissue has been studied, both in animals and/or humans; (ii) retrospective, cross-sectional, or prospective “in vivo” studies in which a correlation between Lyso-Gb3 and renal clinical events or renal function biomarkers has been studied.Key MessagesTen studies presented evidence of “in vitro” renal damage due to Lyso-Gb3; 4 studies presented correlation between plasma Lyso-Gb3 and renal events in FD patients; and 3 studies reported correlation between urinary Lyso-Gb3 and renal events in FD patients.ConclusionThe small number of publications and the methodological heterogeneity do not allow a statistical analysis of them. Regarding the “in vivo” and “in vitro” renal effects of Lyso-Gb3 in FD: There is evidence of harmful effects on renal cells in vitro due to Lyso-Gb3 but this evidence is not sufficient to use Lyso-Gb3 (neither plasma nor urinary) as a biomarker for monitoring renal damage in FD patients. Plasma Lyso-Gb3 is a useful biomarker in (i) FD diagnosis and (ii) stratification of phenotype and severity of FD. In FD “classic” patients receiving enzyme replacement therapy (ERT), it is useful to determine plasma Lyso-Gb3 periodically because an inconsistent reduction during ERT may indicate the formation of neutralizing anti-agalsidase antibodies.

Introduction

Fabry disease (FD; OMIM 301500) is caused by deficient activity of lysosomal enzyme α-galactosidase-A (αGAL-A), due to genetics variants in the GLA gene [1]. GLA is a 14-kb gene located on Xq22.1 that encodes the αAGA-L enzyme. It is a polypeptide of 429 amino acids, and the Human Gene Mutation Database lists over 900 variants, of which 69% account for missense mutations [2].This results in impaired catabolism of globotriaosylceramide (Gb3) and related glycosphingolipids (GSLs), with progressive accumulation in various cell types, including capillary, renal, cardiac, neural and endothelial cells [1]. In kidney tissue, all cell types are affected by Gb3 deposition [1]. The prevalence of FD has been estimated to be between 1 in 50,000–100,000 people [2].

FD is inherited in an X-linked manner: hemizygous males are affected; heterozygous females may be as severely affected as males or asymptomatic throughout a normal life span [1]. Due to the X-linked inheritance, females (heterozygous) were considered to be asymptomatic carriers; however, over time, it has become evident that females exhibit a diverse range of clinical symptoms and experience a level of clinical severity often equivalent to that observed in males (hemizygous) [3]. The variation in phenotypes is a result of a process known as “Lyonization,” in which one of the two X-chromosomes in somatic cells is randomly inactivated [1, 3].

“Classic” FD phenotype is presented in males with severely reduced (<1–3% of mean normal) αGal-A activity, marked Gb3 accumulation in vascular endothelial cells, cardiomyocytes, smooth muscle cells, podocytes, and childhood onset of symptoms followed by early progressive multi-organ failure [1]. The first clinical manifestations, including neuropathic pain in the extremities (chronic paresthesia and pain episodic severe crises), gastrointestinal discomfort, hypohidrosis, angiokeratomas, and cornea verticillata typically emerge during childhood or early adulthood [1].

In heterozygous FD females, the spectrum of disease severity ranges from asymptomatic to a severe “classic” phenotype and is in part, dependent on the GLA genetic variant and the X chromosome inactivation (Lyonization) profile [1]. Males with “late-onset” FD phenotype have varying levels of residual αGal-A activity, age of onset, and clinical manifestations, although usually limited to single organ [1].

Life expectancy in FD affected patients is shorter than the general population due to premature onset of kidney disease, cardiomyopathy, malignant arrhythmias, and cerebrovascular events [4]. FD nephropathy is primarily manifested by pathological albuminuria on childhood and subsequent estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) decrease on adulthood [1, 5]; end-stage renal disease (ESRD) occurs by age 40 years in males with “classic” FD [6]. In “classic” FD females, kidney disease is more variable, usually onset later [7]; while among patients of both genders with “late-onset” FD phenotype, nephropathy may be the only manifestation or be absent [1].

Renal damage is a consequence of pathological and progressive GSL accumulation, which occurs in different magnitudes among FD phenotypes, but is a constant renal tissue phenomenon in all patients with renal involvement [8].

Some GSLs can be quantified in body fluids in routine practice, and their utility as non-invasive FD biomarkers has been investigated [9]. GSLs are complex lipids consisting of glycans conjugated to a ceramide-core and comprise a diverse group of over 300 molecules, which are involved in vital cellular and immune processes [9]. Gb3 [10, 11], galabiosylceramide [12], and globotriaosylsphingosine (Lyso-Gb3; the deacylated form of Gb3) [13, 14] are the GSLs characteristically accumulated in cells, tissues, and body fluids in FD patients.

GSLs can be classified on basis of electrical charge as neutral, acidic (anionic), or basic (cationic) [9], and according to their core-structure as (i) ganglio-series, (ii) globo-series, (iii) isoglobo-series, (iv) muco-series, (v) lacto-series type 1, (vi) lacto-series type 2, and (vii) gala-series [9]. Gb3 is classified into neutral and globo-series GSLs sub-group. Gb3 molecules with modifications in the sphingosine are called analogs, while those with modifications in the fatty-acid chain are called isoforms [9, 15]. Lyso-Gb3 (Gb3 analog), is a cationic amphiphile with a large polar sugar moiety, rendering it relatively hydrophilic and water-soluble [14], product of acid ceramidase enzyme (EC: 3.5.1.23) activation, when the primary process αGal-A fails [16].

In renal tissue, Lyso-Gb3 is able to activate fibrogenic pathways, an important variable in the progression of renal damage to ESRD in FD patients [2, 8]. Lyso-Gb3 can be measured in plasma and/or dried blood spots (DBS) and is considered the gold standard methodology for comparing Lyso-Gb3 results [17, 18]. Plasma Lyso-Gb3 levels can be estimated as 1.5 times the concentration in DBS. An adequate correlation between plasma concentrations of Lyso-Gb3 versus DBS was reported [17].

There is evidence regarding the usefulness of plasma Lyso-Gb3 for differentiating pathogenic versus non-pathogenic GLA gene variants [19, 20]. Plasma Lyso-Gb3 is an effective marker for “classic” and “later-onset” patients with FD, and can identify some unrecognized FD cases [21]; as a disadvantage, Lyso-Gb3 can result in normal plasma values among pre-symptomatic patients. A significant correlation between plasma Lyso-Gb3 and disease severity, classified by gender, phenotype and clinical events, has been described but, there is lack of information from clinical studies regarding the correlation between Lyso-Gb3 and renal events in humans and, the few results have certain discrepancies [18, 22–24].

Therapeutic strategies specific therapies for FD currently include (i) enzyme replacement therapy (ERT): intravenous administration of agalsidase (recombinant αGAL-A) and (ii) chaperone therapy. There is evidence that agalsidase-α and agalsidase-β significantly reduce plasma Lyso-Gb3 levels in male patients with “classic” FD phenotype with very high baseline values. In contrast, Lyso-Gb3 reduction among males with “late-onset” FD phenotype and FD females decline slightly because baseline Lyso-Gb3 levels are under-elevated [14, 23, 25–28]. In general, plasma Lyso-Gb3 levels decrease during the first 3 months of ERT treatment [29].

It is still unclear whether Lyso-Gb3 is a surrogate marker correlates with clinical events reduction among FD patients [30, 31]. In clinical practice it is necessary to have disease-specific biomarkers, non-invasive and useful in diagnosis, prognosis and therapeutic response. A recent experts consensus regarding biomarkers in FD concluded that “evidence relating to the prognostic value of lyso-GL3 is mixed” [26]. Biomarkers for monitoring renal damage in patients affected by FD are currently eGFR and albuminuria, used for monitoring nephropathy of any cause [5, 7]. Specific non-invasive biomarkers of renal damage due to FD are lacking in clinical practice.

Recently, West et al. [2] published a review highlighting the role of Lyso-Gb3 in FD in general with a focus on Fabry nephropathy. Furthermore, Veldman et al. [32] highlighted the need to have surrogate markers that correlate with clinical events in FD, with the objective of an adequate evaluation of the specific therapies effectiveness.

The objective of this work was to review the evidence on the renal effect of Lyso-Gb3 in FD and the correlation of plasma and/or urinary Lyso-Gb3 with renal adverse events. Additionally, the in vitro renal effects of Lyso-Gb3 were reviewed.

Methods

Search Strategy

The following electronic databases were searched: PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane, and Google academic. The search in each database was performed separately. Search terms used were (Fabry disease and [Lyso-Gb3 or Lyso-GL3] and [“renal” or “kidney”]). Bibliographic search was conducted from January 2022 to January 2024.

Eligibility Criteria

The eligibility criteria were structured using the PICOS (i.e., population, intervention, comparison, outcome, and study type).

Inclusion Criteria

(i) “In vitro” studies in which a direct effect of Lyso-Gb3 on kidney cells has been studied, both in animals and/or humans; (ii) retrospective or cross-sectional or prospective “in vivo” studies in which a correlation between Lyso-Gb3 (plasma and/or urinary) and renal clinical events or renal function biomarkers has been studied.

Exclusion Criteria

Sub-analyses of other works and reviews were excluded to avoid duplication of reported cases and content, respectively. Consensus and expert recommendations were excluded.

Selection Process

Data Extraction, Data Items, and Risk of Bias

Two investigators conducted the search independently, and a third investigator corroborated the information overlap. Database search results were imported into EndNote (Clarivate, Philadelphia, PA, USA) and duplicates were removed. Extracted data items included sample size and characteristics, study design, setting, nature of the included interventions, outcomes measured, and results. Finally, we recorded relevant information in an ad hoc database to sort the publications and summarize the main results (Fig. 1). PRISMA-S checklist was used to corroborate the search procedure.

Discussion

The main evidence found in our results is in favor of “in vitro” renal damage due to Lyso-Gb3 in FD, the “in vivo” data are less consistent. The harmful effect of Lyso-Gb3 “in vitro” on renal cells was reported by multiple authors. These results show several known pathways of renal damage activated or dysregulated by Lyso-Gb3 [14, 33–42]. All pathophysiological pathways studied are involved in renal fibrosis (Table 1).

Regarding correlation between plasma Lyso-Gb3 and renal events among FD patients only two prospective studies were found. (i) Aerts et al. [14] found a significant association between plasma Lyso-Gb3 and FD phenotype and severity. In their prospective analysis they reported a statistically significant correlation between plasma Lyso-Gb3 and left ventricular mass index but NOT with renal function parameters. (ii) van der Veen et al. [44] reported an association between plasma Lyso-Gb3 and the progression of kidney disease manifestations among untreated FD patients.

Two other retrospective analyses were found. (i) Sakuraba et al. [42] reported an association between plasma Lyso-Gb3 and renal events in FD patients prior to starting treatment. Another important result of Sakuraba is the low prevalence of anti-agalsidase-ɑ antibodies in “classic” FD patients. In their prospective analysis they observed that plasma Lyso-Gb3 declines rapidly in “classic” FD males and more moderately in “late-onset” FD males and females receiving ERT. The presence of anti-agalsidase antibodies has a negative effect on plasma Lyso-Gb3 decrease among “classic” FD males receiving ERT. Furthermore, they reported a significant correlation between plasma Lyso-Gb3 and FD phenotype and severity. (ii) Nowak et al. [43] reported a significant association between plasma Lyso-Gb3 and renal events (kidney transplantation or chronic dialysis included in a composite endpoint with cardiac event and/or neurological event and/or death). An important result of Nowak is that high levels of plasma Lyso-Gb3 pre-ERT are associated with a higher probability of adverse events.

Finally, when publications that studied the correlation between urinary Lyso-Gb3 and renal events were analyzed, it was observed that the three studies found had the purpose of contrasting both variables in their objectives; however, all were cross-sectional in design, and only one found a significant association in certain FD phenotypes [47]. While two other studies did not report an association between both variables [45, 46]. Auray-Blais et al. [45], reported a higher specificity of Lyso-Gb3 than Gb3 in urine for FD diagnosis, highlighting the importance of the urinary Lyso-Gb3/creatinine ratio, and found that the main variable determining urinary Lyso-Gb3 excretion is the genetic mutation type.

We previously reported that there is no evidence regarding the utility of Lyso-Gb3 for monitoring renal damage in FD patients receiving migalastat [48], which is consistent with a post hoc analysis of Bichet et al. [26]. The evidence for the usefulness of monitoring renal damage in FD patients receiving ERT using Lyso-Gb3 is scarce and controversial, as our results show. Lyso-Gb3 as a biomarker may not be suitable for monitoring treatment renal response, probably because the reversal of dysregulated cellular mechanisms in FD may not be possible with treatment after a certain point, at least in podocytes [31]. In general, there is no robust evidence regarding the effectiveness of specific FD therapies, even though some of them, such as ERT, have been available for more than 2 decades [32]. Major contributors that have been identified are study population heterogeneity (concerning gender, age, phenotype, FD disease stage) and study design differences (control group, outcomes assessed), as well as the short duration of studies [32]. Homogenization of data in the FD registry has been proposed by experts to improve this situation [32]. More research studying this topic is necessary to establish more conclusive discussions.

Conclusion

Regarding the “in vivo” and “in vitro” renal effects of Lyso-Gb3 in FD, there is evidence of harmful effects on renal cells in vitro due to Lyso-Gb3 but this evidence is not sufficient to use Lyso-Gb3 (neither plasma nor urinary) as a biomarker for monitoring renal damage in FD patients. Our results agree with those proposed by other authors, who consider plasma Lyso-Gb3 as a useful biomarker in (i) FD diagnosis and (ii) stratification of phenotype and severity of FD. In FD “classic” patients receiving ERT, it is useful to determine plasma Lyso-Gb3 periodically because an inconsistent reduction during ERT may indicate the formation of neutralizing anti-agalsidase antibodies.

Limitations Section

The small number of publications and the methodological heterogeneity do not allow a statistical analysis of them. Therefore, many discussion points are narrative and hypothetical. This is the main limitation of our results and, on the other hand, it shows the lack of evidence on the subject.
