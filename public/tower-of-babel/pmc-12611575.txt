Adalimumab as a Potential Therapeutic Option for Metastatic Crohn's Disease With Genital Lesions and Edema: A Case Report
Authors: Hoda Haghshenas, Behnaz Darban, Mohammad Aref Bagherzadeh, Mehdi Karimi, Maryam Ataollahi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMetastatic Crohn's disease (MCD) is a rare and complex extraintestinal manifestation of Crohn's disease (CD) that often profoundly affects patients' quality of life. This report presents a meticulously detailed case of a 12‐year‐old Iranian girl with persistent labial swelling and genital lesions, later diagnosed as MCD. The case highlights the diagnostic challenges and successful treatment with adalimumab. The study sheds light on the diagnostic intricacies, therapeutic dilemmas, and limited treatment options associated with MCD, a rare condition. This case study advances our understanding of MCD treatment and paves the way for exploring the promising efficacy of adalimumab in managing this challenging condition.Keywords: adalimumab, biological agents, case report, cutaneous manifestation, inflammatory bowel disease, metastatic Crohn's disease

Introduction

Metastatic Crohn's disease (MCD) also known as cutaneous CD is a rare extraintestinal cutaneous manifestation associated with CD [1]. MCD is characterized histologically by noncaseating granulomas with Langerhans giant cells, epithelioid histiocytes, and plasma cells in biopsy samples [1, 2, 3]. This case report presents a rare clinical presentation that exhibited persistent labial swelling for 7 months, accompanied by redness and edema in the perianal region, later confirmed as MCD.

Case Description

A 12‐year‐old Iranian girl was admitted to the pediatric ward of Faghihi Hospital due to severe swelling of the genitalia accompanied by itching, fever, and anal pain. She initially noticed a small nodule on the right labia majora 8 months earlier, which gradually enlarged. Over the following 7 months, she developed a prolonged low‐grade fever, constipation, anal pain during defecation, decreased appetite, and a 12% unintentional weight loss. Her past history included premature puberty, successfully treated with somatropin (CinnaTropin). At admission, the patient had no reported family history of active Crohn's disease.

On physical examination, there was a tender, pedunculated polypoid mass (12 × 6 cm) on the right labia majora, with multiple “knife‐cut” ulcers in the inguinoscrotal fold and a perianal fissure with a skin tag (Figure 1). Abdominal examination was unremarkable. Laboratory investigations revealed normal blood counts and liver function, moderately elevated ESR, high CRP, and a positive fecal calprotectin test.

Diagnosis

Given the patient's persistent genital swelling and perianal lesions unresponsive to antibiotics, a diagnostic colonoscopy and gastroendoscopy were performed. Colonoscopy revealed multiple large fissures and skin tags in the perianal area, mild erythema and erosions in the transverse and descending colon, and severe rectal erosions. The terminal ileum could not be intubated due to marked edema and friability at the ileocecal valve, which made safe passage technically unfeasible. Gastroendoscopy was unremarkable, and microbiological cultures were negative for bacteria, fungi, and mycobacteria. HIV and VDRL tests were negative, and T‐cell markers were within the normal range.

Empirical mesalamine therapy was initiated while awaiting pathology results, but no clinical improvement was observed. Histopathological examination of a labial skin biopsy subsequently revealed noncaseating granulomas, confirming the diagnosis of MCD after exclusion of other differential diagnoses (Figure 2).

(a) In the dermis, non‐necrotizing granulomas of the tuberculoid type can be seen. H&E staining at 100× magnification. (b) The granuloma exhibits giant cells, epithelioid histiocytes, and plasma cells. H&E staining at 400× magnification.

Treatment

Following the confirmation of metastatic Crohn's disease (MCD) by histopathology, induction therapy with infliximab was initiated. The patient received the standard intravenous induction protocol (weeks 0, 2, and 6) with the intention to continue maintenance infusions every 8 weeks.

To improve long‐term adherence and allow for outpatient management, the treatment strategy was switched to subcutaneous adalimumab (Figure 3). The induction dose consisted of 160 mg, followed by 80 mg 2 weeks later, and then maintenance with 40 mg every 8 weeks.

After 2 weeks of treatment with infliximab, healing is evident in the perianal area, with a reduction in inflammation and fissures (a). Additionally, there is a decrease in swelling in the right labia majora, with the wounds disappearing (b).

Adalimumab was administered in combination with azathioprine (50 mg orally once daily), prednisolone (50 mg orally once daily, tapered to discontinuation over 8 weeks), and folic acid (5 mg once daily). This combined regimen aimed to optimize disease control and minimize the development of anti‐drug antibodies. The patient was discharged from the hospital after receiving the induction doses of infliximab, tolerated the treatment well, with no infusion reactions or adverse effects. Follow‐Up and Outcomes

Following biologic therapy, the patient demonstrated a gradual but significant clinical improvement. Over the first 3 months, genital swelling and perianal fissures markedly regressed, and systemic symptoms including fever, anal pain, and altered bowel habits, resolved.

At 11 months of follow‐up, only slight residual edema of the right labia majora was noted, with no pain, discharge, or bleeding. The patient had normal bowel habits and gained weight, increasing from 37 to 50 kg.

A repeat gastroendoscopy, colonoscopy, and MR enterography had been planned during follow‐up; however, due to clinical stability and resource limitations, MRE was not performed. Discussion

Crohn's disease (CD) is a subgroup of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) characterized by granulomatous lesions of the intestinal tract. Cutaneous presentations (such as erythema nodosum and pyoderma gangrenosum) are one of the extraintestinal manifestations in CD patients [2, 3].

Metastatic Crohn's disease (MCD) is a rare condition characterized by a nonspecific clinical presentation. Most patients diagnosed with MCD have a previous diagnosis of CD; however, some present without gastrointestinal manifestations. Therefore, the diagnosis should be considered in any patient with persistent skin lesions, even in the absence of a previous CD diagnosis. MCD diagnosis can be confirmed by histopathologic detection, as well as ruling out other differential diagnoses [1, 4].

Although no randomized controlled trials specifically target MCD, evidence is derived from case reports, case series, and treatment guidelines for cutaneous extraintestinal manifestations. Current guidelines recommend anti‐TNF agents, with infliximab as first‐line therapy, followed by other biologics such as adalimumab, golimumab, ustekinumab, and JAK inhibitors in refractory cases [5, 6, 7]. MCD treatment is more dependent on some factors, such as patient preference, risk of therapy, complications, level of disease morbidity, medication cost, and drug monitoring [6]. According to previous studies, most patients without treatment had persistent symptoms, and single therapy has not had successful efficacy, so in past approaches (single therapy), MCD tends to be chronic and cause morbidity [8, 9]. More recently, anti‐TNF alpha biologic agents, including infliximab and adalimumab, have become available [10, 11]. Several reports have shown significant clinical improvement and even remission following induction with 160 mg, followed by 80 mg and then 40 mg every other week [12, 13, 14].

In this case, after adequate evaluation and rollout of the MCD differential diagnosis, a MCD diagnosis was made and confirmed by biopsy. Single therapy and nonspecific treatments, as mentioned in previous articles, did not improve lesions and symptoms. However, anti‐TNF agents effectively reduced the MCD symptoms in this case and showed improvement in the follow‐up, which is consistent with past reports [15]. Conclusion

Finally, this study demonstrated that MCD is an inflammatory skin disease, and that lesions that persist for a long time and do not respond to conventional treatment should be clinically suspected, even in patients without a prior history of Crohn's disease or active CD. Additionally, MCD responds better and more quickly to treatment with biological agents (anti‐TNF) than to conventional and first‐line therapies.

This study demonstrated the effectiveness of adalimumab as a therapy for Metastatic Crohn's Disease (MCD) in a pediatric patient, highlighted the limitations of conventional therapy, and the potential significance of anti‐TNF biologic medicines in MCD management. The results contribute to the growing body of data supporting adalimumab's use, especially in individuals who have not responded to traditional therapies. The study advocates for further research into biological treatments for MCD and recommends them as a first‐line choice for refractory patients.
