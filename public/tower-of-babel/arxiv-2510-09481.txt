Particle creation in a cosmological background in analogy to the Schwinger effect
Walter D. van Suijlekom, Michael F. Wondrak, Heino Falcke

Abstract
. We consider a gravitational analogue of the Schwinger effect in a cosmological context. While the Schwinger effect is usually attributed to a static electric background, its derivation is actually based on a switching on/off of the electric field in the infinite past/future. Motivated by this, and our previous work on particle production in a gravitational background, we consider a long pulse of the gravitational field in an FLRW-spacetime, thus simulating a static background. We rigorously derive particle production by a novel application of the Heun equation. In fact, the recently obtained connecting formulas between its local solutions can be used to determine the Bogolyubov coefficients, and subsequently the particle production probabilities, in the limit of an infinitely long pulse. The particle production in the FLRW-model is found to have a lower threshold on the outgoing frequencies, which can be related to the duration of the time interval of the switching on/off the background field. For large frequencies and in the spatially flat case, we find black-body radiation whose temperature is inversely proportional to the radius of the apparent horizon that appears in the FLRW-model during the phase of scale change. We compare our findings to Schwinger’s result on particle production for a long pulse of an electromagnetic background field, for which we also include a detailed derivation.



1.
Introduction

There is a striking conceptual similarity between the Schwinger effect and Hawking radiation: where the former is a mechanism of particle production in a constant electromagnetic background field, the latter describes particle production in a gravitational background. This has been studied at length in the literature; see
[
15
,
6
]
for reviews.
In our previous work
[
25
,
10
]
, we also built on this analogy, basing ourselves on heat kernel methods.
The derivation of the effective Lagrangian therein was based on a Wick rotation to the Euclidean section of the spacetime manifold, thus mathematically
justifying the use of heat-kernel methods. Early works along these lines are
[
27
]
and
[
12
]
. A crucial point that should be stressed is that the Euclidean section is periodic in time, which corresponds to the consideration of a thermal state in Lorentzian spacetime
[
24
]
,
i.e.
the so-called Hartle–Hawking state. A variation on the exploited methods yields both supporting and contrasting results; see, for instance,
[
13
,
8
,
2
,
4
,
26
]

for recent and ongoing discussions.

This paper is an attempt to shed further light on the origin of particle production in both an electromagnetic and a gravitational background field. Schwinger derived particle production for a switch on and off of an electric field pulse, considering the limit where the length of the pulse interval goes to infinity. Motivated by this, we consider an FLRW-model where the gravitational background is switched on and off, and then consider the analogous limit of a long pulse.

We point out the similarities and the contrasts between the electromagnetic and gravitational case. We perform our analysis in the rigorous context of canonical quantization of free scalar fields in the pertinent backgrounds. In the limit of a long pulse of these background fields, we consider the Bogolyubov transformations relating early time modes to later time modes. In particular, we identify the outgoing modes obtained by such transformations when applied to an incoming vacuum state. Such modes are then understood as the potential source of particle production. For a related study based on the phase integral approximation method and numerics using a different time profile, see
[
3
]
.

Even though the derivation of the Bogolyubov transformations and the analysis of the probability of particle production may be technically somewhat involved, at the conceptual level the derivation seems less complicated. In fact, in all cases, the recipe is as follows.

(1)

Derive the field equation for a quantum scalar field in a background (gravitational/electric) field which is turned on during a certain (long) time interval;

(2)

Solve the field equation for modes that have positive frequency at early times;

(3)

Derive from the connecting formulas for the local solutions of the field equations the Bogolyubov transformations relating early time modes to later time modes; the latter may be both positive and negative frequency modes;

(4)

In the Heisenberg picture, consider the vacuum for the early time observables;

(5)

Using the Bogolyubov transformations, analyze the particle content for this vacuum state using later time observables;

(6)

Consider a field pulse in the limit of an infinitely long time interval, which thus becomes a constant field background;

(7)

Interpret the particle creation as being due to the constant field background and/or its switching on/off.

We apply this in Section
2
to a long electric field pulse to (re)derive the Schwinger effect
[
23
,
15
]
. Section
3
then considers a novel gravitational model, which is based on a scale function that describes a gravitational pulse in an FLRW-spacetime. Interestingly, in the latter case we discover the appearance of the Heun differential equation, for which the connecting formulas between local solutions have only recently been investigated
[
17
,
5
]
. Their approximation for long time intervals derived in
[
5
]
will be used to obtain explicit expression for the Bogolyubov transformations, which then yield the corresponding probabilities for particle creation. Interestingly, for large frequencies, and in the spatially flat case, we find black-body radiation whose temperature is inversely proportional to the radius of the apparent horizon during the phase of scale change.
We finish with some concluding remarks in the Outlook, Section
4
.

In the appendix we consider two additional, alternative models for long gravitational pulses with simpler scale functions. They confirm the results obtained in Section
3
but are less flexible for a full analysis since both the duration of the time interval and the rate of change of the scale function during the turning on/off are controlled by the same parameter. Instead, the solution to the Heun equation in Section
3
allows to take the limit of an infinitely long pulse without reducing the rate of change to become 0.

2.
The Schwinger effect revisited: a long electric field pulse

The derivation of the Schwinger effect is well-documented in the physics literature
[
22
, Chapter 13]
, including the derivation via Bogolyubov transformation as presented here (see
[
15
]
). The main reason to include a detailed derivation in the present manuscript is to confront it with particle creation in a gravitational context, as discussed in the subsequent sections.

2.1.
Quantum scalar field with a background electromagnetic field

We recall the description of a quantum scalar field in a (classical) electromagnetic background field; see
e.g.

[
15
]
. The relevant field equation is

(1)

(\partial_{\mu}+ieA_{\mu})(\partial^{\mu}+ieA^{\mu})\phi+m^{2}\phi=0.

We consider the four-potential
A_{\mu}
to be of the form

A_{\mu}(x)=(0,0,0,A_{3}(t))

so the electromagnetic field is given by

{\bf E}(t)=\left(0,0,-\frac{dA_{3}}{dt}\right).

Solutions to (
1
) are given by modes of the form

\phi(x)=(2\pi)^{-3/2}(2\omega_{-}(\mathbf{p}))^{-1/2}e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\psi_{\mathbf{p}}(t)

where
\psi_{\mathbf{p}}(t)
satisfies the ODE

(2)

\ddot{\psi}_{\mathbf{p}}+\omega^{2}(\mathbf{p},t)\psi_{\mathbf{p}}(t)=0;\qquad\omega^{2}(\mathbf{p},t)=m^{2}+p_{\perp}^{2}+(p_{3}-eA_{3})^{2},

and where we have written
p_{\perp}^{2}=p_{1}^{2}+p_{2}^{2}
and
\omega_{-}(\mathbf{p})=\lim_{t\to-\infty}\omega(\mathbf{p},t)
. Introducing the modes

f_{\mathbf{p}}(x)=(2\pi)^{-3/2}(2\omega_{-}(\mathbf{p}))^{-1/2}e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\psi_{\mathbf{p}}(t).

we may expand the field as

(3)

\phi=\int d^{3}\mathbf{p}\left(A_{\mathbf{p}}f_{\mathbf{p}}+A_{\mathbf{p}}^{\dagger}f_{\mathbf{p}}^{*}\right)

with annihilation and creation operators
A_{\mathbf{p}},A_{\mathbf{p}}^{\dagger}
at early times.
The strategy is now to look for solutions
\psi_{\mathbf{p}}
with positive frequency when
t\to-\infty
,
i.e.

(4)

\psi_{\mathbf{p}}(x,t)\sim e^{-i\omega_{-}(\mathbf{p})t};\qquad(t\to-\infty).

and determine the form of
\psi_{\mathbf{p}}
at
t=+\infty
. If we write
\omega_{+}(\mathbf{p})=\lim_{t\to\infty}\omega(\mathbf{p},t)
then we clearly have

(5)

\psi_{\mathbf{p}}(t)\sim\alpha_{\mathbf{p}}e^{-i\omega_{+}(\mathbf{p})t}+\beta_{\mathbf{p}}e^{i\omega_{+}(\mathbf{p})t};\qquad(t\to\infty),

for some coefficients
\alpha_{\mathbf{p}},\beta_{\mathbf{p}}
. In other words, we have a mixture of positive and negative frequency solutions. Since the Wronskian for the ODE (
2
) is conserved, we find that

(6)

|\alpha_{\mathbf{p}}|^{2}-|\beta_{\mathbf{p}}|^{2}=1.

Moreover, we may expand the field operator in terms of outgoing modes

(7)

\phi=\int d^{3}\mathbf{p}\left(a_{\mathbf{p}}g_{\mathbf{p}}+a_{\mathbf{p}}^{\dagger}g_{\mathbf{p}}^{*}\right)

where now
g_{\mathbf{p}}\sim(2\pi)^{-3/2}(2\omega_{+}(\mathbf{p}))^{-1/2}e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot x}e^{-i\omega_{+}(\mathbf{p})t}
(as
t\to\infty
) is the positive frequency outgoing mode. By comparing Equations (
3
) and (
7
) we conclude that the annihiliation and creation operators
a_{\mathbf{p}},a_{\mathbf{p}}^{\dagger}
for the outgoing quantum field are related to the analogues
A_{\mathbf{p}},A_{\mathbf{p}}^{\dagger}
for the incoming field via the following
Bogolyubov transformation
:

a_{\mathbf{p}}=\alpha_{\mathbf{p}}A_{\mathbf{p}}+\beta_{\mathbf{p}}^{*}A_{-\mathbf{p}}^{\dagger}.

The expectation value of the number of particles can now be computed as follows. Suppose that the initial state of the system is described in the Heisenberg picture by the vacuum vector
|0\rangle
,
i.e.
with no particles at
t\to-\infty
. In other words we have

A_{\mathbf{p}}|0\rangle=0;\qquad(\forall\mathbf{p}).

The expectation value
n(\mathbf{p})
of the number of particles present at
t\to\infty
in mode
\mathbf{p}
is then given by

n(\mathbf{p}):=\langle 0|a_{\mathbf{p}}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{p}}|0\rangle=|\beta_{\mathbf{p}}|^{2},

which yields for the
S
-matrix that

|\langle 0_{\text{out}}|0_{\text{in}}\rangle|^{2}=\exp\left\{-V/(2\pi)^{3}\int d^{3}\mathbf{p}\log(1+n(\mathbf{p}))\right\}.

where the system is placed in a box of volume
V
. Moreover, the probability of observing at late times
n
particles in mode
\mathbf{p}
is

P_{n}(\mathbf{p})=\left|\frac{\beta_{\mathbf{p}}}{\alpha_{\mathbf{p}}}\right|^{2n}\left(1-\left|\frac{\beta_{\mathbf{p}}}{\alpha_{\mathbf{p}}}\right|^{2}\right).

so that
n(p)=\sum_{n\geq 0}n~P_{n}(p)
.

2.2.
Electric field pulse and hypergeometric equation

We now consider the following explicit vector potential,

(8)

A^{3}(t)=-\frac{E}{b}\tanh bt;\qquad{\bf E}(t)=(0,0,E/\cosh^{2}bt).

Figure 1.
Vector potential (in units of
E/b
) and electric field (in units of
E
) for an electric field pulse as in (
8
) (for
b=10^{-1}
).

Upon writing
u=e^{2bt}
this becomes
A^{3}=-\tfrac{E}{b}\tfrac{u-1}{u+1}
while (
2
) reads

(9)

\psi_{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime\prime}+u^{-1}\psi_{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime}+u^{-2}\left(\left(\frac{m}{2b}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{p_{\perp}}{2b}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{p_{3}}{2b}+\frac{eE}{2b^{2}}\frac{u-1}{u+1}\right)^{2}\right)\psi_{\mathbf{p}}=0.

This is a ODE with three singular points (at
u=0,-1
and at
u=\infty
). Let us analyze the behavior at the singular points
u=0,-1
. Around
u=0
we find that
\psi_{\mathbf{p}}
satisfies

\psi_{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime\prime}+u^{-1}\psi_{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime}+u^{-2}\frac{\omega_{-}^{2}(\mathbf{p})}{4b^{2}}\psi_{\mathbf{p}}=0,

which is solved by
\psi_{\mathbf{p}}=u^{\pm c_{-}}
where
c_{-}=i\omega_{-}(\mathbf{p})/2b
. In order to account for the positive frequency at
u=0
we take
\psi_{\mathbf{p}}\sim u^{-c_{-}}
.

The leading behavior at
u=-1
is given by

\psi_{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime\prime}+\left(\frac{eE}{b^{2}}\frac{1}{u+1}\right)^{2}\psi_{\mathbf{p}}=0.

This is solved by
\psi_{\mathbf{p}}\sim(1+u)^{d}
where

d=\tfrac{1}{2}+\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{4}-\alpha^{2}};\qquad\alpha=\frac{eE}{b^{2}}.

If we now write
\psi_{\mathbf{p}}=u^{-c_{-}}(1+u)^{d}F(u)
we find that
F
should satisfy the following differential equation:

u(1+u)F^{\prime\prime}(u)+\left(\left(1-i\frac{\omega_{-}}{b}\right)(1+u)+2du\right)F^{\prime}(u)+\left(-\alpha^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{b}p_{3}+d\left(1-i\frac{\omega_{-}}{b}\right)\right)F(u)=0.

This is a hypergeometric differential equation, which is usually written as

z(1-z)\frac{d^{2}w}{dz^{2}}+\left(c-(a+b+1)z\right)\frac{dw}{d}-abw=0.

The local solution at
z=0
with characteristic exponent
0
is denoted by
{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b,c;z)
, the
hypergeometric function
. In our case of interest we find that the constants
a,b,c
are given by

a=d-i(\omega_{-}+\omega_{+})/2b;\qquad b=d-i(\omega_{-}-\omega_{+})/2b;\qquad c=1-i\omega_{-}/b

while
z=-u
. We conclude that a local solution of (
9
) at
u=0
is given by

\psi_{\mathbf{p}}=u^{-c_{-}}(1+u)^{d}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(d-i\frac{\omega_{-}+\omega_{+}}{2b},d-i\frac{\omega_{-}-\omega_{+}}{2b},1-i\frac{\omega_{-}}{b};-u\right).

The local behavior of the hypergeometric function at
u=0
ensures that
\psi_{\mathbf{p}}\propto u^{-c_{-}}
at
u\sim 0
.

We may relate the local solution at
u=0
to the ones at
u=\infty
since we have
[
1
, Eq. 15.3.7]

\displaystyle{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b,c;z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(c)\Gamma(b-a)}{\Gamma(b)\Gamma(c-a)}(-z)^{-a}{}_{2}F_{1}(a,1-c+a,1-b+a;1/z)

(10)

\displaystyle+\frac{\Gamma(c)\Gamma(a-b)}{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(c-b)}(-z)^{-b}{}_{2}F_{1}(b,1-c+b,1-a+b;1/z).

This implies that at
u\sim\infty
we have

(11)

\psi_{\mathbf{p}}\sim\frac{\Gamma(c)\Gamma(b-a)}{\Gamma(b)\Gamma(c-a)}u^{-c_{-}+d-a}+\frac{\Gamma(c)\Gamma(a-b)}{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(c-b)}u^{-c_{-}+d-b}=\frac{\Gamma(c)\Gamma(b-a)}{\Gamma(b)\Gamma(c-a)}u^{c_{+}}+\frac{\Gamma(c)\Gamma(a-b)}{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(c-b)}u^{-c_{+}}

where we have introduced
c_{+}=i\omega_{+}(\mathbf{p})/2b
. We conclude that the incoming annihilation and creation operators are related to the outgoing ones via Bogolyubov coefficients of the form

\alpha_{\mathbf{p}}=\frac{\Gamma(c)\Gamma(a-b)}{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(c-b)}\left(\frac{\omega_{+}(\mathbf{p})}{\omega_{-}(\mathbf{p})}\right)^{1/2};\qquad\beta_{\mathbf{p}}=\frac{\Gamma(c)\Gamma(b-a)}{\Gamma(b)\Gamma(c-a)}\left(\frac{\omega_{+}(\mathbf{p})}{\omega_{-}(\mathbf{p})}\right)^{1/2}.

We use the identities
\Gamma(1-z)\Gamma(z)=\pi/\sin(\pi z)
,
\overline{\Gamma(z)}=\Gamma(\overline{z})
as well as
\sin(x-y)\sin(x+y)=\sin^{2}x-\sin^{2}y
to derive

\left|\frac{\beta_{\mathbf{p}}}{\alpha_{\mathbf{p}}}\right|^{2}=\frac{\sin^{2}\pi d+\sinh^{2}({\pi(\omega_{-}(\mathbf{p})-\omega_{+}(\mathbf{p}))}/{2b})}{\sin^{2}\pi d+\sinh^{2}({\pi(\omega_{-}(\mathbf{p})+\omega_{+}(\mathbf{p}))}/{2b})}

while for the expectation value
n(\mathbf{p})
we find

n(\mathbf{p})=|\beta_{\mathbf{p}}|^{2}=\frac{\sin^{2}\pi d+\sinh^{2}(\pi(\omega_{-}(\mathbf{p})-\omega_{+}(\mathbf{p}))/2b)}{\sinh(\pi\omega_{-}(\mathbf{p})/b)\sinh(\pi\omega_{+}(\mathbf{p})/b)}.

2.3.
Particle creation for a long electric field pulse

We will now analyze these coefficients for small
b
, that is, of a long electric field pulse.

First, note that

\omega_{\pm}(\mathbf{p})^{2}=\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}\left(m^{2}+p_{\perp}^{2}+\left(p_{3}-\frac{eE}{b}\tanh bt\right)^{2}\right)=m^{2}+p_{\perp}^{2}+\left(p_{3}\mp\frac{eE}{b}\right)^{2}.

From this we see that
n(\mathbf{p})
is exponentially suppressed whenever
|p_{3}|\gg eE/b
.
Moreover, upon expanding the square root in powers of
1/b
:

\omega_{\pm}(\mathbf{p})=\frac{eE}{b}\mp p_{3}+\frac{m^{2}+p_{\perp}^{2}}{2eE}b+\mathcal{O}(b^{2}),

while

d=\tfrac{1}{2}+\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{4}-\alpha^{2}}=i\frac{eE}{b^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(1).

Using these expressions, we find that for
|p_{3}|\leq eE/b
(
cf.

[
15
, Eq. 4.80]
)

\displaystyle n(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\sinh^{2}\pi\frac{eE}{b^{2}}+\sinh^{2}(\pi p_{3}/b)}{\sinh\pi\left(\frac{eE}{b^{2}}-\frac{p_{3}}{b}+\frac{m^{2}+p_{\perp}^{2}}{2eE}\right)\sinh\pi\left(\frac{eE}{b^{2}}+\frac{p_{3}}{b}+\frac{m^{2}+p_{\perp}^{2}}{2eE}\right)}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\exp\left\{2\pi\frac{eE}{b^{2}}\right\}}{\exp\left\{\pi\left(\frac{2eE}{b^{2}}+\frac{m^{2}+p_{\perp}^{2}}{eE}\right)\right\}}=\exp\{-\pi(m^{2}+p_{\perp}^{2})/eE\};\qquad(b\to 0).

We thus find that for
b\to 0
:

n(\mathbf{p})\sim\exp\{-\pi(m^{2}+p_{\perp}^{2})/eE\}\theta(eE/b-|p_{3}|),

in terms of the Heaviside
\theta
-function. We use this to obtain

|\langle 0_{\text{out}}|0_{\text{in}}\rangle|^{2}=\exp\left\{-V/(2\pi)^{3}\int d^{3}\mathbf{p}\log(1+n(\mathbf{p}))\right\}.

When expanding the logarithm and performing the integration over
\mathbf{p}
we obtain Schwinger’s result
[
23
]
(
cf.

[
15
, Eq. 4.83]
):

\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int d^{3}\mathbf{p}\log(1+n(\mathbf{p}))=\frac{e^{2}E^{2}T}{8\pi^{3}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n^{2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{n\pi m^{2}}{eE}\right\}

where
T=1/(2b)
.
For the probabilities
P_{n}(\mathbf{p})
we first determine that

\left|\frac{\beta_{\mathbf{p}}}{\alpha_{\mathbf{p}}}\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{1+\exp\left\{\pi(m^{2}+p_{\perp}^{2})/eE\right\}}

For large momenta we have
\left|{\beta_{\mathbf{p}}}/{\alpha_{\mathbf{p}}}\right|^{2}\sim\exp\{-\pi(m^{2}+p_{\perp}^{2})/eE\}
and, accordingly,

P_{n}(\mathbf{p})=\exp\{-n\pi(m^{2}+p_{\perp}^{2})/eE\}\left(1-\exp\{-\pi(m^{2}+p_{\perp}^{2})/eE\}\right).

3.
Particle creation in FLRW-spacetimes with a gravitational pulse

3.1.
Scalar fields in FLRW-spacetime

We briefly recall the description of a free hermitian quantum scalar field in an FLRW-spacetime background, referring to
e.g.

[
19
, Chapter 2]
for more details. We write the metric on
\mathbb{R}\times\Sigma
as

ds^{2}=-dt^{2}+a(t)^{2}ds_{\Sigma}^{2}

for some scale function
a(t)
, and where
ds_{\Sigma}^{2}
is a fixed volume form on a three-dimensional Riemannian manifold
\Sigma
. The free field equation
\Box\phi=0
becomes

(12)

a^{-3}\partial_{t}(a^{3}\partial_{t}\phi)+a^{-2}\Delta_{\Sigma}\phi=0,

in terms of the spatial Laplacian
\Delta_{\Sigma}
. The latter has positive eigenvalues:
\Delta_{\Sigma}\phi_{\lambda}=\lambda^{2}\phi_{\lambda}
with
\lambda^{2}\in\sigma(\Delta_{\Sigma})\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, the spectrum of
\Delta_{\Sigma}
; we also write
\phi_{-\lambda}=\phi_{\lambda}^{*}
.
1
1

1

The reader should be warned that we adopt the mathematicians’ convention of writing
\lambda
for an eigenvalue of the Laplacian, in contrast to the physicists’ convention of writing
\lambda
for a wavelength.
In terms of a time variable
\tau
defined by

(13)

\tau=\int^{t}a^{-3}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}

the field equation (
12
) becomes

(14)

\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial\tau^{2}}+a^{4}(\tau)\Delta_{\Sigma}\phi=0.

If
\phi_{\lambda}
are the eigenfunctions
\phi_{\lambda}
of
\Delta_{\Sigma}
, we may decompose the field operator
\phi
as

(15)

\phi=\sum_{\lambda}A_{\lambda}f_{\lambda}+A_{\lambda}^{\dagger}f_{\lambda}^{*}

in terms of respective annihiliation and creation operators
A_{\lambda},A_{\lambda}^{\dagger}
and where
f_{\lambda}=\phi_{\lambda}\psi_{\lambda}
. The function
\psi_{\lambda}
is a function of
\tau
only and from (
12
) we derive that it satisfies the following ODE

(16)

\frac{d^{2}\psi_{\lambda}}{d\tau^{2}}+\lambda^{2}a^{4}(\tau)\psi_{\lambda}=0.

We consider an asymptotically static
a(\tau)
and impose the initial condition that at
\tau\to-\infty
the function
f_{\lambda}
is the positive frequency solution (at scale
a_{-\infty}=\lim_{\tau\to-\infty}a(\tau)
),
i.e.

(17)

f_{\lambda}\sim\phi_{\lambda}e^{-i\omega_{\lambda}t}\equiv\phi_{\lambda}e^{-i\omega_{\lambda}a_{-\infty}^{3}\tau};\qquad(\tau\to-\infty),

where
\omega_{\lambda}=|\lambda|/a_{-\infty}
.
The strategy is now to look for solutions
\psi_{\lambda}
satisfying this initial condition, and determine the form of
\psi_{\lambda}
at
\tau=+\infty
. If we write
a_{\infty}=\lim_{\tau\to\infty}a(\tau)
then we clearly have for
\tau\to\infty
:

(18)

\psi_{\lambda}(\tau)\sim\alpha_{\lambda}e^{-i|\lambda|a_{\infty}^{2}\tau}+\beta_{\lambda}e^{i|\lambda|a_{\infty}^{2}\tau};\qquad(\tau\to\infty),

for some coefficients
\alpha_{\lambda},\beta_{\lambda}
. In other words, we have a mixture of positive and negative frequency solutions; as we will see, and as is well-known, this is the origin of particle production in a curved spacetime. Since the Wronskian for the ODE (
16
) is conserved, we find that

(19)

|\alpha_{\lambda}|^{2}-|\beta_{\lambda}|^{2}=1.

Moreover, we may expand the field operator in terms of outgoing modes

\phi=\sum_{\lambda}a_{\lambda}g_{\lambda}+a_{\lambda}^{\dagger}g_{\lambda}^{*}

where now
g_{\lambda}\sim\phi_{\lambda}e^{-i|\lambda|a_{\infty}^{2}\tau}
is the positive frequency outgoing mode. We conclude that the creation and annihiliation operator
a_{\lambda},a_{\lambda}^{\dagger}
for the outgoing quantum field are related to the analogues
A_{\lambda},A_{\lambda}^{\dagger}
for the incoming field via the following Bogolyubov transformation:

a_{\lambda}=\alpha_{\lambda}A_{\lambda}+\beta_{\lambda}^{*}A_{-\lambda}^{\dagger}.

The expectation value of the number of particles can now be computed as follows. Suppose that the initial state of the system is described in the Heisenberg picture by the vacuum vector
|0\rangle
,
i.e.
with no particles at
\tau\to-\infty
. In other words we have

A_{\lambda}|0\rangle=0;\qquad(\forall\lambda\in\sigma(\Delta_{\Sigma})).

The expectation value
n(\lambda)
of the number of particles present at
\tau\to\infty
in mode
\lambda
is then given by

(20)

n(\lambda)=\langle 0|a_{\lambda}^{\dagger}a_{\lambda}|0\rangle=|\beta_{\lambda}|^{2}=\frac{|\beta_{\lambda}/\alpha_{\lambda}|^{2}}{1-|\beta_{\lambda}/\alpha_{\lambda}|^{2}},

which yields for the
S
-matrix that

|\langle 0_{\text{out}}|0_{\text{in}}\rangle|^{2}=\exp\left\{-\sum_{\lambda}\log(1+n(\lambda))\right\}.

Moreover, the probability of observing at late times
n
particles in mode
\lambda
is

(21)

P_{n}(\lambda)=\left|\frac{\beta_{\lambda}}{\alpha_{\lambda}}\right|^{2n}\left(1-\left|\frac{\beta_{\lambda}}{\alpha_{\lambda}}\right|^{2}\right).

3.2.
Particle creation in instantaneous long gravitational pulse: Heun equation

The above ODE (
16
) can be solved exactly in some cases, see
e.g.

[
19
, Section 2.8]
and references therein. Here we will analyze an FLRW-model for a pulse in the scalar function

where we include two parameters to control the shape: a parameter

a(\tau)=\left(a_{2}^{4}+\left(a_{2}^{4}-a_{1}^{4}\right)\left(\left(\frac{e^{b(\tau-T)}}{1+e^{b(\tau-T)}}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{e^{b(\tau+T)}}{1+e^{b(\tau+T)}}\right)^{2}\right)\right)^{1/4}.

Recall that the Ricci scalar curvature for FLRW spacetimes is given in the time variable

R(t)=6\left(\frac{\ddot{a}(t)}{a(t)}+\frac{\dot{a}(t)^{2}}{a(t)^{2}}+\frac{\kappa}{a(t)^{2}}\right)

\kappa

\Sigma
. In terms of the parameter
\tau

(23)

\displaystyle R(\tau)=6\left(\frac{a^{\prime\prime}(\tau)}{a(\tau)^{7}}-2\frac{a^{\prime}(\tau)^{2}}{a(\tau)^{8}}+\frac{\kappa}{a(\tau)^{2}}\right)

We have illustrated the scale function in Figure
2
and the corresponding curvature scalars in Figure

.

A useful coordinate transformation is
u=e^{b\tau}
while setting
u_{\pm}=e^{\pm bT}

(27)

\alpha=-2c+d_{+}+d_{-},\qquad\beta=d_{+}+d_{-}.

We conclude that a local solution of (

One may check that the leading behavior of the local Heun function

Low frequency modes

\displaystyle\lim_{a\to 0}\frac{|\beta_{\lambda}|^{2}}{|\alpha_{\lambda}|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(2c)\Gamma(-2c)}{\Gamma(-2c)\Gamma(2c)}

\lim_{a\to 0}\frac{|\beta_{\lambda}|^{2}}{|\alpha_{\lambda}|^{2}}=1
so that no particle production appears for the low frequencies in the limit of a long time interval.

3.4.
Physical implications

Let us conclude this section with a few words on the physical implications and possible interpretation of the results just obtained.

We have found in the FLRW-model defined in (
22
) that in the limit
T\to\infty
there is a lower threshold for particle production at eigenvalue
\lambda_{M}=b/(\sqrt{2}a_{1}^{2})
. In terms of outgoing frequency
\omega_{\lambda}=|\lambda|/a_{2}
this means that only particles are produced with frequency greater than
b/(\sqrt{2}a_{1}^{2}a_{2})
. Interestingly, as we will show next, the reciprocal of this frequency threshold is approximately the duration of the scale change. A physical interpretation of this relation could be that the spatial change is taking place too fast compared to the frequency for an effect to happen.

Let us then derive the claimed relation between the frequency threshold and the duration of the scale change.
Consider a first approximation of
a(t)
given by the linear interpolation
a_{\text{lin}}(t)
between the scales
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
for a time interval
[t_{0},t_{0}+\Delta t]
of length
\Delta t
,
i.e.

(36)

a_{\text{lin}}(t)=\frac{a_{2}-a_{1}}{\Delta t}(t-t_{0})+a_{1}.

In this case, using Eq. (
13
)

\Delta\tau\sim\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta t}a_{\text{lin}}^{-3}(t)dt=\frac{1}{2(a_{2}-a_{1})}\left(\frac{1}{a_{1}^{2}}-\frac{1}{a_{2}^{2}}\right)\Delta t\sim\frac{\Delta t}{2a_{1}^{2}a_{2}}

for
a_{2}\gg a_{1}
. Given this linear approximation of
a(t)
, a time interval
\Delta\tau=1/b
thus corresponds to
\Delta t=2a_{1}^{2}a_{2}/b
as claimed above.

Looking at the profile for particle production (
35
) we observe that in the spatially flat case this corresponds for large frequencies to blackbody radiation. Indeed, in the infinite volume limit we may replace the outgoing frequency
\omega_{\lambda}
by a continuous variable
k
, so that the average particle density becomes:

\langle\mathcal{N}\rangle=(2\pi^{2}a_{2}^{3})^{-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk~k^{2}\left(e^{\beta k}-1\right)^{-1}.

where the inverse temperature
\beta
obtained from (
35
) is
\beta\sim 2\pi a_{1}^{2}a_{2}/b
for
a_{2}\gg a_{1}
.

In analogy with Hawking radiation, it is now tempting to search for a gravitational horizon that is responsible for such a temperature, using the following Ansatz relating the temperature and the horizon radius
R_{H}
:

\beta=2\pi R_{H}.

In the context of our FLRW-metric it seems most natural to consider the
apparent horizon
, discussed in detail at several places in the literature (see for instance
[
18
]
or the text book
[
11
]
and references therein) and for which such a relation is argued to hold in
[
7
]
. It is found that the radius of such an apparent horizon is given in terms of the scale function
a(t)
and spatial curvature
\kappa
by

R_{H}=\left(\left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right)^{2}+\frac{\kappa}{a^{2}}\right)^{-1/2}

The above linear interpolation of the scale change can also be used to approximate the radius of the apparent horizon in the FLRW-universe under consideration. In the spatially flat case we have that
R_{H}=|a/\dot{a}|
, so that if
a
is constant (and equal to
a_{1}
or
a_{2}
) the horizon is absent (
i.e.

R_{H}\to\infty
). Instead in the time interval of scale change we compute from the above linear approximation (
36
) that in the middle of that interval

R_{H}\sim 2a_{1}^{2}a_{2}(a_{1}+a_{2})/(2(a_{2}-a_{1})b)\sim a_{1}^{2}a_{2}/b

using once again that
a_{2}\gg a_{1}
. The agreement with the temperature found in the particle production profile for blackbody radiation is striking. When combined with the already found reciprocal relation between the frequency threshold
\lambda_{M}
and the duration
\Delta t
of the time interval of scale change, this suggests that the radiation is strongly connected to the apparent horizons that are present during these very time intervals.
Moreover, our rigorous framework supports the claims in
[
7
]
who applied the Parikh-Wilczek tunneling method —whose applicability in this context was criticized in
[
9
]
— to the apparent horizon to find the same relation between temperature and
R_{H}
as we do.
It is interesting to note that also radiation from accelerating observers, due to the Unruh effect, involves the presence of an apparent horizon.

4.
Outlook

We have considered particle creation both in the case of a long background electric field pulse and a gravitational pulse in an FLRW-spacetime. We have derived probabilities for particle production in terms of the pertinent Bogolyubov coefficients, which were expressed in terms of connecting formulas for the hypergeometric functions and the Heun functions, respectively. In that sense, at the conceptual level the two cases are very similar, however, the resulting outcomes in terms of the probability amplitudes differ. Let us summarize these differences here.

For the electric field pulse, we stress that the coupling to the scalar field is via the electromagnetic vector potential, which has a
\tanh
-profile. Also there is spatial anisotropy, since the electric field is a non-zero constant only in one the three directions.
Concerning the output for the electric field, the resulting particle production can be expressed in terms of the 3-momenta, while the slope parameter
b
appears in terms of the duration of the interval.

For the gravitational field pulse, the coupling is via the scale function, and is isotropic in the spatial directions. In fact, the whole spatial geometry is scaled uniformly. Nevertheless, from the Heun equation we derive particle production to happen with a lower frequency threshold, above which it can be approximated by blackbody radiation. We identified an apparent horizon that is present during the time interval of scale change and which could be related to the temperature of the radiation. Note that a similar relation between particle production and transition time has been found in the context of de Sitter space, see
[
3
]
and the further analysis in
[
14
]
.

In order to further elucidate the role that is played by the time interval of scale change for particle production in the FLRW-model considered in Section
3
, we include in Appendix
A
the analogous derivation for another FLRW-spacetime. The corresponding scale function describes an adiabatic change of scale, and, in fact, the limit of a long gravitational field pulse limit coincides with the adiabatic limit of slow change. We find that in that limit particle production is suppressed. This is further support for the claim that there is a close relation between the particle production and the time interval of scale change.

Let us compare the results of this paper to our previous works
[
25
,
10
]
where we applied heat kernel methods to derive particle production in a gravitational background. There, the use of Euclidean methods corresponds to the consideration of a thermal equilibrium state, the so-called Hartle–Hawking state. Instead, in the present work we consider the vacuum state in FLRW (at scale
a(\pm\infty)=a_{2}
) but by an observer in a spacetime at scale
a_{1}
. The formalism is different and uses canonical quantization, and Bogolyubov transformations between the in- and outgoing vector states; this makes it difficult to compare these results directly. However, one may be tempted to relate the radiation from the apparent horizon to the existence of a thermal state, very much as the one obtained in Euclidean methods. However, a full analysis and physical consideration of these two approaches goes beyond the scope of the present work and we leave it for future study.

One other interesting aspect of our approach with a gravitational pulse width going to infinity is that it resembles a static situation, where, however, the radiation comes from the switch-on and switch-off of the scale factor change in a finite time interval in the infinite past or future and the associated appearance of an apparent horizon. In a similar vein, it would be interesting to consider the case of gravitational pair production as in
[
25
,
10
]
for black holes, e.g. Hawking radiation, and potentially other stellar remnants, to see which role the switch-on of the matter configuration and the appearance of apparent horizons plays in this case. After all, there is no truly static spacetime in the real universe and every object needs to form first—often on time scales much faster than their lifetimes.

Appendix A
Particle creation in long gravitational pulse

We may also consider a scale function in an FLRW-spacetime which is similar to the expression considered in the context of the Schwinger effect in
2
. In contrast to the scale function considered in
3.2
there is only one parameter
b
that controls both the length of the time interval,
i.e.

[-1/b,1/b]
, and the rate of change (which is proportional to
b
). Concretely, we may take similar to (
8
):

(37)

a(\tau)=\left(a_{2}^{4}-\frac{\left(a_{2}^{4}-a_{1}^{4}\right)}{\cosh^{2}(b\tau/2)}\right)^{1/4},

which changes scale from
a_{2}
at
-\infty
then exponentially decreases to
a_{1}
, and then returns to
a_{2}
at
+\infty
. Note that in the limit
b\to 0
the time interval becomes infinitely long, while the rate of change becomes adiabatic.

Let us then analyze the differential equation (
14
) for the above
a(\tau)
. A useful coordinate transformation is
u=e^{b\tau}
so that it becomes:

(38)

\psi_{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}+u^{-1}\psi_{\lambda}^{\prime}+\lambda^{2}b^{-2}\left(\frac{a_{2}^{4}}{u^{2}}-\frac{4(a_{2}^{4}-a_{1}^{4})}{u(1+u)^{2}}\right)\psi_{\lambda}=0,

where the prime denotes a derivative with respect to
u
.

Let us analyze the behavior of the differential equation (
38
) at the singular points
u=0
and
u=-1
.
At
u=0
the leading behavior is then

\psi_{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}+u^{-1}\psi_{\lambda}^{\prime}+\lambda^{2}b^{-2}u^{-2}a_{2}^{4}\psi_{\lambda}=0;\qquad(u\sim 0)

with solutions near
u=0
given by
\psi_{\lambda}\sim u^{\pm c}
where
c=i|\lambda|b^{-1}a_{2}^{2}
. In order to account for the positive frequency condition at
u=0
we take
\psi_{\lambda}\sim u^{-c}
.

At
u=-1
we find leading behavior

\psi_{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}+4\lambda^{2}b^{-2}(a_{2}^{4}-a_{1}^{4})\frac{1}{(1+u)^{2}}\psi_{\lambda}=0;\qquad(u\sim-1)

with solutions given by
\psi_{\lambda}\sim(1+u)^{d}
where

d(d-1)=-4\lambda^{2}b^{-2}(a_{2}^{4}-a_{1}^{4})

This suggests the following Ansatz for solutions of (
38
) of the form

\psi_{\lambda}(u)=u^{-c}(1+u)^{d}h(u)

where then
h(u)
is found to satisfy the following differential equation:

(39)

h^{\prime\prime}(u)+\left(\frac{1-2c}{u}+\frac{2d}{1+u}\right)h^{\prime}(u)+\frac{(d-2c)d}{u(1+u)}h(u)=0.

Again, this is a hypergeometric equation and a solution of (
38
) is given by

\psi_{\lambda}(u)=u^{-c}(1+u)^{d}{}_{2}F_{1}(d,-2c+d,1-2c;-u).

The properties of the hypergeometric function ensure that for this choice,
\psi_{\lambda}
behaves as
u^{-c_{-}}
at
u\sim 0
,
i.e.
the positive frequency solution at
\tau\to-\infty
.

Similar to the derivation of (
11
), the connecting formulas (
10
) for the hypergeometric function can be applied to relate the solution at
u=0
to the ones at
u=\infty
. In the present case, this yields:

\displaystyle\psi_{\lambda}\sim\frac{\Gamma(1-2c)\Gamma(-2c)}{\Gamma(-2c+d)\Gamma(1-2c-d)}u^{-c}+\frac{\Gamma(1-2c)\Gamma(2c)}{\Gamma(d)\Gamma(1-d)}u^{c}=:\alpha_{\lambda}u^{-c}+\beta_{\lambda}u^{c},

in terms of the respective positive and negative frequency modes as
\tau\to+\infty
. The ratio of these Bogolyubov coefficients can now be computed and we find for their absolute value:

\frac{|\beta_{\lambda}|^{2}}{|\alpha_{\lambda}|^{2}}=\frac{\sin^{2}\pi d}{\sin^{2}\pi d-\sin^{2}2\pi c},

where
c=i\lambda b^{-1}a_{2}^{2}
and
d=\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{1/4-4(a_{2}^{4}-a_{1}^{4})\lambda^{2}b^{-2}}
.
For large
\lambda
we have
d\sim 1/2+2i\lambda b^{-1}\sqrt{a_{2}^{4}-a_{1}^{4}}
so that this behaves as

\frac{|\beta_{\lambda}|^{2}}{|\alpha_{\lambda}|^{2}}\sim\frac{\cosh^{2}\left(2\pi\lambda b^{-1}(\sqrt{a_{2}^{4}-a_{1}^{4}})\right)}{\cosh^{2}\left(2\pi\lambda b^{-1}(\sqrt{a_{2}^{4}-a_{1}^{4}})\right)+\sinh^{2}\left(4\pi\lambda b^{-1}a_{2}^{2}\right)}\sim e^{-4\pi\lambda b^{-1}(a_{2}^{2}-\sqrt{a_{2}^{4}-a_{1}^{4}})}

If we write this as
\exp(-\beta\omega_{\lambda})
in terms of inverse temperature
\beta
and the outgoing frequencies
\omega_{\lambda}=|\lambda|/a_{2}
, we conclude that the probabilities
P_{n}(\lambda)
in (
21
) behave as for blackbody radiation with
\beta=4\pi a_{2}(a_{2}^{2}-\sqrt{a_{2}^{4}-a_{1}^{4}})b^{-1}
. In fact, we also have for large
\lambda
that

n(\lambda)=|\beta_{\lambda}|^{2}\sim e^{-\beta\omega_{\lambda}}.

In contrast to the Schwinger effect, as well as to the FLRW-model considered in
3
, we find that in the limit of a long gravitational pulse given by (
37
) for
b\to 0
—which is also the adiabatic limit— the particle production is exponentially damped.

Appendix B
Particle creation in a ‘bouncing’ universe

We may also consider a universe where a collapse is followed by an expansion. Recall that a collapse from
a_{2}
to
a_{1}
is described by the scale function

(40)

a(u)=\left(a_{2}^{4}+u\left[(a_{1}^{4}-a_{2}^{4})(u+1)+\gamma\right](u+1)^{-2}\right)^{1/4}

where as before
u=e^{b\tau}
and
b,\gamma
are some parameters. We glue the
\tau
-interval
[-\infty,\infty]
(labeled by I) to another such interval (labeled by II), again from
[-\infty,\infty]
but now with the scale function
a(u^{-1})
. In other words, the scale function in the second interval is given by the same formula
a(u)
but with
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
interchanged. This describes an expansion from
a_{1}
to
a_{2}
which is the exact mirror image of the previous collapse. We will exploit this symmetry in deriving and then composing the Bogolyubov transformations.

The scale function in (
40
) for interval I has been considered in
[
19
, Section 2.8]
, where it was shown that incoming positive frequency solutions
\psi_{\lambda}^{+}\sim u^{-c_{2}}
(at
u\sim 0
) with
c_{2}=i|\lambda|a_{2}^{2}
evolve to the solution

\psi_{\lambda}^{+}\sim\alpha^{I}_{\lambda}u^{-c_{1}}+\beta^{I}_{\lambda}u^{c_{1}},\qquad(u\sim\infty),

where now
c_{1}=i|\lambda|a_{1}^{2}
. The Bogolyubov coefficients are given by Eq. (2.114) in
loc.cit.
:

\displaystyle\alpha^{I}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{a_{1}}{a_{2}}\right)\frac{\Gamma(-2c_{1})\Gamma(1-2c_{2})}{\Gamma(d-c_{1}-c_{2})\Gamma(1-c_{1}-c_{2}-d)}

\displaystyle\beta^{I}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{a_{1}}{a_{2}}\right)\frac{\Gamma(2c_{1})\Gamma(1-2c_{2})}{\Gamma(d+c_{1}-c_{2})\Gamma(1+c_{1}-c_{2}-d)}.

where
d
solves
d(d-1)=\gamma\lambda^{2}/b^{2}
.

Interestingly, if we would have started with an incoming
negative
frequency solution
\psi_{\lambda}^{-}\sim u^{c_{2}}
we can follow the same computation with
c_{2}
replaced by
-c_{2}
, so that

\psi_{\lambda}^{-}\sim\gamma^{I}_{\lambda}u^{-c_{1}}+\delta^{I}_{\lambda}u^{c_{1}},\qquad(u\sim\infty),

where now

\displaystyle\gamma^{I}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{a_{1}}{a_{2}}\right)\frac{\Gamma(-2c_{1})\Gamma(1+2c_{2})}{\Gamma(d-c_{1}+c_{2})\Gamma(1-c_{1}+c_{2}-d)}

\displaystyle\delta^{I}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{a_{1}}{a_{2}}\right)\frac{\Gamma(2c_{1})\Gamma(1+2c_{2})}{\Gamma(d+c_{1}+c_{2})\Gamma(1+c_{1}+c_{2}-d)}.

For the interval II the scale function is
a(u^{-1})
, or, equivalently, it is (
40
) with
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
interchanged. But then the same applies to the Bogolyubov transformations, to yield that positive/negative frequency solutions
\psi_{\lambda}^{\pm}\sim u^{\mp c_{1}}
(at
u\sim 0
) evolve to a combination:

\displaystyle\psi^{+}\sim\alpha^{II}_{\lambda}u^{-c_{2}}+\beta^{II}_{\lambda}u^{c_{2}},\qquad(u\sim\infty),

\displaystyle\psi^{-}\sim\gamma^{II}_{\lambda}u^{-c_{2}}+\delta^{II}_{\lambda}u^{c_{2}},\qquad(u\sim\infty).

with

\displaystyle\alpha^{II}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{a_{2}}{a_{1}}\right)\frac{\Gamma(1-2c_{1})\Gamma(-2c_{2})}{\Gamma(d-c_{1}-c_{2})\Gamma(1-c_{1}-c_{2}-d)},

\displaystyle\beta^{II}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{a_{2}}{a_{1}}\right)\frac{\Gamma(1-2c_{1})\Gamma(2c_{2})}{\Gamma(d-c_{1}+c_{2})\Gamma(1-c_{1}+c_{2}-d)}.

and

\displaystyle\gamma^{II}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{a_{2}}{a_{1}}\right)\frac{\Gamma(1+2c_{1})\Gamma(-2c_{2})}{\Gamma(d+c_{1}-c_{2})\Gamma(1+c_{1}-c_{2}-d)}

\displaystyle\delta^{II}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{a_{2}}{a_{1}}\right)\frac{\Gamma(1+2c_{1})\Gamma(2c_{2})}{\Gamma(d+c_{1}+c_{2})\Gamma(1+c_{1}+c_{2}-d)}.

P_{1}(\lambda)

The particle creation obtained from this bounce by gluing the intervals I (collapse) and II (expansion) corresponds to the composition of these Bogolyubov coefficients. Indeed, if we start with an incoming positive frequency
f_{\lambda}^{+}
at
\tau\to-\infty
in interval I, this mode becomes a linear combination of positive and negative frequency modes at
\tau\to\infty

f_{\lambda}^{+}=\left(\alpha_{\lambda}^{I}\alpha_{\lambda}^{II}+\beta_{\lambda}^{I}\gamma_{\lambda}^{II}\right)u^{-c_{2}}+\left(\alpha_{\lambda}^{I}\beta_{\lambda}^{II}+\beta_{\lambda}^{I}\delta_{\lambda}^{II}\right)u^{c_{2}}=:\alpha_{\lambda}u^{-c_{2}}+\beta_{\lambda}u^{c_{2}}.
