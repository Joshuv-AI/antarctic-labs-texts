Development and external validation of a machine learning model for predicting post-CABG acute kidney injury: insights from MIMIC database and real-world cardiac surgery cohort
Authors: Siyuan Cheng, Shukai Ge, Xia Sun, Yang Zhang, Wei Wang, Jun Wei, Yun Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAcute kidney injury (AKI) is a severe complication following coronary artery bypass grafting(CABG) While machine learning models trained on large-scale intensive care unit (ICU) databases are increasingly prevalent, their ability to generalize to specialized surgical cohorts in real-world settings remains poorly characterized. This study aimed to develop a post-CABG AKI prediction model using general ICU data and rigorously evaluate its transferability to an independent cardiac surgery center.MethodsA retrospective dual-center study was conducted. The development cohort comprised 6,347 post-CABG patients from the MIMIC database. An independent external validation cohort included 534 patients from the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University (AHXY). Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression was employed for feature selection. Nine ML algorithms were benchmarked, with Random Forest selected as the optimal model. Performance was assessed using the area under the precision-recall curve (PR-AUC), area under the ROC curve (AUC), and decision curve analysis. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were used to interpret feature contributions.ResultsIn the MIMIC internal testing set, the Random Forest model demonstrated moderate discrimination, achieving a PR-AUC of 0.888 (95% CI: 0.871–0.904) and an AUC of 0.709. However, upon deployment in the external AHXY cohort, the model exhibited significant performance attenuation, with the PR-AUC dropping to 0.309 and the AUC decreasing to 0.548. This near-random predictive performance indicates a lack of direct real-world generalizability without further adaptation. SHAP analysis identified body weight, serum phosphate, and red cell distribution width as key predictors. The performance decay was attributed to significant population heterogeneity (e.g. BMI differences) and the specific pathophysiological milieu of cardiac surgery not fully captured by general ICU variables.ConclusionGeneral-purpose ICU models may face a substantial cross-domain transferability gap without local adaptation when applied to specialized sub-specialties like cardiac surgery across different demographic populations. Our findings suggest that direct deployment of such models carries clinical risks and highlight the imperative for localized model recalibration or transfer learning to account for domain-specific variations before clinical adoption.

Introduction

Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) remains the gold standard treatment for complex multi-vessel coronary artery disease, with millions of procedures performed annually worldwide [1]. Despite advancements in surgical techniques and perioperative management, acute kidney injury (AKI) continues to be one of the most frequent and devastating complications following CABG, occurring in up to 30% of patients [2]. Post-CABG AKI is independently associated with prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) stay, increased risk of chronic kidney disease progression, and a significantly higher rate of short- and long-term mortality [3, 4]. Early identification of high-risk patients is therefore paramount to implement preventive strategies and optimize clinical outcomes [5].

Traditional risk stratification tools, such as the EuroSCORE II and the Society of Thoracic Surgeons score, were primarily designed to predict mortality rather than organ-specific complications like AKI [6]. Furthermore, these conventional logistic regression-based models often struggle to capture the complex, non-linear interactions between multifaceted perioperative risk factors, including hemodynamic fluctuations, systemic inflammatory response, and nephrotoxic exposures [7].

The emergence of machine learning (ML) has revolutionized clinical predictive modeling, offering superior performance in processing high-dimensional data from Electronic Health Records (EHRs) [8]. Unlike traditional models, advanced ML algorithms (e.g., tree-based ensembles) can implicitly model these non-linear pathophysiological events by identifying complex multidimensional patterns within routinely monitored physiological trends and laboratory markers, effectively using them as proxies for hemodynamic instability and systemic inflammation. Large-scale databases, most notably the MIMIC, have provided a robust foundation for developing sophisticated general-purpose AKI prediction algorithms [9]. The MIMIC database was selected for model development and internal validation in this study because it provides a highly granular, large-scale, and publicly accessible repository of critical care data, serving as an internationally recognized benchmark for training baseline clinical AI models.However, a critical gap persists between algorithmic development and clinical implementation [10]. Most models trained on general ICU populations fail to account for the unique pathophysiological milieu of cardiac surgery-characterized by cardiopulmonary bypass-induced ischemia-reperfusion injury and significant fluid shifts [11, 12]. Consequently, there is growing concern regarding whether an algorithm optimized on general ICU data can maintain its predictive integrity when deployed in a specialized cardiac surgery center.

In this study, we aimed to bridge this gap by developing a ML-based framework for post-CABG AKI prediction using a large-scale cohort from the MIMIC database (N = 6,347). Crucially, we extended beyond internal validation by testing the model’s performance in a real-world, independent external cohort from the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University (N = 534). Furthermore, we utilized SHAP to deconstruct the “black box” of the optimal model, identifying the core drivers of renal risk. By rigorously evaluating the model’s cross-domain transferability and identifying the performance decay during sub-specialty migration, this study provides critical insights into the necessity of localized recalibration for AI-driven clinical decision support systems in cardiac surgery.

Methods

Data source

This study utilized a dual-center design comprising a large-scale public database for model development and a retrospective real-world cohort for external validation. The development cohort was derived from the MIMIC database (versions IV), a freely accessible critical care database comprising de-identified health-related data associated with over 40,000 patients admitted to the critical care units of the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (Boston, MA, USA). Access to the database was granted after completion of the National Institutes of Health web-based training course “Protecting Human Research Participants” (certification number: 68561984). The external validation cohort was collected from the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University (AHXY). This dataset included granular perioperative data from patients undergoing cardiac surgery at the Department of Cardiothoracic Surgery. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University (No. XYFY2025-KLIS4-01 from The Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University)., and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Study population

The study focused on adult patients who underwent CABG. To ensure robustness and comparability, identical inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to both the development cohort (MIMIC Database) and validation cohort (Fig. 1). Patients from the MIMIC-IV database who underwent CABG were retrospectively identified using International Classification of Diseases procedure codes (ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-PCS). Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) evidence of pre-existing end-stage renal disease or requirement for chronic renal replacement therapy prior to admission; (2) ICU length of stay less than 24 hours, as AKI diagnosis within this timeframe may be confounded by immediate postoperative fluctuations; or (3) significant missingness in key baseline variables (>30%). Ultimately, 6,347 eligible patients were included in the final development cohort. While the exclusion of a significant proportion of initial CABG patients (approximately 80% in the MIMIC cohort and 90% in the AHXY cohort) restricts the immediate “real-world” breadth of the dataset, this rigorous filtering was deemed methodologically necessary. It ensured baseline data completeness and strict adherence to precise, time-bound KDIGO AKI diagnostic criteria, removing noisy or insufficiently documented cases that could severely confound algorithmic training.Fig. 1Flowchart of patient selection

Flowchart of patient selection

This cohort was randomly partitioned into a training set (70%, N = 4,443) for model derivation and an internal testing set (30%, N = 1,904) for internal validation. For external validation, patients who underwent CABG at the Department of Cardiothoracic Surgery, Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University, between January 2013 and December 2023 were consecutively enrolled. Applying the same exclusion criteria, 534 patients formed the independent external validation cohort.

Data extraction and definitions

To ensure model generalizability, variables commonly available in both the MIMIC and AHXY databases were selected. Demographic characteristics, preoperative comorbidities, preoperative laboratory values, and intraoperative details were extracted. Rigorous quality control was performed during data preprocessing. Variables with a missing rate exceeding 30% were excluded. For remaining variables with missing values, the MissForest algorithm,a non-parametric imputation method based on Random Forest,was employed to impute missing data, which has been shown to outperform traditional mean/median imputation in medical datasets [13].

Study endpoint

The primary endpoint was the development of postoperative AKI within 7 days after surgery. AKI was defined according to the globally accepted kidney disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) clinical practice guidelines. Specifically, AKI was diagnosed if any of the following criteria were met: 1.Increase in serum creatinine (SCr) by ≥0.3 mg/dL (≥26.5 μmol/L) within 48 hours; 2. Increase in SCr to ≥1.5 times baseline, which is known or presumed to have occurred within the prior 7 days; 3. Urine volume < 0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 hours [14]. Baseline creatinine was defined as the most recent outpatient SCr value within 90 days pre-admission or the admission SCr if outpatient data were unavailable.

Statistical analysis

Given the high dimensionality of EHR data (comprising over 80 initial candidate variables), we utilized the LASSO regression to minimize overfitting and eliminate redundant features. Although LASSO is a linear, additive selection procedure, it was strategically employed here as a first-pass filter to aggressively eliminate highly collinear and uninformative noise variables. By retaining 27 non-zero features, we preserved sufficient feature space diversity, allowing the subsequent tree-based and boosting algorithms to naturally capture and model the non-linear effects and interactions among these critical variables. The optimal penalization coefficient (λ) was determined using 10-fold cross-validation based on the minimum plus one standard error rule, resulting in the selection of 27 robust predictors.

The MIMIC cohort was randomly partitioned into a training set (70%) and an internal testing set (30%). Nine ML algorithms were trained and compared: including Random Forest, XGBoost, SVM, LightGBM, Logistic Regression, K-Nearest Neighbors, Elastic Net, Naive Bayes and Neural Network. Hyperparameters were optimized using grid search with 5-fold cross-validation.Specifically, tree-based models (Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM) underwent exhaustive grid search over key parameters (e.g., number of estimators, max depth, learning rate). For the Neural Network, to maintain computational feasibility while ensuring a fair comparison, a simplified multi-layer perceptron architecture was utilized alongside a randomized search over a restricted hyperparameter grid (e.g., limited hidden layers and learning rates).

Performance was assessed using AUC and, given the class imbalance of AKI (incidence≈24%), special emphasis was placed on the PR-AUC. Calibration was evaluated using calibration plots. Clinical utility was determined via decision curve analysis (DCA) to calculate the net benefit across distinct threshold probabilities.

To overcome the “black-box” nature of ML models, SHAP was employed. Subsequently, global feature importance was calculated and SHAP dependence plots were generated to visualize the marginal contribution of each feature to the AKI risk prediction.

Baseline characteristics were compared using the Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables (depending on data distribution), and the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.2). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Feature selection and model benchmarking

To address the high dimensionality of the electronic health record data and mitigate the risk of overfitting, we employed LASSO regression. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the model effectively compressed the feature space. Using the λmin criterion to maximize information retention while removing redundant noise, we identified 27 robust predictors from the initial pool of variables. Predictive performance in the development cohort

Among the nine algorithms evaluated, the Random Forest classifier exhibited the most robust performance across cross-validation folds (Fig. 3A), effectively balancing bias and variance. While the Random Forest model provided only a modest incremental benefit in overall discrimination compared to the traditional Logistic Regression model (median AUC 0.709 vs. ≈0.68, ΔAUC ≈ 0.03), it exhibited slightly greater stability across cross-validation folds. Furthermore, tree-based architectures inherently capture non-linear variable interactions—which are prevalent in high-dimensional perioperative data—without requiring the manual a priori specification of complex interaction terms. Given these pragmatic methodological advantages, alongside its native compatibility with SHAP for non-linear feature attribution, Random Forest was selected as the primary model for final evaluation. While the model retained basic predictive directionality (AUC > 0.5), this attenuation highlights the substantial cross-domain transferability gap attributable to the pathophysiological heterogeneity and vast demographic shift between general ICU patients and specific cardiac surgery populations described in Sect. 3.1.

Discussion

A machine learning framework for predicting AKI following CABG was successfully developed and validated using a large-scale intensive care database. The principal finding of this study is twofold: while the Random Forest algorithm demonstrated moderate discrimination (AUC 0.709) and robust PR-AUC within the development cohort derived from MIMIC, its performance exhibited significant attenuation when deployed in an independent external cardiac surgery cohort (AUC decreased to 0.548). Approaching the 0.5 threshold of random chance, this near-random external performance legitimately raises profound concerns regarding the direct generalization and immediate real-world applications of the model. However, this stark contrast does not negate the potential of machine learning; rather, it quantifiably reveals a critical out-of-distribution domain shift gap often overlooked in translational medicine. Results underscore that algorithms trained on general-purpose ICU data, despite their high internal validity, may struggle to generalize to specific sub-specialties like cardiac surgery without adaptationImportantly, an algorithm with moderate baseline discrimination (AUC~0.71) is intrinsically more vulnerable to severe performance degradation when exposed to uncalibrated external populations. This highlights the potential clinical risks of blindly applying off-the-shelf AI models and confirms the absolute necessity of localized recalibration or domain adaptation before clinical implementation.

The observed performance discrepancy can be partially explored through the lens of feature importance and potential pathophysiological mismatch, though the model’s modest baseline discrimination limits our ability to draw definitive mechanistic conclusions. SHAP analysis identified body weight, serum phosphate, and red cell distribution width (RDW) as top predictors. First, the role of body weight illustrates a potential signal misinterpretation. In the general ICU population (MIMIC), higher weight often correlates with better survival reserves—the “obesity paradox.” Conversely, in the CABG cohort [15], elevated BMI is frequently associated with metabolic syndrome and higher perioperative risks, leading the model to misinterpret this protective/risk signal [16–18]. Second, the etiology of AKI in MIMIC is highly heterogeneous (e.g., sepsis, nephrotoxins) [19, 20], whereas post-CABG AKI involves a unique pathophysiological milieu characterized by cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB)-induced hemolysis, ischemia-reperfusion injury, and non-pulsatile perfusion [21–23]. While the general model successfully captured systemic inflammatory signals (e.g., elevated RDW), it lacked the specificity to detect the distinct hemodynamic hits intrinsic to cardiac surgery [24, 25]. Nevertheless, the consistent association between elevated serum phosphate and AKI risk across cohorts suggests that certain metabolic markers may provide useful, albeit incomplete, prognostic signals [26, 27].

Our findings resonate with recent landmark studies questioning the generalizability of medical AI. Wong et al. [28] externally validated a widely used sepsis prediction model and similarly reported a substantial decline in accuracy (AUC 0.70) across different hospital systems, identifying data heterogeneity as a primary barrier to deployment. In contrast, Tseng et al. [29] developed a cardiac-surgery-specific AKI model that achieved high performance (AUC > 0.80) by incorporating granular intraoperative variables (e.g., CPB duration). Similarly, a recent study by Shotwell et al. [30] evaluating an AKI prediction model across broad surgical cohorts demonstrated that while broad models offer general utility, they frequently underperform compared to customized models in highly specific surgical sub-populations. This reinforces our hypothesis that domain-specific features are essential for high-precision tasks. Furthermore, unlike studies that relied solely on internal random-split validation within MIMIC, such as Luo et al. [31], who reported an AUC of 0.85, our inclusion of a real-world external validation set provides a more honest and rigorous assessment. It demonstrates that models built purely on aggregated big data, without accounting for domain shifts, may be insufficient for precision medicine without proper domain adaptation techniques.

Limitations must be considered while interpreting this study. First, retrospective design increases selection bias. Specifically, the exclusion of approximately 80% of the initial MIMIC cohort and 90% of the AHXY cohort inevitably introduces significant selection issues. While this rigorous exclusion was methodologically mandated to ensure strict adherence to precise KDIGO criteria and baseline data completeness (as detailed in Sect. 2.2), it results in a highly curated sub-population. Consequently, the model’s performance may not fully represent the broader, unselected ‘real-world’ CABG population, where missing data, irregular testing intervals, and non-standardized documentation are common clinical realities. MIMIC’s CABG patients were identified using ICD codes, which may have eliminated granular perioperative characteristics like aortic cross-clamp time from the external cohort to maintain feature uniformity, limiting the model’s possible upper limit. The inability to adjust for crucial intraoperative variables—such as off-pump versus on-pump techniques, bypass and clamp times, intraoperative ultrafiltration, and preoperative ejection fraction—significantly limits the model’s ability to predict AKI driven by direct surgical factors. Second, the demographic and epidemiological divergence between the training set (US population) and the validation set (East Asian population) involves a major distribution shift. This profound difference in baseline characteristics (as seen in the disparate prevalence of diabetes and BMI profiles) is likely a primary driver of the model’s external failure, perhaps even more so than the ICU-to-surgery transition [32]. This failure to generalize suggests that transfer learning mechanisms should be studied instead of universal models. A feasible strategy to get high-performance, site-specific clinical decision assistance is to pre-train the MIMIC-derived model and fine-tune it with a smaller dataset of local heart surgery cases.

Conclusion

In conclusion, while machine learning algorithms derived from massive, general-purpose ICU databases (MIMIC) can achieve moderate internal validity for predicting post-CABG AKI, this study demonstrates a critical vulnerability in their cross-domain transferability. The substantial performance attenuation observed in the external cardiac surgery cohort underscores that one-size-fits-all models cannot replace context-specific validation. The distinct pathophysiological features of cardiac surgery, coupled with profound population demographic heterogeneity, necessitate that future implementation strategies prioritize local recalibration and transfer learning rather than assuming broad generalizability from out-of-the-box tools. Only by bridging the gap between algorithmic capability and local clinical reality can we safely integrate AI-driven decision support into precision perioperative care.
