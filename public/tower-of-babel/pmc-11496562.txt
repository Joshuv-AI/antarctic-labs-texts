Estimating Excess Mortality During the COVID-19 Pandemic Between 2020–2022 in Korea
Authors: Minjeong Jang, Soyoung Kim, Sunhwa Choi, Boyeong Ryu, So Young Choi, Siwon Choi, Misuk An, Seong-Sun Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe persistent coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic has had direct and indirect effects on mortality, making it essential to analyze excess mortality to fully understand the impact of the pandemic. In this study, we constructed a mathematical model using number of deaths from Statistics Korea and analyzed excess mortality between 2020 and 2022 according to age, sex, and dominant severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 variant period.MethodsNumber of all-cause deaths between 2010 and 2022 were obtained from the annual cause-of-death statistics provided by Statistics Korea. COVID-19 mortality data were acquired from the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency. A multivariate linear regression model with seasonal effect, stratified by sex and age, was used to estimate the number of deaths in the absence of COVID-19. The estimated excess mortality rate was calculated.ResultsExcess mortality was not significant between January 2020 and October 2021. However, it started to increase monthly from November 2021 and reached its highest point during the omicron-dominant period. Specifically, in March and April 2022, during the omicron BA.1/BA.2-dominant period, the estimated median values for excess mortality were the highest at 17,634 and 11,379, respectively. Both COVID-19-related deaths and excess mortality increased with age. A notable increase in excess mortality was observed in individuals aged ≥ 65 years. In the context of excess mortality per 100,000 population based on the estimated median values in March 2022, the highest numbers were found among males and females aged ≥ 85 years at 1,048 and 910, respectively.ConclusionThis study revealed that the prolonged COVID-19 pandemic coupled with its high transmissibility not only increased COVID-19-related deaths but also had a significant impact on overall mortality rates, especially in the elderly. Therefore, it is crucial to concentrate healthcare resources and services on the elderly and ensure continued access to healthcare services during pandemics. Establishing an excess mortality monitoring system in the early stages of a pandemic is necessary to understand the impact of infectious diseases on mortality and effectively evaluate pandemic response policies.

INTRODUCTION

Considering the decreasing trend in coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19)-related deaths, hospitalizations, and admissions to intensive care units along with the high level of population immunity to severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), the World Health Organization (WHO) finally declared an end to COVID-19 as a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) in May 2023. This was approximately three years and four months after its declaration as a PHEIC in January 2020.1 As of October 2023, a cumulative total of 771,549,718 confirmed COVID-19 cases and 6,974,473 COVID-19-related deaths, which has resulted in significant social and economic consequences, had been reported worldwide.23 In South Korea, 34,571,873 confirmed COVID-19 cases and 35,934 COVID-19-related deaths were reported as of August 31, 2023.4

The number of COVID-19-related deaths reported during the pandemic varied depending on the capacity for laboratory confirmation testing and number of unreported cases within the surveillance system. With several reasons, the number of deaths attributed to COVID-19 during the pandemic may have been underestimated.56 Furthermore, the prolonged COVID-19 pandemic may have implications not only for COVID-19-related deaths but also for overall mortality. Non-COVID-19-related deaths of patients who were not provided adequate treatment due to the limited capacity of the healthcare system also affected the death toll.78 To assess the direct and indirect effects of an infectious disease pandemic on mortality, it is necessary to analyze excess mortality.

Excess mortality is defined as the difference between the total number of deaths for a given period and the number that would have been expected in the absence of a crisis.9 This is often used to evaluate the impact of infectious disease outbreaks and intervention policies.561011 In South Korea, excess mortality was monitored by calculating the difference between the maximum number of deaths in the past three years and the actual number of deaths and has been reported monthly since August 2020 by Statistics Korea.11

During the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 and 2021, the WHO reported an estimated excess mortality as 133–166 million worldwide.5 Several articles have reported that South Korea responded to the initial COVID-19 pandemic by implementing strong interventional policies such as contact tracing, testing, and treatment.121314 South Korea reported neither a significant nor a minimal increase in mortality rates among the general population in 2020,71516 and excess mortality was not clearly identified or reported until 2021.1718 However, the analysis of excess mortality related to COVID-19, especially for 2022, when there were significant increases in the numbers of confirmed cases and related deaths, is insufficient.

SARS-CoV-2 exhibited changes in severity and infectivity characteristics as virus variants emerged.192021 The Korean government observed that the severity and fatality rates varied according to the dominant variant period.22 However, the trends and magnitude of excess mortality according to the dominant variant period has not been analyzed in South Korea. To understand the overall impact of COVID-19 mortality regarding the characteristics of the circulating virus, an analysis is needed to assess the occurrence and magnitude of excess mortality based on the severity difference of the viruses.

In this study, we aimed to quantify the direct and indirect impacts of COVID-19 on mortality over the three-year COVID-19 pandemic period (2020–2022). Specifically, we examined the monthly trends of COVID-19-related deaths and excess mortality due to COVID-19 in 2020–2022 by sex, age group, and dominant variant period.

METHODS

Data

The number of deaths (n = 3,745,724) in the annual cause-of-death statistics were retrieved from MicroData Integrated Service by Statistics Korea for the years 2010–2022 to analyze the mortality rates during the COVID-19 pandemic and for the development of excess mortality models. Mortality rates were calculated using the annual age-specific population data from Statistics Korea’s National Statistical Portal for the years 2017–2022. The standard population used for the age-standardized mortality rates was the average of the age-specific populations for the years 2017–2022. The age groups were categorized into 10-year intervals up to 79 years and 80 years and above. The baseline mortality rate before COVID-19 was calculated using the average yearly values.

In this study, “excess mortality” is defined as the difference between the total number of deaths during the COVID-19 pandemic period (2020–2022) and number of expected deaths in the absence of the COVID-19 pandemic. To calculate excess mortality, we developed an all-cause-death model using the cause-of-death statistics as mortality data from Statistics Korea from 2010 to 2019, and the model was projected to the end of 2022 to estimate the number of deaths in the absence of COVID-19. The total number of deaths for the excess mortality calculation was based on the number of deaths from 2020–2022. The analysis was conducted monthly and stratified by sex and age. Age groups were divided into four subgroups based on the age ranges provided in Statistics Korea’s excess mortality reports: Group 1 (≤ 14 years), Group 2 (15–64 years), Group 3 (65–84 years), and Group 4 (≥ 85 years).

The periods of dominance of the COVID-19 variants were classified according to the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) as pre-dominance period of the Delta variant (January 2020 to June 2021), dominance period of the Delta variant (July 2021 to December 2022), dominance period of the omicron BA.1 and BA.2 variants (January to June 2022), and dominance period of the omicron BA.5 variant (July to December 2022).22 This classification was determined by weekly detection rates of SARS-CoV-2 variants, with 50% of tested samples confirmed to contain the specific variant. In this study, the months including the starting weeks of these dominant variant periods were used as starting months.

In this study, ‘COVID-19-related death’ is as follows. COVID-19-related deaths included deaths of individuals who were determined to have died due to COVID-19 under Articles 11, 12, and 40–5 of the Infectious Disease Control and Prevention Act along with the assessment of medical professionals reported and confirmed in the KDCA COVID-19 Information Management System from January 20, 2020, to December 31, 2022.2324 Cases with external causes of death, such as traffic accidents or suicide, were excluded from the analysis.

The annual mortality rates from 2017 to 2022 were calculated to assess changes in mortality rates after COVID-19 onset. Using the direct age standardization method and adjusting for age-specific mortality rates, age-standardized mortality rates during the same period were also considered. We defined 2017–2019 as the pre-COVID-19 non-pandemic period and compared the mortality rates of this period with that of the COVID-19 pandemic period (2020–2022).

Mathematical model

An all-cause death model was constructed to predict the number of deaths in the absence of COVID-19 using the number of deaths data from 2010 to 2019. To identify the best model, we compared the Seasonal Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (SARIMA), Long-Short Time Memory (LSTM), and multivariate linear regression models. Among the three models, the multivariate linear regression model was chosen because of the lowest determination coefficient, R2, and interpretability of the model. The group-specific model determination coefficients for each model are presented in Supplementary Table 1 and the models with estimated parameters are described in Supplementary Fig. 1. This research was approved by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) Bioethics Committee, which waived the requirement for informed consent (2023-04-06).

RESULTS

During the COVID-19 pandemic period (2020–2022), the total number of deaths that occurred over the three years was 995,567. A total of 304,948 deaths occurred in 2020, 317,680 in 2021, and 372,939 in 2022. This exceeded the total number of deaths over the three years before the COVID-19 pandemic (2017–2019), which was 879,464.

Yearly mortality rates before and after the COVID-19 pandemic (2017–2022)

We compared mortality rates for three years before (2017–2019) and three years after (2020–2022) COVID-19 onset (Fig. 1). The crude mortality rates per 1,000 people in the pre-COVID-19 years were 5.6, 5.8, and 5.7 in 2017, 2018, and 2019, respectively. During the post-COVID-19 years, the rates per 1,000 people were 5.9, 6.2, and 7.3 in 2020, 2021, and 2022, respectively, showing a continuous increase after COVID-19 onset. When age was standardized, only the mortality rate in 2022 was higher than that in the pre-COVID-19 period (6.5 in 2022 compared with the average of 6.1 from 2017 to 2019).

Monthly excess mortality during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2022)

To examine excess mortality during the COVID-19 pandemic, we generated model-estimated values to assess the monthly excess mortality over the three years of COVID-19 (Fig. 2). Excess mortality was not significant between January 2020 and October 2021. A notable increase in excess mortality was observed since November 2021, with the highest levels occurring during the omicron BA.1/BA.2-dominant period, when the numbers of COVID-19 cases and deaths surged. In particular, March and April in 2022 had the highest median estimates for excess mortality at 17,634 and 11,379 deaths, respectively. Excess mortality during the omicron BA.5-dominant period was also higher than that during the delta-dominant period (July 2021 to December 2022). The overall trend of excess mortality was found to be closely related to the pattern of COVID-19-related deaths.

Age group and sex-specific excess mortality (2020–2022)

When excess mortality was analyzed by age group and sex, the older age groups experienced higher rates of excess mortality, with a remarkable increase in individuals aged ≥ 65 years (Fig. 3). Specifically, during the pre-delta-dominant period, excess mortality did not show significant results across all age groups. Since the delta-dominant period, excess mortality had been observed in all groups except in Group 1 (≤ 14 years).

Age group and sex-specific excess mortality (2020–2022). Age groups were divided into four subgroups based on the age ranges provided in Statistics Korea’s excess mortality reports: Group 1 (≤ 14 years), Group 2 (15–64 years), Group 3 (65–84 years), and Group 4 (≥ 85 years).

The highest rates of excess mortality were observed during the omicron BA.1/BA.2-dominant period, particularly in March 2022. Among the age groups, Groups 3 and 4 (≥ 65 years) had relatively higher excess mortality rates, with Group 4 (≥ 85 years) showing the highest rates. In March and April 2022, the months with the highest excess mortality rates, males in Group 3 (4,293 and 2,757 deaths) and females in Group 4 (6,054 and 3,753 deaths) had the maximum excess mortality. Since the end of the delta-dominant period, except for Group 1, all groups had excess mortality, which were higher than the COVID-19-related mortality rates reported in these age groups.

Age group and sex-specific excess mortality per 100,000 population (2020–2022)

Analyzing excess mortality per 100,000 population based on the median estimates revealed that both males and females in Group 4 (≥ 85 years) had the highest rates of excess mortality. Males in this age group, except in February 2021, consistently had a higher excess mortality rate during the entire COVID-19 pandemic period. By period, the highest excess mortality per 100,000 population for males (n = 117) and females (n = 70) in Group 3 (65–85 years) were observed in March 2022. In Group 4, males (n = 1,048) and females (n = 910) had the highest excess mortality per 100,000 population during the omicron BA.1/BA.2-dominant period in March 2022 (Table 1).

P-scores in each group and epidemic period (2020–2022)

The P-score during the COVID-19 period was calculated as approximately 8.8 (Table 2). This indicates that the number of observed deaths during the COVID-19 period was approximately 8.8% more than the number of predicted deaths. No significant difference was observed between the males and females. A negative P-score was observed in only Group 1 (−3.9) because the observed deaths in the group was smaller than the expected deaths. The older age group showed a higher P-score, and the P-score in Group 4 (17.0) was remarkably higher than that of the other age groups (3.5 in Group 2 and 6.0 in Group 3). When the omicron BA.1/BA.2 variant was the dominant strain, the P-score reached 24.4, which is the highest among all the P-scores obtained for the epidemic period.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we examined excess mortality by age group and sex over a three-year period during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2022). Our analysis revealed that the highest excess mortality and COVID-19-related deaths were observed during the omicron BA.1/BA.2-dominant period in 2022, and by age group, the elderly, especially those aged ≥ 85 years, experienced the highest burden of excess mortality and COVID-19-related deaths.

During the omicron-dominant period, although severity decreased, transmissibility increased, leading to a rapid escalation of the numbers of confirmed cases and COVID-19-related deaths.22 Regarding exploration of confirmed cases during the omicron-dominant period, excess mortality during this period may have included undiagnosed COVID-19 deaths. As the pattern of excess deaths observed in the study results was similar to that of the COVID-19-related deaths, the massive COVID-19 infection may have influenced the increased excess mortality rate. Age has been reported as an important risk factor for COVID-19 mortality and complications, which could have influenced mortality in the elderly population.26

Furthermore, the omicron variant had a high transmission rate, which led to an increase in the number of cases, not only among the general population but also among essential healthcare workers, caregivers, service industry employees, drivers, and public sector workers. This resulted in reduced social activities due to quarantine measures for these groups, causing disruption in the healthcare system and social services.2728 This could have impacted the accessibility of health services, especially by the elderly, a population that depends heavily on caregivers and has a higher medical facility utilization rate for the treatment of underlying conditions, leading to higher excess mortality in the ≥ 85 years age group. This also could have contributed to the overall increase in mortality.

In our analysis, we considered changes in population structures across different age groups and sexes and compared mortality outcomes across different virus variant periods to understand the impact on excess mortality by the characteristics of COVID-19. Notably, during the omicron-dominant period, the rates of both COVID-19-related and all-cause deaths were significantly higher among the elderly population. This indicates the impact of prolonged COVID-19 pandemic transmission characteristics and the combined effects of pandemic control policies. These results can serve as scientific evidence for decision-making in pandemic control, particularly for protecting vulnerable groups such as the elderly. Despite the relaxation of pandemic control measures based on the virus severity, the prolonged pandemic and increased transmission factors could lead to high mortality rates in high-risk groups, such as the elderly. Thus, it is essential to focus on medical responses and resources for high-risk groups to ensure continued access to healthcare services, especially during the pandemic.

Monitoring excess mortality is essential to assessing the impact of infectious disease pandemics.29 In the United Kingdom (March 2020) and the United States (February 2020), excess mortality was reported from the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic. Establishing robust monitoring systems for excess mortality supports assessing the health impact of infectious diseases on mortality, estimating the magnitude of infectious disease disasters, and evaluating pandemic control policies to better prepare for future pandemics.

While our study revealed valuable insights into excess mortality trends, further in-depth analyses of specific causes of death, place of death, and regional characteristics are necessary to understand the detailed circumstances behind excess mortality, assess the pandemic control measures, and identify gaps for improvement. Given the dependency of excess mortality results on analytical methods, caution is warranted in their interpretation. Therefore, comparing the results with those of alternative estimation methods and datasets can enhance the robustness of the findings.

In conclusion, the prolonged COVID-19 pandemic coupled with its high transmissibility did not only increase COVID-19-related deaths but also had a significant impact on overall mortality, particularly in the elderly. Therefore, it is crucial to concentrate healthcare resources and services on the elderly and ensure continued access to healthcare services. Establishing an excess mortality monitoring system in the early stages of a pandemic could support the understanding of the impact of infectious diseases on mortality and effectively evaluate pandemic response policies.
