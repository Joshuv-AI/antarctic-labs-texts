Clinical learning environment, supervision and teacher evaluation scale: psychometric evaluation of the Persian version
Authors: Hamideh Hakimi, Aziz Shamsi, Mansoureh Ashghali Farahani, Safoura Dorri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClinical education is pivotal in nursing education programs, with students’ feedback being crucial for enhancing educational quality. Hence, a reliable tool is essential to assess students’ perspectives. This study aimed to validate the Clinical Learning Environment, Supervision, and Nurse Teacher (CLES + T) scale from the viewpoint of students.MethodsThis methodological study was conducted between November 2016 and December 2018. The CLES + T underwent forward translation from English to Persian and back-translation to English. To ensure qualitative face validity, the questionnaire was reviewed by 10 nursing faculty professors in Iran. Quantitative face validity was determined by calculating the item impact index with the target group. Content validity was evaluated using the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) and Content Validity Index (CVI). Construct validity was established by administering the questionnaire to 427 nursing students, followed by confirmatory factor analysis. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient.ResultsThe findings show that the value of Cronbach’s alpha coefficient and composite reliability for research constructs is above 0.7. Therefore, the research constructs have a very favorable fit. Regarding structural validity, items 2, 3, and 13 were removed from the final questionnaire due to factor loadings below 0.4. Also, for two constructs, supervisory relationship and pedagogical atmosphere, a value lower than 0.5 was calculated, which is due to not obtaining a high AVE value. The value of the coefficient of determination for all the endogenous structures of the model are in the acceptable range, which indicates a very favorable fit of the structural model of the research.ConclusionsThe Persian version of the CLES + T tool exhibited appropriate validity and reliability, making it a valuable instrument for students to evaluate clinical mentors, supervisors, and the clinical environment.

Background

Nursing, as a profession predominantly practiced in the clinical environment [1], places great importance on clinical education within the nursing curriculum [2]. The clinical environment serves as a vital space for the development of clinical skills, fostering professional identity, and the application of theoretical as well as practical knowledge [3]. Through inpatient internships, students have the opportunity to integrate their psychomotor, cognitive, and emotional skills, thus enhancing their competency in the practical implementation of knowledge, skills, attitudes, and values within the clinical setting [4].

Iranian students are often trained in clinical environments that do not have enough facilities and equipment. Also, they do not have the support of nursing staff and they are less involved in clinical activities in the lower semesters, while they are exploited during the internship period [2]. Research in Iran has revealed that students perceive the clinical environment in diverse ways, with some describing it as supportive, pleasant, and positive [5], while others may perceive it as less favorable and lacking in support [2, 6, 7]. Communication quality between students, clinical instructors, and nursing staff significantly impacts students’ perception of the clinical environment [8, 9]. Factors such as unit culture, teamwork, and positive staff morale all contribute to students’ understanding of the clinical environment [10]. Conversely, challenging hierarchical structures, absence of nursing guidelines, and inadequate awareness of students’ learning needs can lead students to perceive the clinical environment as unsupportive [11]. Studies have identified key issues deemed important by students regarding clinical training, including insufficient attention to clinical education, lack of availability of an adequate number of clinical trainers, poor coordination between clinical and faculty training, limited hospital resources and infrastructure, unavailability of trainers to meet educational needs, and absence of incentives for students [11, 12].

The clinical learning environment is very important in the quality of clinical learning [2]. To optimize the outcomes of clinical education, it seems essential to evaluate and assess the clinical learning environments provided to nursing students. Numerous studies have been conducted using various tools to evaluate the clinical learning environment, including the Dundee Ready Education Environment Measure (DREEM), it measures the educational environment in health professional education programs, it has five subscales, Students’ perception of learning, Students’ perception of teachers, Students’ academic self-perceptions, Students’ perception of atmosphere, Students’ social self-perceptions [13]; Postgraduate Hospital Educational Environment Measure (PHEEM) that is a tool designed to evaluate the educational environment for postgraduate medical trainees. Three subscales that respectively address perceptions of role autonomy, teaching, and social support [14]; health education learning environment survey (HELES), it designed to assess the learning environment for medical students, it has six subscales: peer relationships, faculty relationships, work-life balance, clinical skills development, expectations, and educational setting and resources [15]; Clinical Learning Environment Inventory (CLEI) which is a survey instrument, designed to assess nursing students’ perceptions of their clinical learning environments, particularly in hospital; Personalization, Student Involvement, Task Orientation, Innovation [16], and Individualization and Undergraduate Clinical Education Environment Measure (UCEEM), it assess the clinical learning environment as perceived by undergraduate medical students, which consists of two overarching domains of experiential learning and social participation, with four subdomains of learning opportunities, preparedness, workplace interaction, and inclusion [17, 18]. These tools do not exclusively measure the persptives of nursing students; rather, they evaluate the medical education enviroment or are used generally for undergraduate students in various disciplines. Clinical learning occurs in a complex social context. Students are often faced with the instructor’s teaching method, the needs of patients, health care workers, and the clinical management model at the same time [2]. Therefore, a scale that can evaluate different aspects of the clinical environment is of particular importance. The Clinical Learning Environment, Supervision, and Nurse Teacher evaluation Scale (CLES + T) distinguishes itself from other questionnaires by evaluating areas such as nursing care and the leadership style of the ward manager (head nurse) and ward facilities. Accurate and valid measurement tools are necessary for reliable assessment providing accurate and trustworthy information. Therefore, it is crucial to identify the indicators that demonstrate the quality of clinical education and to develop a reliable tool to measure them. Saarikoski et al., based on literature reviews and experimental studies in 2002 and 2008, identified important factors influencing learning in hospitals and introduced a measurement tool called the Clinical Learning Environment, Supervision, and Nurse Teacher evaluation scale (CLES + T) [19, 20]. CLEST scale has been psychoanalyzed in different countries such as Spain, Italy, Greece, Czech Republic, Norway, Sweden, Poland, Slovakia, Dutch, Turkey and Albama [21–31].This scale assesses concepts such as the clinical environment, supervisory relationships, and the role of nurse teachers in clinical practice from the nursing students’ perspective, which have not been evaluated by other tools specific to the nursing clinical field. Given the absence of a valid tool in Iran to measure students’ perceptions of the clinical environment and clinical instructors, we aimed to translate the CLES + T tool, which has not been previously translated and validated in Iran, and evaluate its validity and reliability.

Methods

This methodological study was conducted between November 2016 and December 2018. Sampling was conducted from two universities: Iran University of Medical Sciences, a public institution, and Islamic Azad University, Lahijan Branch, a private institution. The inclusion criteria for the study were undergraduate nursing students who had completed at least one clinical course in the nursing program and voluntarily consented to participate in the study. The exclusion criterion for the study was questionnaires that were incompletely filled out. The student population of the two universities was 500, with the majority being reluctant to participate in the study. Therefore, the sampling period was prolonged. They were selected using simple random sampling via a random number table. The internship for all students was in educational and medical hospitals affiliated with the government.

Instrument

The instrument used in this study was the CLES + T scale, an enhanced version of the CLES. Originally developed by Saarikoski et al. in 2002, the CLES assessed the clinical environment and supervision from the perspective of nursing students. This scale comprises 27 items across five domains: ward atmosphere (five items, Cronbach’s alpha = 0.73), leadership style of the ward manager (four items, Cronbach’s alpha = 0.76), premises of nursing care on the ward (four items, Cronbach’s alpha = 0.73), premises of learning on the ward (six items, Cronbach’s alpha = 0.84), and supervisory relationship (eight items, Cronbach’s alpha = 0.98). In 2008, Saarikoski et al. developed an expanded version of the CLES, incorporating the evaluation of bedside trainers. The expanded questionnaire comprised 34 items divided into the following domains: ward educational environment (nine items), supervisory relationship (eight items), leadership style of the ward manager (four items), ward nursing facilities (four items), and the role of the nurse teacher in clinical practice (nine items). The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the role of the nurse teacher in clinical practice was 0.90, while the Cronbach’s alpha for the other domains ranged from 0.77 to 0.96. Students responded to the questions using a 5-point Likert scale: 1 - Completely disagree, 2 - Somewhat disagree, 3 - Neither agree nor disagree, 4 - Somewhat agree, 5 - Completely agree. The scale score ranges from 27 to 135. The CLES + T has been translated into 33 different languages and is widely used by researchers in over 50 countries [32].

Translation

Written permission for the use of the CLES + T scale was obtained from the developer of the scale, Dr. Saarikoski Mikko. According to Behling and Law (2000), the forward-backward translation technique [33] was used to translate the scale from English into Persian. One bilingual expert translated the English version of the questionnaire into Farsi. Subsequently, another bilingual expert translated the Farsi version back into English. Then, two bilingual experts and reseatch team compared the original English version with the Persian translation to create a finalized Persian version of the CLES + T questionnaire.The Persian Version of CLES + T is provided in Appendix 1.

Face validity

To assess face validity, the questionnaire was administered to 30 internship students who were in the second to fourth year of the nursing degree program using simple random sampling. For qualitative assessment of the Persian CLES + T, students were invited to evaluate each item on the appropriateness, difficulty, relevance, and ambiguity. For quantitative assessment, the students were asked to determine the suitability of the items on a Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (Extremely Unsuitable) to 5 (Extremely suitable). All collected questionnaires were carefully examined and analyzed. Two questionnaires with incomplete responses were excluded from the analysis.

Content validity

Content validity consists of both quantitative and qualitative phases, evaluating whether the instrument correctly measures the intended objective. The Lawshe’s method (1979) was used for content validity [34]. In content validity, all the items in the instrument are analyzed, and questions that measure important aspects of the instrument are selected and included. For content validity, convenience sampling was used in both the quantitative and qualitative approaches. In the qualitative content validity assessment, the questionnaire was distributed to five nursing faculty members familiar with the process of tool development, they were Ph.D in nursing education They were asked to review each item for appropriate wording and grammar, comprehensibility, and cultural relevance. Any suggestions for improvement were also requested. After collecting the questionnaires, the proposed modifications were evaluated. In the quantitative approach, the content validity ratio (CVR) and content validity index (CVI) were used. The modified questionnaire was emailed to 10 nursing faculty members, who were asked to assess the necessity of each item on a three-point spectrum: “necessary, useful but not necessary, not necessary.” To calculate the content validity index, the Likert scale responses for relevance.

Construct validity

To establish construct validity, the sample size adequacy is determined based on the number of instrument items. According to Klein (2014), a minimum sample size of 200 is defensible, with 10 to 20 samples per item recommended in factor analysis [35]. With 34 items in the instrument, a sample size of 340 individuals was calculated. Taking into account a 10% attrition rate, the questionnaire was distributed to 450 nursing undergratuate students from the second to the fourth year at two Universities. Twenty-three questionnaires were removed due to incomplete responses, resulting in 427 completed questionnaires included in the study. The questionnaires were made available to nursing students during their breaks. It took an average of 15 min to complete the questionnaire.

Statistical analysis

Utilizing SMART PLS3 software, confirmatory factor analysis was implemented based on Structural Equation Modeling (SEM). In this analysis, the Partial Least Squares (PLS) method was employed; initially, the measurement models were examined to assess factor loadings, the validity of observed indicators, and the reliability of the measurement instruments, followed by an evaluation of the structural model fit via indices such as R² and Q².

Reliability

Internal consistency of CLEST was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha (α) and Average Inter-Item Correlation (AIC) coefficient. The α values greater than 0.7 were considered acceptable. Te value of AIC between 0.2 and 0.4 was accepted for internal consistency [36]. Cronbach’s alpha score was 0.91 for the entire scale. The domain of the educational environment and the role of the nurse teacher demonstrated the highest reliability. Discussion

This article presents the translation and validation of the Clinical Learning Environment, Supervision and Teacher (CLES + T) instrument into Persian, with the aim of providing a more accurate and comprehensive evaluation of clinical education from the perspective of nursing students in Iran. The findings indicate that the Persian version of the CLES + T possesses adequate validity and reliability, and can be used as a credible tool for students to assess their clinical mentors, supervisors, and the learning environment.

Numerous studies in Iran have evaluated clinical learning environments; however, they have often utilized more generic instruments such as DREEM, PHEEM, and MEEM [5–7, 9, 18], which were not specifically designed for the nursing context. In contrast, the CLES + T instrument, by focusing on key domains of nursing education, including nursing care, supervisor leadership style, and unit facilities, offers a more precise and specialized view of students’ experiences. This distinct feature justifies its application and sets it apart from other available tools.

This research aligns with the pioneering studies by Saarikoski et al. (2002 and 2008), who originally designed and validated the CLES + T and emphasized the importance of the three key elements: the clinical mentor, supervisor, and learning environment. Furthermore, the findings support behaviorist learning theories which highlight the crucial role of the environment (its structure and conditions) in the learning process. In Iran, previous studies such as those by Delaram (2007) and Jokar and Jokar (2010) have pointed to the challenges of clinical education and the influence of environmental factors [43, 44], thereby confirming the need for an instrument like the CLES + T to more accurately identify these issues.

One of the most significant findings of this study was the removal of three items (questions 2, 3, and 13) from the final version of the instrument. These items pertained to “individual supervision of the student,” “receiving continuous feedback from the ward nurse,” and “the willingness of staff to supervise the student’s work,” respectively. This outcome goes beyond a simple statistical result, offering important insights into the structural and cultural differences of clinical education in Iran. While international instruments are designed based on Western educational and clinical contexts, some of the concepts or behaviors they measure may be understood or practiced differently within the Iranian culture. These differences can be attributed to several key factors:

Roles and Responsibilities of Mentors and Clinical Staff: In the Iranian educational system, the primary responsibility for student supervision and evaluation predominantly lies with the clinical mentors, who are faculty members. Typically, more experienced faculty members are assigned to deliver theoretical courses and supervise graduate theses, whereas those with less experience focus on clinical instruction. Due to time constraints and the absence of an official evaluative role, hospital clinical staff are involved to a lesser extent in direct supervision and the provision of continuous feedback to students. This arrangement contrasts with findings from studies conducted in other countries, such as those by Papastavrou (2016) [28] and Moreno (2015) [29], where clinical staff have a more active role in student education and supervision.Group-Based Education and Time Constraints: As Heidari (2013) noted, due to a shortage of faculty members and a large number of nursing students, a single mentor typically works with a group of students [45]. In such a system, the opportunity for one-on-one feedback and “individual supervision” is limited. These conditions likely rendered the corresponding item less relevant, leading to its low factor loading and subsequent removal.

The Organizational Relationship of Mentors: In Iran, nursing instructors are employed by universities and typically do not have formal organizational affiliations with hospitals [46]. This structural arrangement may contribute to students perceiving their university mentors as primarily responsible for their evaluation, with limited expectations for formal supervision or feedback from hospital staff. In contrast, in many other countries, clinical nurses hold official roles within both educational and evaluative processes. Additionally, Iranian students often view the professor’s role as uniquely authoritative, likely influenced by cultural norms and the lack of advanced clinical academic degrees such as the Doctor of Nursing Practice (DNP) within the Iranian system. Consequently, clinical nurses may be seen as less qualified to assess students academically. Furthermore, students generally prefer informal and supportive interactions with ward staff over formal supervision and evaluation, potentially to avoid the stress of judgement affecting their grades. These cultural and structural factors may explain why concepts like “supervision” or “continuous feedback” from clinical nurses show low relevance in this context, as reflected in their removal from factor analyses.Despite the removal of three items for the reasons mentioned, the results of this study, consistent with studies in Germany, Norway, Austria, and Saudi Arabia [46–49], confirm the adaptability and dynamism of the CLES + T instrument. However, the differences in factor analysis results and the number of removed items across various studies demonstrate that the educational system and clinical conditions of each country have a direct impact on how students perceive and experience clinical education. For instance, in the study by Mueller (2012), items related to the nursing mentor were removed, a finding attributed to the limited hours of student interaction with the mentor in that educational system [48]. This contrasts with Iran, where due to a different and more extensive clinical education structure, the removal of this domain would not have been logical, and its high reliability in our study (0.91) confirms this.

Limitations

This study conducted a thorough analysis; however, it has some limitations. First, the participants were nursing students from only two universities located in different regions of Iran, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other clinical settings. Additionally, the focus on undergraduate students excludes the perspectives of graduate students. Second, the concurrent validity of the scale was not assessed in this study. We recommend that future research examine the test-retest reliability of the instrument and evaluate its applicability across various hospitals and universities throughout Iran.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the findings suggest that the CLES + T instrument shows potential for effective localization within the Iranian context. The removal of three items should not be viewed solely as a statistical result but rather as an opportunity to better understand certain challenges present in the Iranian nursing education system. These insights may assist educational leaders in reconsidering the role of clinical staff in student supervision and feedback, and highlight the importance of targeted training to support their involvement. It is further recommended that future research qualitatively explore the reasons behind the exclusion of these items through interviews with students and clinical staff, which may contribute to a deeper and more comprehensive localization of the instrument. Such efforts could ultimately enhance the quality of clinical nursing education in Iran.
