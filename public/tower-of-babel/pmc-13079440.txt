Assessment of the Reliability of a Laser‐Based Visuomotor Feedback Device for Assessing Head Movement Control in Chronic Neck Pain: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Amin Norouzi Fashkhami, Maryam Mohammadi, Sogol Norouzi Fashkhami, Alireza Akbarzadeh Baghban
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimChronic neck pain is often associated with impaired cervical proprioception and motor control. Laser‐based visuomotor feedback systems have emerged as promising tools to assess and retrain cervical function; however, their reliability properties remain underexplored. This cross‐sectional study aimed to evaluate the intra‐rater test–retest reliability of a laser‐based feedback device for assessing head movement control in individuals with chronic neck pain.MethodsTo assess proprioceptive control and head movement precision, a custom‐built laser‐based visuomotor feedback device was employed. Thirty participants with chronic neck pain, aged between 25 and 65 years, who were referred to Esteghlal Physiotherapy Clinic in Tehran, completed two identical test sessions, 24 h apart. Each participant performed three trials for each of four predefined head movement paths (1–1, 2–2, 3–3, and A–G) using a laser pointer mounted on the forehead, which projected a beam onto a screen. Movement speed and success (percentage of path completed without deviation) were recorded. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) with 95% confidence intervals, standard error of measurement (SEM), and minimal detectable change at 95% confidence (MDC95) were calculated for speed and success metrics to assess test–retest reliability.ResultsThe device demonstrated good to excellent reliability. ICC values for speed ranged from 0.868 to 0.958 across all paths, and for success from 0.753 to 0.963, with all p‐values < 0.001. Paths 2–2 and 3–3 showed the highest reliability for both metrics. Averaging across both sessions slightly improved consistency, suggesting a benefit in multi‐day assessments. For averaged measures, SEM for speed ranged 0.065–0.122 s/cm (MDC95 0.180–0.339 s/cm) and SEM for success ranged 7.71–19.08 mm (MDC95 21.37–52.90 mm), with the largest absolute error observed for success on the complex A–G trajectory.ConclusionThe laser‐based visuomotor feedback system demonstrated good to excellent reliability for assessing cervical motor control within a small sample of individuals with chronic neck pain recruited from a single clinical setting. The inclusion of absolute reliability indices confirmed the system's consistency and precision across different trajectories. These findings support its potential as a practical and reliable tool for both clinical assessment and research applications in sensorimotor control.Keywords: head movements, laser, neck pain, proprioception, reproducibility of results, visual feedback

Introduction

Neck pain is one of the most common musculoskeletal conditions worldwide, with a lifetime prevalence exceeding 30% in the general population and rising to approximately 65% among the elderly [1, 2]. Chronic neck pain, typically defined as pain persisting for more than 3 months, is often associated with functional impairments such as limited cervical range of motion, postural instability, dizziness, and deficits in sensorimotor control [3].

Among the key elements contributing to these deficits is proprioceptive dysfunction [4], which reflects impaired awareness of cervical position and movement [5, 6]. The cervical region, especially the deep neck muscles, is densely populated with muscle spindles, making it critical for fine motor control and accurate joint position sense [7, 8]. Disruption of afferent signaling from these structures can lead to altered head position sense and compromised motor coordination [5].

Given these concerns, accurate assessment of cervical proprioception has become a focal point in clinical and research settings. Traditionally, tools such as the Cervical Range‐of‐Motion (CROM) device, inclinometers, goniometers, and smartphone‐based systems have been used to evaluate joint position error (JPE) through the Head Repositioning to Neutral Test (HRNT) [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. However, these methods may lack the dynamic feedback necessary to guide and correct movement in real time. Recent advances in wearable inertial sensors and immersive VR/AR‐based feedback systems have also been explored to assess and retrain proprioception, demonstrating promising results in both research and clinical populations [14, 15].

Building on these advances, laser‐based visuomotor feedback systems have been introduced as innovative tools for assessing and retraining cervical motor control. These devices provide real‐time visual (and often auditory) feedback by projecting a laser from the participant's head onto a screen or target, allowing individuals to visualize and self‐correct their movements during tasks requiring head repositioning or trajectory tracking [16]. The auditory signal serves as an additional error indicator, aimed at improving participant awareness and maintaining attention during the task. Prior research has suggested that combining visual and auditory feedback may enhance sensorimotor integration and motor learning, particularly in individuals with proprioceptive deficits [17, 18]. By engaging multiple sensory modalities, such systems may enhance sensorimotor integration and promote motor learning, particularly in individuals with impaired proprioception [19, 20].

Despite their growing use in rehabilitation protocols, there is currently limited evidence regarding the psychometric properties—specifically, the reliability—of laser‐based visuomotor feedback devices, particularly within populations suffering from chronic neck pain. For instance, AlDahas et al. (2023) [16] examined measurement properties of cervical joint position error using a laser‐based approach in individuals with and without chronic neck pain, but their focus was limited to joint position error outcomes. In contrast, the present study specifically evaluates the test–retest reliability of a custom‐built visuomotor feedback system across multiple head movement trajectories, thereby extending the available evidence and addressing an important gap in the literature.

Therefore, the present study aimed to assess the reliability of a laser‐based visuomotor feedback device in measuring cervical movement control among individuals with chronic neck pain. The findings will inform its potential utility in future interventional studies and clinical evaluations targeting cervical proprioceptive dysfunction.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This cross‐sectional study was designed to evaluate the intra‐rater test–retest reliability of a laser‐based visuomotor feedback device for assessing cervical motor control in individuals with chronic neck pain. The reporting of this study followed the COSMIN reporting guideline for studies on measurement properties (reliability and measurement error) [21]. A completed COSMIN checklist is provided as Supporting File 1.

Participants

A total of 40 individuals were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 10 were excluded (6 not meeting inclusion criteria, 3 declined to participate, and 1 for other reasons). Therefore, 30 participants with chronic neck pain, aged between 25 and 65 years, were finally included from Esteghlal Physiotherapy Clinic in Tehran, Iran. All participants completed both Day 1 and Day 2 assessments, and their data were included in the analysis. The process of enrollment, exclusion, and analysis is summarized in Figure 1.

Patient flow diagram showing participant eligibility assessment, exclusion, enrollment, and completion of testing.

All participants had experienced neck pain for more than 3 months and were fluent in Persian. Individuals were excluded if they had received physical therapy for neck pain in the past 6 months, suffered from neurological, vestibular, or psychological disorders, had uncorrected visual or hearing impairments, or were diagnosed with cervical radiculopathy, spinal malignancies, or inflammatory arthropathies. A history of spinal surgery, vertebral fractures, or current pregnancy also served as exclusion criteria. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The study was approved by the ethics committee of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences.

Sample Size

Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling method. The sample size (n = 30) was chosen based on consistency with previous reliability studies on cervical sensorimotor control, which typically included 25–40 participants [16, 22, 23] and on methodological guidance from Fleiss, J. L. (1986) [24].

Instrument

To assess proprioceptive control and head movement precision, a custom‐built laser‐based visuomotor feedback device was employed. The apparatus consisted of a lightweight laser pointer mounted on a headband worn by the participant, a screen placed 90 cm in front of the subject, and a digital camera integrated with software for motion tracking. Video was recorded using a 12.3‐megapixel 4K camera connected to a Raspberry Pi 5 microprocessor (2.4 GHz, 8 GB RAM) and displayed on a 7‐inch IPS LCD screen (1080 × 1920 pixels). A calibration object of known length (100‐mm ruler) was positioned on the display plane to determine the spatial conversion factor (mm/pixel). The pixel coordinates extracted from the recorded video were subsequently converted to millimeters using this factor for quantitative analyses. The laser device was positioned at a fixed horizontal distance of 90 cm in front of the participant, consistent with prior studies [25, 26]. The vertical height was individually adjusted for each participant so that the laser beam was aligned horizontally with the participant's nose level while seated. The laser projected a visible beam onto the screen, allowing participants to visually track their head movements along predefined paths. These included straight horizontal, vertical, diagonal, and complex curved lines. The system incorporated an auditory alert (a beep) that was activated when the laser deviated from the designated path, providing real‐time corrective feedback. The addition of auditory feedback was intended to complement visual feedback by enhancing error detection during trajectory tracking, thereby strengthening the assessment of JPE. To support this rationale, we have added several relevant references that demonstrate the positive impact of auditory feedback—particularly when combined with visual cues—on proprioceptive accuracy and motor performance [18, 27, 28].

Procedure

Each participant underwent two identical testing sessions, one on Day 1 and the other on Day 2, separated by a 24‐h interval to evaluate test–retest reliability. This interval was chosen to minimize fatigue or carry‐over effects that could bias same‐day testing, while also avoiding clinical or functional changes that might occur over longer intervals. Such an interval is commonly adopted in reliability studies of motor performance and proprioception [29, 30]. During each session, participants were instructed to sit upright and perform slow, controlled head movements to follow the laser beam along four specified paths (1–1, 2–2, 3–3, and A–G), as shown in Figure 2. Path 1–1 represented a horizontal straight line, path 2–2 a vertical straight line, path 3–3 a diagonal line, and path A–G a curved trajectory. These paths were selected to vary in complexity, with the straight lines representing simple trajectories and the curved A–G path representing a more complex movement pattern. The movements were performed three times for each path while minimizing trunk movement and compensatory motions. The auditory alert guided the participant to maintain accuracy throughout the trajectory. The examiner ensured consistent positioning and instructions across both sessions. All assessments were performed by a physiotherapist with more than 12 years of clinical experience in musculoskeletal physiotherapy, including expertise in cervical proprioception and motor control evaluation. To minimize procedural variability, the same examiner conducted all sessions, delivered standardized instructions, and handled the device consistently across participants.

Outcome Measures

All trials were recorded using a fixed camera. Video data were processed using the OpenCV library, where each frame was converted into an RGB format to isolate and track the laser point. Previous research has demonstrated that computer‐vision‐based tracking algorithms can achieve high spatial accuracy when compared with gold‐standard motion capture systems, with mean errors typically below 3 mm [31]. The system extracted numerical coordinates of the laser trace and computed performance indicators, including speed and success rate. These values were used to quantify motor performance and consistency across sessions. Speed was calculated as the total movement time (s) divided by the trajectory length (cm). Thus, values are expressed in seconds per centimeter (s/cm). This metric reflects how quickly the participant was able to complete each trajectory relative to its length. Success was defined as the cumulative length of the path completed without exceeding a deviation tolerance of 3 mm (≈5 pixels) from the trajectory boundaries. Values are expressed in millimeters, representing the absolute uninterrupted path length achieved within the tolerance zone. This measure has been referred to as “percentage of path completed” in some previous work [18, 27], but in our system, it is quantified as length in millimeters.

Statistical Analysis

To evaluate the test–retest reliability of the laser‐based system, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated using a two‐way mixed‐effects model with consistency type. ICC values were interpreted as follows: < 0.5 indicated poor reliability, 0.5–0.75 moderate, 0.75–0.90 good, and > 0.90 excellent reliability, in accordance with established guidelines [32]. Prior to conducting ICC analyses, data were examined for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and for homogeneity of variances using Levene's test. Absolute reliability was expressed with the standard error of measurement (SEM) and the minimal detectable change at 95% confidence (MDC95). Bland–Altman limits of agreement were computed for representative outcomes. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 26 (IBM Corp.).

Table 4 presents the ICC values of average measures of Days 1 and 2 with their 95% confidence intervals and significance levels (p‐values). All metrics showed statistically significant reliability (p < 0.001). Speed measurements demonstrated excellent reliability across all paths, with ICCs above 0.85. Notably, paths 2_2 (ICC = 0.958) and 3_3 (ICC = 0.955) exhibited the highest ICCs for both speed and success metrics, exceeding 0.95, indicating exceptional test–retest consistency. While success scores were generally reliable, path A–G showed relatively lower ICC (0.753), suggesting that this trajectory may introduce greater variability. Overall, reliability improved slightly on Day 2 compared to Day 1, and was maximized when using the average values across both days. This pattern highlights the benefits of multi‐day measurements to enhance stability and accuracy. These findings confirm the laser‐based system's suitability for reproducible measurement of head movement performance.

In addition to relative reliability (ICCs), absolute measurement error was quantified using SEM and MDC95 (Table 4). For the averaged measures, SEM for speed ranged from 0.065 s/cm (A–G) to 0.122 s/cm (1_1), corresponding to MDC95 values of 0.180–0.339 s/cm, respectively. For success, SEM ranged from 7.71 (3_3) to 19.08 mm (A–G), corresponding to MDC95 values of 21.37–52.90 mm. Notably, averaging across two sessions reduced SEM and MDC compared with single‐session estimates (Table 3 vs. Table 4), indicating improved absolute precision with multi‐day assessment.

Discussion

This study investigated the intra‐rater test–retest reliability of a laser‐based visuomotor feedback device for assessing cervical motor control in individuals with chronic neck pain. The findings demonstrated good to excellent reliability for speed measures across all movement paths, and moderate to excellent reliability for success rates, with lower consistency observed for the most complex trajectory (A–G). Averaging performance across two sessions improved stability, highlighting the benefit of repeated measurements. Overall, these results suggest that the laser‐based system is capable of providing reproducible and objective assessments of cervical proprioception.

The variability in reliability across different trajectories may be attributed to differences in task complexity and attentional demands. In particular, the A–G path, which required multidirectional tracking with higher cognitive load, showed lower reliability compared with simpler linear trajectories. Similar findings have been reported by Alahmari et al. (2017) [8] and de Zoete et al. (2020) [9], who noted that more complex proprioceptive tasks are associated with greater measurement variability. Muscle fatigue and reduced concentration during more demanding tasks may have also contributed to this observation. From a clinical standpoint, the lower reliability observed in the A–G path suggests that such complex tasks may be less suitable for routine assessments in practice. However, they may still provide valuable insights into subtle deficits in patients with higher functional demands, supporting their potential use in research or advanced rehabilitation settings.

Task complexity emerged as a factor influencing reproducibility, with simpler, straighter trajectories yielding higher stability than the more complex curved path. This pattern indicates that standardized, less complex tasks may be more suitable for routine assessments, while more complex tasks could be useful in detecting subtle deficits in sensorimotor control. The improved reliability observed when data were averaged across sessions is consistent with evidence that repeated exposure enhances consistency, likely due to short‐term familiarization and reduced variability [33].

The integration of real‐time visual and auditory feedback may have contributed to the high reproducibility of this system. Nevertheless, it should be noted that the auditory feedback may have influenced participants' performance by facilitating immediate error correction, which could potentially bias the observed reliability. Multimodal feedback is known to enhance error detection, proprioceptive accuracy, and motor learning [18, 27, 28]. More recent findings also highlight the importance of external feedback for promoting motor learning and performance consistency [20]. Given that individuals with chronic neck pain often present with impaired proprioception [5], providing such augmented feedback is particularly relevant for improving measurement reliability.

In addition to relative reliability, the absolute indices of SEM and MDC—were also analyzed to provide a complementary perspective on the precision of the system. These indices reflect how consistently the device can reproduce the same outcome under identical conditions and indicate the smallest change that can be interpreted as a true improvement beyond measurement noise. The pattern observed across trajectories suggested that simpler and more linear paths were associated with lower absolute error, whereas more complex movements tended to show greater variability. Importantly, a reduction in SEM and MDC was observed when results were averaged across sessions, emphasizing that repeated testing or an initial familiarization trial can improve the stability and precision of the measurements in both clinical and research settings.

Compared with traditional tools such as the CROM device, inclinometers, or smartphone‐based systems [10, 11, 12], the laser‐based system offers distinct advantages, including real‐time corrective feedback, reduced examiner dependency, and detailed trajectory‐based metrics. Recent systematic reviews and empirical studies have further emphasized the need for reliable and valid measures of cervical joint position error and proprioceptive function [16, 34]. These contemporary findings underscore the importance of developing innovative approaches, such as laser‐based feedback systems, to improve assessment quality in clinical and research settings.

Taken together, the results of the present study confirm that the laser‐based visuomotor feedback device provides a reliable and clinically relevant method for assessing cervical motor control in individuals with chronic neck pain. By integrating multimodal feedback and objective trajectory tracking, this system addresses limitations of traditional methods and aligns with recent literature calling for modern, reproducible, and engaging assessment tools in musculoskeletal rehabilitation.

The clinical implication of these findings is that reliable tools for assessing cervical motor control can support physiotherapists in identifying proprioceptive impairments, tracking patients' progress, and tailoring individualized rehabilitation programs. Given that impaired proprioception and motor control are common in chronic neck pain, the demonstrated reliability of this laser‐based system indicates its potential utility for both baseline assessments and outcome monitoring in clinical settings. From a research perspective, these findings provide preliminary evidence supporting the system's validity as a measurement tool, which can be employed in future intervention trials to evaluate changes in cervical motor control.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the laser‐based visuomotor feedback system provides good to excellent intra‐rater reliability for assessing head movement speed and trajectory accuracy within a small sample of individuals with chronic neck pain recruited from a single clinical setting. In addition to high relative reliability (ICC), the analysis of absolute measurement error (SEM and MDC) confirmed that the system can detect consistent and repeatable performance across different trajectories. The lower measurement error and improved agreement observed when averaging repeated sessions suggest that familiarization and repeated assessments can enhance precision.

Clinically, these findings indicate that this laser‐based system can serve as a reliable and user‐friendly tool for evaluating cervical motor control, supporting clinicians in identifying proprioceptive impairments and monitoring rehabilitation outcomes. From a research perspective, the study provides methodological evidence for the system's use in trials examining interventions targeting sensorimotor control. Further studies with larger and more diverse samples are warranted to establish normative data, explore inter‐rater reliability, and evaluate responsiveness to therapeutic change.
