Long-term postdischarge immunonutrition management in elective colorectal cancer surgery patients an interim analysis of a Chinese multicenter randomised controlled trial
Authors: Mandula Bao, Fei Huang, Pu Cheng, Zhaoxu Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPostoperative malnutrition is prevalent in cancer patients, and enteral nutrition improves short-term outcomes. However, long-term data on post-discharge immunonutrition (IMN) effects on nutritional and immune status in colorectal cancer (CRC) patients after radical surgery are limited. Multicenter, randomized controlled trial (ChiCTR2300071078) across 20 Chinese centers. Screened CRC patients were randomized (2:1) to 30-day post-discharge oral IMN (IMPACT Oral®) or standard nutrition. Primary endpoint: change in prealbumin (ΔPA). Secondary endpoints: changes in albumin, transferrin, BMI, PG-SGA, glucose, lymphocyte count, and immunoglobulins (IgG, IgM, IgA). Interim analysis included 563 patients (373 IMN, 190 control; similar demographics). Compared with controls, the IMN group showed significantly greater ΔPA, reduced ΔPG-SGA, and increased Δlymphocyte count, ΔIgG, and ΔIgM (all P < 0.05), indicating sustained improvement in nutritional and immune status. Introduction

The 2024 Global Cancer Report, published by the World Health Organization (WHO), identifies CRC as a major health issue, with an estimated 40,800 new cases and 19,600 deaths occurring annually [1]. In 2024, the National Cancer Center released the China Cancer Statistics, which revealed that in 2022, CRC was the second most commonly diagnosed malignant tumor in China and accounted for the fourth highest number of cancer-related deaths [2].

Numerous studies have shown that malnutrition is a prevalent health issue among patients with cancer [3]. A study by the Chinese Anti-Cancer Association revealed that a significant proportion of cancer patients in China suffer from malnutrition, with 79.4% experiencing some form of malnutrition and 58% exhibiting moderate to severe malnutrition. Patients with digestive tract tumors have the highest incidence of malnutrition [4]. Severe malnutrition can lead to an increased incidence of postoperative complications and mortality in patients with CRC, along with prolonged hospital stays and increased economic burden [5,6]. Malnutrition can occur for many reasons. First, cancer is known to disrupt metabolic balance and immunological competence, leading to a weakened response to surgical trauma and accelerating tumor recurrence, progression, and tissue infiltration. Second, although patients with early-stage CRC can be successfully treated with surgery, major surgical procedures are often followed by a catabolic state characterized by proteolysis, the breakdown of branched-chain amino acids, weight loss, and immunosuppression of multifactorial origin, which can exacerbate the risk of postoperative complications and prolong hospital stays [7,8]. Therefore, the timely restoration of nutritional status and immune function in patients with CRC following surgery is crucial for their postoperative recovery [9].

IMN, or more precisely immune-modulating nutrition, is broadly defined as the provision of nutrients in amounts greater than normal dietary intake to modulate the activities of the immune system [10,11]. IMN not only supplies energy and protein (via nitrogen) but is also believed to regulate the inflammatory response and mitigate the adverse effects of postoperative immune system impairments [12]. Some research has demonstrated that short-term perioperative IMN for patients with CRC undergoing surgery is effective at improving their nutritional and immune status. A randomized, controlled study involved 60 patients with CRC, who were divided into an immunonutrition support group (n = 30) and a control group receiving conventional enteral nutrition support (n = 30). The immunonutrition support group was administered immunonutritional agents 7 days prior to surgery, whereas the control group received only a conventional diet. The concentrations of PA and TFN in the immunonutrition group were higher than those in the control group (p < 0.05). Additionally, the postoperative immunoglobulin G (IgG) concentration in the immunonutrition group was significantly greater than that in the conventional nutrition group (13.35 ± 2.06 g/l vs. 9.59 ± 2.23 g/l; P < 0.05) [13]. Another study included 28 patients who were divided into an immunonutrition support group and a control group without nutritional support at a 1:1 ratio. The immunonutrition support group was given immunonutritional support from 6 days before surgery until the day before surgery and from the 3rd day to the 7th day after surgery. The control group received traditional nutritional support during the same period. The results revealed a significant increase in the number of CD4 lymphocytes on the day before surgery compared with the baseline parameters (P < 0.05) in the immunonutrition support group but not in the traditional group [14].

Although IMN therapy has been widely adopted in clinical practice and endorsed by numerous authoritative institutions, including the European Society of Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) and the Chinese Medical Association Branch of Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition (CSPEN), existing studies on the effects of IMN on the nutritional and immune status of patients with CRC have largely been confined to the short-term perioperative period (typically ranging from one week before surgery to two weeks after surgery). Moreover, research has indicated that the risk of nutritional deficiency and immunosuppression following gastrointestinal tumor surgery persists for an extended period, rather than just a short time post-operation. In fact, some patients may even experience an increased risk after discharge [15]. Long-term observational data concerning the impact of IMN on CRC patients' immunotrophic status after surgery are lacking. Currently, a large-scale, randomized controlled observational study is needed to determine whether long-term, continuous oral immunonutrition supplements after discharge can enhance the immunonutritional status, bolster the immune response, and consequently improve the prognosis for patients with CRC who have undergone radical surgery.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was a multicenter, randomized, controlled, open-label trial, two-parallel-arm, interventional clinical trial that involved adult patients with colorectal cancer who had undergone colorectal surgery. This study was conducted according to the guidelines laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki and all procedures involving human subjects/patients were approved by the Ethics Committee of the National Cancer Center/National Clinical Research Center for Cancer/Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College (Approval No. 23/086–3825) and by the local ethics committees of the 20 participating medical centers. Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects/patients. The study was registered on www.chictr.org.cn (Chinese Clinical Trial Registry, www.chictr.org.cn, registration number ChiCTR2300071078) after receiving approval from the local ethics committee.

Inclusion criteria

Patients who were diagnosed with colorectal adenocarcinoma and aged 18-75 years and who underwent radical colorectal surgery were recruited. The inclusion criteria also included body mass index (BMI) ranging from 18.5 to 28 kg/m2, a haemoglobin (HB) concentration of no less than 90 g/L, an albumin (ALB) concentration of no less than 2.5 g/dL, and no use of blood products within one week before inclusion in the study.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria specified patients who had received preoperative (neoadjuvant) chemotherapy or radiotherapy prior to enrollment were excluded, chronic inflammatory bowel diseases, renal insufficiency (creatinine [CR], ≥2 times the upper limit of normal; haemodialysis), cardiac insufficiency (New York Heart Association [NYHA], >3), hepatic insufficiency (alanine aminotransferase [ALT], ≥2 times the upper limit of normal; total bilirubin [Tbil], ≥2 times the upper limit of normal value), severe respiratory insufficiency (partial pressure of arterial oxygen [PaO2], <70 mmHg), current infection, hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism, diabetes mellitus receiving medical and dietary treatment, and congenital or acquired immunodeficiency. Postoperative adjuvant therapy was not considered an exclusion criterion. Previous abdominal surgery did not include metabolic or bariatric procedures; no such patients were included in this cohort.

Sample size calculation

Sample size calculations were based on the previous multicenter randomized trial conducted in China and published by GUO et al. [16]. The hazard ratio set for this study was 0.8, the expected percentage of events during the study period was 0.7, and the proportion allocated to patients in the experimental group was 0.66. A superiority test with a cut-off value of 5% was used, and α = 0.05 and β = 0.2 were set. A two-sided Z-test was adopted, with a total of 800 cases in the study group vs. 400 cases in the control group. Considering that about 20% of the evaluation data were invalid due to reasons such as dropout and loss to follow-up, a total of 1340 patients actually needed to be included (960 cases in the IMN group vs. 480 cases in the control group). Informed consent process

This study was approved by the local ethics committee of the twenty participating medical centers in China. All participants received a detailed oral explanation provided by the research team members and read the written informed consent form before enrollment. The informed consent included the purpose of the study, intervention measures, potential risks and benefits, data confidentiality methods, and the right to withdraw from the study at any time. On the premise of full understanding and voluntary participation, each subject signed the written informed consent form.

Interventions

The nutritionist provided individualized instructions to all patients on administering the daily oral dose of enteral feed and on documenting it. Furthermore, the daily intake and caloric count were carefully calculated.

IMN group: Patients in this group were provided with nutrition education and guidance to incorporate 500 mL/day of postoperative oral supplementation in the form of an IMN-enriched enteral feed (IMPACT Oral®) into their regular diet for thirty consecutive days, beginning the day after discharge. IMPACT Oral® is rich in protein, arginine, omega-3 fatty acids, and RNA. The detailed composition of the oral supplements is presented in Table 1.

Randomization and blinding

Randomization was conducted by each participating surgeon using the central randomization system (https://edc-cloud.medsci.cn). After the patients were enrolled, the system automatically assigned them to groups, and each patient was assigned a participant number, which placed them in either the IMN group or the Control group. The ratio of enrolled patients in the IMN group to those in the Control group was 2:1. To ensure a balanced evaluation, the ratio of patients with colorectal cancer to those with rectal cancer was set at 1:1. The random assignment sequence was concealed until the procedure was allocated to the patients. Due to the nature of the nutritional intervention and the absence of a placebo control, full double-blinding was not feasible. Patients and treating clinicians were aware of group allocation, while outcome assessment and statistical analysis were performed using predefined protocols to minimize bias.

Outcome measures

Baseline measurements were obtained during outpatient evaluation prior to hospital admission and enrollment, and follow-up measurements were performed on the first day after discharge and at 30 days after intervention.

We also measured the following nutritional and immunologic parameters on the first day after discharge, and the 30th day after intervention: PA, ALB, TFN, BMI, PG-SGA, GLU, LAV, IgG, IgM, and IgA.

Next, we calculate the ΔValue for each data point, which involves subtracting the pre-intervention value from the post-intervention value of the same data point (ΔValue = Valuepost intervention - Valuepre intervention). We then compare the ΔValues of the two sets of data to determine if there are significant differences in the growth of nutrition and immunity between the two groups of patients after 30 consecutive days of intervention.

Multiple studies have demonstrated that PA is more effective than ALB and other nutritional parameters for monitoring postoperative nutritional status [17,18]. Therefore, we selected ΔPA as the primary efficacy indicator, with ΔALB, ΔTFN, ΔBMI, ΔPG-SGA, ΔGLU, and other nutritional indicators, as well as ΔLAV, ΔIgG, ΔIgM, and ΔIgA as secondary efficacy indicators.

Statistical methods

The statistical analyses were conducted by SPSS 27.0 version for Windows (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, United States). The measurement data were presented by the mean ± standard deviation (x ± s) and analyzed by Student's t-test. The data were presented as percentages, and the comparison between the IMN and Control groups was conducted using the Chi-Square (χ2) test. A p-value less than 0.05 is typically regarded as indicating a statistically significant difference, as it suggests that the observed result is unlikely to have occurred by chance alone. Continuous variables were analyzed using Student's t-test. In addition, comparisons of changes (Δvalues) between groups were performed, and baseline comparability was ensured. Given the interim nature of the analysis, no multivariate adjustment was performed; however, this will be incorporated in the final analysis.

Results

Patients

Between August 2023 and December 2024, the study initially enrolled 650 patients. Thirty-five patients were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria (n = 14), declined to participate (n = 11), or for other reasons (n = 10). In the IMN group, 14 patients did not receive the allocated intervention due to incorrect supplement intake or patient refusal, whereas in the Control group, the number was 5. Furthermore, twenty-three patients were lost to follow-up because of invalid contact information or patient refusal, whereas in the Control group, the number was 10. Ultimately, 563 patients were included in the definitive interim analysis: 373 in the IMN group and 190 in the Control group. Nutritional and immunologic parameters

The nutritional and immunologic parameters for patients were analyzed and listed in detail in Table 3. In terms of nutrition, as illustrated in Fig. 2, the ΔPA in the IMN group was significantly higher than that in the Control group (P < 0.05). This indicates that the nutritional improvement of patients in the IMN group was more pronounced than that in the control group after 30 consecutive days of nutritional immunotherapy post-discharge. Similarly, the ΔPG - SGA scores in the IMN group decreased significantly compared to those in the Control group, which indicates that both the subjective overall assessment and objective nutritional index were significantly improved after oral IMN (P < 0.05). However, other nutritional indicators in the IMN group also increased more than those in the Control group, though no significant difference was observed between the two groups (P > 0.05). And as shown in Fig. 3, compared with the Control group, the IMN group demonstrated a superior enhancement in immune status after 30 days of nutritional immunotherapy, marked by significantly elevated levels of ΔLAV, ΔIgG, and ΔIgM (P < 0.05). In contrast, the greater increase in ΔIgA levels in the IMN group failed to reach statistical significance (P = 0.091).

Discussion

Malnutrition is a prevalent yet frequently underestimated issue among patients with digestive cancers [7]. Research indicates that the incidence of nutritional risk and malnutrition in gastrointestinal cancer patients can reach as high as 84.3% to 90.0%, with malnutrition affecting 45% to 60% of CRC patients, a rate that significantly increases following radical surgery [19,20]. Severe malnutrition significantly increases the risk of postoperative complications and mortality rates in CRC patients [3,4]. It also leads to extended hospital stays and heightened economic burdens. Therefore, nutritional therapy has become an important part of multidisciplinary comprehensive treatment for CRC. Standardized nutritional therapy has been shown to significantly enhance the quality of life and prognosis for cancer patients, as evidenced by studies indicating that targeted nutritional interventions can reduce complications, improve survival rates, and maintain body functions [21,22]. Therefore, it is crucial to continue providing nutritional support to patients after their discharge from the hospital to reduce the occurrence of malnutrition.

Currently, the special nutrients commonly referred to as IMN and clinically applied mainly include omega-3 fatty acids, glutamine, arginine, dietary fiber, nucleosides, and RNA. Omega-3 fatty acids are essential polyunsaturated fatty acids that primarily consist of eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), which serve as critical nutrients for immune modulation. EPA and DHA, specific types of fatty acids, enhance cell membrane fluidity, facilitate messenger transmission and receptor function on the cell membrane, decrease the production of inflammatory mediators, improve the body's cellular immune response, and mitigate inflammation. Additionally, they exhibit biological effects such as inhibiting cancer cell proliferation, reducing tumor burden, and minimizing nutrient consumption [21,23,24]. Glutamine, the most abundant free amino acid in the body, is crucial for maintaining the integrity of the intestinal barrier and promoting the growth and differentiation of intestinal cells. Arginine, as the sole substrate for nitric oxide (NO) synthesis, plays a crucial role in innate antimicrobial immunity, which is essential for the host's first line of defense [25]. This amino acid not only supports cardiovascular health by promoting vasodilation and improving blood flow but also contributes to immune regulation and cellular growth, as evidenced by its extensive use in biomedical research and applications. It also plays an important role in maintaining the physiological balance of the gastrointestinal tract and regulating the metabolism of many kinds of lymphocyte [26,27]. RNA supply is necessary to avoid T-lymphocyte decrease and to raise IL-2 levels and the CD4/CD8 ratio [11,[28], [29], [30]]. A meta-analysis of 12 studies, which included 1124 patients who underwent gastric cancer surgery and received either immunonutrition or conventional enteral nutrition, revealed that PA concentrations were significantly higher in the immunonutrition group (MD: 24.98, 95% CI: 3.21 to 46.74, P = 0.02, I2 = 92%) compared to the control group [31]. Importantly, our findings specifically demonstrate that postdischarge IMN leads to measurable improvements in dynamic nutritional markers (ΔPA) and immune parameters, rather than merely supporting general nutritional concepts.

PA is a small protein molecule synthesized by the liver, with a half-life of just 1.9 days, shorter than that of albumin [18]. Consequently, it can more rapidly reflect the body's metabolic changes and liver function status. It is primarily used as an indicator of nutritional status and inflammation. In postoperative patients, PA reflects the quality of nutritional status, and lower levels suggest malnutrition or the presence of inflammation. A retrospective study involving 462 patients with stage I gastric cancer revealed that a low preoperative serum prealbumin level was an independent risk factor for recurrence following D2 radical gastrectomy. Multivariate analysis indicated that, after accounting for other influencing factors (such as lymph node metastasis and the completion of adjuvant chemotherapy), the preoperative serum prealbumin level was significantly associated with the risk of recurrence (p < 0.001) [32]. Additionally, other studies show that a decrease in PA indicates postoperative malnutrition and has been confirmed to increase the incidence of postoperative and wound infections [33]. The PG-SGA is a crucial instrument for evaluating the nutritional status of cancer patients, integrating patient self-assessment with clinical observation to assess indicators such as weight changes, dietary intake, symptoms, activity levels, and physical condition. Based on the stratified results of the assessment, personalized nutritional therapy plans are developed for cancer patients, thereby improving treatment outcomes and quality of life. A Brazilian study examined the correlation between PG-SGA scores and survival outcomes in 250 older patients with CRC. It was observed that individuals with severe malnutrition, classified as PG-SGA Class C, exhibited a significantly increased risk of mortality compared to those with a better nutritional status, classified as PG-SGA Class A [23]. Our study revealed that the ΔPA in the IMN group was significantly higher than that in the Control group (P < 0.05). Similarly, the ΔPG-SGA scores of patients in the IMN group were also significantly lower than those of the Control group (P < 0.05). This suggests that the continuous administration of immunonutrition post-discharge can consistently enhance patients' nutritional status compared to the control group, thereby establishing a foundation for any subsequent tumor-related treatments the patients might undergo. Although the absolute difference in ΔPA between groups was modest (∼2.4 g/L), even small changes in prealbumin may reflect meaningful improvements in short-term protein synthesis and inflammatory status in postoperative patients.

The immunosuppression experienced by patients due to tumors is also a long-term phenomenon. It does not dissipate immediately after the tumor is excised but rather involves a prolonged recovery process. Studies indicate that this process can usually persist for more than one month, and in some cases, it may even extend to over four months [34]. Furthermore, postoperative malnutrition directly results in inadequate synthesis of immunoglobulins, including IgG, IgA, and IgM, diminished activity of the complement system, reduced phagocytic capacity of neutrophils and macrophages, weakened immune responses, and decreased humoral and cellular immune functions, rendering patients more susceptible to infections. Many clinical and experimental studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of IMN in reinforcing the body's cellular and humoral immunity. A meta-analysis conducted by Yue T et al. [35], which included 20 studies and 1613 patients, revealed that omega-3 fatty acids significantly increased humoral immune function indices, such as IgA [standardized mean difference (SMD) = 0.54, 95%CI 0.10-0.99], IgM (SMD = 0.52, 95%CI 0.05-0.99), and IgG (SMD = 0.65, 95% CI 0.47 to 0.84); as well as T cell immune function indices, including CD3+ (SMD = 0.73, 95% CI 0.54 to 0.92), CD4+ (SMD = 0.76, 95% CI 0.53 to 0.98), and the ratio of CD4+/CD8+ (SMD = 0.66, 95% CI 0.39 to 0.92). Xu J et al. [13] randomly divided sixty patients with gastrointestinal cancer into two groups, each of which was fed with IMN and conventional diet, respectively, for 7 days before surgical procedures. The result of the trial revealed that postoperative IgG levels in the IMN group were higher than those in the control group. Huang Z et al. [36] conducted a retrospective study on 102 patients, who underwent gastrointestinal surgery, including 52 cases receiving IMN diet and 50 cases on conventional nutrition diet during the perioperative period. Post-operation patients exhibited notably elevated levels of IgA, IgG, IgM, complement C3, and complement C4 compared to the control group at both 1st and 7th days post-operation (P < 0.05). However, the observation periods of the aforementioned studies were limited to short-term perioperative treatment. In contrast, our research has demonstrated that long-term oral administration of immunonutritional supplements post-discharge can continuously increase the levels of LAV, IgG, and IgM in patients compared to the Control group (P < 0.05). The results indicated that the continuous use of IMN after surgery could enhance the body's cellular and humoral immunity over the long term.

Recently, integrated nutritional-inflammatory indices such as the C-reactive protein–albumin–lymphocyte (CALLY) index have gained attention as prognostic markers in colorectal cancer. Emerging evidence from multicenter cohort studies suggests that higher CALLY scores are associated with improved survival outcomes and lower recurrence rates [37,38]. In addition, recent meta-analyses have further supported the prognostic relevance of combined nutritional and inflammatory markers in surgical CRC populations [39,40]. Although our study did not evaluate composite indices such as CALLY, the observed improvements in albumin-related parameters and lymphocyte counts in the IMN group indirectly support the potential role of immunonutrition in modulating these integrated pathways. Future analyses from our ongoing trial will incorporate such indices to further validate their clinical utility.

However, this study has several limitations. Firstly, the scope of our investigation was constrained due to an inadequate sample size, which prevented us from drawing definitive conclusions. Secondly, our analysis of the cellular immune response was limited to the absolute lymphocyte count, as we did not examine specific lymphocyte subsets. Other immune cells, such as T cells, B cells, NK cells, and macrophages, were neither observed nor analyzed. Thirdly, our research concentrated on the impact of long-term postoperative IMN on the short-term prognosis of individuals diagnosed with CRC. There is a lack of observational studies that evaluate the effect of long-term postoperative IMN on the survival of these patients. We need to complete data entry to increase the sample size and include observations on not only the effect of long-term postoperative IMN on the short-term prognosis of patients with CRC but also its influence on their long-term survival. In addition, potential confounding factors such as comorbidities, variations in surgical procedures, and postoperative complications were not fully adjusted for in this interim analysis, which may have influenced nutritional and immune outcomes.

Furthermore, this interim analysis focused primarily on biochemical markers, and clinical outcomes such as postoperative complications, recurrence, and survival were not included, which represents an important limitation.

From a clinical perspective, our findings support the incorporation of structured postdischarge immunonutrition into standard postoperative care pathways for CRC patients. Future research should focus on long-term clinical endpoints, including complication rates, recurrence, and survival outcomes, as well as the integration of composite nutritional-inflammatory indices to guide individualized nutritional strategies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this interim analysis demonstrates that long-term postdischarge oral immunonutrition significantly improves nutritional status and immune function in patients undergoing colorectal cancer surgery. These findings support the clinical value of continued nutritional intervention beyond hospitalization. Further studies are warranted to determine its impact on long-term outcomes, including survival and recurrence, and to explore the integration of nutritional-inflammatory indices into postoperative management.
