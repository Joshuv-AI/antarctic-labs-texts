Virtual simulation in nursing education: a scoping review
Authors: Hannan Dong, Chen Zhou, Lichun Xing, Dongying Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Despite rapid uptake across diverse modalities, a comprehensive map of the evidence covering the post-pandemic surge is lacking. Background

Nursing education is undergoing substantial transformation driven by technological innovation and persistent challenges to traditional clinical training. Shortages of clinical placements, rising patient acuity, patient-safety priorities, and ethical concerns about novice learners’ real-patient encounters have collectively created demand for alternative pedagogical approaches [1, 2]. The COVID-19 pandemic intensified this demand: restrictions on in-person clinical activities compelled nursing programmes worldwide to rapidly adopt technology-enhanced learning, and many of these adaptations have persisted as curricular fixtures even as restrictions have eased [3, 4]. Concurrently, declining head-mounted-display costs, the maturation of consumer-grade extended-reality hardware, and the wide availability of authoring tools such as Unity and Unreal Engine have lowered the technical and financial barriers to deploying virtual simulation in academic and clinical training settings [3, 5].

Virtual simulation encompasses a broad spectrum of computer-generated environments designed to replicate clinical scenarios, ranging from fully immersive virtual reality (VR) using head-mounted displays (HMDs) to screen-based virtual simulations on standard desktop or laptop computers [6]. In this review, virtual simulation is defined as any computer-generated, interactive learning environment that represents clinical situations for educational purposes, spanning immersive, screen-based, and blended modalities, and excluding purely physical manikin-based or standardised-patient simulation without a virtual component. Additional modalities include augmented reality (AR), which overlays digital information on the physical environment; mixed reality (MR), which blends interactive virtual artefacts with the real world; 360-degree video, which provides immersive but largely non-interactive scenario viewing; and virtual patient simulations featuring interactive digital avatars capable of natural-language dialogue [7, 8]. A recent mapping review by Cant and Ryan [9] catalogued a broad range of virtual simulation applications available for nursing curricula, spanning screen-based virtual simulations, virtual reality simulations, and virtual games, underscoring the diversity of modalities now accessible to nurse educators. These modalities offer distinct affordances for experiential learning, allowing students to practise skills, develop clinical reasoning, and build confidence in safe, repeatable environments without risk to patients or burden on already-stretched clinical placements [7, 10].

The pedagogical rationale is grounded in established frameworks: Kolb’s experiential learning theory [11], the National League for Nursing (NLN) Jeffries Simulation Theory [12], and Benner’s novice-to-expert model [13]. Together these support the cyclical, experience-based, and incremental skill development that virtual simulation is well-positioned to facilitate.

Several reviews have examined virtual simulation in nursing and broader healthcare education, but the field’s rapid expansion since 2020 has outpaced existing syntheses. Shin et al. [14] provided an early meta-analysis of simulation-based nursing education, and Plotzky et al. [15]—the most directly comparable prior synthesis—conducted a systematic mapping review of VR in nurse education, identifying 37 studies through 2020. An earlier scoping review by Verkuyl and Mastrilli [16] also examined virtual simulations in nursing education; the present review differs in that it focuses on the substantial post-2019 expansion of the field, providing a more current and comprehensive evidence map. Other recent reviews have addressed narrower subdomains, including immersive VR effectiveness [17], VR for clinical-skill acquisition [18], and AR/MR beyond surgical contexts [19]. None, however, offers a comprehensive evidence map across the full virtual-simulation spectrum and the breadth of nursing curricular domains for the post-pandemic period.

The present scoping review extends this prior work in three complementary respects. First, it focuses on the post-2020 publication surge, a period during which the volume of primary research has expanded substantially beyond that captured by earlier syntheses such as Plotzky et al. [15], which mapped 37 studies through 2020. Second, it broadens the scope from immersive VR alone to the full modality spectrum, including interactive immersive VR and 360°/180° video VR, screen-based virtual simulation, AR, MR, virtual gaming simulation, and virtual patient simulation. Third, it applies the JBI scoping-review methodology [20]—well suited to mapping heterogeneous, rapidly evolving evidence [21, 22]—and reports in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR checklist [23], thereby providing a methodologically transparent update to the foundation laid by earlier syntheses.

The objective was to systematically map evidence on virtual simulation in nursing education between 2019 and 2025, addressing five questions: (1) what types of virtual simulation are used; (2) in which nursing education domains they are applied; (3) what research designs are employed; (4) what outcomes are measured and what are the key findings; and (5) what gaps and future research directions exist.

Methods

Review framework and protocol registration

This scoping review followed the JBI methodology for scoping reviews [20] and the five-stage framework of Arksey and O’Malley [22] as refined by Levac et al. [24]. The review protocol was registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF; DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/TKQPJ). Registration occurred after completion of the database searches. The registration is reported for transparency, and no substantive changes were made to the eligibility criteria, information sources, data extraction framework, or synthesis approach after registration. Reporting followed the PRISMA-ScR checklist [23]; the full database search strategies are provided in Additional file 1, and the completed checklist in Additional file 2. The OSF record provides a permanent, time-stamped archive of the methods to enable independent verification.

Research question (PCC framework)

The research question was framed using the JBI Population–Concept–Context (PCC) framework. Population: nursing learners at all levels, comprising undergraduate and postgraduate nursing students, pre-registration (preservice) nurses, and registered nurses undertaking educational interventions, together with nursing educators; students from other health professions were excluded. Concept: virtual simulation, including VR, AR, MR, screen-based virtual simulation, and virtual patient simulation. Context: nursing education settings, including universities, colleges, hospitals, and clinical training centres. The overarching question was: “What is the extent, range, and nature of evidence on virtual simulation used in nursing education?”

Search strategy

A three-step JBI-recommended search strategy was used. An initial limited search of PubMed and CINAHL identified relevant terms in titles, abstracts, and indexing. A full search across PubMed, CINAHL (via EBSCOhost), Web of Science, and Scopus was then conducted, restricted to English-language publications from January 2019 to December 2025; the final search was executed on 15 January 2026 to allow indexing of late-2025 records. The search combined two concept blocks (nursing education AND virtual simulation) using MeSH/CINAHL Headings and free-text synonyms; the complete strategy is provided in Additional file 1. Reference lists of all included studies were hand-searched, yielding no additional eligible studies. To maximise sensitivity, the search combined a broad nursing-education block with the virtual-simulation block; nursing educators were captured through the general education terms rather than a separate population descriptor (e.g. “Faculty, Nursing”), since studies involving educators are consistently indexed under these broader terms, and adding a dedicated descriptor would not have increased retrieval. Likewise, contextual elements of the PCC framework (e.g. universities, hospitals, and clinical training settings) were applied at the eligibility-screening stage rather than embedded as search terms, so as not to narrow retrieval and inadvertently exclude eligible educational studies.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they were: (1) published 2019–2025; (2) in English; (3) original primary research (quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-methods); (4) conducted with nursing learners—including undergraduate or postgraduate nursing students, pre-registration (preservice) nurses, and registered nurses participating as learners in structured nursing educational interventions—or with nursing educators; (5) investigating virtual simulation for educational purposes; and (6) peer-reviewed. Publication year was determined by the date of first online publication rather than by the date of issue assignment, and eligibility against the 2019–2025 window was judged on that basis. For six studies these two dates fall in different calendar years; the full reference list of the included studies cites the final issue details for each reference, whereas all year-distribution analyses reported here use the online publication year. The review period was set to commence in 2019 to capture the pre-pandemic baseline and the subsequent post-COVID-19 expansion of virtual simulation, thereby focusing on contemporary evidence and complementing earlier reviews that covered preceding periods. Eligibility was restricted to English-language, peer-reviewed primary research on grounds of feasibility (the review team’s language capacity and the absence of translation resources) and because English is the predominant language of publication in this field; the potential for language and publication-type restriction to omit relevant studies is acknowledged in the Limitations. Excluded were reviews, conference abstracts, editorials, letters, and theses; studies on other health-professional students; studies using only manikin-based or standardised-patient simulation without a virtual component; and non-peer-reviewed or grey literature.

Study selection

Citations were imported into EndNote for initial deduplication, then into Rayyan for a second round. Two reviewers independently screened titles/abstracts and assessed full texts; disagreements were resolved by discussion, with a third reviewer adjudicating remaining conflicts (42 records at the title/abstract stage; 16 at the full-text stage). Inter-rater reliability was κ = 0.86 for title/abstract and κ = 0.92 for full-text screening, indicating high agreement. Both reviewers additionally confirmed that all included studies met the pre-specified eligibility criteria.

Data extraction

A standardised extraction form was developed in Excel and pilot-tested on five randomly selected studies; the form was refined after pilot testing to improve the clarity and consistency of the intervention, technology, outcome, and platform-related fields. Two reviewers independently extracted: author/year, country, objective, design, sample size, participant characteristics, virtual simulation technology and platform, nursing domain, intervention dose, control group, outcomes, assessment tools, and key findings. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion against the source publications. The data-standardisation and mapping framework used to harmonise extracted variables across studies is provided in Additional file 3, and the complete data-extraction matrix for all 198 included studies in Additional file 4.

Data analysis and presentation

Consistent with JBI scoping-review methodology, we did not undertake a formal methodological quality appraisal and did not apply a quality threshold for inclusion [20]. Independent verification of eligibility by the two reviewers was distinct from methodological quality appraisal. To contextualise the nature of the evidence base, study design, sample size, comparator characteristics, and assessment tools were charted descriptively at the study level (Additional file 4) rather than used to generate formal quality ratings, and their implications are considered in the Limitations. Data were synthesised using descriptive numerical analysis (frequencies, percentages, ranges) and descriptive categorisation of extracted data, and presented in tables, a figure, and narrative summary.

Results

The database search retrieved 5,990 records. After 2,742 duplicates were removed, 3,248 records were screened. Title/abstract screening excluded 2,910 records, leaving 338 reports sought for retrieval. Of these, 27 full texts could not be retrieved (owing to no institutional access or unavailable full text), and 311 were assessed for eligibility. A total of 113 reports were then excluded. Study characteristics

Publication volume rose sharply across the review period, growing from two studies in 2019 to 82 in 2025; annual output more than tripled between 2022 and 2025, and almost two-thirds of all included studies (n = 128; 64.6%) appeared in the final two years. The included studies were conducted across six continents, in 30 individual countries or regions, with a further three studies conducted across multiple countries; the evidence was strongly concentrated in East Asia. South Korea contributed the most studies (n = 42; 21.2%), followed by the USA (n = 37; 18.7%) and China, Taiwan (n = 22; 11.1%); Turkey, Canada, China (Hong Kong SAR), and Norway each contributed a smaller but notable share (Additional file 6, Table S1). Representation from Africa, South America, and Central Asia was notably limited.

Quasi-experimental designs were the most common (n = 55; 27.8%), followed by randomised controlled trials (RCTs; n = 45; 22.7%); the remainder comprised mixed-methods, pilot/feasibility, cross-sectional/descriptive, qualitative, one-group pre–post, and other designs (full distribution in Additional file 6, Table S2). Sample sizes ranged from 8 to 1,331 participants (median = 64.0; mean = 96.6); 139 studies (70.2%) enrolled fewer than 100 participants and only 18 (9.1%) enrolled ≥ 200 participants. Comparator conditions varied widely, including traditional teaching (lecture- or textbook-based), alternative simulation methods (manikin- or standardised-patient-based), and different virtual modalities; nonetheless, a substantial proportion of studies used single-group designs without a formal control group, limiting causal inference.

Virtual simulation technology types

Immersive virtual reality (VR) was the most frequently reported modality. Interactive immersive VR—delivered in almost all cases through a head-mounted display (HMD), and in one study through a room-scale projection system—was used in 110 studies (55.6%), and pre-recorded 360-degree or 180-degree video VR in a further 15 (7.6%); aggregated, immersive VR accounted for 63.1% (n = 125). Screen-based virtual simulation was the next most frequent modality (47; 23.7%). The remainder comprised augmented reality (AR; 8; 4.0%), virtual gaming simulation (6; 3.0%), mixed reality (MR; 5; 2.5%), virtual patient simulation (5; 2.5%), and other or unspecified approaches (2; 1.0%) (Table 1). Across the 198 included studies, commonly used commercial platforms included vSim for Nursing (Laerdal/Wolters Kluwer), Body Interact, Shadow Health, and CareGiVR. Many studies also used custom Unity- or Unreal Engine-based applications, and a substantial share employed researcher-developed tools. Nursing education domains

Studies covered a broad curricular range. Clinical skills (59; 29.8%) was the largest single domain, encompassing assessment, reasoning, prioritisation, and procedural competence. Emergency and critical care (33; 16.7%) and maternal/paediatric nursing (33; 16.7%) followed; the latter included paediatric assessment, developmental care, neonatal resuscitation, and obstetric scenarios. Mental health nursing (16; 8.1%) addressed therapeutic relationships, psychiatric assessment, and stigma reduction; gerontology (10; 5.1%) and perioperative nursing (10; 5.1%) focused on dementia care and surgical safety, respectively. Other domains included communication training (13; 6.6%), community/public health (6; 3.0%), and cultural competence (4; 2.0%) (Table 2). A further set of studies addressed domains not elsewhere classified (other domains, 12; 6.1%), and a small number spanned multiple curricular domains (2; 1.0%). Favourable findings for self-confidence and self-efficacy were frequently reported across technology types and nursing domains, although these findings were heterogeneous and were not formally pooled or appraised for methodological quality, with students showing higher post-intervention scores than baseline and—where reported—than control groups. For example, Yu and Yang [25] reported significantly greater gains in self-efficacy in the virtual-simulation group than in controls (t = 4.93, p < 0.001), and Cha et al. [26] found significant increases in performance confidence (t = 2.19, p = 0.031) and self-efficacy (t = 3.43, p = 0.001). Many comparative studies reported improvements in knowledge following virtual simulation relative to traditional didactic methods, with reported gains in spatial (anatomy), procedural (medication administration), and scenario-based (clinical decision-making) domains; gains from desktop-based simulation were sometimes comparable to those from traditional teaching. Effect estimates were reported inconsistently across the included studies and were not extracted systematically for this review, so the magnitude of reported effects could not be summarised, and no inference about the relative effectiveness of modalities can be drawn from this mapping. By way of illustration, Chang et al. [27] reported higher knowledge scores in the virtual-simulation group than in controls (80.9 vs 74.4; p < 0.001; Cohen’s d = 0.69), and Chan et al. [28] found significantly greater knowledge improvement in the virtual-reality group than in controls (F = 6.412, p = 0.013).

Studies measuring clinical skills frequently reported improvements in procedural accuracy, task completion time, protocol adherence, and clinical judgment following virtual simulation. Where immersive virtual reality was compared with lower-fidelity alternatives, reported findings were mixed; these comparisons were not synthesised in the present review. With respect to specific procedural skills, Chang et al. [27] reported large effect sizes favouring the virtual-simulation group for medication-administration skills (Cohen’s d = 0.89) and suctioning skills (d = 0.75); Yu and Yang [25] found significantly better infection-control performance in the virtual-reality group than in controls (t = 4.89, p < 0.001); and Al-Mugheed et al. [29] reported significantly greater improvement in standard-precautions knowledge, attitudes, and compliance in the game-based virtual-reality group than in controls. Mixed-methods studies showed that students perceived virtual simulation as a safe space to make and learn from mistakes, enabling deeper engagement with content difficult to revisit in real clinical encounters. Satisfaction and engagement were frequently reported as favourable among the studies that measured them, with students citing interactivity, realism, novelty, and self-paced learning as key drivers; some studies attributed increased motivation to gamification, realistic avatars, and high-fidelity environments. Reported drawbacks included cybersickness with headset-based VR (most pronounced in extended sessions), technical issues such as software bugs and hardware-pairing problems, and initial technology-related anxiety, particularly among students with limited prior gaming or VR experience.

Twenty-one studies measured anxiety or stress outcomes, most often in psychiatric, emergency, and end-of-life domains, and several reported reductions in anxiety following repeated virtual practice. These reports were neither pooled nor appraised, so no overall effect on anxiety can be inferred from this mapping. Assessment instruments included the Lasater Clinical Judgment Rubric, NASC-CDM scale, Simulation Design Scale, Student Satisfaction and Self-Confidence in Learning scale, Empathy Toward the Mentally Ill Scale, System Usability Scale, and various knowledge tests. A substantial proportion of studies relied on researcher-developed or modified instruments, raising concerns about cross-study comparability.

Summary of evidence characteristics

Additional file 6 (Table S2) summarises the characteristics of all 198 studies. The evidence base is marked by rapid growth, geographic concentration in East Asia and North America, methodological predominance of quasi-experimental designs, a clear preference for immersive VR, broad domain application, and predominantly favourable reported outcomes—although heterogeneity in designs, outcome measures, and instruments limits comparative inferences.

Discussion

Summary of key findings

This review mapped the recent evidence on virtual simulation in nursing education. Rather than restating the mapped frequencies detailed in the Results, the following sections interpret the principal patterns and their implications: the consolidation of immersive virtual reality as the dominant modality alongside the sustained presence of screen-based simulation as a secondary approach, with augmented and mixed reality present but comparatively limited; the geographic concentration of the evidence in East Asia and North America; the continued predominance of quasi-experimental designs with small samples alongside a rising share of RCTs; and the predominantly favourable but methodologically heterogeneous reported outcomes, whose interpretation is tempered by limited longitudinal follow-up.

Evolution of virtual-simulation adoption

The predominance of immersive virtual reality (63.1% in aggregate) marks a clear shift from the screen-based paradigm of the pre-2020 literature [14, 15, 17]. Augmented reality remained a comparatively minor share of the mapped literature (4.0%), suggesting that, despite growing interest, it has not yet displaced immersive or screen-based approaches as a mainstream modality in nursing education [19, 30]. Several external factors may have contributed to the growth of immersive virtual reality, although the present review did not directly evaluate determinants of technology adoption; these include declining head-mounted-display costs and the commoditisation of standalone devices, which have removed a major financial barrier for academic adopters; the maturation of content-creation ecosystems (Unity, Unreal Engine, proprietary nursing platforms) has democratised scenario development for educators with limited programming expertise; and accumulating evidence of cognitive and affective benefits in spatial, procedural, and empathy-based learning has shifted educator expectations [5]. Yet the persistent presence of screen-based virtual simulation in more than one in five included studies indicates that immersive formats have not displaced it: platforms such as vSim for Nursing and Body Interact were frequently reported for clinical-reasoning training and may offer a more accessible option in some resource-constrained settings, although implementation costs and feasibility were not systematically evaluated in this review [31].

The comparatively small number of mixed-reality studies may indicate an earlier stage of adoption, although this review cannot determine whether this pattern reflects cost, implementation complexity, pedagogical factors, or other barriers [19]. The complementary affordances of augmented and mixed reality—overlaying clinical information onto physical environments and blending virtual artefacts with real-world practice—position them as natural bridges between simulated (in-silico) and bedside learning; future research should clarify how they complement, rather than replace, virtual reality and screen-based approaches. Compared with earlier maps, the field’s evolution is striking: Plotzky et al. [15], searching through 2020, identified 37 studies and Chen et al.’s [17] meta-analysis synthesised 12 RCTs, whereas the present review captures 198 primary studies, with almost two-thirds published in 2024–2025. The scopes of these reviews are not directly comparable, and the difference in yield therefore reflects both the growth of the literature and the broader eligibility criteria applied here; taken together with diversification beyond head-mounted-display virtual reality into augmented reality, mixed reality, 360° video, and virtual-patient platforms, it nonetheless supports the case for an updated, broader-scope evidence map.

Geographic and methodological considerations

The geographic concentration in South Korea, the USA, and China, Taiwan raises questions about generalisability and cultural transferability. South Korea’s lead likely reflects strong technology infrastructure, sustained government investment in educational technology research, and an established simulation culture in nursing education [32]. Similar enabling conditions in the USA and China, Taiwan have produced large bodies of evidence, but the cultural, curricular, and resource contexts of these countries may not translate directly to other settings. The limited representation from several world regions—particularly Africa, South America, South Asia, and Central Asia—is a notable equity concern, as these regions face the most acute nursing-workforce shortages and could benefit substantially from technology-enhanced training that supplements limited placement capacity [33]; targeted research and platform-development efforts in these settings should be prioritised.

Methodologically, the predominance of quasi-experimental designs and of studies without formal control groups indicates that the evidence base, though rapidly expanding, remains limited in rigour. The share of randomised controlled trials (22.7%) is encouraging and exceeds earlier reviews of simulation-based nursing education [34], but small sample sizes (median = 64.0, with seven in ten studies enrolling fewer than 100 participants) constrain power and limit generalisability. The continued reliance on quasi-experimental and single-group designs, rather than randomised controlled trials, may reflect the practical constraints of conducting adequately powered trials in educational settings, including limited research funding, variable technological infrastructure, cohort-size limitations within individual programmes, and the logistical and ethical challenges of randomising students within a shared curriculum; these constraints may also contribute to the concentration of the evidence in better-resourced settings. Nonetheless, this share of randomised controlled trials represents a notable methodological strengthening relative to earlier reviews of simulation-based nursing education. Multi-site, adequately powered RCTs with standardised outcomes are needed to support firmer recommendations for practice and to enable robust meta-analytic synthesis [35].

Educational applications and outcomes

Virtual simulation has been applied across the nursing curriculum, from fundamental skills to specialised domains such as psychiatric nursing, end-of-life care, and disaster preparedness, reflecting its flexibility for scenarios that are otherwise rare or ethically challenging in clinical settings [9]. The prominence of clinical decision-making and communication aligns with contemporary priorities emphasising higher-order cognitive and interpersonal competencies [36]. The limited representation of community/public-health and cultural-competence applications is notable given the strategic importance of these competencies for addressing global health inequities; future development should prioritise scenarios engaging students in social determinants of health, intercultural communication, and community-based practice.

Three caveats apply to the predominantly favourable outcomes reported across confidence, knowledge, skills, and satisfaction. First, the high proportion of positive findings may reflect publication bias, with null and negative results under-represented [37]. Second, the predominance of short-term pre-post designs limits inference about durability and transfer to clinical practice; only a small minority of studies assessed retention beyond the immediate post-intervention period or evaluated downstream effects on patient care [38]. Third, the lack of standardisation in outcome measures and assessment tools impedes cross-study comparison and meta-analytic synthesis, with researcher-developed or modified instruments used in a substantial proportion of studies.

Implications for nursing education practice

The patterns mapped in this review—the diversity of modalities in current use, the concentration of evidence in confidence-, knowledge-, and skills-related domains, and the identified gaps in longitudinal and comparative evidence—inform several practical considerations for nursing educators and curriculum designers. First, virtual simulation should complement, rather than replace, traditional clinical experience and manikin-based simulation; blended approaches integrating virtual and physical modalities may offer complementary educational affordances, although comparative effectiveness was not established in this scoping review [39]. Second, educators should match technology to learning objectives rather than defaulting to the most immersive option available: immersive virtual reality has most often been applied to spatial, procedural, and emotionally salient learning, whereas screen-based virtual simulation—which retains a substantial presence in the mapped literature—has more often been applied to clinical-reasoning-focused learning. The present review did not compare the effectiveness of these modalities. Third, adequate technical support, structured faculty development, and well-designed pre-briefing and debriefing should accompany implementation to optimise learning and to mitigate cybersickness, technical issues, and initial technology-related anxiety [40]. Fourth, curricular integration should be explicit and intentional rather than ad hoc, with virtual simulation experiences mapped to specific competencies and scaffolded progressively across the programme. Given the scarcity of longitudinal and cost-effectiveness evidence identified in this review, these recommendations should be regarded as provisional and revisited as stronger evidence accumulates.

Limitations of this review

Consistent with scoping review methodology, no formal quality appraisal and no GRADE-equivalent certainty assessment were undertaken [20, 22]; this reflects the purpose and methodological scope of a scoping review rather than a shortcoming of this particular review, and it means that the findings map the breadth rather than the strength of the available evidence and should not be read as causal or effect-size claims. Limitations specific to this review are as follows. First, restriction to English-language publications and to four databases (PubMed, CINAHL, Web of Science, Scopus) may have excluded relevant studies indexed elsewhere or in other languages. Second, heterogeneity in how technologies and outcomes were described required some subjective categorisation. Technology categories were assigned on the basis of the modality descriptions reported in each source publication; hardware details were incompletely reported in some studies, and modality boundaries—particularly between mobile-delivered, headset-delivered, and overlay-based systems—are not always sharply defined, so the reported modality proportions should be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive. Third, the direction of the findings reported by each included study was not charted as a standardised study-level variable; descriptions of favourable or positive findings in this review therefore reflect the narrative reports of individual studies and should not be read as a quantified proportion of studies showing benefit. Sources of funding for the included primary studies were likewise not charted, as funding information was not among the pre-specified extraction variables, so the potential influence of study funding on the reported findings could not be examined. Fourth, publication bias likely shaped the strongly positive tone of the included literature [37]. Fifth, indexing of late-2025 publications was likely incomplete at the search date (15 January 2026), so the 82 studies recorded for 2025 should be regarded as a lower bound. The exclusion of grey literature may also have reduced coverage of implementation case reports and cost-effectiveness analyses. Finally, the review protocol was registered on the Open Science Framework after the database searches had been completed; this retrospective registration, although reported transparently, is acknowledged as a limitation. Notwithstanding these limitations, the review has several strengths, including a comprehensive four-database search, a large and current evidence base of 198 studies, duplicate independent screening and extraction, and transparent reporting in accordance with PRISMA-ScR. With respect to the nature of the evidence, although scoping reviews do not formally grade certainty, the design profile of the included studies—a rising but still minority share of randomised controlled trials alongside a predominance of quasi-experimental and single-group designs—indicates an evidence base that is expanding in rigour yet remains limited in the strength of causal inference it can support.

Three structural gaps warrant particular attention. First, longitudinal evidence on the transfer of virtual-simulation learning to clinical performance and patient outcomes remains scarce, limiting confidence in causal claims about effectiveness. Second, several world regions, particularly Africa and South America, are markedly under-represented, despite acute nursing-workforce shortages in many of these settings and their potential to benefit from scalable technology-enhanced training. Third, the absence of shared outcome-measurement frameworks and validated assessment instruments hinders cross-study comparison and meta-analytic synthesis.

Future research directions

Several research priorities emerge directly from the gaps mapped in this review. First, the field needs adequately powered, multi-site RCTs with standardised outcome measures and longitudinal follow-up to establish long-term effectiveness and transfer of learning to clinical practice. Cost-effectiveness analyses comparing virtual simulation with traditional simulation and clinical placement are notably scarce despite their decisive role in institutional adoption decisions. Studies from currently under-represented world regions are needed to assess feasibility, cultural adaptation, and contextually appropriate implementation models, including low-cost mobile and screen-based modalities where high-end virtual reality is not viable. Validated competency-assessment frameworks tailored to virtual simulation environments would strengthen comparability across studies and enable meaningful synthesis. Optimal pedagogical integration—including dose–response relationships, the role of facilitated debriefing, and interaction with manikin-based simulation and clinical placement—warrants systematic investigation. Beyond these evidence-derived priorities, and reflecting broader trends in the wider field rather than findings of the present review, emerging technologies such as artificial-intelligence-driven adaptive learning, haptic feedback, and multiplayer collaborative environments are anticipated to reshape virtual simulation over the coming decade and merit early empirical attention.

Conclusions

Across 198 primary studies published between 2019 and 2025, virtual simulation in nursing education has expanded rapidly, with annual publications more than tripling between 2022 and 2025 and 64.6% of all included studies appearing in 2024–2025 alone. Within the included literature, immersive virtual reality was the most frequently reported modality, screen-based platforms retained a notable secondary presence in reasoning-focused and resource-constrained contexts, and augmented and mixed reality contributed a smaller but emerging share of the evidence. Across a broad range of nursing education domains, the included studies predominantly reported favourable findings for outcomes such as confidence, knowledge, skills, and satisfaction; however, as a scoping review, this study maps the extent and nature of the evidence rather than appraising or quantifying intervention effectiveness, and these reported patterns should be interpreted as descriptive rather than as confirmation of effect.

Nevertheless, important structural gaps remain—notably the scarcity of longitudinal evidence, the limited representation from several world regions, and the lack of shared outcome-measurement frameworks (discussed above). Addressing these through multi-site, adequately powered RCTs with standardised, validated instruments, longitudinal follow-up, and cost-effectiveness analyses in resource-constrained contexts would help to build a more robust and equitable evidence base to inform future nursing-education research and practice.
