Multiplex serological profiling reveals diverse avian and mammalian influenza A virus exposure in swine

ABSTRACT
SummaryAnimal-origin influenza viruses pose significant pandemic threats, with swine serving as key hosts. Serological surveillance in pigs remains limited in regions with intense human-animal-avian contact. Between March 2020 and July 2022, we collected 4,069 pig serum samples in Cambodia, of which 1,321 (32.5%) were influenza A virus (IAV) seropositive by ELISA. We developed a multiplex microsphere immunoassay comprising a broad panel of hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA) antigens. Seroprevalence was highest for pandemic H1N1/pdm09 (35.3–45.7%) and lower for classical swine H1 (16.4–17.7%) and European avian-like swine H1 (∼15%) lineages. Lineage-specific or shared exposures to multiple lineages were observed, indicating complex infection histories. H3 responses varied by clade, while antibodies to avian H5, H7, and H9 were detected in <8% of IAV-positive sera. These findings underscore the ongoing zoonotic risk posed by diverse IAVs circulating in pigs and highlight the need for integrated serological and genomic surveillance.

Introduction

Pandemic influenza viruses of animal origin remain a persistent threat to global health. Swine influenza A viruses (swIAVs) have impacted both animal and human health, and the global pork industry, through their sustained circulation and continual evolution worldwide.1,2 Swine uniquely serve as important intermediate hosts, being susceptible to both avian and human influenza A viruses (IAVs). A major driver of swIAV diversification is reassortment where gene segments are exchanged during co-infection, which allows the emergence of influenza strains with zoonotic and pandemic potential.3,4 To date, at least seven major swIAV lineages have been identified in pigs, including regionally diversified lineages such as the North American classical swine (CS) H1N15,6 and the European avian-like (EA) swine H1N1 virus7 and more globally mixed strains, including the pre-2009 human-origin seasonal swH1N1 derived from the 1918 Spanish flu,8,9 human-origin seasonal H3N2,10,11 swH1N2 reassortants carrying the North American triple-reassortant (TRIG) internal gene cassette,12,13 and the swine-origin H1N1/2009 pandemic (H1N1/pdm09) lineage.14,15 Due to insufficient surveillance, these reassortant viruses often circulate silently in swine for years, or even decades, before emerging in humans. Although the H1N1/pdm09 virus has transitioned into a seasonal strain in humans, repeated reverse zoonotic transmission has led to its widespread establishment in pig populations across Asia, Europe, and the Americas.16,17,18,19,20 In Southeast Asia, studies examining the ecology and diversity of influenza viruses circulating in pigs are scarce, complicating efforts to control disease outbreaks and prevent zoonotic spillover, particularly at the human-animal interface. Nevertheless, over the last decade, multiple distinct genetic lineages such as H1N1/pdm09, CS H1N1, seasonal H3N2, pre-2009 seasonal H1N1, and triple reassortment viruses have been repeatedly detected in Vietnam, Thailand, Laos, and Myanmar.18,19,21,22

In Cambodia, we conducted swine influenza surveillance at pig slaughterhouses during the COVID-19 pandemic. Despite stringent control measures and travel restrictions, genomic sequencing and phylogenetic analyses revealed extensive genetic diversity among H1 and H3 swIAV lineages co-circulating in pigs between 2020 and 2022. These included pdm09 H1, CS H1, EA swine H1, pre-2009 seasonal H1 and seasonal H3, with pdm09 H1 predominating. Many of these lineages circulated undetected in pig populations for over 15 years,16 mirroring the cryptic circulation of swine H1N1 viruses prior to the emergence of the H1N1/2009 pandemic. Notably, we previously identified an undetected EA swine H1N2 reassortant virus containing gene segments from both Asian and North American swine lineages, highlighting the pandemic risk posed by ongoing reassortment in swine.16 Originating from avian viruses in the late 1970s, EA swine H1N1 viruses acquired mammalian adaptation through a gradual shift in receptor-binding preference from α2,3 to α2,6-linked sialic acids and several adaptive mutations enhancing replication and transmissibility in pigs.23 Since 1983, these viruses have spread efficiently among pigs and expanded from Europe into East Asia (China) and Southeast Asia. Serological studies in China report seropositivity rates of up to 14% among pig farm workers.24 Sporadic human infections continue to be reported, including swH1N1 cases in Vietnam (2024),25 swH1N2 in the US (2025),26 EA swine H1N1 in China (2023),27 and a fatal infection in Brazil (2023),28 highlighting persistent public health concerns.

Within Cambodia, highly pathogenic avian H5N1 viruses are endemic in domestic poultry, where frequent genetic reassortment drives continuous viral evolution.29 Since 2003, the country has reported 75 laboratory-confirmed human cases of avian H5N1 infection, causing 46 deaths with a case fatality rate of 61% according to the World Health Organization.30 Since 2014, H5 clade 2.3.2.1c viruses have diversified (recently reclassified as clade 2.3.2.1e31), and replaced the earlier circulating clade 1.1.2 viruses. Beginning in 2021, both clade 2.3.2.1e and 2.3.4.4b viruses have co-circulated in domestic chickens and ducks. Additionally, an H5N1 reassortant between both clades emerged in the country in 2023, resulting in 16 confirmed human cases between February 2023 and August 2024.32 This recent surge in H5N1 infections in Cambodia underscores the potential zoonotic threat posed by emerging influenza viruses and reinforces the urgent need for enhanced surveillance systems. While genomic sequencing is critical for identifying and characterizing circulating subtypes and lineages, its effectiveness in domestic animals, including pigs, is constrained by the timing of sample collection—often missing the acute phase of infection—and thus may underestimate the true disease prevalence and overall burden.

In contrast, serological surveillance offers a powerful complementary approach by detecting antibody responses indicating prior exposure, including in asymptomatic or undetected infections. Traditional commercial serologic assays, such as ELISA, primarily target the conserved nucleoprotein (NP), and are useful for detecting general influenza A exposure but lack subtype-level resolution. Other assays such as hemagglutination inhibition (HI) and microneutralization (MN) assay rely on live viruses and are labor-intensive, typically limited to testing one subtype at a time, making them impractical and less efficient for larger population studies. As the genetic diversity of various viral subtypes continues to expand, there is a critical need for efficient and scalable serological platforms to assess population-level immunity and determine seroprevalence at subtype- or lineage-level. Multiplex serologic approaches have demonstrated the capacity to evaluate host antibody responses across a broad range of viruses, including sarbecoviruses (such as SARS-CoV-2 variants),33,34 influenza viruses,35 filoviruses,36,37,38 and flaviviruses such as Zika and dengue.39

Building on our previous screening of over 4,000 pig serum samples using an NP-based ELISA, we selected all IAV-seropositive samples for further characterization using a multiplex microsphere immunoassay we developed to capture the breadth and diversity of influenza A antibody responses in swine populations in Cambodia. This immunoassay is capable of simultaneously detecting binding antibodies against a diverse panel of 17 influenza hemagglutinin (HA) and 6 neuraminidase (NA) proteins, representing major influenza subtypes and lineages circulating in human and animal populations (Table S1). The selection of antigens was guided by our genomic surveillance efforts16 and includes relevant swine and zoonotic influenza subtypes, such as highly and low pathogenic avian H5, H7, and H9.

Results

High seroprevalence of pandemic H1N1/pdm09 antibodies among pigs

Between March 2020 and July 2022, we collected 4,069 serum samples from pigs at 18 slaughterhouses across four provinces in Cambodia. Using a NP-targeted ELISA,40 we found that 1,321 (32.5%) of the samples were positive for IAV (Figure 1 and Table S2), indicating substantial prior exposure to IAVs, despite the implementation of COVID-19 control measures and African swine fever outbreaks. To characterize seroprevalence at HA and NA subtype level, we developed a custom multiplex bead-based assay incorporating HA and NA antigens, including pandemic H1N1/pdm09, CS H1N1, EA swine H1N1, pre-2009 human seasonal H1N1, seasonal H3N2, avian H5, H7 and H9 subtypes, and influenza B virus (IBV)/Victoria lineage (Figure 2 and Table S1). Serological exposure to diverse H1 and H3 influenza A lineages

We next assessed serological evidence of exposure to other genetically distinct H1 lineages that co-circulate in pigs. To represent the EA swine lineage, we included two HA antigens: one from a Cambodian swine reassortant H1N2 strain (A/swine/Cambodia/PFC54/2021) and another from a human EA swine-origin H1N1 strain (A/Pavia/65/2016). Serological evidence of EA swine H1 exposure was detected in 15.2–15.5% of IAV-seropositive pigs (Figure 2A and Table 1). The comparable seropositivity observed for both antigens suggests probable sustained transmission of EA swine viruses among pigs across Southeast Asia and Europe, consistent with largely silent regional circulation.

Similar levels of seropositivity were observed for CS H1 (16.4–17.7%) and pre-2009 human seasonal H1 (20.1%). Notably, the H1N1/pdm09 HA antigens appeared to show lineage specificity: positive control antiserum raised against human H1N1/pdm09 (indicated by pink triangles in Figure 2) reacted specifically with pdm09 antigens and showed no detectable cross-reactivity with other tested HAs, including CS H1, EA swine H1, pre-2009 seasonal H1, seasonal H3, avian HAs (H5, H7 and H9) or the IBV/Victoria lineage (Figures 2A and 2B).

Beyond H1, human-origin swine H3N2 viruses circulating in Cambodian pigs displayed greater genetic diversity, reflecting frequent spillover from humans. Based on our earlier phylogenetic analyses, these viruses segregated into two distinct clades, designated as group 1 and 2 as described by Zeller et al.16 To capture this diversity, our multiplex panel included H3 antigens from two Cambodian swine H3N2 strains (clade 1: A/swine/Cambodia/PFC64/2022; clade 2: A/swine/Cambodia/PFC16/2020) along with two additional representative H3N2 strains from each clade. Serological analysis revealed varying levels of swine H3-HA antibodies, with higher seroprevalence to clade 2 (32.4%–32.8%, Figure 2B) compared with clade 1 antigens (13.3%–16.5%), suggesting clade 2 predominance or a closer antigen match to recently circulating strains. Phylogenetically, H3N2 clade 2 viruses were closely related to swine H3N2 strains from Thailand, whereas clade 1 viruses clustered more closely with swine H3N2 lineages from China. These patterns suggest that geographic proximity and regional animal movement may facilitate virus dissemination, contributing to the observed differences in serological responses among Cambodian pigs.

All sampled pigs had no history of influenza vaccination. Comparison of median fluorescence intensity (MFI) values across HA antigens showed that pdm09 H1 and EA swine H1 antigens elicited modestly higher responses than CS H1 and pre-2009 seasonal H1 (Figure 2C), possibly indicating more recent infections. Overall, Cambodian pigs exhibited heterogeneous seroprevalence across multiple H1 and H3 lineages, reflecting widespread exposure to diverse IAVs and ongoing IAV transmission within swine populations.

A subset of pig sera showed positivity exclusively to the pdm09 H1 lineage (red arrows in Figure 3A), while remaining seronegative to other HA antigens. In contrast, other samples exhibited reactivity to multiple HA antigens, consistent with co-exposure or co-infection. For example, some samples reacted to both EA swine H1 and CS H1 antigens but showed lower MFI values against pdm09 H1, whereas others showed predominant reactivity to H3 and pdm09 H1 antigens (dotted blue boxes in Figure 3A). To illustrate the breadth and magnitude of HA-specific responses at the individual sample level, a radar plot was generated to show relative MFI reactivity across HA antigens (Figure S1). The analysis of representative individual samples further showed that, despite co-reactivity to pdm09 and EA swine H1 antigens, MFI values were markedly higher for one lineage, suggesting differences in infection timing or exposure history (Figure S1). In some instances, comparable MFI values were observed for both H3 and EA swine lineages, indicative of more recent co-infections.

To further contextualize these serological patterns, we compared the genetic divergence among the tested HA antigens and reconstructed an evolutionary tree (Figure 3B). EA swine H1-HA is genetically and phylogenetically distant from both the pdm09 (73.3–74.1% nucleotide similarity) and CS lineage (73.7–74.5%, Table S3). In comparison, pdm09 and H3 HAs are even more divergent, sharing only 49.1–50.6% nucleotide similarity. A small subset of samples showed broad cross-reactivity across multiple HA antigens (dotted gray box at top of Figure 3A), likely reflecting antibodies targeting conserved HA2 regions or nonspecific binding, but these represented only a minor proportion of the total samples. In addition to H1 and H3, a minority of samples displayed higher reactivity to avian HAs, although less pronounced than for other antigens.

Taken together, these results indicate that serological profiles of IAVs among pigs in Cambodia are highly dynamic, influenced by the timing of exposure, duration of antibody responses, and prior infection history.

Divergent antibody signatures of H1 and H3 subtypes

We next compared the degree of shared HA reactivity using topological network analysis (Figure 3C). Among H1N1/pdm09 antigens, they shared similar levels of reactivity (denoted by red solid circles in Figure 3C), with 415 and 407 of 545 sera reactive to A/Wisconsin/67/2022, also positive for A/California/07/2009 and A/swine/Cambodia/PFC63/2021, respectively (Figure S2). Strong linkage was also observed among H3-HA clade 2 antigens (A/swine/Cambodia/PFC16/2020 and A/swine/Thailand/CU-P53/2012), sharing 346 positive sera (blue solid circles in Figures 3C and S2). In contrast, avian HA antigens (H5, H7, and H9) showed limited cross-reactivity with H1 or H3, which is unsurprising given their distinct evolutionary origins and greater phylogenetic divergence (Figure 3B). Shared reactivity between H1 and H3 antigens was less pronounced.

To further investigate antibody reactivity between H1 and H3 antigens, we performed Spearman correlation analyses of the immune profiles for each pig. Hierarchical clustering revealed two distinct clusters, corresponding to differential serological responses to H1 and H3 antibodies (Figure 4A). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) confirmed a statistically significant separation between H1 and H3 immune profiles (p < 0.001), with marked differences in the number of seropositive samples across all eight H1-HA and four H3-HA antigens (Figure 4B). Notably, pig sera seropositive for H1-HA—including human pdm09, CS, EA swine and pre-2009 human seasonal lineages—consistently exhibited lower reactivity to H3-HA antigens (Figures 4C and S3). Conversely, H3-HA seropositive samples demonstrated reduced reactivity to H1-HA (Figure 4C). Limited reactivity to avian HAs, with a single H5-positive in the virus neutralization assay

From a zoonotic perspective and given the circulation of highly pathogenic H5N1 viruses in Cambodia, we examined reactivity to avian influenza antigens representing H5, H7, and H9 subtypes to evaluate potential cross-species exposure. In contrast to the higher seropositivity observed for swine and human-origin H1 and H3 strains, only 5–7.9% of IAV-positive pig serum samples exhibited seropositivity against avian H5, H7, and H9 subtypes (Figure 2B).

We further evaluated MFI-positive sera using HI and virus neutralization (VN) assays with live highly pathogenic H5N1 strains representing clade 2.3.2.1c and 2.3.4.4b viruses. Among 14 sera tested, one sample neutralized the H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b virus with a VN titer of 80, despite testing HI-negative (Table 2). This sample was collected on 12 January 2022 in Takeo Province, which borders Phnom Penh, the most densely populated province. Notably, clade 2.3.4.4b viruses were recently detected in domestic ducks from live poultry markers between 2018 and 2021 in multiple locations, including Takeo, Sammaki, and Chba Ampov.41 The remaining sera did not neutralize H5 viruses from either clade, suggesting that these MFI-positive samples likely contain binding rather than neutralizing antibodies. Differences for other antigens showed little variation between provinces, consistent with NP ELISA results, which showed relatively similar seroprevalence across the four provinces (30.0–36.9%, Table S2). We also performed Bayesian multivariate analyses to assess the associations between IAV and IBV lineage seropositivity and farm type. We show that seropositive pigs from smallholder farms were more likely to test positive for avian H5 and H9 compared to those from commercial farms (Figure 5B). This likely reflects typical smallholder farm practices, such as housing pigs in open pens or allowing them to forage, which increases opportunities for inter-species contact, particularly between pigs and domestic or wild birds, thereby raising the risk of avian influenza exposure. These results underscore the heightened cross-species spillover risks associated with smallholder farms due to limited biosecurity. Interestingly, breeding sows were more likely than finisher pigs (those near slaughter age) to possess pre-existing antibodies to all tested IAV antigens, likely owing to their longer lifespans and cumulative exposure to circulating viruses (Figure 5C). Collectively, these results highlight the impact of farm and pig age on swIAV exposure and offer valuable insights for designing targeted surveillance strategies to identify potential pandemic hotspots.

Low serological responses in pigs to neuraminidase and influenza B proteins

To complement our HA-focused analysis, we evaluated antibody responses in pigs against selected NA proteins. Discussion

Pandemic IAVs have historically caused significant mortality and morbidity in humans. These viruses typically originate from animal reservoirs and acquire the capacity for sustained human-to-human transmission through sequential adaptation events.14 The pandemic H1N1/pdm09 influenza virus exemplifies this process, highlighting the critical role of swine as an intermediate host in the emergence of reassortant strains that had circulated undetected in pigs for nearly a decade before its detection in humans,14,47 largely due to insufficient surveillance. While genomic surveillance is indispensable for tracing viral origin and identifying emerging variants, multiplex serological assays provide complementary insights by revealing prior exposure histories and population-level immunity in both animal and human hosts.

In this study, we developed a 23-plex microsphere immunoassay targeting a broad diversity of HA and NA antigens representing multiple influenza lineages. The analysis of NP-positive pig sera collected in Cambodia between 2020 and 2020 demonstrated seroreactivity to a diverse range of H1 lineages, including pandemic H1N1/pdm09, CS, EA swine, and pre-2009 human seasonal lineages. These findings are consistent with our previous genomic surveillance in Cambodia,16 and illustrate the complex multi-lineage influenza ecology within swine populations.

Our earlier virus surveillance across four provinces showed IAV prevalence of ∼2% by real-time qPCR and identified nine distinct swine IAV groups, at least seven of which had not been reported for 2–15 years. These included multiple human-origin H3 lineages that circulated cryptically in pigs for approximately a decade, pdm09, pre-2009 seasonal H1 lineages, and a distinctive EA swine H1N2 reassortant carrying North American internal genes. Co-detection of multiple viral lineages within the same surveillance period were common, including pdm09 and H3, and these patterns were similarly reflected in the serological data. These findings highlight frequent reassortment opportunities and the dynamic evolutionary landscape of swine influenza viruses in Cambodia.

Here, we observed differential H1 and H3 antibody responses in pigs, although the underlying mechanisms are unclear. One plausible explanation is variation in exposure history. H1 subtype belongs to HA phylogenetic Group 1 (which also includes H5 and H9), whereas H3 belongs to Group 2 (which includes H4, H7, H10, H14, and H15). The divergent origins of H1 and H3 HA may influence patterns of cross-reactive immune responses. Supporting this, experimental vaccination studies in swine have shown that sequential exposure to antigenically distinct influenza strains can substantially modify the breadth and magnitude of subtype-specific antibody responses.48,49 In particular, heterologous prime-boost regimens elicit broader and more cross-reactive H1 and H3 in pigs compared to homologous vaccination strategies.48,49 Collectively, these observations suggest that the contrasting H1 and H3 serological patterns observed in our study likely reflect differences in cumulative exposure history and immune imprinting rather than intrinsic differences in subtype immunogenicity.

We observed high seroprevalence (35.3–45.7%) against the pdm09 H1 antigen, consistent with the higher frequency of H1N1/pdm09 genomic detections in pigs. Notably, pdm09 seroprevalence was broadly similar across four provinces in Cambodia, although Kampong Speu showed a higher median MFI for pdm09 H1. Interestingly, genomic surveillance detected fewer H1N1 sequences in Kampong Speu compared with Kandal, Phnom Penh, and Takeo, suggesting that pigs in Kampong Speu may possess protective antibodies against pdm09. Furthermore, the higher median MFIs may reflect more recent infections, as previous studies have shown that antibodies in IAV-naïve pigs decline rapidly, with HI titers dropping below 40 within 8–15 weeks post-infection.50 Seropositivity to CS H1 (16.4–17.7%) and human pre-seasonal H1 (20.1%) lineages was also evident. H3-specific responses varied by clade, with higher seropositivity for clade 2 viruses (32.4–32.8%), which are phylogenetically and geographically related to swine H3N2 viruses from Thailand, compared to clade 1 viruses (13.3–16.5%) originating from swine in China. Both clades derive from human seasonal H3N2 viruses, reflecting repeated reverse zoonotic transmission. Reverse zoonosis of human seasonal H3N2 viruses into pigs has been widely reported in Australia, Vietnam, the United States, and other regions.17,19,51 Importantly, human infections with swine-origin pdm09 H1N1, pre-2009 seasonal H1N1 and H3N2 viruses are also documented, reinforcing the bidirectional transmission risk at the human-swine interface.51

Of particular interest, approximately 15% of pigs were seropositive for EA swine H1 antigen, showing comparable reactivity to two EA strains: a swine virus (A/swine/Cambodia/PFC60/2021) and a human virus (A/Pavis/65/2016). A subset of sera exhibited predominantly EA-specific antibody responses with high MFI values, suggesting substantial prior exposure. Although only four EA swine H1N2 reassortant viruses were genomically detected in Cambodia in 2021, evolutionary analysis indicates that these viruses likely circulated since 2014.16 The detection of EA antibodies in a large proportion of pigs suggests that genomic surveillance alone substantially underestimates virus circulation. These findings highlight important gaps in surveillance and underscore the value of integrating phylogenomic and serological approaches to improve early detection and better understand the prevalence of EA swine lineages in pigs.

A serologic study conducted in China tested 40,343 pig serum samples collected across 17 regions between 2016 and 2021, and detected 24.8% seropositivity to EA H1N1 subtype, and 7.95% to pandemic H1N1/2009 virus using HI assays.52 Genomic analyses further showed that the EA G4 genotype was predominant in pigs across 10 provinces in China and exhibited efficient replication in human airway epithelial cells and aerosol transmission in ferrets.52 More recently, Sun et al. reported substantial genomic diversity among EA H1N1 viruses circulating in pigs from China between 2016 and 2023, identifying at least 20 genotypes.24 Their accompanying serological data showed that 43% of pig farm workers were seropositive for EA H1N1 virus, compared to 14% in the general population, indicating occupational exposure risk. Experimental transmission studies in pigs confirmed efficient virus spread via direct contact. Additional investigations have demonstrated that EA swine viruses preferentially bind mammalian alpha-2,6-linked sialic acid receptors and harbor polymerase mutations associated with enhanced pathogenicity and transmissibility in humans.53,54 In 2018, the first human infection with an EA swine H1N1 reassortant virus was reported in a nine-year-old boy in China; his family raised chickens while neighbors kept pigs.54 The proximity of multiple animal species in rural farms and live bird markets continues to facilitate virus mixing and cross-species transmission. Systematic surveillance remains crucial for the early detection and risk assessment of emerging influenza strains, particularly in regions where swine production coexists with live poultry markets and diverse wild and domestic animal populations.

We detected low seropositivity (<8% of all IAV NP-positive samples) for H5, H7, and H9 viruses, including to both H5-HA clade 2.3.2.1c and 2.3.4.4b viruses. Notably, one pig serum was positive for H5 clade 2.3.4.4b by virus neutralization assay (VNT) despite being negative by HI assay. The observation that sera are HI-negative yet VNT-positive is consistent with previous influenza serology studies55,56 and reflects biological differences between the assays. HI specifically detects antibodies that block HA-receptor interactions on red blood cells, whereas VNT measures the ability of antibodies to inhibit viral infection in cell culture. Neutralization in the absence of detectable HI activity likely reflects antibodies targeting non-receptor-binding epitopes on HA (e.g., the stalk domain) or low-titer cross-reactive responses that fall below the HI detection threshold. In Cambodia, both clade 2.3.2.1e (previously classified as clade 2.3.2.1c) and 2.3.4.4b viruses are endemic in domestic poultry, with continuing sporadic human H5N1 cases.57 The detection of avian H5 antibodies in pigs highlights the close human-avian-pig interface and potential for zoonotic spillover. Serologic investigation of anti-H5 IAV antibodies in domestic pigs from Nigeria collected in 2015–2016 found 8.4% were positive for clade 2.3.2.1c.58

In contrast to HA, NA-specific responses were markedly lower in pigs. Studies of antibody responses in pigs are limited, but differential HA and NA responses have been reported in other animals such as mice.59 Our findings suggest that HA may be a more suitable target for multiplex serological detection of IAV in pigs. Notably, a recent study show that porcine HA stem-binding antibodies can confer protection in experimental pig models,60 with important implications for the design of effective influenza vaccines in pigs.

Multiplex bead-based immunoassays have been widely applied in the diagnosis and serological evaluation of other important viruses such as SARS-CoV-2, demonstrating high efficacy in detecting spike-specific antibodies,61 which are analogous to the HA proteins of influenza viruses. Similar platforms have also been deployed to detect swine diseases including African swine fever virus (ASFV) and porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV).62 Since early 2024, the emergence of highly pathogenic H5N1 in cattle has led to unprecedented outbreaks among dairy cows, spreading widely across at least 15 states in the United States.63 This raises concerns about the potential host expansion of H5N1 infection to other mammalian hosts, with the first H5N1 in pigs in the USA recently detected.64

Further studies are needed to evaluate the susceptibility of pigs to contemporary strains of avian influenza viruses. Additional experiments are also needed to evaluate the assay’s sensitivity and specificity across different subtypes and lineages. Longitudinal in vivo experiments following pig infection will also be critical to monitor the waning of B-cell responses over time. Moreover, developing multiplex assays that specifically target the receptor-binding domains of the HA globular head would help determine whether pre-existing antibodies possess neutralizing activity and confer protection. Such an approach would not only improve our understanding of immune responses to influenza viruses but also strengthen risk assessment efforts for potential spillover transmission events and cross-protective immunity. The insights gained here have important implications for outbreak preparedness, vaccine development and disease management in both humans and swine populations.

Limitations of the study

We designed an efficient multiplex serology tool and identified prior exposure to a diverse range of IAVs in pigs, including highly pathogenic avian influenza H5. Given the increasing genetic and antigenic diversity of influenza viruses, such assays offer a valuable complementary approach for the timely assessment of serological dynamics in rapidly evolving viruses. Experimental validation of the multiplex assay, however, was constrained by the limited availability of virus neutralization assays and other resources in a lower-income setting. Nevertheless, we detected one H5-positive serum sample capable of neutralizing a highly pathogenic H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b virus, indicating that our assay can detect H5-specific antibodies. The low number of HI-positive sera is not unexpected; similar patterns have been observed in US cattle, where H1 and H3 HI-positive rates were low despite 34% IAV seropositivity based on NP ELISA.65 While many antibody responses detected may be binding rather than neutralizing, these results underscore the assay’s potential for identifying prior H5 exposure. Additionally, IBV antibody responses were assessed only in sera that were NP ELISA-positive; nonetheless, the resulting panel of 1,236 samples remains substantial and enabled evaluation of IBV seroreactivity within the same cohort.

Experimental model and study participant details

Ethics statement and biosafety

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine (IRB number: 16635) and Animal Welfare and Ethical Research Board (ref. 2019-12), the National Ethics Committee for Health Research in Cambodia (ref. 105), and the Human Research Protection Office (HRPO, ref A-21055) and Animal Care and Use Review Office (ACURO) of the USAMRDC Office of Research Protections. All work involving live highly pathogenic avian influenza (H5) viruses was performed in certified biosafety level-3 (BSL-3) laboratories at Institut Pasteur Cambodia under enhanced biocontainment conditions and in accordance with institutional biosafety regulations and national guidelines. All personnel wore appropriate personal protective equipment, and all procedures were approved by the institutional biosafety committee.

Method details

Sample processing and testing

Between March 2020 and July 2022, 4,069 sera samples were collected from pigs across 18 slaughterhouses in Cambodia. These pigs were transported from smallholder (<100 pigs) and commercial (≥100 pigs) farms as previously defined.40 Four provinces, Kampong Speu, Kandal, Phnom Penh, and Takeo, were selected as sampling sites. We previously tested all sera samples for the presence of influenza A nucleoprotein-specific antibody using the ID Screen Influenza A antibody multi-species ELISA kits (IDVet-Innovative Diagnostics, France), yielding 1,321 IAV-positive samples.40 Of these, 85 samples were excluded due to insufficient volume, leaving 1,236 positive sera for subsequent multiplex serological experiments.

Multiplex bead coupling and serological assay

To evaluate the prevalence of antibodies against specific influenza subtype or lineages, we first selected a panel of recombinant proteins representing various lineage, including H1N1/pdm09, pre-2009 seasonal H1N1, EA swine H1N1 or H1N2 reassortant, seasonal H3, H5, H7, H9 and influenza B/Victoria lineage. The HA and NA proteins were monomers and were obtained either commercially source from Sino Biological Inc or synthesized by GenScript Biotech (Table S1). These proteins were modified to exclude transmembrane regions and to include polyhistidine tags. All IAV-positive sera were analyzed using a bead-based multiplex microsphere immunoassay on the Bio-Plex 200 platform (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA), with minor modifications from previously established assays.36,37 For bead coupling, 1.25 × 106 unique MagPlex carboxylated microsphere beads (DiaSorin, USA) were activated in 100 μL of activation buffer containing 5 mg/mL 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC) (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA), and 5 mg/mL N-hydroxysulfosuccinimide (Sulfo-NHS) (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) for 20 min at room temperature in the dark with agitation. Following activation, beads were washed thrice with PBS-T (1× PBS, 0.05% Tween 20) followed by coupling with 30 μg of recombinant HA or NA proteins (key resources table) for 2 h at room temperature in the dark, with continuous agitation. Coupled beads were then washed thrice with PBS-T and stored in beads storage buffer (1× PBS, 1% BSA, 0.05% sodium azide, protease inhibitor cocktail tablet) until use.

For the multiplex serological assay, 100 μL of diluted serum samples (1:500 in 1× PBS) were incubated with protein-coupled beads for 45 min at room temperature with continuous shaking. Unbound antibodies were removed by three washes with PBS-T, followed by incubation with biotinylated Protein A (for IgA) and biotinylated Protein G (for IgG) at a 1:1 ratio of A:G (final concentration, 1ug/mL per protein) (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). Unbound Protein A and G were removed by three PBS-T washes and the coupled beads-antibody-Protein A/G complexes were incubated with 100 μL of streptavidin-phycoerythrin (1:1000) (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA). After three additional washes, median fluorescence intensity (MFI) for each antigen-antibody complex was measured using the Bio-Plex 200 system and analyzed with Bio-Plex Manager software according to the manufacturer’s instructions. We also selected representative mouse and ferret antiserum samples as positive controls. Animal antisera for different influenza H1, H5, H7, and H9 subtypes, was generated by experimentally infecting animals with known influenza virus subtypes (Table S4). Negative pig control sera were obtained from healthy pigs (Sus scrofa) of high health status at the National Large Animal Research Facility (IACUC no. 2021/SHS/1623) in Singapore.

Data modeling and analysis

Antigen-antibody complexes were quantitatively measured as median fluorescence intensities (MFI) using the Bio-Plex 200 platform. For each HA or NA protein, we employed an expectation–maximization algorithm to plot the distribution of MFI values and categorize them using the Mclust function in R package mclust66 (Figure S4). The log likelihood corresponding to the specific number of clusters was determined and the optimal model selected based on the Bayesian Information Criteria. The final MFI cutoff values for positive samples were then determined (Table 1).

Phylogenetic analysis and genetic distance

Full-length hemagglutinin sequences representing influenza A subtypes and lineages (Table S1) were retrieved from the NCBI and GISAID databases. Sequences were aligned using MAFFT implemented in Geneious PRIME 2025.1.2 (Biomatters Ltd., New Zealand). Maximum-likelihood tree of the HA gene were reconstructed using the RAxML v.8.2.1167 as implemented in Geneious, with 1,000 bootstrap replicates to assess node support. Pairwise HA sequence similarities were calculated and expressed as percentages.

Hemagglutination inhibition (HI) assay

We used a panel of 14 H5-positive samples detected from multiplex assays to assess neutralizing activity against live H5 viruses. From the H5-positive sera, we selected 10 samples that were MFI-positive for both clade 2.3.2.1c and 2.3.4.4b, along with two samples positive for only one clade each, resulting in a total of 14 samples tested (Table 2). Hemagglutination inhibition assays were performed following standard WHO protocols (WHO Manual). Briefly, serum samples were first treated with receptor-destroying enzyme (RDE) derived from Vibrio cholerae and incubated at 37°C for 16 h to eliminate non-specific hemagglutination inhibitors followed by heat inactivation at 56°C for 1 h. 2-fold serial dilutions of the RDE-treated sera were prepared in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), starting at a dilution of 1:20, in U-bottom 96-well microtiter plates. Each dilution was incubated with four hemagglutinating units of one of the test viruses at 37°C for 30 min. Afterward, 1% turkey red blood cells were added, and the plates were incubated at 4°C for 1 h. Hemagglutination patterns were assessed in duplicate. Two representative H5N1 virus strains from different H5 clades were used: one from clade 2.3.2.1c (A/Cambodia/NPH230032/2023) and the other from clade 2.3.4.4b (A/duck/Cambodia/W47h1PPOreu241D1T/2023/H5N1). Negative controls (sera without virus) were included to detect non-specific hemagglutination and served as the assay’s sensitivity threshold. Positive control sera were derived from ferrets experimentally infected with clade 2.3.2.1e (A/duck/Vietnam/NCVD-1584/2012) and clade 2.3.4.4b (A/Ghana/AVL-76321VIR7050-39/2021) viruses. The A/duck/Vietnam/NCVD-1584/2012 virus was originally classified as H5N1 clade 2.3.2.1c but was renamed as clade 2.3.4.4e according to the updated WHO/FAO/WOAH H5 nomenclature.31 Correlates of protection to avian influenza infection are not fully established, but HI titers ≥40 are generally associated with a 50% reduction in the risk of infection.68

Virus neutralization (VN) assay

Heat-inactivated serum samples (1:10 in RDE) were further diluted 1:1 in PBS (starting dilution 1:20), followed by 2-fold serial dilutions. Equal volumes of diluted serum and virus suspension containing 100 TCID50 were mixed and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. The same virus strains used in the HI assay were tested in the VN assay. The serum-virus mixtures were then transferred to confluent MDCK (Madin-Darby Canine Kidney) cell monolayers in 96-well plates and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. After incubation, the inoculum was aspirated, and the cells were washed once with infection medium. Cultures were then maintained for 3 days at 37°C. Supernatants were subsequently tested for hemagglutinin agglutination (HA) activity to assess residual viral replication. A back-titration of the diluted virus inoculum was performed in parallel on MDCK cells to verify the addition of 100 TCID50 per well. Neutralizing antibody titers were defined as the reciprocal of the highest serum dilution that completely inhibited virus replication. While correlates of protection for avian influenza are not fully established, neutralization titers of ≥80 have been shown to correlate well with protection against avian influenza strains.68

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data analysis

Median fluorescence intensity (MFI) values for HA and NA antigens across all serum samples were visualised using boxplots overlaid with jitter data points, generated in R (version 4.4.1) using the ggplot2 package (version 4.0.2). Heatmaps were produced using either the heatmap.2 function in the gplots package (version 3.3.1) or the Heatmap function in the ComplexHeatmap package (version 2.26.0). For visualisation of seropositive MFI values using heatmaps, MFI was rank scaled using a custom function in R. Within each set of values, the original MFI measurements were first converted to ranks, assigning the lowest value the smallest rank and the highest value the largest rank. These ranks were then rescaled to a range between 0 and 1 by subtracting the minimum rank and dividing by the range between the maximum and minimum ranks. This transformation preserves the relative ordering of the values while standardizing them for comparison in heatmaps.

Given an array of values xof length k, denoted byx={x1,x2,…,xk},

the values were first replaced by their corresponding ranks. Next, the final rank-scaled values, denoted byxR={x1R,x2R,…,xkR},

were calculated using the formula:xiR=Rank(xi)−min⁡(Rank(x))max⁡(Rank(xi))−min⁡(Rank(x)).

This transformation involves ranking the original values and rescaling those ranks to fall within the (0, 1] range. Network plots were used to investigate patterns of seropositivity among HA and NA antigens and visualized using gephi (version 0.10.1) where each node represented one antigen. Node size was proportional to the total number of positives detected in a given antigen while edges were weighted based on the number of shared seropositives between antigens. Radar charts were created using the fmsb package (version 0.7.6) to visualise MFI values of HA antigens across different lineages from selected pig sera. Principal component analysis (PCoA) computed using Jaccard distance was used to examine differences between overall swine sero-profiles and conducted using the dudi.pco and s.class functions of the ade4 (version 1.7.23). To test for significance between these profiles we employed a Permutational Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA) test through the adonis function in the vegan package (version 2.7.2). Statistical differences between the number of seropositives belonging to each antigen within the H1 or H3 cluster were tested using a Wilcoxon test with p values corrected for false discovery rate using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Chord diagrams were generated using the chordDiagram function in the circlize package (version 0.4.17). Lastly, a Bayesian hierarchical logistic regression was developed for the binary positivity status for each HA protein following our previous method.40 Briefly, posterior adjusted odds ratio was estimated for farm type (smallholder or commercial pig farms) and pig type (sow or finisher) through the Hamilton Monte Carlo algorithm, running three independent chains for at least 4000 iterations with the first 1,000 being discarded as burn-in. Convergence was confirmed by a visual inspection as well as Rhat values and effective sample sizes. Variables representing origin farm province, batch size (as a continuous variable using generalized additive model), sex of pigs were included as potential confounders and batch ID was used to account for a clustering effect of pigs originated from the same farms.
