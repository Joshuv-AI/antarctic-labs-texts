Estimating the impact and cost-effectiveness of antenatal care approaches in 24 low- and middle-income countries: a modelling study
Authors: Nisaa Wulan, Debra ten Brink, Tharindu Wickramaarachchi, Sam Ali, Aris T Papageorghiou, Nick Scott
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntenatal care (ANC) is a critical intervention for improving maternal and newborn health outcomes, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). This study modelled the potential impact and cost-effectiveness of a theoretical community-based and risk-stratified model of ANC in LMICs.MethodsA decision-analytical model was developed to estimate health outcomes (maternal anemia, maternal deaths, newborn deaths, preterm births, stillbirths, low birth weight cases, and small for gestational age births) for 24 LMICs with demographic and health surveys conducted between 2018–2024. Three ANC scenarios were considered for the 2025–2034 period; status-quo (no changes in ANC-delivered intervention coverage); no ANC (a counterfactual where ANC-delivered intervention coverage was set equivalent to women reporting no ANC contacts); and a new ANC scenario (a community-based ANC delivery with risk stratification, assuming a 20% reduction in women with no ANC contacts, and equivalent quality/coverage of interventions for women with at least one ANC contact as those currently receiving four to seven contacts). Economic costs were estimated for each scenario from a healthcare provider’s perspective using an ingredient-based approach, and the cost per disability-adjusted life year (DALY) averted was calculated relative to the status-quo. Probabilistic sensitivity analyses were conducted to reflect parameter uncertainty.ResultsCompared to the no ANC scenario, the status-quo scenario was estimated to cost US$75.16 per DALY averted across the 24 sample countries. Compared to the status-quo, the new ANC scenario was estimated to avert 11.9 million (4.1%) of maternal anemia cases, 0.1 million (4.1%) of maternal deaths, 0.3 million (1.9%) newborn deaths, 0.4 million (0.7%) preterm births, <0.1 million (<0.1%) stillbirths, 7.7 million (6.3%) low birth weight cases, and 8.0 million (5.4%) small for gestational age births. This was estimated to cost US$133.76 per DALY averted compared to the status-quo across the 24 sample countries, varying between 4% and 52% of gross domestic product per capita.ConclusionsA community-based risk-stratified ANC delivery model could be cost-effective in LMICs if it can achieve greater ANC coverage and consistency of interventions within ANC contacts. This analysis supports continued investigation of innovative ANC delivery approaches to improve maternal and newborn health outcomes in LMICs.Keywords: Antenatal care, Maternal, Newborn, Preterm births, Stillbirths, Low birth weight, Small for gestational age, Cost-effectiveness, Modelling, Low- and middle-income countries

Background

Antenatal care (ANC) contacts provide a critical opportunity for preventive care and early detection of risks that impact maternal, fetal and newborn health. These contacts are key to identifying and addressing complications during pregnancy [1], with the potential to reduce maternal and perinatal mortality, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Studies indicate that receiving ANC contacts is positively correlated with increased rates of facility-based deliveries, which is crucial in reducing maternal mortality [2]. However, challenges remain in increasing the coverage and improving the quality and consistency of ANC provided in LMICs [3, 4].

The World Health Organization (WHO) updated its ANC guidelines in 2016 [1], advocating for at least eight contact moments during pregnancy to enhance maternal and fetal outcomes. These guidelines include essential interventions such as nutritional counselling, counselling on tobacco and alcohol use, iron and folic acid supplementation, screening for diabetes mellitus and HIV, conducting an ultrasound before 24 weeks gestation, treatment for asymptomatic bacteriuria, and administering tetanus toxoid vaccination, among others [1]. Additionally, the WHO emphasizes the importance of context-specific recommendations to address local health needs and resources, acknowledging that a one-size-fits-all model may not be appropriate across diverse settings [1]. Despite the guidelines being advocated by WHO, the 2023 WHO sexual, reproductive, maternal, newborn, child, and adolescent health policy survey suggested that only 54% of low-income countries and 57% of lower-middle income countries have national policies or guidelines recommending at least eight ANC contacts for a normal pregnancy [5].

Previous studies have considered the potential for alternate models of ANC in various settings, as a way of improving the coverage and quality of care. For example, using telehealth interventions, community health volunteers in India were able to improve the coverage of critical services, such as iron and folic acid supplementation, by 12.7% [6]. Similarly, group ANC, which brings women together in facilitated sessions, has shown promise as a cost-effective model to enhance both ANC attendance and the quality of care in resource-limited settings [7]. Group ANC is currently recommended by WHO, aiming to improve the utilization and quality of ANC in the context of rigorous research [1]. A large multicenter randomized trial comparing the standard model of ANC with a new model which was tailored to women’s obstetric and clinical history demonstrated comparable maternal and perinatal outcomes, and at reduced cost in some settings [8], providing early evidence for the value of risk-stratified ANC.

Economic modelling can be used to evaluate the potential impact of theoretical ANC models, to determine how likely it is that they would be cost-effective and to specify conditions under which they may be worth pursuing further. Previous research has identified specific maternal, newborn, and child health interventions to be cost-effective [9, 10]; however, the majority of existing economic evaluations focus on discrete clinical interventions delivered within ANC rather than on the broader delivery model itself. Despite a growing volume of economic modelling in maternal and child health, research specifically examining the cost-effectiveness of strategies to improve access to ANC in LMICs remains limited. In this study, we estimated the potential impact and cost-effectiveness of a theoretical community-based risk-stratified model of ANC in LMICs, and to benchmark this against the estimated impact and cost-effectiveness being achieved by current approaches to ANC.

Methods

Setting

A total of 24 LMICs that conducted the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) [11] between 2018 and 2023 (Table 1) were included in the model. The majority of countries (n = 20) considered in this analysis are in Sub-Saharan Africa.

Health outcomes

We assessed number of maternal anemia cases, maternal deaths, newborn deaths, preterm births, stillbirths, low birth weight cases, and small for gestational age births.

Baseline population and epidemiological data

The estimated number of pregnancies among women of reproductive age in each country was sourced from the 2024 World Population Prospects [12] adjusted for stillbirths and abortions. Epidemiological data for baseline health outcomes was extracted primarily from the WHO Global Health Observatory database (a repository of global health statistics and data managed by WHO) [13], UNICEF–WHO database [14], and the Lives Saved Tools (LiST) [15]. Detailed baseline health outcomes for each country are presented in Supplementary Material 1: Table S1.

ANC coverages and interventions

Baseline coverage of ANC contacts and intervention coverage by number of ANC contacts were populated from the cross-sectional DHS [11] based on the most recent survey data for each country (as of October 2024). The number of contacts per pregnancy was categorized as: ANC 0: no contacts; ANC 1: one contact; ANC 2-3: two to three contacts; ANC 4–7: four to seven contacts; and ANC 8 + : eight contacts or more. This analysis included all women of reproductive age (15–49 years) who reported a livebirth in the two years preceding the survey.

The DHS program uses standardized questionnaires on maternal and child health, which were used as input data for this study due to their comparability across countries [16]. Six ANC interventions were identified in DHS that were also in the WHO recommendations: iron and folic acid (IFA) supplementation; blood monitoring; blood testing; urine testing; tetanus vaccination; and malaria prevention through intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy (IPTp). Three additional nutritional interventions, balanced energy protein (BEP) supplementation, multiple micronutrient supplementation (MMS), and calcium supplementation, were identified that aligned with WHO recommendations for inclusion in ANC however their current coverage is estimated to be low [17, 18]. The baseline coverage of these additional nutritional interventions was extracted from LiST [15] as it is not available in DHS.

Model

A decision-analytical model was constructed in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft 365 Subscription, Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) [19] to estimate the impact and cost-effectiveness of ANC approaches. The baseline coverage of ANC contacts and intervention coverage by number of ANC contacts for each country were from the DHS data, as described in the previous section. The impact of ANC interventions was approximated by taking the WHO recommendations for routine care [1], and for each recommended intervention conducting a review of available literature to determine if quantitative effect size estimates were available to inform modelling. Specifically, an intervention required a quantified impact on one or more of the health outcomes considered (Supplementary Material 1: Table S2). Where multiple effect size estimates were available for some interventions, priority was given to meta-analyses with pooled data. Where studies reported the effect size estimate as an odds ratio (OR), these were converted to country-specific relative risks (RR) using baseline epidemiological indicators (p the probability of y health outcome without x intervention). For instance, in the case an effect size for x intervention on y health outcome was reported as an OR, the converted RR would be RR = OR / (1 – p + (p * OR)).

Model parameters

Three of the six identified ANC activities in DHS were considered to be proxies for other interventions: blood pressure monitoring for access to hypertensive disorder case management; blood testing for syphilis detection and treatment; and urine testing for antibiotics for asymptomatic bacteriuria

Each ANC contact category (ANC0, ANC1, ANC2-3, ANC4-7, ANC8 + ) was linked to country-specific intervention coverages across the domains of the WHO recommendations for routine ANC (Fig. 1). The intervention effects were adjusted for specific affected fractions (parameters are available in Supplementary Material 1: Table S3), for example the impact of the tetanus vaccine was applied only to newborn deaths due to tetanus. As WHO recommends many ANC interventions or activities not measured by DHS, the interventions modelled represent a limited subset of the total impact (Fig. 1, not modelled box).

Additionally, ANC attendance is strongly correlated with health facility delivery which has well-established evidence for reducing maternal and newborn mortality, and increased the probability of delivering in a health facility by 14% [20]. Thus, health facility delivery was considered as an effect of ANC in mortality outcomes.

New ANC scenario

In this study we have modelled a theoretical ANC scenario, based on what it achieves in terms of coverage and contact frequency, rather than what its implementation definition is. We refer to the new ANC scenario as “community-based” and “risk-stratified”, anticipating that it would integrate two conceptual components: (i) a delivery platform intended to increase ANC coverage for pregnant women experiencing structural and accessibility barriers leading to reduce inequities in access to care; and (ii) a clinical framework through which pregnant women were assigned to different ANC pathways according to pregnancy risk (Fig. 2).

Risk stratification

In the model, it is assumed that initial engagement is able to provide an assessment and assign women according to risk of adverse maternal or perinatal outcomes (low-risk, moderate-risk or high-risk pregnancy pathways). This would be through standardized individualized screening prediction models based on maternal factors and history at community level or primary care settings [1]. This early risk stratification would enable lower-risk pregnancies to receive fewer in-person ANC contacts (including the potential use of video contacts for follow-up after the initial assessment), whereas higher-risk pregnancies may require more in-person ANC contacts.

Community delivery

Increased coverage of ANC is assumed to be possible through the use of different delivery models. For example, community-based or group ANC models that have been tested in various settings and have demonstrated the ability to deliver standard clinical assessment and essential ANC interventions, including monitoring blood pressure and weight or identifying any warning signs) [7, 21].

The purpose of the theoretical ANC model is to allow us to estimate its potential to be cost-effective, and hence whether further investigation is warranted, as opposed to concluding that a particular design should be recommended. Specific implementation strategies would differ substantially depending on health system context, workforce capacity, technology availability, and other considerations.

ANC scenarios

Three scenarios were considered for each of the 24 modelled countries (Table 2): (i) no ANC scenario: intervention coverage in each country reduced from current levels to be equivalent to intervention coverage among women reporting zero ANC contact; (ii) status-quo: current coverages of ANC interventions maintained; and (iii) new ANC scenario: a relative reduction of up to 20% in the proportion of women receiving zero ANC contacts (due to community delivery leading to increased coverage), and a risk assessment with high sensitivity used to assign women to different ANC pathways (Fig. 2). The detailed assumptions of scenarios are given in Table 2.

Scenarios and assumptions included in the analysis

The analysis is framed based on the assumption on a reduction of up to 20% of women with zero ANC visit due to community delivery leading to increased coverage of women with at least 1 ANC contact to achieve equivalent coverage of interventions as women currently receiving ANC 4-7 contacts over 2025-2034 inclusive.

The selection of a reduction point was considered on the basis of the findings from the Global Network for Women’s and Children’s Health Research (Global Network), a multi-country research network in LMICs [22]. All countries, except for Pakistan, included in the analysis of Global Network reported about 5% of women not receiving any ANC visits [23].

In this analysis, on average, the proportion of pregnant women not attending any ANC visits across countries was 7.2%. An assumption of up to 20% reduction would result the proportion of pregnant women not receiving any ANC visits reduce to 5.8%. We considered a 20% reduction to be feasible because it would be approximately equivalent to bringing national estimates in line with what is observed in studies conducted across communities in low-resource settings in semi-urban and rural areas in LMICs [23].

WHO recommends group ANC as an alternative to traditional one-on-one model of ANC for pregnant women in the context of rigorous research [1]. Systematic reviews and evidence syntheses analyzing existing literature on group ANC models in LMICs suggested studies reporting a positive association between group ANC and more ANC attendance (e.g. four, six, and eight contacts), facility-based delivery, and improved provision of quality care [7, 24, 25].

Costs

The economic costs of interventions were estimated from a healthcare provider’s perspective, using an ingredient-based approach considering the costs of consumables, human resources, logistics, and overheads. The cost per ANC visit and specific intervention unit costs in the new model were assumed to be the same as conventional ANC, since while using a risk-stratified approach may enable the use of cheaper technologies (e.g. video or phone contacts as a potential substitution of some in-person contacts), this may be offset by some outreach costs which are unknown. For women at high risk the types of contacts are likely to be similar to current ANC. Intervention unit costs were informed by LiST, a widely used model for estimating the impact and costs of maternal and child health interventions in LMICs [26]. All costs are presented in 2024 US$. Detailed unit costs are in Supplementary Material 1: Table S4. Costs were not validated by countries.

Healthcare worker costs were calculated by estimating the number of minutes spent for the ANC contact and additional minutes for each intervention (Supplementary Material 1: Table S5). Healthcare worker time for most of the ANC interventions was assumed to be a part of the ANC visit time, except for management of hypertensive disorders and syphilis detection and treatment that were assumed to require additional healthcare worker time.

Commodity costs and country-specific healthcare worker salaries were extracted from LiST, with commodity costs increased by 21% to account for supply chain and wastage costs [15]. LiST default program and infrastructure investment costs of 15% and 26% were added and applied to all interventions.

Cost-effectiveness

For each scenario, health outcomes were converted to disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) as follows. Years of life lost were calculated for maternal deaths based on the average age of pregnancy and the expected years of life remaining, and for newborn deaths and stillbirths based on life expectancy (the maximum life expectancy from countries in the sample was applied to all countries to avoid weighting deaths differently). Years lived with disability were calculated for anemia based on the proportion of mild anemia (54%), moderate anemia (42%) or severe anemia (3%) from the WHO Global Health Observatory repository of global health statistics and data [13], combined with corresponding Global Burden of Disease disability weights [27]. A total disutility of 0.4067 for preterm, low birth weight and small for gestational age births was used based on the causes of preterm birth complication on the hierarchical disease cause list from the GBD study [27].

We considered cost-effectiveness analyses for the status-quo versus no ANC scenario and new ANC scenario versus status-quo, and calculated incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICER) as a proportion of gross domestic product (GDP) per capita of the corresponding country. In addition to GDP-based thresholds, we also considered cost-effectiveness thresholds reflecting country-specific opportunity costs to better capture the marginal productivity of LMIC health systems.

Time horizon and discounting

Health outcomes and intervention costs were projected for the 10-year period 2025–2034 inclusive. A lifetime time horizon was used for DALY calculations, but only for health outcomes occurring in this 10-year window.

A discount rate of 3% per annum was applied to future costs and DALYs. The consideration of discount rate was to reflect time preference to ensure comparability across interventions with different time horizon, in line with standard health economic guidelines [28].

Uncertainty analysis

A probabilistic multivariate uncertainty analysis was conducted with parameters and unit costs sampled from their uncertainty bounds. The central 95-percentiles of 100 simulations were used to generate confidence intervals for health outcomes, costs, and cost-effectiveness.

Sensitivity analysis

Univariate sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the influence of key parameters on cost-effectiveness outcomes. This included the healthcare worker time for facility-based ANC appointments; the difference in time and cost between community-based and facility-based ANC appointments; the impact of the new ANC scenario on the proportion of women with no ANC appointments or the coverage of interventions among women with more than one ANC appointment; and discounting rates.

In addition, in the main analysis it is assumed that the new ANC scenario results in women having equivalent intervention coverage to those currently receiving four to seven ANC contacts. This means that there is an improvement for women with ANC less than four contacts and a slightly reduction for women receiving ANC eight or more contacts (assuming that they would also be eligible for the new model, and some may choose to adopt it). An extra scenario was conducted where the new model led to no changes for women receiving ANC eight or more contacts.

Results

Baseline intervention coverage

The proportion of women reporting receiving at least eight ANC contacts ranged from a minimum of 0.3% in Rwanda to a maximum of 38.7% in Ghana (Supplementary Material 1: Table S6). Ethiopia and Philippines had the highest proportion of women reporting not receiving any ANC contacts at 26.2% and 23.7%, respectively. Overall, health facility delivery increased with the number of ANC contacts pregnant women received, with 90.9% of women receiving eight or more ANC contacts delivering in a health facility compared with 38.3% of women receiving no ANC contact. Similar patterns were found for other interventions, with coverage consistently higher among women receiving more ANC contacts (Supplementary Material 1: Table S6).

Estimated impact

In all scenarios, the nutrition-specific interventions (BEP, Calcium, and MMS) were excluded from analysis because their coverage is zero across all countries. In the status-quo scenario, assuming current coverages of interventions continued from 2025–2034, an estimated 292 million maternal anemia cases, 2 million maternal deaths, 14 million newborn deaths, 68 million preterm births, 10 million stillbirths, 123 million LBW cases, and 147 million SGA cases could occur from 2025 to 2034 in the 24 countries modelled (Fig. 3a).

Outcomes for 24-country sample in the no ANC scenario, status-quo, and new ANC scenario, cumulatively over 2025-2034 inclusive (a); difference in cumulative outcomes between no ANC scenario vs status-quo over 2025-2034 inclusive (b). ANC antenatal care

The no ANC scenario assumed all interventions were discontinued except for IFA supplementation, malaria prevention (IPTp), and tetanus toxoid vaccination since some coverage of these interventions occurred through other delivery points (e.g., community outreach programs) by pregnant women who reported no ANC contacts. Compared to the status-quo scenario, the no ANC scenario had an additional 57.4 million (19.7%) maternal anemia cases, 0.7 million (35.4%) maternal deaths, 1.7 million (11.8%) newborn deaths, 5.3 million (7.7%) preterm births, and 0.1 million (0.6%) stillbirths, 39.9 million (32.5%) LBW cases, and 39.8 million (27%) SGA cases over the 2025 and 2034 period (Fig. 3a).

The new ANC scenario assumed the proportion of pregnant women not receiving any ANC contacts was reduced by 20% from an average across countries of 7.2% to 5.8%, and the proportion of women receiving four to seven ANC contacts increased from an average across countries of 53.3% to 96.0% due to the community-based delivery of ANC interventions. The model estimated the new ANC scenario could avert 11.9 million (4.1%) maternal anemia cases, 0.1 million (4.1%) maternal deaths, 0.3 million (1.9%) newborn deaths, 0.4 million (0.7%) preterm births, 7.7 million (6.3%) LBW cases, and 8.0 million (5.4%) SGA cases over the 2025–2034 period compared to the status-quo scenario (Fig. 3b). The impact on stillbirths was estimated to be minimal, with a reduction of around 5000 ( < 0.1%) cases over the 2025–2034 period compared to the status-quo scenario.

Cumulative DALYs in each scenario are presented in Supplementary Material 1: Table S7.

Estimated total costs

It is estimated there would be 615 million pregnant women across the 24 countries modelled between 2025 and 2034. The average annual intervention costs of the no ANC scenario, status-quo, and new ANC scenario were US$59.9 million, US$855.2 million, and US$1.1 billion (undiscounted), respectively (Fig. 4). This is equivalent to the average annual cost per pregnant woman of US$0.97 in the no ANC scenario, US$13.90 in the status-quo, and US$17.62 in the new ANC scenario.

Estimated cost-effectiveness

The discounted cost per DALY averted in the status-quo versus no ANC scenario was US$75.16 aggregated across all modelled countries. Assuming an increased coverage of the ANC4-7 category and a reduced coverage of the ANC0 category through community-based delivery, the new ANC scenario could cost about US$133.76 per DALY averted compared to status-quo across the 24 countries.

Country-specific costs per DALY averted varied significantly between countries influenced by baseline epidemiological indicators, ANC coverage and economic indicators (Table 3). Compared to the status-quo, the new ANC scenario could cost between US$21.99 to US$1149.67 per DALY averted across the 24 modelled countries, which corresponds to between 4% of GDP per capita in Ethiopia, Gabon, Nigeria, and Zambia to 52% of GDP per capita in Ghana.

Sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis was performed through adjusting alternate assumptions for combinations of the parameters described in the previous section, with the aggregate cost per DALY across the 24 countries ranging between US$53.19 and US$353.26 (Supplementary Material 1: Table S8). Achieving intervention coverage for all women equivalent to women currently receiving at least eight ANC contacts, applying no discount rates, and reducing the proportion of women with no ANC contacts from 20% to 100% in the new ANC scenario influenced on the cost per DALY averted the most.

When the new ANC scenario was assumed to lead to no changes among women with ANC eight or more contacts, the total intervention cost for the new model was slightly higher than the main analysis (US$11.2 billion vs US$10.8 billion); however, the new ANC scenario was more impactful in averting health cases and more cost-effective (cost per DALY averted is US$115.62 vs US$133.76 in the main analysis) (Supplementary Material 1: Figs. S1 and S2).

Discussion

This analysis modelled the potential health outcomes of a theoretical community-based risk-stratified model of ANC, estimating that across 24 LMICs it could avert 11.9 million (4.1%) of maternal anemia cases, 0.1 million (4.1%) of maternal deaths, 0.3 million (1.9%) newborn deaths, 0.4 million (0.7%) preterm births, 7.7 million (6.3%) low birth weight cases, and 8.0 million (5.4%) small for gestational age births compared to the status-quo. The relative reduction in stillbirths was smaller at 5000 ( < 0.1%) due to limited quantitative effect size estimates of ANC interventions on stillbirths, and interventions influencing stillbirths were primarily nutritional interventions (e.g., BEP and MMS) with minimal coverage in all countries modelled. The analysis only modelled a subset of WHO-recommended interventions where coverage data of ANC interventions were available in DHS, thus, the estimates are likely conservative given limited data on key ANC interventions such as ultrasound or psychosocial care.

The status-quo was estimated to cost an additional US$12.93 per pregnant woman compared to the no ANC scenario, and achieve an aggregate cost-effectiveness of US$75.16 per DALY averted across the 24 countries. The new ANC scenario was estimated to cost an additional US$3.72 per pregnant woman and achieve an aggregate cost-effectiveness of US$133.76 per DALY averted compared to continuing the status-quo. While the cost per DALY averted was higher in the new ANC scenario compared to the status-quo, the new ANC scenario was estimated to achieve greater impact. On the one hand, this suggests that it may be more cost-effective to continue expanding the current model of ANC; however, in practice, doing this has faced significant challenges related to access, equity, and quality. Therefore, different approaches such as the one considered in this analysis are likely to be necessary to reach more pregnant women. Studies have also documented barriers in reaching high levels of facility-based ANC coverage including transportation costs, distance and prolonged waiting time [29–31].

While this analysis does not stratify by equity markers, DHS data consistently show that women from lower wealth quintiles and rural areas had markedly lower ANC contact and quality [32–34], underlining the potential equity gains of community-based ANC delivery. Equity is a critical consideration in ANC redesign; the proposed model targeted women without any ANC contacts and could have the potential to reduce disparities in coverage and health outcomes. Achieving this will require a proactive approach targeting rural and socioeconomically disadvantaged populations and community mobilization strategies to address barriers to engagement with ANC.

The new ANC scenario was assumed to be delivered through community-based and risk-stratified approach to reduce the proportion of women with zero ANC contacts by 20%, resulting in a higher overall coverage of ANC due to improved accessibility. Group ANC models of care are delivered at the community level and have been adapted and implemented in numerous LMIC settings, integrating provider health assessments with education and community support [7, 24, 35]. Various models of group ANC in LMICs have been associated with improvements in ANC attendance—at least four ANC contacts [25, 36], at least six ANC contacts [25], and at least eight ANC contacts [37]. ANC attendance was not the only outcome of interest assessed by these studies but also other outcomes such as increased facility-based delivery, and improved health literacy and quality of care [25, 38, 39]. Risk-stratified approaches have been primarily applied in high income countries, and for this analysis it was assumed the assessment of medical risk factors with high sensitivity could assign women to different ANC pathways in this new ANC scenario. Operationalizing this model at scale will require working with clinical partners to define the details, and practical approaches to risk stratification, such as simplified clinical algorithms or digital decision support tools [40]. Implementation research will be essential to assess the feasibility, safety, and acceptability of such approaches in diverse LMIC contexts.

There were substantial variations of cost per DALY averted for the new ANC scenario compared to the current model across countries, ranging from US$21.99 in Sierra Leone to US$1149.67 in Ghana. However, this range of the costs per DALY averted is less than per capita GDP in all modelled countries. Factors influencing variations in estimated ICERs included the characteristics of the baseline ANC interventions (e.g. the proportion of facility births among those receiving no ANC contacts), baseline health outcomes and the prevalence of conditions, and economic variables (e.g., country’s high GDP per capita).

In resource limited settings, investing in one intervention means foregoing the opportunity to spend those funds on another, resulting in the so-called opportunity costs. Cost-effectiveness thresholds that consider the opportunity cost, as described by Woods et al. [41], through calculating a health system’s marginal productivity could provide a more accurate threshold for determining the cost-effectiveness of an intervention. A range 1%–51% of GDP per capita was recommended for LMICs on the basis of opportunity costs reflecting the marginal productivity of their health care systems [41]. The costs per DALY averted for the new ANC scenario were estimated to fall within this range for each of the 24 modelled countries, excluding Ghana (52% of GDP per capita due largely to minimal DALYs averted between the new ANC scenario and status-quo).

Many details about a community-based and risk-stratified model have not been specified in this study, because the aim was to explore whether such a model had potential to be impactful and cost-effective, and hence whether it would be worth pursuing further. The added value of this study is to show that a theoretical ANC model that can increase coverage among women receiving sub-optimal ANC has potential to be cost-effective. There is now a need for further research into more detailed design, particularly around how a community-based delivery and separately a risk stratification can be implemented in practice to reach more women, and how this would need to be adapted for different settings.

When offering a new ANC model, for equity purposes it would likely be available for all women, and hence some women who currently have eight or more ANC contacts (and for whom the system is currently working well) may choose to take it up. Therefore, while the new model is likely to greatly benefit women who currently have sub-optimal ANC, for the subgroup of women who currently have at least eight ANC contacts and choose to take up the new model, there is potential for worse outcomes given associations between ANC contacts and health outcomes [42]. In our analysis, this effect of “downgrading care” is captured, and there are still estimated to be net benefits. We conducted a sensitivity analysis where only women who currently have less than 4 ANC contacts took up the new model. The overall intervention cost was slightly higher than the main analysis (US$11.2 billion vs US$10.8 billion) though found to be more cost-effective compared to the status-quo (cost per DALY averted is US$115.62 vs US$133.76). Therefore, the new model may be more cost-effective if it could be directed to those most in need of additional ways to receive care.

Studies have generated cost-effectiveness results for other community-based interventions focusing on countries with poor maternal and newborn health outcomes [43, 44]. Despite these encouraging cost-effectiveness studies offering potential improvements in maternal and newborn health outcomes, there are implementation challenges to consider including the geographical distribution of health workforces and infrastructures especially in rural communities and hard-to-reach areas. Leadership of local government and mobilizing resources have also been considered as a key component in delivering community health interventions [45, 46].

Our model was limited by a number of considerations or assumptions. First, this modelling analysis did not consider fetal growth monitoring and routine ultrasounds as recommended by WHO [1] due to limited quantifiable direct effects on health outcomes and data coverage conducted by DHS. This may result in an underestimation of the impact of ANC; however, this would be applicable across all scenarios. Second, there were key assumptions related to the new ANC scenario. For example, it assumes there were accurate risk predictions for low, medium, and high-risk pregnancies. It also optimistically assumes there was an increase in coverage of interventions and a reduction in the proportion of pregnant women without ANC contacts of up to 20%, due to community-based delivery of ANC. Third, the no ANC scenario models the coverage of interventions for all women to be equal to the coverage among women not currently attending ANC, but there may be categorical differences between women attending versus not attending ANC. For example, some women currently attending ANC may source interventions from elsewhere if ANC were not available, and so this counterfactual scenario might be pessimistic, which would overestimate the cost-effectiveness of current ANC. Conversely, women currently accessing ANC may be more likely to have additional risk factors or comorbidities than women not accessing ANC, which would make current ANC more impactful that we have modelled. Fourth, this analysis did not consider the measurement of quality of care of modelled interventions. There is much variability in the number of ANC contacts across countries, this may indicate the lack of accessibility of public sector ANC leading to underutilization of ANC services. Fifth, DALY weights on stillbirths are uncertain. Sensitivity analyses ranged from treating a stillbirth as an entire lifetime lost (70 years) to not considering these impacts; however, since the ANC interventions considered had minimal evidence of impact on stillbirths, this made minimal difference to our results. Sixth, in our model, DALY was assigned to each health outcome using standards disability weights without fully accounting a possibility of overlapping cases at the individual level. Low birth weight, preterm birth, and small for gestational age overlap conceptually, and assigning DALYs independently to each condition may lead to an additive effect on disease burden. Finally, DHS data are based on self-reports, hence subject to recall bias the reported measures of interventions received during ANC.

Conclusions

This modelling analysis estimated the potential impact of a theoretical ANC model aiming to improve access to ANC services for women experiencing structural and accessibility barriers, thereby reducing inequities in access to care. It illustrates that a community-based risk-stratified ANC delivery model can potentially be cost-effective if it could achieve greater ANC coverage and consistency of interventions within ANC contacts. The modelled ANC scenario had a higher estimated cost per DALY averted than existing models; however, the lack of expansion of current ANC models suggests that alternative approaches may be necessary. Our study also estimated the ICERs associated with the modelled ANC scenario are likely to fall within 4% (Ethiopia, Gabon, Nigeria, and Zambia) to 33% (Cambodia) of GDP per capita across countries (except for Ghana at 52% of GDP per capita), suggesting that they would likely be cost-effective. This analysis provides a rationale to inform policy discussions around innovative approaches for improving ANC service delivery to achieve better maternal and newborn health outcomes in LMICs. Finally, in settings where ANC coverage has plateaued the proposed model offers a scalable alternative to expand reach and accelerate progress toward universal ANC access.
