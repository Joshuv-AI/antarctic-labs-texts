Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma involving the vagina and a pre-existing uterine leiomyoma
Authors: Fumiko Yagi, Wataru Yamagami, Akihisa Ueno, Shunki Kiyokawa, Yuya Nogami, Masatoshi Sakurai, Kyoko Masuda, Masahiro Jinzaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lymphomas involving the female genital tract are uncommon, and vaginal involvement is particularly rare. In addition, the involvement of a pre-existing uterine leiomyoma by lymphoma appears unusual. Herein, we report the case of a woman in her sixties who presented with abnormal genital bleeding. Pelvic magnetic resonance imaging revealed a newly developed submucosal mass in the posterior vaginal wall and a previously known posterior uterine wall leiomyoma containing a newly emerged area of marked restricted diffusion. Both lesions showed relatively homogeneous internal signal characteristics and similar diffusion restriction, suggesting a shared pathological process. As the imaging findings indicated a gynecologic malignant neoplasm requiring surgical management, the patient underwent a simple total hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, and excision of the vaginal wall mass. Histopathological examination revealed diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, a nongerminal center B-cell subtype, in the vaginal lesion, with morphologically identical lymphoma cells infiltrating the uterine leiomyoma. Postoperative fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography showed no additional lesions or abnormal uptake elsewhere, and the patient was subsequently treated with polatuzumab vedotin, rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and prednisone. This case underscores the importance of considering lymphoma in the differential diagnosis when a vaginal mass shows a submucosal location, marked diffusion restriction, and relative internal homogeneity because early biopsy may help distinguish this entity from surgically managed gynecologic malignancies and thereby guide appropriate treatment.

Introduction

Lymphoma involving the female genital tract is rare, and vaginal involvement is particularly uncommon [[1], [2], [3]]. As the therapeutic strategy for lymphoma differs fundamentally from that for most gynecological malignant neoplasms, preoperative recognition is of considerable clinical importance. In contrast to epithelial malignancies or uterine sarcomas, which are often managed surgically, lymphoma is primarily treated with systemic therapy [2,4]. Failure to consider this entity in the differential diagnosis may result in unnecessary upfront surgery and delay appropriate treatment.

Previous radiological reports have suggested that genital tract lymphoma may show several characteristic imaging features, including submucosal or stromal location, preservation of the overlying mucosa, relative internal homogeneity, and markedly restricted diffusion on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [1,2,5]. However, these findings may be overlooked when the lesion arises at an unusual site or coexists with another pelvic mass suggestive of a more common gynecologic malignancy. Therefore, MRI may suggest the possibility of lymphoma but cannot replace histopathologic and immunohistochemical confirmation.

Here, we report a rare case of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) involving the vagina and a pre-existing uterine leiomyoma. This case is noteworthy not only because of its exceptional rarity but also because it illustrates an important diagnostic pitfall: when imaging findings raise even a limited possibility of lymphoma, particularly in the presence of a submucosal vaginal mass with marked diffusion restriction and relative homogeneity, early biopsy should be actively considered to avoid unnecessary radical gynecologic surgery and delay in the initiation of systemic chemotherapy.

Case report

A woman in her sixties was referred to our institution for further evaluation of abnormal genital bleeding and a suspected uterine neoplasm. She had a known history of uterine leiomyoma and had received hormone replacement therapy. Her gynecologic and reproductive history included menarche at 13 years of age, menopause at 53 years of age, and 3 pregnancies and 3 deliveries. She had no known history of immunosuppression, autoimmune disease, organ transplantation, or HIV infection. She had no significant medical history. Serum carcinoembryonic antigen, carbohydrate antigen 19-9, squamous cell carcinoma antigen, cancer antigen 125, and lactate dehydrogenase were all within normal limits. Lactate dehydrogenase was included as part of the routine initial laboratory assessment at our institution. Cervical cytology showed atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance, whereas endometrial cytology was negative for malignancy. Endometrial biopsy was attempted, but an adequate specimen could not be obtained. Because MRI showed no endometrial cavity lesion, dilation and curettage and hysteroscopic assessment were not performed.

Pelvic MRI revealed 2 distinct but radiologically similar lesions (Fig. 1). The first was a newly developed mass measuring approximately 3 cm in diameter, located on the posterior vaginal wall. The lesion protruded ventrally and elevated the overlying mucosa, indicating its submucosal location. The other was a 6-cm mass in the posterior uterine wall, corresponding to a previously known leiomyoma. Comparison with the MRI obtained 11 years earlier (Fig. 2) showed the pre-existing posterior intramural leiomyoma but no vaginal mass or intratumoral component with restricted diffusion within the leiomyoma. Therefore, the current vaginal mass and the restricted-diffusion component within the leiomyoma were interpreted as newly developed findings rather than residual or recurrent lesions. Both the vaginal and uterine lesions showed relatively homogeneous internal signal characteristics and conspicuous diffusion restriction, suggesting a shared pathological process. Although several imaging features, including a submucosal location, relative internal homogeneity, and marked restricted diffusion, were retrospectively suggestive of lymphoma, the unusual vaginal location and coexistence of a newly altered component within a known uterine leiomyoma complicated the preoperative diagnosis. Imaging findings were considered to indicate a gynecologic malignant neoplasm requiring surgical management, including a malignant vaginal tumor or uterine sarcoma. The possibility of tumor-to-tumor metastasis from a vaginal malignant neoplasm to a pre-existing uterine leiomyoma was also considered. Accordingly, a preoperative biopsy was not performed, and the patient underwent a simple total hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, and excision of the vaginal wall mass.

Histopathological examination of the vaginal lesion revealed diffuse proliferation of large atypical lymphoid cells with a high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, frequent mitotic figures, and atypical mitosis. Importantly, there was no continuity between the lesion and overlying squamous epithelium, supporting a subepithelial origin. Immunohistochemically, the tumor cells were negative for CK AE1/AE3, EMA, ER, S100, desmin, h-caldesmon, and α-SMA, arguing against carcinoma, leiomyosarcoma, melanoma, and rhabdomyosarcoma. Additional staining showed positivity for CD20, CD79α, BCL2, BCL6, and MUM1, with negativity for CD10 and Epstein-Barr virus-encoded RNA in situ hybridization. The Ki-67 labeling index was 80%-90%. Postoperative fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) revealed no definite additional lesions or abnormal FDG uptake, and bone marrow biopsy showed no evidence of lymphoma involvement. Based on these findings, the disease was classified as stage IE according to the Lugano modification of the Ann Arbor classification. The patient was subsequently referred to the hematology department and treated with 6 cycles of polatuzumab vedotin, rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and prednisone (Pola-R-CHP) chemotherapy at approximately 3-week intervals. After the second cycle, arrhythmia occurred, and the dose of doxorubicin was reduced by 50% from the third cycle onward. Although 2 additional doses of rituximab monotherapy were planned according to the standard Pola-R-CHP treatment schedule, they were not administered because the patient declined further treatment. FDG-PET performed after completion of the sixth cycle showed no definite residual or recurrent disease.

Discussion

The present case illustrates an important diagnostic and therapeutic pitfall: DLBCL involving the vagina and a pre-existing uterine leiomyoma mimicked a surgically managed gynecologic malignancy. This combination is unusual because vaginal involvement by lymphoma is rare, and lymphoma involving a uterine leiomyoma has been described only rarely [[1], [2], [3],6,7]. Recognition of this possibility is clinically important because lymphoma is primarily treated with systemic therapy rather than surgery [2,4].

Genital tract lymphoma is an uncommon extranodal manifestation of lymphoma, and the vagina is a particularly rare site of involvement [1,2,4]. Because lymphoma is generally managed with systemic therapy rather than primary surgery, preoperative diagnosis is of considerable clinical importance. By contrast, other malignant neoplasms involving the vagina and uterus, including primary vaginal malignancies with uterine involvement and uterine sarcomas, are often treated surgically. In the present case, the coexistence of a vaginal mass and an altered uterine mass led to the possibility of a surgically managed gynecologic malignancy. In addition, the possibility of tumor-to-tumor metastasis from a malignant vaginal neoplasm to a pre-existing uterine leiomyoma was considered preoperatively. Tumor-to-tumor metastasis is a rare phenomenon in which one true neoplasm metastasizes to another true neoplasm and should be distinguished from direct contiguous spread or collision tumors [8,9]. Although this concept was radiologically entertained in the present case, the final pathologic findings were more appropriately interpreted as DLBCL involving the vagina and a pre-existing uterine leiomyoma.

From a radiological standpoint, the vaginal lesion in the present case showed several features that, in retrospect, were suggestive of lymphoma. Previous reports have described vaginal lymphoma as an infiltrative or lobulated mass that may present as diffuse vaginal wall thickening or as a solid submucosal tumor, typically with preservation of the overlying mucosa. On MRI, the lesion is often relatively homogeneous and may show slightly to moderately high T2 signal intensity, uniform enhancement, and markedly restricted diffusion, reflecting high cellularity. Thus, a submucosal location, preserved mucosa, relative internal homogeneity, and conspicuous diffusion restriction may provide important clues for diagnosis [1,2]. In the present case, the combination of these findings in the vaginal lesion was concordant with the previously described imaging characteristics of genital tract lymphoma. Moreover, the newly developed intratumoral component within the known uterine leiomyoma showed MRI features similar to those of the vaginal lesion, suggesting that the two lesions represent the same pathological process, without evidence of a synchronous non-lymphomatous gynecologic malignancy. Nevertheless, these findings were not considered sufficiently specific to establish the diagnosis of lymphoma preoperatively, particularly because the lesion arose in an unusual vaginal location and coexisted with a newly altered component within a known uterine leiomyoma. Radiomics and AI-assisted MRI interpretation may eventually support differential diagnosis in challenging gynecologic tumors; however, evidence specific to genital tract lymphoma remains limited, and histopathologic and immunohistochemical confirmation remains indispensable [10].

Another important point is the extreme rarity of lymphoma involving an uterine leiomyoma and the need for careful pathological distinction from benign or reactive lymphoid infiltrates. Very few reports have described DLBCL arising in or involving a leiomyoma [6,7]. Uterine leiomyomas with massive lymphoid infiltration, although rare, are recognized as benign or reactive phenomena that may histologically simulate lymphoma [11,12]. However, in the present case, the infiltrating cells within the leiomyoma were cytologically malignant, had a high proliferative index, and were morphologically similar to those in the vaginal lesion. CD20 positivity in both lesions further supported the idea that they represented the same B-cell lymphomatous process. Although a previous case documented histopathologic intravascular tumor emboli, no definite evidence of intravascular tumor emboli was identified in this case [6]. These findings support a true lymphomatous infiltration rather than reactive inflammatory cell infiltration or degenerative changes. This distinction is clinically critical because reactive lymphoid infiltration does not require systemic lymphoma-directed therapy, whereas true lymphomatous involvement does.

Treatment of limited-stage DLBCL involving rare extranodal sites should be individualized. Although abbreviated chemoimmunotherapy followed by radiotherapy is an accepted option for selected patients with limited-stage DLBCL, evidence supporting consolidative radiotherapy for extranodal DLBCL of the female genital tract is limited [13,14]. In the present case, radiotherapy was not administered after second-opinion and multidisciplinary assessment because no residual localized disease was evident after surgery and no PET-positive disease was detected after systemic therapy. Pola-R-CHP was selected as systemic therapy because it has emerged as a first-line treatment option for previously untreated DLBCL, supported by the phase 3 POLARIX trial showing improved progression-free survival [15].

Therefore, the principal teaching point in this case was practical. Recent literature has emphasized that primary lymphoma of the female genital tract may masquerade as gynecologic malignancy and that histopathologic confirmation is essential before selecting treatment [[16], [17], [18]]. Lymphoma should be considered when a vaginal lesion shows a submucosal location, preserved mucosa, marked restricted diffusion, and relative internal homogeneity, especially when a known leiomyoma develops a new component with similar MRI characteristics. Under such circumstances, image-guided or transvaginal biopsy should be considered before surgery. If lymphoma is confirmed histopathologically, systemic therapy may be initiated without major gynecologic surgery, depending on the disease stage, symptoms, and multidisciplinary assessment.

Conclusion

Here, we report a rare case of DLBCL involving the vagina and a pre-existing uterine leiomyoma. This case is noteworthy not only because of its exceptional rarity but also because it highlights an important radiologic pitfall: vaginal lymphoma may mimic surgically managed gynecologic malignancies, particularly when a uterine mass is also present. In the present case, the key imaging findings were a submucosal vaginal mass with preserved mucosa, relative internal homogeneity, and markedly restricted diffusion, together with a newly developed intratumoral component within a known uterine leiomyoma that showed similar MRI characteristics. When such findings raise the possibility of lymphoma, a biopsy should be considered to establish an early diagnosis and guide appropriate treatment.
