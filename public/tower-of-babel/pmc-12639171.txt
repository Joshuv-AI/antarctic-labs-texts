Electrochemical sensor based on Co3O4 and Au nanoparticles for simultaneous determination of As3+ and Hg2+ by stripping voltammetry
Authors: Marzieh Nodehi, Mehdi Baghayeri, Hanyeh arbabi, Sahra Khosrojerdi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nowadays, the monitoring of heavy metals in environmental samples, especially freshwater sources, is of global concern. Mercury (Hg) and arsenic (As) are known as heavy metals with high toxicity. They present high risks to both human health and the environment even at trace concentrations. Therefore, the development of sensitive, selective and fast monitoring methods for the measurement of metal ions is significant due to the increasing use of industries and the serious environmental effects of these heavy metals. In this work, a glassy carbon electrode (GCE) modified with cobalt oxide nanoparticles (Co3O4) and gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) characterized using scanning electron microscope (SEM) and anodic stripping voltammetry (ASV) and applied as a catalytic surface for the simultaneous detection of Hg2+ and As3+ ions in environmental water samples. Important parameters such as electrolyte type, electrolyte concentration, accumulation potential and accumulation time were systematically optimized. The catalytic surface as an efficient sensor exhibited an excellent linearity with wide dynamic range from 10 to 900 ppb for As3+ and 10 to 650 ppb for Hg2+. Recovery between 96% and 116% were obtained after the analysis of real samples: river and drinking water confirming the sensor’s high accuracy and reliability.

Introduction

Rapid population growth, frequent natural disasters, and unpredictable climate changes have led to a scarcity of clean drinking water in numerous developing nations. Furthermore, contamination by heavy metal ions including lead (Pb²⁺), mercury (Hg²⁺), cadmium (Cd²⁺), arsenic (As³⁺), and thallium (Tl⁺) poses significant challenges to ensuring safe water supplies worldwide. Therefore, the development of sensitive, selective, portable and cost-effective approaches for the determination of heavy metals is of great importance1. Heavy metals naturally occur in the earth’s crust, but widespread human activities have altered their geochemical cycles and disturbed their biochemical equilibrium2. Globally, heavy metal pollutants are among the main causes of surface water quality degradation. These metals have become major environmental pollutants due to their non-biodegradability, high toxicity and tendency to bioaccumulate in living organisms from various sources. It is worth noting that some heavy metals can have toxic effects even at very low concentrations3,4. Water and soil contamination with heavy metals has attracted widespread attention due to their high persistence, accumulation in food sources, and harmful effects on human health. With increasing urbanization, industrialization, and the use of pesticides to increase the quality and yield of agricultural products, heavy metals are increasingly released into the soil. These toxic substances are transported to groundwater through irrigation or heavy rainfall, contributing to water pollution in upstream consumption areas. Heavy metals are also known to cause damage to animal and human organs, and genetic abnormalities in aquatic species have been confirmed due to their presence in surface waters5. To assess the harmful effects of heavy metal ions, major international agencies including the World Health Organization (WHO), the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) have undertaken extensive investigations. Among the different heavy metals, arsenic and mercury are recognized as leading contributors to metal-induced health disorders. The functional toxicological process of these ions involves the inhibition of essential cellular enzymes and the disruption of antioxidant metabolic pathways. This process results in the generation of free radicals, which induce oxidative stress, damage DNA, and promote lipid peroxidation, thereby negatively impacting human health. Such complications are associated with a wide range of diseases, including cancer, cardiovascular disorders, hematological and skeletal diseases, and neurological symptoms such as tremor6.

Hg 2+ and its different oxidation state are important components of air and water pollution. The main sources of Hg pollution include the discharge of mining and industrial wastes into water systems, which causes the accumulation of Hg containing pollutants in sediments at the bottom of lakes and rivers. The use of these contaminated waters by aquatic organisms leads to bioaccumulation and transfer of Hg to the human body7. Hg is a metal commonly used in thermometers and barometers; however, its main industrial application is in the electrochemical production of chlorine, where it acts as an electrode material. Among occupational groups, dental professionals are particularly at risk of exposure to Hg. Hg concentrations in the air of some dental treatment units were reported to be as high as 20 µg/m38. Hg and its compounds have a significant impact on environmental quality and human health. Even small amounts of Hg can lead to acute toxicity or chronic disease, with harmful effects on the brain, kidneys, and lungs. The WHO has established a maximum allowable concentration of Hg2+ in drinking water at 1 ppb. Therefore, accurate quantitative and qualitative detection of Hg2+ is crucial for environmental surveillance, ensuring food safety, and clinical diagnostics9. Hg ranks third among toxic metals in the ATSDR 2022 substance priority list, so measuring it is important.

Arsenic is a metalloid element whose name originates from the Greek term arsenikon, meaning strong or powerful. It naturally exists in the Earth’s crust and can enter groundwater and surface water through leaching from arsenic-rich geological deposits. Consumption of arsenic-contaminated groundwater is a significant global health concern. Various regions around the world including Bangladesh, India, Taiwan, the United Kingdom, Thailand, and several states in the United States are at risk of exposure. States such as Hawaii, California, and New Hampshire have reported arsenic contamination in environmental water supplies10. Most arsenic species found in the environment exist in inorganic forms, such as As5+, As3+, elemental arsenic, and arsine (AsH₃). It is important to note that different forms of arsenic exhibit varying levels of toxic properties. Inorganic arsenic compounds are generally considered more toxic than organic compounds11. Furthermore, As3+ is more common than As5+ and is significantly more toxic and soluble in aqueous environments compared to As5+12–15. Exposure to arsenic in its trivalent form (As³⁺) has been linked to numerous harmful health impacts, such as skin lesions, as well as respiratory, cardiovascular, gastrointestinal disorders, and it exhibits genotoxic, mutagenic, and carcinogenic properties16. In traditional Chinese medicine, arsenic trioxide has been used in very small amounts to treat acute promyelocytic leukemia17. WHO, the total concentration of arsenic in drinking water must remain below 10 ppb18. Arsenic can enter the human body through the consumption of contaminated water and food. Rice, a major agricultural crop and staple food in many parts of the world especially in Asia has been reported to contain relatively higher levels of arsenic than other foods. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), the maximum permissible concentration of arsenic in rice is 1 mgkg−119.

Several techniques are available for detecting heavy metal ions, including atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS), inductively coupled plasma spectroscopy (ICP), and infrared spectroscopy. However, these traditional methods are expensive, require complex instruments operated by skilled personnel, and do not allow for rapid measurements20–22. In addition, qualitative and quantitative sample testing must be performed in a laboratory environment, and the effects of sample texture on the results are significant. Preparing samples before analysis and performing analyte preconcentration are often necessary steps that can be time-intensive23. Conversely, electrochemical methods have gained recognition as effective alternatives to spectroscopic approaches for detecting heavy metal ions, owing to their high sensitivity, rapid analysis, portability, and cost-effectiveness24. Among the various electrochemical techniques, stripping voltammetry stands out as the only method capable of detecting analyte concentrations in the picomolar range, often reaching levels as low as 10 pM or below25–28. This technique involves two main steps: initially, the target analyte is accumulated onto the electrode surface, followed by an electrochemical process that re-oxidizes or re-reduces the species, returning it to the solution for quantification. The effectiveness of stripping voltammetry lies in this two-step mechanism preconcentration and stripping. One of its important advantages is the ability to simultaneously detect multiple elements in a sample, provided that their peak potentials are sufficiently distinct. To increase the sensitivity in the separation step, advanced voltammetric techniques such as differential pulse voltammetry (DPV) and square wave voltammetry (SWV) are used. These methods significantly improve the detection limits and allow measurements down to femtomolar levels. In 2019, Wu et al. created an electrochemical sensor by modifying a GCE with magnetic iron nanoparticles and fluorine-functionalized carbon nanotubes, enabling the simultaneous detection of heavy metal ions using stripping voltammetry25. Manikandan et al. used a paraffin-impregnated graphite electrode for the electropolymerization of xylene in the potential range of −0.8 to −0.4 V, leading to the fabrication of a poly(xylene)-based electrode that was subsequently used for the determination of Hg2+ ions using stripping voltammetry26. Yiwei et al. developed an oligonucleotide-functionalized nanoporous gold electrode for sensitive detection of Hg2+ ions in well water samples using differential pulse voltammetry27. The development of new and effective techniques for the detection of contaminants in environmental samples especially heavy metals has recently attracted much attention. Among the emerging processes, the application of sensors has shown promise. Currently, biocompatible nanomaterials based on noble metals, polymers, and carbon are widely used in sensor design.

In recent years, the use of nanocomposites containing AuNPs as modified electrodes in electrochemical sensors for arsenic detection by stripping voltammetry has received great attention due to the high sensitivity and selectivity of these NPs. The AuNPs surface is a suitable site for the oxidation of arsenic due to its outstanding electrochemical properties. As Alicia D. Robles and et al. have investigated the mechanism of arsenic oxidation on the electrode surface by stripping voltammetry28. Liu et al. electroreduced graphene oxide and AuNPs on the surface of GCE for stripping voltammetric of As3+29. Suhainie Ismail et al. reported an electrochemical sensor based on silica/AuNPs for, monitoring of arsenic30. Jaramillo et al. used of nanoporous Au microelectrode for arsenic meauserment31. The AuNPs can hold arsenic on its surface either covalently or through physical adsorption, facilitate electron transfer between arsenic and the electrode. Under certain conditions, arsenic may form temporary complexes with AuNPs on the electrode surface, which improves the electrochemical reaction32. The AuNPs surface, due to its catalytic properties, can prevent the adsorption of interfering compounds and increase the selectivity towards arsenic33. AuNPs alone have a high specific surface area, but when composited with other materials such as metal oxides, carbon nanostructures, this surface area increases and more sites are provided for arsenic adsorption and reaction. Metal oxides may have lower conductivity than AuNPs, but their combination with AuNPs increases conductivity and electron transfer rate. Composites base AuNPs increase the mechanical and chemical stability of the sensor and prevent the aggregation and clumping of AuNPs32. Among metal oxides, tin oxide (SnO2) and Co3O4 are two excellent substrates for the deposition and distribution of AuNPs on the electrode surface34,35. These porous semiconductors have been investigated as promising materials for a wide range of applications, such as energy storage, catalysis, sensors, electrochemistry, and magnetism due to their high porosity and high surface area36. Therefore, these two metal oxides are benefit for the voltammetric measurement of arsenic stripping because in this process, the electrode surface needs to be able to absorb and retain metal ions on the electrode surface, which can be improved by materials with high permeability. However, between SnO2 and Co3O4, Co3O4 exhibits high chemical stability than SnO2. Also, the electrocatalytic activity and surface reactivity of Co3O4 are much higher than SnO2 due to the more active surface for electron donation37. The combination of Co3O4 with AuNPs shows an excellent synergistic effect to increase stability, conductivity, and electrocatalytic effect38. Mohammad Rahman et al. developed a sensor based on Au-coated trimetallic oxide Co3O4/CdO/ZnO nanoparticles synthesized via a simple method for efficient methanol detection39. Lee et al. developed a sensor for acetone detection, which is of great importance in fields such as medical diagnostics, indoor air quality monitoring, and various industries. This sensor used novel Au@Co3O4 nanoparticles for stable acetone detection, which were synthesized via a simple low-temperature (70 °C) procedure40. Zhao et al. developed an electrochemical sensor for the detection of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), an important energy source and biomarker associated with various diseases. The sensor was based on MOF-derived porous Co3O4 modified with AuNPs and nucleic acids and used an aptamer for ATP detection as a novel electrocatalytic signal probe41.

In this work, we fabricated the porous and stable Co3O4 nanoneedle by co-precipitation that were uniformly coved with electrodeposited AuNPs. The AuNPs/Co3O4NPs/GCE electrode after characterization via SEM, EDS and ASV, showed excellent electrochemical performance for simultaneous measurement of Hg2+ and As3+ hazardous ions using ASV under optimized experimental conditions. Moreover, the interference experiments of coexisting cations and anions (FeCl2, Pb(C2H3O2), Cr(NO3)3, Bi(NO3)2, NiSO4, CuCl2) were investigated. The stability and reproducibility of AuNPs/Co3O4NPs/GCE electrode were also examined. In addition, the ultrahighly sensitive determination of Hg2+ and As3+ in different water samples was investigated in detail.

Experimental

Reagents and solutions

In this study, all chemicals were purchased from reputable suppliers and used without further purification. Mercury(II) nitrate (Hg(NO₃)₂, 99.5%), arsenic trioxide (As₂O₃, 99.5%), ethanol (C₂H₅OH, 99.9%), sodium nitrate (NaNO₃, 99.5%), copper(II) chloride (CuCl₂, 99.5%), ferric chloride (FeCl₃, 98%), potassium chloride (KCl, 99.5%), sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄, 98%), nitric acid (HNO₃, 70%), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 36%), alumina powder (Al₂O₃, 80%), potassium ferrocyanide (K₄[Fe(CN)₆], > 99%), and potassium ferricyanide (K₃[Fe(CN)₆], > 99%) were all obtained from Merck, Germany. Lead (Pb, 95%) and nickel(II) sulfate (NiSO₄, > 99%) were supplied by Sigma, USA, while bismuth(III) nitrate (Bi(NO₃)₃, 97%) was obtained from Fluka, Switzerland. All chemicals were of analytical grade and used directly in experimental procedures without additional purification.

Hg2+ Stock Solution (100 ppm): 0.016 g of Hg(NO₃)₂ was accurately weighed and dissolved in a 50 mL volumetric flask using double-distilled water. The resulting solution served as the stock analyte solution, from which more diluted solutions were prepared. As stock solution (100 ppm): 0.033 g of As₂O₃ was accurately weighed and transferred to a 250 mL volumetric flask. After partial dissolution in a small amount of NaOH to enhance solubility, the volume was adjusted with double-distilled water. 0.745 g of KCl was dissolved in 100 mL of double-distilled water for 0.1 M KCl electrolyte solution. For preparation of K₄Fe(CN)₆ and K₃Fe(CN)₆ mixture with 5 mM concentration: 0.092 g of K₄Fe(CN)₆ and0.082 g of K₃Fe(CN)₆ were dissolved in 50 mL of double-distilled water. 36 mL of 0.1 M acetic acid and 64 mL of 0.1 M sodium acetate were mixed in a 100 mL volumetric flask for make acetate buffer (pH = 5). The pH was adjusted to 5.0 using saturated NaOH or concentrated acetic acid. 0.1 M sodium nitrate electrolyte was prepared by dissolving 0.324 g of NaNO₃ in 50 mL of double-distilled water. Britton-Robinson buffer (B-R): To prepare this buffer, 0.123 g of boric acid, 0.133 mL of phosphoric acid, and 0.114 mL of acetic acid were mixed and diluted to 50 mL with double-distilled water.

Apparatus

Square wave voltammograms were recorded using a VA Computrance 797 polarographic system (Metrohm, Switzerland). A PalmSens device (Model 4.8 BV, Netherlands), equipped with PS-Trace software (version 4.8), was employed for cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). Three-electrode cell setup consisted of an Ag/AgCl (3 M KCl) reference electrode, a platinum wire as the counter electrode, and a GCE (Azar Electrode Co., Iran) as the working electrode. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were obtained by MIRA3 TESCAN to determine the size and morphology. Solution pH was measured with a digital pH meter (AZ Instruments, Taiwan). Ultrasonic bath for cleaning the GCE surface from Pars Nahand, Iran. All experiments were conducted at ambient temperature. EIS was performed at an applied potential of 0.2 V with an amplitude of 10 mV, over a frequency range of 0.1 to 50,000 Hz, in a 5.0 mM K3Fe(CN)6/K4Fe(CN)6 (1:1) solution containing 0.1 M KCl as the supporting electrolyte. The ASV measurements were conducted in a 10 mL KCl solution (0.1 M) with varying concentrations of As3+ and Hg2+. During the pre-concentration step, the potential was set to −1.4 V for 300 s while stirring, allowing for the simultaneous deposition of the target metals onto the electrode surface, in stripping step the potential scanned from − 1.0 to 0.75 V with a step voltage of 5 mV, frequency of 50 Hz, and a potential amplitude of 20 mV.

Preparation of AuNPs/Co3O4NPs/GCE

Synthesis of Co3O4NPs

1 g of Co(CH₃COO)₂·4 H₂O and 2.2 g of CTAB were dissolved in 70 mL of a mixed solvent composed of ethylene glycol and water in a 6:1 volume ratio. The prepared solution was then transferred into a 100 mL Teflon-lined autoclave and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 200 °C for 12 h. After the reaction, the autoclave was allowed to cool naturally to room temperature. The resulting product was collected via centrifugation and thoroughly washed with deionized water and ethanol to remove residual impurities. Finally, the obtained material was calcined in air at 350 °C for 3 h to produce Co₃O₄ nanoparticles.

Preparation of Co3O4NPs/GCE

The GCE was first mechanically polished using an alumina slurry until a reflective, mirror like surface was obtained. It was then cleaned by sonication in ethanol followed by double distilled water for 2 min each, and left to air-dry at room temperature. Following this, 5 µL of a Co3O4 NPs suspension (1.0 mg mL⁻¹ in ethanol) was carefully drop-cast onto the surface of the GCE and allowed to dry under ambient conditions. The modified electrode was denoted as Co3O4NPs/GCE.

Making of AuNPs/Co3O4NPs/GCE

AuNPs were electrochemically deposited onto the Co3O4NPs/GCE electrode by placing the Co3O4NPs/GCE in a voltammetric cell containing 10 mL of 0.6 mM Au solution and 0.5 M H2SO4. CV was performed for 25 cycles in the potential range of −0.2 to + 1.6 V with a scan rate of 0.05 V/s to deposit the AuNPs on the electrode surface. The electrode preparation steps are shown in the Scheme. 1.

Real samples preparation

The real samples used included drinking water from Sabzevar city, Karab River water, and well water from Hakim Sabzevari University. The water samples were analyzed using the developed sensor by preparing a 0.1 M KCl solution with the real samples. Electrochemical measurements were performed both without and with added concentrations of As³⁺ and Hg²⁺ ions.

Results and discussion

Electrodeposition of AuNPs on the surface of Co3O4NPs/GCE

To confirm the electrochemical deposition of AuNPs onto the porous Co3O4 nanoneedle surface, CV was performed over 25 successive cycles. Figure 1 illustrates the electrodeposition behavior of AuNPs on the GCE surface. The voltammograms reveal two oxidation peaks (a1 and a2) alongside two reduction peaks (c1 and c2) corresponding to the AuNPs. Notably, the anodic peak areas (a1 and a2) are smaller compared to the reduction peaks, indicating incomplete reoxidation of the AuNPs deposits during each cycle. Additionally, following the initial scan, the reduction peak of Au³⁺ shifts toward more positive potentials, which implies the progressive formation of an AuNPs layer on the electrode. With each subsequent scan, the amount of AuNPs deposited increases, as reflected by the gradual emergence and growth of a new redox couple (c1/c2). The anodic peak a2, observed at approximately 1.3 V from the second cycle onwards, intensifies with continued cycling, signifying the generation of gold oxide species. Correspondingly, the cathodic peak c2 around 0.9 V during the reverse scan is attributed to the reduction of these oxides.

Characterization of Co3O4NPs and AuNPs/Co3O4NPs/GCE

The morphology and elemental composition of the AuNPs/Co3O4NPs/GCE composite were examined using SEM and EDS. Additionally, the distribution of AuNPs on the Co3O4NPs/GCE surface was analyzed with the same techniques. SEM images of the Co3O4NPs/GCE, presented in Figs. 2A, reveal that the Co3O4 nanoparticles are uniformly arranged on the electrode surface, forming a nanotube-like structure. The presence of white areas in the image serves as evidence of the increased conductivity of Co3O4NPs. Therefore, the successful synthesis of Co3O4NPs can be confirmed. In the inset of Fig. 2A with a scale of 200 nm, it can be seen that the width of the Co3O4NPs is estimated to be around 50 to 100 nm. The SEM images of AuNPs/Co3O4NPs/GCE (Figs. 2B) illustrate that AuNPs are evenly dispersed across the Co3O4NPs/GCE surface, which exhibits uniformly sized nanostructures about 100 nM. Incorporating AuNPs into the composite enhances its overall electrical conductivity.

EDS analysis was used to identify the elements in nanocomposite. As can be seen, Fig. 3A and B show the EDS pattern of Co3O4NPs/GCE and AuNPs/Co3O4NPs/GCE, respectively. The findings verify that Co, O, and Au are the primary elements present in the sample.

As seen from the XRD pattern the sample exhibits distinct diffraction peaks well indexed to the cubic spinel Co3O4NPs with the standard JCPDS card No. 01–074-1657 (Fig. 4A). The prominent peaks which are observed at approximately 2θ values of 18.9°, 31.3°, 36.8°, 44.8°, 59.3°, and 65.2° represent the (111), (220), (311), (400), (511), and (440) crystal planes, respectively, signifying the existence of pure spinel Co3O4NPs. The highest intensity peak seen at approximately 36.8° indicates preferential orientation along the (311) plane characteristic for spinel cobalt oxide. The absence of other peaks reveals that the synthesized phase is single phase with no impurities identifiable by XRD. Furthermore, peak broadening shows the formation of nanocrystalline Co3O4. The XRD patterns confirm successful synthesis of pure cubic spinel Co3O4NPs with good crystallinity. The particle size of the Co3O4NPs was calculated from the Debye–Scherrer equation obtained from the XRD pattern. The calculated average crystallite size of the predominant diffraction peaks at 2θ ≈ 19°, 31°, 37°, 45°, 59°, and 65° was approximately 18–20 nm which confirms the nanocrystalline nature of the sample34.

The presence of cobalt and oxygen elements in the synthesized Co3O4NPs was also confirmed by elemental mapping analysis in Fig. 4B.

Optimization of experiments condition

Evaluation of electrolyte kind

The effect of kind of electrolytes on the electrode responses were investigated by different electrolyte like, phosphate buffer solution (PBS) (pH = 5), 0.1 M KCl solution, Britton-Robinson buffer (pH = 5), and acetate buffer solution (ABS) (pH = 5). As shown in the Fig. 5A, the As3+ and Hg2+ stripping peaks in the PBS (pH = 5) and Britton-Robinson buffer (pH = 5) are relatively weak. Although the As3+ current is significant in PBS, the Hg2+ current is low. In contrast, the As3+ and Hg2+ stripping peaks in the 0.1 M KCl solution is well-defined. Based on these findings, the 0.1 M KCl solution was selected as the electrolyte for further measurements and used in subsequent steps of the study.

Evaluation of KCl concentration

ASV was performed using the AuNPs/Co3O4NPs/GCE electrode in various concentrations of KCl (ranging from 0.01 to 0.2 M). The voltammograms clearly demonstrate the variation in the peak currents of As3+ and Hg2+ as a function of KCl concentration in the inset if Fig. 5B Notably, an increase in KCl concentration up to 0.1 M leads to a corresponding enhancement in the current response of both analytes. This improvement can be attributed to the elevated ionic strength, which reduces solution resistance and consequently decreases the ohmic drop, facilitating higher current flow. However, further increases beyond 0.1 M result in a decline in peak currents. Therefore, 0.1 M KCl was identified as the optimal concentration for electrolyte preparation in subsequent experiments.

Evaluation of accumulation time and accumulation potential

Another critical parameter affecting the sensitivity of the electrode’s dissociation response is the accumulation potential. The influence of the accumulation potential was examined within the range of −1.5 to 0.9 V, with an accumulation time of 280 s applied. The corresponding voltammograms are presented in Fig. 5C. As observed in the Fig. 5C, an increase in potential leads to higher peaks for both As3+ and Hg2+, up to a potential of −1.4 V. Beyond this potential, the electrode surface is compromised, and accurate peaks for As3+ and Hg2+ are no longer formed. At a potential of −1.4 V, the peaks for both As3+ and Hg2+ reach their maximum values (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, the decrease in the stripping peak currents of As3+ at potentials more negative than − 1.4 V can be attributed to hydrogen (H2) generation at these negative potentials, which interferes with the reduction of As3+.

Voltammetry measurements were conducted using the AuNPs/Co3O4NPs/GCE electrode at varying accumulation times to investigate the uptake of As3+ and Hg2+. The ions were accumulated within a time range of 220 to 340 s by applying a potential of −1.4 V to the electrode surface. During this pre-concentration phase, extending the accumulation time allows more ions to adsorb onto the electrode surface. As illustrated in Fig. 5D, increasing the accumulation time to 280 s resulted in a noticeable increase in the resting currents of both As3+ and Hg2+. Beyond this point, no significant change in the current response was observed. Therefore, 280 s was selected as the optimal accumulation time for further analysis.

Assessment of the electrocatalytic behavior of various modified electrodes

The electrochemical behavior of the modified electrodes was systematically evaluated after each fabrication step of the using CV and EIS. The electrochemical properties of GCE, Co3O4NPs/GCE, and AuNPs/Co3O4NPs/GCE electrodes were investigated in a 5 mM K₃Fe(CN)₆ and K₄Fe(CN)₆ solution containing, 0.1 M KCl solution. Figure 6A presents the cyclic voltammograms for the GCE, Co3O4NPs/GCE, and AuNPs/Co3O4NPs/GCE electrodes. The results show the electrode without any modifier, which exhibits the lowest peak current in both the ion. However, by incorporating Co3O4NPs, as seen in curve (b), the peak current decreases due to the semi conductivity of the nanoparticles. Curve (c) displays the cyclic voltammogram for the AuNPs/Co3O4NPs/GCE electrode surface, which shows a significant increase in peak current of As3+ and Hg2+compared to the GCE and Co3O4NPs/GCE. This enhancement is attributed to the presence of AuNPs, which serve as a conductive substrate, leading to a threefold increase in current and confirming the successful synthesis of the AuNPs/Co3O4NPs/GCE electrode. The surface area of ​​the different electrodes was determined by the Randers-Swick equation and by changing the potential scan rate in the 5 mM of K4Fe(CN)6/K3Fe(CN)6 containing 0.1 M KCl as an electrochemical probe solution. The result obtained show high surface area for AuNPs/Co3O4NPs/GCE (0.2145 cm2) compared to GCE (0.0145 cm2), AuNPs/GCE (0.1470 cm2) and Co3O4NPs/GCE (0.1207 cm2)42.

The electrochemical characterization of different modified electrodes was also studied by the EIS. EIS is a powerful technique employed to investigate surface characteristics associated with recognition events at the electrode interface. Figure 6B demonstrate Nyquist plots for GCE, Co3O4NPs/GCE and AuNPs/Co3O4NPs/GCE in a 5 mM [Fe(CN)₆]³⁻/⁴⁻ solution and 0.1 M KCl. The surface resistance (Rct) of the bare GCE electrode was found to be 2646 Ω. Upon the deposition of Co3O4NPs onto the GCE surface, the electron transport resistance increased to 7586 Ω. However, when the surface of the GCE was further modified with a AuNPs/Co3O4NPs nanocomposite, the Rct decreased significantly to 1824 Ω. These results indicate the successful synthesis of Co3O4NPs and grat electrodeposition of AuNPs. The observed decrease in Rct upon modification of the electrode with nanoparticles and the nanocomposite can be attributed to the decreasing in kinetic resistance, resulting from enhanced charge transfer at the electrode surface. The equivalent circuit of the obtained Nyquist curves is shown in inset of the Fig. 6B.

Electrochemical investigation of Hg2+ and As3+ ions

Figure 7 illustrates the ASV obtained using bare GCE, Co3O4NPs/GCE, and AuNPs/Co3O4NPs/GCE electrodes under conditions with and without the presence of Hg2+ and As3+ ions. As depicted, no observable peaks were detected on the bare GCE in the absence of the target ions, confirming that the electrode surface was clean and free of any interfering contaminants (curve a). However, when 700 ppb As3+ and 900 ppb Hg2+ were introduced to electrochemical cell, the stripping peak of As3+ and Hg2+ were observed on the surface of GCE (curve b). Modification of GCE by Co3O4NPs (curve c) lead to increasing adsorption site for As3+ and Hg2+ ions in the preconcentration step. Therefore, the current of Hg2+ and As3+ increase on the surface of Co3O4NPs/GCE. The recorded currents for As3+ and Hg2+ ions after modification of AuNPs (curve d) were significantly increases than the unmodified GCE under same concentrations due to the best conductivity and electrocatalysis effect of AuNPs. Finally, the highest current for As3+ and Hg2+ were obtained at the surface of AuNPs/Co3O4NPs/GCE (curve e). The improved response is primarily due to the distinctive features of Co3O4NPs, including their high surface area and favorable electrical properties, combined with the remarkable chemical and electronic characteristics of AuNPs.

Assessment of the analytical capabilities of the sensor

The electrochemical behavior and analytical capability of the AuNPs/Co3O4NPs/GCE electrode for the sensitive and simultaneous detection of As3+ and Hg2+ were investigated using ASV. To assess the mutual independence of the two analytes during detection, two experimental series were conducted. In the first set, the concentration of Hg²⁺ was systematically varied while maintaining As³⁺ at a constant concentration of 200 ppb (Fig. 8A). In the second set, the concentration of As³⁺ was varied, with Hg²⁺ held constant at 200 ppb (Fig. 8B). The resulting ASV responses for different concentrations of both ions are shown in Fig. 8C. As observed in Fig. 8A, the peak current associated with As³⁺ remained nearly unchanged despite increasing concentrations of Hg²⁺, indicating minimal interference from Hg²⁺. Conversely, Fig. 8B demonstrates that the anodic peak current for As³⁺ increased proportionally with its concentration, while the signal for Hg²⁺ remained stable. In Fig. 8C, simultaneous increases in the peak currents for both As³⁺ and Hg²⁺ were observed with rising concentrations of the respective ions, confirming the sensor’s ability to detect both ions concurrently without cross-interference. These findings validate the selectivity and reliability of the proposed sensor for the simultaneous quantification of As³⁺ and Hg²⁺ in complex sample matrices. The obtained results show that a linear relationship for As3⁺ from 10 to 900 ppb as follows: Ip (µA) = 0.0974 CAs3+ (ppb) + 27.436 with a linear correlation coefficient of R2 = 0.9926. Furthermore, a linear relationship for Hg2+ from 10 to 650 was obtained base on the following equation: Ip (µA) = 0.0981 CHg2+ (ppb) + 30.523 with a linear correlation coefficient of R2 = 0.9715. The linear regression equation for simultaneous detection was obtained based the following equation: Ip (µA) = 0.0482 CAs3+ (ppb) + 3.9609 (R2 = 0.9808) for As3+ in the range 100–900 ppb and the linear regression equation Ip (µA) = 0.0814 CHg2+ (ppb) + 34.048 (R2 = 0.9998) for Hg in the range10-720 ppb. The limit of detection and limit for As3+ and Hg2+ base on the simultaneous calibration slope and 3Sb were obtained 0.31 and 0.46 ppb, respectively. The analytical performance of the AuNPs/Co₃O₄NPs/GCE sensor was benchmarked against other previously reported modified electrodes used for the detection of As³⁺ and Hg²⁺either individually or simultaneously as summarized in Table 1. Notably, the AuNPs/Co₃O₄NPs/GCE sensor demonstrates a low detection limit and a broad linear range compared to the other reported systems. As anticipated, it exhibits excellent performance in the simultaneous determination of both Hg²⁺ and As³⁺. The repeatability of the AuNPs/Co₃O₄NPs/GCE sensor was measured by preparing an electrode and simultaneously analyzing 200 ppb of As3+ and Hg2+ for three times. The relative standard deviations (RSD) of 4.1% for arsenic and 2.8% for Hg2+ were obtained. Also, to check the reproducibility, three electrodes were prepared and analyzed under the same conditions. The RSD were determined to be 3.4% and 4.7% for As3+ and Hg2+, respectively. The results obtained indicate excellent repeatability and reproducibility of the sensor.

Evaluation of interfering effects in analytical measurements

One of the key advantages of electrochemical techniques in metal ion detection is their capability to minimize the interference from coexisting metal ions during the analysis of the target ion. Following the optimization of As3+ and Hg2+ detection parameters, the influence of various cations and anions was systematically evaluated. Initially, As3+ and Hg2+ were measured under optimized conditions in the absence of any potential interferents. Subsequently, specific concentrations of interfering species were introduced, and the resulting peak currents were compared with the control. Species inducing a deviation of less than ± 10% from the control were considered non-interfering, otherwise, they were classified as interfering, requiring concentration adjustment to fall within the acceptable range. The results are summarized in Table 2. As shown, only the addition of CuCl2 salt in the measurement of As3+ and NiSO4 during the measurement of Hg 2+ exhibited significant interference.

Conclusions

In this study, a sensor based on AuNPs and Co3O4NPs was successfully developed. Characterization results confirmed the stable and accurate synthesis and deposition of the nanomaterials onto the GCE surface. The sensor was applied for the simultaneous detection of As³⁺ and Hg²⁺ using stripping voltammetry. The combined use of AuNPs and Co3O4NPs exhibited a synergistic enhancement in the electrochemical response, significantly increasing the oxidation peak currents for both ions compared to the bare electrode. The modified AuNPs/Co3O4NPs/GCE demonstrated excellent sensitivity due to its capacity for preconcentration and efficient detection of the target analytes. The calculated detection limits for Hg²⁺ and As³⁺ were 0.31 ppb and 0.46 ppb, respectively. Finally, the sensor was successfully employed for the determination of As³⁺ and Hg²⁺ in real water samples, yielding reliable and satisfactory results.
