TV over Bernoulli products: the small parameter regime
Ariel Avital, Aryeh Kontorovich, George Salafatinos

Abstract
. We study the total variation distance (TV) between two n n -fold Bernoulli product measures parametrized by p = ( p 1 , … , p n ) \textup{{\bf p}}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}) and q = ( q 1 , … , q n ) \textup{{\bf q}}=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}) , respectively, in the tiny and small regimes. In the tiny regime, we have p i , q i ≲ 1 / n 2 p_{i},q_{i}\lesssim 1/n^{2} , and in the small regime, p i , q i ≲ 1 / n p_{i},q_{i}\lesssim 1/n . We discover that in the tiny regime, the TV distance behaves as ‖ p − q ‖ 1 \|\textup{{\bf p}}-\textup{{\bf q}}\|_{1} , while in the small regime, it behaves as ∑ i = 1 n | p i ​ ∏ j ≠ i ( 1 − p j ) − q i ​ ∏ j ≠ i ( 1 − q j ) | , \sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big|p_{i}\prod_{j\neq i}(1-p_{j})-q_{i}\prod_{j\neq i}(1-q_{j})\Big|, both up to absolute constants. Along the way we discover some identities of possible independent interest.

1.
Introduction

For
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in[0,1]^{n}
, consider the Bernoulli product measures

\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{p}):=\operatorname{Ber}(p_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes\operatorname{Ber}(p_{n}),\qquad\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{q}):=\operatorname{Ber}(q_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes\operatorname{Ber}(q_{n}),

on the Hamming cube
\{0,1\}^{n}
.
The total variation distance

\operatorname{TV}(\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{p}),\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{q}))=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\left|\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{p})(x)-\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{q})(x)\right|

is both fundamental and notoriously difficult to compute exactly.
From the algorithmic perspective,
computing
\operatorname{TV}(\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{p}),\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{q}))
exactly
is
\#\mathrm{P}
-hard in general
[
1
]
,
motivating
a series of
efficient approximation schemes for product measures

[
2
,
3
,
4
]
.
From the analytic perspective,
TV is often bounded
by more tractable divergences (KL, Hellinger,
\chi^{2}
, etc.),
whose tensorization properties are well understood;
see
[
5
]
for a recent discussion in the present setting.

This work continues the program initiated in
[
5
,
6
]
of
approximating the TV over product measures in terms of readily computable elementary functions; the forthcoming paper
[
7
]
further builds on the results we prove here.
Our point of departure is
Theorems 1.1 and 1.2 of

[
5
]
, which show, respectively, that

\operatorname{TV}(\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{p}),\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{q}))\ \gtrsim\ \|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\|_{2},

and

\operatorname{TV}(\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{p}),\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{q}))\ \leq\ \|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\|_{2},

the latter
under the additional assumption that
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}

are
symmetric about
1/2

(i.e.,
\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{q}
).
In the Appendix, we extend the argument to
quasi-symmetric
pairs, obtaining the upper bound with an extra factor
\sqrt{2}
.
Taken together, these results indicate that in a neighborhood of
1/2
, TV behaves like
\ell_{2}
, up to universal constants.

The focus of the present note is complementary: we turn to the opposite extreme of the parameter space, namely
p_{i},q_{i}
very close to
0
(and, by symmetry, very close to
1
), and identify regimes in which
\operatorname{TV}\big(\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{p}),\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{q})\big)
admits a particularly simple description.
A key tool is the
slice decomposition
: if
\Delta_{k}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})
denotes the total absolute discrepancy between the two measures on the
k
-th Hamming slice, then

(1.1)

\displaystyle 2\operatorname{TV}(\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{p}),\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{q}))=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\Delta_{k}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}),

so controlling TV reduces to understanding which slices dominate.

Main results.

For a subset
S\subseteq[n]
and a parameter vector
\mathbf{y}\in[0,1]^{n}
,
let

P_{S}(\mathbf{y})
denote the mass assigned by
\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{y})
to the atom with ones in
S

(see (
2.1
) below),
and define the slice discrepancies
\Delta_{k}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})
as in Definition
2.1
.
Our first theorem shows that in the tiny regime, the TV distance is equivalent to
\left\lVert\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right\rVert_{1}
up to constants.

Theorem 1.1

(Tiny regime:
\ell_{1}
geometry)
.

For
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in[0,1/n^{2}]^{n}
,

\frac{1}{4}\,\left\lVert\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right\rVert_{1}\ \leq\ \operatorname{TV}(\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{p}),\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{q}))\ \leq\ \left\lVert\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right\rVert_{1}.

Our second theorem shows that in the small regime, the entire
\ell_{1}
distance between the product measures is controlled by the singleton slice.

Theorem 1.2

(Small regime: singletons control TV)
.

For
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in[0,1/(2n)]^{n}

with
n\geq 2
, write

\Delta_{1}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big|p_{i}\prod_{j\neq i}(1-p_{j})-q_{i}\prod_{j\neq i}(1-q_{j})\Big|.

Then

\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta_{1}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})\ \leq\ \operatorname{TV}(\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{p}),\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{q}))\ \leq\ \left(2-\frac{1}{n}\right)\Delta_{1}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}).

2.
Definitions and notation

We write
A\lesssim B
to mean
A\leq CB
for a universal constant
C>0
,
and
A\gtrsim B
similarly;
A\asymp B
means both
A\lesssim B
and
B\lesssim A
.
For integer
n\geq 1
,
[n]:=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.
For a parameter vector
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\in[0,1]^{n}
and a subset
S\subseteq[n]
, define the product Bernoulli mass

(2.1)

P_{S}(\mathbf{y})\;:=\;\prod_{i\in S}y_{i}\,\prod_{i\notin S}(1-y_{i}).

In particular,
P_{\emptyset}(\mathbf{y})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-y_{i})
is the probability of the all-zeros atom.

Definition 2.1

(Slice discrepancies)
.

For
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in[0,1]^{n}
and
S\subseteq[n]
, define

\delta_{S}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}):=P_{S}(\mathbf{p})-P_{S}(\mathbf{q}).

For each
k\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
define the absolute
k
-slice discrepancy

\Delta_{k}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}):=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]:\,|S|=k}\left|\delta_{S}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})\right|.

We will often abbreviate
\delta_{S}:=\delta_{S}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})
and
\Delta_{k}:=\Delta_{k}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})
when the pair
(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})
is clear. In particular (
1.1
) holds.
We also write

\mathbf{x}:=\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q},\qquad x_{i}:=p_{i}-q_{i},\qquad\left\lVert\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right\rVert_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|x_{i}\right|.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section we assume

(3.1)

\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in\Big[0,\frac{1}{n^{2}}\Big]^{n}.

Only the lower bound

\frac{1}{4}\left\lVert\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\operatorname{TV}(\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{p}),\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{q}))

requires proof;
the upper bound

\operatorname{TV}(\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{p}),\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{q}))\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right\rVert_{1}
is classic
[
5
, Disp. (1.4)]
.

For each
i\in[n]
, define

P_{-i}(\mathbf{y}):=\prod_{j\neq i}(1-y_{j}),\qquad\text{so that}\qquad P_{\{i\}}(\mathbf{y})=y_{i}\,P_{-i}(\mathbf{y}).

Lemma 3.1
.

Under (
3.1
), for every
i\in[n]
,

P_{-i}(\mathbf{p})=\prod_{j\neq i}(1-p_{j})\ \geq\ \frac{3}{4}.

Proof.

If
n=1
, the product is empty and equals
1
.
Assume
n\geq 2
.
Since
p_{j}\leq 1/n^{2}
, we have

P_{-i}(\mathbf{p})\geq\Big(1-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\Big)^{n-1}.

For
n=2
this equals
3/4
.
For
n\geq 3
, Bernoulli’s inequality gives

\Big(1-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\Big)^{n-1}\geq 1-\frac{n-1}{n^{2}}=\frac{n^{2}-n+1}{n^{2}}\geq\frac{3}{4},

since
4(n^{2}-n+1)\geq 3n^{2}
is equivalent to
(n-2)^{2}\geq 0
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Under (
3.1
), for every
i\in[n]
,

\left|P_{-i}(\mathbf{p})-P_{-i}(\mathbf{q})\right|\ \leq\ \sum_{k\neq i}\left|x_{k}\right|.

Proof.

If
n=1
then
P_{-1}(\cdot)\equiv 1
and the claim is trivial.
Assume
n\geq 2
and consider the segment
\mathbf{u}(t):=\mathbf{q}+t(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q})
.
The function
t\mapsto P_{-i}(\mathbf{u}(t))
is differentiable, and by the mean value theorem

P_{-i}(\mathbf{p})-P_{-i}(\mathbf{q})=\sum_{k\neq i}\Big(-\prod_{j\neq i,k}(1-u_{j}(\xi))\Big)x_{k}\quad\text{for some }\xi\in(0,1).

Taking absolute values and using
0\leq(1-u_{j}(\xi))\leq 1
gives

\left|P_{-i}(\mathbf{p})-P_{-i}(\mathbf{q})\right|\leq\sum_{k\neq i}\left|x_{k}\right|.

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Fix
i\in[n]
.
Using
P_{\{i\}}(\mathbf{y})=y_{i}P_{-i}(\mathbf{y})
we expand

\displaystyle P_{\{i\}}(\mathbf{p})-P_{\{i\}}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=p_{i}P_{-i}(\mathbf{p})-q_{i}P_{-i}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=(p_{i}-q_{i})P_{-i}(\mathbf{p})+q_{i}\big(P_{-i}(\mathbf{p})-P_{-i}(\mathbf{q})\big)

\displaystyle=x_{i}P_{-i}(\mathbf{p})+q_{i}\big(P_{-i}(\mathbf{p})-P_{-i}(\mathbf{q})\big).

By the reverse triangle inequality,

\left|P_{\{i\}}(\mathbf{p})-P_{\{i\}}(\mathbf{q})\right|\geq\left|x_{i}\right|\,P_{-i}(\mathbf{p})-q_{i}\,\left|P_{-i}(\mathbf{p})-P_{-i}(\mathbf{q})\right|.

Apply Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
3.2
to obtain

\left|P_{\{i\}}(\mathbf{p})-P_{\{i\}}(\mathbf{q})\right|\geq\frac{3}{4}\left|x_{i}\right|-q_{i}\sum_{k\neq i}\left|x_{k}\right|.

Summing over
i
yields

\Delta_{1}\geq\frac{3}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|x_{i}\right|-\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\sum_{k\neq i}\left|x_{k}\right|=\frac{3}{4}\left\lVert\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right\rVert_{1}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|x_{k}\right|\sum_{i\neq k}q_{i}.

Because
q_{i}\leq 1/n^{2}
, for each fixed
k
we have
\sum_{i\neq k}q_{i}\leq(n-1)/n^{2}\leq 1/4
.
Therefore the last term is at most
\tfrac{1}{4}\left\lVert\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right\rVert_{1}
, and

\Delta_{1}\geq\Big(\frac{3}{4}-\frac{1}{4}\Big)\left\lVert\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right\rVert_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right\rVert_{1}.

Finally,

\operatorname{TV}(\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{p}),\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{q}))=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\Delta_{k}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})\geq\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta_{1}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})\geq\frac{1}{4}\,\left\lVert\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right\rVert_{1}.

The upper bound
\operatorname{TV}(\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{p}),\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{q}))\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right\rVert_{1}
is standard and holds for all
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in[0,1]^{n}
.
∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

In this section we assume
n\geq 2
and work in the
“small” parameter domain

(4.1)

\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in[0,\lambda_{n}]^{n},\qquad\lambda_{n}:=\frac{1}{2n}.

It is convenient to also introduce

(4.2)

\beta_{n}:=\frac{\lambda_{n}}{1-\lambda_{n}}=\frac{1}{2n-1}.

All slice discrepancies
\Delta_{k}
below are understood for the same pair
(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})
.

Theorem 4.1

(
\Delta_{0}
bound)
.

For
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in[0,\lambda_{n}]^{n}
,

\Delta_{0}\ \leq\ \left(2-\frac{1}{n}\right)\Delta_{1}=\frac{2n-1}{n}\,\Delta_{1}.

Proof.

Let
g(\mathbf{y}):=P_{\emptyset}(\mathbf{y})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-y_{j})
and define the segment
\mathbf{u}(t):=\mathbf{q}+t(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q})
.
By the fundamental theorem of calculus,

P_{\emptyset}(\mathbf{p})-P_{\emptyset}(\mathbf{q})=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}\,g(\mathbf{u}(t))\,dt.

Write
x_{k}:=p_{k}-q_{k}
.
Since
\partial g/\partial y_{k}(\mathbf{y})=-\prod_{j\neq k}(1-y_{j})
,

\frac{d}{dt}\,g(\mathbf{u}(t))=-\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}\prod_{j\neq k}(1-u_{j}(t)).

Define

B_{k}:=x_{k}\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{j\neq k}(1-u_{j}(t))\,dt.

Then
P_{\emptyset}(\mathbf{p})-P_{\emptyset}(\mathbf{q})=-\sum_{k=1}^{n}B_{k}
, so

\Delta_{0}=\left|P_{\emptyset}(\mathbf{p})-P_{\emptyset}(\mathbf{q})\right|\leq\sum_{k}\left|B_{k}\right|
.

Next fix
k\in[n]
and consider
h_{k}(\mathbf{y}):=P_{\{k\}}(\mathbf{y})=y_{k}\prod_{j\neq k}(1-y_{j})
.
Differentiating along the same segment gives

\delta_{\{k\}}=h_{k}(\mathbf{p})-h_{k}(\mathbf{q})=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}\,h_{k}(\mathbf{u}(t))\,dt.

A direct computation yields

\frac{d}{dt}\,h_{k}(\mathbf{u}(t))=x_{k}\prod_{j\neq k}(1-u_{j}(t))\;-\sum_{m\neq k}x_{m}\,u_{k}(t)\prod_{j\neq k,m}(1-u_{j}(t)).

Integrating and rearranging shows

B_{k}=\delta_{\{k\}}+\sum_{m\neq k}x_{m}\int_{0}^{1}u_{k}(t)\prod_{j\neq k,m}(1-u_{j}(t))\,dt.

Taking absolute values and summing over
k
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|B_{k}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|\delta_{\{k\}}\right|+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{m\neq k}\left|x_{m}\right|\int_{0}^{1}u_{k}(t)\prod_{j\neq k,m}(1-u_{j}(t))\,dt.

For
k\neq m
we use

u_{k}(t)\prod_{j\neq k,m}(1-u_{j}(t))=\frac{u_{k}(t)}{1-u_{k}(t)}\prod_{j\neq m}(1-u_{j}(t))\leq\beta_{n}\prod_{j\neq m}(1-u_{j}(t)),

because
u_{k}(t)\in[0,\lambda_{n}]
implies
\frac{u_{k}(t)}{1-u_{k}(t)}\leq\beta_{n}
.
Therefore

\left|x_{m}\right|\int_{0}^{1}u_{k}(t)\prod_{j\neq k,m}(1-u_{j}(t))\,dt\leq\beta_{n}\left|x_{m}\right|\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{j\neq m}(1-u_{j}(t))\,dt=\beta_{n}\left|B_{m}\right|.

Summing over
k\neq m
yields

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{m\neq k}\left|x_{m}\right|\int_{0}^{1}u_{k}(t)\prod_{j\neq k,m}(1-u_{j}(t))\,dt\leq\beta_{n}(n-1)\sum_{m=1}^{n}\left|B_{m}\right|.

Hence

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|B_{k}\right|\leq\Delta_{1}+\beta_{n}(n-1)\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|B_{k}\right|.

Since
\beta_{n}(n-1)=\frac{n-1}{2n-1}<1
, we can absorb to obtain

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|B_{k}\right|\leq\frac{1}{1-\beta_{n}(n-1)}\,\Delta_{1}=\frac{2n-1}{n}\,\Delta_{1}.

Finally
\Delta_{0}\leq\sum_{k}\left|B_{k}\right|
gives the claim.
∎

Theorem 4.2

(
\Delta_{2}/\Delta_{1}
bound)
.

For
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in[0,\lambda_{n}]^{n}
,

\Delta_{2}\ \leq\ \frac{3(n-1)}{2(2n-1)}\,\Delta_{1}.

Proof.

Fix
a<b
.
Write
\delta_{a}:=\delta_{\{a\}}
,
\delta_{b}:=\delta_{\{b\}}
, and
\delta_{ab}:=\delta_{\{a,b\}}
.

We introduce an auxiliary quantity chosen so that a certain linear combination of singleton and doubleton masses isolates
\delta_{ab}
cleanly in odds coordinates:

S(\mathbf{y};a,b):=\beta_{n}\big(P_{\{a\}}(\mathbf{y})+P_{\{b\}}(\mathbf{y})\big)-2P_{\{a,b\}}(\mathbf{y}).

A direct expansion shows

(4.3)

\frac{\beta_{n}}{2}(\delta_{a}+\delta_{b})-\delta_{ab}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(S(\mathbf{p};a,b)-S(\mathbf{q};a,b)\Big)\;=:\;\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta S_{ab}.

By the triangle inequality,

(4.4)

\left|\delta_{ab}\right|\leq\frac{\beta_{n}}{2}\big(\left|\delta_{a}\right|+\left|\delta_{b}\right|\big)+\frac{1}{2}\left|\Delta S_{ab}\right|.

Summing (
4.4
) over all
a<b
yields

(4.5)

\Delta_{2}\leq\frac{\beta_{n}}{2}(n-1)\Delta_{1}+\sum_{a<b}\frac{1}{2}\left|\Delta S_{ab}\right|.

It remains to bound
\sum_{a<b}\left|\Delta S_{ab}\right|
.
Introduce odds coordinates
o_{i}(\mathbf{y}):=\frac{y_{i}}{1-y_{i}}
.
On
[0,\lambda_{n}]
we have
0\leq o_{i}(\mathbf{y})\leq\beta_{n}
.
Note the factorizations

P_{\{a\}}(\mathbf{y})=P_{\emptyset}(\mathbf{y})\,o_{a}(\mathbf{y}),\qquad P_{\{a,b\}}(\mathbf{y})=P_{\emptyset}(\mathbf{y})\,o_{a}(\mathbf{y})o_{b}(\mathbf{y}).

Therefore
S(\mathbf{y};a,b)=P_{\emptyset}(\mathbf{y})\,H(\mathbf{y};a,b)
where

H(\mathbf{y};a,b):=\beta_{n}\big(o_{a}(\mathbf{y})+o_{b}(\mathbf{y})\big)-2o_{a}(\mathbf{y})o_{b}(\mathbf{y}).

Write

\Delta P_{\emptyset}:=P_{\emptyset}(\mathbf{p})-P_{\emptyset}(\mathbf{q})

and
\Delta o_{i}:=o_{i}(\mathbf{p})-o_{i}(\mathbf{q})
.
Then

\Delta S_{ab}=P_{\emptyset}(\mathbf{p})\big(H(\mathbf{p};a,b)-H(\mathbf{q};a,b)\big)+H(\mathbf{q};a,b)\,\Delta P_{\emptyset}.

Using the bilinear identity

H(\mathbf{p};a,b)-H(\mathbf{q};a,b)=(\beta_{n}-2o_{b}(\mathbf{q}))\,\Delta o_{a}+(\beta_{n}-2o_{a}(\mathbf{p}))\,\Delta o_{b},

and the relations

P_{\emptyset}(\mathbf{p})\,\Delta o_{a}=\delta_{a}-o_{a}(\mathbf{q})\,\Delta P_{\emptyset},\qquad P_{\emptyset}(\mathbf{p})\,\Delta o_{b}=\delta_{b}-o_{b}(\mathbf{q})\,\Delta P_{\emptyset},

one obtains the identity

\Delta S_{ab}=(\beta_{n}-2o_{b}(\mathbf{q}))\delta_{a}+(\beta_{n}-2o_{a}(\mathbf{p}))\delta_{b}+2o_{a}(\mathbf{p})o_{b}(\mathbf{q})\,\Delta P_{\emptyset}.

Taking absolute values and using
o_{i}(\cdot)\in[0,\beta_{n}]
gives

\left|\Delta S_{ab}\right|\leq\beta_{n}\left|\delta_{a}\right|+\beta_{n}\left|\delta_{b}\right|+2\beta_{n}^{2}\left|\Delta P_{\emptyset}\right|.

Summing over
a<b
and using
\left|\Delta P_{\emptyset}\right|=\Delta_{0}
yields

\sum_{a<b}\frac{1}{2}\left|\Delta S_{ab}\right|\leq\frac{\beta_{n}}{2}(n-1)\Delta_{1}+\beta_{n}^{2}\binom{n}{2}\Delta_{0}.

Apply Theorem
4.1
to bound
\Delta_{0}\leq\frac{2n-1}{n}\Delta_{1}
.
Since
\beta_{n}=\frac{1}{2n-1}
, we obtain

\beta_{n}^{2}\binom{n}{2}\Delta_{0}\leq\beta_{n}^{2}\binom{n}{2}\frac{2n-1}{n}\Delta_{1}=\frac{n-1}{2(2n-1)}\Delta_{1}=\frac{\beta_{n}}{2}(n-1)\Delta_{1}.

Therefore
\sum_{a<b}\frac{1}{2}\left|\Delta S_{ab}\right|\leq\beta_{n}(n-1)\Delta_{1}
.
Substituting into (
4.5
) gives

\Delta_{2}\leq\frac{\beta_{n}}{2}(n-1)\Delta_{1}+\beta_{n}(n-1)\Delta_{1}=\frac{3}{2}\beta_{n}(n-1)\Delta_{1}=\frac{3(n-1)}{2(2n-1)}\Delta_{1},

as required.
∎

Lemma 4.3

(
\ell_{1}
control by the
1
-slice)
.

For any
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in[0,\lambda_{n}]^{n}
,

\left\lVert\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right\rVert_{1}\leq K(n)\,\Delta_{1},\qquad K(n):=\frac{2n-1}{n\,(1-\lambda_{n})^{n-1}}.

In particular, if
\Delta_{1}=0
then
\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{q}
.

Proof.

Fix
j\in[n]
and consider
F(t):=P_{\{j\}}(\mathbf{q}+t(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}))
for
t\in[0,1]
.
Then
F(1)-F(0)=\delta_{\{j\}}
.
By the mean value theorem, there exists
t_{j}\in(0,1)
such that

\delta_{\{j\}}=F^{\prime}(t_{j})=\nabla P_{\{j\}}(\mathbf{u}^{(j)})\cdot\mathbf{x},\qquad\mathbf{u}^{(j)}:=\mathbf{q}+t_{j}(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q})\in[0,\lambda_{n}]^{n}.

Since
P_{\{j\}}(\mathbf{y})=y_{j}\prod_{\ell\neq j}(1-y_{\ell})
, we have

\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}}P_{\{j\}}(\mathbf{y})=\prod_{\ell\neq j}(1-y_{\ell}),\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{m}}P_{\{j\}}(\mathbf{y})=-y_{j}\prod_{\ell\neq j,m}(1-y_{\ell})\ \ (m\neq j).

Therefore

\delta_{\{j\}}=\Big(\prod_{\ell\neq j}(1-u^{(j)}_{\ell})\Big)x_{j}-\sum_{m\neq j}u^{(j)}_{j}\Big(\prod_{\ell\neq j,m}(1-u^{(j)}_{\ell})\Big)x_{m}.

Taking absolute values and using
u^{(j)}_{\ell}\leq\lambda_{n}
gives

\displaystyle\Big(\prod_{\ell\neq j}(1-u^{(j)}_{\ell})\Big)\left|x_{j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\delta_{\{j\}}\right|+\sum_{m\neq j}u^{(j)}_{j}\Big(\prod_{\ell\neq j,m}(1-u^{(j)}_{\ell})\Big)\left|x_{m}\right|

\displaystyle\left|x_{j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left|\delta_{\{j\}}\right|}{(1-\lambda_{n})^{n-1}}+\sum_{m\neq j}\frac{u^{(j)}_{j}}{1-u^{(j)}_{m}}\left|x_{m}\right|\leq\frac{\left|\delta_{\{j\}}\right|}{(1-\lambda_{n})^{n-1}}+\beta_{n}\sum_{m\neq j}\left|x_{m}\right|.

Summing over
j
and noting
\sum_{j}\left|\delta_{\{j\}}\right|=\Delta_{1}
gives

\left\lVert\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\frac{\Delta_{1}}{(1-\lambda_{n})^{n-1}}+\beta_{n}(n-1)\left\lVert\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right\rVert_{1}.

Since
1-\beta_{n}(n-1)=\frac{n}{2n-1}
, we can absorb to obtain

\left\lVert\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\frac{1}{1-\beta_{n}(n-1)}\cdot\frac{\Delta_{1}}{(1-\lambda_{n})^{n-1}}=\frac{2n-1}{n\,(1-\lambda_{n})^{n-1}}\,\Delta_{1},

as claimed.
If
\Delta_{1}=0
then
\left\lVert\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right\rVert_{1}=0
and hence
\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{q}
.
∎

We will need a simple extremal bound for Poisson–binomial masses under our small-parameter restriction.

Lemma 4.4

(Poisson–binomial extremum under small odds)
.

Let
N\geq 1
and let
r_{1},\dots,r_{N}\in[0,1)
.
Write odds
a_{i}:=\frac{r_{i}}{1-r_{i}}
and assume
\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\leq 1
.
Let
X=\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}
with independent
X_{i}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(r_{i})
.
Then for every
m\in\{1,2,\dots,N\}
,
\mathbb{P}[X=m]
is nondecreasing in each coordinate
r_{i}
.
If moreover
0\leq r_{i}\leq\lambda\leq\frac{1}{N+1}
for all
i
, then for every
m\in\{1,\dots,N\}
,

\mathbb{P}[X=m]\leq\binom{N}{m}\lambda^{m}(1-\lambda)^{N-m}.

Proof.

Write
f_{m}:=\mathbb{P}[X=m]
.
Using the generating function factorization

\prod_{i=1}^{N}\big((1-r_{i})+r_{i}z\big)=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}(1-r_{i})\right)\prod_{i=1}^{N}(1+a_{i}z),

we have
f_{m}=f_{0}\,e_{m}(a_{1},\dots,a_{N})
, where
e_{m}
is the
m
th elementary symmetric polynomial.
For
m\geq 1
,

e_{m}(a)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\,e_{m-1}(a_{1},\dots,\widehat{a}_{i},\dots,a_{N})\leq\frac{1}{m}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\right)e_{m-1}(a),

(where
\widehat{a}_{i}
indicates that
a_{i}
is omitted)
so

\frac{f_{m}}{f_{m-1}}=\frac{e_{m}}{e_{m-1}}\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\leq 1.

Thus
f_{m-1}\geq f_{m}
for all
m\geq 1
.

Now fix
j\in[N]
and write
X^{(-j)}:=\sum_{i\neq j}X_{i}
.
Differentiating the pmf with respect to
r_{j}
gives the standard identity

\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{j}}\,\mathbb{P}[X=m]=\mathbb{P}[X^{(-j)}=m-1]-\mathbb{P}[X^{(-j)}=m].

Since removing one coordinate can only decrease
\sum_{i}a_{i}
, the law of
X^{(-j)}
still satisfies the hypothesis

\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{r_{i}}{1-r_{i}}\leq 1
.
Therefore
\mathbb{P}[X^{(-j)}=m-1]\geq\mathbb{P}[X^{(-j)}=m]
for every
m\geq 1
, and the derivative is nonnegative.
This proves coordinatewise monotonicity of
\mathbb{P}[X=m]
for
m\geq 1
.

For the final inequality, if
r_{i}\leq\lambda\leq 1/(N+1)
then

\sum_{i}\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}=\frac{N\lambda}{1-\lambda}\leq 1
, so the monotonicity applies at the endpoint vector
(\lambda,\dots,\lambda)
.
Thus
\mathbb{P}[X=m]
is maximized when all
r_{i}=\lambda
, in which case
X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(N,\lambda)
and

\mathbb{P}[X=m]=\binom{N}{m}\lambda^{m}(1-\lambda)^{N-m}
.
∎

Lemma 4.5

(Recursive bound for
\Delta_{k}
)
.

Fix
k\in\{2,3,\dots,n\}
and let
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in[0,\lambda_{n}]^{n}
.
If
\Delta_{1}=0
then
\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{q}
and hence
\Delta_{k}=0
for all
k
.
If
\Delta_{1}>0
, then

\frac{\Delta_{k}}{\Delta_{1}}\leq\frac{\beta_{n}(n-k+1)}{k}\cdot\frac{\Delta_{k-1}}{\Delta_{1}}+\binom{n-1}{k-1}\lambda_{n}^{k-1}(1-\lambda_{n})^{n-k-1}\cdot\frac{K(n)}{k},

where
K(n)
is as in Lemma
4.3
.

Proof.

Fix
S\subseteq[n]
with
|S|=k
and fix
i\in S
.
Using
P_{S}(\mathbf{y})=\frac{y_{i}}{1-y_{i}}P_{S\setminus\{i\}}(\mathbf{y})
we write

\displaystyle\delta_{S}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{i}}{1-p_{i}}P_{S\setminus\{i\}}(\mathbf{p})-\frac{q_{i}}{1-q_{i}}P_{S\setminus\{i\}}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{i}}{1-p_{i}}\big(P_{S\setminus\{i\}}(\mathbf{p})-P_{S\setminus\{i\}}(\mathbf{q})\big)+\left(\frac{p_{i}}{1-p_{i}}-\frac{q_{i}}{1-q_{i}}\right)P_{S\setminus\{i\}}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{i}}{1-p_{i}}\,\delta_{S\setminus\{i\}}+\frac{p_{i}-q_{i}}{(1-p_{i})(1-q_{i})}\,P_{S\setminus\{i\}}(\mathbf{q}).

Taking absolute values and summing over
i\in S
gives

k\left|\delta_{S}\right|\leq\sum_{i\in S}\left(\frac{p_{i}}{1-p_{i}}\left|\delta_{S\setminus\{i\}}\right|+\frac{\left|x_{i}\right|}{(1-p_{i})(1-q_{i})}P_{S\setminus\{i\}}(\mathbf{q})\right).

Summing further over all
S
with
|S|=k
yields

\displaystyle k\Delta_{k}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{|S|=k}\sum_{i\in S}\frac{p_{i}}{1-p_{i}}\left|\delta_{S\setminus\{i\}}\right|+\sum_{|S|=k}\sum_{i\in S}\frac{\left|x_{i}\right|}{(1-p_{i})(1-q_{i})}P_{S\setminus\{i\}}(\mathbf{q}).

For the first term,
\frac{p_{i}}{1-p_{i}}\leq\beta_{n}
on
[0,\lambda_{n}]
, and a multiplicity count gives

\sum_{|S|=k}\sum_{i\in S}\left|\delta_{S\setminus\{i\}}\right|=(n-k+1)\sum_{|T|=k-1}\left|\delta_{T}\right|=(n-k+1)\Delta_{k-1}.

Hence the first term is at most
\beta_{n}(n-k+1)\Delta_{k-1}
.

For the second term, re-index by
i
and write
T=S\setminus\{i\}
(so
|T|=k-1
and
i\notin T
):

\displaystyle\sum_{|S|=k}\sum_{i\in S}\frac{\left|x_{i}\right|}{(1-p_{i})(1-q_{i})}P_{S\setminus\{i\}}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\left|x_{i}\right|}{(1-p_{i})(1-q_{i})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\subseteq[n]\setminus\{i\}\\
|T|=k-1\end{subarray}}P_{T}(\mathbf{q}).

For each such
T
, the factor
(1-q_{i})
appears in
P_{T}(\mathbf{q})
(since
i\notin T
), so we can cancel it:

\frac{1}{(1-p_{i})(1-q_{i})}P_{T}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{1}{1-p_{i}}\left(\prod_{j\in T}q_{j}\right)\left(\prod_{\ell\notin T,\ \ell\neq i}(1-q_{\ell})\right).

Thus

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\subseteq[n]\setminus\{i\}\\
|T|=k-1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{(1-p_{i})(1-q_{i})}P_{T}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{1}{1-p_{i}}\,\mathbb{P}\!\left[\sum_{\ell\neq i}Z_{\ell}=k-1\right],

where
Z_{\ell}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(q_{\ell})
are independent.

Since
q_{\ell}\leq\lambda_{n}
and
\lambda_{n}\leq 1/n=1/((n-1)+1)
, Lemma
4.4
(with
N=n-1
,
\lambda=\lambda_{n}
, and
m=k-1\geq 1
) gives

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\sum_{\ell\neq i}Z_{\ell}=k-1\right]\leq\binom{n-1}{k-1}\lambda_{n}^{k-1}(1-\lambda_{n})^{n-k}.

Moreover
1-p_{i}\geq 1-\lambda_{n}
, so
\frac{1}{1-p_{i}}\leq\frac{1}{1-\lambda_{n}}
.
Therefore the entire second term is bounded by

\binom{n-1}{k-1}\lambda_{n}^{k-1}(1-\lambda_{n})^{n-k-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|x_{i}\right|.

Apply Lemma
4.3
to bound
\sum_{i}\left|x_{i}\right|\leq K(n)\Delta_{1}
.
Putting everything together,

k\Delta_{k}\leq\beta_{n}(n-k+1)\Delta_{k-1}+\binom{n-1}{k-1}\lambda_{n}^{k-1}(1-\lambda_{n})^{n-k-1}K(n)\,\Delta_{1}.

Divide by
k\Delta_{1}
(for
\Delta_{1}>0
) to obtain the claim.
∎

Define a numerical sequence
B_{k}(n)
by
B_{1}(n):=1
and, for
k\geq 2
,

(4.6)

B_{k}(n):=\frac{n-k+1}{k(2n-1)}\,B_{k-1}(n)+\binom{n-1}{k-1}\left(\frac{1}{2n-1}\right)^{k-1}\frac{2}{k}.

Corollary 4.6

(Universal slice bounds)
.

For every
k\in\{2,\dots,n\}
and every
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in[0,\lambda_{n}]^{n}
,

\Delta_{k}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})\leq B_{k}(n)\,\Delta_{1}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}).

Proof.

If
\Delta_{1}=0
then
\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{q}
by Lemma
4.3
, hence
\Delta_{k}=0
.
If
\Delta_{1}>0
, Lemma
4.5
yields the recursion (
4.6
) as an upper bound, starting from
B_{1}(n)=1
.
∎

Lemma 4.7

(Closed form for
B_{k}(n)
)
.

For every integer
k
with
2\leq k\leq n
,

B_{k}(n)=\frac{2k-1}{k(k-1)}\binom{n-2}{k-2}\,\frac{n-1}{(2n-1)^{k-1}}.

Proof.

By induction on
k
.
For
k=2
,

B_{2}(n)=\frac{3}{2}\cdot\binom{n-2}{0}\cdot\frac{n-1}{2n-1}=\frac{3(n-1)}{2(2n-1)},

matching Theorem
4.2
.
Assume the formula holds for
k-1\geq 2
.
Using (
4.6
) and
\binom{n-1}{k-1}=\frac{n-1}{k-1}\binom{n-2}{k-2}
, we compute

\displaystyle B_{k}(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{n-k+1}{k(2n-1)}\cdot\frac{2(k-1)-1}{(k-1)(k-2)}\binom{n-2}{k-3}\frac{n-1}{(2n-1)^{k-2}}+\frac{2}{k}\binom{n-1}{k-1}\frac{1}{(2n-1)^{k-1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{n-1}{k(2n-1)^{k-1}}\left[\frac{(n-k+1)(2k-3)}{(k-1)(k-2)}\binom{n-2}{k-3}+\frac{2}{k-1}\binom{n-2}{k-2}\right].

Using
\binom{n-2}{k-3}=\binom{n-2}{k-2}\cdot\frac{k-2}{n-k+1}
, the bracket becomes

\binom{n-2}{k-2}\left[\frac{2k-3}{k-1}+\frac{2}{k-1}\right]=\binom{n-2}{k-2}\cdot\frac{2k-1}{k-1}.

Substituting yields the desired formula.
∎

Theorem 4.8

(Summation identity)
.

For every
n\geq 2
,

\sum_{k=2}^{n}B_{k}(n)=\frac{n-1}{n}.

Proof.

Set
t:=\frac{1}{2n-1}
.
Using Lemma
4.7
and the change of variables
j=k-2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^{n}B_{k}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}\frac{2j+3}{(j+1)(j+2)}\binom{n-2}{j}(n-1)t^{j+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{t}{n}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(2k-1)t^{k-2},

where we used the identity

\frac{1}{(j+1)(j+2)}\binom{n-2}{j}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\binom{n}{j+2}
.

Let
A(t):=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}t^{k}=(1+t)^{n}
.
Then
A^{\prime}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}k\binom{n}{k}t^{k-1}=n(1+t)^{n-1}
.
A short computation gives

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(2k-1)t^{k-2}=\frac{2n}{t}(1+t)^{n-1}-\frac{1}{t^{2}}(1+t)^{n}.

Subtracting the
k=0
and
k=1
terms (equal to
-t^{-2}
and
nt^{-1}
) yields

\sum_{k=2}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(2k-1)t^{k-2}=\frac{2n}{t}(1+t)^{n-1}-\frac{1}{t^{2}}(1+t)^{n}+\frac{1}{t^{2}}-\frac{n}{t}.

Multiplying by
t/n
gives

\sum_{k=2}^{n}B_{k}(n)=2(1+t)^{n-1}-1-\frac{(1+t)^{n}-1}{nt}.

Finally, with
t=\frac{1}{2n-1}
we have
1+t=\frac{2n}{2n-1}
and

\frac{(1+t)^{n}-1}{nt}=\frac{2n-1}{n}\left(\left(\frac{2n}{2n-1}\right)^{n}-1\right)=2\left(\frac{2n}{2n-1}\right)^{n-1}-\frac{2n-1}{n}.

Substituting cancels the
(1+t)^{n-1}
terms and yields

\sum_{k=2}^{n}B_{k}(n)=-1+\frac{2n-1}{n}=\frac{n-1}{n}.

∎

Theorem 4.9
.

For every
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in[0,\lambda_{n}]^{n}
,

\sum_{k=2}^{n}\Delta_{k}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})\ \leq\ \frac{n-1}{n}\,\Delta_{1}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}).

Proof.

If
\Delta_{1}=0
then
\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{q}
by Lemma
4.3
, so every
\Delta_{k}=0
.
Assume
\Delta_{1}>0
.
By Corollary
4.6
,

\Delta_{k}/\Delta_{1}\leq B_{k}(n)
for
k=2,\dots,n
.
Summing over
k
and using Theorem
4.8
gives

\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\Delta_{k}}{\Delta_{1}}\leq\sum_{k=2}^{n}B_{k}(n)=\frac{n-1}{n}.

Multiplying by
\Delta_{1}
completes the proof.
∎

Combining Theorem
4.1
and Theorem
4.9
gives

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\Delta_{k}\leq\left(1+\frac{2n-1}{n}+\frac{n-1}{n}\right)\Delta_{1}=\left(4-\frac{2}{n}\right)\Delta_{1},

which immediately implies Theorem
1.2
.

Appendix A
An
\ell_{2}
bound for quasi-symmetric pairs

It is shown in

[
5
, Theorem 1.2]
that
for
symmetric
pairs satisfying
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{p}
,
we have

\operatorname{TV}(\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{p}),\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{q}))\leq\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\|_{2}
.
Here we record a simple extension to a larger class of
quasi-symmetric
pairs, with an additional factor
\sqrt{2}
.

Definition A.1

(Quasi-symmetric pairs)
.

A pair
(u,v)\in[0,1]^{2}
is
quasi-symmetric
if either
u\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\leq v
or
v\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\leq u
.
Vectors
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in[0,1]^{n}
are quasi-symmetric if each coordinate pair
(p_{i},q_{i})
is quasi-symmetric.

Theorem A.2
.

If
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in[0,1]^{n}
are quasi-symmetric, then

\operatorname{TV}(\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{p}),\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{q}))\ \leq\ \sqrt{2}\,\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\|_{2}.

Remark: the choice

\mathbf{p}=(1,1)
,

\mathbf{q}=\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)
shows that the optimal
constant must be at least
3/\sqrt{8}
.

Lemma A.3

(
[
8
]
, Disp. (2.20))
.

For any probability measures
P,Q
on a finite set
\Omega
,

\operatorname{TV}(P,Q)\leq\sqrt{1-\left(\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\sqrt{P(\omega)\,Q(\omega)}\right)^{2}}.

Lemma A.4

(
[
8
]
, p. 83)
.

Let
P=\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{p})
and
Q=\operatorname{Ber}(\mathbf{q})
on
\{0,1\}^{n}
. Then

\sum_{x\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}}\sqrt{P(x)Q(x)}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sqrt{p_{i}q_{i}}+\sqrt{(1-p_{i})(1-q_{i})}\right).

Lemma A.5
.

Let
p,q\in[0,1]
satisfy
p\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\geq q
and define

b(p,q):=\sqrt{pq}+\sqrt{(1-p)(1-q)}.

Then

1-b(p,q)^{2}\ \leq\ 2(p-q)^{2}.

Proof.

A direct expansion shows the identity

(A.1)

1-b(p,q)^{2}=\left(\sqrt{p(1-q)}-\sqrt{q(1-p)}\right)^{2}.

Let
A=p(1-q)
and
B=q(1-p)
. Then
A-B=p-q
. Using

\sqrt{A}-\sqrt{B}=\frac{A-B}{\sqrt{A}+\sqrt{B}},

and (
A.1
), we obtain

(A.2)

1-b(p,q)^{2}=\frac{(p-q)^{2}}{\left(\sqrt{p(1-q)}+\sqrt{q(1-p)}\right)^{2}}.

It remains to lower bound the denominator.
Define unit vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
:

\textup{{\bf u}}=(\sqrt{p},\sqrt{1-p}),\qquad\textup{{\bf v}}=(\sqrt{1-q},\sqrt{q}),\qquad\|\textup{{\bf u}}\|_{2}=\|\textup{{\bf v}}\|_{2}=1.

Then

\textup{{\bf u}}\cdot\textup{{\bf v}}=\sqrt{p(1-q)}+\sqrt{q(1-p)}.

Let
\theta
and
\phi
be the angles of
u
and
v
from the
x
-axis. Since
p\geq 1/2
,
we have
\theta\in[0,\pi/4]
, and since
q\leq 1/2
, we have
\phi\in[0,\pi/4]
.
Therefore
|\theta-\phi|\leq\pi/4
, so

\textup{{\bf u}}\cdot\textup{{\bf v}}=\cos(\theta-\phi)\geq\cos(\pi/4)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.

Substituting into (
A.2
) yields

1-b(p,q)^{2}\leq\frac{(p-q)^{2}}{(1/\sqrt{2})^{2}}=2(p-q)^{2},

as desired.
∎

Proof of Theorem
A.2
.

Since any pair
p_{i},q_{i}
may be simultaneously reflected about
1/2
without affecting the TV,
we may assume, for all
i
,

p_{i}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\geq q_{i}.

By Lemmas
A.3
and
A.4
,

\operatorname{TV}(P,Q)\leq\sqrt{1-\prod_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}^{2}},\qquad b_{i}:=\sqrt{p_{i}q_{i}}+\sqrt{(1-p_{i})(1-q_{i})}\in[0,1].

For numbers
y_{i}\in[0,1]
one has
1-\prod_{i}y_{i}\leq\sum_{i}(1-y_{i})
, so with
y_{i}=b_{i}^{2}
,

\operatorname{TV}(P,Q)\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-b_{i}^{2})}.

By Lemma
A.5
applied coordinatewise (recall
p_{i}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\geq q_{i}
), we have

1-b_{i}^{2}\leq 2(p_{i}-q_{i})^{2}.

Therefore

\operatorname{TV}(P,Q)\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}2(p_{i}-q_{i})^{2}}=\sqrt{2}\,\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\|_{2},

which completes the proof.
∎
