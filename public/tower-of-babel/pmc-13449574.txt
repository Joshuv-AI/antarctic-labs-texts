Large-scale structural variations induced by transposable elements promote population-specific divergence in Triticum turgidum ssp. dicoccoides
Authors: Liya Bida, Khalil Kashkush
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLarge-scale structural variations (SVs) are significant drivers of genome evolution and crop adaptation, yet they remain poorly explored in wheat due to the complexity of its large, allopolyploid genome. Wild emmer wheat (Triticum turgidum ssp. dicoccoides), the direct progenitor of domesticated wheat, serves as a vital reservoir of genetic diversity. In this study, we performed a comparative genomic analysis using high-quality assemblies of wild emmer (accession Zavitan) and bread wheat (cv. Chinese Spring) to identify and characterize large-scale SVs and their association with transposable elements (TEs).ResultsWe identified 17 large-scale genome-specific SVs, ranging in size from 1 kb to 1 Mb, many of which were associated with Gypsy and Copia retrotransposons. PCR-based genotyping of 17 loci across 68 wild emmer wheat accessions from seven geographically distinct populations validated these variations as highly polymorphic, with several showing strong population-specific distributions. Most identified SVs encompass high-confidence genes, including those encoding disease resistance kinases (e.g., LR10-like) and metabolic enzymes (e.g., anthranilate O-methyltransferase), suggesting that these structural rearrangements might be associated with functional diversity and potential environmental adaptation in the Fertile Crescent.ConclusionsOur findings highlight the significant role of TE-mediated rearrangements in generating structural diversity within the wheat genome. The identification of gene-containing SVs that are specific to certain wild emmer populations underlines the potential of wild progenitors for discovering novel alleles for wheat improvement and environmental resilience.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09173-7.Keywords: Wild emmer wheat, Structural variation, Transposable elements, Population genetics, Adaptation, Wheat improvement

Background

Wheat is one of the most important staple crops globally, providing nearly 20% of the world’s caloric intake [1]. Its significance in global food security cannot be overstated, especially as the global population continues to grow and climate change increasingly impacts agricultural productivity. Bread wheat is a relatively young domesticated allopolyploid species created from two different allopolyploidization events that occurred throughout the evolutionary history of the Triticum-Aegilops group of three diploid genomes. The first allopolyploidization event occurred about 0.8 million years ago between a male diploid donor of A subgenome, similar to Triticum Urartu, and a female donor of B subgenome related to Ae. speltoides led to the creation of the emmer wheat, which is found in nature as a wild species called wild emmer wheat, Triticum turgidum ssp. dicoccoides (2n = 4x = 28, genome composition BBAA). The second allopolyploidization event occurred around 9,000 years ago [2] between the domesticated emmer as a female genome donor and Ae. tauschii, as a male genome donor of the D subgenome, created the bread wheat Triticum aestivum (2n = 6x = 42, genome BBAADD) [3]. In the allohexaploid bread wheat genome, genes typically exist as homoeologous triads [4]. However, approximately half of the wheat gene pool consists of non-triad groups, such as dyads (genes present in only two of the three subgenomes). Because dyads are predominantly localized in the distal, recombination-active regions of chromosomes, they are highly susceptible to structural rearrangements [4, 5]. Furthermore, dyads are frequently enriched in functions related to environmental adaptation, making them ideal genetic markers for identifying the structural variations (SVs) that drive wheat evolution and improvement.

As wheat allopolyploids are relatively young, they are expected to show limited genetic variation due to the so-called “polyploidy diversity bottleneck.” This bottleneck occurs because only a few individuals from the progenitor species were involved in the allopolyploid speciation event. Furthermore, the newly formed allopolyploid quickly becomes reproductively isolated from its parental species. Additionally, there has been insufficient time for accumulating mutations [6]. Nevertheless, allopolyploid species spread faster than their diploid progenitors [7], as can be seen specifically for the bread wheat, which, soon after its formation and domestication in the South Caspian, was successfully spread worldwide. The explanation for wheat allopolyploid’s success is the mechanism of allopolyploidization. This mechanism is unique in its ability to create new species in a single step, creating two “genetic shocks.” The inter-genetic hybridization followed by whole genome duplication led to burst genomic changes, including revolutionary (occurring immediately after allopolyploidization) and evolutionary (occurring during the life of the allopolyploid species) genomic changes in wheat allopolyploids that may not be achieved at the diploid level. These changes include the creation of new traits, new intergenomic interactions, buffering of mutations, epigenetic alteration, gene expression modification, and non-random sequence deletions. Domestication favored allopolyploids in wheat, probably as it allowed for rapid adaptation to the new cultivation environment [2]. Nevertheless, thousands of years of domestication and the involvement of recurrent picking of desirable individuals with desirable traits, such as harvest qualities, led to a significant loss of genetic variation [8, 9]. The repetitive emphasis on high yields and industrial breeding for standardized characteristics has further diminished the natural resistance of domesticated wheat against biotic (e.g. diseases, pests) and abiotic (e.g. climate change) stress factors [10–12].

Climate change raises the need to investigate the progenitor of the bread wheat, the wild emmer wheat, which has a rich allelic repertoire and can be a potential donor of missing and beneficial traits to the domesticated wheat [13, 14]. Wild emmer wheat (Triticum turgidum ssp. dicoccoides) is the primary progenitor of cultivated tetraploid wheats and contributed the A and B genomes to hexaploid bread wheat [15], and the highest biodiversity of wild emmer wheat in Israel, Jordan, southern Syria, and southern Lebanon than in other distribution areas [16, 17], it was suggested that wheat was originated in the fertile crescent in the vicinity of Mt. Hermon and the catchment area of the Jordan River [18, 19]. Over the following thousands of years, wild emmer wheat was spread from its original area. Wild emmer can be found and thrive in various environments and can hold habitats with different biotic and abiotic conditions, such as soil types, temperatures, and elevation (from 200 m below the sea level in Jordan Valley to 1,600 m above sea level in Mt. Hermon in Israel). It can be found in open oak forests, evergreen dwarf shrub formations, pastures, abandoned fields, and on the edge of cultivated fields [11, 18].

Due to the climatic, topological, and geological diversity existing in Israel, many studies on the genetic diversity of wild emmer populations were done in Israel [16, 20–22]. Investigating large-scale genomic rearrangements and stability in wheat is crucial for understanding the evolution, diversity, and adaptability of this crop. Such research sheds light on complex genetic structures, including various inversions and translocations, through comparative genomic analyses of different wheat species. These studies reveal critical genomic rearrangements that may reflect the plant’s past adaptations and potential for future resilience. Grasping these genetic intricacies enables the development of wheat varieties with enhanced yields, disease resistance, and stress tolerance, while also preserving genetic diversity. This knowledge is foundational for breeding strategies designed for secure food production in the face of global challenges.

The availability of sequence drafts for wheat species, including bread wheat and wild emmer wheat, facilitated the identification and characterization of large-scale genomic rearrangements (ranging in size from 100 kb to 2 Mb), including insertions/deletions, inversions, translocations, and introgressions. Those rearrangements were associated with allopolyploidization and domestication processes [23–26]. Recent genomic studies in polyploid species, particularly bread wheat and its progenitors, have highlighted the pivotal role of large-scale chromosomal rearrangements in driving rapid adaptive evolution. While single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) have historically been the focus of diversity studies, structural variations (SVs), such as large indels and translocations, are now recognized as major contributors to phenotypic plasticity [27]. These rearrangements can directly impact gene dosage, alter regulatory landscapes, and facilitate the clustering of adaptive gene complexes [28]. In wheat, these changes are often mediated by transposable element activity, specifically within the distal, gene-poor regions of the subgenomes where recombination rates are higher, allowing for the emergence of novel traits related to environmental resilience [29]. Recent studies, such as the wheat pangenome project [30] and research on wild emmer environmental adaptation [31], have demonstrated that populations exhibit significant structural variation associated with local adaptation to varying precipitation and temperature gradients. Globally, this aligns with findings in other polyploid complexes, where “structural pangenome’’ are revealing that a single reference genome fails to capture the functional diversity necessary for modern crop improvement [32].

In this study, we have investigated whether large-scale structural variations (SVs) play a role in genetic diversity in 68 accessions of wild emmer wheat, collected from seven different geographically isolated sites, by studying 17 genome-specific large-scale genomic rearrangements. To this end, using PCR screening, we observed polymorphism among wild emmer wheat accessions; in some cases, population-specific patterns were observed. The potential role of these SVs in the adaptation of wild emmer wheat is discussed.

Results

Identification and molecular characterization of large-scale SVs

To elucidate the genetic diversity and evolutionary dynamics between wild emmer and bread wheat, and within wild emmer wheat accessions, 17 cases of large-scale sequence rearrangements were identified and analyzed (Table 1). These were categorized into three discovery modules: (1) six cases (5B1, 5B2, 5B3, 5B5, 3B2, 3B4) previously reported by our group [5]; (2) nine cases (5A11, 5A12, 5A13, 3A14, 3A16, 2A23, 2A24, 7A28, 7A30) discovered using dyad markers; and (3) two cases (5B18, 5B19) identified through a comparative analysis of subgenomes. Locus nomenclature is based on the chromosome number and genome type (e.g., 5B2 refers to a rearrangement on chromosome 5 of subgenome BB). The identification strategy utilized 1,738 gene dyads [4] as high-resolution markers to pinpoint rearrangements within the A-subgenome. In this analysis, the B-subgenome member of the dyad served as a primary anchor. This choice was strategic because the B-subgenome of bread wheat is documented as having the lowest sequence order conservation and organization compared to the A and D subgenomes [33], providing a highly sensitive background for identifying genomic regions prone to reorganization. By focusing on these regions and extending the search to 0.5 Mbp on each side of the marker genes, we were able to identify SVs that standard SNP-based diversity assessments might otherwise miss.

The selection of the 9 SVs from the initial pool of 1,738 gene dyads was the result of a rigorous, multi-stage filtering process designed to isolate high-confidence, large-scale structural variations (> 1 kb) with potential functional impact. Initial candidates were identified by comparing inter-genic distances between dyad members in the Zavitan and CS42 assemblies, followed by a manual curation via dot-plot analysis to confirm genuine biological polymorphisms. A primary challenge in this selection was the inherent complexity of the wheat genome, specifically regarding the identification and precise characterization of rearrangement breakpoints within highly repetitive regions. The presence of dense transposable element (TE) clusters often precluded the design of stable, specific PCR primers, which was a mandatory requirement for our population-level screening. Consequently, the objective of this study was to establish a robust set of high-confidence cases that could be reliably validated. These selected loci served as precise molecular markers to investigate the distribution and evolutionary dynamics of SVs across 68 accessions from seven diverse wild emmer wheat populations, ensuring that the results were based on verifiable molecular evidence rather than bioinformatic artifacts. To polarize the evolutionary direction of these SVs, we utilized the ancestral state as a reference to distinguish lineage-specific deletions in bread wheat from potential recent TE-mediated insertions or introgressions.

To ensure the accuracy of the identified SVs, we performed a comprehensive cross-validation of all 17 loci against the most recent genome releases, including Zavitan WEWSeq v2.0 and IWGSC RefSeq v2.1. While updated genome assemblies have become available since the initiation of this study, we confirmed the reliability of the WEWSeq v.1.0 and IWGSC v.1.1 frameworks by cross-checking all 17 target loci against the latest versions. No significant discrepancies in gene models or structural boundaries were observed.

Genomic signatures of TE-mediated rearrangements and gene interruptions

Several identified SVs were characterized by high TE density, suggesting they acted as triggers for larger-scale genomic rearrangements (Table 1). At locus 5A12, dot plot comparison revealed a 31.5kbp insertion in bread wheat enriched with Copia superfamily TEs (85%) (Fig. S1a). This insertion interrupts TRIDC5AG056530 in wild emmer, a gene sharing > 80% identity with the leaf rust resistance kinase LR10-like (Fig. 1a). This gene is vital for defense against Puccinia triticina, which poses a significant threat to global wheat yields [34]. At locus 5A11, a 49kbp segment in wild emmer was replaced by a 7kbp Gypsy-rich segment in bread wheat. The wild emmer segment contains TRIDC5AG053440, a wall-associated receptor kinase 5-like (WAK5) gene. PCR screening showed this allele is exclusive to northern populations (Turkey and Syria), suggesting these accessions harbor ancestral lineages distinct from the southern populations. (Fig. S1b, Fig. S2a).

Subgenome replacements were similarly uncovered at locus 5B18 (Fig. S1j) involving the replacement of a ~ 918.6 kb wild emmer segment with a ~ 199.1 kb bread wheat segment (Fig. 1b) and locus 5B19 (Fig. S1k), which featured an ~ 860 kb wild emmer segment (including disease resistance protein RGA4/RGA5-like) replaced with an ~ 864 kb bread wheat segment containing orthologous resistance genes and SAUR36 auxin-responsive protein isoforms (Fig. S2f).

Analysis of seven loci (5A13, 3A14, 3A16, 2A23, 2A24, 7A28, and 7A30) revealed significant sequence loss in bread wheat relative to wild emmer, frequently driven by microhomology- mediated repair (MMEJ). Loci 5A13 (Fig. S1c) and 5B3 exhibited signatures typical of double-strand break (DSB) repair via MMEJ. At 5A13, a 13.5 kb deletion in bread wheat is flanked by a 3 bp ‘AGA’ motif (Fig. S2b), whereas wild emmer retains both microhomology copies. Similar mechanisms using mononucleotide repeats were observed at locus 5B3 [23].

The identified rearrangements represent lineage-specific deletions in bread wheat, where large ancestral genomic segments found in wild emmer (accession Zavitan) are absent in the modern bread wheat genome (cv. Chinese Spring). These deletions resulted in the significant loss of multiple high-confidence (HC) protein-coding genes in bread wheat, affecting various biological pathways. Specifically, the deletion at locus 3A14 (Figs. S1d, S2C) led to the loss of EXECUTER 2 and HOX15-like genes, while the 41kbp deletion at locus 3A16 (Figs. S1e, Fig. 1c) removed genes involved in secondary metabolism and iron acquisition (anthranilate O-methyltransferase 3-like and 2’-deoxymugineic-acid 2’-dioxygenase-like). Other functional losses include the senescence-related gene SAG39-like at locus 2A23 (Figs. S1f, S2d), a tRNA-Gly gene at locus 2A24 (Fig. S1g), and the cellular trafficking gene vacuolar protein sorting-associated protein 13b-like at locus 7A30 (Figs. S1i, S2e). In contrast, locus 7A28 (Fig. S1h) represented a sequence deletion where no high-confidence protein-coding genes were identified within the missing segment.

Validation and population-scale distribution of SVs

In this study, we aimed to investigate the fundamental occurrence and genomic behavior of large-scale sequence variations (SVs) within the wild emmer genome. The primary objective was to determine if such massive rearrangements represent a recurring evolutionary phenomenon in this species; therefore, our experimental design prioritized high-resolution mechanistic validation over an exhaustive germplasm survey. While extensive diversity panels of up to 263 accessions have been utilized for global genomic mapping [35], our focus on the biological “proof-of-concept” for SVs was best served by a strategically selected panel of 68 accessions. This selection was designed to prioritize a complete latitudinal and eco-climatological transect across seven primary populations in the Fertile Crescent, providing sufficient biological replication to assess population-specific divergence and the evolutionary impact of these SVs without the requirement of testing all globally available accessions. To assess the distribution of the 17 SVs, we screened the 68 wild emmer accessions across the seven populations (Mt. Hermon, Amiad, Tabgha, Mt. Amasa, Turkey, Syria, and Lebanon) using PCR primers designed from indel breakpoints Supplemental Table 1). All PCR reactions were also performed in bread wheat and wild emmer accession Zavitan for validation.

Several distinct geographic distribution and polymorphism patterns were detected across the screening panel (Table 2). Monomorphic and fixed SVs suggested an early fixation event in the wild emmer lineage; specifically, loci 5A13 (Fig. 2), 2A24, and 3B2 (Fig. S2m) were fixed across all tested accessions. Locus 7A28 (Fig. S3g1, S3g2) was monomorphic in Mt. Hermon, Tabgha, and Amiad, while 3B4 (Fig. S3l), 5B3 (Figs. S3k1 and S3k2), and 5B1 (Fig. S3i) showed high degrees of fixation across populations. In contrast, clear population- specific variants highlighted significant structural divergence. For instance, the bread wheat-like allele at locus 5A11 was exclusive to northern populations (Turkey and Syria), suggesting these accessions harbor ancestral lineages distinct from the southern populations (Fig. 3). Complementary southern-specific patterns were observed at locus 5B19 (Fig. S3e1, S3e2) and locus 5B5 (Fig. S3n1), which were restricted to the Amiad and Tabgha populations. Locus 5A12 proved widespread across populations (Figs. S3a1 and S3a2), where wild emmer accessions lacking the wild emmer allele consistently carried the bread wheat allele (Fig. S3a2), a trend shared by loci 3A14 (Figs. S3b1 and S3b2) and 3A16 (Figs. S3c1 and S3c2). Structural heterogeneity was also verified; locus 7A30 (Figs. S3h1 and S3h2) yielded smaller PCR products in some accessions due to the loss of a Helitron TE, whereas locus 5B5 (Fig. S3n2) displayed higher molecular weight bands caused by a Harbinger family TE insertion. Finally, extensive allelic diversity was captured at loci 5B18 (Figs. S3d1 and S3d2) and 5B2 (Fig. S3j), where multiple accessions concurrently carried both wild emmer and bread wheat alleles, a pattern replicated in loci 2A23 (Figs. S3f1 and S3f2) and 3B2 (Fig. S2m).

To evaluate the population structure and geographic distribution of the identified SVs systematically, a frequency heatmap was generated across the seven Fertile Crescent populations (Fig. 4). The analysis revealed distinct geographic partitioning, with a clear clustering of alleles in the northern (Turkey and Syria) versus southern (Israel) populations. As shown in the geographic inset (Fig. 4, top right), these sampling sites represent a broad latitudinal transect. For instance, locus 5A11 showed complete fixation in the northern accessions but was entirely absent in the southern populations, while locus 5B19 displayed the exact opposite pattern. This sharp differentiation in SV allele frequencies provides evidence for limited gene flow and suggests that these large-scale rearrangements may be associated with microclimatic adaptations across the Fertile Crescent. Formal statistical testing confirmed that the observed geographic partitioning is highly significant. For instance, the reciprocal fixation of alleles at loci 5A11 (exclusive to the north) and 5B19 (exclusive to the south) yielded a p-value < 0.001, providing a robust statistical basis for the limited gene flow and population-specific divergence described.

Discussion

In this study, we conducted a detailed comparative genomic analysis to explore large-scale genomic rearrangements between wild emmer wheat and bread wheat. Utilizing dyads as genomic markers, we identified significant genomic differences between these two species. We addressed these and previously identified SVs from our lab and screened them across seven isolated wild emmer wheat populations in the Fertile Crescent. Four of these populations are from Israel (Mt. Hermon, Amiad, Tabgha, and Mt. Amasa), in addition to accessions collected from Syria, Turkey, and Lebanon. The results of this study indicate significant structural differences that may be associated with phenotypic diversity and adaptation in these species.

To navigate the repetitive complexity of the allopolyploid genome, we utilized 1,738 gene dyads [4] as anchoring points. This identification logic focused on high-resolution structural differences (> 1 kb) that standard SNP-based methods often overlook, providing a distinct advantage over previous studies, such as those utilizing the Fatima Gypsy LTR-retrotransposon [23], by precisely pinpointing rearrangements that alter gene architecture. A critical concern in such studies is the reliability of reference assemblies. While updated pangenomic resources have emerged, we proactively cross-checked all 17 target loci against the WEWSeq v.1.0 and IWGSC v.1.1 frameworks, confirming their stability and structural integrity. PCR validation using primers designed from indel breakpoints allowed us to screen a diverse panel of wild emmer wheat accessions. This analysis demonstrates that these SVs represent genuine biological polymorphisms rather than bioinformatic artifacts [23].

Mechanistic drivers of genomic plasticity: TEs and MMEJ

Our analysis indicates that transposable elements, particularly the Gypsy and Copia superfamilies, are heavily enriched at SV breakpoints, acting as the primary architects of genomic diversity necessary for wild emmer to thrive in diverse ecological niches. The frequent association between TEs and SVs supports the model of TE-mediated unequal crossing over and transposition-linked deletions as fundamental drivers of wheat genome evolution [36]. Beyond TEs, the identification of microhomology signatures, specifically 3 bp ‘AGA’ motifs, indicates that Microhomology-Mediated End Joining (MMEJ) serves as a critical secondary repair pathway. In an allopolyploid context, the presence of multiple homoeologous genomes provides a ‘buffering’ mechanism. This ‘genetic shock’ allows the A-subgenome to tolerate significant structural turnover while the homoeologous partner maintains essential functional stability [33]. Furthermore, as wild emmer is predominantly self-pollinating, the reported polymorphism reflects fixed homozygous states among individuals in a population rather than unstable heterozygous states, allowing these variants to contribute to long-term evolutionary resilience [37].

Population differentiation and geographic structuring

The distribution of SV alleles reveals a clear geographic structure across the Fertile Crescent, pointing toward limited gene flow and local adaptation. Our data move beyond descriptive PCR statistics to show distinct partitioning of alleles. The geographic partitioning of structural diversity is prominently illustrated by the distribution of specific alleles. For instance, the bread wheat-like allele at locus 5A11 was found to be exclusive to northern populations (Turkey and Syria), suggesting these accessions harbor ancestral lineages distinct from those in the southern Levant (Fig. 3). In contrast, other cases displayed complementary population-specific patterns restricted to the southern regions. Specifically, the wild emmer alleles at locus 5B19 (Fig. S3e1, S3e2) and locus 5B5 (Fig. S3n1) were found only in the Amiad and Tabgha populations. These southern-specific segments further emphasize the localized nature of structural variation and its potential role in adaptation to specific microclimates within the Fertile Crescent. Locus 5A13 stands out as monomorphic across all examined wild emmer accessions but distinct from bread wheat, suggesting a fixation event triggered during early allopolyploidization that conferred a foundational selective advantage. These results indicate that structural diversity might be associated with geographic isolation and microclimatic pressures.

Functional implications: biotic stress resistance and breeding potential

The SVs identified are not merely genomic curiosities; the potential genes and regulatory sequences they contain may be associated with ecological resilience. Our analysis highlighted high-value functional genes, including LR10-like kinases (TRIDC5AG056530), Wall-Associated Receptor Kinase 5 (TRIDC5AG053440), and anthranilate O-methyltransferase. These genes are involved in biotic stress resistance, specifically against Puccinia species and the necrotrophic fungus Rhizoctonia cerealis, and secondary metabolism [34, 38, 39]. Additionally, the inclusion of stress-signaling proteins from the HOX family (TRIDC3AG003030) and vacuolar protein sorting-associated proteins (TRIDC7AG044240) highlights the complex nature of cellular responses to stress [40, 41]. The loss or interruption of these ancestral alleles in modern bread wheat signifies a functional erosion caused by the domestication bottleneck [13, 14]. By characterizing these population-specific structural signatures, this study offers a targeted roadmap for breeders to recapture ancestral genetic diversity and restore environmental resilience in contemporary wheat cultivars.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this work provides a high-resolution map of large-scale SVs in wild emmer wheat and highlights a possible association between structural diversity, TE activity, and geographic population structuring. By validating these loci across diverse populations, we have identified a suite of genomic targets that can be integrated into future wheat breeding programs aimed at recapturing the genetic diversity and adaptive potential of wheat’s wild ancestors.

Methods

Plant material

In this study, seeds of 68 wild emmer wheat accessions were grown in a greenhouse at Ben Gurion University of the Negev under common garden conditions. The wild emmer wheat accessions were obtained from four geographically well-defined populations: (1) Mt. Hermon, Israel, 8 accessions, (2) Amiad, Israel, 10 accessions, (3) Tabgha, Israel, 10 accessions, (4) Mt. Amasa, Israel 10 accessions. In addition, seeds were collected from three regional representative collections from (1) Syria, 10 accessions, (2) Lebanon, 10 accessions, and (3) Turkey, 10 accessions. The Syrian, Lebanese, and Turkish accessions were provided by Prof. Hakan Ozkan (Cukurova University) to represent the wider genetic diversity across the northern distribution range of the Fertile Crescent. While the Israeli populations represent specific localized micro-habitats, the regional sets from Syria, Lebanon, and Turkey provide a broader geographical context for identifying SVs that may be absent in the southern Levant. The plant material used in this study was collected in accordance with local and international guidelines. Detailed geographical background data, including latitude, longitude, altitude, and mean annual precipitation for each collection site, are provided in Supplemental Table 1. For details on the eco-geographical conditions of the Israeli populations, see Table S1 in our previous publication [42]. Genomic DNA was extracted from leaves four weeks post-germination using the Geneaid Kit (Geneaid Biotech Ltd., New Taipei City, Taiwan).

The taxonomic classification and nomenclature adopted in this study follow the system where wild emmer wheat is classified as Triticum turgidum ssp. dicoccoides (2n = 4x = 28, genome BBAA) and bread wheat as Triticum aestivum (2n = 6x = 42, genome BBAADD). This framework is consistent with the genomic assemblies utilized for the comparative analysis, specifically the WEWSeq v1.0 for wild emmer and IWGSC RefSeq v1.0 for bread wheat.

Computer-assisted analysis

Genomic databases

The genome drafts of two Triticum species were used in this study: (1) Wild emmer WEWSeq v1.0 assembly, a whole genome draft of emmer wheat, based on Zavitan accession that was sequenced using whole-genome shotgun sequencing, covering 10.1-Gbp of the genome (87.5%) [43] with contigs and scaffold with N50 of 57 kb and 7 Mb respectively. (2) Bread wheat IWGSC RefSeq v1.0 assembly, based on T. aestivum Chinese Spring accession covering 14.5-Gbp of the genome (97%) [43] with contigs and scaffold with N50 of 52 kb and 7 Mb, respectively, generated by the International Wheat Genome Sequencing Consortium (IWGSC).

Identification and characterization of large-scale sequence variations in wild emmer wheat vs. bread wheat

Large-scale SVs in bread wheat vs. wild emmer were identified and characterized in a previous study in our lab (see below). In addition, in this study, we utilized publicly accessible databases to identify additional large-scale SVs to have a larger set of SVs cases for PCR screening in wild emmer populations. Here, we used two strategies to identify SVs:

Dyads as genetic markers for large-scale sequence rearrangements

To identify potential large-scale sequence rearrangements between bread wheat and wild emmer wheat, orthologous gene sets were retrieved from publicly available databases [4] and Ensembl Plants BioMart (http://plants.ensembl.org/biomart/martview). The analysis focused on gene dyads conserved between the B and D subgenomes of bread wheat. Groups were strictly filtered to exclude any genes with additional homeolog copies. Orthologs for the remaining dyads were then identified in the A and B subgenomes of wild emmer wheat. For candidate regions in the A subgenome, flanking sequences were aligned to the bread wheat A-genome reference to detect inconsistencies in synteny. To further characterize these regions, a ‘chromosome walking’ approach was implemented, extending the analysis up to 0.5 Mbp upstream and downstream of each marker gene to examine the surrounding genomic landscape. All sequence alignments were performed using BLASTn with an E-value threshold of 1e− 10.

Identification of breakpoints and characterization of large-scale SVs

Structural variations were identified through dot plot alignments of orthologous genomic loci using UGENE software (v.49.1), with a minimum repeat length of 100 bp and ≥ 95% identity. Breakpoints were defined as the junctions between high-similarity regions (≥ 95% identity) and low-similarity regions. Locus lengths were calculated as the distance between the 5’ and 3’ breakpoints. Sequence alignments were performed using BLAST+ (v.2.9.0) with an E-value threshold of 10–100.

For loci where orthology was initially unclear, a chromosome walking approach was utilized by aligning extended flanking sequences from wild emmer to the bread wheat genome. Characterization of identified SVs involved gene and transposable element (TE) annotation using the EnsemblPlants genome browser (WEWSeq v.1.0 and IWGSC v.1.1) and the CENSOR software tool. Protein-level validation was conducted via NCBI BLASTx. Primary reference frameworks included the accession Zavitan (WEWSeq v.1.0) and cv. Chinese Spring (IWGSC v.1.1) assemblies.

PCR analysis: validation of identified SVs and scanning of SVs within wild emmer populations

To validate and scan the identified genomic rearrangements in wild emmer and bread wheat, several steps were undertaken. Primers were designed using the online tool PRIMER3 version 4.1.0, based on the flanking sequences of identified breakpoints for each rearrangement (see Supplemental Table 2 for primer sequences). These primers targeted specific loci in bread wheat accession (Chinese spring 42, CS42) and/or wild emmer accession (Zavitan, Z) to differentiate their genomic DNA amplification through site-specific PCR analysis based on the newly discovered SVs. Following the validation of existing SVs between Zavitan and Chinese Spring 42, these SVs were screened across seven different populations of wild emmer to assess the distribution of SVs within and between populations. This additional step was crucial to determine whether each SV was unique or specific to some populations, or broadly distributed across them, meaning non-specific, thereby providing insights into the evolutionary dynamics and genetic diversity among these populations. The reaction mixture for each PCR included 10 µl PCRBIO HS Taq Mix Red, 7 µl ultrapure water, 1 µl forward primer (10µM), 1 µl reverse primer (10µM), and 1 µl of genomic DNA template (20 ng/µl), with the conditions set at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, the calculated annealing temperature for 15 s, and 72 °C for 15 s. The PCR products were then visualized using 1% agarose gel. Figures were prepared with GIMP (version 2.10.32; https://www.gimp.org/), and Microsoft PowerPoint (www.microsoft.com).

Statistical analysis

To evaluate the significance of population-specific divergence, allele frequency distributions between the northern (Turkey, Syria, Lebanon) and southern (Israel) population groups were compared using Fisher’s exact tests. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Additionally, the association between geographic distance and structural diversity was assessed to confirm the observed partitioning.
