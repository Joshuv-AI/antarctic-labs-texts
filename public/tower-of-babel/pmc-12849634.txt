External validation of the updated international IgA nephropathy prediction tool in a Chinese population
Authors: Xinru Du, Jing Zhou, Mingqi Ma, Hongyu Yu, Jia Lv, Xuehong Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe updated post-biopsy International IgA Nephropathy (IgAN) Prediction Tool (IIgAN-PT) requires validation in diverse clinical settings. We evaluated its performance in a Chinese cohort characterized by severe pathology and aggressive immunosuppression therapy.MethodsPatients with biopsy-confirmed IgAN from two medical centers in China were included. We calculated the predicted risk for each patient. The primary outcome was the first occurrence of end-stage kidney disease(ESKD, eGFR < 15 ml/min/1.73 m2, dialysis or transplantation) or a permanent decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate to less than 50% of the baseline time value at one year after kidney biopsy. Model performance was assessed by discrimination, calibration, and reclassification.ResultsIn our cohort, 14.1% of patients experienced the primary outcome. The updated post-biopsy IIgAN-PT did not demonstrate significantly superior discriminatory ability as compared to the original biopsy IIgAN-PT. Kaplan–Meier curves for risk subgroups showed poor separation in low- and intermediate-risk groups but marked separation in the high-risk group. However, the calibration performance of the post-biopsy IIgAN-PT improved. The updated IIgAN-PT significantly improved the Net Reclassification Index(NRI) compared to the original IIgAN-PT, but showed limited improvement in Integrated Discrimination Improvement(IDI) for clinical risk reclassification. Subgroup analysis indicated that immunosuppression therapy significantly reduced the model's discriminatory ability.ConclusionIn this external validation, the updated post-biopsy IIgAN-PT demonstrated improved calibration over the original IIgAN-PT for risk assessment at 1-year post-biopsy, leading to more accurate absolute risk estimates. However, due to the limitations of the updated IIgAN-PT in distinguishing between low-risk and intermediate-risk patient groups, its application value for refined risk stratification in clinical decision-making is constrained.Registry: Chinese Clinical Trial Registry, TRN: ChiCTR2400093550, Registration date: 06 December 2024.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03723-9.

Introduction

IgA nephropathy (IgAN) is the most prevalent form of glomerulonephritis and the most common cause of end-stage kidney disease in Asian patients [1]. The age of onset of IgA nephropathy is concentrated in the 20 and 40 s [2]. The risk of progression to end-stage kidney disease varies widely. Risk stratification of patients is therefore essential.

The 2024 Kidney Disease Improving Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines recommend the International IgAN Prediction Tool (IIgAN-PT) as the preferred method for predicting the risk of a 50% decrease in eGFR at kidney biopsy or of kidney failure as the method of choice [3]. This prediction tool has two versions: one for use at the time of biopsy and another updated in 2022 for use up to two years post-biopsy [4].

A recent external validation study by Lu et al. in a large Chinese population confirmed the model's superior performance [5]. However, its discriminatory ability and calibration in regional cohorts with more pronounced pathological damage and more aggressive treatment remain unclear. Therefore, this study aims to externally validate the updated IIgAN-PT in a contemporary multicenter cohort from Northeast China exhibiting these characteristics, evaluating the model's applicability and limitations within this specific clinical setting.

Methods

Study population

Clinical data were collected from 388 patients with biopsy-proven IgAN from two tertiary hospitals in northeastern China from January 2013 to December 2021. Exclusion criteria include: biopsies with fewer than 8 glomeruli per section, absence of MEST score (mesangial proliferation M, endothelial proliferation E, segmental sclerosis S, interstitial fibrosis/tubular atrophy T), age under 18 years, concomitant systemic diseases, progression to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) within the first year post-biopsy, follow-up of less than 1 year, and absence of at least one eGFR measurement both before and after the 1-year post-biopsy milestone to ensure reliable baseline and longitudinal eGFR data. Finally, 388 patients were included in the cohort. The project was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Jilin University (2,024,058) and the Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Jilin University (2,024,645), and the committee exempted patients from written informed consent because all data were anonymized and retrospective. The study conformed to the standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and was registered at https://www.chictr.org.cn (ChiCTR2400093550).

Variable definitions

The definition and collection of all predictor variables for this external validation cohort remained consistent with the original and updated IIgAN-PT derivation cohort. Predictors of the original with IIgAN-PT model included age at biopsy, eGFR (calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration, Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration, CKD-EPI, formula [6]), mean arterial pressure (MAP, calculated as 1/3 × systolic + 2/3 × diastolic blood pressure), 24-h urine protein quantification, race/ ethnicity (race was categorized as Chinese, white, Japanese, or other), Oxford Pathology Staging Score, and the use or absence of renin–angiotensin–aldosterone receptor system (RAAS) blockers and immunosuppressants before the biopsy. Kidney pathology biopsy results were independently scored by two kidney pathologists. Both the original and updated IIGAN-PT comprise two models, which differ only in whether they include the race/ethnicity predictor variable. Predictor variables for the updated IIgAN-PT were redefined based on a baseline time of 1 year after biopsy, age, prior use of immunosuppressants, or RAAS blockers were determined at baseline. The primary outcome was the first occurrence of ESKD (eGFR < 15 ml/min/1.73 m2, dialysis, or transplantation) or a permanent decline in eGFR to 50% of the value at the lower 1-year baseline time.

Calculation of predicted risk and delineation of risk subgroups

We calculated linear predictors and predicted probabilities of the primary outcome for each patient using the original prediction tool given by Barbour et al. [4, 7] and the model equations provided by the post-biopsy prediction tool. Based on the percentile of linear predictors derived from the prediction model, we categorized IgAN patients into four different risk subgroups using the same methodology as in the study of the post-biopsy prediction tool: < 16th percentile, lowest risk; 16th–50th percentile, low risk; 50th–84th percentile, intermediate risk; and > 84th percentile, high risk.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1). Continuous variables are presented as median with interquartile range, and categorical variables as numbers and percentages. Model performance was evaluated in terms of discrimination, calibration, and reclassification.

Discrimination: The model's ability to distinguish between patients with and without the primary outcome was quantified using Harrell's C statistic. The C statistic and its 95% confidence interval were estimated via bootstrap resampling with 1,000 repetitions to ensure robust interval estimates. In addition, the difference in C statistics between the updated and original models was calculated, with its 95% confidence interval also derived from 1,000 bootstrap samples. Discrimination was further assessed visually using Kaplan–Meier survival curves across pre-defined risk subgroups, and the discriminatory power between subgroups was quantified by calculating hazard ratios with the lowest-risk group as the reference.

Calibration: The agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes was assessed. Calibration curves were plotted by grouping patients into deciles based on their linear predictor. For each decile, the mean predicted risk was plotted against the observed risk, estimated at 4 years using the Kaplan–Meier method. The overall calibration was summarized by the calibration slope, where a value of 1 indicates perfect calibration. To further evaluate model calibration, we employed risk stratification to plot comparisons between model-predicted risk values and observed risk values, both with and without the race/ethnicity variable.

Clinical risk reclassification: assessment and analysis using NRI and IDI. For continuous NRI and IDI, a value greater than 0 indicates improvement, a value less than 0 is negative improvement, and a value equal to 0 indicates no improvement. 95% confidence intervals for NRI and IDI that do not contain 0 can be considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics and outcomes

The flowchart of patient enrollment is shown in Fig. 1, and their clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1. Discrimination

Discrimination was assessed using the C statistic and Kaplan–Meier risk-stratified survival curves. The model's ability to rank patients by risk (discrimination) showed limited overall improvement. Although the C statistics numerically increased compared to the original IIgAN-PT (0.82 vs. 0.85 for the race/ethnicity model and 0.83 vs. 0.84 for the without race/ethnicity), the confidence intervals for the change in the C statistic contained 0, indicating no statistically significant improvement, as shown in Table 3. This indicates that the original IIgAN-PT and the updated IIgAN-PT performed similarly in predicting the occurrence of the outcome event. Risk subgroups were also analyzed, and as shown in Fig. 3, regardless of whether the original or updated model was used, the survival curves for the lowest-risk, low-risk, and intermediate-risk groups showed significant overlap, with only the high-risk group demonstrating clear separation. This indicates the model's limited ability to distinguish between low- and intermediate-risk patients. Calculating the risk ratios for each subgroup for both the original and updated IIgAN-PT, as shown in Tables 4 and 5, with the lowest risk group as the reference, showed that only the risk ratio for the high-risk group was the largest and most significant. This indicates that the prediction tool excels only in distinguishing the high-risk group from others, while failing to adequately differentiate between low- and intermediate-risk groups. Reclassification

Reclassification using the NRI and IDI showed limited improvement in predicting 4-year risk reclassification with the updated post-biopsy race/ethnicity model when compared with the original race/ethnicity model. The NRI and IDI were 0.30 (95% CI, 0.04–0.53) and 0.05 (95% CI, − 0.01–0.12), respectively, and there was no significant difference in the post-biopsy without the race/ethnicity model when compared with the original model. The NRI and IDI were 0.11 (95% CI, − 0.14–0.40) and 0.02 (95% CI, − 0.04–0.08), respectively, as shown in Table 3.

Calibration

Figure 4 shows a comparison of observed risk and predicted risk of reaching the endpoint event 4 years after the 1-year baseline time of the biopsy, with the dashed line indicating perfect calibration. The results show that for the without race/ethnicity model, the 4-year risk calibration of the updated IIgAN-PT was significantly improved, and for the race/ethnicity model, although the calibration plot error line of the updated IIgAN-PT was larger, it had a more even distribution of data points, had better calibration performance overall, and had significantly improved accuracy in predicting extreme values compared with the original IIgAN-PT. Meanwhile, we calculated the calibration slopes, as shown in Table 2, the calibration slopes of the 2 models of the original IIgAN-PT were 1.28 and 1.38, respectively, and the calibration slopes of the two models of the updated IIgAN-PT were 0.95 and 1.02, which were much closer to 1, suggesting that the updated IIgAN-PT demonstrated better calibration. Model performance in the immunosuppressant subgroup

Subgroup analysis based on immunosuppression therapy revealed its profound impact on post-biopsy models performance. Regarding discrimination, the models exhibited excellent performance in the non-immunosuppression cohort (C-index ≥ 0.90), which was substantially attenuated in the immunosuppression cohort (C-index 0.79) (Supplementary Table 6). In both cohorts, discrimination among low- and intermediate-risk groups remained limited, with clear separation achieved only for the high-risk group; this separation was more pronounced in the non-immunosuppression cohort (Supplementary Fig. 6).

A notable dissociation was observed in model calibration. The non-immunosuppression cohort showed a superior overall calibration slope, yet its subgroup-level estimates were variable. Conversely, the immunosuppression cohort, despite a suboptimal overall slope indicating systematic overestimation, demonstrated near-perfect agreement between predicted and observed events within each risk subgroup (Supplementary Table 6, Supplementary Figs. 7–8). This indicates that treatment introduced a uniform shift in absolute risk without disrupting the model's internal risk stratification.

Discussion

In 2022, the post-biopsy IIgAN-PT model (based on a 1-year baseline after kidney biopsy) was created following updates to the original IIgAN-PT. The updated IIgAN-PT has better calibration as well as higher discrimination. When the post-biopsy IIgAN-PT models were applied without modification at a landmark time point 2 years after biopsy, its predictive performance also surpasses that of the original IIgAN-PT. Therefore, the updated IIgAN-PT can forecast long-term kidney outcomes within 1 to 2 years following biopsy.

As emphasized by Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD), prognostic models need to be demonstrated for their utility before they can be applied in clinical practice[8]. While a recent validation in a Chinese cohort reported excellent performance [5], our study provides a critical and complementary perspective by evaluating the model in a distinct, contemporary Northeast Chinese cohort characterized by a high prevalence of severe pathological lesions (M1: 93.5%; T1-2: 48.9%) and aggressive immunosuppression therapy (55.4%).

We found that the updated model demonstrated improved calibration performance. This was evidenced by calibration slopes closer to 1 and better agreement between predicted and observed risks across most risk subgroups (Fig. 4, Table 2). But its discriminative capability showed limited improvement. The C statistic did not significantly increase, and the survival curves for the lowest, low, and intermediate-risk groups overlapped considerably, with significant separation achieved only for the high-risk group (Fig. 3, Tables 3 and 4). This indicates that the post-biopsy IIgAN-PT’s ability to rank patients according to their risk—that is, to tell who will progress faster than whom—is not substantially better than the original version, particularly within the intermediate- and low-risk spectrum.

We analyzed the reasons for the separation between model calibration and discrimination capability, which include the following aspects: First, our cohort exhibited significantly higher prevalence of M1 (93.5%) and T lesions (48.9%) in the Oxford Pathology Score. This severe, near-universal pathological damage may have undermined the model's ability to stratify risk using pathological variables. Second, further analysis of the immunosuppression subgroup revealed that model performance may be significantly influenced by treatment modality: in the cohort of patients not receiving immunosuppression therapy (whose disease progression more closely aligns with the “natural course” underlying model training), the post-biopsy IIgAN-PT demonstrated excellent discriminatory ability (C statistic ≈0.90). However, within the immunosuppression therapy group, discriminatory ability declined markedly (C statistic 0.79). This phenomenon may occur because patients receiving immunosuppression often exhibit poorer baseline risk characteristics. Immunosuppression therapy can substantially reduce proteinuria, potentially altering the disease trajectory that the model was designed to predict. Specifically, it may “flatten” the risk gradient among intermediate-risk patients, thereby diminishing the model's discriminatory power. This highlights a critical limitation of static predictive models when confronted with dynamic, effective therapeutic interventions.

Furthermore, calibration analysis stratified by immunosuppression revealed that in the non-immunosuppression group, the model accurately captured the overall risk trend (with a good calibration slope) but exhibited slight bias in specific risk estimates across subgroups. Conversely, in the immunosuppression group, the model generally overestimated risk (poor calibration slope), yet it excelled in predicting event rates across risk strata. This indicates that patients can still be accurately stratified by risk even after immunosuppression therapy.

Based on our core finding—calibration is accurate, but discrimination is limited—this tool may offer genuine assistance to clinicians in practice. This implies the tool can be used to estimate a patient's absolute risk, particularly for high-risk individuals, with a relatively reliable value. However, when deciding whether to initiate or escalate intensive treatment for intermediate-risk patients, one should not overly rely on the risk stratification provided by this tool, as its ability to distinguish between “intermediate-risk” and “low-risk” is inadequate. Clinical decision-making requires a comprehensive evaluation that incorporates dynamic indicators, such as ongoing proteinuria monitoring and eGFR slope.

Another thing we need to note is that research on factors influencing IgAN prognosis is still progressing. The predictor variables of current international IgAN prediction tools are already validated risk factors related to IgAN prognosis. However, some recent studies have shown [9–13] that Gd-IgA1, hematuria, complement C3, complement C4, and some biomarkers in urine are associated with disease prognosis. In addition, the next few years are likely to see dramatic changes with regard to the treatment of IgAN both in primary supportive measures and targeted therapies, with the advent of drugs such as sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors, endothelin receptor blockers, complement antagonists, and targeted release formulations such as budesonide. All of these are likely to improve long-term outcomes for IgAN, with obvious implications for risk calculation tools. In addition, is there a need for improved models to enable dynamic prediction of disease risk to echo the need for continuous monitoring in the KDIGO guidelines and thus be more clinically useful?

This study also has some limitations. First, the cohort of our study excluded patients who did not have a kidney biopsy to obtain the Oxford MEST score, meaning that we may have missed some high-risk patients. Second, as our hospital was a tertiary care hospital, most patients may have been followed up in a primary care hospital, and the cohort excluded patients who lacked clinical data 1 year after kidney biopsy, resulting in a high rate of missed patients, with only 388 in the cohort, which may introduce selection bias. In addition, the cohort size in this study was relatively small, with fewer than 100 primary outcome events occurring—an ideal requirement for validating prognostic models—which may lead to instability in the model performance assessment. Finally, we were unable to document the specific types, dosages, and treatment durations of immunosuppressants in detail, which limits our ability to conduct an in-depth analysis of the precise impact of treatment on the model's predictive signals.

In summary, our external validation provides a critical real-world assessment of the updated IIgAN-PT model in a challenging clinical setting. While the model delivers well-calibrated absolute risk estimates, its risk stratification capability remains limited, particularly in low-to intermediate risk patient cohorts. This key finding contrasts sharply with the excellent discriminatory performance of post-biopsy IIgAN-PT reported in other cohorts. This suggests the post-biopsy IIgAN-PT efficacy depends on specific clinical contexts. For research cohorts like ours—characterized by high proportions of severe pathology and aggressive treatment regimens—clinicians can trust the model's calibration. However, risk stratification results for non-high-risk patients should be interpreted with caution. Consequently, future refinements of predictive tools must account for the moderating effects of treatment and extreme pathological phenotypes to ensure robust predictive performance across all patient subgroups.
