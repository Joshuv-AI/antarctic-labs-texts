The challenge of accounting for less frequently consumed foods with a high impact on nutrient intake estimates using the EFSA Comprehensive Food Consumption Database: The example of preformed vitamin A
Authors: Silvia Valtueña Martínez, Torsten Bohn, Ionut Craciun, Lucia Fabiani, Rita Sofia Ferreira de Sousa, Zsuzsanna Horvath, Sofia Ioannidou, Georg Lietz, Anastasia Livaniou, Giovanni Passeri, Androniki Naska
Published: 2024

In the context of the current revision of the tolerable upper intake levels (UL) for vitamins and minerals, the European Commission requested EFSA to provide harmonised intake estimates of micronutrients from the background diet (i.e. excluding fortified foods and food supplements) in European populations. As for the exposure assessment to other chemicals (e.g. food additives, contaminants), national dietary surveys present in the EFSA Comprehensive Food Consumption Database (hereafter referred to as Comprehensive Database) which rely on 2 to 9‐day food records or 24‐h recalls were used for that purpose (EFSA, 2011). The limitations of these dietary assessment methods to estimate high intakes (at the 95th percentile) of nutrients occurring at high concentrations in less frequently consumed foods became apparent during the evaluation of the UL for preformed vitamin A (EFSA NDA Panel, 2024).

Preformed vitamin A is a special nutrient for two reasons: (a) daily intakes show the largest inter‐ and intra‐individual (day‐to‐day) variability among nutrients and (b) preformed vitamin A at high intakes can be effectively stored, and does accumulate, especially in the liver and other tissues such as adipose tissue. Thus, the intake of highly concentrated sources of preformed vitamin A with very irregular consumption patterns, such as liver and products thereof, can be either missed or largely overestimated using food records or 24‐h recalls covering few survey days, and can lead to both overestimation and underestimation of vitamin A intake. These two characteristics (high concentrations in food sources rarely or irregularly consumed, and bioaccumulation) are shared by other nutrients (e.g. selenium in Brazil nuts, long‐chain omega‐3 fatty acids in fish) and chemical hazards (e.g. methylmercury and dioxins in fish).

This editorial aims to address the challenges faced during the intake assessment of preformed vitamin A from the background diet and the measures that could be implemented in the future to obtain more reliable intake (and exposure) estimates for nutrients (and other chemicals) to be used in risk assessment.

USE OF THE EFSA COMPREHENSIVE DATABASE FOR NUTRIENT INTAKE ESTIMATES FROM THE BACKGROUND DIET

The EFSA Comprehensive Database provides a compilation of existing national information on food consumption at individual level. Guidance on the use of the Comprehensive Database is available (EFSA, 2011). The EFSA guidance for the collection of national food consumption data was first published in 2009 (EFSA, 2009) and updated in 2014 (EFSA, 2014) to facilitate the collection of more harmonised food consumption data from all European Union Member States (EU Menu methodology).

In the context of the EU menu project, consumption data are collected using 24‐h recalls or dietary records to cover at least two non‐consecutive days, with balanced representation of week and weekend days, and the different seasons of the year, within a survey. Most dietary surveys included in the EFSA Comprehensive Database rely on 2–3 consumption days. 1 Whereas it is acknowledged that food consumption data collected over two non‐consecutive days, can be used to estimate the habitual intake distribution of a population (Dodd et al., 2006), that may not be the case for less frequently consumed foods, the consumption of which may easily be missed or overestimated in 2‐day data collections, or for foods consumed by less than 50% of the population that contain high levels of nutrients (e.g. liver and fish). To address these challenges, combined implementation of different assessment methods (e.g. multiple 24‐h recalls and a non‐quantitative food frequency questionnaire, often referred to as food propensity questionnaire) followed by statistical modelling have been applied (Engle‐Stone et al., 2014; Souverein et al., 2011). Data from the short food propensity questionnaire (FPQ) foreseen to complement the 24‐h recall or food record in the EU Menu project on the (frequency of) consumption of some less frequently eaten foods have not been transferred to EFSA.

Guidance on the target population and exclusion criteria, on the age groups to consider, on sampling (i.e. the selection of a subset of individuals from the general population for the survey) and on sample size has also been provided (EFSA, 2014). Several percentile values can be calculated to characterise the distribution, and the acceptability of the use of percentile (P) values (in relation to the reliability of estimates) strongly depends on sample size. For a simple random sample, the sample size (n) should satisfy the rule n (1 – P) ≥ 8 for high (> P75) percentile values. For a complex sample, for instance skewed to high intake levels, the minimum sample size requirements are higher (Kroes et al., 2002). Similar conclusions can be derived using a binomial distribution (Conover, 1971), and the same assumptions hold true for low percentiles.

Overall, it has been recommended that, for each age group included in the EU Menu surveys, data from at least 260 individuals (130 males and 130 females) should be collected per population group and survey to obtain reliable and representative average and P95 intake estimates for most foods and nutrients, unless large differences in consumption patterns exist within a country (i.e. adjustments should be made for large countries with large regional differences, or to include specific populations like pregnant women, or vegetarians). Such number of individuals with at least two non‐consecutive consumption days are not available for about one third of the population groups within the surveys used to estimate intakes from the background diet, the younger population groups being mostly affected. As discussed before, high and low percentiles (rather than mean/median values) are mostly affected by a smaller sample size.

THE PROBLEM OF LESS FREQUENTLY CONSUMED FOODS FOR SPECIFIC NUTRIENTS: THE CASE OF PREFORMED VITAMIN A

In EFSA safety assessments, intake estimates are required for risk characterisation, where population intakes at high percentiles (usually the P95) are compared with established health‐based guidance values (HBGV). 2 In the latest mandate from the European Commission to update the UL for specific micronutrients, including preformed vitamin A, the interest was to obtain intake estimates from the background diet (from natural food sources, excluding fortified foods and food supplements) to allow calculations of the difference between high intakes of the micronutrient in the population and the UL. This difference can inform the setting of maximum amounts of vitamins and minerals in fortified foods and food supplements while ensuring consumer protection.

Dietary intakes for all micronutrients were estimated linking food consumption data at individual level in the EFSA Comprehensive Database (including 83 different food consumption surveys covering 29 European countries and 154,388 individuals) to food composition data in the EFSA food composition database (FCDB). Food consumption data provided by non‐EU Countries (i.e. pre‐accession countries and the UK) and dietary surveys recording 1 day only were excluded. In most cases, when different dietary surveys were available for one country and age class, only the most recent was used. Out of the 49 food consumption surveys considered for the preformed vitamin A intake assessment, 40 recorded 2–3 days of food intake only. Intakes were calculated using the observed individual means method (deterministic approach). Statistical modelling to approximate habitual intakes from few (2–3) survey days was not undertaken, owing to the large number of dietary surveys included and the limited time and resources available. In addition, enough individuals to ensure sufficient reliability for mean, but mostly for the P95 estimates, were not available for about one third of the population groups across surveys. Whereas the recommended sample size was reached for adults in most surveys, it was not the case for younger population groups, which is also a result of the further breakdown of groups in this scientific opinion regarding other children and adolescents compared to the classification originally requested in the dietary surveys.

Dietary sources of preformed vitamin A are of animal origin and include staple foods consumed on a daily or weekly basis, such as butter, some cheese and eggs, as well as animal liver and other offal, which are typically consumed with a lower frequency or not at all. Whereas the content of preformed vitamin A in the former ranges from 40 to about 700 μg/100 g, it goes up to 15–20,000 μg/100 g in liver, with food products thereof containing around 5000 μg/100 g (e.g. liver‐based sausages and spreadable pastes) (EFSA NDA Panel, 2024).

population intakes of preformed vitamin A will follow a skewed distribution, with participants reporting the consumption of animal liver falling in the highest percentiles, even if such products are consumed only on a weekly or even monthly basis;

When offal consumption is reported in one of the 2–3 survey days, intakes can be overestimated if offal is consumed less frequently than on a weekly basis. Conversely, if offal is consumed but not captured in the 2–3 survey days, actual intakes will be underestimated. Whereas these two errors are expected to compensate for each other in large samples regarding mean intakes, they can have a substantial impact on estimates of both low and high percentiles of intake, particularly when the distribution of intakes is expected to be skewed and sample size is small. An additional source of uncertainty is the use of a single (representative) value for preformed vitamin A for each pertinent food code in the EFSA FCDB. Whereas this approach may be acceptable for most foods and nutrients, it may not be the case for preformed vitamin A in liver and products thereof owing to the very high and variable content of retinol and retinyl esters in these products as compared to other foods and the large impact their actual composition may have on preformed vitamin A intake.

EFSA's standard intake estimates (i.e. using data as reported in food consumption surveys) of preformed vitamin A from the background diet, excluding fortified foods and food supplements, led to very high P95 intakes across surveys for all population groups, except vegetarians. The highest contribution to preformed vitamin A intake was from liver, other offal and products thereof, which accounted for up to 72% of the intake in adults and up to 76% in older adults. Excluding offal, the highest P95 for preformed vitamin A for all population groups was two to eight times lower depending on the population group, except vegetarians. The highest P95 of EFSA's standard intake estimates of preformed vitamin A from the background diet widely exceeded the UL in all population groups and were much higher than the highest P95 intake estimates calculated at country level (published data), which considered also fortified foods or supplements in the intake assessment. Part of this discrepancy is attributed to the fact that national surveys also used a combination of dietary assessment methods (e.g. FFQs, dietary interviews) to adjust for the frequency of consumption of liver and products thereof, an adjustment that was not possible to make using the EFSA Comprehensive database as data from the FPQ in the respective EU menu surveys were not available.

METHODS TO ACCOUNT FOR LESS FREQUENTLY CONSUMED FOODS IN RISK ASSESSMENTS

To obtain a more realistic picture on the P95 intake of preformed vitamin A from the background diet for the whole population, the frequency of offal consumption among consumers was adjusted to once per month, twice per month and once per week to build three different intake scenarios using the consumed portion sizes by each individual as reported in the EFSA Comprehensive Database. Intake estimates excluding offal consumption were also calculated (EFSA NDA Panel, 2024). Assuming a frequency of consumption in offal consumers of 1 serving/week, the highest P95 estimated across surveys for males (taken as the highest observed intakes) was two to three times lower than those obtained using the standard intake assessment for virtually all population groups, whereas a frequency of consumption of one serving/month lead to P95 intake estimates close to intake estimates excluding offal. The two servings/month scenario leads to a mixed picture. These intake scenarios allowed the NDA Panel to conclude, in the risk characterisation, that the UL for preformed vitamin A is not expected to be exceeded in European populations if consumption of ‘liver, offal and products thereof’ is limited to once per month or less. These theoretical scenarios, however, cannot account for the underestimation of the percentage of offal consumers in the population.

Other methodologies have been developed to account for the intra‐individual variability in dietary intakes resulting from day‐to‐day variation in food choices and random reporting errors, which inflates the variance of the usual nutrient intakes of the group leading to distorted estimates of the prevalence of low or high consumers (Souverein et al., 2011), and that cannot be adequately captured through short‐term measurements of intake. Statistical procedures have been applied to calculate usual intakes from two or more 24‐h dietary recalls/food records per participant (Dekkers et al., 2014; Nusser et al., 1995; Slob, 2006; Tooze et al., 2006). Usual intakes have been estimated using a combination of dietary assessment methods, such as two or more 24‐h dietary recalls/food records and non‐quantitative food frequency questionnaires (e.g. FPQ). The analysis of data collected through combined dietary assessment methods (such as two or more 24‐h dietary recalls/food records and a FPQ) aims at: (a) accounting for days without consumption of a particular food (non‐consumers, left tail of the distribution); (b) allowing for consumption data that are positively skewed (right tail of the distribution); (c) distinguishing the within‐person from the between‐person variation; (d) allowing for the correlation between the probability of consuming a particular food and the amount of consumption; and (e) relating participants' characteristics (e.g. sex, age, ethnicity, educational attainment) to usual intake (Souverein et al., 2011).

The selection of the appropriate method to calculate usual nutrient intakes should rely on a clear and concise definition of the objective of the assessment (van Klaveren et al., 2012). If the interest is in consumers with a relatively high intake corresponding to the right tail of the distribution, a conservative exposure assessment may be needed.

TRANSLATING FREQUENCY OF CONSUMPTION INTO PORTION SIZE FOR RISK MANAGEMENT PURPOSES

The conclusion that the UL for preformed vitamin A is not expected to be exceeded in European populations if consumption of ‘liver, offal and products thereof’ is limited to once per month or less was not linked to the definition of (standard) portion sizes. Rather, the NDA Panel provided to risk managers the number of consumption occasions and portion sizes (mean, minimum and maximum weight) per food item and food consumption survey, age group and country, as found in the EFSA Comprehensive Database ((EFSA NDA Panel, 2024); in Annex C, Table 16).

There are three reasons for this. First, establishing Food‐Based Dietary Guidelines is in the remit of EU Member States; second, the amount of liver products consumed on a single occasion was highly variable across consumers and surveys; and third, EFSA intake estimates intend to show the variability across EU Member States linked to differences in food consumption patterns, rather than in food composition, assuming a common food market. Whereas this approach may be acceptable for most foods and nutrients, the collection and use of accurate food composition data at national level for liver and products thereof to calculate preformed vitamin A intake may be an exception worth making. This is because the content of retinol in liver and products thereof is extremely high as compared to other foods and, even if less frequently consumed, they can significantly contribute to high intakes of a nutrient that is effectively stored. In addition, it is known that the preformed vitamin A content of liver and products thereof much depends on animal feeding practices, with high variations within and across countries (Howells & Livesey, 1998; Majchrzak et al., 2006). Therefore, country or even region, specific data regarding the vitamin A content of edible liver products are needed to translate the frequency of consumption and observed portion sizes into actual intake levels from these products to manage the risk of excess preformed vitamin A intake at European level.

MOVING FORWARD

Following the completion of all EU Menu projects in 2023, EFSA has launched the ERA EU Menu project (Ocké et al., 2024) in order to map the landscape of methods and tools available for/used in national dietary surveys and the food consumption data received so far in order to update the EU Menu guidance (EFSA, 2014).

During the next round of national dietary surveys (EU Menu 2), EFSA aims at developing a central FPQ, which can be adapted to country‐specific contexts, to accurately estimate the intake of food supplements and of foods that are not consumed daily, the intake of which cannot be reliably estimated using food consumption data of 2 days. For many countries, this was the case for fish and seafood, liver and offal, and pulses/legumes (Carvalho et al., 2023). The EU Menu phase 2 is expected to be launched in late 2026, following the update of the EU Menu Guidance. Data from the FPQ will be transferred to the EFSA's data warehouse and will be connected to the Comprehensive Database.

To obtain accurate nutrient intake estimates, complete and up‐to‐date food composition data are equally important. Considering a profound improvement of the EFSA food classification system (FoodEx2) after 2015, the evolution of food composition databases in Europe and the increased research interest for high quality and harmonised food composition data at European level, EFSA saw an opportunity for the creation and maintenance of an Open Access European Food Composition Database (EU FCDB).

For this purpose, EFSA is currently working on a project 3 that aims at:

Defining the quality standards for inclusion, and the methodology used for evaluation, of composition data from national FCDBs, including fortified foods and food supplements, as well as the data model to be used for data submission and

Defining the quality standards for inclusion, and the methodology used for evaluation, of composition data from national FCDBs, including fortified foods and food supplements, as well as the data model to be used for data submission and

Collecting and making publicly available the food composition data collected, mapped to FoodEx2, from at least 16 EU, European Free Trade Association (EFTA) and Instrument for Pre‐accession Assistance (IPA) countries.

Collecting and making publicly available the food composition data collected, mapped to FoodEx2, from at least 16 EU, European Free Trade Association (EFTA) and Instrument for Pre‐accession Assistance (IPA) countries.

The EU FCDB project is expected to be finalised by the end of 2025.

Finally, EFSA has launched a project in 2023 to address different statistical challenges, better understand the limitations of the statistical methodologies and criteria in use and identify possible alternative approaches. This project will investigate methods to estimate the median and the extreme percentiles of intakes (e.g. 2.5%, 99%, 99.9%) and derive criteria for the reliability of the estimate (e.g. based on minimum sample size). The investigation will also include methodologies for usual intake estimation, building on and updating the work previously developed for EFSA (Goedhart et al., 2012; van Klaveren et al., 2012). The project will be completed in 2025.
