Maternal weight and hypertension as risk factors for juvenile idiopathic arthritis: a prospective population-based pregnancy cohort study
Authors: Vilde Øverlien Dåstøl, Ida Henriette Caspersen, Kristine Løkås Haftorn, Sigrid Valen Hestetun, Srijana Bastakoti, Ole Andreassen, Anne Lise Brantsæter, Siri Eldevik Håberg, Karen H Costenbader, Ketil Størdal, Helga Sanner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe intrauterine environment may influence childhood immune-mediated disease risk. We investigated whether maternal prepregnancy body mass index (BMI), gestational weight gain (GWG) or hypertensive disorders during pregnancy (HDP) were associated with juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) in children and whether genetic susceptibility modified associations.MethodsWe linked the Norwegian Mother, Father and Child Cohort Study (MoBa) to the Norwegian Patient Registry to identify children with JIA. Logistic regression estimated adjusted ORs (aORs) for associations with BMI, GWG and HDPs. Causal mediation analyses assessed whether hypertension mediated the GWG–JIA association. In a genotyped subsample, we tested interactions between exposures and a JIA Polygenic Risk Score (PRS).ResultsAmong 78 901 eligible children, 265 developed JIA (genotyped subsample: 192 JIA/44 053 non-JIA). By the end of follow-up, all children were ≥14 years old and 85% were ≥16 years old. Maternal prepregnancy BMI was not associated with JIA. Each 1 SD (~6 kg) increase in GWG was associated with modestly higher odds of JIA (aOR 1.12, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.26), particularly among women with prepregnancy obesity (aOR 1.40, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.77). Maternal hypertension and pre-eclampsia were associated with higher JIA risk (aOR 1.43, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.99 and aOR 1.61, 95% CI 1.10 to 2.37). Hypertension did not mediate the GWG–JIA association (p>0.05). Among children with lower PRS, maternal obesity was associated with a lower JIA risk (aOR 0.37, 95% CI 0.17 to 0.80).ConclusionHigher GWG and HDPs were independently associated with increased JIA risk. Genetic susceptibility may modify associations with maternal BMI, supporting a role for pregnancy health in JIA development.

Introduction

Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) is the most common inflammatory rheumatic disease in children, affecting more girls than boys (ratio 2:1).1–3 Its aetiology remains unknown, though evidence supports a multifactorial origin involving genetic and environmental influences, including early-life exposures.4 5 The contribution of maternal health during pregnancy and the intrauterine environment have been less examined.

Obesity is a state of chronic, low-grade systemic inflammation, reflected by increased levels of proinflammatory cytokines and hormones.6 7 Maternal obesity and excessive gestational weight gain (GWG) during pregnancy may affect fetal development through inflammation, hyperinsulinaemia and oxidative stress8 and has been associated with increased risk of adverse outcomes such as asthma, type 1 and 2 diabetes, coronary heart disease and stroke in offspring.7 9 10 Moreover, obesity increases the risk of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, which also may impair placental function and fetal growth.7 8 11 12

While several studies have investigated factors related to fetal growth, such as birth weight in relation to JIA risk with inconsistent results,13–19 studies examining maternal obesity or hypertensive disorders of pregnancy as potential risk factors are lacking. One previous case–control study reported lower odds of JIA among children of mothers with GWG within the recommended range,19 suggesting a potential protective effect of a favourable intrauterine environment.

This study aimed to assess whether maternal prepregnancy body mass index (BMI), GWG and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy are associated with children’s risk of JIA. We also examined whether birth weight was associated with JIA risk given its strong correlation with GWG and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy. We also aimed to explore potential mediating mechanisms, genetic interactions and sex-specific associations due to the known female predominance of JIA.

Methods

Study population

The Norwegian Mother, Father and Child Cohort Study (MoBa) is a population-based pregnancy cohort study conducted by the Norwegian Institute of Public Health. Pregnant women and their partners were recruited from all over Norway from 1999 to 2008 and are still followed up. The women consented to participation in 41% of the pregnancies, and the cohort includes approximately 114 000 children, 95 000 mothers and 75 000 fathers.20

MoBa was linked to data from The Medical Birth Registry (MBRN), a national health registry containing information about all births in Norway. Data in the MBRN are collected through mandatory reporting by the midwife or medical doctor at birth and are based on information from prenatal, delivery and postnatal medical records.21 Thus, the MBRN provides nationwide coverage of births in Norway for all MoBa participants linked through national personal identification (ID) numbers, although some variables contain missing values. We also linked MoBa to data from the Norwegian Patient Registry (NPR) using national personal ID numbers. From 2008 onwards, the NPR has received data from all public hospitals and specialists with public funding in Norway. Given that paediatric rheumatology care in Norway is almost exclusively provided within these settings, linkage to the NPR allows for near-complete ascertainment of children with JIA in the MoBa cohort.

Our source population consisted of 109 482 liveborn singletons. After applying exclusion criteria, our complete case sample consisted of 215 children with JIA and 64 940 children without JIA. The corresponding imputed datasets included 265 children with JIA and 78 636 children without JIA (figure 1).

Exposures

Our four main exposures were maternal prepregnancy BMI, GWG, birth weight and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy.

Prepregnancy BMI was calculated from self-reported weight and height (weight in kg and height in metres squared) as reported in the recruitment questionnaire. BMI was analysed per one SD increase and categorised in accordance with the WHO standards22 as underweight (<18.5), normal weight (18.5–24.9) (reference), overweight (25–29.9) and obese (30 and higher).

Prepregnancy BMI was calculated from self-reported weight and height (weight in kg and height in metres squared) as reported in the recruitment questionnaire. BMI was analysed per one SD increase and categorised in accordance with the WHO standards22 as underweight (<18.5), normal weight (18.5–24.9) (reference), overweight (25–29.9) and obese (30 and higher).

GWG was defined as the total change in weight (kg, continuous variable) between self-reported prepregnancy weight and weight at end of pregnancy (reported in the 6-month postpartum questionnaire). To evaluate timing of GWG, we further calculated trimester-specific average weekly weight gain rates (kg/week). Operationally, we defined the average weight change per week for first, second and third trimesters based on maternal weight at four timepoints (pre pregnancy, around gestational week 15–18, around gestational week 30 and at end of pregnancy). The gestational week of weight measurements during pregnancy varied slightly but were on average (mean) 17.5 weeks (SD 4.3) and 28.6 weeks (SD 2.3). For each woman, average weekly weight gain rate in each trimester was defined as the change in weight (kg) in that gestational interval divided by the number of weeks between measurements, yielding a crude rate (kg/week).

Birth weight was analysed as both a continuous variable (analysed in 100 gram increments) and as a categorical variable (3.0–3.99 kg serving as reference, compared with <3.0 kg and ≥4.0 kg to capture a potential non-linear relationship). These data were collected from the MBRN.

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy.

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy.

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy were assessed using both registry-based and self-reported data. Information on hypertension was obtained from two sources: (1) MBRN and (2) from maternal questionnaires completed at gestational weeks 15–18 and at 6 months postpartum. Although questionnaire-based blood pressure values were self-reported, they were not self-measured, as participants transcribed values recorded during routine antenatal care from their pregnancy health card.

Hypertension was defined as any positive response to the questions: ‘Do you have, or have you had high blood pressure (before pregnancy; during pregnancy)?’, with separate response options for before pregnancy and during pregnancy (gestational weeks 15–18) or ‘Have you had high blood pressure since you completed the previous questionnaire (yes, last part of pregnancy; yes, after birth)’, also with separate response options for the last part of pregnancy and after birth (6 months postpartum), or a recorded diagnosis of hypertension in the MBRN, capturing both pre-existing chronic and gestational hypertension (binary variable: no/yes).

Information on pre-eclampsia was obtained from two sources: (1) registry-based pre-eclampsia variables from the MBRN or (2) self-reported hypertension combined with proteinuria in the 6-month post-partum questionnaire (binary variable: no/yes). Proteinuria was defined as a positive response to the question: ‘Have you had protein in your urine since you completed the previous questionnaire (yes, last part of pregnancy; yes, after birth)’, with separate response options for the last part of pregnancy and after birth.

Lastly, we constructed a composite exposure representing any hypertensive disorder of pregnancy defined as the presence of at least one of the following conditions recorded in MBRN: chronic or gestational hypertension, pre-eclampsia or eclampsia. This variable was coded as a binary variable; no/yes.

Outcome

A diagnosis of JIA in the child was obtained from the NPR. We required at least two registrations of International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes M08 (juvenile arthritis) or M09 (juvenile arthritis in diseases classified elsewhere) in the NPR between 1 January 2008 and 31 December 2022 to be defined as a child with JIA. For the final year of follow-up (31 December 2022 to 31 December 2023), a single registration of M08 or M09 was used, as some children may have only had one hospital contact before end of follow-up. We excluded children with only one ICD-10 code of M08 or M09 prior to 31 December 2022 and no specialist care follow-up after this, as these children might have been referred with questions about JIA but concluded to not have JIA. This definition has previously been validated by our group to have a positive predictive value (PPV) of 93.4%.23 A child without JIA was defined as having zero registrations of ICD-10 codes M08 or M09.

Other covariates

Based on the previous literature on potential confounding factors, we included maternal age, parity, birth year, gestational age and mode of delivery, obtained from the MBRN. Maternal education level and smoking during pregnancy were self-reported by the mother in questionnaires. We also included maternal history of diabetes reported in the MBRN, which includes data on type 1 and type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM), unspecified diabetes, gestational DM and registered use of antidiabetics. In a validation study, 88.3% of women registered with gestational diabetes in the MBRN were confirmed by medical record review and no one had false negatives.24 In our study, we categorised maternal history of diabetes as none/pregestational (type 1 DM, type 2 DM, unspecified DM and use of antidiabetics)/gestational diabetes due to small numbers.

Maternal history of inflammatory rheumatic disease was defined by at least two registrations of the following clinical diagnoses in the NPR: JIA (M08/M09), ankylosing spondylitis/spondylarthritis (M45/M46), psoriatic arthritis (M07), rheumatoid arthritis (M05/M06), systemic connective tissue diseases/other systemic involvement of connective tissue (M35), localised scleroderma (L94), systemic sclerosis (M34), myositis (M33), systemic lupus erythematosus (M32) and/or systemic vasculitis (M30 and M31).

Genotyping data

Umbilical cord blood collected at birth was genotyped as part of the MoBa study, with DNA extraction, quality control and imputation procedures reported elsewhere.25 26 Briefly, children’s polygenic risk scores (PRSs) for JIA were calculated using summary statistics of a previously published genome-wide association study of JIA27 using PRSice V.2.3.3.28 The first 10 principal components (PCs) were included in our analyses to account for population stratification.

Statistics

Study sample characteristics were reported as mean (with SD) for continuous variables and by distribution differences (counts and percentages) for categorical variables.

We used logistic regression models to estimate the association between each exposure and JIA diagnosis, which were reported as unadjusted and adjusted ORs (aORs) with 95% CIs. A robust cluster variance estimation was used to account for the presence of heteroskedasticity and siblings with the same mother. We included an interaction term between the exposures and sex in all analyses, in addition to conducting sex-stratified analyses to observe any potential sex differences, given that JIA is more common in girls than in boys.1

For models examining the associations between maternal weight or hypertensive disorders of pregnancy and JIA risk in the child, we adjusted for maternal age, education, parity, prepregnancy BMI, smoking during pregnancy, maternal history of rheumatic disease, maternal history of diabetes and child’s birth year. GWG was modelled as a linear term (per 1 SD increase) after testing for non-linearity, where a quadratic term did not improve the model fit (p>0.05). Interaction terms between all the exposures and sex, GWG and hypertension, and maternal prepregnancy BMI and hypertension were included in the main logistic regression analyses (in each separate model) to investigate potential effect modifications and a p value<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Associations with GWG were also examined after stratifying for BMI category to assess for potential differences in effect estimates between the BMI groups. To assess the importance of timing of GWG, trimester-specific GWG rates were analysed as main exposures in separate regression models.

When studying the association between birth weight and JIA risk, we adjusted for the above-mentioned potential confounders plus hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, mode of delivery and gestational age. Birth weight (per 100 g) was modelled using both linear and quadratic terms to test for non-linearity. The quadratic term significantly improved model fit (p<0.01), indicating an inverted U-shaped association. To further investigate this pattern, birth weight was categorised as <3 kg, 3–4 kg (reference) and ≥4 kg in the final model.

When assessing potential gene–environment interactions, we included an interaction term between the PRS and the exposures in the logistic regression analyses, and a p-value of the interaction term <0.05 was regarded as significant. In these models we included the first 10 genetic PCs as covariates to account for population stratification and did not include maternal history of rheumatic disease to avoid over-adjustment, as both the PRS and history of rheumatic disease could capture aspects of genetic heritability. When a significant interaction was detected, predicted risks of JIA were estimated at fixed PRS values (−1, 0 and +1 SD), representing low, average and high genetic susceptibility, respectively.

Causal mediation analyses were conducted within a counterfactual framework to assess whether either hypertension or birth weight mediated the association between GWG and JIA. Natural direct, indirect and total effects were estimated using logistic regression models for both the mediator and the outcome. Effects were estimated comparing the mean GWG to 1 SD above the mean. The mediation model evaluating the association between GWG and hypertension was adjusted for maternal age, education, parity, prepregnancy BMI, smoking during pregnancy, maternal history of rheumatic disease, maternal history of diabetes and child’s birth year. When evaluating birth weight as a potential mediator, the model was additionally adjusted for hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, mode of delivery and gestational age. All models were clustered at the pregnancy level.

The proportion of participants with missing information on at least one covariate was 17%. Smoking during pregnancy had the highest proportion of missing values (13%). We imputed missing observations in covariates with multiple imputations by chained equations using the ‘mice’ package in R. As outcome data were available through registry linkage, this allowed us to include additional JIA cases with missing covariate information in the imputed analyses. We generated 20 imputed datasets using five iterations per dataset. The first imputed dataset was used for descriptive statistics. When examining exposure–outcome associations, we ran regression models across all imputed datasets. Coefficients and standard errors were combined across all imputations using Rubin’s rules to obtain pooled effect estimates and 95% CIs. The mediation analysis was based on a complete case dataset. Results from both the imputed datasets and complete case analyses are presented in the tables, with the imputed datasets serving as the main analytical sample. All statistical analyses were conducted in R V.4.3.029 and Stata V.17.0 statistical software (StataCorp).

Results

Study sample characteristics

Our study sample included 265 children with JIA (104 boys (39%) and 161 girls (61%)) and 78 636 without JIA (figure 1). At the end of follow-up in 2023, most (85%) participants reached 16 years of age, but the youngest were 14 years old (born in 2009). Of these, we had genotyping data on 192 children with JIA (72%) and 44 053 (59%) without. Maternal characteristics differed across categories of hypertensive status, prepregnancy BMI, birth weight and available PRS data of the child when comparing numerically (table 1 and online supplemental tables 1–3). For instance, women with hypertension overall had lower rates of college education and were more often primiparous than those without hypertension. Obese women had a higher prevalence of hypertension during pregnancy (26%) compared with underweight women (6%) and lower total GWG (11 kg vs 15 kg).

Maternal weight

We did not find any indication that JIA risk varied with maternal prepregnancy BMI (table 2). However, a modest increased risk of JIA in the child was observed per 1 SD (6 kg) increase in gestational weight in the complete case analyses (aOR 1.19, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.34), with results attenuated in the imputed datasets (aOR 1.12, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.26) (table 2). We observed a significant interaction between GWG and sex (p=0.02) and after sex-stratification we observed higher effect estimates of 1 SD GWG in girls (aOR 1.25 (95% CI 1.08 to 1.45), but not in boys (aOR 0.96, 95% CI 0.80 to 1.14) (online supplemental table 4, 5). Moreover, stronger effect estimates of this GWG were observed in women categorised as obese before pregnancy (aOR 1.40, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.77), suggesting that the GWG association was modified by both prepregnancy obesity and sex of the child (5). When analysing GWG rates (kg/week), we observed an increased risk of JIA per 1 SD (0.15 kg/week) increase in overall weight gain rate in the complete case analysis (aOR 1.19, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.34), with results attenuated in the imputed datasets (aOR 1.10, 95% CI 0.98 to 1.24) (table 2). This modest association was only observed in female children (aOR 1.20, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.39) (online supplemental table 4). In trimester-specific analyses, third trimester showed the highest risk estimate, where a 1 SD increase (0.34 kg/week) yielded an aOR of 1.11 (95% CI 0.99 to 1.24), while weight gain in the first trimester was not associated with increased JIA risk (table 2). The GWG rates were conducted on a smaller subsample as participants also needed information from questionnaire 3 (30 weeks gestation (complete case n=206 JIA, 62 602 non-JIA and imputed datasets n=254 JIA, 75 717 non-JIA)), but within these subsamples, a 1 SD increase in GWG was also associated with increased JIA risk in the complete case analysis (aOR 1.17, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.33), with results attenuated in the imputed datasets 1.10 (95% CI 0.98 to 1.24).

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy

We observed an increased risk of JIA with maternal hypertension (aOR 1.43, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.99) and pre-eclampsia (aOR 1.61, 95% CI 1.10 to 2.37). The composite variable of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (hypertension, pre-eclampsia and eclampsia) was associated with JIA risk in complete case analysis (aOR 1.50, 95% CI 1.05 to 2.13) but did not reach statistical significance in the imputed datasets (aOR 1.36, 95% CI 0.98 to 1.89) (table 4). No significant interactions were observed with sex (p>0.05), but after sex-stratification, the increased risk associated with hypertensive disorders was only evident in girls and attenuated in boys (online supplemental table 7). No interactions between GWG and hypertension, or between maternal prepregnancy BMI and hypertension, were observed (p>0.05).

Mediation analyses

By conducting mediation analyses, we explored whether maternal hypertension or birth weight could explain some of the associations seen between GWG and JIA; however, no evidence of mediation was observed (p>0.05).

Gene–environment interactions

We observed an interaction between the JIA PRS and maternal prepregnancy BMI, especially in the overweight group (p<0.01), indicating that the effect of maternal BMI on JIA risk differed depending on the child’s genetic susceptibility to JIA (table 5). By fixing the PRS at −1, 0 and +1 SD from the mean PRS-value, we were able to explore whether the association between maternal BMI and JIA risk differed at low, average and high genetic susceptibility to JIA. Our model predicted that at low genetic risk of JIA (−1 SD), maternal overweight was associated with a lower JIA risk, compared with normal BMI, with an aOR of 0.37 (95% CI 0.17 to 0.80). This was also seen with average genetic risk of JIA (0 SD), with an aOR of 0.61 (95% CI 0.38 to 0.98), but not at high genetic risk (aOR 0.99, 95% CI 0.68 to 1.44). We found no indications of gene–environment interactions between the JIA PRS and GWG, hypertensive disorders or birth weight (all p’s>0.05) (online supplemental table 8).

Discussion

In this large, prospective, population-based pregnancy cohort including 265 children with JIA and 78 636 without, we offer three main contributions to increase the understanding of prenatal influences on JIA risk. First, we observed that both higher maternal GWG, especially in the third trimester, and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy were associated with an increased risk of JIA. Although associations were weak, this suggests that maternal systemic inflammation during pregnancy may play a role in JIA aetiology. Second, we found no association between prepregnancy BMI and JIA risk, suggesting that pregnancy-specific pathways may be more important than long-term maternal adiposity. Third, our exploratory analyses provide evidence that maternal BMI may interact with the child’s genetic susceptibility, providing insight into the underexplored area of gene–environment interactions in JIA.

Our finding of a modestly increased JIA risk with GWG and hypertensive disorders supports the broader hypothesis that an adverse intrauterine environment can increase the risk of immune-mediated diseases later in life.30 31 A proinflammatory intrauterine environment, characterised by excessive GWG, hypertensive disorders and upregulation of inflammatory cytokines,31–33 may therefore be relevant to JIA aetiology. These results are also in line with a previous study reporting a protective effect on JIA risk of GWG within the recommended range.19 As we found no evidence of mediation or effect modification, our results suggest that GWG and hypertensive disorders may influence JIA risk through distinct pro-inflammatory intrauterine pathways. Importantly, these exposures may be modifiable, offering potential implications for prevention. The modest third-trimester association may indicate that late gestation represents a period of fetal sensitivity to the maternal metabolic environment, although key processes in fetal immune development are initiated earlier and continue across gestation.34 This finding requires replication in other cohorts.

The lack of association with maternal BMI before pregnancy and JIA risk may suggest that the associations we observed for GWG point to distinct pathophysiological pathways. However, exploring causality and specific mechanisms was beyond the scope of our study. Underlying confounding structures may also contribute to these findings. Prepregnancy BMI may be more influenced by diet, physical activity, genetics and socioeconomic status, whereas GWG may reflect pregnancy-specific conditions, such as oedema, gestational DM or rheumatic inflammatory disease. However, adjusting for gestational diabetes and rheumatic disease did not alter our results. It is also important to note that during MoBa recruitment (1999–2008), gestational diabetes screening in Norway was risk-based, targeting women aged ≥35 years.35 Registry data may therefore be subject to under-ascertainment, limiting our ability to assess mediation by gestational diabetes. Additionally, the small proportion of obese women in our study (9%), reflecting the generally healthy participants in MoBa,36 may have reduced the power to detect associations with prepregnancy BMI.

Given that maternal weight gain during pregnancy increases birth weight independently of genetic factors of the child,37 it was also relevant to consider fetal growth as a potential pathway linking maternal factors to JIA risk. We did, however, not observe any associations between birth weight and risk of JIA, which is in line with previous studies,13–19 nor did birth weight significantly mediate the association between GWG and JIA risk.

In exploratory analyses, we examined potential gene–environment interactions and sex-specific differences. Our results suggested that maternal adiposity may influence JIA risk differently depending on the child’s underlying genetic susceptibility. When genetic risk was low, maternal overweight was associated with reduced risk of JIA. This may reflect residual confounding by lifestyle or socioeconomic factors correlated with maternal overweight, which may be more influential when underlying genetic susceptibility is low. Additionally, it is based on a small number of JIA cases and should therefore be interpreted with caution but may suggest a possible gene–environment interaction in JIA. Sex-stratified analyses also showed higher effect estimates in girls than in boys, consistent with evidence that female fetuses mount a more pronounced inflammatory response to maternal inflammation.38 JIA is also more common in girls than in boys,1 but reasons remain unclear.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of our study is the large population-based study sample with detailed exposure, genetic and outcome information. The prospective design and individual linkage to national health registries substantially reduces risk of information bias in the exposure and outcome assessments, including recall bias and measurement errors. A further strength is the validated case definition (PPV of 93.4%).23 We were able to adjust our analyses for maternal history of inflammatory rheumatic disease and diabetes, without relying on self-reported information. Importantly, by using genotyping data, we were also able to explore gene–environment interactions, which remain an understudied area in JIA research.

In addition to the limitations regarding residual confounding, a major limitation of our study is the lack of data on JIA subtypes. Given that subtypes differ in their genetic architecture,39 their associations with environmental risk factors may also vary. Our results are likely driven by the most common subtype, oligoarthritis,3 which is also more common in girls. Potential selection bias is another important limitation, as MoBa only had a 41% participation rate. Compared with the general pregnant population in Norway, MoBa participants were generally older, more often married, had lower parity and were less likely to smoke at the end of pregnancy. However, previous studies have shown that this self-selection has limited impact on exposure–outcome associations and that the prevalence of chronic hypertension and pre-eclampsia is comparable between MoBa participants and the general pregnant population in Norway.40 This suggests that internal validity is preserved. Although data on pre-eclampsia were obtained from MBRN, the moderate validity (PPV 83.9%)41 and the use of a combined variable incorporating self-reported proteinuria and hypertension may have introduced some exposure misclassification. Likewise, the weight variables have not been validated, and measurement error may have introduced exposure misclassification. However, this is likely to be non-differential in regard to later JIA diagnosis. Furthermore, JIA is a rare disease, and our study only included 265 JIA cases, which may have reduced the ability to detect small effect estimates. As obesity and its complications vary across ethnic groups,42 it is also important to acknowledge that MoBa participants were mostly of Northern European descent in a high-income country, possibly limiting generalisability to more diverse populations. As with all other observational studies, we are only able to identify associations and cannot establish causality. Finally, paternal genetic risk and disease-related characteristics, including paternal inflammatory rheumatic disease, may contribute to JIA risk in the child, but were beyond the scope of the present study.

Conclusion

Higher GWG and hypertensive disorders during pregnancy were associated with increased risk of JIA, possibly through distinct proinflammatory intrauterine pathways. From a public health perspective, our study supports the importance of optimising maternal health during pregnancy, but these findings warrant further investigations in independent cohorts before informing preventive strategies.
