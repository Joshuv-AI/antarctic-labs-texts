Prediction Error in Quality-Adjusted Life Years in Economic Evaluations of Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors: A Comparison Based on Projected and Observed Updated Survival
Authors: Jinyu Chen, Yichen Zhang, Kexin Han, Sheng Han, Luwen Shi, Xiaodong Guan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurvival data from pivotal clinical trials are critical for estimating quality-adjusted life years (QALYs). However, immature survival data require extrapolation beyond observed follow-up to project outcomes, introducing potential prediction error into QALY estimates used in economic evaluations. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) present unique extrapolation challenges due to delayed responses and extended survival benefits. We therefore quantify the QALY prediction error of early extrapolations by benchmarking them against updated follow-up at a common time horizon.MethodsUsing reconstructed individual patient data derived from published Kaplan–Meier curves of pivotal trials, this study assessed the accuracy of early survival extrapolations for ICIs approved in China by comparing early projections with QALYs obtained from updated data at the same horizon. A partitioned-survival framework using overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) informed state occupancy, and QALYs were obtained via restricted mean survival time (RMST) integration of health-state utilities at the target horizon. Statistical analyses evaluated bias, precision, and agreement between extrapolated and updated QALY estimates. Linear regression and sensitivity analyses assessed the impact of target extrapolation horizon (T) on prediction error.ResultsIn total, 14 randomized controlled trials (4839 patients) were included for analysis. The mean deviation between extrapolated and observed QALYs was − 0.01 (95% CI − 0.03 to 0.01), with a mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.03 (95% CI 0.01 to 0.04). Strong agreement existed between extrapolated and updated QALYs (Spearman’s ρ = 0.98, 95% CI 0.94 to 0.99, P < 0.001). Consistently, OS extrapolations showed minimal deviation (MAE: 0.56 months), while PFS tended to be underestimated (MAE: 1.91 months). Moreover, predictive error increased significantly with longer extrapolation periods for QALY (MAE increase: 0.011 QALY/year, P = 0.004) and OS (MAE increase: 0.342 months/year, P = 0.010).ConclusionsNear-horizon QALY prediction error was modest on average but increased with longer target extrapolation horizons. These findings support transparent reporting of extrapolation uncertainty and suggest that structured evidence reassessment may be particularly valuable as longer follow-up becomes available.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s41669-026-00660-z.

Key Points for Decision Makers

In this sample of immune checkpoint inhibitor trials, restricted-horizon QALY prediction error based on early survival extrapolation was modest on average.Longer target extrapolation horizons were associated with greater prediction error, indicating that extrapolation uncertainty should be reported transparently when longer projections are required.These findings support the value of structured evidence reassessment as additional follow-up data become available, while not establishing the accuracy of lifetime-horizon extrapolations.

Introduction

As a core component of the Health Technology Assessment (HTA) system, pharmacoeconomic evaluation of oncology drugs integrates clinical effectiveness and economic value data, directly influencing reimbursement decisions globally [1–4]. Survival data from pivotal clinical trials form the foundation of cost–effectiveness analyses, which typically adopt lifetime horizons and therefore require extrapolation beyond observed follow-up. However, the immaturity of clinical data at the time of assessment often introduces predictive inaccuracies—a persistent challenge in the field [5]. A review of the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) cancer HTA reports (single technology appraisals) found that 41% (20/49) were considered by evidence review groups/appraisal committees (ERGs/ACs) to involve immature survival data, with maturity judgments based primarily on the proportion of events and survival-curve behavior [6]. This evidentiary gap compels assessors to employ survival extrapolation methods to estimate lifetime benefits—a process that inherently introduces uncertainty, which can propagate into uncertainty in policy-relevant outcomes such as quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) [7, 8]. To maintain inferential transparency, it is important to characterize how early extrapolations affect QALYs without invoking unverifiable lifetime assumptions.

The clinical use of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), a promising class of oncological therapeutics, introduces an additional layer of complexity. Their unique pharmacodynamic characteristics—including delayed response and potential long-term survival benefits—poses dual challenges to traditional survival extrapolation models [9–11]. Existing assessment practices reveal that survival projections based on early trial data for ICIs commonly exhibit conservative estimation biases [12]. This systematic underestimation leads to significant fluctuations in QALY calculations, highlighting the need to quantify extrapolation-driven QALY prediction error at observed follow-up horizons.

While previous studies have highlighted the error margins in predicting oncology survival metrics such as overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) [13], much of the existing validation literature focuses on survival outcomes and often evaluates lifetime projections, where conclusions can be dominated by unverifiable tail assumptions [14–18]. In this context, our study provides a complementary empirical assessment of prediction error expressed in QALY terms for ICIs approved in China. In line with prior validation studies that compare early extrapolations with later follow-up, we restrict our analysis to a common observed horizon so that prediction error can be benchmarked against subsequently available data. Within this established validation framework, we report results in QALY terms to align the outcome metric more closely with economic evaluation outputs. By focusing on an empirically benchmarkable observed horizon rather than unrestricted lifetime projections, our analysis is intended to complement existing survival-validation studies and to inform interpretation of QALY-based economic evaluation results.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This retrospective observational study evaluates the accuracy of early survival extrapolations in estimating QALYs for ICIs approved in China. Using published pivotal trial data, we compare QALY projections derived from early survival data with observed QALYs based on updated survival follow-up at the same time horizon to quantify bias and uncertainty introduced by early extrapolation.

A partitioned survival model (PSM) was implemented for QALY estimation, incorporating reconstructed individual patient-level data (IPD) derived from digitised Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves [19, 20]. In our framework, OS and PFS inform state occupancy over time, and utilities are integrated via restricted mean survival time (RMST) [21] at the target horizon to obtain QALYs. Because our estimand is QALY prediction error at observed horizons, we do not present lifetime PSM outputs that would require unverifiable tail assumptions. Statistical analyses focused on assessing prediction bias, precision, and agreement, while sensitivity analyses validated model robustness under alternative parametric assumptions using multi-criterion diagnostics. Key methodological components, including partitioned survival modeling, KM digitization and reconstructed IPD, and RMST estimation, are described in greater detail in the Statistical Analysis section.

Data Sources and Sample Identification

Drug labels were reviewed via the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) database and manufacturer websites to identify all ICIs approved in mainland China as of 30 September 2024. Pivotal trials supporting specific cancer indications were identified through clinical trial registries (e.g., National Clinical Trial identifiers) and cross-referenced with Medline/PubMed and ClinicalTrials.gov for peer-reviewed publications up to the same cutoff date. Exclusion criteria included: (1) absence of updated data for either OS or PFS, (2) absence of KM survival curves, or (3) missing at-risk population data critical for IPD reconstruction.

Health utility values were primarily sourced from pivotal trial publications. When unavailable, values were supplemented from economic evaluations specific to the corresponding cancer indications.

Data Extraction and Validation

For each included trial, the following data were extracted: (1) cancer type/indication, (2) trial sample size, (3) OS and PFS maturity, and (4) median survival follow-up duration. KM curves for OS and PFS were digitized using Engauge Digitizer software (version 12.1), with IPD reconstructed via the Guyot et al. algorithm [20]. The accuracy of reconstructed IPD was verified by comparing survival rates at key timepoints against original publications, ensuring consistency remained within ± 0.02 (see Supplementary Table S1 for further details).

Statistical Analysis

Quality-Adjusted Life Years Calculation

A three-state PSM, comprising progression free (PF), progressed disease (PD), and death states, was constructed to simulate disease trajectories. In this model, progression was operationalized according to the PFS endpoint definition and assessment approach reported in each individual trial publication. State transitions were modeled using OS and PFS curves [22], where the OS curve quantifies the duration from model entry to death, thereby estimating the proportion of surviving patients alive over time. Conversely, the PFS curve delineates the time from model entry to exit from the PF state due to either disease progression or death, establishing the temporal distribution of patients remaining progression free.

To calculate QALYs, RMST [23] was employed. RMST, defined as the integral of the survival curve up to a predefined time horizon, was combined with health utility values through a formulaic approach: QALYsT=RMSTPFST×UtilityPF+RMSTOST-RMSTPFST×UtilityPD. Utilities were assumed constant over time, with PF and PD values and their data sources provided in Supplementary Table S3. OS- and PFS-RMST differences are reported alongside QALY differences to aid interpretation. For each trial, a common truncation horizon (T) was prespecified on the basis of the updated publication. When a (reverse Kaplan–Meier) median follow-up time was reported, T was set to that value. If the median follow-up was not available, T was defined as the last available time point of the updated Kaplan–Meier curve (i.e., the maximum observed follow-up in the digitized KM data). The same T was applied to both OS and PFS to ensure internal coherence within the partitioned-survival framework. The extrapolated time horizon was aligned with the follow-up horizon reported in the updated publication. In this study, the extrapolation horizon refers to the target horizon T derived from the updated publication, rather than the additional interval beyond the initial publication follow-up. Specifically, when an initial publication reported survival data up to 12 months, and a subsequent updated publication extended follow-up to 24 months, the survival curve from the initial publication was extrapolated to 24 months using a smooth parametric distribution. Both RMST and parametric extrapolations were implemented using the R programming language, utilizing the “survHE (version 2.0.1)” and “flexsurv (version 2.3.2)” packages.

Parametric distributions evaluated for survival extrapolation included exponential, generalized gamma, Gompertz, log-logistic, log-normal, and Weibull models [7]. For each treatment arm and endpoint, the preferred distribution was selected on the basis of the Akaike information criterion (AIC) in combination with clinical plausibility assessment and diagnostic checks, including visual fit to Kaplan–Meier curves and hazard-shape assessment (Supplementary Table S2 and Supplementary Figs. S5 and S6).

Bias, Precision, and Agreement

Mean deviation (MD) and mean absolute error (MAE) were calculated using “observed” QALYs derived from updated KM curves through reconstructed IPD and RMST integration at the same time horizon as the reference standard. Specifically,MD=1n∑i=1nQALYpred,i-QALYobs,i and MAE=1n∑i=1nQALYpred,i-QALYobs,i. Importantly, these benchmark QALY estimates are derived from digitized survival curves and reconstructed IPD rather than original trial-level QALY measurements based on prospectively collected quality-of-life data. As such, minor digitization and reconstruction error may persist. Accordingly, prediction error in this study should be interpreted as deviation relative to the updated KM–RMST-based reference, rather than against an error-free ground truth. Positive deviation indicated overestimation, whereas negative values reflected underestimation. MAE quantifies the magnitude of predictive deviation regardless of direction. Subsequently, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for mean deviation and MAE were estimated using a trial-level cluster bootstrap to account for within-trial dependence between arms. Specifically, trials were resampled with replacement, and all corresponding arms within a selected trial were kept together; percentile-based CIs were reported from the bootstrap distribution. For correlation and regression analyses, the primary unit of analysis was the trial. When arm-level observations were analyzed, statistical inference was adjusted for trial-level clustering using cluster-robust standard errors, thereby avoiding the assumption of independence between arms within the same trial.

Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to assess the strength of association between extrapolated and observed QALY outcomes. In addition, linear regression analysis was conducted to explore the relationship between MAE and the target extrapolation horizon (T) for OS and PFS endpoints. This analytical approach enabled systematic assessment of whether extended survival extrapolation periods were associated with increased prediction error in parametric QALY estimation.

Sensitivity Analysis

Robustness was tested using Bayesian information criterion (BIC) for parametric extrapolation selection. Additional sensitivity analyses examined Spearman’s correlation and linear regression precision. Visual-fit and hazard-shape diagnostics—alongside assessment of clinical plausibility—were employed to support the evaluation of extrapolation models. These diagnostics were not restricted to sensitivity analyses; in this subsection, they were used to assess the robustness under alternative parametric forms, with particular attention to the behavior of PFS curves and potential deviations from proportional hazards. All analyses remained anchored to observed follow-up horizons to preserve the estimand. To enhance transparency, Trial-specific visual-fit plots comparing extrapolated curves (fitted to the initial publication) with updated KM follow-up within the common horizon (T) are presented in Supplementary Figs. S5 and S6. These diagnostics aimed to determine whether underestimation of PFS was primarily attributable to functional-form rigidity or to structural features of early PFS data. All statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.1) using ggplot2 (version 3.5.1) for visualization. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Selection of Pivotal Trials

A systematic review of NMPA approval notifications identified 79 clinical trials, of which 65 were excluded owing to eligibility criteria. The final analysis included 14 eligible RCTs encompassing 4839 patients. The inclusion flow diagram is presented in Fig. 1, and detailed characteristics of the included RCTs are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Description of Included Trials

The 14 pivotal trials predominantly targeted advanced or metastatic malignancies, with respiratory/intrathoracic cancers (64.3%, n = 9) representing the most common site, followed by lip/oral cavity/pharynx (14.3%, n = 2) and digestive organ cancers (14.3%, n = 2). First-line therapy was evaluated in 92.9% (n = 13) of trials. Median survival follow-up duration increased from 12.4 months in initial publications to 36.0 months in updated publications. Data maturity demonstrated substantial progression between publication stages, OS increased from 34.8% to 60.4%, while PFS maturity rose from 51.1% to 82.1% (Supplementary Table S4).

Bias, Precision, and Agreement

Comparative RMST analyses revealed close concordance between observed and extrapolated QALY estimates (mean deviation − 0.01, 95% CI − 0.03 to 0.01; MAE 0.03, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.04). While OS RMST showed minimal deviation (0.31 months, 95% CI − 0.17 to 0.80; MAE 0.56, 95% CI 0.17 to 0.95) and PFS analyses identified systematic underestimation in extrapolated data (mean deviation − 1.78 months, 95% CI − 3.23 to − 0.34; MAE 1.91, 95% CI 0.52 to 3.29) (Table 1).

Discussion

This study provides an empirical assessment of restricted-horizon QALY prediction error derived from early survival extrapolations for ICIs. By anchoring comparisons to later observed follow-up at a common horizon, the analysis complements existing survival-validation literature while expressing the results in a policy-facing metric.

Our findings corroborate established evidence of temporal error accumulation in survival modeling, where prior studies report 2.06-month and 1.76-month increases in MAE per extrapolation year for OS and PFS, respectively [13, 24]. Extending these observations to health utility metrics, we demonstrate a statistically significant linear association between the target extrapolation horizon (T) and QALY prediction error, revealing a fundamental methodological challenge: while extrapolation remains essential for modeling long-term outcomes from limited trial data, its inherent inaccuracies progressively compromise cost–effectiveness estimates as the projection horizon expands [25]. Typical 5–10 year simulation periods in cancer economic evaluations suggest that, under linear approximation observed associations, cumulative QALY errors may reach 0.11 [26]. This estimate is illustrative only and should not be interpreted as a validated long-horizon forecast. Long-term error trajectory may be nonlinear, and our inferences are anchored to the empirically observed range of the target extrapolation horizon (T). For context, with China’s 2023 per capita GDP at $12,614.06, this magnitude of QALY error would translate to $126.14 per capita net benefit discrepancies, highlighting the sensitivity of cost–effectiveness results to extrapolation error [27]. However, the long-horizon error trajectory is uncertain and may be nonlinear, and our inferences are anchored to the empirically observed range of extrapolation durations. Prediction error increased with longer target extrapolation horizons (T) and occurred in both directions, underscoring the need for structured evidence-update mechanisms—such as planned reassessment when longer follow-up becomes available—to manage decision risk, rather than implying a single prescriptive pricing approach. These results are intended to complement, rather than replace, lifetime decision models by quantifying empirically verifiable prediction error within the observed horizon [28]. Methodologically, our findings advocate the adoption of standardized HTA protocols that mandate quantification of the impact of extrapolation through sensitivity analyses. [29] This approach enables explicit mapping of prediction uncertainty to decision risk in ICI reimbursement policies, thereby balancing urgent therapeutic needs with sustainable resource allocation through risk-adjusted coverage policies.

Previous studies have demonstrated that standard parametric models tend to underestimate the mean survival duration of ICIs [30, 31], without specifically reporting prediction error in QALY terms. In contrast, our study expresses prediction error using QALYs as the primary outcome, a metric that combines survival and health-related quality of life, and is commonly used in economic evaluations [32]. Among the evaluated samples, 64.3% (9/14) showed QALY underestimation relative to updated clinical trial data, whereas 35.7% (5/14) demonstrated overestimation.

Notably, systematic underestimation was more pronounced for PFS than for OS. This pattern may reflect structural characteristics of PFS measurement in oncology trials. Unlike OS, PFS depends on scheduled radiologic assessments, introducing interval censoring and timing sensitivity. In the context of ICIs, pseudo-progression and delayed immune-related responses may further distort the shape of early PFS curves, leading to instability into hazard estimation when conventional parametric models are applied [9, 10]. These features may partially explain why extrapolations based on early PFS data tend to underestimate longer-term outcomes.

In addition, visual inspection and hazard-shape diagnostics revealed delayed separation and nonproportional hazards in several PFS curves, which standard monotonic parametric distributions may not adequately capture. Although our sensitivity analyses did not materially alter aggregate error estimates, these findings suggest that more flexible approaches (e.g., spline-based, piecewise, or hybrid models) may be particularly relevant for modeling PFS in the ICI setting [33]. Future validation studies with larger samples could formally compare the predictive performance of flexible versus standard parametric approaches within observed horizons.

We acknowledge that information criteria may favor nonmonotone hazard forms (e.g., log-normal or log-logistic) when fitting early follow-up data. Although these distributions can provide superior statistical fit within the observed period, they may imply declining hazards and heavy tails if extrapolated far beyond available follow-up. Because our estimand is restricted to an empirically verifiable horizon defined by updated follow-up data, our results do not assess or validate lifetime tail behavior. For lifetime decision models, explicit evaluation of long-term plausibility and external validation is essential, particularly when selected parametric distributions imply nonmonotone hazards.

This bidirectional variability underscores the limitations of relying solely on parametric models, particularly for ICIs, where long-term survival and quality-of-life outcomes may diverge from conventional assumptions owing to factors such as durable treatment responses or delayed immune-related adverse events [10]. These discrepancies highlight the need for more flexible extrapolation approaches—such as mixture-cure models or landmark analyses—or for incorporating external data sources such as cure fractions combined with background mortality rates or registry data, to improve the accuracy of survival projections. [14, 31, 34–36] In the primary analysis, we used standard parametric forms to evaluate a workflow that remains commonly applied in oncology economic evaluations and is readily comparable with the existing extrapolation-validation literature. This choice should not be interpreted as evidence that flexible models are unnecessary. In the immuno-oncology setting, more flexible approaches—such as spline-based, piecewise, mixture-cure, or hybrid models—may better capture delayed treatment effects, nonproportional hazards, or plateau-like survival patterns, particularly for PFS. A formal head-to-head comparison between standard and flexible models was beyond the scope of the present study and should be addressed in future validation work.

These implications for reimbursement policies are significant: underestimations may restrict patient access to cost-effective therapies, while overestimations risk overpayment and misallocation of limited healthcare resources. To address these challenges, policymakers should consider implementing dynamic pricing mechanisms or performance-based risk-sharing agreements [37] that ensure reimbursement decisions are aligned with evolving clinical and economic evidence.

Our study has several limitations. First, to estimate parametric model parameters and RMST, we reconstructed IPD rather than using primary survival data. Nevertheless, previous studies have demonstrated that RMST derived from reconstructed IPD displays excellent accuracy and predictive error compared with the gold standard, indicating that reconstructed IPD is an acceptable substitute for original trial data in RMST calculations [38]. Second, because some clinical trials do not publicly disclose patient quality-of-life (QoL) data, we sourced certain health utility values from published literature, which may introduce minor discrepancies relative to the original trial observations. Third, although parametric survival models are commonly extrapolated over a 5–10-year horizon, our estimand evaluates prediction error over a restricted, empirically verifiable horizon (T), defined by updated trial follow-up. This design ensures that extrapolation error can be directly benchmarked against observed data rather than being driven by additional tail assumptions. Consequently, a key limitation is that we do not evaluate lifetime-horizon prediction performance typically required for reimbursement decision-making. Evaluating lifetime performance would require additional approaches, such as simulation studies or incorporation of external long-term data sources. Uncertainty over lifetime horizon would plausibly be greater, but quantifying it necessitates assumptions beyond observed data. Fourth, our results may be influenced by specific treatment types or cancer indications; however, owing to the small sample size, subgroup analyses were not feasible. Similarly, we were unable to formally stratify PFS bias by tumor type or hazard-shape classification, nor to fit multivariable models jointly incorporating target extrapolation horizon (T) and initial data maturity. These factors are conceptually distinct and may represent important sources of heterogeneity in extrapolation performance that warrant evaluation in larger datasets. Fifth, we did not formally compare standard parametric models with more flexible extrapolation approaches. Given the potential for delayed treatment effects, nonproportional hazards, and plateau-like survival patterns in immuno-oncology, flexible models may offer better fit and predictive performance in some settings, particularly for PFS. Sixth, utilities were assumed constant and not age-adjusted, which may overestimate absolute QALY levels, especially over longer horizons. Because both projected and benchmark QALYs were evaluated over the same follow-up horizon (T), age-adjustment would mainly affect absolute QALY levels rather than their difference, although it may slightly reduce absolute deviations. Incorporating age-adjusted utilities will be important for future lifetime-horizon analyses.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates a positive association between target extrapolation horizon (T) and QALY prediction error for ICIs, indicating that longer projection horizons introduce greater uncertainty into QALY estimates at observed horizons. These findings highlight the need for predefined horizon selection and transparent reporting of extrapolation-driven uncertainty in economic evaluations. Considering these challenges, future research should focus on developing methods that reduce observed horizon prediction error (e.g., flexible or hybrid approaches justified by hazard structure and data maturity). In addition, our findings support the consideration of structured evidence updates and planned reassessment processes when longer follow-up becomes available. It is important to emphasize that, because our estimand is restricted to empirically verifiable horizons, these results should not be interpreted as evidence that lifetime tail extrapolations or unrestricted lifetime QALYs are accurate. Any reimbursement implications should be therefore considered in conjunction with broader lifetime modeling and external validation.
