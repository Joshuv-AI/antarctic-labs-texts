Two-dimensional electronic spectra from trajectory-based dynamics: pure-state Ehrenfest and spin-mapping approaches
Annina Z. Lieberherr, Joseph Kelly, Johan E. Runeson, Thomas E. Markland, David E. Manolopoulos

Abstract
Two-dimensional electronic spectroscopy (2DES) provides a detailed picture of electronically nonadiabatic dynamics that can be interpreted with the aid of simulations. Here, we develop and contrast trajectory-based nonadiabatic dynamics approaches for simulating 2DES spectra. First, we argue that an improved pure-state Ehrenfest approach can be constructed by decomposing the initial coherence into a sum of equatorial pure states that contain equal contributions from the states in the coherence. We then use this framework to show how one can obtain a more accurate, but computationally more expensive, approximation to the third-order 2DES response function by replacing Ehrenfest dynamics with spin mapping during the pump-probe delay time. We end by comparing and contrasting the accuracy of these methods in reproducing the exact linear, pump-probe, and 2DES spectra of two Frenkel exciton models: a coupled dimer system and the Fenna–Matthews–Olson (FMO) complex.

Introduction

Two-dimensional electronic spectroscopy

;

;

;

;

;

These methods have been successful in simulating the population dynamics of nonadiabatic systems ranging from system-bath models to systems with atomistic force fields,

;
Aieta et al. 2025
;

;
Lawrence et al. 2024
Extending them to allow for the calculation of advanced spectroscopies such as 2DES offers the opportunity to create practical methods to simulate and understand the information encoded in these spectra. However, this poses an additional challenge because it requires the calculation of multi-time population and coherence correlation functions.

;
Hamm 2005

Methods from various tiers of the MQC hierarchy have recently been extended to calculate 2DES spectra. Higher level approaches that employ partial linearization, such as the partially linearized density matrix (PLDM) formulation,

;
Provazza et al. 2018

For example, to compute the 2DES spectrum of a Frenkel exciton model representing the Fenna–Matthews–Olson (FMO) complex, with a ground state, 7 singly excited states, and 21 doubly excited states, a PLDM-based approach incorporating spin mapping variables (spin-PLDM) used

Provazza et al. 2018

10^{6}

Using a lower level of the MQC hierarchy, with full linearization of both the electronic and nuclear degrees of freedom, the linearized semiclassical initial value representation

However, while fully linearized methods are less computationally expensive than PLDM, they still require roughly an order of magnitude more trajectories to converge than Ehrenfest dynamics because they involve averaging over an initial distribution of mapping variables. The lower computational cost of Ehrenfest dynamics makes it more suitable for simulations with
ab initio

30\text{\,}\mathrm{f}\mathrm{s}

When simulating a 2DES spectrum, the dynamics of the electronic system must be initialized in a coherence, which complicates the implementation of the Ehrenfest method. There are various ways to deal with this, two of which have been suggested in the previous literature. Van der Vegte
et al.
van der Vegte et al. 2013
used a classical path approximation in which the force on the nuclei was taken to be the average of the Ehrenfest forces of the two states appearing in the coherence. Atsango
et al.
Atsango, Montoya-Castillo, and Markland 2023
developed what we shall refer to here as a ‘polar’ pure state Ehrenfest method, in which the coherence is first decomposed into a sum of four pure states which are then propagated using standard Ehrenfest dynamics.

Here we shall describe a different decomposition of the coherence into ‘equatorial’ pure states, each of which contains an equal contribution from the two states in the coherence. We will show that this equatorial decomposition allows us to collapse two of the three pure state summations that arise in the third-order response function and that this leads to significant improvements in accuracy at a 32-fold reduced computational cost compared to the polar decomposition.
Atsango, Montoya-Castillo, and Markland 2023
The resulting scheme is equivalent to making the classical path approximation
van der Vegte et al. 2013
during the first (
t_{1}
) and last (
t_{3}
) stages of the time evolution that is used to calculate the response function, but it differs during the delay time
t_{2}
between the pump and probe pulses of a pump-probe experiment. We will end by showing that the lack of detailed balance in Ehrenfest dynamics leads to incorrect peak heights in pump-probe and 2DES spectra, and that spin mapping can be used to alleviate this problem.

III
Pure-state Ehrenfest Methods

Ehrenfest dynamics was originally introduced in the wavefunction picture
McLachlan 1964
;
Tully 1998
but it can also be obtained in a density matrix formulation.
Grunwald, Kelly, and Kapral 2009
The action of light-matter interaction operators on the density matrix leads to the construction and propagation of coherences. There are multiple ways to represent these coherences in terms of pure states, which lead to different accuracies and efficiencies within Ehrenfest theory
Montoya-Castillo and Reichman 2016
;
Atsango, Montoya-Castillo, and Markland 2023
. In this section, we provide the background and notation needed to understand this issue, and introduce the pure-state decompositions that will be compared in Sec.
V
.

In the Ehrenfest approach
McLachlan 1964
;
Tully 1998
one separates the total Hamiltonian into a chosen partitioning of system and bath Hamiltonians,
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{S}}
and
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{B}}
, and the coupling between them,
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{SB}}
. Upon invoking a mean field interaction between the system and bath, and taking the classical limit of the bath degrees of freedom, one obtains

\hat{H}({\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{q}})=\hat{H}_{\mathrm{S}}+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{SB}}(\boldsymbol{q})+H_{\rm B}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})\hat{I},

(6)

where
\hat{I}
is the identity operator on the system Hilbert space and
\boldsymbol{q}
and
\boldsymbol{p}
are the classical positions and momenta of the bath degrees of freedom. The Ehrenfest equations of motion are

\displaystyle\dot{|\Psi\rangle}

\displaystyle=-i(\hat{{H}}_{\mathrm{S}}+\hat{{H}}_{\mathrm{SB}})|\Psi\rangle,

(7a)

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}

\displaystyle=-\langle\Psi|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{q}}\hat{H}|\Psi\rangle,

(7b)

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{p},

(7c)

where
|\Psi\rangle
is the normalized wavefunction of the system which evolves under the time-dependent Schrödinger equation. The bath degrees of freedom (
\boldsymbol{p},~\boldsymbol{q}
) evolve under classical equations of motion experiencing the average force from the quantum state
|\Psi\rangle
. To obtain the response functions in Eq. (
2
), the quantum trace is approximated by the mixed quantum-classical trace

\mathrm{Tr}\{\cdot\}\approx\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\mathcal{K}}}\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{p}\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{q}\;\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{S}}\{\cdot\},

(8)

where
\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{S}}
is a trace over the system degrees of freedom and
\mathcal{K}
is the total number of bath degrees of freedom. We assume that the initial density operator can be factorized as

\hat{\rho}_{0}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})=\rho_{\mathrm{B}}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})\hat{\rho}_{0,\rm S},

(9)

where
\hat{\rho}_{0,\rm S}=|0\rangle\langle 0|
is the system reduced density matrix and
\rho_{\rm B}({\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{q}})
is the bath density of the classical degrees of freedom. The latter can either be sampled from the classical Boltzmann distribution or from the Wigner distribution as described in Sec.
IV.1
.

Starting from the ground-state density in Eq. (
9
), the initial dipole interactions
\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{\mu}_{-}
and
\hat{\mu}_{+}\hat{\rho}_{0}
in Eq. (
2
) yield the coherences

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{+}\hat{\rho}_{0}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})=|\mu\rangle\rho_{\mathrm{B}}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})\langle 0|,

(10a)

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{0}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})\hat{\mu}_{-}=|0\rangle\rho_{\mathrm{B}}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})\langle\mu|,

(10b)

where
|\mu\rangle=\hat{\mu}_{+}|0\rangle
and
\langle\mu|=\langle 0|\hat{\mu}_{-}
. These coherences present a challenge for Ehrenfest dynamics since they have zero trace and therefore cannot be propagated directly.
Montoya-Castillo and Reichman 2016
;
Atsango, Montoya-Castillo, and Markland 2023
To overcome this issue, one can decompose each of them into a sum of pure states,

where
|a\rangle\langle b|
is the coherence,
|j\rangle\langle j|

is a scalar weight. There are many such pure-state decompositions. Here, we shall consider the particular decompositions depicted in Figs.

However, it degrades in accuracy when

Another problem with the polar pure states is that they contain unequal contributions from the two states in the coherence. For example,

;
Shi and Geva 2005
It would thus be advantageous to develop a scheme in which each pure state contains equal contributions of the two states

III.2
Equatorial Decomposition

We shall show in this section that the proliferation of polar pure state branching trajectories can be avoided by defining the equatorial pure states

\displaystyle|\phi_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|a\rangle+e^{ij\frac{\pi}{2}}|b\rangle),

\sum_{j=0}^{3}w_{j}^{(\rm e)}e^{-ij\frac{\pi}{2}}=2.

\displaystyle F_{j}(\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=-\langle\overline{\overline{\phi}}_{j}|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{q}}\hat{{H}}|\overline{\overline{\phi}}_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{\langle a|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{q}}\hat{{H}}|a\rangle}{\langle a|a\rangle}+\frac{\langle b|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{q}}\hat{{H}}|b\rangle}{\langle b|b\rangle}\right],

in which a single coherence is propagated on the average potential energy surface of its bra and ket states during all three time intervals. Each of the 4 equatorial pure states that is propagated through
t_{2}
is converted into a coherence by the next dipole interaction, but since this can be dealt with using the resummation trick in Eq. (
III.2
) it only produces one new state to be propagated through
t_{3}
. The net result is thus that, while the polar decomposition requires the propagation of 64 pure states through
t_{3}
, the equatorial decomposition only requires the propagation of 4. The
t_{3}
evolution dominates the calculation because it has to be done separately for each value of
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
that are used to calculate the 2DES spectrum, so the resummation amounts to a reduction in the overall computational effort by a factor of 16.

We can also find a further factor of 2 by exploiting the symmetries of the rephasing (Eq. (
3
)) and non-rephasing (Eq. (
4
)) response functions. For each of the three physical processes captured by 2DES, SE (
\Phi_{1}
,
\Phi_{4}
), GSB (
\Phi_{2}
,
\Phi_{5}
), and ESA (
\Phi_{3}
,
\Phi_{6}
), one of the contributing terms is a rephasing pathway while the other is a non-rephasing pathway. The density matrix propagated through
t_{2}
in each of these pairs is the adjoint of the other, e.g.
\mu_{+}|0(t_{1})\rangle\langle\mu(t_{1})|
and
|\mu(t_{1})\rangle\langle 0(t_{1})|\mu_{-}
in
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{4}
, respectively.
In the equatorial decomposition, one can exploit this symmetry simply by complex-conjugating the weights of the pure states,

\displaystyle|b\rangle\langle a|

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{3}w_{j}^{(\rm e)\ast}|\phi_{j}\rangle\langle\phi_{j}|,

(24)

where
|\phi_{j}\rangle
are the pure states obtained from decomposing
|a\rangle\langle b|
.
The non-rephasing response functions can therefore be obtained for free during the calculation of the rephasing functions, saving a further factor 2 in computational effort. With this additional saving, the equatorial pure state method only requires two times more force evaluations per initial trajectory than the mean classical path approximation of van der Vegte
et al.

whereas the polar pure-state decomposition requires 64 times more.

III.3
Use of Spin-Mapping to Improve Population Dynamics

Ehrenfest dynamics has significant detailed-balance issues which lead to incorrectly predicted quantum state populations. We shall therefore investigate whether the population dynamics that occurs during the
t_{2}
evolution can be improved by replacing Ehrenfest propagation with a more accurate method based on generalized spin mapping.
Runeson and Richardson 2020

We choose spin mapping because it has been shown to give better population dynamics than Ehrenfest for the FMO model,
Runeson and Richardson 2020
because it is straightforward to implement, and because it can use time steps of a similar size to Ehrenfest (as opposed to MASH, for example, which requires smaller times steps
Runeson and Manolopoulos 2023
).

Spin mapping differs from Ehrenfest dynamics in three crucial respects: the initialization of the system variables, the calculation of the reduced density matrix, and the equations of motion for the bath variables. We shall now explain how we implement these changes in the present context.

For each of the four pure states
|{\phi}_{j}\rangle\langle{\phi}_{j}|
created at the beginning of the
t_{2}
evolution [Eq. (
13
)], we first normalize
|{\phi}_{j}\rangle
to obtain
|\tilde{\phi}_{j}\rangle=|\phi_{j}\rangle\langle\phi_{j}|\phi_{j}\rangle^{-1/2}
, and keep
\langle\phi_{j}|\phi_{j}\rangle
for later use. We then sample
M
normalized system wavefunctions
|c_{m}\rangle
from the density
|\tilde{\phi}_{j}\rangle\langle\tilde{\phi}_{j}|
, where
M
is the number of states in the relevant excitation manifold (
M=1
for the GSB response functions and
M=N
for SE and ESA, where
N
is the number of singly excited states). The sampling is done in such a way that the initial spin-mapping estimator of the density operator is exactly equal to
|\tilde{\phi}_{j}\rangle\langle\tilde{\phi}_{j}|
when averaged over
m
from 1 to
M
. The ‘doubly focused’ sampling algorithm we use to do this is described in detail in the supplementary information (SI, Sec. S1).

Second, to calculate the spin-mapping density operator of each sample
|c_{m}(t)\rangle
during the
t_{2}
interval, we use the estimator
Runeson and Richardson 2020

\hat{\rho}_{{\rm S},m}(t)=\sqrt{M+1}|c_{m}(t)\rangle\langle c_{m}(t)|-\frac{\sqrt{M+1}-1}{M}\hat{I}_{M},

(25)

where
\hat{I}_{M}
is the identity operator within the manifold. In the ground state manifold where there is just one non-degenerate state
|0\rangle
, it is easy to verify that Eq. (
25
) simplifies to the Ehrenfest reduced density matrix
|0\rangle\langle 0|
. Therefore, introducing spin mapping does not change the calculation of the GSB response functions. However, it will modify the reduced density matrix for the SE and ESA pathways when there is more than one coupled excited state.

During the
t_{2}
evolution, each of the
M
system wavefunctions
|c_{m}(t)\rangle
satisfies its own time-dependent Schrödinger equation [Eq. (
7a
)] and generates its own force

F_{m}({\boldsymbol{q}})=-{\rm Tr}\left\{\hat{\rho}_{{\rm S},m}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{q}}\hat{H}\right\}

(26)

on the bath variables. The bath trajectories are therefore different for each of the
M
wavefunctions. At the end of the
t_{2}
evolution, we diagonalize each
\hat{\rho}_{\rm S,m}(t_{2})
to obtain a weighted sum of
M
pure states,

\hat{\rho}_{{\rm S},m}(t_{2})=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\rho_{k,m}(t_{2})|k,m(t_{2})\rangle\langle k,m(t_{2})|,

(27)

each of which can then be treated in the same way as
|\phi_{j}(t_{2})\rangle\langle\phi_{j}(t_{2})|
is treated in the equatorial pure state Ehrenfest calculation. The only differences are that, after the application of the next dipole operator, we now have
M^{2}
pure states to propagate through
t_{3}
instead of just one, and that the contribution of each of these to the target response function must be multiplied by an appropriate weight,
w_{j,k,m}=\langle\phi_{j}|\phi_{j}\rangle\rho_{k,m}(t_{2})
.

In summary, using this implementation of spin mapping for the
t_{2}
evolution yields an approach that leaves the GSB pathways unchanged but is expected to improve the population dynamics of the SE and ESA pathways, at the cost of having to run
N^{2}
more trajectory branches for each of them.

IV
Application to Frenkel Exciton Hamiltonians

We will now define the Frenkel exciton Hamiltonian we have used in our benchmark calculations and explain how the methods we have described above can be used to compute its linear and nonlinear response functions.

IV.1
Hamiltonian

The Frenkel exciton Hamiltonian consists of a set of
N
sites, each with a two-level electronic system coupled to a bath of harmonic oscillators,

\hat{{H}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\hat{H}^{g}&0&0\\
0&\hat{H}^{s}&0\\
0&0&\hat{H}^{d}\end{array}\right)+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{B}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{p}},\hat{\boldsymbol{q}}).

(28)

The bath Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{B}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{p}},\hat{\boldsymbol{q}})
consists of
K
harmonic oscillators on each of the
N
electronic sites, giving
{\mathcal{K}}=KN
in Eq. (
8
):

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{B}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{p}},\hat{\boldsymbol{q}})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\hat{p}_{kn}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\omega_{k}^{2}\hat{q}_{kn}^{2}\right).

(29)

Here
\omega_{k}
are the bath mode frequencies and
\hat{q}_{kn}
and
\hat{p}_{kn}
are their mass-scaled position and momentum operators.

The Hamiltonian
\hat{{H}}
is block-diagonal in the ground, singly excited and doubly excited manifolds, with respective Hamiltonians
\hat{H}^{g}
,
\hat{H}^{s}
and
\hat{H}^{d}
.
The ground state energy
\varepsilon_{0}
is set to zero, and therefore
\hat{H}^{g}=\varepsilon_{0}|0\rangle\langle 0|=0
.
The Hamiltonian of the singly excited manifold is

\hat{H}^{s}=\hat{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{S}}+\hat{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{SB}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{q}}),

(30)

where
\hat{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{S}}
is the Hamiltonian for the excitonic system and
\hat{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{SB}}
is the system-bath coupling.
The system Hamiltonian is

\hat{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{S}}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{n}|n\rangle\langle n|+\sum_{m>n}^{N}J_{nm}\left(|n\rangle\langle m|+|m\rangle\langle n|\right),

(31)

where
\varepsilon_{n}
is the site excitation energy,
J_{nm}
is the coupling between sites
n
and
m
, and
N
is the number of sites.
The system-bath coupling Hamiltonian is

\hat{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{SB}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{q}})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{k}\hat{q}_{kn}|n\rangle\langle n|,

(32)

with coupling strengths
c_{k}
.

The Hamiltonian in the doubly excited manifold consists of states that are simultaneous excitations of two different sites. It does not matter which order they are excited in. We use the states
|nm\rangle
with
m>n
as a basis for
\hat{H}^{d}
, which can then be written as

\hat{H}^{d}=\hat{H}^{d}_{\mathrm{S}}+\hat{H}^{d}_{\mathrm{SB}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{q}})

(33)

with
Hein et al. 2012

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{d}_{\rm S}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m>n}^{N}(\varepsilon_{n}+\varepsilon_{m})|nm\rangle\langle nm|

\displaystyle+\sum_{m>n>l}^{N}J_{nm}\left(|ln\rangle\langle lm|+|lm\rangle\langle ln|\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{m>l>n}^{N}J_{nm}\left(|nl\rangle\langle lm|+|lm\rangle\langle nl|\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{l>m>n}^{N}J_{nm}\left(|nl\rangle\langle ml|+|ml\rangle\langle nl|\right)

(34)

and

\hat{H}^{d}_{\mathrm{SB}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{q}})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{m>n}^{N}c_{k}(\hat{q}_{kn}+\hat{q}_{km})|nm\rangle\langle nm|.

(35)

The dipole moment operator couples the ground state to the singly excited manifold, and the singly excited manifold to the doubly excited manifold. The excitation component of the dipole coupling between the ground and singly excited states is

\hat{\mu}^{sg}_{+}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mu_{n}|n\rangle\langle 0|,

(36)

and that between the single and double excitation manifolds is
Hein et al. 2012

\hat{\mu}^{ds}_{+}=\sum_{m>n}^{N}\left(\mu_{m}|nm\rangle\langle n|+\mu_{n}|nm\rangle\langle m|\right).

(37)

The full dipole excitation operator is thus

\hat{\mu}_{+}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&0&0\\
\hat{\mu}^{sg}_{+}&0&0\\
0&\hat{\mu}^{ds}_{+}&0\end{array}\right),

(38)

and the full dipole moment operator is
\hat{\mu}=\hat{\mu}_{+}+\hat{\mu}_{-}
with
\hat{\mu}_{-}=(\hat{\mu}_{+})^{\dagger}
.

For simplicity, we use the same parameters
\{c_{k},\omega_{k}\}
for the bath associated with each of the
N
electronic sites (Eqs. (
29
) and (
32
)). Each bath has a Debye spectral density,

J(\omega)=\frac{2\lambda\omega_{c}\omega}{\omega^{2}+\omega_{c}^{2}}=\frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{c_{k}^{2}}{\omega_{k}}\delta(\omega-\omega_{k}),

(39)

where
\lambda
is the reorganization energy and
\omega_{c}
is the characteristic bath frequency. The bath relaxation time is
\tau_{c}={\omega_{c}}^{-1}
.
We discretize the spectral density using a method
Wang, Thoss, and Miller 2001
;
Craig, Thoss, and Wang 2007
that gives the correct reorganization energy,
\lambda=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{J(\omega)}{\omega}\mathrm{d}\omega
, for any choice of
K
.

The initial bath degrees of freedom can either be sampled from a classical Boltzmann distribution, or from a Wigner distribution to allow for thermal quantum effects. Both can be written in the form
Wang, Sun, and Miller 1998

\rho_{\mathrm{B}}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})=\prod_{k,n}\frac{\sigma_{k}}{\pi}\exp\left[-\frac{2\sigma_{k}}{\omega_{k}}\left(\frac{p_{kn}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\omega_{k}^{2}q_{kn}^{2}}{2}\right)\right],

(40)

where
\sigma_{k}=\tanh\left({\beta\omega_{k}}/{2}\right)
in the Wigner case and
\beta\omega_{k}/2
in the classical limit, with
\beta={1}/{k_{\rm B}T}
. The system density operator is initialized in the ground state,

\hat{\rho}_{0,\rm S}=|0\rangle\langle 0|.

(41)

IV.2
Nonlinear Response Functions in the Equatorial Decomposition

To show how the equatorial decomposition is used in more detail, we will now derive an explicit expression for the
\Phi_{3}
response function, and then summarize how one obtains the analogous expressions for the remaining five response functions.

For
\Phi_{3}
, we begin by propagating the equatorial pure state
|\phi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle+|\mu\rangle)
through
t_{1}
, where we no longer include the pure state index,
j
, because only one state remains after summing over the pure states for the
t_{1}
propagation. Because
|0\rangle
and
|\mu\rangle
belong to different manifolds, we can invoke Eq. (
III.2
) in the form

\mathcal{G}(t_{1})(\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{\mu}_{-})=|0(t_{1})\rangle\langle\mu(t_{1})|,

(42)

and

|0(t_{1})\rangle=\sqrt{2}\hat{P}_{g}|\phi(t_{1})\rangle,

(43a)

|\mu(t_{1})\rangle=\sqrt{2}\hat{P}_{s}|\phi(t_{1})\rangle,

(43b)

where
\hat{P}_{g}=|0\rangle\langle 0|
is the projection on the ground state and
\hat{P}_{s}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}|n\rangle\langle n|
is the projection on the singly excited manifold.
The nuclei experience the average force

F(\boldsymbol{q})=-\frac{1}{2}\left[\langle 0|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{q}}\hat{{H}}|0\rangle+\frac{\langle\mu|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{q}}\hat{{H}}|\mu\rangle}{\langle\mu|\mu\rangle}\right]

(44)

during the
t_{1}
evolution.

The interaction with the second dipole moment operator acts on the ket
|0(t_{1})\rangle
to give
\hat{\mu}_{+}|0(t_{1})\rangle
. After this operation, both states in the resulting coherence are in the singly excited manifold,
\hat{\mu}_{+}|0(t_{1})\rangle\langle\mu(t_{1})|
. We then use Eq. (
11
) to expand this in terms of four new equatorial pure states

|\phi_{3,j}^{(t_{1})}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\hat{\mu}_{+}|0(t_{1})\rangle+e^{ij\frac{\pi}{2}}|\mu(t_{1})\rangle\right],

(45)

where the subscript ‘3’ indicates the target response function,
\Phi_{3}
.
The states
|\phi_{3,j}^{(t_{1})}\rangle
evolve along different nuclear trajectories during
t_{2}
in accordance with Eq. (
7
) and under the force defined in Eq. (
17
). The third dipole moment operator then acts on each pure state separately, but for each of them this operation creates only one new state to be propagated through
t_{3}
,

|\phi_{3,j}^{(t_{2},t_{1})}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\;\hat{\mu}_{+}|\phi_{3,j}^{(t_{1})}(t_{2})\rangle+|\phi_{3,j}^{(t_{1})}(t_{2})\rangle\;\right].

(46)

All that remains after the evolution through
t_{3}
is to project out the necessary components of the time-evolved state
|\phi_{3,j}^{(t_{2},t_{1})}(t_{3})\rangle
, apply the final dipole moment operator, and evaluate the trace in Eq. (
2
). The final result for
\Phi_{3}
is thus

\displaystyle\Phi_{3}(t_{3},t_{2},t_{1})

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{p}\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{q}\,\rho_{\mathrm{B}}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})\,\sum_{j=0}^{3}2w_{j}^{(\rm e)}\times

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{3,j}^{(t_{2},t_{1})}(t_{3})|\hat{P}_{s}\hat{\mu}_{-}\hat{P}_{d}|\phi_{3,j}^{(t_{2},t_{1})}(t_{3})\rangle,

(47)

where
\hat{P}_{s}
and
\hat{P}_{d}
are projection operators onto the singly and doubly excited manifolds, respectively, and the factor of 2 comes from Eq. (
23
).

\Phi_{6}
can be obtained from the same expression simply by replacing the weights
w_{j}^{(\rm e)}
with their complex conjugates.

\Phi_{1}
has the same Feynman diagram as
\Phi_{3}
up to the third dipole interaction. We can therefore reuse
|\phi_{3,j}^{(t_{1})}(t_{2})\rangle
to define the states for the
t_{3}
propagation,

|\phi_{1,j}^{(t_{2},t_{1})}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[|\phi_{3,j}^{(t_{1})}(t_{2})\rangle+\hat{\mu}_{-}|\phi_{3,j}^{(t_{1})}(t_{2})\rangle\right].

(48)

The final result is

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}(t_{3},t_{2},t_{1})=

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{p}\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{q}\,\rho_{\mathrm{B}}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})\sum_{j=0}^{3}2w_{j}^{(\rm e)}\times

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{1,j}^{(t_{2},t_{1})}(t_{3})|\hat{P}_{g}\hat{\mu}_{-}\hat{P}_{s}|\phi_{1,j}^{(t_{2},t_{1})}(t_{3})\rangle.

(49)

By replacing the weights with their complex conjugates, we obtain
\Phi_{4}
.

For
\Phi_{2}
, the analogue of Eq. (
45
) is

|\phi_{2,j}^{(t_{1})}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[|0(t_{1})\rangle+e^{ij\frac{\pi}{2}}\hat{\mu}_{-}|\mu(t_{1})\rangle\right]

(50)

Since this is proportional to the ground state
|0\rangle
, it can be propagated through
t_{2}
(to within an irrelevant overall phase factor) simply by evolving the nuclei on the ground state potential (under the influence of
H_{\rm B}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})
).
We can then propagate the states

|\phi_{2,j}^{(t_{2},t_{1})}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\hat{\mu}_{+}|\phi_{2,j}^{(t_{1})}(t_{2})\rangle+|\phi_{2,j}^{(t_{1})}(t_{2})\rangle\right]

(51)

through
t_{3}
and obtain
\Phi_{2}
as

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}(t_{3},t_{2},t_{1})=

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{p}\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{q}\,\rho_{\mathrm{B}}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})\sum_{j=0}^{3}2w_{j}^{(\rm e)}\times

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{2,j}^{(t_{2},t_{1})}(t_{3})|\hat{P}_{g}\hat{\mu}_{-}\hat{P}_{s}|\phi_{2,j}^{(t_{2},t_{1})}(t_{3})\rangle.

(52)

This time we get
\Phi_{5}
by complex conjugating the weights.

The key simplification in this algorithm is the summation trick in Eq. (
III.2
), which is only possible because the pure states used in the
t_{1}
and
t_{3}
evolutions lie on the equator and have identical nuclear motion paths.
More generally, pure states at any given latitude on the Bloch sphere have the same nuclear trajectories. However, equal contributions to the forces on the nuclei from the states in the
|a\rangle
and
|b\rangle
manifolds only occur on the equator.
With anything other than equatorial pure states, we would populate the wrong excitation manifolds and produce dynamics that are inconsistent with the Feynman diagrams.
In the supplementary information, we compare the polar and equatorial wavefunction dynamics in detail (SI, Sec. S2).

IV.3
Linear Absorption Spectroscopy

Here we compare how the polar and equatorial Ehrenfest methods, our spin-mapping adaptation, and the mean classical path approximation treat the linear absorption spectrum, which is proportional to the Fourier transform

I(\omega)=\mathrm{Re}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-i\omega t}R^{(1)}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

(53)

of the first-order response function

R^{(1)}(t)=\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{\mu}_{-}\mathcal{G}(t)[\hat{\mu}_{+}\hat{\rho}_{0}]\}.

(54)

This response function only depends on one time variable: it is equivalent to calculating the third-order response of a nonrephasing pathway through
t_{1}
only (e.g., Eq. (
2d
)). The equatorial Ehrenfest, mean classical path, and spin mapping methods all treat the
t_{1}
evolution in the same way, and therefore yield the same result when applied to the linear spectrum. However, the polar Ehrenfest method yields a slightly different result because of a subtle difference in the way it treats the evolution of the bath degrees of freedom.

In the polar Ehrenfest method, four pure states are propagated up to time
t
. However, the polar states
|\psi_{0}\rangle=|\mu\rangle
and
|\psi_{1}\rangle=|0\rangle
do not contribute to
R^{(1)}(t)
, since they vanish in the final trace with
\hat{\mu}_{-}
.
Only the coherences
|\psi_{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|\mu\rangle+|0\rangle)
and
|\psi_{3}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|\mu\rangle+i|0\rangle)
contribute, giving

\displaystyle R^{(1)}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left\{\hat{\mu}_{-}\left(|\psi_{2}(t)\rangle\langle\psi_{2}(t)|+i|\psi_{3}(t)\rangle\langle\psi_{3}(t)|\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left\{\hat{\mu}_{-}|\mu(t)\rangle\langle 0(t)|\right\}.

(55)

This is almost the same answer as one obtains in the equatorial decomposition [see Eq. (
42
)], except that the nuclear degrees of freedom in the polar Ehrenfest method evolve under the force

F_{\mathrm{pol}}(\boldsymbol{q})=-\frac{1}{1+c_{\mu}}(\langle\mu|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{q}}\hat{{H}}|\mu\rangle+\langle 0|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{q}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}|0\rangle),

(56)

where
c_{\mu}=\langle\mu|\mu\rangle=\sum_{n}\mu_{n}^{2}
.
This is subtly different from the equatorial force in Eq. (
44
) because, motivated by the success of the Wigner-averaged classical limit for linear spectroscopy,
Egorov, Rabani, and Berne 1999
;
Shi and Geva 2005
we have chosen to use balanced forces in the equatorial Ehrenfest method as described in Sec. III.B. This results in a small difference between the predictions of the polar and equatorial methods for the linear spectrum of the biexciton model, as we will see in Fig. 5 below.

VI
Conclusion

We have shown how one can develop a more accurate and computationally efficient pure-state Ehrenfest method
Atsango, Montoya-Castillo, and Markland 2023
by decomposing coherences into equatorial pure states. Unlike the original polar pure states,
Atsango, Montoya-Castillo, and Markland 2023
these equatorial pure states are compatible with a resummation trick that significantly streamlines the calculation [see Eq. (
III.2
)]. We have benchmarked the accuracy of this approach by using it to simulate the 2DES and pump probe spectra of two Frenkel exciton models, a 2-site biexciton model and a 7-site FMO complex, both with numerically exact HEOM results for comparison. For the biexciton model, we have shown that the number of force evaluations is reduced by a factor approaching 32 compared with the previous polar Ehrenfest approach,
Atsango, Montoya-Castillo, and Markland 2023
while at the same time giving more accurate results.

The pure-state decomposition that precedes the delay time evolution in the equatorial Ehrenfest method cannot be streamlined by resummation. However, it could be eliminated by making a mean classical path approximation to the bath dynamics as suggested by van der Vegte
et al.
.
van der Vegte et al. 2013
This would further reduce the number of force evaluations by a factor of 2, and it is unlikely that it would entail much loss of accuracy since the error in Ehrenfest dynamics is dominated by its failure to satisfy detailed balance whether or not one makes the mean classical path approximation.
van der Vegte et al. 2013
To address the detailed balance issue, we have used the equatorial pure state formulation to introduce spin mapping variables for the delay time evolution, and shown that they do indeed slightly improve the accuracy of 2DES spectra, although at an additional computational cost [see Fig. 10].

The equatorial Ehrenfest approach is able to converge the 2DES spectrum sufficiently well for analysis purposes with just 2,000 trajectories. This is true for both of the models we have considered here, which require
\sim 3\times 10^{8}
force evaluations with this many trajectories (SI, Sec. S5). Recently, a machine learned potential trained on
ab initio
data using an equivariant transformer
Vaswani et al. 2017
;
Tholke and De Fabritiis 2021
architecture was found to generate energies and forces at a rate of 25 configurations per second using the TorchMD-Net package
Pelaez et al. 2024
on a single GPU for a Nile blue chromophore solvated by 175 ethanol molecules (1619 atoms).
Kelly et al. 2025
Calculating
3\times 10^{8}
forces from this machine learned potential would take over a year on a single GPU, but with 24 GPUs the calculation could be completed within 3 weeks. Thus, the present equatorial approach, or the mean classical path approximation,
van der Vegte et al. 2013
in conjunction with ongoing advancements in machine learned potentials, could offer a path to fully atomistic simulations of condensed phase 2DES spectra based on
ab initio
data.

We have, of course, not entirely eliminated the failures of Ehrenfest dynamics itself. The partial introduction of spin mapping variables does not fully satisfy detailed balance and therefore still gives inaccurate peak intensities in the 2DES spectrum at long time delays. In some systems, this problem could be exacerbated by zero-point energy leakage from high-frequency bath modes when using an initial Wigner distribution for the vibrational bath. The failure of Ehrenfest dynamics to satisfy detailed balance could be fixed by replacing the delay time evolution with a method that does, such as MASH.
Runeson and Manolopoulos 2023
The zero-point energy leakage from Wigner initial conditions could be addressed by using a variational polaron transformation
Yarkony and Silbey 1976
;
Wang and Zhao 2020
to eliminate the high-frequency bath modes from the calculation, as has recently been done in a study of exciton energy transfer in biological light-harvesting complexes.
Runeson and Manolopoulos 2025
We may explore these possibilities in future work.
