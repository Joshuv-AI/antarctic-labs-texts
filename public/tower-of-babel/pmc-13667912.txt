Mitochondrial Fis1 tunes Rab7A activity via TBC1D15 modulating STING degradation
Authors: Eunice Dominguez-Martin, Linlin Lei, Jennifer D Petersen, Peng-Peng Zhu, Katherine R Tucker, Richard J Youle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYThe mechanisms governing the final trafficking steps of the cyclic dinucleotide innate immune receptor stimulator of interferon genes (STING) are not fully understood. Here, we identify the mitochondrial protein Fis1 as a regulator of STING endolysosomal degradation. The absence of Fis1 in HeLa cells stabilizes STING, boosting downstream signaling without affecting its initial endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-to-Golgi traffic. Instead, Fis1 loss impairs STING delivery to endolysosomes. Fis1 recruits the Rab7A-GAP TBC1D15 to mitochondria and upon STING activation, TBC1D15 localization shifts to the Golgi. In the absence of Fis1, the interaction between TBC1D15 and Rab7A is disrupted, leading to decreased Rab7A GTPase activity and impaired STING degradation. Our findings reveal a mitochondria-controlled axis where Fis1 tunes Rab7A activity via TBC1D15, which is required to ensure proper STING degradation, directly linking mitochondria to lysosomal trafficking and the termination of innate immune signaling.

INTRODUCTION

Fis1 is a C-tail-anchored outer mitochondrial membrane protein with a cytosolic N terminus that contains two tetratricopeptide-repeats (TPRs). Fis1 was initially identified in Saccharomyces cerevisiae as a protein required for the recruitment of the yeast Drp1 homolog, Dnm1, during mitochondrial fission.1 A more complex functional redundancy and specialization of Drp1 mitochondrial receptors has evolved in animal cells.2–4 Thus, in mammalian cells, the absence of Fis1 does not impair mitochondrial fission to the same extent as the absence of Drp1.1,3,5–9 The marginal role Fis1 plays in animal mitochondrial fission, however, contrasts with the lethality its systemic genetic ablation causes in mice.10,11 In human cells, Fis1 interacts with the Tre-2/Bub2/Cdc16 family member 15 (TBC1D15),12 a Rab7A GTPase-activating protein (Rab-GAP).13,14 Recruitment of TBC1D15 by Fis1 to the mitochondria modulates Rab7A activity during both the PINK-Parkin-mediated mitophagy7 and the remodeling of mitochondria-lysosome contacts.15,16 Consistent with findings in human cells,6,7 the specific deletion of Fis1 in mouse skeletal muscle causes aberrant mitophagosome formation and is also associated with an increased synthesis of cytokines in animals subjected to endurance exhaustive exercise.10 Overexpression of Fis1 in mice subjected to a high-fat diet decreased the synthesis of type I interferons17 suggesting Fis1 plays a role in regulating inflammatory responses through an unknown mechanism. Further, Fis1 and TBC1D15 were identified among uncharacterized hits the absence of which increased the activation of a reporter driven by interferon-stimulated response elements (ISRE) in a genome-wide CRISPR-interference screening for changes in the activation of the innate immune cyclic dinucleotide sensor stimulator of interferon genes (STING).18

STING is a transmembrane endoplasmic reticulum innate immune receptor19 that traffics to the Golgi upon binding to cyclic-dinucleotides20–23 of either bacterial origin or endogenously synthesized in response to double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) by the 2′3′-guanosine monophosphate-adenosine monophosphate dinucleotide (cGAMP) synthase (cGAS).24,25 Then, STING acts as a scaffold, mediating the interaction between the interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) and the TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1). IRF3 phosphorylation by TBK1 is required to trigger the synthesis of type I interferons.19,26,27 Active STING is trafficked from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) through the Golgi to Rab7A-containing endolysosomal compartments for its degradation.28–31 Basal STING protein levels are also regulated through lysosomal degradation during homeostatic conditions downregulating the tonic activation of the pathway.32

We characterized the mechanism underlying the relationship between STING-mediated signaling and mitochondrial Fis1 in HeLa cells and found that the absence of Fis1 increases basal and active STING stability, boosting the downstream signaling induced by cGAMP. STING ER to Golgi trafficking follows similar dynamics in wild type (WT) and FIS1ko cells causing Golgi cisternae swelling. However, active STING delivery to Rab7A-positive vesicles decreases in cells lacking Fis1. Consistently, STING degradation is impaired in cells lacking the Rab7A-GAP TBC1D15 or Rab7A. In the absence of Fis1, TBC1D15 is not recruited to the mitochondria and its interaction with Rab7A is hampered, unbalancing Rab7A GTPase activity. We propose that mitochondrial tethered Fis1 is required to recruit and tune the affinity of TBC1D15 for Rab7A, thus ensuring proper trafficking of activated STING to late endosomes. Our data unveil a mechanism by which mitochondria can influence Rab7A activity beyond local processes through the Fis1-TBC1D15 axis.

RESULTS

The absence of Fis1 increases STING stability in HeLa cells

To test whether the absence of Fis1 could alter STING-TBK1-IRF3 signaling, we compared the phosphorylation status of these proteins in WT and Fis1 knock-out (FIS1ko) HeLa cells after treatment with cGAMP or with no treatment (NT). The phosphorylation levels of STING and IRF3 were higher upon cGAMP treatment in cells lacking Fis1 compared to WT-treated cells (Figures 1A and 1B). Consistently, the abundance of IFNb, IFIT3, and ISG15 transcripts was enhanced (Figure 1C) in the absence of Fis1. After cGAMP treatment, STING protein levels decreased in both WT and FIS1ko due to degradation at endolysosomal compartments. However, compared to WT cells, STING protein levels remained higher in FIS1ko cells treated with cGAMP (Figures 1A and 1C). We assayed STING protein stability using a cycloheximide (CHX) pulse-chase assay under basal growth conditions and found that STING half-life(T1/2) in WT cells was around 12.6 h, whereas no decrease on STING protein levels was detected in FIS1ko cells after 24 h of CHX treatment (Figures 1D and 1E; Figure S1M). As a control, we followed TIMM17A protein stability and observed that its half-life (T1/2) was similar in WT and FIS1ko cells (WT T1/2~9.02 h, FIS1ko T1/2~7.1 h) (Figures 1D–1F; Figure S1N). When cells were treated with cGAMP at the same time that protein synthesis was inhibited with CHX, we observed increased STING stability in cells lacking Fis1 (Figures 1G and 1H).

We confirmed the increased STING stability caused by the absence of Fis1 in a different FIS1ko clone (Figures S1A and S1B) and in FIS1ko HCT116 cells (Figures S1E and S1F). Further, we observed that the increased STING stability phenotype caused by the absence of Fis1 was rescued by reintroducing 3xFLAG-Fis1 (F-Fis1) but not by introducing the empty vector (Figures 1I and 1J; Figures S1C and S1D).

To rule out a crosstalk between STING activation and mitophagy, we tested if activation of STING via cGAS-synthesized cGAMP or by directly adding cGAMP to WT HeLa+mCherry-Parkin cells could induce PINK1 stabilization. We detected PINK1 stabilization only upon mitochondria depolarization with antimycin and oligomycin (AO) and not when cells were transfected with dsDNA or treated with cGAMP, suggesting STING activation does not induce mitophagy (Figure S1G).

To assess whether Fis1 membrane anchoring was required for the proper degradation of activated STING, we generated a cytosolic version of Fis1 truncating amino acids (aa) 121–152 (Fis1ΔC). We found that the expression of Fis1ΔC in FIS1ko cells did not rescue the phenotype caused by the absence of Fis1 (Figures S1H–S1K). We also tested a chimeric form of Fis1 exclusively targeted to the mitochondria, in which we replaced the Fis1 C-tail with the transmembrane helix (aa 101–135) of the mitochondrial protein OMP25 (Fis1ΔC-OMP25) and observed that cells expressing this Fis1 form displayed a similar ratio of STING degradation as when WT Fis1 was reintroduced (Figures S1J and S1K). The subcellular localization of all the herein described Fis1 constructs was corroborated by confocal microscopy (Figure S1L).

Trafficking of STING to Rab7A-positive vesicles is hindered in cells lacking Fis1

We treated with cGAMP WT and FIS1ko cells stably expressing mEGFP-STING and followed STING trafficking from the ER to the Golgi. Immunofluorescent detection of the protein disulfide isomerase (PDI) (Figure S2) and the trans-Golgi network 46 protein (TGN46) (Figure 2A) was used to label the ER and the Golgi, respectively. In both WT and FIS1ko cells, STING trafficking dynamics upon a cGAMP treatment were similar (Figures 2A–2C; Figure S2). We detected only a slightly higher percentage of cells with STING signal at the ER after 8 h of treatment with cGAMP in FIS1ko cells (Figure 2B). We noticed that the TGN46 signal changed upon activation of STING with cGAMP, shifting from being restricted in clear Golgi stacks to becoming dispersed and diffused throughout the perinuclear region (Figures 2D and 2E). We assessed the cell ultrastructure changes caused by activating STING with cGAMP in WT and FIS1ko cells using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). We detected that the Golgi morphology changed drastically in both WT and FIS1ko cells as a consequence of STING activation (Figure 2F). Upon cGAMP treatment, Golgi stacks swell and were dispersed through the perinuclear region, similar to the observations of TGN46 immunodetection (Figure 2D). The Golgi stacks, including dilated cisternae, had a larger area in cells lacking Fis1 compared to WT (Figure 2G), while the area of multivesicular bodies (MVBs) (Figure 2H) and vesicles containing electron dense material such as late endosomes and lysosomes (LE/L) was similar in WT and FIS1ko cells (Figure 2I).

We followed the trafficking of mEGFP-STING to mCherry-Rab7A-positive vesicles (Figure 3A; Figure S3) and observed an increase in the percentage of mEGFP-STING within mCh-Rab7A signal that peaked in WT cells after 6 and 8 h of cGAMP treatment. The percentage of mEGFP-STING signal detected with mCh-Rab7A vesicles remained stable in FIS1ko cells and did not increase upon a cGAMP treatment (Figures 3A and 3B). Congruently with previous reports,28,33 we observed that knocking down (kd) RAB7A impaired STING degradation significantly (Figures 3C and 3D). A slight increase in the percentage of mEGFP-STING colocalization (from 1.49% to 2.43%) with mCh-Rab5A positive early endosomes was observed in WT cells after 8 h of STING activation with cGAMP, this was not detected in FIS1ko cells (Figures 3E and 3F). The mEGFP-STING signal within mCh-Rab11-positive regions increased similarly in both WT and FIS1ko cells (Figures 3G and 3H). We knocked down RAB5A and RAB11, decreasing their expression by ~32.1% and by ~45.09%, respectively, and did not observe any changes in STING degradation (Figures S3B, S3C, S3D, and S3E).

We detected the delivery of mEGFP-STING to lysosomes using immunofluorescent detection of LAMP1. We assessed the delivery of mEGFP-STING to lysosomes through an assay commonly used to measure the delivery of cargoes to lysosomes when assaying autophagy flux34; treating the cells for 8 h with cGAMP or with cGAMP and chloroquine (CQ), which increases lysosomal pH and decreases lysosomal degradation. The mEGFP-STING signal in LAMP1 vesicles was low in both WT and FIS1ko cells after 0 and 8 h of treatment with cGAMP (Figures 3I and 3J). However, the mEGFP-STING signal accumulated in LAMP1-positive vesicles in cells treated with cGAMP plus CQ in WT cells, while the absence of Fis1 halted this accumulation (Figures 3I and 3J).

STING degradation is impaired in cells lacking the Rab7A-GAP and Fis1 interactor TBC1D15

Given that we observed altered trafficking of STING to Rab7A-positive vesicles in the absence of Fis1, we tested whether avoiding Fis1-TBC1D15 interaction, by deleting the region of Fis1 required to interact with TBC1D15, impaired STING degradation. STING was degraded to a similar extent upon cGAMP treatment in WT cells and in FIS1ko expressing an mEGFP-tagged WT form of Fis1. When a mutant form of Fis1 lacking aa 77–98 (the region of Fis1 required to recruit TBC1D15)12,35 was stably expressed in FIS1ko cells, a decreased rate of STING degradation was observed, comparable to the rate observed in FIS1ko cells (Figures 4A and 4B). The Δ77–98 truncation impaired the detection of the protein by Fis1 antibody (Figure 4A), but we corroborated its expression and mitochondria localization by detecting the mEGFP tag (Figures 4A and 4C).

We examined STING protein levels in HeLa TBC1D15ko cells and found, similar to FIS1ko cells, that cGAMP-activated STING stability was higher compared to WT cells (Figures 4D and 4E). This increased stability of active STING in TBC1D15ko was associated with increased STING phosphorylation (Figures 4D and 4E).

TBC1D15 and TBC1D17 are functionally redundant during mitophagy7; however, we observed that the absence of TBC1D17 in HeLa cells did not increase active STING stability (Figures 4F and 4G), suggesting that despite TBC1D17 and TBC1D15 protein sequences share around 50% identity, they have evolved non-overlapping functions.

The observed stabilization of active STING in cells lacking TBC1D15 was validated in a different KO clone (Figures S2A and S2B). In addition, we reintroduced an mEGFP-tagged form of TBC1D15 isoform 1 in to TBC1D15ko cells and observed that the stabilization of cGAMP-activated STING was rescued (Figures 4H and 4I), while we did not observe rescue of the phenotype by introducing the empty vector (Figures S2C and S2D).

We expressed mEGFP-TBC1D15 into cells lacking both FIS1 and TBC1D15 (FIS1/TBC1D15dko), its expression did not rescue the increased stabilization of active STING observed in the double knock out compared to WT cells, suggesting FIS1 displays upstream epistasis to TBC1D15 (Figures 4J and 4K).

To test whether the hyperactivation of STING in cells lacking Fis1 or TBC1D15 could be due to impaired mitochondrial homeostasis, we imaged immunofluorescently stained mitochondria in WT, DRP1ko, FIS1ko, and TBC1D15ko cells (Figures S2E–S2J). As previously reported,2–5,7 the absence of Drp1 caused a phenotype with elongated and swollen joined mitochondria (Figure S2F). In contrast, the absence of Fis1 or TBC1D15 did not cause any similar apparent alterations in mitochondrial morphology (Figures S2G–S2J). We tested if the hyperactivation of STING in the absence of Fis1 or TBC1D15 could be due to impaired mitophagy leading to increased cGAS activation. Intracellular cGAMP was measured by ELISA after inducing mitophagy with AO in WT, FIS1ko, or TBC1D15ko cells, all expressing Parkin (mCh-PRKN). Mitophagy induction did not increase cGAS activity upon AO treatment in any of the tested conditions (Figure S2K).

In agreement with previous reports,7,12,36 TBC1D15 localization was influenced by Fis1 expression levels and localization (Figures 5A–5C; Figure S5). When WT Fis1 was stably expressed, TBC1D15 was enriched at the mitochondria (Figures 3A and 3B; Figures S5A, S5B, S5E, and S5F). While when cytosolic Fis1ΔC was expressed, TBC1D15 was detected mainly in the cytoplasm with Fis1 (Figures S5A and S5B).

In the absence of Fis1 expression, TBC1D15 localized either at the cytosol or accumulated at the perinuclear region of cells, in close vicinity to the markers Golgi matrix protein130 (GM130) (Figures 5C and 5D) and TGN46 (Figures 5E and 5F; Figures S5D, S5E, and S5G). When a mutant form of Fis1 lacking the region that interacts with TBC1D15 (Fis1Δ77−98) was expressed, mCh-TBC1D15 displayed very low colocalization with the mitochondria marker TOMM20 and accumulated at the perinuclear region of cells in close vicinity to the Golgi marker TGN46 (Figures S5E–S5G).

We then followed the subcellular localization of mScarlet-tagged (mSca) STING stably expressed in FIS1/TBC1D15dko cells in which Fis1WT and mEGFP-TBC1D15 were reintroduced (FIS1/TBC1D15dko+mEGFP-TBC1D15+mSca-STING+Fis1). We acquired images of fixed cells after either 4 or 6 h of cGAMP treatment or left with NT. Consistent with the described passage of STING through the Golgi during its activation20–23 and our previous results (Figures 2A and 2B), we observed that mSca-STING redistributed from the ER to the perinuclear region of cells after being exposed to cGAMP (Figure 5A). We also observed that upon STING activation with cGAMP, the subcellular localization of mEGFP-TBC1D15 shifted from the mitochondria to the perinuclear region of these cells (FIS1/TBC1D15dko +mEGFP-TBC1D15+mSca-STING+Fis1). The percentage of cells in which TBC1D15 accumulated at mitochondria decreased over time following cGAMP treatment (Figures 5A and 5B). After 8 h of treatment with cGAMP, we observed in WT cells an increase of mEGFP-TBC1D15 signal localized within the signal of both GM130 and TGN46 Golgi markers(Figures 5C–5F). In the absence of Fis1 expression, the proportion of TBC1D15 signal detected within the GM130 marker decreased upon 8 h of treatment with cGAMP but remained localized surrounding it (Figures 5C and 5D). A similar proportion of TBC1D15 accumulated in regions positive to TGN46 in FISko cells, irrespective of the treatment (Figures 5E and 5F).

We detected a slight increase in the percentage of TBC1D15 signal within the lysosomal marker LAMP1 signal in the presence of Fis1, which was not observed in its absence (Figures 5G and 5H).

Fis1 absence hampers Rab7A interaction with TBC1D15, impairing Rab7A GTPase activity

Given that TBC1D15 is the main Rab7A-GAP and that the absence of, either or both, Fis1 and TBC1D15 caused a similar increase on STING stability upon cGAMP activation, we hypothesized that Fis1 absence could be involved in regulating the TBC1D15-mediated stimulation of Rab7A GTPase activity. To test this hypothesis, we assessed if Fis1 was required for the association between TBC1D15 and Rab7A. We immunoprecipitated (IP) an HA-tagged form of Rab7A that was stably expressed in WT and FIS1ko cells (Figure 6A) and tested for TBC1D15 co-immunoprecipitation. When we analyzed the eluted HA-bound fraction by immunoblotting against TBC1D15, we found that the absence of Fis1 caused a decrease in the co-immunoprecipitation of TBC1D15 with HA-Rab7A in lysates from both cGAMP-treated and -untreated cells (Figures 6A and 6B). We then evaluated if the observed decreased association between Rab7A and TBC1D15 imbalanced Rab7A GTPase activity by immunoprecipitating GTP-bound Rab7A with a specific antibody against this form of the protein. In accordance with the positive control of WT cell lysates incubated with GTP, which stimulated GTP loading into Rab7A (Figure 6C), an increased level of Rab7A protein was immunodetected when GTP-Rab7A was pulled down from lysates of FIS1ko cells in all the conditions tested. We corroborated that the absence of Fis1 in these conditions increased STING stability assaying whole cells lysates (Figure 6D).

Rab7A S72 phosphorylation (pRab7A) has been suggested to contribute to Rab7A GTPase activity regulation,37 based on the observation that the overexpression of a phosphomimetic S72E form of Rab7A delays EGFR degradation.38 Further, TBK1 phosphorylates Rab7A S72 during STING activation,39 while the overexpression of S72E Rab7A impairs active STING degradation,40 suggesting Rab7A phosphorylation may be regulated during STING trafficking. We analyzed by immunoblot the levels of Rab7A S72 phosphorylation and observed an increase of S72 pRab7A induced by activation of STING with cGAMP in WT cells (Figures 6E–6G). The absence of Fis1 caused higher basal levels of pRab7A (Figures 6E and 6F), congruent with the observed increase of immunoprecipitated GTP-bound Rab7A. The stable overexpression of a GTP-locked form of Rab7A (HA-Rab7AQ67L)41,42 caused a slight increase in active STING stability in FIS1ko cells (approximately a 0.26 increase) (Figures 6G–6I). Together, our data suggest that the reduced affinity between TBC1D15 and Rab7A, caused by the absence of Fis1, dampens Rab7A GTPase activity.

DISCUSSION

Mitochondria play a key role in innate immune signaling through the mitochondria antiviral signaling protein (MAVS) and through activation of the cGAS-STING pathway by mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA).43 In this work, we report a mechanism by which mitochondria can influence STING signaling independent of mtDNA. We found that the absence of the mitochondrial protein Fis1 in HeLa cells stabilizes STING, boosting its downstream signaling when activated by cGAMP possibly due to an increased initial pool of STING and due to a decreased degradation rate of active STING. The increased levels of cytokines transcription that we observed in HeLa cells when Fis1 was absent suggest that decreased Fis1 expression could impair systemic STING downstream signaling and could be the underlying cause of the previously reported links between Fis1 and inflammation in mice.10,17

Our data show that STING trafficking through the Golgi changes its structure; specifically, we observed it causes dispersion and dilation of the cisternae. Dilation of the Golgi cisternae could be observed when the secretory capacity of cells is exceeded or compromised.44,45 Also, changes in the Golgi structure have been reported when the proton gradient, increasing from cis to trans, is lost upon treatment with the Na+/H+ ionophore monensin,46–48 due to expression of viral ion channels49,50 or in the absence of the Golgi pH regulator anion channel (GPHR).51 Large STING oligomers are assembled during STING activation at the ER52 and are trafficked to the Golgi as large cargoes possibly dilating the Golgi cisternae during its passage. Further, it has been proposed that STING could act as a proton channel,53,54 which could imbalance the ion equilibrium of Golgi cisternae similarly to other agents that disturb the Golgi pH gradient, contributing to the swelling of the Golgi stacks.

The area of Golgi stacks in cells lacking Fis1 was larger compared to WT cells, which could be due to an imbalanced trafficking flow of STING. Recently, the absence of TAX1BP1 has been reported to impair STING endolysosomal degradation and also Golgi morphology.55

Similar to the phenotype of mutations in proteins required for STING traffic from the TGN to endolysosomes,29–31,56–59 the absence of Fis1 does not impair STING trafficking from the ER to the Golgi, suggesting its absence hampers a later stage of STING trafficking. Congruently, a lesser percentage of STING accumulated in Rab7A-positive vesicles in the absence of Fis1 after more than 6 h of activation with cGAMP. Furthermore, lack of Fis1 hampers STING delivery to lysosomes, suggesting it is required for proper lysosome-mediated STING degradation.

Fis1 interacts with the Rab7A-GAP TBC1D15 recruiting it to mitochondria.12 The Fis1-TBC1D15 interaction is required locally during mitophagy7 and has been suggested to control the untethering of mitochondria-lysosome contacts.15 On the other hand, our data show that TBC1D15 localization shifts from Fis1-mediated attachment to the mitochondria to become enriched at the Golgi upon STING activation with cGAMP. This is consistent with the identification of TBC1D15 among enriched proteins in proximity to STING at late stages of its trafficking.29 In the absence of Fis1, TBC1D15 is constitutively located at the Golgi and decreases its colocalization with the cis-Golgi marker GM130 upon cGAMP treatment, either due to impaired regulation of its localization during STING trafficking or due to the increased dilation of the Golgi stacks.

As observed when Rab7A expression is dampened, the absence of TBC1D15 halts STING degradation, suggesting that TBC1D15 is regulating Rab7A during the late-stages of STING trafficking, as it does during endosomal traffic, by stimulating its GTPase activity13,14 and possibly by triggering its inactivation and removal during endolysosome maturation.60 However, we uncovered an additional regulatory layer in which the mitochondria play a role in Rab7A-mediated traffic. The recruitment of TBC1D15 to the mitochondria before it localizes to the Golgi is required to increase its affinity for Rab7A. In turn, a high affinity of TBC1D15 for Rab7A is required during processes that require an increased demand for Rab7A cycling, such as the late stages of activated STING trafficking and proper maturation of endolysosomal vesicles.

Lysosomal STING degradation could be impaired in the absence of Fis1 due to decreased untethering of mitochondria-lysosome and lysosome-lysosome contacts, as observed when a mutant form of Fis1 deficient in recruiting TBC1D15 to the mitochondria is expressed, causing lysosomal clustering and decreased lysosomal-loading of dextran.16 However, delayed degradation of active STING inside the lysosomes would not maintain the activation of downstream TBK1-IRF3 signaling as we observed.

The role of TBC1D15 on endosomal-GLUT4 glucose transporter translocation,61 together with our data, suggests that TBC1D15 affinity for Rab7A could adjust the rate of Rab7A-dependent trafficking processes. Based on our data, we propose that recruitment of TBC1D15 to the mitochondria by Fis1 is required to increase its affinity for Rab7A, possibly by modulating posttranslational modifications in TBC1D15. TBC1D1 and TBC1D4 Rab-GAP recruitment to GLUT4 storage vesicles is regulated by phosphorylation, which adjusts their affinity for client Rab-GTPases, representing a mechanism to tune their GTPase activity.62–68 In conclusion, our study uncovers a mechanism by which mitochondria influence STING signaling through Fis1 and its interactor, the Rab-GAP TBC1D15, and how these proteins could influence inflammation. Our findings highlight the importance of mitochondrial regulation of immune signaling and suggest that targeting the Fis1-TBC1D15-Rab7A axis could offer therapeutic potential for conditions associated with dysregulated STING activity.

Limitations of the study

While we have demonstrated that the absence of Fis1 increases STING stability due to impaired TBC1D15 interaction with Rab7A, which hinders its GTPase activity in HeLa cells and we corroborated the Fis1-dependent STING stabilization phenotype in HCT116 cells, future studies in additional cell lines, particularly primary immune cells and in vivo models will be necessary to determine the full generalizability of the Fis1-TBC1D15-Rab7A axis in regulating STING trafficking and innate immune signaling across different physiological contexts. In addition, the precise subcellular spatial location where TBC1D15 acts on Rab7A during STING late-stage trafficking remains to be determined together with the specific downstream mechanisms by which impaired Rab7A cycling leads to STING stabilization.

Further research will shed light on the mechanisms regulating TBC1D15, including its possible posttranslational modifications, such as phosphorylation. In addition, further research on the mechanisms influencing subcellular localization of TBC1D15 and its dynamics, will help elucidate not only the role of Fis1 on the regulation of TBC1D15 but also the mechanisms by which TBC1D15 is recruited to other cellular compartments such as the Golgi and damaged lysosomes.69

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Human and bacterial cell culture

HCT116, HEK 293T and HeLa cells were purchased from ATCC. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium with or without phenol red (DMEM, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 10% (v/v) Fetal Bovine Serum (Sigma), 1 mM Sodium Pyruvate (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 2 mM GlutaMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cell lines were stored in Bambanker freezing media (Fujifilm Wako Chemicals). Cells were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination and contamination was never detected in the cells used for this study. Cell line authentication was not performed.

NEB 5-alpha Competent E. coli (New England Biolabs) and NEB Stable Competent E. coli, High Efficiency cells (New England Biolabs) were grown on Luria Bertani broth or agar plates (KD Medical). Plasmid-bearing bacteria selection was performed using carbenicillin 100 μg/mL (GoldBio) or kanamycin 50 μg/mL (Sigma-Aldrich).

METHOD DETAILS

Generation of CRISPR gene knockout cell lines

CRISPR sgRNAs targeting common exons by all splice variants of FIS1, TBC1D15, TBC1D17 were designed. Oligonucleotides coding for the sgRNA were aligned and cloned into the BbsI-HF site of pSpCas9(BB)-2A-Puro (PX459) V2.0 (Addgene plasmid #62988). HeLa KO cell lines were generated by transfecting cells with the sgRNA plasmid using jetOPTIMUS DNA Transfection Reagent (Polyplus) followed by selection with 1μg/mL of puromycin (Invivogen) for 48h. The transfected cells pool was transferred to media without antibiotic until clonal growth was detected, then single cell colonies were isolated by limiting dilution and expanded in 96-well plates. Screening of KO clones was performed by sequencing the PCR product obtained from the amplified target locus to identify indel generation. Indels were further ratified from clones lacking protein expression of the targeted gene by cloning in pGEM-T Easy (Promega) the PCR product from the amplified sgRNA-targeted locus and then sequencing the cloned inserts. Primers used for sgRNA generation and for KO validation are compiled in the Key resources. HeLa DRP1ko was kindly provided by Dr. Mariusz Karbowski.

Cloning and generation of stably cell line generation

For lentiviral and retroviral vector constructs, inserts were amplified by PCR using Phusion Hot Start II High-Fidelity polymerase (Thermo Fisher) and were cloned by Gibson assembly (New England Biolabs) in the previously digested target vectors (pBABE-puro, pHAGE). TBC1D15 was amplified from pLenti-GIII-CMV-TBC1D15 (Applied Biological Materials), FIS1 from pBABE-puro/3FLAG-TEV-hFIS1, and OMP25 fragment (aa 118–145)70 from pcDNA3.1-OMP25. Constructs were transformed into NEB 5-alpha Competent E. coli or NEB Stable Competent E. coli. HeLa cell lines with stable overexpression of the indicated genes were generated by infection with lentiviruses or retroviruses. Viruses were packaged in HEK293T cells by transfecting the vector and appropriate helper plasmids with polyethylenimine (PEI, Polysciences) prepared in Opti-MEM (Thermo Fisher). Fresh media was added 24h after transfection. Virus-containing media was harvested 24h after the media change, filtered through a 0.45 mm PVDF filter (Millipore Sigma) and added to target HeLa cells with 8 μg/mL polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich). Media was then changed after a 24h incubation with viral-containing media and 2 μg/mL of puromycin (Invivogen) was added to select cells transduced with pBABE-puro derived constructs. Cells were then directly used in experiments or optimized for expression by FACS.

Cell treatments

Cells were plated 48h prior to experiments in 10% (v/v) FBS supplemented DMEM at a density of −0.1 × 10^6 cells/mL. For treatments with Oligomycin (Calbiochem, Sigma-Aldrich) and Antimycin A (Sigma-Aldrich) were performed as previously reported.71 To activate cGAS with dsDNA, cells were transfected 4 μg/mL of Poly-dAdT (Invivogen) with lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using the standard company protocol. To activate STING, cells were shifted to DMEM containing 1% (v/v) FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) 24h prior to the experiment, after washing twice with HBSS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) similarly to how was previously reported.72 Cells were incubated with media containing H2O-diluted cGAMP at a final concentration of 15 μg/mL for 8h. TBC1D15ko#5+pHAGE-mEGFP or pHAGE-mEGFP-TBC1D15 experiments were performed using 60 mg/mL of cGAMP for 4h. For microscopy experiments, DMEM without phenol red, supplemented with HEPES (Thermo Fisher Scientific), was used. 300 μg/mL of cGAMP for the indicated times was used in experiments where mEGFP or mScarlet-tagged STING was overexpressed. Chloroquine (Sigma-Aldrich) was used to a final concentration of 20mM.

2′3′-cGAMP ELISA

cGAMP production was measured via 2’3’-cGAMP ELISA Kit (Cayman Chemical Company) using the standard protocol provided by the manufacturer. After the specified treatments, −3×10^6 cells were lysed in a cold shaker with M-PER buffer (Thermo Fisher). Protein concentration of clarified lysates was assayed using Pierce BCA Protein Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and ≃20 μg of protein was loaded per well.

Immunoblot assays

After the indicated treatments, cells were washed twice with cold HBSS or 1× PBS (Lonza) and lysed with 1× LDS supplemented with sodium fluoride (Sigma-Aldrich) and cOmplete, Mini, EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche). LDS lysis Buffer was home-made without colorants following the recipe of Thermo Fisher for 4× LDS NuPage buffer (106 mM TRIS-HCl (IBI Scientific), 141 mM Trizma base (Sigma-Aldrich), 2% LDS (Research Products International), 10% glycerol (Bio Basic), 0.51 mM EDTA (Quality Biological)). Samples were lysed by incubating at 99°C, for 10 min shaking at 1000 rpm. Protein concentrations were determined by Pierce BCA Protein Assay, then 20 to 40 μg of DTT-denaturalized (Sigma-Aldrich) protein was separated through cold SDS-PAGE using 4–12% Bis-Tris 1.5 mm precast gels (GenScript) or 8% Bis-Tris 1.0 mm precast gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific). MOPS or MES buffer (GenScript) was used depending on the molecular weight of the proteins to be detected. Gels were electrophoresed at 50V for 20min and then at 100V until the dye front reached the end of the gel and then were transferred to polyvinyl difluoride membranes (0.2 μm (Bio-Rad) or 0.45 μm (Millipore Sigma)) using the Trans-Blot Turbo system (Bio-Rad).

Tris-Buffered Saline containing 0.05% Tween 20 (Sigma-Aldrich) was used to prepare blocking and antibody incubation solutions and to perform membrane washes. For membrane blocking 4% skim milk (Millipore Sigma) was used, and for the antibody incubation, 1, 2 or 4% of BSA (Fisher Scientific) with 0.1% sodium azide (Sigma-Aldrich) was used. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies either for 2h at RT or O/N at 4°C. The following antibody dilutions were used: pSTING: 1:500 (Cell Signaling Technology); STING: 1:1000 (Cell Signaling Technology), pTBK1: 1:1000 (Cell Signaling Technology), TBK1: 1:1000 (Cell Signaling Technology), pIRF3: 1:1000 (Abcam), IRF3: 1:1000 (Cell Signaling Technology), Fis1: 1:2000 (Proteintech); Actin: 1: 5000 (Sigma-Aldrich); HSP90: 1:5000 (Cell Signaling Technology), TBC1D15: 1:2000 (Abcam), TBC1D17: 1:4000 (Proteintech), HA 1:2000 (Cell Signaling Technology), Rab7A: 1:1000 (NewEast Biosciences), pRab7A: 1:1000 (Abcam), and PINK1: 1:1000 (Cell Signaling Technology). PINK1 antibody was added after blocking with 2% BSA.

Membranes were washed thoroughly and then incubated at RT for 1 or 2h with HRP-linked secondary antibody against the appropriate reactive species, prepared in blocking buffer. After washing secondary antibodies, the membranes were developed using either Amersham ECL Prime Western Blotting Detection Reagent (Cytiva) or SuperSignal West FEMTO Maximum Sensitivity Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and imaged using a ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad). For stripping and re-probing, membranes were incubated with Restore PLUS Western Blot Stripping Buffer (Thermo Fisher) for 1h at RT with shaking.

Immunoprecipitation experiments

For HA-beads (Pierce), specified HeLa cell lines were seeded as previously described and 8–10 × 106 cells were lysed at 4°C shaking at 1000 rpm for 30 min in 1× Lysis/Assay Buffer (NewEast Biosciences) supplemented with 2× EDTA-free cOmplete protease inhibitor and 20mM of NaF. Protein concentration of clarified lysates was determined by Pierce BCA Protein Assay and 500 μg of protein was diluted (maintaining 1 part of Assay Buffer to 5 parts of TBSE buffer (20mM Tris-HCl pH 7.6 (Quality Biological), 150mM NaCl (Quality Biological), 1mM EDTA)) and incubated for 2h at 4°C with pre-equilibrated magnetic HA-beads. Then beads were washed at RT 3× with TBSE containing 0.05% Tween 20, 2× cOmplete protease inhibitor and 20mM of NaF. After a wash with ultrapure H2O, protein was eluted from HA-beads for 10min at 98°C in 30mL of home-made 2× LDS. The eluted fraction was analyzed by immunoblot in parallel to 5% of the input protein lysate (25mg).

The protocol for adherent cells provided by the company with the Rab7 Activation Assay Kit (NewEast Biosciences) was used to prepare the in vitro GTPgS/GDP protein loading for positive and negative controls and to immunoprecipitate Rab7A-GTP with anti-Rab7-GTP monoclonal antibody. Briefly, 8–10 × 106 cells were lysed as previously described for HA-beads immunoprecipitation, and 800 μg of protein was incubated with 1mL of anti GTP-Rab7and 20 μL of Protein A/G agarose for 2h at 4°C. Beads were then washed 5× with 1× Lysis/Assay Buffer and the protein bound to beads was eluted for 10min at 98°C in 20mL of home-made 2× LDS. The eluted fraction was analyzed by immunoblot in parallel to 2.5% of the input protein lysate (20 μg).

Immunofluorescence microscopy

As described in the previous sections, cells were seeded on #1.5 glass 8 well chambered plates (Cellvis). After the specified treatments, cells were fixed for 15 min at 37°C with either 2 or 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Electron Microscopy Sciences) prepared in 1× PBS.

For immunofluorescence detection, cells were then permeabilized with 0.1% or 0.2% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich). PFA and Triton X- concentrations were adjusted depending on the primary antibodies used. Fixed cells were then incubated with primary antibodies diluted in 1× PBS containing 5% (v/v) FBS (R&D Systems) or 1%BSA (for LAMP1), after being blocked with 10% (v/v) FBS in 1× PBS. The following primary antibodies were used at the specified concentrations for either 2h at RT or O/N at 4°C: TOMM20: 1:1000 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology); FIS1: 1:500; PDI: 1:400 (Thermo Fisher); GM130: 1:200 (BD Transduction Laboratories), TGN46: 1: 500 (Biorad), LAMP1 1:200 (CS). Cells were washed with 5% (v/v) FBS in 1× PBS thoroughly and were then incubated with Alexa Fluor labeled secondary antibodies for 2h at RT or with GFP booster Atto 488 (Proteintech) in 0.2%Triton, 10%FBS in PBS. Secondary antibodies were washed with 5% (v/v) FBS in PBS, and PBS. For nuclei staining, 1:5000 DAPI was used (Thermo Fisher). Samples were conserved in Ibidi mounting medium (Ibidi) and imaged.

Acquisition of images was performed using a Zeiss LSM 880 Airyscan Confocal Microscope with a 63 × 1.4 NA Plan-Apochromat objective, laser lines (in nm): 405, 488, 561 and 633, Piezo high-precision stage and using the Zen software platform (Carl Zeiss Microscopy). The “4 Color FrameShift” configuration of beam splitters and emission filters suggested by Wu and Hammer 202173 was used. Zen software was used for Airy-scan image pixel reassignment and deconvolution (using “Auto” setting) for the stitching of tiles. FIJI (ImageJ) was used for merging and aligning channels (to avoid bleaching, TGN channel was acquired independently of other channels in some experiments), to adjust brightness and contrast of the representative images presented, and to prepare images for publication.74 Some of the microscopy images shown in the Figures were processed with the “smooth” function to ease visualization.

Transmission electron microscopy

Cells stably expressing mEGFP-STING were seeded 48h before being treated on Aclar film coverslips (Electron Microscopy Sciences (EMS)) in 12-well plates. Coverslips were prepared by cutting Aclar into squares that fit into the wells of 12-well plates. Coverslips were previously rinsed in distilled water and sterilized with 70% ethanol and transferred to a 12-well plate, where they were rinsed ten times with autoclaved ultrapure water and rinsed once with culture medium before plating of cells. Cells were left untreated (NT) or were treated with 300μg/mL of cGAMP for 16h and then were fixed at room temperature with 2% paraformaldehyde (EMS), 2% glutaraldehyde (EMS), and 50 mM calcium chloride (Quality Biological, Inc) in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (EMS). Peroxidase staining was performed with some modifications of the previously described method by Zhang,Q. et al.75 After fixation, coverslips were rinsed twice in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer, followed by a 10-min incubation in cacodylate buffer containing 50 mM glycine (Sigma-Aldrich) and a final rinse in cacodylate buffer. Coverslips were rinsed twice with cacodylate buffer and then post-fixed with 3% glutaraldehyde in cacodylate buffer. For EM processing, coverslips were rinsed twice for 10 min in cacodylate buffer, and then stained with reduced osmium containing 1% osmium tetroxide (EMS) and 1% potassium ferrocyanide (Sigma-Aldrich) on ice for 60 min. Next, coverslips were rinsed three times with cacodylate buffer and dehydrated through a graded series of 5-min ethanol rinses prior to infiltration in EmBED812 epoxy resin using the manufacturer’s hard resin formulation (EMS). To embed cells, Aclar coverslips were laid cell-side-up on a backing piece of Aclar, and then 2–3 gelatine capsules (EMS) filled with resin were inverted on top of the coverslips and placed in a 60°C oven for 2 days.

For sectioning, Aclar coverslips were peeled away from the resin, and en face ultrathin sections of embedded cells were cut to a thickness of 70 nm using an ultramicrotome (EM UC7, Leica Microsystems) with a diamond knife (DiATOME). Sections were picked up on 1 mm-slot copper grids with a thick formvar support film (EMS), and post-stained with 3% Reynold’s lead citrate (EMS) for 1 min. Sections were viewed using a JEOL1400 Flash transmission electron microscope (JEOL USA, Inc.) operated at 120 KV. Images were recorded with a Biosprint29 CMOS detector (Advanced Microscopy Techniques). FIJI (ImageJ) was used to visualize and to adjust brightness and contrast of the representative images presented, and to prepare images for publication.74

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

ELISA-detected cGAMP concentrations were calculated using the manufacturer-provided spreadsheet, values were normalized to the calculated protein concentration per well and graphed using Graphpad Prism 10.

The qPCR obtained results, for each biological sample, the Ct value of the gene of interest was normalized against the b-actin Ct to calculate ΔCt. Each ΔCt was normalized to the average ΔCt of untreated samples to generate the ΔΔCt value. Relative gene expression was then analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt formula.76 Results were plotted and statistically analyzed using Graphpad Prism 10.

Densitometric quantifications of immunoblot images were performed using the FIJI Gel analysis tool.74 The pixel values obtained from quantifying three independent blots for pSTING, STING, pTBK1, TBK1, pIRF3, IRF3, pRAB7A and RAB7A were normalized using the internal loading control (HSP90 or Actin) and were plotted either relativized to the WT condition without treatment or as the ratio between cGAMP treated to untreated conditions. The immunoprecipitated TBC1D15 to HA ratio was calculated from the pixels of blot images from 4 co-immunoprecipitation assays.

To calculate STING and TIMM17A half-life, the natural logarithm (ln) of normalized protein levels at each time point relative to t = 0 from three independent experiments was plotted. Then a Simple Linear regression was generated, and the half-life (t1/2) was calculated using the equation N(t) = N0e−kt, in which k is the slope of the obtained line and t1/2 = ln(2)/k.

To quantify STING trafficking or TBC1D15 localization at the mitochondria, the percentage of cells in which mEGFP signal colocalized respectively with 633 or 405 channels was quantified manually guided by images generated by the FIJI plugin Colocalization finder. The proportion of mEGFP-STING signal within mCherry labeled organelles or in LAMP1-positive vesicles, and of mEGFP-TBC1D15 signal within Golgi markers or LAMP1-positive vesicles, was performed using FIJI. Same size regions of individual cells (regions of interest: ROIs) were thresholded using respectively the 405, 561 or 633 channels to generate a mask. Then the fluorescence inside the mask, total fluorescence of the ROIs and background were calculated and the proportion inside/outside the mask was calculated and plotted as percentage. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated from same size ROIs of individual cells using Coloc 2 FIJI plug in.

Organelles were manually classified based on morphological characteristics in cell ultrastructure images acquired by TEM and the area was manually quantified using FIJI.

Plots and statistical analysis were performed using Graphpad Prism 10 and statistical details can be found in the figure legends. Each point value and all relevant p-values obtained from the statistical comparisons performed are depicted in the graphs.

Plots and statistical analysis were performed using Graphpad Prism 10. To analyze the difference between two groups a two-tailed Student’s t tests was used. To assay multi-group comparisons a one-way ANOVA followed by a post hoc test was performed (Sidak’s or Bonferroni). For experiments involving two independent variables, two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni pos hoc test was performed. An alpha of 0.1 was set when performing comparisons. Data are expressed as mean ± SD from at least three independent experiments. Specific statistical details and the number of cells analyzed for imaging analyses can be found in the figure legends. Each point value and all relevant p-values obtained from the statistical comparisons performed are depicted in the graphs.
