Men’s health needs assessment in the Toledo District of Southern Belize
Authors: John Rovers, Matthew Nicasio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBelize is a small country in Central America with a growing burden of non-communicable disease (NCD), including hypertension and diabetes. Toledo District is the southernmost and poorest district in the country. Reliable national level health data for Belize is readily available, but the data is rarely disaggregated by sex or district. Reducing the burden of NCDs is a high priority for the Ministry of Health and Wellness. Belize’s progress to date on Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3 (Good Health and Wellbeing) has been modest with many indicators stagnating or progress increasing at less than 50% of the required rate. SDG 3 describes the need to reduce the risks of NCDs and to strengthen the capacities of the healthcare workforce.ObjectiveThe objective was to perform a men’s health needs assessment to identify and prioritize men’s health needs in the Toledo District.MethodsThis was a mixed methods study. Qualitative data were collected from semi-structured interviews. Interviews were recorded, transcribed, and analyzed using Thematic Analysis. Quantitative data included epidemiological data from national vital statistics or disease registries and other public sources. Data were collected between January and June 2017.ResultsBelizean men have among the highest risk for cardiac or diabetes related illness or death in the Americas. Diabetes and hypertension are responsible for 4.49% and 1.23% of Disability Adjusted Life Years in men respectively and are increasing by 2.51% annually.Fifty-seven interviews (55 individuals and two groups) from nine villages were carried out. Four themes emerged from the qualitative data. Men in Toledo:• have poor health literacy;• have reasonable access to health resources, but do not use them;• inability to clearly articulate health priorities;• do not process risk well.ConclusionsMen in Toledo suffer from a high prevalence of NCDs including hypertension and diabetes and understand health and risks poorly. This may contribute to Belize’s struggle to achieve the goals of SDG 3.4.1. Strengthening the healthcare workforce by improved training of community health workers (CHWs) and providing health education to men in Toledo is required to address these concerns.

Introduction

Belize is a small country in Central America. In 2022, its total midyear population was 441,471, of which males and females were nearly equivalent in numbers (220,739 male; 220,732 female) [1]. Toledo is the southernmost district in Belize with a population of 41,536 (20,788 male; 20,748 female). Toledo is mostly rural with 6,801 residents living in the capital, Punta Gorda, and 34,735 residents living in rural Toledo. Although Belize is considered an upper middle-income country by the World Bank with a Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita of $6,948, Toledo is the poorest district in the county with an estimated 60.3% of the population living in multi-dimensional poverty [2, 3].

Belize is quite ethnically diverse. The population consists largely of those who identify as Creole (approximately 30% of the population and of mixed African/European heritage), Garifuna (approximately 7% of the population and Afro-Caribbean Black). Mayan (approximately 11% of the population and indigenous to Central America and includes various sub-groups including Q’echi and Mopan), Mesitzo (approximately 44% of the population and of mixed indigenous/Spanish heritage), and other groups including East Indian (approximately 10% of the population and originally from South Asia). Many Belizeans identify as belonging to more than one ethnic group [4, 5].

The Belizean healthcare system is dependent on public financing with 11–13% of fiscal spending (4.2% of GDP) allocated to health. Out of pocket spending on health is estimated to be 21.8% of total healthcare spending [6].

An evaluation of access to healthcare services should utilize a positive perspective of available assets. Physical healthcare assets in Belize include 463 physicians (10.5 per 10,000), 70 dentists (1.6 per 10,000), 1,159 nurses (26.3 per 10,000), 223 community health aides (5.1 per 10,000), and 112 pharmacists/dispensers (2.8 per 10,000) [1]. In 2022, there were 21 hospitals in the country (7 government, 14 private) and 92 health centers (N = 37) or health posts (N = 55). In the Toledo District, there are two Polyclinics that offer walk in care in Punta Gorda and San Antonio. There is also a small hospital in Punta Gorda as well as a dentist and an eye clinic. A larger, regional hospital in Dangriga is 102 miles to the north. Each village has a community health worker (CHW). In the Belizean system, health care, including prescription medication, is free at the point of service [7]. As such, it is reasonable to conclude that access to healthcare services is fair. The biggest barrier to access to care is probably time. Those living outside of Punta Gorda or San Antonio must take the bus to travel to a hospital or polyclinic. Bus fare is on the order of $2–5 (BZD) but the journey to and from a health facility combined with waiting to be seen can result in it taking an entire day to seek and receive care.

Less tangible healthcare assets may also promote access to healthcare services. In the PEN-3 cultural model, access to healthcare is also said to depend on one’s cultural identity, relationships and expectations, and degree of cultural empowerment [8].

According to Freedom House, Belize has several valuable assets that benefit health. It has a Global Freedom Score of 88/100 [9]. The government is stable, and respects civil liberties. Elections are free and fair, and people have a choice of political party. The rule of law is respected, as are personal autonomies and individual rights. Women and most indigenous peoples are well represented in government, although Mayans are less well represented. [9]Taken together, these factors suggest that non-tangible assets that can promote health exist. Health workers and NGOs should be aware of and employ these assets in their work.

Globally, the burden of disease caused by noncommunicable disease, notably hypertension and diabetes continue to increase [10]. Given that the median age in Belize is only 26.4 years, as this young population continues to age, the consequence of increasing chronic disease is of growing concern [11, 12]. By 2055, the average age of Belizeans will be nearly 40 by which age the prevalence of NCDs can be expected to increase over current numbers [13]. Consequently, in collaboration with Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), the Belizean Ministry of Health and Wellness’ Annual Report for 2020 has as its third priority, “Health and wellness promoted throughout the life course using an integrated primary healthcare approach to reduce noncommunicable diseases (NCD) and their risk factors, communicable diseases, mental health disorders, urban violence, and injuries [14].

Reliable health data for Belize are readily available but relatively little is disaggregated by sex or by district. Although national level data can provide perspective on the health status of a country, health indicators for a community may be different than those gathered at the national level [15]. Morey and colleagues evaluated ethnic and gender disparities in Belize for adult premature mortality in the early 2000’s [16], They found that the risk of premature death for those aged 15–59 was 13.5% in women and 22.7% in men. The remainder of their analysis was disaggregated by ethnicity and not by sex. Creole and Garifuna Belizeans had three times the risk of premature death than Mayan or Mestizo. They suggest that different underlying social determinants of health (SDOH) are responsible for the difference. As such, interventions for premature death in Belize should be targeted according to ethnicity. More recently, Dekker and colleagues performed a mixed-methods assessment of Type II diabetes and hypertension care in the Toledo district [17]. Although specific to the Toledo region, their results were not disaggregated by sex. Overall, they found the prevalence of both hypertension and Type II diabetes was 12%

By 2018, Belize had met 68% of all 17 SDG’s. However, by 2023, progress had decreased to 65% of goals met. Belize’s progress to date on SDG 3 (Good Health and Wellbeing) has been modest with many indicators stagnating or progress increasing at less than 50% of the required rate [19].

Since progress towards SDG #3 may not be uniform across the entire Belizean population, neither may be the stated priorities of individuals living in each district. Disaggregated data may prove helpful to develop the most effective educational, public health, or clinical interventions for a given district.

Objective

Given the lack of disaggregated health data, the objective was to perform a men’s health needs assessment to identify and prioritize what men in the Toledo District believe their health needs are.

Methods

Study setting

This study was carried out in collaboration with Hillside Health Care International which operates Hillside Clinic [20]. Hillside Clinic is a non-governmental organization (NGO) located near Punta Gorda Town, which is the largest town in the Toledo District. Hillside Clinic provides Toledo residents with free, year-round services in home health care, public health, rehabilitation, medical care, mobile clinics, community outreach, and pharmacy. Informal information provided by Hillside staff indicates that relatively few men seek care, either in clinic or during a mobile visit to a village. This is thought to be a function that most men in Toledo are subsistence farmers and unwilling or unable to give up a day’s work in the field to attend to health problems. This may also be a function of men historically being reluctant to seek out health care services when ill [21–23].

Needs assessment

Data sources and collection

The needs assessment used the Mobilizing for Action Through Planning and Partnerships (MAPP) process to develop a mixed-methods approach utilizing both quantitative and qualitative data [24].

Sources of quantitative data included epidemiological data from national vital statistics or disease registries and other public sources. Epidemiological data is helpful to explore how serious a problem is, how it is distributed across a population, and contributing factors that may improve or exacerbate it.

Sources of qualitative data included semi-structured individual and group interviews, as well as key informant interviews. A convenience sample of men living in nine communities in the Toledo District were interviewed as well as key informants of four Peace Corps volunteers, and four program directors (medical, nursing, rehabilitation, pharmacy) at Hillside Clinic. Qualitative data is useful to explore men’s lived experiences and health priorities.

A 13-question list of structured questions was prepared and tested in seven men who were not eligible to participate in the formal data collection. Several small changes in how questions were worded were made as a result.

Data was collected between January and June 2017.

Data analysis

Qualitative data were analyzed by Thematic Analysis [25]. Interviews containing qualitative data were digitally recorded and professionally transcribed into MS Word files. A code book was created, and open coding of the transcripts was performed by author JR to identify up to 2 Major Responses given by each respondent for a given question. The Major Reponses, demographic data, and relevant analyst comments were entered into MS Excel question by question to allow for preliminary analysis. The unit of analysis was by question, rather than by individual.

The MS Excel data were imported into NVivo and the responses identified earlier in MS Word were gathered and further analyzed by axial coding to identify more specific concepts. E.g., Question 1 – “What do you believe health is?” was open coded in MS Word to identify the general kinds of Major Responses provided such as “Eating healthy food”, “Body is in good working condition”, “Health is life” etc.

In the NVivo axial coding, these Major Responses were reduced into specific codes that multiple responses had in common such as “behavior”, “ability to work”, “not being sick” etc. Axial codes were further reduced into sub-codes if responses were more specific than “behavior”. E.g., “behavior – nutrition”, “behavior – activity”, “behavior – care seeking”. Content analysis was then performed on the axial codes and the number of responses for each was tabulated.

The four Hillside Clinic program directors and author JR then reviewed all transcripts, axial codes, and the results of the content analysis to identify the major themes emerging from the data. Qualitative data were analyzed inductively to identify relevant themes emerging from the data. We also performed a content analysis on the five most common open coded responses to each interview question.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Drake University Institutional Review Board (IRB 2016–17023) and the Director of Health Services from the Belizean Ministry of Health (via a letter to author JR). Interview respondents provided either informed written consent or informed verbal assent prior to being interviewed. This study adhered to the requirements of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Quantitative data

Belize has largely completed its epidemiological transition and the bulk of the burden of disease is now chronic disease or injury [6, 26]. According to PAHO, deaths from non-communicable diseases (NCDs) in Belize are the third highest in the Americas with 500 women and 750 men per 100,000 dying annually from an NCD [27]. Men aged 30–70 have a 23% chance of dying from an NCD and 47.5% of NCD deaths are in people under aged 70. Belizean men have 275 cardiac and 60 diabetes related deaths per 100,000 per year which are the third and fifth highest prevalence in the Americas. Prevalence of raised fasting glucose is 8.5% in men and 8.1% in women (third highest in the Americas). Overall, 24% of Belizeans have an elevated blood pressure. In men, 20.3% have an elevated blood pressure. (12th highest in the Americas). Leading causes of death are cardiovascular disease (13.6%), malignant neoplasms (10.0%), unintentional injury (6.6%), and diabetes mellitus (6.2%) [1]. Older data puts the prevalence of hypertension and diabetes mellitus even higher [28]. In 2009, a household survey suggested the prevalence of diabetes was estimated to be 13.1% while the prevalence of hypertension was estimated at 28.7%.

According to the Global Burden of Disease data, diabetes in Belize accounts for 5.64% of total Disability Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) (4.49% in men, 7.08% in women) and is increasing at a rate of 2.51%. Hypertensive heart disease accounts for 1.31% of total DALYs (1.23% in men, 1.42% in women) and is also increasing by 2.51% annually [29].

Inability to process risk

When asked to describe the health effects of cigarettes, alcohol, illicit drugs, or sexually transmitted infections (STIs), responses were frequently incorrect, or indicated that, because the respondent had no personal experience with the item, they were unable to answer the question. Discussion

A World Health Organization (WHO) report on NCDs states that the world is not on track to meet SDG 3.4.1 on Health and Wellness [10]. By 2020, only 31 Member States were on track to achieve a 33% reduction in premature mortality due to NCDs and the trend line towards meeting SDG 3.4.1 has been largely unchanged since 2015. The report further notes that differences in epidemiological risk factors for NCDs, as well as local socio-cultural and political factors will influence the interventions necessary to meet SDG 3.4.1 which suggests that disaggregated data will be needed to design appropriate programs.

As noted above, by 2018, Belize had met 68% of all 17 SDG’s. However, by 2023, progress had decreased to 65% of goals met. Belize’s progress to data on SDG 3 has been modest with many indicators stagnating or progress increasing at less than 50% of the required rate [19]. It appears that Belize’s progress on controlling NCDs is similar to the global rates of progress.

Data from the current study supports that hypertension and diabetes are major NCD concerns in Belize and that men’s understanding of them is poor. Given the limited progress made towards SDG 3 and a population of men with poor understanding of chronic illness, interventions intended to enhance progress should be designed on two levels.

The first of these would be the national level. A recent WHO report advises that high level, government interventions must be adopted [10]. Strategic interventions at this level include leadership, improved policy coherence, coordination across government sectors, and enhanced stakeholder engagement, including those suffering from NCDs. Such interventions are likely to require enhanced sustainable funding, better information technology infrastructure, and improved disease monitoring.

Disaggregated data may best be used at the second, more local level, such as in the Toledo District studied in this project. This data can be used to train CHWs which is consistent with SDG 3.c and SDG 3.d. The CHWs in turn can use their training to educate men in Toledo about NCDs. The role of diet in NCDs is important – especially in diabetes. Respondents frequently mentioned that eating proper foods was important to health. However, Belizean diets are heavy in rice, beans, and corn tortillas and low in Vitamin A, Calcium, and Potassium [30]. CHWs can use this information, coupled with men’s understanding of the role of diet in NCDs to educate them on controlling disease through diet. The literature provides several suggestions for how such educational materials should be designed. However, it must be noted that the literature on men’s health education draws from a wide variety of languages and cultures. How this may affect program design in Belize is unclear.

The literature repeatedly points out that men’s health behaviors and attitudes can be quite different from women’s. Shepherd notes that cultural expectations of men and socialization into the male role require that programs be tailored specifically to a male audience [21]. Aoun and colleagues found that a workplace-based intervention created using the health belief model was an effective approach [22]. Several studies in Black men found that creating trust between health workers and men is vital for program success [31, 32].

An evaluation of several US men’s health projects found that programs should be culturally sensitive to the target population and demonstrate concern to increase rapport between health workers and men [33]. Doing so allows for better dialogue and two-way communication. These authors also suggested that health workers engage more with health policy to create the system changes needed to enhance health in men with NCDs.

In a study of Latino men, Peak and colleagues found that men were keen to receive information, and preferred that the information be conveyed in as accessible a format as possible [34].

Depending on the setting, this could include programs delivered in a variety of locations including health departments, clinics, schools, worksites, or churches.

In a study of marginalized Danish men, Pedersen and colleagues found that most of their respondents felt that their interactions with the health system were marred by health workers being condescending [35]. They advised that men’s health interventions be mutually respectful, based in two-way communication, and that health workers remain mindful that men have different beliefs, behaviors, and expectations than women.

And, as noted above in the discussion on PEN3 cultural factors, CHW’s and others are well advised to emphasize existing resources when educating men rather than focus on any deficits that may exist.

Limitations

Several limitations to this study are noted. Although English is the official language of Belize, and all respondents spoke English, it may not have been their first language. This may have affected how respondents interpreted interview questions and how they answered. It may explain why answers of “unsure” or “unclear” were common.

Social desirability bias is always a potential risk in qualitative interviews. In the present study, a sample size of 57 and repeated similar answers as shown in the Content Analysis should mitigate against the risk of such bias.

Data for this study were collected between January and June 2017. The impact of Covid, economic changes, and recent changes in the political priorities of Belize’s biggest donor nation (United States) are unknown and may influence these results.

Data collected in one of the group interviews were difficult to interpret. In the Jalacte interview, the number of respondents was constantly changing and varied from a few to over a dozen. It was challenging to follow who was speaking and the information the respondent was trying to convey.

Ideally, qualitative research requires a team of analysts to evaluate the data. Data collection and early analysis was performed while author JR was on sabbatical leave in Toledo and additional analysts were not available. This concern was addressed by having all four program directors and author JR review all transcripts, axial codes, and the results of the content analysis to identify the major themes emerging from the data.

Conclusions

Men in the Toledo district of Southern Belize suffer from a high prevalence of NCDs including hypertension and diabetes and have a poor understanding of health and risk. This may contribute to Belize’s struggle to achieve the goals of SDG 3.4.1. Strengthening the healthcare workforce by improved training of CHWs as well as providing health education to men in the Toledo district is required to address these concerns.
