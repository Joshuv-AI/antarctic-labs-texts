Role of SQSTM1/p62 in regulating Mallory-Denk body in alcohol-associated liver disease
Authors: Kaitlyn Hinz, Hui Qian, Brandon Peiffer, Zhaoli Sun, Hong-Min Ni, Wen-Xing Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlcohol-associated liver disease (ALD) is a global health problem without an effective treatment. Mallory-Denk body (MDB) is a protein aggregate commonly found in alcohol-associated hepatitis (AH). MDB primarily contains ubiquitinated proteins, cytokeratin 8 and sequestosome 1 (SQSTM1)/p62. Stress granule (SG) is a cytosolic, membrane-less aggregate composed of various RNA-binding proteins and untranslated mRNA. However, the role and mechanisms of MDB and SG induced by alcohol and their implications in the pathogenesis of ALD remain largely unknown.MethodsSQSTM1/p62 whole body knockout and matched wild-type mice were subjected to the Gao-binge alcohol model or fed a 3,5-diethoxycarbonyl-1,4-dihydrocollidine (DDC) diet alongside Gao-binge alcohol model. ALD mouse liver tissues and human AH liver tissues underwent immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining and western blot analysis for SG and MDB markers.ResultsWe found that the livers of patients with AH had higher levels of SQSTM1/p62 (MDB marker) and Ras-GTPase-activating protein-binding protein 1 (an SG marker) using IHC staining, and these increased protein levels were enriched in detergent-insoluble fractions compared with healthy individuals. We further discovered that Gao-binge alcohol feeding increased insoluble SG markers, such as phosphorylated eukaryotic initiation factor 2 in mouse livers. Mice fed a DDC diet with Gao-binge alcohol had greater hepatic MDB formation and liver injury than those fed either diet alone. Loss of SQSTM1/p62 led to reduced protein aggregation involved in SGs and MDBs but increased liver injury in DDC plus Gao-binge alcohol-fed mice, indicating that SQSTM1/p62 is required for MDB formation and protects against alcohol-induced liver injury.ConclusionChronic plus binge alcohol exposure increases hepatic MDBs and moderate levels of SGs. p62/SQSTM1 is critical for the formation but is not essential for the clearance of MDBs, a process that may act as an adaptive protective mechanism against ALD.

Introduction

Alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD) is a leading cause of chronic liver disease-related deaths. The pathophysiology of ALD is characterised by hepatic steatosis, hepatocytic death, inflammation, increased megamitochondria, hepatocyte degeneration and ductular reaction (DR), which further progress to fibrosis, cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).13 Mallory-Denk bodies (MDBs), which are membrane-less cytoplasmic protein aggregates, are present in approximately three-fourths of patients with alcohol-associated hepatitis (AH) and in nearly all patients with alcohol-associated cirrhosis.4 5 MDBs generally contain high levels of sequestosome 1 (SQSTM1)/p62, ubiquitin (Ub) and cytokeratin 8 (CK8).57 MDBs are a hallmark of ALD and other chronic liver diseases, including metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis and HCC.5 8 9

MDB formation is triggered by cellular stress, which leads to the accumulation of misfolded proteins in the cytosol of hepatocytes.5 6 10 Mice exposed to 3,5-diethoxycarbonyl-1,4-dihydrocollidine (DDC) and a high-fat diet exhibit MDB formation.7 11 A crucial step in MDB formation is the stress-induced upregulation of CK8, which becomes cross-linked by transglutaminase 2.12 13 Accumulating ubiquitinated CK8 and misfolded proteins can overwhelm the proteasome, failing protein quality control.14 Additionally, chronic alcohol consumption impairs hepatic lysosomal biogenesis, leading to insufficient autophagy,15 16 which may further contribute to the accumulation of MDBs. The Ub-associated (UBA) domain of p62 directly binds to ubiquitinated K8 and misfolded proteins tagged with Ub, facilitating their sequestration into aggresomes or MDBs through the Phox and Bem1 (PB1) domain of p62.17 p62 plays a crucial role in the degradation of protein aggregates and aggresomes via autophagy by acting as an autophagy receptor. This is achieved through its interaction with microtubule-associated protein 1A/1B-light chain 3 (LC3) via its LC3-interacting region (LIR) domain.1719 Although MDBs have been recognised in ALD for decades, the mechanisms by which MDBs are formed and cleared in ALD, and how they contribute to the pathogenesis of ALD, remain unclear.

Stress granules (SGs) are cytosolic, membrane-less aggregates composed of various RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) and untranslated mRNA.20 21 They also contain eukaryotic initiation factor 2 (eIF2) and Ras-GTPase-activating protein-binding protein 1 (G3BP1), along with RBPs such as Hu antigen R protein (HuR) and T-cell intracellular antigen-1 (TIA-1).22 SGs can fuse and flow like liquid droplets, allowing for rapid exchange of molecules within the droplet and with the surrounding cytoplasm due to the absence of a physical lipid barrier. Some of these non-membrane-bound organelles can mature into a gel-like state or solidify into amyloid-like aggregates.23 The formation of these organelles is driven by liquid-liquid phase separation from the surrounding cytoplasm, resulting in a condensed phase that incorporates participating molecules. p62 has been shown to undergo liquid-liquid phase separation, oligomerising through its PB1 domain and clustering UBA domains from each p62 oligomer. This creates a multivalent hub that is accessible to various partners.24 Additionally, p62 acts as an RNA-binding protein and binds to vault RNAs, which are non-coding RNAs approximately 88–100 nucleotides long, via its ZZ domain.2426 Subsequent interactome studies have shown that p62 is enriched with RBPs and may also be involved in SG formation.27 SGs have been demonstrated to play a protective role in cells during early environmental stress.28 Notably, aged p62 knockout (KO) mice exhibit obesity as well as increased liver injury and inflammation when subjected to the Gao-binge alcohol model.29 However, it remains unclear whether p62 plays a role in the formation of SGs and MDBs in mice fed alcohol, thereby contributing to ALD.

In this study, we found that human AH livers exhibit increased SGs and MDBs, which are associated with increased hepatic p62 aggregates. By using a clinically relevant Gao-binge alcohol mouse model, in conjunction with a DDC diet, we demonstrated that p62 is critical for the formation of MDBs but is not essential for the clearance of MDBs. This process may provide a protective effect against alcohol-induced liver injury.

Materials and methods

Animal experiments

Whole-body p62 KO mice were generated by Dr Thomas Rülicke’s laboratory and characterised in Kurt Zatloukal’s laboratory as previously described.2931 The p62 KO mice were backcrossed to the C57BL/6J background for more than nine generations before being used for the experiment. All mice were specific pathogen-free and received humane care in a barrier rodent facility under standard experimental conditions (room temperature 22±1°C, relative humidity 50±10%, and 12-hour light/dark cycle). The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Kansas Medical Center approved all procedures and research ethics (IACUCIPROTO2024-387_24-04-387). The eight-week-old male p62 KO mice and their matched wild-type (WT) counterparts (generated from breeding the p62 heterozygous mice) were subjected to the chronic plus binge (Gao-binge) alcohol model as previously described.32 33 Briefly, the mice were acclimated to the Lieber-DeCarli liquid control diet (F1259SP, Bio-Serv, Flemington, NJ, USA) for 5 days, followed by further feeding with the liquid control or the ethanol diet (F1258SP, Bio-Serv, 5% ethanol) for 10 days. The volume of the control diet given to the mice was matched to the volume of ethanol diet consumed. On the last day of feeding, the mice were administered 5 g/kg ethanol or 9 g/kg maltose dextran and sacrificed 8 hours later. Blood and liver tissues were collected for biochemical and histological analysis. For the DDC diet and Gao-binge alcohol model, 8–10 weeks old male p62 KO and matched WT mice were fed a DDC diet or control chow diets (#AD5001, Research Diets, New Brunswick, New Jersey, USA) with free access to food/water for 4 weeks. After 4 weeks of feeding, some of the animals were sacrificed, while the others were placed on the Gao-binge alcohol model. Liver injury was assessed by measuring serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alkaline phosphatase (ALP). Liver sections were prepared, and H&E staining was performed as previously described.34

Human samples

The human healthy and AH liver tissues were obtained from the Clinical Resource for Alcoholic Hepatitis Investigations at Johns Hopkins University. Detailed information regarding these liver tissue samples was previously described.35 Research Ethics Approval for Human Participants: Johns Hopkins University (IRB 00197893).

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in our research’s design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans.

Lysate preparation and western blot analysis

The process for extracting soluble and insoluble proteins has been previously outlined.29 The livers were homogenised using a Triton X-100 lysate buffer containing 0.25 mol/L sucrose, 10 mmol/L 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (pH 7.5), 1% Triton X-100, 150 mmol/L NaCl and freshly added 1 mmol/L dithiothreitol. The samples were sonicated for 10 s three times and then placed on ice for 15 min. After this, the lysates were centrifuged for 15 min at 13 000 rpm in a microcentrifuge at 4°C. The resulting supernatants were collected as the Triton X-100 soluble fraction without disturbing the pellet. The pellets were then reconstituted in 2% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and sonicated for three intervals of 15 s. Following this, the lysates were spun again for an additional 5 min at 13 000 rpm in a microcentrifuge at 4°C. The supernatants obtained from this step were collected as the Triton X-100 insoluble fraction. Protein concentrations were quantified using the Pierce Bicinchoninic Acid Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). A total of 30 µg of protein from each liver soluble and insoluble lysate was separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes and blocked with 5% non-fat milk in Tris buffered saline with Tween-20 at room temperature for 1 hour with shaking. The membranes were probed with various primary and secondary antibodies, and the protein bands were developed using the SuperSignal West Pico Plus chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Scientific, Cat#34578). Detailed information regarding the antibodies used is provided in online supplemental table 1. The results from the western blot analysis were evaluated using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Electron microscopy

Human AH liver tissues were finely cut and fixed using 2% glutaraldehyde in a phosphate buffer at pH 7.4 (0.1 mol/L), followed by fixation with 1% osmium tetroxide. After the dehydration process, thin sections of the tissues were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Observations were made using a JEM 1016CX electron microscope (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), and images were acquired digitally.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded liver or adipose tissue sections were deparaffinised, rehydrated and then incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide (Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA, Cat#H323500), blocking endogenous peroxidase activity, for 10 min at room temperature. The tissue sections were stained with different primary and secondary antibodies, then visualised with NovaRED HRP substrate (Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA, Cat# SK4805). After visualisation, the tissue sections were counterstained with haematoxylin (Ricca Chemical Company, Arlington, TX, USA, Cat# 3530–32). Finally, the slides were covered with the mounting medium (Thermo Scientific, 8310–16) and observed under a Nikon microscope (Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The detailed antibody information is listed in online supplemental table 1.

Statistical analysis

All data obtained were analysed using GraphPad Prism software V.9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA). Data were analysed by Student t-test for two-group comparison, one-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s HSD post hoc test for multigroup comparison to evaluate the statistical differences by GraphPad. The value of p<0.05 was considered a statistically significant difference among groups. All experimental data were reported as mean±SE.

Results

Livers of patient with AH contain increased hepatic markers of MDBs and SGs

H&E staining results revealed increased pink colour dense-packed protein aggregates in livers of patient with AH but not in healthy human livers (figure 1A, black arrow heads). To further characterise these aggregates, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining to probe the presence of MDB markers p62 and CK8, as well as the SG marker G3BP1. Increased p62 and CK8-positive aggregates were readily found in AH livers but not in healthy livers, suggesting increased MDBs in human AH livers (figure 1B, black arrows). Moreover, IHC staining also revealed increased G3BP1-positive staining SG structures in AH livers (figure 1B, red arrows). Quantitative analysis of these IHC imaging data revealed significantly increased p62 and G3BP1-positive staining, but not CK8-positive staining, in AH liver tissues (figure 1C). Electron-dense membrane-less aggregate/aggresome was also detected by electron microscope analysis of human AH sample (figure 1D, arrow). Consistent with the IHC staining data, western blot analysis showed increased levels of p62 and showed an increasing trend of levels of ubiquitinated proteins and CK8/CK18 in both detergent soluble and insoluble fractions in AH livers compared with healthy livers. While the levels of phosphorylated eIF2α (p-eIF2α) increased, the levels of HuR and TIA-1 did not show marked changes in both the detergent-soluble and insoluble fractions in AH livers compared with healthy control livers. Interestingly, while the levels of G3BP1 monomer markedly decreased in both the detergent soluble and insoluble fractions of AH livers, the levels of G3BP1 multimers that are found in SG were markedly increased in the insoluble fractions of AH livers compared with healthy human livers (figure 1E–F). Together, these results indicate that patients with AH have increased hepatic MDBs and SGs.

Gao-binge alcohol feeding induces mild hepatic formation of MDBs and SGs, which is further reduced in p62 KO mice

We next explored whether hepatic p62 is required for the aggregation/solidification of MDBs and SGs in Gao-binge alcohol-fed mouse livers. We performed IHC staining and western blot analysis on Gao-binge alcohol fed WT and p62 KO mouse liver tissues for Ub, CK8 and p62 (markers for MDBs), and HuR, G3BP1 and p-eIF2α (markers for SG). IHC staining analysis showed increased diffuse cytosolic staining of CK8 and p62 in some hepatocytes near the central vein area in Gao-binge alcohol-fed WT mice (figure 2A). However, these CK8 and p62 positive stained aggregations were less evident compared with human AH liver tissues (figure 1A). Gao-binge alcohol feeding also increased cytosolic Ub staining in WT mouse livers, which tends to be in a more aggregated form than CK8 and p62. IHC staining showed that HuR was mainly localised in hepatocyte nuclei in control diet-fed mouse livers. However, Gao-binge alcohol feeding caused an increased cytosolic HuR staining, in addition to the nuclear HuR staining in WT mouse livers. Similarly, Gao-binge alcohol only induced mild cytosolic diffuse G3BP1 positive staining in WT mouse livers. Nonetheless, there was increased staining of Ub and CK8, but not G3BP1 and HuR staining in Gao-binge alcohol fed-p62 KO mouse livers compared with WT mice (figure 2A–B). Western blot analysis showed that Gao-binge alcohol feeding increased levels of insoluble CK8 but did not affect the levels of soluble CK8 and had little effect on the levels of both soluble and insoluble p62 in WT mouse livers. Gao-binge alcohol feeding also markedly increased hepatic levels of insoluble p-eIF2α and G3BP1, but had little effect on HuR and soluble G3BP1. Similar to the IHC staining results, western blot analysis showed an overall decreased trend of levels of insoluble CK8, soluble G3BP1 and soluble HuR in alcohol-fed p62 KO mouse livers (figure 2C–D). Together, these results suggest that Gao-binge alcohol feeding increased mild hepatocytic MDB and SG formation in mouse livers and that this effect was attenuated by the loss of p62.

Hepatic p62 is not essential for cholestatic injury induced by a DDC diet

The DDC diet is known to promote the formation of hepatocytic MDBs and cause cholestatic liver injury by inducing the development of intraductal porphyrin plugs and obstructing bile ducts. Both WT and p62 KO mice that were fed a DDC diet for 4 weeks showed significantly increased serum levels of ALT, AST and ALP compared with mice fed a chow diet (figure 3A–C). Additionally, both WT and p62 KO mice on the DDC diet exhibited increased serum and hepatic bile acid levels (figure 3D–E). Importantly, no significant differences in serum ALT, AST, ALP levels or bile acid levels, both serum and hepatic, were observed between the DDC diet-fed WT and p62 KO mice. These findings indicate that p62 is not required for the cholestatic liver injury induced by a DDC diet in mice.

Hepatic p62 is dispensable for DDC-diet induced cholestatic injury and hepatocytic MDB formation

H&E staining revealed an increased presence of ductular structures (figure 4A, arrowheads) and the accumulation of porphyrins (figure 4A, arrows) in the livers of both WT and p62 KO mice fed a DDC diet. Additionally, DDC-fed WT mice had increased areas of Sirius Red staining, which was further increased in the livers of DDC diet-fed p62 KO mice (figure 4B–C). Western blot analysis indicated that the hepatic levels of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and CK19 showed an elevated trend in WT and p62 KO mice on the DDC diet (figure 4D). The DDC diet also tended to decrease in hepatic levels of p62 while tended to increase in LC3-II, suggesting that DDC diet feeding may increase hepatic autophagic flux in WT mice (figure 4E). Consistent with the Sirius Red staining results, the levels of hepatic α-SMA were higher in DDC-fed p62 KO mice than in WT mice (figure 4D). The DDC diet also decreased soluble Ub levels in the livers of both WT and p62 KO mice (figure 4F–G). In WT mice, detergent-soluble and insoluble CK8/CK18 levels increased after DDC diet feeding. In contrast, only the insoluble CK8/CK18 levels and soluble CK18 levels tended to rise, while soluble CK8 levels did not change in DDC diet-fed p62 KO mice livers. Furthermore, feeding a DDC diet increased hepatic levels of soluble G3BP1 but tended to decrease in those of insoluble G3BP1 monomers in WT mice. Although the baseline level of soluble G3BP1 was higher in p62 KO mice than WT mice, the DDC diet did not affect this baseline and decreased insoluble G3BP1 levels in p62 KO mice. No G3BP1 multimers were detected in WT and p62 KO mice on the DDC diet (figure 4F–G). These data suggest that the DDC induced hepatic MDBs but inhibits SGs in mouse livers. p62 was not essential for the cholestatic liver injury and the formation of MDBs induced by the DDC diet.

Loss of p62 does not affect the recovery of liver injury or MDBs after withdrawal of DDC diet

We evaluated whether p62 influences the recovery from liver injury and MDBs after the withdrawal of DDC. At 15 days after the withdrawal of DDC (hereafter referred to as post-DDC), serum levels of ALT, AST and ALP returned to baseline in both WT and p62 KO mice (figure 5A–C). Moreover, elevated serum and hepatic bile acid levels also returned to baseline in WT and p62 KO mice after 15 days of post-DDC treatment (figure 5D–E). However, histological analysis via H&E staining revealed that DR and porphyrin accumulation persisted in both WT and p62 KO mice post-DDC (figure 5F). Results from the Sirius Red staining of liver tissues and western blot analysis for hepatic α-SMA revealed similar levels of Sirius red staining areas and hepatic levels of α-SMA in WT post-DDC. However, areas of Sirius Red staining decreased and hepatic levels of α-SMA tended to decrease in p62 KO mice post-DDC (figure 5F–H). Interestingly, the levels of insoluble CK8 in the liver were lower in WT than in p62 KO mice post-DDC and tended to decrease compared with those fed the DDC diet (figure 5I). These findings suggest that liver injury and MDB formation induced by the DDC diet can be recovered after DDC withdrawal, irrespective of p62 status. However, porphyrin accumulation persists even after the DDC diet was withdrawn.

Alcohol feeding increases liver injury and inflammation but not cholestasis in DDC diet primed mice, regardless of p62

WT and p62 KO mice were fed a DDC diet for 4 weeks, then switched to the Gao-binge alcohol model (figure 6A). Serum levels of ALT and AST but not ALP increased in alcohol-fed WT and p62 KO mice compared with control diet-fed mice, although the changes did not reach statistical significance. Serum ALT levels were more than twice as high in p62 KO mice compared with WT mice, although they were not statistically significantly different (figure 6B–D). Serum and hepatic bile acid levels were not affected by alcohol in both WT and p62 KO (figure 6E–F). Western blot analysis showed that ethanol feeding tended to increase in hepatic levels of α-SMA in p62 KO mice but not in WT mice. Moreover, hepatic levels of CK19 tended to increase in Gao-binge alcohol-fed p62 KO mice compared with WT mice (figure 6G). Histological analysis via H&E staining revealed that porphyrin accumulation was not affected by alcohol in both WT and p62 KO mice, and alcohol induced similar accumulation of hepatic lipid droplets in both genotypes (figure 6H). IHC staining readily detected CK19-positive DR in all groups, but was higher in alcohol-fed p62 KO mice (figure 6H). Moreover, the number of F4/80-positive macrophages/Kupffer cells appeared more abundant in alcohol-fed WT and p62 KO mice. Myeloperoxidase-positive neutrophil numbers tended to increase in alcohol-fed WT but not p62 KO mouse livers. In contrast, Sirius red staining areas were only slightly increased in alcohol-fed WT and p62 KO mice (figure 6H–I). These findings suggest that alcohol may further increase DDC diet-initiated liver injury and DR in p62 KO mice but not cholestasis, inflammation and fibrosis.

Lack of p62 impairs alcohol-induced MDB formation in DDC diet primed mice

Western blot analysis indicated that alcohol tended to increase in both soluble and insoluble CK8 levels without affecting Ub levels in WT and p62 KO mice post-DDC treatment. However, the levels of soluble CK8 were much higher, whereas the levels of insoluble CK8 tended to be lower in alcohol-fed p62 KO mice compared with alcohol-fed WT mice, suggesting a critical role of p62 in the formation but not clearance of MDBs post-DDC (figure 7A–B). Alcohol-feeding also increased both soluble and insoluble levels of p62 post-DDC treatment, suggesting impaired autophagic degradation of p62 by alcohol (figure 7A–B). Alcohol feeding did not affect the levels of both soluble and insoluble G3BP1 in both WT and p62 KO mice, although alcohol feeding tended to increase in soluble but not insoluble HuR levels in both WT and p62 KO mice (figure 7A–B). The levels of both soluble and insoluble Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1) were markedly increased in post-DDC and alcohol-fed post-DDC p62 KO mouse livers (figure 7C–D). The levels of glutathione synthetase (GCLM) but not glutamate cysteine ligase catalytic (GCLC) significantly increased in post-DDC and alcohol fed post-DDC WT, but not p62 KO, mouse livers (figure 7C). IHC staining showed an increasing trend in p62 and increased CK8-positive aggregates (MDB) in alcohol-fed WT mouse livers (figure 7E–G). Together, these data indicate that p62 was necessary for the formation of alcohol-induced MDBs but is not essential for the clearance of MDB.

Discussion

Many patients with AH have been identified with MDBs, as well as those with other chronic liver diseases, such as viral hepatitis and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease.5 7 36 MDBs generally serve as markers of cellular stress and a prognostic factor for these chronic liver diseases.36 37 SGs share similar characteristics with MDBs; both are cytosolic, membrane-less aggregates composed of various proteins.5 22 38 Interestingly, dynamic SGs can transform into solid aggregates and mature into inclusion bodies after prolonged stress.39 These inclusion bodies are enriched with ubiquitinated proteins and p62, which facilitate their degradation through autophagy.39 One of the primary functions of SGs is to halt translation, conserve energy and redistribute cellular resources to manage stress.21 22 While SGs typically disassemble after stress is alleviated, some can solidify into abnormal aggregates that require degradation through autophagy.39 40 In this study, we observed a high prevalence of MDBs and SGs that were positive for p62, CK8/CK18 and G3BP-1 in the livers of patients with AH. Moreover, data from experiments using Gao-binge alcohol-fed WT and p62 KO mice indicate that p62-mediated formation of MDBs and SGs may help protect against ALD.

The homeostasis of MDBs and SGs is primarily influenced by their formation and turnover/clearance. While the formation of these structures is generally triggered by stress response signals,38 their turnover and clearance are mainly facilitated by autophagy due to their insoluble nature.5 41 42 As an autophagy receptor protein, p62 is crucial for the selective autophagic removal of organelles and protein aggregates.5 42 43 Our findings indicate that p62 plays a critical role in forming MDBs and SGs, but its significance in their clearance is less pronounced. This conclusion is based on several observations. First, in mouse livers subjected to Gao-binge alcohol feeding, we observed increased levels of insoluble CK8, G3BP-1, HuR and p-eIF2α in WT mouse livers. However, these increases were less pronounced in p62 KO mouse livers, suggesting that p62-mediated autophagic clearance might not contribute to the accumulation of MDBs and SGs in alcohol-fed mice. Second, the levels of insoluble CK8 were similar between WT and p62 KO mouse livers after DDC withdrawal, indicating that p62 is dispensable for MDBs clearance. Notably, due to the high expression of CK8 in ductular cells, future work is needed to use isolated hepatocytes to confirm that CK8 expression is indeed present in hepatocyte MDBs. Third, in DDC-primed mouse livers, the levels of insoluble CK8 were lower in alcohol-fed p62 KO mice than in WT mice. Notably, Gao-binge alcohol feeding alone increased some SG markers, such as HuR and p-eIF2α, but did not increase G3BP-1 multimers. The DDC diet did not induce SG formation despite significantly increasing MDB formation in mouse livers. This suggests that the conditions leading to increased MDB formation may not necessarily trigger SG formation, indicating that MDB formation can occur independently of SG formation. Previous findings that the absence of p62 results in the failure to form large, mature MDBs in DDC-fed mice,30 align with our current clinically relevant ALD mouse models that further support a role of p62 in the formation but not the clearance of MDBs.

MDBs primarily consist of keratins (CK8 and CK18) and other protein components, including p62, Ub and various misfolded proteins.5 7 36 Although the exact sequential events regulating MDBs assembly are not fully understood, observations from in vivo studies using CK8 KO mice and cultured cells suggest that disrupting the ratio of CK8 to CK18, along with increased post-translational modifications such as heightened phosphorylation of CK8 and CK18 can result in increased cross-linking of these keratins.44 45 Cross-linked CK8 and CK18 may form an initial scaffold that recruits other MDB components like p62, as immunofluorescence microscopy studies have shown colocalisation of p62 with CK8 and CK18 in cultured cells.46

As a multidomain protein, p62 serves as an intracellular scaffold and signalling hub, interacting with various proteins across multiple cellular pathways.5 10 47 The N-terminal PB1 domain can oligomerise, resulting in the formation of p62 homo-oligomers. The UBA domain located at the C-terminal binds to Ub-labelled proteins, leading to the formation of protein aggregates. The oligomerisation mediated by the PB1 domain, along with phosphorylation by casein kinase 2 and tumour necrosis factor receptor-associated factor (TRAF) family member-associated nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) activator (TANK)-binding kinase 1, enhances the binding affinities of p62 with other proteins. This includes the interaction between the UBA domain of p62 and ubiquitinated misfolded proteins, as well as the interaction between the LIR domain of p62 and LC3. These interactions promote the relocation of ubiquitinated protein aggregates to autophagosomes for degradation.5 10 47 However, the effects of alcohol on p62 phosphorylation and its influence on MDB formation remain unclear and warrant further investigation. Additionally, p62 interacts with KEAP1 through its KEAP1 interaction region, which facilitates nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) activation via the non-canonical KEAP1-NRF2 pathway.5 NRF2 has been shown to regulate the expression of CK8 and may promote MDB formation in HepG2 cells.48 Increased hepatic KEAP1 in post-DDC and alcohol-fed post-DDC p62 KO mice may indicate decreased NRF2 activation in p62 KO mice, which could also contribute to the reduced CK8 expression. Therefore, a deficiency in p62 might lead to decreased hepatic NRF2 activation and lower CK8 levels, possibly resulting in less MDB formation. Future studies using post-DDC and alcohol-fed post-DDC NRF2 KO mice could further clarify NRF2’s role in MDB formation. Lastly, alcohol consumption impairs both autophagy/lysosomal and proteasomal degradation pathways, resulting in the accumulation of misfolded proteins that may further promote MDB formation in hepatocytes.15 16 49

In comparison to MDBs, the effects of alcohol on SG formation are less well understood. Alcohol exposure has been shown to increase hepatic levels of p-eIF2α,50 which may help initiate SG formation in ALD. Future studies must investigate hepatic SGs by genetically or pharmacologically inhibiting eIF2α in ALD mouse models. Moreover, the exact mechanisms by which p62 is involved in forming MDBs and SGs warrant further investigation in future studies.

Rodent models that use alcohol feeding alone in WT animals can only replicate some early features of ALD and do not produce the full spectrum of AH phenotypes.51 In the present study, we observed a moderate increase in insoluble CK8 in the livers of Gao-binge alcohol-fed mice compared to those of human AH livers. These findings suggest that Gao-binge alcohol feeding alone may not induce severe MDB formation in mice. In contrast to alcohol feeding alone, mice fed a DDC diet for 4 weeks exhibited more pronounced MDB formations, severe liver injury, cholestasis, inflammation, fibrosis and accumulation of progenitor cells (DRs), effectively mirroring the features of AH. Interestingly, most of these phenotypes improved after the DDC diet was withdrawn. In the mice primed with DDC feeding, liver injury, cholestasis and fibrosis largely recovered even after subsequent Gao-binge alcohol feeding. However, levels of MDBs and DRs remained significantly higher in the Gao-binge alcohol-fed mice. This persistence is likely due to impaired clearance of MDBs resulting from compromised lysosomal function induced by alcohol.15 16 DRs often occur in response to liver injury as a reparative process, involving either increased proliferation of cholangiocytes or the transdifferentiation of hepatocytes into cholangiocytes.52 53 While liver injury largely recovered, serum ALT levels remained relatively elevated, particularly in p62 KO mice. Although the relevance of the DDC model to ALD remains unclear, the combination of DDC and alcohol feeding presents a promising model for future mechanistic studies of AH, as it exhibits many hallmarks of the disease, including MDB formation.

In the present study, we found that p62 is not essential for DDC diet-induced cholestatic liver injury or MDBs formation. However, p62 KO mice showed increased Sirius Red staining areas and hepatic α-SMA levels. Notably, p62 directly interacts with vitamin D receptor and retinoid X receptor, promoting their heterodimerisation in hepatic stellate cells, which inhibits liver fibrosis.54 Therefore, it is likely that the deficiency of p62 in hepatic stellate cells may contribute to the increased fibrosis in DDC-fed p62 KO mice. Future studies using hepatic stellate cell-specific p62 KO mice may help elucidate the cell type-specific role of p62 in MDBs and liver fibrosis.

While MDBs have long been recognised in AH, it is unclear whether they contribute to the pathogenesis of ALD or merely serve as prognostic markers. MDBs may act as a protective mechanism against proteotoxicity because soluble protein aggregates are generally more toxic than insoluble protein aggregates or inclusion bodies.41 55 Similarly, SGs may function as adaptive protective mechanisms for cell survival by sequestering and storing RNAs, proteins and other cellular components, thereby mitigating the immediate impact of stress on translation and other cellular processes. In a mouse model of MDBs, CK8 KO mice show minimal formation of MDBs in hepatocytes and experience more progressive liver injury.45 In the present study, we observed reduced formation of MDBs and SGs in mice lacking p62, which is associated with increased liver injury in both young and aged p62 KO mice fed with alcohol.29 In conclusion, p62 promotes the formation of MDBs and SGs, but not their clearance, as protective mechanisms against ALD (figure 8). Further research is needed to understand better how p62 contributes to alcohol-induced MDB and SG formation, as this may help identify novel therapeutic targets related to protein aggregates in treating ALD.

Study limitations

There are several limitations to this study. First, in addition to p62, mammalian cells have multiple autophagy receptor proteins, such as neighbour of BRCA2 gene 1 (NBR1), optineurin and nuclear dot protein 52.5 It is quite possible that other autophagy receptor proteins like NBR1 may compensate for the loss of p62 in promoting the autophagic removal of MDBs, which could explain why p62 is not essential for MDB clearance in our study. Second, although the Gao-binge alcohol model is currently considered the best mouse model to mimic human ALD, it does not produce clear hepatic MDB formation. While the combination of DDC plus alcohol feeding can markedly induce MDB formation in mouse livers, humans do not typically consume DDC, so the relevance to alcohol-related MDBs in humans remains uncertain.
