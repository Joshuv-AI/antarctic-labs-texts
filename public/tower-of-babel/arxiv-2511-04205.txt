LLM-as-a-Judge is Bad, Based on AI Attempting the Exam Qualifying for the Member of the Polish National Board of Appeal
Michał Karp, Anna Kubaszewska, Magdalena Król, Robert Król, Aleksander Smywiński-Pohl, Mateusz Szymański, Witold Wydmański

Abstract
This study provides an empirical assessment of whether current large language models (LLMs) can pass the official qualifying examination for membership in Poland’s National Appeal Chamber (Krajowa Izba Odwoławcza). The authors examine two related ideas: using LLM as actual exam candidates and applying the ’LLM-as-a-judge’ approach, in which model-generated answers are automatically evaluated by other models. The paper describes the structure of the exam, which includes a multiple-choice knowledge test on public procurement law and a written judgment, and presents the hybrid information recovery and extraction pipeline built to support the models. Several LLMs (including GPT-4.1, Claude 4 Sonnet and Bielik-11B-v2.6) were tested in closed-book and various Retrieval-Augmented Generation settings. The results show that although the models achieved satisfactory scores in the knowledge test, none met the passing threshold in the practical written part, and the evaluations of the ’LLM-as-a-judge’ often diverged from the judgments of the official examining committee. The authors highlight key limitations: susceptibility to hallucinations, incorrect citation of legal provisions, weaknesses in logical argumentation, and the need for close collaboration between legal experts and technical teams. The findings indicate that, despite rapid technological progress, current LLMs cannot yet replace human judges or independent examiners in Polish public procurement adjudication.

1
Introduction

With the growing popularity of Large Language Models (LLMs) it is observed that people want these
Artificial Intelligence (AI) models to accomplish more sophisticated tasks. One of the areas that
is particularly well suited for taking advantage of them is law. This domain of human endeavor is
dominated by text and thus is particularly well suited for receiving help from LLMs. Inspired by
this observation, in this research we want to find out if an LLM can pass an exam for a judge in the
Polish National Appeal Chamber (pol.
Krajowa Izba Odwoławcza, NAC
), verifying its competence
in a well defined testing procedure where theoretical knowledge of provisions has to be combined
with practical ability to apply these laws in a legal case.

On the other hand, the term LLM-as-a-judge
7
, functions as a name for the procedure when an LLM is applied
to judge if a text generated by this or another LLM is valid vis-a-vis certain reference answer.
The idea to follow such a procedure stems from two facts: it is impossible to apply well-defined
metrics such as precision and recall to a generated content and that human evaluation of generated content
is time consuming and costly. One solution is to fed the LLM-as-a-judge with the generated content and the
reference content and ask it, how much the generated content reflects the features of the reference answer.

In this research we show that combining these two approaches in the context of law may lead to unexpected an
unwanted outcomes. In the rest of the paper we introduce the exam for judges in the Polish National Appeal Chamber,
how this exam was approached with the use of LLMs and what were the outcomes of the preliminary experiments
we have conducted to evaluate the probability of the models passing the exam. Then we describe the actual exam
taken by the LLMs in the same regime as the human participants and compare the assessment made
by the real examining committee with the assessment made by LLM-as-a-judge system. We show that
although the metrics achieved by our models in the preliminary experiments were pretty satisfactory,
the final exam was not passed by any of the models.

1.1
Research Motivation and Organizational Impact

The Public Procurement Office, which provides operational support to the NAC, initiated this research to evaluate the extent to which Large Language Model (LLM) technology could support the work of Chamber adjudicators in various capacities including charge synthesis, documentation searches, identification of precedential rulings in analogous factual circumstances, legal provision analysis for identifying adjudicatory trends, and general knowledge assessment within the specialized field of public procurement law. The experimental results will inform management decisions regarding the scope of further implementation of this technology within the institutional framework.

The potential positive impact of successful examination performance by AI systems on the organization extends beyond technological validation. Such outcomes could demonstrate the feasibility of AI-assisted legal research tools, potentially enhancing the efficiency and consistency of adjudicatory processes while maintaining the high standards of legal analysis required in public procurement dispute resolution. This technological advancement could contribute to more streamlined case processing and improved accessibility of legal reasoning patterns, ultimately serving the broader goal of ensuring fair and efficient public procurement procedures in Poland.

2
Exam for a Judge in the National Appeal Chamber

2.1
National Appeal Chamber

The National Appeal Chamber is a specialized adjudicatory body established within the Polish public procurement system, operating as the primary instance for resolving procurement-related disputes.
The Chamber serves as the competent authority for hearing appeals submitted in public procurement proceedings and replaced the previous system of arbitration panels that had operated since 2007.

2.2
Scope of Competence and Legal Framework

The Chamber’s primary responsibilities encompass: (1) hearing appeals in cases referenced in Article 513 of the Public Procurement Law (
Prawo Zamówień Publicznych
)
23
; (2) reviewing applications for lifting contract conclusion prohibitions as specified in Article 578(1) of the Public Procurement Law; and (3) adopting resolutions containing opinions on contracting authorities’ objections to the results of preliminary and ad-hoc controls conducted by the President of the Office.
The legal foundation for the Chamber’s operations is established in Articles 473-492 of the Public Procurement Law. The Chamber comprises no more than 100 members, appointed by the minister responsible for economic affairs from among candidates meeting statutory requirements who achieved the best results in qualification proceedings. Members maintain independence in their adjudication and are bound exclusively by applicable legal provisions.

2.3
Qualification Process Structure for Chamber membership

The recruitment process for Chamber membership follows a rigorous three-stage assessment protocol regulated by the Regulation of the Prime Minister of February 22, 2021, regarding the procedure for conducting qualification and supplementary qualification proceedings for candidates for National Appeal Chamber membership
24
. The examination structure comprises: (1) a knowledge assessment consisting of 50 single-choice questions testing theoretical understanding of public procurement and civil law, worth a maximum of 100 points; (2) a written practical examination requiring legal analysis of a factual case study within a 120-minute timeframe, also worth 100 points; and (3) a qualification interview evaluating personal characteristics and professional competencies, worth up to 30 points.

2.4
The 2025 Recruitment Cycle

The most recent qualification proceeding was announced on March 7, 2025, by the Minister of Development and Technology, acting pursuant to Article 477(6) of the Public Procurement Law and Section 18(1) of the Prime Minister’s Regulation of February 22, 2021. The recruitment cycle established a limit of 13 positions to be filled through the qualification process, with application submissions accepted until March 3, 2025.

The qualification requirements mandate candidates to possess: Polish citizenship; completion of legal studies; successful completion of judicial, prosecutorial, notarial, attorney, or legal counsel examinations; minimum five years of professional experience in public procurement and civil law; full legal capacity; enjoyment of full public rights; clean criminal record; and appropriate security clearance for accessing classified information.

2.5
Required Legal Knowledge Areas

The qualification examination encompasses a comprehensive range of legal domains essential for effective adjudication in public procurement matters. The statutory scope of required knowledge, as defined in Annex 1 to the Prime Minister’s Regulation, includes fourteen distinct areas of legal expertise.

The primary focus centers on Polish public procurement legislation, which includes the Public Procurement Law, the Concession Act
2
, their implementing regulations, and the development of jurisprudence through decisions of the Supreme Court, district courts, Constitutional Tribunal, and the Chamber itself. Candidates must demonstrate comprehensive understanding of European Union law governing public procurement and concessions, including fundamental Treaty principles, key directives (2014/24/EU, 2014/25/EU, 2014/23/EU, 2009/81/WE), remedies directives, the Common Procurement Vocabulary regulation, and the jurisprudence of the Court of Justice of the European Union.

Essential civil law competencies include mastery of the Civil Code, Civil Procedure Code (excluding enforcement and interim measures), Construction Law, and criminal law provisions concerning economic crimes. The examination also requires knowledge of public finance principles, including organizational forms of public finance sector entities, financial management rules, and liability for violations of public finance discipline.

Administrative law components encompass the Code of Administrative Procedure, organizational principles of governmental administration and local self-government, while commercial law knowledge includes the Commercial Companies Code and unfair competition regulations. Specialized areas include public-private partnership law, intellectual property law covering copyright scope and protection, and classified information protection regulations.

3
Related Work

From the perspective of our experiments, the most relevant point of reference comes from prior work on professional bar examinations in the United States and Brazil. These exams are not only high-stakes gateways to legal practice, but also carefully designed assessments of both doctrinal knowledge and applied reasoning, which makes them particularly instructive for evaluating the capabilities of large language models
11
17
6
.

In the U.S., research has centered on the Uniform Bar Examination (UBE), which is divided into three parts: the multiple-choice Multistate Bar Examination (MBE), the essay-based Multistate Essay Examination (MEE), and the Multistate Performance Test (MPT), which simulates real-world legal tasks such as drafting memoranda or client letters. Early studies reported that GPT-4 outperformed the average human examinee on the MBE, suggesting that the model had mastered a wide range of doctrinal material
11
. Performance on the essay and practical sections, however, was more difficult to evaluate, as these tasks required structured argumentation and professional writing. Human examiners had to be brought in to ensure fair and consistent grading. Later re-evaluations cautioned that whether GPT-4 could truly be said to “pass” the bar depended heavily on the scoring rubric and scaling methodology used. When more conservative mappings to the official score distribution were applied, the model’s performance fell closer to the 40–60th percentile of human test-takers, tempering initial claims of equivalence with licensed practitioners
17
.

A comparable experiment was conducted in Brazil with the Exame da Ordem dos Advogados do Brasil (OAB), which similarly unfolds in two stages. The first is an 80-question multiple-choice test covering broad areas of law, while the second requires the drafting of a procedural petition and answers to open-ended problem questions within a chosen legal domain. Here, too, LLMs performed well in the objective phase, surpassing the threshold required to advance
6
. But in the second stage, which demands argumentation, statutory interpretation, and adherence to formal requirements of Brazilian legal practice, the models underperformed significantly. Legal specialists judged their submissions to be incomplete, incoherent, or procedurally deficient, confirming that the gap between factual recall and professional reasoning remains substantial.

These findings from the U.S. and Brazil illustrate a broader pattern: LLMs demonstrate surprising competence on knowledge-based, multiple-choice questions but struggle with open-ended tasks that require structured reasoning, practical judgment, and procedural accuracy. They also underscore the indispensable role of human evaluators, both in grading free-response answers and in calibrating what it means to “pass” under official standards.

A growing body of research has generalized these lessons to other professional and educational benchmarks. Early studies on the UBE showed that GPT-4 could exceed human averages on doctrinal knowledge, while later critiques emphasized the fragility of such claims under different scoring assumptions
11
;
17
. Similar observations were made for the Brazilian OAB exam
6
and for other high-stakes tests such as the LSAT, where models falter on logic-intensive reasoning tasks
16
.

These results align with methodological insights from broader evaluation frameworks. Benchmarks like MMLU
9
, AGIEval
30
, and evaluations of GPT-4 on the USMLE
19
highlight the importance of transparent reporting—covering inference settings, explicit metrics, and percentile mappings—as well as careful calibration of uncertainty. Without such standards, claims that LLMs “pass” exams are easily overstated or irreproducible.

Another recurring lesson is the necessity of human oversight.
Beyond domain-specific exams, automatic LLM-as-judge evaluation has been explored with G-Eval, which uses chain-of-thought and a structured form-filling rubric to rate model outputs; it reports substantially higher correlations with human raters, while also noting biases of LLM judges toward LLM-generated text
14
. In the legal domain, a preregistered empirical study of proprietary RAG-based legal research tools (Lexis+ AI, Westlaw AI-Assisted Research, Ask Practical Law AI) finds hallucination rates of 17–33% and wide variation in responsiveness and accuracy—undercutting “hallucination-free” claims and reinforcing the need for human verification and careful evaluation design
15
.
Tasks involving legal reasoning, statutory interpretation, or judgment writing cannot be reliably graded by LLMs alone. This is particularly evident in the growing literature on “LLM-as-a-Judge” approaches, which explore the use of models as automated evaluators of other models’ outputs. While attractive for their scalability, these methods have been shown to introduce systematic biases, such as favoring verbose answers, over-weighting the order in which options are presented, and producing inconsistent scores depending on prompt framing. Surveys of the field emphasize that these biases cannot be eliminated simply by scaling models, and instead require corrective mechanisms such as expert annotation, majority-vote consensus across multiple models, or hybrid pipelines in which human judgment plays a decisive role
7
. The experience from bar exam evaluations confirms these concerns: where grading depends on argumentative quality and legal reasoning, human examiners remain indispensable.

Closely related is the problem of data contamination: performance may be artificially inflated if exam items have leaked into training corpora. Recent work has documented this issue across multiple benchmarks and calls for systematic validation of experimental results
4
.

Finally, specialized legal benchmarks have been developed to go beyond general-purpose knowledge testing. Datasets such as LegalBench
8
, LawBench
5
, and the Reasoning-Focused Legal Retrieval benchmark
29
offer tasks curated by legal experts that emphasize statutory reasoning and applied legal analysis. In parallel, holistic evaluation frameworks such as HELM
12
advocate multidimensional assessment that accounts not only for accuracy, but also calibration, robustness, fairness, and efficiency. Together, these strands of research suggest that future evaluations of LLMs on bar exams—and legal reasoning more generally—must integrate retrieval, reasoning, calibration, and systematic human-in-the-loop validation to yield a reliable picture of model capabilities.

4
Research Questions

The primary motivating factor of our research was to find out to what extent LLMs can
pass the exam. But such a crude question can be broken down into smaller, more
detailed questions presented below:

1.

Can AI pass the exam qualifying for the member of the Polish National Appeal Chamber?

2.

How good are AI models in answering direct legal questions related to the Polish public procurement?

3.

Which models are the best in assessing formal requirements of the legal procedure and are they good enough to perform such an assessment?

4.

Which models stand out in summarizing the legal research in a form of a judgment and can they be employed as a tool facilitating judgment writing?

5.

Can an LLM-as-a-judge be employed to assess the validity of the exam?

Regarding the first question, that was our primary motivating force. We wanted to know
if it is already feasible for the Polish language and Polish jurisdiction to apply
LLMs currently, like it was done for English (for USA legislation) and Brazilian-Portuguese
(for Brazilian legislation).

The second question relates to the fact that the exam consists of two parts. The first one
is concerned with the knowledge of the law related to the public procurement. It is
a test with just one valid answer out of 3, so a we could test a typical Retrieval Augmented
Generation (RAG) scenario and also closed-book scenario, when the only source of knowledge
is the model itself.

The third question relates to the fact that when the cases is decided in KIO (or any other court), there is a large
number of formal requirements that have to be validated that influence various aspects
of the decision, such as the type of decision or if the factual matters should ever be
evaluated, e.g. if the protest was submitted too late. Since these factors take the form
of binary or fixed-set-outcome decisions, they can be evaluated with well defined, machine
learning measures such as precision and recall. By extracting them from the general
decision-making process, we would be able to see if there are areas where the models
can be safely applied, with limited risk of spectacular failure.

The fourth research question was directly related to the first one, but it concentrates
on the assessment of the ability of the LLMs to write a judgment. This seems to be the
most interesting part of the whole experiment, since if we find that one of the models
excels in this task, we can hope that at least one model will pass the exam and in a
broader context, can be used to facilitate the judgment process for KIO.

The last question is concerned with the major approach to testing LLM generated
content. Currently, methods such as LLM-as-a-judge
7
are
applied in a growing body of research. They are based on the assumption that when
the judge LLM is given the reference answer and the answer generated by judged LLM,
the judge can assess – on par with a human evaluator – the correctness of the
generated answer. This approach stems from the fact that manual evaluation is
expensive, especially when the amount of output is huge. However in the case
of law, we are not aiming at “correct on average”, but “always correct”
paradigm. So we wanted to see how far away the LLM used as a judge is from the
assessment given by human evaluators in an exam designed for people.

5.3
Advanced RAG Pipeline

The foundation of our research system rests upon a comprehensive data acquisition and preprocessing pipeline (
advanced RAG pipeline
) that systematically transforms raw legal texts from multiple authoritative sources into a vectorized, searchable repository. This methodology encompasses both the collection of diverse legal documents and their subsequent processing to ensure consistent representation while preserving semantic richness and legal context essential for effective information retrieval.

5.3.1
Data Collection Strategy

Our system incorporates legal documents from three distinct and complementary sources, each requiring specialized collection methodologies due to varying data formats and access mechanisms. The data acquisition process was designed to ensure comprehensive coverage of Polish public procurement law from multiple authoritative perspectives.

The

These documents represent the practical application of legal principles in real-world scenarios and offer insights into judicial decision-making processes. The PDF files underwent optical character recognition and text extraction processes via Docling

The

The summaries provide structured overviews of significant legal developments and regulatory interpretations in a more condensed format than full judicial decisions.

The

5.3.2
Document Processing Pipeline

Following data collection, all documents underwent a standardized preprocessing pipeline designed to ensure consistency across different source formats and document types. The processing methodology transforms the collected raw documents into a uniform representation suitable for vectorization and search operations.

Text Segmentation and Chunking Strategy

The preprocessing pipeline employs a straightforward token-based segmentation approach designed to balance computational efficiency with embedding model constraints. Raw documents underwent systematic chunking using the SentenceTransformers-based token text splitter, configured with a fixed maximum chunk size of 256 tokens to ensure compatibility with the underlying embedding models’ input limitations. This chunking process utilizes the
sdadas/mmlw-retrieval-roberta-large

3
model’s tokenization scheme to ensure consistency between the preprocessing and vectorization stages, creating uniformly sized text segments that can be efficiently processed by the vector embedding pipeline.

Temporal Information Extraction and Document Identification

Each processed document underwent temporal analysis to extract publication dates using pattern matching techniques specifically designed for Polish date formats. The system employed regular expressions to identify and parse dates in the canonical Polish legal format, converting these temporal markers into standardized epoch timestamps for efficient chronological indexing and retrieval. Document uniqueness was ensured through cryptographic hashing using SHA-256 algorithms applied to the full document text, creating unique identifiers that prevent duplication while enabling efficient existence checking during the upload process.

Quality Control and Filtering Mechanisms

The preprocessing pipeline incorporated quality control measures to ensure that only substantive content enters the knowledge base. Document chunks underwent length validation, with segments containing fewer than 100 characters being excluded from the final collection to eliminate low-information content such as headers, footers, or fragmented text segments. Additionally, the system implemented deduplication mechanisms at both the document and chunk levels, preventing redundant information from artificially inflating search results or degrading retrieval performance.

This comprehensive data collection and preprocessing methodology ensured that our document repository maintains high standards of content quality, temporal accuracy, and semantic richness while providing the vectorized foundation necessary for effective hybrid search operations across diverse legal document types.

5.4
Hybrid Search Architecture and Information Retrieval

The information retrieval component employs a hybrid search architecture that integrates semantic vector-based search with traditional keyword-based text matching. This approach ensures comprehensive coverage of user information needs while maintaining high precision across diverse query types and legal contexts.

5.4.1
Query Processing and Vectorization Strategy

The search process begins with domain-specific query preprocessing designed to optimize semantic understanding and retrieval effectiveness. Incoming queries undergo contextual enhancement through prefix application that signals search intent to the vectorization model. Queries are augmented with the prefix
"[query]: "
to align with the model’s training paradigm and enhance semantic comprehension in legal contexts.

Enhanced queries are transformed into high-dimensional vector representations using the specialized
sdadas/mmlw-retrieval-roberta-large-v2

3
embedding model designed for multilingual legal document retrieval without pooling mechanisms. This model has been optimized for cross-lingual legal text comprehension and provides robust semantic understanding across various linguistic and semantic patterns within legal queries.

5.4.2
Multi-Collection Search Architecture

The search system operates across a multi-collection architecture organizing legal documents by content granularity and document type. The
sentences
collection contains fine-grained segments from judicial decisions enabling precise retrieval of specific legal reasoning. The
summaries
collection provides higher-level analytical content facilitating broader contextual information retrieval. The
PZP
collection offers comprehensive statutory commentary supporting detailed regulatory research.

Each collection maintains parallel storage in Typesense
26
with both full-text indexing for keyword matching and vector representations for semantic similarity search. Corresponding full-document collections preserve complete textual content, enabling comprehensive context retrieval when detailed analysis is required.

5.4.3
Hybrid Search Execution and Ranking

The core search execution employs a hybrid methodology that simultaneously leverages multiple retrieval signals to optimize result relevance. The system performs parallel execution of semantic vector similarity matching and traditional keyword-based text search across relevant document collections.

Semantic search utilizes cosine distance calculations between query vectors and document embeddings to identify conceptually related content that may lack exact terminological overlap. Keyword-based search ensures exact term matching for precise legal terminology and specific regulatory references requiring literal correspondence. Typesense’s integrated hybrid reranking mechanism combines both signals, applying scoring algorithms that weight semantic similarity and textual relevance according to query characteristics.

The search process initially retrieves an expanded result set (five times the requested number) to accommodate subsequent deduplication and ranking optimization. Retrieved results undergo post-processing including intelligent deduplication that consolidates multiple segments from identical source documents by retaining only the segment with lowest vector distance. Final ranking considers vector similarity scores, keyword matching relevance, document metadata, and collection source information.

5.4.4
Mixed Collection Search and Cross-Domain Retrieval

For comprehensive coverage, the system supports mixed collection search enabling simultaneous querying across all document types. Mixed searches execute parallel queries across
sentences
,
summaries
, and
PZP
collections while maintaining collection provenance metadata. Cross-collection deduplication prevents redundant information retrieval while preserving perspective diversity. Final result aggregation combines and ranks results from all collections using unified scoring criteria, ensuring relevant information surfaces regardless of source collection while maintaining balanced representation across different document types.

This hybrid search architecture effectively addresses diverse legal information needs while maintaining high standards of relevance, precision, and coverage across Polish public procurement law.

5.7
RQ4 – Substantive assessment

While the formal assessment was limited to verifying compliance with procedural requirements, the second stage concerned the substantive evaluation of the appeal. The central question here was whether the individual allegations advanced by the appellant were justified in view of the factual record and the provisions of the Public Procurement Law. This stage therefore reflects the actual adjudicative task of the Chamber and is the most demanding component of the exam.

5.7.1
Preliminary check

As in real proceedings, the first step was to determine whether the contracting authority had already accepted all allegations. If so, the appeal was considered moot and the procedure was discontinued without entering into the merits. This reflects Article 522(1) Public Procurement Law, which requires the Chamber to discontinue proceedings when the contracting authority grants the relief sought by the appellant. In our implementation, such cases were explicitly flagged as “discontinued” to ensure that no substantive reasoning was generated where it was unnecessary.

5.7.2
Extraction of allegations

If at least one allegation remained in dispute, the process followed to identify the set of claims raised in the appeal. Each allegation was extracted together with its current status (current, withdrawn, or accepted by the contracting authority), the provisions of law invoked in support, and the factual circumstances on which it was based. Allegations withdrawn by the appellant were retained in the record but marked as “inactive” so that the subsequent justification could note their existence while omitting further analysis. In contrast, allegations accepted by the contracting authority were marked as “satisfied”, which in practice required no adjudication but remained part of the structured dataset for completeness.

5.7.3
Characterisation of allegations

For each allegation, a structured record was then constructed, including:

•

thesis
– a short formulation of the alleged violation (e.g. “failure to reject a non-compliant bid” or “improper calculation of deadlines”);

•

research question
– a more generalised query phrased in a way that could be used to search jurisprudence, formulated to capture the underlying legal problem beyond the specific facts;

•

provisions of law
explicitly cited by the appellant (articles, sections, and points of the PPL or related acts);

•

facts
on which the allegation relied, typically concrete procedural events such as the publication of a notice, the submission of a document, or the amount of an entry fee;

•

an indicator whether the allegation was
decidable
given the available information. In cases where crucial factual details were missing or contradictory, the allegation was marked as undecidable.

5.7.4
Substantive evaluation

For allegations classified as decidable, we generated a draft substantive outcome. Three basic categories were distinguished:
upheld
,
dismissed
, and
partially upheld
. Each draft decision was accompanied by a concise justification. The justification had to link the identified facts with the relevant legal norms, demonstrate why the conditions of the legal provision were or were not met, and articulate the reasoning in a manner consistent with the style of authentic KIO judgments.

The justification typically included: (i) identification of the precise legal basis, (ii) demonstration of how the factual findings satisfied (or failed to satisfy) the statutory prerequisites, (iii) discussion of the appellant’s and contracting authority’s positions, and (iv) a reasoned conclusion. Where appropriate, references to established case law of the Chamber or higher courts were added. This step was particularly important, since one of the key weaknesses identified in current LLM outputs was a tendency to cite irrelevant or even non-existent provisions. By forcing the justification to operate only on explicitly extracted provisions and facts, the risk of hallucinated reasoning was reduced.

5.7.5
Treatment of undecidable allegations

Allegations that could not be decided due to insufficient factual information were explicitly marked as
undecidable
. This category was introduced to avoid conflating two distinct scenarios: (1) allegations that were legally unfounded, and (2) allegations that could not be assessed because the case description lacked necessary detail. In practice, this distinction is critical, as the former leads to dismissal of the claim, while the latter would normally result in a request for supplementation of the appeal or an evidentiary ruling. By introducing the undecidable label, we aimed to capture this nuance in the automated system.

5.7.6
Integration with the written judgment

Finally, the structured merit assessments at the level of individual allegations served as the foundation for generating the draft written judgment. By aggregating the outcomes across all allegations, the system produced a coherent decision that mirrored the structure of actual KIO rulings, with a dispositive part (operative ruling) and a justification section. In this way, the merit assessment bridged the gap between granular allegation-level reasoning and the overall adjudicatory output.

5.8
RQ5 – LLM-based Assessment

To substantiate the central thesis of this paper, we developed a small-scale framework designed to verify written test answers generated by a Large Language Model. The primary objective of this framework was to leverage a separate, high-capability LLM (GPT-4o) to function as an impartial examiner, tasked with scoring the generated texts against a predefined set of standards. The goal of this experiment was to assess whether an LLM is capable of adjudicating these papers with the same level of scrutiny as a human legal expert.

5.8.1
Establishment of Evaluation Criteria

The foundation of our assessment protocol was a rigorously defined set of evaluation criteria. To ensure the objectivity and relevance of the evaluation, these criteria were not arbitrarily created but were derived directly from official documentation provided by the examination committee. This initial step involved a careful analysis of the source documents to extract each distinct evaluation metric and its corresponding maximum point allocation. The resulting schema served as the ground truth for the LLM examiner, instructing it on precisely what to measure and how to weight each aspect of the generated output. The final instruction for evaluation was broken down into 8 criteria:

•

Judgment Construction (max. 20 points)
– The way model structures essential elements of the document, such as the sentence and justification;

•

Legal Provisions (max. 30 points)
– Whether the model uses existing, necessary and applicable laws in their reasoning;

•

Factual Analysis (max. 30 points)
– Model’s ability to connect facts, forming a logic chain that leads to the final conclusion;

•

Formulation Style (Sentence) (max. 10 points)
– Whether the sentence written by the model is complete, enforceable and justified;

•

Legal Language (max. 5 points)
– Richness of formal, legal language;

•

Vocabulary (max. 2 points)
– Measures the level of vocabulary used throughout the document;

•

Clarity and Conciseness (max. 2 points)
– Whether the document has an underlying flow of logic that explain it’s contents clearly;

•

Formulation Style (General) (max. 1 points)
– Whether the style used across the document was consistent and suitable for the document’s type;

5.8.2
Input Data Normalization

Prior to evaluation, the raw outputs from the primary model required systematic preparation. The corpus of official model answers was transformed into a standardized, machine-readable format to ensure consistent processing by the evaluation workflow. For this purpose, we selected Markdown as the normalization format due to its simplicity and robust support across API-driven services. Each answer was converted into a self-contained Markdown document, preserving all necessary formatting and content while stripping extraneous metadata. This normalization step was critical for eliminating parsing errors and ensuring that the LLM examiner received clean, uniformly structured data for every evaluation instance.

5.8.3
Implementation of the Automation Workflow

The core of the evaluation framework was an automated workflow constructed using the
n8n
platform. This workflow was engineered to manage the entire process of submitting an answer for review and retrieving the corresponding assessment. The flow was instantiated with three primary components: firstly, a carefully engineered system prompt that provided the GPT-4o model with its persona as an examiner, the full list of evaluation criteria, and precise instructions for scoring and providing justifications. Secondly, the workflow was configured to ingest the list of Markdown-formatted answers as its primary input data. Finally, it established a direct connection to the GPT-4o API endpoint, which would execute the evaluation task upon receiving prompt presented in Listing
Appendix
and the input text.

5.8.4
Execution and Structured Data Capture

With the workflow in place, the final step was its execution. The
n8n
flow iterated through each normalized answer, dispatching an API request to GPT-4o containing the system prompt and the specific answer text. Upon receiving the model’s response, which contained the points awarded for each criterion along with qualitative feedback, the workflow extracted this information. The structured output data was then automatically appended as a new row in a designated Google Sheet. This method of structured data capture ensured that the results were immediately aggregated in a format suitable for subsequent quantitative analysis, statistical review, and visualization without the need for manual data entry or post-processing.

6
Experiments

6.1
RQ1 – The Exam

The experiment replicated the first two components of the official qualification procedure for membership in the National Appeal Chamber: the single-choice knowledge test and the written case. According to the official qualification guidelines, the knowledge test probes the theoretical command of Poland’s Public Procurement Law and the core civil-law doctrines, asking candidates to select the correct single answer from from 3 or sometimes 2 options. The written case then examines practical competence: starting from a factual scenario and documents, the task is to identify the relevant provisions, qualify the issues under procurement and civil law, weigh competing interpretations, and draft a reasoned, formally correct decision. In other words, where the test checks “what you know”, the case checks “how you think and write as a quasi-adjudicator”. The interview stage that forms part of the official NIC process was not included in this experiment.

The research group participated in the exam after the official procedure for the real candidates was finished. Yet in order to simulate the real exam, the
research team did not know the questions and the case to solve in advance and operated under the same time constraints as the human participants.
The answers and the case submitted to the jury were an outcome of a single pass of the appropriate algorithms, without any modifications or
corrections from the research team. By imposing these constraints, we wanted to follow the original examination procedure as close as it was possible.
That allowed us to examine the first research question (“Can AI pass the NIC exam?”) in a more direct and more competitive way, since
the usual way the models are tested against benchmarks involves unlimited tries, without any time constraints.

Operationally, we conducted the exercise in a hybrid format, with part of the team working on-site and others participating online, and
we livestreamed the process to staff across both institutions for transparency and learning. After completing the 50-question test
and the two-pass treatment of the case (formal check followed by substantive analysis), the full submission was reviewed by a group of
experts in public procurement law (the jury of the official exam), who assessed the outputs against the same standards used in the
official qualification procedure.
The answer for the first research question we postpone to the section with the final conclusions (cf. Section
7
).

6.3.1
Validation set

Since the data from the previous years exam’s is very limited (5 cases in total), we have extended the set of data to include
information from numerous cases decided by KIO, by applying an automated issue extraction pipeline.
To ensure the quality and accuracy of such a dataset (
validation dataset for formal assessment
),
we implemented a multi-step verification procedure. First, a Python script extracted and categorized
data into a structured form. Subsequently, one researcher manually verified each entry against the
source documents for its completeness and validity.

This verification process consisted of:

1.

Comparing the notice and appeal publication dates listed in the dataset against the official case abstract.

2.

Verifying that the date of the action leading to the appeal matched the information in the abstract.

3.

Calculating the statutory deadline for the appeal by identifying its legal basis in the abstract and comparing it to the recorded date.

4.

Validating the dataset’s entry for whether the appeal was filed in time. In cases of discrepancy,
the official case protocol was retrieved using the KIO Search
tool
7
7

7

https://www.gov.pl/web/uzp/orzecznictwo-NAC2
for a final determination.

To obtain formal assessment results for a single case, we run the entire process and extracted the the final outcome (either the appeal was valid or not
vis-a-vis the formal requirements).
This means that some responses are conditioned on values obtained from the model within the same run
(e.g., to extract variables related to the deadline, we use the previous response as to whether the order was published in the BZP or in the OJEU).

6.3.2
Results

To evaluate the performance of various models, we evaluated their results against the manually verified dataset. We calculated precision, recall, and the F1-score to assess performance. Precision measured the fraction of items flagged as positive by the script that were genuinely positive, while recall measured the fraction of all actual positive items that were correctly identified. The F1-score, as the harmonic mean of precision and recall, was then used to provide a single, robust measure of the extraction script’s overall effectiveness. The data collection process resulted in a significant class imbalance, for example appeals not raised within the deadline constituted only 4% of the dataset.

RQ4 – Substantive Assessment by the Jury

Despite targeted prompt-engineering for legal applications and substantial efforts by
the research team to make the models follow legal reasoning, none of the
evaluated systems was able to produce a judgment that was consistent,
logically sound, and grounded in the appropriate legal provisions.
Even though these judgments were generated using the full legal
research pipeline (cf.
5.3
), giving the models access to

of the Written Test of the Exam, underscoring that the intricate and codified nature of law continues to be a major obstacle for current language models.

Although pervasive factual fabrication was not observed, the models frequently committed critical citation errors, such as generating non-existent legal statutes or misapplying valid provisions to irrelevant points within their arguments. Their reasoning was often disjointed, compromising the logical coherence of the documents and resulting in flawed, unpersuasive argumentative structures.

Conversely, the examining committee noted that the models performed exceptionally well in

This creates a significant risk: LLM-generated texts may convey a

7
Conclusions

Our study set out to test whether contemporary large language models (LLMs) can meet the demanding requirements of the Polish National Appeal Chamber (KIO) qualification exam and whether the emerging
LLM-as-a-judge
paradigm can be trusted to evaluate such performance. The results point to a clear and consistent picture, reinforced by the expert assessments of the examining committee composed of experienced KIO members and judges.

All three tested models – GPT-4.1, Claude 4 Sonnet, and Bielik-11B-v2.6 – achieved moderate results in the multiple-choice knowledge test, scoring between 70 and 74 points, far below the committee’s expectations of more than 90%. In controlled RAG-enhanced settings, GPT-4.1 reached up to 88.4% accuracy, yet this capability did not translate into practical legal writing. None of the systems managed to pass the written judgment task: GPT-4.1 scored 37 points, Claude 30, and Bielik only 8 out of 100. Judges expressed disappointment that even in formulaic statutory interpretation tasks, the models failed to achieve the precision expected from a competent human candidate. This discrepancy confirms the fundamental gap between factual retrieval and structured legal reasoning: while LLMs can recall and quote norms, they cannot yet apply them coherently to context-specific disputes.

The core of the failure emerged in the written judgment task. Although GPT-4.1 achieved above 97% accuracy in formal information extraction (e.g., identifying dates, parties, or procedural steps), all models demonstrated severe deficiencies in substantive analysis. As the committee observed, their written decisions contained structural and interpretive flaws: incorrect judicial panels, missing signatures and locations, incomplete or misplaced reasoning, and citations of non-existent or irrelevant provisions (e.g., a hallucinatory Article 514(4) PZP). These were not minor lapses but, as judges described, “cardinal errors” exposing a lack of legal logic and procedural understanding. Bielik’s submission was deemed “a schematic draft fit for disqualification”, Claude’s was “a patchwork of mismatched fragments”, and GPT-4.1, despite stylistic polish, was found to have “invented a problem not present in the case,” filling its reasoning with unrelated jurisprudence. The average substantive accuracy across models remained below 40%, revealing that current LLMs cannot replicate the interpretive depth and argumentative precision expected in adjudication.

A critical observation from the committee was that the most advanced models (notably GPT-4.1) produced outputs that, at first glance, resembled authentic judicial documents in format and tone – sometimes to the point of seeming professionally drafted to non-experts. However, the committee expressed concern that this “deceptive fluency” could fool inexperienced legal practitioners into accepting flawed recommendations or arguments, thereby amplifying errors or misapplications of law. As one commissioner noted, the risk that “the AI leads a lawyer astray” is especially high if such systems are used in high-stakes legal drafting or adjudication without robust oversight.

The committee’s comparative evaluation provided granular insight into the models’ relative strengths and weaknesses:

•

Bielik-11B-v2.6:
Lowest overall performance (8/100). Showed partial recognition of statutory language but lacked coherence, logical structure, and even basic judgment formalities. Judges concluded it would be “disqualified outright” in real conditions.

•

Claude 4 Sonnet:
The most verbose output (over 3,500 words) but plagued by contradictions, hallucinated provisions, and procedural confusion. Despite attempts at reasoning, the work was described as “a collection of mismatched fragments.”

•

GPT-4.1:
Highest overall score (37/100) and closest in appearance to a real ruling, yet with deeply flawed legal analysis. Judges noted that “it looks legitimate – until you read it carefully,” warning of its potential to mislead.

These evaluations corroborate our quantitative results: fluency and apparent professionalism can mask serious logical errors. Across all models, factual correctness averaged 72%, while legal reasoning accuracy fell below 40%.

Automatic grading by another LLM produced results substantially diverging from human assessments. The automated examiner consistently overvalued stylistic conformity and verbosity, occasionally ranking fatally flawed responses as “near-passing”. The judges unanimously warned that such automation could reinforce bias and overconfidence, creating a false sense of adequacy in contexts where correctness must be absolute.

Despite these shortcomings, both the committee and experimental data highlight realistic domains of safe application. LLMs demonstrated high reliability in document structuring, summarizing factual backgrounds, verifying procedural elements, and identifying missing formal requirements – tasks where objectivity and verifiability limit the risk of harm. When supervised by legal professionals, such systems can improve efficiency by 30–40% in preliminary analysis and drafting stages. As one judge aptly stated:
“AI may help us with the mechanical work – what it must never do is decide for us”
. This principle defines the boundary between augmentation and adjudication.

The collective evaluation underscored that genuine legal reasoning cannot be reduced to linguistic pattern-matching. Even the most fluent models fail to grasp the purposive and contextual logic that underlies judicial reasoning. Committee members repeatedly stressed that LLMs lack the interpretive awareness, empathy, and ethical responsibility necessary for legal decision-making. The results reaffirm that adjudication is an act of interpretation, not mere text generation, and that accountability remains inherently human.

In summary, our findings – empirically grounded and aligned with the examining committee’s insights – demonstrate that despite their linguistic mastery, large language models remain fundamentally incapable of replacing a human judge. Their apparent expertise conceals fragility, and their fluency can mislead. The
LLM-as-a-judge
paradigm, though conceptually intriguing, must not be mistaken for competence. The safe path forward lies in supervised augmentation – LLMs assisting in mechanical or preliminary stages of legal work – never in autonomous adjudication. Real progress in legal AI will depend on interdisciplinary cooperation between technologists and jurists, ensuring that future systems embody not only fluency and recall, but genuine interpretive competence and accountability. As the committee concluded, current AI systems may be promising tools, but
law still requires judgment, and judgment remains human.

8
Limitations

The study is subject to several limitations that need to be considered when interpreting the results and designing future research.
Firstly, none of the evaluated LLMs had been fine-tuned to meet the specific requirements of Polish legal reasoning, particularly in the domain of public procurement adjudication. The decision not to fine-tune the models for legal analysis was, however, deliberate, reflecting both the substantial temporal and financial costs associated with such domain-specific training.
Instead, the development effort focused on identifying rulings that addressed the same or closely related substantive issues, following a text-based (linguistic) approach to data extraction rather than a logic-based (symbolic) approach. While this approach facilitated retrieval, it also introduced limitations: semantically similar but contextually unrelated provisions, sometimes drawn from entirely different statutes, could be retrieved if they shared a similar vocabulary. This resulted in hallucinations and improper citations of legal provisions by the models, identified by the committee’s detailed substantive assessment in both parts of the exam. Proper differentiation would require an analysis of the provision’s grouping, the legislative chapter in which it appears, and its intended purpose, none of which was undertaken in this trial. The adopted approach, however, further reflected the differences between the models, resulting from their original characteristics and processing mechanisms, as all of them received the same input while extracting elements from the material in different ways.

Secondly, although the team worked closely with one legal practitioner, a more intensive and structured collaboration with multiple lawyers specialising in the relevant legal field could have improved the outcome of the study. Lack of such collaboration was specifically visible in two areas: gaps in domain-specific procedural legal knowledge of the technical team and suboptimal selection of data used to inform the models. The lack of knowledge was visible in the recurring error across all submissions concerning the composition of the adjudicating panel: although the case scenario provided for a single-member panel, all model-generated judgments indicated a three-member panel, suggesting a misapplication of procedural rules. Regarding the composition of the training data, in addition to statutory provisions, the models were also provided with examples of jurisprudence. This decision was based on preliminary trials indicating that the models performed better, according to the technical team, when given access to rulings elaborating on the application of the law, rather than relying solely on statutory text. However, the evaluation committee observed that the knowledge test questions were based primarily on statutory provisions rather than case law. The inclusion of case law may have introduced unnecessary complexity, as synthesizing multiple judicial decisions could have increased the likelihood of errors. This effect was also evident in the practical test, where the models often retrieved and relied on examples of case law that did not necessarily correspond to the specific factual or legal context of the case at hand. Closer interdisciplinary collaboration could improve data selection for model training and reduce knowledge-related errors.

A further limitation concerns evaluator blinding. The team initially planned a fully blinded, “Turing-style” assessment, but legal constraints, specifically, that official exam questions could not be disclosed prior to publication of recruitment results, made blinding infeasible. As a result, the assessment committee knew in advance that the analysed outputs were generated by AI. This awareness may have introduced expectancy and confirmation biases
13
, potentially affecting scoring in both the knowledge and practical components. At the same time, the KIO judges maintained that the written works deviated so markedly (and negatively) from their human counterparts that knowing who – or rather, what – authored them did not bias their evaluation.

8.1
Future research

Building on the limitations above, we see several ways to strengthen validity, robustness, and practical relevance in subsequent studies.

One of the most important areas of research is fine-tuning LLMs to write rulings or judgments (supervised fine-tuning). The training dataset for each training sample should contain the content of the case, fragments of regulations, rulings, and judgments on the basis of which the answer (input) and the content of the judgment (output) should be provided. During inference, fragments of regulations, rulings, and judgments would be searched for automatically. In this context, both the above-mentioned and new, more advanced information-retrieval pipelines would be worth considering. Future work should move beyond purely linguistic similarity toward a structure- and logic-aware stack. Concretely, you could use boundary-aligned, overlapping chunking at legal units. Also, hybrid retrieval methods (sparse + dense) with a law-specific cross-encoder re-ranker are worth exploring. Similarly, the quality of results could be improved by using reinforcement learning, including RLHF. Rewards should be given for correct decisions, correct construction of answers, logical references to regulations and historical judgments, and alignment with the subjective assessment of the expert. It is impossible to construct the training set using only exam cases due to their small number, so it is necessary to consider more advanced methods than the reverse engineering methods presented.

Another interesting direction is to conduct the described experiment (exam) using multi-task agent systems, such as GPT-5 pro, or at least implement them at a specific stage of the process. The design of dedicated, more complex multi-agent systems could also significantly improve results.

Cooperation with legal experts should be deepened. Sustained collaboration between legal experts and technical teams should span task decomposition, dataset curation (including hard negatives), rubric design, and error taxonomy. Embedding domain review into retrieval/ranking development and agent policies can reduce knowledge-related errors (e.g., panel composition) and improve training/evaluation data quality.

Where legally permissible, double-blind setups should be adopted, the presentation and labeling of AI versus human texts should be randomized. These steps will help isolate model performance from expectancy and confirmation biases and improve external validity.

Ethical Considerations

All materials used in this study were drawn exclusively from publicly available sources and contained no personally identifiable or otherwise sensitive information. Collaboration with the human legal expert (judge) was conducted in accordance with ethical research practice: participation was voluntary, the nature and purpose of the study were explained in advance, and written informed consent was obtained before any involvement.

The preparation of the document was supported by LLMs, but the design and conduction of the experiments and all findings are genuine.

6. **Vocabulary:** Measures the fundamental correctness of general and legal vocabulary, including grammar and spelling. (0-2 points)

7. **Clarity and Conciseness:** Evaluates the ability to express complex reasoning in a clear, concise, and logical manner that is understandable to all parties. (0-2 points)

8. **Formulation Style (General):** Provides a holistic assessment of the document’s overall professionalism, consistency in tone, and stylistic polish. (0-1 point)
