Stimulating flow in MOBA games: a moderated serial mediation model of gamification mechanics, perceived competence, and player retention
Authors: Huicheng Han, Changwook Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultiplayer Online Battle Arena (MOBA) games rely on sustained player participation to maintain competitive matchmaking and community vitality, yet player churn remains a persistent challenge. Although gamification mechanics are widely embedded in MOBA design, how specific mechanics translate into long-term retention through internal psychological processes is not fully understood.MethodsDrawing on the Stimulus–Organism–Response (S–O–R) paradigm and integrating Self-Determination Theory and Flow Theory, we proposed a serial mediation model in which goal clarity, immediate feedback, and reward systems enhance continuance intention via perceived competence and flow experience. We collected cross-sectional survey data from 584 active MOBA players and tested the measurement and structural models using confirmatory factor analysis and structural equation modeling. Indirect effects were examined using bias-corrected bootstrapping, and hierarchical regression assessed whether task complexity moderates the perceived competence–flow relationship.ResultsThe measurement model demonstrated strong reliability and validity. All three gamification mechanics positively predicted perceived competence, with immediate feedback showing the strongest effect. Perceived competence strongly predicted flow, which in turn strongly predicted continuance intention. Bootstrapped mediation tests supported significant serial indirect effects for all three stimuli, indicating partial mediation. Task complexity positively moderated the competence–flow link, such that competence translated into substantially stronger flow under high-complexity matches.ConclusionMOBA retention is driven not only by gamification mechanics themselves but by their capacity to cultivate competence and facilitate flow, particularly in strategically demanding contexts. Optimizing immediate feedback and maintaining an adaptive challenge–skill balance appear to be key levers for sustaining long-term player engagement.Keywords: Multiplayer online battle arena, Gamification mechanics, Perceived competence, Flow experience, Continuance intention, Task complexity

Introduction

Research background

Digital gaming has evolved into one of the world’s largest entertainment industries. Recent market reports estimate that the global games market generates on the order of ∼$10 billion in annual revenue and reaches billions of players worldwide [1, 2]. In parallel, esports has matured into a mainstream digital spectacle, supported by professional leagues, streaming platforms, and large-scale live events [3, 4]. Within this ecosystem, Multiplayer Online Battle Arena (MOBA) titles such as League of Legends, Dota 2, and mobile MOBA variants represent a core competitive genre characterized by intense teamwork, rapid feedback loops, and highly skill-dependent outcomes.

For MOBA developers and publishers, sustaining an active player base is strategically crucial. MOBA games are typically operated as live-service platforms in which long-term engagement supports balanced matchmaking, stable communities, and ongoing monetization. Consequently, understanding the psychological drivers of player retention, commonly operationalized as continuance intention, is both theoretically and practically important. Continuance intention has long been treated as a key post-adoption outcome in information systems research and is widely used to explain repeated usage in digital services [5].

Gamification as a design lever: defining key constructs

To foster engagement and long-term participation, contemporary games increasingly rely on gameful design features that structure goals, deliver performance information, and make progress salient. In the broader literature, gamification is commonly defined as the use of game design elements in non-game contexts [6, 7] and, more generally, as a service-oriented design approach that creates gameful experiences by supporting user value creation [8–10]. Empirical syntheses further suggest that gamification can positively affect motivation and engagement, although its effects depend on which design elements are implemented and for whom [11].

In competitive online games, especially MOBA titles, three mechanics are particularly central to the moment-to-moment player experience. Goal clarity refers to the transparency of objectives, winning conditions, and progression pathways that reduce cognitive ambiguity and help players focus on execution rather than rule inference [12]. Immediate feedback refers to the timeliness and clarity of performance-related cues that allow players to connect actions to outcomes and calibrate their performance in real time [13]. Reward systems refer to performance-contingent signals such as rank progression, badges, and achievement markers that may be experienced as informational indicators of progress and competence [14].

These mechanics are not merely cosmetic additions to the play environment. In the present study, they are conceptualized as design-based informational stimuli rather than internal psychological states. Their theoretical relevance lies in the competence-related cues they provide during play and in the way these cues shape players’ appraisals of efficacy and mastery. This positioning is consistent with motivational accounts suggesting that sustained engagement is supported when individuals experience effectiveness and competence [15, 16]. It is also consistent with empirical work showing that specific gameful design features can enhance motivation in part by supporting competence satisfaction [17, 18].

Research gap and study motivation

Two theoretical perspectives are especially influential for explaining persistence in interactive digital environments, namely Self-Determination Theory and Flow Theory. Self-Determination Theory argues that high-quality motivation is supported when basic psychological needs, especially competence, are satisfied [15]. Flow Theory, in turn, conceptualizes an optimal experience of deep absorption that is intrinsically rewarding and often emerges when perceived skills are well matched to perceived challenges [19, 20]. In online contexts, flow has repeatedly been linked to positive affective responses and continued use intentions [21–23].

Despite the relevance of these perspectives for digital gaming, prior work has often taken one of two paths. Some studies have examined gamification primarily in terms of its relatively direct association with engagement-related outcomes. Others have treated need satisfaction and flow as separate explanatory routes. As a result, less is known about how concrete gamification mechanics may translate into player retention through a sequential psychological process. This omission is especially important in MOBA settings, where performance-relevant cues are continuous, socially salient, and closely tied to players’ sense of mastery. Moreover, the classic challenge-skill proposition of flow suggests that the effect of perceived competence on flow may depend on how strategically and cognitively demanding the match environment is. Yet task demands are not always incorporated as boundary conditions in competence-to-flow models, even though task complexity is a well-established construct in organizational and behavioral research [24–26].

Accordingly, the present study asks how and under what conditions gamification mechanics influence player retention through the sequential psychological processes of perceived competence and flow experience. Within this framework, perceived competence refers to players’ sense of efficacy and mastery during play, flow experience refers to a deeply absorbed and intrinsically rewarding state, continuance intention refers to the intention to continue playing over time, and task complexity refers to the perceived strategic and cognitive demands of the match context. Drawing on the Stimulus–Organism–Response framework [27, 28], we propose a serial mechanism in which gamification mechanics as external stimuli enhance perceived competence as an internal evaluative state, which then facilitates flow experience and, ultimately, strengthens continuance intention. We further examine task complexity as a moderator of the competence-to-flow pathway. Using survey data from 584 active MOBA players, the study aims to disentangle the differential effects of goal clarity, immediate feedback, and reward systems on perceived competence, to validate a competence-to-flow-to-retention mechanism, and to clarify when this pathway becomes stronger under conditions of higher task complexity.

Literature review and hypotheses development

Theoretical foundation

This study is grounded in the Stimulus–Organism–Response (S–O–R) paradigm, which explains how environmental cues (stimuli) shape individuals’ internal states (organism) and, in turn, their behavioral responses [27]. In interactive digital settings, S–O–R has been widely applied to model how designed environments (e.g., atmospherics and service cues) influence users’ affective/cognitive reactions and downstream behavioral intentions [28, 29].

In competitive online games, we conceptualize gamification mechanics as salient design stimuli that continuously provide informational structure (e.g., goals, feedback, rewards). These stimuli shape players’ internal psychological states that are most proximal to sustained engagement. To theorize this “Organism” component more precisely, we integrate Self-Determination Theory (SDT) and Flow Theory.

SDT proposes that high-quality, self-sustained motivation is supported when basic psychological needs are satisfied, especially the need for competence (feeling effective and capable in one’s actions) [15, 30]. In achievement-oriented competitive games, competence is particularly central because progression, ranking, and performance comparisons make mastery highly salient [16, 31, 32]. Complementing SDT, Flow Theory describes an optimal experience characterized by deep absorption, intrinsic enjoyment, and distorted time perception [19, 33, 34]. Flow is most likely when perceived skill is well matched to environmental challenge, creating a state of effortless control and total immersion [19, 35].

Synthesizing these perspectives within S–O–R, we argue that gamification mechanics (Stimuli) foster players’ perceived competence (Organism), which facilitates flow experience (Organism), thereby increasing continuance intention (Response). In addition, the strength of the competence-to-flow link should depend on perceived task complexity, reflecting the challenge–skill balance principle in flow.

Gamification stimuli and perceived competence

Gamification refers to the use of game design elements in non-game or service contexts to shape user experience and behavior [6, 8]. In digital products (including online games), gamification mechanics often function as informational structures that clarify what to do, how well one is doing, and what achievements are recognized. Within the present framework, these mechanics are not treated as psychological states in themselves. Rather, they are viewed as competence-relevant informational events that shape players’ judgments of effectiveness and mastery. Such informational cues are theoretically important for competence because competence involves perceived effectiveness, and perceptions of effectiveness are strongly shaped by goal structure, performance feedback, and competence-signaling outcomes [12, 13, 15]. This interpretation is also consistent with SDT, which emphasizes that informational cues can support competence when they help individuals interpret their actions as effective and self-endorsed [15, 30].

Goal Clarity (GC) → Perceived Competence. Goal clarity reflects the transparency of objectives, winning conditions, and progression pathways. From the perspective of goal-setting theory, clear goals reduce ambiguity and provide an interpretable standard against which performance can be evaluated [12]. In complex real-time competitive environments, clearly structured goals reduce ambiguity and cognitive friction, allowing players to allocate cognitive resources to execution and learning rather than rule inference [12]. When players can reliably interpret what constitutes effective performance, they are more likely to experience efficacy and mastery, strengthening perceived competence. Goal clarity should therefore strengthen perceived competence. H1a: Goal clarity positively influences perceived competence.

Immediate Feedback (IF) → Perceived Competence. Immediate feedback captures the timeliness and clarity of performance-related cues. Feedback intervention theory and broader feedback research suggest that clear, task-relevant feedback supports performance calibration and perceived efficacy, particularly in fast-paced tasks requiring continuous adjustment [13]. In competitive games, rapid feedback loops enable players to connect actions to outcomes, supporting competence appraisals and reinforcing effective behaviors. As a result, immediate feedback should strengthen competence appraisals by improving perceived effectiveness during play. H1b: Immediate feedback positively influences perceived competence.

Reward Systems (RS) → Perceived Competence. Reward systems include performance-contingent signals such as rank progression, badges, and achievement markers. From the standpoint of cognitive evaluation theory within SDT, rewards can undermine motivation when experienced as controlling, but they may also support competence when they are interpreted as informational signals of progress and successful performance [14, 15]. In competitive contexts, rewards tightly linked to demonstrated skill can be internalized as competence symbols, reinforcing mastery perceptions [16]. Under these conditions, reward systems should contribute positively to perceived competence. H1c: Reward systems positively influence perceived competence.

Perceived competence and flow experience

Flow is a peak experiential state involving deep absorption and intrinsic enjoyment [19, 34]. A core condition for flow is that individuals believe they can meet task demands; when perceived competence is high, attention shifts away from self-monitoring and toward fluent task engagement. This mechanism has been emphasized in online flow measurement and in game-specific flow frameworks [21, 36]. In competitive games, competence satisfaction should therefore function as a gateway that increases the likelihood and intensity of flow. H2: Perceived competence has a positive effect on flow experience.

Flow experience and continuance intention

Continuance intention is a central outcome in IS continuance research and captures users’ intention to persist with a system over time [5]. Flow is inherently rewarding (autotelic), making repeated engagement more likely because users seek to re-experience the positive state [19]. Empirical work on online contexts further indicates that flow relates to sustained usage intentions in interactive systems, including online games [22]. Accordingly, players who more frequently reach flow states should report stronger intentions to continue playing. H3: Flow experience positively influences continuance intention.

The moderating role of task complexity

Flow theory emphasizes challenge-skill balance: high flow is most likely when challenge and skill are jointly high [19, 35]. Task complexity captures the degree of cognitive demand, decision interdependence, and coordination requirements inherent in a task environment [24, 37]. When task complexity is high, competence should translate more strongly into flow because the environment provides sufficient challenge for competence to be fully engaged. Conversely, when complexity is low, even competent players may experience reduced immersion because the task does not demand sustained attention and strategic effort. H4: Task complexity positively moderates the relationship between perceived competence and flow experience, such that the relationship is stronger at higher levels of task complexity.

Materials and methods

Study design, participants, and procedure

This study employed a cross-sectional online survey design. Data were collected between August and October 2025 from MOBA players recruited via major community hubs, including Reddit (e.g., r/leagueoflegends, r/DotA2), Discord servers, and regional gaming forums (e.g., Baidu Tieba). To be eligible, participants were required to have played at least one MOBA title within the past month and to complete all key survey sections.

Several procedural safeguards were implemented to enhance data quality and mitigate response biases. First, participants were informed of the academic purpose of the study and provided digital informed consent before proceeding. Responses were collected anonymously to reduce social desirability concerns. Second, three instructed-response attention checks (e.g., “For this question, please select ‘Strongly Agree’.”) were embedded to identify inattentive responding. Third, a minimum completion time of 300 seconds was enforced based on pilot testing to exclude implausibly fast submissions.

A total of 650 responses were initially recorded. After excluding 66 responses failing attention checks or quality criteria, 584 valid responses remained for analysis (effective recovery rate: 89.8%). Measures

All constructs were measured using multi-item scales adapted from established literature and tailored to the MOBA context. Unless otherwise stated, items were rated on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 7 (Strongly Agree). For each participant, all items were anchored to the MOBA title they played most frequently.

Gamification mechanics (Stimuli)

Consistent with a design-feature perspective on gamification, three gamification mechanics were operationalized as exogenous stimuli: Goal Clarity (GC; 4 items), Immediate Feedback (IF; 4 items), and Reward Systems (RS; 5 items). Item wording was adapted to the MOBA environment (e.g., clarity of win conditions/objectives; real-time performance cues; perceived fairness and meaning of in-game rewards), drawing on prior empirical operationalizations of gamification features in information systems contexts [38]. Higher scores indicate stronger perceived presence/quality of each mechanic.

Perceived competence (Organism)

Perceived Competence (PC) was measured with four items adapted from the competence satisfaction component of the Basic Psychological Need Satisfaction and Frustration Scale (BPNSFS) [39]. Items captured perceived mastery and efficacy during MOBA play (e.g., feeling capable of handling in-game challenges). Higher scores indicate stronger competence satisfaction while playing.

Flow experience (Organism; higher-order construct)

Flow Experience was modeled as a higher-order latent construct reflected by three first-order facets that are commonly emphasized in online flow measurement: Absorption (AB; 4 items), Enjoyment (ENJ; 4 items), and Time Distortion (TD; 3 items). This specification was adopted because flow is commonly conceptualized as a multidimensional experiential state rather than as a single undifferentiated feeling, especially in online and game-based environments. Prior work has shown that absorption, enjoyment, and altered time perception represent distinct but related aspects of the broader flow experience [21, 36]. In line with this view, the present study treated AB, ENJ, and TD as first-order facets that together reflect the broader higher-order Flow construct. Items were adapted to MOBA gameplay based on established flow conceptualizations and measurement work in online environments [21]. Higher scores indicate a stronger tendency to experience immersive, enjoyable, and time-distorted play states.

Continuance intention (Response)

Player retention was operationalized as Continuance Intention (CI), a widely used behavioral intention proxy in IS continuance research [5]. CI was measured with four items capturing the likelihood of continuing to play and maintaining the game as an ongoing activity. Higher scores indicate stronger intentions to continue playing.

Task complexity (Moderator)

Task Complexity (TC) was measured using three items adapted from classic task complexity work [25], tailored to MOBA matches (e.g., perceived strategic depth, mental demand, and coordination complexity). Higher scores indicate that the participant typically experiences MOBA matches as more complex and demanding.

Control variables

To account for potential confounding influences in the moderation analysis, we included common demographic and gaming-experience controls: gender, age, education level, monthly gaming budget, years of MOBA experience, competitive rank, primary MOBA platform/title indicator, and typical session context (solo vs. team-up). These were coded as described in Table 1 and treated as covariates in the regression models.

Data analysis strategy

We followed the two-step analytical procedure recommended for latent-variable modeling [40]. First, we evaluated the measurement model using Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) with Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimation in AMOS 26.0. Reliability and convergent validity were assessed via standardized factor loadings, Cronbach’s α, composite reliability (CR), and average variance extracted (AVE). Discriminant validity was examined using the Fornell–Larcker criterion [41]. To evaluate potential common method bias in self-reported survey data, we additionally inspected full-collinearity variance inflation factors (VIF), following the full-collinearity assessment approach [42].

Second, we tested the structural model using structural equation modeling in AMOS 26.0. Indirect effects were examined with bias-corrected bootstrapping to obtain 95% confidence intervals [40]. To test the moderating role of task complexity in the perceived competence-to-flow pathway, we conducted a hierarchical regression using mean-centered predictors and an interaction term.

In both the CFA and SEM, Flow was specified as a higher-order latent construct reflected by the three first-order facets of Absorption, Enjoyment, and Time Distortion. This specification allowed us to model the shared variance among these facets while preserving their conceptual distinctiveness [21, 36]. More specifically, the full-collinearity assessment evaluates whether each focal construct shows problematic collinearity when treated as an endogenous variable in the model [42]. Under this approach, variance inflation factor values below 3.3 are commonly interpreted as inconsistent with severe common method bias. In the present study, this diagnostic was used as a complementary assessment of common method bias rather than as a definitive test, given the single-source self-report design. In addition, several procedural safeguards were implemented during data collection, including anonymous participation, instructed-response attention checks, and a minimum completion-time criterion, in order to reduce the likelihood of method-related inflation. Results

Sample and data distribution

Participant profile

The final sample consisted of 584 valid responses collected through a rigorous screening process. As shown in Table 1, the sample displayed a broad distribution of socio-demographic characteristics. The gender composition was relatively balanced, with a slight male predominance. Participants were predominantly young adults, and most reported tertiary education. The behavioral profile likewise reflected diverse MOBA experiences across titles, platforms, and levels of competitive involvement. The sample captured active players with substantial experience in the genre, providing an appropriate basis for examining competence, flow, and continuance intention in competitive MOBA settings. The behavioral profile further indicates that the sample captured diverse MOBA play experiences across platforms and titles. Mobile-first titles such as Honor of Kings or MLBB accounted for 41.9% of the sample, while PC-based titles such as League of Legends (36.3%) and Dota 2 (14.4%) were also well represented. The majority of respondents reported substantial genre experience: 69.5% had played MOBA games for more than three years, and 57.9% were ranked at Platinum level or higher. Moreover, 65.1% primarily played in pre-arranged team settings (often termed “duo/trio/5-stack” play; i.e., playing with one, two, or four known teammates rather than solo queue), indicating that the sample reflects typical competitive match contexts in which perceived competence and flow experiences are likely to occur.

Descriptive statistics and normality assessment

Descriptive statistics and distribution diagnostics for the study variables are reported in Table 2. All measures used a 7-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree; 7 = Strongly Agree), where the neutral midpoint is 4.0. Overall, the mean scores were close to the midpoint, ranging from 3.75 (Perceived Competence, PC) to 4.25 (Enjoyment, ENJ). Immediate Feedback (IF; M=4.05, SD=0.65) and Task Complexity (TC; M=4.09, SD=0.71) were slightly above the midpoint, whereas Goal Clarity (GC; M=3.82, SD=0.72), Reward Systems (RS; M=3.91, SD=0.78), and Perceived Competence (PC; M=3.75, SD=0.81) were close to but slightly below the midpoint. In contrast, the flow facets—Absorption (AB; M=4.12, SD=0.68), Enjoyment (ENJ; M=4.25, SD=0.62), and Time Distortion (TD; M=4.18, SD=0.74)—as well as Continuance Intention (CI; M=4.21, SD=0.75) were above the midpoint, indicating relatively stronger immersive experience and persistence intentions among active MOBA players.

Measurement model assessment

Following the two-step approach recommended by [40], a Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was first conducted to evaluate the reliability and validity of the measurement model prior to testing the structural relationships. The CFA model exhibited a satisfactory fit to the empirical data, with all indices surpassing recommended benchmarks: , CFI = 0.962, TLI = 0.958, RMSEA = 0.041, and SRMR = 0.035. The measurement model comprised the three gamification stimuli (GC, IF, RS), perceived competence (PC), continuance intention (CI), task complexity (TC), and three first-order flow facets (AB, ENJ, TD), which were specified to reflect a higher-order Flow construct in the CFA/SEM. This specification follows the view that absorption, enjoyment, and time distortion capture distinct but related aspects of immersive experience in online gameplay [21, 36]. In the CFA, Flow was specified as a higher-order latent construct indicated by these three first-order facets. Convergent validity was assessed using standardized factor loadings, Average Variance Extracted (AVE), and Composite Reliability (CR). As reported in Table 4, standardized factor loadings ranged from 0.712 to 0.915, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.70 [45]. All factor loadings were statistically significant (p<0.001), indicating that the observed indicators adequately represent their intended latent variables.

Structural model and hypothesis testing

Structural path analysis

Following the validation of the measurement model, we tested the structural model to evaluate the hypothesized relationships among gamification mechanics (GC, IF, RS), perceived competence (PC), flow experience (Flow), and continuance intention (CI). In the SEM, Flow was specified as a higher-order latent construct reflected by three first-order facets (AB, ENJ, TD). The structural model demonstrated an excellent fit to the data: , CFI = 0.968, TLI = 0.962, RMSEA = 0.043, and SRMR = 0.038, meeting commonly used cutoff criteria for acceptable-to-good fit [44, 46].

Standardized path coefficients and hypothesis testing results are summarized in Table 5. Consistent with H1a–H1c, all three gamification stimuli significantly predicted perceived competence: Goal Clarity (β = 0.325, p < .001, ), Immediate Feedback (β = 0.382, p < .001, ), and Reward Systems (β = 0.284, p < .001, ). Immediate Feedback exhibited the strongest contribution to perceived competence, highlighting the central role of real-time performance cues in competence formation within competitive MOBA contexts. Supporting H2, perceived competence had a substantial positive effect on Flow (β = 0.642, p < .001, ). In turn, Flow significantly predicted continuance intention (H3: β = 0.584, p < .001, ), indicating that the optimal experience of Flow is a key psychological driver of player persistence. To align with the subsequent mediation decomposition, direct paths from the three stimuli to CI were also estimated. These direct effects remained statistically significant but smaller in magnitude, namely Goal Clarity (β = 0.115, p < .001, ), Immediate Feedback (β = 0.092, p < .01, ), and Reward Systems (β = 0.124, p < .001, ). The model explained 41.2% of the variance in PC (), 52.4% in Flow (), and 48.5% in CI (), indicating substantial explanatory power for the endogenous constructs. Task Complexity (TC) was examined as a moderator in a separate hierarchical regression framework (interaction term PC × TC), reported in the moderation subsection.

To further clarify how gamification mechanics shape players’ continuance intention (CI), we tested the indirect effects using a bias-corrected bootstrapping procedure with 5,000 resamples [47, 48]. For the moderation regression, factor scores for PC and the higher-order Flow construct were extracted from the CFA and used as observed variables; TC was operationalized by its composite score. Specifically, we decomposed the total effects of Goal Clarity (GC), Immediate Feedback (IF), and Reward Systems (RS) on CI into direct effects and indirect effects transmitted through the hypothesized serial pathway (Stimulus → Perceived Competence (PC) → Flow → CI). In the present study, however, VAF is used as a descriptive complement to the bootstrap evidence rather than as a stand-alone test of mediation strength, and small differences among VAF values are not interpreted as indicating substantively different mediation categories.

Discussion

This study investigated how core MOBA gamification mechanics shape players’ continuance intention (CI) through a competence–flow pathway, and when this pathway becomes stronger under varying task complexity. Drawing on the S-O-R logic and motivational accounts of gamification as the use of game design elements in non-game settings [6, 8], we proposed and supported a serial mechanism in which gamification mechanics (stimuli) enhance perceived competence (organism), which facilitates flow experience (organism) and ultimately increases continuance intention (response). In addition, we identified a boundary condition: task complexity strengthens the competence-to-flow link, consistent with the challenge–skill balance premise in flow theory [19].

The differential impact of gamification mechanics on perceived competence

A key finding is that the three mechanics are not equally diagnostic for competence formation in competitive MOBA play. Immediate Feedback (IF) was the strongest predictor of perceived competence, exceeding the effects of goal clarity (GC) and reward systems (RS). This pattern is theoretically meaningful in MOBA environments, where performance information is continuous, time-sensitive, and closely coupled with micro-level execution [50, 51]. Fast, unambiguous feedback loops (e.g., combat outcome cues, performance dashboards, and real-time skill confirmation) provide frequent self-evaluative signals that help players calibrate mastery perceptions and maintain a sense of efficacy during demanding matches. This interpretation aligns with broader gamification evidence showing that design elements that foreground performance information and feedback can reliably contribute to motivational and learning-related outcomes [52, 53].

Goal clarity emerged as the second strongest driver of competence. In high-information, rule-dense competitive genres, clear objectives and transparent win conditions reduce cognitive ambiguity, allowing attention to be allocated to mastery-relevant actions rather than to interpreting the task structure. Reward systems also contributed positively but with the smallest effect among the three mechanics, suggesting that rewards in MOBA contexts may function more as signals of progress and social recognition than as direct sources of competence [54, 55]. These results indicate that competence is primarily built from informational mechanics (feedback, clarity) rather than from symbolic mechanics (rewards), at least in the retention pathway examined here.

Serial mediation: competence as the gateway to flow and continuance intention

Our results provide strong support for the proposed serial mediation. Perceived competence was a substantial predictor of flow, and flow was a strong predictor of CI. Importantly, the indirect effects from the three mechanics to CI through the PC→Flow channel were significant, while direct effects remained significant but smaller, indicating partial (complementary) mediation. Conceptually, this means that gamification mechanics do not merely “retain” players through surface appeal; instead, they meaningfully shape the internal psychological conditions (mastery and immersion) that make continued play self-reinforcing. This mechanism also connects gamification research to IS continuance logic: continuance intention reflects a forward-looking commitment to persist with a system or service [5]. In our context, flow appears to be the experiential state translating competence into this persistence-oriented intention. Put differently, competence enables players to meet match demands; flow makes the experience sufficiently absorbing and rewarding for players to choose to return. This integrated explanation helps clarify why specific mechanics matter for CI, rather than treating gamification as a monolithic feature bundle [56].

The present finding that flow mediates the link between competence and continuance intention is also consistent with emerging evidence from other technology-mediated contexts. Flow experience has been shown to mediate the association between self-determination and engagement in GenAI-assisted informal digital learning of English [57]. Related work has also linked engagement-related states such as self-efficacy to lower anxiety in large language model learning contexts [58]. These findings suggest that flow may represent a broader optimal experience through which internal psychological resources are translated into continued engagement.

Task complexity as a boundary condition: when competence translates into flow

The moderation findings refine the model by demonstrating that the competence-to-flow relationship is contingent on perceived task complexity. When task complexity is high, the slope of PC→Flow is steeper: competence becomes more “usable” for producing immersion because the environment supplies sufficient strategic demand to engage skills fully. When task complexity is low, competence has a weaker effect on flow, plausibly because the environment does not require sustained cognitive and tactical investment, making deep absorption harder to maintain. This pattern is consistent with the foundational flow proposition that optimal experience is most likely when perceived skills and perceived challenges are simultaneously high [19, 59, 60].

At the same time, the size of the moderation effect should be interpreted with appropriate caution. The interaction term explained an additional 2.5% of the variance in Flow, which indicates a modest effect. However, such effects are not uncommon in moderation research, where interaction terms typically function to identify boundary conditions rather than to account for large amounts of variance on their own. In the present study, the theoretical value of the interaction lies in showing that the impact of competence on flow depends in part on how demanding the match environment is, rather than operating identically across all play contexts.

For MOBA design, this effect is practically meaningful even if it is not large in statistical magnitude. A 2.5% increment in explained variance does not imply that task complexity is the primary determinant of flow, but it does indicate that challenge calibration can alter the extent to which existing competence becomes immersive. In practical terms, this suggests that matchmaking systems, ranked structures, and mode design may benefit from preserving forms of challenge that are demanding yet interpretable, because even a modest improvement in the competence-to-flow conversion process may matter at scale in live-service environments where small shifts in engagement can accumulate over large player populations. Our results therefore imply that retention-oriented design should not only increase competence signals (feedback and clarity) but should also preserve or scaffold meaningful complexity so that competence can be converted into immersion rather than boredom.

Theoretical implications

This study contributes to gamification and game experience research in three ways. First, it specifies a psychologically grounded process model in which design features are linked to behavioral persistence through internal evaluative and experiential states. This helps clarify how gamification mechanics can be understood as competence-relevant design cues rather than as psychological states per se. Second, it shows that mechanics differ in their motivational informativeness for competence formation, highlighting immediate feedback as a particularly central mechanism in high-tempo competitive play. Third, by demonstrating moderation, it offers a contingency-based account: the same level of competence does not always yield the same degree of flow, because the task environment conditions how effectively competence can be translated into immersive experience.

Practical implications for MOBA design and live operations

The present findings yield several practical implications for the design and operation of competitive MOBA games. Most importantly, the results suggest that retention is supported not simply by adding more game features, but by shaping design elements in ways that strengthen players’ competence appraisals and make flow more likely. This broader design logic is consistent with recent work showing that game design features can influence satisfaction, continuance, and actual usage through psychologically meaningful design qualities rather than through superficial stimulation alone [61, 62].

First, feedback systems should be treated as a priority design lever. Because immediate feedback emerged as the strongest predictor of perceived competence in our model, developers should focus on improving the clarity, timing, and interpretability of performance-related cues. In practice, this may include clearer combat readability, faster skill-hit confirmation, more informative assist and contribution indicators, and post-match analytics that help players understand why a play sequence succeeded or failed. Recent work on incentive-system design likewise suggests that feedback elements can serve as especially salient moments for communicating performance information and sustaining engagement [63, 64]. In the MOBA context, the key implication is that feedback should not merely report outcomes, but should help players build a more intelligible sense of effectiveness and improvement.

Second, goal clarity should be deliberately reinforced throughout the player journey. Because goal clarity was also positively associated with perceived competence, designers should reduce interpretive ambiguity in both onboarding and ongoing play. This may involve more transparent tutorials, clearer role expectations, better signaling of short-term and long-term objectives, and progression interfaces that make performance standards easier to interpret. In psychologically demanding competitive environments, players are more likely to experience competence when they understand what the game is asking of them and how progress is being evaluated. Goal-related design therefore functions not only as instruction, but also as a support for self-evaluation and mastery.

Third, reward systems should be aligned more closely with competence signals than with passive accumulation. In the present study, reward systems showed a weaker but still significant contribution to perceived competence. This pattern suggests that rewards may be most effective when they communicate meaningful improvement, skill expression, or role contribution rather than merely time investment. Concrete examples might include skill-based progression badges, role-specific mastery markers, recognition for effective team contribution, or post-match commendation systems tied to strategic performance. This interpretation is consistent with recent research showing that the design of incentive systems matters for how rewards and feedback are experienced and attended to [63]. For MOBA design, the practical lesson is that rewards should ideally confirm competence rather than distract from it.

Fourth, matchmaking and mode design should preserve meaningful challenge. Our moderation results suggest that competence is most likely to translate into flow when match environments are perceived as strategically demanding. This implies that retention is not necessarily maximized by making play uniformly easier. Instead, matchmaking systems should seek forms of challenge that remain demanding but interpretable and fair. Prior research has shown that matchmaking experiences and skill disparities can meaningfully affect player churn in competitive games [65], while recent work on flow further indicates that higher flow tends to emerge when skill and challenge are both elevated [26]. In practical terms, this means that matchmaking algorithms, ranked systems, and limited-time modes should be tuned not only for balance in a narrow statistical sense, but also for the preservation of an experience in which competence can still become immersive.

Finally, live-operations teams should monitor not only behavioral retention outcomes but also the psychological conditions that precede them. If competence and flow are central mechanisms linking design to continuance intention, then game teams may benefit from monitoring player-reported perceptions of effectiveness, progress, and immersion alongside conventional operational metrics. Short in-client pulse surveys, post-match sentiment prompts, and telemetry-informed segmentation may help teams identify where players are losing clarity, competence, or challenge. Such an approach would allow live-operations decisions to respond not only to whether players leave, but also to the psychological reasons why engagement weakens.

Conclusion

Summary of key findings

This study clarifies how core gamification mechanics foster player continuance intention in competitive MOBA settings by validating a competence–flow retention pathway. Across the proposed S–O–R process, goal clarity, immediate feedback, and reward systems positively predicted perceived competence; perceived competence strongly increased flow experience; and flow, in turn, robustly predicted continuance intention. The pattern of significant indirect effects alongside smaller but significant direct effects indicates a partial mediation structure, suggesting that gamification mechanics influence retention both directly and through the sequential development of competence and flow. Furthermore, task complexity moderated the competence–flow relationship, such that the positive effect of competence on flow was stronger under high-complexity match conditions. This finding aligns with flow theory’s challenge–skill balance proposition and extends it to the competitive gaming context. Among the three mechanics, immediate feedback emerged as the most influential competence-building element, underscoring the importance of rapid, mastery-relevant performance cues in sustaining engagement in high-tempo competitive play.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes firm causal inference. Although the proposed competence–flow–continuance pathway is theoretically grounded, the temporal ordering among these variables cannot be established empirically from the present data alone. Accordingly, the observed associations should be interpreted as consistent with, rather than definitive proof of, the proposed sequential mechanism.

Second, all focal constructs were measured through self-report instruments. This raises the possibility that some relationships may have been influenced by common method variance, social desirability, or recall-related inaccuracy. Although our diagnostic tests suggested that common method bias was unlikely to be a major threat in the present dataset, the results should still be interpreted with appropriate caution because shared measurement format can inflate associations among psychologically proximal constructs.

Third, participants were recruited through online gaming communities, which may have introduced self-selection bias. Players who are active in such communities are likely to be more involved, more experienced, or more motivated than the broader population of MOBA users. As a result, the findings may not generalize fully to more casual players, less socially connected players, or player populations embedded in different cultural contexts.

Fourth, the present model focused on three gamification mechanics, namely goal clarity, immediate feedback, and reward systems. Although these mechanics were theoretically central to our framework, they do not exhaust the range of design features that may shape retention. Other elements, such as social affordances, narrative framing, community systems, or identity-related mechanics, may influence continuance intention through additional psychological routes not captured here.

Fifth, the study was limited to the MOBA genre. Competitive MOBA games are characterized by rapid feedback, strategic coordination, and relatively high performance salience, all of which may intensify the relevance of competence and flow. These dynamics may not operate in the same way in other game genres, such as role-playing games, shooters, sandbox games, or casual games. The present findings should therefore be generalized beyond MOBA settings with caution.

Sixth, although the moderation effect of task complexity was statistically significant, the interaction term explained only a modest increment in variance. This suggests that task complexity is likely to be only one of several conditions shaping the translation of competence into flow. Other moderators, including player skill level, motivational orientation, or team coordination demands, may also be important and warrant further examination.

Seventh, the internal consistency of some measures was very high, with several Cronbach’s alpha values exceeding 0.90. Although this supports reliability, it may also indicate partial redundancy among certain items. This possibility does not invalidate the present findings, but it suggests that future work may benefit from refining some scales to preserve construct breadth while reducing overlap in item content. Although the diagnostic evidence in the present study does not suggest that common method bias is severe, a single-source self-report design cannot completely eliminate this possibility. Future research would therefore benefit from multi-method approaches that combine self-report measures with behavioral telemetry, observational indicators, or other complementary forms of assessment.

Future research directions

Building on these findings, several directions for future research emerge. Longitudinal studies could examine how perceived competence and flow evolve over time and how these changes relate to retention during important gameplay transitions, such as rank progression or major content updates. Experimental designs that manipulate specific gamification mechanics, such as feedback speed, reward contingency, or goal visibility, would provide stronger evidence for the causal pathways proposed here. Cross-cultural comparisons would help determine whether the observed relationships generalize across different gaming cultures, platforms, and player demographics. Future research could also extend the present model by incorporating additional psychological mediators, such as autonomy and relatedness, as well as broader outcomes, including in-game spending, social participation, or burnout-related disengagement. In light of the moderating role of task complexity, subsequent studies may also examine how matchmaking systems and game balancing practices shape the challenge–skill balance that supports flow. Finally, qualitative approaches, including interviews and diary methods, may complement survey-based work by capturing the lived experience through which players interpret competence, immersion, and the decision to continue playing.
