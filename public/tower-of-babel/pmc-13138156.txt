Real-world efficacy and safety of omalizumab in chronic spontaneous urticaria: Insights from the Middle East
Authors: Shuayb Elkhalifa, Eiman Mahmoud, Fulvio Salvo, Hamad AlHameli, Inmaculada Cerecedo, Irfan Shafiq, Mohamed Abuzakouk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) is a mast cell–mediated condition that can cause significant impairment in quality of life. Second-generation H1-antihistamines are recommended as first-line treatment, but a proportion of patients do not achieve adequate control and require add-on therapy. Omalizumab is the preferred biologic for CSU that is unresponsive to high-dose H1-antihistamines.ObjectiveOur aim was to assess the efficacy and safety of omalizumab in patients with CSU refractory to high-dose second-generation H1-antihistamines within a Middle Eastern tertiary care health care setting and to then relate the findings to current international urticaria guideline recommendations.MethodsA retrospective observational study was undertaken at a tertiary allergy clinic from September 2015 to September 2018. A total of 75 adults with CSU of more than 6 months’ duration and unresponsive to 4-fold doses of H1-antihistamines received omalizumab and were followed in the clinic. The primary outcome was change in Urticaria Control Test score before and after treatment. Additional data included responder classification, comorbidities, laboratory findings, treatment intervals, and adverse events.ResultsThere was a significant improvement in Urticaria Control Test score following omalizumab treatment (mean change –7.56; P < .001), with no association between outcome and baseline IgE levels. The response rates were 84% complete, 13% partial, and 3% poor. Most complete responders achieved control after 1 or 2 doses. Vitamin D deficiency and raised IgE level were frequent but did not predict response. Adverse events were infrequent and mild.ConclusionOmalizumab is effective and well tolerated in CSU that is refractory to high-dose H1-antihistamines, providing rapid and sustained symptom control in most patients. Current international guidelines continue to recommend omalizumab as the first-choice add-on therapy in this setting, with other biologics remaining under investigation.Key words: Chronic spontaneous urticaria, omalizumab, real-world study, biologic therapy, Middle East, Urticaria Control Test, antihistamine-refractory, treatment efficacy, safety, vitamin D deficiency, IgE

Introduction

Chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) is a mast cell–mediated skin disorder characterized by the spontaneous occurrence of wheals, angioedema, or both for more than 6 weeks without an identifiable specific trigger. The lifetime prevalence of acute urticaria is estimated at around 20%, whereas CSU affects approximately 0.5% to 1% of the population at any given time.1 CSU is associated with substantial impairment in quality of life, affecting sleep, daily activities, work performance, and psychological well-being.2

Although the underlying cause of CSU is not always identified, current evidence recognizes distinct pathogenic subtypes, including type I autoallergic CSU and type IIb autoimmune CSU, alongside cases with no identified mechanism (CSU of unknown cause).3 Histamine release from cutaneous mast cells is a central event in symptom generation, with additional contributions from other mediators such as platelet-activating factor, leukotrienes, and cytokines.4

The 2022 international urticaria guideline (which was developed jointly by the European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology; Global Asthma and Allergy European Network; European Dermatology Forum; and Asia Pacific Association of Asthma, Allergy, and Clinical Immunology) recommends a stepwise approach to management.5 First-line therapy consists of standard doses of nonsedating second-generation H1-antihistamines. If symptom control is inadequate after 2 to 4 weeks, the dose may be increased up to 4-fold.5 In patients unresponsive to high-dose H1-antihistamines, the preferred add-on treatment is omalizumab, a recombinant humanized mAb targeting IgE. Omalizumab has demonstrated efficacy across multiple randomized controlled trials and real-world studies, providing rapid and sustained improvement in urticaria activity and quality of life.6,7

Recent updates to the evidence base confirm that omalizumab maintains a favorable safety profile and high rates of clinical response in patients with antihistamine-refractory CSU.8 In addition, dupilumab and remibrutinib are emerging as potential new options, but long-term data are limited and no option has supplanted omalizumab as the recommended next step in the treatment algorithm.9, 10, 11

The Middle East and North Africa region has a limited volume of published data on the use of biologics for CSU despite a growing prevalence of the condition and increasing access to advanced therapies.12 This study evaluates the efficacy and safety of omalizumab in patients with CSU that is refractory to high-dose second-generation H1-antihistamines in a tertiary center in the United Arab Emirates, and it relates these findings to current international recommendations. Despite extensive global evidence supporting the efficacy of omalizumab in patients with CSU, those from the Middle East and North Africa region remain underrepresented in both pivotal clinical trials and real-world observational studies. Reporting outcomes from this region is important to validate international guideline recommendations across diverse health care systems and populations with distinct demographic, genetic, and environmental characteristics.

This was a retrospective observational study conducted at the Allergy and Immunology Clinic of the Cleveland Clinic Abu Dhabi in the United Arab Emirates between September 2015 and September 2018. The study was approved by the clinic’s institutional research ethics committee and performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The United Arab Emirates operates a mixed public-private health care system with open access to specialist services. Initially, patients with chronic urticaria are commonly managed in primary or secondary care. Referral to tertiary allergy and immunology services occurs when symptoms persist despite guideline-directed escalation of antihistamine therapy. At the tertiary level, patients undergo structured assessment to confirm CSU and exclude chronic inducible urticaria, hereditary angioedema, urticarial vasculitis, and other mimicking conditions before biologic therapy is initiated.

Eligible patients were aged 14 years or older, had a diagnosis of CSU persisting for more than 6 weeks, and had symptoms despite treatment with up to 4-fold the standard dose of nonsedating second-generation H1-antihistamines. CSU was diagnosed according to the current guideline criteria of the European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology; Global Asthma and Allergy European Network; European Dermatology Forum; and Asia Pacific Association of Asthma, Allergy, and Clinical Immunology.5 Patients with chronic inducible urticaria as their sole presentation, hereditary angioedema, urticarial vasculitis, or other primary dermatologic conditions were excluded.

All patients received omalizumab as an add-on to their existing antihistamine regimen. Omalizumab was administered subcutaneously at a dose of 300 mg every 2 to 4 weeks, with dosing interval adjustments based on the treating physician’s clinical judgment and patient response.

The primary outcome measure was change in Urticaria Control Test (UCT) score from baseline to after treatment (following each dose of omalizumab). The UCT is a validated patient-reported tool assessing disease control over the preceding 4 weeks, with scores ranging from 0 (no control) to 16 (complete control).13

Secondary outcomes included (1) clinical response classification into complete, partial, or poor responders based on UCT score, symptom resolution, and antihistamine requirement; (2) change in concomitant medication use, including antihistamines and corticosteroids; (3) associations between treatment response and baseline laboratory variables, including levels of total serum IgE, vitamin D, anti–thyroid peroxidase antibodies, antithyroglobulin antibodies, and C-reactive protein (CRP); and (4) adverse events reported during omalizumab therapy.

The baseline investigations included measurement of the levels of total serum IgE (using immunoassay), 25-hydroxyvitamin D (using chemiluminescent assay), anti–thyroid peroxidase and antithyroglobulin antibodies (using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay), and CRP (using immunoturbidimetric assay).

Two complementary statistical approaches were applied. The first was mixed-effects regression modeling, according to which UCT scores before and after omalizumab administration were modeled as the dependent variable, with time (pretreatment vs posttreatment) and baseline total IgE level used as fixed effects and a random intercept for each patient to account for repeated measures. Interaction between IgE level and time was tested. The second approach was responder group comparison, in accordance with which patients were categorized as complete, partial, or poor responders based on their UCT score. Demographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics were compared using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were analyzed using the Student t test or Mann-Whitney U test, and categoric variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. All statistical tests were 2 tailed with a significance level of P < .05. Analyses were conducted using R, version 3.5.1 (the package lme4, version 1.1-21), and SPSS, version 25 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY).

Results and discussion

A total of 75 patients with CSU were included. The study population comprised 45 females (60%) and 30 males (40%), with a mean age of 35.7 years (range 18-65). All had symptoms for more than 6 weeks and had been unresponsive to up to 4-fold doses of second-generation H1-antihistamines. Eight patients (11%) also had angioedema at baseline. Mixed-effects regression analysis demonstrated a significant improvement in UCT score following omalizumab treatment (mean change –7.56 [95% CI = –9.04 to –6.11]; P < .001). There was no evidence that baseline total IgE level influenced treatment response (interaction term P = .432).

Of the study cohort members, 63 (84%) were classified as complete responders, 10 (13%) as partial responders, and 2 (3%) as poor responders. Most complete responders (n = 54 [85.7%]) achieved symptom control after 1 or 2 doses of omalizumab. Of the partial responders, 4 (40%) reported improvement after the first dose, but all continued to require daily antihistamines. The poor responders showed minimal change in UCT score and continued to require high-dose antihistamines (Figs 1 and 2). No statistically significant differences were observed between complete responders and partial or poor responders across baseline demographic or laboratory variables. Following omalizumab initiation, 48 complete responders (76.2%) reduced antihistamine use to an as-needed basis, and 11 (17.5%) discontinued antihistamines entirely. Two patients who were previously dependent on oral corticosteroids were able to taper and discontinue steroid therapy. In the partial responder group, 4 patients (40%) reduced antihistamine use, but none discontinued treatment. The poor responders showed no reduction in medication use.

Low serum vitamin D level was observed in 29 complete responders (46%) and in 8 partial responders (80%) (P = .043). Raised total IgE level was found in 29 complete responders and 6 partial responders. The mean total IgE levels were 419 IU/mL (range 101-1407) in complete responders and 392 IU/mL (range 122-1292) in partial responders. Both of the poor responders had normal IgE levels. Testing showed the presence of antithyroid antibodies in 12 complete responders (19%) and 1 partial responder (10%). The mean CRP level was 28.6 mg/L in complete responders and 6.6 mg/L in partial responders. The mean CRP values were higher in complete responders than in partial responders; however, CRP levels demonstrated marked variability with a small number of high values influencing the mean. The median CRP values were comparable between groups, with no statistically significant association between CRP and treatment resistance observed.

Omalizumab was administered at intervals ranging from 2 to 8 weeks, with most patients receiving 1 dose every 4 weeks. The duration of therapy ranged from 11 to 38 months (mean 10.7 months).

Omalizumab was well tolerated. Two patients (2.7%) reported mild adverse effects: one experienced localized itching and the other experienced mild perioral swelling; neither required discontinuation of treatment. No serious adverse events occurred.

To our knowledge, this study is the first and largest series from the Middle East and North Africa region to evaluate the efficacy and safety of omalizumab in patients with CSU who were unresponsive to high-dose second-generation H1-antihistamines. The high complete response rate (84%) and rapid onset of symptom control observed are consistent with outcomes reported in pivotal randomized controlled trials6,7 and recent real-world studies.14, 15, 16

This study represents the largest reported real-world cohort evaluating omalizumab for CSU from the Middle East. Although omalizumab efficacy has been well established globally,14, 15, 16 regional real-world data remain limited. Our findings provide important validation of the international guideline recommendations in a Middle Eastern tertiary care setting with broad access to biologic therapies.

In line with recent European real-world data,17 baseline total IgE level was not a reliable predictor of treatment response. These findings support current guideline statements that IgE should not be used in isolation to guide treatment decisions in CSU.

Although CRP level has been associated with autoimmune CSU and treatment resistance in some cohorts, our data do not support a predictive role for CRP level. The observed elevation in mean CRP level among complete responders was driven by outliers and likely reflects intercurrent inflammation or comorbid conditions rather than CSU disease activity.

The 2022 international urticaria guideline continues to recommend omalizumab as the preferred add-on therapy in antihistamine-refractory CSU; the recommendation is based on omalizumab’s proven efficacy, favorable safety profile, and patient-reported benefits in terms of quality of life.5,8 Our findings align with this recommendation, with the majority of patients achieving either complete or partial control after the first 2 doses. Importantly, the absence of correlation between baseline IgE level and treatment response supports the guideline statement that total IgE level is not a reliable predictor of omalizumab efficacy in CSU.8 Response to omalizumab did not show a statistically significant correlation with baseline total IgE levels. However, both nonresponders had normal (low) IgE values. Although the number of poor responders is too small for formal statistical analysis, this observation suggests that low baseline IgE level may be associated with a reduced likelihood of response to omalizumab.

Vitamin D deficiency was common, particularly among partial responders, although the clinical relevance of this association remains uncertain. Several studies have suggested that vitamin D supplementation may improve urticaria control in deficient individuals,18 but robust evidence is lacking. Similarly, the presence of thyroid autoimmunity in a subset of patients is in keeping with reported associations between CSU and autoimmune thyroid disease,19 although its influence on biologic treatment outcomes remains unclear.

Our results also highlight the durability of response to omalizumab, with many patients able to reduce or discontinue antihistamines and some patients ceasing corticosteroid therapy entirely. This steroid-sparing effect is a notable advantage, given the potential adverse effects of long-term corticosteroid use.20

Safety outcomes were favorable, with only mild, transient adverse effects in 2 patients and no serious reactions. This is consistent with the extensive postmarketing safety data supporting the tolerability of omalizumab.15

The therapeutic landscape for CSU is evolving,8, 9, 10 with emerging agents such as dupilumab8 and remibrutinib10 demonstrating good efficacy in large clinical trials.8, 9, 10 Although both are expected to be incorporated into future treatment guidelines5 as alternatives to omalizumab for patients who remain symptomatic despite high-dose H1-antihistamines, the extensive clinical experience and well-established safety profile of omalizumab remain unmatched at present.14

This study is limited by its retrospective design and the recruitment period (2015-2018). The cohort reflects early real-world use of omalizumab following its introduction in the United Arab Emirates. Importantly, omalizumab remained the only guideline-recommended biologic for antihistamine-refractory CSU throughout this period, and the core management algorithm has remained stable, supporting the continued relevance of these findings. This study’s retrospective design and single-center setting limit generalizability. UCT scoring, although validated, is subject to patient recall bias. Collection of laboratory data using tools such as basophil histamine release assays or autologous serum skin testing, which might provide further insight into CSU subtypes, was not systematically performed.

Omalizumab is a highly effective and well-tolerated therapy for patients with CSU who are unresponsive to high-dose second-generation H1-antihistamines, producing rapid and sustained symptom control in most patients. These findings reinforce its role as the preferred add-on therapy in the current international guidelines. Although newer agents may expand future options, omalizumab remains the standard against which emerging treatments should be measured.

Clinical implications

Omalizumab offers reliable and well-tolerated symptom control in CSU that is unresponsive to high-dose antihistamines, which is consistent with its position as the preferred add-on therapy in international guidelines.
