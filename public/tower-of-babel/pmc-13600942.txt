Fc-optimized GITR antibody enhances a CD4 T cell–dendritic cell crosstalk to promote antitumor immunity
Authors: Yahel Avraham, Natasha Barth, Tzlil Yair Bar-On, Barak Toval, Aviya Habshush Menachem, Jasmine Blanga, Ella Herzog, Hagar Rotem, Yuval Shapir Itai, Tali Feferman, Moshe Biton, Rony Dahan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Targeting the stimulatory immune checkpoint glucocorticoid-induced TNFR-related protein (GITR) using agonistic monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) is a promising strategy for cancer immunotherapy that activates effector T cells and eliminates regulatory T cells. The antitumor activity of anti-GITR mAbs depends on the engagement of the fragment crystallizable (Fc) domain to their receptors (FcγRs); however, this has not been comprehensively investigated in human anti-GITR mAbs. Here, we used Fc protein and glycan engineering to modify the FcγR interactions of anti-GITR human mAbs and characterized them in humanized mice. We identified an Fc-optimized human IgG scaffold that enhances antitumor efficacy through multiple FcγR-mediated mechanisms, including regulatory T cell depletion and mutual engagement and activation of CD4+ T cells and dendritic cells, leading to antitumor cytotoxicity of CD4+ T cells and enhanced CD8+ T cell activity. Our findings suggest a strategy to optimize human anti-GITR mAbs, harnessing beneficial immune pathways to improve their therapeutic potential.

Main

Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) that target immune checkpoint receptors on T cells are a revolutionary approach to stimulate the immune system against cancer cells. They are categorized into antagonists or agonists on the basis of their induction mode1–3. Antagonistic mAbs block inhibitory signaling pathways on T cells, thereby dampening immunosuppressive mechanisms. This category includes the US Food and Drug Administration-approved immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) mAbs targeting programmed cell death protein 1 (PD1), PD1 ligand 1 (PDL1), cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA4) and lymphocyte activation gene 3 (LAG3). ICB can elicit a durable antitumor immune response in various cancers but most individuals do not respond or acquire treatment resistance, resulting in relapses. Agonistic mAbs offer an alternative or a synergistic approach aimed at stimulating coregulatory T cell receptors4. Preclinical studies have demonstrated the potential of agonistic anti-glucocorticoid-induced TNFR-related protein (GITR) mAbs to elicit potent antitumor responses as monotherapy or combination therapy with ICB5–7. However, limited efficacy has restricted clinical development5–8.

The fragment crystallizable (Fc) domain of checkpoint mAbs can have an important role in mediating their antitumor activity, depending on Fcγ receptor (FcγR) engagement9. A better mechanistic understanding of FcγR pathways can provide guidance on the checkpoint mAb Fc engineering to harness beneficial pathways for improved therapeutic potency10–12. Fc redesign of anti-GITR mAbs may potentially benefit from two distinct, possibly opposing FcγR pathways. Engagement of activating FcγRs can lead to intratumoral regulatory T cell (Treg) depletion, further enhancing antitumor immunity13–16. Conversely, engagement of the inhibitory FcγRIIb can lead to antibody crosslinking, which promotes high-order GITR clustering at the cell membrane, thereby enhancing agonistic signaling, as previously demonstrated with other tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily (TNFRSF) immune-stimulating receptors16–18. Preclinical studies suggest a predominant role of the activating FcγRs over the inhibitory FcγRIIb in promoting the antitumor response of GITR agonists involving Treg depletion19. However, these studies characterized mouse and other nonhuman IgG scaffolds of anti-GITR mAbs, which were not optimal for inducing specific FcγR pathways or necessarily clinically relevant.

In this study, we evaluated the in vivo outcomes of distinct human FcγR (hFcγR) pathways of anti-GITR mAbs and explored the hierarchy of mechanisms underlying their antitumor activity. We identified an optimal human IgG scaffold that showed superior activity and a unique mode of action compared to clinically tested subclasses and Treg-depleting mAbs against other targets.

Results

FcγRs mediate the antitumor activity of anti-GITR mAb

We investigated whether the antitumor activity of human anti-GITR IgG requires interactions with hFcγRs and how Fc engineering modifications can increase that activity. Therefore, we generated a panel of human Fc variants with predetermined binding affinities to the different hFcγRs to use as IgG backbones. Our modified Fc panel consisted of a wild-type IgG1, an aglycosylated IgG1-N297A mutant that lacks the ability to engage hFcγRs, an afucosylated IgG1-Fc (aFuc) with a selectively enhanced binding to the activating hFcγRIIIa, an IgG1-G236A mutant20 with a selectively enhanced binding to the activating hFcγRIIa and an IgG1-G237D;P238D;H268D;P271G;A330R mutant (V11)21 with a selectively enhanced binding to the inhibitory hFcγRIIb (Fig. 1a and Extended Data Fig. 1a). Importantly, these Fc modifications did not affect the Fab-mediated GITR-binding affinity (Extended Data Fig. 1b). We compared the in vivo efficacy of these anti-GITR Fc variants and evaluated hFcγR–Fc interactions in humanized FcγR mice22. Mice with established MC38 colon adenocarcinoma tumors were treated with the anti-GITR mAb variants and monitored for tumor growth (Fig. 1b and Extended Data Fig. 1c) and overall survival (Extended Data Fig. 1d). We observed significant differences in the antitumor efficacy mediated by different Fc variants, indicating that Fc–FcγR interactions dictate the activity of agonistic anti-GITR mAbs. The Fc-dysfunctional N297A variant triggered a mild but significant activity. As N297A possibly retains residual attenuated binding to the high-affinity FcγRIa23, we evaluated its activity in mice that express all hFcγRs except for FcγRI (hFcγR, R1−/−). The N297A variant induced similar antitumor activities in hFcγR-R1−/− and hFcγR mice, implying an FcγR-independent activity (Extended Data Fig. 1e). This notable mild activity was abrogated when selective binding enhancement to the inhibitory FcγRIIb was introduced to the IgG scaffold (V11 Fc). The V11 variant did not significantly inhibit tumor growth (Fig. 1b). In contrast, the G236A or aFuc variants, which enhance mAb binding to activating FcγRIIa24 or FcγRIIIa25, respectively, significantly increased antitumor potency and extended animal survival (Fig. 1b and Extended Data Fig. 1c,d). All the Fc variants showed comparable agonistic activity in the presence of FcγRs; however, the Fc-null variant had a lower half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) because of its lack of FcR engagement (Extended Data Fig. 1f). To evaluate the efficacy of the anti-GITR variants in producing long-term immune memory, we rechallenged mice that achieved a complete response. These mice, which had initially rejected the primary tumor, were monitored for 62 days after treatment and then reinoculated with MC38 tumors into the contrary flank without subsequent treatment. While tumors rapidly developed in control naïve mice, mice previously treated with anti-GITR mAbs, regardless of the Fc variant, rejected the tumors and survived (Extended Data Fig. 1g). The long-term protection against tumor reappearance was T cell dependent, as T cell-depleting mAbs abolished the rechallenge antitumor effect (Extended Data Fig. 1h). This suggests the involvement of an anti-GITR-induced T cell memory mechanism. Fc engineering enhances antitumor immunity

Both aFuc and G236A Fc variants similarly increased antitumor activity compared to the parental IgG. Thus, we evaluated whether enhanced engagement of anti-GITR mAb to both hFcγRIIa and hFcγRIIIa could further increase the antitumor effect. We generated another Fc variant that carries both the G236A substitution and reduced fucosylation in its core Fc glycan (‘GA-aFuc’) to a degree similar to that of the aFuc variant (Extended Data Fig. 1a). The Fc modifications did not affect IgG properties, such as Fab binding to GITR (Extended Data Fig. 1i). Indeed, this variant demonstrated enhanced dual binding to hFcγRIIa and hFcRγIIIa but not to the inhibitory hFcγRIIb (Fig. 1c). Because of the potent antitumor effect of the aFuc and G236A variants, we initiated treatment in mice with a larger average size of MC38 tumors (~115 mm3 compared to ~50 mm3 in Fig. 1b) to evaluate additional improvement in therapeutic outcome. Treating advanced MC38 tumor-bearing mice using aFuc, G236A or GA-aFuc Fc variants resulted in significant antitumor activity. The GA-aFuc Fc variant demonstrated highest efficacy, implying that enhanced binding to both activating hFcγRIIa and hFcγRIIIa provides a significant therapeutic benefit over either FcγR alone (Fig. 1d and Extended Data Fig. 1j). We then compared the GA-aFuc variant’s potency to that of commonly used IgG scaffolds of clinical GITR agonists, namely, N297A (TRX518)26 and various IgG1 scaffolds (MK-4166 (ref. 27), REGN6569 (ref. 28), BMS-986156 (ref. 29), GWN323 (ref. 30), AMG228 (ref. 31) and INCAGN0187 (ref. 32)). Treating advanced MC38 tumor-bearing mice using the GA-aFuc variant resulted in significant antitumor potency (5/9 tumor-free mice) compared to N297A (0/9 tumor-free mice) and IgG1 (1/9 tumor-free mice) (Fig. 1e). All mice that initially responded to the GA-aFuc Fc variant rejected reinoculated tumors in a T cell-dependent manner, indicating induction of long-term immune memory (Extended Data Fig. 1k). This superior potency of the GA-aFuc variant was also observed in B16 melanoma, where GA-aFuc but not IgG1 or N297A resulted in significantly reduced tumor growth (Fig.1f and Extended Data Fig. 1l). In advanced MCA-205 fibrosarcoma, GA-aFuc and IgG1 exhibited similar potency, superior to N297A, implying a lower Fc-mediated potency threshold in this more immunogenic model (Fig. 1g). These additional tumor models support the generality of Fc-mediated superior activity across multiple cancer types, with some variations in potency between the models, presumably because of the inherent differences in the characteristics of the tumors, stroma and immune cells within the tumor microenvironment (TME). Altogether, the GA-aFuc scaffold, with Fc modifications enhancing binding to activating FcγRIIa and FcγRIIIa, is an optimal human IgG scaffold for agonistic anti-GITR mAb.

Complement pathways or pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics do not contribute to the GITR mAb’s potency

To further characterize the differential activity of anti-GITR Fc variants, we evaluated the pharmacokinetics of the Fc variants in MC38 tumor-bearing hFcγR mice. The clearance rates of Fc variants from the blood were similar to those of the parental IgG1, except for the V11 variant, which had a slightly faster rate (Extended Data Fig. 1m). This possibly suggests a FcγR-mediated internalization of the anti-GITR mAbs by tumor macrophages, as previously suggested for anti-PD1 mAbs33. Next, an in vivo binding assay was performed to determine the membranal or intracellular occupancy of the mAbs in GITR-expressing or FcγR-expressing immune cells in the TME. The Fc-dysfunctional N297A variant displayed higher cell surface occupancy in GITR-expressing CD8+ and CD4+ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, as well as lower internalization within macrophages compared to other Fc variants (Extended Data Fig. 1n,o). Notably, the IgG scaffolds with FcγR-binding capability exhibited similar behavior (Extended Data Fig. 1n,o). Hence, the pharmacokinetics and receptor occupancy behaviors do not dictate the observed differential antitumor activities of Fc variants. We then evaluated the efficacy of Fc variants in FcγR-knockout (KO; FcγRα-chain−/−) mice with advanced MC38 tumors. Their activity was diminished to the Fc-dysfunctional variant level (Fig. 1h), supporting the role of FcγR pathways in the enhanced antitumor activity. Consistent with our data, the antitumor activity of an anti-GITR rat and mouse IgG are abrogated in Fcγ-chain-deficient mice lacking activating mouse FcγRs19. We then switched to mouse Fc–FcγR systems and generated an agonistic anti-GITR clone on Fc-optimized mIgG2a and Fc-dysfunctional mIgG2a-N297A backbones, which preferentially bind to the activating mouse FcγRI and FcγRIV34 or do not interact with mFcγRs35, respectively. As observed with human Fc variants in the humanized Fc–FcγR system, the mouse Fc variants preserved pharmacokinetics properties and binding abilities to GITR in the mouse Fc–FcγR system (Extended Data Fig. 1p,q). Likewise, the Fc-optimized mIgG2a variant showed significantly stronger antitumor activity compared to the Fc-dysfunctional N297A mAb (Extended Data Fig. 1r,s). As Fc activity can induce complement pathways, we investigated whether they contribute to GITR mAbs-mediated tumor growth inhibition. A similar effect was observed for these two variants in complement component C3-KO mice, implying that complement is not necessary for the beneficial effect of the Fc-active variant (Fig. 1i). Collectively, our data indicate that the engagement of hFcγR pathways governs the superior potency of Fc-optimized anti-GITR mAbs.

Fc-mediated Treg elimination by anti-GITR mAbs

Rat and mouse subclasses of anti-GITR mAbs were previously shown to cause tumor Treg depletion through FcγR-dependent mechanisms19. Indeed, Tregs expressed higher GITR levels than T cells in the TME (Extended Data Fig. 2a). Therefore, we speculated that our Fc-optimized GITR mAb increases Treg depletion in the TME. We observed a rapid decrease in Treg frequencies within the TME following treatment with IgG1 and the Fc-optimized GA-aFuc at 24 h and through 8 days after treatment (Fig. 2a). Tregs were not depleted after treatment with the Fc-dysfunctional N297A variant, indicating the requirement of activating FcγR pathways for Treg targeting. Notably, Treg depletion was selective to the tumor and absent in the draining lymph nodes (dLNs) and spleen (Extended Data Fig. 2b). Moreover, the Fc-mediated depletion in the TME was selective toward Tregs, as the frequencies of effector CD4+ and CD8+ T cells remained unchanged (24 h) or increased over time (CD8+ T cells, day 8) (Fig. 2a). Correspondingly, we observed a similar trend of Treg depletion following IgG1 and GA-aFuc treatments in the TME of MCA-205 fibrosarcoma and B16 melanoma (Extended Data Fig. 2c). To better understand the effect of the Fc-mediated Treg depletion on the immune cell composition, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of 19,210 sorted TME-derived CD45+ cells taken from mice 4 days after treatment. We identified 16 cell clusters and distinguished immune cell populations on the basis of hallmark gene expression (Extended Data Fig. 2d,e). The analysis confirmed the GITR (Tnfrsf18) expression hierarchy, in which Tnfrsf18 frequency and expression levels were higher in Treg than in other immune cells in the TME (Extended Data Fig. 2f). The scRNA-seq analysis confirmed the Fc-mediated Treg depletion identified by flow cytometry (Fig. 2a and Extended Data Fig. 2g). Furthermore, we identified genes associated with five distinct Treg subclusters, consistent with previous studies36: ‘activated’ cluster (Tnfrsf members, including OX-40 and GITR, and additional Treg activation-associated genes, including Ikzf2), ‘interferon (IFN)-responsive’ cluster (enriched with type I IFN-induced genes, such as Isg15 and Ifit3), ‘proliferative’ cluster (Mcm6), ‘LN-homing/resting’ cluster (Ccr7 and S1pr1) and ‘TH-1-like’ cluster (typically expressed by TH-1 cells, such as Ccl3, Ccl4 and Ccl5). The activated Treg subpopulation was abundant in the TME of untreated and Fc-dysfunctional anti-GITR variant-treated mice but significantly decreased in Fc-optimized variant-treated mice (Fig. 2b). This Treg subset has been reported as the most functionally suppressive, showing a correlation with ICB resistance36. An analysis of Treg differentiation in the TME 4 days after treatment revealed decreases in Tregs expressing 4-1BB, LAG3 and CD25 in mice treated with either IgG1 or GA-aFuc variants (Extended Data Fig. 2h). These markers characterize activated and TH-1-like Treg phenotypes and are known to be associated with impaired survival outcomes36,37. Frequencies of CCR7 and IFNγ-expressing Treg, markers of the LN-homing and IFN-responsive subsets, respectively, remained comparable across the groups (Extended Data Fig. 2i). This analysis confirms that the activated and TH-1-like Treg subpopulations were selectively eliminated by the Fc-active IgG1 and GA-aFuc variants. We conducted ligand–receptor network analysis between Tregs (sender cells) and dendritic cells (DCs), monocytes and macrophages (receiver cells) following anti-GITR mAb treatments. We observed numerous Treg–myeloid ligand–receptor interactions in untreated mice and Fc-dysfunctional variant-treated mice. This rich crosstalk was significantly abrogated in the Fc-optimized variant-treated mice (Fig. 2c). Differential analysis for Treg genes using the MultiNicheNet communication analysis implies their reduced expression by remaining TME Treg following treatment with the Fc-optimized variant (Extended Data Fig. 2j). DCs mediate the therapeutic effect of Fc-optimized GITR mAb

Both IgG1 and the GA-aFuc Fc variant showed similar Treg depletion (Fig. 2a) despite the superior therapeutic activity induced by the latter, indicating an additional FcγR-mediated mechanism. Therefore, we analyzed FCGR2A and FCGR3A gene expression patterns in the TME to identify other effector immune cells engaged by the GA-aFuc variant. While DC subsets mainly expressed FCGR2A, NK cells expressed FCGR3A and macrophages expressed high levels of both (Fig. 3a). Accordingly, treatment with the Fc-optimized GA-aFuc variant in mice bearing MC38 carcinoma and B16 melanoma increased DC activation and frequencies in tumors and dLNs compared to treatments with IgG1 or the Fc-dysfunctional N297A variant (Fig. 3b–d). In MCA-205 adenocarcinoma-bearing mice, both GA-aFuc and IgG1 induced DC activation, unlike N297A (Fig. 3e), aligning with their similar efficacy in this tumor model with a lower FcγR activation threshold. We then switched back to the mouse Fc–FcγR system. Corresponding to the stronger antitumor activity of mIgG2a compared to IgG2a-N297A, mIgG2a also increased Treg depletion efficiency (Extended Data Fig. 3a) and DC activation in the tumor (Extended Data Fig. 3b) and dLNs (Extended Data Fig. 3c), supporting the generality of these pathways in mouse and human Fc–FcγR systems. These observations suggest that an FcγR-dependent DC activation mechanism may contribute to the enhanced antitumor efficacy of the Fc-optimized variant. To investigate this hypothesis, we used genetic models with DC deficiencies. ZBTB46-DTR mice serve as an inducible model for eliminating type 1 and 2 conventional DCs (cDC1 and cDC2)38 and XCR1-iDTR mice serve as an inducible model for eliminating cDC1 cells39. Tumor growth suppression was abolished when cDCs were depleted in tumor-bearing ZBTB46-DTR and XCR1-iDTR mice shortly before anti-GITR mIgG2a treatment (Fig. 3f-g). These results support that cDCs, particularly cDC1, are necessary and sufficient for the antitumor Fc-mediated mechanism of anti-GITR mAbs. As DCs highly express FcγRIIa but not GITR, we hypothesized that they are also engaged by the mAb’s Fc region. To better understand how the Fc-optimized mAb enhances DC function and antitumor immunity, we characterized phenotypic changes in DCs driven by the treatments with anti-GITR Fc variants. We performed scRNA-seq of cDC1, cDC2 and mregDC (encompassing mature cDC1 and cDC2 states) isolated from tumors of mice treated with either the Fc-enhanced (GA-aFuc) or Fc-silent (N297A) variants 4 days after treatment. Differential gene expression analysis showed that anti-GITR treatments had divergent effects on the DC subpopulations (Fig. 3h–j). DCs from GA-aFuc-treated tumors exhibited coordinated transcriptional changes compared to DCs from Fc-silent N297A variant-treated tumors. As shown in the heat map, GA-aFuc-associated cDC1s upregulated genes linked to type I IFN responses (Ifit3, Tgtp2, Ifi203), complement and FcγR engagement (C1qa, FCGR3A, FCGR2A), cell-cycle regulation (Ccnb1, Cenpu, Sgo2a), inflammatory responses (Ccl17, Cxcl2, Lrrc25), activation-associated immediate-early signaling (Cd69, Egr1, Zfp36l1, Cd83), migration-related programs (Cx3cr1, Ccl5, Lrch1) and cytokine-related signaling (Casp4, Stat5b, Ap3d1) (Fig. 3h). In addition to pathway-focused signatures, inspection of the broader differentially expressed set revealed increased expression of genes associated with vesicle and secretory trafficking (Dnajc14, Tmed4, Qpct) and metabolic adaptation (Mthfs, Alkbh1) (Extended Data Fig. 3e). GA-aFuc-associated cDC2s underwent functional and metabolic reprogramming, as shown by upregulated genes linked to energy metabolism (Pgm2l1, Galt, Dld), NF-κB signaling and cell survival (Nfkb1, Nfkbia, Bcl2a1a), protein processing and secretory pathway (Sdf2l1, Selenos, Sar1a), chemokine signaling (Cxcl14, Cx3cl1, Ccl17) and cytoskeleton organization (Mrtfb, Strip1, Bcl2) (Fig. 3i). GA-aFuc-associated mregDCs displayed Fc-dependent effector functions, as shown by upregulated genes linked to phagocytosis (Cfp, Pik3cb, Lgals3bp), IgG handling and binding and phagocytosis (Fcgrt, Hip1, Rac1), FcR signaling (Fcer1g, Lyn, Vav3) and complement activation (C1qa, C1qb, C1qc). In parallel, these cells upregulated genes involved in actin cytoskeleton organization (Svil, Capg, Mtss1), migration-associated programs (Tiam1, Dock7, Synpo2) and antigen processing and MHC I and II presentation (Erap1, H2-DMb1, Unc93b1) (Fig. 3j). Consistent with these signatures, pathway analysis highlighted phagocytosis, FcγR and complement responses, migration and antigen processing and presentation pathways in response to Fc-enhanced variant treatment. Interestingly, FCGR3A expression was upregulated across all cDC subsets following GA-aFuc variant treatment (Fig. 3k), possibly further enhancing its activity through hFcγRIIIa as a secondary treatment phase.

Altogether, the data suggest that Fc-mediated activation by GITR mAb promotes differentiation, maturation and antigen processing by both cDC1 and cDC2 in the TME. Meanwhile, mregDCs undertake potent Fc-mediated phagocytosis leading to antigen processing and cross-presentation and crosstalk or activation of T cells.

Next, we explored how CD4+ T cells activate DCs through FcγR-dependent mechanisms, speculating that the Fc-optimized anti-GITR mAb can bridge these cells because of its high binding affinities to GITR expressed on CD4+ T cells and hFcγRs expressed on DCs. Therefore, we analyzed dLNs from MC38 tumor-bearing hFcγR mice 4 days after treatment with N297A or GA-aFuc anti-GITR variants. We observed increased frequencies of myeloid–T cell doublets in the dLNs of Fc-optimized mAb-treated mice compared to untreated or Fc-dysfunctional variant-treated mice. The doublet formation consisted of CD4+ T cell–cDC2 and CD4+ T cell–macrophage in Fc-optimized mAb-treated mice (Fig. 4f). Imagestream analysis of doublets bound to anti-GITR antibodies in the dLNs revealed an increased frequency of anti-GITR-positive doublets in the GA-aFuc-treated group compared to the N297A-treated group (Fig. 4g), primarily DC–CD4 effector cell (CD4+FoxP3−) doublets (Fig. 4h). When comparing the Fc-optimized and Fc-silent treatments, the DC–Treg doublet proportion increased (6% to 10%), the DC–CD4 effector cell doublet proportion increased (36% to 55%) and the DC–CD8 proportion decreased (58% to 35%). We analyzed the cellular location of GITR mAbs within the doublets and found that only GA-aFuc was located on DCs, while both Fc variants were similarly located on T cells (Fig. 4i,j), indicating Fab-mediated binding to T cells and Fc-mediated binding to DCs. Additionally, GA-aFuc but not N297A was located at the interacting membrane surfaces of the DC–T cell doublets, implying physical bridging between FcγR+ DCs and GITR+ T cells by this Fc-enhanced GITR mAb. Furthermore, most DC–T cell doublets in the GA-aFuc-exposed but not N297A-exposed dLNs contained GITR mAbs within DCs, indicating FcγR-mediated internalization by activated DCs (Fig. 4k and Extended Data Fig. 4e).

Treg-independent DC activation is unique to GITR mAb

We questioned whether the identified dual activation mechanism of CD4+ T cells and DCs by Fc-optimized anti-GITR mAb is because of Treg depletion40. We selected the Treg-depleting anti-CD25 and anti-CCR8 mAbs41–43, as their targets were overexpressed by intratumoral Tregs (Fig. 5a). Fc-engineered IgG versions, mainly afucosylated IgG1 for increased FcγR-mediated Treg depletion, are currently under clinical development44–46. The CCR8-targeting and two CD25-targeting mAbs (PC-61 clone blocks IL-2 binding to CD25 whereas 7D4 clone does not block IL-2 binding) were produced with the Fc-potent mIgG2a backbone, equivalent to the mouse anti-GITR mAb backbone. All mAbs similarly depleted Treg in tumors; however, anti-CD25 and, to a lower extent, anti-CCR8 mAbs depleted Treg in dLNs, unlike the anti-GITR mAbs (Fig. 5b). This can be explained by the high CD25 expression and CD25 to GITR density ratio on dLN Tregs. The lower CCR8+ Tregs in the dLNs possibly led to a less significant Treg depletion by anti-CCR8 mAb (Fig. 5c). Interestingly, increased DC activation, demonstrated through elevated CD80 and CD86 expression, was exclusive to the anti-GITR mAb (Fig. 5d). Treg depletion-mediated antitumor activity was then assessed using the ZBTB46-DTR model that lacks cDCs38. While the GITR mAb antitumor effect was cDC-dependent, the anti-CCR8 mAb antitumor effect was maintained in the absence of DCs (Fig. 5e). Next, we compared the modes of action of anti-GITR and anti-CTLA4 mAbs. CTLA4, a clinically validated target for immunotherapy, is expressed by intratumoral Tregs and reported to promote DC activation46,47. Both GITR and CTLA4 were expressed on Tregs and effector CD4+ T cells in the TME (Fig. 5f). Anti-CTLA4 mAb treatment resulted in tumor Treg depletion and dLN DC activation (Fig. 5g,h). These data imply a unique mode of action by the Fc-optimized GITR mAbs compared to other Treg-depleting antibodies, involving CD4+ T cell-dependent DC activation, intratumor-restricted Treg depletion and DC-mediated antitumor activity. To further distinguish between Treg depletion and DC activation mechanisms, we experimentally disrupted engagement with the activating hFcγRs within the TME through intratumoral injection of hFcγRIIa-blocking and hFcγRIII-blocking mAbs. Treg elimination levels remained similar under these settings, presumably because of the high expression of hFcγRIIa and hFcγRIIIa by macrophages and ADCC/ADCP-mediating effector cells. However, Fc-mediated DC activation in the TME and dLNs was abolished in the presence of the activating FcγR-blocking mAbs (Fig. 5j). This finding implies that Fc-mediated Treg depletion and DC activation by the Fc-optimized mAbs occur through separate, independent pathways. Accordingly, treatment with the anti-GITR IgG1 subclass in MC38 and B16 tumor-bearing mice showed Treg depletion similar to that of the Fc-optimized variant but with significantly reduced DC activation and migration to the dLNs and overall antitumor efficacy (Figs. 2a and 3b–d and Extended Data Fig. 2c). In contrast, similar DC activation was observed with IgG1 and GA-aFuc treatments of MCA-205-bearing mice, resulting in similar antitumor potencies (Figs. 1f and 3e). Altogether, our data suggest that Fc-mediated DC activation enhances the therapeutic potential of GITR mAbs.

Cytotoxic and effector CD4+ T cell activities engaged by Fc-optimized anti-GITR mAbs

Intriguingly, GITR is highly expressed on CD4+Foxp3− effector T (Teff) cells in the TME (Fig. 5a). CD4+ Teff cells can promote antitumor immunity through multiple pathways, including licensing of cDCs to prime CD8+ cells as CD4+ helper cells48 and direct tumor cell killing as CD4+ cytotoxic cells49. scRNA-seq analysis of CD4+ T cells in the TME following treatment with the Fc-optimized (GA-aFuc) anti-GITR mAbs revealed an intrinsic cytotoxic program: upregulation of Gzma, Gzmb, Prf1 and Nkg7 compared to untreated or Fc-dysfunctional variant-treated mice (Fig. 6a). In addition, the cytotoxicity program score (for example, Eomes, Gzmk, Ctla2a, Ifng, Gzmb, Gzma, Runx3, Nkg7, Cst7, Fcrl6, Slamf7, Cx3cr1, Prf1 and Lamp1) was enriched in the GA-aFuc group compared to untreated and Fc-dysfunctional groups (Fig. 6b). We isolated CD8+, CD4+CD25− and CD4+CD25+ dLN T cells from MC38 tumor-bearing hFcγR mice 4 days after treatment with anti-GITR Fc variants and coincubated them with cancer cells to test their killing capacity. In line with the scRNA-seq data, CD4+CD25+ and CD8+ T cells isolated from Fc-optimized variant-treated mice but not Fc-dysfunctional treated mice exhibited significantly increased cancer-cell-killing capacity compared to cells isolated from untreated mice (Fig. 6c). We also noted a significant increase in Gzmb-expressing CD8+ and CD4+CD25+ T cells in response to the GA-aFuc variant (Fig. 6d). We quantified the killing capability of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells isolated from MC38 tumor-bearing hFcγR mice primed by the GA-aFuc variant towards other cancer cells. The effect was exclusively effective against MC38 adenocarcinoma cells but not B16 melanoma, AT3 mammary carcinoma or MCA-205 fibrosarcoma cells, indicating an antigen-specific killing (Fig. 6e). Additionally, the killing ability of MC38-specific CD4+ T cells was found to be MHC II dependent (Fig. 6f). We isolated CD4+ T cells from MC38 tumor-bearing hFcγR mice 4 days after treatment with Fc-optimized GITR mAbs or control and coincubated them with MC38 cancer cells in transwells to test whether their killing ability was contact dependent. Consequently, the superior cancer-cell-killing activity of CD4+ T cells from Fc-optimized variant-treated mice was lost when physically separated from the cancer cells. (Fig. 6g). Combined, these results highlight the role of intratumoral Foxp3−CD4+ Teff cells in the antitumor activity of Fc-optimized GITR mAbs. Source Data

We titrated the weakened agonistic and the agonistic Fc-optimized anti-GITR mAbs using an equivalent GA-aFuc backbone to determine the matching doses that result in comparable levels of GITR binding and Treg depletion. The weakened agonistic mAbs demonstrated comparable dose-dependent Treg reduction in the TME to the agonist mAbs, at twice the dose. (Extended Data Fig. 5d). Furthermore, both the agonist and the weakened agonist increased DC activation but not frequencies in the TME (Fig. 7c,d). These observations indicate that Treg depletion and DC activation are Fc dependent rather than reliant on GITR agonism. The variants were then compared at their respective doses in controlling tumor growth. Intriguingly, the weakened agonistic variant successfully suppressed tumor growth to a greater extent than the agonistic variant (Fig. 7e), suggesting that the therapeutic activity of the Fc-optimized anti-GITR mAbs in situ is independent of GITR agonism. Lastly, we generated an N297A Fc-dysfunctional variant of the weakened agonistic mAbs. In contrast to the mild but significant antitumor effect of the Fc-dysfunctional agonistic variant, the Fc-dysfunctional weakened agonist variant completely lacked such an effect (Extended Data Fig. 5e). Thus, the weak agonism identified in vitro was not directly translated into low in vivo efficacy, where the Fc–FcγR-mediated activities have a greater role.

Discussion

Human IgG scaffolds of anti-GITR mAbs are less effective in clinical evaluation than mouse IgG scaffolds in preclinical studies. Here, we propose that these differences can be explained by the mode of action in mouse versus human Fc–FcγR settings. We identified a key Fc-dependent effector mechanism engaged by the surrogate mIgG2a GITR mAbs but compromised by the clinical-level human IgG scaffold, including the benchmark IgG1 subclass9. These cross-species differences underscore the advantages of using Fc–FcγR systems from the same species to mechanistically model IgG activity and hFcγR-expressing mice to characterize and optimize immunotherapies before application in human populations. In this study, we sought to optimize the Fc domain of human anti-GITR mAbs to better recapitulate the in vivo mechanisms mediated by their mouse surrogates, thereby improving therapeutic effects. We created a potent human anti-GITR mAb, based on an Fc-optimized G236A-aFuc IgG1 scaffold with enhanced binding to both activating hFcγRIIa and hFcγRIIIa. Our data support the development of anti-GITR mAbs with enhanced Fc effector function as an approach to overcome some of the limited clinical efficacy observed in early-phase clinical trials. It is added to a growing list of next-generation Fc-optimized checkpoint mAbs, which have demonstrated improved therapeutic potential through distinct FcγR mechanisms, including anti-PDL1 (ref. 12), anti-CTLA4 (refs. 13,46), anti-CCR8 (ref. 44), anti-CD47 (ref. 10) and anti-CD40 (refs. 11,39) mAbs.

We identified a mechanism of Fc-mediated Treg elimination that enhances the antitumor activity, previously attributed to activating FcγRs19. We also discovered a new FcγR-dependent mechanism involving cDCs that is essential for eliciting the in vivo response. cDCs are involved in priming and regulating antitumor immunity51, with their abundance and activation state in the TME associated with tumor growth control and positive prognosis. Their role during anti-PD1 mAb treatment and other T cell-targeting immunotherapies is gaining recognition52,53. Building on this knowledge, we recently developed a bispecific antibody that directly engages the PD1+ T cell–DC crosstalk, exhibiting potent antitumor immunity, superior to the traditional PD1 blockade54. Our study extends these efforts to GITR-targeted immunotherapy. We demonstrate that active Fc incorporation (mIgG2a or GA-aFuc hIgG1) into anti-GITR mAbs directly enables bridging between CD4+ T cells and DCs, thereby further potentiating antitumor immunity in preclinical settings. Both cDC1 and cDC2 subsets contributed to the antitumor mechanism of Fc-optimized anti-GITR mAbs. The direct engagement of CD4+ T cell–cDC2 doublets by the mAbs activates NF-κB signaling in cDC2 and facilitates CD4+ T cell activation, in turn licensing cDC1 cells, which have an indispensable role in the overall therapeutic efficacy, as supported by the genetic cDC1 depletion model. Activated cDC1 cells are then set to prime CD8+ T cells, creating a positive cDC2/CD4–cDC1/CD8 feedback loop55. Furthermore, mregDCs in the TME, composed of mature cDC1 and cDC2, activate Fc-mediated pathways, including phagocytosis and antigen processing and presentation by MHC I and MHC II to CD8+ and CD4+ T cells.

We discovered an additional unique mechanism underlying the in vivo activity of anti-GITR mAbs, involving effector CD4+ T cell–DC crosstalk. Intratumoral CD4+ helper T cells have been reported to prime DC-mediated CD8+ T cell differentiation, which facilitates their response to immunotherapy40,48. Accordingly, we observed CD4+ helper T cell-dependent DC activation and elevated CD8+ T cells in the TME following Fc-optimized anti-GITR mAb administration. Surprisingly, we detected a CD4+ T cell subset with cytotoxic activity against cancer cells in mice treated with the Fc-optimized mAbs, presumably further enhancing the antitumor immune response. While antitumor cytotoxic activity is primarily attributed to the CD8 compartment of T cells, the CD4 compartment is increasingly recognized49. In conclusion, functional CD4+ T cells, acting as both helper and cytotoxic cells, are critical for suppressing tumor growth by the Fc-optimized anti-GITR mAbs.

GITR-targeted immunotherapy has not yet realized its full potential, as shown in preclinical studies. Our study addressed this critical gap by deciphering the contribution of FcγR pathways to antitumor activity using mouse and human GITR IgGs under matching Fc–FcγR settings. We provide a rationale for prioritizing Fc-optimized, rather than target-optimized, anti-GITR mAbs for clinical evaluation.

Methods

Mice

The ethically compliant animal studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Weizmann Institute of Science (license nos. 14040519-2, 05440925-2, 01250123-2, 07911021-3, 01390125-3 and 04170522-2).

hFcγR mice (mouse FcγRa−/−, human FCGR1A+, FCGR2AR131+, FCGR2B+, FCGR3AF158+ and FCGR3B+) and FcγR-KO mice (mFcRα−/−, deleted for Fcgr2b, Fcgr3, Fcgr4 and Fcgr1) were kindly provided by J. Ravetch. hFcγR-R1−/− mice were generated by backcrossing of the hFcγR strain22. C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Envigo, C3-KO mice from Jackson (strain 029661). XCR1-iDTR mice were generated by crossing Rosa-lox-stop-lox-iDTR56 (kindly provided by S. Jung) and XCR1-Cre57 (kindly provided by B. Malissen) mice. For the generation of ZBTB46-DTR bone marrow (BM) chimeras, C57BL/6 mice were reconstituted for 8 weeks after lethal irradiation (one irradiation at 950 rad) and intravenously transferred with 5 × 106 BM cells from ZBTB46-DTR mice (Jackson Laboratory, 019506). ZBTB46-DTR hemizygous mice were BM donors. The animals were bred and housed as previously described39,54. For all in vivo experiments, animals of both sexes aged 8–10 weeks were randomized and assigned to experimental groups.

Antibody generation and characterization

The anti-mGITR mAb sequence, clone DTA-1, was kindly provided by H. Alvarez-Jares (AbbVie). The variable heavy-region and light-region sequences of the parental mAb were synthesized and inserted into mammalian expression vectors with various Fc backbones (mono-human IgG1, human kappa, mIgG2a, mouse lambda or bispecific). The weakened agonistic anti-GITR bispecific construct (GITR/IC bsAb) uses the RSV-targeting Synagis mAb sequence in place of one of the GITR-binding arms. This bsAb is produced as previously described39,54. For correct heavy–light chain pairing, the RSV arm was expressed in the CrossMab format (CH1–CL swapping), while the wild-type domain was maintained for the GITR-targeting arm58. For heavy-chain heterodimerization, amino acid substitutions were introduced in the CH3 domain: Y349C;T366S;L368A;Y407V in the RSV arm and S354C;T366W in the GITR-targeting arm59. For the generation of Fc domain variants of hIgG1 (N297A, G236A or G237D;P238D;H268D;P271G;A330R), site-directed mutagenesis was performed by PCR (Agilent Technologies) using specific primers according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Mutant plasmid sequences were validated by direct DNA sequencing (Life Science Core Facility, Weizmann Institute of Science). The open reading frame sequences of 3G8 (anti-RIII) and IV.3 (anti-RIIA) heavy and light chains were kindly provided by J. Ravetch. Antibody production and purification were performed as previously described39,54. For nonfucosylated Fc glycan, 2-deoxy-2-fluoro-L-fucose (200 µM, Carbosynth) was added to the transfection medium.

Mass spectrometry

Samples were trypsin-digested (S-trap method), followed by hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography enrichment of glycopeptides. The resulting peptides were analyzed using nanoflow liquid chromatography (nanoAcquity) coupled to high-resolution, high-mass-accuracy mass spectrometry (Fusion Lumos). The data were searched using Byonic against the hIgG1 glycosylated peptide and Fc glycan databases. The identifiers were verified manually and quantified using Skyline (version 19.1.0.193). The experiment was performed by the Nancy and Stephen Grand Israel National Center for Personalized Medicine, Weizmann Institute of Science.

Binding ELISA

Binding specificity and affinity of monospecific and bispecific antibodies were determined by ELISA using recombinant mGITR (Sino Biological), along with hFcγRIIA, hFcγRIIB and hFcγRIIIA (Sino Biological). ELISA plates (Greiner Bio-One, 675061) were coated overnight at 4 °C with recombinant extracellular domain of mGITR (1 μg ml−1), mFcγRs (1 µg ml−1) or hFcγRs (2 µg ml−1). All subsequent steps were performed at room temperature. After washing, the plates were blocked for 1 h with 1× PBS with BSA (2% for GITR and 10% for FcγRs) and then incubated with serially diluted IgGs (1 h for mFcγRs and 2 h for hFcγRs) in blocking solution. After washing, plates were incubated for 1 h with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-hIgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch) or HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (Thermo Fisher). Detection and quantification were performed as previously described39,54.

Pharmacodynamics assay

MC38 tumor-bearing hFcγR mice were injected with anti-mGITR hIgG1 Fc variants. Then, 24 h after treatment, tumors were collected, processed and prepared for flow cytometry as described below (tissue processing). Surface binding of mAbs was assessed using AffiniPure F(ab′)2 fragment goat anti-hIgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch). Surface and intracellular binding were assessed using labeled mAbs. Before injection, Fc variant mAbs were labeled with the SAIVI AlexaFluor-647 antibody labeling kit (Thermo Fisher) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Tissue processing

Spleens were dissected through a 70-µm nylon cell strainer, erythrocytes were lysed by a 5-min incubation with red blood cell lysis buffer (Zymo Research), followed by a PBS wash. Lymph nodes were dissected through a 70-µm nylon cell strainer, followed by a PBS wash. Tumors were processed as previously described39,54. The tumor cells were stained for flow cytometry and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) for scRNA-seq, as described below.

Antibody quantification in serum

MC38 tumor-bearing hFcγR and C57BL/6 mice were injected with anti-mGITR hIgG1 Fc variants (200 μg per mouse), anti-GITR mIgG2a or anti-GITR mIgG2a-N297A (100 μg per mouse). The treated mice were bled at the indicated time points after injection. Serum was stored at −80 °C until analysis. Antibody concentration in serum was determined using a standard colorimetric ELISA assay. Briefly, assay plates were coated with recombinant mGITR (1 µg ml−1; Sino Biological) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Plates were then blocked for 2 h with 1× PBS with 10% fetal calf serum. Serial dilutions were performed on the plates and incubated for 2 h. After washing, plates were incubated for 1 h with HRP-conjugated anti-hIgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch) or HRP-conjugated anti-mIgG (Thermo Fisher). Absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a SpectraMax Plus spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices) and the background absorbance from negative control samples was subtracted.

Tumor challenge and treatment

Tumor cell lines were maintained as previously described39,54. MC38 (2 × 106 cells), B16F10 (2.5 × 105 cells, 1:1 with Matrigel (Corning)) and MCA-205 (5 × 105 cells) cells were implanted and tumor growth was monitored as previously described39,54. When the sum of tumor length and width reached approximately 50 mm3 (5–10 days after tumor inoculation) or 115 mm3 (10–14 days after tumor inoculation), mice were randomized by tumor size (day 0) and treated by intraperitoneal injection of mAbs or PBS. Mice were treated three times every 3 days with anti-mGITR hIgG1 Fc variants and mIgG2a variants (DTA-1) (100 µg per mouse), anti-mCCR8 mIgG2a (SA214G2; 250 µg per mouse), anti-mCD25 mIgG2a (PC-61 and 7D4; 200 µg per mouse), anti-mCTLA4 (9H10, BioXcell; 200 µg per mouse) or anti-GITR/IC hIgG1 bsAb Fc variants (100, 200 or 400 µg per mouse). Mice were administered a 100-µg intratumoral injection of IV.3-blocking and 3G8-blocking mAbs on the same day as anti-GITR mAb administration. Starting the day before anti-GITR mAb administration, XCR1-iDTR and ZBTB46-DTR BM chimeras were given diphtheria toxin (20 ng per g body weight) every other day, with a total of four administrations for XCR1-iDTR and five administrations for ZBTB46-DTR BM chimeras. Mice were monitored for 8–77 days after treatment initiation or until they were killed following the Weizmann Institute of Science IACUC limitations for tumor size (1,500 mm3). The maximal tumor size or burden of 1,500 mm3 was not exceeded. Tumor-free mice were defined as mice with complete response and rejection of the primary tumor without reoccurrence for 62–64 days after treatment onset. For the rechallenge experiments, tumor-free mice were reinoculated with MC38 tumor cells (2 × 106) into the contrary flank.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions were prepared and stained for surface antigens as previously described39,54. For intracellular FoxP3 and granzyme B staining, an additional staining step was performed using the True-Nuclear transcription factor buffer set kit (BioLegend) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All samples were analyzed on CytoFLEX LX (Beckman Coulter). Unless otherwise specified, cell populations were defined by live CD45+ and the following markers (BioLegend): DCs: CD11c+, MHC II+, F4/80−, CD19−, CD3−; macrophages: CD11b+, MHC II+, F4/80+, NK1.1−; NK cells: NK1.1+; cDC1s: MHC II+, CD11c+, XCR1+, CD19−, CD64−, F4/80−, SIRPα−; cDC2s: MHC II+, CD11c+, SIRPα+, CD19−, CD64−, F4/80−, XCR1−; CD4+ Teff cells: CD3+, CD11b−, CD4+, CD8a−, Foxp3−; CD8+ T cells: CD3+, CD11b−, CD8a+, CD4−; Tregs: CD3+, CD11b−, CD4+, CD8a−, Foxp3+. For CD4+ T cell–cDC2 doublet analysis, MHC II+, CD3+ CD4+, CD8a−, CD11c+, F4/80−, SIRPα+ and XCR1− were used as markers, along with the higher area versus height ratio of FSC. For CD4+ T cell–macrophage doublet analysis, MHC II+, CD3+ CD4+, CD8a−, CD11c+ and F4/80+ were used as markers, along with the higher area versus height ratio of FSC. To validate cell depletion, an alternative marker or nonoverlapping epitopes were used: NKp46, CD8b (YTS156.7.7) and CD4 (RM4-4). For absolute cell count, CountBright absolute counting beads (Invitrogen) were used and calculated according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For FACS validation of mGITR expression, the following antibodies were used: mGITR (clone DTA-1)–FITC (BioLegend, 126308) and isotype control rat IgG2b (RTK4530)–FITC (BioLegend, 400633).

Flow cytometry sorting and scRNA-seq

Each sample was incubated with a different hashtag TotalSeq-B antibody (B0301–B0310) and anti-CD45.2–PE. Immune cells (CD45+) were sorted using the SH800 SONY cell sorter and resuspended in PBS + 0.04% BSA. Immune cells (40,000) were sorted for each mouse and pooled following the manufacturer’s instructions. A single-cell suspension (30,000) was loaded onto Next GEM Chip G (15,000 cells) and then run on a Chromium Controller instrument to generate GEM emulsion (10x Genomics). The 3′ scRNA-seq libraries and cell surface protein libraries were generated according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Chromium single-cell 3′ reagent kits user guide (version 3.1 Chemistry Dual Index) with feature barcoding technology for cell surface protein). Final libraries were quantified using NEBNext library quant kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs) and high-sensitivity D5000/D1000 TapeStation (Agilent). Libraries were pooled according to targeted cell number, aiming for ~25,000 reads per cell for Gene Expression libraries and ~5,000 reads per cell for cell surface protein libraries. Pooled libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 instrument using an S1 100-cycle reagent kit (Illumina).

Imaging flow cytometry

Cells were stained and analyzed by imaging flow cytometry as previously described54. For localization experiments, the following channels were used: (brightfield), 2 (XCR1) 3 (FoxP3), 4 (CD3), 5 (F4/80), 6 (CD11c), 7 (Vio Blue live/dead), 8 (CD172a), 9 (brightfield of second camera), 10 (CD8a), 11 (SAIVI AlexaFluor-647 labeled antibody) and 12 (CD4). Live-cell doublets were gated according to the area/aspect ratio (ratio between the minor axis and the major axis of a best-fit ellipse for the nuclear object) of the brightfield image. Cell populations were defined using the following markers (BioLegend): DCs: F4/80−, MHC II+, CD11c+; T cells: CD3+; Treg: CD3+, CD4+, CD8−, FoxP3+; CD4 T cells: CD3+, CD4+, CD8−, FoxP3−; CD8 T cells: CD3+, CD4−, CD8+. GITR mAb Fc variants were labeled before injection with the SAIVI AlexaFluor-647 antibody labeling kit (Thermo Fisher). Relevant doublets were gated as CD3+ and CD11c+ events. Cells were further selected on the basis of the CD11c-stained region size (Area_M06) versus the distance between the staining center of CD3 and CD11c (Delta Centroid X Y_M04_CD3_M06_CD11c). An additional selection was made for cells with the appropriate area/aspect ratio for both CD3 and CD11c. For GITR mAb localization experiments, antibody staining-positive cells were selected for analysis. To determine the intensity of the antibody staining (Ch11) in each population, the interface region was subtracted from the dilated CD3 and CD11c masks. The antibody mean intensity was calculated for CD3 or CD11c masks within doublets.

scRNA-seq data analysis

Processing FASTQ reads into gene expression matrices

Raw sequencing data were processed using the Cell Ranger toolkit (version 9.0) by 10x Genomics. FASTQ files were generated by bcl2fastq (version 2.20.0.422) with default arguments and further mapped to a combined reference genome containing GRCh38 (human) and GRCm39 (mouse) assemblies to construct count matrices. To create a custom transcriptome for our humanized mouse, human FCGR genes (FCGR1A, FCGR2A, FCGR2B, FCGR3A and FCGR3B) were added from the human transcriptome (GRCh38).

Quality control

Raw expression matrices were loaded into R (version 4.1.1) and analyzed with the Seurat R package (version 4.3.1). The data contained 19,210 cells from 12 mice. Quality control was performed to exclude poor-quality cells from further analysis. Filtering thresholds were as follows: genes per cell < 7,500, reads per cell (unique molecular identifier count) < 40,000 and mitochondrial genes < 5%.

Dimensionality reduction, graph clustering and uniform manifold approximation and projection visualization

The raw RNA expression matrix was normalized and scaled using the Seurat function ‘SCTransform’, including the percentage of mitochondrial gene expression as a regression factor. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the normalized SCTransform data (Seurat RunPCA function). For k-nearest neighbor graph-based clustering, we used Seurat functions FindNeighbors and FindClusters. Two-dimensional representations were generated using the top 30 PCA vectors as input to the RunUMAP function. Top differentially expressed genes were found for each cluster using the findAllMarkers function (comparing a given cluster to all other clusters). Clusters were labeled on the basis of the expression of the top differential gene in each cluster, along with canonical immune cell markers.

The MSigDBM8 gene sets were identified that contain cluster marker genes for cell types identified in single-cell sequencing studies of mouse tissue. We also used databases from The Immunological Genome Project (ImmGen; https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/mouse/collections.jsp)60, Data Browsers (https://www.immgen.org/Databrowser19/DatabrowserPage.html), Gene Skyline (http://rstats.immgen.org/Skyline/skyline.html) and My Geneset (http://rstats.immgen.org/MyGeneSet_New/index.html).

Cell–cell communication analysis

To investigate the downstream effect of GITR Fc-enhanced treatment on the interaction between Tregs and myeloid cells, we used MultiNicheNet to identify putative ligands and receptors mediating the interaction. To this end, we defined Tregs as sender cells and DCs, macrophages and monocytes as receiver cells. Potential ligands were extracted if they were expressed in at least 5% of the sender cells within their respective clusters (P < 0.05). We prioritized the predicted ligand–receptor interactions with the default prioritization weights and visualized the top 50 interactions across the two treatment conditions using circus plots (Fig. 5). Cell–cell communication analysis was performed using R package ‘Multinichenetr’, with the default prioritization weights. Multinichenetr package functions use scRNA-seq expression data as input and return predicted ligand–receptor interactions as output. The graph visualizes an overview of the top 50 interactions using condition-specific circos plots. The package with accompanying documentation is available from GitHub (https://github.com/saeyslab/multinichenetr). The CD4 cytotoxicity signature (UCell) was analyzed using the R package for scoring gene signatures in scRNA-seq datasets. The package with accompanying documentation is available from GitHub (https://github.com/carmonalab/UCell)61.

Differential gene expression analysis

Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 by comparing GA-aFuc versus N297A within each DC subset (cDC1, cDC2 and mregDC). DESeq2 results included the log2 fold change (log2FC), Wald test statistics, raw P values and Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted P values for multiple-testing correction.

Gene selection and pathway enrichment analysis

For pathway enrichment analysis, gene lists were generated from the DESeq2 results by selecting genes upregulated in GA-aFuc (log2FC > 0). Specifically, the top 300 genes ranked by the DESeq2 Wald statistic were used as input for Metascape. The Wald statistic, calculated as the log2FC divided by its standard error, reflects the strength of evidence for differential expression. Enrichment significance for each pathway was reported as −log10 P values and used to rank enriched pathways within each DC subset. Analyses involving Metascape were performed as previously described62.

Data visualization

Heat maps were generated to display the DESeq2 log2FC (GA-aFuc versus N297A) values for representative genes grouped by pathway. Pathway enrichment significance derived from Metascape62 (−log10 P values) was displayed as adjacent annotation blocks. Gene–gene scatter plots were generated to compare average log2-normalized gene expression between GA-aFuc and N297A conditions, with differentially expressed genes highlighted on the basis of DESeq2 criteria (P < 0.05 and |log2FC | ≥ 0.5).

Cell depletion studies

For the rechallenge experiment, CD4+ (250 µg per mouse, GK1.5; BioXCell) and/or CD8+ (250 µg per mouse, 2.43; BioXCell) T cell depletion was performed on days −2, 3 and 7 of the second tumor inoculation round. For the primary response, T cell depletion was performed 1 day before every anti-GITR mAb treatment and 2 days after the last treatment (days −1, 2, 5 and 8). NK cell depletion with anti-NK1.1 (250 µg/mouse, BioXCell) was performed 1 and 2 days before and after the first treatment with anti-mGITR hIgG1 Fc variants (days −2, −1, 1 and 2). Antibodies were administered by intraperitoneal injection.

Cancer cell killing assay

MC38 tumor-bearing hFcγR mice were treated with the indicated mAbs and dLNs were collected 4 days after treatment. CD8+ (EasySep mCD8+ T cell isolation kit), CD4+CD25− and CD4+CD25+ (EasySep mCD4+CD25+ Treg isolation kit II) or CD4+ (EasySep mCD4+ T cell isolation kit) cells were isolated according to the manufacturer’s instructions and incubated with cancer cells (MC38, B16F10, AT3 and MCA-205) at a ratio of 2:1 in favor of the immune cells. For MHC II blockade, cells were incubated with anti-mMHC II (20 µg ml−1 Y-3P; BioXCell). Cells were cultured in RPMI medium containing 25 mM HEPES, 1% L-glutamine, 10% FBS (heat-inactivated), 1% penicillin–streptomycin, 1% nonessential amino acids, 1% pyruvate and 0.05 mM β-2-mercaptoethanol (Sigma). They were incubated for 24 h in U-shaped 96-well plates (Thermo Fisher) or HTS Transwell 96-well plates (1 µm; Corning) in a humidified incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Single-cell suspensions were collected for FACS. Cancer cell killing was calculated as the percentage of dead CD45− cells divided by total CD45− tumor cells or relative to control.

Generation of a stable mouse GITR agonism reporter assay

HEK-Dual NF-κB SEAP reporter cells (Invivogen) at 70% confluence were transfected in six-well cell culture plates (Corning) with pcDNA 3.1-mGITR expression vector (3 μg) with a geneticin selectable marker (Genescript) or empty vector (pcDNA 3.1) using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s procedure. Stable cell lines were established after geneticin selection (Invivogen, 1,000 μg ml−1) for 14 days and target expression was evaluated by FACS. Transfected cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 25 mM glucose, 6 mM L-glutamine, 1,000 μg ml−1 geneticin, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, 100 µg ml−1 Normocin, 10 µg ml−1 Blasticidin and 100 µg ml−1 Zeocin and cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Stable transduction of human FcγR-expressing HEK293T cells

To generate third-generation lentiviral particles, HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, sodium pyruvate, L-glutamine and nonessential amino acids. For viral production, cells were seeded on 10-cm plates precoated with 0.01% poly(L-lysine) and transfected using Expifectamine with packaging plasmids (PLP1, PLP2 and PLP-VSVG) and expression plasmids (pLenti, Genescript) encoding genes of interest and puromycin resistance. The following hFcγRs were used: FCGR2A (R131 variant; adapted from NM_001136219.1, GFP-fused), FCGR2B (T232I variant; adapted from NM_001002274.1, mPlum-fused) and FCGR3A (F158 variant; adapted from NM_001127593.1, GFP-fused). Viral supernatants were collected, clarified by centrifugation, filtered through 0.22-µm filters and concentrated using PEG8000 (BioPrep) precipitation. For transduction, newly plated HEK293T cells were exposed to the viral supernatant and, after incubation, they were selected with 1.56 µg ml−1 puromycin to enrich for stably transduced populations. Successful transduction was confirmed by fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry using fluorescent reporters (GFP or mPlum).

Mouse GITR agonism activity

Mouse GITR HEK NF-κB reporter cells were seeded with or without in-house generated hFcγR HEK293T cells in a ratio of 1:1 (0.6 × 105 cells per well) and incubated with anti-GITR hIgG1 Fc variants as indicated. Agonistic activity was assessed according to the manufacturer’s procedure (Invivogen).

Statistics and reproducibility

Flow cytometry data analysis was performed using FlowJo (version 10.6.2). All other data analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software). Quantitative data are presented as the means ± s.e.m. unless otherwise indicated. When geometric mean intensity is presented, the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) is shown. When the fluorescence intensity value of the antibody is deduced from an isotype control, the ΔMFI is presented. No data were excluded from the study, except for mice in tumor model experiments that were killed because of necrotic tumors before the treatment regime was completed. For flow cytometry experiments, populations of <30 cells were excluded from analysis. Mouse numbers were taken into consideration for in vivo studies to ensure that biological effects would be detected and to enable comparisons between groups. This was also determined on the basis of the results of preliminary experiments. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes but our sample sizes are similar to those reported in previous publications12,39,54. Group allocation was randomized for all in vivo experiments on the basis of tumor size, sex and age. Tumor measurements were performed blindly; in all other in vivo and in vitro studies, the researchers were not blinded to the treatment groups when performing the experiments. For each dataset, the normality of the population and/or population residuals (Gaussian distribution) was confirmed using Shapiro–Wilk and/or D’Agostino–Pearson tests. For normal distributions, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test was used to compare all groups with three or more treatments. When two groups were compared, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test (two-tailed, unequal variance) was used to determine statistical significance. When data were not normally distributed, a nonparametric test was used; a Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons or a Mann–Whitney test was used when two groups were compared. For survival curves, a log-rank test with a Bonferroni-corrected threshold was performed. P < 0.05 was considered significant in all statistical tests and exact P values are indicated on the graphs and in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.
