Reply
Authors: Jennifer Y Zhou, Dion Stub
Published: 2025

We thank Dr Pyrpyris and colleagues for their thoughtful remarks on our study of urgent vs elective transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR).1 They raise several important considerations for interpreting our findings and for clarifying the role of urgent TAVR in the care of patients with aortic stenosis.

First, we agree that more detailed characterization of the indications for urgent TAVR, including cardiogenic shock status, would provide valuable context. Unfortunately, such information was not available in our data set. Our findings, however, do underscore the importance of routinely documenting urgency status and the indication for urgent procedures in structural heart registries. Given that urgent TAVR accounted for nearly 1 in 10 cases in our cohort and was associated with poor outcomes, systematic collection of these data would improve understanding, enable benchmarking across centers, and inform future research in this high-risk yet common scenario.

Second, the higher incidence of acute kidney injury in the urgent cohort is an important observation and consistent with prior reports.2 In our study, this excess risk was observed irrespective of pre-existing chronic kidney disease, suggesting that the hemodynamic consequences of acute valve syndrome may confer additional susceptibility to renal injury. Strategies to mitigate this complication are critical. We read with interest the authors’ suggestion regarding the potential role of sodium glucose co-transporter-2 inhibitors and agree that evaluation of renoprotective therapies in both elective and urgent settings is an important avenue for future research.

Finally, the role of urgent TAVR relative to balloon aortic valvuloplasty (BAV) remains debated. In our cohort, procedural success and most short-term outcomes were similar between urgent and elective TAVR, supporting its feasibility when required. Poorer long-term outcomes likely reflect the advanced disease and extravalvular cardiac damage characteristic of this population, rather than procedural factors. While TAVR may carry a higher upfront risk than BAV,3 it avoids the “2 hits” of sequential procedures, as most patients undergoing BAV ultimately require definitive valve replacement. Indeed, 14% of our urgent TAVR cohort had previously undergone BAV, underscoring its limitations. Prospective studies comparing urgent TAVR and BAV will be important to determine the optimal strategy for this high-risk group.

Ultimately, our study highlights the burden and outcomes of urgent TAVR in a contemporary multicenter registry. As Dr Pyrpyris and colleagues note, several questions remain. Future studies should aim to define its role more clearly, optimize care in this vulnerable population, and explore whether earlier detection and treatment of aortic stenosis could reduce the need for urgent TAVR altogether.
