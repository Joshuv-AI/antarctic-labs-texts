The melorheostosis with severe knee and ankle deformity treated by total knee arthroplasty with V-Y quadricepsplasty: a case report and literature review
Authors: Zhao-Yu Chen, Hao-Tian Wu, Dan Xing, Qiang Liu, Yan Ke, Jian-Hao Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

The pathological bone hyperostosis and soft tissue fibrosis frequently resulted in intolerable pain and functional loss. Background

Melorheostosis, also known as Leri’s disease, is an extremely rare disease with pathological bone hyperostosis and soft tissue fibrosis [1]. The classical radiological performance appears as a sign of skeletal hyperostosis like dripping wax flowing down from candle, thus it is also called the candle disease of bone [2, 3]. Since the earliest introduction by Leri in 1922, several cases of melorheostosis have been reported [4]. However, evidence is still limited about the management for the disease, due to its low incidence of 0.9 per million [5].

Melorheostosis is a congenital disorder with unclear etiopathogenesis. Diagnosis for melorheostosis is made mainly based on clinical features and radiological performances [1, 6]. There are years of asymptomatic period before variable clinical manifestations [7]. The progression of disease accelerates among children and young adults [8]. Patients tend to seek for medical help because of intolerable pain or functional loss with extreme joint stiffness [7, 9]. In radiological examination, patients usually perform a classical dripping wax sign on X-ray films, while other findings are also possible in melorheostosis, such as “osteoma-like”, “osteopathia striata-like” and “myositis ossificans-like” hyperostosis [6].

Current management for the disease includes pharmacological therapy, physical therapy and surgery [10]. The conservative therapy are helpful to restore the musculoskeletal functions as well as relieving the pain [11, 12]. When the patients suffer from severe deformity with poor range of motion (ROM), conservative treatment tends to fail and the surgery is highly recommended [13]. However, there is no published consensus on surgical management for melorheostosis since the stiffness is reported post TKA in several cases, and more evidence is required for surgeons to make treatment decisions for patients with ≥ 2 joints affected [5, 12, 14].

There are two articles introducing knee arthroplasty for melorheostosis [14, 15]. However, both of them performed total knee arthroplasty (TKA) with common parapatellar approach without any discussion on treatment strategies for patients with severe stiffness in multiple joints. As for these patients with bone hyperostosis, TKA by parapatellar approach may not be sufficient for intraoperative exposure, and joint stiffness frequently occurs postoperatively [16]. Under such condition, an uncommon V-Y quadricepsplasty may be appropriate [17]. The approach was initially introduced by Coonse and Adams with an aim to obtain the exposure and lengthen the quadriceps tendon for patients with severe knee stiffness [18]. In this approach, a second incision is made from the proximal ending of the parapatellar incision, extending laterally and distally through the quadriceps [19]. The converted V-shape incision not only provided great exposure, but also allowed extending of quadriceps by directly suturing the proximal margins of the medial and lateral incisions. Thus, V-Y quadricepsplasty effectively reduces the risk of joint stiffness for patients with severe knee deformity [17, 19, 20]. While the V-Y quadricepsplasty increases the risk of extension lag post surgery, the rehabilitation played an important role post V-Y quadricepsplasty [20]. In this report, we will: [1] introduce a typical case of melorheostosis affecting multiple joints including knee and ankle; [2] share the first experience of V-Y quadricepsplasty in TKA for melorheostosis when parapatellar approach fails in surgical exposure; [3] have the first discussion on the utility of V-Y quadricepsplasty and rehabilitation for patients with severe ROM loss in multiple joints.

Case presentation

Initial symptoms

A female patient initially found diffuse lesions on her left calf skin at the age of 20 (Table 1). The lesions stared as small patches on left calf, but soon spread to her left knee and ankle skin, without significant pain or loss of joint function. At the age of 30, she got her left knee and ankle painful and swollen after she twisted her ankle by accident. From then on, the ROM of the joints continued decreasing rapidly. At the age of 34, she complained about the intolerable pain and abnormally elevated temperature on left knee and ankle. The pain got worse when squatting down or standing for a long time and oral pain medications failed to control.

Diagnosis and previous surgeries

At the age of 36, she received physical and radiological examination in local hospital. At that moment, the ROM of her left knee was restricted to a flexion of 20°−40°, and she failed to let the heel touch the ground while walking. X-rays indicated a typical performance of bone sclerosis as dripping wax flowing down. Moreover, the disease merely involved only one limb of patient, which was consistent with characteristics of the disease [7]. Consequently, the first surgery of arthroscopy was conducted, with an aim of histopathological confirmation and symptom release. During the first surgery, the bone masses in the space of femorotibial joint in knee and tibiotalar joint in ankle were removed. The histopathological examination demonstrated the irregular dense bone without specific findings or cellular atypia. Unfortunately, her pain and restriction of ROM was not released, and the Knee Society Score (KSS) decreased from 29 for knee and 35 for function preoperatively, to 24 for knee and 25 for function postoperatively. Six years after the first surgery, she received a revised resection of masses in ankle at the age of 42, due to the poor plantarflexion ROM in ankle. But the surgery failed again, with 10°−40° plantarflexion ROM postoperatively. The contracture of knee and restricted motion of ankle resulted in the limp while walking. Since the age of 42, her pain resolved spontaneously, but the impaired motion of knee and ankle was not improved, which severely restricted her daily activities. The long-term stiffness with poor ROM contributed to high risk of contracture post arthroscopy [5]. Inadequate mass resection and soft tissue release in arthroscopy might be one of the reasons for surgical failures. Additionally, the severe pain with hot skin and swelling during the surgery suggested an active phase of disease, which also contributed to the poor surgical outcome [10].

Arthroplasty and rehabilitation

At the age of 52, the patient presented to our orthopedic department, with complaints of severe morbidity in walking. At our department, physical examination revealed severe deformity in her left knee and ankle with diffuse skin lesions, with a normal skin temperature. There was a 40° flexion contracture with fully restricted ROM in her left knee. Her left ankle was restricted with a 10°−40° plantarflexion ROM. The KSS for knee and function were 30 and 25, respectively. She could not bend her knee or let her heel touch the ground while walking. The long-term morbidity in activities resulted in muscular atrophy and strength decrease in her left limb. The circumference of left thigh (15 cm above superior patellar pole) was 5 cm less than that of right side (34 cm vs. 39 cm), and the circumference of left calf (10 cm below inferior patellar pole) was 4 cm less (26 cm vs. 30 cm). The SARC-CalF score was 12 [21]. She had normal motions in hips and in right limb.

The full-length standing X-ray, weight-bearing films, and patellofemoral skyline films were taken (Fig. 1). Radiological examination showed the dense sclerotic cortical hyperostosis involving the whole left limb from hip to ankle. There were great masses in knee and ankle and the flowing hyperostosis along femur and tibia resembled dripping wax from candle.

The case was fully discussed at the grand rounds. Since the stiffness and functional loss were mainly caused by mechanical factors of bony masses, a surgery was suggested rather than conservative therapy. Considering the severe bony ankylosis and the failure of previous mass resection, the efficacy of mass resections and soft-tissue releases was limited for this 52-year-old patient with a high demand for physical activities. Besides, published reports presented a high recurrence of contracture for patients receiving a mass resection and soft-tissue release, which suggested an arthroplasty for these patients [5, 8, 22]. The patient eventually received TKA for better improvement of ROM and functions for her left knee. However, the initial attempt of parapatellar incision approach failed to provide adequate space for surgeons since the patellofemoral joint was affected heavily with masses at both medial and lateral sides of patella (Fig. 2a-c). Consequently, an uncommon approach of V-shape incision in quadriceps was adopted. In this approach, a second incision was conducted in addition to the primary parapatellar incision, which started from the proximal end of the parapatellar incision and extended laterally and distally through the quadriceps (Fig. 2d, e). These two incisions resulted in an inverted V-shape incision in quadriceps and the complete exposure was easily obtained. After removal of the masses, the knee presented partial improvement in ROM and could be bent to a common position for TKA. The articular surfaces were heavily affected by the masses. Large quantities of bony masses were observed on the lateral femoral condyle, lateral tibial plateau and tibial eminence. As for the articular surface of patella, the masses mainly affected the edge of the articular surface. The cartilage of patellar surface was rough but not completely worn out. Besides the masses, there were degenerative changes with interval narrowing on the medial femorotibial articular surfaces. In addition to the exposure, the bony resection was difficult as well because of the severe hyperdense of the bone (Fig. 2f). We initially cut the masses to better identify the boundary between masses and articular surfaces, and then made the resections under the careful protection for soft tissues and vessels. During the surgery, the soft-tissue releases were conducted before and after bony resection. The posterior joint capsule was adequately released during the medial compartment release process. After achieving the extension space, the gap-balancing techniques provided a satisfying flexion space. A posterior stabilized prosthesis (PERSONA Posterior Stabilized, Zimmer, USA) was successfully implanted (Fig. 2g). Considering that the lateral tibial plateau was heavily affected by the osteonecrosis, numerous holes were drilled to ensure the cement grip and improve the prosthetic stability for the patient. A patellar replacement was prepared before surgery. In the surgery, the masses on the edge of patellar surface and both of the medial and lateral sides of patella were removed. The patellar tracking was evaluated well and the degeneration in patellar surface was acceptable. Considering the age and the quality of patellar surface, the patella was polished and reserved. At the end of surgery, the V-shape incision was converted into Y-shape to lengthen the quadriceps tendon by 1 cm in extensor mechanism reconstruction. Within 1 cm proximal to the incisions, the proximal margins of medial and lateral incisions were sutured directly in order to lengthen the quadriceps. Beyond 1 cm proximal to the incisions, the distal margin was sutured to the proximal margin of the medial incision, and likewise for the lateral incision (Fig. 2h). During the surgery, the left knee obtained a flexion of 100° under gravity before capsule suturing and a flexion of 80° under gravity after capsule suturing. In addition, a constrained condylar knee prosthesis was prepared for alternative in case that the posterior stabilized prosthesis failed.

After surgery, X-rays confirmed the fixed position of prosthesis and the patient initiated passive and active knee exercise during rehabilitation (Fig. 3). A hinged knee brace was adopted over the six weeks postoperatively for better soft-tissue healing. During the follow-up, the passive and active flexion and extension exercises were adopted during the early rehabilitation for better ROM and muscle strength. The flexion started from a small range < 30° and increased by 10° per day if tolerated, with a limitation of 80°, which was limited to the maximum flexion range under gravity after capsule suturing intraoperatively. At 6-week follow-up, a 20° extension lag was observed in left knee, and active exercises increased for improvement in muscle strength, especially for quadriceps. The extension lag narrowed to 10° at 3-month follow-up. Besides, the rehabilitation in ankle was also conducted. Considering that the dorsiflexion was extremely restricted preoperatively, the main purpose of exercise in ankle was to improve the function of dorsiflexion through passive and active training. At 3-month follow-up, she achieved a ROM of 15°−75° in her left knee. The posteroanterior film and lateral film three months post TKA presented a good prosthesis condition and any abnormal changes were not detected on the interface of the implant-tissue interface. After manipulation under anesthesia (MUA) at that time, the ROM in knee was improved to 5°−90° and she was able to go upstairs and ride recumbent bikes which was impossible before TKA. The ROM in ankle was greatly improved during rehabilitation exercise as well. Her heel could touch the ground while walking at 3-month follow-up. The extension lag was not observed at 1-year follow-up. At the latest 2-year follow-up, the patient obtained a remarkable decrease in the preoperative pains, and large improvements for her ROM in left knee (0°−90°) and in left ankle (plantarflexion: 0°−40°; dorsiflexion: 0°−10°). The KSS for knee and function increased to 83 and 80, respectively. The recovery of the extensor mechanism was satisfying and the muscle strength of quadriceps was competent for daily physical activities. During the follow up, there were no reports on any recurrence of soft-tissue contractures, prosthesis loosening, periprosthetic infections, periprosthetic fractures or any other severe TKA-related complications in this patient.

Discussion and conclusions

Melorheostosis is threatening patients’ quality of life with severe deformity and morbidity [7]. Unfortunately, there is limited reports on treatment of the rare disease, especially for surgical intervention [13–15]. In this case, we shared the first experience of V-Y quadricepsplasty in TKA for melorheostosis when parapatellar approach fails in surgical exposure, with the discussion on utility of V-Y quadricepsplasty and rehabilitation for patients with severe ROM loss in multiple joints.

There is no published consensus on treatment for melorheostosis. In this case, the hyperostotic masses and soft-tissue contracture contribute to the restriction of ROM. Conservative treatment remains symptomatic, which is unable to reverse these structural changes [11, 12]. The surgical options include soft-tissue release, mass resection, and arthroplasty [5, 12, 15]. Considering the risk of knee stiffness post surgery, TKA was the most effective therapy in our case [5, 8, 14, 22].

The main challenges in TKA for melorheostosis included the surgical exposure, prevention against joint stiffness and rehabilitation for functions. In published cases, a common parapatellar approach was competent for exposure in TKA [14, 15]. However, when we performed the common incision, masses at lateral side of patella hindered the patellar eversion and restricted the surgical exposure. Consequently, we performed the V-Y quadricepsplasty, with an aim to obtain the exposure and lengthen the quadriceps tendon for patients with severe knee stiffness [18]. Since the introduction of V-Y quadricepsplasty, several studies have demonstrated its effect on improvement of exposure and ROM [23–25]. Scott et al. presented seven cases of V-Y quadricepsplasty for facilitating exposure. After surgery, the patients obtained an average increase of 49° in knee flexion [24]. Aglietti et al. also reported an improvement of ROM from 30°−40° to 20°−70° in a patient treated by TKA with V-Y quadricepsplasty [23]. Although V-Y quadricepsplasty is beneficial for exposure and flexion improvement, there is a risk for loss of function in extensor mechanism because the vastus lateralis tendon is cut by lateral incision [26]. Scott at al. and Trousdale et al. both reported the possibility of extension lag in the approach [24, 27]. In regard to this concern, we recommend to lengthen the quadriceps tendon by 0.5–1.2 cm when reconstructing the extensor mechanism, which is considered as safe lengthening with low risk for severe extension lag as well as sufficient for improvement of flexion post TKA [28]. Besides, the measurement of flexion under gravity is important for assessment of the reconstruction of extensor mechanism [29]. The flexion of 90°−100° under gravity before capsule suturing and flexion of 80°−90° under gravity after capsule suturing were thought to be appropriate for achieving satisfying ROM post TKA [17, 30]. Besides the V-Y quadricepsplasty, adequate mass resections and soft-tissue releases are also beneficial to prevention of joint stiffness postoperatively. It is noted that more radiological examinations including computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging are recommended if X-ray films are not sufficient for surgical design preoperatively. Moreover, the rehabilitation is important after the reconstruction in surgery. Patients who received quadricepsplasty are supposed to take continuous flexion and extension exercises post-surgery [31]. During the early rehabilitation post TKA, the flexion range starts from a small range, increases by 10° per day if tolerated, and should be limited to a maximal range which is determined under gravity during the surgery in case of excessive tension of quadriceps [27, 29, 32]. The short-term extension lag is common after the V-Y quadricepsplasty. While after the regular rehabilitation, the muscle strength for active extension will be greatly improved without the permanent lag. Besides, a hinged knee brace is highly recommended for ambulation for six weeks post-surgery, in order to provide better condition for soft-tissue healing [31].

It was noted that there was also a restriction of ROM in her left ankle, but the total ankle arthroplasty was not as suggested as TKA, because of the risk of complications including delayed wound healing, skin necrosis and periprosthetic infection [15, 33]. In this case, the patient presented more severe deformity in knee compared with ankle. Published studies on biomechanics revealed the effects of knee contracture on the lower limb ligament [34]. Consequently, the restriction of ROM in ankle was considered to be compensation of the deformity in knee, and the ROM in ankle might be improved after TKA and rehabilitation. During the follow-up, this patient achieved great improvement on ROM in ankle post TKA, which supported our opinion that once the deformity in knee was removed, the ROM in ankle might be improved after rehabilitation [15]. The rehabilitation was important for improvement in ROM of ankle. Rehabilitation was focused on the recovery of dorsiflexion function, because patients with a long-term flexion deformity in knee tended to present a large angle of plantarflexion as compensation [15]. Moreover, the preoperative SARC-CalF assessment indicates a risk of sarcopenia in our patient. The muscular atrophy is frequently seen in those with joint stiffness, and the loss of strength is harmful to rehabilitation post-surgery [35]. Consequently, the muscle strength exercise is required for these patients as well.

Although our case shared insights into surgical and physical treatment for melorheostosis, several limitations existed. The patient presented at our department at the age of 52, which was considered to be at relatively late phase of the disease. Some of the medical records were missing after a long-term disease progression. And it remained uncertain how to effectively manage the disease at an earlier phase to improve the symptoms for patients. Additionally, the case report presented the clinical evidence for only one patient. Clinical-pathological analyses and basic science investigations of more cases will contribute to a deeper understanding of the disease’s pathogenesis and optimal treatment strategies. Future research could focus on the molecular biological characteristics of this condition, its influencing factors, and the long-term prognosis for patients.

In conclusions, the TKA with V-Y quadricepsplasty was successfully conducted in the melorheostosis patient with flexion contracture and restriction of ROM in knee. After the systematic postoperative rehabilitation exercise including the passive and active flexion and extension training for knee, and the passive and active dorsiflexion training for ankle, the patient obtained greatly improved ROM in both of knee and ankle. While further evaluation is warranted to assess the long-term success of TKA and rehabilitation outcomes, current recovery of ROM in knee and ankle has improved the quality of patient’s life. Overall, this case shares insights into the comprehensive management strategies, both surgical and non-surgical, for patients with significant involvement of both the knee and ankle joints. It also presents an unconventional approach for knee arthroplasty in cases of restricted surgical exposure. Surgeons can benefit from considering this unique approach when faced with similar unusual challenging cases in clinical practice.
