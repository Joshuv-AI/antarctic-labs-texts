Delayed cardiogenic shock following spontaneous coronary artery dissection in a young, healthy female: a case report
Authors: Leili Abdan, Maria Saadatian, Homina Saffar, Zahra Abdan, Hamidreza Hekmat, Negar Omidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD) is a rare, non-atherosclerotic cause of acute coronary syndrome that predominantly affects young, healthy women without conventional cardiovascular risk factors. Reporting such cases is essential to raise awareness of their variable presentation and diagnostic challenges, particularly when initial findings are unremarkable.Case presentationWe discuss a case of SCAD in a 37-year-old Iranian woman with no prior medical history or cardiovascular risk factors. The patient was initially presented with non-specific symptoms and a normal ECG. Her condition rapidly progressed to cardiogenic shock, complicated by ventricular arrhythmias, necessitating intensive medical management and hemodynamic support. Coronary angiography confirmed SCAD. Following multidisciplinary care, her cardiac function gradually improved, and the patient was discharged with close follow-up.ConclusionsThis case highlights the necessity for clinicians to consider SCAD in young female patients presenting with chest pain, even in the absence of classic risk factors or abnormal initial investigations. Early recognition and a multidisciplinary approach are crucial for optimizing outcomes in this rare yet potentially life-threatening condition.

Introduction

Spontaneous Coronary Artery Dissection (SCAD) is a rupture in the epicardial coronary artery wall and the most prevalent reason for acute coronary syndrome in young females without conventional cardiovascular risk factors [1]. SCAD is a rare cause of acute coronary syndrome and sudden cardiac death [2, 3]. The etiology of SCAD is not well known, and one hypothesis suggests that it may be multifactorial [4]. Genetics, hormonal changes, pregnancy, and arteriopathy are all potential risk factors. Physical activity and emotional stress are known to be disease triggers [5–7]. The most prevalent presentation of SCAD is ST-elevation myocardial infarction; however, the in-hospital mortality rate is low regardless of primary medical treatment [8].

The complications of SCAD include ventricular arrhythmias, congestive heart failure and cardiogenic shock [1]. Percutaneous coronary artery intervention (PCI) or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) are not the first-line treatment in a stable patient, and PCI could be accompanied with increased complications [4]. The long-term prognosis of patients who survive SCAD is generally favorable [1]. However, they remain at risk for recurrent coronary artery dissection and major adverse cardiac events. Therefore, follow-up and monitoring by a cardiologist are essential [4].

Case presentation

A 37-year-old Iranian female with no significant medical or familial history presented with retrosternal burning chest discomfort spreading to her left arm, persisting during the preceding 18 h. According to the patient’s history, the patient was admitted to another emergency department (ED) the previous night with similar chest pain but was discharged after normal troponin levels and recording an electrocardiogram (ECG). However, the pain continued to persist and intensify, prompting her to seek care at our ED.

In the ED, an initial ECG was obtained from the patient, which remained within normal limits (Fig. 1A). Physical examination, including blood pressure (BP), heart rate (HR), cardiac auscultation, peripheral pulses, and jugular venous pressure (JVP), was normal. During the interval awaiting the troponin results, the patient’s chest pain exacerbated, and she experienced pre-syncope accompanied by nausea and diaphoresis. An ECG performed at this time revealed ST-segment elevation in leads I and aVL and precordial leads and ST-segment depression in inferior leads (Fig. 1B). Consequently, she was promptly referred to the catheterization laboratory (Cath-lab), where imaging revealed a dissection flap in both the proximal left anterior descending artery (LAD) and left circumflex artery (LCX), accompanied with diminished distal TIMI flow (Fig. 2). The patient exhibited hypotension and worsening chest pain. Given the absence of distal flow and a reduction in systolic blood pressure to 80 mmHg, vasopressor therapy (norepinephrine) was initiated. The patient was transferred to the coronary care unit (CCU). A consultation with a surgeon was done, after which she was placed under close observation and medical management. Due to persistent hypotension and ongoing chest pain (cardiogenic shock), continuous vasopressor therapy and analgesic support were required. In light of the aforementioned circumstances, further consultation with a cardiothoracic surgeon was sought to deliberate on the critical decision regarding the potential necessity of coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG). Considering the patient’s young age, improved clinical status, and stabilized hemodynamics, a decision was reached to transfer her back to the Cath-lab with a team comprising a cardiothoracic surgeon and an anesthesiologist for further patient management. Fortunately, during the second angiography, a notable improvement in LAD flow was observed (Fig. 3). Accordingly, the patient was deemed a suitable candidate for medical treatment alone, and surgical intervention was no longer considered necessary. Medical therapy was continued, resulting in a rise in blood pressure within two hours, ultimately leading to hemodynamic stability and resolution of chest pain. A subsequent ECG demonstrated normalization of the previously elevated ST segments. Notably, echocardiographic assessment revealed severe left ventricular systolic dysfunction with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 25–30% and akinesia of the anterior circulation. Initial laboratory tests in the emergency room were normal. The initial troponin level was 46.9 ng/L, which continued to increase to 2780 and 40,000 ng/L. During hospitalization, the NT-proBNP level was 7233 pg/mL. The patient received treatment with a dual antiplatelet regimen, statin and beta-blocker alongside nitrate, sacubitril–valsartan, spironolactone, and furosemide. Throughout her hospital stay, the patient experienced a reduction in ejection fraction (EF) and subsequently entered a state of acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF). Nevertheless, with appropriate treatment and supportive care, her condition exhibited significant improvement. A coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) performed 10 days later indicated that LAD had mixed plaque and a small dissection with good runoff, and LCX was patent (Fig. 4). Upon discharge, an echocardiography revealed severe left ventricular systolic dysfunction with a LVEF of 30% and akinesia of the anterior circulation, along with the presence of an apical left ventricular thrombus (LV Clot) and mild pericardial effusion. The patient remained hospitalized for approximately 14 days before being discharged in overall good health. During a follow-up echocardiography conducted 40 days post-discharge, the patient’s LVEF had improved to 40%, with no evidence of LV clot or pericardial effusion.

Discussion

SCAD, originally considered a rare cause of acute coronary syndrome (ACS), has gained attention in recent years, currently accounting for approximately 20% of ACS cases in young women [2, 3]. SCAD is caused by a spontaneous non-atherosclerotic separation of any coronary artery wall, with the LAD being more commonly involved in women [3, 4]. SCAD clinical manifestations include chest pain or equivalent symptoms, STEMI changes in the ECG, and raised cardiac enzymes. A smaller number of patients may develop ventricular arrhythmias, cardiogenic shock, or sudden cardiac arrest. The “typical” SCAD patient is a young to middle-aged woman without traditional cardiovascular risk factors [3, 5, 8]. SCAD is often seen in states associated with high systemic estrogen levels such as exogenous hormone (oral contraceptives or infertility treatments and hormonal replacement therapy) and pregnancy [7, 9, 10]. Coronary angiography is the gold standard and primary imaging technique for patients with ACS. However, in cases of diagnostic uncertainty, other imaging modalities can be used. Meanwhile, CCTA has been used for initial diagnosis of SCAD as well as to assess healing [3, 6, 8]. Our patient was a young female with no known cardiovascular risk factors, presenting with symptoms consistent with ACS, ECG changes, and elevated cardiac biomarkers. Coronary angiography of the patient revealed involvement of the LAD, and CCTA was used to assess vascular flow and monitor treatment progress, which collectively indicate a typical case of SCAD.

SCAD with cardiogenic shock is a critically important clinical scenario that necessitates a multidisciplinary approach to avoid the numerous potential complications associated with this disease process. In the study by Krittanawong et al., 11% of the patients with SCAD hospitalization experienced cardiogenic shock. The patients faced a higher risk of complications, including death, the need for mechanical circulatory support, blood transfusions, ventricular tachycardia, acute kidney injury, mechanical ventilation, and respiratory failure [11, 12]. There are no clear guidelines for managing SCAD patients, particularly those accompanied by cardiogenic shock, and treatment options include conservative medical management as well as revascularization methods such as percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and CABG surgery [4, 5]. Conservative treatment is preferred in patients with SCAD, as the propensity for spontaneous healing and resolution over time in SCAD lesions is reported to be 70%–97% [11, 13]. Patients with ongoing ischemia, recurrent chest pains, left main and proximal or mid-LAD artery dissection, ventricular arrhythmias, or hemodynamic instability may benefit from revascularization [2, 13]. Revascularization in SCAD patients is challenging, especially in cases of underlying cardiogenic shock, and is associated with a high risk of complications [2, 3, 7]. In a 5-year follow-up of SCAD patients who underwent CABG surgery, bypass graft occlusion due to competitive flow was observed [14]. Lei et al. discussed the case of a 40-year-old woman with no prior medical history admitted for SCAD and cardiogenic shock. Coronary angiography revealed SCAD originating from the mid to distal LAD, while echocardiography demonstrated LVEF of 30%. The patient was successfully managed with a percutaneous left ventricular assist device and pharmacologic therapy without requiring revascularization, and the second angiography showed a healed dissection in the LAD [12]. Catellani et al. reported a case of ostial diagonal artery sub-occlusion with SCAD on the middle tract and cardiogenic shock in a 42-year-old woman with no cardiovascular risk factors who subsequently underwent stent placement. In order to manage the cardiogenic shock and hemorrhagic shock resulting from traumatic hepatic bleeding, Impella CP implantation was performed. The authors highlighted that urgent revascularization in patients with SCAD and significant clinical instability is life threatening [15]. Our patient initially received pharmacologic treatment, and due to ongoing chest pain, hemodynamic instability, and the anatomical location of the SCAD lesion, she was considered a candidate for CABG. Nevertheless, due to hemodynamic stabilization and clinical improvement, the decision was made to proceed with conservative patient management.

Medical therapy is provided to such patients, aiming to relieve symptoms and prevent immediate complications and recurrent SCAD. Patients with left ventricular dysfunction are managed according to standard heart failure guidelines and given beta-blockers (BB), angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACE) or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARB), and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists [1, 3, 5]. The use of statins and dual antiplatelet therapy in these patients is controversial. Yet in general clinical practice, they are used in the same way as in atherosclerotic ischemic heart disease [4, 6, 8]. Aspirin, clopidogrel, rosuvastatin, bisoprolol, sacubitril–valsartan, nitroglycerin, spironolactone, furosemide, and apixaban were prescribed to our patient due to a severe decrease in LVEF and the presence of thrombosis on echocardiography.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our case supports previous findings that conservative treatment may be superior to revascularization in a similar patient population. Our patient underwent close monitoring in the hospital, including admission to the CCU, vasopressor therapy, and a multidisciplinary approach. Finally, both the patient’s clinical condition and follow-up imaging demonstrated a successful recovery attributed to our non-aggressive approach. Fortunately, despite the absence of specific treatment guidelines for SCAD patients, the procedure resulted in a favorable outcome.
