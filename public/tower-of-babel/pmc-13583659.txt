Monitoring Changes in Clinical Trial Primary Outcomes Using Large Language Models
Authors: Ian Bulovic, Susmitha Wunnava, Wonjin Yoon, Adam G. Dunn, Timothy Miller, Florence T. Bourgeois
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study investigates the prevalence of meaningful changes to the outcomes in preregistered clinical trials using large language models.

Introduction

Prospective registration of clinical trials is intended to promote transparency, deter selective reporting, and ensure the quality of clinical evidence.1,2 Central to this process is prespecification of primary outcomes before participant enrollment begins.3 Changes to primary outcomes after trial initiation, whether through addition, removal, or modification, can introduce bias and undermine confidence in reported results.4 Although ClinicalTrials.gov archives all versions of trial records, monitoring significant outcome changes across versions has required extensive manual curation and has been infeasible at scale.5 Leveraging advances in large language models (LLMs) to perform classification tasks at scale, we estimated the prevalence of meaningful changes to primary outcomes for clinical trials studying Food and Drug Administration (FDA)–regulated products.

Methods

This cross-sectional study is reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline. The study did not require approval from an institutional review board or informed consent because no human participants were involved and only publicly available clinical trial records were analyzed. We analyzed interventional trials studying FDA-regulated therapeutics and devices that were prospectively registered in ClinicalTrials.gov, started from January 1, 2015, through December 31, 2024, and listed as completed as of September 25, 2025 (eMethods in Supplement 1). For each trial, we compared the last prospective registration record before enrollment began with the most recent record. Meaningful outcome changes were defined a priori as addition of a new primary outcome, removal of a prespecified outcome, or modification of the outcome time frame. To classify changes, we first evaluated multiple LLMs on an annotated set of 100 trials and selected the best-performing model, GPT-5 mini (OpenAI), for full-scale classification (eMethods in Supplement 1); this model was queried on September 26, 2025, to classify changes using structured prompts and examples. To account for classification errors, we estimated prevalence and regression coefficients using probabilistic sampling informed by the model’s measured performance on the annotated sample (eMethods in Supplement 1), incorporating uncertainty in classifier performance metrics and resulting estimation errors. Multivariable logistic regression evaluated associations between prespecified trial characteristics and outcome changes; these characteristics included start year, intervention type, industry funding, randomization, enrollment, and therapeutic area. Given that therapeutic areas were not mutually exclusive, each was modeled as a separate binary indicator, with trials not in that area serving as the reference group. This analysis also incorporated the same uncertainty adjustment. Statistical analyses were conducted in Python version 3.13.0 (Python Software Foundation) using pandas, statsmodels, and scikit-learn libraries. All P values were 2-sided, and a significance level of α = .05 was used for all statistical tests.

Results

Among 15 199 prospectively registered trials, an estimated 4678 (30.8%; 95% CI, 26.7%-34.5%) had at least 1 meaningful change to a primary outcome after enrollment start. Time frame modifications were most common (17.8%; 95% CI, 12.7%-22.3%), followed by outcome additions (12.5%; 95% CI, 10.1%-14.9%) and removals (8.5%; 95% CI, 6.7%-11.1%) (Figure, A). A decrease in the prevalence of outcome changes was observed over the study period, ranging from 48.9% (95% CI, 42.6%-54.8%) in 2016 to 8.0% (95% CI, 6.1%-9.6%) in 2024 (Figure, B).

In multivariable analysis, a more recent trial start year was associated with lower odds of outcome change (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] per 1-year increase, 0.83; 95% CI, 0.82-0.85) (Table). Trials with industry funding had higher odds of outcome change than non–industry-funded trials (aOR, 1.32; 95% CI, 1.21-1.43). Compared with drug trials, device trials (aOR, 1.46; 95% CI, 1.32-1.62) and biologic trials (aOR, 1.27; 95% CI, 1.11-1.46) had higher odds of outcome change. Therapeutic area was also associated with outcome changes, with higher odds observed for trials studying products treating neoplasms (aOR, 1.62; 95% CI, 1.41-1.85), nervous system diseases (aOR, 1.24; 95% CI, 1.09-1.42), and respiratory tract diseases (aOR, 1.28; 95% CI, 1.12-1.47).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of a large cohort of prospectively registered trials, nearly one-third (30.8%) had meaningful changes to primary outcomes after enrollment began. Although the prevalence of outcome changes declined over time, such changes remained common, particularly in industry-funded trials and selected therapeutic areas. These findings extend prior work based on manual review and suggest that scalable, automated monitoring of trial registrations may be feasible using LLMs.

A study limitation is that classification relied on model-based inference rather than direct manual adjudication, although we adjusted estimates for model performance. Because model performance was assessed on a limited set of trials, we could not determine whether classification accuracy varied across the trial characteristics we examined. In addition, we focused on outcome changes that automated methods can identify objectively and did not assess changes to the outcome measure itself. Nonetheless, our findings demonstrate that changes to primary outcomes remained common and that LLMs may offer a practical approach to support efforts by regulators, journals, and funders to monitor the integrity of the clinical evidence base at scale.
