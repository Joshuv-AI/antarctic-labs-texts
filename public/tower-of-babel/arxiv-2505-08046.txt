Abstract
Mobile jammers pose a critical threat to 5G networks, particularly in military communications. We propose an intelligent anti-jamming framework that integrates Multiple Signal Classification (MUSIC) for high-resolution Direction-of-Arrival (DoA) estimation, Minimum Variance Distortionless Response (MVDR) beamforming for adaptive interference suppression, and machine learning (ML) to enhance DoA prediction for mobile jammers. Extensive simulations in a realistic highway scenario demonstrate that our hybrid approach achieves an average Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) improvement of 9.58 dB (maximum 11.08 dB) and up to 99.8% DoA estimation accuracy. The framework’s computational efficiency and adaptability to dynamic jammer mobility patterns outperform conventional anti-jamming techniques, making it a robust solution for securing 5G communications in contested environments.

II
Background and Related Work

Beamforming, essential for enhancing signal quality and mitigating interference in modern wireless communications like 5G, has evolved from conventional fixed-weight methods to adaptive solutions
[
3
]
. While conventional beamforming offers simplicity, its fixed patterns struggle against dynamic interference, particularly mobile jammers. Adaptive techniques like MVDR and Linear-Constrained Minimum Variance (LCMV) adjust parameters based on the signal environment.

MVDR beamforming optimizes array weights to maximize sensitivity towards a desired DoA while suppressing other sources
[
4
]
. However, its performance degrades if the desired DoA is inaccurate. To address this, MVDR is coupled with high-resolution DoA estimation algorithms like MUSIC, which uses eigenspace decomposition to accurately identify signal directions even in multi-source scenarios
[
5
,
6
]
. LCMV also minimizes interference but imposes linear constraints to preserve the target signal
[
7
]
. A practical limitation is that LCMV is often restricted to a uniform linear array (ULA), whereas MVDR offers more flexibility, adapting well to a uniform rectangular array (URA) used in this work
[
6
]
.

Traditional anti-jamming strategies focusing on spatial filtering are effective against stationary jammers but fail when confronting mobile ones that dynamically change positions
[
8
]
. At the network layer, multipath communications and secret sharing schemes enhance resilience by distributing data across multiple paths to counter jamming attacks
[
9
,
10
]
. The combination of MUSIC’s accurate DoA estimation with ML support and MVDR’s adaptive nulling presents a promising approach for mobile interference mitigation.

Finally, accurately evaluating performance through signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) in multi-antenna systems facing mobile jammers presents challenges. Standard SNR calculations may not adequately handle dynamically changing interference or the specifics of signal combination after beamforming, necessitating careful consideration of the calculation methodology, as addressed in our proposed framework. The limitations of current DoA estimation with mobile sources, real-time adaptation challenges, and the need for better ML integration highlight the necessity for the comprehensive approach developed in this paper.

Our proposed framework integrates MUSIC-based DoA estimation, MVDR beamforming, and ML enhancement for DoA estimation support. We consider a

j(t)
is the jamming signal,

\theta
and elevation angle
\phi
. Subscripts
s
and

III-A

Anti-Jamming System

MUSIC-Enhanced DoA Estimation:

The MUSIC algorithm estimates an angle of arrival by exploiting the eigenstructure of the received signal’s covariance matrix
R

[
5
]
:

R=E\{x(t)x^{H}(t)\}

(2)

Eigendecomposition of
R
separates the signal subspace (
U_{s}
) from the noise subspace (
U_{n}
)
[
11
]
:

R=U_{s}\Lambda_{s}U_{s}^{H}+U_{n}\Lambda_{n}U_{n}^{H}

(3)

This allows for high-resolution DoA estimation by using
R
and scanning over a grid of possible angles, in our case -90
∘
to 90
∘
, then evaluating the MUSIC pseudospectrum. Peaks in the pseudospectrum show the estimated DoA of an incoming signal.

Adaptive MVDR Beamforming:

Beamforming is a spatial filtering strategy where various weight geometries and gain tapering methods manipulate transmitter and receiver antennas. A conventional beamformer is designed to stay the same between collections, while an adaptive beamformer responds to environment, allowing the antenna array to dynamically change. The MVDR beamformer is an adaptive beamformer that computes optimal weights
w_{opt}
to minimize output power while preserving the desired signal gain (in the direction
\theta_{s}
, assumed to be the desired signal’s DoA)
[
12
]
:

w_{opt}=\frac{R^{-1}a(\theta_{s},\phi_{s})}{a^{H}(\theta_{s},\phi_{s})R^{-1}a(\theta_{s},\phi_{s})}

(4)

This process adaptively creates nulls in the direction of interference sources such as the mobile jammer. In order to effectively shape the beam, we apply
w_{opt}
to the antenna array.

Machine Learning Integration:

To enhance estimation accuracy, particularly when the initial MUSIC DoA estimation error exceeds a predefined threshold, a linear regression ML model is employed. Linear regression was chosen due to its simplicity in implementation and relationship between input output. The model is trained using MATLAB’s fitlm
[
13
]
where the input features
(predictors)
are sequences of three consecutive ground truth azimuth angle values, and the output
(response)
is the subsequent ground truth azimuth value. Over 500 DoA data points from the simulated mobility patterns of the scenario were used as training data shown in Fig.
2
. The model predicts the DoA’s next azimuth angle
\hat{\theta}_{t+1}
based on the past
k
measurements
(\theta_{t},\theta_{t-1},...,\theta_{t-k})
:

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\beta_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\beta_{i}\cdot\theta_{t-i}

(5)

While
k=3
is only used in our experiments, the above linear prediction model can be easily generalized by leverging more historical measurements. The high statistical significance of the
\theta_{t-2}
coefficient (p-value
\approx 3.4\times 10^{-76}
) in our experiments confirms strong temporal dependency, justifying the model’s predictive capability.

SNR Calculation:

To reliably evaluate performance, we use a specific SNR calculation method that averages antenna signals across the
M\times N
array elements to prevent artificial gain inflation and ensures consistency between simulation and potential implementation, both before and after MVDR processing. The combined signal is
x_{2}(t)=\frac{1}{N+M}\sum_{i=1}^{N+M}|x(t)_{i}|
. Signal and noise powers are calculated by averaging
|x_{2}(t)|
over known signal-present (
a_{1}
to
b_{1}
) and signal-absent (
a_{0}
to
b_{0}
) regions:

Signal Power

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b_{1}-a_{1}+1}\sum_{i=a_{1}}^{b_{1}}|x_{2}(i)|

(6)

Noise Power

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b_{0}-a_{0}+1}\sum_{i=a_{0}}^{b_{0}}|x_{2}(i)|

(7)

SNR (dB)

\displaystyle=20\log_{10}\left(\frac{\text{Signal Power}}{\text{Noise Power}}\right)

(8)

The SNR improvement is calculated as:

\text{SNR}_{improvement}=\text{SNR}_{after}-\text{SNR}_{before}

(9)

IV-C

Discussion

Limitations:

Despite robust performance, limitations exist in this adaptive beamforming for anti-jamming setup. SNR calculation accuracy can decrease in very high-mobility scenarios. The current ML model might require retraining for drastically different mobility patterns not encountered during training. Furthermore, the framework’s performance against multiple simultaneous jammers needs investigation. Only one jammer was implemented for sake of simplicity. To introduce a multi-jammer system more jammer objects and channels would need to be initalized and added at collection time. Lastly, due to the nature of the simulation, the propagation of signals occurs at every collection prior to the processing step inside the iterative for loop. This causes latency within the system, as multi-threading was not explored.

V
Conclusion and Future Work

This paper presented a novel anti-jamming framework integrating MUSIC DoA estimation, MVDR beamforming, and machine learning to mitigate mobile 5G jammers. Simulations confirmed its effectiveness, achieving an average
9.58 dB
SNR improvement and high DoA accuracy (up to
99.8%
) in realistic highway scenarios. Future work will focus on addressing the limitations, potentially exploring deep learning models and optimizing for multi-jammer environments. This project demonstrated adaptive beamforming for anti-jamming as a proof of concept in simulation. Next steps would be implementation in a lab setting with USRP software defined radios and PCB directional antennas.
