Does the integrated electronic medical record system have a positive adoption in community hospital settings?
Authors: Diwash Timilsina, Shiva Raj Acharya, Jeevan Bhatta, Mandeep Pathak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn developing nations, there is limited research evidence and an information gap in the adoption of Integrated Electronic Medical Record System (EMRs). This study aimed to evaluate the adoption of recently integrated EMRs in community hospitals in Nepal.MethodsTwo community hospitals with integrated EMRs were purposively selected. Both hospitals used the Bahmni EMRs, an open-source platform designed for low-resource settings that integrates clinical, laboratory, pharmacy, and administrative functions. Among the total of 165 EMRs users, 121 successfully participated in this quantitative study from September 05 to October 23, 2022. The study utilized the technology acceptance model to assess the attitudes towards the adoption of integrated EMRs, based on EMRs usage duration, system capability, learning acceptability, and users’ behavioral intentions.ResultsThe mean scores for attitudes toward EMRs, EMRs learning acceptability and users’ behavioral intention to use EMRs were 46.55 ± 7.9, 33.41 ± 5.9, and 36.61 ± 5.7, respectively. Participants with postgraduate education (vs. undergraduate and high school) and those working in the laboratory and administration departments (vs. OPD, pharmacy and IPD) had higher mean scores for attitude and learning acceptability (P < 0.01). Increased duration of EMRs use, (B, 0.81, CI: 0.61–1.15, P < 0.001), increased EMRs system capability (B, 0.43; CI: 0.21–0.73, P = 0.001), increased EMRs learning acceptability (B, 0.71; CI: 0.50–0.94, P < 0.001), and increased EMRs users’ behavioral intentions (B, 0.51; CI: 0.35–0.67, P < 0.001) significantly increased the attitudes toward the use of EMRs.ConclusionThe adoption of integrated EMRs was positive. Integrated EMRs could improve the quality and efficiency of healthcare services with low-cost resources. Therefore, central and local healthcare systems must prioritize the expansion of integrated EMRs across multiple community hospitals and healthcare centers.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Integrated electronic medical record system, Adoption, Community hospital

Introduction

Electronic Medical Record System (EMRs) represent a significant leap forward in the digitalization of healthcare, offering a computer-based system that facilitates the recording and access of patient medical data and reports across hospital networks [1]. While the technological foundational technology of EMRs emerged in the 1970s, with initial implementations at academic healthcare facilities [2], the past two decades have witnessed substantial evolution in their integration capabilities, particularly in low-and middle-income countries (LMICs) [3, 4]. Integrated EMRs extend beyond clinical data management and encompass a broad spectrum of health information systems (HIS), such as staff rostering, pharmaceutical, radiology, and laboratory information systems [5–7]. This comprehensive integration has transformed the dynamics of doctor-patient communication and inter-institutional collaboration among healthcare providers [8–11].

Experience from LMICs has demonstrated both the potential and challenges of EMRs implementation. Studies have highlighted that successful adoption depends on multiple interconnected factors, including technological infrastructure, user training, organizational readiness, and sustained technical support [11–15]. In Southeast Asian LMICs, research has shown that the choice of technology platform significantly impacts adoption rates, with open-source systems demonstrating better adaptability to resource-limited contexts [6, 7, 16, 17]. Infrastructure challenges, including unreliable electricity and internet connectivity, have emerged as critical barriers in rural healthcare settings across developing nations [9, 10, 13, 16].

In Nepal, the digital health landscape presents unique challenges and opportunities. The Government of Nepal’s eHealth Strategy 2017 endorsed the implementation of health information systems, yet practical progress has been slow [18, 19]. Many hospitals, particularly in remote areas, continue to maintain paper-based records. As of early 2022, when this study was conducted, only five hospitals in Nepal had implemented integrated EMRs [20]. Unlike the predominantly government-owned or private facilities that initiated EMRs adoption in other LMICs, the Nepali context features a unique public-private partnership (PPP) model in community hospitals [3, 19]. This model operates with limited government technical support and financial resources, relying heavily on non-governmental organizations and philanthropic partners for implementation and sustainability. The absence of national-level technical infrastructure and standardized EMRs policies has meant that each implementing hospital must develop its own capacity and support systems.

The adoption of EMRs has been shown to positively impact user satisfaction, service efficiency, and the overall quality of healthcare delivery [1, 9]. The decision to adopt and effectively use EMRs is heavily influenced by personal commitment and professional enthusiasm among healthcare practitioners [9, 10]. However, resistance to healthcare or any technology often hampers the realization of its full potential [11, 16], prompting the development of various approaches to understanding the factors influencing technology adoption. The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) evaluates perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use as key indicators for assessing the adoption of implemented technology, which is particularly relevant in the context of healthcare information systems [8, 12, 13, 21].

Given Nepal’s unique implementation context of remote geographical locations, limited infrastructure, minimal governmental support, and reliance on PPP models, understanding EMR adoption in this setting provides valuable insights for similar resource-constrained environments globally. Thus, this study aimed to evaluate the attitudes and perceptions toward the use of integrated EMRs in community hospitals in Nepal, with the hypothesis that there will be a positive adoption of these systems. To our knowledge, this is the first-ever study to evaluate the attitudes toward the adoption of integrated EMRs among users from multiple departments in community hospital settings in Nepal.

Methods

Study settings

This quantitative cross-sectional study included 121 users of integrated EMRs from two community hospitals in the remote parts of the Gorkha (Hospital A) and Achham (Hospital B) districts of Nepal. These two hospitals were selected based on their similar type of settings (community hospital), size (50-bedded) and use of integrated EMRs in full phase over a year.

Both hospitals implemented the Bahmni EMRs, an open-source, integrated hospital information system built on OpenMRS. Bahmni was specifically designed for low-resource settings and providers modules for patient registration, outpatient and inpatient management, laboratory, radiology, pharmacy, and billing. At the time of study, all core modules had been operational for at least 12 months, with implementation having occurred in a phased manner over 18–24 months prior to data collection. In this study, a community hospital refers to a facility that is predominantly utilized by local community people, located in a remote area of the country, and operating on a public-private-philanthropic (PPP) model. Other health facilities that implemented EMRs were either government-run or privately owned. The study was conducted from September 05 to October 23, 2022. The study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Swansea University (SUM RESC 2022-057). Both verbal and written informed consent were obtained from all participants.

Study participants and assessment of EMRs adoption

Two community hospitals were purposively selected where integrated EMRs had been in use for more than a year. The study population comprised all healthcare workers who had been using the Bahmni EMRs as part of routine clinical, administrative, or supportive duties. This included all cadres of staff across all departments who interacted with the system: doctors, nurses, paramedics, radiologists, laboratory technicians, pharmacists, and administrative personnel. In both hospitals, the EMRs were implemented in a phased manner across departments as follows: Registration/Billing → OPD → IPD/Lab/Radiology/Pharmacy. The total number of integrated EMRs users in these hospitals, which included doctors, nurses, radiologists, laboratory technicians, pharmacists, and the administration team, was reported to be 165. All current integrated EMRs users across the two hospitals were initially included in the study. We utilized the TAM, referencing previous studies [12, 21]. A sample size of above 120 participants was found to be adequate for statistical analysis in a TAM-based study [6]. The TAM concept assumes that the adoption process will succeed or fail depending on the individual adopting the new technology and how he or she perceives the change [21]. Based on the TAM, we assessed the EMRs adoption and associated factors across the selected hospitals (Fig. 1).

All these sections’ questionnaires were constructed and validated by referencing previous studies [6, 9, 10, 12, 17, 21, 22].

Data collection

The survey questionnaire consisted of six sections with a total of 39 questions. The survey was conducted online using Google Forms. Initially, the online questionnaire link was shared with the hospital management team. After verification, it was delivered to all recruited participants through their respective department managers. Out of 165 EMRs users, 121 participants submitted fully completed online questionnaires, resulting in a response rate of 73.33%. The 44 non-respondents did not differ significantly from respondents in terms of departmental distribution or staff cadre based on administrative records. The study samples were deemed adequate and representative, considering the limited number of integrated EMRs users and the fact that only five hospitals in the entire country had implemented integrated EMRs at the time of the study [20].

Statistical analysis

The findings are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Mean differences between the groups based on age, education, working department, and hospital were assessed using ANOVA and independent t-tests. Furthermore, a full model multiple regression was employed to investigate the factors associated with attitudes towards the adoption of EMRs. The data were analyzed using SAS 9.4. The significance level was set at P < 0.05, with a 95% confidence interval (CI).

However, there were no significant differences between age groups with EMRs attitudes, use duration, capability, learning acceptability, and users’ behavioral intentions (all P > 0.05). There was a significant difference in the mean score of attitudes towards EMRs, duration of EMRs use, and EMRs learning acceptability across the education levels of the participants. Participants with postgraduate education had higher mean scores for attitude (51.14 ± 5.6, P < 0.01), duration of EMRs use (18.43 ± 3.8, P < 0.01), and learning acceptability (36.71 ± 5.7, P < 0.05) compared to those with an undergraduate, high school/diploma, or vocational training education (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study assessed attitudes towards the adoption of integrated EMRs in hospitals located in remote areas of Nepal. The study showed a positive adoption of EMRs among participants. In addition, we observed a positive correlation between EMRs learning acceptability, use duration, system capability, users’ behavioural perceptions, and EMRs adoption. The successful implementation of the Bahmni system in these resource-constrained settings demonstrates the viability of open-source EMR platforms in contexts with minimal government technical support and limited resources.

Our findings align with and extend previous research from other LMICs that has emphasized the role of user characteristics in EMRs adoption. An increase in education levels among end users has led to better implementation and adoption of EMRs, as supported by our study [15, 23]. This finding is consistent with research indicating that doctors and physicians have more positive attitudes toward EMRs than nurses do [24, 25]. However, the majority of our study participants had a diploma-level education and were under thirty-five years old. This suggests that younger individuals are the primary users of the system, and their innovative, adaptable capabilities and cognitive flexibility may explain this observation [11]. Previous research has focused predominantly on the acceptance, adoption, and perceptions of EMRs within single user groups, such as nurses, doctors, or other health professionals [8, 22, 26]. However, our study is a pioneering study for Nepal and other developing nations by including and assessing multiple EMRs users across all hospital departments. The perception of each user group is crucial when adopting a new integrated EMRs [16, 27]. Integrated EMRs can only function well and be largely successful when there is perfect coordination between all departments.

We observed higher positive behavioural perceptions toward the adoption of EMRs in the community hospital from the Achham district. This may be attributed to factors such as geographical location, hospital infrastructure, managerial preferences, cultural factors, users’ skills, and resources [28, 29]. Additionally, variations in local implementation support, availability of continuous technical assistance, and differences in training approaches between the two sites may contribute to this difference. Further studies should systematically compare the differences in EMRs adoption across multiple healthcare centers, considering these confounding factors. Our study demonstrated a positive relationship between EMRs use duration, system capability, learning acceptability, and users’ intentions toward EMRs adoption, aligning with previous findings [8, 13, 15, 30]. These findings are particularly significant given the limited government support and infrastructure challenges in our study setting. The positive adoption despite these constraints suggests that with appropriate training, technical support from implementing organizations, and the selection of suitable technology platforms, success is achievable. EMRs can be successfully deployed in even the most resource-limited environments.

The TAM framework proved helpful in understanding the adoption process in this resource-constrained context. Our results demonstrate that perceived usefulness (reflected in attitude score) and perceived ease of use (reflected in learning acceptability) mediated the relationship between system characteristics and behavioral intentions, consistent with TAM theory. However, our findings also suggest that in LMIC settings, external factors such as infrastructure reliability, technical support availability, and duration of implementation play a more prominent role than in developed countries’ contexts. The significant association between use duration and positive attitudes suggests that allowing sufficient time for system stabilization and user adaptation is crucial. This extended adoption period may be necessary to overcome initial infrastructure challenges, build user confidence, and demonstrate system value, especially in well-resourced settings where these factors may be less prominent. Future research should explore modifications to TAM that incorporate contextual factors specific to resource-limited settings.

The study also highlights the necessity of developing tailored training and skill programs, along with continuous resource support for healthcare professionals, to ensure the proficient use of EMRs. This initiative can create a favorable environment for integrating EMRs into healthcare settings [30]. Furthermore, engaging relevant stakeholders and fostering a culture of learning and adaptation within healthcare organizations can enhance the successful implementation of EMRs. These practices build a sense of ownership and confidence in using EMRs [31]. Additionally, providing refresher training on new features and updated functionalities of the EMRs will boost user adoption [9].

This study has several limitations. Due to the cross-sectional design, causal inferences cannot be drawn. Since this study utilized an online-based survey tool, it may entail potential response and selection bias. This study was conducted in 2022, and as of mid-2024, EMRs have been introduced to other hospitals. Therefore, it may not fully represent all users’ adoption towards EMRs. Variations in EMRs adoption across different groups might stem from differences in demographic, cultural, and geographical attributes of hospitals. Most importantly, while we described the Bahmni system and its operational modules, we did not conduct detailed comparisons of specific module usage patterns or functionality preferences between the sampled hospitals. Different departments may have utilized different subsets of available modules with varying intensity, which could partially explain departmental differences in adoption attitudes. Therefore, future research should address EMRs adoption by considering geographical attributes, the availability of local resources, financial allocation, module-specific usage patterns, and conducting before-and-after comparative studies to evaluate the impact of EMRs implementation on clinical and operational outcomes.

In conclusion, this study highlighted positive attitudes, learning acceptability, and user behavioral perceptions toward the adoption of EMRs, despite limited infrastructure and minimal government support. The implementation of integrated EMRs could significantly enhance the delivery, management, efficiency, and quality of healthcare services with minimal resources. Therefore, this study suggests that adequate digitization can play an important role in the healthcare system and supports the further expansion of integrated EMRs across community healthcare centers to enhance service delivery.
