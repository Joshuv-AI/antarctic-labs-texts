Capturing professionals’ decision-making in creative workflows is essential for reflection, collaboration, and knowledge sharing, yet existing methods often leave rationales incomplete and implicit decisions hidden. To address this, we present Clear framework that structures reasoning into cognitive decision steps—linked units of actions, artifacts, and self-explanations that make decisions traceable. Building on this framework, we introduce ClearFairy , a think-aloud AI assistant for UI design that detects weak explanations, asks lightweight clarifying questions, and infers missing rationales to ease the knowledge-sharing burden. In a study with twelve creative professionals, 85% of ClearFairy ’s inferred rationales were accepted, increasing strong explanations from 14% to over 83% of decision steps without adding cognitive demand. The captured steps also enhanced generative AI agents in Figma, yielding next-action predictions better aligned with professionals and producing more coherent design outcomes. For future research on human knowledge-grounded creative AI agents, we release a dataset of captured 417 decision steps.

1.
Introduction

Capturing and documenting professionals’ cognitive processes and decision-making during creative workflows (e.g., design, writing, or video editing) is essential to understanding professional knowledge and applying this knowledge
(
Fischer et al. 2020
)
. As decisions in creative contexts often rely on heuristics and contextual insights, uncovering the underlying rationale is key in understanding each decision
(
Lee 1997
)
. Since much of creative work takes place in traceable software, professionals’ decisions and rationales can be documented, where these documentations can support self-reflection
(
Burge and Brown 2000
)
, foster mutual understanding among collaborators
(
Peña-Mora and Vadhavkar 1997
)
, and enable knowledge transfer from experts to novices
(
Kimball and Holyoak 2000
)
. Furthermore, these decisions can be collected as
datasets
to analyze large-scale patterns
(
Chang et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2018
)
or to train AI models that support creative workflows
(
Fuchs et al. 2023
)
.

To capture such decisions for sharing and reuse, prior approaches employ in-task annotations, retrospective interviews, and in-situ self-explanations. Among these, self-explanation—where professionals verbalize their rationales while working—has been shown to capture richer decision-making information than retrospective reflection
(
Hoffman et al. 1995
;
Collins et al. 1991
)
, while also imposing a lower burden than in-task annotations
(
Krosnick et al. 2021
)
. This advantage is more pronounced in artifact creation workflows (e.g., 3D modeling or slide design) than in text production (e.g., programming) as the verbalization complements, rather than duplicates, the task’s modality—aligning with Mayer’s Redundancy Principle
(
Mayer 2005
)
. Also, in creative domains such as UI/UX design, self-explanation is a familiar practice for professionals as they often prefer verbal explanations when documenting their decisions
(
Feng et al. 2023
)
.

However, self-explanation through verbalizing is not a natural part of everyday design work; rather, it is typically elicited in contexts such as critique, mentoring, or documentation. Within these contexts, capturing high-quality explanations through verbalization still faces important limitations. First, verbalized self-explanations are often incomplete or result in “
weak explanations
”
(
Nielsen et al. 2002
;
Ericsson and Simon 1980
)
—explanations that provide insufficient rationale for the decision—as opposed to
strong explanations
. Second, while clarifying questions are important for capturing sufficient decision rationales, answering every question in real time can interfere with the creative process
(
Mark et al. 2008
)
. Third, verbal reports alone may not reveal implicit decisions
(
Polanyi 1968
)
, which often require contextual linkage to workflow traces (e.g., user actions or in-progress design artifacts) to surface subtle cognitive processes
(
Nielsen et al. 2002
)
. While other methods such as screen recordings can capture the user’s actions or artifacts, they do not proactively segment users’ decision steps or elicit rationales.

To this end, we propose
Clear
, a conceptual framework designed to support knowledge sharing and reusing through self-explanation by structuring a creative professional’s cognitive processes into
“cognitive decision steps”
(
Figure 1
). A cognitive decision step represents a unit of decision-making grounded in dynamically linked self-explanations, actions, and artifacts that reflect contextual and adaptive reasoning. The framework outlines how users’ streams of explanations and actions can be segmented into cognitively meaningful
chunks
, and how these chunks can be linked into discrete decision steps. By making cognitive processes traceable and interpretable, the framework enables fine-grained assessment of users’ rationales in context by considering explanations, actions, and artifacts together. It also provides a foundation for structured knowledge capture, through which underspecified rationales can be detected, clarified, and organized into decision steps. This, in turn, supports the capture of higher-quality self-explanations.

Building on
Clear
, we introduce
ClearFairy
, a think-aloud AI assistant for the UI/UX domain (
Figure 1
).
ClearFairy
captures a professional’s series of cognitive decision steps while prompting sufficient rationale from the user by automatically detecting weak explanations and generating lightweight clarifying questions. Over the course of a session,
ClearFairy
progressively learns from a user’s explanations to anticipate and infer rationales for future decisions, which the user can simply verify or refine. In doing so, the system reduces the burden of constant elaboration typically imposed by clarification practices, while still ensuring that decision rationales are captured in sufficient depth.
ClearFairy
is implemented as a Figma plugin

, embedding knowledge capture into the design environment itself. As shown in
Figure 1
, it operationalizes the
Clear
through a generative pipeline that segments decision steps, evaluates the sufficiency of their rationales, elicits or infers missing reasoning, and organizes the results into a structured workflow documentation.

To evaluate the effectiveness of
ClearFairy
in capturing professionals’ cognitive decision steps within knowledge sharing scenarios of creative workflows, we conducted a within-subjects study with 12 creative professionals (i.e., interface design experts) across two web design tasks, where
ClearFairy
was compared to a baseline think-aloud approach.
ClearFairy
captured strong explanations for 83.2% of all cognitive decision steps, compared to 13.9% in the baseline. Notably, 85% of the system-inferred rationales were accepted by participants, reflecting strong alignment with their reasoning and easing the cognitive burden of decision justification. Participants reported that the system’s inferences enabled them to recall deeper rationales, supporting the capture of implicit decisions—such as those based on expert conventions, aesthetic intuition, and personal preferences.

To validate the practical utility of captured cognitive decision steps, we explored two applications: (1) inferring professionals’ next actions, and (2) simulating their design actions through a tool-calling agent to generate coherent design outcomes aligned with professional practices. For next-action inference, in 66.7% of cases, professionals preferred the actions inferred by a Large Language Model (LLM) when it was provided with the cognitive steps, rather than without them. For design simulation, we built a ReAct-based
(
yao2022react
)
LLM-powered design agent in Figma that generates artifacts directly on the canvas. Providing captured decision steps to this agent produced outputs that were rated as more structured, detailed, and professionally aligned, compared to using only a task prompt.

For future research, we release a dataset of 417 cognitive decision steps from 12 creative professionals, each containing a decision, associated actions, and rationale. Finally, we discuss broader implications of capturing implicit decision steps in creative workflows, focusing on their potential to support learning and advance AI agents.

This work presents the following contributions:

•

Clear
: A conceptual framework that proposes how to structure and capture
“cognitive decision steps”
in creative workflows for knowledge capture by linking action logs, self-explanations, and artifacts.

•

ClearFairy
: An AI assistant for knowledge capture based on
Clear
and implemented as a Figma plugin that elicits rationales behind decisions and progressively learns to infer the creative professional’s rationale.

•

Findings from a User Study (N=12)
: Demonstrating that
ClearFairy
helps professionals articulate implicit decisions and capture significantly more strong explanations without increasing the burden of knowledge-sharing.

•

Cognitive Decision Steps with Reasoning Dataset
: A dataset that contains 417 cognitive decision steps captured from creative professionals’ workflows, where each step includes a
decision and actions
,
contextual information
, and
rationale behind the decision
.

Related Work

Our work builds on prior research across workflow segmentation, workflow capture tools, and generative model interventions to support creative professionals’ reasoning and knowledge capture.

2.1.
Workflow Segmentation: An Approach Incorporating Rationale, Behavior, and Artifacts

Workflow segmentation techniques have been proposed for diverse purposes, such as workflow navigation
(
Pongnumkul et al. 2011
;
Truong et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2021b
)
, key step search
(
Pavel et al. 2014
;
Chang et al. 2017
;
Kong et al. 2012
)
, or authoring
(
Chen et al. 2024a
;
Chi et al. 2012
;
Lafreniere et al. 2014
;
Pu et al. 2023
)
.
To segment workflows, prior work has primarily relied on application usage logs (e.g., commands or tools)
(
Lafreniere et al. 2014
;
Kong et al. 2012
;
Chi et al. 2012
)
, specific keywords in transcripts
(
Truong et al. 2021
;
Chang et al. 2017
)
, or predefined sub-goals
(
Weir et al. 2015
;
Pavel et al. 2014
;
Kim et al. 2014
)
.
However, there is limited work that explores workflow segmentation that reflects the subject’s cognitive processes (e.g., knowledge and reasoning).
Capturing a subject’s cognitive process in a workflow—why certain decisions were made
(
Brockmann and Anthony 2002
;
Lee 1997
)
, what actions were taken
(
Droll and Hayhoe 2008
)
, and what reasoning supports those decisions
(
Wickens et al. 2013
)
—is crucial for knowledge sharing.
According to Lee
(
Lee 1997
)
, understanding a person’s cognitive process requires identifying the “rationale” that explains “
why
” and “
how
” a decision was made.
Prior work
(
Carroll and Tech 2003
;
Moran and Carroll 1996
)
also argued that, beyond this rationale, it is crucial to capture the subject’s behavior and produce artifacts to understand their implicit knowledge, reasoning, and decision-making process in detail.

In this work, we propose a workflow segmentation approach, called
Clear
, that focuses on capturing a human’s cognitive decision steps along with their underlying reasoning. Our approach integrates the subject’s verbalized explanation, observed behaviors, and generated artifacts to capture the subject’s cognitive process.

2.2.
Workflow Capturing and Documentation Tools

Workflow capture has been actively studied not only for knowledge sharing but also for sharing collaborators’ decision-making processes
(
Kim et al. 2021
;
O’Leary et al. 2018
;
Park et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2020
)
or enabling self-exploration of previously executed tasks
(
Dragunov et al. 2005
)
.
For instance, Chronicle
(
Grossman et al. 2010
)
and Screentrack
(
Hu and Lee 2019
)
record the overall workflow and log the user’s actions as a history. Waken
(
Banovic et al. 2012
)
tracks user interactions with tools by reverse engineering them from task recording video.
To share decision-making processes among collaborators, Winder
(
Kim et al. 2021
)
links user’s explanations to workflow artifacts, and Charrette
(
O’Leary et al. 2018
)
allows users to save and share subsets of work artifacts as snapshots.
Prior work
(
Gruber and Russell 1996
;
Hertzum et al. 2009
)
also suggests that self-explanation through think-aloud protocols can capture experts’ reasoning without drastically interfering in their workflows.
Building on this,
Co-notate

(
Rasmussen et al. 2019
)
enables users to create video and audio recording to reflect on significant events (e.g., problems, ideas, or decisions) during the design process.
Additionally, Krosnick et al.
(
Krosnick et al. 2021
)
proposed
think-aloud computing
that prompts users to share details during their workflows by visualizing the type and amount of explanation needed.

Although prior work has actively explored self-explanation-based workflow capture, these systems passively record user actions while relying on users to actively provide explanations. However, users may unintentionally overlook parts that should be explained
(
Sela et al. 2000
)
—often due to expert blind spots
(
Wang et al. 2021a
;
Nathan and Petrosino 2003
)
—but continuous self-explanation is burdensome
(
Van Den Haak et al. 2003
)
. Therefore, our work presents a knowledge-capture tool that identifies points where rationales have been insufficiently explained in the user’s explanation and proactively prompts users with clarification questions.

2.3.
Generative Model’s Intervention to Support Human’s Thought Process

As generative models, such as Large Language Models (LLMs) or Vision-Language Models (VLMs), are capable of producing contextually plausible answers or suggestions, prior work has investigated how users can leverage these responses effectively. In particular, generative models have been used to provoke student thinking in educational contexts
(
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Favero et al. 2024
)
, prevent fixation and promote inspirational ideation
(
Smith et al. 2025
;
Wadinambiarachchi et al. 2024
;
Lee et al. 2024a
;
Liu et al. 2024a
;
Xu et al. 2024
;
Pu et al. 2025a
)
, and, more broadly, facilitate human critical thinking
(
Danry et al. 2023
;
Mukherjee et al. 2023
;
Lee et al. 2024b
;
Drosos et al. 2025
)
. To interactively intervene in the human creative process, recent HCI research has proposed systems that offer real-time, context-aware suggestions to scaffold user reasoning and augment decision-making in various tasks
(
Feng et al. 2024
;
Riche et al. 2025
;
Pu et al. 2025b
;
Chen et al. 2024b
;
Liu et al. 2024b
)
.
Beyond supporting creative and critical thinking, researchers have also explored employing LLMs as interactive partners that help users better articulate their thoughts by inducing reflection
(
Wei et al. 2024
)
. For example, Thinking Assistants
(
Park et al. 2024
)
poses reflection-oriented inquiries grounded in expert knowledge, which helped users reach better decisions, and Kumar et al.
(
Kumar et al. 2024
)
showed that LLM-supported self-reflection increased self-confidence and improved learning outcomes in students.

While generative models have shown promise in supporting the human thought processes through interventions, how such models can elicit users’ self-explanation during workflows remains underexplored. Specifically, think-aloud protocols could help externalize reasoning but place the cognitive burden of what to explain and when to users
(
Nielsen et al. 2002
;
van Someren et al. 1994
)
, while generating clarification questions can interrupt the task workflow
(
Mark et al. 2008
)
. To address these challenges, we propose a system that leverages the reasoning capabilities of generative models to reduce this burden by predicting missing inferences and providing suggestions as knowledge capture progresses. In doing so, our system functions as a copilot-style assistant that supports rationale articulation, scaffolding users’ reasoning and helping make implicit knowledge more explicit during creative workflows.

3.
Clear
Framework: Cognitive Linking of Explanations, Actions, and Results for Decision Step Segmentation

This section introduces the
Clear
Framework, which is designed to capture and analyze professionals’ decision-making in creative workflows by dynamically linking explanations, actions, and their resulting artifacts. Specifically, the framework proposes a method for structuring users’ cognitive thought processes into flexible, context-aware segments or decision steps—providing an in-depth, contextual understanding of how the user reasons about their decisions during complex tasks.

3.1.
Definition of Cognitive Decision Step

We define a
Cognitive Decision Step
as
“a unit of decision-making defined based on
dynamic
,
adaptive
, and
contextual
boundaries, comprising one or more linked explanations, actions, and artifacts.”

This concept serves as the fundamental unit in our framework.

Dynamic
:
While prior work has explored methods for capturing decision-making units within users’ workflows, they focused on
retrospective
segmentation based on predetermined definitions of sub-goals in the workflow
(
Weir et al. 2015
;
Pavel et al. 2014
;
Kim et al. 2014
)
or significant action types
(
Lafreniere et al. 2014
;
Kong et al. 2012
;
Chi et al. 2012
)
.
However, a creative task workflow consists of diverse and sequential decisions, where capturing the rationale behind these decisions
as they occur
is essential to gain a deeper understanding of the user’s thought process
(
Lee 1997
;
Regli et al. 2000
)
.
Thus, instead of post-hoc segmentation,
cognitive decision steps
are structured dynamically during the user’s workflow in real-time.

Adaptive
:
Thought processes can vary across individuals based on their expertise levels and task execution styles—influencing the way that each individual makes decisions
(
Sweller 1988
)
.
Due to these differences, a user’s natural flow of thought in pursuit of one decision may vary not only across individuals but also depending on expertise and task context.

An experienced UI designer who wants to make a button stand out might make it bigger, adjust its contrast with the background, and add a drop shadow based on their knowledge of design trends.
On the other hand, a more novice designer with the same overall goal may first make it bigger to differentiate it from other buttons, adjust its contrast so it does not disappear in the background, and add a drop shadow to follow references they collected.

In the example, although the outcome achieved by both designers is the same, their thinking processes and individual decisions considered differ. Instead of fixed segments, each
cognitive decision step
is flexibly bounded to capture variations in thought flow shaped by individual differences, expertise, and task context.

Contextual
:
In a creative task workflow, each decision can only be fully understood through the professionals’ explanations, actions, and the resulting artifacts
(
Nielsen et al. 2002
)
. To understand a professional’s cognitive decision steps within a workflow, it is necessary to observe these elements at a fine-grained level and identify the contextual links that bind them into decision steps. By examining these linked explanations, actions, and artifacts together, one can gain a deeper understanding of the professional’s decisions in context.

When editing a video, an editor might say,
“This transition feels too abrupt—I’ll add a fade and slightly slow down the clip before it.”

Here, the editor’s explanation provides insight into their thinking process but, to fully understand their cognitive decisions, we need more context about the actions taken by the editor (e.g., what type of fade was added, how much the clip slowed down) and the artifacts (e.g., what content is present in the previous and next clip).
Cognitive decision steps
can represent the whole context by linking the explanations, actions, and artifacts, which are cognitively connected and relevant to a decision.

3.2.
Components of a Cognitive Decision Step

In a self-explanation-based creative workflow capturing scenario,
Clear
tracks three types of data:
self-explanations
,
actions
, and
artifacts
resulting from the professional’s actions (
Figure 2
). Since we focus on creative workflows, such as creation or editing that produce an actual artifact, every action incurs changes in the artifact (
Figure 2
-Action and Artifact). Each component provides unique insights into the creative professional’s decision-making process:

•

Explanations
reveal the professional’s reasoning and intent behind decisions.

•

Actions
show the digital interactions or operations performed within a workflow.

•

Artifacts
represents the visual state of the workspace after each action, recorded as screenshots that reflect the outcomes of those actions.

. Explanations provide the rationale behind several actions, and artifacts capture the resulting changes of each action.

By considering these data together, the framework segments the workflow into meaningful decision steps.

These components are structured into cognitive decision steps based on the following
principles
.

3.3.
Principles for Forming Cognitive Decision Steps

This section defines how the three components can be linked: that is, how they can be considered cognitively connected and thus belong to the same cognitive decision step. Humans tend to speak and act in ways that reflect their ongoing thinking, leading to a coherent sequence of behavior. However, such cognitive relationships are often implicit and not directly observable. To address this, we propose objective rules for identifying cognitive links among low-level units of explanation, action, and artifact.

Building on these principles, we introduce an approach for structuring cognitive decision steps based on the natural continuity and interrelation between explanations, actions, and their resulting artifacts. Rather than imposing a predefined structure, our method identifies cognitive coherence through the following patterns:

•

Cognitively Linked Explanations
: A sequence of explanations is directly linked in meaning and refers to the same object or sub-goal.

•

Cognitively Linked Actions and Their Resulting Artifacts
: Multiple actions occur sequentially and are logically connected in pursuit of a shared goal.

•

Cognitively Linked Explanations and Actions-Artifacts
: An explanation explicitly refers to a specific action or artifacts, or an action clearly reflects a preceding explanation.

3.3.1.
Cognitively Linked Explanations

When a series of explanations flows continuously around the same object or intent, they often reflect a single cognitive unit in working memory rather than discrete steps, aligning with chunking theory in verbal protocols
(
Miller 1956
;
Ericsson and Simon 1980
;
Ericsson and Simon 1993
;
Cowan 2001
)
. In other words, uninterrupted segments of speech are likely already grouped based on a meaningful connection in the speaker’s mind, so capturing them as a single decision step also aligns with how our limited working memory naturally bundles linked information.

We define cognitively linked explanations by identifying cases where consecutive sentences describe the
same mentioned purpose or element
, indicating that they originate from the same decision step (
Figure 3
). Even if some remarks are separated in time or interspersed with other thoughts, as long as they explicitly refer back to the same element or intent, they can be chunked into a single step in the timeline of decision-making.

Figure 3.
Construction of cognitively linked explanations. Cognitively linked explanations are created when consecutive utterances refer to the same goal or element within the workflow.
This figure shows how \frameworkname{} groups consecutive explanation segments based on their semantic and referential similarity. The top row shows individual explanation chunks mentioning various goals and elements. The bottom row demonstrates how these chunks are grouped into cognitively linked explanation clusters—each corresponding to a shared design goal or referenced element.

For instance, if a designer consistently refers to “making the button more visible” across consecutive sentences, all such statements revolve around the same design goal and thus can be considered one cognitive unit (e.g., “I want to make it bolder. Let’s change the color. Maybe also adjust the size a bit.”).

3.3.2.
Cognitively Linked Actions and Their Resulting Artifacts

When multiple sequential actions take place under a sub-goal, they can form a “chunked action”, an idea rooted in the extension of chunking from memory items to motor or procedural steps
(
Verwey 1996
;
Lashley 1951
)
. By grouping linked actions together into one coherent unit, we reflect how people perform consecutive actions in a cognitive decision step.

In particular, in a creative workflow, we define that linked actions can appear when various actions occur sequentially on one object or when the same action is performed on multiple objects (
Figure 4
).

Consider a video editor is controlling hue adjustments on a single clip: she first shifts the greens toward yellow to warm up the foliage, then tweaks the reds to appear slightly more orange for skin tone correction. Though each change is a separate action, these are all parts of a single linked action aimed at adjusting the hue of the clip.

The editor can also give a cross-dissolve transition effect to multiple clips at once to make the transition smooth. This kind of action should also be considered a linked action.

Figure 4.
Construction of cognitively linked actions with artifacts. Cognitively linked actions are formed when the same action is performed on multiple elements, or when a sequence of actions is performed on the same element.
This figure shows how \frameworkname{} groups cognitively linked actions with artifacts. The top section shows individual actions and the corresponding artifacts they produce. In the bottom section, these actions are clustered into 4 groups: (1) one group contains the same action applied across multiple elements (A, C, F, G), (2) the other two groups capture a single action on a single element (D, E each), and (3) the other one with the repeated actions on a single element (B).

3.3.3.
Cognitively Linked Explanations and Actions & Artifacts

After making links introduced in the previous steps, links between explanations and actions can be confirmed. If an explanation references a specific action or artifact—or an action clearly manifests a prior explanation—both are best treated as a single cognitive decision step, because explicit connections in discourse
(
van Dijk and Kintsch 1983
;
Kintsch 1988
;
Clark and Schaefer 1989
)
or event structure
(
Zacks et al. 2007
;
Zacks and Tversky 2001
)
naturally bind them into one conceptual unit. This dynamic linkage—whether through direct mention or bridging inferences—indicates that explanation and action with its results are cognitively inseparable, forming one integrated decision step.

We define cognitively linked explanations and actions (with their changes on the results) by identifying cases where an explanation explicitly describes an action before or after it is performed, as well as instances where an action is a direct response to an explanation (
Figure 5
). These criteria establish a cognitive connection between what a user articulates and what they do, enabling a structured understanding of their decision-making process.

Figure 5.
Formation of a Cognitive Decision Step by linking explanations with linked actions and artifacts. A cognitive link is established when an explanation directly refers to an associated action or artifact’s part.
This figure shows how \frameworkname{} groups explanations, actions, and artifacts into structured Cognitive Decision Steps. The top row shows explanations grouped by shared goals or referenced elements, while the middle row displays groups of actions performed either on multiple elements using the same type of action or continuously on a single element, each paired with the resulting artifact. The bottom row integrates these layers: cognitively linked explanations are connected to their corresponding action-artifact clusters, forming complete cognitive decision steps.

4.
Design Goals

Based on the proposed
Clear
, we design a system to capture creative professionals’ explicit and implicit decisions during these processes.
Here, we define three design goals for this system:

DG1: Structure the professional’s workflow based on their cognitive decision-making process through their self-explanations, action logs, and task artifacts.
: A creative professional’s expertise and knowledge are embedded in their decision-making process within the context of a task and are reflected through their
self-explanations
,
actions
, and their resulting
artifacts

(
Carroll and Tech 2003
;
Moran and Carroll 1996
)
.
Thus, we aim to capture data from all three channels and, by identifying interconnections between these, we can then structure and contextualize the professionals’ overall decision-making process.

DG2: Detect insufficient rationales in professionals’ decisions and clarify reasoning through in-situ prompting.
: Due to inherent blind spots
(
Wang et al. 2021a
;
Nathan and Petrosino 2003
)
and the cognitive load associated with generating detailed self-explanations
(
Nielsen et al. 2002
)
, experts tend to provide weak explanations
(
Gruber and Russell 1996
)
with insufficient rationale for their decisions.
While professionals can be asked to clarify after completing their workflows, this risks losing critical information regarding their decisions
(
Ericsson and Simon 1993
)
. To address this, we aim to identify decisions where an expert’s rationale is insufficient and prompt clarifications from the user through context-aware, question-based interactions, while allowing them to respond at lightweight, opportune moments without disrupting their workflow.

DG3: Infer the professional’s rationale and document their decision steps to reduce the burden of knowledge-sharing.
: While a professional engages in a workflow, asking them to self-explain and prompting them with clarification questions can potentially capture their cognitive processes and decisions. However, repeatedly answering such questions could impose a significant knowledge-sharing burden that can disrupt the creative workflow
(
van Someren et al. 1994
;
Mark et al. 2008
)
. To alleviate this burden, we aim to infer the rationale behind a professional’s decision automatically based on their previously captured decision steps.

5.
ClearFairy

In this section, we introduce
ClearFairy
through a user scenario, followed by a detailed description of its pipeline.

5.1.
User Scenario and Interface

With about five years of experience as an interface designer, Lillie is preparing materials to show junior designers at her company how she makes design choices and why. While working on her company’s website landing page, Lillie realizes that this could be an ideal moment to explain to junior designers how to apply the company’s design style and rules. Instead of separately working on these materials, she decides to use
ClearFairy

5.2.
Technical Pipeline

In this section, we describe the technical pipelines of
ClearFairy
.
The technical pipeline consists of five interconnected components, each supporting a key stage in the real-time structuring of cognitive decision steps. As illustrated in Figure
10
, the system begins by segmenting the user’s workflow into cognitive decision steps. It then evaluates the sufficiency of the rationale for each step and, when needed, prompts clarification or infers missing reasoning. Finally, it documents the structured decision steps into a searchable and reusable format.

The five components of the pipeline are:

Clear
-Based Workflow Segmentation Pipeline
: Automatically segment a creative professional’s workflow into their cognitive decision steps (
Figure 10
-a)

Rationale Evaluation Module
: Detect cases where the explanation provided by the user in a cognitive decision step either contains insufficient rationale (a weak explanation) or provides no rationale at all (
Figure 10
-b).

Question Generation Module
: In the case of decision steps that have a weak or empty rationale, generate and prompt rationale clarification questions (
Figure 10
-c).

Rationale Inference Module
: Based on the decision steps’ rationale previously provided by the user, infer the user’s rationale for a new decision step that has a weak or empty explanation (
Figure 10
-d).

Decision Step Documentation Pipeline
: This pipeline (
Figure 10
-e) documents the user’s workflow by summarizing (Decision Step Summary Module) and organizing their cognitive decision steps, including:

•

Decision and Actions: the core design decisions and actions (with their details) taken by the user in this step.

•

Rationale: A detailed reason behind the user’s key decision.

•

Progression: A detailed description of how the workflow logically progressed based on the user’s decisions.

•

Screenshots of Artifact: Visual records of the working artifact.

•

Rationale Clarification Question and Answer: If there was a Q&A process in this step.

Each part employs various Large-Language Model- (LLM) and Vision-Language Model- (VLM) based prompting methods.

5.2.1.
Workflow Segmentation into User’s Cognitive Decision Steps Based on
Clear
Framework

Based on the proposed
Clear
in Section
3
, we introduce a method for segmenting a design workflow into the cognitive decision steps using the user’s think-aloud explanations, action logs, and screenshots of the artifact.

To segment workflows into cognitive decision steps, we follow a four-stage process designed to mirror how reasoning unfolds in context. We first group related explanations and actions independently, then link them based on semantic and visual context. Finally, we assign any leftover statements to ensure coherence.

The pipeline consists of four modules that leverage LLM and VLM: 1) sentence grouping (Sentence Linking Module), 2) action grouping (Action Linking Module). 3) linking between sentences and actions (Sentence and Action & Artifact Change Linking Module), and 4) assigning remaining sentences (Sentence Assigning Module).

Explanation sentence Linking Module

The user’s spoken explanations are transcribed with STT (Google Cloud Speech-To-Text
2
2

2

https://cloud.google.com/speech-to-text?hl=en
) and segmented into sentences using the KSS (Korean Sentence Splitter) library.

To find linked sentences, we designed a few-shot prompt (Appendix
A.1.1
) based on
Clear
(Section
3.3.1
) which groups consecutive sentences that describe the same: 1) design element (e.g., font, layout, button), 2) properties (e.g., size, color, shape), 3) design purpose, or 4) a specific design sub-task.

Action Linking Module

Based on Section
3.3.2
, we defined the rules for linking related actions when a user 1) performs the same action consecutively on a single element or 2) takes the same actions on multiple elements simultaneously. In Figma, we can track the user’s action log whenever an action event occurs by using the Figma Plugin API
3
3

3

https://www.figma.com/plugin-docs/
.

The tracked action log stores the ID of the element on which the action was performed and details related to the changes made to that element (e.g., the type of property changed and the specifics of the change). For each action log, we also capture a screenshot of the entire Figma canvas and store it together with the log.

However, even if actions are performed consecutively on a single element, they may have different purposes. For example, adjusting the size of a rectangular button and changing its color could serve different purposes, whereas resizing a rectangular button and moving its position might serve the same layout-related purpose. This indicates that there are various ways to group actions performed on a single element. We compared three methods of linking actions on the same element and selected the approach that groups only sequential identical actions on the same element (see Section
6.2.2
).

Sentence and Action & Artifact Linking Module

After making linked actions and linked sentences, establishing cognitive steps requires linking the actions with the sentences by identifying their relationships as we described in the Framework (Section
5
). To create these links, we designed a zero-shot VLM-based prompt (Appendix
A.1.2
) that finds connections between action-screenshot pairs and sentences.

To perform this task effectively, it is essential to understand the design context. Rather than simply providing action logs and sentences, we include the screenshots as input so that the VLM can better comprehend the design context when creating the links. Specifically, we designed a prompt that links each sentence group to the relevant timestamp(s) in the action logs and their corresponding artifact screenshots whenever the transcript describes or relates to the design action or the artifact’s screenshot. Through this process, segments composed of explanations, actions, and artifacts are created.

Sentence Assigning Module

Since the segments created in the previous linking process are formed solely based on clear and objective relationships between sentences and between sentences and actions, certain types of explanations—such as evaluations or plans (“Looks fine” or “Now, I’m planning to revise this part a bit further”) that might emerge in a think-aloud environment
(
Krosnick et al. 2021
)
—could remain unlinked. To find and decide whether to include these remaining sentences in the most relevant segment or not, we designed another LLM-based assignment module (Appendix
A.1.3
).

In this module, we first gather any sentences not assigned to any segment. For each missing sentence, we prompt the LLM with: (1) the full transcript for broader context; (2) the unassigned sentence itself; (3) the immediately preceding left segment, which is identified by the grouped sentence number in the sentence grouping module; and (4) the immediately following right segment. We instructed the module to choose whether the unassigned sentence should attach to the left segment, the right segment, or be left alone as “unrelated”, based on semantic continuity and additional instructions (e.g., if it describes an evaluation of a just-finished task, assign left; if it proposes a plan for a next step, assign right). This ensures a more natural grouping of any remaining think-aloud statements.

5.2.2.
Rationale Evaluation Module on Cognitive Decision Steps

To check whether each user’s decision steps contain a sufficient rationale for the decision or not, we designed an LLM-based rationale evaluation module. A rationale is an explanation of how and why an artifact, or some portion of it, is designed the way it is
(
Regli et al. 2000
)
. Rationale offers more than the decisions; it also offers the reasons behind each decision, including its justification, other alternatives considered, and argumentation leading to the decision
(
Lee 1997
)
.

Building upon the definition of weak and strong explanation by Gruber and Russell
(
Gruber and Russell 1996
)
, we defined three types of explanation quality: sufficient rationale (“Strong explanation”), insufficient rationale (“Weak explanation”), and empty rationale (“Empty explanation”). Specifically, we defined a specific category within type of explanation based on the prior work.

A strong explanation clearly demonstrates how and why a decision was made, often by 1) referencing situational relevant factors (mark as S-SR)
(
Guarino et al. 2009
)
, 2) drawing on prior knowledge, principles, or heuristics (S-PK)
(
Brockmann and Anthony 2002
)
, or 3) considering alternatives with concrete reasoning on the comparison dimensions (S-CA)
(
Lee 1997
)
.
A weak explanation
(
Gruber and Russell 1996
)
depends on superficial justification on the situation relevance (W-SR), personal preference & heuristics without referencing relevant prior experience (W-PK), or lack of decision factors even when alternatives are mentioned (W-CA). Finally, an empty explanation provides little to no reasoning (E). These categories reflect the essential details that a sufficient rationale must include.

We prompt the few-shot LLM-based module (Appendix
A.2.1
) by providing (1) the definitions of each explanation category (Strong/ Weak/Empty) and (2) a single cognitive decision-step excerpt from the transcript. We instruct the module to identify which of the predefined categories (e.g., S-SR, S-PK, S-CA, W-SR, W-PK, W-CA, E) apply to the designer’s explanation, along with a short rationale for why those categories are chosen. After obtaining the model’s output (e.g., S-SR, W-CA), the code groups the categories as “Strong”, “Weak”, or “Empty”. It then applies simple logic to decide on an overarching type: if both “Strong’ and “Weak” are present, it becomes “strong”; if only “Weak” is present, it becomes “weak”; if Empty is present alone, it becomes “empty”, and so on.

5.2.3.
Question Generation Module

We designed a zero-shot LLM-based module (Appendix
A.3.1
) that generates a clarification question to prompt the users when the Rationale Evaluation Module’s (Appendix
A.2.1
) output is “Weak” or “Empty” explanation. In this prompt, we provide the definitions and criteria like in Appendix
A.2.1
and the Rationale Evaluation together. Finally, the prompt requests a specific, context-based follow-up question that could address the explanation’s shortcomings and help transform it into a “Strong Explanation”.

The generated questions are placed on the relevant design objects in Figma by calculating the object’s location in the decision step’s action logs (
Figure 7
-c). By localizing the questions on their workspace directly, the user can notice it without moving to a separate screen, and they can easily distinguish what parts require more explanation to provide a sufficient rationale.

5.2.4.
Decision Step Summary Module

To document the decision step—when a decision step with a “strong explanation” is captured or when the user responds to a clarification question—we designed an VLM-based module (Appendix
A.4.1
and
A.4.2
) that can summarize the relevant decision step. We prompt the model with the transcript containing that decision step, the specific decision step (excerpted from the overall transcript), and screenshots of the artifacts included in the cognitive decision step. The model is instructed to summarize the main design decisions, actions, rationale, and progression involved in the decision step. The output is then stored in the database, displayed in the web system, and used as input for the rationale inference module.

5.2.5.
Rationale Inference Module

To lower the burden of responding to questions, we designed an LLM-based rationale inference module (Appendix
A.3.2
) that infers a missing or insufficient rationale for a specific decision step by referencing the user’s past, well-justified decisions. We provide the full transcript and the specific decision step, similar to the decision step summary module, and the reasons why the rationale evaluation module classified it under a weak or empty category, including the documented previous decision step summaries.

We instruct the module to (1) review the full context and pinpoint the decision step that lacks rationale, (2) examine previous decision summaries for overlap in specific elements, actions, or design goals, and (3) derive a plausible rationale based on any concretely similar past decisions, rather than abstract parallels or general design principles.

5.3.
Implementation Details

ClearFairy
is implemented as a Figma plugin based on the ReactJS framework for front-end and a Python Flask server with SQLite as the back-end. The LLM and VLM model in every module is OpenAI’s GPT-4o (
gpt-4o-2024-08-16
) with a temperature of 0.3. Our pipeline performs real-time segmentation when thresholds are reached—for example, more than 20 action logs, more than 20 recognized sentences, or a pause longer than three seconds since the last action.

6.
Technical Evaluation

To evaluate the performance of our workflow segmentation pipeline (Section
5.2.1
) and rationale evaluation module (Section
5.2.2
), we conducted a technical evaluation through a data collection study. In the study, we collected three types of workflow data from six interface designers: 1) think-aloud recordings, 2) action log data, and 3) screenshots of the entire content in the design file. Then, we processed the collected data to create ground-truth dataset of cognitive decision steps and rationale evaluations for each decision step, and then evaluated our pipeline’s output on this dataset.

6.1.
Data Collection Study

To capture the self-explanations, action logs, and screenshots, we invited designers to use our system without the question generation and rationale inference features.

6.1.1.
Participants

The study involved six professionals (4 females and 2 males; Age_MEAN=25.5, SD=1.2) who were compensated at a rate of 50,000 KRW/hour for participation. Every participant has an educational background in design (e.g., major in graphic design, industrial design, or interface design) and design experience of more than 1 year in their field (MEAN=2.9 years and SD=0.6).

6.1.2.
Procedure

The data collection process was structured into two phases that took around 60 minutes in total: an introduction phase (15 mins) and a design task phase (40 mins). Participants were instructed to imagine themselves as a live streamer who is demonstrating and explaining their design process to a live audience. They worked on two given tasks: designing the main page for a design portfolio website, and the project page for the same website. During each task, participants were instructed to start the system’s recording when they started a design step and stop the recording when said step was completed. During recording, participants explained their thought process following the think-aloud protocol
(
Ericsson and Simon 1980
)
.

6.1.3.
Statistics of the Collected Data

A total of 52 recordings were collected, excluding three recordings due to excessively long action logs that surpassed the gpt-4o-2024-08-16 model’s max input token length. The average length of a recording was 205.90 seconds (SD=65.43, MIN=99, MAX=313), resulting in a total of 10,707 seconds of recorded data. The average number of action logs per recording was 36.81 (SD=12.53, MIN=13, MAX=67), bringing the total to 1,914 actions. The average number of sentences in the transcribed audio was 14.85 per recording (SD=9.75, MIN=4, MAX=47), totaling 772 sentences across all recordings.

6.1.4.
Creating the Ground-Truth Dataset

To create the ground-truth dataset, two authors watched through the recordings and segmented them into decision steps. Specifically, the authors focused on segmenting the transcripts of the audio recordings. The two authors first segmented the transcripts for 12 recordings (23% of the total) individually and then compared the results. The inter-rater reliability (IRR) based on Cohen’s Kappa was 0.672, indicating substantial agreement. Any conflicts in the segmentations of the initial 12 recordings were merged. Afterward, the two authors each segmented 20 more videos, creating a ground-truth dataset of transcripts segmented into decision steps for 52 recordings.

6.2.
Performance of Cognitive Decision Step Segmentation Pipeline

We verified the design of our segmentation pipeline by conducting an ablation study where we tested the effect of each module in Section
5.2.1
. We compared the results with a baseline, which segmented the recordings based on only the transcript data—adopting the segmentation prompt from TutoAI
(
Chen et al. 2024a
)
. The baseline instructs an LLM to segment the transcript at points where the user makes distinct decisions by considering the relationship between sentences in the transcript.
All modules in our pipeline and the baseline used gpt-4o-2024-08-16 through the OpenAI API with the temperature set to 0.0.

6.2.1.
Metrics

To evaluate the performance of segmentation, we calculated the precision and recall of the predicted transcript segmentations. Also, we utilized the WindowDiff metric
(
Pevzner and Hearst 2002
)
, which measures the difference between two texts by comparing the lengths of matching and non-matching segments in a sliding window to account for minor segmentation misalignments.

6.2.2.
Results

5.2.1
), we considered that there could be multiple distinct methods to group actions performed on a single element in Figma. Rather than deciding on a single method from the start, we explored how performance at segmenting cognitive decision steps depends on how we link the actions on an element. Specifically, we defined the following three versions of action grouping:

•

V1: Group all consecutive actions on a single element, regardless of action type.

•

V2: Group consecutive actions if they are all
layout-related
(i.e., changing shape or position) or if they are all
style-related
(i.e., any action not related to layout).

•

).

Although V1 shows overall good performance, it groups too many actions together, causing it to segment accurate decision steps too broadly and, consequently, perform lower on recall.

6.3.
Performance of Rationale Evaluation Module

Accurately evaluating whether a user has provided sufficient rationale for a cognitive decision step is crucial to ensure that
ClearFairy
only asks necessary clarification questions. In this section, we assess how closely our Rationale Evaluation Module’s (Section
5.2.2
) judgments align with human judgments.

6.3.1.
Ground-Truth Dataset and Metrics

Creating the Ground-Truth Dataset

From the 52 collected recordings, we created a total of 292 cognitive decision steps, with an average of 48.67 decision steps per participant (SD=15.31, MAX=68, MIN=32). For each of these collected steps, two authors evaluated whether the step contained a strong, weak, or empty explanation to create our ground-truth dataset. First, we randomly sampled 20% of all the decision steps (60 decision steps) and the two authors independently evaluated all steps in this subset—resulting in a Cohen’s Kappa of 0.705, indicating substantial agreement. Subsequently, each author annotated an additional 20% of the data and creating a total of 180 annotated decision steps—around 65% of all collected steps. Among these 180 data points, there were 30 strong explanations, 108 weak explanations, and 42 empty explanations.

Metric

We calculated the average accuracy of the Rationale Evaluation Module by comparing its predicted evaluations of the explanations for each cognitive step against the ground-truth evaluations.

6.3.2.
Results

The results indicate that our Rationale Evaluation Module aligns with human evaluations for the decision steps with 80.19% accuracy (SD=1.4%). We report accuracy for each explanation type: accuracy was 81.11% accuracy (SD=1.92%) for “Strong explanations”, 77.78% (SD=1.5%) for “Weak explanations”, and 85.71% accuracy (SD=2.38%) for “Empty explanations”.

7.
User study

Our evaluation aims to address the following research questions:

•

RQ1: How does
ClearFairy
’s questioning and clarification process during the user’s decision steps help in explaining explicit and implicit decisions?

•

RQ2: How is the rationale inferred by
ClearFairy
received, and what impact does providing such inference have on the self-explanation process?

•

RQ3: How can the decisions captured in the workflow through
ClearFairy
and the documented decisions be utilized?

To answer these research questions, we conducted a within-subjects user study with eight interface designers, comparing
ClearFairy
with a baseline---a think-aloud condition without question-based interaction---to evaluate its effectiveness in knowledge capture. We provided the designers with two different web design tasks, and they performed self-explanation-based web design as a form of design livestreaming
4
4

4

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=clSHs94hNNc
using both
ClearFairy
and the baseline.

7.1.
Participants

We recruited twelve creative professionals (compensated 50,000 KRW/hour) who either majored in design-related fields (e.g., graphic, industrial, or interface design) and who are currently working as interface or UI/UX designers.
The participants (7 females and 5 males; MEAN_age=29.3, SD_age=3.8) had, on average, 5.5 years of experience in UI/UX design (SD=4.1 years, MAX=15 years, MIN=1.4 years)

7.2.
Task Design and Procedure

The study was conducted in person. Participants were assigned two distinct web interface design tasks in a knowledge-sharing scenario: one involved designing an HCI lab’s website, which required making a wide range of design choices, and the other involved designing a basic product page for an e-commerce website, which entailed fewer design choices and adhered to more predefined rules. We showed several examples of knowledge-sharing videos
5
5

5

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Q2IupOJHrzM
before starting the task.

To reflect realistic design workflows, participants prepared reference websites ahead of time and referred to them during the design process. Standardized design assets were provided for both tasks. For the lab website task, this included the lab name, introductory text, and project information. For the e-commerce website task, participants received product images, details, and product category information.

Following a 15-minute introduction, participants worked for 30 minutes in each condition, either with
ClearFairy
or the baseline system. After each session, participants completed a survey lasting approximately 5 minutes. The order of tasks and conditions was counterbalanced to mitigate ordering effects. Finally, a 30-minute in-depth interview was conducted to gather in-depth feedback. During the in-depth interview process, we reviewed the participant’s documented decisions from the workflow together and discussed usage scenarios regarding how these captured decisions could be utilized either by themselves or by others (e.g., novice designers or collaborators).

7.3.
Measures

As a quantitative metric, we compared the total number of strong, weak, and empty explanations at the level of each captured decision step—evaluating whether each step included a sufficient rationale. After the study, participants’ answers were processed by our rationale evaluation module (Section
5.2.2
) to assess whether the clarification process captured missing rationales. Additionally, in the
ClearFairy
condition, we measured the acceptance rate of system-inferred rationales during the workflow.

For qualitative evaluation, we incorporated NASA-TLX items
(
Hart and Staveland 1988
)
into the survey to assess task load and added questions to gauge participants’ perceptions of the self-explanation-based workflow. These questions focused on whether the process clarified their decision-making and underlying rationale, improved the articulation of applied knowledge, promoted reflection on decisions, and fostered metacognitive
(
Livingston 2003
)
insights. Participants also rated the ease of expressing their knowledge, the perceived burden, and the overall effectiveness of capturing both explicit and implicit knowledge (7-Likert scale). To analyze these non-parametric data, we conducted a Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

During the in-depth interviews, participants compared the two conditions in terms of the knowledge capture process and the cognitive burdens experienced. Also, they discussed how sharing their workflow while receiving system-generated questions differed from similar real-world scenarios (such as giving presentations or teaching over Zoom
6
6

6

https://www.zoom.com/
). In the
ClearFairy
condition, we asked about the impact, quantity, and quality of the system’s questions. Participants also considered what knowledge or insights might have been missed without questions and evaluated how capturing their workflow in this manner could benefit both themselves and others. Finally, while reviewing the documented decisions within the workflow, we discussed how these captured decisions could be utilized.

8.
Results

This section describes the findings of our user study, organized around the three research questions. First, we examine how
ClearFairy
’s questioning and clarification process supports users in articulating both explicit and implicit rationales of their decision steps (RQ1). Second, we analyze how the inferred rationales are received and how they impact the self-explanation process, particularly in aspects of minimizing cognitive burden and enabling deeper reflection (RQ2). Finally, we explore how the captured and documented decisions can be utilized in various usage scenarios, including by the designers themselves, novice designers, collaborators, and design assistants in the form of generative agents (RQ3).

8.1.
RQ1: Capturing Rationales Behind Decisions

In the baseline and system conditions, participants created, on average, 49.33 (SD=14.01) and 34.75 (SD=7.44) decision steps, respectively, and provided weak or empty explanations for 86.14% (SD=9.9%) and 80.67% (SD=10.58%) of those steps on average (
Figure 12
-Baseline). However, through the Q&A process with participants,
ClearFairy
transformed an average of 79.13% (SD=9.52%) of decision steps that were initially judged as weak or empty. As a result,
ClearFairy
captured strong explanations containing sufficient rationale for 83.16% (SD=8.07%) of all decision steps explained by participants. In contrast, in the baseline condition, only 13.86% (SD=9.87%) of the captured decision steps were accompanied by strong explanations (
Figure 12
-
ClearFairy
).

Participants reported in the survey that during the self-explanation process, the system’s questions enabled them to explain not only their explicit decisions (
Figure 11
; Q16, Mean_diff=1.58, p<0.01) but also those that were implicit decisions that felt so natural to them that they would not have thought to explain them otherwise (
Figure 11
; Q17, Mean_diff=3.42, p<0.01). Through this process, participants noted that they were not only able to clarify their rationale more explicitly (
Figure 11
; Q7, Mean_diff=1.50, p<0.01), but also articulate the knowledge they were drawing on (
Figure 11
; Q8, Mean_diff=1.33, p<0.01). Furthermore, they reported gaining a better understanding of how to provide good explanations (
Figure 11
; Q12, Mean_diff=1.75, p<0.01).

Participants (P1, P3, P5–P7, P9, P11–P12) explained that the process enabled them to articulate implicit decisions that had previously felt too, natural, intuitive, or self-evident to describe. Also, some participants (P2, P3, P5–P10) added that the questions corresponded well with their decision units and encouraged them to reflect more deeply on the reasoning behind their decisions. After going through this Q&A process, they also found it easier to anticipate the kinds of questions that might follow based on the explanations they had provided.

ClearFairy
’s Q&A process revealed several implicit knowledge types that informed design decisions. For instance, decisions based on
expert conventions
were noted when participants explained that placing key elements—such as title or crucial navigation tabs—in the upper-left corner aligns with standard reading patterns and common industry practices. Similarly,
aesthetic intuition
played a role, with participants describing adjustments to button colors, background hues, or text highlights in order to maintain visual harmony and convey a refined design tone. Some decisions were influenced by
personal preferences
, where designers opted to retain familiar system elements or choose device-specific layouts that best suited their individual tastes. Additionally,
tool usage experience
was evident as participants detailed leveraging features like Figma’s auto-layout hug setting and component definition settings in their design software to manage spacing efficiently and adapt to varying content sizes. Decisions regarding
the design process itself
emerged when participants emphasized setting up frames, grids, or transitioning to subsequent design phases as foundational steps.
Other captured knowledge types included
efficiency strategies
,
application of design principles
, and
trial-and-error processes
, such as reusing and adjusting previous components, refining visual details, and comparing layout alternatives.

8.2.
RQ2: Supporting Effective Knowledge Sharing Without Added Burden

The Q&A process with
ClearFairy
helped participants articulate their rationales more easily and reduced the burden typically associated with knowledge sharing (
Figure 11
; Q15, Mean_diff=2.08, p<0.01). Participants (P1, P6-P8, P10-P11) noted that, compared to explaining continuously on their own in the baseline condition, the prompted questions provided moments to pause and reflect more deeply (
Figure 11
; Q9, Mean_diff=2.83, p<0.01). Despite feeling there were more questions than typically asked by audiences, all participants found the burden low due to the system’s inference support. There is also no significant difference in NASA-TLX survey results (
Figure 11
; Q1-Q6, p>0.05).

In the workflow,
ClearFairy
generated an average of 20.25 inferred rationales (SD=8.59), accounting for 68.44% of weak and empty explanations. Importantly, participants did not blindly accept the system’s inferences: knowing that their responses would influence the quality of future inferences, they carefully reviewed each one—on average supplementing 24.20% (SD=15.71%) and rejecting 15.01% (SD=6.68%) (
Figure 12
). While a few participants (P4 and P10) felt that some inferences were slightly repetitive due to limited initial input, the majority (P1–P3, P5–P9, P11–P12) emphasized that these inferences helped them refine their reasoning. They often treated them as
rationale-eliciting inferences
, using the system’s plausible suggestions as prompts to elaborate more detailed and nuanced rationales, which represented 24.2% (SD=15.71%) of all inference cases (
Figure 12
-
ClearFairy
’s Inferred Rationale).

Participants also found the interaction natural (
Figure 11
; Q13, Mean_diff=1.58, p<0.05), particularly appreciating the ability to control response timing and resume their workflow smoothly. As they provided richer explanations, subsequent inferences became more specific and aligned with their thinking, creating a productive feedback loop. This loop also enabled participants to surface and articulate hidden rationales that they would not have explained otherwise, without adding extra burden. This process not only reduced the burden of knowledge-sharing but also deepened reflection: participants gained greater awareness of what they knew, the direction they were pursuing, and how they might proceed with their work (
Figure 11
; Q10, Mean_diff=1.75, p<0.01; Q11, Mean_diff=1.92, p<0.01), highlighting the system’s potential to support metacognitive insight
(
Livingston 2003
)
.

8.3.
RQ3: Diverse Usage Scenarios of Decisions Captured by
ClearFairy

To explore how captured decision steps could be used in different contexts during the interview session, we discussed the potential value of documentation (
Figure 9
) from multiple perspectives: designers themselves, novices, collaborators, and design agents.

From the Designer’s Own Perspective

Participants found the documented decision steps helpful for revisiting their own design rationale, especially during revision or when facing design challenges (P3, P4, and P7, P10-P11). Some viewed it as a simulation before explaining their work to others (P2 and P12), and others reflected on how to streamline their future workflows (P6). Several participants expressed interest in reusing the captured intuitions for future projects (P3, P8-P9).

From the Perspective of Novice Designers

Participants believed the documentation could help novices learn not just outcomes but also trial-and-error processes and decision-making strategies (P1, P3, P6, and P10). It was seen as a valuable educational tool to understand how others tackle design problems, although filtering might be necessary due to information density (P2, P7, and P11-P12).

From the Perspective of Collaborators

Participants noted that understanding the reasoning behind design decisions could improve collaboration, especially when building shared design systems or communicating with clients or developers (P1, P5, P7-P9). Documentation of decisions enabled collaborators to trace back choices, reducing unnecessary trial and error (P3-P4, and P10).

From the Perspective of Design Agents

Participants explained that if agents could access their cognitive decision steps, they could learn personal preferences and offer better reference-based suggestions (P1, P9, and P12). Repetitive tasks like alignment or arrangement layout could be automated in a personalized way (P2, P3, P5, and P12). Others wanted agents to propose multiple alternatives and receive feedback, like a Github Copilot in their process (P3, P4, P6-P7, and P10-P12). Some suggested that by understanding their patterns, agents could also support more creative directions (P8-P9, and P12).

9.
Application of Captured Cognitive Decision Steps

To demonstrate the practical value and utility of the detailed cognitive decision steps captured by our framework, this section presents two applications: predicting a professional’s next action (decision and rationale) in a creative workflow, and generating actual artifacts with Model Context Protocol
7
7

7

https://modelcontextprotocol.io/introduction
-based design agents with tool-calling capabilities in Figma.

9.1.
Next Decision Step Generation

The cognitive decision steps captured through our framework and system provide rich information about a user’s unique workflow.
This information could be leveraged by generative agents
(
Huang et al. 2024
;
Park et al. 2023
)
to infer the user’s next action based on the current workflow to provide shadowing support (e.g., GitHub Copilot
8
8

8

https://code.visualstudio.com/docs/copilot/overview
) by providing suggestions or proactively performing actions for the user, as suggested by our study participants (Section
8.3
).
To explore how captured cognitive decision steps influence generative models’ ability to infer users’ next actions, we conducted a small-scale experiment comparing predictions made with and without these decision steps as context.

9.1.1.
Approach

Participants in the user study performed two different web design tasks under two different conditions (Section
7.2
). From the decision steps collected in the baseline condition, we randomly selected five instances of consecutive pairs of steps.

For each pair, we generated next-step summaries in three ways: (1) without any information (Baseline), (2) using captured decision steps from the
ClearFairy
condition but excluding the user’s clarified rationale data (Without Rationale), and (3) including the user’s clarified rationale data from the
ClearFairy
condition (With Rationale). Participants were then shown summaries of the two preceding steps (see the examples in Appendix
B.1
) along with relevant workflow screenshots and asked to select which of the three steps presented the most suitable “next step” in the workflow. Specifically, they were instructed to pick the next step they would most likely take. Each participant completed this selection process for five distinct cases and additionally provided a long-form explanation about why they did not choose the other options.

9.1.2.
Results

Across the five survey cases per participant, the baseline was selected an average of 1.00 times (SD = 0.74). Participants who did not choose the Baseline in certain cases stated that it felt too different from the previous steps or contained “hallucinated” decisions or actions. In the cases where the baseline was chosen, participants believed it included more plausible and high-level decisions (not specific), even if it lacked certain detailed descriptions of actions.

Meanwhile, the “With Rationale” condition was chosen 3.33 times on average (SD = 0.65). Participants who chose “With Rationale” noted that the “Without Rationale” condition’s rationale seemed insufficient or inappropriate for them, whereas those who rejected “With Rationale” viewed it as having fewer or more minimal actions, making it appear less comprehensive. The “Without Rationale” version was chosen on average 0.67 times (SD = 0.65).

We observed that when the next step was generated based on captured decision steps, it tended to produce finer-grained decisions. Furthermore, in the “With Rationale” condition, the decision step closely reflected the rationale of the user who had shared it. While generated decisions in the baseline showed the highest level of granularity, they often produced coarse-grained decisions, sometimes abruptly shifting to a different sub-task without completing the current one. In contrast, when the next steps were generated using the actual decision steps (in both “With Rationale” and “Without Rationale” conditions), no hallucinations were reported.

9.2.
Design Artifact Creation with Tool-Calling Agent

We investigate whether incorporating human decision steps into the agent’s design generation process results in differences in the produced design artifacts. This exploration aims to examine whether providing an agent with professionals’ cognitive decision steps can guide its tool-based design process to produce final artifacts that align with professionals’ output.

9.2.1.
Approach

We implemented a basic agent that can design directly in Figma. Specifically, we leverage the implementation of the Figma plugin, ‘‘Talk to Figma
9
9

9

https://github.com/sonnylazuardi/cursor-talk-to-figma-mcp
’’, which provides a Model Context Protocol
10
10

10

https://modelcontextprotocol.io/introduction
that allows agents to call “tools” in Figma (e.g., commands or actions like
create_rectangle
) through Figma’s API (see the detailed propmt in Appendix
B.2.2
). Our agent can call 49 specialized tools (Appendix
B.2.4
), which are organized into 6 functional categories (e.g., Style Tools) to enable comprehensive design automation. We implemented the agent based on the ReAct structure
(
yao2022react
)
, which prompts the LLM to perform structured reasoning-action loops with a self-feedback module (Appendix
B.2.3
), guiding the agent to reason about and refine its outputs through multiple iterations. We carefully engineered the prompts to guide the agent by including Figma API usage.

We explored two conditions for design artifact generation: one where the tool-calling agent operated with only the task prompt, and another where we additionally provided design style guidelines extracted from captured decision steps, enabling the agent to align outputs with professionals’ style. To explore this, we conducted artifact generation across tasks at various granularities, including component-level (e.g., subscribe button, navigation menu buttons), section-level (e.g., navigation bar, hero section), and page-level designs (e.g., video streaming main page, startup landing page).

9.2.2.
Results

Figure 13
shows the overall result of the generated artifacts by our agent. Depending on the given style guidelines from decision steps, the generated artifacts include different styles specifically, regarding the size, color, or layout. An interesting observation is that when a professional’s decision step captured the process of componentizing an element to reuse the element (e.g., button), this was reflected in the extracted design guidelines, and the agent accordingly produced the button in a component form (
Figure 13
-a, P4). Another example is when the guideline indicated that color should be used to emphasize certain text; we observed that this choice was indeed manifested in the resulting artifact (
Figure 13
-b, P9).

Overall, the agent occasionally produced incomplete layouts, particularly at higher levels of granularity where more complex artifacts were required (
Figure 13
-c). However, outputs guided by decision step–derived guidelines incorporated significantly more core elements compared to the baseline without such guidelines (
Figure 13
-c).

In this exploration, we observed that even subtle but consistent differences in style and content emerged in the generated artifacts depending on the captured decision steps. At the same time, we also found that the tool-calling agent’s overall performance in designing on the canvas (e.g., using frame auto-layout much like a human) was limited. Its effectiveness strongly depended on how the agent itself was designed, including factors such as prompt engineering, the range and type of available tools, and the inclusion of feedback modules within the agent pipeline.

10.
Discussion

This section describes the broader implications of capturing more implicit human cognitive decision steps, focusing on their potential applications in supporting learning and advancing generative agents, as well as the generalizability and limitations of our approach.

10.1.
Beyond Natural Language: Supporting More Implicit Decisions through Multimodal Interaction

While many participants found
ClearFairy
helpful for articulating implicit reasoning, participants (P1, P4, P6, and P11) noted that certain decisions were difficult to explain using natural language alone. Instead, they expressed a preference for referencing previously recorded decision steps or directly annotating specific areas of the current artifact (similar to Stylette’s approach
(
Kim et al. 2022
)
) to convey their thinking and perspective.
This reflects the nature of creative workflows, where many decisions are deeply embedded in the contextual fabric of the work
(
Fischer et al. 2020
)
. To better support such workflows, future work can consider alternative response modalities beyond language—such as direct visual annotations, referencing past interactions, or selecting from visual examples.

P2, P3, and P9 suggested going beyond simple rationale clarifications and envisioned
ClearFairy
conducting a design evaluation-based review of the user’s design, followed by Q&A to elicit otherwise implicit intentions.
While
ClearFairy
already surfaces implicit decisions through its clarification and inference process, this feedback points to future directions where generative models could extend such capabilities by engaging in UI design review–based
(
Duan et al. 2024
)
questioning to capture hidden layers of reasoning.

10.2.
Applications of Human Cognitive Decision Steps in Learning

Captured cognitive decision steps—especially those of professionals—present a valuable opportunity to support novices. This aligns with the notion of over-the-shoulder learning, where an expert looks over the shoulder of a novice’s workflow, helping them as they accomplish a task
(
Twidale 2005
)
. The cognitive apprenticeship framework
(
Collins et al. 1991
;
Li et al. 2024
)
also emphasizes modeling expert thinking to support novice understanding. If professional decision steps and their accompanying rationales can be captured as structured data, they could serve as reusable, interactive guidance during novice workflows. Several participants (P1, P3–P7, and P11-P12) mentioned this type of application during interviews, noting its potential to help novices directly observe how professionals navigate a sequence of decisions.
For instance, when a novice encounters a decision point, the system could present similar scenarios in which professionals made cognitive decisions, along with their reasoning. This form of interaction may promote deeper internalization and embodiment of professional strategies and decision-making processes
(
Son et al. 2024
)
.

10.3.
Capturing Human Cognitive Decision Steps for Agent Planning and Action

Generative model-based agents typically consist of a planning module for generating task sequences, a memory module for referencing prior experiences, and an action module for selecting the next move
(
Huang et al. 2024
;
Park et al. 2023
)
. While recent work aims to strengthen the reasoning process of these agents and utilize it as a foundation for subsequent actions
(
Sun et al. 2025
;
Yang et al. 2025
)
, the reasoning generated by generative models often remains high-level and lacks the fine-grained detail required for precise decision-making. As demonstrated in our Application section (Section
9.1
), the actions generated by LLMs are frequently coarse, occasionally skipping intermediate steps. For instance, after creating a button in the top navigation bar, the agent might skip the necessary adjustment of its alignment and prematurely proceed to populate the body content.

In contrast, the cognitive decision steps captured through our system are more fine-grained, reflecting the sequence of micro-decisions that occur during professionals’ workflows. As prior work (e.g., Auto-Intent
(
Kim et al. 2024
)
) demonstrated that LLM-generated intents improved agent performance, we argue that capturing human cognitive decision steps and incorporating them into agents’ action or planning modules can similarly enhance the agent’s ability to execute more deliberate, precise, and context-sensitive actions. As shown in our second exploration (Section
9.2
), these finer-grained actions led to more coherent artifacts that aligned more closely with professional outputs.

As demonstrated in our second exploration (Section
9.2
), these finer-grained actions not only improved the agent’s process but also led to final artifacts that were more structured, stylistically consistent, and better aligned with professional outputs.

10.4.
Generalizability of
Clear
Beyond Creative Workflows

While
Clear
was designed with creative workflows for knowledge-sharing and demonstrated through UI/UX design scenarios, the underlying principles are rooted in general cognitive theories. Thus, its applicability extends beyond creative tasks. Any domain that involves iterative decision-making—such as literature reviews in research, idea exploration for innovation, or even collaborative workflows with LLMs—could benefit from structuring and capturing cognitive decision steps.
Moreover,
Clear
does not impose a fixed granularity for decision segmentation. Instead, it allows steps to be flexibly structured according to the nature of the task, the user’s workflow, and the user’s thought process. This adaptability suggests that
Clear
has the potential for wide generalizability across domains where fine-grained reasoning and decision tracking are critical.

10.5.
Limitations and Future Work

This work has several limitations: First, some participants (P4 and P5) noted that the types of questions asked by
ClearFairy
were often similar. Since the current Question Generation Module focuses solely on rationale clarification, future work should explore a wider variety of question types—such as Socratic Questions
(
Padesky 1993
)
—to capture cognitive decision steps more comprehensively.

Second, the system is primarily designed to evaluate rationales revealed through self-explanations. However, it is also important to identify and clarify cognitive decision steps based on actions alone, even when no accompanying explanation is provided. Actions can implicitly reflect reasoning, and future work should explore how unlinked actions may convey intent and how such reasoning can be captured.

Third, while
ClearFairy
is currently implemented as a Figma plugin, it should be extended to support more general creative workflows. Many creative tasks unfold across different software platforms, and applying
Clear
in such contexts may require developing an OS-level system for broader workflow capture.

Lastly, although participants actively reviewed and sometimes rejected or elaborated on system-generated inferences, future work should consider cases of misinference and the risk of users’ over-reliance. Future systems could mitigate these issues by providing lightweight cues such as confidence indicators or tags for potentially weak inferences to support more calibrated trust.

11.
Conclusion

This work introduced
Clear
, a conceptual framework for structuring creative professionals’ decision-making into cognitive decision steps, and
ClearFairy
, a think-aloud AI assistant that operationalizes the framework within a creative workflow. By linking self-explanations, actions, and artifacts in real time, our system detects weak rationales, generates clarifying questions, and infers missing reasoning to capture richer explanations while reducing the knowledge-sharing burden. In a study with 12 creative professionals,
ClearFairy
significantly increased the proportion of strong explanations and surfaced implicit decisions while minimizing cognitive burden. Beyond empirical validation, this work explored how captured decision steps can be reused for generative applications, including next-action inference and tool-based design simulation, where they enhanced the alignment, coherence, and professional quality of outputs. We also released a dataset of 417 decision steps to support future research. Taken together, our contributions highlight the potential of cognitive decision steps as a foundation for knowledge sharing, reflective practice, and more deliberate and human-aligned AI agents in creative workflows.

Figma tool operation guidelines:

1. Figma Tool Basics

- Each design element (node) is either a container (Frame/Component) or a leaf (Shape/Text).

- Nodes expose uniform structural properties regardless of type.

- Coordinates are global, relative to the canvas origin (0, 0) at the top-left corner.

- Do not assume local coordinates unless explicitly provided.

2. Node Hierarchy

- All nodes belong to a single rooted tree mirroring the layer list.

- Parent-child relationships define the hierarchy; a node’s index in its parent determines both z-order and sidebar order.

- If a child’s size exceeds its parent container’s bounds and the parent has ’clipsContent’ enabled, the excess will not be visible.

- If an element is placed inside a frame but positioned outside the frame’s bounds (while ’clipsContent’ is enabled), it will be unobservable.

- Do not place nodes directly at the root unless they are intentional top-level elements.

COMPONENT CREATION RULES:

- For buttons: Create a frame with background color, corner radius, and auto-layout, then add text inside it. Do NOT create separate rectangle shapes inside the frame.

- For logos: Create a frame with the logo’s background shape (color, border radius, etc.), then add text or vector elements inside it. The frame itself should be the logo’s visual container.

- For cards: Create a frame with background and styling, then add content (text, images) inside it.

- For navigation bars: Create a frame with background, then add navigation items inside it.

- The frame itself should be the visual component, not just a container for separate shapes.

POSITIONING EFFICIENCY:

- Plan element positions before creating them to minimize move_node calls.

- Use Auto Layout strategically - if you enable it, let it handle positioning automatically.

- Avoid excessive repositioning - create elements in their intended locations when possible.

- Only use move_node when absolutely necessary for final adjustments.

3. Container Layout (Auto Layout)

- Use frames strategically for components that need to contain other elements (e.g., buttons with text, cards with content, navigation bars).

- IMPORTANT: When creating interactive components like buttons, the frame itself should serve as the visual shape/background, not as a separate container.

- For buttons: Create a frame with the desired background color, corner radius, and styling, then add text directly inside it using auto-layout.

- Avoid creating separate shape elements inside frames for simple components - let the frame be the component’s visual representation.

- When Auto Layout is applied, Figma automatically manages direction, gap, padding, and resizing of the container and its children.

- Manual size or position edits must account for Auto Layout’s automatic adjustments.

- Enable Auto Layout only when certain about the intended layout behavior; otherwise, it may cause unexpected shifts.

- When adding children to an Auto Layout container, verify that spacing and alignment remain correct.

- CRITICAL: If content overflows outside the container and becomes hidden, immediately resize the frame so that all content remains visible. Never leave child nodes partially or completely hidden.

AUTO LAYOUT POSITIONING RULES:

- NEVER use ’move_node’ inside Auto Layout frames - Auto Layout will override absolute positioning.

- To reorder elements in Auto Layout: Use ’move_node_into_frame’ with the ’index’ parameter to specify the desired position (0 = first, 1 = second, etc.).

- To change stacking order: Use ’reorder_node’ with ’TOP’, ’BOTTOM’, ’FORWARD’, or ’BACKWARD’ directions.

- To adjust spacing: Modify the Auto Layout frame’s gap, padding, or alignment properties instead of moving individual nodes.

- If you need precise positioning: Consider disabling Auto Layout temporarily, make adjustments, then re-enable it.

FRAME VISIBILITY RULES:

- Ensure child nodes are visible when they should be: If nodes inside a frame are cut off or hidden when they should be visible, use ’resize_node’ to increase the frame’s width and height to accommodate all content.

- Check frame bounds when adding elements: When adding elements to a frame, verify that the frame’s width and height are sufficient to display all children. Use ’resize_node’ if adjustments are needed.

- Use ’clipsContent’ wisely: If a frame has ’clipsContent’ enabled, ensure the frame is large enough to show all intended content by using ’resize_node’.

- Adjust frame size when content is hidden: After adding new elements to a frame, use ’resize_node’ to expand the frame dimensions if any content becomes hidden or cut off.

- Consider padding: Account for frame padding when calculating required frame dimensions. Use ’resize_node’ to add extra space for padding.

- MANDATORY: If any child element is not fully visible within its parent frame when it should be visible, you MUST use ’resize_node’ to increase the frame’s dimensions until all content is completely visible.

4. Text Mechanics

- Text nodes have font family, style, size, and other typographic properties independent of layout.

- Resizing a text node does not scale the text; overflow text is clipped or wrapped based on settings.

- Always check node size and alignment to avoid unintended overflow.

- When placing text inside Auto Layout, ensure padding and alignment work together with text constraints.

5. Naming Rules

- Every node must have a unique, descriptive name.

- Do not assign the same name to multiple nodes.

- If a name already exists, generate a unique variant (e.g., ’btn_primary_v2’).

- Use consistent naming patterns for similar element types (e.g., ’btn_primary’, ’btn_secondary’, ’txt_title’).

6. Preserve Existing Structure

- Avoid unnecessary changes to nodes unrelated to the current task.

- Do not delete, rename, or move unrelated layers.

7. Property Changes Must Be Intentional

- Only modify properties (size, color, position, etc.) relevant to the requested change.

- Keep unrelated attributes (e.g., shadows, strokes) intact.

8. Maintain Consistency

- If multiple elements share a style (color, typography, size), maintain that consistency unless explicitly instructed otherwise.

9. Avoid Overlapping or Hidden Elements

- Ensure new elements do not unintentionally overlap or become hidden behind other layers.

10. Validate Final Layout

- After modifications, confirm that hierarchy, naming, alignment, and spacing remain correct and consistent.
