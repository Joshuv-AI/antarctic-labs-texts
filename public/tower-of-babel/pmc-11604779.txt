A ferritin-related dietary pattern is positively associated with iron status but negatively associated with vitamin D status in pregnant women: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Arpita Das, Chyi-Huey Bai, Jung-Su Chang, Ya-Li Huang, Fan-Fen Wang, Chien-Yeh Hsu, Yi-Chun Chen, Jane C-J Chao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeVitamin D supplementation positively impacts anemia, but the relationship between a ferritin-related dietary pattern, iron parameters, and vitamin D in anemia is unclear.MethodsThis cross-sectional study analyzed data from the 2017–2019 National Nutrition and Health Survey, including 1423 pregnant women over 15 years old. Dietary intake was assessed using a semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire and a 24-hour dietary recall. The FrDP was identified using reduced rank regression with blood parameters as response variables and 32 food groups as predictors. Linear and binomial logistic regression analyses evaluated the associations between the FrDP and serum biomarkers, adjusting for demographic and dietary variables. Risk associations between the FrDP and vitamin D concentrations were also assessed.ResultsThe FrDP explained 18.5% of the variation in 6 response variables and was characterized by high consumption of fish, soy, gluten pasta, nuts, organ meat, pickled vegetables, and marine plants. The FrDP correlated positively with serum hemoglobin (r = 0.76), iron (r = 0.52), ferritin (r = 0.79), folate (r = 0.86), and vitamin B12 (r = 0.86). Linear regression revealed a positive association between the FrDP and serum iron. Women in the highest FrDP tertile were less likely to have low serum iron (OR = 0.65, 95% CI 0.50–0.85) but more likely to have low 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations (OR = 1.79, 95% CI 1.32–2.43).ConclusionsThe FrDP was positively associated with serum iron but negatively associated with serum 25(OH) vitamin D in pregnant women.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00394-024-03547-z.Keywords: Dietary pattern, Ferritin, Iron, Vitamin D, Pregnant women

Introduction

Anemia in pregnancy has emerged as a significant global public health concern, impacting 36.5% of pregnant women in 2019 [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) defined gestational anemia with hemoglobin (Hb) concentration < 6.83 mmol/L (< 11 g/dL) [2], and recommended serum ferritin concentration < 0.034 nmol/L (< 15 µg/L) during the first trimester as an indicator of iron deficiency in pregnant women [3]. Notably, data revealed that Africa exhibited the highest prevalence of gestational anemia at 57.1%, and closely followed by Southeast Asia at 48.2% [4]. A recent meta-analysis from 52 studies between 1991 and 2021 confirmed a prevalence rate of 36.8% for gestational anemia [5]. A study conducted by Cheng et al. further underscored the persistence of iron-deficiency anemia as a significant community health issue in northern Taiwan, impacting 19.5% of pregnant women in their final trimester [6]. The potential risks associated with gestational anemia encompass a broad spectrum including fetal and maternal morbidity and mortality, fetal growth restriction, abortion, as well as an elevated likelihood of premature birth, low birth weight, hypertension, and neurological defects in newborns [7].

Ferritin, as a biomarker of serum iron storage, plays a pivotal role in maintaining iron homeostasis and regulating essential biochemical pathways [8]. Iron homeostasis hinges on the intricate interactions of iron utilization, storage, and transportation [9]. Iron regulatory proteins interact with iron-responsive elements, effectively governing the utilization and storage of iron, while hepcidin regulates iron transportation by diminishing iron export from the cell surface after binding to ferroprotein, ultimately leading to cellular degradation [9–11]. Furthermore, vitamin D demonstrates a potential role to suppress hepcidin by inhibiting hepcidin antimicrobial peptide gene expression, consequently enhancing ferroprotein’s functionality [12]. Iron-related biomarkers, such as ferritin and transferrin saturation [13], along with anemia-related biomarkers including folate, vitamin B12 [14], and vitamin D [15], are pivotal in the comprehensive study of anemia.

Despite the lack of observed associations between serum hemoglobin, ferritin, or vitamin D levels in the first trimester and infant birth weight among pregnant women [16], heightened risks of trimester-dependent anemia have been reported among pregnant women deficient in cholecalciferol [17]. In the existing research, the dietary patterns associated with iron- or anemia-related biomarkers among pregnant women are not well understood. Considering the pathophysiology of anemia, it is evident that ferritin levels can be influenced by iron status or the presence of anemia. Therefore, this study employs a reduced rank regression (RRR) model to identify dietary patterns, using iron- and anemia-related biomarkers as response variables and food groups as predictors. Furthermore, the study aims to validate the associations between the identified dietary pattern and the risk of adverse iron- and anemia-related biomarkers in pregnant women.

Methods

Study population and data collection

The Nationwide Nutrition and Health Survey data from pregnant women in Taiwan (NAHSIT 2017–2019) were acquired through a rigorous stratified sampling method, involving the selection of participants from 11 recognized hospitals based on their respective administrative districts and the number of pregnant women they served. In terms of inclusion criteria for the pregnant women, eligibility was contingent upon the following conditions: a willingness to participate and sign the informed consent, age of ≥ 15 years during pregnancy, proficiency in either Mandarin or Taiwanese for effective communication, a recipient of a maternal handbook, and maintenance of routine follow-up during pregnancy. The exclusion criteria comprised individuals who: were affected by chronic metabolic disorders or malignant diseases, gastrointestinal disease, liver disease, kidney disease, lung disease, or cancer, (n = 41), had experienced a miscarriage (n = 0), had undergone in vitro fertilization procedures (n = 0), were incapable of evaluating their nutritional status due to diagnosed psychological or emotional illnesses (n = 5), or were gravid with multiple fetuses (> 3) (n = 33) (Fig. 1).

Study population selection based on exclusion criteria. a, b,c In the final analysis, a total population of 1423 was retained for evaluating the association between the dietary pattern and pregnancy-associated anemia

A total of 1502 pregnant women were included in the cross-sectional study, and the data collected encompassed the following components: sociodemographic information, including age, region, trimester, parity, education, and income, sun exposure duration, anthropometric data such as pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI), dietary intake, and biochemical measurements consisting of eight serum biomarkers. Sociodemographic, sun exposure, and anthropometric data were obtained through self-reported questionnaires. Registered dietitians were recruited and underwent training to acquire dietary data through face-to-face interviews, employing semiquantitative food frequency questionnaires (sFFQ), 24-hour dietary recalls, and vitamin/mineral supplementation questionnaires. A total of 1423 and 1422 pregnant women completed the sFFQ and 24-hour dietary recall, respectively.

For biochemical analyses, blood samples were drawn during routine perinatal visits. The study was conducted in strict adherence to ethical standards and followed the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The approval was received from the Joint Institutional Review Board of Taipei Medical University, Taiwan (TMU-JIRB N201707039).

Assessment of a dietary pattern

Dietary assessments were carried out using a validated semiquantitative sFFQ and 24-hour dietary recall. To mitigate potential recall bias, we supported dietary data collection with visual aids, such as food pictures, exchange charts, measuring cups, and spoons. The sFFQ is widely recognized as a standard method for retrospective dietary record assessment in epidemiological studies [18]. Furthermore, the sFFQ not only allows us to gather historical dietary information but also provides insights into dietary habits that may be linked to both communicable and non-communicable diseases [19]. In the present study, sFFQ comprised of 59 food groups, which were further categorized into 32 food categories based on the similar macronutrient content. To calculate the sFFQ, we converted daily and weekly consumption frequencies into monthly rates (consumption/month) to obtain predictive frequencies. Additional details about the food groups and data collection methods were previously described [20]. In addition to the sFFQ, we utilized 24-hour dietary recall to address potential estimation errors in assessing energy and macronutrient intake [21].

In final, to derive a dietary pattern, we employed the RRR method. RRR is particularly advantageous as it integrates both a posteriori and a priori approaches, allowing us to explore the relationship between dietary patterns and the risk of disease or specific conditions based on existing knowledge [22, 23]. Furthermore, the RRR method excels in predicting multiple responses from predictor variables while reducing dimensionality and maintaining high predictive accuracy [24, 25]. As a result, the identification of a dietary pattern using the RRR method in relation to serum ferritin levels can be regarded as a comprehensive biomarker for monitoring iron status among pregnant women.

Measurements of anthropometric and biochemical data

Pre-pregnant BMI was calculated using the formula of weight (in kg) divided by height (in m2) [26]. To assess perinatal biochemical parameters, fasting blood samples were collected from the median cubital or cephalic vein. Measurement methods of serum Hb (mmol/L), iron (µmol/L), ferritin (nmol/L), total iron-binding capacity (TIBC) (µmol/L), transferrin saturation (%), folate (nmol/L), vitamin B12 (pmol/L), and 25(OH) vitamin D levels (nmol/L) were described previously [20]. Definition for gestational anemia and reference ranges of anthropometric and biochemical parameters were given previously [20].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS (version 22.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). Continuous variables were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Categorical variables were evaluated by Chi-squared test, and results are presented as the number and percentage. Between-group differences were compared by Tukey’s post-hoc test.

A dietary pattern was derived through the use of an RRR model, employing the PROC PLS function within SAS. In total, 32 food groups served as predictors, while serum Hb, iron, ferritin, TIBC, folate, and vitamin B12 levels functioned as responses, aiming to identify a dietary pattern with a factor loading value of > 0.20 (Supplementary Fig. 1) [27]. Among all the dietary patterns, the one with the highest explained variance was retained for further analysis, and named as the ferritin-related dietary pattern (FrDP). The dietary pattern score was calculated by summing up food frequency intake, weighted by factor loadings, and subsequently divided into tertiles, with tertiles 1 to 3 (T1–T3) representing the lowest, moderate, or highest intake of the FrDP, respectively.

To assess the association of the FrDP with iron- or anemia-related status, linear regression and binomial logistic regression analyses were carried out using three distinct models. Model 1, referred to as the crude model, was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for trimester and parity, and model 3 included adjustments for the variables in model 2 in addition to daily intakes of calories (kcal), macronutrients (g and % of energy), iron (mg), and folate (µg) derived from 24-hour dietary recalls. Results from the regression analyses are presented as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Finally, to verify risk associations, the FrDP was dichotomized into low and high consumption levels according to the dietary pattern score. Relative risk associations with insufficient serum 25(OH) vitamin D (≤ 75 nmol/L was considered insufficient or below, and > 75 nmol/L as sufficient or above) [28] were assessed. In all the analyses, a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Ferritin-related dietary pattern

Figure 2 illustrates the ferritin-related dietary pattern (FrDP) derived from the RRR model. The identified FrDP displayed positive correlations with vitamin B12, folic acid, ferritin, Hb, and iron, while exhibiting a negative correlation with TIBC (Supplementary Table 1). The FrDP (factor extracted from RRR) accounted for 2.13% of Hb, 3.69% of iron, 2.64% of ferritin, 6.13% of TIBC, 4.96% of folic acid, and 3.37% of vitamin B12. In total, it explained 18.50% of the overall variation across the six response variables.

Factor loadings of the ferritin-related dietary pattern (FrDP) > 0.20. a aFactor loading of > 0.20 represents positive associations, and <-0.20 represents negative associations

This derived FrDP was characterized by high consumption of aquatic fish, shellfish, seafood, soy products, gluten pasta, nuts, nut products, animal organ meat, blood, deep-water fish, pickled vegetables, marine plants and kelp, all of which displayed an absolute factor loading of > 0.20 [27].

Basic characteristics of subjects across FrDP tertiles

Table 1 indicates the basic characteristics of subjects across the tertiles of FrDP. Among all the sociodemographic characteristics, only trimester and parity showed significant differences across the tertiles of FrDP scores. Pregnant women in the highest FrDP tertile (T3) were mostly in the third trimester (37.3%) of pregnancy and primiparous (60.5%). Pre-pregnant BMI values were not significantly different across the tertiles of FrDP.

Iron markers and vitamin D concentration across FrDP tertiles

Table 2 demonstrates the biochemical characteristics of pregnant women across the tertiles of FrDP. Pregnant women in the highest FrDP tertile (T3) were likely to have higher serum Hb, iron, and ferritin levels, but lower TIBC and 25(OH) vitamin D levels compared to those who were in the lowest FrDP tertile (T1). However, there were no significant differences in transferrin saturation, serum folate, and serum vitamin B12 levels among pregnant women in different FrDP tertiles. We did not further analyze the data based on pregnant women with or without anemia because the number of pregnant women with anemia was too few after categorization by Hb levels < 6.83 mmol/L (< 11 g/dL) in trimesters 1 and 3 (n = 15) or < 6.52 mmol/L (10.5 g/dL) in trimester 2 (n = 292).

Dietary intake of the subjects

Supplementary Table 2 shows the dietary intake of participants across the tertiles of FrDP. Dietary intake from a 24-h dietary recall demonstrated that pregnant women in the highest FrDP tertile (T3) were likely to have lower daily intakes of energy (kcal), carbohydrate (g), fat (g), and folate (µg), but increased daily intake of protein (g) compared to those who were in the lowest FrDP tertile (T1). Almost > 45% of pregnant women used dietary supplements containing multi-vitamins/-minerals (61.7%) and folate (45.5%). However, other nutrient intake including iron, vitamin B12, and vitamin D, and the use of supplements such as multi-vitamins/-minerals, folate, and calcium did not show significant differences across the tertiles of FrDP.

Associations of FrDP with serum biochemical variables

Table 3 presents linear associations between the FrDP and serum biochemical variables using different adjusted models. In model 1 (crude model), positive associations were observed between the FrDP and serum Hb (β = 0.06, 95% CI 0.01–0.26), iron (β = 0.11, 95% CI 0.06–0.18), or ferritin (β = 0.07, 95% CI 0.06–0.39). However, the FrDP showed negative associations with serum TIBC (β = -0.08, 95% CI -0.07 to -0.02) and 25(OH) vitamin D (β = − 0.08, 95% CI -0.08 to -0.02). After partial adjustment in model 2, the FrDP had a positive association with serum iron (β = 0.09, 95% CI 0.05–0.17) and a negative association with 25(OH) vitamin D (β = -0.05, 95% CI -0.07 to -0.00). Finally, after complete adjustment with the covariates in model 3, the FrDP only illustrated a positive association with serum iron (β = 0.10, 95% CI 0.05–0.17), but a negative correlation tendency with serum 25(OH) vitamin D (β = -0.05, 95% CI -0.06 to -0.00, P = 0.054).

Risk associations of the FrDP with serum biochemical variables

Table 4 shows the odds between the FrDP and serum biochemical variables. In model 1, pregnant women in highest FrDP tertile showed decreased risks of low serum iron (OR = 0.61, 95% CI 0.47–079) and low serum ferritin (OR = 0.70, 95% CI 0.54–0.90), but increased risks of low serum TIBC (OR = 1.47, 95% CI 1.11–1.94) and serum 25(OH) vitamin D insufficiency (OR = 1.94, 95% CI 1.44–2.62) compared to those who were in the reference group. After full adjustment for confounding factors, pregnant women in highest FrDP tertile still reduced the risk of low serum iron by 35% (OR = 0.65, 95% CI 0.50–0.85), and elevated the risk of serum 25(OH) vitamin D insufficiency by 79% (OR = 1.79, 95% CI 1.32–2.43).

Relative risks of the FrDP with serum vitamin D status

Supplementary Table 3 demonstrates the relative risk between the FrDP and serum 25(OH) vitamin D status. We evenly divided the FrDP score into two levels based on consumption frequencies. The proportion of serum 25(OH) vitamin D insufficiency or below among pregnant women in low or high consumption of the FrDP was 69.2% and 72.8%, respectively. However, pregnant women in either low or high consumption of the FrDP did not affect the relative risk of vitamin D insufficiency (p > 0.05).

Discussion

Dietary patterns related to serum iron and vitamin D status

In the present study, pregnant women in the highest FrDP tertile had higher concentrations of serum Hb, iron, and ferritin, but lower TIBC and vitamin D levels. Correspondingly, the FrDP was positively correlated with serum iron, but inversely associated with serum 25(OH) vitamin D status. A dietary pattern rich in fruit, dried vegetables, nuts, milk, eggs, fish, processed or fast foods, cakes, and wheat buns were positively associated with serum iron status [29]. Amuthenie and Sivakanesan reported that serum ferritin levels were positively correlated with the consumption of beef, mutton, prawns, soy meat, green leafy vegetables, mangoes, pomegranates, and watermelons in pregnant women [30]. However, previous studies showed that high intakes of the animal-based dietary pattern, such as red meat, poultry meat, processed meat, organ meats, and eggs, the plant-based dietary pattern, such as rice and flour, mushrooms, legumes and beans, melons, roots and tubers, dark-colored vegetables, murine plants, and nuts, and the seasoning dietary pattern, such as herbs and spices, salt, and sugary drinks, were negatively associated with serum iron or ferritin status [20, 27, 31]. The carnivore, plant-based, dairy and nondairy alternatives, and low-fat dairy and non-refined bread dietary patterns showed positive correlations with serum 25(OH) vitamin D status [20, 32]. In contrast, the sweets dietary pattern was negatively correlated with serum 25(OH) vitamin D [32].

FrDP and serum vitamin D status

Surprisingly, we found a negative association between the FrDP and serum 25(OH) vitamin D. The highest positive factor loading of the FrDP was represented by aquatic fish, shell, shellfish, and seafood, followed by general soy products and gluten pasta. A meta-analysis reported that the consumption of fatty or lean fish improved 25(OH) vitamin D status by 6.8 or 1.9 nmol/L with an average elevation by 4.4 nmol/L compared to the controls [33]. Additionally, the consumption of fish for a short (4–8 weeks) or long period of time (around 6 months) elevated 25(OH) vitamin D levels by 3.8 or 8.3 nmol/L on average, respectively [33]. These data suggested that the type of fish, particularly fatty fish, and the duration of the consumption could affect an increase in serum 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations [33]. Similarly, a cross-sectional study conducted in Nepal demonstrated that the significant determinants of serum 25(OH) vitamin D status were dependent on the consumption frequency of fish and dairy products, and the type of meat [34]. However, no significant correlation was found between vitamin D3 content (ng/g tissue) and fat (%) in fish muscle [35]. The possible mechanism for a negative association between the FrDP and serum vitamin D status in the present study could be due to the presence of more ergosterol (vitamin D2)-rich food sources in the FrDP (general soy products and gluten pasta, pickled vegetables, and marine plants and kelp) which had lower intestinal absorption rate [36] and less efficient increases in serum 25(OH) vitamin D levels [37] compared to vitamin D3.

Serum vitamin D and iron status

Vitamin D modulates iron metabolism because of being the core component of the hepcidin regulatory pathway, and has become a topic of concern due to its ferritin- and ferroprotein-influencing activities [12]. Serum 25(OH) vitamin D status was negatively correlated with serum ferritin (r = -0.20) in pregnant women with gestational diabetes [38]. However, serum 25(OH) vitamin D status in each trimester was positively associated with serum Hb, and pregnant women with serum vitamin D deficiency (< 50 nmol/L; <20 ng/mL) in trimester 1 increased the risk of anemia [39]. Similarly, deficient serum 25(OH) vitamin D (< 50 nmol/L) was associated with reduced Hb in women of reproductive age [40]. A prospective study reported that serum vitamin D status was positively correlated with serum Hb (r = 0.49), iron (r = 0.79), and ferritin (r = 0.78), but negatively correlated with TIBC (r = -0.74) in pregnant women [41]. Braithwaite et al. [42] also found that serum 25(OH) vitamin D was positively associated with serum ferritin (β = 0.09, 95% CI 0.04–0.15) and hepcidin (β = 0.06, 95% CI 0.01–0.11) in late pregnancy. These findings revealed inverse associations, which were inconsistent with the existing literature.

Limitations and strengths

With novel outcomes, the present study has several limitations. First, we used a cross-sectional study design with self-reported sociodemographic information, anthropometric data, FFQ, and 24-h dietary recall, which could have self-reported bias and no conclusions about the causality. Additionally, we did not have sufficient information on skin pigmentation, parathyroid hormone, hepcidin, inflammatory biomarkers, and uses of medication, sunscreen, or dietary supplements in details, which could affect vitamin D status. Furthermore, we did not assess the factors influencing dietary intake such as morning sickness. In spite of these limitations, the present study has several strengths. The foremost strength of this study was the use of nationwide nutrition survey data, a stratified sampling method to retain the original characteristics of the population, and the use of sociodemographic and anthropometric measurements and most iron-related biomarkers. Correspondingly, we performed various models for adjusting potential confounding factors to support our hypothesis.

Conclusions

The FrDP is characterized by a substantial intake of aquatic fish, shellfish, seafood, soy products, gluten pasta, nuts, nut products, animal organ meat, blood, deep-water fish, pickled vegetables, marine plants and kelp. Pregnant women in the highest FrDP tertile exhibit an augmentation in serum iron-related biomarkers, accompanied by a reduction in serum vitamin D levels. Furthermore, pregnant women in the highest FrDP tertile demonstrate a decreased likelihood of low serum iron levels but an increased likelihood of vitamin D insufficiency. For further confirming the associations between the FrDP and serum iron or vitamin D status in pregnant women, a randomized controlled clinical trial will be suggested for the future study.
