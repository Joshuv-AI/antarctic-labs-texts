Assessing and strengthening community‐based coastal governance
Authors: Mark Andrachuk, Graham Epstein, Shauna Mahajan, Zephania Arnold, Sinezio Junior, Dresy Lovasoa, Monalisa Mabandla, George W Maina, Caroline Njeri, Samson Obiene, Geoffrey Odhiambo, Lenice Ojwang, Samantha Petersen, Vatosoa Rakotondrazafy, Joshua Rambahiniarison, Lilia Rasolofomanana, Tojo Rasolozaka, Maafaka Ravelona, Craig Smith, Pascal Thoya, Abel Valdivia, Milton Jose Xavier

ABSTRACT
AbstractCommunity‐based coastal governance is essential for biodiversity conservation and sustainable livelihoods, yet many communities continue to face significant challenges in establishing and sustaining effective governance systems. We sought to improve understanding of the current status of community‐based coastal governance across five countries in the Southwest Indian Ocean (SWIO) region of Africa. Using the Elinor tool and data system, we examined how specific governance attributes relate to perceived social and ecological outcomes and sought insights for community‐managed areas (CMAs) in the SWIO region. Progress toward strong community‐based governance of CMAs has been uneven across the region and across governance attributes. Although some CMAs have established clearly defined management boundaries and property rights, many other CMAs are still working to improve some attributes, such as congruent regulations, transparency, accountability, and operational capacity. Perceived ecological and social outcomes correlated with different governance attributes. Perceived ecological outcomes were positively correlated with measures of property rights and local governance, whereas perceived social outcomes were correlated with local governance and operational capacity. The strength of CMA governance tended to improve in the first 5–10 years after establishment, then governance attributes plateaued or declined thereafter. These findings coincide with typical funding cycles, thus reinforcing the value of continued investment in community‐based coastal governance. Specifically, our findings point to the importance of providing long‐term capacity development and securing durable conservation finance to ensure the success of CMAs. More broadly, these findings contribute to a growing body of evidence of the importance of paying attention to when and how to leverage different types of support and capacity building.Keywords: coastal communities, community‐based coastal governance, conservation, equity, small‐scale fisheries, social and ecological outcomes, Southwest Indian Ocean, Comunidades costeras, conservación, equidad, gobernanza costera basada en la comunidad, pesca artesanal, resultados ecológicos y sociales, suroeste del Océano Índico, 基于社区的沿海治理, 沿海社区, 小型渔业, 公平, 社会与生态结果, 保护, 西南印度洋

INTRODUCTION

Community‐based coastal governance, including community‐led and collaborative governance, is a promising approach for conserving biodiversity and supports sustainable livelihoods (Gurney et al., 2021). A growing number of studies link community leadership (or joint leadership) in coastal conservation and natural resource management to improved outcomes for both people and nature (Armitage, 2005; Cinner et al., 2016; Mast et al., 2025; Saunders et al., 2020; Trialfhianty et al., 2025). Unfortunately, ongoing impacts from colonization and global political change have eroded customary systems of community‐based governance of natural resources in many parts of the world (Aswani et al., 2012; Blaikie et al., 2006; Johannes, 1978; McClanahan et al., 2005). The expansion of marine uses, such as coastal tourism, port development, and offshore extractive industries, has further constricted customary and traditional marine tenure systems, leading to loss of local control over coastal resources (Obura et al., 2015; Randone et al., 2018; Thoya et al., 2022).

Recognizing communities’ critical knowledge, skills, and capacities for managing local resources, support for community‐based and community‐led approaches to conservation and natural resource management has grown over recent decades (Collier, 2020; Dobson & Lynch, 2003; Glaser et al., 2015; van der Ploeg et al., 2024). Strengthening these approaches can also contribute to achieving global conservation targets, including Global Biodiversity Framework (GBF) Target 3, which includes reference to effectively governed protected areas (Secretariat of the Convention on Biological Diversity, 2022).

Supporting the development of strong community‐based and collaborative governance of coastal resources thus represents an important opportunity for communities and external conservation stakeholders to pursue their shared objectives. Despite a general understanding of the high‐level attributes that contribute to their long‐term success (Cox et al., 2010; Ostrom, 1990), significant gaps remain regarding our understanding of the current status of community‐based coastal governance in many regions and the place‐based strategies communities adopt to govern their resources and social relations. These gaps often result in a mismatch between conservation interventions and what communities need to strengthen local governance (Booth et al., 2023; Brown, 2003; Guerrero et al., 2021). Rapid, low‐cost assessment tools designed to monitor governance and management, such as the Elinor tool and data system (Mahajan et al., 2024), can help address this challenge by helping communities and their partners to better understand the current status of local governance, identify gaps, and develop targeted evidence‐based interventions for strengthening governance systems. When applied across multiple communities, coordinated assessments can facilitate the transfer of local innovations in governance and management and allow external partners to realize efficiencies by addressing issues shared by several communities at regional or national scales (Hoffman, 2022).

We drew on data collected using the Elinor tool from 52 community‐managed areas (CMAs) across five countries in the Southwest Indian Ocean (SWIO) region of Africa. We explored three central questions. First, what is the current status of community‐based governance across the region, and how does this vary across attributes of effective and equitable governance? Second, what is the relationship between attributes of effective governance and perceptions about ecological and social outcomes of CMAs? Third, how does the strength of CMAs (vis a vis community‐based coastal governance and its attributes) change over time? We also composed an overview of the SWIO region and recent work conducted to support CMAs. We sought to contribute to literature on when and how to leverage different types of support and capacity building for community‐based coastal governance.

METHODS

Study area

The SWIO region spans the East African coastline from Kenya to South Africa, including Tanzania (mainland and Zanzibar), Mozambique, and the islands of Madagascar, Comoros, Mauritius, and the Seychelles (Appendix S1). The region is a global biodiversity hotspot, with over 10,000 km2 of coral reefs, including some of the world's most climate‐resilient reefs, approximately 7000 km2 of mangroves, migratory corridors for marine megafauna, and endemic species (Barkley et al., 2019; Erftemeijer et al., 2022; Vogt‐Vincent et al., 2024). These marine ecosystems are, however, facing mounting threats from climate change, overexploitation, expansion of coastal tourism, port development, and extractive industries (Obura et al., 2015; Randone et al., 2018; Samoilys et al., 2017; Thoya et al., 2022).

The coastal areas of the SWIO are populated by more than 60 million people, whose cultures, traditions, and livelihoods are closely intertwined with the ocean (Bosire et al., 2015). Local communities are deeply knowledgeable about their local environments and have strong incentives to conserve coastal ecosystems for current and future generations (Cinner et al., 2009; Rakotoson & Tanner, 2006).

In recent decades, community‐based coastal governance has reemerged in the region as countries have adopted legislation that devolve natural resource management rights to local community organizations. This process started earlier in Madagascar (1996) with the creation of the GELOSE law, which provided the legal framework for transferring natural resources management rights to local communities. In Tanzania (2003), the mainland's Fisheries Act (number 22) authorized local authorities to establish beach management units (BMUs) to support fisheries management and is largely seen as the key enabling factor for community leadership in marine governance (Katikiro et al., 2024). Similar legislation followed in Kenya, with the 2007 Fisheries Regulations mandating BMUs to have jurisdiction over their respective comanagement areas. South Africa legally recognized the possible gazettement of small‐scale fishing zones in the Marine Living Resources Amendment Act 5 of 2014, and although areas have yet to be identified, progress has been made in identifying and mobilizing small‐scale fishers into cooperatives and allocating small‐scale fishing rights (Kowalski & Smith, 2025). In Mozambique, the Ministerial Diploma No. 83/2023 formally outlined processes for establishing CMAs called APGCs, building on previous regulations that formalized community participation in conservation by allowing community‐based organizations (CBOs) to establish community conservation areas.

An overview of the different types of community‐based governance arrangements we assessed is in Table 1. Although governance arrangements differed in structure, scale, and context, they shared the common goal of supporting community participation and leadership in marine resource management. For simplicity, we refer to them collectively as community‐managed areas. In practice, CMAs are typically managed by BMUs or other types of CBOs.

Data collection and analyses

Data were collected using the Elinor data tool and platform, an open‐source monitoring tool (www.elinordata.org), as part of a nongovernmental organization (NGO)‐led efforts to understand and strengthen locally based governance in the SWIO region. We used the Elinor tool because it provides a rigorous and consistent method to assess governance and management effectiveness, as well as the barriers that actors face in conservation decision‐making (Mahajan et al., 2024). Additionally, because the Elinor tool was designed to apply across all types of area‐based conservation, its methods were well suited for assessing existing and emerging CMAs in the region. The long‐term aim is for the tool to support repeated assessments across the SWIO region, thus ensuring the collection and storage of comparable data over time. The Elinor tool includes 35 questions distributed across 10 attributes of equitable governance and effective management (Appendix S2) (Mahajan et al., 2024). These attributes are grouped into four categories: property rights, local governance, implementation, and perceived outcomes. For instance, there are five attributes of inclusive and equitable management that fall under the local governance category. Assessing effective governance is inherently indirect because it reflects the combined outcome of multiple attributes, including clear and congruent regulations, transparency and accountability, enforcement, and capacity for operations and adaptive management. When we write about strengthening local governance, we are referring to improving outcomes across these categories.

Each of the 35 questions were assessed using customized 4‐point scales, which were combined into a composite attribute score scaled from 0 and 10. These attribute scores were summed to calculate an overall Elinor score ranging from 0 to 100. Higher scores generally indicate greater progress toward equitable governance and effective management. The Elinor tool also encourages reporting of qualitative information for each question, some of which was used to contextualize quantitative results. The Elinor protocol underwent an ethics peer review (based on guidance from Cornell et al. [2018]) before version 2023.1 was finalized. We used this version here. These steps have ensured that data collection and storage adhere to the highest ethical standards (e.g., contacting, sharing information with, and seeking approval from local leaders; obtaining verbal informed consent from all participants; and de‐identifying transcripts and data reported in the Elinor data system to preserve the confidentiality of participants).

We used data collected from 2021 to 2024 on 52 CMAs across five countries in the SWIO region, including Kenya (11), Madagascar (16), Mozambique (10), South Africa (8), and Tanzania (7). The CMAs were selected based on their engagement in conservation activities with participating NGOs (WWF and CORDIO East Africa). As a result, our sample is neither random nor necessarily representative of all CMAs in each country or the SWIO region. We collected data through focus group discussions (n = 51) involving six to 48 participants and one key informant interview. In all sites, facilitators selected participants, men and women, who were knowledgeable about the use and management of resources in their community. Although application of the Elinor tool varied across countries and CMAs based on differences in the capacities and resources of field staff, all sites were evaluated using a common instrument. The variations in data collection methods used with each CMA (e.g., number of participants) could have influenced the results, but we assessed broad trends rather than conducting site‐to‐site comparisons.

The Elinor protocol was translated prior to data collection into local languages. Field staff who led data collection guided participants through the Elinor questionnaire and took written notes on their responses, which were then digitally transcribed. This practice is in line with existing norms for engaging local community members and was adopted to facilitate open dialogue.

The quantitative data from the Elinor assessment were analyzed using descriptive statistical techniques and visual interpretation of graphs developed in R 4.4.1. We used boxplots to summarize the distribution of overall Elinor scores across CMAs in each country and a heatmap to visualize the average attribute‐level scores by country. Spearman rank correlations were used to explore associations between governance attributes and perceived social and ecological outcomes. This method is appropriate for ordinal data and does not require assumptions related to normality or linearity. To explore how governance changes over time, we plotted governance scores against the number of years since each CMA had been established, examining both overall scores and individual governance attributes. Together, these approaches provided an overview of trends, associations, and variations within the dataset. Qualitative data were available for some sites, although the level of detail varied considerably. As a result, we did not undertake formal thematic coding of these data. Instead, we reviewed and analyzed the available qualitative information to identify common barriers and opportunities experienced by communities as they seek to strengthen local governance. Although this analysis did not directly inform our core results, it supported our interpretation of the quantitative findings.

RESULTS

Across the five countries included in this study, CMAs in Tanzania had the highest median overall governance score because local organizations have been expanding and strengthening their roles in coastal governance over the last two decades (Figure 1). In contrast, South Africa and Mozambique showed lower overall scores because the processes of defining rights, identifying the location and boundaries of CMAs, and transferring authority to local communities are ongoing. CMAs in Kenya and Madagascar appear to have made substantial progress toward stronger local governance but also exhibited greater variability. Despite variations across countries, the overall country medians, which were at or below 65 out of 100, underscore the need for continued investment in building and strengthening community‐based governance across most CMAs in the SWIO region.

Progress across different governance attributes

Results for each of the 10 governance attributes assessed with the Elinor questionnaire generally reaffirm that progress toward strong community‐based governance of CMAs has been uneven across the region. CMAs in South Africa and Mozambique show comparatively limited progress across most governance attributes relative to CMAs in the other three countries (Figure 2). Importantly, the heatmap helps to visually identify areas of strength and potential gaps in CMA governance across the SWIO region and within individual countries.

Elinor attribute average scores for each country (the higher the score, the greater the progress toward good governance).

With the exception of CMAs in Tanzania, progress toward perceived social outcomes was limited, and there was considerable room for improving perceived ecological outcomes across the SWIO region. It was not surprising to see differences in social and ecological outcomes across the CMAs assessed because these attributes are perception based. Assessed CMAs in Tanzania, for example, aimed to support the economic well‐being of households and made significant progress by working with external conservation partners to develop community microfinance and sustainable livelihood projects, including seaweed and sea cucumber aquaculture, agriculture, and the establishment of public fish auctions.

Communities across the SWIO region faced significant challenges in developing inclusive and equitable local management and subsequent implementation of local governance (Figure 4). The relatively low scores estimated for CMAs in Mozambique and South Africa were not unexpected, given the recent evolution of policies in both countries related to the roles of community organizations in conservation governance. Nonetheless, for CMAs in Kenya and Madagascar, where national policies that allow for the formal recognition of rights have been in place for more than 14 years, gaps in inclusive and equitable management remained.

Relationship between governance and perceived outcomes

The data collected as part of the Elinor tool allowed us to explore patterns between perceived outcomes and each governance attribute. Figure 3 reports Spearman rank correlations between each of the eight governance and management attributes and measures of perceived social and ecological outcomes. This figure also includes a composite governance score, calculated as the sum of all eight governance attributes. The correlations between governance attributes and perceived outcomes were consistently positive and statistically significant with the exception of the correlation between enforcement and perceived social outcomes. Stronger governance was generally associated with better perceived outcomes. None of these correlations, however, were particularly strong (generally defined as r > 0.7), and because of the cross‐sectional nature of the data, we were unable to confirm the direction of this relationship (i.e., communities with better perceived outcomes may report stronger governance).

These correlations nonetheless revealed several patterns that may be relevant to conservation theory and practice. Perceived ecological outcomes, for instance, had higher and significant correlations (r = 0.40–0.53) with governance attributes related to property rights and local governance, and lower correlations for those related to implementation. In contrast, perceived social outcomes were weakly correlated with property rights but more strongly associated with attributes of local governance and operational capacity. These patterns were illustrated by responses from four village BMUs that collectively manage the JOJIBAKI collaborative fisheries management area in Tanzania. Respondents indicated that their household economic well‐being had improved in recent years, whereas local governance reforms strengthened women's representation on management committees and improved coordination across the four communities to reduce free riding. Finally, where associations between perceived social outcomes and individual governance attributes varied in strength, some of the strongest correlations were observed with the composite governance score. Governance attributes appeared to have greater influence when functioning together, even when individual attributes, such as enforcement, did not show strong independent associations with outcomes.

Strength of community‐based governance and the age of CMAs

The CMAs (and the CBOs that support them) we assessed ranged from newly established to those that have been in place for more than 20 years. This variance allowed us to generate insights about how governance may develop as CMAs mature. As shown in Figure 4, governance strength generally increased within the first 5–10 years of establishment. Beyond this point, however, scores tended to level off at relatively low to intermediate values and even declined for some attributes. In general, CBOs appeared to make substantial early progress in clearly defining property rights within the first 5 years of their establishment and tended to maintain these gains over time. In contrast, operational capacity and adaptive management appeared to pose significant challenges for newer and more established CMAs. Organizations managing older CMAs appeared to struggle to maintain the relatively high levels of transparency and accountability in local governance observed in organizations of intermediate age.

Perceived social and ecological outcomes exhibited a similar pattern; scores increased as organizations matured (up to about 10 years). Perceived social outcomes appeared to decline as organizations aged beyond this point, such that organizations operating for more than 20 years were comparable to those that were recently established. However, because these insights were derived from a cross‐sectional analysis of CBOs of varying ages, they should be interpreted cautiously and could benefit from further empirical validation because we did not track governance development within the same CMAs and organizations over time.

DISCUSSION

We examined progress toward equitable governance and effective management across 52 CMAs in five SWIO countries, generating insights for theory and practice. Our research has some limitations, many of which stem from the realities of applied research. First, we relied on a relatively small and nonrandom sample of CMAs and CBOs across a large, contextually heterogeneous region. Although the results provided meaningful insights into the governance status of CMAs assessed, they are not necessarily representative of the status and trends of CMAs within the region or countries. Second, data collection relied on interview and focus groups, which are inherently social processes and may have been affected by interpersonal dynamics and power relationships among participants and between researchers and participants (Kornbluh, 2023; Nduna, 2020). These dynamics may mask variation of perspectives within communities and bias assessments toward the views of local leaders. Furthermore, variation in data collection methods, including focus group sizes and composition, may have influenced the results. Third, although governance is an important driver of conservation outcomes, other factors, such as market dynamics and climate change, that were not assessed in our study are increasingly shaping the incentives, opportunities, and constraints that communities face when investing their time and resources in coastal governance and the outcomes they experience (Cinner et al., 2018; Isaacs, 2011; Raemaekers et al., 2011). Notwithstanding these limitations, our results showed meaningful progress toward more equitable governance and improved management and highlighted the need for sustained efforts to translate governance gains into lasting social and ecological outcomes.

Progress across the SWIO region

Progress toward equitable governance and effective management varied considerably across countries and governance attributes, and there is substantial room for improvement across the SWIO region. Variability across countries is not surprising given that enabling legislation for community‐based coastal governance has been operational much longer in Kenya, Madagascar, and Tanzania, whereas the support for comanagement is more recent in South Africa and Mozambique. Although this unevenness underscores the need for continued investment to strengthen community‐based coastal governance, it also presents an opportunity to learn from the experiences of other countries in the region on how best to accelerate progress toward equitable management. For example, to enable inclusive decision‐making, policies that require minimum female representation on local management committees or that support the development of public fish auctions to promote more equitable distribution of benefits—both adopted by relatively successful CMAs in Tanzania—could be tested and adapted elsewhere where contextually appropriate (Epstein et al., 2015; Turner et al., 2018).

Association of stronger governance with better outcomes for people and nature

Our results showed that stronger governance was consistently associated with better perceived social and ecological outcomes. All eight governance attributes (excluding the two outcomes‐based attributes) were positively correlated with outcomes, and all but one relationship was statistically significant (Figure 3). These findings align with literature that connects positive biodiversity conservation outcomes to governance attributes, such as stakeholder participation (Cinner et al., 2016; Mast et al., 2025; Persha et al., 2011), operational capacity (Gill et al., 2017), and enforcement (Edgar et al., 2014). Although future research may consider exploring the relative importance of these different attributes in driving outcomes, growing evidence suggests that governance attributes often operate synergistically, meaning that improving the strength of one attribute may not necessarily lead to improved outcomes in the absence of other governance attributes. Edgar et al. (2014), for instance, found that marine protected area effectiveness increases when multiple governance features, such as enforcement and no‐take areas, are combined. Similarly, Gutiérrez et al. (2011) found that fisheries comanagement success improved substantially when more than eight governance attributes were present. The relatively strong correlation observed between the composite governance score and perceived outcomes lends some additional support to this hypothesis. This in turn highlights the importance of developing integrated governance systems rather than emphasizing individual attributes or isolated interventions.

Implementation challenges across the SWIO region

We found that although several CMAs made considerable progress on property rights and local governance, most have struggled implementing governance effectively. Operational capacity, enforcement, and capacity for adaptive management consistently scored low, which may create challenges to deliver desired outcomes. Implementation challenges are not, however, unique to CMAs; many so‐called paper parks operated by government agencies face similar constraints (Di Minin & Toivonen, 2015; Rife et al., 2013). Unlike the development of management plans, which often involve one‐time, albeit sometimes significant, investments, implementation requires sustained funding for hiring staff, purchasing equipment, and operations. These costs are frequently prohibitive for many communities (Agrawal & Goyal, 2001; Balmford & Whitten, 2003; Brown et al., 2018; Keane et al., 2008). Although some CMAs in our study have managed to temporarily address these gaps by developing capacity to manage and secure external funding, these activities are at risk once funding expires (Bonham et al., 2014; Bos et al., 2015). As a result, there is a role for community partners, NGOs, and funding agencies to ensure CBOs have the capacity to absorb and manage funds while also developing innovative approaches to secure and diversify long‐term sources of funding. Blended finance approaches that combine local revenue, such as user fees and licenses, with external revenue from governments and donors and leveraging mechanisms, such as conservation trust funds, to ensure durability of finance may be useful in this regard (Meyers et al., 2020).

When external conservation organizations are involved (i.e., conservation NGOs), it is critically important that inclusive governance processes and equitable management rules are adequately established before investing in enforcement or compliance activities that could otherwise reinforce inequalities (Lord & Sareen, 2025) or exacerbate conservation problems meant to be resolved (Ntuli et al., 2021; Witter, 2021).

Trends in progress toward strong governance

The observed relationships between the governance attributes and the age of CMAs responsible for the local management of CMAs provide some leading evidence to suggest that progress in building strong community‐based governance may stall over time. Indeed, CMAs managed by CBOs for roughly 5–10 years tended to exhibit relatively stronger governance than those established more recently. However, this trend did not extend to CMAs that have existed for more than 10 years, and many long‐established CMAs still show governance gaps that may undermine their long‐term resilience.

Several mechanisms may explain these patterns. First, and perhaps foremost, the ecological recovery of species and habitats following conservation interventions can take a considerable amount of time and sustained investment (Watts et al., 2020), varying both with respect to the ecological attributes of species and the level of protection offered by the CMA (Edgar et al., 2014). As a result, community‐based governance that seeks to achieve a balance between conservation and livelihoods, often characterized by high levels of poverty and food insecurity (Le Manach et al., 2012; Manhice et al., 2022), struggles to achieve desired social and ecological outcomes.

Second, term‐limited and project‐based funding may provide opportunities to improve local governance but struggle to develop truly strong and effective governance systems that require longer‐term commitments. Third, although resolving problems related to boundary designation, property rights, and resource use rules can be complex, they can often be effectively addressed through focused conservation interventions and sustained thereafter. In contrast, some attributes, such as operational capacity, which has been linked to better conservation outcomes (Gill et al., 2017), require ongoing investments and can limit the development of strong governance across many of the assessed CMAs. These findings suggest that strengthening governance and realizing desired outcomes is a long‐term process that benefits from sustained commitments and support. At the same time, tools like Elinor can help accelerate progress by identifying transferable governance innovations and supporting the development of targeted, evidence‐based interventions to address shared governance challenges across CMAs.

Many of today's biodiversity hotspots were shaped by a long history of community‐based coastal governance. Although colonial and exclusionary conservation approaches have eroded community‐based knowledge and capacity, strong community‐based coastal governance, when supported with partnership with government agencies and NGOs, remains a promising approach toward more sustainable and equitable outcomes for people and nature. Tools like Elinor can play an important role by helping CBOs and their partners assess their current status, identify gaps, and prioritize investments at local, country, and regional levels. Future studies would benefit from more systematic case selection, a standard approach to implementing the Elinor tool (e.g., consistency in composition of participants and improved qualitative data collection), and additional data on other critical factors, such as funding for CMAs, and actual social and ecological outcomes to enable more robust cross‐site comparisons. We provide theoretical and evidence‐based foundations for strengthening CMAs as a mode for community‐based coastal governance. Rather than advocating for a singular path forward, supporting the full spectrum of community‐based governance attributes through long‐term capacity development and durable conservation finance can ensure this critical form of area‐based conservation delivers positive change for people and nature.
