Chronic Kidney Disease in Patients With Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus: A Cross‐Sectional Population‐Based Study
Authors: Neora Alterman, Yoel Toledano, Noga Fallach, Victor Gross, Galia Zacay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsChronic kidney disease (CKD) is a common complication of Type 2 diabetes that frequently co‐occurs with cardiovascular disease. We aimed to characterise CKD in adults with Type 2 diabetes, quantify its prevalence across severity levels and examine pharmacological treatment patterns and healthcare utilisation.Material and MethodsWe conducted a population‐based cross‐sectional analysis using electronic medical records from a national health organisation in Israel. CKD status was defined using estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (uACR). Extracted variables included demographic and clinical characteristics, comorbidities, medication use, community consultations and hospital encounters.ResultsThe cohort included 62 661 adults with Type 2 diabetes, of whom 30.7% met criteria for CKD: mild, moderate and severe CKD risk levels accounted for 19.7%, 6.4% and 4.6%, respectively. Higher CKD level was associated with greater cardiovascular morbidity, with heart failure showing the strongest relationship (adjusted prevalence ratio 2.4; 95% CI 2.3–2.6 for the most advanced stage vs. no CKD). Other diabetes complications, including retinopathy and neuropathy, also increased with CKD severity (adjusted prevalence ratios 2.0; 95% CI 1.9–2.1 and 1.4; 95% CI 1.3–1.5, respectively). Metformin, lipid‐lowering agents and ACE inhibitors/ARBs were the most frequently used medications. With CKD progression, rates of nephrology, endocrinology and cardiology consultations rose, as did emergency department visits and hospitalisations.ConclusionsCKD is common amongst Israeli adults with Type 2 diabetes and is strongly linked to cardiovascular and other diabetes‐related complications. More advanced CKD stages correspond to substantially higher healthcare utilisation, reflecting escalating healthcare burden with CKD progression.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes is a systemic, multifactorial metabolic disease that affects 7%–13% of adults worldwide, making it one of the most common chronic conditions today. Its prevalence has doubled since 1990 and is expected to continue to rise [1], largely due to increasing rates of obesity, sedentary lifestyles and ageing populations. Diabetes currently ranks as the eighth leading cause of global disease burden and is projected to become the second leading cause by 2050, highlighting its substantial public health impact [2].

Diabetes leads to a wide range of microvascular and macrovascular complications affecting multiple body organs. One of the most prominent complications is chronic kidney disease (CKD) which affects 20% to 40% of adults with diabetes [2]. In Israel, its prevalence is estimated at approximately 30% [3]. Several risk factors for the development of kidney disease in diabetes have been established. They include duration of diabetes, obesity, older age, male sex, hypertension, smoking, cardiac disease, poor glycaemic control, hyperlipidaemia and family history of kidney disease [4, 5].

The coexistence of cardiac and renal disease is well described, characterised by a bidirectional effect in which deterioration in one organ accelerates deterioration of the other. This interplay is commonly termed the ‘cardiorenal syndrome’ [6, 7]. When a background of diabetes is also present, a triad of metabolic, renal and cardiac disease is created. Then, the complexity and severity of the cardiorenal syndrome becomes even more pronounced. Individuals with diabetes and CKD thus face competing risks for mortality from end‐stage renal disease and cardiovascular disease [8].

Timely screening and early intervention are essential for preventing or slowing CKD progression and reducing further complications. Management of CKD focuses on four major pillars: cardiovascular risk reduction, glycaemic control, blood pressure control and inhibition of the renin‐angiotensin system (RAS). Hence, progression of diabetic nephropathy may be slowed [2]. Several epidemiological studies have portrayed the close interconnection between Type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease and CKD [7, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Fewer studies have, however, characterised these associations across distinct CKD stages [13]. Moreover, researchers that have examined stages of CKD did not often use a clinically informative definition that combines both eGFR and uACR into a single risk stratification system, as suggested by ‘Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes’ (KDIGO) guidelines [14]. This framework, which integrates both kidney filtration and albuminuria, has been shown to outperform either measure alone for prediction of mortality, cardiovascular events and kidney failure [15, 16]. Furthermore, although cardiovascular morbidity and mortality have been well‐studied, there is limited information about non‐cardiovascular diabetes complications in relation to CKD, for example, retinopathy and neuropathy [9]. Additionally, whilst hospitalisation patterns amongst diabetes patients with CKD have been described [12], detailed data on specialised care are lacking.

Our objective was to evaluate the prevalence of all‐cause CKD at varying levels of severity amongst adults with Type 2 diabetes in Israel and to characterise associated comorbidities, both cardiovascular and other diabetes‐related complications. We further aimed to assess pharmacological treatment patterns in these patients and describe their utilisation of healthcare services.

Methods

Study Population and Data Source

We carried out a cross‐sectional study based on all adult patients with Type 2 diabetes insured through ‘Meuhedet Health Services’ (MHS) on 27 October 2025. MHS is one of Israel's four national healthcare organisations providing services to 1.4 million people across the country. MHS maintains a centralised clinical database that integrates patient information from both community and hospital care, encompassing diagnostic records, medication purchases, laboratory test results, referrals for specialised procedures and more. The initial study population included 71 147 individuals extracted from the validated diabetes registry of MHS. We excluded individuals missing laboratory tests for urine albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (uACR) or estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) measurements from the past 3 years and patients that had undergone kidney transplants.

Covariates

CKD was defined based on results of the latest uACR and eGFR measurements and a previous measurement performed between 3 months to 3 years prior. The better result (i.e., less impaired) of the two measurements for each laboratory indicator was used. This is aligned with the KDIGO definition for CKD, requiring the abnormalities in kidney function to persist for a minimum of 3 months [14]. If only a single measurement was available, that value was used. CKD was defined based on KDIGO clinical criteria for risk of morbidity and mortality: low risk of complications, that is, ‘no CKD’, and moderate, high and very high risk of complications [14] (Table 1). We also extracted history of diagnosis of various comorbidities for each patient and inclusion in cancer registries. See list of diagnoses codes in Table S1. We further retrieved the number of packages purchased in the past year of medications to treat diabetes and hypertension as well as cholesterol‐lowering drugs. These were categorised as: no purchase, purchase of 1–9 packs in the past year (i.e., partial adherence) and purchase of more than nine packs in the past year. This cut‐off was selected to signify 80%–100% adherence, whilst taking into account that prescription fulfilment in Israel is on a quarterly basis [17]. We gathered information about visits to community‐based consultants alongside emergency room visits and hospitalisations in the past year. Additional clinical details, information about risk factors and area‐based sociodemographic data were also extracted. Socioeconomic status (SES) was gathered using ‘Points Location Intelligence’ which is based on the Israel Central Bureau of Statistics data according to residency address [18]. SES is defined on a scale of 1–10, with 1 as the lowest, categorised into low (1–3), medium (4–7) or high (8–10) SES.

Statistical Analyses

Continuous variables were described using mean and standard deviation and categorical variables using frequency and proportions. Statistical differences between groups were assessed using ANOVA or chi‐squared tests, respectively. To examine the association between CKD risk level and various comorbidities, we used the ‘Modified Poisson Regression’ method appropriate for common outcomes [19]. We calculated prevalence ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), fitting generalised linear models with a Poisson distribution and log link with robust standard errors using the ‘no CKD’ sub‐group as reference. Adjustments were made for age, sex, socioeconomic status, duration of diabetes and smoking status. We carried out a sensitivity analysis further adjusting for potential confounding factors such as systolic blood pressure, low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol and haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level. Another sensitivity analysis was conducted to account for possible selection bias arising from exclusion of individuals with missing data using inverse probability weighting. We modelled the probability of having missing information (predominantly uACR measurements) using: age, sex, socioeconomic status, population sector, diabetes duration and eGFR level, where for the latter 632 missing measurements were imputed as the median. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.0 [20].

Ethics Approval

The study received approval from the MHS ethics committee (Meu‐25‐0016).

Results

Study Population Characteristics

From 71 147 patients with Type 2 diabetes mellitus, we excluded 8063 and 135 individuals due to missing uACR and eGFR, respectively, as well as 288 kidney transplant patients. Missing individuals tended to have a short diabetes duration (< 5 years) and a normal to high (G1) eGFR level (Table S2). Overall, the study population for analysis included 62 661 patients (88% of the initial population). CKD risk level classification based on eGFR and uACR measurements is presented in Table 1. Latest laboratory tests were quite recent, with eGFR measured within the preceding year in 92% of the study population and uACR in 80%. Most previous laboratory tests were also relatively recent being within the 3‐year period (97% for eGFR and 89% for uACR). Therefore, the better of the two measurements was used for these individuals. We found that 30.7% of our study population had CKD, of which 19.7%, 6.4% and 4.6% were at moderate/high/very high risk for complications, respectively (Table 2). Accounting for missing data using inverse probability weighting led to only slightly modified prevalence estimates of 19.4%, 6.3% and 4.5%, respectively. CKD risk level was positively associated with age and duration of diabetes. Individuals at moderate or higher risk level for CKD were more likely to be male, of the general Jewish population, current or past smokers and have higher blood HbA1c levels than those with no CKD.

Comorbidities

Cardiovascular disease was common in the study population with prevalence increasing with higher CKD risk level (Figure S1). Additional comorbidities investigated included diabetic retinopathy, neuropathy, age‐related macular degeneration (AMD), metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and cancer. All but MASLD showed higher prevalence at later stages of CKD. Following adjustment for potential confounders, positive ‘dose–response’ associations between CKD risk level and each comorbidity were still observed (Figures 1 and S2). The exceptions to this finding were several disorders, including AMD, MASLD and hypertension. For hypertension, the adjusted prevalence ratio calculated was 1.1 (95% CI 1.1, 1.1) at every risk level of CKD compared to no CKD, with no observed increase across CKD levels. The strongest associations detected were with diabetic retinopathy and heart failure reaching an adjusted prevalence ratio of 2.0 (95% CI 1.9, 2.1) and 2.4 (95% CI 2.3, 2.6) compared to no CKD, respectively. Another common diabetes complication associated with CKD stage was neuropathy, reaching an adjusted prevalence ratio of 1.4 (95% CI 1.3, 1.5). A sensitivity analysis of further adjustment for potential confounders showed slightly attenuated associations, whilst preserving the overall pattern (Figure S3). An additional sensitivity analysis accounting for missing data showed very similar results to the main analysis (Figure S4).

Medication Use

Anti‐hypertensive and anti‐hyperlipidemic medications were given to the majority of patients with diabetes (Figure 2A). The most common medications used were anti‐hyperlipidemic drugs and ACE inhibitors/angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARB). Patients at more advanced stages of CKD were generally more likely to use at least one of the medications examined.

Medication purchases during past year by CKD stage. (A) Cardiovascular risk reduction medications. (B) Diabetes medications.

The most common medication for diabetes management was metformin, followed by sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and glucagon‐like peptide 1 (GLP‐1) receptor agonists (Figure 2B). Patients with CKD, especially the latest stage, were more likely to use insulin, dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 (DPP4) inhibitors and SGLT2 inhibitors. In contrast, they were less likely to use metformin compared to patients in earlier stages of the disease. We did not observe pronounced differences in the use of GLP‐1 receptor agonists by CKD status. For all medications, there was a substantial subgroup of patients who were only partially adherent with treatment, defined as purchase of 9 or less packs in the past year.

Healthcare Resource Utilisation

Almost all patients in our study population (98%–99%) had visited family physicians in the past year. Additionally, the proportion of patients who visited a consultant of any specialty in the past year was 72% for those with no CKD and 74%, 79% and 82% for each increasing risk level of CKD. Figure S5 depicts the proportion of individuals who visited each discipline of consultancy in the past year. More than half of the patients visited an ophthalmologist in the past year with no association with CKD stage. Patients at higher stages of CKD were more likely to visit a nephrologist, endocrinologist, cardiologist and a vascular surgeon. Patients with more advanced CKD were more likely to visit an emergency room, be hospitalised in the past year and have longer hospitalisations (Table 3).

Discussion

In our study, the prevalence of CKD amongst Israeli adults with Type 2 diabetes was 30.7%. CKD was found positively associated with all the cardiovascular disorders analysed in this report. Higher CKD levels were associated with progressively greater comorbidity risk. The strongest association was detected for heart failure. Non‐cardiovascular complications, including retinopathy and neuropathy, were also strongly related to CKD severity. Patients with more advanced CKD were more likely to use various medications. Further, they demonstrated higher healthcare utilisation across both community and hospital care.

Our study focused on CKD of any cause in patients diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus. The prevalence identified closely aligns with the 2024 national estimate of 32.6% for the Israeli population [3]. The small difference likely reflects variation in CKD definitions. Our study applied a stricter approach, requiring both eGFR and uACR measurements in accordance with KDIGO criteria [14]. Also, our definition used the better result from the two most recent laboratory measurements to ensure that reduced kidney function persisted for at least 3 months, in line with KDIGO clinical guidelines [14].

Our findings show that cardiovascular disorders and CKD commonly coexist, even after accounting for shared risk factors such as age, duration of diabetes, smoking, socioeconomic status and sex. Further adjustments for blood pressure, LDL and HbA1c weakened some of the associations examined slightly. Unlike cardiovascular disease diagnosis, CKD status was determined based on laboratory tests obtained within the previous 3 years. Kidney function typically deteriorates slowly, with a median time to progress to the next eGFR stage being 8, 5 and 4 years for Stages 3a, 3b and 4, respectively [21]. Thus, the most recent laboratory measurement likely represents the patient's long‐lasting CKD risk level. Birkeland et al. [11] carried out a longitudinal cross‐national study of patients with Type 2 diabetes and exemplified the bi‐directionality of risk where heart failure was found to increase the risk of incident CKD two‐fold and vice versa. Our study also highlighted heart failure as the most strongly associated cardiovascular manifestation and further adds to these findings by showing a ‘dose–response’ association amongst the various stages of CKD. Additional studies have shown this association, but separately for decline in kidney filtration rate and for proteinuria [13].

Hypertension showed a slightly higher adjusted prevalence amongst individuals with CKD compared to those without the disorder; however, it did not vary across CKD risk categories. This likely reflects the high lifetime prevalence of hypertension in the age group studied—76% in our diabetes population—which led to ‘saturation’ of this diagnosis which leaves little room for differentiation across CKD severity levels. Accordingly, the gradient of the crude prevalence of hypertension across CKD risk categories compared to individuals without CKD was primarily explained by age differences.

Our study also evaluated additional complications of diabetes. CKD risk level was positively associated with both neuropathy and PVD, two key contributors to diabetic foot ulcer, a common diabetes complication, with a lifetime prevalence of 19%–34% [22]. In contrast to AMD, which is not a known complication of CKD or diabetes and for which no clear association with CKD was observed, we found a positive association between CKD risk level and the diagnosis of diabetic retinopathy. This relationship is explained biologically by the fact that retinopathy and kidney dysfunction share underlying microvascular injury mechanisms arising from uncontrolled glucose levels. Indeed, some researchers have proposed that diabetic retinopathy may even serve as a prognostic marker for CKD progression in individuals with Type 2 diabetes [23].

Increased CKD severity correlated with a higher prevalence of cardiovascular disease, peripheral vascular disease, retinopathy and neuropathy due to shared injury pathways. Driven by diabetes‐related hyperglycemia and chronic hypertension, these pathways induce systemic inflammation and fibrosis. This creates a feedback loop of diffuse macro‐ and microvascular damage, simultaneously accelerating renal dysfunction and multi‐system organ pathology [24].

Pharmacological treatment in our population was generally consistent with the best clinical practise for individuals with diabetes, although medication adherence was not complete. Studies in various settings observed a similar occurrence of partial adherence and have shown that roughly 40%–60% of patients with Type 2 diabetes deviate from their prescribed medication regime [17, 25]. ACE/ARB inhibitors, the recommended first‐line therapy for hypertension, were widely prescribed. Their use increased to 75% when CKD was present, reflecting its additional indication for renal protection [26]. In this cohort, metformin remains the most prescribed therapy for glucose control in Israel, despite the more recent inclusion of GLP‐1 receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors as first‐line options for specific subgroups of patients with diabetes [27]. The indications for prescription of GLP‐1 receptor agonists to treat Type 2 diabetes have recently been expanded to include CKD irrespective of body‐mass index (BMI), but we did not observe higher utilisation amongst patients with CKD [28]. This is likely explained by insufficient time to observe shifts in clinical practise.

In Israel, most diabetes care is managed by family physicians, with referrals for consultations made only when required. Consequently, only one‐third of patients saw an endocrinologist or diabetes specialists in the past year, though rates increased with more advanced CKD. Visits to cardiologists and vascular surgeons were also more common at more severe CKD levels, consistent with increased cardiovascular morbidity. Approximately half of the diabetes patients visited an ophthalmologist in the past year, consistent with recommended annual or bi‐annual eye examinations [29]. We found no difference in the rate of ophthalmologist visits by CKD risk levels, likely since severe retinopathy is often treated in hospital‐based outpatient clinics, which were not captured in this study. Similarly, neurological consultation rates did not rise with CKD risk level, plausibly because diabetic neuropathy does not require ongoing neurologic follow‐up once diagnosed [29].

Our study demonstrated higher use of inpatient medical care with increasing CKD risk levels, a pattern consistent with findings from the US [12, 30]. However, the rise in healthcare service utilisation observed in our study within the Israeli healthcare setting was not as sharp as reported in the mentioned studies. This might be explained by differences in the counties' healthcare systems.

A key strength of our study is its data source: a national healthcare organisation that provides health services to the entire Israeli population. The study population of adults with Type 2 diabetes was derived through a validated registry, thus minimising selection bias. The large sample size provided substantial statistical power, enabling precise estimates of outcome prevalence and associations. CKD was defined using a clinically relevant four‐level risk classification, allowing granular assessment of disease severity. In addition, we examined a broad spectrum of comorbidities, extending beyond cardiovascular disease to include other diabetes‐related complications. Our analysis also captured multiple dimensions of healthcare utilisation including medication purchases as well as community‐based and inpatient hospital care.

Several limitations should be considered in this study. First, the main constraint is its cross‐sectional design, which does not allow us to infer causality from the associations described. We cannot determine whether a given comorbidity preceded the development of CKD or followed it. This is particularly relevant for cardiovascular morbidity, which has a broadly recognised bidirectional relationship with CKD. Second, the exclusion of individuals that had not undergone a uACR laboratory test in the preceding 3 years may have introduced selection bias, as these individuals have a shorter duration of diabetes and are less likely to have CKD. However, accounting for their exclusion from the study using inverse probability weighting did not materially alter prevalence estimates or associations. Third, our definition of CKD based on the single less impaired measurement of the two latest laboratory tests for kidney function may have led to misclassification of some individuals. However, such misclassification is more likely to have biassed our results towards the null, given the conservative assignment to CKD severity categories. Fourth, an additional limitation is that some diagnoses, such as MASLD, are under‐reported in clinical records, introducing information bias and attenuating observed associations. Fourth, information about some risk factors such as smoking may have not been of high quality, a limitation shared with many other studies that are based on routinely collected data. Finally, we lacked information on the reason for consultancy or hospital visits, thus limiting interpretation of healthcare utilisation patterns by CKD risk level.

To conclude, CKD was highly prevalent in this large, population‐based study of Israeli subjects with Type 2 diabetes. In parallel to CKD, a broad array of cardiovascular disorders and other diabetes‐related complications were detected. The risk for these comorbidities was increased even after accounting for several additional risk factors. More advanced CKD level was linked with greater medication use and higher healthcare consumption, highlighting the clinical and management utility of the KDIGO framework for CKD classification and underscoring the substantial burden of this morbidity [14].
