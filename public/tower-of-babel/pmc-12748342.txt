FoxO1, together with Notch1, promotes microglial activation to induce pathological changes in the retinal vasculature under hypoxia
Authors: Xiyu Wu, Junbin Liu, Haoxian Zhu, Guanrong Wu, Xinyu Chen, Tai Guo, Xiang Fang, Qianli Meng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicroglia, the resident immune cells in the retina, play important roles in the retinopathies. Although the role of the Notch signaling pathway in microglial activation and inflammation in neuroinflammatory diseases has been extensively studied, less is known about the effects of Notch signaling on retinal microglia in ischemic retinopathies. Here, we demonstrated that hypoxia triggers Notch1–FoxO1 nuclear translocation in retinal microglia, driving proinflammatory and proangiogenic phenotypes that disrupt vascular homeostasis. We first showed that hypoxia induced microglial activation and upregulated the levels of proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α and proangiogenic factors FGF2, VEGF, and PDGF-β, which promoted retinal vascular endothelial cell dysfunction, marked by increased cellular permeability, migration, and tube formation. We then identified the Jagged1–Notch1 pathway and FoxO1 nuclear translocation as a pivotal signaling axis driving this proinflammatory microglial response under hypoxia. Finally, we demonstrated the therapeutic potential of this axis by showing that inhibition of Notch1 or FoxO1 in hypoxia-activated microglia and in the eyes of oxygen-induced retinopathy mice ameliorated both inflammation and pathological neovascularization in the retina. These findings suggest that FoxO1, together with Notch1, promotes microglial activation to induce retinal vasculopathy under hypoxia, indicating that targeting the Notch1–FoxO1 axis may be a therapeutic strategy for ischemic retinopathies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-06024-w.Keywords: Notch1 pathway, FoxO1, Microglia, Retinal vascular endothelial cells, Hypoxia, Ischemic retinopathy

Introduction

Ischemic retinopathies, such as diabetic retinopathy, retinal vein occlusion, and retinopathy of prematurity, pose significant risks to vision [1]. The common pathogenic mechanisms underlying these conditions include ischemia and hypoxia, which trigger a cascade of pathological alterations in the retinal vasculature, including disruption of the blood‒retinal barrier (BRB), retinal vessel occlusion, and onset of retinal neovascularization [2]. Nevertheless, effective preventive strategies remain limited, highlighting the need to elucidate the pathophysiological mechanisms and identify novel therapeutic targets for ischemic retinopathies.

Microglia are the resident immune and antigen-presenting cells in the retina [3], playing pivotal roles in maintaining tissue homeostasis and responding to pathological conditions [4, 5]. Microglia in the retina are not only involved in the inflammatory response but also participate in angiogenesis. Activated and infiltrating microglia drive the progression of retinal inflammation through the release of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines [6]. In the oxygen-induced retinopathy (OIR) model, retinal microglia migrate to intraretinal neovascular clusters, occasionally establishing direct interactions with the vascular basement membrane [7]. However, the precise mechanisms by which retinal microglia regulate inflammation and neovascularization are still poorly defined.

The Notch signaling pathway governs cell fate through the direct binding of Notch ligands to receptors located on neighboring cells. Four different Notch receptors (Notch1, Notch2, Notch3, and Notch4) and five classic Notch ligands [Jagged 1 (JAG1), JAG2, and Delta-like proteins (Dll1, Dll3, and Dll4)] have been identified in mammals [8]. Upon ligand–receptor interaction, Notch signaling induces proteolytic cleavage, releasing the Notch intracellular domain (NICD), which translocates to the nucleus and binds to the recombination signal binding protein for immunoglobulin kappa J region (RBP-J) to regulate gene expression. It has been reported that the Notch pathway is involved in microglial activation and the inflammation process in neuroinflammatory diseases [9, 10]. Moreover, the Notch pathway engages in crosstalk with many other pathways, such as the toll-like receptor (TLR), NF-κB, Wnt, and Forkhead box O1 (FoxO1) pathways, to synergistically regulate biological functions [11–14]. Notably, FoxO1, a multifunctional transcription factor, enhances Notch signaling by interacting with RBP-J to drive the dissociation of corepressors [15].

Although Notch signaling has been extensively studied in brain microglia, its role in retinal microglia under hypoxia remains unclear. To define the role of hypoxia-activated retinal microglia in pathological angiogenesis via the Notch1–FoxO1 axis, we first observed the effects of the hypoxic environment on microglia, and then evaluated their functional impact on retinal vascular endothelial cells. Finally, we explored the intercellular crosstalk between FoxO1 and Notch1 signaling as the potential mechanistic basis for vascular pathology.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

The human microglial clone 3 cell line (HMC3) was obtained from Procell (CL-0620, Wuhan, China). HMC3 cells were cultured in minimum essential medium (MEM; PM150410, Procell, Wuhan, China), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S). Human retinal microvascular endothelial cells (HRMECs) were acquired from Bioesn Biotechnology (BES2536HC, Shanghai, China). HRMECs were incubated in low-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; PM150220, Procell) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. The conditioned medium (CM) from HMC3 cells was co-cultured with HRMECs for 24 h under either 21% O2 (normoxia) or 1% O2 (hypoxia). For inhibition treatment, HMC3 cells were exposed to 10 µg/mL of GSI (a Notch1 inhibitor; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) or 5 µg/mL of AS1842856 (a FoxO1 inhibitor; Merck) for 24 h under either 21% O2 or 1% O2.

OIR murine model

C57BL/6J male and female mice were purchased from the Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center (Guangzhou, China). The OIR model was established as previously described [16]. On postnatal day 7 (P7), the mice were transferred to a sealed oxygen chamber with 75% O2, where they were maintained for 5 days. On postnatal day 12 (P12), they were transferred to a room under normoxic conditions and maintained there until postnatal day 17 (P17). Mice reared under normoxic conditions from birth until P17 were designated as the control group. In the intervention experiment, GSI (10 µg/µL), AS1842856 (5 µg/µL), or an equal volume of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; 1 µL) was injected into the vitreous cavity of one eye of an OIR mouse at P12. The eyes of the OIR mice and controls were harvested at P17 for further experiments. All protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (number: KY2023-735-01) and conformed to the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cell proliferation was assessed via CCK-8 assay (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan). HMC3 cells or HRMECs were seeded in 96-well plates with 100 µL of medium per well. HRMECs were then incubated with 10 µL of CM from HMC3 cells exposed to either 21% O2 or 1% O2 for 24 h. After incubation, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm after incubation in the dark for 4 h.

Western blot analysis

The cells and retinas were lysed in lysis buffer (89900, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and the protein concentration was assessed using the BCA protein assay kit (23227, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Protein samples were then separated by SDS‒PAGE on 7.5%, 10%, and 12.5% gels. After electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) by electroblotting. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 1 h before incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with the corresponding species-specific secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1–2 h (Suppl. Table S1). The western blot images are representative of at least three independent experiments.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from HMC3 cells using an RNA extraction kit (AG21017, Accurate Biotechnology, Changsha, China), and the RNA concentration was subsequently quantified using a spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using Evo M-MLV RT Premix (AG11706, Accurate Biotechnology, Changsha, China). An RT-qPCR thermal cycler (qTOWER3G, Analytik Jena, Jena, Germany) was used to analyze mRNA expression. All primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), and the sequences are listed in Suppl. Table S2.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

CM from HMC3 cells exposed to various interventions was diluted at a ratio of 1:20 for measuring IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α levels; at a ratio of 1:10 for measuring IL-10 and TGF-β levels; and at a ratio of 1:5 for measuring FGF2 levels. The media were subsequently processed according to the instructions of the ELISA kits (Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA). The absorbance was recorded at a wavelength of 450 nm. Blank controls (wells without samples) were included, and their values were subtracted from sample readings. All assays were performed in triplicate.

Cell permeability assay

HRMECs were seeded into the upper chamber of Transwell inserts for culture. CM from HMC3 cells was added to the HRMECs at a dilution of 1:10 and incubated for 24 h before 20 µL of FITC-dextran (5 mg/mL) was added to the HRMECs in the upper chamber. Following 2 h of incubation in the dark, 100 µL of the medium from the bottom chamber was collected from each group and transferred to a new black flat-bottom 96-well plate. The fluorescence intensity at 488 nm was measured using a multimode microplate reader (Spark, Tecan, Switzerland).

Ki-67 assay

HRMECs were digested and seeded in 24-well plates. After they had fully attached to the bottom of the plate, the cells were cultured in DMEM containing 1% FBS for 4–6 h. Subsequently, complete DMEM containing 10% FBS was added along with CM from HMC3 cells exposed to various interventions at a 1:10 dilution. The cells were then incubated for 24 h, followed sequentially by fixation, permeabilization, and blocking. Ki-67 staining was performed using a Ki-67 Cell Proliferation Detection Kit (C2305S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) before images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (THUNDER Imager 3D, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Scratch migration assay

HRMECs were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h. CM from HMC3 cells was subsequently added to the wells at a 1:10 ratio and incubated for additional 24 h. A scratch was made in each well via a pipette tip before images were captured under an inverted microscope. Subsequent images were acquired 12 h later, and scratch closure was quantified using ImageJ software (V 1.52p; NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). The cell migration rate was quantified according to the following formula: cell migration rate = ([initial scratch area − later scratch area]/initial scratch area) × 100.

Tube formation assay

HRMECs pretreated with CM from HMC3 cells were seeded in 48-well plates precoated with Matrigel (356237, Corning, NY, USA) and incubated for 11 h. Live cells were subsequently stained with Calcein AM (C2012, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 1 h, followed by image acquisition using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Nikon TI-S, Tokyo, Japan).

Nuclear and cytoplasmic protein separation

Nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins were sequentially extracted using a Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (KGB5302; KeyGEN BioTECH, Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cytoplasmic and nuclear proteins were quantified by western blot analysis.

Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP)

Proteins were extracted from HMC3 cells with immunoprecipitation lysis buffer (87787, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). After overnight incubation at 4 °C with target protein antibodies or IgG isotype controls, protein complexes were precipitated using protein A/G agarose beads and subsequently eluted in SDS‒PAGE loading buffer before western blot analysis was performed.

Retro-orbital injection and retinal flat mounts immunofluorescence staining

After mice were anesthetized at P17, 100 µL of FITC-dextran (25 mg/mL) was injected retro-orbitally. The eyes were then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. The retinas were dissected and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min, blocked with 5% goat serum for 30 min, and then incubated overnight with Isolectin B4 (IB4; I21413, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Panoramic images of the retina were captured using “Tile” and “Z-Stack” scanning on a confocal microscope (LSM-900, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) after mounting.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Immunofluorescence staining of paraffin-embedded ocular tissue sections

The fixed eyes were embedded in paraffin and sectioned. The sections were later deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). After dehydration with ethanol, butanol, and xylene, the sections were mounted with neutral resin, and images were subsequently captured using an upright microscope (DM2000, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

To conduct immunofluorescence staining of the paraffin-embedded eye sections, deparaffinization and antigen retrieval were performed. After the sections were blocked with 5% goat serum and 3% BSA for 30 min, they were incubated with primary antibodies overnight (Suppl. Table S1). The sections were then incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies in the dark at room temperature for 1–2 h. Finally, the sections were mounted with DAPI-containing mounting medium and imaged under a confocal fluorescence microscope (LSM-900, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Cellular immunofluorescence staining

HRMECs were seeded in confocal dishes and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 30 min, washed with PBS, and blocked in PBS with 3% BSA for 30 min. The cells were then incubated with primary antibodies for 2 h and further incubated with secondary antibodies in the dark for 1 h at room temperature (Suppl. Table S1). The dishes were mounted with DAPI-containing mounting medium and imaged under a confocal fluorescence microscope (LSM-900, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates. The data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean. Statistical analysis and quantitative plotting were conducted using SPSS 20.0 (Chicago, IL, USA) and GraphPad Prism 8.0 (San Diego, CA, USA) software. Unpaired t-tests were performed to evaluate significant differences between two groups, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to evaluate significant differences among multiple groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Hypoxia-induced microglial activation triggers functional behavior changes in retinal vascular endothelial cells

Given the critical role of microglia in inflammation and angiogenesis, we first investigated the effects of hypoxic conditions on their polarized phenotype and pro-angiogenic property. The proliferation of HMC3 cells significantly increased 12–72 h after hypoxic exposure (Fig. 1A), while the protein expression of iNOS and IL-1β showed a time-dependent rise 24–72 h after hypoxic exposure (Fig. 1B). Considering that inflammatory markers were markedly upregulated at 24 h and cell viability was stable, this time point was chosen for subsequent experiments. The increased iNOS and IL-1β levels following 24 h of hypoxic exposure were similar to those observed under lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation under normoxic conditions; conversely, the CD206 and Arg-1 expression under hypoxia was significantly lower than that observed under IL-4 stimulation under normoxia (Fig. 1C). The concentrations of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, were increased in the CM of HMC3 cells exposed to hypoxia (Fig. 1D), whereas the levels of the anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and TGF-β were decreased (Fig. 1E). These results suggest that hypoxia induces microglia polarization to the M1 type.

We next examined the mRNA expression levels of the key angiogenesis-associated genes FGF2, PDGF-β, VEGFA, MMP9, MMP2, and ANGPT2 after 24 h of hypoxia. We observed significant upregulation of FGF2, PDGF-β, and VEGFA under hypoxic conditions compared with that in the control group, with FGF2 exhibiting the most pronounced upregulation (Fig. 1F). Correspondingly, the level of FGF2 was also increased in the CM of HMC3 cells exposed to hypoxia (Fig. 1G).

To examine the impact of hypoxia-induced microglial activation on functional behavior changes in retinal vascular endothelial cells, we collected CM from HMC3 cells exposed to normoxia or hypoxia and co-cultured it with HRMECs for 24 h. We observed that the CM from hypoxia-stimulated HMC3 cells increased vascular leakage (Fig. 2A), decreased the levels of the tight junction proteins Zo-1 and occludin, and increased the expression of the apoptotic marker cleaved caspase-3 (Fig. 2B, C) in HRMECs under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions but especially under hypoxic conditions. We also found that hypoxia-induced microglial CM increased the proliferation (Fig. 2D, E), migration (Fig. 2F), and tube formation of HRMECs (Fig. 2G), consistent with the features of pathological angiogenesis. These findings suggest that activated microglia have a significant role in disrupting endothelial homeostasis under hypoxia.

Hypoxia increases the expression of JAG1, Notch1, and FoxO1 in microglia both in vitro and in vivo

To identify the specific ligand and receptor underlying Notch signaling pathway-mediated microglia activation under hypoxia, we measured the mRNA levels of genes in the Notch family, including Notch1, Notch2, Notch3, and Notch4; JAG1 and JAG2; and Dll1, Dll3, and Dll4, in HMC3 cells subjected to 24 h of hypoxia. We observed upregulation of JAG1 and Notch1 expression but no significant differences in the expression levels of other Notch ligands or receptors between cells cultured under normoxic and hypoxic conditions (Fig. 3A). The protein levels of JAG1, Notch1 and FoxO1 significantly increased 24–72 h after hypoxic exposure, but the expression level of p-FoxO1 significantly decreased (Fig. 3B).

When we next examined Notch1 and FoxO1 levels in the retinas of OIR mice, we observed that the OIR P17 mice had obvious ischemic retinopathy, presenting extensive retinal vascular leakage, areas of avascularity, and neovascularization compared with the control mice (Fig. 4A). The retinal thickness of the OIR P17 mice was decreased, and numerous endothelial cell nuclei had penetrated the inner limiting membrane (ILM) of their retinas (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining revealed marked increases in the populations of iba1+iNOS+ cells (Fig. 4C), iba1+Notch1+ cells (Fig. 4D), and iba1+FoxO1+ cells (Fig. 4E) within the retinas of the OIR P17 mice. These cells were predominantly localized in the inner retinal layers, particularly within the neovascular regions that penetrated the ILM. Western blot analysis revealed elevated protein levels of JAG1, Notch1, FoxO1, iNOS, IL-1β, and iba1 in the retinas of the OIR P17 mice compared with those in the control mice (Fig. 4F). These results suggest that microglia are activated toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype and that the JAG1–Notch1 signaling pathway and FoxO1 contribute to hypoxia-induced retinopathy.

Nuclear translocation of FoxO1 and Notch1 promotes the activation of proinflammatory microglia under hypoxia

We subsequently investigated the expression, distribution, and interaction between the Notch1 pathway and FoxO1 in HMC3 cells under hypoxia. Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractionation assays revealed increased nuclear expression of NICD, FoxO1, RBP-J, and Hes1 in HMC3 cells under hypoxic conditions, while cytoplasmic levels remained stable (Fig. 5A). Immunofluorescence microscopy revealed significant nuclear co-localization of Notch1 (magenta), RBP-J (red), and FoxO1 (green) in HMC3 cells subjected to hypoxia (Fig. 5B). Co-IP assays demonstrated that in HMC3 cells cultured under hypoxic conditions, FoxO1 interacted with both RBP-J and NICD, while NICD was associated with FoxO1, and RBP-J similarly interacted with FoxO1 (Fig. 5C).

To clarify the effects of Notch1 and FoxO1 on microglia under hypoxia, we performed inhibition of Notch1 or FoxO1 via the application of GSI (a Notch1 inhibitor) or AS1842856 (a FoxO1 inhibitor) to HMC3 cells cultured under hypoxic conditions. The results showed that treatment with GSI or AS1842856 significantly decreased the nuclear fluorescence intensities of Notch1 and FoxO1 (Fig. 5D). Western blot analyses demonstrated that in HMC3 cells cultured under hypoxic conditions, GSI treatment led to a marked downregulation of the protein levels of iNOS, IL-1β, Hes1, and FoxO1, and AS1842856 treatment substantially reduced the protein expression of iNOS, IL-1β, Hes1, and Notch1 (Fig. 5E). We also observed a significant decrease in the levels of the proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α (Fig. 5F) and a significant increase in the levels of the anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and TGF-β (Fig. 5G) in the CM of HMC3 cells treated with GSI or AS1842856. Furthermore, FGF2 levels were markedly decreased by the suppression of Notch1 or FoxO1 (Fig. 5H, I). These results suggest that Notch1–FoxO1 nuclear translocation and interaction are critical for the activation of proinflammatory microglia under hypoxia.

Inhibition of Notch1 or FoxO1 expression in microglia abrogates the functional behavior changes in retinal vascular endothelial cells under hypoxia

To determine whether Notch1–FoxO1 interaction is involved in hypoxia-activated microglia promoting the functional behavior changes of retinal vascular endothelial cells, we co-cultured HRMECs with CM from HMC3 cells pretreated with GSI or AS1842856 under hypoxic conditions. Our results showed that the CM from hypoxia-activated microglia with either GSI or AS1842856 treatment significantly decreased vascular leakage (Fig. 6A); increased the expression levels of Zo-1 and occludin; reduced the levels of cleaved caspase-3 (Fig. 6B, C); and inhibited proliferation (Fig. 6D, E), migration (Fig. 6F), and tube formation of HRMECs (Fig. 6G). Notably, both inhibitors exerted comparable efficacy. These findings suggest that inhibition of either Notch1 or FoxO1 in microglia ameliorates the homeostasis of vascular endothelial cells and prevents their transformation into pathological neovasculature.

Inhibition of Notch1 or FoxO1 ameliorates vasculopathy and the inflammatory response in the retinas of OIR mice

Finally, we assessed the roles of Notch1 and FoxO1 in modulating angiogenesis and the inflammatory response in vivo. After intravitreally injecting OIR mice with GSI or AS1842856 at P12, we performed FITC-dextran perfusion and IB4 immunostaining of the retina at P17. Our results revealed that the administration of GSI or AS1842856 significantly reduced retinal vascular permeability, diminished the extent of avascular region, decreased the area of pathological neovascularization, increased retinal thickness, and reduced the quantity of endothelial cell nuclei penetrating the ILM (Fig. 7A, B). Western blot analysis showed that both GSI and AS1842856 markedly inhibited the expression levels of iNOS and IL-1β in the retinas of OIR mice. Notably, GSI administration decreased FoxO1 protein levels, whereas AS1842856 administration decreased protein expression of Notch1 (Fig. 7C). These findings suggest that inhibition of Notch1 or FoxO1 ameliorates vasculopathy and the inflammatory response in hypoxia-induced retinopathy.

Discussion

Our findings revealed that hypoxia-activated microglia exhibit increased levels of the proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α and the proangiogenic factors FGF2, VEGF, and PDGF-β, which in turn promote pathological changes in retinal vascular endothelial cells, including increased cellular leakage, proliferation, migration, and tube formation. We further identified the JAG1–Notch1 pathway and FoxO1 as key regulators of this process, with Notch1 and FoxO1 co-translocating into the nucleus and promoting the inflammatory activation of microglia under hypoxia. Inhibition of either Notch1 or FoxO1 attenuated inflammatory responses and pathological neovascularization in vitro and in vivo. These findings clarify the interaction between the JAG1–Notch1 pathway and FoxO1 in the activation of microglia to exacerbate hypoxia-induced ischemic retinal diseases, and indicate the potential therapeutic value of targeting Notch1 and FoxO1 in the treatment of these disorders.

Generally, activated microglia are divided into proinflammatory M1 and anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes [17]. However, with the development of technologies such as single-cell RNA sequencing, microglia have been found to be highly heterogeneous [18], suggesting that the traditional classifications of M1 and M2 may not accurately represent the states of microglia. Our results revealed that under hypoxic conditions, microglia exhibit an M1 phenotype characterized by elevated levels of iNOS, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, concurrently with decreased expression of M2 anti-inflammatory markers, such as Arg-1, CD206, IL-10, and TGF-β. Furthermore, a concomitant increase in the expression of proangiogenic factors such as FGF2, VEGFA, and PDGF-β was also observed. This co-expression pattern of both pro-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic factors in hypoxic microglia suggests a potential novel phenotype, the definitive characterization of which requires further experimental investigation. Functional studies demonstrate that microglia closely interact with retinal blood vessels under both physiological and pathological conditions, primarily through their roles in immune surveillance, angiogenesis, inflammatory responses, and BRB maintenance. Our previous study demonstrated that LPS induces microglial phenotypic switching toward a proinflammatory state and disrupts the BRB, ultimately aggravating vascular pathological changes [19], which we confirmed occurs under hypoxic conditions in the present study. We further showed that hypoxia induced microglia to secrete the potent proangiogenic factors FGF2, VEGF, and PDGF-β. These proinflammatory and proangiogenic factors might collectively contribute to pathological changes in the retinal vasculature, as evidenced by the promotion of HRMEC leakage, apoptosis, proliferation, migration, and tube formation by CM from hypoxia-treated HMC3 cells. Mechanistic studies indicate that hypoxia or LPS can activate TLR4–NF-κB signaling in microglia and upregulate VEGFR1, thereby enhancing the secretion of inflammatory and angiogenic mediators including IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, VEGF-A, and FGF2 [20–23]. Notably, FGF2, a type of FGF, previously implicated in angiogenesis and tissue regeneration [24–26], has been identified as a promising target for ischemic retinopathy [29, 30].

Pathological neovascularization is the typical hallmark of ischemic retinopathies. Among the molecular regulators of angiogenesis, the Notch1 signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in coordinating endothelial behavior and vascular patterning. The interplay between Dll4-mediated Notch signaling and VEGFA-VEGFR2 signaling can inhibit the differentiation of endothelial cells into tip cells [27–29]. During sprouting angiogenesis, the Dll4–Notch signaling pathway can maintain arterial blood–retinal barrier homeostasis by inhibiting transcytosis [30]. In contrast, the JAG1 ligand is predominantly expressed in stalk cells and exerts distinct regulatory effects [31]. Under diabetic conditions, JAG1 expression is upregulated in retinal endothelial cells, leading to increased vascular permeability [32]. Under hypoxia, JAG1-mediated Notch1 activation has been shown to promote IL-33–driven pathological angiogenesis [33]. Although the regulatory role of Notch signaling in retinal vascular endothelial cells has been extensively characterized, its functional implications in retinal microglia remain poorly defined. In this study, we found that the expression of JAG1 and Notch1 increased in microglia under hypoxia, whereas the levels of other Notch ligands and receptors remained unchanged. Furthermore, we observed that inhibition of Notch1 reduced the expression of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and FGF2 and abrogated retinal vasculopathy. By suggesting that the JAG1–Notch1 signaling axis may act as a critical driver of hypoxic-induced inflammatory responses and pathological angiogenesis in microglia, our findings provide a novel mechanistic insight into the pleiotropic functions of Notch ligands and their receptors in neurovascular pathophysiology.

FoxO1, the first gene identified in the FoxO family, is a key transcription regulator involved in physiological processes such as apoptosis, differentiation, metabolism, and oxidative stress [34]. Its expression is increased with the activation of Notch1 during the process of cellular differentiation [15]. In FoxO1 knockout mouse models, deletion of the Notch1 gene has been observed [35]. Several studies have demonstrated that FoxO1 can translocate into the nucleus in conjunction with the active fragment of the Notch1 — NICD, where it interacts with the nuclear transcription factor RBP-J to participate in various nuclear response processes and multiple biological functions, including vascular regeneration [36], modulation of mitochondrial fission to influence intestinal stem cell differentiation [37], and hepatic glucose metabolism [14]. However, the interplay between FoxO1 and Notch1 in retinal microglia has yet to be elucidated. Our data demonstrated a significant upregulation of FoxO1 in both hypoxic-treated HMC3 cells and retinal microglia from OIR mice. Moreover, we observed that hypoxia promoted the co-localization and co-expression of Notch1 and FoxO1 within the nuclei of microglia and that inhibition of Notch1 reduced the expression of FoxO1 and ameliorated the inflammation and pathological damage in the retinal vasculature and vice versa. These results suggest that FoxO1 synergistically cooperates with Notch1 to enter the nucleus and interact with RBP-J to promote the proinflammatory and proangiogenic phenotypes of microglia, thereby involving retinal vasculopathy in its response to hypoxic conditions.

Although the Notch signaling pathway and FoxO1 are known to be vital in regulating inflammation and angiogenesis [38–41], targeting FoxO1 with drugs is challenging because of its complex posttranscriptional modifications, various transcriptional functions, and lack of a ligand-binding domain [42]. In contrast, components of the Notch1 pathway have been validated as effective therapeutic targets. For example, dual inhibition of the Notch ligands JAG1 and Dll4 has been shown to significantly reduce tumor growth and invasion [43]. GSI has shown efficacy in treating diseases such as triple-negative breast cancer [44], acute lymphoblastic leukemia [45], and Alzheimer’s disease [46]. Therefore, targeting the Notch1 pathway may not only inhibit JAG1–Notch1 pathway activation but also attenuate the function of FoxO1 and subsequently decrease the production of downstream effectors such as IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and FGF2.

Our study provides significant insights into the mechanism by which FoxO1 interacts with Notch1 to promote microglial activation to induce pathological changes in the retinal vasculature under hypoxia and contribute to ischemic retinal diseases. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. One limitation was that we performed the in vitro experiments in cell lines, which do not adequately reflect the biological characteristics of primary cells obtained from animals or humans. Another limitation was that our study focused on the effect of microglia on retinal vascular endothelial cells despite the retinal neurovascular unit being composed of various cell types. The crosstalk and interactions among the different types of retinal cells in intricate pathological contexts are worthy of further investigation. Finally, while PDGF-β mRNA was upregulated in hypoxia-activated microglia, its protein expression and functional relevance to vasculopathy remain to be confirmed.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that the JAG1–Notch1 pathway and crosstalk with FoxO1 may contribute to the proinflammatory and proangiogenic phenotypes of microglia to promote pathological changes in retinal vascular endothelial cells under hypoxia, suggesting that targeting the Notch1–FoxO1 axis may be a therapeutic strategy for ischemic retinal disorders. Further studies are needed to explore the interactions between microglia and other retinal cells and evaluate the efficacy of the combined inhibition of Notch1 and FoxO1.
