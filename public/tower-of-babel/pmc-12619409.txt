Cost analysis for the implementation of LUCF mitigation toward NDC target in Indonesia
Authors: Annuri Rossita, Rizaldi Boer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe government of Indonesia, as a party to the UNFCCC, has committed to reducing its emissions unconditionally by 31% and conditionally up to 43% from the Business as Usual (BAU) level. Land Use Change and Forestry (LUCF) has been targeted as the main sector to meet this commitment, with a great contribution from carbon-rich ecosystems (e.g., peatlands). The sector is expected to reach about 63% of the target. Financially, participation from the private sector to meet the target is crucial. While abundant studies have calculated mitigation costs from the land sector, most of the studies were outdated, and the indirect and transaction costs are rarely taken into consideration. As we perceive such information as key for planning mitigation strategies, we aim to assess the cost required for the implementation of LUCF mitigation with the inclusion of formal transaction costs.ResultsThis study uses the Comprehensive Mitigation Assessment Process (COMAP) model, an open spreadsheet model that captures both carbon and economic benefits from mitigation activities. The results showed that mitigation costs for the LUCF sectors ranged from the lowest of USD 10 to almost USD 3,200 per ha, with the most cost-effective options (USD per ton of C) being forest conservation and peatland management activities. To achieve the unconditional NDC target, the total cost required for investment and life cycle costs amounted to USD 11,229 million and USD 34,280 million, respectively, of which 53% of it expected to be provided by the private sector, while the remaining 47% from the state budget.ConclusionsThe study found that mitigation activities by the private require higher life cycle costs due to a large portion of indirect and transaction costs that accounted for 10–43% and 4–19% of the total cost, respectively. Considering the financially significant contribution from the private sector to achieve the NDC target, and to increase the participation of non-party actors to the NDC target, this study proposes more policy instruments made available for cutting these formal transaction costs and to more diversify financing sources.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13021-025-00344-x.Keywords: Indonesia’s NDC, Mitigation cost, Mitigation potential, Peatland, Private sector, Transaction cost

Background

The 6th IPCC Assessment Report revealed that, after 2030, the remaining future GHG emissions will likely exceed the remaining carbon budget for the 1.5 °C temperature limit [1]. These findings stressed the role of net negative emission technologies from land-based sectors for achieving net-zero GHG emissions. In Indonesia, the Land-Use Change and Forestry (LUCF) sector contributed to around 51% of the total national emissions, exceeding the energy sector, which was only 32–34% [2–4]. The Government committed to reducing its emissions unconditionally by 31% and conditionally up to 43% from the BAU level in 2030 in their Enhanced Nationally Determined Contributions (ENDC) [5, 6]. Considering its great share of national emissions, LUCF is the key sector which expected to cover around 60% of the target, with a major contribution from carbon-rich ecosystems (e.g., peatlands) [5].

As investment is one of the keys to unlocking the great potential of land-based mitigation in developing countries [7], reliable and updated mitigation costs are urgently needed. Researches showed that the average mitigation cost for the LUCF sector started from about 2.5–9.5 USD/ton C [8, 9], while for the energy sector, it ranges from 7.36 USD/ton C for geothermal energy, to more than 21.82 USD/ton C for solar cells [8], possibly stretch to 70 USD/ton C [10]. The wide gap in cost estimation between the two sectors is mainly caused by the underestimated LUCF cost from merely calculating the direct costs component [11]. As land-based mitigation activities most frequently interact with land users, there are risks of indirect costs (e.g., administration for policy compliance) and transaction costs, an expense incurred beyond the production cost or all of the costs arising from the exchange (business) with other parties [11, 12]. Therefore, we argue that existing LUCF mitigation cost references are outdated and no longer adequately reflect the evolving policy landscape and forestry sector dynamics in Indonesia.

Referring to [13], transaction costs are divided into three broad categories, i.e., search and information costs, bargaining and decision costs, and policing and enforcement costs. In the LUCF sector case, the social cost is one of the most common sources of transaction costs burdened on the private sector to address land conflicts. These expenses were stated as the main barrier for companies to accelerate the establishment of their plantation; therefore, making the LUCF mitigation less attractive to the private and potential investors [14, 15].

The significant role of the LUCF sector in ENDC achievement and the lack of updated and reliable national-level cost assessment motivated us to conduct a study that could estimate mitigation costs for Indonesia’s LUCF mitigation activities. The inclusion of the indirect and transaction costs, which were gathered from primary data, will provide more realistic cost information to support the strategic framing of mitigation actions by weighing the costs, benefits, and environmental impacts under the limited land resources. Such information could also identify which mitigation options are more likely to meet the target with the least cost, while also maintaining the essential environmental services and production objectives in maximizing economic and social benefits.

Under these motives, this study aims to assess the cost-effectiveness of several LUCF mitigation options by taking into account the indirect and transaction costs for estimating the total cost required to meet both unconditional and conditional Indonesia NDC targets.

Methods

This study used the Comprehensive Mitigation Assessment Process (COMAP) model to assess the cost-effectiveness of mitigation options (e.g., investment cost and life cycle cost) per ha and per ton of C unit [16], which has been used in similar studies [17–19]. The spreadsheet-based feature of the model offers a rapid assessment with low computational time. The open spreadsheet enables an easy interface for diverse users, from academics to policymakers, which benefits the transparency of the analysis. With these features, we were able to modify the model to accommodate the analysis for carbon-rich ecosystems like peatland. The updated COMAP model in capturing the declining soil carbon from unsustainable peatland management is considered a novel use of the COMAP model in this study compared to existing literature.

We assessed several mitigation options in the forestry sector, which include sink enhancement through tree planting activities in low carbon stock lands, enrichment planting of degraded natural forests, carbon loss avoidance through forest protection activities, and improved forest management through the Reduce Impact Logging (RIL) technique. We also assessed mitigation options in peatland intending to reduce the rate of peat decomposition in degraded and cultivated peatland through peat restoration and peat water management. Mitigation options in this study have been set to consistently follow LUCF mitigation activities as stated in the official document of Indonesia’s NDC for three scenarios of BaU and Counter Measure (CM) 1 and 2; thus, covering a similar implementation timeframe from 2013 to 2030.

Mitigation options

Reforestation

Tree planting in mineral soil aims for degraded and unproductive land with low carbon stock (i.e., dry shrubs, grassland, and bare land). Carbon stock for the baseline condition of dry shrubs, grassland, and bare land is assumed to be 29.9 ton of C/ha [20–26], 7.2 ton of C/ha [24, 27–29], and 6.5 ton of C/ha [20], respectively. As reforestation activities are intended for production, at the end of the rotation, the trees will be harvested for timber and non-timber forest products. Species used for reforestation assessment in mineral soil are Acacia (Acacia mangium), Sago (Metroxylon sp.), Rubber (Hevea brasiliensis), Meranti (Shorea sp.), and Teak (Tectona grandis).

For the peatland, reforestation means planting native tree species that are tolerant to peat natural characteristics or paludiculture [30]. The intention is to continue the land utilization for economic benefits while minimizing the environmental degradation impact. Native species used for this activity are Sago (Metroxylon sp.), Meranti (Shorea balangeran), and Jelutung (Dyera polyphylla). As the activity is supposed to be aligned with the community plantation program, the baseline conditions for peatland restoration are existing agricultural land (i.e., dryland agriculture, mixed dryland agriculture), grassland, and bare land, with soil emission assumed to be 13.91 ton of C/ha/year, 9.55 ton of C/ha/year, and 13.91 ton of C/ha/year, respectively [31].

During the pre-planting period, drained peatland needs to be rewetted using canal block construction. The rewetting process will reduce the soil emission to 1.5 tC/ha/year [31]. After rewetting, we assumed the dryland agriculture would die. However, for the mixed dryland agriculture, we assumed only the perennial trees survive the after-effect of rewetted peatland. In this case, the carbon stock will be cut into 16.65 tC/ha [28, 32, 33]. For grassland and bare land, carbon stock after rewetting was assumed to be 7.2 tC/ha [24, 27–29], and 6.5 tC/ha [20], respectively.

Improved water management in peatland

The objective of improving peatland water management is to avoid the over-drained peatland, particularly during a dry season. In this activity, the water level from the current condition, which we refer to as the baseline scenario or poor water management, will be increased close to the peat surface by at least 40 cm, which we refer to as the mitigation scenario or good water management1. The water level for baseline condition is 70 cm for Acacia (Acacia crassicarpa), and 65 cm for palm oil (Elaeis guineensis Jacq), which will be increased to 40 cm from the surface. For rubber, we set 60 cm as the water level baseline and 20 cm as the mitigation scenario.

Mitigation potential or carbon benefits from this activity refer to the value of emission reduction between the mitigation scenario of good water management and the baseline scenario of poor water management. The mitigation cost or additional spending required for the private sector was only the cost for education and training to manage the water level, as we assumed that the infrastructure for water management or water gate is already available. Contrary to the community, mitigation cost consists of additional costs for education and training and the construction of water management infrastructure.

Rehabilitation of degraded lands

The concept of land rehabilitation is relatively akin to reforestation, except for the emphasis on carbon conservation. Under the definition, when trees reach a mature stage, wood harvesting is prohibited. Species used for rehabilitation programs in mineral land are Acacia, Sago, Rubber, Meranti, and Teak. The baseline land cover for rehabilitation activity in mineral land consists of dry shrub, bare land, and grassland with a carbon stock amounting to 29.9 tC/ha [20–26], 7.2 tC/ha [24, 27–29], and 6.5 tC/ha [20], respectively.

Rehabilitating peatland comprises two main activities i.e., canal blocking and tree planting using species tolerant to flooding conditions, such as sago, meranti, Ramin (Gonystylus bancanus), and jelutung. For peatland rehabilitation executed by the private sector, we assume the baselines were dry shrub and wet shrub with a total carbon stock and soil emission for both baselines are 26.7 ton of C/ha [20, 23, 34–36] and 5.18 ton of C/ha/year [31], respectively. The baseline conditions for rehabilitation by the community are bare land and wet shrubs with total carbon stock 6.5 [20] and 26.7 ton of C/ha [20, 23, 34–36], respectively, and 13.91 and 5.18 ton of C/ha/year [31] of soil emission, respectively. After the rewetting activity, the emission factor from peat soil is assumed to be 1.5 tC/ha/year [31].

Enhanced natural regeneration (ENR)

The main purpose of Enhanced Natural Regeneration is to accelerate the regeneration of degraded natural forests by conducting enrichment planting using endemic species. In peatland, this program includes the rewetting process to restore the hydrological condition of the drained peatland and to accelerate the process of biomass regeneration. Under flooded conditions, the emission factor of the degraded forest is assumed to decline from 5.3 to 1.5 tC/ha/year [31].

Reduced impact logging (RIL)

The baseline condition for this option assumed that no RIL activity taking place in the logging concessionaire, of which the concessionaire adopted a Conventional Logging (CL) technique. Conventional logging is a common logging method that highly depends on a tractor for skidding; hence, considered to be the least sustainable than the RIL technique which highly considers the ecological aspect to ensure minimum destruction impact. Therefore, in the mitigation scenario we assumed that in the logged forest, timber was harvested by applying RIL. In this case, RIL implementation will generate a positive mitigation potential compared to conventional logging. Data used for RIL and CL activities were obtained from the RIL project of Karya Lestari Company in East Kalimantan [37]. The difference in cost-effectiveness between RIL and CL refers to mitigation costs for RIL implementation.

Forest protection

Forest protection is necessary to improve forest preservation by increasing forest monitoring (e.g., patrolling), strengthening community-based forest management programs, including moratorium policies, and improving fire control for the peat forest. Mitigation costs required for forest protection consist of the initial cost for infrastructure and operational needs for patrolling, and the annual cost for education and training, community empowerment, forest security, and fire control. In the NDC, the implementation of this program aims to reduce the deforestation rate by approximately 50% percent compared to the BAU rate and to avoid degradation of the primary forest from illegal activities [5].

As deforestation and forest degradation rates for the scenario of Counter Measure (CM) 1 and 2 written in the NDC are different, the model estimates the cost of forest protection for each scenario. Carbon stock for primary forest and secondary forest in mineral soil is assumed to be 266.0 and 197.7 ton of Biomass per hectare [38], respectively. Carbon stocks used for primary forest and secondary forest in peat soil are assumed to be 192.7 and 159.3 tons of Biomass per hectare [38], respectively. Deforestation and degradation in peat soil are assumed to result in the annual soil carbon loss of 13.86 and 5.18 tons of C per ha per year [31], respectively.

We defined indirect cost as expenses outside direct (e.g., production, planting) that mainly fulfill the company’s obligations (e.g., retribution cost, CSR, etc.) to the Ministry of Forestry (MoF), as the authority for the Indonesia forestry concessionaire. Referring to these definitions and assumptions, it is important to note that for each mitigation option and actors (e.g., private sector and community), the cost component and its value are distinctive. Mitigation area

The allocated area for mitigation activities depends on the available land for each forest function, obtained from the Ministry of Forestry (MoF)2. According to Constitution No.41/1999, forest areas in Indonesia are divided into four functions, i.e., production forest, protection forest, conservation forests, and other land use areas (known as APL). A production forest is a forest area that functions to produce timber and non-timber forest products. Production forest consists of Permanent Production Forest (HP), and Convertible Production Forest (HPK), a forest area in which its function can be converted to a non-forest area for development or as APL (i.e., for agriculture, settlement, etc.). Protection Forest (HL) is a forest area with the main function of maintaining the hydrology system, flooding prevention function, erosion control, seawater intrusion, and soil fertility. Conservation Forest (HK) is a forest area with high flora and fauna biodiversity (National Park, Forest Park, Hunting Park, etc.).

Each mitigation option was assigned to a specific forest function referring to the land availability in 2013 as the initial year of assessment in this study. In mineral soil, the total area covered by natural forest for about 84.9 M ha, and unproductive lands (shrubs, grassland, and bare land) account for about 26.2 M ha, while in peatland is about 6.6 and 3.7 M ha respectively (Table 2). Primary forest in minerals, around 50% of 84.9 M ha [40] is the target area for forest protection activity to avoid degradation and the remaining forests aim for activity reducing deforestation and sustainable forest management (RIL and ENR), while the non-forest area, or unproductive land, is allocated for reforestation and land rehabilitation.

Carbon inputs to measure mitigation potential

Mitigation potential is defined as an indicator that portrays the amount of carbon sequestered or maintained from mitigation activity. To calculate the mitigation potential for each mitigation option, we collect the carbon data inputs from various literature yet still maintained to use similar references as for Indonesia’s national GHG inventory. The carbon data inputs include aboveground and belowground carbon, of which we distinguish based on species and soil types. In this study, we assumed that land management is distinctive by owners (e.g., private sector, community); therefore, we set separate carbon inputs for each actor to depict different rates of mitigation potential (see supplementary table S1−5).

Unlike the original assessment of Indonesia’s NDC, in this study, we calculate the emission reduction (in this study referred to as mitigation potential), as a cumulative carbon sequestered from one cycle activity of each mitigation option, rather than dynamically calculate them in an annual scale. The mitigation potential value also stores the value of below-ground biomass, soil carbon, and carbon product, while for NDC official document only covers the above-ground biomass. In addition, we also disaggregate the mitigation activities into more detailed options (i.e., the variety of species and the actors involved). Thus, this may lead to the incomparable result of total emission reduction and not guarantee the full similarity to Indonesia’s NDC.

Model uncertainties

As the COMAP model was designed with a relatively simple function and mathematical process, the input data and assumptions largely contributed to the uncertainty of the results. These cover the uncertainties of input data for carbon emission reduction potential and cost-effectiveness estimates. In this study, we attempted to use national-level datasets that have passed the peer-reviewed process similar to the NDC method, whilst the cost data were obtained from field interviews and interview-based references (please refer to the supplementary table S1−6).

In addition to the data source, the calculation of the life-cycle cost information is likely affected by the choice of discount rates. To test the discount rates’ effects on cost effectiveness, we performed a sensitivity analysis for the ± 1–2% upper and lower bounds from the baseline discount rate settings (5%), the moderate range of common discount rates for assessing climate mitigation costs [41, 42]. The sensitivity analysis showed that the increasing discount rate leads to lower mitigation cost estimation, and vice versa. In addition, the higher the mitigation scale (e.g., scenario CM2 compared to BaU and CM1 scenarios), the less responsive it is to the discount rate change. The effect of a ± 1–2% discount rate on total mitigation costs is lower in the CM2 scenario (13–39%) compared to the CM1 scenario (14–41%) (please refer to the supplementary table S7).

Results

Cost-Effectiveness

The cost assessment in this study found that most of the cost components spent by the private sector were for non-direct mitigation activities (Fig. 1). These findings explained the high investment (Table 5) and life cycle cost (Table 6) needed for the private sector to execute forestry business as part of a land-based mitigation scheme. The portion of indirect and transaction costs for the company ranged about 10–43% (65–1,344 USD/ha) and 4–19% (96–101 USD/ha) from the total life cycle cost, respectively.

In the USD/ton C unit, the indirect and transaction costs made a clear difference to the mitigation impact, particularly for the life cycle cost (Tables 5 and 6), where suppressing the non-direct mitigation cost potentially increased the attainable emission reduction. This study also found that the effort to conserve natural forests was considered to be more cost-effective (in the USD/ton C unit) than reforesting the critical land (Tables 5 and 6). In addition, this study also confirmed that mitigation activities in peatland require the least life cycle cost per ton C (Table 6) albeit the high investment per ha is required to restore the hydrological condition of the degraded peat (Table 5).

Of all mitigation activities, improved peatland water management is the cheapest option, as it only requires cost for education and training programs for private who already own proper water management in the concession area. In general, the mitigation cost for LUCF we obtained in this study was higher than the other studies for regional and global assessment [15, 17, 43–45]. The result of this study also shows a nearly consistent mitigation cost as stated in Indonesia’s NDC breakdown document for the LUCF sector [46].

Cost for the implementation of LUCF mitigation activities toward NDC target

As a developing country, information regarding the total mitigation cost for its conditional and unconditional target is central to developing a clearer road map and designing a strategy for climate finance. Based on the result by multiplying mitigation cost per ha (Table 6) with the allocated area per mitigation option (Table 4), we found that to achieve the CM 1 NDC target, the total cost required for investment and one cycle expenses are USD 11,229 million and USD 34,280 million, respectively (Fig. 2). As an unconditional target, these amounts of cost should be provided by our national budget, which from the total life cycle cost required for CM1, 53% is expected to be provided by private sectors, while 47% by the state and public budget.

The total cost as provided in Fig. 2 was a cumulative cost for one cycle of the project until 2030. The estimated annual life cycle cost was USD 1,904 million, which is approximately equal to 1% of Indonesia’s GDP in 2019. The result obtained from this study showed that the previous cost estimation for Indonesia’s unconditional target was still underestimated (USD 5,557 million) [3] compared to this study (USD 34,280 million) after the inclusion of indirect and transaction costs. To achieve bolder commitment under the CM 2 scenario, there is an additional cost from the international source of funds required for investment, and the life cycle cost amounted to USD 2,969 million and USD 6,393 million, respectively.

Discussions

Heading to the initial year of NDC’s implementation, information regarding mitigation costs is urgently needed, along with the upcoming mandatory task to report the NDC implementation under the Biennial Transparency Report. While the long-term assessment for the LUCF mitigation spans uncertainties, we argue that the mitigation cost effectiveness could offer valuable insights for potential policy intervention. Considering the critical role of the discount rate in estimating the total mitigation costs, we advise interpreting mitigation costs as a range or relative magnitude to BaU, rather than fixed value results.

Our attempts to estimate mitigation costs with the inclusion of indirect and transaction costs have revealed two main findings. First, there is a high share of transaction costs for the private sector, which will eventually make land-based mitigation schemes less attractive for investment. Second, our results showed a higher mitigation cost estimate compared to the previous findings, due to more comprehensive cost components covered in our analysis (Table 1) and more recent cost information from primary data collection. However, as this study only includes formal transaction costs, there are still potentially uncaptured informal transaction costs (i.e., costs to resolve land conflict, bribery costs) [47, 48] remain uncovered.

To address the above findings, we suggest two policy directions. First, there should be an exemption of formal transaction costs made by the government through various financing schemes (e.g., incentives, tax reduction, etc.), which we addressed in detail for each mitigation type in the following passage. Second, there is an urgency to diversify financing sources by exploring other options, such as philanthropic and green bonds4, which have gained interest worldwide for sustainable projects [49–51]. Additionally, we argue that it is necessary to further reassess a fair share of financing sources for a feasible NDC achievement.

Incentive schemes are needed to accelerate the forest plantation implementation rate. Referring to the large area allocated for private-based mitigation activities through the establishment of forest plantations (Table 4), policy instruments to encourage investments and/or for cutting transaction costs (e.g., incentive schemes) are required to increase the participation of non-party actors, which then supports the country heading to a better land-based emission trajectory [52]. The incentive for the indirect and formal transaction costs will put less pressure on companies that also face other informal transaction costs for addressing land tenure, to avoid other potential illegal transaction costs [48]. Examples of incentive schemes available globally include low-cost seedlings and provision of infrastructure, tax concessions, loans for concessionaires, support for product marketing and research, Payment for Ecosystem Services (PES), etc [53–55].

In parallel to the incentive scheme, a more stringent regulation is still needed to ensure the increasing planting rate. In some cases, the exemption of the transaction cost might not be sufficient to accelerate the HTI plantation. In late 2017, only 14% of the designated HTI concession was an actual plantation, while the remaining area was mainly covered by unproductive land [46]. Additional schemes (i.e., regulation and sanction) are required to complement the incentive program and to increase the annual planting rate from the private sector. Boosting the HTI planting rate is crucial to achieving the timber production target in 2030, which equals 81% of the available plantation area [46].

Land competition for timber plantations and energy plantations urgently needs to be addressed to control wood production from natural forests. Under NDC mitigation strategies, ensuring a sufficient wood supply from the plantation area is expected to reduce wood demand from the natural forest; thus, maintaining the deforestation rate of CM 1 and CM 2 targets. However, given the projected rise of wood demand in the future [56, 57] and the expected land demand for energy plantations under the NDC target [6], balancing wood supply and demand from timber plantations will be an enormous challenge. Therefore, many studies have suggested the development of second-generation biomass energy from the forests’ byproducts (e.g., residual biomass) and the use of alternative biomass for wood-based products (e.g., oil palm trunk, rubber) [57–60].

Protecting natural forests in the concession area could be synergized with the REDD + mechanism. It is worth noting that a timber plantation permit is not usually granted only in unproductive areas. A large natural forest with essential ecosystem services was identified inside the concession area (i.e., HGU, HTI, and indicative of a social forestry area). These forests were under the threat of being deforested. Alternatively, the protected forest can be assigned to the MoF to receive the incentive from the REDD + program if the area overlaps with the REDD + work-designated area.

Incentive scheme to acknowledge concessionaries who obtain Sustainable Forest Management (SFM) certification. Almost half of Indonesia’s forest is a secondary forest, signifying the extent of forest degradation in Indonesia. These secondary forests have a varied percentage of vegetation cover, of which the area with > 70% vegetation cover should be aimed for RIL practices, while the remaining area for ENR. The area with the lowest vegetation cover needs to be urgently targeted for the SFM program. Currently, SFM certification in Indonesia is merely perceived as a compliance act and is less suitable for small-scale actors [54]. To increase the amount of certified SFM in the concessionaire area, Indonesia could adopt fiscal incentive mechanisms like tax reduction in Brazil and Peru, and non-fiscal incentives (e.g., technical assistance for small-scale actors, networks for international buyers [54, 61].

Social forestry program for increasing critical land rehabilitation in areas with high deforestation risk. By referring to the result in Table 6, conserving the forest is more cost-effective than reforesting critical land. However, it was found that critical land is currently still increasing and there is still a large amount of forest area with a high-rank bio-geophysics index [46] means that these forests will release significantly high emissions if converted to non-forest areas. This indicates the urgency for implementing social forestry to assist the rehabilitation program in critical land by referring to the Ministerial Law of MoEF No.83/20165. Budget spending for this program is a two-way benefit, in which conserving the forest will also lead to a higher incentive received for the government (i.e., from the REDD + program) while achieving the national SDG target.

Social forestry potentially encourages community roles for mitigation activities. Though the indirect cost for community-based mitigation activities was lower than the private, support schemes are still required to lower the production gap between community plantations and the privates and thus increase the mitigation potential of community plantations. For a region with a strong social capital, the scheme options are community forest (HKm), village forest (HD), and partnership scheme with the private. These schemes intend to attract public participation to the land-based mitigation program. However, as most of the critical land in non-forest areas (APL) is now cultivated with seasonal cropping, reforestation activity should be modified into the agroforestry program. If the land were under the FMU work area, the activity needs to be in synergy with social forestry and, optionally, community timber plantation.

The more cost-effective mitigation in peatland compared to mineral comes with a societal and market challenge. The inclusion of two different ecosystems (mineral and peatland) in this study enables us to make a comparative assessment of mitigation costs and benefits. Despite the more cost-effective mitigation activities in peatland than in mineral soil, the execution of these options was more challenging. For peatlands under the concession area, on-site implementation of improved water management is low due to the declined risk on land productivity of commercial commodities under the wetter condition of peat soil [62]. Moreover, for community peatland, there is still low interest in planting native peat species with considerably lower profit and unavailable promising market for the product compared to other commercial commodities [63]. Meanwhile, the annual rate for restoring the peatland was still insufficient to catch up with the NDC target, with just 10% actual implementation in 2018 from the cumulative designated mitigation area in 2030.

The long-term effort for restoring the peatland requires sustained financing from both domestic and international. Under the constraint of limited available land, mitigation in peat needs to be effectively undertaken as it requires at least 60 years for peatland to ecologically recover the carbon regulating function [64]. With a relatively higher mitigation cost per ha for peat than mineral soil, the current source of funding to restore Indonesia’s peatland still highly depends on the state budget. The recent report of the Peat Restoration Agency (BRG) stated that the total funding required to restore peatland until 2020 amounted to IDR 10,600 billion, equal to approximately USD 672 million, of which around USD 111.57 million has been received from international support [65]. A more recent report on activities in 2022 and 2023 revealed an additional IDR 94 billion in expenses for restoration activities in these periods from the state budget, excluding the amount of international support [66, 67]. Referring to the results of this study, to achieve CM1 and CM2 emission reduction targets for peat restoration, an additional budget of USD 536 million and USD 1,198 million are required, which recommends the urgency to diversify the financing source for upscaling peatland restoration work.

While the carbon market potentially funds LUCF mitigation activities, additional transaction costs may emerge. Globally, Article 6 of the Paris Agreement provides an alternative financing source for supporting countries in achieving their NDC targets. At the national level, the Government of Indonesia recently released Presidential Regulation No. 98/2021 regarding carbon economic value to encourage business entities to adopt sustainable practices and reduce emissions6. The profits from the carbon trade are potentially a promising financing source for carbon-sequestering sectors like LUCF. However, the derivative regulations of carbon economic value stated that the private sector who are willing to join the carbon market needs to develop either a Mitigation Action Design Document (DRAM) or a Project Design Document (PDD) to be integrated under the company’s work plan. In this case, there is a potentially uncaptured, larger indirect cost to enable the private sector to make a carbon transaction.

Transaction costs signified inefficient forestry business mechanisms and a lack of assurance and protection for implementing forestry business. Beyond its value, the transaction cost was more perceived as an indication of the nonexistence of impartial justice for the land and forestry sector [13, 39]. In addition, the amount of transaction cost also indicates the possibility of one entity taking advantage of another and the inefficient work of an institution to provide low-cost measurement and contract enforcement. Many studies have also raised the issue of bribery and the fallen of institutions to be fully responsible when issuing the permit for land utilization [68, 69], which also relates to the land tenure issue and low confidence in spatial information [70] to become the tools for the closure.

The LUCF mitigation plan is an indirect synergy with the agriculture sector. The success of the implementation of LUCF mitigation depends on the agricultural conditions, where high planting intensity and improved productivity with support from advanced technology will lower the pressure on the forest and avoid the possibility of new forest clearing. The increasing deforestation rate exceeding the deforestation quota will make the emission less likely to be offset by carbon sequestration. Therefore, cross-sectoral coordination is vital to avoid potential leakage with the agriculture extensification effort.

Conclusion

Mitigation costs for the LUCF sectors ranged from USD 10 to almost USD 3200 per ha. The most cost-effective options are the activities intended for forest conservation and peatland management. From all LUCF mitigation options, reforestation activities, particularly the ones executed by the private sector, were the options that require high investment and life cycle costs. This was due to the huge amount of indirect and transaction costs weighed on the company, which accounted for 10–42% and 4–19% of the total life cycle cost, respectively. This high cost is considered to be the main barrier for the private to accelerate the plantation area in achieving the NDC target. Thus, incentive schemes for cutting transaction costs are required to increase the participation of non-party actors towards the NDC target. To achieve the unconditional target, the total cost required for investment and one-cycle expenses are USD 11,229 million and USD 34,280 million, respectively, with 53% of it expected to be provided by the private sector, and 47% by the state budget. Additional costs from international support required for investment and life cycle costs to achieve bolder commitment under the CM2 scenario amounted to USD 2,969 million and USD 6,393 million, respectively. As 2020 is the starting point of NDC implementation, information regarding mitigation costs is needed to support the development of a land-based mitigation roadmap and to complement future assessments regarding Indonesia’s trajectory of emission reduction with consideration of available funding.
