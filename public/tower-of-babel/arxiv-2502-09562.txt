On extensions of Cohen Structure Theorem
Elena Caviglia, Amartya Goswami, Luca Mesiti

Abstract
. The aim of this paper is to extend Cohen structure theorem beyond local rings. Both Cohen structure theorem and Nagata’s generalization of it are special cases of our results. We investigate for which rings R R there exists a maximal ideal 𝔪 \mathfrak{m} of R R such that the canonical projection R → R / 𝔪 R\to R/\mathfrak{m} has a section, so that R / 𝔪 R/\mathfrak{m} is isomorphic to a field κ \kappa contained in R R . We present two equivalent characterizations of this property and use them to exhibit two classes of rings that satisfy it. Moreover, we provide several examples (not necessarily local or complete local), as well as methods to construct new examples.

13H99, 13E05, 13B30.

1.
Introduction

Let
A
be a local Noetherian ring with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
. The ring
A
is said to be
equicharacteristic

if
A
has the same characteristic as its residue field
A/\mathfrak{m}
. A
field of representatives
for
A

is a subfield
\kappa
in
A
that maps onto
A/\mathfrak{m}
under the canonical projection. Since

\kappa
is a field, the restriction of this mapping to
\kappa
gives an isomorphism between
\kappa
and
A/\mathfrak{m}
.
The
Cohen structure theorem says that

Theorem 1.1
.

Every equicharacteristic complete local Noetherian commutative ring
A
admits a field of representatives.

Over the years, this pivotal result in commutative algebra has proved to be significant–for example, in proving category equivalence (see
[
8
, Proposition 1.1]
) between the
category of all finite
R
-modules of finite projective and all finite
R
-modules of finite injective dimension for noncommutative Cohen–Macaulay local rings
R
; in application (see
[
7
, Theorem 3.1]
) of asserting the existence of a local extension ring
S
of local Noetherian
R
under certain conditions on
S
; in proving a result on
2
-step solvable groups of finite Morley rank (see
[
6
, Section 3]
); in showing the existence of a local field (see
[
10
, Theorem 5]
) of a topological
quotient
ring
A
(with characteristic
either
zero or a prime) of a commutative,
special
basic
ring; in characterizing a special local ring (see
[
12
, Theorem 2]
) which is
Hausdorff with respect to the
U
-adic topology; in theorems (see
[
18
]
) on quotient rings of simple subvarieties; in constructing functors (see
[
2
, Theorem 1.4]
) between the category of complete residual
perfections of a ring and the full subcategory of the category of
\mathds{F}_{p}
-algebras whose
objects are perfect; in showing the existence of a functor (see
[
11
, Proposition 3.1.4]
) from based fields to based Cohen rings which
is a quasi-inverse of the residue field functor; in showing a
K
-isomorphism (see
[
9
, Proposition 5.3]
) of a complete
n
-discrete
k
-field containing a perfect subfield
K
(with trivial valuation); in establishing the reciprocity law (see
[
14
, Lemma 2.8]
) along a vertical curve for residues of differential
forms on arithmetic surfaces; showing well-definiteness of a family of invariants (see
[
17
]
) associated to any local ring whose residue
field has prime characteristic; in Dieudonné theory (see
[
5
]
) over complete regular local rings; in passaging from an order-by-order solution to a formal solution (see
[
3
, Theorem 2.4]
); in proving an embedding lemma (see
[
1
, Lemma 4.6]
) for
\mathds{Z}
-valued fields.

Apart from its numerous applications, as discussed above, significant research has been dedicated to the structure theorem itself. In
[
4
]
, local ring actually means a commutative unitary Noetherian ring with a unique maximal ideal; whereas in
[
15
]
Nagata obtained a similar structural result weakening the Noetherian condition. The proof of the latter result was further simplified in
[
16
]
. On the other hand, a weaker version of Cohen structure theorem has been proved for any local ring (not necessarily complete or Noetherian) in
[
13
]
.

Our aim in this paper is to extend Cohen structure theorem, studying which rings
R
have the following property:

(\star)\;

There exists a maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
of
R
such that the canonical projection
R\xrightarrow{\pi}R/\mathfrak{m}
has a section, so that
R/\mathfrak{m}
is isomorphic to a field
\kappa
contained in
R
.

In other words,
(\star)
asks
R
to contain a subfield
\kappa
and a maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
such that
R/\mathfrak{m}\cong\kappa
, in a way that gives a section to the canonical projection
R\to R/\mathfrak{m}
. Of course
R/\mathfrak{m}
is always a field, but we are interested in when this field is a subring of
R
. Notice that property
(\star)
is stronger than having

R/\mathfrak{m}\cong\kappa\subseteq R,

as we want the ring homomorphism
R/\mathfrak{m}\cong\kappa\subseteq R
to be a section of the canonical projection
R\to R/\mathfrak{m}
.
For a local Noetherian ring, property
(\star)
coincides with having a field of representatives. So by Theorem
1.1
, every equicharacteristic complete local Noetherian commutative ring satisfies property
(\star)
. For example, the ring of formal power series
\kappa\llbracket x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rrbracket
over a field
\kappa
with maximal ideal
\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rangle
. And of course a trivial example is any field
\kappa
. Moreover, notice that if a ring
A
contains a field
\kappa
, then it is equicharacteristic.

However, there exist numerous rings that satisfy property

(\star)
without being complete local or even local (see Section
2
). Our aim is to gain a deeper understanding of the class of rings—much broader than those satisfying the assumptions of Cohen’s structure theorem—that satisfy property

(\star)
.

We show how Cohen structure theorem as well as Nagata’s generalization are special cases of our result (see Theorem
3.7
). We first prove two equivalent characterizations (Theorem
3.2
and Theorem
3.5
) of property
(\star)
, which will then help us check property
(\star)
more easily. In fact, usually way more easily than checking the completeness property required in Cohen structure theorem. We achieve our characterizations considering a semidirect product of rings. Moreover, we produce several methods to construct examples of rings that satisfy property
(\star)
(see for example Proposition
4.1
and Proposition
4.9
). We present numerous examples of different kinds of rings that satisfy property
(\star)
.

To set up the ground, let us settle with the notation and terminologies. Unless otherwise specified, all rings considered in this paper will be unitary with identity element denoted as 1, and all ring homomorphisms will preserve 1. All the ideals we consider will be two-sided. We denote the set of maximal ideals in a ring
R
by
\mathrm{Max}(R),
whereas the notation
\mathfrak{m}^{*}
denotes the set of all non-zero elements in a maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}.
By
U(R)
, we denote the set of invertible elements in
R
. If
\kappa
is a field, then the set of all invertible elements in
\kappa
is denoted by
\kappa^{*}
. When
\kappa\subseteq A
, then we sometimes represent it as the canonical inclusion map
\iota\colon\kappa\to A,
and we write
\iota(\kappa)
. Such a ring
R
can be seen as a
\kappa
-algebra. Whenever we say ‘
R
is a
\kappa
-algebra’, we mean that there exists a copy of the field
\kappa
included in
R
.

2.
Motivating examples

In this section, we present several motivating examples that led us to study the problem of interest.

Example 2.1
.

Rings of polynomials
\kappa[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
with coefficients in a field
\kappa
clearly satisfy property
(\star)
. Indeed,
\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle
is a maximal ideal. Moreover the ring homomorphism

\kappa[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\to\kappa

that sends every polynomial
p
to its degree 0 coefficient
p(0)
is surjective, and its kernel is precisely
\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle
. By the first isomorphism theorem, we obtain that

\kappa[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]/\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle\cong\kappa.

And, of course, a copy of
\kappa
is included in
\kappa[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
. It is easy to check that

\kappa[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]/\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle\cong\kappa\subseteq\kappa[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]

gives a section to the canonical projection
\kappa[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\to\kappa[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]/\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle
.

Remark 2.2
.

Although
\mathds{R}[x]
satisfies property
(\star)
, exhibited by
\langle x\rangle\in\mathrm{Max}\left({\mathds{R}[x]}\right)
and the field
\mathds{R}\subseteq\mathds{R}[x]
, not every maximal ideal of
\mathds{R}[x]
works. Indeed,
\langle x^{2}+1\rangle\in\mathrm{Max}\left({\mathds{R}[x]}\right)
, but
\mathds{R}[x]/\langle x^{2}+1\rangle\cong\mathds{C}\not\subseteq\mathds{R}
.

The following example shows that there are non-radical rings that also satisfy property
(\star)
.

Example 2.3
.

Consider the quotient ring
R=\kappa[x]/\langle x^{2}\rangle
, where
\kappa
is a field. The ideal
\langle[x]\rangle
is maximal in
R
and the quotient
R/\langle[x]\rangle
is isomorphic to
\kappa
. Indeed, the ring homomorphism that sends
[p]\in R
to the degree zero coefficient of the polynomial
p
is surjective and has kernel
\langle[x]\rangle
.
Moreover, it is easy to check that this ring homomorphism gives a section to the canonical projection
R\to R/\langle[x]\rangle
. So
R
has property
(\star)
. But
R
is not radical as
[x]
is a non-zero nilpotent element of
R
.

The following is another example of a class of rings that satisfy property
(\star)
, although being of different nature.

Example 2.4
.

Let
\kappa
be a field and let
X
be a set. Consider the ring
\operatorname{Hom}(X,\kappa)
of all functions from
X
to
\kappa
, with sum and product defined pointwise. This is a commutative unitary ring, thanks to the fact that
\kappa
is so. A copy of
\kappa
is embedded in
\operatorname{Hom}(X,\kappa)
, given by the constant functions. Let
a\in X
and consider the set

\mathfrak{m}_{a}:=\left\{{f\in\operatorname{Hom}(X,\kappa)}\left.\right|\,{f(a)=0}\right\}.

Clearly
\mathfrak{m}_{a}
is an ideal of
\operatorname{Hom}(X,\kappa)
. Moreover the ring homomorphism

\operatorname{Hom}(X,\kappa)\to\kappa

defined by
(f\colon X\to\kappa)\mapsto f(a)
is surjective and has kernel
\mathfrak{m}_{a}
. Then, by the first isomorphism theorem,

\operatorname{Hom}(X,\kappa)/\mathfrak{m}_{a}\cong\kappa.

As
\kappa
is a field, this also implies that
\mathfrak{m}_{a}
is a maximal ideal. Finally, notice that the homomorphism

\operatorname{Hom}(X,\kappa)/\mathfrak{m}_{a}\cong\kappa\subseteq\operatorname{Hom}(X,\kappa)

gives a section to the canonical projection. Indeed, given a function
f\colon X\to\kappa
, we have that
f=\operatorname{const}_{f(a)}+(f-\operatorname{const}_{f(a)})
. As
f-\operatorname{const}_{f(a)}\in\mathfrak{m}_{a}
, we obtain that
[f]=[\operatorname{const}_{f(a)}]
.

Remark 2.5
.

Different maximal ideals
\mathfrak{m}
of
\operatorname{Hom}(X,\kappa)
exhibit, together with
\kappa
, that
\operatorname{Hom}(X,\kappa)
has property
(\star)
. So the maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
involved in property
(\star)
is not unique in general. Fixed
\mathfrak{m}
, though, there can only be one field
\kappa
up to isomorphism that makes property
(\star)
hold, which is
R/\mathfrak{m}
.

We now show an example of a local
\kappa
-algebra
(A,\mathfrak{m})
such that
\kappa
is a maximal field contained in
A
, but which does not satisfy property
(\star)
.

Example 2.6
.

Consider the ring
\mathds{Q}[x]
of polynomials with rational coefficients and its localization at its maximal ideal
\langle x^{2}+1\rangle
:

R:=\mathds{Q}[x]_{\langle x^{2}+1\rangle}=\left\{{\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\in\mathds{Q}(x)}\left.\right|\,{(x^{2}+1)\text{ does not divide }g(x)}\right\}\subset\mathds{Q}(x).

Then
R
is a local ring whose residue field is isomorphic to
\mathds{Q}[x]/\langle x^{2}+1\rangle\cong\mathds{Q}(i)
(where
i^{2}=-1
). Indeed, the function

\mathds{Q}[x]_{\langle x^{2}+1\rangle}\to\mathds{Q}[x]/\langle x^{2}+1\rangle\quad\left(\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\mapsto[f(x)]\cdot[g(x)]^{-1}\right)

is a surjective ring homomorphism with kernel
\langle x^{2}+1\rangle\mathds{Q}[x]_{\langle x^{2}+1\rangle}
. The desired isomorphism follows from the first isomorphism theorem.
Obviously
R
does not contain a copy of
\mathds{Q}(i)
in it. Notice that
R
admits a maximal subfield, namely,
\mathds{Q}
. Indeed, let
\kappa
be a field such that
\mathds{Q}\subseteq\kappa\subseteq R
. Then the canonical map

R\to R/m\cong\mathds{Q}(i)

sends
\kappa
isomorphically to a subfield of
\mathds{Q}(i)
. Thus, up to isomorphism, we have
\mathds{Q}\subseteq k\subseteq\mathds{Q}(i)
, and since the extension
\mathds{Q}\subseteq\mathds{Q}(i)
has degree 2 and
k\neq\mathds{Q}(i)
, we must have
k=\mathds{Q}
. Thus,
\mathds{Q}
is a maximal subfield of
R
. It turns out that
R
is a local
\mathds{Q}
-algebra, where
\mathds{Q}
is a maximal subfield of
R
, but its residue field is not isomorphic to
\mathds{Q}
.

Example 2.7
.

Let
\kappa
be a field and consider
R:=\kappa\times\kappa
. Then
R
has a copy of
\kappa
embedded in it, given by the diagonal. Indeed, the ring homomorphism

\kappa\to\kappa\times\kappa

that sends
a
to
(a,a)
preserves 1 and is injective. Notice that the pairs of the form
(a,0)
with
a\in\kappa
do not give a copy of
\kappa
embedded in
\kappa\times\kappa
, as the corresponding injective group homomorphism
\kappa\to\kappa\times\kappa
does not preserve 1.
We have that
\kappa\times 0
is a maximal ideal in
\kappa\times\kappa
. Indeed, in general all ideals of
S\times T
(with
S,T
rings) are products of an ideal in
S
and an ideal in
T
. So if an ideal of
\kappa\times\kappa
strictly contains
\kappa\times 0
, it needs to be the whole
\kappa\times\kappa
. The ring homomorphism
\kappa\times\kappa\to\kappa
that sends
(a,b)
to
b
is surjective and its kernel coincides with
\kappa\times 0
. So by the first isomorphism theorem,
(\kappa\times\kappa)/(\kappa\times 0)\cong\kappa
. It is easy to check that

(\kappa\times\kappa)/(\kappa\times 0)\cong\kappa\subseteq\kappa\times\kappa,

and, this gives a section to the canonical projection. Indeed, given
(a,b)\in\kappa
, we have that
(a,b)=(b,b)+(a-b,0)
, whence
[(a,b)]=[(b,b)]
.

3.
Main results

To present our main results, we first establish two equivalent characterizations of property

(\star)
. These formulations will facilitate a simpler verification of property

(\star)
for a given ring. In particular, for a local ring, our characterizations are often significantly easier to check than the completeness condition required in Cohen’s structure theorem. We identify two classes of rings that satisfy property

(\star)
, one of which strictly generalizes both the class of rings satisfying the assumptions of Cohen structure theorem and those satisfying Nagata’s assumptions. Furthermore, we provide a method for constructing examples of rings that satisfy property

(\star)
. In the next section, we shall present additional methods for constructing such examples.

Let us first recall the definition of semidirect products of rings.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
B
and
S
be associative but not necessarily unitary rings. Let

\lambda\colon S\to\operatorname{End}_{{B}\mbox{-}\operatorname{Mod}}\left(B_{B}\right)

be a ring homomorphism and

\rho\colon S\to\operatorname{End}_{{B}\mbox{-}\operatorname{Mod}}\left({}_{B}B\right)

be a ring anti-homomorphism such that for every
s,

t\in S
and
x,

y\in B
, we have

\lambda(s)\circ\rho(t)=\rho(t)\circ\lambda(s);

\rho(s)(x)\cdot y=x\cdot\lambda(s)(y).

The
semidirect product

B\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}_{\lambda,\rho}\hskip 1.29167ptS
of the (not necessarily unitary) rings
B
and
S
with respect to
\lambda
and
\rho
is constructed as follows. Its underlying additive group is given by the direct sum
B\oplus S
. We then equip it with a product using
\lambda
and
\rho
as follows. Given
(b,s),(c,t)\in B\oplus S
, we define

(b,s)\cdot(c,t):=(b\cdot c+\lambda(s)(c)+\rho(t)(b),s\cdot t).

It is straightforward to check that this is a not necessarily unitary ring. Moreover, the injective group homomorphisms
i_{B}\colon B\to B\oplus S
defined by
b\mapsto(b,0)
and
i_{S}\colon S\to B\oplus S
defined by
s\mapsto(0,s)
preserve products, with respect to the product in
B\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}_{\lambda,\rho}\hskip 1.29167ptS
defined above.

Remark 3.1
.

If
S
has 1 and both
\lambda
and
\rho
preserve 1, then also
B\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}_{\lambda,\rho}\hskip 1.29167ptS
is unitary, with
1=(0,1)
. Moreover
i_{S}(1)=(0,1)=1
, so
i_{S}
is an injective ring homomorphism and
S\subseteq B\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}_{\lambda,\rho}\hskip 1.29167ptS
.

For the rest of this paper, whenever
S
has 1 and we consider
B\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}_{\lambda,\rho}\hskip 1.29167ptS
, we implicitly assume that
\lambda
and
\rho
preserve 1, so that
B\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}_{\lambda,\rho}\hskip 1.29167ptS
is a unitary ring containing a copy of
S
.

The following theorem is inspired by a personal communication between the second author and George Janelidze.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
R
be a ring. The following two holds:

(1)

If there exists a not necessarily unitary ring
\mathfrak{m}
, a field
\kappa
and
\lambda,\rho
as in Definition
3.1
such that
R:=\mathfrak{m}\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}_{\lambda,\rho}\hskip 1.29167pt\kappa
, then
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathrm{Max}\left({R}\right)
,
\kappa\subseteq R
and
R/\mathfrak{m}\cong\kappa
, in a way such that the homomorphism
R/\mathfrak{m}\cong\kappa\subseteq R
gives a section to the canonical projection, so that
R
satisfies property
(\star)
.

(2)

If
R
satisfies property
(\star)
, exhibited by
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathrm{Max}\left({R}\right)
and a field
\kappa\subseteq R
, then
R\cong\mathfrak{m}\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}\hskip 1.29167pt\kappa
, with
\lambda
and
\rho
acting by multiplication in
R
, thanks to the inclusion
\kappa\subseteq R
.

In other words, property
(\star)
is equivalent to the ring being a semidirect product of a not necessarily unitary ring and a field. Moreover, we can always restrict to semidirect products with
\lambda
and
\rho
simply given by multiplication in
R
.

Proof.

(1) Assume that
R:=\mathfrak{m}\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}_{\lambda,\rho}\hskip 1.29167pt\kappa
, with
\kappa
a field. By Remark
3.1
,
i_{\kappa}
is an injective ring homomorphism, so that a copy of
\kappa
is included in
R
. There is a natural projection map on the second component

\pi_{2}\colon\mathfrak{m}\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}_{\lambda,\rho}\hskip 1.29167pt\kappa\to\kappa.

Of course, this is a surjective homomorphism of groups. By construction of the multiplication of the semidirect product,
\pi_{2}
also preserves the multiplication (
\lambda
and
\rho
are only used in the first component), and we have that
\pi_{2}(0,1)=1
, so that
\pi_{2}
preserves 1. The projection map
\pi_{2}
is thus a surjective ring homomorphism.
Notice that

\ker(\pi_{2})=\left\{{(x,u)\in\mathfrak{m}\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}_{\lambda,\rho}\hskip 1.29167pt\kappa}\left.\right|\,{u=0}\right\}\cong\mathfrak{m}

(as rings without
1
). So a copy of
\mathfrak{m}
is a two-sided ideal of
\mathfrak{m}\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}_{\lambda,\rho}\hskip 1.29167pt\kappa
. Therefore, by the first isomorphism theorem, we obtain

(\mathfrak{m}\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}_{\lambda,\rho}\hskip 1.29167pt\kappa)/\mathfrak{m}\cong\kappa.

Since
\kappa
is a field, this also guarantees that
\mathfrak{m}
is a maximal ideal of
\mathfrak{m}\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}_{\lambda,\rho}\hskip 1.29167pt\kappa
. Moreover, the ring homomorphism

(\mathfrak{m}\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}_{\lambda,\rho}\hskip 1.29167pt\kappa)/\mathfrak{m}\cong\kappa\subseteq\mathfrak{m}\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}_{\lambda,\rho}\hskip 1.29167pt\kappa

gives a section to the canonical projection. Indeed, given
(x,u)\in\mathfrak{m}\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}_{\lambda,\rho}\hskip 1.29167pt\kappa
, we have that
(x,u)=(0,u)+(x,0)
. Since
(x,0)
belongs to the copy of
\mathfrak{m}
, we conclude that
[(x,u)]=[(0,u)]
.

(2) Assume that there exist
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathrm{Max}\left({R}\right)
, a field
\kappa\subseteq R
and an isomorphism
j\colon R/\mathfrak{m}\cong\kappa
such that the ring homomorphism
\iota\circ j\colon R/\mathfrak{m}\cong\kappa\subseteq R
gives a section to the canonical projection
\pi\colon R\to R/\mathfrak{m}
. We can construct the semidirect product
\mathfrak{m}\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}\hskip 1.29167pt\kappa
, with
\lambda
and
\rho
acting by multiplication in
R
, thanks to the inclusion
\kappa\subseteq R
. More precisely, we define

\lambda\colon\kappa\to\operatorname{End}_{{\mathfrak{m}}\mbox{-}\operatorname{Mod}}\left(\mathfrak{m}_{\mathfrak{m}}\right)

by sending
u\in\kappa
to the endomorphism
x\in\mathfrak{m}\mapsto u\cdot_{R}x\in\mathfrak{m}
. Notice that

\lambda(u)(x+y)=u\cdot_{R}(x+y)=u\cdot_{R}x+u\cdot_{R}y,

\lambda(u)(x\alpha)=u\cdot_{R}(x\cdot_{R}\alpha)=(u\cdot_{R}x)\cdot_{R}\alpha.

So
\lambda(u)
is linear. Moreover,

\displaystyle\lambda(u+v)(x)

\displaystyle=(u+v)\cdot_{R}x=u\cdot_{R}x+v\cdot_{R}x,

\displaystyle\lambda(u\cdot v)(x)

\displaystyle=u\cdot_{R}v\cdot_{R}x=\lambda(u)(\lambda(v)(x)),

\displaystyle\lambda(1)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{id}.

Therefore,
\lambda
is a ring homomorphism. Analogously, we define

\rho\colon\kappa\to\operatorname{End}_{{\mathfrak{m}}\mbox{-}\operatorname{Mod}}\left({}_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m}\right)

by sending
u\in\kappa
to the endomorphism
x\in\mathfrak{m}\mapsto x\cdot_{R}u\in\mathfrak{m}
, and this is a ring anti-homomorphism. It is easy to check that
\lambda
and
\rho
satisfy the compatibility axioms of Definition
3.1
.
It remains to prove that
R\cong\mathfrak{m}\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}\hskip 1.29167pt\kappa
. Consider the function

\phi\colon\mathfrak{m}\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}\hskip 1.29167pt\kappa\to R

defined by
(x,u)\mapsto x+u
(thanks to the inclusion
\kappa\subseteq R
). This is of course a group homomorphism and
\phi(0,1)=1
. Moreover

\displaystyle\phi((x,u)\cdot(y,v))

\displaystyle=\phi(x\cdot y+u\cdot y+x\cdot v,u\cdot v)

\displaystyle=x\cdot y+u\cdot y+x\cdot v+u\cdot v

\displaystyle=(x+u)\cdot(y+v).

So,
\phi
is a ring homomorphism. We now construct a function

\psi\colon R\to\mathfrak{m}\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}\hskip 1.29167pt\kappa

by defining
z\mapsto(z-j([z]),j([z]))
. To see that such a function is well defined, consider
z\in R
. Since
j
(actually,
\iota\circ j
) gives a section to the canonical projection
\pi\colon R\to R/\mathfrak{m}
,

\pi(z-j([z]))=\pi(z)-\pi(j([z]))=[z]-[z]=0.

and thus,
z-j([z])\in\ker(\pi)=\mathfrak{m}
. It is straightforward to show that
\psi
is a ring homomorphism, using the fact that
j
is so and that
\lambda
and
\rho
are simply given by multiplication in
R
.
Finally, we show that
\phi
and
\psi
are inverses of each other. Of course
\phi\circ\psi=\operatorname{id}
. Given
(x,u)\in\mathfrak{m}\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}\hskip 1.29167pt\kappa
,

\psi(\phi(x,u))=(x+u-j([x+u]),j([x+u])).

To show that
j([x+u])=u
, it suffices to show that
[x+u]=j^{-1}(u)
. Since
\pi\circ\iota\circ j=\operatorname{id}
is, in particular, injective, it suffices to show that
\pi(j([x+u]))=\pi(j(j^{-1}(u)))
. But
\pi\circ\iota\circ j=\operatorname{id}
means to show that
[x+u]=[u]
, which is true as
x\in\mathfrak{m}
. So
j([x+u])=u
, and we conclude that
\psi\circ\phi=\operatorname{id}
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

The first part of the proof of (2) of Theorem
3.2
does not use the fact that
\mathfrak{m}
and
\kappa
exhibit property
(\star)
for
R
. So, every time we have
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathrm{Max}\left({R}\right)
and a field
\kappa\subseteq R
, we can construct the semidirect product
\mathfrak{m}\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}\hskip 1.29167pt\kappa
, with
\lambda
and
\rho
simply given by multiplication in
R
. We will denote such a semidirect product as
\mathfrak{m}\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}\hskip 1.29167pt\kappa
(without specifying
\lambda
and
\rho
in the notation of the semidirect product). Moreover, we always have the ring homomorphism

\phi\colon\mathfrak{m}\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}\hskip 1.29167pt\kappa\to R

that sends

to

.

The purpose of the next theorem is to aid in identifying two classes of rings that satisfy

(\star)
. However, we first need a lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
R
be a ring,
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathrm{Max}\left({R}\right)
and
\kappa
be a subfield of
R
. Then the ring homomorphism
\phi
of Remark
3.3
is injective, so that
\mathfrak{m}\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}\hskip 1.29167pt\kappa\subseteq R
.

Proof.

Let
(x,u),(y,v)\in\mathfrak{m}\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}\hskip 1.29167pt\kappa
such that

x+u=\phi(x,u)=\phi(y,v)=y+v.

Then
x-y=v-u
. But
x-y\in\mathfrak{m}
and
v-u\in\kappa
. Since
\mathfrak{m}\cap\kappa=\{0\}
, we must have
x-y=0=v-u
. So
(x,u)=(y,v)
.
∎

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
R
be a ring,
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathrm{Max}\left({R}\right)
and
\kappa\subseteq R
be a field. The following properties are equivalent:

(1)

The inclusion
\phi:\mathfrak{m}\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}\hskip 1.29167pt\kappa\subseteq R
is an isomorphism, which is equivalent to
\mathfrak{m}
and
\kappa
exhibiting property
(\star)
for
R
(thanks to Theorem
3.2
).

(2)

For every element
a\in R
, there exist
x\in\mathfrak{m}
and
u\in\kappa
such that
a=x+u
, which is equivalent to
R\cong\mathfrak{m}\oplus\kappa
as additive groups.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.4
, the ring homomorphism
\phi\colon\mathfrak{m}\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}\hskip 1.29167pt\kappa\to R
is injective. Then
\phi
is an isomorphism if and only if it is a surjective function, which means that every element
a\in R
is expressible as
\phi(x,u)
for some
(x,u)\in\mathfrak{m}\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}\hskip 1.29167pt\kappa
. By construction of
\phi
, this holds if and only if every element
a\in R
is expressible as
x+u
with
x\in\mathfrak{m}
and
u\in\kappa
.
∎

Corollary 3.6
.

If
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathrm{Max}\left({R}\right)
and a field

exhibit property
(\star)
for a ring
R
, then
\mathfrak{m}
and any other field
\kappa^{\prime}
such that
\kappa\subseteq\kappa^{\prime}\subseteq R
exhibit property
(\star)
for
R
. Hence,
\kappa
is always a maximal field (up to isomorphism) contained in
R
.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathrm{Max}\left({R}\right)
and
\kappa\subseteq R
exhibit property
(\star)
for
R
, and let
k^{\prime}
be a field such that
\kappa\subseteq\kappa^{\prime}\subseteq R
. By Theorem
3.5
, every element
a\in R
is expressible as a sum
x+u
with
x\in\mathfrak{m}
and

. But notice that

, since
\kappa\subseteq\kappa^{\prime}
. So, by using Theorem
3.5
again, we obtain that
\mathfrak{m}
and
\kappa^{\prime}
exhibit property
(\star)
for

.
By Remark

, fixed

, there can only be one field up to isomorphism that exhibit property

for

. So
\kappa\cong\kappa^{\prime}
. As
\kappa^{\prime}
was arbitrary, we conclude that
\kappa
is a maximal field (up to isomorphism) in

.
∎

Theorem 3.7
.

The following two classes of rings satisfy property
(\star)
:

(A)

Local rings
(R,\mathfrak{m})
such that every invertible element of
R
can be written as
x+u
with
x\in\mathfrak{m}
and
u\in\kappa
, a fixed field
\kappa\subseteq R
;

(B)

Rings

such that

with
\kappa
a subfield of
R
and every non-invertible element of

can be written as
x+u
with

in a fixed maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
of
R
and
u\in\kappa
.

Proof.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a local ring belonging to class (A). Since every non-invertible element
a
of
R
is in
\mathfrak{m}
, it can be written as a sum
x+u
with
x\in\mathfrak{m}
and
u\in K
by simply taking
x=a
and
u=0
. Moreover, by assumption, also every invertible element
a
of
R
can be written as
x+u
with
x\in\mathfrak{m}
and
u\in K
. So,
R
satisfies property
(\star)
by Theorem
3.5
.

Suppose now
R
be a ring belonging to class (B). Then, by assumption, every non-invertible element
a
of

can be written as
x+u
with
x\in\mathfrak{m}
and

. Moreover, every invertible element
a
of

is contained in

with

and

.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

\operatorname{U}(R)\subseteq\kappa
if and only if
\operatorname{U}(R)\cup\{0\}=\kappa
. Indeed, all non-zero elements of the field
\kappa
are invertible.

The following proposition shows that the classes (A) and (B) are essentially disjoint.

Proposition 3.9
.

The rings that belong to both classes (A) and (B) are only the fields.

Proof.

It is trivial to check that every field is in class (A) and in class (B). Let us suppose now
R
be a ring that is in class (A) and in class (B), and let
\mathfrak{m}
be the unique maximal ideal of
R
. Consider an element
a\in\mathfrak{m}
and suppose
a\neq 0
. Observe that
a+1
is either invertible, or non-invertible in
R
. But if
a+1
is invertible, then by (B), it must be
a+1\in\kappa
, which implies that
a\in\kappa
, a contradiction. If
a+1
is not invertible, it must be
a+1\in\mathfrak{m}
, which implies that
1\in\mathfrak{m}
, once again, a contradiction. Hence, we must have
a=0
, and so
\mathfrak{m}=\{0\}
, which implies that
R
is a field.
∎

Proposition 3.10
.

All equicharacteristic complete local Noetherian commutative rings belong to class (A).

Proof.

Let
R
be an equicharacteristic complete local Noetherian commutative ring. By Cohen structure theorem (Theorem
1.1
),
R
satisfies property
(\star)
, exhibited by some
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathrm{Max}\left({R}\right)
and a field
\kappa\subseteq R
. By Theorem
3.5
, every element
a\in R
is expressible as a sum
x+u
with
x\in\mathfrak{m}
and
u\in\kappa
. In particular, this holds for all elements
a\in\operatorname{U}(R)
. Moreover
A
is in particular local.
∎

Remark 3.11
.

Thanks to Example
4.11
and Proposition
3.10
, we will see that the set of (A) type rings is strictly larger than the set of rings that satisfy Cohen structure theorem’s assumptions.

The set of (A) type rings is also strictly larger than the set of rings that satisfy the assumptions of Nagata’s generalization of Cohen structure theorem (
[
15
]
). Indeed, this holds by the same argument of the proof of Proposition
3.10
.

Remark 3.12
.

For a given local ring

R
, it is generally much easier to verify whether it belongs to class (A) than to determine whether it is complete. More broadly, our results provide a simpler and more efficient method for checking whether a given ring satisfies property
(\star)
.

Remark 3.13
.

The classes (A) and (B) are not exhaustive. Example
2.4
shows that
\operatorname{Hom}(X,\kappa)
satisfies property
(\star)
without being in (A) or in (B). Indeed, it is not local (as long as
X
is not a singleton set), as
\mathfrak{m}_{a}
is a maximal ideal for every
a\in X
. And every function which does not vanish anywhere is invertible, so that
\operatorname{Hom}(X,\kappa)
has far more invertible functions than the non-zero constant functions.
However, most of our examples of rings that satisfy property
(\star)
are in class (A) or class (B), and it is quite easy to check if a ring belongs to class (A) or class (B). In fact, it is quite easy to check if a ring satisfies condition (2) of Theorem
3.5
, to understand whether it satisfies property
(\star)
or not.

4.
Applications and further examples

In this section, we present some applications of the results we proved in Section
3
. Moreover, we present some methods to construct examples of rings that satisfy property
(\star)
. One of such methods is given by localizing rings that satisfy property
(\star)
. Another one is an application of Theorem
3.2
, which says that all semidirect products of a not necessarily unitary ring and a field are rings that satisfy property
(\star)
. In fact, the following general result holds, which says that we can take semidirect products with any ring

that satisfies property

Proposition 4.1

Let

Since

for some
\lambda^{\prime},\rho^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime},\rho^{\prime\prime}

\lambda^{\prime}
and
\rho^{\prime}
to be the restrictions of
\lambda
and
\rho
to

and

satisfy all the conditions of Definition
3.1
to ensure that we can construct the semidirect product

\lambda

Remark 4.8
.

The only possible semidirect products of the form
\mathds{Z}/n\mathds{Z}\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}_{\lambda,\rho}\hskip 1.29167pt\mathds{Z}/p\mathds{Z}
with
n\in\mathds{Z}_{+}
and
p
a prime number are the ones with
n=p
, where
\lambda
and
\rho
are simply given by multiplication in
\mathds{Z}/p\mathds{Z}
(or the trivial ones
0\hskip 1.29167pt{\rtimes}\hskip 1.29167pt\mathds{Z}/p\mathds{Z}
). Indeed, in order to define a (unitary) semidirect product, we need a ring homomorphism

\lambda\colon\mathds{Z}/p\mathds{Z}\to\operatorname{End}_{{\mathds{Z}/n\mathds{Z}}\mbox{-}\operatorname{Mod}}\left(\mathds{Z}/n\mathds{Z}_{\mathds{Z}/n\mathds{Z}}\right)

that preserve 1. So, we need to send 1 to the identity endomorphism. But then the period of the identity endomorphism must divide the period of 1 in
\mathds{Z}/p\mathds{Z}
, which means that
n
divides
p
. There are then two possibilities: either
n=1
and
\mathds{Z}/n\mathds{Z}=\{0\}
or
n=p
. When
n=p
, sending
1
to the identity endomorphism, then it completely determines
\lambda
, since
\mathds{Z}/p\mathds{Z}
is cyclic. It is easy to see that such
\lambda
is given by taking multiplications in
\mathds{Z}/p\mathds{Z}
, and analogously for
\rho
.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
R
be a ring that satisfies property
(\star)
, exhibited by
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathrm{Max}\left({R}\right)
and a field
\kappa\subseteq R
. Assume then that
R\setminus\mathfrak{m}
does not contain any zero divisors Then the localization
R_{\mathfrak{m}}
of
R
at
\mathfrak{m}
satisfies property
(\star)
. More precisely,
\mathfrak{m}R_{\mathfrak{m}}
is the only maximal ideal of
R_{\mathfrak{m}}
and

R_{\mathfrak{m}}/(\mathfrak{m}R_{\mathfrak{m}})\cong\kappa\subseteq R\subseteq R_{\mathfrak{m}}.

Proof.

Thanks to Theorem
3.2
and Theorem
3.5
, it suffices to show that for every
\dfrac{a}{b}\in R_{\mathfrak{m}}
, there exist
\dfrac{x}{c}\in\mathfrak{m}R_{\mathfrak{m}}
and
u\in\kappa
such that
\dfrac{a}{b}=\dfrac{x}{c}+\dfrac{u}{1}
. Since
R
has property
(\star)
, by Theorem
3.2
and Theorem
3.5
, there exist
y
,
z\in\mathfrak{m}
and
v
,
w\in\kappa
such that
a=y+v
and
b=z+w
. Moreover
w\neq 0
since
b\in R\setminus\mathfrak{m}
. We notice that
\dfrac{a}{b}-\dfrac{v}{b}=\dfrac{y}{b}\in\mathfrak{m}R_{\mathfrak{m}}
and so it suffices to write
\dfrac{v}{b}
as a sum of an element of
\mathfrak{m}R_{\mathfrak{m}}
and an element of
\kappa
. We now observe that

\dfrac{w}{b}=\dfrac{b}{b}+\dfrac{w}{b}-\dfrac{b}{b}=\dfrac{b}{b}+\dfrac{w-z-w}{b}=1-\dfrac{z}{b},

and
\dfrac{z}{b}\in\mathfrak{m}R_{\mathfrak{m}}
. Since
w
is invertible, we obtain

\dfrac{1}{b}=\dfrac{w^{-1}}{1}\cdot\dfrac{w}{b}=\dfrac{w^{-1}}{1}+\dfrac{w^{-1}}{1}\cdot 1-\dfrac{z}{b},

and
\dfrac{w^{-1}}{1}\cdot 1-\dfrac{z}{b}\in\mathfrak{m}R_{\mathfrak{m}}
. So, there exists
f\in\mathfrak{m}R_{\mathfrak{m}}
such that
\dfrac{v}{b}=v\cdot w^{-1}+f
, and hence,
\dfrac{a}{b}
can be written as a sum of an element in
\mathfrak{m}R_{\mathfrak{m}}
and an element in
\kappa
.
∎

Remark 4.10
.

As a consequence, all integral domains that satisfy property
(\star)
are contained in a ring of class (A).

Example 4.11
.

Consider the ring
\kappa[x]
of polynomials with coefficients in a field
\kappa
and its localization at its maximal ideal
\langle x\rangle
:

R:=\mathds{\kappa}[x]_{\langle x\rangle}=\left\{\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\in\mathds{\kappa}(x)\mid x\text{ does not divide }g(x)\right\}\subset\mathds{\kappa}(x).

Since
\kappa[x]
has property
(\star)
, by Proposition
4.9
,
R=\mathds{\kappa}[x]_{\langle x\rangle}
has also property
(\star)
, exhibited by
\kappa
and the only maximal ideal
\langle x\rangle R
of
R
.

We notice that
\kappa[x]_{\langle x\rangle}
is not complete. Indeed, since
\kappa[x]
is Noetherian and
\langle x\rangle
is a maximal ideal of
\kappa[x]
, it is well known that the completion of
\kappa[x]_{\langle x\rangle}
coincides with the completion of
\kappa[x]
, which is the ring
\kappa\llbracket x\rrbracket
of formal power series, and not all formal power series are rational (i.e. expressible as a fraction of polynomials).

Remark 4.12
.

Example
4.11
shows that the class (A) of rings considered in Proposition
3.9
strictly generalizes both the classes of rings satisfying the assumptions of Cohen structure theorem and rings satisfying the assumptions of Nagata’s generalization.
