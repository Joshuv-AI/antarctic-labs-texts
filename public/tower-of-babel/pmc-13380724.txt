Integrative Network Pharmacology Analysis of Gut Microbial Metabolite–Mediated Gut–Eye Axis Regulation in Major Blinding Retinal Diseases
Authors: Bin Wei, Jingfen Zhang, Yichong Duan, Na Cheng, Xiaorong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo systematically investigate the shared and disease-specific mechanisms by which gut microbial metabolites may regulate age-related macular degeneration (AMD), diabetic retinopathy (DR), and retinal vein occlusion (RVO) through a network pharmacology framework.DesignBioinformatics-based network pharmacology study.SubjectsPublicly available gut microbial metabolite, metabolite-target, and disease-associated gene data sets related to AMD, DR, and RVO were analyzed.MethodsGut microbial metabolites were retrieved from gutMGene, and potential human targets were predicted using the Similarity Ensemble Approach and SwissTargetPrediction. Disease-associated genes were collected from GeneCards, Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man, and the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database. Intersection genes were analyzed using Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analyses. Protein–protein interaction networks were constructed using the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins database, and candidate hub targets were prioritized in Cytoscape. Drug-likeness and toxicity profiles of key metabolites were evaluated using SwissADME and ADMETlab 3.0. An integrated microbiota–substrate–metabolite–target (M-S-M-T) network was constructed to characterize gut–eye axis regulation.Main Outcome MeasuresIntersection genes, enriched biological functions and pathways, high-centrality candidate hub targets, predicted drug-likeness and toxicity profiles of metabolites, and M-S-M-T regulatory relationships.ResultsA total of 226 gut microbial metabolites and 1518 predicted host targets were identified. Protein–protein interaction analysis identified interleukin 6, tumor necrosis factor, protein kinase B alpha, and tumor protein p53 as recurrent high-centrality candidate hub targets. Age-related macular degeneration (AMD), diabetic retinopathy (DR), and retinal vein occlusion (RVO) are the 3 principal retinal diseases leading to irreversible visual impairment worldwide, with their incidence steadily increasing in parallel with population aging, the global diabetes epidemic, and lifestyle transitions, thereby constituting a major public health challenge.1, 2, 3 Reports from the World Health Organization and the International Agency for the Prevention of Blindness indicate that >360 million people worldwide are at risk of vision loss due to these 3 conditions. In China, >10 million individuals are estimated to be affected, and AMD, DR, and RVO are among the major causes of visual impairment and blindness.4 Beyond their concurrent epidemiological trends, these diseases share highly consistent patterns in terms of disease burden and threat to vision, highlighting the urgent need for systematic investigation of their common pathological mechanisms.

Age-related macular degeneration is a progressive neurodegenerative disease predominantly affecting older adults, with a prevalence of approximately 11.3%–11.8% among individuals aged ≥65 years and a marked increase with advancing age.5 Its pathogenesis involves multiple interconnected processes, including oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, complement dysregulation, and genetic susceptibility.6 Diabetic retinopathy, a microvascular complication caused by diabetes, affects around 20%–30% of patients with diabetes worldwide7 and is characterized by hyperglycemia-induced metabolic disturbances, chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and neurodegenerative changes.8 Retinal vein occlusion, one of the most common retinal vascular disorders, arises from multifactorial venous occlusion leading to impaired venous outflow, and its pathophysiology encompasses thrombosis, endothelial injury, inflammatory responses, and increased vascular permeability.9,10

Although AMD, DR, and RVO differ in etiology and clinical presentation, they share core pathological features, including chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, pathological neovascularization, and cell injury and apoptosis. These diseases are fundamentally driven by interactions among systemic metabolic status, immune–inflammatory responses, and the local ocular microenvironment, conferring a typical “multifactorial, multipathway, multitarget” systemic nature. However, current clinical management remains largely focused on local intraocular interventions and fails to adequately modulate upstream systemic pathological environments. Moreover, treatment strategies are predominantly oriented toward postonset therapy, limiting their utility in primary prevention and early intervention. Accordingly, there is a pressing need for novel systemic therapeutic concepts and intervention targets.

In recent years, the concept of the “gut–retina axis” has emerged as a frontier topic in ophthalmic research. Accumulating evidence suggests that the gut microbiota (GM) can influence systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and immune homeostasis via its metabolites that enter the peripheral circulation.11 Dysbiosis-induced alterations in metabolite profiles may intensify chronic systemic inflammation and promote a pro-oxidative state, thereby indirectly driving ocular disease progression. Conversely, beneficial gut microbes produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), indole derivatives, and other bioactive metabolites that can exert protective effects on systemic and ocular tissues by suppressing proinflammatory cytokines, modulating immune responses, and scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS).12

Notably, gut microbial metabolites serve as key functional “effector molecules” of the gut–eye axis and represent critical mediators of cross-organ communication among different diseases. However, despite increasing evidence linking the GM to retinal diseases, there remains a lack of systematic studies delineating how microbial metabolites exert coordinated or convergent regulatory effects across AMD, DR, and RVO, particularly with respect to upstream key targets and pathways.

Against this backdrop, the present study applies a network pharmacology strategy and takes gut microbial metabolites as a systemic entry point to comprehensively analyze their potential regulatory roles in AMD, DR, and RVO. We simultaneously examine disease-specific molecular mechanisms and, more importantly, extract shared pathological regulatory networks to reveal a unified molecular basis of the gut–eye axis across multiple retinal diseases. By integrating metabolite target prediction, disease gene collection, enrichment analyses, protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction, absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) assessment, and microbiota–substrate–metabolite–target (M-S-M-T) network modeling, we aim to decipher, at a systems level, how gut microbial metabolites collectively influence the development and progression of these 3 retinal diseases. This approach has the potential to overcome the limitations of conventional treatment paradigms that rely primarily on local interventions, to support the development of novel multitarget, low-toxicity, systemically acting strategies, and to extend the scope of cross-organ research on the GM–host metabolism–retinal disease axis, with substantial basic and translational significance.

Methods

Data Sources and Databases

Information on gut microbial metabolites and their host targets was obtained from the gutMGene database, which systematically curates associations among gut microbes, metabolites, and host genes. Structural information for the metabolites was retrieved from the PubChem database in Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System format.

Metabolite targets were predicted using the Similarity Ensemble Approach (SEA) and SwissTargetPrediction (STP), with the species set to Homo sapiens. Disease-related genes for AMD, DR, and RVO were collected from GeneCards (relevance score ≥10), Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM), and the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD) and compiled into candidate gene sets for each disease. Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery database. Protein–protein interaction analyses were conducted via the the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins database. Drug-likeness and toxicity properties of metabolites were assessed using SwissADME and ADMETlab 3.0. Network visualization and analysis were carried out in Cytoscape.

Identification of Gut Microbial Metabolite Targets

Gut microbial metabolites and their corresponding host target genes were first extracted from gutMGene, and the Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System structures of these metabolites were obtained from PubChem. The Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System strings were then imported into SEA and STP for human target prediction. For SEA, only targets with probability >0.1 were retained to reduce low-confidence target predictions. A Venn diagram was used to take the union of targets predicted by SEA and STP, thereby generating a comprehensive set of putative metabolite targets and to increase coverage of potential target genes while maintaining confidence through subsequent filtering steps.

Identification of Disease-Related Genes and Intersection Genes

Age-related macular degeneration, DR, and RVO related genes were independently retrieved from GeneCards, OMIM, and CTD. To reduce low-confidence disease associations, only GeneCards entries with a relevance score ≥10 were retained. These genes were treated as disease-associated candidate genes rather than confirmed disease-causal genes, because the strength of supporting evidence may vary across databases. Intersection analysis with predicted metabolite targets was then used to prioritize smaller candidate gene sets for downstream analyses. Intersection genes between gut-derived metabolite targets and disease-specific gene sets were then identified via Venn diagram analysis, yielding key genes potentially mediating the effects of gut microbial metabolites on AMD, DR, and RVO.

GO and KEGG Enrichment Analyses

For each disease, the intersection genes were submitted to the Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery database for GO enrichment analysis, including biological process, cellular component, and molecular function categories, as well as KEGG pathway enrichment. An adjusted P value (p.adjust) <0.05 was adopted as the threshold for significant enrichment. Bioinformatics tools were employed to visualize the GO terms and KEGG pathways, thereby highlighting the functional and signaling patterns of the intersection genes. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway data were obtained from the KEGG database.13

Construction of PPI Networks and Prioritization of Candidate Hub Targets

Intersection genes were imported into the the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins database to construct PPI networks, using a minimum interaction confidence score of 0.7 to improve network reliability. The resulting networks were visualized in Cytoscape. The CytoNCA plugin was used to calculate degree centrality (DC) for each node, and genes with relatively high DC values were considered high-centrality candidate hub targets for prioritization within the networks, rather than confirmed disease-specific causal drivers.

ADMET Evaluation of Key Metabolites

Key metabolites corresponding to prioritized targets and with topological polar surface area ≤90 were selected for ADMET evaluation as a preliminary physicochemical screen to prioritize compounds with potentially favorable permeability-related properties. Drug-likeness was assessed using SwissADME based on Lipinski’s rule of 5 (molecular weight <500, hydrogen bond donors ≤5, hydrogen bond acceptors ≤10, Moriguchi octanol-water partition coefficient ≤4.15, number of violations ≤1, bioavailability score >0.1, topological polar surface area ≤90). ADMETlab 3.0 was used to predict toxicity-related properties, including human hepatotoxicity, drug-induced liver injury, carcinogenicity, and human ether-à-go-go-related gene channel blockade, as well as other relevant endpoints.

Construction of the M-S-M-T Network

Based on the prioritized candidate hub targets, key metabolites, their corresponding substrates, and microbial sources, an M-S-M-T network was constructed in Cytoscape. This hierarchical network was used to visualize multilevel regulatory relationships and to reveal potential mechanisms by which gut-derived signals influence retinal disease within the gut–eye axis framework.

Results

Overlap between Gut Microbial Metabolite Targets and Disease-Associated Genes

From gutMGene, 226 gut microbial metabolites were retrieved, and SEA/STP-based prediction yielded 1518 candidate host targets (Fig 1A). Disease gene collection from GeneCards, OMIM, and CTD identified 2747 AMD-related, 1102 DR-related, and 1097 RVO-related genes. Intersection analysis showed that metabolite targets overlapped with 47 AMD, 44 DR, and 40 RVO genes (Fig. 1B–E), of which 38 were shared among all 3 diseases (Fig. 1D). GO and KEGG Enrichment Analyses

To explore the potential functional roles of gut-derived metabolites, GO enrichment analysis was performed separately on the intersection genes for AMD, DR, and RVO (Fig. 2A, B). Despite being conducted independently, the 3 disease-specific GO profiles displayed strikingly similar functional characteristics. At the biological process level, enriched terms were predominantly associated with cellular responses to bacterial components such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), regulation of ROS metabolism, modulation of extrinsic apoptotic signaling, and negative regulation of inflammatory responses. At the cellular component level, enrichment was mainly observed in membrane microdomains, vesicular lumens, early endosomes, and inflammasome complexes. At the molecular function level, intersection genes were enriched in mitogen-activated protein kinase activity, oxidoreductase activity, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF) receptor binding. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analysis further confirmed the high degree of pathway-level homology among the three conditions (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Fig. 1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase–protein kinase B (PI3K-Akt), TNF, VEGF, Toll-like receptor, and NOD-like receptor pathways were significantly enriched in all 3 diseases, collectively orchestrating inflammatory cascades, oxidative stress responses, and vascular homeostasis. These pathways constitute a shared inflammatory–oxidative–vascular signaling module in AMD, DR, and RVO.

PPI Networks and Candidate Hub Target Prioritization

Protein–protein interaction networks constructed for AMD, DR, and RVO intersection genes (Fig. 3A–C) revealed highly structured networks composed of modules related to inflammatory regulation, oxidative stress, and vascular function. In the AMD network, key nodes included tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin 6 (IL-6), tumor protein p53 (TP53), and AKT serine/threonine kinase 1 (AKT1). In the DR network, interleukin 1 beta, IL-6, TP53, and AKT1 emerged as dominant nodes, whereas TNF, IL-6, AKT1, and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma were central in the RVO network. Comparative analysis demonstrated that IL-6, TNF, AKT1, and TP53 consistently exhibited high DC across all 3 networks, identifying them as recurrent high-centrality candidate hub nodes. ADMET Evaluation of Key Metabolites

To assess the feasibility of metabolite-based interventions, key metabolites with topological polar surface area ≤90 were evaluated using SwissADME and ADMETlab (Tables 2 and 3). None of the metabolites showed predicted human ether-à-go-go-related gene channel blockade, hepatotoxicity, or carcinogenicity. Cytochrome P450 inhibition or substrate potential remained within low-risk ranges, and predicted cytotoxicity toward A549 and HEK293 cell lines was also within safe limits. Toxicity-related signaling endpoints such as nuclear receptor aryl hydrocarbon receptor and stress response antioxidant response element did not exhibit abnormal activation. Of particular note, indole-3-propionic acid, tryptamine, phenylalanine, and indole-3-lactic acid—metabolites shared across the 3 diseases—displayed excellent performance in terms of drug-likeness, membrane permeability, and ADMET safety, suggesting considerable potential as cross-disease systemic modulators. M-S-M-T Network Analysis

To further elucidate the hierarchical regulatory architecture of gut-derived metabolite signaling, an integrative M-S-M-T network was constructed (Figs. 4 and 5). In the AMD subnetwork, AKT1 and IL-6 were tightly associated with indole-3-propionic acid, tryptamine, and microbial taxa such as Escherichia coli and Bifidobacterium. In the DR subnetwork, TNF and IL-6 served as central nodes connecting indole-3-lactic acid, phenylalanine, and microbes including Bacteroides and Enterococcus faecalis. The RVO subnetwork was characterized by modules centered on TP53 and AKT1, linked to metabolites such as phenylacetic acid and 5-methoxyindole-3-acetic acid and to microbial genera such as Lactobacillus. Although the 3 subnetworks exhibited distinct branch structures, integrative analysis revealed a highly conserved regulatory framework driven by shared high-centrality candidate targets (IL-6, TNF, AKT1, TP53), shared metabolites (indole-3-propionic acid, tryptamine, phenylalanine, indole-3-lactic acid), and overlapping microbial sources (e.g., E. coli, Bifidobacterium). Discussion

The composition of the GM is closely linked to host health and disease, influencing metabolism, immunity, and inflammation through multiple mechanisms. Emerging evidence indicates that many interactions between the host and GM are mediated by the microbial metabolome; that is, small molecules produced by gut bacteria act as key signaling mediators in systemic circulation.14 Using network pharmacology as the core methodological approach, this study focused on gut microbial metabolites as novel regulatory entities and integrated multiple databases—including gutMGene, PubChem, CTD, and OMIM—to systematically analyze 3 major blinding retinal diseases: AMD, DR, and RVO. We constructed an integrated “GM–metabolite–host target–key signaling pathway” network, identified a panel of potential key metabolites and prioritized candidate hub targets, and sought to provide a theoretical foundation for overcoming the limitations of traditional local therapies and for exploring systemic intervention strategies. Importantly, this framework was designed for candidate prioritization and systems-level hypothesis generation rather than for establishing direct causality. This analysis strategy was selected because the gut–eye axis is inherently multilayered, involving microbial metabolites, host targets, signaling pathways, and disease phenotypes across multiple retinal disorders. A network pharmacology framework therefore allowed us to integrate metabolite target prediction, disease-associated gene collection, overlap analysis, functional enrichment, PPI topology, ADMET prioritization, and M-S-M-T network modeling within a unified systems-level workflow. This stepwise design was intended not only to identify disease-related candidate molecules, but also to distinguish shared versus relatively disease-enriched regulatory patterns across AMD, DR, and RVO. Importantly, the purpose of this strategy was to prioritize candidate pathways and hub nodes for subsequent validation.

As the leading cause of blindness in the elderly, AMD has been increasingly linked to gut microbial dysbiosis. Substantial evidence indicates that dietary patterns are closely associated with AMD onset and progression,15, 16, 17 providing a rational basis for the hypothesis that AMD may be indirectly modulated through dietary influences on the GM. Mendelian randomization studies suggest that certain taxa, such as Gammaproteobacteria, may promote progression to geographic atrophy and choroidal neovascularization, whereas species such as Ruminococcus obeum may confer protection.18 These findings echo our M-S-M-T network, in which E. coli and Bifidobacterium—identified as candidate AMD-related microbes—play important roles in inflammatory and metabolic regulation. Using metagenomic analyses, Zinkernagel et al reported an increased Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio and higher abundance of Negativicutes in patients with AMD, whereas healthy individuals were enriched in Spirochaetes and Bacteroides.19 Negativicutes abundance was positively associated with complement factor H single nucleotide polymorphisms, key AMD genetic risk variants that can activate local complement pathways, induce chronic inflammation, and damage retinal pigment epithelium.20,21 Clinical studies have also shown decreased abundance of SCFA-producing bacteria in the feces of AMD patients, along with lower plasma concentrations of acetate and butyrate, which are closely related to disease progression.22,23 In our study, gut-derived metabolites such as indole-3-propionic acid exhibited favorable safety and anti-inflammatory potential in ADMET evaluation, consistent with these clinical observations. Mechanistically, the AMD oxidative stress module identified here (e.g., heme oxygenase 1 and nuclear factor erythroid 2-like 2) aligns with Bringer et al’s24 report that fatty acid elongation-related genes are downregulated in the GM of patients with AMD, suggesting that metabolites such as indole-3-propionic acid may enhance retinal pigment epithelium antioxidant capacity via activation of nuclear factor erythroid 2-like 2. The inflammatory module involving Toll-like receptor 4 and NFKB1 may be linked to increased systemic LPS due to dysbiosis, a mechanism similar to that reported by Zhao et al in patients with DR, where LPS activated systemic inflammatory pathways that contribute to retinal damage.25 These findings collectively provide important reference points for understanding chronic inflammatory mechanisms in AMD.

Diabetic retinopathy is one of the most representative microvascular complications of diabetes, and its burden is increasing as diabetes prevalence continues to rise.26 Previous studies have documented significant differences in GM composition among patients with DR, healthy individuals, and diabetic patients with or without DR.27 16S ribosomal RNA sequencing has shown that Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes dominate at the phylum level, and decreased abundance of certain Pasteurellaceae genera may serve as microbiological markers of DR risk.28,29 These findings are consistent with our M-S-M-T network, which identified DR related candidate microbes such as Bacteroides and Enterococcus faecalis. In the present study, 1102 DR-related genes were identified, among which 44 intersected with metabolite targets. VEGFA, IL-6, and AKT1 (DC ≥33) were prioritized as high-centrality candidate nodes, forming the molecular basis for GM involvement in DR regulation. Other studies have shown that patients with DR exhibit reduced gut microbial diversity, overgrowth of pathobionts, and increased LPS release, which collectively activate toll-like receptor 4/NF-κB signaling, amplify systemic inflammation, and compromise intestinal barrier integrity, thereby exacerbating “gut leakiness” and retinal inflammation.30 Clinical data further demonstrate that plasma acetate and butyrate levels are significantly lower in patients with DR than in diabetic individuals without retinopathy, and SCFA deficiency is strongly associated with retinal neurovascular damage.12,31 Our KEGG results revealed significant enrichment of NF-κB, MAPK, and VEGF pathways, aligning with these mechanistic insights. Absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity evaluation showed that DR-related metabolites such as indole-3-lactic acid have favorable safety profiles and may regulate inflammatory cytokine release. The M-S-M-T network provides a clear visualization of the “microbiota–metabolite–target” cascade, offering important clues for understanding the dual roles of gut microbial metabolites in DR progression and for identifying early intervention targets.

Compared with AMD and DR, RVO has been less extensively studied in the context of the GM. Retinal vein occlusion pathogenesis is closely related to systemic inflammatory status, endothelial dysfunction, and thrombosis, and it has been hypothesized that gut microbial metabolites may contribute to RVO by influencing hemorheology and coagulation.30 For example, elevated levels of the GM-derived metabolite trimethylamine N-oxide have been linked to increased thrombotic risk, while certain indole derivatives can regulate vascular smooth muscle cell function via the aryl hydrocarbon receptor, thereby influencing vascular homeostasis.32

In our M-S-M-T network, the RVO module focused on candidate hub targets such as TP53 and AKT1, which were linked to metabolites including phenylacetic acid and 5-methoxyindole-3-acetic acid and to microbial groups such as Lactobacillus and Clostridium. Mechanistically, the “inflammation-mediated occlusion” module involving TNF and IL1B suggests that proinflammatory metabolites produced by gut microbes may enhance local retinal inflammation and aggravate venous occlusion, whereas the “vascular repair” module involving AKT1 and MAPK3 may reflect the PI3K-Akt–mediated endothelial repair promoted by SCFAs, particularly butyrate.24 In our RVO network, AKT1 (DC ≥32) was prioritized as a high-centrality candidate node regulating vascular repair and cell survival, consistent with these proposed mechanisms and providing network-based support for a “gut–retina axis” hypothesis in RVO. A cross-disease comparative framework was particularly important in the present study because it enabled us to move beyond disease-specific associations and to identify recurring metabolite–target–pathway modules that may represent shared systems-level features of the gut–eye axis. Integrating our findings with existing literature, AMD, DR, and RVO appear to share a central pathological axis centered on inflammation and oxidative stress, with gut microbial metabolites exerting bidirectional regulatory effects along this axis. On one hand, SCFAs (such as butyrate and acetate) can inhibit NF-κB activity, reduce IL6 and TNF production, and downregulate MAPK signaling, thereby attenuating the propagation of inflammatory signals from the gut to ocular tissues. This is consistent with the significant KEGG enrichment of NF-κB and MAPK pathways in our study and with the anti-inflammatory effects of SCFAs reported by Bringer et al.24 On the other hand, indole derivatives can scavenge ROS and downregulate oxidative stress–related genes, protecting retinal cells and aligning with our GO enrichment of ROS metabolic processes and with the antioxidant and neuroprotective effects of these metabolites reported by Shen et al.33 Importantly, overlapping targets such as AKT1, IL6, and TNF represent recurrent high-centrality candidate nodes across the 3 disease networks. Interleukin 6 and TNF function as “gut-to-systemic inflammatory mediators” that become aberrantly activated during dysbiosis and subsequently circulate to the eye, where they trigger or amplify local NF-κB–mediated inflammatory cascades. These cytokines have been identified as shared inflammatory drivers in both AMD and DR.24,25 AKT serine/threonine kinase 1 serves as a key integrative node for multiple protective signals derived from SCFAs and indole metabolites: it regulates retinal pigment epithelium and photoreceptor survival in AMD, confers antiapoptotic protection to the neurovascular unit in DR, and promotes endothelial repair in RVO via the PI3K-Akt pathway. This multilevel integration is in line with the multilayer regulatory framework of “GM–metabolites–host targets” proposed by Liu et al.34

Current treatment strategies for retinal diseases largely remain confined to “primarily local interventions with insufficient systemic modulation” and “focus on postonset treatment rather than primary prevention.”35 Our findings suggest that gut microbial metabolites constitute promising systemic regulatory targets. First, oral supplementation or nutritional interventions could be used to modulate beneficial metabolites such as SCFAs and indole derivatives, thereby improving systemic inflammatory and oxidative stress states at the source and compensating for the limited capacity of local treatments to modify systemic pathological environments. Second, multitarget strategies centered on cross-disease shared targets such as AKT1, IL6, and TNF may simultaneously address the common pathological axis of AMD, DR, and RVO, while reducing the risk of resistance or limited efficacy associated with single-target therapies. In light of our ADMET assessment and M-S-M-T network results, key metabolites such as indole-3-propionic acid and butyrate comply with Lipinski’s rules and show no major toxic liabilities, supporting their potential as candidate systemic modulators for further investigation, although blood–retinal barrier penetration, ocular exposure, ocular clearance, and retinal delivery feasibility remain to be established. In addition, targeted probiotic formulations based on beneficial taxa identified in the M-S-M-T network, or the use of prebiotics to enhance endogenous SCFA production, may achieve long-term, gentle, and reversible modulation of retinal disease risk through a “microbiota–metabolite” dual regulatory mechanism. These metabolites and associated microbial taxa may also serve as candidate biomarkers for early screening, disease stratification, and treatment response evaluation in AMD, DR, and RVO.

By integrating GO/KEGG enrichment, PPI network construction, M-S-M-T network modeling, and comprehensive drug-likeness and ADMET evaluation, this study provides a systems-level view of the potential regulatory mechanisms, candidate hub targets, and translational implications of gut microbial metabolites in AMD, DR, and RVO. At the disease-specific level, we suggest potential mechanistic roles of metabolites in AMD pathogenesis, addressing the limitations of previous studies that largely focused on association rather than mechanistic chains. We also propose a more complete DR “metabolite–target–pathway–pathology” regulatory model, highlighting the coordinated regulation of insulin signaling and inflammatory pathways by metabolites. Furthermore, we provide an integrative network-based hypothesis for a gut–eye axis in RVO, showing that metabolites may influence disease through endothelial function modulation, antiplatelet effects, and anti-inflammatory mechanisms. At the common-mechanism level, this study is the first to systematically delineate gut–eye regulatory principles across 3 retinal diseases from the perspective of metabolites, targets, pathways, and phenotypes, and to propose a novel theoretical framework of “gut microbial metabolites–candidate hub targets–shared pathways–cross-disease intervention.” This framework challenges traditional “local, single-target” treatment paradigms and emphasizes multitarget systemic interventions achieved by modulating the GM and its metabolites. Such strategies have potential advantages, including noninvasiveness, long-term efficacy, and a relatively low risk of complications.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our study relies primarily on network pharmacology to integrate multiple public databases and predict potential targets and mechanisms of gut-derived metabolites in AMD, DR, and RVO. In addition, recurrent enrichment of pathways such as inflammation, apoptosis, PI3K-Akt, MAPK, and cytokine signaling may partly reflect shared chronic cellular stress and inflammatory activation rather than disease-exclusive mechanisms. Because the disease-associated gene sets are relatively broad and include genes with heterogeneous levels of evidence, large intersecting networks may introduce connectivity bias and may preferentially highlight broadly connected proteins such as TP53, AKT1, IL6, and TNF. Accordingly, these nodes should be interpreted as candidate high-centrality hub targets within the present analytical framework rather than confirmed disease-specific causal drivers. This study was designed to generate candidate pathways and potential targets for subsequent experimental validation and does not establish causality. Second, we did not fully account for in vivo metabolite concentrations, stability, or bioavailability. It remains unclear whether the metabolites identified can reach target-effective levels under physiological conditions, and interindividual dietary variability may confound metabolite levels. Furthermore, some metabolites (e.g., tryptamine, phenylalanine) derive from both diet and GM, and our current approach cannot precisely quantify their relative contributions, potentially exaggerating “microbiota-driven” effects. We also did not systematically examine tissue distribution, particularly retinal accumulation, and thus lack direct evidence that these metabolites achieve biologically effective concentrations in the retina, limiting inferences about their actual impact within the gut–eye axis. Future studies should therefore combine pharmacokinetic analysis, stable isotope tracing, animal models, and clinical cohort investigations to characterize metabolite origins, exposure levels, tissue distribution, and temporal dynamics.35 Such multidimensional validation will be critical for enhancing the robustness, reliability, and translational value of the conclusions drawn from this study.
