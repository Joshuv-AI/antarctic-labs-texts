Spontaneous Fundal Rupture of an Unscarred Uterus at 28 Weeks Gestation in a Multiparous Woman: A Case Report
Authors: Rachelle Marasi Soki, Ali Tayebwa, Lilian Christine Nankwanga, Christopher Stephano Shimote, Luc Lwatumba Isevisumbu, Jackson Kakooza, Catherine R Lewis, Prosper Akankwasa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpontaneous uterine rupture in an unscarred uterus is an exceedingly rare but life-threatening obstetric emergency associated with substantial maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality. Prelabor fundal rupture at 28 weeks is particularly unusual because most uterine ruptures occur in previously scarred uteri.Case PresentationWe report a case of spontaneous fundal rupture in a 36-year-old multiparous woman (G5P4) at 28 weeks of gestation with no previous uterine surgery. The patient presented with sudden severe abdominal pain, progressive abdominal distension, hemodynamic instability, and intrauterine fetal demise. Bedside ultrasound revealed massive hemoperitoneum. Emergency laparotomy confirmed a 4×5 cm fundal rupture with extrusion of fetal and placental contents. The defect was repaired in two layers, followed by intensive multidisciplinary postoperative care.ConclusionSpontaneous rupture of an unscarred uterus should remain in the differential diagnosis of pregnant patients presenting with acute abdominal pain, shock, and hemoperitoneum, even in the absence of previous uterine surgery, because it may mimic placental abruption or other causes of acute abdomen. Prompt recognition, aggressive resuscitation, timely surgical intervention, and coordinated multidisciplinary care are critical to maternal survival.

Introduction

Uterine rupture is defined as a full-thickness disruption of all layers of the uterine wall (endometrium, myometrium, and serosa), resulting in communication between the uterine cavity and the peritoneal cavity.1 It is a rare but catastrophic obstetric complication associated with significant maternal hemorrhage, shock, and high perinatal mortality.2

The overall incidence of uterine rupture in pregnancy is approximately 0.07% (1 in 1416 pregnancies). Most cases occur in scarred uteri. Spontaneous rupture of an unscarred uterus is much rarer, with an estimated incidence of 0.012% (1 in 8434 pregnancies) in developed countries and higher rates (up to 0.11%) in developing countries.1,3

Uterine rupture occurs in clinically distinct settings. Intrapartum rupture most often occurs during labor and is commonly associated with a previous cesarean or other uterine scar, obstructed or prolonged labor, or uterotonic stimulation, when labor-related mechanical stress acts on a vulnerable uterine wall. By contrast, spontaneous prelabor rupture of an unscarred uterus occurs before active labor in the absence of a recognized uterine scar and may therefore lack the usual intrapartum warning. Clinical recognition can be difficult because the presentation may resemble placental abruption or other causes of acute abdomen, particularly when abdominal pain, hemoperitoneum, and hemodynamic instability predominate.4–7

The pathophysiology of spontaneous rupture in an apparently normal, unscarred uterus is not fully understood. Proposed predisposing conditions include higher parity, placenta accreta spectrum (including placenta percreta), congenital uterine anomalies, occult focal myometrial weakness or defects, previous uterine instrumentation or unrecognized myometrial injury, connective tissue disorders, uterine overdistension from multiple gestation or polyhydramnios, trauma, and inappropriate uterotonic use. Placenta percreta is particularly relevant to fundal rupture because abnormal placental invasion can thin and disrupt the myometrium, although many cases have no demonstrable underlying cause.4,5,8

We present a case of a spontaneous prelabor fundal rupture at 28 weeks in a multiparous woman with no documented previous uterine surgery or intrauterine procedure, presenting with massive hemoperitoneum, profound maternal hemodynamic instability, and fetal demise. No definitive structural or placental etiology was identified. The case therefore illustrates the diagnostic challenge of rupture in an apparently unscarred uterus and reinforces the need to consider this diagnosis in pregnant patients with acute abdomen and shock even when a conventional uterine scar is absent.

Case Presentation

A 36-year-old multiparous woman, gravida 5 para 4, at 28 weeks of gestation presented to the obstetric department with sudden onset of severe abdominal pain associated with progressive abdominal distension, generalized body weakness, and dizziness. Gestational age was calculated from the reported last menstrual period using Naegele’s rule. The abdominal pain was diffuse, constant, and progressively worsened over several hours prior to presentation. She also reported reduced fetal movements before admission. There was no history of trauma, fever, vomiting, urinary symptoms, or intimate partner violence.

The antenatal period was uneventful, with three visits at a peripheral health facility before the current presentation. A previous ultrasound reportedly showed an anterior fundal placenta and normal uterine echotexture, with no uterine anomaly or suspicion of placenta accreta spectrum documented. There was no documented history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or previous uterine surgery. All previous deliveries were spontaneous vaginal births with no history of cesarean section, myomectomy, dilatation and curettage, or other intrauterine procedures. Detailed records of serial fetal growth measurements from the peripheral facility were not available for review.

Symptoms began approximately 2 hours before hospital arrival. On arrival, the patient appeared acutely ill, pale, restless, and in severe painful distress. Initial assessment revealed marked hemodynamic instability. Her extremities were cold and there was delayed capillary refill. The initial blood pressure was unrecordable with a weak thready pulse. After a one‑liter crystalloid bolus, the blood pressure improved to 80/50 mmHg, with a pulse rate of 128 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 24 per minute, and temperature of 36.8°C.

Abdominal examination revealed a markedly distended abdomen with generalized tenderness, guarding, and rebound tenderness. The uterine contour was difficult to appreciate because of diffuse abdominal tenderness and distension. Fetal parts were easily palpable superficially, and fetal heart sounds could not be detected using Doppler ultrasonography. There were no palpable uterine contractions. Speculum examination revealed no active vaginal bleeding, and the cervical os was closed on digital examination.

Initial laboratory investigations demonstrated severe anemia with a hemoglobin of 5.8g/dL and hematocrit of 19.4%. Coagulation profile, renal function tests, and serum electrolytes were within normal ranges (Table 1). An urgent bedside point-of-care obstetric ultrasound scan performed within 20 minutes of presentation demonstrated a singleton pregnancy with absent fetal cardiac activity and a large volume of free intraperitoneal fluid with mixed echogenicity visualized within Morrison’s pouch, the paracolic gutters, and the pouch of Douglas, highly suggestive of massive hemoperitoneum. The uterus appeared distorted with difficulty in clearly delineating the uterine fundal contour. Fetal parts appeared unusually superficial and closely approximated to the maternal anterior abdominal wall. Given the ultrasound findings in the context of maternal hemodynamic instability, a diagnosis of suspected spontaneous uterine rupture with massive hemoperitoneum was made. Concealed placental abruption was also considered because of the acute abdominal pain, fetal demise, and shock. However, massive intraperitoneal free fluid, a distorted fundal contour, and unusually superficial fetal parts favored uterine rupture. Uterine dehiscence was less likely because there was no known uterine scar and the severity of hemoperitoneum suggested a complete wall defect. Rupture of uterine or other pelvic vessels could explain hemoperitoneum and shock but would not explain the abnormal uterine contour and superficial fetal parts. The combined clinical and ultrasound findings therefore justified immediate operative exploration.

A multidisciplinary team involving obstetricians, anesthetists, neonatologists, and intensive care specialists was urgently convened. Aggressive fluid resuscitation was initiated, blood products were arranged, and informed consent was obtained from the patient for emergency exploratory laparotomy with possible uterine repair or hysterectomy depending on intraoperative findings.

The patient was immediately transferred to the operating theatre and placed under general anesthesia in the supine position. The abdomen and perineum were prepared and draped under sterile conditions. A midline infraumbilical laparotomy incision was made. Upon entering the peritoneal cavity, massive hemoperitoneum with approximately 2500–3000 mL of blood and clots was encountered.

Further exploration revealed a spontaneous fundal rupture of the uterus measuring approximately 4×5 cm involving the upper uterine segment, with active bleeding from the ruptured edges (Figure 1). The fetus, enclosed partially within intact membranes, together with placental tissue, was found protruding through the uterine defect into the peritoneal cavity. A fresh stillborn fetus was delivered through the rupture site, and the placenta was subsequently evacuated. The uterine rupture margins were carefully assessed. Following evacuation of blood clots and copious peritoneal lavage, the uterine defect was repaired in two layers using delayed absorbable sutures with successful hemostasis achieved (Figure 2). The adnexal structures appeared grossly normal, and no tissue from the rupture margins or placental bed was submitted for histopathological examination. Given adequate hemostasis and the patient’s relatively young age, a decision was made to avoid hysterectomy. Intraoperatively, the patient received 3 units of packed red blood cells, 1 unit of whole blood, 3 units of fresh frozen plasma, and 3 liters of crystalloid fluids. Hemodynamic stability gradually improved during surgery. Following the procedure, the patient was transferred intubated to the intensive care unit (ICU) for postoperative monitoring and recovery. Broad-spectrum antibiotics, thromboprophylaxis, analgesia, and blood product replacement were continued postoperatively as indicated. The patient showed progressive clinical improvement, was successfully extubated, and was discharged to the general ward after 48 hours in the ICU.

Psychological counseling and emotional support were provided to the patient and her family regarding the unexpected fetal loss and life-threatening maternal complication. She was discharged home in stable condition five days later with follow-up arrangements for postoperative review and counseling regarding future pregnancy risk of rupture, long term contraception or preconception review before any future pregnancy, and elective caesarean section for future pregnancies.

Discussion

Spontaneous uterine rupture of an unscarred uterus is extremely rare, particularly before term, and is associated with higher risks of severe maternal hemorrhage and adverse perinatal outcomes compared to ruptures in scarred uteri.2,5 This case is clinically important because prelabor rupture at 28 weeks in a uterus without a previous surgical scar can rapidly progress to massive hemoperitoneum, hemorrhagic shock, and fetal death, while the absence of a conventional uterine scar may lower initial clinical suspicion. In a systematic review, Sgayer et al5 reported that uterine rupture in unscarred uteri often presents with abdominal pain (most common symptom), and a significant proportion occur at term, though pre-labor mid-gestational cases like the present one have also been documented. Higher parity has been reported as a risk factor and may contribute through cumulative myometrial strain and reduced uterine wall tensile strength.3,4,7

In this case, the fundal location and pre-labor presentation at 28 weeks are consistent with previously described spontaneous ruptures of an unscarred uterus. Multiparity may have contributed background mechanical stress to the myometrium, but it is insufficient to establish the cause. A previous antenatal scan reportedly showed an anterior fundal placenta and normal uterine echotexture, with no documented suspicion of placenta accreta spectrum or uterine anomaly. Intraoperatively, the rupture margins were assessed primarily to guide surgical repair; however, no systematic gross or histopathological assessment for infection, necrosis, connective-tissue-related fragility, or congenital uterine abnormality was documented. Histopathological examination was not performed, and consequently, occult abnormal placentation or focal uterine wall pathology could not be excluded. The most appropriate interpretation is spontaneous rupture of uncertain etiology rather than an event attributable to multiparity alone.

Comparison with previously reported preterm, prelabor ruptures demonstrates substantial heterogeneity in mechanism and presentation. LeMaire et al8 described a 16-week fundal rupture associated with histologically confirmed placenta percreta. Chen9 reported an apparently idiopathic right fundal rupture at 26 weeks in a nonlaboring, previously unscarred uterus; the membranes remained intact, the uterus was repaired, and the pregnancy was prolonged to 37 weeks. At a gestational age similar to the present case, Tan et al6 described a left cornual/posterior rupture with intact amniotic sac extrusion and fetal leg entrapment in a hemodynamically stable patient with a live fetus. Mengiste et al10 reported a 22-week rupture after unsupervised high-dose misoprostol, with a 5-cm anterior lower-segment defect and friable necrotic edges. In contrast, our patient had sudden severe pain, hemoperitoneum, profound hemodynamic instability, fetal demise, and extrusion of fetal and placental contents through a 4×5 cm fundal defect. These reports illustrate that rupture of an unscarred uterus can arise through different mechanisms and range from relatively contained defects to catastrophic free rupture. In the present case, the exact mechanism remains undetermined.

The clinical presentation of uterine rupture is frequently nonspecific, including acute abdominal pain, hemodynamic instability, reduced or absent fetal movements, and signs of shock, as seen in this case. Fetal demise is common in such advanced ruptures due to extrusion of contents and acute placental separation.2 Bedside ultrasound is the initial imaging modality of choice in unstable patients, typically revealing hemoperitoneum, distorted uterine contour, and extrauterine fetal parts; however, definitive diagnosis is almost always intraoperative.5,6

Management of uterine rupture prioritizes maternal stabilization through aggressive fluid resuscitation, blood product transfusion, and immediate surgical intervention. In hemodynamically unstable patients, emergency laparotomy is the standard of care. Surgical options include primary uterine repair when technically feasible and fertility preservation is desired, or subtotal/total hysterectomy in cases of extensive tissue damage, uncontrolled bleeding, or signs of infection.1,2 Repair was feasible here due to the localized fundal defect and achievement of hemostasis after two-layer closure.

A multidisciplinary approach involving obstetricians, anesthetists, intensivists, hematologists, and neonatologists is essential for optimizing outcomes. Postoperative care should include intensive monitoring, broad-spectrum antibiotics, thromboprophylaxis, correction of anemia, and psychological support for perinatal bereavement.4,5 Counseling on future pregnancies is critical and should address the risk of recurrent rupture. A recent systematic review estimated an overall recurrence risk of approximately 10% (95% CI 6–17%), although the evidence is heterogeneous, and data specific to prior preterm fundal rupture remain limited.11 No rupture-specific interpregnancy interval has been established. General interpregnancy guidance recommends avoiding very short intervals and counseling about the risks and benefits of conception sooner than 18 months.12 Any subsequent pregnancy should receive early specialist antenatal care in a facility capable of emergency operative delivery and blood transfusion, with early assessment of placental implantation and uterine anatomy. Planned cesarean delivery should occur before the onset of labor; the exact gestational age should be individualized according to the site and extent of the previous rupture, symptoms, placental findings, fetal maturity, and local resources. Labor should be avoided because recurrent rupture may occur before or during labor.13,14

Several limitations are notable in this case. First, it describes a single patient and therefore cannot establish causation or be generalized to all cases of spontaneous rupture in an unscarred uterus. Second, histopathological examination of the rupture margins and placental implantation site was not performed; consequently, occult myometrial abnormalities, degenerative or inflammatory changes, and placenta accreta spectrum could not be confirmed or excluded. Third, the exact underlying etiology remains undetermined, and multiparity should therefore be regarded only as a possible background risk factor rather than the proven cause. Finally, detailed records of prior antenatal imaging, including serial fetal growth measurements and comprehensive assessment of the uterine wall and placental interface, were not available. Nevertheless, uterine rupture should remain in the differential diagnosis of acute abdomen, hemoperitoneum, and shock in pregnancy even when the uterus is apparently unscarred and the patient is not in labor.

Conclusion

Spontaneous rupture of an unscarred uterus remains an exceptionally rare obstetric emergency. Diagnosis should be guided by the overall clinical picture rather than by the presence or absence of traditional risk factors. In a pregnant patient with acute abdominal pain, hemoperitoneum, hemodynamic instability, fetal compromise or demise, and abnormal uterine or fetal findings on examination or bedside ultrasound, the absence of labor or previous uterine surgery should not exclude rupture from the differential diagnosis. Because true prevention may not always be possible, early recognition, rapid resuscitation with timely access to blood products, emergency laparotomy when indicated, and coordinated multidisciplinary perioperative and postoperative care remain central to improving maternal outcomes.
