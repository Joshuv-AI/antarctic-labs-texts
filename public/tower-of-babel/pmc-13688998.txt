The impact of cash transfer on children’s nutrition outcome in humanitarian context of Somalia, a systematic review: mixed-methods synthesis

Background

Globally undernutrition among children is a persistent public health problem [1]. In 2023, about 22.3% and 6.6% of children were stunted and wasted respectively with great disparities among regions and countries [2]. This means, achieving global nutrition target set in 2030 is not on track [2]. Due to poverty, undernutrition is exceptionally higher (30% affected) in eastern Africa [2, 3]. This worsens during dry season and displacement in Agro-pastoral communities [4, 5]. Undernutrition has long term consequences including lower life expectance rate for African children [6]. Unlike chronic, acute malnutrition is a persistent problem in Somalia despite interventions [7]. This is mainly due to protracted conflict and its consequences such as internal displaced peoples, diarrhea and household food insecurity [8, 9].

Humanitarian responses to nutrition emergencies were previously centered on direct food aid, supplementary feeding programs, and therapeutic feeding for treating acute malnutrition [10], which is not effective if not integrated with nutrition sensitive interventions at crisis contexts [11, 12]. This is because, Nutrition specific interventions address immediate determinants of malnutrition only.

In recent years, however, humanitarian organizations adapted the use of cash and voucher to responding crisis with intention to expand in the future [13, 14]. Despite growing experience of cash transfer programs [14], the true impact on child nutritional status, particularly acute malnutrition is inconclusive [15–17] and thus humanitarian organizations spent less percentage of their money [14].

Globally, several studies including Systematic review show that cash transfers have a positive impact on childhood linear growth but not weight for height or weight for age in children [18, 19]. However, other studies found that cash transfers had a positive influence on children’s anthropometric indices [20–23].

In Somalia, there are many pocket studies on cash transfer and nutrition outcomes, with varying results [24–26]. While cash transfer improves food security including dietary diversity and coping strategy during crisis in low-income countries including Somalia (less controversial) [25, 27], the impact of cash transfer on nutrition status among under five children and pregnant and lactating women (PLW) is difficult to understand.

Therefore, this systemic review was conducted to synthesize and assess available evidence on cash transfer (conditional and unconditional) and acute undernutrition for under five children and PLW in Somalia.

What is known?

The impact of cash transfer on child nutrition outcome varies across individual studies and there is no systematic review of effect of cash transfer on child’s anthropometric indices in Somalia.

Methods

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines was used to conduct this systematic review [28].

Eligibility criteria (inclusion and exclusion criteria)

We considered all published and unpublished interventional studies (randomized controlled trial, non-randomized controlled trial, Quasi-experimental and before and after studies) and observational qualitative and quantitative studies (cross-sectional, cohort and case-control studies) that were conducted in Somalia and available in the English language. Additionally, case studies and evaluation reports with the outcome of interest were also considered. However, the inclusion of qualitative and non-randomized studies in this review, limits causal inference.

Studies were required to report on the following primary outcome to be included: Households with children under five years and/or PLW that received cash or in-kind nutritional supplements from nutrition sensitive programs to address nutritional outcome such as weight for height/(wasting), height for age and body mass index. In contrast, concept studies, newsletters, and programs targeting animal insurance were excluded.

Search strategy

No restrictions were applied during the search, except for language. Thus, an exhaustive search was conducted to identify all studies available in the English language, free, and published or preprint in the electronic databases such as PubMed/Medline, Science Direct, and Google scholar. This was complemented with a manual search of reference lists of eligible studies. Using keywords created based on the population, intervention, comparator, and outcome (PICO) framework and word combination strings with MeSH indexing, an electronic literature search of studies and reports was carried out. The following search strategy were applied to find relevant published studies in PubMed/Midline databases, Science Direct, Web of science: (“Cash transfer” OR “conditional cash transfer” OR “unconditional cash transfer” OR “income support” OR “Voucher” OR “social assistance” OR “social protection” OR “monetary transfer”) AND (“child nutrition” OR “infant nutrition” OR “child growth” OR “malnutrition” OR “undernutrition” OR “stunting” OR “wasting” OR “Women nutrition” OR “Women undernutrition” OR “PLW” AND “Somalia”))). Moreover, refined combination of key terms, conditional cash transfer/ un-conditional cash transfer, child undernutrition, Pregnant and lactating nutrition, anthropometry, was searched in Google Scholar database.

Study selection and data extraction

All studies with English versions that were available in databases and non-indexed publications, such as reports and preprints, accessed via Google Scholar/Google, were uploaded to the Zotero citation manager. After the duplicate studies were removed, the remaining studies were assessed by screening the titles and abstracts of all studies against the inclusion criteria by two independent reviewers (Ahmed Tahir Ahmed, and Abdikarim Mouse Duale). The two reviewers were assessed the full texts of all identified studies against the eligibility criteria. The reviewers extracted relevant information such as study characteristics, study participants and the outcome of interest by using Microsoft excel. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus by all reviewers.

For additional clarifications, the corresponding authors of two included studies [29, 30] with incomplete data were contacted through email by the reviewers of this current study. The number of children under five who took part in the study were not reported in the first included study [29] with missing data. However, the number of focus group discussions (FGDs) and key informant interviews (KIIs) conducted was not reported in the second study [30]. Despite follow-up, the reviewers received no response from the corresponding authors of the aforementioned articles for two months.

Outcome and data items

The review investigated the studies and reports that assessed the impact of conditional/un-conditional cash transfer on nutrition outcome among cash transfer beneficiary (cash only or cash with other health interventions) households with children less than 5 years and PLW in Humanitarian settings in Somalia. Wasting(acute) was the outcome of interest since humanitarian context was focused. However, stunting and underweight were also captured as secondary outcome as these indicators have limitations in humanitarian setting. Wasting among children between 6 and 59 months, is defined as percentage of weight for height/length < -2 SD or middle upper arm circumference (MUAC) between 11 and 12.5 cm and presence of bilateral pitting edema. Wasting for PLW is MUAC < 23 cm. Cash transfer is direct provision of money to recipients in humanitarian settings, allowing them flexibility to meet their needs.

Risk of bias in individual studies (quality assessment)

Two independent reviewers (Ahmed Tahir Ahmed and Abdikarim Mouse Duale) evaluated included studies using a standardized critical evaluation tool developed by JBI-SUMARI [31]. Each included study was assessed using a separate set of criterion items from the study design checklists. A checklist encompassing 13 criteria for randomized controlled trials (RCT), 15 criteria for cluster RCTs and 11 criteria for randomized impact evaluation studies was applied. Nine criteria for quazi-experimental, ten for non-RCT and 11 for non-randomized prospective cohort studies were employed accordingly. Furthermore, a checklist of ten criteria was applied to evaluate qualitative studies. The third reviewer (Abdifatah Elmi Farah) was invited to make a final verdict on all evaluated studies (supplementary file 1, risk of bias in individual studies).

Data synthesis

The Zotero citation manager was used for studies selection, and the data was extracted using a Microsoft Excel sheet. Because statistical pooling was not possible due to differences in study design and association measurement, the quantitative and qualitative results were described as narrative summary, illustrated and supported by tables and figures. However, lack of meta-analysis limits comparability and strength of conclusions. To provide a clear interpretation of the findings, quantitative and qualitative findings were integrated with concurrent data analysis [32].

Result

Study selection

A total of 302 studies were identified from the following databases: PubMed/Medline, Science Direct and Google scholar and manual searching. Four duplicate studies and 275 studies were excluded after evaluating the title and abstract. The remaining 24 full texts were evaluated according to the eligibility criteria, and 15 of them were excluded for various reasons, such as study protocols without results, studies that did not report on the outcomes of interest, and studies conducted with Somalis living outside Somalia. Characteristics of included studies

This review included nine studies, most of which were carried out in humanitarian contexts in southern Somalia. Seven quantitative studies, three of which were non-randomized and four of which were randomized, had the following design and a low to moderate risk of bias: randomized quazi experimental study [24], randomized controlled trial [33, 35], randomized impact evaluation study [29], non-randomized controlled trial [25], and non-randomized prospective cohort [26, 34]. Additionally, two mixed (descriptive and qualitative) research [30, 36] with high risk of bias was also included. Of the seven quantitative interventional studies, only one [34] was targeted pregnant and lactating women as participants, whereas the other six [24–26, 29, 33, 35] focused on children aged 6–59 months. The beneficiary families, including mothers and children, were the subject of the remaining qualitative research [30, 36].

For additional clarifications, the corresponding authors of two included studies [29, 30] with incomplete data were contacted through email by the reviewers of this current study. The number of children under five who took part in the study were not reported in the first included study [29] with missing data. However, the number of focus group discussions (FGDs) and key informant interviews (KIIs) conducted was not reported in the second study [30]. Despite follow-up, the reviewers received no response from the corresponding authors of the aforementioned articles for two months.

The modality of intervention was the effect of cash transfer only for three studies [25, 30, 36], cash plus cash for two studies [29, 33], cash plus voucher in two studies [26, 34] and rest of the three were cash with behavioral change communication/Nutrition counseling [24, 33, 35]. All studies were appraised and included despite its assessment summary (Table 1).

Risk of bias in individual studies (quality assessment)

Our summary assessment finding shows that, from the nine included studies, two qualitative studies (30, 36) had high risk of bias and the rest seven longitudinal studies [24–26, 33–35] had low-moderate risk of bias. In a nutshell, for all evaluated studies including qualitative (low quality) studies were included the synthesis to avoid publication bias (Table 1).

Impact of cash transfer on nutrition

This systematic review included a total of 4310 children aged between 6 and 59 months and 514 PLW in seven randomized/nonrandomized/quasi-experimental trial studies. Additionally, 800 households (HHs) without mentioning the number of under five children from one randomized study [29] were also included in the synthesis. Regarding qualitative studies [30, 36], total of 11 key informant interview (KIIs) and unknown number of KIIs and FGDs were included for this review.

Nine studies in total, seven randomized/nonrandomized trials/quasi-experimental studies and two qualitative studies were included in this review. However, because study design varied, we chose not to do a meta-analysis.

Effect of cash transfer only or cash plus cash (more cash) on nutrition

This systematic review showed that humanitarian cash transfer programs don’t have significant impact on reducing acute under nutrition in humanitarian settings in Somalia. The four included studies [24–26, 29, 35], concluded that, in the humanitarian context of Somalia, cash alone [25, 30, 36], cash plus cash [29, 33], cash with voucher assistance [26] didn’t significantly reduce acute under nutrition among under five children (P-value > 0.05) or 95% CI is crossing 1.

Nevertheless, only one study on PLW [34] found that both cash alone and cash with voucher significantly reduced acute malnutrition in PLW according to mean MUAC difference respectively: 0.9 cm (0.6–1.3), p = 0.001 1.3 cm (1.1–1.5), p = 0.001 (Tables 1 and 2). Additionally, as beneficiaries’ perception, not measured effects, the finding of qualitative studies [30, 36] suggested that cash transfers helped beneficiaries’ families and children nutrition.

Effect of cash with social behavioral change communications (SBCC)/nutrition counseling/ M-health audio message on nutrition

Only one study on children aged 6–59 months [33], found that cash with behavioral change communication significantly (P < 0.05) reduced acute undernutrition. The rest of included studies however, concluded that, cash with nutrition counseling and cash with Mobile health audio messages [24, 35] didn’t not significantly (P > 0.05) reduce acute undernutrition in children, AOR = 0.79,95%CI:0.26–2.36, p = 0.67), and 1.73(0.98; 3.07) respectively (Tables 1 and 2).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to systematically review various studies to better understand the effect of cash transfer programs (conditional and unconditional) on acute undernutrition in children under-five and PLW in Somalia. The majority of included quantitative studies (four randomized and two non-randomized) concluded that cash alone, cash + cash, cash with voucher and cash with nutrition counselling didn’t significantly reduce acute malnutrition in children respectively, in the humanitarian settings of Somalia.

However, there is limited study that revealed cash with vouchers in PLW and cash with behavioral change communication significantly reduce acute malnutrition in PLW and children under five respectively. This was also supported by the qualitative research findings as beneficiaries’ perception, not measured effects, suggested that cash transfers helped beneficiaries’ families and children nutrition.

Effect of cash transfer only or cash plus cash (more cash) on nutrition

This systematic review showed that humanitarian cash transfer programs don’t have significant impact on reducing acute under nutrition in Somalia [24–26, 29, 35]. This finding is in line with two systematic reviews conducted globally including Sub-Saharan countries [18–19]. Contributing factors were existing missed opportunities of cash transfers in Somalia as some households prioritized the cash usage for non-Nutrition activities such as to debt repayment, school fees and for clothing purposes [37]. It is also affected by persistent gaps in service provision for vulnerable communities in Somalia due to many factors including security, infrastructure, conflict, clan, etc. [30, 38]. The findings in this study contradicts with a review done in Latin America where cash transfer programs improved health and nutrition outcomes among children from poor households [39]. This was contributed by important factors such as strong political support with accountability and community participation [39]. however, various factors could have caused why cash transfer is not working in Somalia, such as lack of strong governance and accountability mechanisms from the gov side and governance weaknesses [30, 38, 42, 43]. Additionally, cash transfer impact variation on nutrition indicators is primarily due to program design and implementation according to a cross country analysis study in sub–Saharan Africa [44]. In order for cash transfer programs to have an influence on beneficiaries’ nutrition status, sufficiently big transfers and careful attention to ensuring regular and reliable payments seem to be crucial [44].

On the other hand, only one included non-randomized trial study on PLW evidenced that cash transfer reduces acute malnutrition in PLW [34]. Additionally, evidence from this review revealed that humanitarian cash transfers have perceived reduction on acute malnutrition among under five children and PLW according to two qualitative studies [30, 36]. This finding is consistent with systematic reviews done in Brazil [20], Ethiopia [22], systematic review conducted in Humanitarian context mainly in Africa [21] and Low-and middle-income countries [23] where significant effect observed between cash transfer and nutrition.

Effect of cash with SBCC/nutrition counseling/ M-health audio message on nutrition

In this review cash with nutrition counseling/Mobile health audio messages don’t have statistically significant impact on acute malnutrition, except one study [33]. This is in line with study done in Bangladesh [41] that showed no evidence of mobile nutrition counseling with cash transfer effectiveness on child wasting. It is also consistent with study done in Senegal [45]. This might be due to inappropriate health and nutrition counseling in terms of time and culture as well as variations in program design and implementation that implemented with cash transfer [24, 35, 44].

The finding of this review contradicted with review study done in low- and middle-income countries that showed cash transfers with health intervention was more effective than cash alone [40]. It is also inconsistent with systematic review among vulnerable populations in low- and middle-income countries [46], and study done in Bangladesh and Pakistan [47]. This variation may be due to variations in program design, implementation and context specific characteristics [30, 38, 44, 48].

Strengthening and limitation

The strength of this review is that it comprehensively reviewed and considered all studies with outcome of interest majorly interventional studies and some qualitative studies. Additionally, because heterogeneity of interventions and outcomes, Meta-analysis was not done that limits the comparability and strength of the conclusion.

Inclusion of studies with high risk of bias (qualitative studies), Small number of statistically significant results, Reliance on qualitative perceptions one preprint evidence are also the limitations of the study.

Conclusion

This systematic review showed mixed evidence on the effect of humanitarian cash transfers on under-nutrition in Somalia. Cash transfer alone does not show consistent evidence of reducing acute malnutrition and that cash combined with other interventions show limited and context specific promise, not confirmed effectiveness.
