Construction and effectiveness test of multimodal data fusion prediction model for intracranial infection after severe craniocerebral injury in children based on deep learning
Authors: Peiliang Zhang, Wenbo Zhang, Jianbin Weng, Zhipeng Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo develop and validate a multimodal data fusion prediction model based on deep learning for the early postoperative identification of intracranial infection in pediatric patients with severe traumatic brain injury (TBI).MethodsA total of 203 pediatric TBI patients who underwent surgery at Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine between March 2022 and May 2025 were included as the internal validation cohort. These patients were stratified into infection group (46 cases) and non-infection group (157 cases) based on the occurrence of postoperative infection. General clinical data were compared between the two groups, and multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify risk factors for postoperative infection. Radiomic features and deep learning features were extracted and screened. Additionally, 101 pediatric patients who underwent surgery during the same period were selected as the temporal validation cohort (25 infected cases and 76 non-infected cases). Four predictive models were constructed for the internal temporal validation cohort: a radiomics model, a clinical model, a deep learning model, and a combined model. The predictive performance of these models was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, clinical decision curve analysis, and calibration curves.ResultsStatistically significant differences (P < 0.05) were observed between the infection and non-infection groups in terms of operative duration, preoperative Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, external ventricular drainage (EVD), incisional cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage, incisional effusion, number of surgeries, hypersensitive C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), and blood lactate levels. Logistic regression analysis identified EVD, incisional effusion, hs-CRP, incisional CSF leakage, and PCT as independent risk factors for postoperative intracranial infection in pediatric TBI patients (P < 0.05). A total of 9 radiomic features and 20 deep learning features were retained. ROC and DCA analyses demonstrated that the combined prediction model exhibited superior predictive performance and greater clinical net benefit compared to the clinical, radiomics, and deep learning models alone.ConclusionThe multimodal data fusion prediction model integrating deep learning, radiomics, and clinical data demonstrates excellent performance in the early postoperative monitoring and identification of intracranial infection in pediatric severe TBI.Keywords: Deep learning, Pediatric, Severe traumatic brain injury, Intracranial infection, Multimodal data, Prediction model

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a common acute and critical illness in neurosurgery, often resulting in significant brain damage in children due to various external forces, and is associated with high rates of disability and mortality [1]. Surgical intervention is the primary treatment for severe pediatric TBI, crucial for survival and prognostic improvement. However, as an invasive procedure, surgery—particularly when requiring reoperation—carries a risk of postoperative intracranial infection, which can adversely affect patient outcomes [2]. Previous studies have identified several risk factors for pediatric TBI, including operative duration, number of surgeries, and injury type [3].

At present, there is a scarcity of clinical research on the predictive modeling for intracranial infection following pediatric TBI surgery. With advances in information technology and data diversity, unimodal analysis is insufficient for comprehensive health assessment. Multimodal data integration—including clinical symptoms, medical history, laboratory results, and imaging—enables multidimensional evaluation and improves diagnostic and predictive accuracy [4]. Nevertheless, the construction of predictive models for postoperative intracranial infection in children through multimodal data fusion remains underexplored.

Deep learning models simulate the analytical and learning processes of the human brain by constructing neural networks that form self-optimizing mathematical models. Through the implementation of loss functions and optimizers during training, such models autonomously extract informative features from original images, thereby circumventing the constraints of manual feature engineering [5].

Artificial intelligence methods, especially machine learning and deep learning, provide new tools for such predictions. Traditional machine learning models (such as logistic regression) rely on artificial feature engineering, while deep learning models (such as convolutional neural networks) can directly learn hierarchical features from raw images, avoiding complex manual processes. In recent years, the application of AI in neurosurgical prognosis prediction has been increasing, demonstrating its great potential [6, 7] Against this backdrop, this study innovatively constructed a multimodal model integrating clinical indicators, radiomics and deep learning features, aiming to provide a better solution for the early identification of intracranial infections after severe TBI in children.

Based on this rationale, this study seeks to develop and validate a multimodal fusion prediction model for intracranial infection following TBI surgery in children, utilizing a deep learning framework. The aim is to provide a valuable tool for early postoperative monitoring to identify children who are developing an intracranial infection shortly after surgery.

Data

General data

A total of 203 pediatric traumatic brain injury (TBI) patients who underwent surgical intervention at Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine between March 2022 and May 2025 were enrolled as the internal validation cohort. The cohort comprised 117 males and 86 females, aged 4–14 years (mean: 8.79 ± 2.25 years). Injury mechanisms included: traffic accidents (n = 114), blunt object impacts (n = 46), falls from height (n = 33), and others (n = 10). Injury classifications were: closed TBI (n = 150) and open TBI (n = 53). Affected anatomical regions included: cerebral lobes (n = 44), basal ganglia (n = 109), and cerebellum (n = 50).

An temporal validation cohort consisted of 101 surgically treated pediatric patients during the same period, including 62 males and 39 females, aged 4–14 years (mean: 8.65 ± 2.43 years). Injury mechanisms were: traffic accidents (n = 53), blunt object impacts (n = 25), falls from height (n = 18), and others (n = 5). Injury classifications included closed TBI (n = 80) and open TBI (n = 21), with affected regions being cerebral lobes (n = 26), basal ganglia (n = 55), and cerebellum (n = 20). No statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics were observed between the internal and temporal validation cohort (P > 0.05).

Inclusion Criteria: (1) TBI diagnosis compliant with the 4th edition of Guidelines for the Management of Severe Traumatic Brain Injury [8] diagnostic criteria; (2) Documented etiology of injury; (3) Age ≤ 14 years with guardian-informed consent.

Exclusion Criteria: (1) Concurrent malignant tumors; (2) Pre-existing intracranial infection at admission; (3) Severe dysfunction of major organs (heart, liver, or kidney); (4) Polytrauma involving thoracic or cervical regions; (5) History of prior craniocerebral trauma or neurosurgical procedures; (6) Autoimmune diseases. Methods

Diagnostic criteria for intracranial infection

In the internal validation cohort, 203 cases were stratified into infection and non-infection groups based on postoperative infection status within one week. This one-week cutoff was chosen because the majority of postoperative healthcare-associated meningitis and ventriculitis tend to manifest early within this timeframe, aligning with common surveillance periods in neurosurgical practice. The diagnostic criteria for intracranial infection complied with the 2021 Chinese Expert Consensus on Diagnosis and Treatment of Central Nervous System Infections in Neurosurgery [9], requiring: (1) Clinical manifestations: fever (>38 °C) or hypothermia (< 36 °C), headache, altered consciousness, vomiting, or papilledema, with meningeal irritation signs on physical examination; (2) Laboratory findings: white blood cell count (WBC) >10.0 × 10⁹/L with neutrophil proportion >80%; (3) Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis: intracranial pressure >200 mmH₂O, turbid/yellow/purulent CSF, CSF leukocyte count >1000/mm³, neutrophil proportion >70%, or glucose < 2.20 mmol/L; (4) Imaging evidence: diffuse cerebral edema or epidural thickening. Cases meeting the above criteria were diagnosed with intracranial infection.

It is important to note that this set of diagnostic criteria, while comprehensive and aligned with practical clinical realities in the studied setting, primarily relies on clinical, laboratory, and imaging findings. This approach is consistent with the principles of some international guidelines for diagnosing healthcare-associated meningitis and ventriculitis (e.g., from the Infectious Diseases Society of America - IDSA), which also acknowledge the challenges in obtaining microbiological confirmation and allow for a clinical diagnosis. However, a potential limitation is that a positive microbiological culture (e.g., from CSF), while strongly supportive, was not an absolute mandatory requirement for diagnosis in this consensus, which might influence the specificity of the case definition. Cases meeting the above criteria were diagnosed with intracranial infection.

Data collection

Preoperative baseline data were recorded for both groups, including: Demographic/clinical parameters: sex, age, injury etiology, time from onset to admission, injury type/location, intraoperative blood loss, intracranial pressure, preoperative antibiotic use (type/duration); Laboratory indices: platelet count (PLT), WBC, serum potassium, activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), neutrophil count, fibrinogen (Fib), serum sodium, hemoglobin, albumin; Surgical metrics: operative duration, preoperative Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score [10], external ventricular drainage, incisional CSF leakage, incisional effusion, number of surgeries; Inflammatory markers: high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), blood lactate.

Radiomic feature extraction

Brain MRI was performed using a 3.0T GE Signa HDx scanner with the following sequences: T1-weighted imaging (T1WI): TR 1650 ms, TE 23.7 ms, matrix 288 × 192, FOV 240 × 240 mm, slice thickness 5 mm, gap 1.5 mm; T2-FLAIR: TR 6880 ms, TE 140.7 ms, matrix 288 × 192, FOV 240 × 240 mm, slice thickness 5 mm, gap 1.5 mm; Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI): TR 5200 ms, TE 74 ms, matrix 128 × 130, FOV 240 × 240 mm, slice thickness 8 mm, gap 0.94 mm. All MRI scans used for radiomic analysis were obtained within 48 h prior to the surgical procedure.

Post-scan processing: (1) Raw DICOM images were exported from PACS and converted to NIFTI format. (2) N4 bias field correction was applied prior to manual region-of-interest (ROI) delineation using ITK-SNAP. The ROIs were delineated slice-by-slice on the axial T2-FLAIR images to encompass the entire area of perilesional edema and contusion, which are most indicative of inflammatory and infectious changes. This process was performed independently by two experienced neuroradiologists (with 8 and 10 years of experience, respectively), who were blinded to the patients’ clinical outcomes and group assignments (infection vs. non-infection). To assess the inter-observer agreement of the radiomic feature extraction, 50 cases were randomly selected for ROI segmentation by both radiologists. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated for each extracted feature. Features with an ICC greater than 0.75 in the initial subset were considered to have good reproducibility and were retained for subsequent analysis. The mean ICC for the final selected features was 0.87 (95% Confidence Interval: 0.82–0.91). (3) Intensity normalization (Z-score) and image resampling were performed to mitigate sequence-dependent variability. (4) T1WI and DWI sequences were co-registered to T2-FLAIR using Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs).

A total of 711 radiomic features (encompassing 7 feature classes) were extracted from T2-FLAIR, DWI, and T1WI sequences via Python libraries, resulting in an initial pool of 2,133 features for analysis, encompassing: 12 Gy-level size zone matrix features;10 shape-based features; 6 Gy-level co-occurrence matrix features; 8 first-order statistics; 11 Gy-level run length matrix features; 102 Laplacian of Gaussian-filtered features; 562 wavelet-transformed features.

Feature selection:1. LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation identified optimal λ (minimizing mean squared error). 2. Features with intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) > 0.75 and non-zero LASSO coefficients were retained. 3. Collinear features were excluded via correlation analysis, yielding the final radiomic signature.

Deep learning model

In this study, the Convolutional neural network (CNN) model was adopted to train the omics features extracted by MRI. CNN combines convolutional layers and pooling layers. During the training process, parameters are iteratively adjusted to gradually obtain the final result, ensuring the continuous improvement of prediction accuracy. Firstly, the ROI is delineated to automatically generate three-dimensional MRI images. ResNet50 is used as the core architecture to improve the network. Pre-training is conducted on ImageNet for initialization. Transfer learning technology is utilized to enhance the generalization ability of the model on the dataset. Deep learning features are extracted by inputting image images. These features are processed by the pre-trained featers of the ResNet50 model, and the last convolutional layer is selected as the output feature to obtain the radiomics features of each patient. During the training process, the original data samples are subjected to three-dimensional rotation, translation, scaling (0.90, 0.95, 1.05, 1.1 times), and flipping to increase the data volume, and the model is trained. Eventually, a well-trained deep learning model is obtained.

Mitigation of class imbalance

For the training set within the cross-validation folds, we applied the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) to generate synthetic samples for the minority class (infection). This process balanced the class distribution prior to model training, ensuring that the models were exposed to an equal number of instances from each class.

The logistic regression model and other potential linear models were fitted on this balanced training set. Subsequently, the hyperparameters (e.g., the kernel parameter g and the penalty parameter c) were optimized using a balanced validation set to ensure the evaluation metric focused on effectively distinguishing both classes.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0. Eliminate the indicators with a missing rate of ≥ 30%, and use the linear multiple imputation method to fill in the missing data. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation ( ± s) and compared using the t-test, while non-normally distributed data were presented as median (Q1, Q3) and analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square (χ²) test. Logistic regression was applied to identify influencing factors of postoperative intracranial infection in pediatric TBI patients. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Image processing, deep learning classification experiments, and model evaluation were conducted using Python 3.9 and PyTorch 2.0.1. Four predictive models were constructed: internal temporal validation cohort, a radiomics prediction model, a deep learning prediction model, and a combined prediction model. For each model, the optimal probability threshold for classifying a case as ‘infected’ was determined by maximizing the F1-score on the training set within the cross-validation folds. This optimized threshold was then applied to the validation sets to calculate binary classifications for metrics such as accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. Given the class imbalance in our dataset, alongside the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and its area under the curve (AUC-ROC), we also computed the precision-recall (PR) curve and the area under the PR curve (AUC-PR) to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of model performance on the minority class. Model performance was assessed via five-fold cross-validation, with evaluation metrics including accuracy, precision, recall, area under the curve (AUC), receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and clinical decision curve analysis.

Feature extraction and selection results

A total of 2,133 radiomic features were extracted from MRI images. Features with an ICC ≥ 0.75 were considered highly stable. After standardization, 858 features were excluded based on variance filtering thresholds. The MRI images were then input into a pre-trained CNN model, from which 1,859 deep learning features were extracted for each image modality. These deep learning features were derived from the fully connected layer output of the pre-trained ResNet50 model.

Following Pearson correlation analysis and Lasso regression, features with non-zero coefficients were selected to construct the classification model. After feature selection, 9 radiomic features (Figs. 2 and 3) and 20 deep learning features (Figs. 4) were retained for final model construction.

Comparison of performance of different forecasting models

A clinical prediction model was constructed using the following parameters: external ventricular drainage, incisional cerebrospinal fluid leakage, incisional effusion, hs-CRP, and PCT. Radiomic features were incorporated into the radiomics prediction model, while deep learning features were utilized for the deep learning prediction model. The fused features were integrated into the fusion prediction model, and a combined model was developed by incorporating clinical factors, radiomic features, and deep learning features.

For temporal validation cohort, 101 pediatric cases from the same period were selected, including 25 infected and 76 non-infected cases. ROC curves were plotted for the Internal and Temporal Validation Cohorts. The results demonstrated that the combined prediction model achieved the highest AUC, significantly outperforming the radiomics prediction model, clinical prediction model, and deep learning prediction model (Fig. 5; Table 3). Additionally, the combined model exhibited the highest accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, recall, and F1-score (Table 3), indicating superior stability and generalizability.

Model calibration

The calibration curve results show that the predicted probabilities of the combined prediction model in the internal validation set and the time validation set are consistent with the actual probabilities, as shown in Fig. 6. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test evaluation showed that there was no statistically significant difference in the results of the combined prediction model of the internal validation set and the time validation set (χ2 = 3.215, P = 0.087, χ2 = 2.759, P = 0.123), indicating good consistency of the combined prediction model.

The clinical decision curve of predictive model

The clinical decision curves for the internal and temporal validation sets are presented in Fig. 7. The decision curve analysis (DCA) demonstrated that the combined model provided greater net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities. The uncertainty of the net benefit estimates was quantified using 1000 bootstrap resamples, which generated 95% confidence intervals. The superior performance of the combined model was statistically stable, as its confidence intervals did not overlap with those of the alternative models across the clinically relevant range.

The threshold probability represents the minimum risk level at which a clinician would initiate preventive interventions. For example, a threshold of 20% implies that a clinician is willing to intervene for 5 patients (1 / 0.20 = 5) to prevent one infection. The identified range of approximately 15% to 55% covers scenarios from a moderate-risk to a high-risk approach, which are most relevant for clinical decision-making. Within this range, the combined model showed a clear and statistically superior net benefit compared to all other models and the strategies of treating all or no patients.

Discussion

Intracranial infection is a common postoperative complication of TBI, which not only impedes wound healing and prolongs recovery but may also exacerbate cerebral edema, hydrocephalus, and even lead to mortality [11, 12]. Early detection of intracranial infection in pediatric TBI patients is therefore critical for improving prognosis.

In this study, the incidence of postoperative intracranial infection among 203 pediatric cases was 22.66%, lower than that reported by Li Shan et al. [13], potentially attributable to differences in age distribution, surgical frequency, and disease profiles. Logistic regression analysis identified EVD, CSF leakage, incisional effusion, hs-CRP, and PCT as independent risk factors for postoperative intracranial infection.

Prior studies have demonstrated that EVD is a significant contributor to postoperative intracranial infection [14]. As an invasive procedure, EVD reduces intracranial pressure and mitigates hydrocephalus but requires strict aseptic conditions. Improper technique or inadequate sterilization may facilitate pathogen colonization, leading to bacterial invasion via the drainage catheter, disruption of the blood-brain barrier, and retrograde migration along the catheter, thereby increasing infection risk [15–17]. Incisional CSF leakage and effusion also elevate infection susceptibility [18], likely due to blood-brain barrier compromise, reduced intracranial pressure, impaired pathogen defense, and localized ischemia-hypoxia, collectively fostering bacterial proliferation [19].

Intracranial infection is associated with hyperactive inflammatory responses [20]. hs-CRP, an acute-phase protein, exhibits marked elevation during tissue injury or pathogen invasion [21]. PCT, a biomarker of bacterial infection and inflammation, is elevated in the serum and CSF of affected children [22, 23], consistent with our findings. This may reflect immune dysregulation triggered by surgical stress, impairing pathogen containment [24]. Thus, prophylactic antibiotics and stringent aseptic protocols are warranted for high-risk patients.

Radiomics is a noninvasive diagnostic approach that converts medical images into high-dimensional data to extract subvisual disease features. By analyzing these radiomic features, statistical models such as random forests, logistic regression, and support vector machines can be constructed [25]. Deep learning, a subset of machine learning, employs artificial neural networks to simulate human cognition. These networks process raw images through interconnected nodes, adjusting synaptic weights to optimize information transfer, demonstrating efficacy in disease diagnosis and prognosis [26].

Recent studies highlight the predictive utility of hybrid models integrating deep learning and radiomic features [27]. In this study, feature selection yielded 9 radiomic and 20 CNN-derived features. Conventional unimodal models relying solely on clinical or radiological characteristics exhibit limited performance [28]. We thus developed four predictive models: clinical, radiomic, deep learning, and a combined multimodal model. The combined model demonstrated superior AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, recall, and F1-score, indicating robust predictive power and generalizability. Decision curve analysis confirmed its clinical applicability.

Our study advances a growing interest in multimodal predictive analytics for pediatric complications. While previous models for postoperative infection have primarily relied on clinical risk factors alone, yielding limited discriminative power (AUC often < 0.85 in prior reports), our results demonstrate that integrating deep learning and radiomic features provides a substantial performance gain. This finding aligns with the broader trend in oncology, where fusion models consistently outperform unimodal approaches by capturing complementary pathological information. Specifically, the superior AUC (0.944) of our combined model over its individual components underscores that clinical data, deep learning features, and radiomic signatures each contribute unique insights into the complex pathophysiology of postoperative infection. To our knowledge, this is the first study to construct and validate such a comprehensive fusion model specifically for predicting intracranial infection in pediatric TBI, addressing a significant gap in clinical prognostication.

However, this study has some shortcomings. The sample sources were all from the same hospital. Although LASSO regression was used for dimensionality reduction processing of the data and cross-validation was used to optimize the model parameters, the incidence of infection events in the training set and validation set was relatively low. Only the infection situation within the past week was observed, which increased the risk of model overfitting and affected the stability and generalization ability of the results. The next step of this study will be to expand the sample size with multi-center and large-sample data to more robustably verify and improve the performance of the prediction model. Additionally, while our diagnostic criteria adhered to established expert consensus, the inclusion of imaging findings such as epidural thickening, which can be non-specific, might have influenced the specificity of the case definition.

The primary clinical value of our model lies in its potential as a decision-support tool for early postoperative monitoring. For these individuals, neurosurgeons could institute enhanced preventive measures, such as extended antibiotic prophylaxis, intensified monitoring of surgical incisions and ventricular drains, or earlier diagnostic procedures. For low-risk patients, the model could support decisions to avoid unnecessary prolonged antibiotic use. This tool aims to augment clinical judgment by providing a data-driven foundation for individualized postoperative management, promoting a more precise and proactive approach to preventing intracranial infection.

The multimodal combined model, incorporating deep learning, radiomics, and clinical data, exhibits high predictive accuracy and clinical utility for pediatric postoperative intracranial infection. This framework supports individualized treatment plans and targeted interventions to mitigate infection risk.
