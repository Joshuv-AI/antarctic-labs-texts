Extraction of Electron and Hole Drift Velocities in Thin 4H-SiC PIN Detectors Using High-Frequency Readout Electronics
Authors: Andreas Gsponer, Sebastian Onder, Stefan Gundacker, Jürgen Burin, Matthias Knopf, Daniel Radmanovac, Simon Waid, Thomas Bergauer, Francesco Della Corte
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSilicon carbide (SiC) has been widely adopted in the semiconductor industry, particularly in power electronics, because of its high temperature stability, high breakdown field, and fast switching speeds. Its wide bandgap makes it an interesting candidate for radiation-hard particle detectors in high-energy physics and medical applications. Furthermore, the high electron and hole drift velocities in 4H-SiC enable devices suitable for ultra-fast particle detection and timing applications. However, currently, the front-end readout electronics used for 4H-SiC detectors constitute a bottleneck in investigations of the charge carrier drift. To address these limitations, a high-frequency readout board with an intrinsic bandwidth of 10 GHz was developed. With this readout, the transient current signals of a 4H-SiC diode with a diameter of 141 μm and a thickness of 50 μm upon UV laser, alpha particle, and high-energy proton beam excitation were recorded. In all three cases, the electron and hole drift can clearly be separated, which enables the extraction of the charge carrier drift velocities as a function of the electric field. These velocities, directly measured for the first time, provide a valuable comparison to Monte Carlo-simulated literature values and constitute an essential input for TCAD simulations. Finally, a complete simulation environment combining TCAD, the Allpix2 framework, and SPICE simulations is presented, which is in good agreement with the measured data.Keywords: silicon carbide (SiC), high-frequency, drift velocity, transient current technique

1. Introduction

Silicon carbide (SiC) is an attractive candidate for future detectors in particle physics experiments due to its wide bandgap and, therefore, extremely low dark currents, even after irradiation and at room temperature [1,2]. The 4H-SiC polytype (with a bandgap of 3.26 eV) has been investigated as a radiation detector material for over 60 years [3,4,5] and has recently received renewed interest in the community due to manufacturing advances in the power electronics industry. Since the 1970s, the charge carrier drift properties inside semiconductors have been studied by injecting charge carriers and evaluating the transient currents [6,7], for example, to extract drift velocities as a function of the electric field and temperature [8]. In the last 30 years, this technique has also been applied very successfully to investigate solid-state detectors in high-energy physics, characterizing radiation damage-induced changes in the electric field in detectors [9,10,11], and the term transient current technique (TCT) has been coined. The material parameters of 4H-SiC are not as well known as for silicon, and significant uncertainties still exist, for example, in high-field mobility models (especially for holes) [12]. One shortcoming of 4H-SiC is currently the limited epitaxial thicknesses that can be grown and depleted, typically 100 μm or less [5]. Combined with an electron saturation drift velocity that is approximately twice as high as for silicon, this results in extremely fast signals. Therefore, in order to characterize the transient current in 4H-SiC, much faster readout electronics than currently used to characterize silicon detectors are required.

This paper presents a novel readout scheme using a monolithic microwave integrated circuit (MMIC)-based low-noise amplifier adapted to read out 50 μm thick 4H-SiC samples with low capacitance. The maximum readout bandwidth of 10 GHz makes it possible to resolve the transient current induced by charge carrier drift inside the detector for the first time experimentally. Charge carriers were injected with different ionization profiles, using a 370 nm pulsed UV laser, alpha particles, and proton beams at a medical synchrotron (MedAustron), and the transient currents were compared.

2. Materials and Methods

In solid-state detector applications, the quest for higher readout bandwidths has been driven mainly by timing applications, such as silicon low-gain avalanche diodes (LGADs) [13,14] or silicon photomultipliers (SiPMs) [15,16]. However, timing applications do not necessarily call for the highest possible bandwidth, but instead limit the bandwidth to the frequency content of the detector signal in order to achieve the best slope-to-noise ratio and time resolution. If, however, the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is sufficiently high, for example, when a large amount of charge is deposited (greater than that of a minimum ionizing particle) or when waveform averaging is used, the readout bandwidth can be further increased and the transient charge carrier drift can be resolved in detail.

Typical silicon-based timing detectors in high-energy physics applications have capacitances of 1–2 pF and a readout bandwidth below 2 GHz, in accordance with the length of the detector signal [14]. To increase the bandwidth (and decrease signal rise times), either the detector capacitance or the input impedance of the amplifier has to be reduced. For the latter, approaches using shunt resistors exist, reducing the input impedance to 10 Ω [17]. However, resistances smaller than this are not practically feasible at GHz frequencies, and most of the signal is lost through the shunt. Another way to reduce the input impedance is to use active feedback. Nevertheless, the bandwidths that can be achieved with this approach are limited due to signal transfer delays. The detector capacitance is a result of the sensor geometry and, in general, for 50 μm thick semiconductor detectors, the electrically active area should be below 0.5 mm2 to achieve capacitances of below 1 pF, which correspond to signal bandwidths above 3 GHz for an input impedance of 50 Ω.

The following sections describe the 4H-SiC sensor used in the measurements, the high-frequency readout design, and a Monte Carlo simulation framework based on technology computer-aided design (TCAD), Allpix2, and SPICE simulations to model the measured transient signals.

2.1. Silicon Carbide Sensors

The silicon carbide PiN diodes used for this study were manufactured by IMB-CNM, Barcelona, as a part of the RD50-SiC-LGAD common project.

They were produced on a 6-inch wafer with an epitaxial layer of 50 μm thickness and a nitrogen n-type doping of approximately 7 × 1013 cm−3 (increasing towards a 1 μm thick epitaxial layer) on a 350 μm substrate. An image of the sensor’s cross-section is shown in Figure 1. Diodes with a small area (0.016 mm2) were chosen to achieve a very low capacitance and to maximize the achievable bandwidth. Figure 2 shows an image of the diode undergoing electrical characterization and the measured capacitance as a function of the bias voltage.

Diode cross-section showing the p-n junction, low-doped epitaxial layer, highly doped substrate, pad (contact), and guard rings.

(a): Photograph of the 4H-SiC diode undergoing electrical characterization. (b): Measured capacitance as a function of the bias voltage when the guard ring is floating or grounded.

Full depletion of the device is reached after around 100 V, with a capacitance of 75 fF (for a floating guard ring). The bias current stays well below 100 pA for all bias voltages and can be attributed to surface currents, since the thermal generation of charge carriers in the epitaxial 4H-SiC is negligible at room temperature. The maximum voltage in the experimental measurements was limited to 1.1 kV. This limitation is not due to a breakdown in the sensor itself (as 4H-SiC can withstand fields of up to 3 MV cm−1), but a result of electric breakdown in the air between the edge of the diode and the ground plane of the readout electronics. Different techniques, such as ultra-high vacuum or encapsulation, could be used in the future to mitigate these problems. Additionally, thinner detectors could be used to access higher-field regions using the same bias voltage ranges.

2.2. High-Frequency Readout Electronics

The core of the readout is an PMA3-14LN+ MMIC-based low-noise amplifier (Mini-Circuits, Brooklyn, NY, USA), with a bandwidth of 50 MHz–10 GHz, a flat gain of (22.6 ± 0.7) dB, and a low-noise figure of around 1.1 dB in the frequency range of interest [18]. A printed circuit board (PCB) based on a Rogers 4350B substrate was designed to accommodate the MMIC, as depicted in Figure 3. The detector capacitance and inductance of the wire bonds form a low-pass filter defined by the amplifier’s input impedance, limiting the bandwidth that can be achieved. Furthermore, the inductance of the wire bond (typically 1 nH per mm wire length) can create a damped oscillator with the detector capacitance. For the detector used in the measurements, this resonance frequency is estimated to be around 9 GHz and can negatively influence the signal transients. These effects will be investigated in more detail with dedicated SPICE simulations in Section 2.4.3.

(a) Readout PCB with mounted detector. (b): Microscope image of the detector on the PCB, wirebonded to a transmission line. In the measurements, the diode’s guard ring was not connected, as oscillations in the signals were observed.

The detector is wire bonded to a transmission line, where a 10 nF capacitor and a 100 kΩ resistor form a bias-tee. The sensor die itself is glued to a high-voltage plane, which is connected to eleven 0805-size 2.2 nF/1 kV SMD capacitors to ground the back side of the PCB, providing a signal return path that is as short as possible. Multiple capacitors are used in parallel to minimize the effects of parasitic inductance. All measurements were performed with a RTP164 oscilloscope (Rohde&Schwarz, Munich, Germany) at a sample rate of 40 GSa/s and an analog bandwidth of 16 GHz. The measured voltage pulses V(t) were converted to current pulses using I(t)=V(t)/(A·50 Ω), assuming an amplification of A=13.5 (22.6 dB) and a 50 Ω input impedance of the MMIC.

2.3. Feature Extraction

The transient current induced by the charge carrier drift between two electrodes can be defined by the Shockley–Ramo theorem [19,20]:

where q is the charge, v→ is the drift velocity, and Ew→ is the gradient of the weighting potential. For selective electron/hole injection at the top/bottom of the device, the resulting transient current will be a rectangular pulse, which can be directly used to measure the drift time (known as the “time-of-flight” technique) [21]. However, for very thin detectors, selectively injecting electrons or holes using ionizing radiation is challenging. For example, 5.5 MeV alpha particles already penetrate more than 15 μm into 4H-SiC [22].

Instead, a uniform charge deposition along the depth of the detector can be used. For a constant electric field (i.e., constant drift velocity), the transient current will then be of triangular shape for the electrons and holes, respectively [23]. The electron and hole drift times (te and th) can thus be extracted by linear fits of the two falling slopes and their zero crossings, as depicted in Figure 4. At the beginning of the pulse, oscillations can be observe, which are attributed to an RLC resonance, further detailed in Section 3.5. For bias voltages V well above the full depletion voltage (where a constant electric field E=V/d can be assumed), the charge carrier drift velocities can be calculated according to ve,h(E)=d/te,h(E), with d=50 μm being the epitaxial thickness of the detector. The electron and hole mobilities are then finally obtained from μe,h(E)=ve,h(E)/E.

Measured triangle-like transient current induced by electron and hole drift in a pulsed UV laser measurement together with linear fits used to extract charge carrier drift velocities in 4H-SiC. The grey vertical line shows the beginning of the signal. For the first falling slope, the measured current (black) is a sum of the electron and hole currents. The electron-only current (red) is therefore obtained by subtracting a fit of the hole current.

2.4. Simulation Framework

In order to verify the results and compare the measured transient currents with theoretical models, electric field simulations in Sentaurus TCAD, Monte Carlo simulations in Allpix2, and SPICE simulations using QUCS-S were performed.

2.4.1. TCAD Simulations

In a first step, the 4H-SiC diode was simulated using Synopsis Sentaurus TCAD (version U-2022.12) in order to extract electrostatic potentials and electric field profiles intended for import into the Allpix2 simulations. The electric fields were simulated using a quasi 1-D geometry using the device depth and a width of 1 μm, as depicted in Figure 5a. By omitting edge termination structures, which have a negligible effect on the electric field in the center of the diode, computation time could be significantly reduced. The p++ implant was specified using measurements performed by the manufacturer, and the doping concentration of the intrinsic epitaxial layer was extracted from the C-V measurements (see Figure 2b). The doping concentration increases towards to the bottom of the geometry, where a 1 μm thick buffer layer with a doping of 1 × 1018 cm−3 is present, which was implemented as a box profile. The simulation setup, including material parameters, material anisotropy, and solving criteria, matches previously published simulation work carried out by the authors [24,25]. From the simulations, the electrostatic potentials and corresponding electric fields at bias voltages from 0 V up to 1100 V were calculated. Selected electric field distributions along a cut across the detector thickness are shown in Figure 5, showing non-linear electric fields due to the non-constant epitaxial doping profiles.

(a): TCAD simulation geometry, with the color representing the effective doping concentration. The doping concentration changes from around 3 × 1013 cm−3 to 8 × 1013 cm−3 as a function of the depth into the detector. (b): Resulting simulated electric fields as a function of the bias voltage.

2.4.2. Allpix2 Simulations

In order to simulate the response of the detector to ionizing radiation (and to take into account the stochastic energy loss), the Allpix2 framework (version 3.2.0) [26] was used. The electric fields and the electrostatic potentials from the TCAD simulations were exported, converted, and subsequently imported into Allpix2. The weighting field was calculated by subtracting the electrostatic potential from two simulations with a 1 V difference.

For the simulation of alpha particles hitting the sensor, the DepositionGeant4 module was used, while the UV laser signals were simulated using the DepositionLaser module. To simulate a laser with a wavelength of 370 nm, an absorption coefficient of 42.25 cm−1 [27] was used, with a cylindrical beam geometry and a pulse duration of 25 ps (approximately 58 ps FWHM). Overall, 100,000 photons were simulated, and the resulting signal was scaled to match the magnitude of the measurements.

Charge carrier propagation was performed using the TransientPropagation module, using 0.5 ps time steps and charge carriers propagated in groups of 100 per step. Finally, the charge carrier transport parameters of 4H-SiC were implemented in Allpix2 using a custom mobility model corresponding to the Caughey–Thomas formula:

with a fixed low-field mobility μlow of 950 cm2V−1s−1 for electrons and 115 cm2V−1s−1 for holes [28,29]. The saturation velocity vsat and the empirical coefficient β are based on the measured parameters (see Section 3.4). 4H-SiC features an anisotropy in many of its material parameters, including the mobility [12]. Material parameters are therefore often stated for the orientations parallel (‖) and perpendicular (⊥) to the c crystal axis (also referred to as the prinicipal axis or [0001] direction). As the epitaxial layer of the detector is grown 4° off-axis (to ensure polytype control) and the charge carrier drift primarily follows the axis along the detector thickness, the mobility parallel to the c-axis is used.

2.4.3. QUCS Simulations

The QUCS (quite universal circuit simulator) [30] is an open-source SPICE (simulation program with integrated circuit emphasis) software. In the QUCS (version 25.2.0), the circuit schematic (shown in Figure 6) was included, and the wire bond was modeled as an inductance of 4 nH. The detector capacitance was specified to be 75 fF, according to the C-V measurements in Figure 2. The attenuation of the SMA cable connecting the readout electronics to the oscilloscope was phenomenologically modeled by including a 2 pF capacitance parallel to the 50 Ω input impedance of the oscilloscope. The input current Idet, provided by the TCAD and Allpix2 simulations described in the previous sections, was included in the QUCS via a file-based current source.

3. Results

3.1. Pulsed UV Laser

A PILAS PIL1-037-40FC picosecond pulsed laser (NKT Photonics, Birkerød, Denmark) with a wavelength of 370 nm was used to inject charges inside the metallization opening in between the pad and the guard ring (compare with Figure 1), with a 1−σ spot size of around 28 μm. The attenuation length of 4H-SiC for this wavelength is around 240 μm [27], which implies that photons are absorbed (and electron–hole pairs are created) almost uniformly along the thickness of the epitaxial layer. The FWHM of the laser pulse was measured by the manufacturer to be 58 ps. Additionally, the laser provides a very low jitter trigger output (verified to be less than 4 ps relative to the laser pulse), which has been used to trigger the oscilloscope and average 1000 waveforms per acquisition, reducing noise. By changing the trigger threshold, the acquisition can be shifted in time, allowing a real-time oscilloscope to be used as a synthetic sampling oscilloscope with a sample rate in excess of 40 GSa/s.

Figure 7 shows the measured UV-TCT transient current waveforms as a function of the bias voltage. At the maximum of the waveform, a “double-peak” can be observed, which can be attributed to a resonance of the wire bond inductance (discussed in further detail in Section 3.5). For the data shown, a synthetic sample rate of 400 GSa/s was used. A slight undershoot can be observed, which is a result of the lower limit of the amplifier bandwidth (10 MHz). At the highest bias voltage, the total signal duration is shorter than 1 ns, with a FWHM of around 300 ps. A clear separation of electron drift (first steep falling edge) and hole drift (slower signal tail) can be seen. The amplitude of the signals increases even after full depletion is reached (around 100 V), as the charge carrier drift velocities continue to grow with higher bias voltages.

UV-TCT transient current as a function of the applied reverse bias voltage.

3.2. Proton Beams at MedAustron

The MedAustron ion therapy center offers proton beams in the energy range between 62.4 MeV and 800 MeV. In order to obtain the highest charge deposition in the detector, the lowest proton energy was chosen, corresponding to an energy loss of around five minimum ionizing particles (MIPs). For the measurements, an additional 22 dB ZX60-14LN-S+ low-noise amplifier from Mini-Circuits was used as a second stage to boost the signals above the noise floor of the oscilloscope. Waveforms were recorded by splitting the signal into two channels, one channel recording the signal at the full analog bandwidth of 16 GHz and the other serving as a trigger using a 500 MHz low-pass filter. For each bias voltage between 100 V and 1.1 kV, 10,000 acquisitions were obtained.

Figure 8 shows a histogram of the transient current waveform amplitude, proportional to the sum of the electron and hole drift velocity. Due to stochastic fluctuations in the interaction of ionizing radiation, the energy loss of the protons follows a Landau distribution, with a most probable value of around 120 keV (or 15 ke [22]) for 62.4 MeV protons impinging perpendicularly on 50 μm 4H-SiC. At a bias voltage of 1 kV, an SNR of around 5 is achieved.

Prompt current (proportional to the deposited energy) for 62.4 MeV protons for different detector bias voltages (red). The distributions have been fitted using a Landau distribution (black), and the region used for waveform averaging (>5 μA) is indicated using gray shading.

The shape of the transient current waveforms is strongly affected by fluctuations and non-uniformities in the energy loss of protons, and in order to compare the observed charge carrier drift to UV-TCT measurements, waveform averaging is required. However, due to the poor SNR, the rising edge of the signals cannot be aligned sufficiently accurately to prevent a “smearing-out” of the signals. To overcome this limitation, waveform averaging was performed using only a subset of waveforms from the high-SNR tail of the energy deposition distribution, as indicated by the gray-shaded area (SNR > 10) in Figure 8. Depending on the bias voltage, this resulted in about 500 waveforms being available for averaging. This “cherry-picking” detaches the information about the amplitude of the waveforms, which was recovered by scaling the averaged waveforms by the most probable transient current obtained from the histograms.

Figure 9 shows the resulting averaged waveforms obtained using a 62.4 MeV proton beam. As expected, good agreement with the data obtained using a UV laser is observed. A slight difference in the structure of the initial signal peak is observed, which can be attributed to the fact that protons deposit their charge very quickly (<1 ps), which corresponds more closely to a Dirac-like excitation, while the pulse width of the UV laser has a similar time scale as the rise time of the readout electronics.

Averaged transient induced current for 62.4 MeV proton beams as a function of the bias voltage. The signal amplitudes are scaled to the most probable value of the transient current.

3.3. Alpha Particles

Finally, measurements were performed using 5.5 MeV alpha particles from a collimated 241Am source (Eckert&Ziegler, Berlin, Germany) in a vacuum. Alpha particles are readily absorbed by 4H-SiC, depositing their charge in the first 15 μm of the detector. Figure 10 shows the measured transient currents as a function of the bias voltage. For each voltage, only around 100 waveforms were averaged, as the acquisition rate was limited by the small active area of the detector and the low activity (1 kBq) of the radioactive source. At high bias voltages, the waveforms are shorter than 500 ps (or 300 ps FWHM), as the slower holes only need to drift a short distance to the anode at the top of the device, compared to traversing the entire device thickness in the case of a uniform charge deposition. This results in an almost rectangular current pulse, as expected from the selective injection of electrons, in clear contrast to the triangular pulses obtained for UV-TCT or proton beams.

Transient currents for 241Am alpha particles as a function of the applied bias voltage.

These values are in accordance with values in the literature [12]. 4. Discussion

Although the nominal bandwidth of the LNA MMIC is 10 GHz, the detector capacitance and parasitic impedances can reduce the bandwidth. Together with the input impedance of the MMIC (50 Ω), the detector capacitance forms an RC low-pass filter. However, with a detector capacitance of 75 fF, the cut-off frequency of this filter is well above 10 GHz. Furthermore, together with the detector capacitance, a wire bond inductance of 4 nH will form an LC-filter, with a pole at 1/(2πLC)≈9.2 GHz. Damped oscillations at this frequency are visible in simulations and measurements, e.g., Figure 12. Furthermore, the inductance of the bond wire results in a limited effective bandwidth of 6 GHz, calculated from a 10–90% rise time of 58 ps.

Figure 13a shows the simulated impulse response function (IRF) of the full circuit model, obtained using a Dirac-like input pulse. In comparison to the transient current of the detector (shown for the example of alpha particles), the IRF is much shorter in time, which implies that the readout electronics are sufficiently fast to resolve the transient current of the detector. Figure 13b shows a comparison of the full IRF with the case where the wire bond inductance is removed. Not only does the wire bond introduce significant oscillations, but it also limits the readout bandwidth, as evidenced by an increased rise time.

(a): Alpha particle measurements shown together with the simulated impulse response function (IRF) of the readout board. (b): IRF with and without the bond wire inductance.

The measured electron saturation velocity of (1.47 ± 0.02) × 107 cm s−1 compares well with literature values [12], as depicted in Figure 14. For the hole saturation velocity, only a few reports exist in the literature, with the majority of these being Monte Carlo simulations. However, the measured hole saturation velocity of (0.69 ± 0.04) × 107 cm s−1 lies inside of the range of reported values, from 0.65 × 107 cm s−1 to 0.86 × 107 cm s−1 [31,32,33]. In the UV-TCT laser measurements, charge was injected between the pad and the charge collection (guard) ring. As this ring was left floating, the electric potential is expected to be identical to the pad (contact of the diode), therefore resulting in a uniform electric field. As a result, these measurements are expected to yield accurate results for the charge carrier drift velocities, even though the charge is not injected directly into the center of the device.

Comparison of the measured electron saturation drift velocity with literature values [12]. The literature values are given for an unspecified crystal direction (vsat), perpendicular to the c-axis (vsat⊥), and parallel to the c-axis (vsat‖), i.e., perpendicular to the wafer surface.

As a cross-check, the analysis of charge carrier drift velocities based on the transient current waveforms was also performed for data obtained using a beam of 62.4 MeV protons. Here, saturation velocities of (1.54 ± 0.02) × 107 cm s−1 for electrons and (0.55 ± 0.04) × 107 cm s−1 for holes were obtained, with βe=1.01±0.02 and βh=1.36±0.03. If β is fixed to the values obtained from UV-TCT measurements (see Table 1), the obtained saturation velocities are (1.58 ± 0.02) × 107 cm s−1 for electrons and (0.68 ± 0.04) × 107 cm s−1 for holes. While the results for protons are comparable to the UV-TCT data, they are not statistically compatible. One possible explanation lies in the way the proton waveforms are computed: As only a subset of high-SNR waveforms was used to obtain an averaged proton waveform (see Section 3.2), this waveform might be correlated to events where Landau fluctuations result in a significant fraction of the total charge being deposited close to one of the readout electrodes (as opposed to a uniform charge deposition). This could give rise to a shorter extracted drift time and therefore explain the higher saturation velocity. In the future, an external triggering scheme should be used to avoid these systematic effects.

Table 2 shows a comparison of the electron and hole saturation velocities obtained for 4H-SiC relative to silicon and other wide-bandgap semiconductor materials. The hole saturation velocities for silicon, gallium nitride (GaN), and 4H-SiC fall into similar ranges, below 1 × 107 cm s−1. For electrons, the wide-bandgap semiconductors clearly have an advantage over silicon. While the performance of timing detectors strongly depends on the charge carrier drift (and this study can help to quantify the performance of 4H-SiC), other factors, such as the charge yield and readout electronics, also have to be considered, which is why different detector materials have to be compared on a device-by-device basis, especially for devices with gain [34].

5. Conclusions

A high-frequency readout capable of resolving the transient current in 50 μm thin 4H-SiC PiN diodes was developed and applied to measurements using a pulsed UV laser, alpha particles, and a 62.4 MeV proton beam. A high effective readout bandwidth of 6 GHz was used to measure the electron and hole drift velocities and to fit a high-field mobility model. From pulsed UV laser measurements, saturation drift velocities parallel to the c-axis of (1.47 ± 0.02) × 107 cm s−1 for electrons and (0.69 ± 0.04) × 107 cm s−1 for holes were obtained, providing valuable input to TCAD simulations. In order to understand the readout electronics in detail and to investigate the effect of parasitic impedances such as the wire bond inductance, simulations leveraging TCAD, Allpix2, and QUCS were performed, which agree well with the measurements. Future studies will aim to apply the developed readout electronics to other detector types, such as silicon-based detectors and low-gain avalanche diodes (LGADs), and will exploit more targeted charge injection methods, such as two-photon absorption.
