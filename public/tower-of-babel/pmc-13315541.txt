Development and comparison of challenge models for three serovars of Avibacterium paragallinarum
Authors: Mengjiao Guo, Haonan Wang, Wei Zuo, Zongyi Bo, Chengcheng Zhang, Dianfeng Chu, Xiaorong Zhang, Yantao Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Infectious coryza (IC), caused by Avibacterium paragallinarum (Av. paragallinarum), imposes a substantial economic burden on global poultry production. This study established and standardized comparative challenge models employing infraorbital sinus injection and intranasal inoculation to evaluate the pathogenicity of Av. paragallinarum serovars A, B, and C in SPF chickens. Infraorbital sinus injection leads to rapid onset, stable incidence, and more severe disease progression. The clinical symptom scores at 1 days post-infection (dpi) were higher in the infraorbital sinus injection group compared to the intranasal inoculation group. Additionally, the clinical symptoms in the 2019/JS31 (serovar B) and 2020/JS80 (serovar C) groups were more severe than those in the 2019/HB64 (serovar A) group. The disease progression in the intranasal inoculation group was more prolonged, with slightly higher clinical symptom scores at 7 dpi compared to the infraorbital sinus injection group. The trends in gross lesions and histopathological lesions were consistent with the clinical symptoms. Bacterial shedding results indicated that the infraorbital sinus injection group exhibited higher bacterial shedding than the intranasal inoculation group, demonstrating a dose-dependent relationship. The 2020/JS80 (serovar C) group had slightly higher bacterial shedding than the 2019/HB64 (serovar A) and 2019/JS31 (serovar B) groups. In all groups, bacterial shedding peaked at 5 dpi. This study provides a basis for investigating the mechanisms of pathogenesis in IC and the disease mechanisms associated with different serovars.

Introduction

Infectious coryza (IC) is an acute respiratory disease of chickens caused by a bacterium known as Avibacterium paragallinarum (Av. paragallinarum) (Blackall et al., 2005). Infectious coryza is characterized by acute inflammation of the upper respiratory tract, typically involving the nasal passages and sinuses, with clinical signs including a serous to mucoid nasal discharge, facial edema, and conjunctivitis (Blackall and Soriano-Vargas, 2019, 2020; Guo et al., 2022a). Rales may be heard in birds with infection of the lower respiratory tract. With a worldwide occurrence, this disease leads to considerable economic losses, predominantly through a sharp drop in egg production (10% to more than 40%), particularly on multi-age farms (Blackall, 1999; Blackall and Soriano-Vargas, 2013, 2020). It often presents in mixed infections with pathogens such as Mycoplasma synoviae, infectious bronchitis virus, and avian influenza virus, leading to substantial economic losses in poultry production (Crispo et al., 2019; Kishida et al., 2004). Since DeBlieck first isolated Av. paragallinarum in 1934 (De Blieck, 1932), the prevalence of this pathogen has gradually expanded both in China and abroad.

Av. paragallinarum isolates were Serovard into three Page serovars A, B, and C using hemagglutination inhibition (HI) test (Blackall et al., 1990a). Kume modified the HI test and further categorized the Av. paragallinarum into three serogroups (A-1 to A-4, B, and C-l to C-4) (Blackall et al., 1990b; Kume et al., 1983). There is only partial cross-immunoprotection between different serovar. Vaccinated chickens were protected only against homologous challenge from this Page serovar (Blackall and Reid, 1987; Kume et al., 1980; Rimler et al., 1977). Studies have shown that the cross‐protection within Page serovar B is only partial (Yamaguchi et al., 1991).A complete cross-protection study using the reference strains of the 9 Kume serovars of Av. paragallinarum has been completed. Within Kume serogroup A, serovars A-1, A-2, and A-3 are strongly cross-protective whereas there is good cross‐protection between serovars A-1 and A-4. Within Kume serogroup C, there was a good level of cross-protection for serovars C-1, C-2, and C-3, with some exceptions. Kume serovars C-1, C-2, and C-3 all provided protection against a C-1 challenge. In contrast, with the serovar C-2 and C-3 challenge, the respective homologous group was significantly better protected than the heterologous groups (Soriano et al., 2004a).

Globally, IC is caused by multiple serovars of Av. paragallinarum. In Latin America, serovars A, B and C are prevalent, with untypable strains reported in several countries (Conde et al., 2011). In Asia, China reports serovars A, B and C (Guo et al., 2022a); Korea has only identified A, while Japan historically saw C-1 dominance (Han et al., 2016; Kume et al., 1983; Namgoong et al., 1981). African countries like South Africa initially had A-1, C-2, and C-3, but vaccination shifted the prevalence toward C-3 (Bragg et al., 1996). In Europe, the Netherlands experienced outbreaks dominated by A-1, with A-2, B-1, and later C-4 also detected (Heuvelink et al., 2018). In North America, the recent U.S. outbreaks have been exclusively linked to serovar C-1, spreading from Pennsylvania across the Midwest (El-Gazzar et al., 2025).

Several factors contribute to the pathogenicity of Av. paragallinarum. Among these, hemagglutinin (HA) antigens have been extensively studied. Mutant studies in both Page serovars A and C, lacking HA activity, demonstrated that the HA antigen is essential for colonization (Sawata and Kume, 1983; Yamaguchi et al., 1993). This antigen has been identified as a 210-kDa trimeric autotransporter adhesin (HMTp210), functioning as a trimeric autotransporter adhesin. HMTp210 mediates hemagglutination, adherence to host cells, and biofilm formation (Wang et al., 2014). The capsule is another key virulence factor implicated in colonization and is considered crucial for the development of IC lesions (Sawata and Kume, 1983; Sawata et al., 1985). It protects the bacterium against the bactericidal effects of normal chicken serum (Sawata et al., 1984). Encapsulated, mucoid phase isolates of Av. paragallinarum frequently convert to non-encapsulated, rough colonies after prolonged in-vitro passage; this loss of capsule is accompanied by a measurable decline in virulence, indicating that the mucoid phenotype is unstable under routine culture conditions (Sawata and Kume, 1983).

Pathogenicity evaluation and vaccine efficacy testing rely heavily on standardized challenge models, with infraorbital sinus injection and intranasal inoculation being the two most commonly used routes (Bragg, 2002; Rimler et al., 1977). Infraorbital sinus injection tends to elicit more severe clinical manifestations, rendering it appropriate for rigorous evaluations of virulence and vaccine protective efficacy (Guo et al., 2022b), while intranasal inoculation mimics natural infection, providing more field-relevant data. However, standardized dose-response relationships and mechanistic differences in bacterial colonization, host immune responses, and vaccine protection between the two routes remain poorly defined. To address these gaps, the present study established and validated infraorbital sinus injection and intranasal inoculation challenge models in SPF chickens, systematically compared their pathogenicity, and evaluated their utility for assessing Av. paragallinarum virulence and vaccine efficacy.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains, and culture conditions

The strain Av. paragallinarum 2019/HB64 (serovar A), 2019/JS31 (serovar B), and 2020/JS80 (serovar C) were isolated from a chicken farm in Hebei province and Jiangsu province (Guo et al., 2022a), the corresponding Kume serovars for 2019/HB64 and 2020/JS80 are A1 and C4, respectively. The isolates were grown on Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA) (Hopebio, China) and Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) (Hopebio, China) supplemented with 5–10% inactivated chicken serum (Solarbio, China) and 0.0025% (wt/vol) NAD (Sangon Biotech, China). Then cultured at 37 °C under 5% CO2 for 12-14 h to reach a concentration of 109 CFU/mL at an OD₆₀₀ of 1.

Birds and ethics statement

Sixty 6-week-old specific-pathogen-free (SPF) White Leghorn chickens were purchased from Beijing Boehringer Ingelheim Vital Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All the birds experimental procedures were performed in accordance with the ARRIVE Guidelines 2.0. All experimental protocols applied in this study were approved by Animal Welfare and Ethical Review Committee of Yangzhou University (202203180).

Challenge experiment design

All birds were allowed to acclimatize in isolators for 3 days prior to initiation of the challenge experiment. To evaluate route-dependent virulence, SPF chickens were experimentally challenged through either intranasal inoculation or infraorbital sinus injection. A total of 60 SPF chickens were randomly divided into 12 groups (5 chickens per group), with each group housed separately in isolators. The challenge strains were Av. paragallinarum isolates 2019/HB64 (serovar A), 2019/JS31 (serovar B), and 2020/JS80 (serovar C). The specific challenge dose is provided in Table 1. Each strain was assigned a high-dose challenge group and a low-dose challenge group. The groups 1-6 were challenged by intranasal inoculation 0.1 mL with different Serovars of Av. paragallinarum (Fig. 1A). Group7-12 were challenged via infraorbital sinus injection 0.1 mL with Av. paragallinarum (Fig. 1B). A total of 0.1 ml of the bacterial suspension was administered into a single nostril. Clinical signs were monitored and recorded daily for 7 consecutive days post-challenge. On 3, 5, and 7 days post-infection (dpi), oropharyngeal swabs were collected to quantify bacterial loads. At 7 dpi, all chickens were euthanized. Clinical signs scores

Clinical signs of IC were recorded from the 1-7 day after challenge. The presence and severity of nasal discharge and facial swelling in the challenged chickens were clinically assessed and scored according to the following standardized grading scale, as previously described (Bragg, 2002). The scoring criteria were defined as follows: a score of 0 indicated no clinical signs. A score of 1 was defined by slight facial swelling with minimal nasal discharge. A score of 2 was defined by apparent facial swelling accompanied by moderate nasal discharge. A score of 3 was defined by marked facial swelling, profuse nasal discharge, and lacrimation.

Bacterial shedding by quantitative real-time PCR

Nasal swab samples were collected at 3, 5, and 7 dpi. DNA was extracted by the Bacteria Genomic DNA Kit (Cwbio, Beijing, China). Refer to previous study, the primer sequences used are as follows: HagA-F: GTAGCGAAGCAGCCAACTTA; HagA-R: TGGGTTCGCTTCACCATAAC (Wen et al., 2016). The quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using AceQ qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., China). The procedure for qPCR: 50 °C 2 min; 95 °C 2 min; 95 °C 15 s, 56 °C 15 s, 1 min, 40 cycles; The melting curve procedure: 95 °C 15 s; 60 °C 1 min, 95 °C 15 s.

Gross lesions and histopathological lesions

At 7 dpi, all chickens were euthanized and examined for lesions in the infraorbital sinuses and trachea. The lesion scoring criteria are as follows: The scoring criteria for infraorbital sinus lesions were as follows: - for no abnormal lesions; + for a small number of hemorrhage and mucus; and ++ for extensive hemorrhage accompanied by caseous exudates. The scoring criteria for tracheal lesions were as follows: - for no abnormal lesions; + for a few hemorrhage; and ++ for extensive hemorrhage.

The tissue samples of infraorbital sinus and trachea were collected and immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 24 hours to ensure optimal preservation of morphological structure. Following fixation, the specimens were systematically processed through a standardized histological procedure, which included dehydration, clearing, and embedding in paraffin wax. Subsequently, the embedded tissue blocks were sectioned into 5 μm thick slices using a rotary microtome, and the resulting sections were carefully mounted onto glass slides. After mounting, the sections underwent deparaffinization in xylene and gradual rehydration through a series of ethanol solutions, followed by staining with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histological examination. Finally, the stained tissue sections were observed and digitally captured using a high-resolution digital slide scanner (NanoZoomer NDP. view2) for detailed microscopic evaluation of pathological changes.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using the GraphPad Software 8.0 (La Jolla, CA, USA). The statistical significance of the data was determined by the Student’s t-test. P value of < 0.05 was considered to indicate a significant difference.

Results

Clinical sign scores and disease progression

All isolated strains induced varying degrees of clinical signs in chickens (Table 1), including typical symptoms of IC such as nasal discharge and facial swelling, accompanied by a depressed mental state and reduced food and water intake. However, the clinical symptoms in the high-dose group were more severe, with higher clinical scores than the low-dose group. Eyelid edema was pronounced, resulting in complete closure of the eye, and profuse nasal discharge led to respiratory distress. Chickens challenged via infraorbital sinus injection exhibited accelerated disease progression and more severe clinical signs and requires a low infectious dose, including marked facial swelling and profuse nasal discharge within 1–2 dpi, with mean clinical scores peaking at approximately 2 by 1 dpi before declining to below 1 by 7 dpi except for the HB64 (serovar A) group. In contrast, intranasal inoculation resulted in a more gradual onset, with mean clinical scores averaging around 1 at 1 dpi, peaking at 3 dpi, and showing a slower decline, ultimately leading to a slightly higher score at 7 dpi and a prolonged clinical course that more closely recapitulates the natural progression of IC observed in field infections. All strains induced clinical signs, when challenged via the intranasal route at an identical dose of 10⁷ CFU, strain 2020/JS80 (serovar C) induced clinical disease in 100% of the chickens, whereas strains 2019/JS31 (serovar B) and 2019/HB64 (serovar A) resulted in an 80% morbidity rate, indicating that 2020/JS80 (serovar C) exhibits higher virulence than 2019/JS31 (serovar B) and 2019/HB64 (serovar A).

Bacterial shedding followed a dose-dependent trend, peaking at 5 dpi for both routes. The bacterial shedding is highest at 5 dpi and decreases by 7 dpi for both doses across all strains. Gross lesions

At 7 dpi, all experimental chickens were euthanized for pathological examination of the infraorbital sinuses and trachea. Observations revealed hemorrhages in the infraorbital sinus of all challenged groups.

Distinct pathological changes were observed depending on the challenge route. Specifically, In the intranasal inoculation group, the 2019/HB64 (serovar A) high-dose group showed hemorrhages and yellow caseous exudate in the infraorbital sinus of 1/5 chickens, while the low-dose group exhibited hemorrhages in 4/5 chickens. For the 2019/JS31 (serovar B) strain, the high-dose group had hemorrhages in 3/5 chickens and caseous exudate in 1/5, whereas the low-dose group showed hemorrhages in 1/5 chickens. The 2020/JS80 (serovar C) group demonstrated more pronounced lesions, with caseous exudate in 3/5 chickens and hemorrhages 1/5 in the high-dose group, and hemorrhages in 4/5 chickens in the low-dose group. In contrast, the infraorbital sinus injection group generally presented milder lesions. For 2019/HB64 (A), the high-dose group had hemorrhages in 2/5 chickens and mucus in one, while the low-dose group showed hemorrhages in 3/5 chickens. The 2019/JS31 (serovar B) high- and low-dose groups both exhibited hemorrhages in three out of five chickens. Histopathological lesions analysis

Pathological changes were detected in the infraorbital sinus and trachea of the infected chickens. Histopathological analysis revealed significant pathological changes in the challenged groups, characterized by disintegration of the infraorbital sinus mucosal architecture with extensive mucosal necrosis, submucosal edema, inflammatory cell infiltration, and concomitant hemorrhage (Fig. 6A and B). Notably, examination of infraorbital sinus sections from the intranasal challenge group, particularly the 2019/HB64 (serovar A) high-dose group, showed abundant erythrocytes, further supporting the observed hemorrhagic manifestations. Tracheas showed severe ciliary loss accompanied by massive inflammatory cell infiltration and marked swelling of the lamina propria (Fig. 7A and B). Discussion

IC typically presents with low mortality but high morbidity. The clinical manifestations can vary depending on the age and breed of the birds (Blackall, 1995). Factors such as poor housing, parasitism, and inadequate nutrition can prolong the disease and increase its severity. Coinfections or secondary infection with fowlpox virus, infectious bronchitis virus, infectious laryngotracheitis virus, Mycoplasma gallisepticum infection usually lead to more severe and prolonged IC cases, accompanied by elevated mortality (Blackall and Soriano-Vargas, 2020; Sandoval et al., 1994). Notably, even in the absence of secondary pathogens, significant mortality can occur in older birds, as demonstrated by an outbreak in California where the mortality rate reached 48% (Blackall and Soriano-Vargas, 2020).

The pathogenicity of Av. paragallinarum varies depending on factors like growth conditions, isolate passage history, and host immune status. Substantial evidence now exists for variation in pathogenicity among Av. paragallinarum isolates. In particular, reference strains for Kume serovars A-1, A-4, C-1, C-2, and C-3 demonstrated higher virulence than those for serovars A-2, A-3, B-1, and C-4 (Soriano et al., 2004b). Variation in virulence also occurs within serovars. For example, Yamaguchi observed that one out of four tested Av. paragallinarum serovar B strains failed to induce clinical signs (Yamaguchi et al., 1990). This documented intra-serovar variation may explain the reported failure of a South African serovar B isolate to cause clinical signs in experimentally infected chickens (Dungu et al., 2009). Their study indicated that the virulence of Av. paragallinarum Serovar B was lower than that of serovar A and C. In contrast, challenge experiments using our isolated strains demonstrated that, based on clinical severity and bacterial shedding, Serovar C exhibited the strongest virulence, followed by serovars A and B, and both serovars A and B were capable of inducing disease in chickens via either intranasal or infraorbital sinus inoculation.

Three strains were both capable of inducing disease in chickens through two different challenge routes; however, clinical symptom scores and bacterial shedding levels followed distinct patterns after intranasal inoculation and infraorbital sinus injection. Among them, we observed that the 2020/JS80 (serovar C) challenge group exhibited the highest clinical scores and the greatest bacterial shedding, suggesting that this isolate may possess higher virulence. These findings indicated that while both challenge methods could induce typical clinical symptoms of IC. Further investigation into the pathogenic mechanisms of Av. paragallinarum is warranted to elucidate these differences.

Determining the pathogenicity of bacteria is of critical importance for predicting the epidemiology and preventing vaccine failure. Mycoplasma hyorhinis infection models can be established via both intravenous and intraperitoneal challenge routes, and the results demonstrate that consecutive two-day intravenous inoculation induces more severe pathological changes (Földi et al., 2023). The clinical manifestations associated with different Av. paragallinarum infection models lack comprehensive evaluation and standardization. Nowadays, two primary animal models are employed to study bacterial virulence: the artificial infraorbital sinus injection model (Rimler et al., 1977) and the in-contact challenge model (Bragg, 2002). While both systems serve to assess pathogenicity, each exhibits distinct characteristics. Our results demonstrate that both challenge routes (infraorbital sinus injection and intranasal inoculation) effectively induced disease in SPF chickens, with no marked differences observed apart from faster disease progression via the infraorbital sinus route. Clinical scoring revealed that the group challenged via the infraorbital sinus injection route exhibited a more rapid disease progression and more severe clinical signs, whereas the group inoculated via the intranasal route demonstrated a more protracted disease course. The reason why infraorbital sinus injection leads to a faster onset and more severe clinical symptoms might be that it bypasses the mucosal barrier of the respiratory tract, allowing bacteria to directly colonize the infraorbital sinus. Numerous studies have demonstrated that multiple challenge routes can be employed for the establishment of pathogen challenge models. For instance, avian Pathogenic Escherichia coli (APEC) can be administered via a wider range of challenge routes, including subcutaneous injection (Gomis et al., 1997), intratracheal injection (Huff et al., 2006), intraperitoneal injection (Landman et al., 2013), and air sac injection(Peighambari et al., 2000). Subcutaneous injection is commonly used to investigate the infection mechanism of APEC following skin barrier disruption. Intraperitoneal injection is suitable for establishing highly pathogenic models to study systemic infection and acute disease progression. For mimicking natural infection routes, intratracheal injection and air sac injection can be selected.

The appropriate challenge method can be selected based on specific experimental objectives. As a respiratory pathogen, Av. paragallinarum is primarily transmitted horizontally, and intranasal inoculation serves as a method to simulate natural infection (Sakamoto et al., 2013). Compared with infraorbital sinus injection, intranasal inoculation more closely mimics the natural route of Av. paragallinarum infection in chickens, while the infraorbital sinus route typically results in more severe clinical signs (Matsumoto and Yamamoto, 1971). Although the intranasal challenge route more closely mimics the natural conditions under which chickens become infected with Av. paragallinarum, our research in recent years has indicated that the incidence rate with this method is unstable.

We hypothesize that infraorbital sinus inoculation facilitates Av. paragallinarum in breaching the chicken respiratory mucosal barrier, thereby enabling rapid colonization and invasion. On the other hand, studies have suggested that Av. paragallinarum may act as an opportunistic pathogen, as non-pathogenic strains of this bacterium have been isolated from the respiratory tract of healthy chickens (Shelkamy et al., 2025). Under conditions of poor husbandry and hygiene, which compromise immune function and induce stress in chickens, resident bacteria of respiratory tract such as Staphylococcus chromogenes can utilize the host's biosynthetic system to provide NAD, thereby facilitating the colonization and subsequent infection by Av. paragallinarum in the respiratory tract (Wu et al., 2021). For vaccine development, a stable disease model is indispensable. Therefore, the intra-infraorbital sinus challenge method can provide research foundation for the development of IC vaccines. The instability associated with the intranasal challenge method requires further investigation.

In conclusion, this study successfully established disease models for Av. paragallinarum Serovars A, B, and C using both intranasal and infraorbital sinus injection routes, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for understanding the pathogenicity of Av. paragallinarum.
