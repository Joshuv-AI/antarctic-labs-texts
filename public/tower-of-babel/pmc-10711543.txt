A soybean trypsin inhibitor reduces the resistance to transgenic maize in a population of Spodoptera frugiperda (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae)
Authors: Sandy Sousa Fonseca, Ana Leticia Zero Santos, Ciro Pedro Guidotti Pinto, Luiz Marques, Antonio Cesar Santos, James Bing, Timothy Nowatzki, Amit Sethi, Guilherme Duarte Rossi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractLepidopteran pests have been successfully managed by the adoption of insect resistant transgenic plants expressing Cry and/or Vip insecticidal proteins derived from Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt plants). Among such pests, Spodoptera frugiperda (Smith, 1797) (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae) is highlighted for its destructive potential in maize crops and for cases of field-evolved resistance to Bt plants. Cry insecticidal proteins expressed in Bt plants are known for their interaction with insect midgut receptors and subsequent midgut cell disruption that leads to target pest death. In the midgut of lepidopteran larval pests such as S. frugiperda, serine proteases are important in dietary protein digestion and activation or degradation of insecticidal proteins. This work was conducted to evaluate if the use of a soybean trypsin inhibitor (SBTI) could disrupt the development of a Bt-susceptible and a Bt-resistant population of S. frugiperda ingesting Bt (expressing Cry1F, Cry1A.105, and Cry2Ab2 Cry proteins) and non-Bt maize plants. The SBTI was produced and purified using recombinant expression in E. coli followed by purification in Ni-Sepharose. Bioassays using non-Bt maize leaves indicated that the development of susceptible and resistant populations of S. frugiperda was not influenced by the ingestion of SBTI. However, when the resistant population consumed Bt maize plants amended with SBTI, high mortality along with a reduction in larval weight and reduced activity of digestive trypsins were observed. Although the mode of action was not elucidated, it is possible that the consumption of SBTI increased susceptibility to Bt maize in the resistant population of S. frugiperda.

Introduction

Maize is a crop of worldwide importance, being cultivated on 22.3 million hectares in Brazil in 2022/2023 (Conab 2022). The productivity of maize is severely impaired by insect pests such as the fall armyworm, Spodoptera frugiperda (Smith, 1797) (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae), a key insect pest that attacks maize from plant emergence through the reproductive stages of the crop (Blanco et al. 2016).

Management of this pest with chemical or microbial insecticide spray applications is hampered by the behavior of S. frugiperda larvae, that includes feeding and taking refuge inside the whorl of maize plants, a difficult place to apply chemical or microbiological control agents (Barros et al. 2010). Today, the use of transgenic maize plants expressing Cry insecticidal proteins derived from the entomopathogenic microorganism Bacillus thuringiensis Berliner (Bt maize) stands out as an effective tactic for the management of S. frugiperda larvae (Bravo et al. 2011, Blanco et al. 2016, ISAAA 2021).

Briefly, the mode of action of Cry insecticidal proteins expressed in transgenic plants involves the hydrolysis and subsequent activation of Cry1 and Cry2 protoxins (around 120–130 kDa) into active forms (around 60–70 kDa) by specific proteases present in the midgut of lepidopteran pests (Bravo et al. 2007). The activated insecticidal proteins bind to specific receptors of midgut cells and, after a series of biochemical and cellular processes, form pores in the midgut cell membrane that leads to midgut cell lysis, osmotic stress, and finally insect death (Bravo et al. 2013).

Despite the efficiency of Bt maize to control target lepidopteran pests, the development of pest populations resistant to the transgenic plants has been an ongoing concern (Sparks et al. 2021). In Brazil, cases of field-evolved resistance in S. frugiperda populations have been recorded for transgenic plants expressing the Cry insecticidal proteins Cry1F and Cry1Ab (Farias et al. 2014, Omoto et al. 2016). To slow the development of pest resistance to Bt maize, insect resistance management (IRM) tactics have been developed and implemented, such as the planting a non-Bt refuge to maintain susceptibility in a portion of the pest population not exposed to the transgenic plants and the use of transgenic plants expressing high doses of more than 1 insecticidal protein active to control the same pest (gene pyramiding) (Tabashnik et al. 2013, Fatoretto et al. 2017, Tabashnik and Carrière 2017). To contribute to the strategy of gene pyramiding, the discovery and development of new insecticidal proteins with different modes of actions are highlighted (Bravo et al. 2011, Fatoretto et al. 2017, Chae et al. 2022).

Mechanisms of resistance to Cry proteins have been suggested, such as midgut receptor alterations, reduced rate of proprotein activation, detoxification of insecticidal proteins through excessive or improper hydrolysis, rapid midgut tissue regeneration, immune-related mechanisms (Jurat-Fuentes et al. 2021) and low permeability or trapping of toxins in the peritrophic membrane (Pérez-Hedo et al. 2012, Jurat-Fuentes and Crickmore 2017). Specifically in S. frugiperda, changes in ABCC2 gene have been associated with resistance to Cry1A proteins (Boaventura et al. 2020).

As Cry proteins interact with digestive enzymes and the midgut epithelium to cause insect death, interference in the digestion of protein in Lepidopteran larvae may alter this interaction. Serine proteases, mainly trypsins, are responsible for protein digestion and represent an essential component for amino acid nutrition (Srinivasan et al. 2006, Terra and Ferreira 2020). Trypsins may play distinct roles, such as protoxin activation or detoxification of Cry insecticidal proteins in Lepidopteran larvae (Pérez-Hedo et al. 2012, Singh et al. 2020).

Protein digestion in Lepidopteran larvae is known to be reduced by plant trypsin inhibitors and may lead to severe nutritional deficiencies that result in insect malformation or death (Zhu-Salzman and Zeng 2015, Singh et al. 2020). Regarding the interaction of trypsin inhibitors with Cry proteins in the digestive system of Lepidopteran larvae, it is hypothesized that reduced activity of trypsins caused by trypsin inhibitors may (i) slow the activation rates of protoxins reducing the efficacy of Cry insecticidal proteins or (ii) slow detoxification rates of insecticidal proteins, or (iii) result in insect malnutrition, with the last 2 hypotheses resulting in increased efficacy of Cry insecticidal proteins.

Considering the importance of serine proteases in the activation or detoxification of Cry insecticidal proteins, as well in the protein digestion and, consequently, in insect nutrition, the main goal of the present work was to test if a soybean trypsin inhibitor (SBTI) could annul the resistance of S. frugiperda larvae to a commercial Bt maize plant expressing the Bt proteins Cry1F, Cry1A.105, and Cry2Ab2.

Material and Methods

Cloning the Soybean Trypsin Inhibitor

The amino acid sequence of the soybean trypsin inhibitor (SBTI) was obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI, access number NP_001238611.2). The nucleotide sequence for the mature form of SBTI (signal peptide removed as indicated by the analysis using SignalP 6.0 – Teufel et al. 2022) was synthesized with codon optimization for expression in E. coli and was cloned into pET24a+ plasmid between the restriction sites XhoI and NdeI (Genone, Rio de Janeiro, RJ, Brazil). The plasmid containing the nucleotide sequence of SBTI was named pET24a+SBTI. The mature SBTI containing a poly-histidine tag was predicted to be expressed in E. coli as a 22.5 kDa protein.

SBTI Production

pET24a+SBTI was resuspended in pure water (2 ng/ml) and 2 µl were transferred to 40 µl of E. coli BL21 [DE3] Codon Plus electrocompetent cells inside an electroporation cuvette previously kept on ice for 10 min. Cells were transformed by an electric pulse (1.8 kV/25 µF, 5 s) using an electroporator (Gene Pulse, BioRad, Hercules, CA). After the electric pulse, 1 ml of LB medium (10 g tryptone, 5 g yeast extract, 5 g NaCl per liter) was added to the cuvette and the cell suspension was transferred to an Eppendorf tube (1.5 ml) and incubated at 37 °C for 1.5 h under orbital shaking of 180 rpm. The culture was spread over a Petri dish (9 cm diameter) containing solid LB medium (10 g tryptone, 5 g yeast extract, 5 g NaCl, 15 g bacterium agar per liter) amended with 50 µg/ml kanamycin and 50 µg/ml chloramphenicol. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 16–20 h.

For heterologous expression of SBTI, 1 single colony of E. coli BL21 [DE3] Codon Plus transformed with pET24a + SBTI was transferred to 50 ml of LB amended with 50 µg/ml kanamycin and kept at 37 °C under 200 rpm orbital shaking for 16 h to form the pre-inoculum. The pre-inoculum was 20-fold diluted inside a 250 ml Erlenmeyer flask (5 ml of the pre-inoculum plus 95 ml of LB amended with 50 µg/ml kanamycin) and incubated at 37 °C under 200 rpm orbital shaking until OD600nm reached 0.4–0.6. At this point, SBTI expression was induced by adding isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactoside (IPTG) at a final concentration of 100 µM in the culture. Samples of the culture (500 µl) were collected at 1, 2, 3, and 4 h after the induction with IPTG. Samples were centrifuged (10,000 g, 5 min, 4 °C) and the supernatants were discarded. Cell pellets were resuspended in 80 µl of 1× SDS-PAGE buffer (31.25 mM TRIS–HCl pH 6.8, 1% SDS, 12.5% glycerol, 2.5% β-mercaptoethanol, 0.005% bromophenol blue), boiled for 10 min and 20 µl of this total protein extraction were applied in a 15% SDS–PAGE gel (Laemmli 1970, Sambrook and Russel 2001). Gels were run at constant voltage of 100 V and protein bands were revealed using 0.12% Coomassie Blue R250 in a mixture of water/ethanol/acetic acid (350/75/75).

Solubility Test of SBTI

After confirming the expression of SBTI, the solubility of SBTI produced was checked. For this, cell pellets were obtained after centrifuging (20,000g, 30 min, 4 °C) 50 ml of bacteria cultivated at 37 °C under 200 rpm orbital shaking and 4 h after the induction with 1 mM IPTG. Cell pellets were resuspended in 10 ml of phosphate buffer saline (PBS – 20 mM NaH2PO4·H2O, 20 mM Na2HPO4·12H2O, 0.5 M NaCl, pH 7.4). Cell lysis started by the addition of 300 μl of lysozyme from chicken egg white (100 mg/ml) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) and 100 μl of DNAse I from bovine pancreas (100 mg ml) (Sigma-Aldrich), samples were incubated (37 °C, 30 min) and kept on ice.

Samples (20 ml) were then sonicated in an ultrasonic bath (3 cycles of 1 min at 40 kHz with 10 s intervals at room temperature) and centrifuged (4,000 rpm, 30 min, 4 °C). Supernatants (soluble fraction) and the sediment (insoluble fraction) were collected in different tubes. The sediment containing inclusion bodies, insoluble proteins, and cell debris was resuspended in 10 ml of cell debris solubilization buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 M urea, 1% Triton X-100). This solution was incubated at room temperature for 5 min and centrifuged (9,000 rpm, 4 °C, 30 min). The supernatant (cell wall and proteins solubilized) was discarded and the sediment (inclusion bodies and insoluble proteins) was resuspended in deionized water (10 ml). Samples of soluble proteins and insoluble proteins obtained after cell debris solubilization were kept at −20 °C and the protein profile of each fraction was observed in 15% SDS–PAGE as described above in the item “SBTI production” using 20 µl of each sample and 4 µl of 6× SDS-PAGE buffer.

Solubilization of SBTI

After solubility testing, the sediment was washed with 10 ml of PBS for Triton X-100 removal after centrifugation (9,000 rpm, 20 min, 4 °C) and supernatant discarded. The sediment was resuspended in PBS amended with 2 M urea and incubated for 16 h at −20 °C. The solution was defrosted at room temperature and centrifuged (9,000 rpm, 15 min, 4 °C). The supernatant containing the solubilized proteins was collected and the protein profile was observed in 15% SDS-PAGE as described above in the item “SBTI production”.

Purification of SBTI

Purification of SBTI was made using 5 ml of Ni Sepharose 6 Fast Flow resin equilibrated with 20 ml of washing buffer (9.9 mM Tris, 49.9 mM NaH2PO4.H2O, and 99.93 mM NaCl, pH 8.0). An aliquot (10 ml) of solubilized samples of SBTI were applied in the column and the eluted volume was collected in two 5 ml fractions. The column was washed with 15 ml of washing buffer and three 5 ml samples were collected. Elution of proteins added to the resin was performed using 10 ml of each imidazole solution at 10, 25, 50, 75, 100, and 250 mM with collection of 5 ml fractions. The protein profile of the collected fractions were observed in 15% SDS–PAGE as described above in the item “SBTI production.”

Imidazole removal in eluted fractions containing SBTI was done using dialysis cellulose membranes (12 MWCO, Sigma-Aldrich) against deionized water at 4 °C. After dialysis, samples were freeze-dried (JJ Científica, São Carlos, Brazil) and resuspended in deionized water. Protein was quantified using Bradford Reagent according the manufacturer’s instructions and bovine serum albumin as a standard (Sigma-Aldrich). Total proteins were quantified in μg/μl and the working solutions were adjusted for 1 µg/µl.

Insects

Two S. frugiperda populations were used in the experiments. The population susceptible to Bt maize (30A37PW, MON89034 × TC1507 × NK603, expression of Cry1F, Cry1A.105, and Cry2Ab2 Bt proteins) was maintained in laboratory without exposure to pesticides or transgenic plants for at least 15 years. The population resistant to Bt maize was kindly provided by Prof. Oderlei Bernardi from the Universidade Federal de Santa Maria-RS, Brazil corresponding to the F29 generation of the resistant population (Bernardi et al. 2017, Garlet et al. 2022). This resistant population was kept under selection pressure since F1 by feeding larvae Bt maize at least each 2 generations. In the present study, we exposed the resistant population to Bt maize (Cry1F, Cry1A.105, and Cry2Ab2 Bt proteins) at F53–59 generations and used the F59 generation for these experiments.

Midgut Extraction and Inhibition of Digestive Trypsins From Larval Spodoptera frugiperda

To check the functionality of the recombinant SBTI produced, trypsin inhibition assays were conducted using the digestive tract of Bt-susceptible 5th instar S. frugiperda. For this, S. frugiperda larvae actively feeding on a carioca (Phaseolus vulgaris) bean-based artificial diet (Truzi et al. 2021) were anesthetized on ice for 10 min. Larvae were inserted into a 125 mM NaCl solution (4 °C), their heads and posterior tips of their bodies were removed, and midgut dissection was made after a longitudinal cut through larval integuments. Midguts were separated from the posterior gut by a transversal cut at the insertion of the Malpighian tubes in the gut. Midgut epithelium containing the peritrophic membrane with its contents was homogenized using 3 strokes of homogenization in a Potter–Elvehjem homogenizer in a proportion of 3 midguts per 600 µl of deionized water. The homogenates were centrifuged (10,000×g, 10 min, 4 °C) and the supernatants were used as a source of digestive enzymes (enzyme extract).

Trypsin activity was measured by incubating 4 µl of the enzyme extract, 10 µl of the inhibitor (1 µg/µl, pure and diluted 10, 20, 30, and 50-fold; 10 µl of water for control reaction) and 36 µl of water at 37 °C for 15 min in Eppendorf tubes. Samples were transferred to a 96 well plate and 160 µl of 1.0875 mM BApNA prepared in 100 mM glycine-NaOH pH 9.0 (Erlanger et al. 1961) were added to each sample. Plates were incubated at 30 °C and spectrophotometric readings were continuously taken at 410 nm at 30 s intervals for 1 h using Multiskan Sky (ThermoScientific). Blanks of substrate and enzyme were included to monitor the formation for any detectable color at 410 nm not related with the trypsin assays. Trypsin activity was calculated using a standard curve of 4-nitroaniline (0–125 pmol/well, y = 0.0203x, R² = 0.9959). One unit (U) of trypsin was defined as the quantity of enzyme required to form 1 pmol of 4-nitroaniline per minute. Means and standard errors (SEM) were obtained from trypsin activity calculated using 3 different enzyme extracts incubated with each dilution of SBTI.

Mortality and Weight of Larval Spodoptera frugiperda Resistant or Susceptible to Bt Maize Fed With Bt or Non-Bt Maize With or Without SBTI

Bt maize expressing Cry1F, Cry1A.105, and Cry2Ab2 Bt proteins or a nearly-isogenic non-Bt maize hybrid (Pioneer Brand hybrid 30A37RR, non-Bt) were planted in the field (3 rows each 10 m long spaced 90 cm apart with 6 plants per meter of each maize hybrid). Leaves were collected at V7–V9 growth stages and were used for feeding S. frugiperda larvae. Collected leaves were washed in sodium hypochlorite (0.025%) baths followed by 3 washes with deionized water. After superficial drying at room temperature, leaves were cut into disks (2 cm diameter) placed in cylindrical plastic containers (50 ml) which contained 1 ml of 1% agar at the base and 1 filter paper disk between the leaf disks and the agar. Five neonate S. frugiperda larvae (less than 24 h after eclosion) were transferred to each container and covered. Two containers represented 1 repetition and 20 containers were replicated for each treatment.

For the treatments containing SBTI, 10 µl of purified SBTI (1 µg/µl) were applied using a fine brush on each side of the leaf disk (6.37 µg cm−2). Treated leaf disks were replaced every 48 h to avoid food deprivation. The following treatments were performed:

Activity of Digestive Trypsins and Alkaline Phosphatases of Larval Spodoptera frugiperda Resistant or Susceptible to Bt Maize Fed With Bt or Non-Bt Maize With or Without SBTI

For measuring the activity of digestive trypsins and alkaline phosphatases, 30 resistant and susceptible S. frugiperda neonates were individually transferred to covered plastic containers (70 ml) containing 1% agar at the bottom with a disk of paper filter covering the agar and the Bt or non-Bt maize leaf disks were placed above the paper disks. Larvae consumed the treatments without SBTI until they reached the 5th instar. Larvae were starved for 12 h prior and refeeding started with the treatments containing or not containing SBTI as previously described. Larvae were collected as soon as the leaf disks were consumed and they were dissected for preparing the digestive enzyme extracts as described above in the item “Midgut extraction”.

Trypsin activity was measured as described in the item “Midgut extraction” exchanging the volume of inhibitor for water. Alkaline phosphatase activity was measured using 50 µl of the enzyme extract, 25 µl of 20 mM 4-nitrophenyl-phosphate (pNPP) prepared in 100 mM Glycine-NaOH pH 9.0 buffer and 25 µl of the same buffer. Plates were incubated at 30 °C and spectrophotometric readings were continuously taken at 405 nm at 1 min intervals for 20 min Multiskan Sky (ThermoScientific). Blanks of substrate and enzyme were included to monitor the formation of any detectable color at 405 nm not related with the alkaline phosphatase assays. Alkaline phosphatase activity was calculated using a standard curve of 4-nitrophenol (0–32 nmol/well, y = 0.106x, R² = 0.9956). One unit (U) of alkaline phosphatase was defined as the quantity of enzyme required to form 1 nmol of 4-nitrophenol per minute. Total protein of the enzyme extracts was estimated according to Bradford (1976) as described in the item “Purification of SBTI”. Means and SEM were obtained from 10 independent replicates composed by the midguts from 3 larvae exposed to each feeding treatment.

Statistics

Larval weight, mortality, trypsin activity, alkaline phosphatase activity, and total protein data were tested for normal distribution and homoscedasticity using, respectively, the tests of Cramer-von Mises and Bartlett (α = 0.05). Data not fitted into these assumptions were transformed using the BoxCox index. Data fitted a normal distribution and homoscedasticity and were analyzed by ANOVA (α = 0.05) and means were compared using the test of Tukey (α = 0.05). All statistical analyses were made using R software (R Core Team 2020).

Results

Expression, Solubilization, and Purification of SBTI

Successful production of SBTI was achieved using E. coli BL21 (DE3) Codon Plus. Expression of SBTI was observed from 1 until 4 h of culture induced by 100 µM IPTG and the protein band of SBTI containing the His-tag showed the expected size of 22.49 kDa (Fig. 1A). The solubility test showed that most of the SBTI was produced in insoluble form (Fig. 1B). Given that significant amounts of SBTI would be necessary for subsequent bioassays, we opted to solubilize the expressed protein using a solubilization buffer containing 2 M urea and the solubilization was successful (Fig. 1C). After solubilization, the SBTI was purified using “Ni Sepharose 6 Fast Flow” resin and was eluted using 100 and 250 mM imidazole (Fig. 1D).

Activity of the Digestive Trypsins of Larval Spodoptera frugiperda in the Presence of SBTI

The solubilized, purified (only the fractions eluted with 100 mM imidazole) and desalted SBTI was analyzed in terms of reduction of the activity of digestive trypsin of larval S. frugiperda. SBTI reduced the activity of trypsins in a dose-dependent matter (F = 26.442; df = 5,12; P < 0.001), with the concentration of 1 µg/µl responsible for the inhibition of approximately 82% of total trypsin activity, and 0.1 µg/µl for approximately 32% of trypsin inhibition (Fig. 2).

Mortality and Weight of Bt-Resistant or Susceptible Spodoptera frugiperda Larvae Fed Bt or Non-Bt Maize, With or Without SBTI

Mortality rates of S. frugiperda larvae were significantly altered by feeding with the different treatments (F = 21.033; df = 5,54; P < 0.001). All susceptible larvae fed with Bt maize with or without SBTI resulted in 100% mortality (data not shown). Resistant larvae fed with Bt maize were impacted by the addition of SBTI and presented a higher mortality rate, i.e., approximately 85% while resistant larvae fed Bt maize without SBTI presented a mortality rate of approximately 48%. Independent of adding SBTI, resistant larvae fed non-Bt maize presented a mortality rate of approximately 25%, and was similar to the mortality observed for susceptible larvae fed on non-Bt maize with or without SBTI (Fig. 3A).

Weight of the surviving larvae measured 6 days after the beginning of feeding also differed among the treatments (F = 33.858; df = 5,351; P < 0.001). Surviving resistant larvae fed with Bt maize containing SBTI showed the lowest weight, approximately 0.75 mg per larva, followed by resistant larvae fed Bt maize without SBTI (1.5 mg per larva). When fed non-Bt maize with or without SBTI, resistant larvae attained the greatest weight (approximately 3.5 mg per larva), a weight superior to that observed for susceptible larvae fed non-Bt maize with or without SBTI (approximately 2.5 mg per larva) (Fig. 3B).

Discussion

Similar to that observed here for the Bt maize-resistant S. frugiperda population, Helicoverpa armigera (Hübner, [1808]) (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae) presented reduced growth and survival rates when ingesting an artificial diet containing Cry1Ac combined with a trypsin inhibitor (Lomate and Hivrale 2013). Trypsins are key proteases involved in Lepidopteran larval digestion, having a significant role in primary digestion of proteins (Srinivasan et al. 2006, Lima et al. 2020). Blocking trypsin activity using trypsin inhibitors, such as SBTI, may result in negative effects on amino acid nutrition and, consequently, in insect development (Macedo et al. 2010, Macedo and Freire 2011, Zhu-Salzman and Zeng 2015, Meriño-Cabrera et al. 2020).

However, the use of trypsin inhibitors for pest management should be evaluated case-by-case because some insect pests, such as S. frugiperda larvae, may overcome the ingestion of trypsin inhibitors by increasing trypsin activity (Oliveira et al. 2013) or expressing trypsin inhibitor insensitive trypsins (Souza et al. 2016). In the resistant population of S. frugiperda consuming only Bt maize, it is possible that increased trypsin activity associated with differentially expressed trypsins may be causing excessive hydrolyzes and inactivation of Cry proteins, but this hypothesis needs further investigation.

In terms of the reduction of the resistance observed for the Bt maize-resistant S. frugiperda larvae ingesting Bt leaf tissue treated with SBTI, it suggests that malnutrition effects conferred by the SBTI may be hampering resistance responses of this S. frugiperda population. One possible mechanism of resistance observed in Cry-resistant insects is rapid midgut tissue regeneration, a nutrient-consuming response (Castagnola and Jurat-Fuentes 2016, Jurat-Fuentes et al. 2021). While well-nourished resistant insects can consume and correctly develop when fed with the Bt plants, the malnourished resistant insects consuming SBTI may not have sufficient nutritive reserves to respond to the consumption of Bt plants.

Another role of digestive proteases may be detoxification through excessive hydrolyzes of Bt proteins, such as Cry1 and Cry2, by the resistant insects (Keller et al. 1996, Abdelgaffar et al. 2019, Pinos et al. 2021). In this case, addition of SBTI could be contributing to reduced putative detoxification. Reduction in protease activity in the midgut of insect pests using inhibitors (Kirouac et al. 2006, Yang et al. 2021) or RNAi to impair the expression of a digestive protease (Guan et al. 2017) was shown to prevent excessive proteolysis and detoxification of Cry insecticidal proteins.

Increased trypsin activity in Lepidopteran larvae as a response to Cry insecticidal protein ingestion was previously observed (Lomate and Hivrale 2013). In the present work, Bt-susceptible and Bt-resistant larvae responded to the ingestion of SBTI by increasing trypsin activity similarly to that observed by Oliveira et al. (2013). A similar increase in trypsin activity was also observed in Bt-resistant larvae responding to the ingestion of Bt maize without SBTI, suggesting that increase in trypsin activity is a common response of this larvae to ingestion of xenobiotics. However, when consuming both SBTI and Cry proteins from Bt maize in combination, the trypsin activity was not increased and mortality rates of the resistant population of S. frugiperda reached approximately 85%, with surviving insects with a pronounced reduction in weight. Altogether, these data suggest that the lack of response in trypsin activity of larval S. frugiperda is involved with high mortality rates of the resistant population feeding on Bt maize treated with SBTI.

Membrane-bound alkaline phosphatases are known as receptors for Cry proteins during the steps of intoxication in susceptible insects (Bravo et al. 2013) and reduction in membrane-bound alkaline phosphatase activity in Cry-resistant insects is associated with resistance mechanisms (Jurat-Fuentes et al. 2011, Guo et al. 2015). Additionally, Cry-resistant insect pests may present alterations of positioning of alkaline phosphatases moving from membrane-bound to soluble (Hernández-Martínez et al. 2010, Caccia et al. 2012). In the present work, it was not observed a significant alteration in the activity of soluble alkaline phosphatases in the resistant insects when compared with susceptible insects, suggesting that altering the membrane-bound form to soluble form of alkaline phosphatase is not a mechanism of resistance to Bt plants in the selected resistant population.

Combining SBTI with Cry proteins could result in an antagonistic effect on larval mortality, given that Cry insecticidal proteins require the activation by trypsins (Bravo et al. 2007). However, this antagonistic effect was not observed probably because Cry1F protein expressed in the Bt maize used in the present work is expressed in a truncated form already activated (Baktavachalam et al. 2015). This fact reinforces that nutritional deficiencies caused by SBTI may be responsible for the reversion of the resistance of the Bt maize-resistant population.

Commercial transgenic traits combining the Bt proteins Cry1Ac and a cowpea trypsin inhibitor were developed for rice (Han et al. 2006) and cotton varieties with improved pest efficacy (Cui et al. 2011). The effects of SBTI on increasing the susceptibility of a Bt maize-resistant S. frugiperda population is shown here for the first time and should provide value for transgenic plant development and insect resistant management.
