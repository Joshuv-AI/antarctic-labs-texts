Advances in plant electrostimulation: from cellular and molecular mechanisms to agricultural applications
Authors: Yu Dong, Ye Tian, Jingxuan Pan, Weiwen Qiu, Xinyu Zhou, Yangyang Xie, Mei Liu, Juntao Wang, Fengru Gao, Liang Jin, Dan Wei, Yanhong Wang, Qiang Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTElectrical stimulation is an emerging physical strategy for agricultural regulation that applies electric fields or currents with defined parameters to modulate plant growth, development, and rhizosphere processes. Owing to its residue-free nature, environmental compatibility, and high controllability, electrical stimulation has considerable potential to improve crop yield and quality while enhancing tolerance to abiotic and biotic stresses, making it increasingly relevant to sustainable agriculture. This review critically synthesizes the mechanisms and applications of electrical stimulation across cellular and molecular, physiological and metabolic, whole-plant, and field scales. Particular emphasis is placed on distinguishing continuous or quasi-continuous stimulation from high-voltage pulsed treatments, which differ in membrane-level mechanisms, reversibility, and safety profiles. Three interrelated factors are identified as central to its effectiveness: regulation of membrane potential and ion transport, root-targeted modulation of plant–soil interactions, and context-dependent matching of stimulation parameters with specific biological responses. We further highlight that translating laboratory-scale benefits into reliable field applications will require intelligent delivery systems capable of stable, scalable, energy-efficient, and feedback-controlled operation under variable environmental conditions. Future research should prioritize multiscale investigation of molecular mechanisms, standardized evaluation of stimulation parameters, development of smart and synergistic regulation platforms, and validation across diverse crops, soils, and production systems. These advances will facilitate the practical deployment of electrical stimulation technologies and support their integration into sustainable and precision agriculture.Keywords: Plant electrostimulation, membrane potential, rhizosphere regulation, crop stress tolerance, sustainable agriculture, bioelectrical signal

Introduction

Globally, intensive crop production increasingly relies on precise regulation of water, nutrients, and the root-zone environment, while prolonged agrochemical use, soil degradation, salinization, and soil-borne pathogens continue to constrain soil-based production systems. 1-3 Controlled-environment and soilless cultivation can alleviate some of these limitations, but their wider adoption remains restricted by capital and energy costs, technical requirements, and management complexity. 4-6 Hydroponic and aeroponic systems enable precise root-zone control; however, recirculating nutrient solutions can facilitate pathogen dissemination and require continuous monitoring of nutrient status, dissolved oxygen, and sanitation. 6-8 Aeroponic systems impose additional demands on equipment reliability and maintenance. 6 Repeated use of cultivation substrates can also degrade physicochemical quality, alter microbial communities, and promote the accumulation of phytotoxic compounds. 9 , 10 Moreover, many conventional substrates act mainly as passive growth media rather than actively regulating plant growth. These constraints limit the scalability and long-term sustainability of conventional soilless production systems and create a need for active physical regulation strategies such as electrical stimulation.

These challenges create a strong need for sustainable physical technologies that can regulate plant growth precisely and actively, while avoiding the limitations of both soil cultivation and conventional soilless systems. Electrical stimulation, defined here as the application of external electrical signals to plants or their growth environment, has therefore gained renewed attention as a potentially environmentally friendly, non-chemical method of crop regulation. 11 , 12 The concept of using electricity to influence plant growth has a long history. In 1783, the French physicist Bertholon introduced the concept of “electroculture” 13 During the following two centuries, researchers investigated a range of approaches, including high-voltage electric fields established between the atmosphere and soil, direct electrode contact with plant tissues, and the incorporation of conductive materials into cultivation media. 11 , 12 However, these early investigations differed widely in plant species, electrical parameters, exposure methods, and experimental conditions. Consequently, their results were often inconsistent and difficult to reproduce.

From a physical perspective, plant electrostimulation encompasses several distinct modes of electrical exposure rather than a single continuum of treatment intensity. 11 , 12 In direct-contact configurations, an applied potential difference can drive electrical current through plant tissues or the surrounding growth medium, whereas field-based configurations expose plants to an externally imposed electric field without necessarily requiring substantial current flow through the tissue. 11 , 12 According to the temporal characteristics of the electrical input, these treatments can be broadly distinguished as continuous or quasi-continuous stimulation, including direct-current (DC) stimulation, weak microcurrents, and low-frequency alternating-current/electric-field treatments, and pulsed stimulation, including high-voltage pulsed electric fields (HV-PEFs) and pulsed electrical discharges. 11 , 12 , 14 Continuous field-based exposure may also include static or high-voltage electrostatic fields, which should be distinguished from high-voltage pulsed electric fields because a high applied voltage does not necessarily imply pulsed delivery or electroporation. 12 , 14 Their biological effects therefore cannot be compared solely on the basis of applied voltage. Relevant descriptors include electric-field strength, voltage, current or current density, frequency, waveform, polarity, exposure duration, and, for pulsed treatments, pulse width and pulse number. 12 , 14 In addition, the term “in situ self-induced electrical stimulation” refers to a rhizosphere bioelectrochemical configuration in which an electrode assembly is placed in the root zone to harness low biopotentials generated within the plant–soil–microbial system under defined circuit modes, rather than to a conventional externally imposed high-voltage electric field. 15 Mohan and Yeruva compared closed-, open-, and short-circuit configurations, with the closed-circuit mode producing the strongest growth and aquaporin responses. 15 Explicit separation of these stimulation modes is essential because they differ in membrane-level action, reversibility, safety margin, and potential agricultural application. 11 , 12 , 14

Recent advances in plant electrophysiology and bioelectronic engineering have provided new momentum for research into electrical regulation of plant growth. Plants possess intrinsic bioelectrical properties generated by ion gradients across plasma membranes, which allow plants to perceive, transmit, and respond to electrical signals. At the cellular level, unequal ion distributions across the plasma membrane, maintained by ion channels and ion pumps, generate the transmembrane potential. 16 , 17 Electrical signals also contribute to long-distance communication among plant tissues. 17-19 These intrinsic bioelectrical properties demonstrate that plants are capable of sensing, transmitting, and responding to electrical information, providing a physiological foundation for the agricultural application of electrical stimulation.

A growing body of evidence indicates that different forms of electrical stimulation can influence crop growth across several levels of biological organization. 11 , 12 At the cellular and molecular levels, electrical treatments can modify ion-channel activity, membrane properties, signal-transduction pathways, and gene expression. 11 , 12 , 15 At the tissue and organ levels, they may regulate photosynthesis, mineral-nutrient acquisition, water transport, and phytohormone signaling. 11 , 12 , 18 , 19 At the seed, whole-plant, and population levels, electrical stimulation has been associated with improved germination, enhanced vegetative development, greater stress tolerance, and increased yield. These responses suggest a multilevel regulatory pathway in which electrical stimuli are first perceived by cells, converted into biochemical and molecular signals, and subsequently expressed through changes in organ development and crop performance. However, previous reviews have often focused on individual processes, such as seed germination or photosynthetic regulation, rather than integrating evidence from cellular, physiological, whole-plant, and field scales. This fragmented approach has hindered the development of a comprehensive understanding of electrically induced plant responses. 11 , 12

By establishing this multiscale framework, the review aims to support mechanistic interpretation, optimization of stimulation parameters, and the standardized application of electrical technologies in sustainable agriculture. With continued development of enabling technologies, electric fields or currents with accurately defined parameters could be applied directly to the root zone and its surrounding environment. Such approaches may provide a means of selectively regulating nutrient transport, rhizosphere microbial communities, root physiological activity, and whole-plant metabolism. 12 , 15

Regulatory effects of electrical stimulation at the cellular and molecular levels

Biophysical distinction between continuous and pulsed electrical stimulation

Continuous or quasi-continuous electrical stimulation includes direct-current (DC) stimulation, weak microcurrents, low-frequency alternating-current (AC) electric fields, and continuous field-based exposures such as static or high-voltage electrostatic fields. 11 , 12 , 20 Depending on field strength and exposure conditions, continuous stimulation primarily influences transmembrane potential (TMP) and membrane-associated transport processes without necessarily inducing the electroporation characteristic of sufficiently intense pulsed treatments. 11 , 12 , 21 , 22 Changes in TMP can influence H+-ATPase activity, ion-channel behavior, proton transport, and Ca2+-dependent signaling, thereby modifying cell elongation, nutrient transport, and downstream metabolic responses. 18 , 22-24 Representative continuous or low-frequency treatments among the studies reviewed here include 60-Hz electric-field exposure in plant roots, 21 , 25 microampere-level stimulation of plant tissue cultures, 26 , 27 and low-current, self-induced rhizosphere bioelectrical stimulation in mung bean and chickpea using defined circuit configurations. 15 Importantly, these responses are strongly dependent on stimulation intensity, polarity, and exposure duration. 21-23 , 25 Within an appropriate parameter window, membrane-potential changes may remain reversible and trigger adaptive physiological responses, whereas stronger or prolonged exposure can disrupt membrane-potential regulation, proton extrusion, and growth. 22 , 25

Pulsed electrical stimulation represents a biophysically distinct treatment mode. High-voltage pulsed electric fields and pulsed electrical discharges deliver electrical energy over short time intervals separated by pulse-free periods. 12 , 14 At sufficiently high field strengths, these pulses can transiently increase membrane permeability through electroporation-like processes. When membrane perturbation remains limited, permeability can recover after treatment, whereas excessive field strength, pulse duration, pulse number, or cumulative exposure can increase the likelihood of irreversible membrane damage and tissue injury. 14 Pulsed treatments are therefore more commonly applied as short-duration pretreatments, particularly for seed priming, rather than as sustained whole-plant stimulation. 28 For example, the studies reviewed in this article include high-voltage pulsed treatment of wheat seeds and pulsed rhizosphere stimulation used to induce negative-air-ion release. 29 Because their biological effects depend on pulse-specific parameters, including voltage or field strength, pulse width, frequency, and pulse number, pulsed treatments should not be interpreted simply as higher-intensity versions of continuous DC or AC stimulation. 12 , 14 , 28

This distinction also helps reconcile apparently contrasting descriptions of beneficial electrical stimulation and electrical injury. These responses may resemble hormesis-like adaptive regulation in which electrical inputs perturb membrane potential and activate ion-, ROS-, and hormone-dependent signaling without causing substantial loss of membrane integrity. As electrical exposure increases beyond this window, the response may shift from reversible signaling and acclimation toward growth inhibition, membrane dysfunction, or tissue injury. 12 , 18 , 19 , 22 , 25 For pulsed high-voltage treatments, a comparable transition may occur between reversible membrane permeabilization and irreversible electroporation. Continuous and pulsed stimulation should therefore be evaluated using separate dose–response frameworks rather than being placed along a single axis of electrical “intensity”. 11 , 14 This distinction is particularly important when comparing studies and defining safe and effective parameter ranges for agricultural applications (Figure 1) (Table 1).

Membrane potential regulation and ion transport

The plasma membrane is a major interface through which continuous low-frequency electrical stimulation is converted into cellular responses. Exposure to 60-Hz extremely low-frequency electric fields (ELF-EFs) has been shown to alter TMP and root elongation in a field-strength-dependent manner. 21 , 25 , 37 Studies in pea (Pisum sativum) demonstrated that root-growth inhibition was more closely associated with electric-field strength and induced membrane potential than with current density. 21 , 25 Similar relationships between induced membrane potential and growth inhibition were subsequently reported in maize (Zea mays) and fava bean (Vicia faba). 37 Brayman et al. further showed that different regions of the cucurbit root elongation zone differed in their sensitivity to 60-Hz electric fields, indicating spatial variation in electrical responsiveness within the root. 38 Together, these findings demonstrate that the biological response to low-frequency electric fields depends not only on the applied field but also on species, tissue type, and developmental region.

Changes in TMP can directly influence ion transport across the plasma membrane, but the direction and magnitude of the response depend strongly on electrical parameters. 22-24 In maize coleoptile cells, DC electric-field treatment altered membrane potential, proton extrusion, and elongation growth, indicating that excessive electrical exposure can interfere with H+-ATPase-dependent membrane regulation. 22 In contrast, weak AC electric fields have been reported to promote root growth and increase endoplasmic-reticulum abundance in root-cap cells. 23 These contrasting responses reinforce the existence of a parameter-dependent transition between reversible electrical regulation and inhibitory electrical stress. 22 , 23 Ca2+ is particularly relevant in this context because it functions as a major second messenger linking membrane electrical changes with downstream signaling pathways. 18 , 19 In hydroponically grown kale, application of a 50 mA electrical current to the nutrient solution for three weeks increased calcium concentration by 72%, total phenolic content by 57%, and antioxidant capacity by 70% relative to the control. 31 Electrical stimulation may additionally influence the acquisition and distribution of other mineral cations, including K+ and Mg2+. 22 , 31 Under saline-alkali stress, high-voltage electrostatic pretreatment of sheepgrass (Leymus chinensis) seeds enhanced K+ accumulation in leaves and reduced the Na+/K+ ratio. 39 These changes indicate improved ionic homeostasis under stress. 39 Importantly, electrically induced ion fluxes are not limited to direct effects on mineral transport or cellular homeostasis; they may also act as early signaling events linking membrane-level electrical responses to downstream biochemical and molecular processes. 18 , 19 , 24

Intracellular signaling and molecular responses

Building on these membrane-level and ionic responses, electrical stimulation can be converted into intracellular signaling events involving Ca2+ transients and reactive oxygen species (ROS). 18 , 19 These secondary signals can regulate ion-channel activity, antioxidant defense systems, protein phosphorylation and dephosphorylation, and metabolic enzyme activity, thereby linking early electrical perception with downstream molecular responses. 18 , 19 At the transcriptional level, electrical stimulation can induce broad but selective changes in gene expression. 15 , 40-42

Under in situ self-induced electrical stimulation, in which an electrode assembly was placed in the rhizosphere and operated under defined circuit configurations, aquaporin-related genes were upregulated in mung bean (Vigna radiata) and chickpea (Cicer arietinum), with the strongest responses observed under closed-circuit operation. 15 Increased aquaporin expression may facilitate cellular and whole-plant water transport and thereby contribute to improved photosynthetic performance and biomass accumulation. 15 Electrical stimulation has also been associated with the activation of defense-related signaling pathways. 40 , 41 Defense-related genes, including Pin2, have been reported to respond to electrical treatment, 40 while salicylic-acid-associated defense responses were accompanied by increased β-1,3-glucanase expression in grapevine. 41 Under abiotic stress, high-voltage electrostatic pretreatment of Caragana korshinskii seeds altered drought-responsive transcriptional profiles, including the activation or restoration of transcripts associated with stress adaptation. 42 Electrical treatment may also contribute to osmotic and ionic adjustment by promoting the accumulation of compatible solutes, including proline and soluble sugars, and by affecting genes associated with K+ transport and Na+/H+ exchange. 39 In Scutellaria baicalensis, electrical stimulation was further associated with changes in flavonoid-related pathways and increased accumulation of major bioactive flavonoids, 43 , 44 illustrating the potential of electrical treatments to regulate secondary metabolism as well as stress responses.

Overall, current evidence supports a hierarchical but stimulation-mode-dependent framework for plant electrostimulation. Continuous or low-frequency electrical inputs primarily influence membrane potential and membrane-associated transport processes, 21-23 , 37 , 38 whereas sufficiently intense pulsed treatments may additionally induce transient membrane permeabilization. 14 These initial membrane-level responses can generate changes in ion fluxes and intracellular signaling, particularly those involving Ca2+ and ROS, 18 , 19 which subsequently influence gene expression, enzyme activity, primary and secondary metabolism, and stress-response pathways. 15 , 40-44 The resulting biological outcome therefore depends not only on downstream signaling networks but also on the mode, intensity, and duration of the initial electrical stimulus.

Regulation of plant physiological and metabolic processes by electrical stimulation

Photosynthesis is a central physiological process through which electrical stimulation may influence plant growth and productivity. 11 , 12 Reported responses include increases in stomatal conductance, improved CO₂ diffusion, and alterations in leaf and chloroplast structure. 35 , 45 , 46 Electrical treatments have been associated with thicker leaves, greater palisade cell density, and increased chloroplast abundance, 46 all of which may enhance the anatomical capacity for light capture and carbon fixation.

Several studies have demonstrated positive effects of electrical stimulation on photosynthetic performance. High-voltage electrostatic exposure increased chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and net photosynthetic rate in cucumber seedlings. 47 Similar improvements in photosynthetic indices were observed by Zhu et al. in Radermachera plants subjected to electric-field treatment. 48 Wang et al. also found that pulsed electric fields improved photosynthetic performance in lettuce. 35 Collectively, these findings indicate that electrical stimulation can enhance photosynthetic capacity through both structural and functional modifications. 35 , 46-48 However, the extent of these responses appears to depend strongly on crop species, developmental stage, treatment duration, and electrical parameters such as voltage, current, frequency, and field intensity. 11 , 12 , 35 , 46 Electrical stimulation should therefore not be regarded as producing a uniform photosynthetic response across plant systems. 35 Its potential may be particularly relevant to controlled-environment agriculture, where environmental variables and electrical treatments can be accurately coordinated to improve carbon assimilation and biomass production. 4 , 33 , 49 Beyond photosynthesis, electrical stimulation can regulate both primary and secondary metabolic pathways. 11 , 12 In primary metabolism, electric-field treatment has been shown to increase nitrate reductase (NR) activity in cucumber roots and leaves, suggesting enhanced nitrate reduction and nitrogen assimilation. 47 Electrical signals may also affect the distribution of photosynthetic products within the plant. Periodic low-voltage pulses applied to maize epicotyls selectively reduced the phloem-mediated transport of 14C-labeled photoassimilates from source leaves to root sinks, while producing little change in the xylem-mediated transport of 32 P-labeled inorganic nutrients. 50 This differential response suggests that electrical stimulation may act selectively on organic assimilate transport rather than causing a general inhibition of vascular transport. Possible mechanisms include changes in sieve-tube membrane potential, callose deposition, cytoskeletal organization, or motor-protein activity. Nevertheless, the precise processes through which electrical signals modify source–sink transport remain insufficiently understood and require further investigation. Electrical stimulation can also redirect metabolic activity towards the synthesis of valuable secondary metabolites. 12 , 43 , 44 , 51 This property has attracted particular interest in medicinal plants and high-value horticultural crops. In Scutellaria baicalensis, both high-voltage electrostatic fields and pulsed electric fields increased the accumulation of baicalein, wogonin, and related flavonoids in roots. 43 , 44 Pulsed treatments were also reported to enhance total flavonoid content. In grapevine, electrical stimulation has been linked to increased production of anthocyanins and resveratrol. 51 The accumulation of these antioxidant-related metabolites may contribute to ROS scavenging, reduced membrane lipid oxidation, and preservation of cellular integrity. 12 , 51 Under saline–alkaline stress, high-voltage electrostatic pretreatment of sheepgrass increased the concentrations of proline and soluble sugars. 39 These compounds contribute to osmotic adjustment, cellular water retention, membrane stabilization, and protection against ionic imbalance. 12 , 39 The beneficial effects of electrical stimulation on stress tolerance may therefore arise from an integrated priming response involving antioxidant activation, osmolyte accumulation, improved ion homeostasis, and membrane protection. 12 , 39 , 52 , 53 Such responses are consistent with a hormesis-like dose–response framework, in which mild electrical perturbation may activate protective and acclimatory pathways while remaining below the threshold for substantial membrane injury. 11 , 12 , 35 , 46 However, direct demonstrations of a complete biphasic dose–response relationship remain limited for many plant electrostimulation systems. 11 , 12 The transition between adaptive stimulation and electrical stress is likely to depend on stimulation mode and dose. 11 , 14 , 46 Excessive voltage, current density, exposure time, or field intensity may increase reactive oxygen species production, disrupt membrane function, inhibit metabolism, and damage plant tissues. 12 , 35 , 46 Consequently, optimization of electrical parameters is essential for obtaining beneficial physiological responses. Future studies should establish crop-specific dose–response relationships and clearly distinguish adaptive priming effects from direct electrical injury. Phytohormonal regulation provides another major mechanism through which electrical stimulation may influence plant growth and development. 19 , 54-56 Plant hormones coordinate a wide range of developmental processes, including cell division, elongation, differentiation, seed germination, and the transition from vegetative to reproductive growth. Electrical signals may modify these processes by affecting hormone biosynthesis, polar transport, spatial distribution, or signal sensitivity. 19 , 34 , 54-56

Evidence from plant tissue culture indicates that auxin transport is particularly important in mediating electrically induced growth responses. In tobacco callus, the stimulatory effect of weak electric currents depended strongly on the presence of exogenous indole-3-acetic acid and its capacity for polar transport. When polar auxin transport was blocked by 2,3,5-triiodobenzoic acid, the growth-promoting effect of electrical treatment disappeared. A similar loss of response occurred when indole-3-acetic acid was replaced with synthetic auxins such as 2,4-D, which have limited polar transport capacity. 30 These findings suggest that weak electrical currents do not simply increase auxin activity, but may instead enhance growth by modifying cellular polarity and facilitating the directional movement of auxin. 30,54 Such regulation could alter local hormone gradients and thereby influence patterns of cell expansion and division. 19 Rathore et al. further demonstrated that the response of tobacco callus to microampere-level currents was dependent on current polarity. Cathodal stimulation increased callus growth by approximately 60–70%, whereas anodal treatment produced little effect or caused slight growth inhibition. 26 This contrasting response indicates that the direction of electrical current is a critical determinant of biological outcome. The polarity-dependent effects may arise from interactions among endogenous bioelectric fields, plasma membrane potential, asymmetric distribution of membrane proteins, and cytoskeletal organization. Together, these processes may regulate auxin transport and establish spatial signals that coordinate cell proliferation and expansion. 19 , 30 Overall, the available evidence supports a model in which electrical stimulation influences plant development partly by reshaping cellular polarity and phytohormone distribution rather than by altering hormone concentration alone. 26 , 30

At the germination and early seedling stage, electric treatment can reshape endogenous hormone balance in a dynamic and stage-dependent manner. Chen et al. examined hormonal changes during the germination of millet seeds following optimized high-voltage electric-field pretreatment. The treatment increased the concentrations of indole-3-acetic acid, zeatin riboside, and gibberellins, while suppressing the accumulation of abscisic acid (ABA). 55 During the period immediately surrounding germination, indole-3-acetic acid and gibberellins increased rapidly, abscisic acid declined markedly, and zeatin riboside reached its maximum concentration at the onset of germination. As a result, the GA/ABA, IAA/ABA, and ZR/ABA ratios were significantly higher in electrically treated seeds than in untreated controls. These shifts were closely associated with enhanced germination vigor and continued to increase following radicle emergence, coinciding with more rapid seedling development. 55 These findings indicate that the effects of electrical stimulation are determined not only by changes in individual hormone concentrations, but also by coordinated changes in the balance between growth-promoting and growth-inhibiting hormones. In particular, the decline in ABA relative to GA, IAA, and ZR appears to create a hormonal environment that favors dormancy release, radicle protrusion, and early seedling growth. 55 However, hormonal responses to electrical treatment are not uniform across species or developmental stages. Marček et al. reported that high-voltage pulse discharges applied to wheat seeds initially increased ABA and reduced bioactive GA₁ in developing roots. At a later stage, five days after treatment, benzoic acid and salicylic acid accumulated, while the shoots showed increased synthesis of bioactive jasmonate conjugates. 56 This temporal sequence suggests that electrical stimulation may first induce a transient stress-like response and subsequently activate developmental and defense-related signaling pathways. Rather than producing a single, sustained hormonal shift, electrical treatment may therefore regulate successive phases of germination through distinct hormone profiles. Early ABA and GA responses may influence germination and root establishment, whereas later changes in salicylic acid, jasmonates, and related compounds may support seedling acclimation and defense. 19 , 56 Electrical stimulation may also affect the transition from vegetative to reproductive development. Lee et al. exposed Arabidopsis thaliana to vertical or horizontal electric fields of 5 kV m−1 and observed increased leaf production, greater biomass accumulation, and earlier flowering relative to untreated plants. The electric-field treatments also increased auxin concentrations in leaves, suggesting that altered auxin status may contribute to both enhanced shoot growth and accelerated reproductive development. 34 Evidence from callus culture, seed germination, seedling establishment, and flowering demonstrates that electrical stimulation acts through a network of interacting hormonal pathways rather than through a single regulator. Its effects may involve changes in hormone biosynthesis, degradation, transport, conjugation, and tissue-specific distribution. The resulting developmental response depends on the form and intensity of stimulation, exposure duration, plant species, tissue type, and developmental stage. 19 , 34

Future research should therefore combine precise characterization of electrical parameters with time-resolved hormone profiling. 12 , 19 Such an approach would help determine whether observed hormonal changes represent direct responses to electrical signals, secondary stress reactions, or downstream consequences of altered growth. Integrating electrical treatments with hormone transport, signaling, and metabolic analyzes will be essential for clarifying how electrical stimulation coordinates development, metabolism, and stress adaptation across the plant life cycle . 12 , 19

Applications of electrical stimulation from seed priming to field production

Seed electro-priming is a simple and economical physical method for enhancing germination, seedling establishment, and stress tolerance. 11 , 12 Electric-field treatments have been shown to accelerate tomato seed germination and improve maize germination and seedling thermotolerance. 32 , 53 Electrical seed treatments encompass several distinct stimulation modes, including continuous electrostatic-field exposure, low-voltage electrical stimulation, and high-voltage pulsed treatments. 11 , 12 , 14 These approaches differ not only in electrical intensity but also in waveform, exposure duration, and membrane-level mechanisms. These approaches may operate through different mechanisms, ranging from electroporation-induced changes in membrane permeability to moderate modification of membrane potential, and therefore differ in effectiveness and potential risk. 14 , 22 , 23 High-voltage pulsed electric fields can rapidly alter seed physiology by increasing membrane permeability and activating hydrolytic processes. 14 , 57 In wheat seeds, Ahmed et al. applied pulsed electric fields of 2, 4, and 6 kV cm−1 at 1 Hz, using a pulse width of 100 μs and 25 or 50 pulses. The strongest response was observed at 6 kV cm−1 with 50 pulses, which increased water uptake, seed germination, and seedling growth relative to the untreated control. 28 High-voltage electrostatic treatment has been shown to improve ionic balance and osmotic regulation in sheepgrass seedlings exposed to saline–alkali stress. 39 Similar seed pretreatments have enhanced drought tolerance in oil sunflower, Caragana korshinskii, and buckwheat, reduced imbibitional chilling injury in soybean, and improved cold tolerance in alfalfa. 42 , 58-61 Taken together, these findings support the potential of both pulsed and electrostatic seed pretreatments under adverse environmental conditions. 53 , 58 However, because excessive field strength or pulse dose may damage cellular membranes or induce inconsistent physiological responses, treatment-specific efficacy and safety thresholds must be established. 12 , 14 , 28 , 53 By comparison, low-voltage electrical stimulation generally produces a milder disturbance of membrane potential and cellular metabolism. 11 , 22 Such stimulation can promote water uptake, activate enzymes, and improve germination without causing obvious membrane injury. 28 , 52 , 53 , 57 It may also increase enzyme activity and soluble protein content, thereby enhancing seed vigor. 22 , 52 For example, electric-field pretreatment of cucumber seeds at 5 kV cm−1 for 3 min or 3 kV cm−1 for 5 min enhanced germination in the low-vigor seed lot and increased antioxidant-enzyme activities, while reducing lipid peroxidation. However, the treatment did not significantly improve subsequent seedling growth. 52 In a separate soil-based experiment, Morales et al. applied a low-intensity electric field of 0.2 V cm−1 for 4 h and increased cucumber germination from 30% to 50%. The treatment also increased biomass by 53%, true-leaf size by 49%, stem elongation by 20%, stem thickness by 6%, and cotyledon length by 5% relative to the non-electrified treatment. 57 Low-voltage stimulation may therefore provide a safer and more controllable approach for improving germination and early-stage stress tolerance. 11 , 12 , 57 Nevertheless, its effectiveness depends on crop species, seed physiological condition, current intensity, treatment duration, and environmental conditions. Standardized screening of treatment parameters is therefore essential before practical application.

Electrical stimulation can also regulate growth and differentiation in plant tissue culture. Minute electric currents on the order of 1 μA increased tobacco callus growth by approximately 70% when the callus was made negative, whereas current in the opposite direction was slightly inhibitory. 26 In a related study, currents of 1–2 μA stimulated shoot differentiation in tobacco callus by up to fivefold. 27 Rathore et al. applied weak currents to callus cultures through agar bridges and silver/silver chloride electrodes to minimize contamination by electrode-reaction products. Their results indicated that the growth response was primarily attributable to the applied electric field rather than to chemical artefacts released from the electrodes. 62 Using a pulse-current stimulator, Wang et al. tested weak pulsed currents of 0.43–4.00 μA for 5, 10, or 20 d in cabbage tissue cultures. Currents of 0.67–2.60 μA promoted shoot differentiation and fresh-mass accumulation, with 1.30 μA applied for 10 d producing the strongest response. Effects were weak below 0.67 μA and inhibitory above 2.60 μA. The pulsed-current configuration also reduced the influence of electrical polarization. 63 The contrasting polarity dependence observed between tobacco and cabbage cultures suggests that the influence of current direction may be species- and culture-system-dependent rather than universal. 26 , 63 High-voltage electrostatic fields have also been reported to increase callus induction and fresh mass in alfalfa leaf cultures. 64 , 65 Collectively, these studies suggest that electrical stimulation can improve regeneration efficiency in vitro, although optimum conditions vary with plant species, explant types, culture systems, and developmental stages.

At the whole-plant level, electrical stimulation has been associated with improvements in growth, yield, and product quality. 11 , 12 In situ self-induced rhizosphere electrical stimulation under low-current conditions promoted vegetative growth, increased root and shoot biomass, and accelerated flowering and pod development in mung bean and chickpea. Under the closed-circuit configuration, plant maturation occurred approximately 10 d earlier than in the control. 15 In potato, electrical treatment was reported to increase tuber number and crop yield under some treatment conditions, although the magnitude of the response varied with the electrical treatment regime. 66 , 67 In hydroponically grown lettuce, low-frequency alternating electric fields increased plant biomass. 68 In plant-factory lettuce, electric-field treatment increased fresh mass and reduced tipburn incidence, thereby improving marketable quality. 33 Comparable responses have also been reported in fruit crops and medicinal plants. In grapevine, electrical stimulation increased soluble solids content and enhanced anthocyanin and resveratrol accumulation, while berry weight was not significantly altered. 51 In Scutellaria baicalensis, electrical stimulation promoted root growth and biomass accumulation and enhanced the accumulation of medicinal flavonoids, including baicalein and wogonin. 43 , 44 These findings suggest that electrical stimulation may enhance not only biomass production but also crop quality, giving it potential value in protected agriculture, high-value horticulture, and medicinal plant cultivation.

The application of electrical stimulation can be extended further to crop populations and field-scale production systems. 11 , 12 Spatial electrostatic-field systems in greenhouses and open fields may affect crop performance directly and may also interact with environmental conditions such as humidity and airflow, while influencing aerosol or particle movement. 69 , 70 However, field responses are not uniformly positive. Soja et al. reported that winter wheat grown nearer to a 380-kV transmission line had slightly lower grain yield than wheat grown at the lowest field exposure, whereas no significant yield differences were detected in maize. 71 These results indicate that long-term exposure to environmental electric and magnetic fields may affect crops, but the magnitude and direction of the response can vary among species and environmental conditions.

In contrast, controllable spatial electric-field systems have shown promise in protected vegetable production. In controlled plant-factory production, Tong et al. applied a 15-kV electric-field system continuously throughout the day to lettuce. Electrical treatment delayed the onset of tipburn from day 15 to day 18, reduced the proportion of tipburn-affected leaves by 61.8%, increased calcium concentration in newly developed leaves by 1.3-fold, and increased fresh and dry biomass by 19.8% and 22.2%, respectively, by day 21 after transplanting. 33 These results demonstrate that controlled electric-field exposure can improve growth and alleviate a calcium-related physiological disorder under controlled-environment conditions, although such responses should not be generalized to field production without further validation. 33 These apparently contrasting outcomes should not be interpreted as evidence that electric fields are intrinsically beneficial or detrimental. Rather, the studies were conducted under markedly different exposure contexts. Crops grown near transmission lines experienced long-term, uncontrolled environmental exposure, whereas greenhouse studies used deliberately configured and controllable electric-field systems. 33 , 71 Differences in field intensity, spatial distribution, exposure duration, and environmental conditions may therefore contribute to the contrasting biological responses. 11 , 12 , 69 However, direct cross-study comparison remains difficult because electrical parameters and experimental conditions have not been reported consistently. 11 , 12 , 69 Evidence for open-field agronomic benefits remains comparatively limited and heterogeneous, and many available studies lack complete reporting of electrical parameters or multi-season validation.

In field-grown grapevines, Mori et al. applied electrical stimulation using electrodes connected to a solar panel with a maximum voltage of 11.6 V (±5%) and a maximum current of 100 mA (±5%). Electrical stimulation significantly reduced disease incidence across two growing seasons. For bunch gray mold and/or ripe rot, disease incidence decreased from 69.7% in the control to 44.9% under electrical stimulation in 2016, and from 90.4% to 67.3% in 2020. Electrical stimulation also significantly reduced the incidence of grape downy mildew on leaves. 41 These findings indicate that electrical stimulation may enhance disease resistance under field conditions, although its effects on fruit yield and quality require further validation. 41

Together, these observations indicate that electrical stimulation has potential beyond laboratory and controlled-environment applications. 11 , 12 , 69 Its effects may arise from both direct regulation of plant physiological processes and indirect modification of the crop microenvironment and disease pressure. 12 , 41 , 69 Field performance, however, is likely to be more variable because it is influenced by soil properties, climatic conditions, crop management, electrode design, and treatment stability. 12 , 69 Despite numerous reports of beneficial responses, the current evidence base remains methodologically heterogeneous. Many studies differ substantially in plant material, cultivation system, electrode configuration, electrical waveform, exposure duration, replication, and measured endpoints. 11 , 12 , 69 , 72 In addition, some reported responses are derived from small-scale laboratory or greenhouse experiments, whereas relatively few have been validated across multiple seasons or locations. Consequently, large percentage changes observed under individual experimental conditions should not necessarily be interpreted as broadly transferable agronomic effects. 69 , 72 Future studies should report complete electrical parameters, biological replication, statistical significance, and environmental conditions to enable meaningful cross-study comparison and future quantitative synthesis. 69 , 72 Future research should therefore focus on developing low-cost, energy-efficient, and field-compatible equipment, together with crop-specific and region-specific treatment protocols. 12 , 69 Long-term field experiments are also required to assess agronomic effectiveness, operational safety, energy demand, and possible ecological impacts before electrical stimulation can be adopted on a large scale (Figure 2).

Effect pyramid of electrical stimulation: from membrane-level modes to physiological and agronomic outcomes. At the membrane level, two distinct modes are shown: high-voltage pulsed electric fields (HV-PEF) induce electroporation/membrane permeabilization, leading to rapid water uptake, hydrolase activation, and ion flux; low-voltage mild electric fields modulate transmembrane potential (TMP), leading to ion channel/transporter regulation and metabolic modulation. These membrane-level cues converge on hormonal and gene expression modulation, antioxidant defense, osmotic adjustment, and ion homeostasis, thereby conferring drought and salt tolerance and chilling tolerance. Ultimately, these responses lead to improved agronomic outcomes, including increased germination rate, seedling biomass, fruit enlargement/filling, yield, and enhanced accumulation of anthocyanins, resveratrol, and flavonoids, as reported in crops such as sorghum, tomato, maize, and grapevine. Root-specific responses to electrical stimulation

Roots form the principal interface between plants and the soil environment and are therefore highly sensitive to electrical signals. 11 , 12 , 69 Experimental evidence indicates that electrical fields do not affect all root tissues uniformly. In squash roots exposed to 60-Hz alternating electric fields, the degree of growth inhibition differed among regions of the root apex, likely because induced transmembrane potentials varied with cell size and position within the elongation zone. 38 Microscopic observations also showed changes in cell morphology and cell-division-related processes, suggesting that electrical stimulation can directly influence both cell division and elongation. 73-75 Electrical fields may also alter the direction of root growth. Under direct-current conditions, Arabidopsis root tips display electrotropic responses, in which the growth axis bends in relation to the applied field. 76 Such directional behavior indicates that roots can perceive and respond spatially to electrical cues. 76 , 77 By modifying root elongation, branching, and orientation, electrical stimulation may alter root-system architecture and could potentially influence root exploration of soil water and nutrient resources. 75 , 76 , 78

Beyond effects on root morphology, electrical stimulation can influence the chemical conditions surrounding roots. Weak electrical currents applied to the root zone have been reported to modify local electrochemical conditions, including pH and ion distributions. 79 , 80 In plant-factory lettuce production, electric-field treatment has been associated with reduced tipburn and increased Ca2+ concentration in newly developed leaves. 33 Electrical pretreatment may also enhance ion selectivity under stress. In sheepgrass, the increased salt tolerance induced by high-voltage electrostatic seed treatment was accompanied by greater selective uptake of K+ by roots and more efficient transport to aboveground tissues. This response would help maintain K+/Na+ balance and reduce ionic toxicity under saline conditions. 39 Overall, roots appear to act as a key physiological gateway through which electrical stimulation is converted into changes in growth, nutrient acquisition, ion homeostasis, and stress tolerance. 15 , 31 , 81 However, these responses depend strongly on field type, current intensity, exposure duration, root developmental stage, and surrounding soil or nutrient-solution conditions. 11 , 12 , 15 , 31 Further research is needed to clarify the sensing mechanisms involved and to determine how electrical treatments can be used reliably to optimize root architecture and nutrient uptake.

Beyond their role in nutrient regulation, roots function as a central interface for plant–soil–microbe interactions, and this complex network may also respond to electrical stimulation. 82-84 By modifying root physiological activity and potentially altering the quantity or composition of root exudates, electrical treatments may reshape rhizosphere microbial communities. 82-84 Qu et al. reported that high-voltage pulsed electric-field treatment of buckwheat seeds changed the structure of endophytic bacterial communities. 85 Similarly, Hu et al. found that a low-voltage direct-current field altered rhizosphere microbial community structure and enhanced the phytoremediation performance of Macleaya cordata in uranium- and cadmium-contaminated soil. 82 At a broader ecological and engineering scale, root-associated electron transfer has also been examined in sediment microbial fuel cells. Although these systems do not directly represent plant electrostimulation, they illustrate the broader bioelectrochemical role of roots in mediating electron exchange with their surrounding environment. 83 , 84 Collectively, these findings indicate that roots are not simply passive recipients of electrical stimuli. Rather, they act as an active regulatory interface through which electrical signals can affect root development, nutrient acquisition, microbial associations, and plant adaptation to environmental stress 12 , 82-84 (Figure 3).

Root responses to electrical stimulation. Electrical stimulation of roots regulates multiple physiological, developmental, and ecological processes: (1) direct regulation of cell division and elongation, including changes in mitotic index and elongating cells; (2) environment/ion-dependent regulation, including K+/Ca2+-mediated ion homeostasis, tipburn alleviation, and elevated ST value; (3) ecological interaction networks, including shifts in endophytic communities; (4) rhizosphere microenvironment, including changes in redox status (Eh) and pH (H+/OH-); (5) sediment microbial fuel cell (SMFC) and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) associations; and (6) anode/cathode-directed electrotropic growth under microcurrent.

Biological effects of electrically induced negative air ion release from plants

High-voltage pulsed electric fields applied to the rhizosphere have been reported to stimulate the release of negative air ions (NAIs) from plants. 29 , 86 NAIs are generally defined as negatively charged molecules or molecular clusters suspended in air and may include oxygen-containing species such as superoxide anion radicals (O₂−). 86 , 87 They have attracted interest because of their potential to promote the deposition of airborne particulate matter, reduce some volatile organic compounds, and suppress certain airborne microorganisms. 87-89 However, their effects on human health remain dependent on exposure conditions and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. 87 , 88 Wu et al. found that, under natural conditions, plants emitted NAIs at relatively low background concentrations of approximately 19–43 ions cm−3. When pulsed electric fields were applied to the rhizosphere soil, NAI emission increased substantially. The strongest response occurred at a pulse voltage of 2.0 × 104 V, a frequency of 1 Hz, and a pulse width of 50 ms, indicating that NAI release is highly sensitive to electrical treatment parameters. 29

Tikhonov et al. classified plant species into three categories according to their capacity to emit NAIs. Low-emitting species, including Crassula portulacea and Echinopsis tubiflora, increased background NAI concentrations by approximately two-fold to threefold. Medium-emitting species, such as Haworthia rasalata, Opuntia brunnescens, and Mammillaria prolifera, produced increases on the order of hundreds of times above background levels. High-emitting species, including Aloe arborescens and Chlorophytum comosum, generated increases of up to several thousand-fold. Among these species examined, Aloe arborescens showed a relatively stable and sustained NAI-emission capacity. It maintained concentrations of approximately 125 × 103 ions cm−3 for 7–8 h, with emissions occurring primarily from the leaf tips. The magnitude and persistence of NAI release were influenced by plant species, developmental stage, ambient temperature, and weather conditions. Notably, exposure to temperatures below 8.5 °C caused a reversible loss of NAI-emitting capacity. Further analysis by Tikhonov et al. suggested that superoxide anion radicals are among the major reactive species involved in electrically induced NAI release from plants. 86 These observations suggest that electrical stimulation may regulate not only plant physiological processes but also the ionic properties of the surrounding air. 86-88 Nevertheless, the mechanisms governing NAI production, the stability of emitted ions, and their broader ecological and health implications require further investigation. 87 , 90

At the mechanistic level, electrically induced NAI emission appears to be closely linked to leaf structural and physiological characteristics, particularly stomatal aperture and stomatal density. NAI-generation capacity was positively associated with stomatal characteristics, with greater stomatal opening and higher stomatal density generally corresponding to stronger NAI release. 91-93 Pulsed electric fields may also modify photosynthetic and redox processes, thereby affecting the production and emission of reactive oxygen species and other charged particles. 88 , 92 From an engineering perspective, controlled electrical stimulation systems have been explored for regulating plant NAI emission. 88 These systems can generate high concentrations of plant-derived NAIs and have potential applications in indoor particulate-matter removal. 88 Overall, electrical stimulation-induced NAI release broadens the potential application of plant electrical stimulation from growth regulation to indoor air-quality management. However, practical adoption will require a clearer understanding of the underlying biological and physicochemical mechanisms, reliable quantification of purification performance under realistic indoor conditions, and comprehensive evaluation of operational safety, energy demand, and long-term effects on both plants and human occupants 87 , 88 , 90 (Figure 4).

Multifaceted plant bioelectrical phenomenon, i.e., negative air ion (NAI) release triggered by rhizosphere high-voltage pulsed electric fields. Electrically induced NAI generation primarily occurs at the leaf tips by means of the superoxide anion radical emission process. This emission is closely associated with increases in stomatal aperture and density and thereby promotes overall negative ion flux. It can also serve as a functional air-purifying interface which participates in removing airborne particulate matter and reducing volatile organic compounds. This process thereby extends the application scope of electrical stimulation from plant growth regulation to indoor air quality management.

Future perspectives

Electrical stimulation is emerging as a physical approach for regulating crop growth, rhizosphere conditions, and plant metabolic processes through the controlled application of electric fields or currents. Its potential benefits include increasing crop yield and quality, strengthening tolerance to environmental stress, and reducing dependence on fertilizers and pesticides. Because it is controllable and leaves no chemical residues, electrical stimulation may be particularly valuable in protected cultivation, soilless production systems, and stress-prone agricultural environments that require precise management.

Despite this promise, several barriers continue to limit its agricultural application. First, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Further research is needed to determine how electrical signals are perceived at the plasma membrane, transmitted through intracellular signaling networks, and integrated with phytohormonal pathways. Second, standardized treatment protocols are lacking. Responses vary widely with crop species, developmental stage, tissue type, cultivation system, and environmental conditions, and the absence of comprehensive parameter databases means that many treatments are still selected through empirical trial and error. Third, limitations in materials and equipment constrain practical deployment. Conductive materials may be costly, insufficiently durable, or poorly suited to long-term agricultural use, while many existing devices lack the sensing and control functions required for large-scale adaptive management. Finally, a substantial gap remains between laboratory observations and field implementation. High-voltage treatments, in particular, may involve safety hazards, inconsistent biological responses, and potential ecological effects that must be carefully evaluated before wider use.

Future research should therefore prioritize mechanistically informed, crop-specific, and environmentally responsive stimulation strategies. Mild continuous or low-frequency stimulation deserves particular attention for sustained regulation because it can modulate membrane potential and ion transport without intentionally inducing membrane permeabilization. High-voltage pulsed treatments should be evaluated separately because their effects depend on pulse-specific dose and may range from reversible permeabilization to tissue injury. Static high-voltage electrostatic fields likewise require separate evaluation because their waveform and membrane-level effects differ from pulsed treatments. Mild continuous or low-frequency stimulation may regulate plasma membrane H+-ATPase activity, Ca2+ signaling, antioxidant defense, osmotic adjustment, and hormone-related pathways, thereby coordinating growth promotion with improved stress tolerance. Nevertheless, beneficial responses depend strongly on voltage or current intensity, field strength, frequency, waveform, treatment duration, crop species, and developmental stage. Integrating multi-omics analyzes, electrophysiological measurements, and real-time phenotyping will be essential for clarifying how electrical inputs are converted into molecular and physiological responses.

Technological development will be equally important. Practical systems should be stable, affordable, energy-efficient, and capable of operating under variable agricultural conditions. Platforms designed for mild continuous or low-frequency stimulation may offer advantages in controllability, operational safety, and compatibility with renewable energy, making them promising for long-term use in greenhouses, plant factories, hydroponic systems, and field environments. Future devices should combine adjustable electrical parameters with environmental sensors, plant-growth monitoring, and feedback-control algorithms so that treatments can be modified in response to changing crop and environmental conditions. High-voltage pulsed technologies may remain valuable for specific short-duration applications, including seed priming and stress induction, but their use requires stricter assessment of energy demand, safety limits, membrane damage, and ecological consequences. Static electrostatic-field systems should be assessed separately according to field configuration, exposure regime, and application context.

Translation into agricultural practice will require long-term, multi-site field trials. Such studies should assess not only crop yield and quality but also energy consumption, carbon footprint, nutrient-use efficiency, soil microbial communities, nutrient cycling, secondary metabolism, and potential ecological risks. Standardized performance and safety indicators will be needed to compare stimulation modes and identify the most appropriate applications. Collaboration among plant scientists, engineers, equipment manufacturers, agricultural enterprises, and growers will also be critical for developing practical protocols, lowering costs, and improving user acceptance. Demonstration projects involving protected vegetables, medicinal plants, seed treatment, and crops cultivated in salinity-, drought-, or cold-affected regions could provide valuable evidence for future scaling.

In summary, electrical stimulation should be regarded not as a universal replacement for existing agronomic practices but as a complementary component of integrated crop management. It may be combined with nutrient management, microbial inoculation, environmental control, and precision cultivation technologies. Mild continuous or low-frequency stimulation appears particularly promising for sustained and adaptive regulation because of its relatively mild biological effects, operational safety, and compatibility with intelligent control systems. High-voltage pulsed treatments may be better suited to targeted, short-duration applications, whereas static electrostatic-field systems should be evaluated separately according to field configuration and exposure regime. As advances in plant electrophysiology, bioelectronic engineering, sensor technology, and intelligent agricultural equipment continue, electrical stimulation may offer new opportunities to enhance crop productivity, quality, and resilience while reducing chemical inputs and associated environmental risks.
