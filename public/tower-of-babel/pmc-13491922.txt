Subsidence Mechanisms and Exploration and Development in Deepwater Gas Reservoirs: Advances and Perspectives
Authors: Zirui Zhang, Weijun Shen, Jinlin Zhang, Xu Yang, Hongchuan Chen, Rupei Pang, Ning Li, Xinyi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Deepwater gas has emerged as a critical domain for global natural gas reserve growth, production enhancement, and supply replacement; yet its long-term development is constrained by production-induced compaction and subsidence. Reservoir compaction, overburden deformation, and seabed subsidence induced by deepwater gas production constitute a complex, coupled, multiphysics geomechanical problem involving three fundamental mechanical issues: identifying the dominant mechanisms governing time-dependent reservoir deformation, establishing constitutive frameworks for compaction and subsidence with clear applicability boundaries, and developing coupled, multiphysics geomechanical numerical methods for complex deepwater stratigraphic settings. This paper provides an integrated overview of the distribution patterns of deepwater gas reservoirs and the progress in exploration and development worldwide and in China. Specifically, the latest progress is reviewed and discussed in terms of five aspects: compaction and subsidence mechanisms in deepwater gas reservoirs, subsidence monitoring systems for deepwater environments, key controlling and influencing factors of production-induced subsidence, characteristics of constitutive models for compaction and subsidence, and coupled multiphysics numerical approaches. By linking these topics to engineering practice, this review aims to provide a systematic reference for subsidence risk identification, monitoring scheme design, and predictive decision-making in deepwater gas fields.

Introduction

In recent years, the global focus of offshore hydrocarbon exploration and development has shifted from shallow water to deepwater and ultradeepwater settings. Deepwater provinces have become major frontiers for reserve additions and important successors to the global oil and gas supply. , In parallel, under China’s “dual-carbon” targets and the ongoing energy transition, natural gas demand has continued to grow steadily. Although domestic supply capacity has increased, import dependence remains high. Under this context, the South China Sea, which is currently the only offshore region in China with conditions suitable for deepwater exploration and development, has gained prominence in terms of both resource endowment and development significance. The South China Sea is estimated to host approximately one-third of China’s offshore oil and gas resources. Approximately 70% of these resources occur in waters deeper than 300 m, covering approximately 1.537 million km2. However, only approximately 160,000 km2 has been explored to date, highlighting the mismatch between a substantial resource base and limited exploration maturity. Moreover, deepwater development is closely linked to China’s strategic interests related to maritime rights and jurisdiction. In August 2024, China National Offshore Oil Corporation (CNOOC) announced the discovery of the Lingshui 36–1 gas field in the Qiongdongnan Basin, with proven gas resources exceeding 100 billion m3. This discovery represents a major step toward establishing trillion-cubic-meter-class gas provinces in the South China Sea, further underscoring the strategic role of deepwater gas as a successor supply source and its medium- to long-term development potential.

Despite the strong potential of deepwater gas resources, exploration and production remain constrained by technical complexity, operational risk, and high costs. Geophysical uncertainty persists because deepwater seismic imaging remains limited, and the interpretation of bright spots can be ambiguous. Engineering operations must also withstand harsh marine conditions and complex seabed geohazards. − High rig and service costs further intensify economic and safety pressures. Although deepwater South China Sea capabilities have improved markedly, long-term depletion-induced compaction and seafloor subsidence may progressively threaten subsea infrastructure. During large-scale development, pore pressure depletion increases the effective stress, driving irreversible reservoir compaction. This deformation propagates through the overburden and results in seafloor subsidence. Deep-water development is particularly sensitive to this process. Spatially nonuniform subsidence can compromise the integrity of subsea wellheads and trees, increase bending and free-span risks for pipelines and umbilicals, and pose seabed stability issues. These effects are most pronounced in areas with steep-slope relief and weak surficial sediments, where localized deformation can be amplified. This is particularly important because engineering hazards are governed more by differential settlement, curvature, and shear deformation than by subsidence magnitude alone. Such an understanding supports risk identification, monitoring design, and development decision-making. , Moreover, because subsidence usually has a delayed onset, evolving rates, and persistence after shut-in, a geomechanics-based understanding of compaction and subsidence processes is essential.

Although compaction and subsidence have been investigated in subsiding fields, their transferability to deepwater gas reservoirs remains insufficiently clarified. The global cases reviewed in this paper, including offshore Brazil, the northern Gulf of Mexico, and offshore East Africa, indicate that deepwater gas reservoirs differ substantially in stratigraphic architecture, reservoir lithology, pressure-depletion patterns, development modes, and infrastructure exposure. Existing studies have provided valuable insights into depletion-induced compaction, delayed deformation, and field-scale subsidence prediction; however, they are often rooted in specific field conditions or particular deformation mechanisms, and a systematic synthesis is still lacking regarding the applicability of subsidence mechanisms, constitutive models, and prediction approaches to different deepwater reservoir types and development settings. In China, research on the subsidence mechanisms associated with deep-water gas development remains limited. Specifically, existing studies remain insufficient in three areas: (i) identifying the dominant mechanisms governing time-dependent reservoir deformation, (ii) establishing constitutive frameworks for compaction and subsidence with clear applicability boundaries, and (iii) developing coupled multiphysics analytical and predictive methods suitable for complex deep-water stratigraphic settings. These gaps highlight the need for a systematic synthesis of current knowledge. Rather than treating subsidence as a simple geometric consequence of reservoir depletion, this Review frames it as a development-constrained geomechanical process, with emphasis on delayed compaction mechanisms and engineering-sensitive responses such as differential settlement, curvature, and shear deformation. Accordingly, this Review synthesizes current progress from an integrated and problem-oriented perspective, with the aim of providing a more robust basis for long-term deepwater field development and risk assessment.

Deepwater Gas Reservoir Exploration and Development

Distribution Characteristics of Deepwater Gas Reservoirs

Global Distribution Patterns

Guided by plate-tectonic theory and more than a decade of exploration practice, the distribution of global deepwater basins is characterized by “three in longitudes and two in latitudes”, as shown in Figure . Specifically, “three in longitudes” represents the Atlantic, East African, and western Pacific continental margins. “Two in latitudes” corresponds to the Neo-Tethys tectonic domain and the circum-Arctic continental margin. These belts differ markedly in terms of their tectonic setting and resource endowment. The Atlantic margin and circum-Arctic belts are both passive-margin systems, but they differ in resource type, exploration maturity, and operating conditions. The Atlantic belt is primarily oil-prone, whereas the circum-Arctic belt has substantial hydrocarbon potential but remains constrained by severe geographic and climatic conditions. The East African margin belt reflects a transform-margin setting and represents a gas-prone deepwater province. The western Pacific margin belt is associated with subduction-related active margins, with deepwater discoveries concentrated mainly in the South China Sea and Southeast Asia. The Neo-Tethys belt corresponds to a subduction–collision domain and includes gas-enriched deepwater basins, such as the Nile Delta Basin. Overall, the “three longitudinal belts and two latitudinal belts” framework indicates that global deepwater gas enrichment is strongly controlled by tectonic setting and basin evolution, with the East African margin, the western Pacific margin, and the Neo-Tethys domain forming major gas-prone deepwater provinces.

Distribution Characteristics of the Northern South China Sea

The South China Sea is currently China’s only offshore region with well-developed deepwater basins. The area within the nine-dash line exceeds 2 million km2, of which approximately 1.5 million km2 is deepwater with water depths greater than 300 m. Using 17°05′N as a boundary, the South China Sea can be subdivided into northern and central–southern sectors. Across these sectors, 12 major basins have been recognized, as shown in Figure , with a total basin area of approximately 750,000 km2. The deepwater area in the northern South China Sea is approximately 200,000 km2, with water depths of 300–3000 m. This area includes multiple Cenozoic depressions with a combined area of approximately 90,000 km2 and represents a strategic successor region for China’s offshore hydrocarbon supply. By 2020, ten medium and large gas fields had been discovered in the deepwater northern South China Sea, with cumulative third-order geological gas reserves of approximately 700 billion m3. , Several deepwater gas fields are located on the continental slope and its toe, where the seabed topography is complex. Therefore, clarifying the mechanisms of development-induced subsidence requires a robust understanding of regional distribution, basin configuration, and geological conditions.

Deepwater Gas Reservoir Exploration and Development in China

In recent years, deep-water gas exploration in China has progressed steadily. An improved understanding of hydrocarbon accumulation models and iterative advances in key exploration technologies have laid a solid foundation for discovering and developing medium- and large-gas fields. A systematic synthesis of geological insights, technological progress, and development history can optimize exploration strategies and target selection, and provide a well-defined geological and engineering context for investigating subsidence mechanisms.

Geological Understanding

Over the course of deepwater natural gas exploration in China, the understanding of hydrocarbon accumulation models has evolved in stages. The focus shifted from shallow-water settings to deepwater domains and, more recently, expanded to deeper intervals and ultradeepwater, ultrashallow targets. During the initial stage (1987–2009), efforts focused on adopting overseas theories and technologies, and exploration was conducted mainly in the shallow-water Qiongdongnan Basin. This stage emphasized anticlinal traps as primary targets. It also established a working model in which coal-bearing strata in transitional marine–continental facies formed the principal source kitchens, and large drape anticlines were the dominant structural trap type. In the breakthrough stage (2010–2015), theoretical and technological advances targeting deepwater and middle-to-shallow intervals shifted exploration to the deepwater Central Depression. This shift generated key geological insights and enabled the discovery of the Central Canyon gas-field cluster, including medium-to-large fields such as Lingshui 17–2, Lingshui 25–1, and Lingshui 18–1. In the expansion stage (2016–2024), exploration extended to deeper deepwater strata and to ultradeepwater, ultrashallow intervals. From 2016 to 2019, an appraisal of deeper deepwater targets supported a long-distance, lateral migration-controlled accumulation model. However, field sizes were generally modest, and outcomes remained limited. From 2020 to 2022, a fault–terrace-controlled trapping concept for transform settings was proposed, which underpinned the discovery of China’s first large deepwater, deep-interval gas field (Baodao 21–1). Between 2020 and 2024, ultrashallow targetspreviously widely regarded as “hazard gas” intervalswere reevaluated as viable exploration targets, guiding the discovery of the large Lingshui 36–1 gas field. In addition, a cluster of lithologic traps within large submarine fan systems sourced from the western provenance has been recognized in ultradeepwater areas. Overall, iterative advances in geological understanding and exploration practices have been mutually reinforcing. They have driven successive discoveries and provided a basis for synthesizing regional accumulation models.

With the progressive refinement of geological understanding, representative gas accumulation models have been successively distilled from key medium-to-large fields. Taking the Yacheng 13–1 gas field as an example, a model has been established featuring efficient hydrocarbon generation and expulsion from coal-bearing source rocks, three-dimensional transport of large sand bodies through carrier faults, long-term accumulation in low-potential zones within large deltaic composite traps, and sealing by thick layers of overpressured mudstone. For the Baodao 21–1 gas field, an enrichment model has been proposed that is characterized by dual land–sea sources of organic matter controlling source kitchens, transform-slope control of trap formation, strongly active fault-controlled transport, and weakly active fault-controlled accumulation. For the Lingshui 25–1W gas field, a gas accumulation model has been developed, highlighting convergence along major intrasource structural ridges, migration and transport through canyons/gully-sourced faults/fracture zones and sandstone carrier beds, and enrichment constrained by effective traps. , For the Central Canyon fields (e.g., Lingshui 17–2 and Lingshui 25–1), a central canyon-type model has been established, emphasizing deep overpressure drive, vertical migration along fractures coupled with lateral transport through sand bodies, multiple independent reservoirs, and multilayer composite accumulations. For the recently discovered Lingshui 36–1 gas field, a model involving bidirectional hydrocarbon supply from dual sources at different depths, sealing by water column/mudstone/gas hydrates, and ridge-focused enrichment has been proposed.

China’s understanding of deepwater natural gas accumulation has continued to mature, evolving from shallow-water anticlinal traps to deepwater central-canyon systems. More recently, it has expanded to include (i) deep-interval plays controlled by transform-related fault steps and (ii) ultradeepwater/ultrashallow sealing systems. Along this trajectory, the corresponding accumulation models have been progressively developed and refined. This evolving geological framework has guided the prioritization of deepwater exploration targets, enabled the discovery of medium-to-large gas fields, and supported sustained exploration progress. It has also underpinned development candidate screening and the design of development plans for deepwater gas reservoirs.

Exploration Technologies

Deepwater exploration in the South China Sea faces three major geophysical challenges. First, acquisition designs developed for shallow-shelf settings and shallow-to-intermediate intervals are not readily transferable to mid-to-deep targets on deepwater slopes. Second, pronounced reservoir anisotropy under structural and lithologic complexity makes reliable reservoir prediction difficult. Third, seismic “bright spots” may have multiple origins, complicating the identification of laterally extensive gas-bearing intervals and robust estimation of gas saturation. To address these issues, seismic technologies have been continuously optimized across acquisition, processing, and interpretation. On the acquisition side, recent advances, such as broadband long-offset surveys and compressed sensing-enabled high-efficiency acquisition, have improved survey design and parameter selection, strengthened weak deep reflections, and enhanced overall data quality. On the processing side, other developments, such as τ–p-domain adaptive deghosting, hybrid multiple-suppression workflows, and broadband processing, have improved resolution and imaging fidelity. For interpretation, a high-accuracy fluid identification method based on the fluid bulk modulus has been proposed to improve fluid property characterization under complex conditions. In addition, a “dual-sweet-spot” strategy based on prestack elastic parameter inversion has been developed to better identify high-quality reservoirs. Overall, these advances have improved imaging quality and reservoir fluid evaluation in complex deepwater settings. However, determining the origins of bright spots and achieving robust gas saturation discrimination still require further refinement.

Development Modes

China’s deepwater gas development began relatively late. However, national key research and development programs and sustained engineering practices have driven substantial progress. Overall, development has progressed through stagesfrom international cooperation and learning to independent research and development and subsequent deployment and scaling-up. This trajectory reflects a shift from coordinated deep-shallow development to independently operated deepwater projects and, more recently, expansion into increasingly extreme operating conditions.

Liwan 3–1 is China’s first deepwater–oil and gas field is located at a water depth of approximately 1500 m. During this stage, offshore facilities were built in collaboration with international companies, and initial technology integration was achieved, for example, in deepwater manifold installation. This practice established a deepwater–shallow-water–onshore development concept, laying an engineering foundation for China’s subsequent independent deepwater gas field development. As indigenous development capabilities improved, CNOOC discovered the South China Sea’s first independently operated large deepwater gas fieldthe Lingshui 17–2 gas fieldat water depths of 1252–1547 m. , The development plan combines a subsea production system with a deepwater semisubmersible floating production facility. This plan is complemented by flow-assurance evaluations and hydrate prevention and control strategies, which supported the Phase I integrated development of the Shenhai-1 project as an independently operated deepwater gas field. , Building on this foundation, Phase II of Shenhai-1 targets conditions characterized by relatively clustered subsea wellheads and complex formation temperature–pressure regimes. A drilling and completion technology suite for deepwater high-pressure gas fields was established and refined. , Collectively, these developments show that China’s deepwater drilling and completion capabilities have expanded from conventional “deepwater, shallow-interval” projects to more complex “deepwater, deep-interval” high-temperature/high-pressure (HTHP) gas fields. This expansion enables broader deployment of independently operated deepwater drilling and completion technologies under increasingly stringent operating conditions. As drilling and completion technologies continue to advance, deepwater gas field development is being extended to more complex environments, posing new challenges for the development and implementation of subsidence-mitigation measures.

Under extreme operating conditions, the Lingshui 36–1 gas fieldreported as the world’s first ultradeepwater, ultrashallow gas fieldhas an average water depth >1500 m, whereas the reservoir burial depth is only approximately 150–300 m. This combination imposes stringent requirements on cost control, operational safety, and wellbore flow assurance. Moreover, long-term production issuesparticularly coupled formation subsidence and subsea wellhead stabilityrequire sustained investigation and engineering validation. The successful exploration of Lingshui 36–1 further expands the development envelope for deepwater natural gas resources. Different development concepts can vary substantially in terms of subsea wellhead distribution, pipeline routing, and production system installation methods. These differences may alter the spatiotemporal pattern of reservoir pressure depletion and the associated compaction response, thereby changing the exposure of critical facilities (e.g., wellheads and pipelines) to subsidence-related risks. Therefore, subsidence mechanisms and risk-transfer pathways should be systematically evaluated under specific development scenarios to support the long-term safe development of deepwater gas fields.

Deepwater Gas Reservoir Exploration and Development Worldwide

Deepwater development is concentrated in mature provinces, such as the Gulf of Mexico, offshore eastern Brazil, and offshore West Africa. It is also expanding into emerging frontiers, including northeastern Latin America, offshore East Africa, the Eastern Mediterranean, and the Black Sea. Overall, deepwater gas discoveries are concentrated in Africa, the Eastern Mediterranean, and offshore Asia–Pacific, typically in ultradeepwater settings at approximately 1500–2400 m depth. Among these regions, offshore East Africa has emerged as a key hotspot for deepwater gas discovery and may underpin future growth in global natural gas reserves. ,

Offshore Eastern Brazil

Since the beginning of the 21st century, a series of major presalt discoveries in the deepwater Santos Basin have positioned offshore eastern Brazil as a global hotspot for deepwater hydrocarbon exploration and production. Northern Gulf of Mexico

The deepwater northern Gulf of Mexico (GoM) is among the world’s most active deepwater hydrocarbon provinces for exploration and development. Its geology is highly complex, with ultrathick Cenozoic successions and multiple salt-tectonic systems. A primary constraint is subsalt imaging, which is hindered by strong velocity contrasts within salt bodies and limited penetration of deep energy. Consequently, exploration breakthroughs depend on continued improvement in subsalt imaging and iterative refinement of velocity model building and deep imaging workflows. From a development perspective, the deepwater northern GoM faces persistent challenges, including shallow drilling hazards, drilling through thick salt with abrupt subsalt pressure changes, narrow safe mud-weight windows, and riser dynamics associated with vortex-induced vibration (VIV). Given the widespread occurrence of salt tectonics, recent studies have examined geological evolution associated with salt-sediment interactions. By integrating lithologic and petrophysical logs, pressure measurements, and drilling event records, researchers suggest that within thick salt sections, the principal stresses are largely controlled by salt-related thrust and buoyancy forces. Building on this understanding, depth-dependent numerical formulations have been proposed to predict pore and fracture pressures and to determine thickness-related risks associated with salt bodies. Offshore East Africa

In recent years, offshore East Africa has seen rapid growth in natural gas discoveries, with deepwater finds in Mozambique’s Rovuma Basin being particularly representative. Over a relatively short period, the basin has delivered large-scale gas discoveries, exemplified by the supergiant Mamba field and the large Coral field. Follow-up drilling confirmed the presence of multiple high-permeability sandstone gas reservoirs across the area. From a development standpoint, projects in the Rovuma Basin generally adopt a liquefied natural gas (LNG) export pathway. The Coral field was the first to enter the development and production phase and began operation in November 2022. Overall, floating liquefied natural gas (FLNG)-centered developments in offshore East Africa provide a viable route for monetizing ultradeepwater resources through integrated offshore production systems. They also provide a representative case for diversifying deepwater gas field development concepts.

Subsidence Mechanisms in Deepwater Gas Reservoirs

As deepwater gas reservoirs move into large-scale development, depletion-induced reservoir mechanical responses increasingly govern long-term operational safety. Reservoir compaction directly drives seafloor subsidence. The resulting deformation propagates through the overburden to the seabed and may compromise the integrity of critical facilities, including subsea wellheads and pipelines. Therefore, subsidence mechanisms in deepwater gas reservoirs should be systematically synthesized to provide a theoretical basis for risk identification, monitoring program design, and predictive assessment.

Compaction Mechanisms

Compaction is a production-driven process in which sustained pore pressure depletion increases the effective stress and reduces the reservoir volume. It is a direct driver of seafloor subsidence. During deepwater gas production, the in situ stress state (including the hydrostatic pressure and overburden load) is jointly borne by pore pressure and effective stress. As production proceeds, pore pressure decreases and effective stress increases. Consequently, a greater fraction of the hydrostatic and overburden loads is transferred to the rock skeleton, promoting pore collapse and causing irreversible reductions in porosity and permeability. , In addition, elevated effective stress may trigger localized shear failure, leading to shear-induced compaction.

Studies of unconsolidated sandstones from the deepwater Mungaroo Formation in offshore Australia indicate that compaction in deepwater gas reservoirs can strongly depend on time. Time-dependent compaction can be interpreted in terms of grain-scale deformation mechanisms. Grain crushing, particle rearrangement, and pore collapse can generate elastic and inelastic strains as well as creep; when coupled with pore pressure diffusion, these strains result in time-dependent compaction at the reservoir scale. Van Stappen and coworkers further reported that even within the near-linear small-strain regime, sandstone deformation involves a substantial fraction of irreversible grain-scale processes. These microscale irreversibilities provide a physical basis for macroscopic viscoplasticity and time-dependent compaction, and they offer direct evidence of the underlying micromechanical mechanisms. This microstructural evidence implies that time-dependent compaction does not rely solely on empirical fitting parameters. Instead, it can be linked explicitly to the observable evolution of grains and fractures, providing a physically grounded basis for constitutive parametrization. Building on micromechanical insights, Keszthelyi et al. developed a bottom-up, creep-based compaction model that integrates microcrack growth and pressure solution. The model explains compaction in the Ekofisk field directly in terms of pore-scale processes and matches the observed compaction behavior well.

Subsidence Mechanisms

Subsidence is the downward displacement of the ground surface relative to a stable reference datum. Subsidence develops primarily in the overburden. Reservoir compaction reduces the reservoir volume, and the resulting stress redistribution increases the effective stress in the overlying strata, causing deformation and compaction that generate the main subsidence signal. The stratigraphic domain involved in subsidence typically includes the compacting reservoir unit, the overburden, adjacent lateral strata, and underlying formations. Compaction is generally concentrated in the reservoir unit and the overlying strata, whereas adjacent lateral strata typically do not compact synchronously. Although vertical displacement is dominant, horizontal deformation may occur under certain conditions. Subsidence induced by reservoir fluid production often has a pronounced time lag that can extend for several years. Two primary mechanisms are typically invoked to explain this behavior: delayed depletion caused by low-permeability layers and intrinsic time-dependent deformation of reservoir rocks.

Delayed depletion in low-permeability layers occurs in strongly heterogeneous reservoirs, where low-permeability rock constitutes a substantial fraction of the system. In late-stage production, a limited number of high-permeability pathways may deplete first, whereas the pore pressure in low-permeability bodies declines slowly over extended time scales. In a spatially correlated bulk stiffness framework, the reservoir-averaged volumetric strain is highly sensitive to gradual pressure drawdown in low-permeability zones. Consequently, surface subsidence can remain time-dependent during late production. This interpretation is consistent with field observations. For example, in a major gas reservoir in the North Adriatic Basin, delayed postabandonment subsidence may reach approximately 10 cm over 50 years. Such behavior is commonly documented during late-stage field development. However, subsidence has also been observed early in production and may persist even after shut-in. To account for this, researchers have proposed a mechanism based on time-dependent reservoir rock deformation, namely, the creep of reservoir sandstones and shale layers under high effective stress. Under constant effective stress, the reservoir strain evolves with time. As illustrated in Figure , reservoir depletion involves a coupled reservoir–overburden response, in which pore-pressure reduction initially causes compaction of the reservoir unit, followed by delayed deformation of the surrounding low-permeability shales due to gradual pore-fluid diffusion and time-dependent rock deformation. Building on the analytical model of Chang et al., which attributes postdepletion time-dependent subsidence to the slow drainage of bounding shales, Voyiadjis et al. developed a three-dimensional finite-element model using subsidence data from the Valentine field as a case study. The model simulates three-dimensional pore-fluid diffusion within bounding shales and matches the observed subsidence well. The calibrated model can predict time-dependent compaction and subsidence, as well as the three-dimensional spatiotemporal evolution of pore pressure, void ratio, vertical strain, and vertical displacement. These results indicate that shale creep is an important factor for improving subsidence prediction.

Measurements of Compaction and Subsidence

Compaction in hydrocarbon reservoirs is difficult to observe directly. In offshore fields, seafloor subsidence provides a key observable indicator of compaction. Current offshore subsidence monitoring technologies can be grouped into six categories: direct measurements; water-level measurements (based on tide gauges); downhole or in-reservoir measurements; seafloor deformation monitoring arrays; pressure–gravity and hydraulic systems; and remote sensing and global navigation satellite system (GNSS)-based techniques. Among these, approaches with demonstrated engineering feasibility for deepwater gas fields include global navigation satellite system-acoustic (GNSS-A), time-lapse (4D) joint gravity–pressure measurements, fiber-optic sensing, microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) arrays, and formation compaction monitoring tools (FCMT). GNSS-A uses preinstalled seafloor stations (typically acoustic transponders combined with GNSS positioning at the sea surface) to infer seafloor motion. Vessel-based GNSS-A can achieve horizontal positioning precisions of ±2–4 cm, reflecting the state-of-the-art observation accuracy reported for this technology; in contrast, unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV)-based real-time GNSS-A shows substantially larger fluctuations (on the order of ±35 cm), highlighting the current performance gap among implementation modes. In practice, this method is constrained by the high costs and logistics associated with vessel operations and repeated surveys.

Time-lapse (4D) gravity–pressure monitoring targets subsidence by jointly tracking production-related mass redistribution (gravity changes) and pressure evolution and is typically treated as a deepwater-capable method, provided that hardware and deployment design are suitable. This review summarizes this category as offering direct measurement potential and long-range monitoring capability while emphasizing deployment and calibration challenges in offshore settings. ,

Fiber-optic sensing enables continuous deformation monitoring via strain-based measurements. Two representative implementations are (i) seabed fiber-optic cables and (ii) fiber Bragg grating (FBG) strain sensors distributed along a single optical fiber. The reported accuracies are typically approximately 1–5 mm, with advantages including high sensitivity and distributed sensing. Limitations include installation complexity, data-handling burden (large data volumes), and the fact that many strain-based configurations provide relative (change) measurements rather than absolute subsidence, requiring careful calibration and interpretation.

MEMS-based monitoring involves the construction of a seafloor terrain/deformation monitoring system by deploying multiple six-axis MEMS sensors on the seabed. The structural configuration and operational mechanism of such a system are conceptualized in Figure , highlighting the deployment of sensor arrays via remotely operated vehicles (ROVs) and their integration with submarine topographic deformation monitoring systems for high-resolution data acquisition. The conceptual layout described in the review uses four vertically aligned arrays with 21 sensors, each spaced at 1 m intervals in a diagonal configuration, covering an area of approximately 30 m × 30 m; the system is designed to monitor subsidence-related variables such as pressure, tilt, and strain, enabling high-resolution characterization of subsidence patterns and rates. Field feasibility has been demonstrated in deepwater. One case report describes deployment at approximately 1200 m water depth for 6 months using an ROV, and additional pressure testing evaluated operation at 3000 m depth, including a 35 MPa pressure-cylinder test for 18 h, with a reported maximum error of 2.47 mm relative to normal-pressure conditions.

FCMT provides a well-based, high-precision means to directly quantify formation compaction. The reviewed FCMT integrates precision sensors via wireline deployment and uses gamma-ray detectors to locate radioactive markers and measure spacing changes; an example specification reports 2.54 mm precision over 30 ft, enabling continuous, real-time recording of formation dimensional changes and mechanical response, which is valuable for reconstructing the temporal evolution of compaction processes. In addition, the review summarizes FCMT as offering direct compaction measurements with approximately 1–10 mm accuracy and applicability from shallow water to approximately 3000 m deep, while also noting its localized nature and dependence on well access and operational constraints.

Key Influencing Factors

Controls on Reservoir Compaction

Mineralogical Composition and Microstructural Features

Compaction deformation is strongly time-dependent, and its magnitude is jointly governed by the clay–mineral composition and pore structure. Previous studies suggest that creep during compaction may be driven by the continuous deformation of clay phases or grain contacts. Both the rate and mode of deformation depend strongly on the clay type: kaolinite-rich sandstones tend to show an elastoplastic response, whereas sandstones containing >45% illite and smectite typically exhibit time-evolving viscoplastic behavior. The disparity in rheological responses is quantitatively evidenced by a comparison of the data in Figure and Figure , where sandstones dominated by smectite/illite exhibit more pronounced time-dependent deformation coefficients than those with high kaolinite contents. Notably, depletion-related viscous deformation is largely irrecoverable and appears to be independent of the specific clay type in the rock matrix. In addition, pore collapse and overall compaction magnitude are highly sensitive to pore-geometry attributes. A higher initial porosity and lower quartz content promote pore closure, grain rearrangement, and contact crushing under the same increase in effective stress, leading to greater volumetric strain accumulation and more irreversible compaction. Finite-element frameworks that couple reservoir and overburden mechanics further highlight that compaction predictions are highly sensitive to input rock mechanical properties. Therefore, lithological and pore-structure heterogeneity must be rigorously translated into constitutive parameters and facies-based differentiation schemes suitable for field-scale simulation. This translation is essential for reliably quantifying the magnitude and spatial distribution of compaction.

Stress Conditions and Loading Rate Effects

During field production, changes in the in situ stress state can substantially increase shear stress. In some cases, shear stress may reach levels sufficient to trigger localized shear failure or slip along preexisting fractures. Consequently, increasing effective stress not only promotes compaction via pore collapse but also may activate shear-induced compaction. As shown in Figure , the reconstructed yield surface of reservoir chalk, constrained by experimental data for pore collapse and shear failure, suggests that production-induced increases in effective stress and deviatoric stress may shift the rock from pore-collapse-dominated compaction toward shear-induced compaction. Moreover, the loading rate governs the relative contributions of elastoplastic deformation and viscoplastic deformation and is a key experimental control for the robust identification of time-dependent compaction. For unconsolidated deepwater sandstones, increasing the loading rate from 0.5 to 1.0 MPa/min strengthens the viscoplastic contribution and may even render it dominant. In contrast, reducing the loading rate to 0.25 MPa/min yields a more elastoplastic response, indicating pronounced rate sensitivity.

Pore Pressure Diffusion and Drainage Scale Effects

Permeability and reservoir thickness control the timescales of pore pressure diffusion and drainage, thereby governing compaction rates and the associated time lag. High permeability (K) accelerates pore pressure redistribution, allowing drawdown to propagate more readily through the reservoir. As a result, the response curve reaches a quasilinear stage earlier, and depletion-driven compaction is amplified. In contrast, under low-K conditions, pressure diffusion is hindered, and compaction typically results in more pronounced tailing and residual compaction. Reservoir thickness further modulates diffusion timescales. Thin reservoirs have shorter drainage pathways and therefore weaker time-dependent effects. In contrast, thick reservoirs have longer drainage paths, which favor greater cumulative compaction and tend to sustain time-dependent responses over longer durations. Controls on Reservoir Subsidence

Number of Producing Wells and Distance from the Subsidence Center

The number of producing wells and the spatial configuration exert first-order control on the geometry of the subsidence bowl, as well as the magnitude of maximum subsidence (s max) and the migration of its location. On the basis of a spatially resolved subsidence analysis of the Groningen gas field, J. J. Lizarraga and G. Buscarnera compared three scenariossingle-well, near-neighbor, and full-field well-pattern cases. When only a single well was considered, the subsidence bowl was approximately axisymmetric, with an s max value of only approximately 2 cm and an influence radius of less than 1 km. When near-neighbor wells within a 5 km radius were included, the bowl expanded markedly, and s max increased to approximately 15 cm. When the full set of 55 producing wells was incorporated, s max further increased to approximately 32 cm, and the location of maximum subsidence migrated toward the west-central part of the field, indicating a distinctly nonlinear and asymmetric response of the subsidence field to well-pattern geometry. The progressive expansion and distortion of the subsidence bowl with increasing well density are illustrated in Figure . In terms of the distance to the subsidence center, the near-well contributions exhibit strong local dominance; for example, approximately 50% of the total subsidence is attributable to the six closest wells, and when the 12 and 34 closest wells are considered, the cumulative contributions increase to approximately 80% and approximately 95%, respectively. These results imply that subsidence near the bowl center is governed primarily by clusters of nearby wells, whereas more distant wells influence mainly the margins of the bowl and the overall spatial footprint. Water Saturation

In development-induced subsidence of chalk reservoirs (e.g., the Ekofisk field), water saturation (S w) and waterflood front advance are key controls of subsidence magnitude. Field production histories indicate that subsidence rates may remain high even under large-scale water injection and approximate injection–production balance, and may evolve out of phase with pressure recovery. This out-of-phase evolution between the subsidence rate and pressure/injection history is documented for Ekofisk in Figure . This behavior suggests that subsidence is not governed solely by the increase in effective stress associated with pore pressure depletion. Related studies attribute this phenomenon to additional plastic compaction induced by water weakening. As S w increases or the waterflood front arrives, the chalk strength and stiffness decrease, the elastic domain contracts, and the volumetric yield cap shifts toward a lower mean effective stress. A conceptual illustration of water-induced weakening and the associated yield cap evolution is presented in Figure . Consequently, the reservoir may enter a plastic compaction regime and accumulate irreversible volumetric strain under limited stress changes or even during pressure rebound. Accordingly, the influence of water injection on subsidence reflects competition between the two effects. The partitioning of subsidence contributions from depletion and water weakening is illustrated in Figure . Pressure recovery tends to mitigate compaction, whereas increasing S w enhances compaction through constitutive weakening. When weakening offsetsor exceedsthe stress-relief effect of pressure recovery, subsidence can persist or intensify episodically. A pronounced decline in the subsidence rate is expected only when pressure recovery returns the stress state to within the cap. Therefore, subsidence prediction for such reservoirs should treat saturation and waterflood-front position as critical state variables that govern the evolution of constitutive parameters and yield boundaries, and should incorporate them explicitly into the modeling framework.

Coupled Multiphysics Prediction of Strain and Subsidence

Multiphysics Coupling Strategies

Numerical simulations of poromechanical behavior span three main domains: geomechanical modeling, reservoir simulation, and fracture mechanics. Geomechanical modeling focuses on the stress–strain response, reservoir simulation represents multiphase flow and heat transfer, and fracture mechanics addresses fracture propagation and geometric evolution. Building on these capabilities, two major coupling paradigms have been developed: modular (staggered) and fully coupled (monolithic) schemes. In modular coupling, the reservoir flow simulator, geomechanical solver, andwhen neededa fracture mechanics module are solved separately at each time step. Key variables are exchanged through a communication interface to predict development-relevant responses, such as stress changes, reservoir compaction, and surface subsidence. In contrast, fully coupled approaches solve mechanical unknowns (e.g., stress and strain) and flow-related unknowns (e.g., pressure and temperature) simultaneously within a single global system of equations. Fully coupled formulations provide high accuracy and strong theoretical consistency under strong coupling, but they also incur the highest computational cost and complexity. Modular coupling can be further classified into uncoupled, explicit (one-way), and iterative (two-way) schemes. Iterative coupling repeatedly solves the flow and stress equations within each time step until convergence is achieved. In principle, once it converges, its accuracy can approach that of a fully coupled formulation. Marte Gutierrez compared fully coupled and partially coupled formulations for production and subsidence modeling. He argued that because production and reservoir compaction occur concurrently, production–subsidence analyses should preferentially adopt a fully coupled flow–deformation framework to capture pressure changes induced by compaction-driven feedback. This study further revealed that coupling effects depend on rock compressibility, drainage length scale, and depletion history.

Compaction Prediction Models

Compaction prediction models can be broadly classified into three categorieselastic, elastoplastic, and viscoplasticbased on the assumed material behavior. Early studies typically assumed linear elasticity and used one-dimensional formulations, in which total reservoir-thickness compaction Δh is given by 1 Δh=hCmσv where h is the reservoir thickness, C m denotes the reservoir compressibility, and σv is the vertical effective stress.

Although elastoplastic models can often predict production-induced compaction with reasonable accuracy, they may fail when applied to weak, unconsolidated materials. For load-induced deformation in unconsolidated sand layers, engineering practices commonly adopt viscoelastic or viscoplastic frameworks. Both superimpose time-dependent viscous effects on pressure-dependent quasistatic deformation. However, they differ in whether the quasistatic component is recoverable. In principle, either formulation can be used. However, the recoverable quasistatic deformation implied by viscoelasticity is often inappropriate for geomaterials because rocks and sediments are prone to tensile damage owing to their low tensile strength. − Consequently, viscoplastic models are more widely used to represent time-dependent deformation in weak, unconsolidated sands. On the basis of Perzyna’s viscoplastic theory, the elastic–viscoplastic (EVP) model decomposes the total strain rate into elastic and viscoplastic components. By combining a stress (load) power-law relationship with a time-decay kernel, the model maps the production history p(t) to the accumulated compaction strain, capturing the delayed response and creep typical of unconsolidated materials. Accordingly, it can be written as follows: 5 ε̇Total=ε̇e+ε̇vp 6 ε̇Total(T)=GHp(T)H‐1ṗ(T)+Cp(T)D(tk+T)E where ε̇Total is the total strain rate, ε˙ e is the elastic strain rate, and ε̇vp is the viscoplastic strain rate. Here, p(T) denotes the applied load history, and ṗ(T) is the corresponding load rate. G and H are the elastic power-law parameters; A and D are the activation parameters for viscoplasticity; B and E are the viscoplastic decay parameters; and C = A · B and t k are characteristic time constants. The implementation of the EVP model is straightforward, can be parametrized using laboratory calibration, and is suitable for compaction-dominated weak reservoirs; however, it is sensitive to the assumed stress path.

The rate-type compaction model (RTCM) was originally proposed by de Waal and was later reformulated by Pruiksma et al. into an isotach expression. An isotach is a locus in the stress–strain space corresponding to a constant loading (strain) rate; each loading rate is associated with a unique isotach, and a specimen subjected to that sustained rate will asymptotically approach the corresponding isotach. The isotach concept unifies compaction behavior under changing loading rates with compaction during the creep stage within a consistent framework. Challenges and Future Perspectives

Challenges

Development-induced subsidence in deepwater gas reservoirs is a long-term, path-dependent geomechanical process that commonly involves a delayed onset, evolving subsidence rates, and possible persistence after production reduction or shut-in. Therefore, a central challenge is to identify the dominant time-dependent deformation mechanisms and to extrapolate them reliably over the full development life cycle. Existing time-dependent compaction models, including rate-type compaction models, time-decay formulations, and viscoplastic constitutive laws, can reproduce delayed subsidence behavior in some field cases. However, their parameters are often difficult to constrain uniquely, and different models may fit historical subsidence data while producing divergent long-term forecasts. This nonuniqueness introduces substantial uncertainty when short-term production and monitoring records are used to predict long-term compaction and seabed subsidence.

Another major challenge is the development of coupled models that are both physically consistent and computationally practical. Deepwater gas development involves heterogeneous pressure depletion, complex stratigraphy, variable drainage length scales, reservoir compaction, overburden deformation, and seabed displacement. These processes are strongly coupled across different spatial and temporal scales. Fully coupled reservoir–geomechanical formulations can improve physical consistency when compaction–flow feedback is strong, whereas modular coupling is often more efficient for screening, sensitivity analysis, and field-scale applications. However, clear criteria are still lacking for selecting the appropriate coupling level under different research and engineering objectives, such as early development assessment, well-pattern evaluation, production optimization, and long-term risk prediction. As a result, balancing predictive accuracy, computational cost, and field applicability remains a key difficulty.

A further challenge is the limited availability of high-precision, long-duration, and spatially continuous monitoring data in deepwater environments. Although GNSS-A, time-lapse gravity–pressure measurements, fiber-optic sensing, MEMS arrays, and downhole compaction tools can provide important observational constraints, their application in deepwater fields is commonly limited by high survey costs, complex offshore deployment, discontinuous temporal coverage, calibration uncertainties, and restricted spatial representativeness. Consequently, key parameters such as drainage length scale, compressibility, viscoplastic properties, and overburden mechanical properties remain weakly constrained. This data limitation also hinders the independent validation of long-horizon subsidence forecasts and restricts the implementation of monitoring-informed model updating during field operations.

Finally, the translation of subsidence prediction into decision-oriented indicators remains insufficient. In deepwater gas development, the practical consequences of subsidence are controlled not only by total vertical displacement but also by differential settlement, local curvature, shear strain, deformation gradients, and their impacts on subsea wellheads, manifolds, pipelines, umbilicals, and seabed stability. However, many current prediction workflows still focus primarily on the magnitude and spatial distribution of subsidence, while the connection between time-dependent reservoir compaction and engineering-sensitive deformation indicators remains underdeveloped. Without such a connection, subsidence prediction is difficult to use directly for development planning, pressure management design, facility layout optimization, inspection scheduling, and life-cycle risk management.

Future Perspectives

A key future direction is to develop field-deployable geomechanical simulators that explicitly incorporate time-dependent compaction and subsidence models. Such simulators should integrate reservoir-flow simulation, time-dependent constitutive laws, overburden deformation, and seabed displacement prediction within a unified computational workflow. For deepwater gas reservoirs, these simulators should be able to represent pressure-depletion history, drainage length scale, viscoplastic or rate-dependent compaction, reservoir-overburden interaction, and deformation transmission to the seabed. The objective is not only to reproduce historical subsidence but also to provide reliable long-term forecasts over the full development cycle, including preproduction design, production, pressure-management adjustment, shut-in, and postproduction deformation. To make this framework practical for engineering use, hierarchical modeling strategies are needed: simplified analytical or semianalytical models can be used for early screening, whereas high-resolution coupled models should be applied to critical areas where wellhead integrity, pipeline response, or seabed stability is sensitive to local deformation.

A second engineering-oriented perspective is to make subsidence simulators directly usable for field development and operational decision-making. Future workflows should translate model outputs into engineering constraints, including allowable pressure drawdown, well pattern and well spacing limits, production allocation strategies, facility layout margins, pipeline routing constraints, and local deformation thresholds. Scenario-based simulations should be used before and during development to compare alternative production rates, pressure depletion trajectories, well patterns, and subsea facility layouts. In this way, subsidence prediction can support not only risk interpretation after deformation has occurred but also proactive design and mitigation, such as optimizing pressure management strategies, avoiding excessive localized depletion, and identifying zones where subsea facilities require larger safety margins.

Long-term monitoring and model updating should also become part of the simulator workflow. Monitoring data from GNSS-A, time-lapse gravity–pressure measurements, fiber-optic sensing, MEMS arrays, and downhole compaction tools can be used to update model parameters, reduce uncertainty, and improve predictive reliability during field operation. A monitoring-informed simulator should provide probabilistic forecasts rather than a single deterministic subsidence curve and should quantify uncertainties in drainage length scale, compressibility, viscoplastic parameters, pressure depletion, and overburden mechanical properties. When predicted or observed deformation approaches predefined engineering thresholds, the updated model should support operational responses, such as adjusting production allocation, reducing local drawdown, increasing inspection frequency for critical subsea facilities, or revising the development plan. This monitoring–simulation–operation feedback loop would enable full-cycle subsidence prediction and transform subsidence modeling from a postevaluation tool into an engineering decision-support system for deepwater gas field development.

To support these applications, benchmark data sets and validation protocols for time-dependent subsidence simulation should be established. These data sets should include reservoir pressure histories, production schedules, geomechanical parameters, compaction measurements, seabed displacement observations, and facility-response indicators where available. Comparative studies are needed to evaluate elastic, elastoplastic, viscoplastic, rate-type, and time-decay models under consistent reservoir and operational conditions. The performance of modular and fully coupled simulators should also be assessed in terms of their prediction accuracy, computational cost, uncertainty reduction, and engineering usability. Such benchmark-based validation would help define the applicability boundaries of different models and coupling schemes, thereby accelerating the transition from research-oriented subsidence models to practical engineering simulators for long-term deep-water gas development.

Conclusions

This review synthesizes the current status of deepwater gas exploration and development, with a focus on seafloor subsidence induced by production-driven pore pressure depletion. We systematically review compaction and subsidence mechanisms, monitoring technologies, controlling factors, and coupled multiphysics prediction frameworks. Our goal is to provide a transferable, geomechanics-based knowledge framework to support subsidence risk identification, monitoring program design, and predictive decision-making. Future priorities include developing transferable coupling selection guidelines and advancing an integrated, multiscale risk assessment framework that links reservoir, overburden, and subsea engineering systems.
