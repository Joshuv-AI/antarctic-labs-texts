Collective effects of neighbouring melting ice objects
Sofía Angriman 1 † \dagger, Detlef Lohse 1,2, Roberto Verzicco 3,1, Sander G. Huisman 1

Abstract
We present a study on the melting dynamics of neighbouring ice bodies by means of idealised simulations, focusing on collective effects. Two vertically aligned, square-shaped, and equally sized ice objects (size on the order of centimetres) are immersed in quiescent fresh water at a temperature of 20 °C 20\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius} . By performing two-dimensional direct numerical simulations, and using the phase-field method to model the phase change, the collective melting of these objects is studied. While the melting of the upper object is mostly unaffected, the melting time and the morphology of the lower ice body strongly depends on the initial inter-object distance. It moreover displays a non-monotonic dependence on the initial object size. We show that this behaviour results from a non-trivial competition between layering of cold fluid, which lowers the heat transfer, and convective flows, which favour mixing and heat transfer.

1
Introduction

Melting is an important and ubiquitous phenomenon present in everyday life. From mundane situations like keeping a drink cold by adding a few ice cubes, to the optimisation of energy supply processes through the use of phase-change materials in latent-heat energy storage systems
(
Pielichowska & Pielichowski 2014
;
Yang et al. 2024a
)
, understanding the details of melting is key. Undoubtedly, ice is among the most relevant substances to consider, as it covers a significant part of the Earth’s surface. In a context of global warming and climate change, where the overall global ice mass is decreasing, and iceberg calving becomes more frequent, accurately quantifying the melting rate of ice bodies like icebergs is necessary for predicting its impact on ocean dynamics
(
Cenedese & Straneo 2023
)
.

In the environment, various physical mechanisms are involved in the melting of ice. For an iceberg, the main melting occurs under the water surface, and radiative heating or melting in air have a minor relevance.
The ice and the surrounding fluid (and flow) are two-way coupled: the flow enhances the melting, and the cold melt water drives the flow
(
Du et al. 2024
)
.
Fluid motion may be generated by natural convection, where the velocity in the flow is induced as a result of the buoyancy of the melt, i.e. it is the temperature difference with the ambient what creates motion
(
Weady et al. 2022
)
. In turn, when an external flow is present, forced convection takes place, which modifies the melting dynamics
(
Hao & Tao 2002
;
Yang et al. 2024b
)
.
The temperature difference with the ambient fluid is an important factor which modifies the flow structure, and the morphology of melting bodies
(
Weady et al. 2022
;
Pegler & Wykes 2020
)
, as a result of the non-monotonic dependence of density of pure water on the temperature.

As a further complication, salt in the ambient water results in double diffusive convection, and the complex interaction between the solutal, thermal, and kinetic boundary layers modifies the ice surface, which can give rise to scallop formation
(
Wilson et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2023a
)
.
Pressurised bubbles and sediments encapsulated in glacier ice have been shown to modify the mixing of the flow when released, thus enhancing melting
(
Wengrove et al. 2023
)
.
The effect of the aspect ratio of an ice block on its melting dynamics has been explored both with and without an ambient flow
(
Hester et al. 2021
;
Yang et al. 2024c
;
Yang et al. 2024b
)
, where flow separation has been reported to affect ice morphology and the overall melting rate increases dramatically.

Ice objects that melt close to another melting object is an additional factor to consider in melting studies. In such cases, not only the ambient conditions are important, but also the interaction between the melt water plumes of the objects, and how this feedbacks on the flow, should be taken into account. The interaction between multiple sources of subglacial discharge has been shown to modify the melting of glaciers in a non-trivial way, highlighting the importance of considering the interplay of plumes in melting phenomena
(
Cenedese & Gatto 2016
;
Cenedese & Linden 2014
)
. Notably, in systems where convection is driven by gradients of concentration — instead of by temperature differences— collective effects have shown to be important, as is the case of droplets dissolving close to each other
(
Chong et al. 2020
)
.
Due to the number of parameters and processes involved, we chose to approach the study of collectivity in ice melting in an idealised system through numerical simulations. We will consider two equally sized ice objects which are fully immersed in fresh water and that are vertically aligned, systematically varying the initial distance between these objects, and considering different object sizes.

The paper is organised as follows: In section
2
we describe the numerical setup, presenting the equations of motion and the range of parameter space explored. In §
3.1
we compare the melting times of the top and bottom ice objects. Section
3.2
presents a discussion on the morphology of the ice, and the observed phenomenology. Finally, we draw the conclusions in §
4
, and present perspectives for future research.

Numerical setup

Figure 1:
(a) Simulation setup. Two ice objects of size
L
are placed one on top of the other at a distance

We perform two dimensional (2D) direct numerical simulations (DNS) of two melting ice objects.
Note that for Prandtl numbers larger than 1, convection in 2D and 3D behave nearly identically
(
van der Poel et al. 2013
)
, so we restrict ourselves to 2D simulations, as we and others did in various other melting studies
(
Purseed et al. 2020
;
Hester et al. 2021
;
Yang et al. 2023b
;
Yang et al. 2024b
)
, some of which even show quantitative agreements between 2D and 3D numerical simulations.
We consider initially square objects of size
L
, at a temperature set equal to their melting temperature
T_{m}=$0\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$
. They are immersed in a closed, adiabatic container of size
20L
in the horizontal direction, and
10L
in the vertical direction (this being the direction of gravity
{\bm{g}}
) filled with fresh, quiescent water initially at temperature
T_{\infty}=$20\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$
(note that by the end of the evolution the water temperature is about
5\%
less). The objects are stacked vertically at a distance
D
, symmetrically with respect to the centre of the domain. The setup is schematically shown in figure
1
(a).

To account for the maximum in density that fresh water displays at
T_{\text{max}}=$4\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$
we consider a non-linear equation of state
(
Gebhart & Mollendorf 1977
)
, relating density
\rho
and temperature
T
,

\rho(T)=\rho_{0}(1-\beta|T-T_{\text{max}}|^{q}),

(1)

where
\rho_{0}
is a reference density and
\beta=$9.3\times 10^{-6}\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
is the generalised thermal expansion coefficient with exponent
q=1.895
. To model the phase change we consider the phase-field method
(
Favier et al. 2019
;
Liu et al. 2021
)
, where a scalar field
\phi
that transitions smoothly from
0
in the liquid to
1
in the solid is evolved in time and space. The position of the interface is then defined implicitly where
\phi=0.5
. Hence, the equations to solve correspond to the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations within the generalised Boussinesq approximation, and an equation for
\phi
. These read, in non-dimensional form,

\centering\begin{cases}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bm{u}+(\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla})\bm{u}&=-\bm{\nabla}p+\sqrt{\frac{\text{Pr}}{\text{Ra}}}\Big(\nabla^{2}\bm{u}-\frac{\phi\bm{u}}{\eta}\Big)+|\theta-\theta_{\text{max}}|^{q}\,\hat{y}\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\theta+(\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla})\theta&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\text{Ra Pr}}}\nabla^{2}\theta+\frac{1}{\text{Ste}}~\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\phi\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\phi&=\frac{6}{5}\,\frac{\text{Ste}}{C\,\sqrt{\text{Ra Pr}}}\Big[\nabla^{2}\phi-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\phi(1-\phi)(1-2\phi+C\theta)\Big].\end{cases}\@add@centering

(2)

Here
\theta=(T-T_{m})/(T_{\infty}-T_{m})=(T-T_{m})/\Delta T
is the non-dimensional temperature difference, and
\bm{u}
is the dimensionless incompressible velocity field (i.e.
\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u}=0
) in units of the free-fall velocity
U_{f}=\sqrt{\beta g\Delta T^{q}L}
. The relevant non-dimensional numbers of the problem correspond to the Rayleigh number
Ra
, the Prandtl number
Pr
, and the Stefan number
Ste
, here defined as

\text{Ra}=\frac{g\beta\Delta T^{q}L^{3}}{\nu\kappa},\quad\text{Pr}=\frac{\nu}{\kappa},\quad\text{Ste}=\frac{c_{p}\Delta T}{\mathcal{L}},

(3)

where
\nu
and
\kappa
are the kinematic viscosity and thermal diffusivity, respectively (assumed to be equal in the solid and liquid phases),
c_{p}
is the specific heat capacity, and
\mathcal{L}
is the latent heat of melting. In eqs.(
2
)
\varepsilon
is the diffuse interface thickness, typically set to the mean grid spacing,
\eta
is the non-dimensional damping time, the typical time over which the fluid velocity is forced to tend to the solid velocity within the object set as
\Delta t
, the time-step (in our study, the solid is at rest).

C
is the phase mobility parameter, which arises from the Gibbs-Thomson relation linking the interface temperature and its curvature, and here fixed at
C=10
(see more details in
Yang et al. 2024c
;
Hester et al. 2020
). Equations (
2
) are solved using the second-order, staggered, finite-difference solver AFiD
(
Verzicco & Orlandi 1996
;
Ostilla-Mónico et al. 2015
;
Van der Poel et al. 2015
)
, with no-slip boundary conditions on all sides. Since the domain is adiabatic, for the temperature field we impose Neumann boundary conditions.
The velocity and temperature fields are solved in a uniform mesh in both directions. The number of grid points are such that there are at least 3 grid points in the thermal boundary layer, which scales as
\delta_{\text{th}}\propto L~\text{Ra}^{-1/4}
. The phase field
\phi
is solved in a refined mesh, using twice as many divisions in both directions as the mesh where temperature and velocity are solved in. We verified that this resolution was sufficient to accurately resolve the melting dynamic. A grid convergence study is discussed in appendix
A
.

We consider objects whose initial size
L
ranges from
0.5~\text{cm}
to
7.5~\text{cm}
, and we fix
\text{Pr}=7
and
\text{Ste}=0.25
(corresponding to water at
T_{\infty}=$20\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$)
, thus spanning Rayleigh numbers in the range
10^{4}\leq\text{Ra}\leq 10^{7}
.
For all cases, the initial ice-to-water area ratio is the same
A_{\text{ice}}/A_{\text{water}}\approx 0.01
.
For each size, we set different initial displacements
D/L
. Figure
1
(b) summarises the parameter space explored in this work. For each object size, we also run an additional case to use as reference, by placing a single object of size
L
in the centre of the domain (i.e., with its centre located at
x/L=10
and at
y/L=5
).

For

. We also studied the effect of varying the inter-object distance horizontally. Differences in the melting time with respect to the single object case are of the order of
3\%
, and we did not observe significant variations between the different distances considered. More details are discussed on appendix
C

We observe that the distance at which
\mathcal{T}_{\text{bot}}/\mathcal{T}_{\text{top}}\approx 1
depends on the Rayleigh number (or equivalently, on the object size). Panel (b) of figure
3
shows the ratio of top and bottom melting times for all of the datasets. Again, two regimes are identified: for small values of
D
, the melting of the bottom object is delayed and up to 22% longer, while when the distance increases bottom melting is enhanced by up to 10%. We observe that
D^{\text{crit}}
, the distance at which
\mathcal{T}_{\text{bot}}/\mathcal{T}_{\text{top}}=1

In order to quantify this dependence, we consider the (purely empirical) relation between the ratio of melting times and the inter-object distance,

\mathcal{T}_{\text{bot}}/\mathcal{T}_{\text{top}}=a~(D/\delta_{\text{th}}^{\text{ini}})^{\alpha}+b,

(4)

which yields
D^{\text{crit}}/\delta_{\text{th}}=[(1-b)/a]^{1/\alpha}
for the critical distance.
We then performed several fits of the data using expression (
4
), sampling each point from a Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
, where
\mu
corresponds to the measured data, and
\sigma
to the typical variation in different realisations, as previously discussed. From each fit we obtain a value of
D^{\text{crit}}
, and then compute the mean value and a standard deviation from all of the sets of data. The result of this is shown in figure
3
(c), where a clear non-monotonic dependence on
Ra
is observed (and is also shown in panel (b) with the shaded grey area). The critical distance first increases, it reaches its maximum value at around
\text{Ra}\approx 3\times 10^{6}
, and then decreases again. Interestingly, for the Rayleigh numbers close to where
D^{\text{crit}}
peaks, the error-bar is larger, implying that for these configurations the system displays a higher sensitivity to initial conditions. As it will be discussed afterwards, this range of
Ra
corresponds to the observed transition from a
stable to a more unstable, fluctuating plume.

We first focus on the regime in which
\mathcal{T}_{\text{bot}}>\mathcal{T}_{\text{top}}
. As seen in the top row of figure
2

D/\delta_{\text{th}}\approx 20

This flow continues along the underside of the bottom object, in close contact to the surface. The resulting layer of cold water acts as thermal shielding, which explains why
\mathcal{T}_{\text{bot}}/\mathcal{T}_{\text{top}}>1
.
Moreover, for the smallest distances considered the cold melt water also accumulates in the region in between the two bodies, further preventing the melting.

We note that when the objects are farther apart, and
\mathcal{T}_{\text{bot}}
is smaller or of the order of
\mathcal{T}_{\text{top}}
, a cavity forms at the bottom of the object below. This is shown in figure

(or equivalently

We find that the presence of a cavity on the underside, that persists for a large part of the evolution of the bottom object, correlates well with enhanced melting. Figure
4

\mathcal{T}_{\text{bot}}<\mathcal{T}_{\text{top}}

This effect can also be observed in the movies provided as supplementary material.
Neither the top objects nor the single melting object develop a cavity on their underside. As noted by
Yang et al. 2024c
, sufficiently large ice bodies are expected to form a lower cavity due to enhanced circulation from flow separation. In our case, however, the cavity beneath the lower object arises from a complex interplay between natural convection from its own melting and forced convection from the meltwater plume of the top block.

As indicated, when a cavity forms, the plume is oscillating from side to side and it forces a circulation on the underside of the lower object. This carves the ice due to enhanced mixing, and results in the formation of the cavity.
To quantify the flapping, we consider the single object case, and estimate the typical width of the region spanned by the plume as it oscillates, as a function of the distance to the object. Figure
4

L

L/2
shows good correlation with the enhanced melting regime
\mathcal{T}_{\text{bot}}<\mathcal{T}_{\text{top}}

Even though the plume of the top object is slightly modified by the presence of its neighbour below, it is interesting that by analysing the properties of the plume of the single object case we find an observable that is linked to the enhanced melting. This is an important point when considering more complex configurations, or different object geometries.

When analysing the ratio of velocities in panel (b) of figure

This Rayleigh number range corresponds to the destabilisation of meltwater plume of the top body, where stronger bulk flow fluctuations are observed. The plume, while not oscillating significantly, carries enough momentum to locally enhance melting and carve the cavity on the upper side of the lower ice block. Despite this local enhancement of melting, cold water remains attached to the surface and accumulates below, so overall, the shielding effect dominates, slowing down the melting.

4
Conclusion

We have studied the collective effects of the melting of two water-immersed and vertically aligned ice bodies. By performing numerical simulations, varying the initial displacement and considering different Rayleigh numbers (corresponding to changing the objects’ size), we have shown that the melting time of the lower object drastically varies, depending on the initial distance to the body above. When the objects are close together, the cold water plume that flows from the upper object onto the lower one is laminar, which shields the bottom object and extends its melting time. When the distance increases, even though the ambient fluid around the lower object is cold, the plume is unstable
and we observe flow separation and an intensification in circulation, resulting in enhanced melting.
We linked this enhancement to the formation of a cavity on the lower face of the bottom object. We observed that in the configuration explored in this work, a good criterion for predicting whether melting will be enhanced or delayed is to quantify the typical flapping amplitude of the plume of a single object (the reference case considered here). This provides hints for the expected behaviour when considering different object shapes, and possibly other configurations.

We observed that the two reported regimes are present for all the object sizes considered. However, the inter-object distance at which this occurs has a strong, non monotonic dependence on the initial Rayleigh number when measured in units of the thermal boundary layer
\delta_{\text{th}}
. The distance has a maximum around the values of
Ra
at which the plumes transition to a turbulent-like state, displaying higher fluctuations. In this range of
Ra
we also observed a change in morphology, with a cavity forming on the upper face of the object below.

The intricacy of the problem at study partially stems from the fact that the cold plume impinging on the bottom object originates from a source which is time dependent, and not uniform in space. Indeed, the “mixed convection” regime under which the bottom object melts is not just the result of a uniform incoming flow of a given velocity interacting with the natural convection generated by the melt water. As we reported, it is the competition between the shielding of the cold water and the enhanced mixing resulting from higher velocity fluctuations what underlays the observed dynamics, which, depending on the Rayleigh number, results in non-trivial morphological changes of the ice.

In view of this, it would be interesting to conduct a study imposing a uniform flow at a given speed and temperature, closer to a regime of mixed convection (i.e., not forced-convection dominated, as done by
Yang et al. 2024b
), to analyse whether these features are sufficient to observe a similar dynamics as the one we observe for the lower object. This could provide insights on how to further disentangle the different contributions of temperature difference and velocity to the overall melting rate.
Future studies could investigate how relevant the temporal dependence of the problem is in the observed dynamics, taking into account the effect of the frequency of oscillation of the melt water plume impacting on the lower object.

Furthermore, to compare with experiments, it would be of interest to perform a study in 3D. Even though the plume structure would be modified as compared to the two dimensional case, we expect to still recover, at least qualitatively, the enhanced and suppressed melting of the lower object. Additionally, evaluating the presence of salt in the ambient and varying the water temperature are interesting effects to explore that can contribute in going towards more natural, realistic scenarios.

Evolution of area and Nusselt number

The temporal evolution of the area
A(t)
of the bottom ice object is shown in figure
7
(a), for three different initial sizes,
Ra
:
2.3\times 10^{4}
,
5.0\times 10^{6}
, and
2.3\times 10^{7}
(corresponding to

, and
5
cm; we observe similar trends for the other datasets). For each object size, we show
A(t)
for the reference case, and for a small and a large inter-object distance
D
(where melting is delayed and enhanced, respectively). Considering that the top object behaves like the reference case, from the figure it can be seen that the top/bottom asymmetry appears early in the evolution of the system. That is to say, for the large inter-object distances, where the final melting times are such that
\mathcal{T}_{\text{bot}}<\mathcal{T}_{\text{top}}
, it also occurs that at a given intermediate time
t_{0}
,
A_{\text{bot}}(t_{0})<A_{\text{top}}(t_{0})

\mathcal{T}_{\text{bot}}>\mathcal{T}_{\text{top}}

The melting time
\mathcal{T}
and the time evolution of the area are related to the surface-averaged heat flow, expressed in non-dimensional form by the Nusselt number
\overline{\text{Nu}}
, through the Stefan boundary condition,

\frac{L/U_{f}}{\mathcal{T}}\propto\frac{\text{Ste}}{\text{Pr}^{1/2}\,\text{Ra}^{1/2}}\,\overline{\text{Nu}}.

(6)

From this expression we can estimate an average Nusselt number from the melting time, and compare it with the Rayleigh number
Ra
. Figure
7
(b) shows
\overline{\text{Nu}}
as a function of
Ra
, computed from the melting time of the bottom object, for all of the initial inter-object distances explored. We show also a power law with exponent
1/4
for reference, and observe that the data follow the scaling reasonably well, consistent with what is observed for heat transfer in natural convection
(
Bejan 1993
)
.

Figure 7:

(a) Normalised evolution of the area of the bottom object as it melts, for three initial values of
Ra
. The solid lines correspond to a small and a large initial inter-object distances. The dashed line shows the temporal evolution of the area of the single object case.
(b) Average Nusselt number for the bottom object, estimated from the initial size and the melting time, as a function of the initial Rayleigh for all of the datasets.
(c) Effective Nusselt for the lower ice body and effective Rayleigh, both computed from the instantaneous area, for the same datasets shown in panel (a).

To check if this scaling holds during the evolution of the system, we can define an effective Nusselt number
\text{Nu}_{\text{eff}}(t)
from the time derivative of the object’s area,

\frac{1}{U_{f}L}\frac{dA(t)}{dt}\propto\frac{\text{Ste}}{\text{Pr}^{1/2}\,\text{Ra}^{1/2}}\,\text{Nu}_{\text{eff}}(t),

(7)

which we compare with an effective Rayleigh number based on the instantaneous object size, i.e.
\text{Ra}_{\text{eff}}(t)=\text{Ra}\,\frac{A^{3/2}(t)}{L^{3}}
