The lived experiences of reproductive-aged women with polycystic ovary syndrome: a qualitative study
Authors: Kaitlyn J Ward, A K Benzley, K L King Stone, E S Wardell, J A Hughes, B M Magnusson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPCOS is a complex health condition that affects up to 20% females of reproductive age worldwide, and approximately 5 million U.S. females of reproductive aged females. Despite the prevalence of the condition, research on the lived experiences of women with PCOS is limited, particularly in the United States.MethodsThis study employed a phenomenological qualitative design to explore the lived experiences of individuals diagnosed with PCOS. We conducted semi-structured interviews via web conferencing with 39 females 18–45 years of age, residing in the U.S. with PCOS. Transcribed interviews were analyzed using the Framework Method.ResultsWe identified ten primary themes in the data 1) Critical shift point, 2) Education, 3) Future Experience, 4) Gaslighting (or blame), 5) Medical Experience, 6) Present Emotional Experience, 7) Social Impacts, 8) Treatment, 9) Well-documented Physical Symptoms, and 10) Not Well-Documented Symptoms and an additional 37 sub-themes. Overall, the experiences of living with PCOS were generally negative, especially in relation to gaslighting or blame, medical experience, symptoms – both well documented and not well documented, present emotional experience, social impacts, and treatment. Additionally, many participants reported a critical shift point that led to them seeking a diagnosis and treatment for their condition, and a lack of education in relation to PCOS.ConclusionsWomen with PCOS experience a wide-ranging physical, mental, and relational impacts. Awareness of the impact of PCOS is needed to reduce gaps in provider knowledge, social disconnect, fear of judgment, and mental health concerns. Background

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is the most common metabolic-endocrine disorder in reproductive-aged women [1]. PCOS affects up to 20% of women of reproductive age worldwide, including approximately 5 million women in the United States [2–4]. Although often considered a reproductive health concern, PCOS affects a wide range of physiological systems, the effects of which extend across the lifespan. PCOS is characterized by anovulation and polycystic ovarian morphology and is associated with menstrual irregularities, metabolic dysfunction, obesity, hirsutism, infertility, and increased risk of endometrial cancer and cardiometabolic disease [5–7]. Additionally, those with PCOS experience higher rates of depression and anxiety, increased suicide risk, and fear in social settings [6, 8, 9]. These physical, emotional, and psychological health changes are associated with reduced quality of life [8].

Although PCOS was first identified in 1935, the etiology is not fully understood but likely includes a combination of genetic, epigenetic, and environmental influences, including diet and lifestyle factors [5, 10, 11]. Diagnosis of PCOS includes signs of ovulatory dysfunction, hyperandrogenism, and/or polycystic ovaries on ultrasound. Despite established diagnostic criteria (e.g. Rotterdam) [12] PCOS diagnosis is frequently delayed due to biological complexity, clinical misconceptions, structural factors within healthcare systems, and broader systemic inequities. PCOS is a heterogeneous condition with four recognized phenotypes and a wide range of physical, psychological, and metabolic manifestations [1]. This substantial variability in clinical presentation complicates recognition of PCOS and contributes to delayed or missed diagnoses. The World Health Organization estimates that up to 70% of women with PCOS worldwide remain undiagnosed [13]. Even among those who ultimately receive a diagnosis, many report waiting more than two years from initial symptom onset to diagnosis [14].

Although research on PCOS has increased substantially in recent years, qualitative studies examining lived experiences remain limited. Most qualitative work has been conducted internationally [15–21]. Of the studies that exist, only two have been conducted in the United States [22, 23]. It is unknown in what ways the experience of US women with PCOS is similar or dissimilar to those in other countries. Existing qualitative studies suggest that women with PCOS experience psychological impairments, diminished self-esteem, poor self-perception, and reduced quality of life related to connection and community [22, 24, 25]. Interviewed women from previous qualitative studies report expected negative health impacts, including obesity, amenorrhea and oligomenorrhea, as well as sexual dysfunction [24, 26]. Further, these studies report experience of psychological distress particularly related to perceived delays in receiving care, negative medical experiences, and lack of education in health care providers [19, 27]. Lastly, these data indicate frustration within the daily lives of individuals living with PCOS due to the impact of symptoms such as hirsutism and insulin resistance, burnout related to the lack of adequate treatment, and the impact of long-term negative effects of PCOS [19, 24].

Research on the impact of PCOS on intimate partner relationships is mixed. A 2018 study by Navid et al. conducted among infertile women in Iran found no difference in overall marital satisfaction, but lower marital communication in infertile women with PCOS, versus infertile women without PCOS [28]. In a 2015 dyadic analysis, De Frène et al., found that women with PCOS who experienced infertility reported an inversely strengthening impact on relational satisfaction, when compared to those without PCOS [29]. Other studies suggest that PCOS has a negative impact on sexual satisfaction of diagnosed women [30]. Finally, a methodologically similar study to the current study reported that women with PCOS reported that “PCOS not only impacted their intimate relationships but also their relationships with family and friends” [31].

Study aims

The purpose of this study was to document the experiences of reproductive-aged women living with PCOS in the U.S. Improved understanding of the health, relational, and psychosocial impacts of PCOS may validate patient experiences and assist healthcare providers and public health professionals in better supporting affected individuals. Qualitative interviews allow participants to describe their experiences in their own words and provide a nuanced understanding of the challenges associated with living with PCOS.

Methods

This study employed a phenomenological qualitative design to explore the lived experiences of individuals diagnosed with PCOS. Semi‑structured interviews elicited participants’ perspectives on symptoms, healthcare experiences, social impacts, and emotional responses. Interview questions were developed based on questions guiding previous PCOS studies [8, 15–21, 24, 26, 27, 32, 33], qualitative research guides [34], and consultations with qualitative researchers. An English‑language version of the interview guide is provided as a supplementary file.

Eligible participants were females, with any gender identity, aged 18–45 years residing in the U.S. with a self-reported PCOS diagnosis that had occurred at least one year prior to the interview. Reproductive-aged women were selected because PCOS is typically diagnosed and clinically managed during the reproductive years, when menstrual irregularities, fertility concerns, and body image–related symptoms are particularly salient [35]. Participants were recruited through PCOS support groups on Facebook after obtaining administrator permission. Interested individuals completed an online eligibility survey and consented to be contacted. Within 24 h of posting, over 1,000 individuals responded, 900 of which were eligible to participate in the study.

Given the substantial response, respondents were stratified by race and ethnicity and gender identity to increase representation of underrepresented groups. Individuals from underrepresented racial and gender groups were preferentially selected, and random selection was used among more represented groups. A total of 121 eligible participants were contacted using their preferred method (email, phone, text) with up to three contact attempts per person. Nonparticipation was primarily due to nonresponse or scheduling constraints. Although thematic saturation was suspected at approximately 30 interviews, due to a lack of new themes emerging in the last few interviews and similar reports of data saturation in comparable studies [15, 16, 32], we completed previously scheduled interviews to ensure demographic representation and honor participant commitments. The final sample included 39 interviews.

Interviews were conducted privately via Zoom (Zoom Video Communications, San Jose CA) between March and July 2024. Consent forms were provided in advance, and verbal consent was obtained at the start of each interview. Four female undergraduate research assistants (KW, AB, EW, JH) completed a structured training program covering qualitative interview technique, including rapport-building, use of open-ended questions, neutral probing, active listening, and management of sensitive topics. Following didactic instruction, each interviewer conducted practice interviews that were video-recorded and reviewed by the study team. Performance was critiqued with feedback provided on question delivery, probing depth, and adherence to the interview guide. Qualitative analysis training was completed with a faculty member from the University of Utah who specializes in qualitative health research. The same undergraduate assistants conducted and audio- and video- recorded all interviews. The mean interview length was 38 min. All interviewers identified as White women. One interviewer disclosed her personal PCOS diagnosis to at least two participants near the end of the interview. Participants were aware that the interviewers were students conducting PCOS research but were not provided with additional personal information. No prior relationships existed between the interviewers and the participants.

Participants received a $30 Amazon e-gift card as compensation. Audio recordings were professionally transcribed verbatim (GoTranscript Inc., Edinburgh, UK). All procedures were approved by the Human Research Protection Program at Brigham Young University. This manuscript was prepared in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist (Supplement File #2) [36].

Data were analyzed using the Framework Method [37]. Four trained researchers (KW, KS, AB, EW) conducted initial open coding on a subset of transcripts to develop an inductive codebook. All transcripts were subsequently double-coded, and themes were refined through consensus discussions among the research team. Atlas.ti (version 24; ATLAS.ti Scientific Software Development GmbH. Berlin, Germany) was used to organize and manage the coding process.

Critical shift point

Many respondents described a “tipping point” when worsening or newly pronounced symptoms prompted them to seek diagnosis or treatment. Increased symptom severity often served as a catalyst for pursuing medical care. One participant explained, “Once I hit about 19, 20, I noticed a lot of different changes. I wasn’t having my periods all the time. I was getting a lot of pain in my ovaries, and I was like, ‘Never had that pain before.’ I knew something was wrong” (P4, age 29). Similar experiences were reported across interviews.

For some, this shift from passive symptom management to active help-seeking brought relief upon receiving a diagnosis. As one participant reflected, “I was relieved. I was sad but I was relieved because I was like, ‘Okay, I finally have answers but… this is something I have to live with forever’” (P2, age 29).

Education

Participants frequently described engaging in self-education following their PCOS diagnosis and for many, subsequently educating others. To better understand their symptoms and advocate for care, participants sought information from sources ranging from social media to peer-reviewed research. As one participant noted, “I’ll go out of my way to read… actual research papers… about what diets have worked for people with PCOS or what medications have worked with PCOS… I can see their accreditations… and know that this is science and real” (P11, age 26). For some, self-education provided reassurance, for others, it enabled more active advocacy with providers and exploration of alternative treatments.

Participants also described a perceived lack of knowledge about PCOS among healthcare providers, family members, and peers. Several recounted clinical encounters that left them feeling dismissed or unsupported contributing to the impression that providers were unfamiliar with PCOS or uncertain how to manage it. One participant reflected, “…these people [healthcare providers] don’t care or they’re just not knowledgeable in it [PCOS]. It’s one of those things where we don’t know the cure, so we don’t even know how… to help you. Therefore, we just won’t” (P32, age 31). Another stated, “I’ve been through probably 15 different doctors and OBGYNs. Nobody seems to understand what PCOS is or anything” (P34, age 26).

In social settings, participants often felt responsible for explaining their diagnosis. Friends frequently responded with “What is that?” (P1, age 29). One participant expressed, “…I do feel like a lot of people are not very informed on the subject [PCOS]… people don’t understand when they’re not going through it” (P4, age 29). Similarly, many reported that family members required ongoing conversations to understand the condition. One participant stated “…a lot of explaining had to be done for my parents. [They]… had just never heard of PCOS…” (P37, age 29) and another saying, “…my dad. He’s still trying to get it” (P2, age 29). Finally, many also described the need to explain fertility implications to partners, sometimes encountering misunderstanding. One participant said, "…it’s just not that easy for someone with PCOS just to have a normal pregnancy… I don’t think [my partner] completely understands…” (P37, age 29). Others reported increased support from partners over time: “I did have to tell him a lot about it, but… he’s pretty understanding” (P31, age 30).

Future experience

Given the chronicity of PCOS participants were asked how they perceived PCOS would impact their future. Concerns centered on fertility, the development of comorbidities, and uncertainty about long-term health. As one participant reflected, “what’s my body going to be like when I’m 50… Am I going to have kids? Am I not? Definitely, I feel like it changes how I view the future” (P21, age 26).

Fertility

Concerns about fertility were prevalent across age and relationship status. Many participants described significant emotional distress following their diagnosis, particularly when confronted with the possibility of infertility. One participant reflected on coming to terms with alternative family building options, stating, “…if it’s severe enough… there is a high chance that I would never have children… It’s been a lot to deal with, emotionally and physically” (P6, age 29). Another recalled being told at age 15 that having children might be difficult, describing the news as, “…really heartbreaking… when that’s all I wanted was to have kids” (P1, age 29).

Fertility concerns extended beyond future uncertainty and shaped present experiences. Participants worried about the impact of infertility on partners and family relationships and described being socially “left behind” as peers had children. Several reported psychological distress, strained relationships, and self-blame. As one participant expressed, “…I feel like I’m not doing what a woman should do… it makes you feel like less of a woman…” (P25, age 23). Another shared, “My infertility is probably the biggest part of my life played into PCOS. It’s been a struggle” (P4, age 29).

Concern about developing comorbidities

Participants described heightened anxiety about the possibility of developing PCOS-related comorbidities. Many referenced risks such as Type 2 diabetes, insulin resistance, and cardiovascular disease, expressing fear of “anything else” being added to their health burden (P35, age 36). Others conveyed uncertainty about long-term consequences, stating, “I don’t know if that causes early menopause or if something’s just going to spontaneously self-combust. I don’t know what that looks like” (P28, age 34). Participants noted that limited understanding of PCOS and its long-term effects intensified these concerns. For some, fear of comorbidities influenced daily behaviors. As one participant explained, “I have to work on my diet, and I have to work on all these things so I don’t become diabetic or all these other scarier things that can happen” (P2, age 29).

Gaslighting (or blame)

Participants consistently described feeling that their symptoms or concerns were dismissed or minimized by healthcare providers, others, and at times themselves. Several recounted clinical encounters that led them to question the legitimacy of their experiences. One participant recalled being told, “Sometimes we make a bigger deal out of things than what they really are” (P17, age 29), while another was asked, “Are you sure it’s not psychosomatic?” (P13, age 24). Others described being offered unrelated or oversimplified advice, such as being told to “just… eat more vegetables” when reporting ovarian pain (P4, age 29). Weight gain was commonly cited as a source of perceived blame, with one participant stating “[Doctors] just blamed everything on my weight… 'because you’re fat, you need to lose weight’” (P16, age 22).

These experiences contributed to frustration and, for some, internalized doubt. As one participant expressed, “For years, y’all turned it on me. For years you gaslit me. For years you made me feel worthless" (P6, age 29). Several described “self-gaslighting”, questioning whether they were exaggerating symptoms or failing in self-care. One reflected, “[my doctor] made me feel like I was lying. I must not be eating as healthy as I’m saying. I must actually not go to the gym” (P12, age 26). Others reported blaming themselves for symptoms. Regarding being overweight, one individual said “…[I] used to blame [myself] all the time. What if I didn’t eat so much in my childhood or…actually worked hard when I went to the gym…?” (P16, age 22). Collectively, these accounts underscore the impact of perceived dismissal and highlight the importance of patient-centered, weight-inclusive communication in clinical care.

Diagnosis and healthcare experience

Because participation required a prior PCOS diagnosis, interviews frequently included discussion of diagnostic testing and provider interactions. Diagnostic experiences varied, though ultrasound and hormone testing were commonly mentioned. Some participants described delayed evaluation, noting that it took visits with multiple clinicians before appropriate testing was conducted. As one participant shared “It took me about … two or three doctors before… they were able to look at my ovaries through ultrasound” (P17, age 29). Another reflected on years of symptoms before receiving confirmatory imaging, stating “Finally… I go to an ultrasound, and lo and behold, I have cysts on my ovaries… I could have just done this so many years prior” (P36, age 33).

Participants often characterized provider interactions as dismissive or limited in scope. One summarized her experience: “You just have this, and I can put you on birth control… Come back to me whenever ready to have a kid” (P11, age 26). Experiences with specialists were mixed. Some reported improved care from fertility or holistic providers, “I’m finally starting to get some relief from some of my PCOS symptoms only recently with this holistic doctor” (P12, age 26), describing them as more knowledgeable or helpful: “The fertility specialist was a lot more knowledgeable” (P38, age 26). Others felt similarly dismissed by specialists, including experiences of being blamed for weight-related symptoms. One participant described her experience going to a nutritionist, saying, “…it definitely hurts… having to go to the nutritionist and being told that clearly you’re not putting in the work that you say you’re putting in because the weight is not coming off” (P24, age 20).

Present emotional experience

Participants expressed a wide range of emotional responses to living with PCOS, particularly related to body image and identity. Many expressed dissatisfaction with their physical appearance, with one stating, “I’ve never really loved myself in my own body” (P1, age 29). Body image concerns often extended into social life, contributing to avoidance of public or social situations. One participant described declining social invitations due to facial hair growth and diminished confidence, noting she would, “have to sit in front of the mirror taking out every hair” (P27, age 25). Others described the ongoing mental burden of fluctuating weight and persistent self-monitoring as “exhausting” (P36, age 33).

Fertility-related identity concerns also shaped emotional experiences. Some participants described feeling that infertility challenged their sense of womanhood, expressing sentiments such as, “I don’t feel like a woman” (P8, age 40) or “I feel like I’m failing as a woman because I can’t have a child” (P28, age 34). Several described feelings of defectiveness (P24, age 20) or grief over unmet expectations of motherhood (P12, age 26). Others reported reframing these expectations through adoption or acceptance of remaining child-free.

Across interviews, participants used words such as frustrating, stressful, and defeating to characterize the emotional burden of PCOS. One reflected, “I’m just stuck living in a body that doesn’t function right… It feels really defeating” (P11, age 26). Many also reported anxiety and depressive symptoms, sometimes attributing them directly to PCOS or the cumulative challenges associated with it “I think it adds more stress and chaos to my anxiety and depression.” (P25, age 23). Despite these difficulties some participants identified, “small wins” including strengthened relationships, successful fertility treatments, or increased social support. One noted, in response to fertility treatments “It’s really cool the friends that have come forward and they’re like, I don’t even want my own kids, but I’ll carry yours” (P1, age 29).

Social impacts

PCOS influenced participants’ social lives, particularly through perceived support or lack thereof. Four subthemes emerged: external support, spouse or partner relationships, support groups, and fear of judgement. External support included healthcare providers, family, and friends. Some participants expressed disappointment in provider support, wishing for, “doctors who will sit and listen to you and give you the best solution” (P27, age 25). In contrast, others described positive family responses, noting that family members, “stop and bother to learn… and try to help me find solutions” (P6, age 29). Support from friends was more variable, with some participants feeling largely on their own “my friends were here and there, but overall, it was very much, ‘This is what I’ve got to deal with, and it’s on my own’” (P28, age 34).

Partner relationships were frequently shaped by fertility concerns and communication challenges. Some participants described tension related to expectations for biological children, “…there are some guys that are like, ‘Well, I really want a son that’s like me and looks like me’” (P1, age 29) and difficulty conveying the emotional and physical impact of PCOS “…it took a lot of learning… A lot of the initial misunderstandings…” (P37, age 29). Others reported supportive partners who demonstrated understanding and empathy, particularly during fertility struggles, “…he’s very kind about it all…he supports me, he understands, and we hate it for me because he knows I’ve always wanted to be a parent…I’m very fortunate I have somebody who treats me with a lot of respect and dignity and kindness” (P6, age 29).

Online support groups were commonly described as important sources of information and normalization. Participants valued connecting with others “who are also struggling just like I am” (P16, age 22). Many reported reassurance from shared experiences within online communities "Oh, wow. All these people deal with this, too…Definitely makes me feel more normal…” (P21, age 26). At the same time fear of judgment, also shaped many social interactions, particularly related to body image and food choices. One participant described “I would be so embarrassed to walk around with a cup of like a Starbucks Frappuccino because I feel like it is such a fattening drink” (P36, age 33).

Treatment

Participants described substantial variability in treatment experiences including medication use, lifestyle modification, fertility treatment, metabolic therapies, and surgery. Access to treatment was frequently shaped by insurance coverage, cost, relocation, and employment benefits. Some participants sought alternatives outside of standard therapies such as hormonal contraception or metformin, including functional medicine approaches or self-directed interventions.

Hormonal birth control was the most commonly reported initial treatment, often presented as the primary or sole option at diagnosis. Several participants described frustration with its routine recommendation and limited explanation. One participant said, “The doctor… pushed really hard on birth control, and I just didn’t want to take birth control anymore” (P10, age 24). Another reflected that providers, “…didn’t really do much other than recommend birth control… they didn’t actually tell me what was wrong” (P18, age 27). Lifestyle recommendations, including diet and exercise, were also frequently emphasized, though many participants reported limited perceived benefit despite significant effort. One participant explained, “I’m not saying that it doesn’t help, but it doesn’t make a difference. I would work my tail off in the gym and be dying and see no difference” (P3, age 23).

Participants pursuing pregnancy described varied experiences with fertility treatments, often influenced by financial or time constraints. One shared, “We’ve been through every treatment they’re willing to do before IVF” (P28, age 34), while another noted substantial financial cost without success (P38, age 26). Some participants reported use of metabolic therapies, including GLP-1 agonists, with mixed results (P9, age 26). Other described surgical interventions such as gastric bypass or hysteroscopy that improved certain symptoms for some, but not all. One shared, “…I had a gastric bypass last year… and I actually started having periods and ovulating, which I’ve never had before” (P34, age 26) and another “…the fertility specialist, she mentioned doing a hysteroscopy… I felt like that really helped… since then I haven’t had any more pain” (P4, age 29).

Well-documented physical symptoms

Participants described a range of physical symptoms commonly reported in the literature, including abnormal hair growth or loss, ovarian cysts, hormone imbalance, low libido, metabolic issues, painful or irregular periods, and weight gain. Painful, heavy, or irregular menstruation was commonly reported, with one participant describing her cycles as “extremely heavy” and “just all over the place” (P21, age 26). Participants also referenced hirsutism, hair loss and metabolic changes. One noted, “My hair was falling out a lot… they did blood work, and it showed I was pre-diabetic” (P33, age 28). Others described difficulty losing weight despite dietary and exercise efforts, attributing this to metabolic dysfunction, “you are trying to eat right and you’re trying to exercise, but you’re not just able to lose the weight… (P9, age 26). Low libido was also discussed with one participant stating, “I have no desire at all.” (P7, age 27). Although symptom severity varied, most participants reported experiencing one or more of these commonly documented manifestations of PCOS.

Not well-documented symptoms

In addition to commonly recognized symptoms, participants described experiences that have been less well-documented, including fatigue, brain fog, and generalized or localized pain. Fatigue was particularly prominent which some described as debilitating. One participant explained, “I physically can’t get up and go” when energy levels drop (P2, age 29). Another described exhaustion that others misinterpreted as lack of effort: “They think like, ‘Oh, you just need to push harder,’ but they don’t know that there’s more hormone things going on” (P29, age 27). For many, fatigue significantly shaped daily functioning.

Participants also reported brain fog and pain as persistent challenges. One described difficulty sleeping and chronic exhaustion, stating, “I’m tossing and turning… I just can’t sleep” (P32, age 31). Another noted that brain fog interfered with memory and work performance: “It makes it really hard for me to remember information… for my job, I have a ton of sticky notes around because… if I don’t write it down…[it] will be [gone] the next day.” (P7, age 27). Reports of pain ranged from headaches to ongoing or unpredictable discomfort. One participant stated, “I’m in pain 24/7 from [PCOS]” (P34, age 26). Another participant described sudden, unexplained pain episodes (P15, age 27). Together, these symptoms contributed substantially to participants’ experience with PCOS.

Discussion

A diagnosis of PCOS has wide-reaching consequences that affect nearly every aspect of women’s lives. Consistent with prior qualitative research, our findings demonstrate substantial physical, emotional, and relational impacts [17, 26]. Although we did not directly measure self-esteem or quality of life, these constructs were reflected in participants’ language and experiences. As with other chronic diseases, PCOS appears to shape identity, relationships, and social functioning.

Many participants expressed feeling dismissed, unheard, or blamed by healthcare providers for their PCOS symptoms. Some participants were explicitly told they were “making up” their symptoms, or that their symptoms were purely psychological. Others were blamed for having PCOS or for the severity of their symptoms. These experiences align with literature on medical gaslighting, in which patients question the legitimacy of their symptoms following dismissive clinical encounters [38–40]. Gaslighting leads victims to question what they know about themselves and is a common experience among women with chronic diseases, including PCOS [38]. Social inequities throughout history shape current experiences of medical gaslighting which disproportionately affects women, racial and ethnic minorities, and LGBTQ + individuals [38, 39]. Reviews of medical gaslighting indicate that women’s symptoms are often minimized or attributed to psychological causes [41, 42], resulting in delayed diagnosis, trauma, mistrust in healthcare professionals, and overall disengagement with the healthcare system [39, 41, 43]. Medical gaslighting also requires women to carry an unnecessarily heavy burden of self-advocacy in healthcare settings [41, 43, 44]. These experiences had lasting effects on participants’ healthcare trajectories. While some sought more supportive providers, others disengaged from conventional care, which may increase risk for untreated metabolic disease, missed screenings, and worsening mental health [39, 41, 43]. Participants’ narratives suggest that validation from providers was strongly associated with hope and continued engagement in care.

Several participants described prolonged and frustrating diagnostic journeys marked by negative provider interactions and oversimplified advice such as “just lose weight” despite the metabolic complexity of PCOS. Interactions such as these reinforced participants’ fear of judgment, intensified existing stigma, and serve as substantial barriers to diagnosis and treatment. These are examples of health-related communicative disenfranchisement which has been demonstrated to be associated with dehumanization, shame, and significant psychological distress in women with chronic illness [38, 43]. These concerns highlight the need for providers to be able to address health communication and education using more inclusive models. Considering socio-economic, environmental, and other aspects of a patient’s life may help to provide more personalized care. Some examples of small changes may include weight-inclusive language and the use of health literacy practices to improve person centered health communication.

Hormonal birth control was frequently presented as the primary treatment option, often with limited explanation of the rationale for its use as one line of treatment for PCOS. Similar concerns have been documented elsewhere [22, 27, 45]. Although many participants understood its potential benefits, some perceived the recommendation as prescriptive rather than collaborative. These experiences underscore the importance of shared decision-making, particularly for chronic conditions requiring long-term management [19, 22, 46]. Despite the advantages of contraception, younger women are increasingly hesitant to use hormonal contraception due to the real and perceived impacts of exogenous hormones on health and well-being [22, 45, 47, 48]. All patients, including women with PCOS, deserve clear communication regarding treatment goals, the risks of treating or not treating, benefits, and alternative treatment options.

Limited provider knowledge and systemic healthcare constraints likely contribute to dismissiveness and narrow treatment approaches [12, 22, 46, 49–55]. Because PCOS presents heterogeneously, effective management requires individualized care and improved clinician education. Structural changes that support patient-centered consultation are warranted.

Participants also described inadequate education about PCOS in both clinical and social settings. A 2025 study similarly identified low PCOS knowledge among young adult women [56]. Lack of awareness may contribute to misunderstanding, marginalization, and delayed care. Low knowledge is common across many chronic diseases. A 2024 systematic review of 84 qualitative studies of women with chronic disease identified limited knowledge and understanding as a barrier to self-management. Management of many PCOS symptoms and sequelae, including metabolic risks, relies on or is augmented by self-managed activities such as physical activity and adherence to dietary recommendations. Broader dissemination of accurate, accessible PCOS information may improve self-management and healthcare engagement [51].

Stigma and fear of judgment from family, friends, and strangers emerged as persistent themes. These experiences constrained social interactions and contributed to negative mental health and poor body image. Participants reported feeling reduced to stereotypes related to weight, appearance, or infertility. These findings mirror prior U.S. studies describing PCOS as alienating [22, 23]. Similar associations between stigma and depression have been observed in other chronic conditions, including multiple sclerosis [57], and depression remains disproportionately prevalent among women with PCOS [19, 22, 23, 58–61].

Body image concerns were prominent, and many women discussed how they felt about their body in relation to their self-worth and expectations of femininity. Some participants described feelings of shame or guilt associated with existing in a larger body subject to the judgment and shame prevalent in modern society. Even women who visually appeared to have lean body size expressed lack of confidence in their appearance and a desire to lose weight. This is consistent with previous research finding that many women with PCOS struggle to accept their body size, shape, and characteristics [22, 23, 62, 63]. These difficulties are compounded by metabolic and hormonal changes which complicate efforts to change aspects of their bodies they dislike.

Prior research has linked higher BMI to reduced body and life satisfaction, which is consistent with our findings [62, 64–67]. Although obesity does not uniformly reduce self-esteem [62–64, 67], body dissatisfaction is associated with anxiety and depression among women with PCOS [65, 68, 69]. Our findings support prior evidence that infertility, hirsutism, and menstrual irregularities independently affect body image and self-esteem [62]. A 2022 systematic review demonstrated that psychological interventions may be effective in improving body image in women with PCOS [70].

Mental health challenges, including depression and anxiety, were widely reported. Women with PCOS have approximately four times the odds of depression compared with peers without PCOS [71]. Although appearance-related symptoms may contribute, elevated depression persists even after controlling for sequelae, suggesting that something inherent to PCOS may contribute to depression risk [71–73].

Participants highlighted the impact of PCOS on intimate relationships, particularly regarding fertility desires and expectations, body image, and understanding how PCOS was affecting their bodies [74]. Concerns around potential infertility and the subsequent impact on current or future partners was repeatedly expressed, with many describing feeling that PCOS left them unable to fulfill socially mandated roles related to pregnancy and childbearing. Much of the prior relational research on PCOS has focused on sexual function, and sexual satisfaction or marital satisfaction [26, 29, 30, 75–77]. These studies consistently find increased sexual dissatisfaction and marital strain in couples affected by PCOS [26, 28–30, 75–77]. This strain is often attributed to infertility and reduced quality of life [26, 30, 75, 77].

One study by Dowdy found that among adolescents with PCOS, caregivers often believed the adolescent had full control over their body and was the cause of their unpleasant symptoms, especially weight gain [78]. Similar misunderstandings may occur in adults with PCOS resulting in negative relational effects. Positive intimate relationships are an important and desirable aspect of adult life for many. Intimate partners can serve as key sources of physical, mental, and social support [28, 79]. Conversely, stress in partner relationships is associated with negative well-being [80, 81]. Misunderstandings from partners and family members may exacerbate relational strain [28, 77–79]. Enhancing partner education may improve relational support and well-being.

Strengths and limitations

This study contributes to a limited body of qualitative research conducted on women with PCOS in the U.S and provides a detailed account of their lived experiences. The findings underscore the need for improved healthcare practices for complex chronic conditions such as PCOS, including more individualized approaches and provider communication that extends beyond menstrual regulation. Broader society implications include the impact of PCOS-related stigma on body image, fertility expectations, social isolation and mental health.

Limitations include volunteer bias and reliance on self-reported PCOS diagnoses. Participants were recruited from PCOS support groups on Facebook, and diagnoses were not independently verified. Only a limited number of sociodemographic variables relevant to data collection were collected. The absence of socioeconomic variables such as income and education limits contextual interpretation of our findings, particularly regarding healthcare access and treatment-seeking behavior. Future qualitative work on PCOS could benefit from incorporating these measures to better situate participant experiences within broader structural contexts. Additionally, preferential sampling by race and gender identity to increase demographic diversity may limit transferability of findings to populations not represented in this sample.

Conclusion

This study examined the lived experiences of women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), emphasizing its physical, psychological, and relational effects. Participants described PCOS as pervasive in daily life, extending beyond irregular menstruation and hirsutism to include chronic fatigue, pain, and significant mental health challenges. Social isolation and stigma were frequently reported, along with perceived gaps in provider knowledge and limited treatment options, often centered on hormonal contraception. Experiences of dismissiveness contributed to distrust in the healthcare system and, in some cases, discontinuation of care. Given the prevalence of PCOS, improved clinician education, expanded treatment approaches, and patient-centered communication are essential to addressing its multidimensional burden.
