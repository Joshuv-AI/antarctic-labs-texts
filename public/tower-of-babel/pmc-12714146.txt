Postoperative Cutaneous Sensory Recovery After Different Surgical Approaches for Microtia and Ear Reconstruction
Authors: Sierra H. Willens, Leonardo Alaniz, Ja’Neil Humphrey, Nikhil Shah, James Antongiovanni, Chizoba A. Mosieri, Daniel C. Jaffurs, Raj M. Vyas, Miles J. Pfaff
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:The most common means of ear reconstruction include a cutaneous flap (CF) or a fascial flap with skin graft (FSG). Each approach has advantages and disadvantages; however, sensory outcomes have yet to be compared. This study aimed to compare sensory outcomes between these 2 ear reconstruction approaches.Methods:Pediatric and adult patients with microtia or who underwent ear reconstruction were included. Sensory recovery was assessed using a 5-filament Semmes-Weinstein monofilament test sensory score before reconstruction, when available, and after single- or 2-stage reconstruction. When available, the ear remnant and the contralateral ear were used as controls.Results:Sensory data were collected from 2022 to 2024. Twenty-eight patients (32 reconstructed ears) were analyzed. Eighteen patients (20 ears) underwent autologous single- or 2-stage CF reconstruction using the Nagata technique (or a modification). Ten patients (12 ears) underwent FSG reconstruction with either porous polyethylene or cartilage constructs. Intragroup comparison of CF patients demonstrated clinically comparable sensation between pre- (ear remnant) and postoperative measurements of the reconstructed ear (as well as when compared with the contralateral ear). Intragroup comparison of FSG patients demonstrated significantly reduced sensation postoperatively when compared with the contralateral ear. The least sensitive regions after surgery included the helix/antihelix and conchal bowl. Intergroup comparison between CF and FSG patients revealed significantly improved sensation in the CF group.Conclusions:CF-based reconstruction demonstrates improved sensation over FSG-based reconstruction. These results add to the list of advantages and disadvantages of each approach and should be considered when treating patients with ear differences.

Takeaways

Question: Does the type of soft tissue coverage used in microtia reconstruction affect long-term cutaneous sensation?

Findings: In a retrospective and prospectively followed cohort of 28 patients, cutaneous flap–based reconstruction resulted in significantly better postoperative sensation than fascial flap with skin graft approaches, regardless of construct type. Sensation in cutaneous flap cases approached that of the unaffected ear, whereas fascial flap with skin graft cases demonstrated diminished sensitivity, particularly in the helix, conchal bowl, and tragus.

Meaning: Soft tissue coverage—not implant type—may be the key determinant of sensory recovery following ear reconstruction.

INTRODUCTION

Microtia is a congenital difference characterized by hypoplastic malformation of the auricle and occurs in 2.06 per 10,000 births.1 Treatment entails partial or complete reconstruction of the cartilage and skin envelope. Although reconstruction with autologous costal cartilage remains the gold standard, implant-based reconstruction with porous polyethylene products, such as MEDPOR (Stryker, Durham, NC) and Su-Por (Poriferous, LLC, Newnan, GA), has emerged as an alternative to traditional means of reconstruction.

The most common means of autologous-based reconstruction follows a 2-stage approach, as popularized by Firmin2 and Nagata.3–7 This approach entails fabrication of an autologous costal cartilage construct with a cutaneous skin pocket in the first stage, followed by elevation of the construct during the second stage. Conversely, an implant or cartilage construct can be covered by a fascial flap with skin graft (FSG) in a single stage.8 Relatively few studies have directly compared these types of reconstructions, and of those, the focus has primarily been on aesthetic outcomes or complication rates.9,10 Although sensory input to the auricle is well described (Fig. 1), there is a lack of outcomes data on sensory recovery in microtia and ear reconstruction. Preservation of protective sensation in reconstructed tissue is important, as it maintains the ability to detect potentially harmful stimuli, such as sharp objects, pressure, and temperature. In a recent systematic review,11 only 2 comprehensive studies analyzed protective sensory outcomes in 2-stage autologous-based cutaneous coverage reconstruction using monofilament tests,12,13 whereas no studies have measured and compared sensation in patients who have undergone FSG-based reconstruction with alloplastic, autologous, or allogenic constructs.

This study sought to compare sensory outcomes in cutaneous flap (CF) versus FSG-based reconstruction techniques in patients with microtia or other ear deformities. We hypothesized that CF-based reconstruction resulted in superior sensory outcomes when compared with FSG-based reconstruction. The results of this study provide new insight into the sensory outcomes of each approach to ear reconstruction to better inform surgeons and prospective patients contemplating ear reconstruction.

METHODS

This study was approved by the institutional review board. All patients who underwent microtia or ear reconstruction secondary to trauma or cancer with available sensory data were included. Exclusion criteria included patients who lacked documented sensory examinations or had cognitive impairment that precluded reliable sensory examination reporting.

Sensory recovery outcomes were evaluated using the Semmes-Weinstein monofilament test (SWMT) (range: 0.07–4 g monofilaments), a standard tool for assessing sensory threshold recovery. This test was administered before and after reconstruction. Only patients of the senior author (M.J.P.) were prospectively followed and thus had sensory examinations before the intervention. All patients had postoperative and, when available for unilateral cases, contralateral sensory examinations. All sensory examinations were performed by the senior author (M.J.P.), minimizing variability and ensuring consistency in the assessment process. For patients with unilateral microtia, the contralateral normal ear served as a control. The sensory examination focused on the following 4 regions of the external ear: the helix/antihelix, tragus, bowl, and lobule; the untreated ear served as a control for comparison in unilateral cases (Fig. 1).

Two groups were compared in this study. The first group consisted of patients who underwent a 2-stage3–7 or single-stage14 CF-based reconstruction with costal cartilage. The second group consisted of patients who underwent FSG with MEDPOR or allogenic costal cartilage (Extearna; MTF Biologics, Edison, NJ). For microtia, patient and surgeon preference and patient characteristics (eg, low hairline, paucity of surrounding skin) helped determine which type of reconstruction was performed. Monofilament testing was applied 3 times per patient encounter, and the average score of these attempts was recorded. Given that monofilament sets test specific integer forces, the median, rather than the average, SWMT score was used to determine overall sensation scores for all patients, providing a central measure of sensitivity outcomes. Complications were reported only if they required operative intervention more than 30 days after surgery and occurred within the prospectively followed cohort.

Within the FSG group, 3 nonmicrotia patients were included (2 porous polyethylene and 1 donor cartilage). One patient underwent an FSG-based reconstruction for a defect involving more than 50% of the right ear following melanoma excision. Another patient, with a history of a 45% total body surface area flame burn, lost the superior half of the right ear, which was reconstructed using the FSG-based approach. Finally, 1 patient with an auricular hematoma required FSG-based reconstruction for a 5 × 6 cm defect of the right ear. In these patients, the contralateral ear served as a control. Surrounding sensation along the ipsilateral ear in these patients was grossly intact before surgery.

For statistical analysis, both inter- and intragroup comparisons were performed between both the groups. A Mann-Whitney U test was conducted to determine differences in sensation between both groups, and a Friedman test was conducted to determine topographical differences in sensitivity between the 4 external ear regions within each group. Dunn–Bonferroni post hoc tests were subsequently carried out to determine which regions were significantly different in sensitivity among both groups. Preoperative sensation, when available, was analyzed with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test to compare preoperative and postoperative sensitivity. For all patients with unilateral conditions, a Wilcoxon signed-rank test was conducted to compare postoperative sensation to the unaffected, contralateral ear.

RESULTS

Sensory data from a total of 28 patients (32 reconstructed ears) were analyzed. Of this cohort, 25 patients had microtia, whereas 3 patients presented for ear reconstruction following either trauma or malignancy (Table 1). Eighteen patients (20 ears) underwent autologous single- or 2-stage CF reconstruction using the Nagata technique. Ten patients (12 ears) underwent FSG reconstruction—10 ears were reconstructed with MEDPOR, and 2 were reconstructed with allogenic costal cartilage. Of those patients followed prospectively (n = 16), only 1 complication in the CF group (delayed wound healing with partial exposure of costal cartilage construct, requiring advancement of soft tissue for closure) was observed. Intragroup Comparison: CF Group

Comparison to Contralateral Scores

In unilateral cases, sensory scores of the CF-reconstructed ear were compared with the contralateral unaffected normal ear. The median SWMT score for the composite contralateral ear versus the summative CF ear were both 0.07 g. More specifically, the median SWMT score for the helix/antihelix was 0.20 g in the CF-reconstructed ear versus 0.07 g in the unaffected ear. Meanwhile, the median SWMT score for the tragus, bowl, and lobule was 0.07 g for both the CF ear and the contralateral normal ear (Fig. 2). Further investigation of these differences revealed that the composite contralateral ear was statistically more sensitive than the summative total score for the CF-reconstructed ear (Z = 2.99; P = 0.003). More specifically, the unaffected tragus (Z = 2.65, P = 0.008), bowl (Z = 2.71, P = 0.007), and helix/antihelix (Z = 3.13, P = 0.002) were more sensitive than the reconstructed tragus, bowl, and helix, respectively. There were no significant differences between the lobules.

Comparison to Preoperative Scores

Fourteen ears had preoperative sensory recordings of the remnant, which were subsequently compared with postoperative recordings. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests showed that the remnant (preoperatively) was more sensitive on the tragus (Z = 2.83, P = 0.005), the bowl (Z = 2.89, P = 0.004), and the helix/antihelix (Z = 3.42, P < 0.001). There were no significant differences between the remnant and the lobule.

Intragroup Comparison: FSG Group

Comparison to Contralateral Scores

The fascial-flap group primarily underwent single-stage MEDPOR-based reconstruction. In 2 cases, however, an FSG was used with donor costal cartilage (Extearna; MTF Biologics). In unilateral cases, sensory SWMT scores of the FSG-reconstructed ear were compared with the contralateral unaffected ear. The median SWMT score for the composite contralateral ear versus the summative FSG ear was 0.07 versus 4.00 g, respectively. More specifically, the tragus, bowl, and helix/antihelix were 4.00 g for the fascial flap-based reconstructed ear versus 0.07 g in the unaffected ear (Fig. 2). Further investigation of these differences via a Wilcoxon signed-rank test revealed that the contralateral ear was more sensitive than the FSG-reconstructed ear (Z = 2.57, P = 0.010). More specifically, the unaffected tragus (Z = 2.43, P = 0.015), bowl (Z = 2.00, P = 0.046), and helix/antihelix (Z = 2.60, P = 0.009) were more sensitive than the reconstructed tragus, bowl, and helix/antihelix, respectively. However, similar to the CF group, there were no significant differences between the unaffected and reconstructed lobules.

Intergroup Comparison: Comparing Sensation Between CF and FSG

Compared with the FSG group, the CF group had significantly higher sensitivity scores in the reconstructed ear as a whole (Z = 4.75, P < 0.001) and in its subparts, including the tragus (Z = 4.77, P < 0.001), bowl (Z = 4.30, P < 0.001), and helix/antihelix (Z = 4.87, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2). There were no significant differences in lobule sensitivity between groups (Z = 0.63, P = 0.862). Within the CF group, the helix/antihelix was the least sensitive region. These trends were further reflected in a subanalysis of microtia patients alone (29 ears). When comparing sensation of the reconstructed ears as a whole, CF-based reconstructions yielded superior sensation compared with FSG-based reconstructions (Z = 4.49, P < 0.001). There were also significant differences between the tragus (Z = 4.50, P < 0.001), the bowl (Z = 4.30, P < 0.001), and the helix/antihelix (Z = 4.63, P < 0.001). There were no significant differences in the lobule between groups (Z = 0.63, P = 0.862).

DISCUSSION

When contemplating treatment options for microtia and ear reconstruction, the discussion often centers on the source of the construct: autologous or alloplastic. Less discussed is the soft tissue coverage. Fascial-flap coverage with an overlying skin graft is necessary to mitigate complication rates with alloplastic reconstruction. With autologous reconstruction, there remains a choice of CF-based reconstruction, which has traditionally been a 2-staged approach, and a single-staged, FSG-based approach. The results from this study are the first in the literature to demonstrate that sensory outcomes are superior in CF-based reconstruction when compared with FSG-based reconstruction, contributing to the ongoing discussion around ear reconstruction approaches and equipping surgeons and patients with more data to make better-informed decisions.

The SWMT is the primary clinical tool for screening and monitoring tactile sensation. At its core, this test is meant to assess the threshold of tactile sensitivity, which refers to the minimum amount of stimulation required to elicit a sensory response. This is especially valuable when testing for protective sensation, which is often a concern for children with reconstruction.15 When it comes to the recovery of sensation after ear reconstruction, all current studies have reported on autologous reconstruction with CF coverage. Oberg et al16 found that the antihelix and helix of reconstructed ears had significantly lower heat sensitivity and were also more likely than other areas of the ear to experience decreased sensation. In line with these results, our study showed that across the reconstructed auricle, CF ears yielded lower monofilament thresholds than FSG ears at the helix/antihelix, concha, and tragus; the lobule did not differ (Fig. 2). These differences likely translate to better day-to-day protective sensation. Denadai et al12 noted recovered sensation at 12 months postreconstruction, with the exception of the postauricular sulcus, which was reconstructed with skin grafting after elevation.

We observed a reduction in sensory recovery in those who underwent FSG-based reconstruction. When using fascial flaps with an overlying skin graft, innervation occurs via a phenomenon known as nerve ingrowth or neurotization. Surrounding nerves from the wound base regenerate, and axons gradually grow into the recipient skin graft. Over time, these nerve fibers undergo remodeling and maturation, allowing for communication with sensory receptors in the graft and surrounding tissues.17 Although the temporoparietal fascial flap does contain the auriculotemporal nerve branches, the results from this study suggest that this may not be sufficient to generate perceptible sensation. One hypothesis is that the temporoparietal fascial flap’s auriculotemporal nerve branches invest the more superficial side of the fascial flap, which is often turned over to cover the construct and thus does not contact the skin graft.

The results of our sensory study demonstrate that those within the CF group exhibited significant improvements in sensitivity scores postoperatively, with sensory values closely approaching those of the contralateral, unaffected ear. However, a statistically significant difference between the CF-reconstructed and contralateral ears was appreciated for several reasons. First, this group had more data points, effectively increasing the study’s power to detect smaller differences. Second, although the median scores were similar between the reconstructed and contralateral ears in the CF group, the distribution of scores around these medians reflected enough variability to produce a significant test result. Finally, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test is highly sensitive to paired differences, even when the overall magnitude is small. In contrast, the FSG group’s sensitivity scores were also statistically significantly lower, but this group demonstrated markedly less-sensitive ears when compared with the unaffected ear, highlighting the clinical significance of this result.

The FSG group in this study consisted of 12 MEDPOR and 2 donor costal cartilage patients. Within this group, sensation was blunted when compared with the contralateral ear and when compared with CF-based patients. Although intragroup comparison could not be reliably performed due to insufficient numbers, SWMT findings were overall similar between alloplastic and costal cartilage–based patients, suggesting that soft tissue coverage, not construct type, dictates postoperative sensory outcomes. Future studies directly comparing the 2 would be necessary to ascertain any influence the construct type may have on postoperative sensation.

This study is not without limitations. The inherently subjective nature of the SWMT, despite being a standardized test, presents its own unique challenge of variability dependent on external factors such as attention levels, which could impact our overall findings. Additionally, differences in underlying etiology—congenital, traumatic, or neoplastic—may result in variable auricular innervation patterns, introducing potential bias. Furthermore, our FSG patients (ie, MEDPOR patients) mostly presented from outside hospitals with a lack of preoperative baseline sensory recordings or complication rates, further limiting comparison. In addition, sensory testing occurred later in the FSG cohort than in the CF cohort, creating an inherent bias toward long-term outcomes in FSG; nonetheless, this time difference also implies that the sensory gains recorded in the FSG group may arise earlier and accelerate more rapidly than those seen after CF reconstruction. Also, it is important to point out that in CF-based reconstruction using the Nagata technique, the design of the flap and incision lines is often tailored to the individual patient, depending on the shape and condition of the residual ear. Similarly, in the FSG approach, the size and configuration of the skin graft are influenced by the specific characteristics of the auricular defect. These variables have the potential to influence postoperative sensation and should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. However, this level of customization stands in line with the principles of autologous reconstruction and in contrast to alloplastic techniques, such as MEDPOR-based reconstruction, which typically rely on standardized, prefabricated implants with limited capacity for patient-specific adaptation. Finally, cold and thermal sensitivities were not assessed in this study, limiting the strength of the study. Nonetheless, our results align with previous studies evaluating sensation after ear reconstruction11–13 and, for the first time, extend those findings by directly contrasting CF and FSG techniques.

CONCLUSIONS

This study aimed to compare sensory outcomes in ear reconstruction using 2 different techniques: CF-based reconstruction with autologous costal cartilage and fascial flap–based reconstruction with implants or cadaveric costal cartilage. This study delineated the sensory outcomes associated with these 2 reconstructive approaches. Findings revealed that CF-based reconstruction resulted in significantly higher sensitivity scores in most regions compared with FSG. Overall, CF-based reconstructions demonstrated better sensory outcomes, suggesting the importance of soft tissue coverage in enhancing postoperative sensation. Although soft tissue, not construct type, seemed to determine postoperative sensation in this study, it is important to note that porous polyethylene constructs require fascial tissue for coverage to limit complication rates.18 These results provide valuable insights for surgical decision-making in ear reconstruction, highlighting the sensory benefits of CF techniques. In summary, when sensation is a priority, CF coverage should be favored in counseling, whereas FSG reconstructions should appropriately set expectations that the helix/antihelix may remain comparatively less sensitive.
