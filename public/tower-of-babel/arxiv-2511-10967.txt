Autocovariance and Optimal Design for Random Walk Metropolis-Hastings Algorithm
Jingyi Zhang, James C. Spall 1

Abstract
The Metropolis–Hastings algorithm has been extensively studied in the estimation and simulation literature, with most prior work focusing on convergence behavior and asymptotic theory. However, its covariance structure—an important statistical property for both theory and implementation—remains less understood. In this work, we provide new theoretical insights into the scalar case, focusing primarily on symmetric unimodal target distributions with symmetric random walk proposals, where we also establish an optimal proposal design. In addition, we derive some more general results beyond this setting. For the high-dimensional case, we relate the covariance matrix to the classical 0.23 average acceptance rate tuning criterion.

Introduction

Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) provides a general framework for sampling from complex distributions by constructing a Markov chain with the desired stationary distribution
[
15
]
. This approach enables efficient approximation of expectations and integrals that are otherwise analytically intractable, and it has found widespread applications in statistics, engineering, and control
[

]

In particular, Monte Carlo particle filters can discretize the state space to enable efficient MAP sequence estimation via dynamic programming
[
4
]
. MCMC has also been applied to Bayesian inference for stochastic kinetic models by sampling joint posteriors of parameters and latent data
[
5
]
, and to system identification for Bayesian parameter estimation in complex dynamical models beyond the reach of traditional techniques
[
12
]
.
In the control domain, particle MCMC has been applied to infer latent trajectories and nonparametric dynamics in Gaussian process state-space models
[
3
]
, MCMC has been used to generate model uncertainty sets in nonlinear dynamical systems via Hamiltonian Monte Carlo sampling of high-dimensional transfer operators
[
16
]
, and ghost sampling, an MCMC-based technique, has been introduced to efficiently simulate power disturbances conditional on rate-of-change-of-frequency violations in power systems
[
10
]
; moreover, MCMC has also been integrated with Bernstein approximation techniques to handle non-affine and dependent chance constrained optimization problems, providing an efficient approach to high-dimensional integration
[
18
]
.

The Metropolis–Hastings algorithm is one of the foundational methods in MCMC, with widespread applications in model estimation, physics, and system identification
[
6
,
2
]
. The theoretical study of MH has been extensive, with a large body of work devoted to convergence behavior and asymptotic properties of estimators
[
17
,
14
,
1
]
.
An important line of research in MCMC has focused on the
scaling and efficiency of Metropolis-Hastings algorithms in high-dimensional settings. For Gaussian random
walk Metropolis (RWM) algorithms, the efficiency of the chain is maximized when the
average acceptance rate is tuned to approximately
0.23

[
13
]
. This “
0.23
rule” has since
become a widely used guideline for practitioners, providing a concrete and interpretable
criterion for tuning proposal variances in high-dimensional problems.

While convergence and asymptotics have been well developed, comparatively little is known about the covariance structure, or equivalently the autocorrelation, of the MH chain itself.
MCMC methods are closely connected with statistical estimation, providing a means to approximate otherwise intractable quantities.
Within this framework, the covariance structure of the samples plays a central role in determining estimation accuracy and efficiency. Further knowledge of the covariances of the samples relates strategies for variance reduction, and potentially guides practical implementation of MCMC algorithms.

This work aims to address this gap.
We first study the scalar case of MH with symmetric random walk proposals, focusing on symmetric unimodal target distributions. In this setting, we establish new theoretical results on the covariance structure and propose an optimal design for the proposal distribution of symmetric random walk form. In addition, we present some general results beyond this restrictive setting. For the high-dimensional case,
under the same setup as Roberts and Gelman
[
13
]
,
we relate the unit-lag covariance matrix to the classical 0.23 average acceptance rate criterion, providing new theoretical connections between covariance properties and established tuning principles.

Before beginning the technical analysis, we emphasize that Metropolis-Hastings chains are not necessarily positively correlated.
As a counterexample, consider the target density
\pi(x)=\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and the proposal density

q(y|x)=\mathcal{N}(-cx,2)
with
c\in(0,1)
.
In this setting, proposed moves tend to push the chain in the opposite direction of the current state,
so that
x_{t}>0
typically leads to
x_{t+1}<0
and vice versa.
Since the covariance reduces to
\mathrm{Cov}(X_{t},X_{t+1})=\mathbb{E}[X_{t}X_{t+1}]
under the symmetry of
\pi
,
the systematic flipping effect yields negative correlation. Both theoretical analysis and numerical evaluations confirm that the covariance is strictly negative for all
c\in(0,1)
.

Section VI concludes with remarks and directions for future work.

II
Preliminaries

Monte Carlo methods provide a general tool for estimation and simulation when analytical solutions are unavailable.
MCMC extends this idea by constructing a Markov chain with the desired stationary distribution, enabling sampling from complex or high-dimensional models
[
15
,
6
]
.
Within this framework, the Metropolis-Hastings (MH) algorithm stands out as a versatile and widely used scheme. In this section, we present the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm, establish the notation, and outline the problem setup.

II-A

Metropolis-Hastings algorithm

Assume a continuous state space with probability density functions. The overall aim of MCMC methods such as Metropolis-Hastings algorithm is to generate samples from a target density of interest, say
\pi(x)
. The MH algorithm is defined by a proposal density
q(y|x)
for transitioning from state
x
to state
y
, and a target density
\pi
of interest.
The MH algorithm accepts a proposed move with probability

\alpha(x,y)=\min\!\left(1,\frac{\pi(y)q(x|y)}{\pi(x)q(y|x)}\right),

and otherwise the chain remains at
x
.
This induces a Markov transition kernel

\mathcal{K}(x,y)=\alpha(x,y)q(y|x)+\delta(y-x)\big(1-r(x)\big),

where
\delta(\cdot)
is the Dirac measure at
0
, and

r(x)=\int\alpha(x,y)\,q(y|x)\,\mathrm{d}y

is the overall acceptance probability at current state
[
6
]
. Under ergodicity and irreducibility assumptions,
the Markov chain converges to the unique invariant distribution.
Let
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B})
denote the underlying measurable state space,
with
\mathcal{X}
the state space and
\mathcal{B}
its associated
\sigma
-algebra, then

\pi(y)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{K}(x,y)\,\pi(dx)\quad\pi\text{-a.s. on }(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}).

where “
\pi
-a.s.” indicates that the equality holds for all
y
except on a
\pi
-null set (i.e. almost surely).

The above formulation specifies the acceptance mechanism and transition kernel in mathematical terms.
For clarity, we now summarize the MH algorithm in a step-by-step procedure.

Step 1.

Initialization.
Choose the initial state
X_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
and set
t=0
.

Step 2.

Given
X_{t}
, generate a candidate
Y
according to the proposal density
q(\cdot|X_{t})
.

Step 3.

Compute the acceptance probability

\alpha(X_{t},Y)=\min\!\left(1,\frac{\pi(Y)q(X_{t}|Y)}{\pi(X_{t})q(Y|X_{t})}\right).

Then set

X_{t+1}=\begin{cases}Y,&\text{with probability }\alpha(X_{t},Y),\\[6.0pt]
X_{t},&\text{with probability }1-\alpha(X_{t},Y).\end{cases}

Increment
t\leftarrow t+1
.

Step 4.

Repeat Steps 2-3 until the terminal iteration
n
is reached, yielding the chain
(X_{0},\dots,X_{n})
.

Our aim in this paper is to analyze the autocovariance structure of the sequence
\{X_{0},X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{t}\}
produced by the MH algorithm. We focus on the larget
t
(post-“burn-in”) where the process has achieved a stationary probability distribution.

II-C

Problem Setup

Consider the unit-lag covariance of the output sequence of the MH algorithm under the stationarity assumption,
i.e., the current state
X_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot)
, which implies that the next state
X_{t+1}\sim\pi(\cdot)
as well.
Stationarity holds provided that the chain is irreducible, aperiodic, and satisfies detailed balance with respect to
\pi(\cdot)
,
ensuring that
\pi(\cdot)
is the unique invariant distribution
[
6
]
; these conditions are known to hold for an MH algorithm.

Our goal is to investigate the multi-lag covariance of a symmetric random walk MH chain,
with particular interest in the structure of the covariance and the optimal design of the proposal distribution.
We begin with the unit-lag covariance,
\mathrm{Cov}(X_{t},X_{t+1})
.

III
Main results

In this section, we present our main theoretical results, deriving an analytic expression for the unit-lag covariance of a Metropolis-Hastings chain with a symmetric random-walk proposal and a symmetric unimodal target. This serves as the foundation for the optimal proposal design studied in Section IV.

Lemma 3.1
.

For a Metropolis-Hastings chain, the general formula for the unit-lag covariance is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(X_{t},X_{t+1})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\pi}^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\iint(x-y)^{2}\pi(x)q(y|x)\alpha(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y.

(1)

Recall that, unless otherwise specified, all integrals are taken over
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Using tower property,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(X_{t},X_{t+1})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X_{t}X_{t+1}]-\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]\mathbb{E}[X_{t+1}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X_{t+1}|X_{t}]\cdot X_{t}]-\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]\mathbb{E}[X_{t+1}].

By stationariy, we know
\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[X_{t+1}]=\mu
. Then computing
\mathbb{E}[X_{t+1}|X_{t}=x]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{t+1}|X_{t}=x]

\displaystyle=\int y\cdot\mathcal{K}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int y\cdot\alpha(x,y)q(y|x)\,\mathrm{d}y+x\cdot(1-r(x))

\displaystyle=x+\int(y-x)\alpha(x,y)q(y|x)\,\mathrm{d}y.

Then by reversibility, i.e.
\pi(x)q(y|x)=\pi(y)q(x|y)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X_{t+1}|X_{t}]\cdot X_{t}]

\displaystyle=\iint x(y-x)\alpha(x,y)q(y|x)\pi(x)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x+\int x^{2}\pi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\iint(x-y)^{2}\alpha(x,y)q(y|x)\pi(x)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x+\int x^{2}\pi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(2)

Applying the Tonelli’s theorem
[
11
]
to justify the interchange of integrals in (
III
) since the integrand
(x-y)^{2}\alpha(x,y)q(y|x)\pi(x)
is nonnegative, and using stationarity, we obtain (
3.1
).
∎

If we further suppose the proposal density is of random walk form (not necessarily symmetric), (
3.1
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(X_{t},X_{t+1})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\pi}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\iint(x-y)^{2}\phi(y-x)\pi(x)\alpha(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

(3)

since the proposal density
q(\cdot|x)
is of random-walk form, i.e.,
Y=X+Z
with
Z\sim\phi(\cdot)
, we have

q(y|x)=\phi(y-x)
and
q(x|y)=\phi(x-y)
. Substituting these expressions into (
3.1
) yields the desired result.

Moreover, when the proposal density is of symmetric random-walk form (not necessarily Gaussian),
that is,
q(y|x)=q(x|y)=\phi(|x-y|)
, equation (
3.1
) takes the following form

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(X_{t},X_{t+1})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\pi}^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\iint(x-y)^{2}\phi(y-x)\min\{\pi(x),\pi(y)\}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y.

(4)

Lemma
3.1
expresses the unit-lag covariance in the quadratic form
(x-y)^{2}
, which naturally connects to random-walk proposals and enables explicit analysis. Building on this, Corollary
3.2
follows when the proposal is further assumed to be of random-walk form.

Corollary 3.2
.

Suppose the proposal density is random-walk (not necessarily symmetric), then the unit-lag covariance is invariant under translation of the target density. Define
\pi_{c}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\pi(x-c),\,c\in\mathbb{R}
, then

\mathrm{Cov}_{\pi_{c}}(X_{t},X_{t+1})=\mathrm{Cov}_{\pi}(X_{t},X_{t+1}).

Proof.

Recalling that the proposal
q(\cdot|x)
is of the random walk form,
q(y|x)=\phi(y-x)
and
q(x|y)=\phi(x-y)
. Thus by definition, the acceptance rate under the shifted target density
\pi_{c}(\cdot)
is

\displaystyle\alpha_{c}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\min\bigg\{1,\frac{\pi(y-c)\,\phi(x-y)}{\pi(x-c)\,\phi(y-x)}\bigg\}.

Recall from (
3.1
) that

\mathrm{Cov}_{\pi_{c}}(X_{t},X_{t+1})
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}_{\pi_{c}}(X_{t},X_{t+1})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\pi}^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\iint(x-y)^{2}\phi(x-y)\pi_{c}(x)\alpha_{c}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\pi}^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\iint(x-y)^{2}\phi(x-y)\pi(x-c)\alpha(x-c,y-c)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\pi}^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\iint(s-t)^{2}\phi(s-t)\pi(s)\alpha(s,t)\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Cov}_{\pi}(X_{t},X_{t+1})

where we applied the change of variables
x-c=s
,
y-c=t
, noting that the variance is invariant under translation.
Hence we may simply denote
\mathrm{Cov}_{\pi_{c}}(X_{t},X_{t+1})
by
\mathrm{Cov}_{\pi}(X_{t},X_{t+1})
.
∎

The argument in Corollary
3.2
extends naturally to higher lags. That is, for lag
k
, the
k
-step transition kernel remains invariant
under translations of the target density
\pi
,
so the covariance is also invariant under translations of the target density. Corollary
3.2
also allows us, without loss of generality, to assume a zero mean (
\mu=0
) when considering symmetric unimodal target densities later.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\phi(x)
be any probability density that is symmetric about 0.
Then,

\displaystyle|x|\phi(x)<\frac{1}{2}\text{ a.s.}

(5)

Proof.

Assume
\exists\,x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
s.t.

|x_{0}|\phi(x_{0})\geq\frac{1}{2}\Rightarrow\phi(x_{0})\geq\frac{1}{2|x_{0}|}.

By symmetry,
\phi(-x_{0})=\phi(x_{0})
, so

\int_{-|x_{0}|}^{|x_{0}|}\phi(x)\,dx\geq\int_{-|x_{0}|}^{|x_{0}|}\frac{1}{2|x_{0}|}\,dx=1.

Since
\phi
is a probability density, equality must hold and
\phi(x)=\frac{1}{2|x_{0}|}
a.s. on
[-|x_{0}|,|x_{0}|]
, i.e.,
\phi
is uniform, that is,

|x|\phi(x)=\frac{|x|}{2|x_{0}|}<\frac{1}{2},\quad\forall\,|x|<|x_{0}|.

At
x=\pm|x_{0}|
, equality may hold pointwise, but probability densities are defined up to sets of measure zero.
We reached a contradiction.
∎

Lemma
3.3
is powerful in that it holds for any symmetric probability density,
including standard distributions such as the normal
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
,
for which
\max_{x}\{|x|\phi(x)\}=e^{-\frac{1}{2}}/{\sqrt{2\pi}}<1/2
. Its general validity makes it an essential tool for the proof of our main theorem.

Theorem 3.4
.

Consider a Metropolis-Hastings chain with a symmetric random-walk proposal density
and a symmetric unimodal target density with mean
\mu
.
Let the centered cumulative distribution function be defined by
\Pi_{\mu}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Pi(x+\mu)
.
Then the unit-lag covariance admits the explicit representation

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(X_{t},X_{t+1})=4\int_{0}^{\infty}x\,[1-\Pi_{\mu}(x)]\,[1-4x\phi(2x)]\,\mathrm{d}x.

(6)

Proof.

First define

\displaystyle f(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int\min\{\pi(x+y),\pi(y)\}\,\mathrm{d}y.

By Corollary
3.2
, we may assume without loss of generality that
\mu=0
, then consider
f(-x)

\displaystyle f(-x)

\displaystyle=\int\min\{\pi(-x+y),\pi(y)\}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\overset{y\mapsto-y}{=}\int\min\{\pi(x+y),\pi(-y)\}\,\mathrm{d}y=f(x)

Hence
f
is even, i.e.,
f(x)=f(-x)
,
\forall x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Recall from equation (
III
) that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(X_{t},X_{t+1})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\pi}^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\iint(x-y)^{2}\phi(y-x)\min\{\pi(x),\pi(y)\}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\pi}^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\int x^{2}\phi(x)\int\min\{\pi(x+y),\pi(y)\}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\pi}^{2}-\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2}\phi(x)\int\min\{\pi(x+y),\pi(y)\}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x.

(7)

where the second equality follows from a change of variables together with the Tonelli’s theorem
[
11
]
and the third equality follows from the fact that
f(\cdot)
is even.

Given the target density is symmetric unimodal, now consider
f(x),\,x\geq 0

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{-x/2}\pi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\;+\;\int_{-x/2}^{\infty}\pi(x+y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad x\geq 0

\displaystyle=\Pi(-x/2)+1-\Pi(x/2)

\displaystyle=2[1-\Pi(x/2)].

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(X_{t},X_{t+1})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\pi}^{2}-2\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2}\phi(x)[1-\Pi(x/2)]\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\pi}^{2}-16\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2}\phi(2x)[1-\Pi(x)]\,\mathrm{d}x.

(8)

We can also express the target variance
\sigma_{\pi}^{2}
via integration by parts as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\pi}^{2}=2\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2}\pi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=4\int_{0}^{\infty}x\,[1-\Pi(x)]\,\mathrm{d}x.

(9)

Substituting (
9
) into (
III
) yields (
6
).

Now we can readily extend the result to the case of any arbitrary symmetric unimodal density with nonzero mean.

By Corollary
3.2
, proposal densities of symmetric random–walk form
q(y|x)=\phi(|y-x|)
is transition-invariant. Then the covariance admits the same form as in the mean–zero case, with
\Pi
replaced by
\Pi_{\mu}
.
∎

Having established the necessary preliminaries, we are now ready to present the main theorem.

Theorem 3.5
.

For a Metropolis-Hastings chain with a symmetric random-walk proposal density
and a symmetric unimodal target density, the unit-lag covariance is strictly positive.

Proof.

Combining Lemma
3.3
and Theorem
3.4
yields

\mathrm{Cov}(X_{t},X_{t+1})=4\int_{0}^{\infty}x\,[1-\Pi_{\mu}(x)]\,[1-4x\phi(2x)]\,\mathrm{d}x>0

This conclusion holds under our standing assumption that the target distribution is non-degenerate, i.e.
\sigma_{\pi}^{2}>0
.
∎

We emphasize that MH chains are not necessarily positively correlated.
If the proposal density deviates from the symmetric random-walk form,
then even under a symmetric unimodal target distribution,
the resulting chain can exhibit strictly negative unit-lag correlation,
as illustrated by the counterexample mentioned in Section I.

IV
Optimal Proposal Design

Based on Theorem
3.4
, we can carry out an optimal proposal design (i.e.  choose the best density
q(y|x)
). We define the optimal proposal design as the choice of a symmetric density
\phi

associated with the proposal
q(y|x)=\phi(y-x)
that minimizes the unit-lag covariance of the Metropolis-Hastings chain under a symmetric unimodal target. This criterion is motivated by the fact that the unit-lag covariance is the leading term in the asymptotic variance expansion of ergodic averages, thereby directly governing estimation efficiency.
Specifically, recall (
6
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(X_{t},X_{t+1})

\displaystyle=4\int_{0}^{\infty}x\,[1-\Pi_{\mu}(x)]\,[1-4x\phi(2x)]\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=4\int_{0}^{\infty}x\,[1-\Pi_{\mu}(x)]\,\mathrm{d}x-16\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2}\phi(2x)[1-\Pi_{\mu}(x)]\,\mathrm{d}x.

(10)

Only the second term depends on
\phi(\cdot)
, and this dependence is linear.
Thus, minimizing
\mathrm{Cov}(X_{t},X_{t+1})
is equivalent to maximizing the second term. If we do not impose the standard regularity condition—namely that the support of the target is contained in the support of the proposal,

\mathrm{Supp}[\pi(\cdot)]\subseteq\mathrm{Supp}[q(\cdot|x)]
, which ensures the chain is irreducible —
then the optimal
\phi(\cdot)
takes the form of a two-point measure, as occurs when the first-order condition admits a unique solution (true for log-concave symmetric unimodal densities)

\phi^{*}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\delta(x-x^{*})+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\delta(x+x^{*}),

where
\delta(\cdot)
is the Dirac measure at 0,
x^{*}=2y^{*}
and

y^{*}\in\arg\max_{y\geq 0}\,y^{2}[1-\Pi_{\mu}(y)].

Note that
\arg\max
may define a set, though for symmetric unimodal target densities it commonly reduces to a unique maximizer.

Now we check if such
y^{*}
exists. First, the finite variance assumption implies
\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]<\infty
, which ensures

\displaystyle\lim_{y\to\infty}w(y)=\lim_{y\to\infty}y^{2}\bigl(1-\Pi(y)\bigr)=0.

(11)

Indeed, since
1-\Pi(x)=\mathbb{P}(X>x)\leq\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]/x^{2}
by Markov’s inequality, the limit can only be
0
, a strictly positive constant, or
\infty
.
The latter two would force
\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]=\infty
, contradicting the finite variance assumption.

From (
11
), and noting that
w(0)=0
,
w(y)>0
for all
y>0
,
\lim_{y\to\infty}w(y)=0
, and
w(\cdot)
is continuous, the Extreme Value Theorem guarantees that both the maximum and the minimum (zero) exist. Hence
w_{\max}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=w(y^{*})
must exist for any non-degenerate target distribution with finite variance.

Equivalently,
y^{*}
can be determined by the first-order condition

\displaystyle 2[1-\Pi_{\mu}(y)]=y\pi_{\mu}(y),

(12)

with the additional requirement that the second derivative at
y^{*}
is negative to ensure optimality.

Remark 4.1
.

equation (
12
) has a unique solution when the target density
\pi(\cdot)
is log-concave.
In this case, define
g(y)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\pi(y)/(1-\Pi(y))
, which is monotone increasing, implying that
h(y)=yg(y)
is strictly increasing.
Hence the equation
h(y)=2
has a unique solution, and since
w(0)=w(\infty)=0
. Moreover, this critical point is necessarily the global maximizer of
w(y)
.
Indeed, if it were a saddle point, then since
w(x)
is continuously differentiable, additional critical points would have to exist, which contradicts the uniqueness.

However, under the regularity condition, the optimization problem becomes an infimum problem rather than a maximization problem. In this case, the maximum achieved by the two-point measure cannot be attained, since the regularity condition is violated. We can prove this rigorously, as shown in Proposition
4.2
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Recall the second term in equation (
IV
). Let
w(y)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=y^{2}[1-\Pi_{\mu}(y)]
for
y\geq 0
and define

\mathcal{J}(\phi)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=4\int_{0}^{\infty}w(s)\,\phi(2s)\,\mathrm{d}s,

for any symmetric proposal density
\phi
about
0
.
Let
w_{\max}=\sup_{y\geq 0}w(y)
and

\mathcal{M}=\{y\geq 0\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,w(y)=w_{\max}\}
, which is nonempty.
If
\phi
is allowed to be a symmetric probability measure,
the supremum
\sup_{\phi}\mathcal{J}(\phi)=w_{\max}
is attained by putting all mass at
\pm 2y
with
y\in\mathcal{M}
.
If
\phi
is required to be a continuous density and
\mathcal{M}
consists only of isolated points, then the supremum is not attained;
instead, one can construct a sequence of increasingly concentrated densities around
\{\pm 2y\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,y\in\mathcal{M}\}
that makes
\mathcal{J}(\phi)
arbitrarily close to
w_{\max}
.

Proof.

By symmetry, with
h(y)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=2\phi(2y)
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\phi)=2\int_{0}^{\infty}w(s)h(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\leq 2\sup_{s\geq 0}w(s)\!\int_{0}^{\infty}h(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=w_{\max}

with equality iff
h
is supported on
\mathcal{M}
.
Thus any symmetric atomic measure supported on
\{\pm 2y\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}y\in\mathcal{M}\}
achieves
w_{\max}
.

If
\phi
is a probability density, then the induced function
h(y)=2\phi(2y)
is continuous in the sense that it cannot concentrate all its probability mass at finitely many points.
The set
\mathcal{M}
consists only of isolated points.
Then away from
\mathcal{M}
, the continuity of
w(\cdot)
guarantees that there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
w_{\max}-w(y)\geq\varepsilon
for all
y
sufficiently far from
\mathcal{M}
.
Since any density
h
must assign positive probability to regions outside arbitrarily small neighborhoods of
\mathcal{M}
, the weighted average
\int_{0}^{\infty}w(s)h(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\leq w_{\max}/2-\varepsilon/2<w_{\max}/2
, and hence
\mathcal{J}(\phi)<w_{\max}
.

Nevertheless, the two-point measure can be approximated arbitrarily well by densities concentrated near the
\{\pm 2y\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}y\in\mathcal{M}\}
, such as narrow Gaussians.
As the concentration increases, the corresponding values of
\mathcal{J}(\phi)
approach
w_{\max}
.
Hence
\sup\mathcal{J}=w_{\max}
, but the maximum is not attained within the class of densities when the maximizers are isolated points.

∎

Thus, when
\phi(\cdot)
is required to be a probability density (by the regularity condition), minimizing the unit-lag covariance becomes an infimum problem. Nevertheless, the value
w_{\max}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{y\geq 0}w(y)
, where
w(y)=y^{2}[1-\Pi(y)]
and
\Pi
is the target cumulative distribution function, can be approximated arbitrarily well. For instance, if
x^{*}=2y^{*}
with
y^{*}\in\arg\max_{y\geq 0}w(y)
, then letting the variance parameter
\sigma^{2}\to 0
in the Gaussian-component bimodal density

\phi(x)\;=\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathcal{N}(x^{*},\sigma^{2})\;+\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathcal{N}(-x^{*},\sigma^{2})\to\phi^{*}\text{ as }\sigma\to 0

yields convergence to the two-point measure
\phi
.

Continuing with the analysis of the unit-lag covariance structure, we next relate it to the “0.23 rule” for random-walk Gaussian Metropolis algorithms via the unit-lag covariance matrix. This classic criterion arises from diffusion limit analysis in high dimensions
[
13
]
and prescribes tuning the average acceptance probability to about
0.23
for optimal efficiency. Here we reinterpret this criterion through the lens of the unit-lag covariance matrix.
We now state Theorem
4.3
, following the notation and the setup of Gelman and Roberts
[
13
]
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and consider the product target

\pi_{d}(\boldsymbol{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}(x_{i}),\qquad\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where each
p_{i}
is a strictly positive
C^{2}
density on
\mathbb{R}
with

\sigma_{i}^{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathrm{Var}_{p_{i}}(X_{i})\in(0,\infty),\,m_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{E}_{p_{i}}\big[(\partial_{x}\log p_{i}(X_{i}))^{2}\big]\in(0,\infty),

and
\mathrm{E}_{p_{i}}[|\partial_{x}\log p_{i}(X_{i})|^{3}],\,\mathrm{E}_{p_{i}}[|\partial_{x}^{2}\log p_{i}(X_{i})|]<\infty
for all
i
.
Consider a stationary random-walk Metropolis chain with the symmetric random walk Gaussian proposal density, i.e.
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{z}
, where
\boldsymbol{z}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\Big(\boldsymbol{0},\tfrac{\ell^{2}}{d}\,\boldsymbol{I}_{d}\Big)
,
\forall\ell>0
,
and
\boldsymbol{I}_{d}
denotes the
d\times d
identity matrix.
Define the averages

\bar{\sigma}_{d}^{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=d^{-1}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}^{2},\,\bar{m}_{d}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=d^{-1}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}m_{i}.
Denote by
\bar{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\lim\limits_{d\to\infty}\bar{m}_{d}
, assuming the limit exists in
(0,\infty)
.
Then the unit-lag covariance matrix satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1})

\displaystyle=\Big(\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1}^{2},\dots,\sigma_{d}^{2})-\frac{\ell^{2}}{2d}\cdot 2\,\Phi\!\big(-\tfrac{\ell\sqrt{\bar{m}}}{2}\big)\,\boldsymbol{I}_{d}\Big)\;+\;\boldsymbol{R}_{d}

(13)

where
\boldsymbol{R}_{d}
is the remainder matrix whose diagonal entries are
o(d^{-1})
and off-diagonal entries are
O(d^{-2})
, so that
\|\boldsymbol{R}_{d}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=o(d^{-1})
, where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{op}}
denotes the operator (spectral) norm, chosen since it directly controls eigenvalues and hence positive definiteness. In particular, the diagonal entries

\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1})_{ii}=\sigma_{i}^{2}\;-\;\frac{\ell^{2}}{2d}\,2\,\Phi\!\Big(-\tfrac{\ell\sqrt{\bar{m}}}{2}\Big)\;+o(d^{-1})

are strictly positive for all sufficiently large d and the matrix
\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1})
is positive definite.

Proof.

By Gelman and Roberts and apply the change of variable, let
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{z}
,

\lim_{d\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\alpha(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{z})]=2\,\Phi\!\big(-\tfrac{\ell\sqrt{\bar{m}}}{2}\big)

and
\boldsymbol{z}\text{ is independent of }\boldsymbol{x}

which implies, for finite large
d

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\alpha(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{z})]=2\,\Phi\!\big(-\tfrac{\ell\sqrt{\bar{m}}}{2}\big)+o(1).

(14)

Following the same idea as Lemma
3.1
, we can write the unit-lag covariance matrix as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1})=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\pi}-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}[\alpha(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{z})\,\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}]

(15)

where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\pi}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\big[(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top}\big]
is the covariance matrix of the stationary distribution with mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\boldsymbol{x}]
.

Then fix
\boldsymbol{x}
and set
f(\boldsymbol{x,z})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\alpha(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{z})
. We look at
\mathbb{E}[\alpha(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{z})\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}]
, for
\boldsymbol{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\ell^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{d}/d)
,
Applying Stein’s Lemma
[
9
]
and the assumption
\mathrm{E}_{p_{i}}[|\partial_{x}^{2}\log p_{i}(X_{i})|]<\infty
gives,

\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}Z_{j}f(\boldsymbol{x,z})|\boldsymbol{x}]=\frac{\ell^{4}}{d^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}[\partial_{ij}f(\boldsymbol{x,z})|\boldsymbol{x}],

\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x,z})|\boldsymbol{x}]=\frac{\ell^{2}}{d}\,\mathbb{E}[f(\boldsymbol{x,z})|\boldsymbol{x}]+\frac{\ell^{4}}{d^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}[\partial_{ii}f(\boldsymbol{x,z})|\boldsymbol{x}].

By the tower property, taking full expectation yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\alpha(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{z})Z_{i}Z_{j}]=O(d^{-2})

(16)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\alpha(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{z})Z_{i}^{2}]=\frac{\ell^{2}}{d}\,\mathbb{E}[\alpha(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{z})]+O(d^{-2})

(17)

Note that
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\pi}=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1}^{2},\dots,\sigma_{d}^{2})
. Using (
16
) and(
17
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\alpha(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{z})\,\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\big]=\frac{\ell^{2}}{d}\,\mathbb{E}[\alpha(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{z})]\,\boldsymbol{I}_{d}-2\boldsymbol{R}_{d},

(18)

also note that

O(d^{-2})+o(d^{-1})=o(d^{-1})

so substituting (
14
) and (
18
) into (
15
) yields (
4.3
) with a remainder matrix

\|\boldsymbol{R}_{d}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=o(d^{-1})
. Now we can write the diagonal entries as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1})_{ii}=\sigma_{i}^{2}\;-\;\frac{\ell^{2}}{2d}\,2\,\Phi\!\Big(-\tfrac{\ell\sqrt{\bar{m}}}{2}\Big)\;+o(d^{-1})

(19)

which is strictly positive for all large
d
since
\sigma_{i}^{2}>0
.

Note that the leading term
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\pi}-\ell^{2}/d\cdot\Phi\!\Big(-\ell\sqrt{\bar{m}}/2\Big)\boldsymbol{I}_{d}
in (
4.3
)
is a real diagonal matrix, hence Hermitian and the remainder
\boldsymbol{R}_{d}
arises from

-\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(\,\mathbb{E}[\alpha(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{z})\,\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}]-\tfrac{\ell^{2}}{d}\,\mathbb{E}[\alpha(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{z})]\boldsymbol{I}_{d}\Big),

it follows that
\boldsymbol{R}_{d}
is real symmetric, hence Hermitian as well. Therefore both the main diagonal term and the perturbation matrix
\boldsymbol{R}_{d}
are Hermitian. Then by Weyl’s inequality
[
7
]
, for Hermitian matrices
\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{E}
,

\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E})\;\geq\;\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})-\|\boldsymbol{E}\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

In our case
\boldsymbol{A}
is the diagonal term and
\boldsymbol{E}=\boldsymbol{R}_{d}
, so

\lambda_{\min}\!\big(\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1})\big)\;\geq\;\min_{i}\!\Big\{\sigma_{i}^{2}-\tfrac{\ell^{2}}{d}\cdot\Phi(-\tfrac{1}{2}\ell\sqrt{\bar{m}})\Big\}-\|\boldsymbol{R}_{d}\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

Since
\min\limits_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}>0
, the subtracted terms are of order
O(d^{-1})
and
o(d^{-1})
, hence strictly smaller than
\min\limits_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}
for sufficiently large
d
. This shows that the covariance matrix is positive definite in high dimension.
∎

Theorem
4.3
shows that in the high-dimensional regime, for a product-form target with non-identically distributed components, the diagonal entries of the unit-lag covariance admit the expansion as equation (

). Gelman and Roberts define the follwoing

Since

coincides with the efficiency criterion in the diffusion limit analysis of Gelman and Roberts, the optimizer

yields the well-known optimal acceptance rate

]

VI
Concluding Remarks and Future Work

Understanding the autocovariance structure of the Metropolis–Hastings algorithm is essential for assessing its efficiency. In this paper, we presented new theoretical results for random-walk MH chains targeting symmetric unimodal densities and introduced an optimal proposal design based on the unit-lag covariance, ultimately relating the analysis to high-dimensional settings in an asymmetric sense. Future work will focus on relaxing the restrictive assumptions on target distributions and extending the framework to multi-lag covariance structures.
