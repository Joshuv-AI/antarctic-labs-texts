Oxidative stress biomarkers and flavonoids in Alzheimer's disease: current clinical evidence and therapeutic perspectives
Authors: Muhammad-Safuan Zainuddin, Narendra Pamidi, Adzzie Shazleen Azman, Kasthuri Bai Magalingam, Saatheeyavaane Bhuvanendran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer's disease (AD) is a major global neurodegenerative disorder associated with high morbidity and mortality, yet current treatments remain largely symptomatic and ineffective in halting neurodegeneration. Growing evidence links oxidative stress potentially being an early event in AD progression. Consequently, therapeutic strategies targeting oxidative stress–mediated cellular damage are increasingly attractive. This review summarizes clinical evidence of oxidative stress biomarkers in AD and examines the neuroprotective mechanisms of flavonoids against oxidative stress in vivo, together with current clinical findings. Despite inconsistencies among studies due to variations in detection methods, a consistent trend of elevated oxidative stress in AD is evident, correlating with disease severity. Although in vivo studies have demonstrated that flavonoids restore antioxidant defences and suppress neuroinflammation, convincing clinical evidence of their efficacy in AD patients remains limited. Overall, these findings emphasize oxidative stress as a therapeutic target requiring further validation efforts.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, oxidative stress, biomarkers, flavonoid, antioxidant, lipid peroxidation, microgliosis

Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most prominent age-related neurodegenerative disease in the world [1]. As AD progresses, it causes severe loss of cholinergic neurons and widespread distribution across brain regions, including the hippocampus, frontal cortex and limbic areas, lead to clinical manifestation of dementia and cognitive deficits [1,2]. AD cases are projected to rise to 66 million by 2030 and 155 million by 2050, respectively, largely due to the ageing population [1,3]. The pathological process of AD is caused by two histological features, mainly senile plaques and neurofibrillary tangles consisting of deposited extracellular fibrillar amyloid-beta (Aβ) and intraneuronal hyperphosphorylated tau protein aggregations, leading to neuronal loss [4,5].

Although amyloid-beta and tau hypotheses have been the primary hypotheses in the underlying pathogenesis of AD, emerging evidence suggests that amyloidegenesis can be exacerbated by oxidative stress, leading to AD development and progression [6]. Therefore, oxidative stress was proposed to be crucial in initiating the signalling pathways involved in AD progression. Abnormal oxidative stress has been seen by an increase in nucleic oxidation reported in different brain regions of AD patients [2,7–9]. Growing evidence suggests that oxidative stress and impairment of innate antioxidative defence may precede the pathological features of AD and clinical manifestations [10,11]. There is a significant observation of oxidative damage to neuronal tissues in atrophied AD brains [12]. These free radicals, such as reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS), are by-products of normal and pathological processes in the human body [13]. However, owing to the compromised antioxidant defence in AD, these highly reactive and unstable molecules can target crucial biomolecules, including proteins, DNA and lipids seen in AD.

Polyphenols are naturally occurring phytochemicals, including phenolic acids, lignans, catechins, stilbenes, and flavonoids [14,15]. These polyphenols are clinically relevant compounds for their bioactivities, including anti-cancer, anti-diabetic, anti-inflammatory, anti-microbial, and anti-mutagenic pre-clinical and clinical studies [12,14]. Polyphenols are potent antioxidants because the presence of hydroxyl (─OH) groups attached to aromatic rings with various conformations creates structural and functional biodiversity. These antioxidants can alleviate oxidative stress by interrupting propagation or preventing the formation of free radicals by different mechanisms [16,17]. As oxidative stress plays a pivotal role in AD pathogenesis, therefore, antioxidants may be beneficial in either treating or preventing Alzheimer's disease [12,18]. Therefore, this review aims to highlight evidence of oxidative biomarkers across different specimens, including post-mortem brain and biofluids in AD patients compared to the healthy population. Additionally, to explore the current literature on flavonoids, both preclinical and clinical settings as prospective treatments against AD.

Oxidative stress hypothesis in Alzheimer's disease

Radical species such as reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) are typically generated in a normal metabolic process that is crucial for physiological signalling, including cell division, immune functions, and the stress response [19]. However, in a pathological state, homeostasis is dysregulated, causing aberrant production and accumulation of toxic free. Accordingly, aberrant oxidative stress is caused by several factors, such as the depletion of endogenous antioxidants, including glutathione (GSH), dysfunctional antioxidative enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (Prx), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx) [17]. The brain is vulnerable to oxidative stress because of its high production of ROS via metabolic activities, susceptibility of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) to radical species attacks, limited intrinsic antioxidant defence in neurons, and inability of exogenous antioxidants crossing the blood‒brain barrier (BBB). Hence, oxidative stress, whereby it influences various AD-related pathologies, including neurotoxicity, synaptic dysfunctions, amyloid-beta aggregation, tau hyperphosphorylation, metal ion accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and cognitive impairment [12,20,21] (Figure 1).

Oxidative stress biomarkers in Alzheimer's disease

Oxidative stress is an imbalance of pro-oxidants and antioxidants that favours pro-oxidant production, leading to nucleic acid, protein, and lipid damages. These damaged biomolecules are classified as DNA/RNA oxidation, protein oxidation, and lipid peroxidation, along with their oxidized molecules, such as malondialdehyde (MDA), 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), thiobarbituric acid-reactive substances (TBARS), F2-isoprostanes (F2-IsoPs), 8-hydroxydeoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), 8-hydroxyguanosine (8-OHG), 8-oxo-2'-deoxyguanosine (8-oxo-dG), protein carbonyls, and 3-nitrotyrosine (3-NT). These can be used as oxidative stress biomarkers in AD [22]. There are reported cases of elevated oxidative stress in both ageing healthy and AD patients, reflecting the brain's health status [23] (Figure 2). This has generated interest in observational studies in identifying the presence of oxidative stress biomarkers in AD patients in different specimens, including the brain and biofluids such as cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), urine, saliva, and blood [10,11,24].

These free radical species (ROS and RNS) indiscriminately target macromolecules, including proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids [25]. Clinical studies looking at oxidative stress biomarkers have associated oxidative stress with age progression and potentially contribute to neurodegeneration in AD [26]. A study by Abdul Sani et al. [21] examined oxidative stress in four age-defined cohorts of Malay individuals, namely, 30-year-old, 40-year-old, 50-year-old, and 60-year-old groups which demonstrated age-related elevation of DNA and protein damages across the groups. Similarly, Nunomura et al. [27] demonstrated that age-associated neurodegenerative disorders seen by increased nucleic acid oxidation (8-OGH). The relative density of 8-OGH immunoreactivity was undetectable in hippocampus and temporal cortex samples below the age of 40, and it was shown that neuronal RNA oxidation is associated with age progression. Interestingly, the author noted that there was a prominent level of RNA oxidation in vulnerable neurons in patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) compared to pre-clinical AD (PCAD) patients. These findings indicate that RNA oxidation damage exceeds normal ageing levels and is associated with earlier stages of AD as it shifts from cognitively normal ageing to AD. Therefore, suggesting that oxidative stress could potentially be an early event preceding AD progression.

Nucleic acid oxidation in Alzheimer's disease

Nucleic acids such as DNA (nDNA), mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), and RNA are susceptible to oxidative stress. Generally, mtDNA is more vulnerable to oxidative damage by free radicals through nitration, carbonylation or hydroxylation due to several factors, such as the absence of protective histones and introns, proximity of ROS generations, and limited repair mechanisms [6,28]. Damage to nDNA and mtDNA can impair transcriptional and post-transcriptional pathways, and promote neuronal deaths typically observed in neurodegenerative diseases [28]. The oxidation of DNA produces 8-hydroxy-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) while RNA oxidation forms 8-hydroxyguanine (8OHG), 8-oxo-guanine (8-oxoGua), and 8-oxo-guanine (8-oxoGuo) [29]. These nucleic acid adducts are used to elucidate the extent of oxidative stress damage in AD.

A handful of case-controlled studies have been conducted to investigate the role of oxidative stress in nucleic acid damages using several nucleic acid oxidation biomarkers, including 8-OHdG, 8-OHG, 8-OHA, 5-OHC, fapyguanine, and fapyadenine in post-mortem brains of different groups with different stages of Alzheimer's disease (AD), mild-cognitive impairment (MCI), pre-clinical Alzheimer's disease (PCAD), and healthy age-matched controls (Table 1). In these studies, there is a general trend of significant nucleic acid oxidations marked by elevations of different biomarkers; however, these observations differ across different brain regions, with the neocortex implicated with cognition, being commonly affected by oxidative damage [30–37]. Interestingly, a meta-analysis by Zabel et al. [10] reported non-significant changes in the nuclear 8-oxoG level in the studied brain regions, such as the frontal lobe, temporal lobe, temporal lobe, and cerebellum, except for a minor increase in the occipital lobe. However, it must be noted that the number of studies (n) included in the meta-analysis was relatively small, potentially leading to the larger effect size observed in respective parameters. Nonetheless, Nunomura et al. [36] observed prominent immunoreaction of 8-OHdG and 8-OHG in the neuronal cytoplasm than intra-nuclear staining across the hippocampus, subiculum, entorhinal cortex, frontal, temporal, and occipital neocortex in AD patients. The author noted that the nucleic acid oxidation was predominately associated with RNA in vulnerable neurons. Indicating that damaged RNA was sub-localised in the cytoplasm may indicate mitochondria being the major source of ROS, leading to oxidative damage in AD [36]. Similarly, it was observed a localization of 8-oxo-dG and DNA/RNA-associated 8-OHG staining in the cytoplasm of PCAD and AD patients in the hippocampus/parahippocampal gyri (HPG) and frontal cortex samples [31,32]. These observations suggest a greater elevation of mitochondrial-specific nucleic acid oxidation biomarkers compared to those derived from nuclear DNA. It was further corroborated by Wang et al. [35] seen by mitochondrial DNA being more susceptible to oxidative damage across four brain regions, such as cerebellum, frontal, parietal, and temporal lobes. These studies also highlighted that oxidative stress is restricted to vulnerable neurons in AD [31,32]. Collectively, these supports oxidative stress hypothesis with the mitochondria being the major site of ROS causing exacerbation of oxidative damages in AD.

Several observational retrospective studies consisting of different stages of Alzheimer's disease, mild-cognitive impairment (MCI), and healthy age-matched controls have investigated peripheral nucleic acid oxidation biomarkers in different biofluids (Table 2). The cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) can provide an ideal and accurate biochemical window, as it contains CNS-specific molecules produced by neurons, microglia and astrocytes that reflect the brain's health status [38,39]. However, there is limited information on nucleic acid biomarkers in CSF, as only two studies reported an elevation of 8-OHdG in CSF [40,41]. Owing to the invasive nature of obtaining CSF via lumbar puncture, this method is rarely performed on patients [42]. Therefore, there is a need to investigate nucleic acid oxidation biomarkers in an accessible manner using peripheral biofluids. Blood-related fluids, including plasma and serum, are ideal sources for prognosis and monitoring tools, as they are less invasive, accessible for large-scale clinical studies for repeated measurements, and have a higher patient consent rate [43]. A study by Mecocci et al. [44] using isolated DNA from blood-derived lymphocytes showed a significant level of 8-OHdG in AD compared to the healthy group. Similarly, several studies have demonstrated a significant elevation of 8-OHG, 8-OHdG and 8-oxoG in blood serum [45–47]. A study by Moslemnezhad et al. [48] showed increased serum 8-OHdG in the AD group compared to age-matched healthy group and, interestingly, a significant increase in severe AD compared to mild AD. Two studies using urine identified a significant elevation of 8-OHdG level in MCI-AD and AD groups, respectively, in comparison to age-matched healthy control group [11,49]. Collectively, these show that nucleic acid oxidation is consistently elevated in the AD groups as the disease progresses compared to healthy controls.

Protein oxidation in Alzheimer's disease

ROS-mediated attack on proteins often results in the oxidation of their backbones and side chains, producing either protein oxidation or protein peroxidation, whereby it leads to the production of protein carbonyls and nitrated proteins, respectively [50]. The protein oxidation results in protein carbonyls by either the addition of carbonyl groups (aldehydes and ketones) or peptide fragmentation on amino acids, such as lysine, arginine, threonine, and proline, along with other mechanisms, such as glycoxidation, while the protein peroxidation produces 3-nitrotyrosine (3-NT) due to nitration of proteins by peroxynitrite [29,51]. Several studies using post-mortem brains have demonstrated that the presence of oxidative and nitrosative stress-induced biomarkers is dependent on brain regions [9,37,52]. Interestingly, Ansari et al. [37] found non-significant changes in protein carbonyl levels between cognitively impaired groups, such as MCI, mAD and LAD, suggesting that protein oxidation may occur in the early event of the disease prior to the onset of symptoms; however, it remains inconclusive due to limited information on it.

Interestingly, study by Scheff et al. [53] reported that individuals with amnestic mild cognitive impairment (aMCI) exhibited significantly elevated protein carbonyls and 3-NT levels compared to non-cognitively impaired (NCI) controls, indicating increased protein oxidation in the disease state. This was accompanied by a marked reduction in pre- and post-synaptic proteins, including drebrin, SAP-97, PSD-95, and syanpsin-1, suggesting impaired synaptic integrity. Notably, a significant inverse correlation was observed between protein oxidation and synaptic-related protein levels, whereby increased in oxidative damage was associated with reduced synaptic protein expression. In addition, Aβ1-42 levels were significantly elevated in aMCI, suggesting a potential link between protein oxidation and amyloid pathology at early disease stages [53]. Similarly, Granold et al. [54] demonstrated increased protein oxidation in AD, as evidenced by elevated protein carbonyls in both neocortex and cerebellum, with more pronounced damage observed in the cortex. Membrane-associated proteins were found to be more susceptible to oxidative stress than cytoplasmic proteins [54]. Furthermore, the tumour suppressor protein p53 exhibited significant oxidative modifications (protein-carbonyls and 3-NT) in both MCI and AD compared to healthy controls, indicating that oxidative damage to p53 could activate the neuronal apoptosis pathway, leading to neurodegeneration. However, the absence of intermediate group analyses limits the implication of whether the progression of these changes across disease stages [55]. Nonetheless, these findings suggest that protein oxidation is closely associated with AD-related pathologies, such as synaptic dysfunction and neuronal apoptosis, contributing to AD progression.

A summary of observational studies on protein oxidation in different samples from three different groups, including AD and MCI patients and age-matched healthy controls (Tables 3 and 4), is presented. These observational studies showed a significant increase of plasma protein carbonyl levels in AD compared to age-matched healthy group [53–60], while McGrath et al. [61] reported non-significant changes in plasma protein carbonyls. Interestingly, several studies reported non-significant changes in plasma protein carbonyls in different stages of AD groups (MCI, mild, moderate, and severe), suggesting that protein oxidation is independent of disease progression and possibly occurs earlier in the disease stage [56,59,60]. Using DNA isolated from blood-derived lymphocytes, it showed significant increase in protein carbonyls and 3-NT levels in both AD and MCI, respectively in comparison of age-matched healthy control [60,61]. Furthermore, Greilberger et al. [62,63] showed significant increase in serum protein carbonyls in neurodegenerative disease, AD and MCI, respectively. However, the author did not compare in-between groups, it remains inconclusive whether the elevation of protein carbonyls is caused by disease progression alone. Similarly, Korolainen et al. [64] showed significant increase in serum protein carbonyls in a probable AD group compared to age-matched healthy control; however, the author also noted discrepancies in the blood plasma and cerebrospinal fluid, as neither showed significant changes in protein carbonyl levels. And non-significant differences in 3-NT levels across the peripheral fluids, possibly due to the amount being below the detection limit [64].

Lipid peroxidation in Alzheimer's disease

Due to its high oxygen consumption required to sustain the brain's metabolic activity, along with its high lipid content, leaving the brain vulnerable to oxidative stress-induced damage. The brain is rich in phospholipids such as polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) consisting of docosahexaenoic acid and arachidonic acid [66]. It has been reported that increased free radical production during oxidative stress induces lipid peroxidation in the brain. These free radicals progressively deplete PUFAs, which compromise the membrane fluidity and permeability. Discrupting normal physiological functions, including neurotransmission and neuronal interactions, which impairs cognition [67,68]. These damaged lipid products can be classified as either oxidized docosahexaenoic acid or oxidized arachidonic acid. The former commonly produces isoprostanes or isofurans, which are typically produced in all tissues and are non-specific to the brain. The latter produces neuroprostanes or neurofurans from the brain grey matter while di-homo-isoprostanes and di-homo-isofurans are formed from white matter [11]. Similarly, lipid peroxidation of PUFAs forms lipid hydroperoxides that undergo non-enzymatic decomposition, producing aldehydes, including malondialdehyde (MDA) and 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) [71,72].

A meta-analysis by Schrag et al. [73] reported a significant increase in plasma and serum lipid peroxidation biomarker (MDA) level in AD. Similarly, a recent meta-analysis by Nantachai et al. [26] highlighted that blood lipid peroxidation biomarkers (MDA/TBARS) were significantly higher in MCI than healthy controls, with a strong association indicating that lipid peroxidation biomarkers are potentially ideal candidate for oxidative stress in neurodegenerative disease. Therefore, MDA is the most studied lipid peroxidation biomarker in different samples from three different groups (Table 5). Flavonoid overview

Flavonoid is a large class of polyphenol representing a major constituent of plant secondary metabolites, with 6,000--10,000 flavonoids reported in the literature [79]. The metabolites have various functions in plants, including attracting pollinators, cellular regulation, and defence systems against biotic and abiotic threats [80,81]. These compounds are abundantly found in different plant sections, ranging from seeds to fruits [81]. In the context of human health, these flavonoids possess pharmaceutically beneficial properties, including antibacterial, anticancer, antiviral, and neuroprotective, therefore it is deeming as medicinally important compounds [79,80]. Classically, flavonoids are known for their antioxidative properties, such as free radical scavenging and metal chelating activities, which are influenced by the position and number of attached hydroxyl groups [82,83]. Therefore, flavonoids can play a critical role in AD, as these molecules can protect against oxidative stress by modulating signalling pathways and cascades involved in innate antioxidative defences, neuroinflammation and immunomodulation.

Flavonoid has a 15-carbon flavan (C6-C3-C6) structure consisting of two benzene rings (A and B) and connected by an oxygen-containing a heterocyclic pyrene ring (C) (Figure 3). Based on the position of benzene, ring (c) is attached to the pyran ring and the degree of unsaturation and oxidation of the pyran ring, these flavonoids can further be subclassified into different groups based on their structure, such as flavanol, flavonol, flavone, flavanone, isoflavone, and anthocyanin [83]. Despite sharing the same core scaffold, the functional difference of these flavonoids is dependent on their substituent groups and degrees of hydroxylation, methoxylation, glucuronidation, and glycosylation, which contribute to various bioactivities [82]. Flavonoids are pleiotropic in nature, allowing them to target multiple pathways in alleviating oxidative stress in AD. Therefore, the current literature on the neuroprotective mechanism of flavonoids in delaying neuronal death in Alzheimer's disease.

Restoration of antioxidant defence in Alzheimer's disease by flavonoids

The innate antioxidant defence consists of endogenous glutathione (GSH) and various classical antioxidative enzymes, including manganese-containing SOD (Mn-SOD), catalase (CAT), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), copper–zinc-containing SOD (CuZn-SOD), which are responsible for maintaining redox homeostasis as a compensatory mechanism against oxidative stress [84,85]. In AD, the antioxidant defence is impaired, causing the accumulation of ROS due to a lack of synthesis and the release of antioxidant molecules [86]. Therefore, flavonoids may provide neuroprotection to neuronal cells by augmenting oxidative stress reflected by recovered antioxidative defences, as reported in vivo by attenuating free radical-induced damage to antioxidant enzymes and promoting the synthesis of these enzymes whereby it improves scavenging activities against these ROS and RNS.

A study by Kumar et al. [87] demonstrated that hesperidin (150 µM) enhances oxidative stress resistance in Caenorhabditis elegans by reducing age-associated ROS accumulation and preventing oxidative damage via acr-16-mediated autophagic activity. Notably, hesperidin also conferred neuroprotective effects in Aβ-transgenic C. elegans model, supporting its role in mitigating proteotoxic stress in a simplified model [87]. Extending these findings to higher-order models, various flavonoids, such as flavanones, flavones, and dihydrochalcones, have been reported to significantly reduced lipid peroxidation markers (MDA, MPO, 4-HNE, and 8-isoprostane) in rodent models, indicating their capacity to attenuate oxidative stress in the brain [20,88–95]. Given the high abundance of PUFAs in neural tissues, the suppression of lipid peroxidation by these compounds is particularly relevant in limiting oxidative damage. This finding is further supported by the restoration of endogenous antioxidant defence systems, including SOD (SOD1-3), GPx, CAT, T-SOD, and endogenous GSH levels, which collectively enhance the scavenging of ROS and RNS in the brain tissues [88–96].

However, only a limited number of studies have investigated the specific molecular pathways underlying these antioxidant effects in AD models. For instance, trilobatin has been shown to modulate the SIRT3/SOD2 axis in APP/PS1 mice and Aβ25-35-induced rats, which restored the redox homeostasis in AD-model [95]. Similarly, in eriodictyol-treated APP/PS1 mice, the vitamin D receptor (VDR) was activated, leading to downstream induction of NRF-2/HO-1 pathway and promoting the synthesis of antioxidant enzymes, including SOD and HO-1 [20]. It can also potentially regulate glutamate-cysteine ligase catalytic (GCLC) and modifier (GCLM) expressions involved in GSH synthesis [20,97]. These mechanisms likely contribute to the observed elevation and maintenance of GSH levels across models, highlighting the role of flavonoids in promoting endogenous antioxidant capacity and maintaining redox homeostasis.

By ameliorating oxidative stress by recovering the endogenous antioxidative defence, it also improves other AD-related pathogeneses (Figure 4). Festidinol significantly attenuated the amyloid-beta production and tau phosphorylation by inhibiting beta-site APP cleaving enzyme 1 (BACE1) and GKS3β [91]. Additionally, flavonoids such as myricetin and morin improved energy production and metabolism by preventing mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis, halting excitatory neurotoxicity and neuronal death [93]. Different flavonoids, including sterubin, festidinol, morin, and myricetin regulated cholinergic system by depressing the acetylcholinesterase (AChE) level and restoring the acetylcholine and maintaining acetylcholine levels comparable to those of healthy controls. The improvement in the cholinergic system significantly improved cognition in their respective animal models [92–94]. The upregulation of GPX4 also prevents ferroptosis in AD in vivo by reduction in transferrin receptor (TfRC) and ferritin heavy chain (FTH) in both the hippocampus and cortex, which improved cognitive function [20]. Overall, alleviation of oxidative stress via modulation and enhancement of innate antioxidant defence may mitigate neuronal damage and contribute to preservation or improvement of cognitive function in vivo.

Inhibition of microgliosis in Alzheimer's disease

Immune cells release inflammatory factors such as chemokines and cytokines in prolonged oxidative stress, causing the neuroinflammatory response commonly seen in Alzheimer's disease brains [98]. Oxidative stress and neuroinflammation formed a vicious cycle in AD, as they stimulate each other and further damage the neuronal cells. In the disease state, there is a redox imbalance in the brain mediated by the priming of immune cells, including microglia and other glial cells, in the brain [99,100]. Triggering numerous inflammatory cascades and pathways promotes the secretion of cytokines, chemokines, and small molecular messengers such as nitric oxide (NO) and ROS [99,101]. A genome-wide association study (GWAS) highlighted various risk-associated genes with AD are enriched in microglia, suggesting the role of microglia in AD pathogenesis [102]. This supports the involvement of microglia in the neuroinflammatory response as a key pathological process in AD, hence it could be a promising therapeutic target in AD.

Microglia can be classified phenotypically into two phases either the resting phase (M0) or (ii) the activated phase (M1/M2) (Figure 5). In the resting phase, microglia (M0) act as active immune surveillance cells in monitoring the brain's homeostatic environment [103]. In the classical activation (M1), it promotes neuroinflammation via the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-4, IL-6, IL-12, and IL-18), chemokines, small molecules (ROS, RNS and NO), abnormal phagocytosis, and neurotoxicity, causing neurodegeneration. In the alternative, activation (M2) acts as a protective role, as it is involved in the anti-inflammatory response by releasing anti-inflammatory cytokines (TGF-β, IL-4, IL-10, and IL-13), neurotrophic factors (NGF, BDNF, neurotrophins and GDNF), pro-survival factors (progranulin and CD206), enhancing phagocytosis, and neuronal survival [103,104]. However, it must be noted that the M1/M2 classification is oversimplistic due to inability to capture the appropriate dynamic nature of microglia in the brain in vivo. Notably, microglial phenotypes are transient rather than separate subtypes, as their polarisation is dependent on the CNS environment, ensuring that microglia play a protective role by switching their phenotype [103,105]. Nonetheless, the neuroinflammatory response in AD, hits its first peak as an initial anti-inflammatory response as a compensatory mechanism; however, as it transitions from MCI to mild AD, it causes further exacerbation of oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, shifting into the M1 phenotype leading to neurotoxicity [103,105]. The persistent and prolonged M1 phenotype aids in AD pathogenesis, such as synaptic dysfunction, neurogenesis inhibition, and neuronal death [99,101,104]. Hence, microglia may act as a double-edged sword due to involvement of both phenotypes in neurodegeneration, thus, fine modulation of microglial activation is crucial for maintaining or recovering normal microglial function in brain homeostasis and preventing neurodegeneration. Therefore, targeting the polarisation of microglia from M1 into M2 by flavonoids may provide an alternative for slowing down or preventing further progression of AD.

Both transgenic and chemically induced AD in vivo models exhibit pronounced microgliosis and astrogliosis in brain regions such as the entorhinal cortex, hippocampus, and prefrontal cortex, thereby recapitulating key pathological features of AD, including cognitive deficits, amyloid plaque, tau phosphorylation, and neuroinflammation [106,107]. A study by Oyeleke et al. [88] demonstrated that the treatment of Aβ1-42-inducedBALB/c mice with Bacopa floribunda flavonoid- and saponin-rich extracts attenuated microgliosis in the hippocampal CA1 region, thereby conferring neuroprotection against Aβ1-42-insult. This observation was supported by reduced Iba-1 immunoreactivity in the hippocampal CA1, reflecting a concomitant decrease in pro-inflammatory cytokine levels. While this study utilised crude extracts rather than isolated compounds, it supports the notion that flavonoid-containing extracts can modulate microglial activation in vivo. Thus, we further explored studies employing stand-alone flavonoids that provide more direct mechanistic evidence in different in vivo models (Table 6 and Figure 6).

In AD-induced Wister rat models, several types of flavonoids, such as hesperidin and sterubin, alleviate neuroinflammatory responses following aluminium chloride exposure, as evidenced by reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine levels [89,92]. Although these studies did not assess a phenotype shift from M1 to M2 microglia, their anti-inflammatory effects are mechanistically relevant given the established relationship between neuroinflammation and cholinergic dysfunction, which is characterized by the upregulation of AChE and the downregulation of ChAT [89,102]. Consequently, both sterubin and hesperidin ameliorate cholinergic dysfunction, leading to improvement in cognitive function in AD-induced Wistar rat models [89,92]. Overall, these findings suggest that flavonoids, either in extracts or isolated compounds, can target microgliosis in AD. Indeed, compounds such as baicalein, cyanidin-3-glucoside, liquiritigenin, trilobatin, nobiletin, and epigallocatechin-3-gallate have been shown to promote a shift from pro-inflammatory (M1-like) to anti-inflammatory (M2-like) microglial states, evidenced by reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, alongside increased anti-inflammatory cytokines by modulating distinct pathways [95,105,108,109,112].

Kou et al. [110] demonstrated that 3-week treatment of luteolin in 3xTg-AD model and 48-hour treatment in rat C6 glioma cells showed dose-dependent inhibition of astrogliosis seen by GFAP reduction and attenuation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and small molecules. It was shown that the improvement of endoplasmic reticulum stress by luteolin-modulated IRE1α/NF-kB/p38 pathway, resulting in improvement in neuroinflammatory response in both models. Importantly, treatment with luteolin resulted in improvement in spatial learning deficits by preventing deterioration of cerebral neurons and ameliorating cortical Aβ plaque in the 3x Tg-AD mice model [112]. Although the authors did not investigate microglial involvement in this study, it may be hypothesised that luteolin could potentially influence microglial polarisation towards a less pro-inflammatory state. This effect may occur indirectly through the inhibition of astrocytosis, particularly by limiting the transformation of astrocytes into the neurotoxic phenotype [111].

Additionally, Xie et al. [104] fed two different doses of baicalein to 3xTg-AD mice and reduced microgliosis by polarizing the M1 to M2 phenotype in both the hippocampal and cortex regions. It is suggested that baicalein exerts a modulatory effect on the CX3CR1/NF-kB pathways in both 3xTg-AD mice and LPS-induced BV2 microglial cells. Due to inhibition of polarisation of M1 phenotype, it reduced AD-related pathology, such as reduction in Aβ1-40 and Aβ1-42 in the hippocampus and cortex in the baicalein-treated group, as well as increased Nissl bodies in hippocampal C1 and CA3 regions, which maintain neuronal integrity. Thus, it promotes synaptic plasticity seen by the elevation of BDNF, PSD95, and SYP, which in in turns, improved learning and memory in the baicalein-treated group. Notably, the absence of additional benefit at the highest dose, it suggests a non-linear dose–response relationship for baicalein, indicating that higher doses may not confer greater efficacy and could potentially be detrimental, as they could be neurotoxic to the neurons.

A study by Sanjay et al. [112] demonstrated that cyanidin-3-O-glucoside (C3G) promotes a shift in microglial activation from M1 to M2 phenotype in HMC3 cells co-treated with C3G and Aβ42 for 24 hours. This was evidenced by the upregulation of M2-associated markers (CD206 and CD163), alongside a reduction in M1-associated markers (CD86 and CD8) and pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-16, and TNF-α). These findings were corroborated in vivo, where C3G-treated APPswe/PS1ΔE9 mice exhibited increased expression of CD206 and Arg1 [112]. Mechanistically, the authors reported that microglial polarisation was mediated through the modulation of peroxisome-proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) and triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells (TREM2), with TREM2 activation further enhancing Aβ42 phagocytosis in HMC3 cells [112]. Similarly, transcriptomic analysis revealed that trilobatin treatment in APP/PS1 mice and Aβ25–35-induced rats modulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) involved in the high mobility group box 1 protein (HMGB1), toll-like receptor (TLR) and NF-kB signalling pathways [95]. In both models, trilobatin attenuated neuroinflammation via suppression of HMGB1/TLR4/NF-kB axis, thereby reducing both microgliosis and astrogliosis [95]. Consistent with these findings, nobiletin treatment in APP/PS1 mice inhibited HMGB1 signalling and reduced microglial activation through the modulation of pyroptosis-related proteins, including NLRP3, ASC, GSDM-N and cleaved caspase-1 [105]. Similarly, rutin suppressed NF-kB pathway signalling in Tau-P301S mice, leading to reduced astrocytic and microglial activation and overall attenuation of neuroinflammation [111].

In chemically induced models, festidinol administration for 90 days attenuated neuroinflammation in AlCl3- and D-galactose-induced mice via suppression of NF-kB/NFAT1/RAGE pathway, thereby reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine release and caspase-3 activity and ultimately limiting neuronal apoptosis in the CA1 and dentate gyrus regions [91]. Similarly, Mandour et al. [90] reported that hesperidin treatment for 28 days in AlCl3-induced AD rats reduced astrocytosis, as evidenced by decreased GFAP-immunoreactive astrocytes in the hippocampus (CA1, CA3, and DG) and prefrontal cortex. This was accompanied by reduced serum levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-1β), with a non-significant increase in the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, suggesting a shift towards an anti-inflammatory milieu [90]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that flavonoids can modulate key inflammatory signalling pathways and microglial activation in chemically-induced models, thereby contributing to the attenuation of neuroinflammation in AD.

The available evidence indicates that microgliosis is a central and dynamic component of Alzheimer's disease pathology and its modulation represents a plausible therapeutic target. While findings from both flavonoid-rich extracts and stand-alone compounds suggest the capacity to attenuate microglial activation and associated neuroinflammation, mechanistic insights remain limited and are often inferred from indirect markers. Furthermore, the continued reliance on simplified microglial classifications and heterogeneous model systems constrains the interpretation of preclinical studies. Nevertheless, these studies provide consistent preliminary support for the role of flavonoids in modulating microglial responses, warranting further investigation using more refined, context-dependent frameworks, and translationally relevant models.

Clinical trials of flavonoids in Alzheimer's disease

The compiled evidence highlights the neuroprotective potential of flavonoids in modulating oxidative stress mechanisms in vitro and in vivo. Accordingly, human clinical trials are essential to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of their efficacy as potential therapeutic interventions for Alzheimer's disease. Several clinical trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov have investigated flavonoid-based interventions in Alzheimer's disease, including S-Equol in Alzheimer's disease 2 (SEAD2, NCT03101085) and Sunphenon EGCG in the early stage of Alzheimer's disease (SUNK-AK, NCT00951834). The SEAD2 trial employed a crossover design, in which patients with AD (N = 40) received S-equol (100 mg/day) for 4 weeks followed by placebo or vice versa. The primary outcome focused on mitochondrial function, which was assessed via cytochrome oxidative/citrate synthase (COX/CS) activity, alongside cognitive and psychiatric measures. In contrast, the SUNK-AK study evaluated escalating doses of epigallocatechin-gallate (EGCG, 200–800 mg/day) in 3-month increments over a 12-month period in patients with early AD, with primary endpoints, including cognitive performance, psychiatric assessments, and brain atrophy measured by MRI. Notably, despite their relevance, no results from either trial have been publicly reported as of 23 April 2026, limiting conclusions regarding their clinical efficacy. Nonetheless, these clinical trials reflect growing interest in the clinical translation of flavonoid-based interventions.

In the absence of publicly available results from these studies, the following discussion will focus on clinical trials with published outcomes to provide a more evidence-based evaluation of flavonoids therapeutic potential in AD (Table 7). The GENIEL (NCT01982578) evaluated the effects of daily genistein (5,7-dihydroxy-3-(4-hydroxyphenyl) chromen-4-one) supplementation in AD patients [113]. The participants received their genistein (120 mg/day) or placebo, with a significant increase in circulating genistein levels observed in the intervention group after 12 months compared to baseline and placebo. While no significant differences were detected across the assessed parameters at 6-month mark, however, there were modest improvements at 12 months, with total dichotomized TAVEC and Centil Rey delayed copy reached statistical significance in the genistein-treated group. Overall, genistein demonstrated a trend toward cognitive improvement over time and was well tolerated, with only mild gastrointestinal side effects reported [113]. A double-blind, parallel-group clinical trial (NCT00205179) evaluated daily soy isoflavone (100 mg/day) supplementation for 6 months in patients with AD, alongside with a battery of neuropsychological and psychiatric assessments [114] Plasma isoflavone levels increased significantly at 3 and 6 months compared to baseline and in the placebo group; however, substantial interindividual variability was observed, likely due to differences in metabolic conversion to genistein, equol, and daidzein. Despite these increases, no significant improvements in global cognition were detected in either the treatment or placebo groups, with both showing a decline in Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores over time. Notably, total plasma equol levels were positively correlated with performance in speeded dexterity and verbal fluency tasks, suggesting a potential link between specific isoflavone metabolites and cognitive function in AD [114].

A study by van Dongen et al. (2000) [115] evaluated the efficacy of a standardised Ginkgo biloba extract (EGb761), containing ginkgo flavonol glycosides (16%–25%) and terpene trilactones (6%), in a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel-group, multicentre trial involving individuals with dementia (AD and vascular dementia) or age-associated memory impairment. The participants were assigned to receive either 160 mg/day or 240 mg/day of EGb761 or placebo for 24 weeks. The findings indicated no significant therapeutic benefit at either dose or prolonged supplementation, although the EGb761-treated group showed slight improvements in self-reported activities of daily living, without corresponding changes in perceived health status [115]. The relatively short intervention period of six months may have limited the ability to detect significant cognitive changes in the treated group. In contrast, longer-term evidence from the GuidAge trial (NCT00276510) [116] was a large-scale, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel-group study that assessed the long-term effects of a high dose (240 mg/day) of EGb761 over five years. Although fewer participants in the treatment group developed AD (61 cases) compared to the placebo group (73 cases), this difference was non-significant. Overall, long-term, high-dose EGb761 supplementation did not significantly reduce the risk of developing AD compared to placebo [116].

A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel-arm Cocoa, Cognition, and Ageing (CoCoA) study [117] evaluated the efficacy of cocoa flavanols on cognitive function in cognitively intact older adults. The participants consumed a daily dairy-based cocoa beverages containing low (48 mg/serving), intermediate (520 mg/serving), or high (993 mg/serving) flavanol contents for 8 weeks. No significant changes in MMSE scores were observed across groups. However, both the intermediate- and high-flavanol groups demonstrated significant reductions in completion time for Trail Making Tests (TMT) A and B, indicating improved executive function and processing speed. Additionally, the verbal fluency test (VFT) scores improved significantly in all the groups, with the greatest enhancement observed in the high-flavanol group. Overall, intermediate and high-flavanol intake was associated with measurable cognitive benefits over the intervention period. Importantly, the levels of total plasma 8-iso-prostaglandin F2α, a marker of oxidative stress, were significantly reduced in the intermediate- and high-flavanol groups both during and following the 8-week intervention [117]. Consistent with these findings, an earlier CoCoA study by Desideri et al. [118] examined the effects of daily cacao flavonol supplementation in individuals with mild-cognitive impairment (MCI), which consisted of low-flavanol (45 mg flavanol/serving), intermediate-flavonol (520 mg flavanol/serving), and high-flavanol (990 mg flavanol/serving) interventions. Similarly, no significant changes were observed in MMSE scores across groups; however, there were significant improvements in both TMT A&B and verbal fluency for both the intermediate- and high-flavonol groups Compared to low-flavonol. Similarly, the total plasma 8-iso-prostaglandin F2α was significantly reduced in the high- and intermediate-flavanol groups [118]. These findings suggest that cocoa flavanol supplementation may support specific domains of cognitive function and reduce oxidative stress in both cognitively intact older adults and individuals with mid-cognitive impairment.

Growing interest has also emerged in combinatorial therapeutic strategies, particularly the co-administration of flavonoids with conventional pharmacological agents, as a potential intervention in Alzheimer's disease. This approach is driven by the rationale that flavonoids may enhance therapeutic efficacy through complementary mechanisms, such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective effects, while potentially mitigating drug-related side effects and targeting multiple pathological pathways simultaneously [119,120]. For instance, the Senolytics to Alleviate Mobility Issues and Neurological Impairments in Ageing (STAMINA, NCT05422885) and Senolytic Therapy to Modulate the Progression of Alzheimer's Disease (SToMP-AD; NCT04063124) trials collectively provide early clinical insights into the feasibility of combinatorial senolytic therapy using dasatinib and quercetin (DQ) in the population at risk or diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease [121,122]. The STAMINA study was a 12-week, single-arm, open-label, pre-post pilot study evaluating intermittent administration of dasatinib (100 mg/day, every 2 days) and quercetin (1250 mg/day, every 2 days) (DQ, IND 157920) in older adults (N = 12), presenting with slow gait and MCI, both of which are considered risk factors for Alzheimer's disease. The findings indicated that DQ was well tolerated, although it showed non-significant improvements in cognitive performance (MoCA), but it showed a reduction in TNF-α, alongside a positive correlation between these measures [121]. Complementing this, an earlier SToMP-AD study was an open-label, proof-of-concept phase 1 trial in early-stage AD patients (N = 5) that evaluated daily oral dasatinib (100 mg/day) and quercetin (1000 mg/day) (DQ, IND 143945–0006) for 12 weeks. This study confirmed that the central nervous system penetrance of dasatinib, with increased serum levels of both compounds, although only dasatinib was detected in CSF. Importantly, the regime was well tolerated, with no early discontinuations and no significant changes in cognitive or neuroimaging outcomes, supporting a favourable safety profile [122]. Although preliminary, these findings suggest that combinatorial senolytic therapy may offer modest benefits, supporting further investigation in populations at risk of Alzheimer's disease.

Overall, these studies suggest that while not all flavonoid classes consistently demonstrate cognitive benefits, as some interventions show limited or non-significant effects. Noteworthily, flavanols in particular appear to yield relatively more favourable outcomes [117,118]. Different flavonoids, such as flavanols and flavanones, are able to cross the blood‒brain barrier (BBB) and exert neuroactive effects, with their metabolites contributing to these actions [119]. However, interpretation remains constrained by small sample sizes, short intervention periods, heterogeneous study populations, and the lack of Alzheimer's disease-specific and oxidative stress biomarkers. In some studies, plasma levels of compounds were not accessed [111,123]; although increase in plasma concentrations indicate absorption, it does not necessarily reflect target-site bioavailability. [119,124]. This is an important consideration, as structural variability among flavonoids can influence their metabolic processing and consequently affect their therapeutic efficacy in clinical settings [116,118]. As a result, clinical studies may have yielded inconsistent outcomes, despite the generally promising findings observed in preclinical models. Furthermore, the use of fewer sensitive cognitive measures [125] and the inconsistent alignment between biochemical changes and functional outcomes, limit definitive conclusions. Despite these limitations, these findings collectively provide preliminary evidence on the biological plausibility of flavonoid-based interventions for AD. Therefore, it underscores the need of more rigorous, long-term clinical trials with robust biomarkers and sensitive cognitive endpoints.

Conclusion

A substantial body of literature has investigated oxidative stress biomarkers targeting various biomolecules, including nucleic acid oxidation, lipid peroxidation, and protein oxidation, across diverse biological samples, such as brain tissue, blood plasma, serum, urine, and cerebrospinal fluid. These studies aim to bridge the relationship between the oxidative stress hypothesis and the development of Alzheimer's disease. Although these findings consistently indicate the presence of oxidative damage across different stages of AD, however, variability in experimental techniques has led to inconsistent and often contradictory results. Nevertheless, the collective evidence suggests that oxidative stress may be present in the early stage of AD, with its severity correlating with disease progression. Flavonoids demonstrate promising neuroprotective potential by mitigating oxidative stress and neuroinflammation in preclinical models. These effects are primarily mediated through the enhancement of endogenous antioxidant defences, including increased antioxidative enzymatic activity and glutathione levels, as well as the modulation of microglial polarization toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype. However, clinical evidence remains inconclusive, likely owing to limitations such as poor bioavailability and insufficient concentration of metabolic factors. Therefore, future studies should focus on elucidating the precise mechanisms of action of flavonoids and improving their translational applicability to establish their therapeutic potential in AD.
