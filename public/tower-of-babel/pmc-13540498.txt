A Bibliometric Analysis of Publications in Uremic Toxins From 1991 to 2024
Authors: Yuh‐Shan Ho, Karim M. Soliman, Wisit Cheungpasitporn, Wannasit Wathanavasin, Ahmed Daoud, Ahmed I. Kamal, Balázs Szamosfalvi, Tibor Fülöp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundUremic toxins are a growing area of research in nephrology, with significant implications in the progression and treatment of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and the management of end‐stage kidney disease (ESKD). This bibliometric analysis aims to evaluate the global research trends, key contributors, and the impact of publications in the field of uremic toxins from 1991 to 2024.MethodsThe data were retrieved from the Science Citation Index Expanded (SCI‐EXPANDED) database. The study analyzed 2210 articles using various bibliometric indicators, including total publications (TP), average number of citations per publication (CPP 2024), and the Y‐index for author contributions. Publication trends were examined across countries, institutions, and authors. The analysis included document types, languages, Web of Science categories, and journals.ResultsThe study revealed a steady increase in uremic toxin research publications. Japan, the USA, and China remained the most productive countries, with Japan maintaining the highest output, while Belgium achieved the highest citation impact. Citations of uremic toxin‐related research peaked 2 years following publication and plateaued after approximately 6 years. The research foci evolved, with CKD, ESKD, indoxyl sulfate, protein‐bound solutes, and gut microbiota gaining prominence. Studies on oxidative stress, inflammation, metabolomics, and advanced adsorption‐based toxin removal strategies increased markedly. International collaboration and interdisciplinary integration across nephrology, microbiology, and biochemistry correlated with higher citation performance and research visibility.ConclusionThis bibliometric study provides an in‐depth analysis of uremic toxin research trends, identifies major contributors, and suggests future research priorities. The findings can inform future research directions, guide funding allocation, and strengthen global collaborative efforts towards improved management of toxin‐related kidney disease.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects approximately 10%–12% of the global population, with uremic toxins playing a crucial role in its progression and complications [1]. These toxins, classified into small water‐soluble compounds, middle molecules, and protein‐bound solutes, accumulate as kidney function declines, leading to a state known as uremia [2, 3]. As CKD progresses to end‐stage kidney disease (ESKD), the importance of understanding uremic toxins becomes even more critical, as they are associated with an increased cardiovascular risk, disease progression, and mortality in patients [2]. The global burden of CKD and ESKD is increasing, driven by rising rates of diabetes, hypertension, and an aging population, which has made CKD the 12th leading cause of death globally in 2017 [2].

Dialysis, the primary treatment for ESKD, aims to remove uremic toxins from the human body, either through hemodialysis or dialysis through the peritoneal cavity. Nonetheless, despite advances in dialysis techniques, current renal replacement therapies are limited in clearing protein‐bound uremic toxins, such as indoxyl sulfate and p‐cresyl sulfate [4, 5]. This persistent gap highlights a critical unmet need for improved therapeutic strategies targeting uremic toxin accumulation across the spectrum of kidney disease.

Research into uremic toxins now extends beyond nephrology, intersecting with cardiology, endocrinology, neurology, microbiology, and pharmacology, reflecting the systemic impact of uremic metabolites [6]. The economic burden of CKD and ESKD is substantial, with Medicare spending for CKD beneficiaries having exceeded $87 billion in 2017 in the United States alone, a significant portion of which is attributed to dialysis costs alone [7]. Over the past three decades, research in this field has expanded significantly, driven by technological advances in analytical chemistry, molecular biology, and bioinformatics, leading to an improved understanding of uremic toxins in both CKD and ESKD [8].

Given the critical importance of uremic toxins in CKD, ESKD, and dialysis, as well as the rapid evolution of this research field, there is a pressing need for a comprehensive analysis of scientific literature. A recent bibliometric analysis of uremic toxin research has been published, primarily focusing on combined search terms including “uremic toxin” and CKD [9]. However, that study differs in search strategy and analytical framework, which may influence the characterization of research trends and impact.

The present study aims to provide a comprehensive macro‐level bibliometric analysis of uremic toxin research from 1991 to 2024, focusing on publication trends, citation impact, and contributions across countries, institutions, and authors. All documents containing “uremic toxin”–related keywords in their title, abstract, or author keywords were systematically retrieved and analyzed. While advanced network‐based approaches, such as co‐authorship, co‐citation, and keyword co‐occurrence analyses, can offer additional structural insights, this study emphasizes standardized bibliometric indicators (e.g., total publications, citation metrics, and Y‐index) to ensure reproducibility and comparability with prior nephrology‐focused bibliometric studies.

By integrating these bibliometric perspectives, this study seeks to identify key contributors, characterize the evolution of research themes, and highlight emerging areas of interest in uremic toxin research. These findings are intended to inform future research priorities, foster international collaboration, and support the development of more effective strategies to mitigate uremic toxicity and improve outcomes in patients with CKD and ESKD.

Data Sources and Methods

Data used in this study were retrieved from the Clarivate Analytics Web of Science Core Collection, specifically the Science Citation Index Expanded (SCI‐EXPANDED) database (data updated on October 22, 2025), using a structured bibliometric workflow. The SCI‐EXPANDED database indexes 9471 journals across 178 Web of Science categories. A topic‐based search strategy was applied using quotation marks (“ ”) and the Boolean operator “OR” to ensure the inclusion of at least one search keyword within the TOPIC fields (title, abstract, author keywords, and Keywords Plus), using the terms “uremic toxin” OR “uremic toxins.” To improve specificity and minimize the inclusion of irrelevant records, a “front page” filter (restricted to title, abstract, and author keywords) was applied. To enhance search sensitivity, uncommon variants (e.g., “uremic toxines”) and potential database inconsistencies (e.g., “uremic toxinshas”) were also considered. This stepwise selection process, including database restriction, keyword refinement, front‐page filtering, and document type selection, was designed to ensure methodological rigor, relevance, and reproducibility of the bibliometric analysis. A total of 3758 documents containing search keywords in terms of TS (topic) were searched out, including 3727 documents (99% of 3758 documents) published between 1991 and 2024 in the SCI‐EXPANDED database. The complete document records from SCI‐EXPANDED and the corresponding citation counts for each year were downloaded into Microsoft 365 Excel. Manual coding was conducted to further enhance the data analysis process [10]. The journals' impact factors (IF 2024) were obtained from the Journal Citation Reports (JCR) published in 2024.

In 2011, the “front page,” including title, abstract, and author keywords, was proposed as a filter to improve search strategy when using the terms of Topic (TS) in the Web of Science Core Collection for the bibliometric study [10]. The “front page” filter can circumvent the introduction of irrelevant publications for bibliometric analyses. The search for documents containing the search keywords on their “front page” yielded a total of 3203 papers, which accounted for 86% of the 3727 documents. After applying additional filters for document type (articles only) and bibliometric relevance, 2210 articles were included in the final quantitative analysis.

To obtain more precise and accurate outcomes in the analysis of scientific research, this study incorporated an additional categorization based on corresponding authors, institutions, and countries. Despite the SCI‐EXPANDED database designating the reprint author as the corresponding author, we opted to use the term “corresponding author.” [10] For unidentified articles in the Web of Science Core Collection, a single author, single institution, and single country were identified as the first author, institution, and country, as well as the corresponding author, institution, and country [10]. In multi‐corresponding author articles, all corresponding authors, institutions, and countries were considered separately [10]. Articles where corresponding authors had addresses but no affiliation names in the SCI‐EXPANDED database were identified, and the addresses were changed to affiliation names [10]. Additionally, affiliations from England, Scotland, and Wales were reclassified as the United Kingdom (UK). Affiliations located in the USSR (the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics) were checked and reclassified as Russia [11], while those in Yugoslavia were checked and reclassified as Croatia or Serbia as appropriate [11]. Affiliations located in Turkey were reclassified and identified as being from Türkiye.

Results and Discussion

Characteristics of Document Types and Languages

Monge‐Nájera and Ho proposed utilizing metrics such as the average number of citations per publication (CPP year) and the average number of authors per publication (APP = AU/TP) to analyze various document types within a research topic [10]. Table 1 presents these metrics for 3203 documents on uremic toxins published in the SCI‐EXPANDED database, categorized into 13 document types. Articles constituted the majority with 2210 entries, representing 69% of the total number of entries and an average of 7.2 authors per article. Reviews, accounting for 651 documents, exhibited the highest CPP 2024 with 47 citations. This high citation rate was primarily due to seminal reviews by Wyss and Kaddurah‐Daouk [12] and Vanholder et al. [13], which garnered a TC 2024 of 2125 and 1323 citations, respectively. Other influential reviews by Kalantar‐Zadeh et al. [14], Ramezani and Raj [15], Bugnicourt et al. [16], and Moe and Chen [17], also made significant contributions with TC 2024 values of 760, 521, 423, and 401 citations, respectively. CPP 2024 was 1.6 times higher for reviews than for original articles. The study identified 200 highly cited documents on uremic toxins (those with a TC 2024 of 100 or more citations), 35% of which were reviews. Additionally, the 651 reviews were published across 227 journals, with Toxins publishing the highest number of reviews (79, or 12% of the total).

It has been recognized that documents in the Web of Science Core Collection could be categorized under multiple document types [10]. For example, 153 proceedings papers, 36 book chapters, 3 early access documents, 3 retracted publications, and 1 withdrawn publication were also classified as articles. Since the content of different document types varies, articles that typically include an introduction, methods, results, discussion, and conclusion were selected for further analysis [10]. A total of 2210 uremic toxin‐related articles were published in 11 different languages. Of these, 98% (2165 articles) were in English, followed by French (15 articles) and Spanish (12 articles). Other languages included German (6 articles), Chinese (3 articles), Polish (3 articles), Hungarian (2 articles), Czech, Japanese, Korean, and Russian (one each). The CPP 2024 value for English articles was 30, significantly higher than the 4.6 for non‐English articles. Similarly, the average number of authors per publication was higher for articles in English (APP = 7.2 authors) compared to non‐English articles (APP = 4.5 authors).

It is generally accepted that citation frequency is highly correlated with the passing of time following publication; newly published papers require time to accumulate citations. As shown in Figure 1, CPP 2024 reached a practical plateau by about 6 years for uremic toxin research, shorter than for other medical topics: ~14 years for fracture nonunion, ~13 years for AI/ML in kidney care, and ~10 years for breast reconstruction. This comparison indicates that, relative to other medical topics, uremic toxin‐related research has a shorter citation life.

Japan's prominence in uremic toxin research reflects both clinical urgency and research infrastructure. The country carries one of the world's highest CKD burdens relative to its population, driven by a rapidly aging demographic, which has historically directed substantial funding and institutional focus towards nephrology. This foundation supported early and sustained contributions to the field, and Japan's established expertise has in turn attracted ongoing international collaboration, further amplifying its output and citation impact.

Compared to the top 10 countries listed in Table 4, Belgium, with a TP of 129 articles and a CP C of 76 articles, achieved the highest CPP 2024 of 54 citations for its total articles and 70 citations for its international collaborative articles. This indicates a strong citation impact across both categories. In contrast, the USA led in CPP 2024 with 43 citations for single‐country articles. France, with an FP of 135 articles and an RP of 134 articles, had the highest CPP 2024 of 44 and 45 citations among first‐author and corresponding‐author articles, respectively. Germany, with an SP of nine articles, achieved the highest CPP 2024 of 36 citations for single‐author articles, highlighting a noteworthy impact despite fewer collaborative efforts. Conversely, Spain demonstrated lower CPP 2024s for their publications compared to the leaders, suggesting varying levels of citation impact relative to their publication outputs.

Figure 3 demonstrates the development trends of the top six productive countries in uremic toxin research between 1991 and 2024, showcasing distinct patterns for each nation. Japan, leading with 411 articles, exhibited significant fluctuations in its publication trend, marked by notable peaks around 2010–2014, 2018, and 2021. This variability reflects Japan's robust research activity and dynamic contributions to the field over time. The USA demonstrated a more consistent upward trajectory in publication output, which was particularly evident from 2014 onwards and culminated in a total of 379 articles by 2024. This trend underscores the country's enduring strength in biomedical sciences and its sustained investment in research infrastructure. China showed minimal contributions to uremic toxin research until approximately 2008, after which a steep rise in publications could be witnessed. This surge likely mirrored China's rapid expansion in scientific research capabilities and heightened focus on biomedical sciences in recent decades. France maintained a relatively steady output throughout the same period, with a gradual increase in publications in later years, totaling 180 articles by 2024. This stability indicates France's consistent engagement in uremic toxin research, contributing steadily to the global scientific discourse on the subject. Overall, Figure 3 highlights each country's unique trajectory in uremic toxin research, shaped by factors such as research infrastructure, funding, and evolving national priorities in biomedical sciences.

This period signifies China's rapid expansion in scientific research capabilities and its increasing emphasis on biomedical sciences. At the institutional level, the corresponding author often serves as the study's home base or origin of the research paper [10]. Word cluster analysis was utilized to identify the primary research focuses and their developmental trends, as outlined by Wang and Ho [26]. This bibliometric analysis of uremic toxin research, ranging from 1991 to 2024, provides valuable insights into the evolution of the field and highlights key areas of focus over the past three decades. The consistent prominence of keywords such as “chronic kidney disease,” “end‐stage kidney disease,” “indoxyl sulfate,” and “hemodialysis” underscores the disease pathogenesis and the therapy of clinical uremia. These findings reflect the ongoing challenges in managing chronic kidney disease, its progression to ESKD, and the continuous efforts to improve dialysis techniques for a better removal of uremic toxins.

The emergence of new keywords in recent years, particularly “inflammation,” “p‐cresyl sulfate,” “gut microbiota,” “protein‐bound uremic toxins,” and “adsorption,” signals a significant shift in research focus. This evolution aligns with the growing understanding of CKD and ESKD as complex, systemic disorders rather than purely renal conditions. The increased attention towards gut microbiota and protein‐bound uremic toxins reflects a more nuanced approach to understanding the origins and impacts of uremic toxins, moving beyond traditional water‐soluble compounds to explore more complex pathophysiological mechanisms.

The unexpected prominence of “gut microbiota” as a keyword in recent years is particularly noteworthy. This trend highlights a growing recognition of the gut‐kidney axis in the production and metabolism of uremic toxins. It suggests that researchers are increasingly exploring the role of the gut microbiome in CKD progression, ESKD development, and uremic toxin generation, potentially opening new avenues for therapeutic interventions targeting the gut to manage uremic toxicity [27, 28, 29].

The shift in research focus observed in this analysis can be attributed to several factors. Beyond descriptive trends, these changes likely reflect broader structural and conceptual shifts in nephrology research. Advancements in analytical techniques, particularly in metabolomics and systems biology, have undoubtedly played a crucial role, enabling researchers to identify and study increasingly complex uremic toxins. Additionally, the growing recognition of CKD and ESKD as systemic disorders with far‐reaching complications has likely driven research towards exploring the broader impacts of uremic toxins on various organ systems. The increasing prominence of gut microbiota–related research further reflects an evolving paradigm that recognizes the gut–kidney axis as a central contributor to uremic toxin generation. This holistic approach is reflected in the increased focus on inflammation and the exploration of novel toxin removal strategies such as adsorption, which may benefit patients across the spectrum of kidney disease, from early CKD to ESKD. Taken together, these findings suggest that technological innovation and evolving disease conceptualization, rather than purely temporal trends, have driven the progression of uremic toxin research.

Despite substantial growth in uremic toxin research, significant translational challenges remain. In particular, protein‐bound uremic toxins, such as indoxyl sulfate and p‐cresyl sulfate, continue to be inadequately cleared by conventional dialysis modalities, underscoring a persistent and clinically relevant therapeutic gap. Future research should prioritize the development of innovative extracorporeal and adsorption‐based technologies, microbiome‐targeted therapies, and precision medicine strategies that integrate metabolomics, multi‐omics profiling, and patient‐specific clinical phenotyping. In addition, improving the mechanistic understanding of toxin generation, transport, and tissue‐specific effects will be critical to identifying actionable therapeutic targets. Bridging the gap between mechanistic discoveries and clinical interventions through well‐designed translational and clinical studies will be essential to improving outcomes in patients with CKD and ESKD.

While this bibliometric analysis provides valuable insights, it is important to acknowledge its limitations. First, this study did not incorporate advanced bibliometric network analyses, such as co‐authorship networks, co‐citation mapping, or keyword co‐occurrence clustering, which may provide further insights into collaborative structures, intellectual linkages, and thematic evolution within the field. The reliance on published literature means that ongoing, unpublished research cannot be captured, potentially missing the most recent developments in the field. Furthermore, regional variations in research priorities and funding may not be fully reflected in this global analysis. In addition, the use of a single database (SCI‐EXPANDED) may have excluded relevant studies indexed in other databases, which could influence the comprehensiveness of the findings.

Despite these limitations, the study offers a comprehensive overview of trends in uremic toxin research, providing valuable guidance for future research directions and funding allocations. Future bibliometric studies integrating network‐based analytical approaches, funding source analyses, and translational outcome assessments may further enhance understanding of the structural dynamics of the field and facilitate the translation of basic science discoveries into clinical applications. As the field continues to evolve, such efforts will be essential in shaping the future of uremic toxin research and improving outcomes for patients with CKD and ESKD.

Conclusion

In summary, this bibliometric analysis provides a comprehensive overview of uremic toxin research between 1991 and 2024. It highlights the field's growth, identifies the leading contributors, and displays shifting research focuses. The findings suggest an increasingly global and multidisciplinary approach to uremic toxin research, with a potential for further international collaborations. This analysis not only maps the current landscape of uremic toxin research but also points towards promising future directions. The field's trajectory suggests a move towards more personalized and holistic approaches to managing uremic toxicity in CKD and ESKD patients. This information can guide future research directions, funding allocations, and collaborative efforts in the field of uremic toxins and nephrology. As the research continues to evolve, these insights can potentially lead to novel therapeutic strategies, improved patient outcomes, and a better quality of life for individuals living with kidney diseases.
