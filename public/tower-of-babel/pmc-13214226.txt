Cortical mapping of real-time ultrasound-imaging visual feedback for ankle dorsiflexion in patients after stroke: a fNIRS study
Authors: Feng-Yi Wang, Hao-Bin Liang, Ringo Tang-Long Zhu, Yang Xu, Qian Zhang, Yu-Yan Luo, Zhen Song, Yong-Hong Yang, Quan Wei, Yong-Ping Zheng, Jack Jiaqi Zhang, Christina Zong-Hao Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFoot drop in patients with stroke compromises gait and mobility. Active ankle movement with real-time ultrasound-imaging visual feedback (UVF) is found to be a clinically effective rehabilitation protocol; however, its underlying neural mechanisms in poststroke brains remains unclear. This functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) study aimed to investigate UVF’s neuromodulatory effects during ankle dorsiflexion (ADF) execution.MethodsFifteen post-stroke patients (SP) and fifteen healthy controls (HC) performed active ADF tasks with and without UVF. The fNIRS measured the change of oxyhemoglobin (ΔHbO) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (ΔHbR) over the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), the primary motor cortex (M1), and the premotor cortex/supplementary motor areas (PMC/SMA). Functional connectivity (FC) between these regions was calculated. The muscle morphology parameters [muscle thickness (MT), pennation angle (PA), fascicle length (FL)] were quantified via ultrasound imaging during movement execution. Two-way mixed ANOVA was used to assess the main and interaction effects of “task” and “group” on the local cortical activation levels and the FC among pairwise regions of interest (ROI). Correlations between muscle parameters and M1-related fNIRS data were analyzed.ResultsUVF did not increase ΔHbO at any ROIs; instead, it reduced ΔHbR in the I PMC/SMA in both groups. UVF also decreased the interhemispheric DLPFC connectivity in both groups, based on residual FC data. Regardless of whether UVF was provided or not, SP consistently showed stronger I PMC/SMA - I DLPFC connectivity than HC. SP also exhibited a UVF‑specific increase in the I PMC/SMA - I M1 connectivity relative to HC. During UVF and with comparable number of ADF, SP showed reduced PA and FL modulation than that of HC. Among MT, FL, and PA, only FL showed a trend-level positive association with interhemispheric M1 connectivity in SP, on top of the local M1 activation.ConclusionsGenerally, UVF during ADF was associated with the reduced bilateral prefrontal coupling and the enhanced ipsilesional premotor–motor connectivity in post-stroke patients. Ankle muscle FL displayed certain level of task sensitivity, and showed a preliminary association with interhemispheric motor coordination, implying for possible network‑level influences during UVF‑guided motor execution.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12984-026-01976-x.Keywords: Ankle dorsiflexor, Brain activation, Brain imaging, Fascicle length, Functional connectivity (FC), Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), Hemiplegic, Poststroke, Rehabilitation training, Ultrasound-imaging visual feedback (UVF)

Introduction

Stroke is the second leading cause of long-term disability in adults worldwide, affecting 174–216 individuals per 100,000 populations annually [1, 2]. Post-stroke motor impairment commonly includes foot drop, resulting from weakness in ankle dorsiflexor at paretic side [3, 4]. A notable finding was that the ankle dorsiflexion (ADF) strength in the paretic limb was approximately 50% weaker than that of the non-paretic limb [4]. This deficit critically compromises foot clearance during gait, as manifested by foot slap during loading response and toe drag in the swing phase [5, 6]. Consequently, post-stroke patients with foot drop experience reduced walking speed, compromised dynamic stability, and increased risk of falls [7]. To address these problems, active ADF exercise is often incorporated into post-stroke rehabilitation programs [8].

Active limb motor tasks integrated with extrinsic feedback have been extensively researched and clinically applied in stroke rehabilitation [9]. Such intervention facilitates alignment of stroke patients’ motor performance with their therapeutic goals, through augmented real-time biofeedback [10]. Conventional biofeedback modalities provide insights into specific facets of motor performance; however, they exhibit several limitations. Surface electromyography (sEMG) is restricted to superficial muscles’ electrical activity, and is prone to crosstalk in neuromuscular activation [11]. Kinetic measurements can capture resultant force output, but obscure the underlying neuromuscular coordination for generating forces [12]. Kinematic analysis quantifies displacements and angular movements, but fails to directly infer muscle’s internal contraction patterns for controlling posture and motions [13]. Conversely, ultrasound-imaging visual feedback (UVF) of muscles introduces a novel paradigm. It visualizes muscle morphology and contraction dynamics during the movement [14], thereby offering a more comprehensive understanding of muscle’s internal activity. Unlike conventional methods, it uniquely offers direct intramuscular activity and detailed information on thickness and fascicle dynamics in healthy adults [15–18] and patients with stroke [19–21]. These parameters serve as quantifiable behavioral outcomes: muscle thickness (MT) determines maximum force capacity, through its role as a physiological cross-sectional area proxy [22, 23]; pennation angle (PA) modulates force transmission efficiency, and influences force production per unit volume and force development rate [23, 24]; and fascicle length (FL) governs contraction velocity, and defines the excursion range for effective force generation via maximum shortening velocity [25]. They further provide mechanistic insights into pathological alterations of motor execution. While research on UVF application specifically in patients with stroke remains limited, the majority of existing studies demonstrating its efficacy have been conducted in healthy adults [15, 26] and individuals with other neuromusculoskeletal conditions [27–29]. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, only one recently published pilot study has preliminarily investigated the efficacy of UVF on community-dwelling chronic stroke survivors [19], but without any direct evidence on the underlying neuromodulatory mechanism.

While UVF seems a promising strategy for improving ADF through real-time feedback of muscle activity, to maximize its efficacy in neurorehabilitation, a systematic investigation into its neuromodulatory mechanism is essential. Stroke recovery is fundamentally a neural process, dependent on cortical activation and the remodeling of neural networks through neurocircuitry reactivation and reconstruction [30, 31]. Therefore, the critical question regarding UVF in this population is not only whether it enhances motor performance through augmented/better feedback, but also how it influences this essential neural plasticity. Previous studies have demonstrated that extrinsic visual feedback was effective in facilitating motor related cortical activation during active limb movements [32–34]. For instance, visual feedback on motion speed enhanced activation in the primary motor cortex (M1) in both stroke patients (SP) and healthy controls (HC) [35]. Similarly, visual feedback on gain-related improvements in force control increased activation in M1, the premotor cortex (PMC), and the supplementary motor area (SMA) [32]. Furthermore, studies of active cycling showed that visual feedback on cycling speed enhanced PMC and SMA activation, correlating with improved motor performance in both SP [36] and healthy adults [37]. Collectively, these findings have indicated that extrinsic visual feedback significantly boosted activation in key motor areas (M1, PMC and SMA). As a form of real-time extrinsic visual feedback, UVF may elicit similar cortical responses and activation patterns. However, its unique capacity to provide direct visualization of muscle morphology could also lead to distinct neural effects.

Cortical activation patterns, which are measurable via neuroimaging and neuro-physiological technologies, such as functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) and electroencephalography (EEG), and functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS); reflect the brain’s real-time processing of sensorimotor tasks [38]. The spatial resolution of fNIRS is intermediate between that of fMRI and EEG [39]. Combined with its portability and tolerance to motion artifacts [40], fNIRS is uniquely suited for mapping cortical activation during ecologically valid tasks like UVF-guided ADF.

This study used fNIRS to monitor hemodynamic responses, reflecting the level of cortical activation, during active ankle dorsiflexion. Cortical activity was compared between conditions of with and without real-time UVF in both SP and HC. It is hypothesized that: (1) cortical activation during active ADF would be greater with real-time UVF than without it; (2) neural activity during active ADF would be more pronounced in SP than in HC, reflecting heightened effort and adaptation following a brain injury [41]; and (3) SP’s dynamic changes in ultrasound-imaging-derived muscle morphology (e.g., thickness) would positively correlated with M1-related cortical activation or functional connectivity (FC).

Methods

Participants

This observational, single-center, cross-sectional study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the West China Hospital, Sichuan University, China (Reference No. 20241696, October 11, 2024). Fifteen post-stroke patients were enrolled from the Rehabilitation Medicine Center of the West China Hospital. The inclusion criteria were with: (1) radiologically confirmed unilateral subcortical stroke without cortically involved lesion; (2) first-ever stroke with an onset duration of more than 30 days; (3) aged between 18 and 80 years; and (4) providing written informed consent to participate in this study. The exclusion criteria were with: (1) severe spasticity of plantar flexors or dorsiflexors of the affected ankle (Modified Ashworth Scale score ≥ 2) [42]; (2) excessive pain in the affected lower limb (≥ 4 on the 10-point Visual Analogue Scale) [43]; (3) history of major trauma or acute injury at the affected lower limb (e.g., fracture, ankle sprain occurring within the past 6 months); (4) contracture of the affected ankle; and (5) cognitive impairment that might hinder participants from understanding and following instructions. Fifteen right-handed healthy control participants without any known psychiatric, neurological, or lower limb impairments were also recruited.

Experimental procedures

Experimental design is illustrated in Fig. 1a. The experiment was conducted in a quiet, enclosed room to minimize distractions. Participants performed the ADF task with and without UVF. Each task consisted of 20 s of dorsiflexion movements followed by 30 s of rest. It was repeated six times in a block design as a trial, accounting for 5 min in total [44]. Participants were instructed to perform ADF with their maximum effort during the 20-second task, at their own pace, and without limits on repetitions (Fig. 1c). With UVF, participants were instructed to target at creating the greatest visible changes on the ultrasound images of their muscles. There was a 7-min rest between each of the two trials (with and without UVF).

C, Contralateral (right hemisphere); I, Ipsilateral (left hemisphere); DLPFC, Dorsolateral prefrontal cortex; M1, Primary motor cortex; PMC/SMA, Premotor and supplementary motor area

An ultrasound imaging system (TE9, Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics Co., Shenzhen, China) was used to visualize the tibialis anterior (TA) during the UVF condition, with a sampling rate set at 40 Hz. An experienced researcher specializing in ultrasound imaging operated the system, and positioned the probe perpendicularly to the TA muscle fibers at the proximal one-third of the line connecting the fibular head and the medial malleolus (Fig. 1a) [45]. The researcher informed participants in advance that, the muscle shown on the ultrasound imaging in real-time was the primary muscle that responsible for ADF. Each post-stroke patient performed tasks using the paretic lower limb. Healthy controls’ task-performing limbs were allocated by matching to the stroke patients’ affected side, by adopting the same side of task-performing limb (i.e., homologous to patients’ affected side) for dorsiflexion tasks.

An fNIRS system (NirSmart-6000 A, Danyang Huichuang Medical Equipment Co., Zhenjiang, China) [46] was used to detect the hemodynamic data during the 5-min ADF task. The sampling rate was set at 11 Hz. The used wavelengths were 730 nm and 850 nm. The fNIRS system was designed with 37 probes, consisting of 21 sources and 16 detectors, with 48 channels presented in these probes. Figure 1b illustrates the positions of the sources, detectors, and channels. The fNIRS system was used to measure hemodynamic activity from the bilateral regions of interest (ROIs): (1) the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC); (2) the M1; and (3) the secondary motor cortex including PMC/SMA.

Clinical assessments; including the Manual Muscle Testing (MMT) for ankle dorsiflexion muscle strength [47], Brunnstrom Recovery Stage (BRS) for motor impairment [48], and Modified Barthel Index (MBI) for activities of daily living (ADL) independence [49]; were performed by an experienced occupational therapist.

fNIRS data acquisition and analysis

The preprocessing of fNIRS signals were performed using the Homer3 toolbox (version 1.87.0) [50] in MATLAB (R2024a, MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA). Channel-wise quality metrics, including coefficient of variation (CV) and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), were calculated for each participant and condition. Channels exhibiting CV > 10% or SNR < 2 were excluded from further analysis [51, 52]. Following quality control, data preprocessing was performed step-by-step, including [51, 53, 54]: (1) Intensity-to-Optical Density Conversion to convert raw light intensity signals to optical density; (2) Principal Component Analysis (PCA) filtering with a variance threshold of 0.97 to remove global physiological noise; (3) Motion Artifact Detection by identifying signal segments with excessive deviation in amplitude (> 50%) or standard deviation (> 20) lasting over 0.5 s, with a 1-s buffer added around each detected artifact; (4) Motion Artifact Correction via spline interpolation with a smoothing parameter of p = 0.99 to preserve the underlying hemodynamic trend; (5) Bandpass Filtering (0.01–0.2 Hz) to remove high-frequency physiological noise (e.g., cardiac pulsations) and low-frequency drift; (6) Optical Density-to-Hemoglobin Conversion using the modified Beer-Lambert law with partial pathlength factors of 6 for both wavelengths, to derive concentration changes of oxyhemoglobin (ΔHbO) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (ΔHbR); and (7) Block Averaging with a time range of −5 to 25 s relative to task onset.

Spatial registration of the 48 channels was mapped onto the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) standard brain template and then imported into the NirSpark software package (V1.8.9, Danyang Huichuang Medical Equipment Co., Zhenjiang, China) for anatomical alignment [55]. The ROIs were defined based on MNI-normalized coordinates, including: (1) right DLPFC (channels 13, 14, 23 & 24); (2) left DLPFC (channels 17, 18, 25 & 26); (3) right M1 (channels 20 & 21); (4) left M1 (channels 29 & 31); (5) right PMC/SMA (channels 19, 22, 34 & 35); and (6) left PMC/SMA (channels 30, 32, 46 & 48). The complete channel-to-ROI mapping with MNI coordinates and anatomical labels is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

To facilitate group-level analysis and comparison, the spatial representation of brain hemispheres was standardized as in all analyses and figures, the right hemisphere consistently represents the ipsilesional hemisphere (i.e., the hemisphere containing the lesion in stroke patients), and the left hemisphere represents the contralesional (non-lesioned) hemisphere. Specifically, for stroke patients with a right-hemisphere lesion, the fNIRS data from the right and left hemispheres were directly labeled as ipsilesional and contralesional, respectively; and were analyzed without spatial flipping. For stroke patients with a left-hemisphere lesion, the fNIRS data from the left and right hemispheres were spatially flipped to the right and left hemispheres, respectively. After flipping, the data originally from the left (lesioned) hemisphere would be represented on the right and labeled as ipsilesional, while the data originally from the right (non-lesioned) hemisphere would be represented on the left and labeled as contralesional for stroke patients with a left-hemisphere lesion. For healthy control participants, the limb performing the ankle dorsiflexion task was matched to the paretic side of the stroke patients. The fNIRS data from these participants were subjected to the same spatial flipping procedure as their matched stroke patient. For example, if a control participant was matched to a patient with a left-hemisphere lesion (whose data were flipped), the control participant’s data were also flipped. This ensured that the “Ipsilateral (I)” (right/ipsilesional hemisphere for standardized space) and “Contralateral (C)” (left/contralesional hemisphere for standardized space) labels were consistent across all participants in the final analysis, as well as in all subsequent results, figures, and tables. The ankle dorsiflexion task was always performed by the limb that contralateral to the ipsilesional hemisphere (i.e., the left limb in the standardized space).

Cortical activation was quantified by block-averaging hemodynamic responses across six task blocks, each spanning a 50-s epoch that segmented into three phases: (1) pre-task baseline (−5 s to 0 s, relative to task onset); (2) task execution (0 s to 20 s); and (3) post-task recovery (20 s to 50 s). To account for the hemodynamic delay (approximately 5 s) that required for HbO to stabilize after task initiation, the ΔHbO and ΔHbR signals from 5 to 25 s were extracted and averaged, representing the task-evoked cortical activity [53].

FC was quantified in 4 steps. (1) Time series extraction [56, 57]: two types of ΔHbO time series were prepared. For raw FC, preprocessed signals were used directly. For residual FC, a General Linear Model (GLM) was implemented to minimize task-evoked coactivation. The task-induced fluctuations were modeled using boxcar regressors and convolved with a canonical hemodynamic response function (HRF) and its first temporal derivatives. The multiple linear regression was then applied to the ROI time series, to regress out task-related components and extract the residuals. (2) Connectivity matrix generation: for each participant, pairwise FC was estimated by computing Pearson correlation coefficients between the prepared time series (either raw or residual), yielding a 6 × 6 symmetric weighted connectivity matrix per condition. (3) Significance testing and spurious connection control [58]: the significance of each pairwise link was assessed using 100 amplitude-adjusted Fourier transform (AAFT) surrogate datasets. This procedure preserved the original amplitude distribution and power spectrum, while randomizing the phase, to eliminate true inter-regional synchrony. A connection was deemed significant if its absolute Pearson correlation coefficient exceeded the surrogate-derived threshold (mean + 2 SD). Original r values were retained for significant links, while non-significant connections were set to zero. (4) Data normalization: all retained coefficients were Fisher’s Z-transformed, to ensure normality for subsequent parametric group-level statistical comparisons [44].

Ultrasound imaging data acquisition and analysis

Real-time ultrasound imaging of the TA was recorded during the UVF-guided ADF task. Muscle morphological parameters—MT, PA, and FL—were quantified between annotated aponeuroses across sequential ultrasound frames, using a validated customized sonomyography (SMG) software [16]. This software was developed based on a custom synthesizing pipeline, and has demonstrated robust performance in extracting and measuring muscle morphological characteristics, with strong correlation to manual measurements (R2> 0.900) [16, 19]. The baseline value (in millimeters or mm for MT, degrees or ° for PA, and mm for FL) for each muscle morphological parameter was determined, based on one pre-task resting-state ultrasound image frame. A single session of muscle contraction was visually determined at the mid part of each block of ADF sessions first. The maximum/peak MT value, maximum/peak PA value, and minimum/trough FL value were then extracted. Normalization was performed by dividing the averaged peak/trough values from six blocks by the baseline value. Figure 2 presents the sample ultrasound images of the TA muscle at rest and at peak contraction, from a representative healthy control and a stroke participant. For the UVF-guided ADF condition, the number of ankle dorsiflexion performed in each 20-s active block was quantified offline, by manually counting the visible muscle contractions from the recorded ultrasound videos.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (v25.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data normality assumptions were verified via Shapiro-Wilk tests. A two-way mixed ANOVA [2 × 2 design: within-subjects factor = task (ADF vs. ADF + UVF); between-subjects factor = group (HC vs. SP)] was conducted to assess within- and between-group effects. Independent t-tests were used to analyze the differences in muscle morphological parameters between the two groups, for normally distributed data. Mann-Whitney U test were used, for non-normally distributed data. Pearson’s correlations (or Spearman’s rank correlations for non-normally distributed data) were performed, to evaluate associations between muscle morphological parameters (MT, PA, and FL) and M1-related neural metrics (C/I M1 activation and interhemispheric M1–M1 FC) in the stroke group. To control for multiple comparisons within this analytic family, Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (BH‑FDR) correction was applied. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Demographics and clinical data

Demographic and clinical characteristics of participants are summarized in Table 1. This study included 15 SP (13 males & 2 females, mean age 55.9 ± 11.3 years) and 15 HC (10 males & 5 females, mean age 46.5 ± 14.8 years), with no statistically significant differences in age or sex between the two groups. Both cohorts exhibited matched limb-side allocation (right/left: 6/9). A detailed summary of lesion laterality and the associated data processing for each participant is provided in Supplementary Table 2. Stroke etiology included 8 infarction and 7 hemorrhage cases, with post-stroke durations of 1–6 months (9 patients), 6–12 months (3 patients), and > 12 months (3 patients). Clinical assessments revealed variability in ankle strength (MMT: 1–4), recovery stages (Brunnstrom: 2–6), and functional independence (MBI: 63.6 ± 19.3). The complete distribution of clinical scale scores for each patient is provided in Supplementary Table 3.

ΔHbO changes

At the ROI level, there was no significant interaction or main effects of “task” and “group” on the ΔHbO of both sides’ DLPFC, M1, or PMC/SMA (p > 0.05, Table 2); although the I PMC/SMA showed a marginally significant interaction effect [F(1,28) = 3.923, p = 0.058, ηp² = 0.123]. For “group” comparisons within each task, the HC group showed a significantly higher ΔHbO than the SP group in the ADF condition (mean difference = 0.038, p = 0.008), while there was no significant group difference in the ADF + UVF condition (mean difference = −0.012, p = 0.598). For “task” comparisons within each group, there was no significant difference between the two tasks in either the HC (mean difference = 0.021, p = 0.257) or the SP (mean difference = −0.029, p = 0.111) group. At channel level, no statistically significant effect was found (p > 0.05, Supplementary Table 6).

ΔHbR changes

At the ROI level, there was no significant interaction or main effects of “task” and “group” on the ΔHbR of both sides’ DLPFC, M1, or C PMC/SMA (p > 0.05, Table 3). There was a significant main effect of “task” on the ΔHbR of the I PMC/SMA [F(1,28) = 5.406, p = 0.028, ηp² = 0.162], where the ΔHbR was significantly lower during the ADF + UVF task than that of the ADF task in both groups. At the channel level (Supplementary Table 7), Channel 35 (located within the I PMC/SMA) showed a significant main effect of “task” [F(1,28) = 5.000, p = 0.033, ηp² = 0.152], with ΔHbR significantly lower in the ADF + UVF task than that of the ADF task. There was no additional significant effects in the rest ΔHbR results.

Muscle morphological parameters

The overall and individualized baseline data of HC (MT: 22.18 ± 2.95 mm, PA: 6.87 ± 2.25°, and FL: 96.08 ± 33.36 mm) and SP (MT: 21.37 ± 3.64 mm, PA: 6.58 ± 2.13°, and FL: 98.05 ± 15.97 mm) group’s muscle morphological parameters are summarized in Supplementary Table 10. Discussion

This study investigated the cortical mapping of ADF with and without UVF in both patients with stroke and their matched healthy controls using fNIRS. The findings showed that UVF did not induce increase in cortical activation as measured by ΔHbO, but elicited reduction in ΔHbR within the I PMC/SMA. No significant group difference in cortical activation magnitude was observed between SP and HC across the examined ROIs. FC analysis revealed that UVF decreased interhemispheric DLPFC connectivity (“task” effect), while SP exhibited stronger I PMC/SMA - I DLPFC connectivity (“group” effect) and altered within-hemisphere motor pathway connectivity under UVF than HC. In addition, FL showed a trend‑level positive association with interhemispheric M1 connectivity in SP during the UVF‑guided task.

Cortical activation: hemodynamic dissociation and neural specificity

The findings partially supported the first hypothesis that real-time UVF did not increase ΔHbO but elicited a localized decrease in ΔHbR within the I PMC/SMA, indicating a selective enhancement of cortical engagement during ADF. Compared with ΔHbO, ΔHbR is generally considered less affected by superficial physiological noise and more closely associated with changes in local oxygen extraction [59, 60]. This makes ΔHbR potentially a more sensitive indicator of task related neural activation [61]. Therefore, the ΔHbR decrease in the I PMC/SMA may reflect a genuine focal neural response to UVF, even in the absence of a corresponding ΔHbO change.

The selective recruitment of the I PMC/SMA was functionally consistent with their established roles in motor planning [62], as well as in sensorimotor integration and the online adjustment of movement based on external feedback [63]. By providing real-time visualization of muscle morphology, UVF appeared to transform a simple repetitive motor task into a more precise task that required continuous performance monitoring and adjustment [64]. The observed reduced ΔHbO in stroke patients during ADF only, which was comparable to healthy controls’ neural activation during ADF + UVF, further suggested that UVF may temporarily enhance recruitment of motor‑planning resources in the I PMC/SMA. This is in accordance with the findings of a previous study, which reported similar patterns of task‑/feedback‑dependent compensatory recruitment of I PMC/SMA in stroke populations [65].

Contrary to the hypothesis that stroke patients would show widespread cortical hyperactivation to compensate for motor deficits [41], this study observed no significant main effect of group for ΔHbO or ΔHbR across the examined ROIs. This absence of a overall group difference might be due to the characteristics of the cohort and the controlled experimental design. This study has adopted a controlled experimental setup, which may have induced comparable task engagement and pacing in the two groups. While the recruited stroke participants demonstrated apparent motor impairments, including muscle weakness (MMT ≤ 2) and reduced dynamic muscle morphological changes (PA and FL), their number of ADF repetitions during the UVF condition was matched between participant groups. Additionally, this study has only considered individuals with subcortical stroke, which might minimize the subject heterogeneity that typically introduced by direct cortical injury. This inclusion criteria for restricting subcortical stroke may have reduced the likelihood of detecting widespread activation differences in participants using fNIRS. Specifically, the stroke participants may have utilized the compensation from nearby intact neural resources, to execute a simple motor task of ADF (+ UVF), resulting in a near-normal or comparable neural activation magnitude that can be detected by fNIRS at cortex [63]. Future studies may utilize modalities of fMRI, which can detect the subcortical activities and changes, to verify this.

FC: cognitive-motor coupling during visuomotor feedback

The observed association of UVF engagement with the decreased connectivity between bilateral DLPFC in both SP and HC, suggests that the presence of real‑time visual feedback might have reduced the extent of interhemispheric prefrontal coordination that required during ADF. One cautious interpretation is that UVF provided participants with augmented/additional task‑relevant visuomotor information, which may have lessened their reliance on bilateral prefrontal co‑monitoring that typically involved in internally guided movements. Under such conditions, participants might have depended more on the direct visuomotor cues that derived from muscle morphology on ultrasound imaging, potentially contributing to the observed reduction in cross‑hemispheric DLPFC coupling [66]. Previous studies reported that DLPFC activation and connectivity varied with task structure and informational demands [67, 68]. The observed reduction in bilateral DLPFC coupling under UVF in the current study may therefore reflect a task‑dependent reallocation of prefrontal involvement, rather than a shift toward any specific cognitive strategy.

The consistently stronger I PMC/SMA - I DLPFC coupling in SP than HC may highlight neural compensation for post-stroke motor deficits [69], regardless of external guidance. The selective increase in I PMC/SMA - I M1 coupling in SP only during ADF + UVF may suggest that, UVF temporarily facilitated more effective interaction within the ipsilesional motor network [70]. This is also supported by the established roles of the PMC/SMA in motor planning and the M1 in task execution [71].

Relationship between FL and corticomotor connectivity

The positive association between FL and interhemispheric M1-M1 connectivity during the UVF‑guided task is a trend, as it did not survive BH-FDR correction. Nevertheless, the direction of this association is mechanistically plausible. FL reflects muscle fascicle excursion and contraction dynamics, which are closely linked to motor unit recruitment strategies and peripheral mechanical efficiency [22]. Therefore, this trend may suggest that individuals with greater fascicle excursion engage in more coordinated bilateral corticomotor interactions. Additionally, FL dynamics did not correlate with local M1 activation amplitudes. This independence suggests that the observed peripheral–central coupling reflected here may relate more to network-level integration than to focal cortical activation. These interpretation align with prior findings that stroke survivors’ motor performance correlated to their interhemispheric coupling levels, and enhanced interhemispheric coupling facilitated the motor recovery after stroke [69, 72].

However, these observations must be interpreted with caution. For the primary outcome of M1 activation, a post-hoc power analysis yielded a statistical power of only 0.11, confirming that the current study was underpowered due to the small sample size. The fact that a trend was nevertheless observed under such low-powered conditions raises the possibility of a meaningful effect, but this interpretation remains tentative. Rather than indicating a definitive physiological relationship, the finding is better understood as a preliminary signal that warrants careful validation.

Future research directions

There are several research directions that future studies can consider building on the evidence. Firstly, in addition to fNIRS and ultrasound imaging data during ADF with and without UVF, future study could also further measure the biomechanical outcomes of muscle electrical signal, joint angle and force output during different conditions. This can facilitate a more comprehensive understanding of the differences, changes, and underlying mechanisms in the neuromotor pathway, by simultaneously investigating brain activity, muscle activation and contraction, and biomechanical outcomes. Secondly, concerning the UVF’s potential in engaging specific cortical regions and modulate motor-network connectivity, some longitudinal interventional studies shall be conducted in the future, to determine whether long-term UVF training could enhance the neuroplasticity and functional recovery in stroke patients or not. Thirdly, in addition to the qualitative visualization of original TA muscle structure, future studies could also employ some processed ultrasound imaging parameters, including but not limited to the values of FL and PA of various muscles. This can help verify what is the fundamental factor that enhanced motor learning and led to improved motor performance, thereby promote precision rehabilitation in future clinical practice. Finally, concerning the observed association between FL and FC, future studies with larger cohorts are needed to explore whether the muscle FL and its dynamic changes could serve as a biomarker for corticomotor integration. This may inspire future developments of more simple assessment tools and rehabilitation strategies.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the study’s control for motor output was partial. While the number of dorsiflexion during ADF + UVF was counted and found to be comparable between two groups, information on each participant’s motion range, speed, and force outcome has remained unclear. This made it difficult to distinguish and determine whether the observed cortical differences were due to the presence of UVF or the potential differences in task execution in participants. Secondly, this study has adopted a cross-sectional design. The absence of longitudinal follow-up and results on long-term effects of UVF has made it difficult to establish the casual relationship between UVF and neuroplasticity. Thirdly, while lower limb motor tasks exhibits minimal functional asymmetry in healthy adults [73], this study did not assess the leg dominance of healthy control participants, or match healthy controls’ non-dominant limbs to stroke patients’ affected limbs, as that of previous upper limb studies. Future studies could consider matching the healthy control participants’ non-dominant limbs to patients’ affected limbs to address this.

Conclusions

This pilot fNIRS study preliminarily showed that, real-time UVF during ADF elicited distinct neurophysiological responses, in both HC and ischemic/hemorrhagic SP with monohemispheric subcortical lesion. While UVF did not produce significant cortical activation as measured by ΔHbO, it induced a ΔHbR decrease in the I PMC/SMA and modulated FC, in a task- and group-specific manner. In SP, FL showed a trend-level positive association with interhemispheric M1 connectivity, suggesting that muscle FL dynamics may be linked to neural network-level processes that engaged during UVF-guided motor execution. These preliminary observations indicate that UVF may help recruit motor-planning pathways, with FL-based muscle metrics offering a potential marker of peripheral–central interactions, in post-stroke rehabilitation. Further studies are still needed to verify and confirm this, before future clinical practice.
