Improvement of restless leg syndrome in maintenance hemodialysis patients with limb ischemic preconditioning: a single-center randomized controlled clinical trial
Authors: Juntian Xu, Yuan Qi, Yushang Tang, Wanfen Zhang, Qiaoyang Zhang, Linfang Xu, Zhongqin Ding, Tongqiang Liu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study evaluated the efficacy and safety of limb ischemic preconditioning (LIPC) in treating restless leg syndrome (RLS) in maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) patients.MethodsA total number of 45 patients participated in the study. They were randomly divided into LIPC group and control group. The LIPC was performed by inflating the limb ischemic preconditioning training device in the patient’s thigh to 200 mmHg to create transient ischemia, whereas control group inflated the device to 20 mmHg. International Restless Legs Syndrome (IRLS), Clinical Global Impression Scale (CGI-S), and Medical Outputs Study Sleep Scale were employed to evaluate LIPC effectiveness. The primary endpoint was the ‘rate of clinical improvement in RLS severity’, defined as the percentage of patients who had an IRLS score decrease of ≥5 points in each group.ResultsAfter intervention, the rate of clinical improvement in RLS severity was 56.5% in the LIPC group and 13.6% in the control group (13 (56.5) vs 3 (13.6), p = 0.003). In addition, the LIPC group’s IRLS, CGI-S scores, the sleep disturbance and somnolence scores showed a significant downward trend compared to the control group (−5.5 ± 5.3 vs − 1.0 ± 3.8, p = 0.002; −1.7 ± 1.2 vs − 0.5 ± 1.4, p = 0.003; −15.5 ± 17.8 vs 3.7 ± 12.0, p < 0.001; −9.9 ± 18.8 vs − 2.4 ± 8.6, p = 0.003). During the study, there were no serious adverse event in any of the patients.ConclusionsLIPC could be employed to effectively and safely alleviate the RLS symptoms in MHD patients.Keywords: Limb ischemic preconditioning, restless leg syndrome, maintenance hemodialysis, chronic kidney disease, sleep quality

Introduction

Restless legs syndrome (RLS) is a sensory motor disorder. Normally, patients with this condition experience unpleasant leg abnormalities which can worsen at night, accompanied by a strong impulse to move both legs [1]. Over time, sleep is disrupted due to this condition, leading to issues, such as decreased quality of life, accumulation of negative emotions, and social disorders [2]. Compared with the rate of RLS in the general population, the incidence rate of RLS in maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) patients is significantly higher, estimated to be within the range 12%–62% [3]. Conventional drugs that can be used to treat RLS include dopamine receptor agonists, gabapentin, carbamazepine, and iron [4]. However, some studies have shown that the long-term use of drugs can lead to worsening of RLS symptoms [5]. Based on these results, effective nondrug therapy may have more advantages than drug therapy.

Recent research results have indicated that the patho­genesis of RLS may include abnormal iron metabolism, dopamine dysfunction, peripheral circulatory disorders, hypoadenosinergic state [6,7]. Limb ischemic preconditioning (LIPC) refers to a medical method that stimulates the production of effector substances in human tissues by causing temporary ischemia in the local area of the limb. In this medical approach, the produced effector substances can reach the local and distant tissues and organs through neurohumoral pathways, thereby facilitating the treatment of various injuries [8]. LIPC is a safe and convenient noninvasive intervention measure. Meanwhile, the results of relevant studies have shown that LIPC can improve the lower limb blood flow perfusion and local circulation by promoting the sympathetic nerve tension [9,10]. In addition, the results of a number of studies have suggested that LIPC can be used to promote adenosine secretion, which improves the hypoadenosinergic state in patients with RLS [11,12]. Therefore, we speculated that LIPC can attenuate RLS symptoms in MHD patients. In this paper, we report our results from a prospective, randomized controlled clinical trial conducted to establish the effectiveness and safety of LIPC in alleviating RLS symptoms in MHD patients.

Research methods

Experimental design

This was a prospective randomized controlled trial, in which MHD patients with RLS were randomly assigned to a treatment group and a control group in a 1:1 ratio using the random number table method. Subsequently, the subjects in these two experimental groups received treatment with LIPC (with an upper pressure limit of 200 mmHg during the intervention) or sham LIPC (with an upper pressure limit of 20 mmHg during the intervention), respectively. The experimental plan was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Changzhou NO.2 People’s Hospital ([2022] KY012-01). In addition, the trial was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registration Center (ChiCTR220066008). All the subjects signed an informed consent form.

Patients

Patients who underwent MHD with RLS at Changzhou NO.2 People’s Hospital from November 2022 to March 2023 were recruited as the study subjects. These patients received hemodialysis treatment with a frequency of 4 h per session, 3 times a week. Meanwhile, the blood flow velocity during the hemodialysis process is controlled between 200–280 mL/min. Besides, all patients were diagnosed by experienced neurologists according to the international standards established by the International RLS Study Group (IRLSSG) in 2014 [13]. The exclusion criteria during diagnosis and treatment were the following: patients with risk of deep vein thrombosis of the lower limbs, coagulation dysfunction, severe hypertension, lower limb muscle joint disorder, or diabetic peripheral neuropathy. In addition, patients with recent acute cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, as well as patients who had undergone blood perfusion therapy within the past 3 months or had recently planned to undergo blood perfusion therapy, were also excluded from the experiment.

Subjects taking medications for RLS were only eligible for enrollment if they were taking stable doses of medications for more than two months and still reporting persistent symptoms. The major therapeutic drugs for RLS are dopaminergic drugs, α2δ channel ligands, and opioid preparations [4]. Besides, iron and antidepressant drugs have an impact on the symptoms of RLS [14,15], and sedative and hypnotic drugs can affect the sleep score of patients, so the use of these drugs also needs to be registered.

Limb ischemic preconditioning

The usage of LIPC device (RIP-906D, Naunve Technology Co., Shenzhen, China) was as follows. The patients adopted the supine position to receive treatment with LIPC (LIPC group) or sham LIPC (control group). In the LIPC group, a limb ischemic preconditioning training device was adopted to compress the upper and middle segments of the patient’s (left or right) thigh for 5 min (200 mmHg). After that, the pressure was released for 5 min (0 mmHg). The entire aforementioned operation was repeated 5 times, with a total number of 50 min of experimental duration. Meanwhile, the lower limbs of both groups of patients receiving LIPC treatment were alternated daily to ensure that both lower limbs received sufficient peripheral intervention. The duration of this was 4 weeks. An LIPC device was provided for use to the subjects in their registered households. Furthermore, each subject was provided with detailed guidance on how to use leg wrap and how to operate the device. The subjects were required to record the daily usage of LIPC devices and any changes in their RLS symptoms. Finally, the coordinator of the study monitored the daily use of LIPC devices by the patients through WeChat to ensure subject compliance.

Primary and secondary endpoint events

According to a study conducted by Ondo et al., the reduction in the International Restless Legs Scale (IRLS) score of 5 points can be defined as the minimal clinically important change [16,17]. Given this threshold, a decrease of ≥ 5 points in the IRLS score of patients after intervention was defined as clinical improvement. The calculation method for clinical improvement rate was as follows: the number of patients with a decrease of ≥ 5 points in each group’s IRLS score/the total number of patients in that group. The clinical improvement rate of RLS in each group of patients was the primary endpoint. The secondary endpoint was the severity of RLS and the changes in the patient’s sleep quality. The severity of RLS was evaluated by the changes in the IRLS score and the Clinical Global Impression Level of Illness Score (CGI-S) [18]. In addition, the sleep quality of the patients was evaluated by the changes in sleep disturbance, sleep adequacy, sleep amount, and somnolence scores included in the Medical Outputs Study Sleep Scale (MOS). Sleep quantity is the number of hours a person sleeps per night. Sleep disturbance measure the ability to fall asleep and maintain a restful sleep. Adequate sleep refers to the adequacy of sleep, that is, sleep sufficient to restore wakefulness. Somnolence is a measure of daytime sleepiness. All sleep domains except sleep quantity were recalibrated on a 0–100 scale that represents the percentage of a particular sleep domain [19].

Evaluation of biochemical indicators

Blood samples were collected after fasting and before dialysis (on a dialysis day) within one week prior to intervention. Specifically, hemoglobin (Hb), serum creatinine (Cr), serum urea nitrogen (BUN), serum iron (SI), serum calcium (SCa), serum phosphorus (SP), and serum albumin were detected through a fully automated biochemical analyzer. Serum ferritin (SF) was detected by chemiluminescent particle immunoassay (CMIA). Vitamin B12 was determined by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Additionally, the total iron binding force (TIBC) was measured by the triazine ferrous colorimetric method. The transferrin saturation (TS) was determined using the formula: TS = (SI/TIBC) × 100%, and parathyroid hormone (PTH) was measured by the electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA). The Kt/V value was obtained from the Daugirdas single compartment model formula.

Data analysis

In this paper, PASS 15 software was adopted to calculate the sample size. Based on the set environment as bilateral α = 0.05, power of test = 80%, the remission rate of the control group was approximately 10% according to the relevant literature evidence and preliminary experimental results. Meanwhile, it is estimated that the remission rate of an LIPC treatment group can reach 55% [11]. The subjects in the experimental and control groups were distributed in a ratio of 1:1, with a 20% withdrawal rate. Hence, the estimated total sample size should be no less than 46 subjects.

Statistical software (SPSS, version 25.0) was utilized for analysis. The values of continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (lower quartile, upper quartile). The values of categorical variables were expressed as numbers (percentages). The independent sample t-test was employed to compare the clinical characteristics and outcome normal distribution variables. The Mann–Whitney test was used for comparisons between non-normal distribution variables. For the difference in skewed data, the Hodges–Lehmann estimate based on the Mann–Whitney U-test was used to calculate 95% confidence intervals for median differences. Additionally, chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was adopted to compare the categorical variables. The outcome variables before and after the intervention were paired by t-tests. Finally, Pearson correlation analysis was employed to evaluate the correlation between the severity of symptoms and the extent of improvement. Overall, all tests were bilateral, with a P-value < 0.05. Most test results had statistical significance.

Results

Situation of the enrolled patients

A number of 46 out of the 65 initially selected MHD patients with RLS fully met the experimental criteria. Through the random number table, these patients were randomly assigned to the LIPC group (n = 23) and the control group (n = 23). In the control group, one patient withdrew from the experiment due to pneumonia after one week of intervention. Finally, 23 patients in the LIPC group and 22 patients in the control group completed the four-week intervention process (Figure 1.).

Primary endpoint

After 4 weeks of intervention, the severity of RLS in 13 (56.5%) subjects in the LIPC group improved clinically, whereas such improvement was observed in only three (13.6%) subjects in the control group. The differences between the test results of the two groups were statistically significant (13 (56.5) vs 3 (13.6), p = 0.003) (Table 2).

Secondary endpoint

At baseline, there was no statistically significant difference in the IRLS score and CGI-S score between the two groups of patients (p > 0.05) (Table 3). After 4 weeks of intervention, the severity indicated by the IRLS score of 9 out of 23 subjects in the LIPC group shifted from ‘very severe’ or ‘severe’ ‘levels to moderate’ or ‘mild’ levels. However, such changes were observed in only two in subjects in the control group (9 (39.1) vs 2 (9.1), p = 0.021) (Figure 2). After the intervention, the IRLS and CGI-S scores of the subjects in the LIPC group showed a significant decrease compared to those scores before the intervention (15.2 ± 3.3 vs 20.7 ± 5.0, p < 0.001; 3.0 ± 1.0 vs 4.7 ± 1.3, p < 0.001). However, no significant change was established in the IRLS and CGI-S scores of the subjects in the control group before and after the intervention (p > 0.05) (Table 3). In addition, there were significant differences in the changes in IRLS scores and CGI-S scores (△ difference) between the two groups of subjects (−5.5 ± 5.3 vs − 1.0 ± 3.8, p = 0.002; −1.7 ± 1.2 vs − 0.5 ± 1.4, p = 0.003) (Table 3).

Untoward reaction

There were no untoward reactions caused by LIPC and no aggravation of RLS during the intervention. No ecchymosis at the compression cuff was present in the patients receiving the intervention. During the intervention process, the subjects had no neurological symptoms such as tremors and sensory abnormalities. In addition, no signs of lower limb weakness were observed after the intervention.

Discussion

We found that four-week LIPC can be used effectively to reduce the severity of RLS in MHD patients. The clinical improvement rate of the patients in LIPC Group reached 56.5%. Along with the severity of RLS and the score of CGI-S, the scores of sleep disturbance and somnolence included in the MOS scale were also improved. However, there was no significant difference in the score of sleep adequacy (included in the MOS scale) before and after the intervention.

We found that the majority of enrolled patients had RLS of ‘moderate’ severity or greater before the intervention, and almost all patients had impaired sleep quality indicated by MOS scale scores. A subset of patients received medical therapy but remained symptomatic.

Currently, pharmacotherapy remains the treatment of choice for most RLS patients. However, considering the burden of renal function in MHD patients and the common side effects of available medications for RLS, effective non-pharmacological treatment would be an attractive option for these patients [5].

This randomized, sham-controlled trial was conducted to test our hypothesis that LIPC could relieve RLS symptoms in MHD patients. Due to the advantages of LIPC, such as ease of operation, noninvasiveness, and safety, increasing clinical research attention has been focused recently on the application of ischemic preconditioning. Correspondingly, the research scope of the related clinical trials has also been constantly expanding [20–22]. As a non-pharmacological intervention, the clinical translational application of LIPC in different diseases has also attracted much attention.

According to our data, LIPC can be used to effectively improve the severity of RLS in MHD patients. RLS refers to a central nervous system disease caused by an iron metabolism disorder and dopaminergic dysfunction This conclusion has been confirmed in a large number of clinical trials on the use of dopamine agonists and iron in the treatment of RLS [7,23,24]. However, peripheral circulation disturbance may also directly lead to the generation and development of RLS. In this regard, McDonagh et al. pointed out that venous dysfunction is associated with RLS [6]. Additionally, Rajaram et al. proposed that vasodilators could be used to alleviate the symptoms of RLS [25]. However, tissue hypoxia and ischemia can lead to peripheral neurons’ dysfunction [26]. In practice, the transient ischemia caused by LIPC can stimulate the release of the endothelial mediators and reduce the sympathetic nerve tension, thereby improving the venous circulation of the lower extremity [9,27].

In addition, adenosine is an important factor associated with RLS disease. The hypoadenosinergic state of the central nervous system has been proven to increase the probability of RLS occurrence [28]. Quiroz et al. proposed that brain iron deficiency (BID) can lead to the hypoadenosinergic state. During this process, the downregulation of adenosine A1 receptors (A1R) is most prominent during the occurrence of BID [29]. Furthermore, multiple studies have shown that LIPC can be used to increase the concentration of peripheral blood and central adenosine, thereby activating A1R in the central nervous system to exert the corresponding effects [11,30]. An increase in the adenosine levels in the blood of young participants was observed in a recent LIPC clinical trial [12]. Meanwhile, the adenosine in the peripheral blood can pass through the blood-brain barrier [31]. Therefore, it can be speculated that LIPC can exert neuroprotective effects against RLS by releasing adenosine and activating the adenosine receptors.

Overall, LIPC can be applied not only to improve the local microcirculation and increase the adenosine concentration, but also to activate the central mitochondria by modulating several signaling pathways, thus reducing excitotoxicity damage [32]. Besides, accumulating evidence has shown that there is a significant correlation between cerebral ischemia and the occurrence of RLS [33,34]. In practice, the potential of LIPC in neuroprotection may be utilized to improve the neurological function in RLS patients.

Numerous studies have shown that RLS can also be considered a sleep disorder [35]. Indeed, the sleep quality of MHD patients also showed varying degrees of decline [36]. Based on this evidence, the evaluation of sleep quality is also an important evaluation indicator for the beneficial effect of LIPC on RLS improvement. Although a significant improvement in the sleep disturbance scores was observed in the LIPC group during the experiment, there was no significant change in the post-intervention scores of the sleep adequacy included in the MOS scores in both the treatment and control groups. Meanwhile, the improvement effect of LIPC on sleep disturbance was in line with the expectations of researchers. This is because the sleep disorders in RLS patients are largely caused by the involuntary leg movements [37]. Furthermore, we observed that the score of ‘sleep adequacy’ included in MOS did not show significant changes before and after the intervention. Similar results were obtained in a previous experiment on progressive exercise applied to improve RLS symptoms in HD patients. In fact, the exercise training could not further improve the daily level of sleepiness in humans [38]. Therefore, the possible explanation for the above results is that the decline in the sleep quality in HD patients is common. The recovery of long-term sleep disorders is a chronic process. Hence, a test period lasting only 4 weeks cannot achieve a significant change in the degree of improvement in patients’ sleep adequacy.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Firstly, subjects’ self-reports were utilized to validate their adherence to the study protocol, without any objective measurements being reported. Secondly, our baseline measures did not include assessment of low serum magnesium levels, which can potentially induce muscle symptoms and consequently impact the severity of RLS. Thirdly, due to the absence of polysomnography data, objective observations regarding sleep duration, sleep quality, and associated movements during sleep could not be obtained. Moreover, no further follow-up was conducted post-trial cessation to ascertain the occurrence of RLS symptoms in the subjects. Lastly, it is worth noting that this experiment had a limited number of observed samples, hence some negative outcomes may have been influenced by the small sample size. Given these challenges encountered in our research endeavor, additional randomized controlled studies with multi-center participation and extended follow-up periods involving larger sample sizes are imperative for further validation of the findings presented in this paper.

Conclusions

To sum up, this study preliminarily verified that LIPC is an effective and safe method to improve RLS symptoms of MHD patients. We observed that LIPC for 4 weeks had a positive effect on improving the severity of RLS in MHD patients, as well as improving the sleep quality of the patients. The exact mechanism by which LIPC affects RLS in MHD patients still needs to be further studied. Long-term follow-up and observation of more objective indicators will help to explore the specific mechanism of LIPC affecting RLS, and also contribute to the sustainability of the observation results, and provide more reliable basis for the efficacy and safety of LIPC in the treatment of RLS.
