Effect of preparation method and edible oil type on the formation of PAHs in popcorns
Authors: Mustafa Kiralan, İsra Toptanci, Sezer Kiralan, Onur Ketenoglu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, the effects of different popcorn-making techniques on the occurrence of PAH4, PAH16, the sum of light PAHs, the sum of heavy PAHs, and also individual PAHs were determined in popcorns. For this purpose, popcorns were prepared using different oils including refined pomace oil (ROP), refined corn oil (RCO), refined sunflower oil (RSO), refined olive oil (ROO), and virgin olive oil (VOO), with and without salt by traditional heating in a pot and microwave heating for 1, 2, and 3 min. Results revealed that popcorn preparation techniques had a significant effect on PAH concentration. Although prolonged microwave heating caused an overall decrease in PAH16 levels due to volatilization and thermal degradation of low molecular weight PAHs, the formation of thermally stable high molecular weight PAHs, particularly PAH4 compounds, increased under extended heating conditions. Besides, high amounts of PAH4 and PAH16 were observed in the samples prepared with heating in a pot. While the heavy PAHs increased with an increment in the temperature level of microwave application, the light PAHs showed a decrease. Similarly, the heavy PAHs’ content increased with the increase of application time in the microwave, while a decrease was observed for the light PAHs. Popcorn samples prepared by ROP contained a higher amount of PAH4 (2.04 µg/kg) and PAH16 (64.19 µg/kg) compared to other oils used. Besides, the samples prepared with ROP contained higher content of AN and FL as light PAHs, and also FA and PY contents were observed at higher levels as heavy PAHs. Regarding the preparation method, AN and FL had higher values as light PAHs, which were found in the samples prepared with the traditional method and also microwave-heated for 3 min. Besides, FA and PY as heavy PAHs occurred at higher levels in the samples prepared with the traditional method.

Introduction

Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) comprise two or more fused aromatic rings composed of carbon and hydrogen atoms in their structural configurations1. PAHs are classified according to their aromatic rings. Light PAHs contain fewer than four aromatic rings, while heavy PAHs contain more than three rings. Heavy PAHs are more stable and more toxic than the light ones1. Many PAHs are considered as toxic, mutagenic and/or carcinogenic to humans2.

PAHs are found in various environmental matrices such as soil, water, air and foods3. Humans are exposed to PAHs through different routes. Especially, the main route of exposure to PAHs for non-smokers is foods in diet4. PAHs can reach food in different exposure routes from both natural sources and human activities. Especially, food processing methods such as barbecuing, grilling, roasting, smoking, heating, drying, baking, and frying contribute to PAHs occurrence in processed foods5–7.

The 16 PAHs have been designated as high priority pollutants by the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). Naphthalene (NA), acenaphthylene (ACL), acenaphthene (AC), fluorene (FL), phenanthrene (PHE) and anthracene (AN) have been determined as light PAHs, while the remaining 10 PAHs have been considered as heavy PAHs including fluoranthene (FA), benz[a]anthracene (BaA), pyrene (PY), chrysene (CHR), benzo[b]fluoranthene (BbFA), benzo[k]fluoranthene (BkFA), benzo[a]pyrene (BaP), dibenz[a, h]anthracene (DBahA), benzo[ghi]perylene (BghiP) and indeno[1,2,3-cd]pyrene (IP)8,9. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IACR) has classified 16 PAHs into four groups: group 1 (carcinogenic to humans), group 2A (probably carcinogenic to humans), group 2B (possibly carcinogenic to humans), and group 3 (not classifiable)10. Among PAHs, only BaP is classified in group 1 as a human carcinogen. DBahA is in group 2A, classified as probably carcinogenic to human. BaA, NA, CHR, BbFA, BkFA and IP are categorized in group 2B, as possible carcinogens. The rest of the PAHs namely, AC, FL, PHE, AN, FA, PY, BghiP are listed in group 3, which are not classifiable as to its carcinogenicity to humans, and ACL does not belong to any groups mentioned above11. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) Panel on Contaminants in the Food Chain recommended four PAHs, namely PAH4 (BaP, BaA, BbFA and CHR), to be considered as a suitable reference for the presence and potential carcinogenic potency of PAHs in foods12.

Popcorn (popped corn) is a widespread snack foods, made from corn kernel. Popcorn is generally prepared with oil and salt cooked by microwave and conventional heating until popping is complete13,14. According to the U.S. Popcorn Board15, American people consume nearly 14 billion quarts of popcorn per year. Most popcorn consumption occurs in the home with 70% of all popcorn consumed. In addition to being highly preferred by consumers, popcorn is important in terms of nutrition, such as high dietary fiber content16.

Many studies have reported about the contaminants of popped popcorn. Acrylamide is one of the most common contaminants studied in popcorns. The heating process of popcorn at temperatures above 120 °C plays a major role in acrylamide formation. Sebastià et al.17 reported high levels of acrylamide content in popcorn samples heated with microwave cooking. Beside to acrylamide, PAHs could be important contaminant sources in popcorns using microwave heating. Habschied et al.18 determined light PAHs such as NA, AC and FL at small amounts in popped popcorn samples heated with microwave heating. These samples also contained heavy PAHs including BaA, CHR and BaP. They prepared popped popcorn samples without oil in a closed special bag, and they also applied only one process parameter in microwave heating for 120 s at 700 W. Since there are limited number of studies regarding the contaminants in popcorn samples, we hereby investigated the impact of various popcorn-popping techniques (microwave heating and traditional wet processing) on PAH4 and PAH16 contents of popcorns prepared with different edible oils (refined olive pomace, refined corn oil, refined sunflower oil, refined olive oil and virgin olive oil) with and without salt addition.

Materials and methods

Sixteen individual polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) standards, including compounds such as acenaphthene, acenaphthylene, anthracene, benz(a)anthracene, benzo(a)pyrene, benzo(b)fluoranthene, benzo(g, h,i)perylene, benzo(k)fluoranthene, chrysene, dibenz(a, h)anthracene, fluoranthene, fluorene, indeno[1,2,3-cd]pyrene, naphthalene, phenanthrene, and pyrene, were obtained from Dr. Ehrenstorfer (Augsburg, Germany). All solvents used in the preparation and analysis steps—including n-hexane, acetonitrile, dichloromethane and acetone (Sigma-Aldrich, Steinheim, Germany)—were of GC grade. For the preparation of individual stock solutions, 10 mg of each PAH compound was accurately weighed and dissolved in 10 mL of acetonitrile, yielding a concentration of 1000 mg/kg. From these, 0.1 mL aliquots were transferred into 100 mL volumetric flasks and diluted with acetonitrile to obtain a mixed working solution containing each analyte at a concentration of 1 mg/kg. Calibration standards at concentrations of 0.25, 0.50, 1, 5, 10, 20, 50, and 100 µg/kg were prepared through serial dilutions of the mixed solution, and to reduce matrix-related interferences, the calibration process was performed using a matrix-matched blank. All standard and sample solutions were stored at 4 °C prior to analysis to prevent degradation and ensure analytical integrity.

Sample preparation

To examine the presence of PAHs, popcorn samples were prepared using various vegetable oils and cooking techniques. Specifically, 100 g of dry corn kernels with moisture content below 14% were combined with refined pomace oil (ROP), refined corn oil (RCO), refined sunflower oil (RSO), refined olive oil (ROO), and virgin olive oil (VOO). Each oil was tested both with (“s” letter in sample name) and without (no “s” letter in sample name) salt addition, and employing two different cooking methods: microwave heating (sample coding: “mo”) and traditional stove top cooking in a pot (sample coding: “to”). For the microwave procedure, kernels were placed in heat-resistant, microwave-compatible glass containers with loosely fitted vented lids to allow steam to escape and heated at 800 W for durations of 1, 2, and 3 min. In the conventional pot method, the corn was cooked over medium heat (approximately 170–180 °C) with constant agitation for the same time intervals to prevent scorching. After cooking, the samples were allowed to cool. To minimize cross-contamination of PAHs between samples, both the microwave oven and the pot were cleaned with acetone after each use. The popped and unpopped kernels were grounded using a laboratory-grade knife mill (Retsch Grindomix GM 300, Retsch GmbH, Haan, Germany) and 3-gram aliquots of each ground sample were weighed and analyzed.

Extraction of PAHs from oil and popcorn

PAHs were extracted from oil samples following the approach outlined by Kıralan et al.19. Corn samples were prepared using the QuEChERS multi-residue extraction method, as described and modified by Mastanjević et al.20. Initially, 3 g of homogenized sample were weighed into a 50 mL centrifuge tube, followed by the addition of 3 mL of acetonitrile and 3 mL of deionized water. The mixture was vigorously shaken using a vortex mixer for 1 min to ensure complete sample dissolution. Subsequently, 3 g of anhydrous magnesium sulfate and 1 g of anhydrous sodium acetate were added to induce phase separation through salt-out partitioning. The tube was immediately shaken and then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min to achieve complete phase separation. For the dispersive solid-phase extraction (d-SPE) cleanup step, 1 mL of the acetonitrile supernatant was transferred to a 5 mL micro centrifuge tube containing a mixture of 150 mg anhydrous magnesium sulfate, 100 mg primary secondary amine (PSA), and 50 mg C18 sorbent. The tube was vortexed and centrifuged again under the same conditions to obtain a clear extract. A 0.5 mL aliquot of the purified extract was evaporated to dryness under a gentle nitrogen stream and reconstituted in hexane for analysis using an Agilent 7890 A gas chromatograph coupled with a 7000B triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, USA).

Results and discussion

Method validation

The analytical method developed for the determination of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) was comprehensively validated in accordance with European Commission regulations (EC No. 333/2007). Validation parameters, including linearity, accuracy, recovery, precision (repeatability and reproducibility), specificity, as well as limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ), were thoroughly assessed. LOD and LOQ were defined as signal-to-noise ratios of 3 and 10, respectively, and experimentally determined through fortified samples. The calibration curves, constructed using matrix-matched standards (corn, oil), covered concentration ranges from 0.25 to 100 µg/kg depending on the matrix and analytical target. All compounds demonstrated satisfactory linearity, with correlation coefficients (R2) exceeding 0.995. The limits for LOD and LOQ, as well as the values for linearity, recovery, repeatability, and reproducibility of the tested compounds obtained using oil and corn matrices are presented in Tables 1 and 2, respectively. Recovery studies conducted at various concentration levels yielded acceptable recovery rates ranging from 80% to 100%, in accordance with EU criteria. The precision of the method was confirmed by relative standard deviation (RSD) values that remained below 15% across all concentration levels. These findings collectively indicate that the method is robust, reliable, and suitable for the accurate quantification of PAHs in complex food matrices. PAH levels in popcorn samples

The PAH content of raw materials such as edible oils and popcorn corn are shown in Table 4. In popcorn corn samples, four kinds of PAHs including NA, FL, AN and FA were detected. The popcorn corn samples had the highest amount of FL with 1.43 µg/kg, followed by NA (1.08 µg/kg) and FA (1.07 µg/kg). Also, AN was detected at an average amount of 0.86 µg/kg. Although PAH4 was not determined in the samples, PAH16 was detected at 4.43 µg/kg level. Besides, three of the detected PAHs were light PAHs and sum concentrations of light PAHs were 3.37 µg/kg, whereas FA was the only heavy PAH component detected with the level of 1.07 µg/kg. Habschied et al.18 reported that only three light PAHs such as NAP, AC and FL were detected in popcorn corn samples. The highest evaluated light PAH was FL with the concentration of 7.22 µg/kg, followed by NA (not detected-3.63 µg/kg). The concentration of AC ranged between not detected to 0.35 µg/kg. Apart from AC, the other two PAHs, NA and FL, were determined in the popcorn corn samples in our study and also showed similarities in concentration with the results of Habschied et al.18. Besides, total of PAH16 content of popcorn corn samples in this study showed similarity with the study by Habschied et al.18 who reported that total PAH16 levels increased up to 7.22 µg/kg. The similar results with the Habschied et al.18 were observed for light PAHs and there was no detection for them in the samples.

As for edible oils, the individual 16 PAHs were not detectable for ROO and VOO samples. However, ROP, RCO and RSO contained only light PAHs, sum of these PAHs were 5.79, 3.64 and 1.91 µg/kg, respectively. While PAH4 was not detectable in ROP, RCO and RSO samples, sum of PAH16 were determined at the amount of 5.79, 3.64 and 1.91 µg/kg, respectively. As regards to individual PAHs, three individual PAHs were present in ROP such as FL, AN, PHE. RCO sample included AN and PHE, while ACL and FL were detected in RSO sample. The highest concentration of PHE was detected in ROP sample (3.39 µg/kg). The highest levels of AN and ACL were 1.96 µg/kg and 1.00 µg/kg in RCO and RSO samples, respectively. The results obtained from this study were in accordance with the results of Yousefi et al.21 who reported the presence of AN, ACL and PHE in edible vegetable oils such as sunflower oil and corn oil consumed in Iran. Shi et al.22 reported a higher level of light PAHs in oils than heavy PAHs. The results of this study are in accordance with the results of Shi et al.22 Tfouni et al.23 did not detect any PAHs in many virgin olive oil samples, and the results of the virgin olive oil used in the study were similar to the results of Tfouni et al.23. The results of our study for ROO showed similarity with the results of Kıralan et al.19 who also reported the absence of PAH4 in refined olive oil.

The impact of the popcorn popping technique on the amount of individual PAHs, PAH4, PAH16, sum of light PAHs and sum of heavy PAHs in the popcorn samples were illustrated in Fig. 1. As seen in Fig. 1, microwave heating mainly promoted the occurrence of PAH4. The PAH4 content of microwave heated popcorn samples prepared with the ROP, RCO and RSO increased with increasing microwave heating time (3 min) (p < 0.05), while the presence of PAH4 could not be determined in popcorn samples prepared in ROO and VOO using microwave application. However, the content of PAH16 decreased in the popcorn oils prepared using all oil types with the increase of microwave heating time especially at 3 min (p < 0.05). Besides, conventional popcorn preparing method had the highest PAH4 and PAH16 contents in popcorn samples prepared in pots compared to those applied in microwave (p < 0.05). Furthermore, a slight increase was observed in the amounts of PAH4 and PAH16 in popcorn samples prepared by both microwave and conventional methods. The results revealed that sum of light PAHs in popcorn samples decreased with the increment of microwave heating time (p < 0.05). As the microwave time extended, sum of heavy PAHs in popcorn samples increased significantly (p < 0.05). In general, sum of heavy PAHs were higher in popcorn samples prepared with traditional method in comparison to microwave heating.

Figure 3 presents proportions of individual heavy PAHs in popcorn samples generated from traditional and microwave cooking methods. As shown in Fig. 3, the concentration of individual heavy PAHs in popcorn samples increased significantly for prolonged intense microwave heating (p < 0.05). Samples prepared with ROP, RCO, RSO and ROO contained the highest FA and PY, while PY was the only heavy PAH detected in the samples using VOO. The concentration of FA in the samples prepared with microwave heating for 3 min using ROP, RCO, RSO and ROO increased up to 13.88 µg/kg, 4.95 µg/kg, 4.22 µg/kg and 1.93 µg/kg, respectively. Besides, samples prepared with traditional method contained the highest FA content compared to microwave heating. In addition to the production technique and oils used, the use of salt also caused a partial increase in both light and heavy PAHs. Salt addition may have influenced heat transfer and moisture loss during the popping process, which could indirectly affect PAH formation under thermal processing conditions. However, the exact effect of NaCl on PAH formation has not been clearly established in the literature. The results of this study are in accordance with previous study by Habschied et al.18. In their study, NA, ACL, FL, AN and PHE were detected in popcorn samples prepared with microwave heating at 700 W for 2.5 min. Besides, FA, BaA, PY, CHR and BaP were also detected in popcorn samples as heavy PAHs. In this study, high values for PAH4 and PAH16 were generally detected in samples subjected to microwave exposure for a long time (3 min) and in pots, and our findings were close to or higher than those reported in the study of Habschied et al.18. Our findings were also consistent with the results reported by Zahoor et al.24, who stated increased AN, FL, PHE and PY contents in four tissues heart, liver, neck, and wings of Aseel chickens fried with microwave oven frying. Besides, these finding revealed that higher temperatures during prolonged microwave heating and also traditional heating conditions lead to the decrease of light PAHs in popcorn samples as a result of volatilization or degradation25. Besides, an increase of heavy PAHs in popcorn samples could be related with prolonged microwave heating and traditional popcorn making method. Purcaro et al.26 emphasized that higher content of heavy PAHs occurred with high temperature applications such as deep-frying.

The highest concentration for PAH4 was observed in the mos3 popcorn sample with a concentration ranging from 1.31 to 1.62 µg/kg when processed up to 3 min of heating. The concentration of PAH16 in popcorn samples decreased with microwave heating time. Besides, the microwave applied samples exhibited lower values for PAH16 (3.86–11.30 µg/kg) when compared to the samples prepared with traditional method (38.46–43.50 µg/kg).

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study shows that popcorn preparing methods such as traditional method and microwave heating method play role on the occurrence and distribution of PAHs. Traditional popcorn making method increased the level of PAH16. Besides, traditional preparing method resulted in significantly higher light and heavy PAHs concentrations, particularly for AN and FL; FA and PY, respectively. Moreover, heavy PAHs were detected in high amounts in popcorn samples prepared with the traditional method and in samples subjected to microwave for 3 min, increased up to 25.94 µg/kg and 11.30 µg/kg, respectively. The results for vegetable oil type have shown that the samples prepared with ROP had an important effect on the formation of PAH4, PAH6, sum of light and sum of heavy PAHs, and ROP exhibited higher values among the samples prepared using other oils. Besides, AN and FL as light PAHs and also FA and PY as heavy PAHs are characterized with the samples prepared in ROP according to multivariate analysis. Moreover, the salt addition to edible oil could significantly promote the formation of PAHs compared with the samples prepared without salt addition.
