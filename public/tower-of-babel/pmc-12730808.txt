Evaluation of the performance and safety of adding the iron chelator MEX-CD1 to dialysate during continuous veno-venous haemodialysis for removing excess labile iron in intensive care patients with sepsis-associated acute kidney injury - the Iron in Intensive Care trial (IRON-I.C.): protocol for a phase I–II randomised crossover pilot study
Authors: Aymeric Couturier, Chris Serrand, Christophe Masseguin, Bernard Allaouchiche, Olivier Tillement, Jean-Yves Lefrant, Saber Davide Barbar

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSepsis-associated acute kidney injury is common in intensive care and is linked to high morbidity and mortality, yet no specific therapy exists beyond supportive care. Excess circulating labile iron contributes to oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction and cell death via ferroptosis. We hypothesise that targeted removal of labile iron during dialysis may reduce this pathogenic process. This study will evaluate the performance and safety of adding a novel iron chelator named MEX-CD1 (Metal EXtraction - Chitosan DOTAGA 1) to dialysate during continuous veno-venous haemodialysis (CVVHD) in critically ill patients with sepsis-associated acute kidney injury.Methods and analysisThis is a single-centre, randomised, open-label, crossover phase I–II pilot study in the intensive care unit of Nîmes University Hospital, France. 14 adult patients with sepsis-associated acute kidney injury requiring renal replacement therapy will receive two consecutive 24-hour CVVHD sessions: one with standard dialysate and one with dialysate supplemented with MEX-CD1 at 50 mg/L. Each patient serves as their own control. The primary outcome is the iron concentration in the effluent to measure iron removal performance. Introduction

Background and rationale

Sepsis is a high-risk factor for mortality in critically ill patients and a major global health challenge, affecting more than 30 million adults annually. Despite adherence to standardised treatment guidelines, mortality rates remain unacceptably high, approximately 32%.1 Sepsis accounts for 45–70% of all cases of acute kidney injury (AKI) among critically ill patients.1 A significant proportion of these patients—approximately 18%2 —develop sepsis-associated AKI (SA-AKI), which is strongly associated with adverse outcomes, including an increased risk of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and death.3 Up to 51% of these patients will require renal replacement therapy (RRT), which is linked to longer intensive care unit (ICU)/hospital stays, higher CKD risk and increased mortality.2 Current management of SA-AKI is largely supportive, with no specific therapeutic interventions available to directly prevent or mitigate kidney injury.

Iron is essential for many fundamental cellular processes, including mitochondrial function, DNA synthesis, cell cycle regulation, hypoxia signalling and immune modulation.4 5

Under physiological conditions, circulating iron represents only about 1% of total body stores and is tightly regulated, being primarily bound to transferrin. A minimal fraction of iron exists outside of transferrin binding, referred to as ‘non-transferrin-bound iron’ (NTBI). NTBI includes a heterogeneous mix of weakly chelated or loosely bound iron species, typically complexed with small molecules such as citrate or albumin. This pool partially overlaps with what is commonly described as labile iron—the catalytically active and redox-reactive fraction of iron that is not tightly sequestered.6 While sometimes informally referred to as free iron, this term is chemically ambiguous and may misleadingly suggest the presence of unbound ionic iron, which is virtually absent under physiological conditions due to its high reactivity and toxicity. Labile iron may also include iron loosely bound in forms such as free heme or low-molecular-weight intracellular complexes. Under normal conditions, NTBI concentrations remain below 1 µmol/L and are often undetectable by conventional methods,7 due to transferrin’s high binding capacity and buffering effect. For clarity and consistency, we will use the term labile iron throughout this manuscript to refer to this redox-active, weakly bound iron pool relevant to oxidative stress and iron toxicity.

During systemic inflammatory states such as sepsis, circulating iron levels increase. The body activates protective iron-sequestering mechanisms, such as ferritin release, to limit labile iron availability. However, in severe sepsis and septic shock, these defences can become overwhelmed, leading to elevated levels of labile iron. This labile iron can catalyse the Fenton and Haber-Weiss reactions, generating hydroxyl radicals and reactive oxygen species (ROS).8 9 Excess ROS induces oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid peroxidation and cell death via ferroptosis. This oxidative damage can exacerbate systemic inflammation, creating a vicious cycle that contributes to multiorgan dysfunction, including AKI.8,11

Labile iron has consistently been implicated in the pathophysiology of AKI across numerous animal models, with diverse injury mechanisms, including ischaemia–reperfusion injury,12,14 gentamicin15 and cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity.16 17 In all of these settings, treatment with iron chelators has been shown to reduce the severity of renal damage, providing strong evidence that iron is involved as a common final mediator of injury in various forms of nephrotoxicity.18

Iron chelation therapy has also shown potential to mitigate oxidative stress and organ injury in sepsis models. Deferoxamine (DFO) improved survival in caecal ligation and puncture-induced sepsis in rats, significantly increasing 36-hour survival rates.19

In humans, a few studies have explored the translational relevance of these animal findings in clinical contexts, yielding promising preliminary results.18 In a retrospective study of 483 ICU patients with AKI (including 123 receiving RRT), elevated serum iron levels (>60 µg/dL) were significantly associated with increased mortality at both 28 days and 90 days.20 A randomised controlled trial in the ICU included 80 patients receiving placebo or DFO plus N-acetyl-cysteine for new-onset hypotension or need for vasopressors. DFO did not significantly reduce AKI incidence, but did reduce AKI severity and serum creatinine levels at discharge.21

Beyond these protective effects, chelators have complex antimicrobial interactions. Iron is an essential nutrient for almost all microorganisms. During sepsis, several proteins limit the availability of iron to pathogenic bacteria, slowing their proliferation; specifically, ferritin and lactoferrin bind circulating free iron. This has been described as a ‘nutritional immunity’ effect.5 22 While chelators can mimic and reproduce this mechanism, limiting iron availability and suppressing growth of certain bacteria,23 they may paradoxically promote growth of pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus or Yersinia spp that can exploit chelator-bound iron via siderophore receptors.24 Additionally, systemic iron chelation carries risks including agranulocytosis and nephrotoxicity.25

These findings highlight the promise and challenges of iron chelation strategies for controlling sepsis-associated inflammation, oxidative damage and AKI.26

Aim of the study

We hypothesise that targeted removal of circulating labile iron during RRT can reduce oxidative stress and improve clinical outcomes in patients with sepsis-associated AKI. Systemic administration of chelators has limitations, including potential toxicity and unintended effects on pathogen growth.

Our approach is innovative because it uses a novel iron chelator, MEX-CD1 (Metal EXtraction - Chitosan DOTAGA 1), which is not administered systemically but instead added to the dialysate. MEX-CD1 is a chelating polymer composed of a reacetylated chitosan functionalised by DOTAGA ligands.27 Due to its high molecular weight (~300 kDa), MEX-CD1 remains confined to the dialysis circuit and does not cross the semipermeable membrane. This property increases the iron extraction gradient through its non-saturable binding capacity. Previous preclinical studies27 have demonstrated its efficacy and good safety profile in animal models.

The aim of this study is to evaluate the performance and safety of adding the iron chelator MEX-CD1 to the dialysate during continuous veno-venous haemodialysis (CVVHD) sessions for the removal of excess labile iron in critically ill patients with sepsis-associated AKI requiring dialysis. We will conduct a single-centre, randomised, crossover phase I–II pilot study comparing CVVHD with standard dialysate and CVVHD with dialysate supplemented with the iron chelator MEX-CD1, to assess its effectiveness in iron elimination and its safety profile in an ICU setting.

Primary objective

To compare the performance of iron removal between CVVHD with standard dialysate and CVVHD with MEX-CD1-supplemented dialysate in critically ill patients with SA-AKI requiring RRT.

Secondary objectives

Efficacy

To compare between the two groups: (1) the plasma iron clearance, (2) the ratio of iron clearance to creatinine clearance, (3) the effect of iron chelation on biomarkers of oxidative stress and (4) the effect on inflammatory markers.

Safety

To evaluate the safety of iron chelation via MEX-CD1 addition to dialysate with monitoring (1) safety parameters during RRT, (2) adverse events (AEs) up to 28 days post-enrolment, (3) changes in iron metabolism markers up to 28 days and (4) elimination of trace elements during dialysis.

Methods and analysis

Study design and setting

This is a single-centre, randomised, open-label, crossover phase I–II pilot study designed to evaluate the performance and safety of adding the iron chelator MEX-CD1 to dialysate during CVVHD (figure 1). The study protocol was prospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier: NCT07236463).

Additional treatments

Trace element supplementation (iron, copper, zinc and selenium) will be standardised for all participants to mitigate the risk of deficiency. At the end of the 48-hour interventional phase, patients will be supplemented with a parenteral formulation containing 8 mg of ferrous gluconate, 70 mg of zinc gluconate, 150 µg of sodium selenite and 2 mg of copper gluconate, administered two times per day for 2 days. All patient care, apart from the experimental iron chelation, will follow standard intensive care practices in accordance with international recommendations. From the 48th hour onward, any remaining dialysis required for patient care will be performed with standard dialysate.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

The primary outcome is defined as the comparison of iron concentrations in the effluent collected during 24-hour dialysis sessions performed under two conditions: using standard dialysate and using dialysate supplemented with the iron chelator MEX-CD1. Iron concentration in the effluent will be measured using a standard inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry technique (ICP-MS).

Rationale for outcome selection and analytical considerations

The quantification of serum labile iron remains methodologically challenging, as no universally accepted gold standard currently exists. To address this, we selected the measurement of total iron in the dialysis effluent as our primary outcome. This approach is based on the rationale that, given the 30 kDa cut-off of the dialysis membrane, transferrin and other iron-binding proteins cannot cross into the effluent. Consequently, the iron recovered in the effluent represents the non–protein-bound fraction—that is, labile (catalytically active) iron. Measuring total iron in this protein-free compartment enables a reliable and specific estimation of labile iron removal using ICP-MS.

We acknowledge that potential adherence of the MEX-CD1 polymer to the dialysis membrane may result in an underestimation of iron removal. To correct for this, we will calculate the ratio between the amount of MEX-CD1 recovered in the effluent and the amount initially introduced into the dialysate. This correction factor will allow us to estimate the extent of polymer loss and adjust the measured iron clearance accordingly.

Sample size

The sample size calculation is based on an expected 30% reduction in plasma labile iron concentration with MEX-CD1-supplemented dialysate, from 7 to 5 µmol/L (SD±2 µmol/L). This assumption is supported by preclinical data demonstrating the iron extraction capacity of the MEX-CD1 polymer. In vitro and in vivo27 animal experiments have shown enhanced iron removal with MEX-CD1-supplemented dialysate, with improvements ranging from +57% to +130% when using MEX-CD1 dialysate concentrations approximately twofold higher than those planned in the current protocol. Therefore, a 30% reduction was considered a conservative yet clinically meaningful effect size.

Based on these assumptions, a sample of 12 patients provides 80% power to detect the effect at a 5% significance level. To account for a potential 15% dropout rate, the total planned sample size is 14 patients.

Data management

Data will be analysed on an intention-to-treat basis. All AEs will be recorded, coded (MedDRA), and reported per regulatory requirements. Data will be securely stored, anonymised and monitored for completeness and consistency. Analysis datasets will be frozen only after quality control and cleaning.

Statistical analysis plan

The statistical analysis will be conducted by the Laboratory of Biostatistics, Clinical Epidemiology, Public Health Innovation and Methodology of Nîmes University Hospital using R V.3.5.1 (R Development Core Team) or later versions.

The normality of the distribution of quantitative variables will be explored graphically and by examining the coefficients of kurtosis and skewness.

Quantitative variables will be expressed as mean±SD or as median and IQR. Qualitative variables will be expressed as number and percentage.

Qualitative and quantitative data will be compared descriptively between the two randomisation groups to assess for clinically relevant differences and to verify the quality of randomisation, without conducting formal statistical tests.

The concentrations in the effluent in the ‘standard’ versus ‘with chelator’ sequences will be compared using tests for paired quantitative data (paired Student’s t-test, Wilcoxon signed-rank test or sign test).

A comparison of periods will also be performed, comparing clearance during the first 24-hour sequence versus the second 24-hour sequence using tests for paired quantitative data (paired Student’s t-test, Wilcoxon signed-rank test or sign test).

To verify the absence of a carryover effect related to treatment sequence order, the total clearance over 48 hours (sum of clearances from both sequences for each patient) will be compared between randomisation groups using an appropriate test based on distributional assumptions.

The number of dialysis-related side effects will be compared between sequences using tests for paired quantitative data (paired Student’s t-test, Wilcoxon signed-rank test or sign test). The occurrence of different types of side effects will be compared between sequences using McNemar’s test.

A sensitivity analysis is planned in which missing information on the primary endpoint will be imputed using the last observation carried forward method, thereby yielding a null effect of the filtration in case of missing data.

The number and type of AEs related to treatment with the MEX-CD1 chelator up to 28 days postdialysis session will be described in the study population.

A descriptive analysis will be performed for the complete iron balance at D0, D1, D2, D7 and D28 after the last session of standard dialysis or dialysis with iron chelation by addition of MEX-CD1 in the dialysate.

Analysis and comparison of copper, zinc and selenium concentrations will use the same methods as for the primary endpoint.

Furthermore, analysis of oxidative stress markers will use mixed linear regression models to evaluate plasma MDA, TBARS, protein thiols and GPX levels according to treatment sequence and sampling time.

Analysis of inflammatory markers will also use mixed linear regression models to evaluate PCT and CRP levels according to treatment sequence and sampling time.

Potential confounding variables will be accounted for by study design and the matched measures.

A difference will be considered statistically significant at a p value≤0.05.

No method for imputing missing data is planned for this study.

Safety monitoring and auditing

AEs and device defects will be documented from the time of consent through day 28, as assessed according to CTCAE V.6.0 (MedDRA V.28.0). Investigators must report serious AEs within 3 days of awareness to the sponsor.

A Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB), independent from the sponsor and free of competing interests, composed of an intensivist with expertise in RRT, a nephrologist and a clinical pharmacist with expertise in pharmacovigilance, will review safety data after the inclusion of four and eight participants and make recommendations on study continuation. Given the study’s primary objective—biological efficacy through estimation of iron removal—an interim efficacy analysis is of limited relevance and is therefore not planned, as the study is not designed to detect differences in clinical outcomes such as mortality or recovery. However, close monitoring of AEs remains essential, justifying the role of the DSMB. Safety analyses will adhere to Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials Harms recommendations.

A clinical research monitor will conduct risk-based monitoring visits during start-up, recruitment and close-out phases. Inspections by regulatory authorities may occur at any time. Investigators will ensure access to data for quality control and compliance with Good Clinical Practice.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Due to the complexity of the intensive care setting and the acute nature of SA-AKI, patient and public involvement in protocol development was not feasible. However, patient representatives will be informed of the study results on request, and the findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and conference presentations to ensure transparency and knowledge sharing with the broader community.

Perspectives

This pilot study is designed as a biological, mechanistic proof-of-concept trial and is not powered to assess clinical outcomes such as AKI recovery or mortality. Its primary objective is to generate quantitative data on iron removal, along with preliminary safety and feasibility signals. Given its single-centre design and small sample size, we acknowledge the limited external validity of the future findings. Accordingly, we explicitly frame this study as hypothesis generating. The results will serve to inform the rationale, dosing strategies and selection of clinically relevant endpoints in a subsequent phase IIb/III multicentre trial.
