A statistic-swapping involution on the Cartesian product of the symmetric group S k ​ n S_{kn} and the generalized symmetric group S ⁡ ( k , n ) S(k,n)
Peter Kagey, Kai Mawhinney

Abstract
We construct a statistic-swapping involution on the Cartesian product of the generalized symmetric group S ⁡ ( k , n ) S(k,n) with the symmetric group S k ​ n S_{kn} , which swaps the number of fixed points in the generalized symmetric group element with the number of k k -cycles in the symmetric group element. This gives a combinatorial proof for a probabilistic observation: the distribution of fixed points on S ⁡ ( k , n ) S(k,n) matches the distribution of k k -cycles on S k ​ n S_{kn} .

1.1
Decomposing the involution

Instead of constructing the involution directly, we first describe and construct an auxiliary function.

Definition 1.3
.

Throughout the paper, we will use the conventions that

\displaystyle\pi

\displaystyle\in S_{kn},

\displaystyle f(\pi)

\displaystyle=(\delta,\sigma)\in D_{k,n}\times S(k,n),\text{ and}

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{x},\tau)\in S(k,n)=\mathbb{Z}_{k}^{n}\rtimes S_{n},

where

D_{k,n}
denotes the set of permutations in
S_{kn}
that consist of
n
disjoint
k
-cycles, that is their cycle type is
(k,\dots,k)=(k^{n})
:

D_{k,n}=\left\{\pi\in S_{kn}\mid\operatorname{type}(\pi)=(k^{n})\right\}.

Theorem 1.4
.

Suppose that
f\colon S_{kn}\to D_{k,n}\times S(k,n)
is a bijection such that
f(\pi)=\left(f_{1}(\pi),f_{2}(\pi)\right)=(\delta,\sigma)
and the number of
k
-cycles in
\pi
is equal to the number of fixed points in
\sigma
.

Then the map
\phi\colon S(k,n)\times S_{kn}\to S(k,n)\times S_{kn}
defined as

\phi(\sigma^{\prime},\pi)=\left(f_{2}(\pi),f^{-1}(f_{1}(\pi),\sigma^{\prime})\right)=\left(\sigma,f^{-1}(\delta,\sigma^{\prime})\right)

is an involution, such that the number of
k
-cycles in
\pi
is equal to the number of fixed points in
\sigma
and the number of
k
-cycles in
\pi^{\prime}
is equal to the number of fixed points in
\sigma^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
(\sigma^{\prime},\pi)\in S(k,n)\times S_{kn}
. Denote
f(\pi)=(\delta,\sigma)
and denote
f^{-1}(\delta,\sigma^{\prime})=\pi^{\prime}
.
To check that this is an involution, we apply the map twice:

\displaystyle\phi(\phi(\sigma^{\prime},\pi))

\displaystyle=\phi\!\left(\sigma,f^{-1}(\delta,\sigma^{\prime})\right)=\phi(\sigma,\pi^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\left(f_{2}(\pi^{\prime}),f^{-1}(f_{1}(\pi^{\prime}),\sigma)\right)=\left(\sigma^{\prime},f^{-1}(\delta,\sigma)\right)=\left(\sigma^{\prime},\pi\right)

Moreover, by the assumption on
f
, if
\pi
has
\alpha

k
-cycles, then
\sigma^{\prime}
has
\alpha
fixed points, and if
\pi^{\prime}
has
\beta

k
-cycles, then
\sigma
has
\beta
fixed points. Thus
\phi(\sigma,\pi)=(\sigma^{\prime},\pi^{\prime})
switches the number of
k
-cycles and fixed points.
∎

, which has the above properties. Our construction repeatedly uses Stanley’s fundamental bijection and its inverse, which we define here.

Definition 1.5

(Stanley’s fundamental bijection

)

\pi=(c^{(1)}_{1}\cdots c^{(1)}_{\ell_{1}})(c^{(2)}_{1}\cdots c^{(2)}_{\ell_{2}})\cdots(c^{(t)}_{1}\cdots c^{(t)}_{\ell_{t}})

is written in canonical cycle notation (i.e., the first letter in each cycle,
c^{(i)}_{1}
, is the largest letter in its cycle, and the cycles are ordered in increasing order by first letter when read from left to right), and

\hat{\pi}=\hat{\pi}_{1}\hat{\pi}_{2}\cdots\hat{\pi}_{n}=c^{(1)}_{1}\cdots c^{(1)}_{\ell_{1}}c^{(2)}_{1}\cdots c^{(2)}_{\ell_{2}}\cdots c^{(t)}_{1}\cdots c^{(t)}_{\ell_{t}}

is written as a word by dropping the parentheses in
\hat{\pi}
.

The map is inverted by placing a parenthesis at the start of the word, before each record read left to right, and at the end of the word.

1.2
The auxilliary bijection

We will now define the auxiliary bijection
f\colon S_{kn}\to D_{k,n}\times S(k,n)
describing each step with the aid of a running example. We start by defining the first coordinate of the output.

Definition 1.6
.

The map
f_{1}\colon S_{kn}\to D_{k,n}
is given by

f_{1}(\pi)=\delta=(\hat{\pi}_{1}\hat{\pi}_{2}\cdots\hat{\pi}_{k})(\hat{\pi}_{k+1}\hat{\pi}_{k+2}\cdots\hat{\pi}_{2k})\cdots(\hat{\pi}_{kn-k+1}\hat{\pi}_{kn-k+2}\cdots\hat{\pi}_{kn}),

which is the result of applying Stanley’s fundamental bijection to
\pi
, and then re-parenthesizing into
n
disjoint
k
-cycles.

Note that the resulting permutation is not necessarily written in canonical cycle notation.

2
k
-cycles map to fixed points

In this section, we prove that if
\pi
has
m

k
-cycles and
f(\pi)=\left(\delta,\sigma^{\prime}\right)
, then
\sigma^{\prime}=(\mathbf{x},\tau)\in S(k,n)
has
m
fixed points.

Lemma 2.1
.

For
\pi\in S_{n}
we have that
\hat{\pi}_{i}
appears at the beginning of a
k
-cycle in
\pi
if and only if

\hat{\pi}_{j}\leq\hat{\pi}_{i}
for all
j<i+k
and

\hat{\pi}_{i+k}>\hat{\pi}_{i}
.

Proof.

First, assume
\hat{\pi}_{i}
appears at the beginning of a
k
-cycle in
\pi
. Then it must be the case that
\hat{\pi}_{i+1},\ldots,\hat{\pi}_{i+k-1}<\hat{\pi}_{i}
(otherwise the cycle containing
\hat{\pi}_{i}
would have length
<k
) and that
\hat{\pi}_{i+k}>\hat{\pi}_{i}
(otherwise the cycle containing
\hat{\pi}_{i}
would have length
>k
). Moreover, since
\hat{\pi}_{i}
appears at the beginning of a cycle, it must be the case that
\hat{\pi}_{j}<\hat{\pi}_{i}
for all
j<i
.

Conversely, if
\hat{\pi}_{i+k}>\hat{\pi}_{i}
and
\hat{\pi}_{j}\leq\hat{\pi}_{i}
for all
j<i+k
, then it follows immediately from Stanley’s fundamental bijection that
\hat{\pi}_{i}
is at the beginning of a
k
-cycle in
\pi
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

If
\left(\hat{\pi}_{i}\hat{\pi}_{i+1}\cdots\hat{\pi}_{i+k-1}\right)
is a
k
-cycle of
\pi
, then
\hat{\pi}_{i}=\overline{\tau}_{m}
for some
m\leq n
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

2.1
, we have
\hat{\pi}_{j}<\hat{\pi}_{i}
for
1\leq j<i+k
and
j\neq i
. In particular,
\hat{\pi}_{i}
is the largest element in its block, so
\hat{\pi}_{i}=\overline{\tau}_{m}
for some
m\leq n
.
∎

Theorem 2.3
.

For
\pi\in S_{kn}
and
1\leq i\leq n
, we have that
\overline{\tau}_{i}
is at the beginning of a
k
-cycle in
\pi
if and only if
\hat{\tau}_{i}
is a fixed point of
f_{2}(\pi)=(\mathbf{x},\tau)
.

Proof.

When applying the inverse of Stanley’s fundamental bijection to
\hat{\tau}\in S_{n}
,
\hat{\tau}_{i}
forms a
1
-cycle (and thus
\tau(\hat{\tau}_{i})=\hat{\tau}_{i}
) if and only if
\hat{\tau}_{i}
is a record when read left to right and either
i=n
or
\hat{\tau}_{i}<\hat{\tau}_{i+1}
.

First, assume that
\overline{\tau}_{i}
is at the beginning of a
k
-cycle in
\pi
, and so
x_{\hat{\tau}_{i}}\equiv k\equiv 0\pmod{k}
. Since
\overline{\tau}_{i}
starts the cycle,
\hat{\tau}_{j}<\hat{\tau}_{i}
for all
j<i
. Also, if
i\neq n
then
\hat{\tau}_{i}<\hat{\tau}_{i+1}
, otherwise then the cycle starting with
\overline{\tau}_{i}
would contain all of the letters in block
i+1
, and thus would have at least
k+1
elements.

Conversely, assume
\hat{\tau}_{i}
is a fixed point of
f_{2}(\pi)=\sigma=(\mathbf{x},\tau)
. Since
x\equiv 0\pmod{k}
and
\hat{\tau}_{j}<\hat{\tau}_{i}
for all
j<i
,
\overline{\tau}_{i}
starts a cycle whose length is a multiple of
k
. Since
\hat{\tau}_{i}<\hat{\tau}_{i+1}
, the cycle starting with
\overline{\tau}_{i}
does not contain all of block
i+1
, and thus its length is
<2k
, and so
\overline{\tau}_{i}
starts a cycle of length
k
.
∎

Proposition 2.4
.

For any
\pi\in S_{kn}
, the number of
k
-cycles of
\pi
equals the number of fixed points of
f_{2}(\pi)
.

Proof.

By
Theorem

2.3
, we have a bijection between the set of
k
-cycles in
\pi
which begin with some
\overline{\tau}_{i}
, and the set of all fixed points of
f_{2}(\pi)
. By
Lemma

2.2
, all of the
k
-cycles of
\pi
begin with some
\overline{\tau}_{i}
, so the result follows.
∎

3
Inverting the bijection

In this section, we construct a (right) inverse to
f
. Together with the fact that the domain and the codomain have the same cardinality, this shows that
f
is a bijection.

Because the composition of maps
\hat{\pi}\mapsto\delta\mapsto\hat{\delta}
involves creating
k
-cycles from
\hat{\pi}
and putting this into canonical cycle structure, it has the result of permuting the blocks of
\hat{\pi}
and cyclically shifting within the blocks. In order to invert this map, we define a cyclic shift on a block.

Definition 3.1
.

For some
0\leq s<k
, and
\operatorname{block}(\hat{\pi},i)=\hat{\pi}_{k(i-1)+1}\hat{\pi}_{k(i-1)+2}\dots\hat{\pi}_{ki}
, we define define
\operatorname{shift}_{s}
as the map that cyclically shifts the subword
s
spaces to the left:

\operatorname{shift}_{s}(\operatorname{block}(\hat{\pi},i))=\underbrace{\hat{\pi}_{k(i-1)+s+1}\hat{\pi}_{k(i-1)+s+2}\dots\hat{\pi}_{ki}}_{\text{length $k-s$ suffix}}\underbrace{\hat{\pi}_{k(i-1)+1}\hat{\pi}_{k(i-1)+2}\dots\hat{\pi}_{k(i-1)+s}}_{\text{length $s$ prefix}}.

Example 7
.

For
\operatorname{block}(\hat{\pi},1)=834
,

\operatorname{shift}_{0}(834)=834,\qquad\operatorname{shift}_{1}(834)=348,\quad\text{and }\quad\operatorname{shift}_{2}(834)=483.

The following theorem allows us to recover the order of the blocks in
\hat{\pi}
from
\hat{\delta}
and
\hat{\tau}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\pi\in S_{kn}
and
f(\pi)=\left(\delta,(\mathbf{x},\tau)\right)
.
Then for each
1\leq i\leq n
there exists a unique
s_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{k}
such that

\operatorname{block}(\hat{\pi},i)=\operatorname{shift}_{s_{i}}\!\!\left(\operatorname{block}(\hat{\delta},\hat{\tau}_{i})\right)\!.

Proof.

Put an ordering on two blocks
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
where
b_{1}>b_{2}
if
\max b_{1}>\max b_{2}
.
By definition of
\hat{\tau}
,

\operatorname{block}(\hat{\pi},i)
is the
\hat{\tau}_{i}
-th smallest block of
\hat{\pi}
. Because
\hat{\delta}
sorts the blocks of
\hat{\pi}
in increasing order, the
i
-th block of
\hat{\pi}
is the
\hat{\tau}_{i}
-th block of
\hat{\delta}
.
∎

The values of
s_{i}
can be recovered from
\mathbf{x}
, which is constructed to make the fixed points of
(\mathbf{x},\tau)
correspond to the
k
-cycles of
\pi
.

3.1

We use the output of Example
3
to invert the map and recover
\pi
from
\left(\delta,(\mathbf{x},\tau)\right)
.

Example 8

Let

We permute the order of the blocks using

Determining

Since

\operatorname{block}(\hat{\pi},3)=\operatorname{shift}_{1}(\mathrm{F1A})=\mathrm{1AF}

Determining

Since
\overline{\tau}_{2}=B

Since

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}

3.2
Shifting blocks

Recall that we may write a permutation
\pi\in S_{kn}
as a word of length
kn
and consider this word in
n
blocks, each of length
k
. We have previously defined
\overline{\tau}_{i}
to be the largest letter in the
i
th block, which will sometimes be referred to as the
i
th block leader.

We introduce the following definition, where both
g_{i}
and
d_{i}
are concerned with the
i
th block leader,
\overline{\tau}_{i}
.
g_{i}
represents the index after
\overline{\tau}_{i}
where the next cycle begins, and
d_{i}
gives the distance from
\overline{\tau}_{i}
to this index.

Definition 3.3
.

Write
\pi\in S_{kn}
in canonical cycle notation. We call a letter of
\hat{\pi}
a record if it begins a cycle of
\pi
, where
\pi
is written in canonical cycle notation. For each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, we define
g_{i}(\hat{\pi})
to be the index of the first record to the right of
\overline{\tau}_{i}
. If no such record exists,
g_{i}(\hat{\pi})=kn+1
.

Lastly, we define
d_{i}(\hat{\pi})
to be the difference between
g_{i}(\hat{\pi})
and the index of
\overline{\tau}_{i}
.

Example 9
.

If
\hat{\pi}=834\,59B\,1AF\,726\,CED
, then the records are
8
,
9
,
B
,
F
. The block leaders
\overline{\tau}_{1},\dots,\overline{\tau}_{5}
are
8
,
B
,
F
,
7
,
E
respectively, and they have indices
1,6,9,10,14
. Then:

\displaystyle g_{1}(\pi)

\displaystyle=5,d_{1}(\pi)=4

\displaystyle g_{2}(\pi)

\displaystyle=9,d_{2}(\pi)=3

\displaystyle g_{3}(\pi)

\displaystyle=16,d_{3}(\pi)=7

\displaystyle g_{4}(\pi)

\displaystyle=16,d_{4}(\pi)=6

\displaystyle g_{5}(\pi)

\displaystyle=16,d_{5}(\pi)=2

To be clear,
g_{1}(\pi)=5
because the next record in
\hat{\pi}
after
\overline{\tau}_{1}=8
is
9
, which has index
5
.
d_{1}(\pi)=4
because the index of
9
is
5
while the index of
8
is
1
, so their difference is
d_{1}(\pi)=5-1=4
.

We prove the following proposition, which asserts that the record following a block leader
\overline{\tau}_{i}
cannot be in the same block as
\overline{\tau}_{i}
.

Proposition 3.4
.

g_{i}(\hat{\pi})>ki
. In particular,
g_{i}(\hat{\pi})
is invariant up to cyclic shifts of
\operatorname{block}(\hat{\pi},i)
.

Proof.

If there is no record to the right of
\overline{\tau}_{i}
, then
g_{i}(\hat{\pi})=kn+1>ki
. Suppose there is a record to the right of
\overline{\tau}_{i}
.
\overline{\tau}_{i}
is the largest letter in
\operatorname{block}(\hat{\pi},i)
. As a result, other elements of
\operatorname{block}(\hat{\pi},i)
can never be the next record under any cyclic shift. It follows that the next record does not lie in
\operatorname{block}(\hat{\pi},i)
, so
g_{i}(\hat{\pi})>ki
. This record will be the same after any cyclic shift of
\operatorname{block}(\hat{\pi},i)
, and the proposition is proven.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let

\hat{w}=\operatorname{block}(\hat{\delta},\hat{\tau}_{1})\cdots\operatorname{block}(\hat{\delta},\hat{\tau}_{i})\operatorname{shift}_{s_{i+1}}\!\!\left(\operatorname{block}(\hat{\delta},\hat{\tau}_{i+1})\right)\cdots\operatorname{shift}_{s_{n}}\!\!\left(\operatorname{block}(\hat{\delta},\hat{\tau}_{n})\right)

and let
s_{i}\equiv x_{\hat{\tau}_{i}}-d_{i}(\hat{\pi})\pmod{k}
. Then
s_{i}
is the unique shift so that

\operatorname{block}(\hat{\pi},i)=\operatorname{shift}_{s_{i}}(\operatorname{block}(\hat{\delta},\hat{\tau}_{i})).

Proof.

We prove inductively, starting with the base case
i=n
.

Base case.

Assume
i=n
and let
\hat{w}=\operatorname{block}(\hat{\delta},\hat{\tau}_{1})\cdots\operatorname{block}(\hat{\delta},\hat{\tau}_{n})
. The index of
\overline{\tau}_{n}
is
k(n-1)+1
because each block of
\hat{\delta}
begins with its block leader. Thus,
d_{n}(\hat{w})\equiv kn+1-(k(n-1)+1)\equiv k\equiv 0\pmod{k}
,
s_{n}\equiv x_{\hat{\tau}_{n}}\pmod{k}
, and thus
\operatorname{shift}_{s_{n}}\!\!\left(\operatorname{block}(\hat{\delta},\hat{\tau}_{n})\right)
is the unique shift that places
\overline{\tau}_{n}
at a distance of
x_{\hat{\tau}_{n}}
from the end of the word. Thus by by
Lemma

3.2
,
\operatorname{block}(\hat{\pi},n)=\operatorname{shift}_{s_{n}}(\operatorname{block}(\hat{\delta},\hat{\tau}_{n}))
.

Inductive step.

Assume that
\operatorname{block}(\hat{\pi},j)=\operatorname{shift}_{s_{j}}\!\!\left(\operatorname{block}(\hat{\delta},\hat{\tau}_{j})\right)
for all
j
such that
i+1\leq j\leq n
, and let

\hat{w}=\operatorname{block}(\hat{\delta},\hat{\tau}_{1})\cdots\operatorname{block}(\hat{\delta},\hat{\tau}_{i})\operatorname{block}(\hat{\pi},i+1)\cdots\operatorname{block}(\hat{\pi},n).

By
Proposition

3.4
,
g_{i}(\hat{w})
is invariant up to cyclically shifting the
i
-th block, so
g_{i}(\hat{w})=g_{i}(\hat{\pi})
. Before shifting, the distance from
\overline{\tau}_{i}
to
g_{i}(\hat{w})
is
d_{i}(\hat{w})
. Thus,
s_{i}=x_{\hat{\tau}_{i}}-d_{i}(\hat{w})
is the unique shift of the
i
-th block so that
\overline{\tau}_{i}
is a distance of
x_{\hat{\tau}_{i}}\pmod{k}
from
g_{i}(\hat{w})
. As a result,
\operatorname{shift}_{s_{i}}\!\!\left(\operatorname{block}(\hat{\delta},\hat{\tau}_{i})\right)=\operatorname{block}(\hat{\pi},i)
by
Lemma

3.2
.

∎

Definition 3.6
.

We define
f^{-1}\!\left(\delta,(\mathbf{x},\tau)\right)=\pi^{\prime}
, the inverse of Stanley’s fundamental bijection applied word

\hat{\pi}^{\prime}=\operatorname{shift}_{s_{1}}(\operatorname{block}(\hat{\delta},\hat{\tau}_{1}))\cdots\operatorname{shift}_{s_{n}}(\operatorname{block}(\hat{\delta},\hat{\tau}_{n})),

where each shift
s_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{k}
is as defined above.

Theorem 3.7
.

The map
f\colon S_{kn}\to D_{k,n}\times S(k,n)
is a bijection.

Proof.

By
Lemma

3.5
, we can see that
{\hat{\pi}}^{\prime}=\hat{\pi}
, and thus
f^{-1}\circ f(\pi)=\pi
, and so
f^{-1}
is a left-inverse of
f
and thus
f
is injective. Because
|D_{k,n}|=(kn)!/(k^{n}n!)
and
|S(k,n)|=k^{n}n!
, the domain and the codomain have the same cardinality:
|S_{kn}|=(kn)!=|D_{k,n}\times S(k,n)|
. Therefore
f
is bijective and
f^{-1}
is a two-sided inverse of
f
.
∎

We have found the desired bijection
f
, and by
Theorem

1.4
, this induces the desired statistic-swapping involution
\phi:S(k,n)\times S_{kn}\to S(k,n)\times S_{kn}
.

3.3
The involution

Recall that
Theorem

1.4
gives a construction of a statistic-swapping involution
\phi\colon S(k,n)\times S_{kn}\to S(k,n)\times S_{kn}
predicated on a statistic-preserving bijection
f\colon S_{kn}\to D_{k,n}\times S(k,n)
. Here we compute an example.

Example 10
.

Continuing from
Example

3
, again let
k=3
,
n=5
, and
\pi=\mathrm{(8345)(9)(B1A)(F726CED)}\in S_{kn}
. Moreover, suppose that

\sigma^{\prime}=\big(\underbrace{(2,0,0,1,0)}_{\mathbf{x}},\underbrace{(2)(31)(4)(5)}_{\tau}\big).

We will compute
\phi(\pi,\sigma^{\prime})
. Note that
\pi
has
1

k
-cycle and
\sigma^{\prime}
has
2
fixed points (i.e. indices
2
and
5
), so
\phi(\pi,\sigma)
should return a permutation with
2

k
-cycles and a generalized symmetric group element with

where

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=\mathrm{(726)(834)(B59)(EDC)(F1A)}

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{x},\tau)=\left((0,1,0,2,1),(2)(3)(514)\right),

\sigma

Following the construction in

Thus

\pi^{\prime}

It is worth briefly noting that when

which has no

4
Further considerations

To conclude the paper, we briefly mention a paper of Duchon and Duvignau
[
4
]
. They construct a reversible insertion algorithm
\mathcal{A}\colon[kn-j+1]^{(j)}\times S_{kn-j}\to S_{kn}
which takes a word in the set
[kn-j+1]^{(j)}=[kn-j+1]\times[kn-j+2]\times\dots\times[kn]
and a permutation in
S_{kn-j}
and outputs a permutation in
S_{kn}
such that the two permutations have the same number of
k
-cycles whenever
j<k
. This, together with our work, gives a combinatorial proof that for all
j<k
,

\frac{\left|\operatorname{Cyc}_{m}(S_{kn-j})\right|}{\left|S_{kn-j}\right|}=\frac{\left|\operatorname{Fxpt}_{m}\!\left(S(k,n)\right)\right|}{\left|S(k,n)\right|},

This induces a statistic-swapping involution
\psi\colon[kn-j+1]^{(j)}\times S_{kn-j}\times S(k,n)
. Define
\psi(\ell,\pi,\sigma^{\prime})=(\ell^{\prime},\pi^{\prime},\sigma)
, where

\displaystyle(\pi^{\prime\prime},\sigma)

\displaystyle=\phi(\mathcal{A}(\ell,\pi),\sigma^{\prime})\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle(\ell^{\prime},\pi^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}^{-1}(\pi^{\prime\prime})

Here
\pi^{\prime}
and
\pi^{\prime\prime}
each have the same number of
k
-cycles as
\sigma^{\prime}
has fixed points. Meanwhile,
\pi
has the same number of
k
-cycles as
\sigma
has fixed points.
