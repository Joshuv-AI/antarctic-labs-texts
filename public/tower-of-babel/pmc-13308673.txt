Circadian clock protein Bmal1 regulates respiratory motor plasticity in male rats
Authors: Aaron A. Jones, Alexandria B. Marciante, Pierce Berardi, Gordon S. Mitchell
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION

Moderate acute intermittent hypoxia (AIH), or repetitive episodes of low inspired oxygen (PaO2 ∼40–50 mmHg), elicits plasticity in phrenic, intercostal, and hypoglossal motor neurons (1). AIH has emerged as a promising therapeutic modality to improve breathing function in people with multiple neuromuscular disorders that cause paralysis (2). The most well-studied form of AIH-induced plasticity is phrenic long-term facilitation (pLTF), a persistent posthypoxic elevation in phrenic nerve burst amplitude (1). We recently established a powerful role for the diurnal rest/active cycle in regulating the magnitude and primary mechanism driving pLTF in anesthetized rats (3, 4), depending on the specific AIH protocol used. Moreover, diurnal cycles interact with AIH protocol to regulate ventilatory long-term facilitation (vLTF), a persistent posthypoxic elevation of ventilation (normalized to metabolic rate) in unanesthetized rats (5). Although diurnal fluctuations in spinal adenosine levels combine with hypoxia-evoked microglial adenosine formation to regulate pLTF (3, 6), time-of-day effects on pLTF (or vLTF) within respiratory motor neurons are not well understood.

To optimize biological functions in anticipation of a continuously changing environment, most organisms evolved an endogenous 24-h time-keeping system, referred to as the “circadian clock” (7). Indeed, many physiological functions exhibit circadian rhythms, including aspects of respiratory control (8–12). Although circadian rhythms are orchestrated by a master tissue clock in the hypothalamic suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), entrainable “local clocks” exist throughout the body. SCN-derived neural or hormonal signals synchronize the molecular transcriptional-translational feedback loop of “clock genes” within cells, allowing for intrinsic timekeeping at a cellular level. This core transcriptional-translational feedback loop consists of a “positive arm” of transcription factors, including Bmal1 and Clock, and a “negative arm” composed of Per and Cry repressor proteins that control 24-h transcriptional profiles of nearly half of all genes (13–15). The harmonization of molecular clocks within individual cells gives rise to tissue and organ clock systems that underlie 24-h rhythms in many physiological functions (16, 17). Clock genes are rhythmically expressed in the phrenic/diaphragm motor system, including the brainstem, ventral midcervical spinal cord, and diaphragm (18, 19). In association, genes critical for phrenic motor plasticity demonstrate 24-h expression patterns in the caudal brainstem and cervical spinal cord (19), potentially contributing to diurnal regulation of AIH-induced pLTF (3, 4) and vLTF (5). However, the role of clock genes (and the circadian network) in regulating respiratory motor plasticity is completely unknown.

Here, we tested the hypothesis that the molecular clock within phrenic motor neurons plays an important role in regulating AIH-induced pLTF (anesthetized) and ventilatory LTF (unanesthetized rats). To accomplish this goal, we injected small-interfering RNAs (siRNA) into the pleural space to knock down the essential circadian clock protein Bmal1 within respiratory motor neurons. Our specific hypotheses were that pLTF and vLTF would be disrupted in specific phases of the diurnal cycle. An AIH protocol consisting of 15, 1-min hypoxic episodes (FIO2 = 0.09) was delivered to assess pLTF and vLTF in the midrest (light) and midactive (dark) phases of the diurnal cycle. In midrest, pLTF was reduced, and vLTF was abolished in rats given siBmal1 versus nontargeting siRNA controls. In contrast, Bmal1 knockdown had little effect on pLTF or vLTF in midactive phase. Thus, Bmal1 and the molecular clock within phrenic motor neurons play a key role in regulating AIH-induced respiratory motor plasticity in a manner dependent on the phase of the diurnal cycle. These findings reinforce the need to consider circadian biology in future studies of AIH-induced respiratory motor plasticity.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

All experiments were approved by the University of Florida Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Young male (300–450 g, 3–6 mo old) Sprague Dawley rats (Envigo, Colony 208 A; Indianapolis, IN) were dual-housed in a controlled environment (24°C; 12-h light/dark cycle) with food (Teklad 2918, Inotiv) and water provided ad libitum. Due to the complex interaction of sex, age, and estrus cycle effects on pLTF and vLTF (20–23), which would require at least sixfold more rats to account for various combinations, this preliminary clock gene study was limited to males to first establish a fundamental basis for circadian regulation of LTF. After arriving from the supplier, rats were entrained for at least 14 days to either a standard vivarium room (lights on/off at 6:00 AM/6: 00 PM) for midrest phase (light) groups, or a reverse light cycle room (lights on/off at 8:00 PM/8:00 AM) for midactive phase (dark) groups. Zeitgeber time (ZT) = 0 was defined as lights on (the beginning of the rest phase), whereas ZT = 12 denoted lights off (the beginning of the active phase). Active phase plethysmography experiments were conducted in complete darkness with the exception of brief exposure to dim red light as needed for rodent handling. For phrenic nerve studies, active-phase rats were anesthetized with isoflurane (2.5%–3%) and transferred to the recording station with the lights off; their eyes were then covered with an opaque rag before turning the lights on. After allocating rats to midrest/active phase groups and room assignment, rats were further allocated to targeting or nontargeting siRNA groups. Care was taken to alternate rat treatment assignments so that family groups were not overrepresented in any group. Sample size estimation (n = 7 for all groups) was based on power analysis of previous phrenic (3, 6, 24–26) and ventilatory (5, 27, 28) LTF studies and our extensive experience with these experimental techniques.

Retrograde Labeling and RNA Interference of Respiratory Motor Neurons

To retrogradely label phrenic motor neurons, intrapleural injections of the β‐subunit of cholera toxin (CtB; Millipore Sigma, #C9903) were performed as in prior studies (6, 25, 26, 29, 30). Seven or more days before tissue harvest, CtB (15 μL/side; 30 μL total/rat; 2 μg/μL in sterile H2O) was injected intrapleurally on each side of the thoracic wall using 50 μL Hamilton syringes at a depth of 6 mm through the fifth intercostal space anterior axillary line. At least 4 days after CtB injection, a pool of siRNAs (Dharmacon) targeting rat Bmal1 (Ref No. E-087911-00-0020) mRNA (n = 28) or a nontargeting sequence (Ref No. D-001910-10-20; n = 28) was delivered intrapleurally (Fig. 1). Each siRNA consisted of four pooled nucleotide duplexes with symmetrical 3′ overhangs (Accell SMARTpool). We used Accell brand siRNAs because they are designed to prevent toxicity and inflammation and demonstrate a strong selectivity for transfecting neurons, but not other CNS cell types (31). Since there is no evidence for trans-synaptic trafficking of molecules when delivered intrapleurally (29), this approach effectively targets spinal respiratory motor neurons, but not interneurons or adjacent glia. Experiment 1: Neurophysiological Recordings in Anesthetized Rats

Surgical preparation.

Terminal neurophysiology experiments were performed as previously described (3, 6, 24–26, 30, 32, 33). Rats were induced with 2.5%–3.0% isoflurane in O2 in a plexiglass induction chamber, weighed, and then transferred to a heated surgical table where anesthesia was continued (2.5%–3.0% isoflurane; 60% O2, balance N2). Body temperature was monitored via rectal thermometer (Fisher Scientific, Pittsburgh, PA) and maintained between 37.5 ± 1.0°C throughout the experiment. A polyethylene catheter (i.d. 1.67 mm; PE 240; Intramedic, MD) was inserted into the trachea through a midline neck incision, and rats were mechanically ventilated (0.07 mL/10 g body mass/breath; 72 breaths/min; VentElite small animal ventilator; Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, MA). End-tidal Pco2 (PETCO2) was monitored throughout experiments using a breathe-through capnograph (Capnogard, Novametrix, Wallingford, CT). The inspired CO2 level was adjusted as needed to maintain end-tidal CO2 within physiological limits (PETCO2 38–45 mmHg) (4).

Anesthesia was then slowly converted to urethane via slow intravenous infusion into a tail vein catheter (1.8 g/kg at 6 mL/h; 24 gauge, Surflo, Elkton, MD) while progressively decreasing inspired isoflurane concentration 0.5% every 6 min. Anesthetic depth was assessed during conversion by lack of response to a toe-pinch; supplemental anesthetic was infused if required. Rats were bilaterally vagotomized at the midcervical level to prevent phrenic nerve entrainment with the ventilator. The right femoral artery was isolated and cannulated with polyethylene tubing (I.D. 0.58 mm; PE 50; Intradermic, MD) to monitor blood pressure (TA-100 Transducer Amplifier, CWE, Inc.) and sample blood gases (PaO2 and PaCO2), acid-base balance (pH, base excess), and hemoglobin with a blood gas analyzer (ABL90 Flex, Radiometer, Copenhagen, Denmark). Once urethane conversion was complete, intravenous fluids were administered to maintain fluid and acid-base balance (1.5 mL/h; 1:4 of 8.4% Na2CO3 in lactated Ringer’s solution). Rats were then paralyzed with pancuronium bromide (2 mg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO).

Phrenic nerve recording.

Using a dorsal approach, the left phrenic nerve was isolated, cut distally, and desheathed. Custom suction recording electrodes filled with 0.9% saline were then placed in the saline-filled phrenic pocket, and the nerve was suctioned with a 60-mL syringe to record respiratory neural activity. Nerve activity was amplified (10 K, A-M systems, Everett, WA), filtered (band-pass 300–5,000 Hz), integrated (time constant, 50 ms), digitized (CED 1401, Cambridge Electronic Design, UK), and analyzed using Spike2 software (CED, version 8.20). Inspiratory phrenic burst activity served as an index of respiratory motor output.

Experimental protocol.

At least 45 min after conversion to urethane anesthesia, CO2 apneic and recruitment thresholds of phrenic nerve activity were determined by 1) lowering inspired CO2 levels or 2) increasing ventilation rate until rhythmic respiratory nerve activity ceased. After ∼1 min, inspired CO2 levels were slowly increased until respiratory rhythmic activity resumed. Baseline conditions were established by raising PETCO2 ∼2 mmHg above the recruitment threshold. Blood samples were taken during baseline to document blood gas levels during stable nerve activity. Arterial Pco2 was maintained isocapnic (±1.5 mmHg) with respect to baseline blood gas values by actively manipulating inspired CO2 concentration and/or respiratory rate. Baseline oxygen levels (60% O2, balance N2 and CO2; PaO2 ≥ 150 mmHg) were maintained throughout experiments, except for hypoxic challenges (PaO2 = 40–50 mmHg). At least 20 min after establishing recruitment threshold and baseline blood gas levels, an AIH protocol consisting of 15, 1-min episodes of isocapnic hypoxia (inspired O2 = 9%, inspired CO2 = 1–3%) separated by 1-min recovery intervals (inspired O2 = 60%, CO2 = 1–3%) was delivered. Blood gas values were taken again at the 1st, 8th, and 15th hypoxic episodes, as well as 30, 60, and 90 min after AIH, as previously described (3). Blood samples during hypoxic episodes were drawn once the FIO2 reached nadir. Generally, this occurred in the final 10–15 s of the hypoxic episode (3). FIO2 was adjusted as needed in order for hypoxic PaO2 ranges to be maintained between 40–50 mmHg. However, 0.09 FIO2 was typically sufficient to achieve a PaO2 of 40–50 mmHg. At the end of the 90-min post-AIH recording period, a maximum chemoreceptor challenge (7% CO2, 10% O2, balance N2) was administered for ∼3 min to verify phrenic nerve integrity.

Experiment 2: Ventilatory Assessment in Unanesthetized Rats

Whole body plethysmography.

Ventilatory data were collected in unanesthetized rats using whole body barometric plethysmography (SCIREQ Scientific Respiratory Equipment Inc.; Montréal, Canada), as previously described (5). Chambers (4 L) were ventilated continuously with 21% O2 (balance N2) using a flow-through method (2 L/min). Pressure differences between the subject and reference chambers were measured via a differential pressure transducer and digitized at 1 kHz using IOX2 software (Emka Technologies; Paris, France). Pressure calibration was performed by injecting a known volume of air (20 mL) into the chamber and measuring the pressure deflection. Chamber temperature and humidity and rat rectal temperature (measured via thermometer) were used to determine a volume correction factor based on the principles of Drorbaugh and Fenn (34). Breath thresholding/filtering to isolate tidal breathing was performed as previously described (5).

Before experiments, rats were subjected to ∼2-h acclimation sessions in plethysmography chambers on 2 separate days. On the day of experiments, rats were weighed, placed in the chamber, and recorded for 90 min after a 15-min adjustment period. Following baseline measurements, rats were transferred to AIH exposure cylinders (see Intermittent hypoxia exposures). Immediately following AIH, rats were transferred back to the plethysmograph chamber and recorded for an additional 90 min to detect vLTF. Metabolic CO2 production (V̇co2) was estimated by measuring the excurrent CO2 during plethysmography via a high-resolution CO2 analyzer (CA-10, Sable Systems; North Las Vegas, NV). CO2 data were acquired digitally at a sampling rate of 1 Hz using a custom program developed in LabView software (National Instruments; Austin, TX).

Intermittent hypoxia exposures.

For plethysmography experiments, intermittent hypoxia protocols were delivered in custom-made Plexiglas cylinders (10 in. length; 4 in. internal diameter) designed for rapid gas transitions (5, 35) that are not feasible in conventional plethysmography chambers. Protocol-specific gas mixtures (variable O2, balance N2) were humidified to ∼30–50% and delivered via a programmable mass-flow controller (Therapeutiq; Kansas City, MO) at a flow rate of 6 L/min. Rats received 15, 1-min episodes of 9% O2 interspersed with 1 min of normoxia (21% O2). This protocol elicits an SpO2 of ∼70% (5), consistent with our target PaO2 range (∼40–55 mmHg) based on the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve for rats (36). Experiments were timed so that midrest phase AIH exposures occurred between 12:00 PM and 1:00 PM (ZT6-7) and midactive phase exposures occurred between 1:00 PM and 2:00 PM (ZT17-18, equivalent to midnight in a normal light cycle room).

Tissue Harvesting

Rats used for plethysmography experiments were perfused with chilled 1X phosphate-buffered saline transcardially under isoflurane anesthesia (3.5% in O2), followed by ∼400 mL chilled paraformaldehyde (4% in 1X PBS, pH 7.4) via peristaltic pump (Masterflex Pump, Cole-Parmer). Rats used for neurophysiology were perfused under urethane anesthesia (see Surgical preparation). Spinal cords were dissected and postfixed for 24 h at 4°C in a tube filled with 4% paraformaldehyde. Tissues were then cryoprotected in 30% sucrose for 48 h at 4°C. Cervical spinal segments (C3–C5) were isolated and embedded using optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound (Fisher Scientific), cut into 20 µm transverse sections using a microtome (Leica SM2010R), and then placed in a 96-well plate containing PBS. For each rat, ∼4 to 6 sections were taken from C3, C4, and C5 each, mounted on charged slides (12–16 sections/slide), and stored at −80°C for immunohistochemistry.

Immunofluorescence

Immunohistochemistry was performed to detect Bmal1 within phrenic motor neurons, as done previously (19). Tissues were first washed in 1X TBS and then incubated for 40 min at 80°C in sodium citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0) for heat-induced antigen retrieval to improve detection in fixed tissues. Tissues were then washed in TBS (with 0.2% TritonX-100) and blocked with 5% donkey serum (in 1X TBS, 0.2% TritonX-100) at room temperature for 1 h. After blocking, tissues were incubated in goat anti-CtB (1:1,500; Ref No. 227040, Millipore) and rabbit anti-Bmal1 (1:1,000; ref no. D2L7G, Cell Signaling) primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The following day, slides were washed in TBS three times and incubated in donkey anti-goat (1:1,000; AlexaFluor 488; Ref No. A11055, Invitrogen) and donkey anti-rabbit (1:1,000; AlexaFluor 594; Ref No. A32754, Invitrogen) secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Slides were washed with TBS three times and cover slipped with ProLong Diamond (with DAPI) antifade mountant (ThermoFisher Scientific). Imaging was conducted using a fluorescence/bright-field microscope (BZ‐X710, Keyence Co., Osaka, Japan) with a ×40 lens. Bmal1 protein was detected using a TexasRed filter (Model No: OP‐87765); CtB immunolabelling was detected via a GFP filter (Model No: OP‐87763). All images were taken in sectioning mode under standard resolution. Bmal1 and CtB were imaged at exposure times of 1/2 and 1/6 s, respectively. All images were captured with the same exposure times and gain settings.

Data Analysis and Statistics

Measurements of peak integrated phrenic burst amplitude and burst frequency (bursts/min) were assessed in 1 min bins immediately before each arterial blood sample at baseline, 30, 60, and 90 min after AIH. Integrated nerve burst amplitude was normalized by subtracting the baseline value, dividing the difference by the baseline value, and reporting percent changes from baseline. Burst frequencies were also normalized to baseline, expressed as an absolute difference in bursts/min (3). Comparisons of PaCO2 and PaO2 were made at baseline, during hypoxic episodes (1, 8, and 15), and again 30, 60, and 90 min after AIH (Tables 1 and 2). Mean arterial pressures and phrenic burst characteristics were reported as raw values during baseline and 30, 60, and 90 min after AIH (Table 2). Breathing data were organized into 30 min bins and compared with equivalent baseline ranges (5). Each bin consisted of the preceding 30-min average for each reported time point. Post-AIH breathing variables were expressed as an absolute change from baseline due to the well-documented rest-active differences in baseline breathing (5, 37, 38). Summary breathing data (Table 3) consisted of 90-min averages for the entire baseline and post-AIH periods. Volumetric breathing data and V̇co2 (mL/min) were normalized to rat body mass (per 100 g).

Our laboratory has extensive experience in quantifying protein expression (including Bmal1) within phrenic motor neurons using immunofluorescence (19, 25, 26, 30). ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD) was used to analyze fluorescent intensity within CtB-positive phrenic motor neuron cell bodies (39). The background intensity level was determined using the mean value of a control region (e.g., near the central canal for each side). A threshold for each CtB image was determined by constructing a pixel intensity histogram for each image and selecting a fixed percentile value across all images. The ImageJ “polygon selection” tool was used to annotate CtB-positive (phrenic motor neuron) and negative (nonphrenic) cells adjacent to phrenic motor neurons (within the same image). The fluorescent intensity of Bmal1 labeling was calculated as the average intensity of the CtB‐positive cells in each section and normalized to control region values (i.e., background subtracted). Intensities of individual nonphrenic cervical cells were calculated as previously described (30). Bmal1 labeling in nonphrenic cells was measured to verify the selectivity of knockdown to phrenic motor neurons. Data across all measured cells of all sections (12–16 sections/animal) were averaged for each rat, representing the measured value for that animal. Image analysis was conducted by an investigator blinded to siRNA treatment.

Data were graphed and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 and expressed as the means ± SD unless otherwise specified. Data involving two experimental variables were analyzed using a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When significance was detected in the overall ANOVA, Sidak’s multiple comparisons test was used to examine individual differences between groups. For time-course data, the ANOVA included repeated measures with sphericity not assumed and a Geisser-Greenhouse correction applied. Tables 1, 2 and 3 were analyzed using a repeated measures three-way ANOVA. Correlations of Bmal1 expression and pLTF/vLTF data were analyzed via simple linear regression. For all datasets, trends were considered significant if P < 0.05.

RESULTS

Bmal1 Is Knocked down within Phrenic Motor Neurons following Intrapleural siRNA Delivery

To determine the magnitude of Bmal1 knockdown following 3 days of intrapleural siRNA treatments, we assessed Bmal1 protein levels in phrenic motor neurons (CtB-labeled) and nonphrenic cells via immunohistochemistry. Bmal1 demonstrated robust nuclear expression within phrenic motor neurons (Fig. 3A), consistent with its role as a transcription factor (14). There was an effect of siRNA (F1,18 = 26.86, P < 0.0001, rest; F1,18 = 7.181, P = 0.0153, active), cell type (F1,18 = 4.402, P = 0.0503, rest; F1,18 = 13.09, P = 0.002, active), and interaction (F1,18 = 53.81, P < 0.0001, rest; F1,18 = 60.79, P < 0.0001, active) on Bmal1 fluorescent intensity. Intrapleural delivery of siBmal1 reduced phrenic Bmal1 expression by ∼33% in the midrest phase (Fig. 3B) and ∼28% in the midactive phase (Fig. 3C) versus siNT controls (P < 0.0001 for both). Importantly, siBmal1 did not affect Bmal1 expression in nonphrenic cells lacking CtB staining (P > 0.05). Thus, siBmal1 effectively knocks down Bmal1 expression within phrenic motor neurons.

Phrenic Bmal1 Knockdown Attenuates pLTF in the Midrest But Not Midactive Phase

To determine the role of Bmal1 in regulating pLTF, we measured integrated phrenic burst amplitude up to 90 min following an AIH protocol consisting of 15, 1-min hypoxic episodes (inspired O2 = 9%; Fig. 4A). During hypoxic episodes, rats achieved a PaO2 of ∼40–50 mmHg (Table 1), consistent with our target range for moderate AIH (3, 6). Throughout the post-AIH period, rats remained hyperoxic (PaO2 > 150 mmHg) and isocapnic (PaCO2 ± 1.5 mmHg vs. baseline), ensuring that changes in chemofeedback from baseline conditions did not affect our results (Table 2). Similarly, diurnal phase and siRNA treatment did not affect mean arterial pressure or phrenic burst frequency at baseline or during the post-AIH period. Since phrenic amplitude did not differ at baseline for any of the groups (Table 2), we expressed pLTF as a percent change from baseline (Fig. 4B). In the midrest phase, there was an effect of time after AIH (F2.9,35.0 = 90.39, P < 0.0001), siRNA (F1,12 = 27.56, P = 0.0002), and interaction (F2.9,35.0 = 10.66, P < 0.0001) on pLTF. DISCUSSION

Although we recently established a powerful role for the diurnal rest/active cycle in regulating AIH-induced respiratory motor plasticity in anesthetized (3, 4) and unanesthetized (5) rats, the role of endogenous circadian clock mechanisms in mediating these time-of-day effects was unknown. Here, we report that the circadian clock gene Bmal1 within respiratory motor neurons plays a prominent role in regulating AIH-induced phrenic motor plasticity. Moreover, we demonstrated that molecular clock disruption impacts respiratory motor plasticity in unanesthetized rats in a manner dependent on diurnal phase. Circadian regulation of respiratory plasticity has particular importance for translation of AIH as a therapeutic modality, given the reverse diurnal cycles of rodents versus humans.

Circadian rhythms are organized at the molecular level largely via a core transcriptional-translational feedback loop composed of the transcriptional activators Bmal1 and Clock and repressors Per and Cry (14). Although many clock genes possess multiple paralogs with overlapping functions, Bmal1 is essential to the proper functioning of the molecular circadian clock in mammals (41). We have previously shown that Bmal1 is expressed by phrenic motor neurons (19), yet its role in regulating respiratory motor plasticity has not been previously studied. Here, we target the molecular clock within respiratory motor neurons by injecting siRNAs into the pleural space to knock down Bmal1 expression. Intrapleural delivery is an effective means of targeting respiratory motor neurons, but not interneurons or glia, since this molecular cargo does not move across synapses (29, 42), and Accell brand siRNAs exhibit selectivity for neurons versus other CNS cell types (31). Indeed, our laboratory has selectively targeted TrkB (26), PKCθ (25), PKCζ (43), A2A receptors (30); serine/threonine protein phosphatases (44); and fractalkine (6) within phrenic motor neurons using intrapleural siRNA delivery. In line with these studies, intrapleural siBmal1 reduced phrenic Bmal1 protein expression by ∼30% in both phases of the diurnal cycle, without effect on adjacent nonphrenic cells, confirming selectivity for respiratory motor neurons.

To determine the effects of clock disruption on phrenic motor plasticity, rats were exposed to 15, 1-min episodes of moderate hypoxia (PaO2 = 40–50 mmHg) in midrest or midactive phase. The 15×1 protocol was chosen for this study since it elicits a profound diurnal rest/active difference in pLTF (3, 4), suggesting possible clock regulation, yet reliably elicits vLTF in both phases of the diurnal cycle, unlike the 3x5 protocol (5). In addition, the 3×5 protocol does not elicit diurnal variation in pLTF magnitude; rather, it elicits a complete switch from a serotonin- to an adenosine-driven mechanism, complicating our ability to interpret data (3). In control rats treated with nontargeting siRNAs, pLTF exhibited profound diurnal variation, with AIH eliciting ∼120% in the midrest, but only ∼30% in the midactive phase. These findings are consistent with prior studies using 15×1 AIH in naïve rats (3, 4), suggesting off-target effects of intrapleural siRNAs were minimal. Following siBmal1 delivery, pLTF was substantially reduced (∼56%) in the midrest, but not in the midactive phase. Thus, Bmal1 regulates plasticity in a time-of-day-dependent manner. Indeed, loss of Bmal1 in mice alters quiet breathing and ventilatory chemoreflexes differently in the rest versus active phase (10, 12).

Since pLTF is most often studied in anesthetized, paralyzed, ventilated, and vagotomized rats (3, 6, 24–26, 30, 32, 33), and these factors may limit interpretation of experimental results, we assessed vLTF in unanesthetized rats using whole body plethysmography. We corrected vLTF for changes in metabolic rate as failure to account for AIH-induced hypometabolism can obscure vLTF expression in poikilocapnic rats (5, 27, 28). Although we did not directly measure PaO2 in unanesthetized rats, 1-min episodes of breathing 9% inspired O2 decrease SpO2 to ∼70% (5), which projects a PaO2 of ∼45–50 mmHg based on the rat oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve (36). This level of hypoxemia is similar to moderate AIH in anesthetized rats (Table 1). Thus, rats used in both pLTF and vLTF experiments received equivalent AIH in terms of the timing and severity of hypoxemia.

Following AIH, rats given siNT demonstrated robust vLTF in both midrest and midactive phases. This vLTF was similar in magnitude (∼23%) to our prior report on naïve rats (5). In contrast, rats given siBmal1 no longer exhibited vLTF in the midrest phase but retained comparable vLTF in the midactive phase versus siNT rats. Thus, as with pLTF, Bmal1 knockdown within all respiratory motor neurons adjacent to the pleural space diminishes rest phase vLTF in unanesthetized rats. Since ΔV̇e/V̇co2 was comparable between siNT and siBmal1 rats 30 min after AIH, Bmal1 disruption may impact the maintenance but not initiation of vLTF.

Although siBmal1 effects on pLTF can be attributed to Bmal1 knockdown (∼30%) within phrenic motor neurons, we cannot rule out contributions of other respiratory motor pools (e.g., intercostals) to siBmal1 effects on vLTF, since V̇e reflects the actions of all respiratory motor pools/muscles working in concert. Intrapleural siRNA delivery (as well as CtB) also targets intercostal motor neurons (29, 43), although we did not demonstrate knockdown within those motor pools. This distinction may explain why phrenic Bmal1 expression was positively correlated with pLTF, but not with vLTF, in the midrest phase. Regardless, the observation that both pLTF and vLTF displayed similar outcomes in siBmal1 rats suggests that siBmal1 effects on V̇e and Vt LTF are mediated at least in part within the phrenic/diaphragm motor system. The specific contributions of intercostal motor neurons to circadian regulation of vLTF warrant further investigation.

Here, we demonstrate a role for the endogenous circadian clock in regulating AIH-induced respiratory motor plasticity. However, mechanisms whereby the molecular clock regulates LTF remain unknown. The circadian clock regulates other forms of neuroplasticity, such as hippocampal long-term potentiation (LTP). Indeed, Bmal1 knockout impairs hippocampal LTP in mice (45), and translocation of Bmal1 to neuronal synapses in the hippocampus controls diurnal LTP regulation (46). Interestingly, hippocampal phospho-ERK expression is reduced specifically in the rest phase of Bmal1 knockout mice (45). Similar effects in the phrenic system could explain midrest phase pLTF and vLTF impairment since spinal ERK activation is necessary for midrest phase AIH-induced pLTF (47).

Transcripts of several genes involved in cellular mechanisms of LTF are rhythmically expressed in the ventral cervical spinal cord, including Bdnf and serotonin receptor 2B (19), suggesting possible regulation by the molecular clock. Clock-responsive (E-box) sites are present in regulatory regions of the Bdnf gene and its high-affinity receptor, TrkB (48). It is unknown whether diurnal effects on LTF result from Bmal1 acting directly on LTF effector proteins or indirectly via other clock genes (e.g., Per, Cry, Rev-erbα, etc.). Additional work is needed to explore mechanisms whereby the molecular clock regulates LTF.

Since time-of-day is a key biological variable crucial for successful translation of preclinical rodent studies to humans (49), the present findings have profound translational implications as we optimize AIH as a therapeutic modality to treat breathing dysfunction in individuals with neuromuscular disorders (2). Rodents and humans differ with respect to their diurnal cycle (and circadian phase) such that the midrest phase corresponds to daytime in nocturnal rodents but nighttime in diurnal humans. Our finding that phrenic Bmal1 knockdown attenuates midrest phase LTF tempts the possibility that environmental factors causing circadian disruption (e.g., Bmal1 disruption) within spinal motor pathways may impact the ability to elicit plasticity during sleep/night in humans. Although circadian regulation of LTF in humans has been postulated (50), endogenous circadian control requires direct, empirical tests. To date, only two human studies have investigated time of day as a variable in AIH-induced ventilatory LTF (51, 52). In these studies on people with obstructive sleep apnea, vLTF was greater when AIH was delivered late (evening) versus early in the active phase (morning), regardless of whether they were awake or sleeping. Future studies are needed to determine whether direct rest/active phase differences exist in humans and whether time-of-day effects on LTF are under endogenous circadian control (requiring removal of external time cues).

Circadian disruption of physiological rhythms and gene expression has been reported in rodent models and humans with neuromuscular injury/disease, such as spinal cord injury and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (53–56). Such circadian disruptions may impact the efficacy or optimal delivery time of therapeutic AIH (5). Thus, understanding circadian effects on LTF in rodent models of neuromuscular disease and/or injury is of considerable importance.

Furthermore, since the current study was limited to male rats, it will be important to examine the role of circadian clock genes in regulating AIH-induced plasticity in females. Genetic disruption of Bmal1 in mice alters diurnal regulation of quiet breathing and ventilatory chemoreflexes in a manner dependent on biological sex (10, 12), with females being slightly more resistant to ventilatory changes caused by Bmal1 knockout. How sex impacts respiratory neuroplasticity at different times of day remains unexplored. Since LTF is sexually dimorphic and controlled by a complex interaction of age and estrus cycle in females (20–23), circadian clock effects on AIH-induced plasticity must be studied in females at different ages and stages of the estrus cycle.

Conclusions

We demonstrate that Bmal1 within respiratory motor neurons exerts powerful, time-of-day-dependent effects on AIH-induced pLTF and vLTF in male rats. In the midrest phase, Bmal1 knockdown within respiratory motor neurons attenuates pLTF and abolishes vLTF, whereas minimal effects are observed in the midactive phase. Since human and rodent AIH studies typically differ with respect to the specific phase of the diurnal (and circadian) cycle, and neuromuscular injury/disease is often associated with circadian disruption, these results highlight a critical need to consider diurnal/circadian rhythms in the design of future studies or human clinical trials. Greater understanding of circadian effects on AIH-induced plasticity and functional recovery will improve efforts to harness AIH as a therapeutic modality for neuromuscular disorders that compromise breathing and other, nonrespiratory movements (i.e., chronotherapeutics).
