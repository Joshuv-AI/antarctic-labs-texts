Construction of exact refinements for the two-dimensional hierarchical B-spline de Rham complex
Diogo C. Cabanas, Kendrick M. Shepherd, Deepesh Toshniwal, Rafael Vázquez

Abstract
The de Rham complex arises naturally when studying problems in electromagnetism and fluid mechanics. Stable numerical methods to solve these problems can be obtained by using a discrete de Rham complex that preserves the structure of the continuous one. This property is not necessarily guaranteed when the discrete function spaces are hierarchical B-splines, and research shows that an arbitrary choice of refinement domains may give rise to spurious harmonic fields that ruin the accuracy of the solution. We will focus on the two-dimensional de Rham complex over the unit square Ω ⊆ ℝ 2 \Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2} , and provide theoretical results and a constructive algorithm to ensure that the structure of the complex is preserved: when a pair of functions are in conflict some additional functions, forming an L-chain between the pair, are also refined. Another crucial aspect to consider in the hierarchical setting is the notion of admissibility, as it is possible to obtain optimal convergence rates of numerical solutions and improved stability by limiting the multi-level interaction of basis functions. We show that, under a common restriction, the admissibility class of the first space of the discrete complex persists throughout the remaining spaces. As such, admissible refinement can be combined with our new algorithm to obtain admissible meshes that also respect the structure of the de Rham complex. Moreover, we detail how our algorithm can be easily included in standard adaptive mesh refinement schemes. Finally, we include numerical results that motivate the importance of the previous concerns for the vector Laplace and Maxwell eigenvalue problems.



1
Introduction

Over the last few years, there have been several developments in numerical methods designed
to approximate the solution of partial differential equations (PDEs). Finite element
exterior calculus (FEEC)
[
Arnold2006
,
Arnold2009
]
is one of these developments, which
has gained a lot of traction since its earlier publications
[
Arnold2002
]
due to its
ability to describe how stable numerical methods can be achieved for a broad class of PDEs.
Key elements used to ensure the stability of discrete methods are the topological and
differential-geometric structure of PDEs, which are captured in FEEC through complexes and,
more specifically, their cohomology classes. By choosing an appropriate finite element
space, it is possible not only to approximate the continuous solution, but also to guarantee
that the method is stable — provided that the continuous and discrete complexes are
cohomologically equivalent.
An example of such a complex, which we approach in this work, is the
de Rham
complex.
Several PDEs can be understood, and successfully discretized, in the context of the de Rham
complex, such as those arising in Stokes flow, Navier-Stokes, Maxwell’s equation,
Vector/Scalar Laplacians, among others
[
Arnold2006
]
.

Another important breakthrough in the field of numerical analysis was the introduction of
spline functions
[
Boor2001
,
Schumaker2007
]
as a compelling alternative to the more
classical finite element method
[
Hughes2000
,
Ciarlet2002
,
Brenner2008
]
. The former
approach was given the name of isogeometric analysis (IGA), and it is explained in great
detail in the seminal paper by Hughes et al.
[
Hughes2005
]
. The motivation behind this
method was to create a better synergy between numerical-analysis and industrial-design
teams, which have been using splines in CAD-based modelling for a long time

[
Farin2006
]
. One of the benefits of the higher smoothness allowed in splines is that
they provide better approximation properties per degree of freedom (DoF) than standard

C^{0}
or
C^{-1}
finite element spaces
[
Evans2009
,
Bressan2019
,
Sande2019
,
Sande2020
]
. Another upside in IGA is the flexibility in defining new spline spaces from
basic splines, and in particular locally refined spline spaces. These allow us to increase
the density of DoFs near relevant local features of the solution being computed, increasing
the accuracy in those regions and reducing the computational cost. Several types of splines
have been explored in the IGA literature, such as T-splines
[
Sederberg2003
,
Sederberg2004
]
, hierarchical B-splines
[
Vuong2011
,
Buffa2015
,
D'Angella2018
]
and
truncated hierarchical B-splines
[
Giannelli2012
,
Giannelli2013
]
, and the more recent
polar splines
[
Speleers2021
]
.
In this work, our attention will be centered around hierarchical B-splines and their
truncated counterpart.
The general idea of hierarchical basis is to take a sequence of function space basis, each
corresponding to a level providing more DoFs than the former, and then applying a
selection mechanism to determine which basis functions from each level are present in the
final hierarchical basis
[
Kraft1998
]
.

When working with a hierarchical B-spline complex in
\mathbb{R}^{n},n\geq 1
, it is not true that
this will generate an exact subcomplex of the continuous one for an arbitrary refinement

[
Evans2018
,
Shepherd2024
]
.
This is due to the fact that a poor choice of the refinement pattern can remove basis
functions from a coarser level and add basis functions from a finer level such that they
describe function spaces with distinct topological properties.
This leads to the introduction of spurious harmonic forms, which can easily ruin the
accuracy and stability of the numerical method being used.

Focusing on the two-dimensional case, the first contribution of this work is a constructive
method to modify the refinement pattern, and to recover an exact complex for spaces of
hierarchical B-splines, by adding some elements to refine. Leveraging the theoretical
results from
[
Shepherd2024
]
, the modified refinement works as follows: when a pair of
functions supported in the refined domain is
problematic
, following the definition
in
[
Shepherd2024
]
, we also refine the support of some additional functions, forming an
L-chain between the pair.
The L-chain is simply a path in taxicab geometry where two points, in our case basis
function indices, are connected through successive exhaustion of their difference in each
dimension.
By this change, we can topologically connect degrees of freedom in such a way that
cohomology is preserved between levels.
We present theoretical results to show that the shape of the L-chain reduces the amount of
recursive checks for problematic pairs compared to other possible refinement paths.
We also provide algorithms to easily implement the refinement scheme in any existing IGA
code.

Another important consideration when discretizing with hierarchical B-spline spaces is the
concept of admissibility, which limits the interaction of functions from very different
refinement levels
[
Buffa2015
]
. Admissibility is a required property to obtain optimal
convergence rates in the theoretical study of adaptive methods
[
Buffa2017
,
Buffa2022
]
.
Additionally, it has been shown on time-dependent problems that admissibility improves the
stability of the method when applying adaptive coarsening
[
Carraturo2019
]
. The second
contribution of this work is to show that admissibility for the first space of the complex
implies the same property, with the same admissibility class, for all the spaces in the
complex. This result is valid for both hierarchical and truncated hierarchical spline bases,
and requires a common assumption in the IGA literature: the refined regions must be formed
by the union of supports of functions from the previous level. Furthermore, our new
algorithm can be easily combined with admissible refinement, to obtain an exact complex such
that all the spaces respect the admissibility property.

The structure of the paper is as follows: in
Section

2
we give a basic
overview of the de Rham complex and its discretization. Next, in
Section

3
we
describe the construction of hierarchical B-splines and the corresponding hierarchical
B-spline de Rham complex. In
Sections

4
and
5
, we present the main
theoretical results for admissibility and exactness, respectively. These are followed by

Section

6
with their accompanying algorithms. We test our approach numerically
in
Section

7
, where we showcase various examples using both the vector
Laplace problem and Maxwell’s eigenvalue problem to illustrate the use of the method as an
adaptive scheme. Finally, in
we give some concluding remarks.

2
Preliminaries

We start by introducing an overview of the basic objects and concepts used in FEEC. We
specialize this overview for the two-dimensional case, which is our primary focus. Moreover,
we simplify the exposition by avoiding the use of differential forms and instead use vector
calculus proxies. For a more general discussion, the reader is invited to consult

[
Arnold2018
]
.

2.1
The Hilbert de Rham complex

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded domain with a piecewise-smooth Lipschitz boundary.
The following sequence,
\mathfrak{R}
, of Hilbert spaces and connecting differential
operators is called the Hilbert de Rham complex,

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{R}~:~0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{1}(\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}(\curl;\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}(\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0\;,}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\grad}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\curl}

where
\iota
is the inclusion operator,
\grad
is the gradient operator defined in
coordinates by
\grad u=(\partial_{x}u,\partial_{y}u)
,
\curl
is the scalar curl
operator in two dimensions and is defined in coordinates as
\curl\boldsymbol{v}:=\partial_{x}v_{2}-\partial_{y}v_{1}
,
L^{2}(\Omega)
is the space of square-integrable functions on
\Omega
, and

\displaystyle H^{1}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\left\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega):\grad u\in[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{2}\right\}\;,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}(\curl;\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{2}:\curl\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{2}(\Omega)\right\}\;.

We will refer to these spaces as
\mathbb{X}^{0}=H^{1}(\Omega),\mathbb{X}^{1}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}(\curl;\Omega)
and

\mathbb{X}^{2}=L^{2}(\Omega)
. Similarly, in this paper, we will particularly work with the
Hilbert de Rham complex with homogeneous boundary conditions, namely

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{R}_{0}~:~0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{0}(\curl;\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}(\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0\;,}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\grad}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\curl}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\int}

where
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{0}(\curl;\Omega)
correspond to
H^{1}(\Omega)
and

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}(\curl;\Omega),
respectively, but with vanishing trace, meaning null boundary
conditions of each space:

\displaystyle H^{1}_{0}\left(\Omega\right)

\displaystyle\coloneq\left\{u\in H^{1}\left(\Omega\right):u|_{\partial\Omega}=0\right\},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{0}\left(\curl;\Omega\right)

\displaystyle\coloneq\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}\left(\curl;\Omega\right):\boldsymbol{v}|_{\partial\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}=0\right\},

where
\partial\Omega
is the boundary of
\Omega
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}
is the outward
unit normal.

The sequences
\mathfrak{R}
and
\mathfrak{R}_{0}
are called complexes because of two
properties. First, each differential operator maps from one space into the next space,
meaning that, for the
\mathfrak{R}
and
\mathfrak{R}_{0}
sequences it respectively holds

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}(\grad)\subset\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}(\curl;\Omega)\;,\qquad\mathrm{Im}(\curl)\subset L^{2}(\Omega)\;,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}(\grad)\subset\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{0}(\curl;\Omega)\;,\qquad\mathrm{Im}(\curl)\subset L^{2}(\Omega)\;,

where
\mathrm{Im}
denotes the image of a given map; similarly, we will use
\mathrm{Ker}
for
the kernel.
Second, the composition of the two operators is identically zero, meaning for example

\curl(\grad f)=0
for any
f\in H^{1}(\Omega)
.
Given these two properties, we can define the following (quotient) spaces called the

k
-cohomology spaces of
\mathfrak{R}
, and
\mathfrak{R}_{0}
, for
k=0,1,2
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{0}(\mathfrak{R}):=\mathrm{Ker}(\grad)/\mathbb{R},\ \mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathfrak{R}):=\mathrm{Ker}(\curl)/\mathrm{Im}(\grad),\ \mathrm{H}^{2}(\mathfrak{R}):=L^{2}(\Omega)/\mathrm{Im}(\curl),

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{0}(\mathfrak{R}_{0}):=\mathrm{Ker}(\grad),\ \mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathfrak{R}_{0}):=\mathrm{Ker}(\curl)/\mathrm{Im}(\grad),\ \mathrm{H}^{2}(\mathfrak{R}_{0}):=L^{2}(\Omega)/\mathrm{Im}(\curl),

The elements of
\mathrm{H}^{k}(\mathfrak{R})
are called harmonic scalar/vector fields, and the
elements of
\mathrm{H}^{k}(\mathfrak{R}_{0})
are called harmonic scalar/vector fields with zero
trace.
These cohomology spaces are important because, first, they capture the topological
properties of the domain
\Omega
and, second, the solutions to scalar and vector
Laplacians are well-defined up to harmonic fields. The following two paragraphs elaborate on
this structure that underlies the Hilbert de Rham complex.

Due to the Universal Coefficient Theorem
[
Hatcher
, Theorem 3.2]
, there is a one-to-one
correspondence between the cohomology spaces and the so-called Betti numbers of
\Omega
,

\beta_{j}(\Omega)
: the vector space dimension of
\mathrm{H}^{j}(\mathfrak{R})
is equal to the
number of
j
-dimensional holes in
\Omega
. That is,
\dim\mathrm{H}^{0}(\mathfrak{R})=\beta_{0}(\Omega)
is the number of connected components of
\Omega
,

\dim\mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathfrak{R})=\beta_{1}(\Omega)
is the number of holes in
\Omega
, and

\dim\mathrm{H}^{2}(\mathfrak{R})=\beta_{2}(\Omega)=0
is the number of voids (none in the planar
setting since there are no two-dimensional holes in
\Omega
). For the space of
differential forms with homogeneous boundary conditions, due to Lefschetz Duality

[
Hatcher
, Theorem 3.43]
, this above-mentioned correspondence between dimension of
cohomology spaces and Betti numbers is flipped in that
\dim\mathrm{H}^{0}(\mathfrak{R}_{0})=\beta_{2}(\Omega),\dim\mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathfrak{R}_{0})=\beta_{1}(\Omega),

and
\dim\mathrm{H}^{2}(\mathfrak{R})=\beta_{0}(\Omega).
In particular, in this work we will
focus only on simply-connected domains
\Omega
with homogeneous boundary conditions, which
implies that
\mathrm{H}^{0}(\mathfrak{R}_{0})=\emptyset=\mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathfrak{R}_{0})
and

\mathrm{H}^{2}(\mathfrak{R}_{0})\cong\mathbb{R}
.

The second reason why cohomology spaces are important is that harmonic fields in

\mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathfrak{R}_{0})
are the solutions to the homogeneous vector Laplace problem,

\Delta\boldsymbol{v}=0
, and the harmonic fields in
\mathrm{H}^{0}(\mathfrak{R}_{0})
are the
solutions to the homogeneous scalar Laplace problem,
\Delta u=0
, with homogeneous
essential boundary conditions. Given the assumption of simply-connectedness for
\Omega
,
this means that both problems admit only the trivial solution.

2.2
Structure-preserving discretizations

The core principle of FEEC is to preserve the structural connections mentioned above in the
discrete setting. This can be achieved by choosing appropriate finite element spaces for
approximating scalar and vector fields. Failure to preserve the cohomological structure in
the discrete setting can cause, in particular, spurious harmonic fields to appear in the
discrete setting, which can lead to highly inaccurate solutions to scalar and vector Laplace
problems. Examples can be found in
[
Arnold2018
]
, for instance, and also in Section

7
of this paper.

The FEEC recipe to preserve the cohomological structure is ensuring that the cohomology
spaces of the discrete and continuous complexes can be isomorphically identified with maps
that are bounded and commute with the operators defined above. By doing so, we can also
ensure that we do not introduce any spurious harmonic scalar/vector fields, as these are
intimately related with the cohomology spaces. In short, we want to construct a discrete
version,
\mathfrak{R}_{h}
, of the de Rham complex
\mathfrak{R}
(or of its counterpart
with homogeneous boundary conditions,
\mathfrak{R}_{0}
), namely,

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{R}_{h}~:~\mathbb{X}^{0}_{h}(\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{X}^{1}_{h}(\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{X}^{2}_{h}(\Omega)\;,}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\grad}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\curl}

for some appropriate choice of finite element spaces
\mathbb{X}^{j}_{h}(\Omega),\;j\in\{0,1,2\}
, and where
h
is a parameter inversely proportional to the number of
DoFs.

The relevant properties of these finite element spaces have been highlighted in

[
Arnold2009
,
Arnold2018
]
, and we showcase them here briefly for ease of readability. For
a more comprehensive overview of the properties and corresponding results, we refer the
reader to the works just mentioned. First, we require that the finite element spaces
considered have enough similarity with the continuous ones to be able to approximate them
arbitrarily well. To be concrete, we necessitate that

\lim_{h\to 0}\inf_{u_{h}\in\mathbb{X}^{j}_{h}}\|u_{h}-u\|=0,\quad u\in\mathbb{X}^{j},j\in\{0,1,2\}.

The second requisite is that the chosen discrete spaces, together with the operators of the
complex
\mathfrak{R}
, also form a complex, or, in other words,

\grad\mathbb{X}^{0}_{h}\subseteq\mathbb{X}^{1}_{h},\quad\curl\mathbb{X}^{1}_{h}\subseteq\mathbb{X}^{2}_{h},

and the composition of the two operators is zero, as in the continuous case. The final
property we mention here is crucial for the correct behaviour of a discrete solution, and is
the one that establishes a connection between the cohomology spaces of the two complexes.
That is, it should be possible to define a set of bounded projection maps from

\mathfrak{R}
to
\mathfrak{R}_{h}
(or from
\mathfrak{R}_{0}
to
\mathfrak{R}_{h}
),
in such a way that a commuting diagram is formed. By guaranteeing the three properties
listed above, one can ensure an accurate and stable solution of the mixed formulation of
Laplace problems.

It is precisely this choice of appropriate finite element spaces that will be tackled in the
rest of the paper, more specifically, when the finite element spaces are hierarchical
B-spline spaces. Whether bounded commuting projectors exist for hierarchical B-spline
complexes is still an open issue and outside the scope of the present work. We limit
ourselves to characterizing and fixing the kind of refinements that introduce spurious
harmonic forms, and therefore invalidate the existence of such projectors altogether.

3
Spline spaces

In this section, all required notation and definitions will be introduced for the univariate
B-splines, and later extended into tensor product B-splines and, finally, the hierarchical
B-spline de Rham complex. We will simplify the notation used in
[
Evans2018
]
and

[
Shepherd2024
]
to the two-dimensional case.

3.1
Univariate B-splines

In order to define univariate B-splines of polynomial degree
p\geq 1
, we need to
define a knot vector. Namely, for
m\geq 1
, a knot vector
\Xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{m+p+1})
is a sequence of non-decreasing real numbers, or
knots, satisfying

\xi_{1}=\dots=\xi_{p}<\xi_{p+1}\leq\dots\leq\xi_{m+1}<\xi_{m+2}=\dots=\xi_{m+p+1}.

We repeat the first and last knots
p
times, which differs from the usual
choice of
p+1
repetitions, and enforces homogeneous boundary conditions for splines of
degree
p
;
we also assume that no knot is repeated more than
p
times.
To simplify the notation, we will consider that
\xi_{1}=0
and
\xi_{m+p+1}=1

from now on.

We will denote by
Z
the set of knots after removing repetitions, in such a way that

Z=\{\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{z+1}\}
is the largest subset of
\Xi
such that

\zeta_{i}<\zeta_{i+1},\ i\in\{1,\dots,z\}.

This induces a partition of the unit interval, given by the subintervals

\mathcal{I}\coloneqq\left\{(\zeta_{i},\zeta_{i+1}):i\in\{1,\dots,z\}\right\}.

(1)

Using the knot vector
\Xi
we can define two spaces that will be used to establish
discrete complexes.
Let
\mathbb{B}^{0}(\Xi)
be the space of piecewise-polynomial functions of degree

p
and regularity
C^{p-r}
at knots with multiplicity
r
that
vanish at
\xi\in\{0,1\}
.
Similarly, we set
\mathbb{B}^{1}(\Xi)
as the space of piecewise-polynomial functions
of degree
p-1
with
C^{p-r-1}
regularity at knots with multiplicity
r
, and
with no conditions imposed at the boundaries.

With the chosen boundary conditions, the dimension of the space
\mathbb{B}^{j}(\Xi)

is
m+j,j\in\{0,1\}
, and we can define a set of
m+j
basis functions that span it.
As mentioned in the introduction, we will choose to work with the B-splines
B^{j}_{i},i\in\{1,\dots,m+j\},
as the basis for
\mathbb{B}^{j}(\Xi)
, and denoting the set as

\mathcal{B}^{j}(\Xi)
. We refer the reader to
[
Piegl1997
,
Schumaker2007
]
for more
details on the construction of B-splines (e.g. using the Cox-de Boor recursion algorithm).
Nonetheless, we highlight a fact about the support of B-splines that will be used
extensively throughout the paper:

\supp(B^{j}_{i})=(\xi_{i},\xi_{i+p+1-j}),\;j\in\{0,1\}\;.

3.2
Tensor Product B-splines

Now that we have fully characterized univariate B-spline spaces, we can use them to
construct a two-dimensional tensor-product B-spline space. Note that we will restrict the
definitions and notation to these conditions, but the construction is altogether identical
for higher-dimensional spaces.

Moreover, keeping in line with our desire to work with hierarchical spline spaces, we will
also introduce the relevant notation for the levels of a hierarchy. We will use
L
to
refer to the highest non-trivial refinement level and
0\leq\ell\leq L
for a general
level.

Let
\mathcal{B}^{j_{1}}_{\ell}(\Xi_{\ell,1})
and

\mathcal{B}^{j_{2}}_{\ell}(\Xi_{\ell,2})
be two univariate spline spaces under the
assumptions of
Section

3.1
. We denote their associated polynomial
degrees and dimensions as
p_{(\ell,k)}
and
m_{(\ell,k)}+j_{k}
, respectively,
where
j_{k}\in\{0,1\}
and

. We can then define the tensor product space

\mathbb{B}_{\ell}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}\coloneqq\mathbb{B}^{j_{1}}(\Xi_{(\ell,1)})\otimes\mathbb{B}^{j_{2}}(\Xi_{(\ell,2)}).

A basis for this space is given by

\mathcal{B}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\ell}\coloneqq\left\{B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\coloneqq B^{j_{1}}_{i_{1}}(\xi_{1})B^{j_{2}}_{i_{2}}(\xi_{2}):B^{j_{k}}_{i_{k}}\in\mathcal{B}^{j_{k}}_{\ell}(\Xi_{(\ell,k)})\right\}\;.

We have used a bold and upright notation for the two-dimensional multi-indexes

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}
, and will do so throughout the paper. To access their

k
-th entry we respectively write, as above,
j_{k}
and
i_{k}
, and we will denote the

i_{k}
-th knot of the level
\ell

\xi_{i_{k},\ell,k}
. Lastly, we will also use the notation

\lvert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}\rvert\coloneqq\lvert j_{1}\rvert+\lvert j_{2}\rvert
to refer to the 1-norm on vector
spaces.

From this point forward, we will mainly refer to multi-variate basis functions either using
the three indices
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\text{ and }\ell
or omitting all of them
whenever they are not necessary — unless otherwise specified. We are now capable of defining
a set of finite element spaces such that the discrete complex they give is cohomologically
equivalent to the continuous complex. The relevant spaces are

\mathbb{B}^{0}_{\ell}\coloneqq\mathbb{B}_{\ell}^{(0,0)}\;,\qquad\mathbb{B}^{1}_{\ell}\coloneqq\mathbb{B}_{\ell}^{(1,0)}\times\mathbb{B}_{\ell}^{(0,1)}\;,\qquad\mathbb{B}^{2}_{\ell}\coloneqq\mathbb{B}_{\ell}^{(1,1)}\;,

where the multi-indices
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}
have been explicitly shown for clarity.
The discrete complex follows naturally
[
Buffa2010
,
Buffa2011
]

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{B}^{1}_{\ell}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{B}^{2}_{\ell}\;.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\grad}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\curl}

To help ease notation, we will use
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}=(0,0)
throughout the paper. Taking into
account the way in which the
\grad
and
\curl
operators change the polynomial degree
and regularity of functions it becomes clear that

\grad(\mathbb{B}^{0}_{\ell})\subseteq\mathbb{B}^{1}_{\ell}\;,\qquad\curl(\mathbb{B}^{1}_{\ell})\subseteq\mathbb{B}^{2}_{\ell}\;.

Finally, we also define the tensor-product mesh
\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
induced by the two sets
of intervals
\mathcal{I}_{(\ell,1)}
and
\mathcal{I}_{(\ell,2)}
, corresponding
to
\Xi_{(\ell,1)}
and
\Xi_{(\ell,2)}
, respectively. We define the
mesh of level
\ell
as

\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}=\left\{I_{1}\times I_{2}:I_{1}\in\mathcal{I}_{(\ell,1)},I_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{(\ell,2)}\right\},

and we will refer to
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
as an element.

3.3
Hierarchical B-splines

With all the single-level tensor-product spline spaces from

Section

3.2
in hand, we will construct a hierarchical B-spline
space. Our focus is primarily introducing the notation required for the main results of this
work. For more details on hierarchical B-spline spaces, their basis functions, and
hierarchical meshes, we point the interested reader to
[
Bracco2019
,
Buffa2022
]
.

We start by defining a set of nested domains and function spaces defined on them,
assigning each domain and corresponding space to a specific refinement level. For the
spaces, we require that
\mathbb{B}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{0}\subseteq\mathbb{B}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{1}\subseteq\dots\subseteq\mathbb{B}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{L}
for all valid

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}
. Additionally, the nested set of closed domains
\Omega_{\ell}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
need to satisfy

\Omega=:\Omega_{0}\supseteq\Omega_{1}\supseteq\dots\supseteq\Omega_{L}\supseteq\Omega_{L+1}\coloneqq\emptyset\;.

Furthermore, we also introduce the following assumption on how the subdomains that determine
the mesh can be chosen.

Assumption 3.1
.

We assume that
\Omega_{\ell+1}
is given as the union of
supports of B-splines in
\mathcal{B}_{\ell}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}
, that is

\Omega_{\ell+1}\coloneqq\bigcup_{B\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}_{\ell}}\closedsupp{(B)},\ \ell=\{0,\dots,L-1\},

where
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}_{\ell}\subseteq\mathcal{B}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}_{\ell}

is some set of level
\ell
B-splines.

This leads us to defining a hierarchical mesh,
\mathcal{Q}
, as

\mathcal{Q}\coloneqq\bigcup_{\ell\in\{0,\dots,L\}}\left\{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}:Q\subseteq\Omega_{\ell}\wedge Q\not\subseteq\Omega_{\ell+1}\right\}.

(2)

As a consequence of
Assumption

3.1
, all elements of
\mathcal{Q}
are pairwise
disjoint and
\bigcup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\overline{Q}=\Omega
.
Any element
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
is denoted as active.

Consequently, a standard way of selecting a basis
\mathcal{H}_{L}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}
for
hierarchical B-spline spaces uses the following algorithm
[
Giannelli2012
,
Buffa2022
]
:

1.

\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}\coloneqq\mathcal{B}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{0}
.

2.

For
\ell=0,\dots,L-1
, let
\mathcal{H}_{\ell+1}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}\coloneqq\mathcal{H}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\ell+1,A}\cup\mathcal{H}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\ell+1,B}
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\ell+1,A}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{B\in\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}:\supp(B)\not\subseteq\Omega_{\ell+1}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\ell+1,B}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{B\in\mathcal{B}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\ell+1}:\supp(B)\subseteq\Omega_{\ell+1}\right\}\;.

We then set
\mathbb{H}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\ell}\coloneqq\spanop(\mathcal{H}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\ell})
, for any level
0\leq\ell\leq L
.
As we did for elements, we denote a basis function
B
as active whenever
B\in\mathcal{H}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{L}
, and as inactive whenever the previous condition is not
true.

The corresponding hierarchical spaces are, then,

\mathbb{H}^{0}_{\ell}\coloneqq\mathbb{H}_{\ell}^{(0,0)}\;,\qquad\mathbb{H}^{1}_{\ell}\coloneqq\mathbb{H}_{\ell}^{(1,0)}\times\mathbb{H}_{\ell}^{(0,1)}\;,\qquad\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\ell}\coloneqq\mathbb{H}_{\ell}^{(1,1)}\;,

and, for each
\ell=0,\dots,L
, the hierarchical complex is

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{H}_{h}~:~\mathbb{H}^{0}_{\ell}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{H}^{1}_{\ell}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\ell}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\grad}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\curl}

(3)

Remark 3.1
.

Under
Assumption

3.1
, the three spaces in
(
3
) are associated to the same hierarchical mesh. Moreover,
under the same assumption, any active element
Q\in\mathcal{Q}\cap\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
of
level
\ell
, is contained in the support of at least one active function of the same
level.

The basis just defined above for hierarchical spaces can be slightly altered to preserve
partition of unity and to reduce the support of the basis functions through a procedure
called truncation. For this, we introduce the higher-level representation of B-splines.
Namely, given
B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell}\in\mathcal{B}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\ell}
, it is possible to write the basis function as

B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell}\equiv\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\ell+1}}c^{\ell,\ell+1}_{B}(B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell})B,\quad c^{\ell,\ell+1}_{B}(B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell})\in\mathbb{R}

(4)

due to nestedness of the function spaces. Using this property, we can then define the
truncation operator at level
\ell+1
as in
[
Giannelli2012
]

\trunc^{\ell+1}(B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell})\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathcal{B}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\ell+1}:\\
\supp(B)\not\subseteq\Omega_{\ell+1}\end{subarray}}c^{\ell,\ell+1}_{B}(B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell})B.

(5)

The truncation operation described above is linear and, in particular, can be written as a
matrix multiplication, see
[
D'Angella2020
]
for more details.

Using this, the new truncated hierarchical basis is given by

1.

\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}\coloneqq\mathcal{B}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{0}
.

2.

For
\ell=0,\dots,L-1
, let
\mathcal{T}_{\ell+1}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}\coloneqq\mathcal{T}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\ell+1,A}\cup\mathcal{H}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\ell+1,B}
, where

\mathcal{T}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\ell+1,A}\coloneqq\left\{\trunc^{\ell+1}(B):B\in\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}~\wedge~\supp(B)\not\subseteq\Omega_{\ell+1}\right\}\;.

Both the hierarchical basis and the truncated hierarchical basis span the same space

[
Giannelli2012
]
, or in other words,
\spanop(\mathcal{T}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\ell})=\spanop(\mathcal{H}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\ell})=\mathbb{H}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\ell}
for all levels

\ell=0,\dots,L
.
However, the truncated basis is preferable in some instances.
By removing redundant terms of the linear combination in (
4
), in the sense
that some basis functions are already present in the next level, we can reduce the support
of the resulting truncated basis functions, and therefore improve sparsity patterns and
matrix conditioning; for instance of the mass matrices associated to scalar and vector
Laplace problems which are associated to the de Rham complex
[
Buffa2022
]
.
Also, the truncated basis recovers the convex partition of unity property of tensor-product
B-splines.

Given a B-spline
B\in\mathcal{B}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\ell+1}
, from the relation
(
4
) we say that
B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell}\in\mathcal{B}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\ell}
is a parent function of
B
if

c^{\ell,\ell+1}_{B}(B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell})\not=0
. We will
also define the notion of mother of a truncated B-spline
[
Giannelli2013
]
. Given a
truncated basis function
T^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell}\in\mathcal{T}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\ell}
, we say that
B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell}

is the mother of
T^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell}
if

T^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell}=\trunc^{L}\left(\cdots\left(\trunc^{\ell+1}\left(B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell}\right)\right)\right)\;,

(6)

and we denote this relationship as
\mot(T^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell})\coloneqq B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell}
. We will also make use of the trivial
identification
\mot(B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell})=B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell}
for any basis function in

\mathcal{H}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\ell}
. Moreover, the definition given by
(
6
) can easily be extended to inactive basis functions, since
(
5
) is still well-defined at every level.

We mentioned before how spurious harmonic forms can be introduced for arbitrary refinement
domains. The reason for this can be understood by carefully considering the
construction of the hierarchical spaces. The way in which the selection mechanism of
active and inactive basis functions in each level works can, in principle, lead to a discrete
complex with different cohomology spaces than the original one. This can happen because the
coarse basis functions we remove from one level and the finer basis functions we replace
them with in the next level can span spaces with different topological properties. See

[
Shepherd2024
, Fig. 7]
and
[
Evans2018
, Fig. 13]
, and the surrounding discussions,
for some illuminating examples of this phenomenon.

4
Admissibility

In adaptive methods for hierarchical splines, optimal order of convergence is proved by
using the property of admissibility, which limits the difference of level between
interacting functions
[
Buffa2015
,
Buffa2017
]
. We will prove that, under

Assumption

3.1
, admissibility for the first space implies it for all the
spaces of the complex.

Definition 4.1
.

We say that
\mathbb{H}^{j}_{L}
,
j\in\{0,1,2\}
, is
\mathcal{H}
- or

\mathcal{T}
-admissible of class
m\geq 2
if on any element
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
,
the non-vanishing basis functions in
\mathcal{H}^{j}_{L}
or
\mathcal{T}^{j}_{L}
,
respectively, belong to at most
m
successive levels.
Concretely, for
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
and any
B^{j}_{i,\ell},B^{j}_{i^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{H}^{j}_{L}
, or
\mathcal{T}^{j}_{L}
, we have

\left(\supp\left(B^{j}_{i,\ell}\right)\cap Q\neq\emptyset\wedge\supp\left(B^{j}_{i^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}\right)\cap Q\neq\emptyset\right)\implies\lvert\ell-\ell^{\prime}\rvert<m.

By extension, we say that the hierarchical complex (
3
) is

\mathcal{H}
- or
\mathcal{T}
-admissible of class
m
if the corresponding previous
condition holds for every
j\in\{0,1,2\}
.

If there are no restrictions on the number of interacting levels we say
m=\infty
.

The following definition relates a basis function with the ones involved when computing the
differential operators, that we call co-face B-splines. From now on, we will use

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\delta}}_{k}
to denote the two-dimensional index with value
1
at position

k
, and
0
in the other position.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell}\in\mathcal{B}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\ell}
and
B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}^{\prime}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}^{\prime},\ell}\in\mathcal{B}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}^{\prime}}_{\ell}
. We say that

B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell}
is a facet B-spline of

B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}^{\prime}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}^{\prime},\ell}
and, conversely,

B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}^{\prime}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}^{\prime},\ell}
is a co-face B-spline of

B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell}
if, for some
k\in\{1,2\}
,

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\delta}}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}=c\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\delta}}_{k},
with
c\in\{0,1\}
.

We also introduce two auxiliary results that relate their support before and after
truncation.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell}\in\mathcal{B}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\ell}
, and
let
B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}^{\prime}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}^{\prime},\ell}\in\mathcal{B}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}^{\prime}}_{\ell}
be a co-face B-spline of

B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell}
.
Then, it holds that
\supp(B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell})\supseteq\supp(B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}^{\prime}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}^{\prime},\ell}).

Proof.

The result holds because the co-face B-spline
B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}^{\prime}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}^{\prime},\ell}
has equal or lower degree, and its local knot vectors are contained in the
local knot vectors of
B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell}
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
T^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell}\in\mathcal{T}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\ell}
and
B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell}=\mot(T^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell})

B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}^{\prime}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}^{\prime},\ell}\in\mathcal{B}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}^{\prime}}_{\ell}

\supp(T^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell})\supseteq\supp(T^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}^{\prime}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}^{\prime},\ell}).

Proof.

Let us consider an active element

,
with

, where

Let

on

Since

Next, let

5
Exact Meshes

We now move on to define precisely when a hierarchical refinement leads to an inexact
complex, and how it can be altered by refining some additional functions to form an exact
complex that satisfies the sufficient conditions in
[
Shepherd2024
]
. To begin, we
introduce some notation to help the readability of the forthcoming proofs. First, we define
the subset
\mathcal{B}_{\ell,\ell+1}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}\subseteq\mathcal{B}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}_{\ell}
composed of B-splines in
level
\ell
whose support is contained in
\Omega_{\ell+1}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{B}_{\ell,\ell+1}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}\coloneqq\left\{B\in\mathcal{B}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}_{\ell}:\supp(B)\subseteq\Omega_{\ell+1}\right\}\;.

Second, we will simplify the notation of B-spline basis functions, and write

\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}}\coloneqq B^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\ell}
, since all
arguments in the proofs rely only on B-splines in the first space of the de Rham complex and
two consecutive hierarchical levels.

Now, we recall the definitions of chains and shortest chains from
[
Shepherd2024
]
, and
we introduce some particular cases of shortest chains.

Definition 5.1

(Chain)
.

Let
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}},\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\ell,\ell+1}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}
.
We say that there is a chain between
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}}
and
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}

if there exists
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and a sequence of B-splines
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{t}}_{l}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\ell,\ell+1}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}},~l=\{0,\dots,r\}
, such that
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}}=\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{t}}_{0}},\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}=\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{t}}_{r}}
and
|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{t}}_{l}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{t}}_{l-1}|=1

for all
l\in\{1,\dots,r\}
.
We will denote this chain by
C_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}:=\{\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{t}}_{l}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\ell,\ell+1}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}~:~l=0,\dots,r\}
, and we will call
r
the
length of the chain.

Definition 5.2

(Shortest chain)
.

Let
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}},\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\ell,\ell+1}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}
. A chain between

\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}}
and
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}
, as in
Definition

5.1
, is said to
be a shortest chain if we have
r=\sum_{k}\lvert j_{k}-i_{k}\rvert
.

Definition 5.3

(Direction-
k
chain)
.

Let
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}},\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\ell,\ell+1}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}
and
k\in\{1,2\}
be
such that
i_{k^{\prime}}=j_{k^{\prime}}
for
k^{\prime}\not=k
.
We say that there is a direction-
k
chain between
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}}
and

\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}
if
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\ell,\ell+1}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}
for every
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{r}}\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
such that

\min\{i_{k},j_{k}\}<r_{k}<\max\{i_{k},j_{k}\}~\wedge~i_{k^{\prime}}=r_{k^{\prime}}=j_{k^{\prime}}\text{ for }k^{\prime}\neq k.

Definition 5.4

(L-chain)
.

Let
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}},\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\ell,\ell+1}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}
.
We say that there exists an L-chain between them if for some
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\ell,\ell+1}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}
there exists a direction-
k_{1}
chain,
C_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}}
,
between
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}}
and
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}}
and a direction-
k_{2}

chain,
C_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}
, between
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}}
and

\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}
, such that
k_{1}\neq k_{2}
.
The union of the chains
C_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}}
and

C_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}
is called the L-chain between

\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}}
and
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}
, and
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{c}}}
is
called its corner element.

The previous definitions are crucial to the main contribution of this paper,

Algorithm

1
, and in particular L-chains will be our main tool for turning inexact
complexes exact through additional refinement. As it was proved in

[
Shepherd2024
]
, the lack of exactness is related to problematic pairs (see

Definition

5.6
): refined functions that are close to each other, but not connected
through a shortest chain of refined functions. Connecting problematic pairs through an
L-chain fixes the pair, and limits the amount of new potentially problematic pairs.

The next results show that the previous two types of chains are shortest chains.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}},\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\ell,\ell+1}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}
have a direction-
k
chain

C_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}
between them.
Then,
C_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}
is a shortest chain.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{\Delta}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}
and assume w.l.o.g that

\Delta_{k}\geq 0
. Then, the direction-
k
chain
C_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}
is
equal to

\{\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}},\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\delta}}_{k}},\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}+2\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\delta}}_{k}},\dots,\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}\}.

It is clear that this chain satisfies the conditions for being a shortest chain, since
its length is
\lvert\boldsymbol{\Delta}\rvert=j_{k}-i_{k}
.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}},\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\ell,\ell+1}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}
. Then, any L-chain between
them is a shortest chain.

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality that
j_{k}\geq i_{k}
, for
k=1,2
. Since the
L-chain is the union of a direction-
1
and direction-
2
chain, it is easy to see
that its length is
(j_{1}-i_{1})+(j_{2}-i_{2})=\lvert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}\rvert
, and
hence it is a shortest chain.
∎

Remark 5.1
.

We note here that
[
Shepherd2024
, Assumption 3]
is more restrictive due to the
higher-dimensional setting. In the conditions of this paper, pair-wise checks are enough
to ensure exactness of the de Rham complex.

To fix problematic pairs it is necessary to join them by a shortest chain of refined
B-splines. However, additional refinement can lead to new problematic pairs that need to be
checked and resolved recursively. The following results show that L-chains reduce the number
of checks to be performed during this process.

Definition 5.7
.

Let
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\ell,\ell+1}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}

Lemma 5.3

(Problematic sides)
.

Let
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}},\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\ell,\ell+1}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}

\lvert t_{k}-j_{k}\rvert<\lvert i_{k}-j_{k}\rvert

Since

\closedsupp{(\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}})}\cap\closedsupp{(\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}})}\subseteq\closedsupp{(\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{t}}})}\cap\closedsupp{(\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}})}

(\ell+1)
-intersection between
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{t}}}
and
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}

\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{t}}}
and
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}

\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}}
and
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}

Definition 5.8

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}},\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\ell,\ell+1}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}

\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}}

\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}

and

The first statement is trivial and follows from the definition of the interaction box.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}},\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\ell,\ell+1}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}
share a minimal
(\ell+1)
-intersection and have an

between them. Moreover, let

C_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}
and

Proof.

We will prove the result directly, by showing that there is a shortest chain between any
pair of B-splines, one in each L-chain, that share a minimal
(\ell+1)
-intersection.

As such, suppose that
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{p}}}\in C_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}

Remark 5.2
.

Let
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}},\beta_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{j}}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\ell,\ell+1}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}
be a problematic pair. There are
two possible L-chains that can be constructed between the pair — depending on the corner
element chosen. It is always preferable to select the L-chain that leads to the highest
number of resolved B-splines, as this reduces the necessary checks for new problematic
pairs.

In this section, we have shown that L-chains can be used to create exact complexes, by using
them to connect problematic pairs, thus creating a shortest chain between them as required
by the conditions of
Theorem

5.1
. In the next section, we will detail how the
theoretical results of this section can be used to implement an algorithm to identify and
solve problematic pairs through refinement procedures.

6
Algorithms

We will now describe a set of algorithms that can be easily incorporated into standard
numerical solvers, and that ensure that the resulting discrete de Rham complex remains exact
after local refinement.

The main algorithm is
Algorithm

1
, which takes as input a hierarchical space with
no problematic pairs, described by its basis
\mathcal{T}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}_{L}
, and a set of
elements marked for refinement, with the implicit assumption that both

\mathcal{T}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}_{L}
and the marked elements respect

Assumption

3.1
. The algorithm returns an updated basis

\mathcal{T}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}_{L_{*}}
corresponding to an exact complex, where we note that

L_{*}
is either
L
or
L+1
, depending on the marked elements. From an implementation
standpoint, we consider that the objects
\mathcal{T}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}_{L}
and

\mathcal{T}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}_{L_{*}}
have information about the full single-level
tensor-product basis at each level, as well as the corresponding hierarchical mesh.

At every level, this algorithm performs the following steps. We first refine the marked
elements given as input, and from those we create the set of possibly problematic pairs that
need to be checked, this will be detailed in
Algorithm

2
. We then enter an
iterative
while
loop to remove the problematic pairs. For each problematic pair we
create a shortest chain in the form of an L-chain, selected according to

Remark

5.2
using the method
get_lchain_corner
, and then refine the
corresponding corner functions for all the selected L-chains. Since the refinement of corner
functions may cause the appearance of new problematic pairs, we generate a new set of pairs
to be checked at the next step of the
while
loop, exploiting

Lemma

5.4
to consider only those pairs which contain at least one of the
refined corner functions. At the end of the loop there are no problematic pairs at the
current level, but
Assumption

3.1
may not be satisfied if the support of
the refined corner functions is not contained in
\Omega_{\ell-1}
. To repair this,
we loop over the newly refined corners to check their support, and if the condition is
violated we use the method
get_a_parent_func
to choose a parent function that will
add the least amount of refinement, and modify the marked elements of the previous level to
ensure that its support will be refined. Since the refinement of the parent function may
again violate
Assumption

3.1
in previous levels, we also store it to check
its support at the next step. Finally, after looping through all levels we update the
hierarchical basis using the new refined mesh.

Theorem 6.2
.

Given a hierarchical basis
\mathcal{T}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}_{L}

and without problematic pairs,

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{T}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}_{L}
and

be the inputs to

1
.
Since the outermost

loop in the algorithm runs from

to
0
, it
obviously terminates.
Therefore, we need to show the following:

1.

The

loop terminates and fixes all problems at level
\ell
.

2.

The
level_corners

Assumption

3.1
.

Let us consider the iteration at some level
\ell\in\{0,\dots,L\}
. The first set
of possibly problematic pairs is given by
Algorithm

2
. We start by
discarding the resolved functions using

5.3
, since fixing all
the pairs with these functions will solve the problems with the resolved ones as well.
Also, given our assumption that
\mathcal{T}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}_{L}
is initially without
problematic pairs, every possible new problematic pair must contain at least of function
whose support’s closure intersects
\texttt{marked\_els}[\ell]
— which contain all the
input marked elements and parents from the previous step.

Inside the

loop, we will check every pair in

using
the method

. The first part checks if a minimal
(\ell+1)
-intersection is present and
the second checks if a shortest chain exists using

5.5
. If a given

5.2
, by adding an L-chain between the pair, and storing its corner function, which is enough to refine the L-chain due to proximity of functions in
Definition

5.5
and the support of B-splines. We
also set

to

, since every added L-chain might
introduce new problems. After fixing all problems in
unchecked_pairs
, we refine
the mesh with all the stored corners, and create the new set of

.
Since only the recently added corners can introduce new problematic pairs, we again call

on

. Thus, we fix every problem at level

\ell
. To see that the

has no problems, then

will stay as

and the loop will finish. It is also guaranteed that eventually no more
problematic pairs will exist — the worst case scenario being a tensor-product refinement
of the current level.

Now, we proceed to show that the final mesh satisfies

3.1
.

Numerical Results

In order to show that the theory holds numerically, we have devised some tests for the
non-homogeneous vector Laplace problem and the Maxwell eigenvalue problem. We focus on these
tests since the homogeneous boundary conditions that arise in the weak formulations of these
problems, both essential and natural, imply the non-existence of harmonic scalar fields in
simply connected domains.

For all the ensuing examples, we chose to work with
h

results of

p
-refinement schemes. Moreover, we will use the same polynomial degree in
every direction. Thus, from now on we will write
p_{(\ell,k)}=p

7.1
Vector Laplace problem

Let the domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the unit square
[0,1]^{2}
. Since the
domain is simply connected, the continuous de Rham complex has no harmonic vector fields,
and as such, neither should our discretized complex. Considering the mixed weak formulation
of the vector Laplace problem with natural, homogeneous boundary conditions, the relevant
objects we wish to find are
\sigma\in H^{1}(\Omega)
and
\boldsymbol{u}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}(\curl;\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle\langle\sigma,\tau\rangle-\langle\boldsymbol{u},\grad\tau\rangle

\displaystyle=0,\quad

\displaystyle\forall\tau\in H^{1}(\Omega),

\displaystyle\langle\grad\sigma,\boldsymbol{v}\rangle+\langle\curl\boldsymbol{u},\curl\boldsymbol{v}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle,\quad

\displaystyle\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}(\curl;\Omega).

Since the mixed weak-formulation has no boundary conditions imposed, we will use the
notation

\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\in\tilde{\mathbb{H}}^{1}_{L}

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{h},\tau_{h}\rangle-\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\grad\tau_{h}\rangle

\displaystyle=0,\quad

\displaystyle\forall\tau_{h}\in\tilde{\mathbb{H}}^{0}_{L}\;,

\displaystyle\langle\grad\sigma_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle+\langle\curl\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\curl\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle\;,\quad

\displaystyle\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\tilde{\mathbb{H}}^{1}_{L},
