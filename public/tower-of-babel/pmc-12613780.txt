Surgical apgar score and delayed neurocognitive recovery in elderly patients: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Maoqiu Wan, Jianlan Ren, Lu Xiao, Yanshu Liu, Ling Wang, Daiying Zhang, Qingyan Liu, Lingyu Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDelayed neurocognitive recovery (dNCR) is a prevalent postoperative complication in elderly patients. Currently, there is no effective treatment for it. Consequently, timely risk identification and prevention of it are crucial.ObjectiveThe aim of our study is to provide a clinical tool for the prediction of dNCR by exploring the relationship between the surgical Apgar score (SAS) and dNCR in elderly patients and investigating its predictive value.MethodsThe elderly patients undergoing general anesthesia at Southwest Medical University Hospital from January 2024 to May 2025 were included in this study. Cognitive function was tested by the MoCA scale before surgery, postoperative day 7 and postoperative day 30. The dNCR and non-dNCR groups were classified according to whether dNCR occurred after surgery. SAS were calculated as the lowest mean arterial pressure (LMAP), lowest heart rate (LHR) and blood loss. The relationship between dNCR and SAS was analyzed, we drew the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve of SAS-predicted dNCR, and calculated the area under the curve, the optimal cut-off, sensitivity and specificity.ResultsThe incidence of dNCR was 23.86%, including 15.7% at 7 days, 19.6% at 30 days, and 14.7% had dNCR both at 7 and 30 days after surgery. Logistic regression analysis showed SAS (OR = 6.326, P = 0.001), Pittsburgh Sleep Index (OR = 3.834, P < 0.001), frailty (OR = 3.388, P = 0.027) and anxiety (OR = 3.520, P = 0.002) were risk factors for dNCR. The AUC value of dNCR ROC curve was 0.876 (95% CI: 0.794–0.948, P < 0.001). When taking 8 as the optimal cut-off, the sensitivity is 0.630 and the specificity is 0.893.ConclusionThis study demonstrated that low SAS is a risk factor for dNCR; and SAS score < 8 is classified as high-risk population. It provides a simple and objective prediction tool for dNCR, not only enabling medical staff to identify patients at risk and take early interventions, but also to support their goal-directed management decisions for the intraoperative circulatory system. Furthermore, we also found that sleep, frailty and anxiety are also influencing factors of dNCR.Trial registrationChina Clinical Experiment Center (ChiCTR2300078388), December 7, 2023.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03295-9.

Introduction

The cognitive functions of patients will change after anesthesia and surgery, especially in the elderly. Moreover, its effects extend beyond the anticipated pharmacological and physiological consequences of anaesthetic drugs. The acute effects of medication, anaesthesia, pain, sleep, nutrition and emotional stress during hospitalisation are usually restored within 30 days. Persistent cognitive impairment occurring between 7 and 30 days after surgery is defined as delayed neurocognitive recovery (dNCR) [1]. It is one of the most common postoperative neurological complications in elderly patients and presents as cognitive decline, deficits in memory, attention, concentration, information processing and executive function.

dNCR belongs to the anterior stage of neurocognitive impairment, with incidence rates of 16.88%−50% [2–5], which increases the incidence of postoperative complications and prolongs recovery time. Furthermore, surgical patients lack cognitive resilience in the face of perioperative stress, which has an adverse effects on the quality of life and work ability, and increases the risk of long-term cognitive decline and death after surgery [3, 6]. Early identification of dNCR risk and subsequent implementation of intervention measures has the potential to mitigate cognitive deterioration.

Studies show that frailty, cognitive reserve, education, age, diabetes, sleep, and anesthesia are risk factors for postoperative cognitive impairment in elderly patients ([7–11]. Moreover, the occurrence of postoperative cognitive impairment is closely related to the intraoperative status of the patients, including blood loss, blood transfusion, perfusion, hypothermia ([2], etc. Some studies have shown that dNCR was related to intraoperative cerebral oxygen saturation ([12] and intraoperative center rate variation [13]. These two methods might be able to predict dNCR. There are also studies predicting dNCR using machine learning methods based on the features of multiple cognitive-related brain networks, and resting-state fMRI non-invasively detects spontaneous activity in brain regions and the functional integrity of brain networks ([14] in the absence of any task or stimulation. However, the detection and acquisition of the aforementioned data is inconvenient, and the cost is expensive, and there is a lack of simple and convenient prediction methods.

Surgical Apgar score (SAS) is a scoring system for predicting postoperative complications and mortality, provides a detailed assessment of the clinical and biological status. It is calculated from the scores of three parameters: lowest heart rate (LHR), lowest mean arterial pressure (LMAP) and blood loss, which quantifies intraoperative parameters, reflects intraoperative stress, and provides predictive information for surgical outcome and prognosis [15–17]. It is widely used in surgical systems, including predicting septic shock, respiratory failure and death risk after emergency exploratory laparotomy [15], acute cardiac injury after noncardiac surgery [18], gynecological complications 30 days after robotic surgery and its severity of rectal cancer [19, 20], etc. The SAS provides metrics for assessing the effectiveness of safety interventions in the operating room.

The aetiology of dNCR remains unclear, it is categorised as a form of perioperative neurocognitive impairment and is associated with comparable risk factors and pathophysiological changes to postoperative delirium. The study by Liu Xiang et al. [21] demonstrated that SAS can predict delirium after pancreaticoduodenectomy. Nevertheless, it is unclear whether the SAS is a sensitive predictor of dNCR. The present study aims to investigate the value of SAS in predicting dNCR in elderly surgical patients and to provide proposed decision support tools for the prevention of dNCR.

Methods

Patients

This study is a prospective cohort study, using convenience sampling to select elderly patients undergoing general anesthesia in Southwest Medical University Hospital from January 2024 to May 2025. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University (KY2023378), all subjects provided written informed consent. and registered on the Platform of China Clinical Experiment Center (ChiCTR2300078388), December 7, 2023. Our study adheres to STROBE guidelines.

Inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥ 60; (2) elective surgery with time of ≥ 2 h; (3) no severe hearing and visual impairment, ability to read. (4) Hospital stay of ≥ 3 days. Exclusion criteria: (1) serious disease of the cardiovascular, respiratory, hepatic, renal, or central nervous system, End-stage disease with life expectancy less than 6 months (metastatic cancer, multiple organ failure, or chemotherapy); (2) having a history of cognitive impairment, dementia, or mental illness, such as severe depression, severe anxiety, autism, schizophrenia, etc.;(3) MOCA < 26; (4) Current use of sedatives, antidepressants, or corticosteroids; (5) alcoholism and drug dependence; (6) Have participated in similar studies; (7) difficulty in follow-up or poor compliance; (8) Uncontrolled hypertension (more than 180/100 mmHg); (9) history of general anesthesia surgery or cerebrovascular accident within 6 months; (10) Re-operation within 30 days; (11) due to severe cognitive impairment, language disorder, and refusal to cooperate, it was impossible to conduct the cognitive assessment. We followed the established research protocol to ensure the quality of the data.

Calculation of sample size

It is reported that the incidence of delayed recovery of neurocognitive function after surgery in the elderly ranges from 16.88% to 25% [1, 2, 22]. Based on this, it can be calculated that at least a sample size of 179–288 cases is required. Considering a 20% dropout rate, the final number is 215–346 cases. Anesthesia and perioperative management

All patients were under static aspiration compound general anesthesia and received the same anesthesia protocol. (1) Induction of anesthesia: midazolam 0.04 mg·kg−1, propofol 2 mg·kg།1, sufentanyl 0.5 µg·kg།1, and cis-atracurium 0.15 mg·kg།1.(2) Anesthesia maintenance: intraoperative sevoflurane (concentration 2% ~ 4%), remifentanil 0.1 µg·kg།1·min།1 continuous intravenous infusion, PETCO2 35 ~ 45 mmHg, airway pressure < 20 cmH₂O. (3) End of anesthesia: after the operation, the surgical incision was locally infiltrated with 5 ml of ropivacaine. Intravenous infusion of neostigmine 30 µg·kg།1antagonized muscle residual effect and atropine 10 µg kg།1reduced bradycardia. Sufentanil was used as a postoperative pain protocol via a patient-controlled analgesic pump (100 mg sufentanil with 100 ml of saline for 2 days). Additional intraoperative medication was recorded. Cases with significant deviations from the plan, such as those requiring large amounts of blood transfusion due to severe bleeding or the use of multiple vasoactive drugs, will be excluded.

Clinical data collection

The age, gender, BMI, smoking history, alcohol history, pain, education, residence status (living alone, living with spouse or child), disease history (hypertension, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cardiovascular disease, anemia, hypoproteinemia), vital signs (respiratory, pulse, blood pressure, heart rate) were collected preoperative. The activity of daily living scale was used to assess the patients’ ability to carry out daily activities; the Nutritional risk screening(2002) was used to assess the nutritional status; the hospital anxiety and depression scale was used to assess the anxiety and depression status; frailty status was assessed using the FRAIL scale; the Pittsburgh sleep index count table was used to assess the sleep status.

After surgery, anesthesia and surgical information were obtained. Including American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status, intraoperative medication, anesthesia duration, surgery duration, hypothermia (< 36℃), blood transfusion, blood loss, hypoxemia (SpO2<90% for 5 min), hospital stay, heart rate, blood pressure, mean arterial pressure, ICU admission, postoperative infection, and the pain score was evaluated using Visual analogue scale (VAS) on a daily basis 7 days after the surgery. Patients who were not available for follow-up and evaluation due to various reasons (e.g. patient refusal, coma, etc.) were excluded from the study.

Surgical Apgar score

The surgical apgar score (SAS) is a significant tool for predicting postoperative complications and evaluating surgical outcomes. In accordance with the scoring criteria proposed by Gawande et al. [23], the SAS is calculated as the sum of the three scores for intraoperative blood loss, lowest mean arterial pressure (LMAP) (MAP = diastolic pressure + 1/3 pulse pressure difference) and lowest heart rate (LHR), reflecting the hemodynamics and circulation conditions during the operation, which can be extracted from anesthesia records (Table 1).

Delayed neurocognitive recovery (dNCR) assessment

Cognitive function was assessed by two professionally trained and clinically qualified doctors using MOCA before surgery, 7 d and 30 d after surgery. Preoperative baseline assessment was carried out by using the MoCA Beijing version. To avoid the learning effect, the MoCA was tested in Mandarin version 7.2 (MoCA-8.2-english-test translation) was used on postoperative day 7, and Mandarin version 7.3 (MoCA-8.3-english-test translation) on postoperative day 30. Based on previous studies and consensus of International Nomenclature Consensus Group [1], dNCR was defined as the Z score (Z score means the difference from the baseline mean was divided by the baseline standard deviation, at least two Z scores for each individual test, or composite Z score) of 7 or 3 days after surgery ≥ 1.96 in this study.

We adopted an assessor-blind method, where the assessors remained blinded to the intraoperative grouping and specific conditions of all patients. We appointed independent blinded assessors: members of the research team who were not involved in the intraoperative management of the patients and were unaware of their specific intraoperative data were specifically responsible for postoperative follow-up and cognitive assessment. Data isolation was implemented: the assessors were unaware of the basic information such as the patient’s name and department, ensuring that the assessors could not access the patient’s anesthesia record sheet or the intraoperative data part in the electronic medical record system. To ensure the consistency of all assessors’ scores, we formally evaluated the inter-rater reliability among the assessors, and the results showed that the inter-rater reliability of scoring items demonstrated consistency (ICC = 0.975; κ = 0.928).

Postoperative delirium assessment

Postoperative delirium (POD) was assessed by a professionally trained and clinically qualified nurse twice daily during hospitalization using the CAM scale (8:00 and 20:00), until 7 days after surgery or discharge. Diagnostic criteria for CAM: ① acute onset, disease fluctuation; ② inattention; ③ thinking disorder; ④ consciousness level change. If the patient has characteristics ① and ② and meets one of ③ or ④, the diagnosis is postoperative delusion. Patients with preexisting delirium were excluded.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out using SPSS23.0. Normally distributed measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s), between-group comparison by one-way analysis of variance, skewed distribution by median (interquartile spacing) [M (Q)], and rank sum test between groups. Count data were presented as frequencies and percentages, and analyzed using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. The factors with a P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were included in the binary logistic regression analysis to determine the independent risk factors for postoperative dNCR. We used binary logistic regression analysis to explore the relationship between SAS and dNCR. The receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) was drawn, the area under the curve and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated, and the Youden index (Youden index = sensitivity + specificity-1) was calculated. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was used to evaluate the fitting degree of the prediction model, and the Bootstrap resampling method was adopted for internal validation. The sampling was repeated 1000 times, and the optimism of the model’s discrimination power (C statistic) was calculated, and the optimism-corrected C statistic was obtained accordingly. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Missing Data Handling: In this study, 11 patients had their main outcome (postoperative MoCA score) data missing due to being unable to complete the cognitive test after surgery. We performed multiple imputation for the missing postoperative MoCA scores. Using the mice package 3.14.0 in R software 4.2.1, 10 imputed data sets were created. The interpolation model incorporates variables that may be related to the missing mechanism or the research outcome. such as age, education, disease history (hypertension, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cardiovascular disease, anemia, hypoproteinemia), vital signs, BMI, SAS, diabetes, hypertension, anemia, frailty status, The Pittsburgh Sleep Index, anxious, hypothermia, delirium, ICU admission, anesthesia and surgical information, the Predictive Mean Matching algorithm was used for interpolation. After imputation, the same Binary logistic regression model was run on each data set, and the results were combined using the Rubin rule.

Result

Patient characteristics

346 subjects were recruited, 317 underwent surgery, and 306 were included in the analysis (There were 2 cases of postoperative coma, 6 cases of incomplete follow-up within 30 days after surgery, 1 case of unplanned reoperation, and 2 cases of inability to complete the cognitive test after surgery), see Fig. 1. Among the respondents, 166 (54.24%) were males and 140 (45.76%) were females. Age 60 ~ 96 years old, Mean age was 71.29 ± 6.79, The types of surgery include hepatobiliary surgery (31), bone and joint surgery (72), spinal surgery (50), urology (26), gastrointestinal surgery (99), thoracic surgery (28). The incidence of dNCR was 23.86%(73), the incidence of dNCR 7 days after surgery was 15.7% (48); The incidence of dNCR 30 days after surgery was 19.6%(60); 14.7% (45) of the patients presented with persistent dNCR at both 7 days and 30 days after surgery. Univariate analysis showed statistically significant differences between the dNCR and non-dNCR groups in BMI, Pittsburgh sleep index, SAS, diabetes, hypertension, anemia, frailty, anxiety, hypothermia, ICU admission and postoperative delirium (Table 2).

Clinical data

Logistic regression analysis

The occurrence of dNCR or not was taken as the dependent variable (assignment: yes = 1, no = 0), the statistically significant variables in univariate analysis (BMI, SAS, diabetes, hypertension, anemia, frailty status, The Pittsburgh Sleep Index, anxious, hypothermia, delirium, ICU admission) were set as independent variables to establish a Logistic regression model for multivariate analysis. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test result shows that the model has good calibration (χ² = 3.294, P = 0.915), indicating that there is no significant difference between the predicted probabilities and the observed probabilities of the model. After controlling for confounding factors, SAS remained independently correlated with the occurrence of dNCR. A one-point reduction in SAS per plus indicated a 6.326-fold increased risk of dNCR (OR = 6.326, P = 0.001). Discuss

This study investigates the association between SAS and dNCR. SAS demonstrates good predictive performance and clinical value for dNCR and can provide a basis for the prevention of postoperative dNCR.

The prevalence of dNCR

This study found that the incidence of dNCR was 23.86%, which was higher than previous studies [2, 3, 22]. It is possible that the population in this study consists of elderly patients with a surgery time of ≥ 2 h, who have significant cognitive fragility and poor resilience of the brain in facing surgery and anesthesia stress. And results differ when using different assessment tools. The incidence of dNCR at 7 days after surgery was 15.7%; The incidence of dNCR at 30 days after surgery was 19.6%; 14.7% of the patients presented with persistent dNCR at both 7 days and 30 days after surgery. It suggests that even after recovery from anesthesia and surgical stress trauma, the postoperative cognition of some elderly patients still demonstrates a continuous decline or impairment. If the at-risk population cannot be identified early and intervention measures are not taken, it may result in cognitive deterioration, evolve into postoperative neurocognitive impairment, and affect long-term cognitive health.

Effect of surgical Apgar score on dNCR

The results of this study indicate that SAS is an independent risk factor for dNCR, and 1 reduction in SAS score increased the risk of dNCR by 6.326 times (OR = 6.326, P = 0.001), and the area under the ROC curve is 0.876, indicating a good predictive value. SAS is calculated based on blood loss, intraoperative lowest heart rate and lowest MAP, which reflect intraoperative hemodynamic stability, is an objective and direct predictor of intraoperative tissue perfusion; the rapid heart rate and low MAP also reflect the instability of the circulatory system, such as the early manifestations of shock caused by blood loss or infection. By calculating SAS, the physiological state of the patient during surgery can be easily assessed [24]. SAS is capable of predicting postoperative complications such as organ dysfunction, cardiovascular problems, metabolic disorders, and sepsis [25]. These factors may affect patients’ cognitive health to different degrees.

Studies in various fields of surgery suggest that a lower SAS is associated with a higher risk of postoperative complications and a poorer prognosis [26]. A low SAS score indicates significant blood loss, a low MAP, and rapid heart rate. Heavy bleeding and low mean arterial depression imply poor cardiovascular condition and systemic organ perfusion function. The brain is an important target organ of the circulatory system and is sensitive to ischemia. This damage may be aggravated by anesthesia and surgery. On the other hand, it has been demonstrated that blood loss leading to poor peripheral circulation and hypothermia, a body temperature lower than 35℃ for 30 min or more, is an important risk factor for perioperative neurocognitive impairment [2]. Intraoperative hemodynamic instability can lead to impairment of the brain’s automatic blood flow regulation function. A healthy brain maintains a stable cerebral blood flow (CBF) within a certain mean arterial pressure (MAP) range (typically around 60–150 mmHg) through an automatic regulation mechanism. When the intraoperative blood pressure drops below the individual’s tolerance threshold, CBF will linearly decrease, resulting in insufficient cerebral perfusion [27]. Inflammation and hypoxia can manifest as an increased heart rate, inflammatory stimulation and insufficient oxygen supply are significant factors contributing to brain nerve damage and are prone to trigger dNCR [9, 28]. Low perfusion leads to a reduction in the delivery of oxygen and glucose, and an increase in heart rate. This causes an imbalance between oxygen supply and demand in the brain during anesthesia, which can lead to neuronal energy depletion (ATP depletion), dysfunction of ion pumps, and subsequently trigger a series of subsequent cellular cascades [9, 28]. Insufficient cerebral perfusion and fluctuations in blood pressure not only affect neurons but also severely impact the blood-brain barrier, causing the damaged BBB to allow inflammatory cells (such as white blood cells) and pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) from the blood to enter the brain parenchyma, triggering neuroinflammatory responses [9, 28].

From an additional perspective, the analysis of SAS predictors indicates that reducing intraoperative bleeding and maintaining intraoperative circulatory stability are crucial for preventing dNCR, which can be accomplished by enhancing the techniques of the surgeon and anesthesiologist, and precision anesthesia and goal-oriented management were implemented. Furthermore, individualized blood pressure management (setting targets based on the patient’s baseline blood pressure) is of utmost importance. Even transient hemodynamic instability should be actively avoided and corrected because it may trigger a persistent inflammatory response. Future research may focus on protective strategies targeting these mechanisms, such as using anesthetic drugs with anti-inflammatory effects, using neuroprotective drugs or anti-inflammatory agents during the perioperative period, etc.

Previous studies have found that SAS < 6 was a good predictor of postoperative delirium [21] or complications within 30 days after surgery [29]. POD is often regarded as a type of “acute brain failure”, while dNCR is more like a delayed recovery after “mild traumatic brain injury”. The mechanisms of the two overlap (such as inflammation, insufficient perfusion), but the severity and duration may be different. dNCR may involve more substantial neural network damage. Zhang Yingxin et al. [30] have clearly pointed out that a SAS score of ≤ 6 has statistical significance in predicting postoperative delirium after major open abdominal surgery. SAS, as a common risk factor, suggests that intraoperative hemodynamic instability may be a common and modifiable “upstream” initiating factor for them. It may trigger acute delirium first, then affect long-term recovery; or it may directly cause subtle brain damage, manifested as dNCR.

However, the difference in our study is that SAS < 8 was identified as a high-risk population. It might be because the patients in this study were elderly and the operation time was ≥ 2 h. A lower SAS score indicates worse intraoperative circulatory and perfusion conditions. The population in this study was older and more likely to have underlying diseases, which reduced their compensatory ability for circulatory stress changes in the brain. Combined with a longer operation time, the risk of brain damage further increased, making them more sensitive to circulatory changes. Even with a SAS score of < 8, the risk of dNCR increases. The brain is sensitive to circulatory and perfusion changes, especially in the aging brain, which has a reduced ability to regulate changes in ischemic hypoxia [21, 30]. When managing such populations, we need to dynamically assess the SAS score values at different time points during the operation.

Moreover, high-risk groups should undergo multidisciplinary and multi-dimensional postoperative management to reduce dNCR. For high-risk patients with a SAS score of < 8, a structured multimodal intervention package should be initiated. This covers the entire perioperative period, including: cognitive and physiological optimization, fine management of hemodynamics guided by cerebral oxygen monitoring during the operation, multimodal analgesia, and active screening for delirium and cognitive impairment and non-pharmacological prevention strategies after the operation. Future studies should prospectively validate the effectiveness of this ‘prediction-intervention’ model in improving the neurological cognitive prognosis of patients. For patients known to be at high risk (such as advanced age and multiple comorbidities), even if they have not been scored yet, cognitive pre-rehabilitation should be considered.

The advantage of the SAS score lies in its ability to reflect the final overall outcome (i.e., the lowest mean arterial pressure, the lowest heart rate, and the total blood loss), regardless of the specific intervention measures taken to achieve this outcome. Therefore, as a comprehensive indicator, it has good applicability and predictive value in the current clinical environment. Implementing screening based on SAS requires almost no additional hardware investment, as all its data (the lowest MAP during surgery, the lowest heart rate, and the estimated blood loss) are items routinely monitored and recorded by modern anesthesia workstations. The main costs will come from the initial staff training and the optimization of the electronic medical record system for integrating the automatic calculation function of SAS.

The influencing factors of dNCR

In addition to SAS, this study also found that the Pittsburgh sleep index, frailty, and anxiety were also influencing factors of dNCR. Every 1-point increase in the Pittsburgh sleep index indicated a 3.834-fold increase in dNCR risk. Good sleep contributes to synaptic remodeling and memory consolidation, while poor sleep quality aggravates neuroinflammation and neuronal damage. The correlation between sleep and dNCR may be mediated by neuroimmunity and neuroinflammation [10]. Long-term poor sleep quality increases the risk of dNCR.

This study also found that preoperative anxiety was an independent influence of dNCR in elderly patients, and the odds of dNCR in anxious patients was 3.520 times that of non-anxious patients. In cognitively healthy older men, mild anxiety symptoms may potentially predict future decline in executive function [31], and the degree of verbal memory decline [32] associated with increased anxiety symptoms in adults aged 65 years and older. However, the study by Chen Zhengwen et al. [33] showed that dNCR was associated with preoperative depression but not with anxiety. A 12-year follow-up study found that anxiety symptoms were not associated with the risk of cognitive decline in a sample of cognitively healthy men and women aged 60 years and older [34]. However, under the stress of surgery and anesthesia, preoperative anxiety may aggravate the postoperative neurocognitive degeneration. Further research is necessary to elucidate the relationship between these two factors.

Frailty is strongly correlated with early postoperative cognition and is marginally related to long-term cognitive dysfunction [35, 36]. Elderly people typically have both physical frailty and cognitive impairment, and cognitive frailty is an independent risk factor in the prediction model established by Jingya Zhang et al. [37] for dNCR in older adults. Our study merely investigated the effects of somatic frailty on postoperative cognition and found that the odds of dNCR in frailty patients were 3.338 times those of non-frailty patients. Perhaps, when facing the same surgical stress trauma, cognitive reserve is less resilient in frail patients, and dNCR is more likely to occur.

Limitations and prospects

First, this is a single-center study with a restricted number of included cases. Different institutions may employ distinct surgical techniques, anesthesia protocols, and serve diverse patient populations. These factors can influence the SAS scores and cognitive outcomes, thereby reducing the generalizability of the research findings. Additionally, the convenience sampling method may introduce selection bias.

Second, although we have determined that SAS can predict dNCR, the sample size of this study limits its statistical power. For smaller effect sizes or more detailed subgroup analyses, the power may be inadequate. This study excluded 3.5% of patients who were unable to complete the cognitive test. These patients might have had more severe cognitive impairments, and their exclusion may have led to an underestimation of the incidence of dNCR.

This study only performed cognitive assessments on the 7th and 30th days after surgery. This may not have captured the earlier dynamic fluctuations in cognitive status or the longer-term recovery process. Future studies with more frequent assessment time points will contribute to a better portrayal of the overall cognitive recovery landscape. Furthermore, although we adjusted for known clinical confounding factors in the multivariate analysis, the possibility of residual confounding, particularly from unmeasured factors such as cognitive reserve or surgical complexity, cannot be excluded. To further validate the predictive value of SAS for dNCR, large-scale… e samples of multicenter data are needed.

Conclusion

The SAS integrates the components of patient susceptibility, surgical complexity, and surgical performance, providing measures of immediate postoperative status and outcome. It quantitatively scores through three key physiological indicators (blood loss, lowest mean arterial pressure, lowest heart rate) during the operation, and reflects the hemodynamics and circulation conditions during the operation.

This study demonstrated that low SAS is a risk factor for dNCR, especially SAS score < 8 is classified as high-risk population; it provides a simple and objective prediction tool for dNCR, not only enabling medical staff to identify patients at risk and take early interventions, but also to support their goal-directed management decisions for the intraoperative circulatory system. Furthermore, we also found that sleep, frailty and anxiety are also influencing factors of dNCR.
