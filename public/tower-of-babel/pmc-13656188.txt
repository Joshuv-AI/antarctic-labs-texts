Matrix Metalloproteinase‐8 Deficiency Alters the Balance Between Viral Clearance and Immunopathology in H1N1 Infection
Authors: Xiaoyun Wang, Joselyn Rojas‐Quintero, Duo Zhang, Joaquin Zuñiga, Caroline A. Owen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMatrix metalloproteinase‐8 (MMP‐8) regulates inflammatory responses, but its contributions to the pathogenesis of influenza A viral (IAV) disease are not known. Plasma MMP‐8 levels were increased 30‐fold in subjects infected with H1N1 or seasonal IAV and were inversely associated with the PaO2/FiO2 ratio, suggesting potential value as a candidate marker of lung injury severity that requires validation in larger cohorts. MMP‐8 levels were elevated in the lungs of H1N1‐infected wild‐type (WT) mice and localized to airway epithelial cells, macrophages, and polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs). H1N1‐infected Mmp‐8 −/− mice exhibited lower mortality and reduced lung viral burdens, but developed more severe lung injury than WT mice. Gene expression profiling revealed enhanced necroptosis and an exaggerated immune response in the lungs of infected Mmp‐8 −/− mice. Infected Mmp‐8 −/− mice had increased airway epithelial necroptosis with higher pulmonary levels of Type I interferons (IFNs), and increased frequencies of Class II MHC molecule‐expressing macrophages and CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, and higher levels of some Th1 cytokines. In vitro, MMP‐8 cleaved IL‐1β and IL‐6, but not Type I IFNs. These findings suggest that Mmp‐8 deficiency is associated with restricted influenza virus spread and increased lung injury, accompanied by enhanced Type I IFN‐associated necroptotic signaling and augmented antiviral immune responses. The latter may partly involve loss of MMP‐8‐mediated processing of pro‐inflammatory mediators. MMP‐8 may represent a host‐response target in severe influenza infection, although therapeutic strategies targeting MMP‐8 will need to balance enhanced viral clearance against the risk of increased lung immunopathology.

Introduction

Influenza A viral (IAV) infections are associated with high morbidity and mortality [1]. Complications associated with IAV, including secondary bacterial pneumonia and cytokine storm, can lead to multi‐organ failure and increased mortality [1]. Although vaccines are developed annually for emerging IAV strains, not all subjects are vaccinated or mount robust protective immune responses to the vaccines [2]. Antiviral drugs (such as oseltamivir, which inhibits viral neuraminidase) are not fully effective in treating IAV infections or reducing IAV‐associated mortality, especially when there is a delay in initiating therapy [3, 4]. Thus, there is an urgent need to identify new targets to develop more effective therapies for IAV infections, including therapies directed against host factors that promote the development of more severe disease.

Matrix metalloproteinase‐8 (MMP‐8) belongs to the structurally related family of Zn2+‐dependent MMPs, which regulate inflammation [5, 6, 7]. MMP‐8 is most highly expressed by polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs) [5, 8]. Pro‐MMP‐8 is produced in PMN precursors in the bone marrow and stored in and subsequently released from the secondary granules of PMNs [5]. Pro‐MMP‐8 is released by PMNs into the extracellular space and then activated by the cysteine switch mechanism. MMP‐8 is also produced in smaller amounts by activated macrophages, vascular smooth muscle cells, and bronchial epithelial cells [9, 10, 11].

MMP‐8 promotes pulmonary fibrosis in mice [7, 8, 9]. MMP‐8 has pro‐inflammatory activities during the systemic inflammatory response syndrome [12], in cardiovascular disease [13], and neuroinflammation [14]. MMP‐8 also promotes inflammation in other organs in experimental animal models [15, 16]. However, Mmp‐8 deficiency in mice leads to greater pulmonary inflammation and injury during lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and bleomycin‐mediated lung inflammation due to loss of Mmp‐8‐mediated proteolytic inactivation of Mip‐1α [6, 7] and allergen‐induced airway inflammation via loss of Mmp‐8‐induced granulocyte apoptosis [17].

Little is known about the contributions of MMP‐8 to IAV infection. Recently, Mmp‐8 expression was reported to be highly induced in whole lung samples throughout IAV (A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 [H1N1]) infection using genome‐wide RNA sequencing [18]. Thus, we tested the hypotheses that: (1) MMP‐8 levels are increased in plasma and/or lung samples from IAV‐infected human subjects and/or wild‐type (WT) mice; and (2) Mmp‐8 deficiency alters host responses to IAV infection, thereby affecting viral clearance and lung injury.

Plasma MMP‐8 Levels

Plasma samples obtained from patients diagnosed with seasonal IAV and pandemic H1N1 were provided by Dr. Joaquin Zuñiga. These study subjects have been described in detail previously [20]. Human plasma MMP‐8 levels were measured in duplicate using a commercially available ELISA kit (R & D Systems, Minneapolis, MN).

Animal Studies

All studies conducted on mice were approved by the Institutional Care and Use Committee at Brigham and Women's Hospital (Protocol No. 3501) and the Charlie Norwood Veterans Affairs Medical Center Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol No. 24‐08‐128). The authors followed ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines for reporting on animal studies.

Mice

Mice were housed in a barrier facility under specific pathogen‐free conditions. Mmp‐8 −/− mice in a pure C57BL/6 background were studied [6, 7]. The Mmp‐8 −/− mice have a normal lifespan, fertility, and lung development, and no abnormality has been identified in any organ in the unchallenged state [16]. C57BL/6 WT parental littermate mice were used as controls for Mmp‐8 −/− mice.

Cell Lines and Virus Stocks

Madin‐Darby canine kidney (MDCK, CCL‐34) cells (American Tissue Culture Collection, Manassas, VA) were maintained in minimum essential medium (MEM) supplemented with L‐glutamine, Earle's salts, 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 0.25 μg/mL amphotericin B (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). The pandemic IAV strain, influenza A/California/07/2009 H1N1 (H1N1), was obtained from the Centers for Disease Control (CDC, Atlanta, GA), propagated in Madin‐Darby Canine Kidney (MDCK) cells, and used to infect mice. Titers of stock were measured using a standard plaque assay on 6‐well plates of confluent monolayers of MDCK cells as described previously [19, 21] using a 0.6% Avicel CL‐611 solution (FMC Corporation, Philadelphia, PA) containing 3 μg/mL of trypsin solution treated with 1‐tosylamido‐2‐phenylethyl chloromethyl ketone (Worthington Biochemical Corporation, Lakewood, NJ).

IAV Infection of Mice

Adult C57BL/6 strain WT and Mmp‐8 −/− mice were infected with A/California/04/2009 H1N1 virus through intranasal instillation [19]. An LD80 inoculum (104 plaque‐forming units [PFU]), resulting in 80% mortality in WT mice, was used to evaluate survival and loss of body weight. A lower LD20 inoculum (103 PFU) was used for experiments requiring tissue harvest, including measurements of lung viral titers, lung inflammation, acute lung injury (ALI), respiratory mechanics, cytokine levels, and immunostaining endpoints to ensure reproducible infection while preserving sufficient numbers of surviving mice for analysis at the indicated time points. The LD80 and LD20 inocula were selected based on preliminary dose–response studies and our previously established infection model [19].

Mmp‐8 Levels

Mmp‐8 levels were measured using quantitative real‐time PCR or a commercial ELISA kit [22]. Lung sections were double immunostained for MMP‐8 and markers of alveolar macrophages (AMs; MAC‐3), lung epithelial cells (pancytokeratin; Pan‐CK), or PMNs (myeloperoxidase; MPO).

Leukocyte and Lymphocyte Subset Enumeration in Bronchoalveolar Lavage (BAL) Samples

WT and Mmp‐8 −/− mice were infected with a 103 PFU H1N1 by the intranasal route and were euthanized at Days 3, 7, and 10 post‐infection (p.i.). Uninfected mice were used as controls. BAL was performed on other cohorts of mice using 8 × 0.5 mL aliquots of PBS [19]. BAL leukocytes were then stained for surface antigens for 30 min at 4°C in PBS containing 2% fetal calf serum and fixed using the Fixation/Permeabilization buffer set (BD Biosciences) following the manufacturer's instructions. Antibodies were purchased from Biolegend (San Diego, CA), eBioscience (San Diego, CA), and BD Biosciences (San Jose, CA). Flow cytometry was performed on a BD LSR Fortessa Cell Analyzer (San Jose, CA) and analyzed with FlowJo software (Tree Star Inc.).

Quantification of Mediators of Inflammation and IFNs in Lung Samples

Murine lungs were homogenized in 1 mL of PBS containing 0.5% Triton X‐100 and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 1 mM 1,10‐phenanthroline, and Mammalian Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). Commercially available ELISA kits were used to measure IL‐1β, IL‐6, IL‐10, CCL‐2, CCL‐5, IL‐17, TNF‐α AND TGF‐β (R & D SYSTEMS, MINNEAPOLIS, MN), G‐CSF, GM‐CSF, M‐CSF, MIP‐1α, MIP‐2, KC (CXCL‐1) (Peprotech, Rocky Hill, NJ), GCP‐2 (CXCL‐6) (MyBioSource, San Diego, CA), IFN‐α and IFN‐γ (Affymetrix/eBioscience, San Diego, CA) and IFN‐β (PBL Assay Science, Piscataway, NJ). Levels of each analyte were normalized to the total lung protein level measured using a commercial kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA).

Lung Viral Titers

Viral RNA copy number was measured using quantitative real‐time reverse‐transcription PCR [19]. RNA was extracted from H1N1‐infected lung tissue using a SurePrep TrueTotal RNA Purification Kit (Fisher Scientific, Fair Lawn, NJ). RNA (1 μg) was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA). Quantitative PCR was performed to amplify H1N1 cDNA using an AriaMx Real‐Time PCR machine (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) and primers (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) for H1N1 IAV (Primer sequences are shown in Table S1). Viral copy numbers were calculated using H1N1 DNA standards of known concentrations and expressed as H1N1 viral RNA copies per μg of total lung RNA.

ALI

To measure the permeability of the alveolar‐capillary barrier in the murine lung, mice were infected with 103 PFU H1N1 by the intranasal route and euthanized on Days 3, 7, and 10 p.i. Uninfected mice were used as controls. BAL was performed with one aliquot of 0.5 mL of PBS, and albumin was measured in BAL fluid (BALF) samples using an ELISA (GenWay Biotech Inc., San Diego, CA) [23].

Respiratory Mechanics

To evaluate lung injury, mice were infected with 103 PFU H1N1 by the intranasal route for 7 days p.i. Uninfected mice were used as controls. Mice were anesthetized using a cocktail of 100 mg/kg ketamine, 10 mg/kg xylazine, and 3 mg/kg acepromazine delivered by the intraperitoneal route. A tracheostomy was performed, and an 18‐gauge cannula was inserted and secured in the trachea using sutures. The animals were connected by the tracheal cannula to a digitally controlled mechanical ventilator (a FlexiVent device; Scireq Inc., Montreal, Quebec, Canada). Ventilator settings were f = 150/min, FiO2 = 0.21, tidal volume = 10 mL/kg body weight, and a positive end‐expiratory pressure (PEEP) of 3 cm of H2O. Murine lungs were inflated to total lung capacity (25 cm H2O) three times, and then tissue elastance and peripheral airway elastance were measured at a PEEP of 3 cm H2O, followed by stepwise quasi‐static compliance (Cst), and volume‐pressure flow curves [24]. After the measurements of respiratory mechanics were completed, mice were humanely euthanized.

Necrostatin‐1 (RIP1 Inhibitor) Treatment in H1N1‐Infected Mmp‐8 −/− Mice

Mmp‐8 −/− mice were infected intranasally with 103 PFU H1N1 influenza virus. At 24 h after infection, mice received an intraperitoneal injection of Necrostatin‐1 (Nec‐1; catalog #480065, Sigma‐Aldrich) at a dose of 6 mg/kg body weight [25, 26]. Necrostatin‐1 was prepared as a 60 mg/mL stock solution in DMSO and diluted 1:100 in saline to a working concentration of 0.6 mg/mL, corresponding to a final DMSO concentration of 1%. Control mice received an equal volume of vehicle containing 1% DMSO in saline. Mice were euthanized on Day 3 p.i. BAL fluid albumin concentrations were measured using an ELISA kit (GenWay Biotech Inc., San Diego, CA). Lung viral titers were quantified by quantitative real‐time reverse‐transcription PCR. H1N1 viral copy numbers were calculated using H1N1 DNA standards of known concentrations.

Mouse Toll‐Like Receptor (TLR) Signaling Pathway PCR Array

WT and Mmp‐8 −/− mice were infected with 103 PFU H1N1 by the intranasal route and euthanized at Days 1, 3 and 7 p.i. Total RNA was isolated from lung samples using a SurePrep TrueTotal RNA Purification Kit (Fisher Scientific, Fair Lawn, NJ), following the manufacturer's instructions. For each experimental group and time point, equal amounts of lung RNA from 6 to 8 mice were pooled before PCR array analysis. 1 μg of pooled RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA). RT2 Profiler mouse TLR signaling pathway PCR Array (QIAGEN, Germany) was used to measure the expression of 84 genes central to TLR‐mediated signal transduction and innate immunity. A heat map was generated using hierarchical cluster analysis to show TLR‐mediated signal transduction and innate immunity gene expression patterns in lung tissues between WT and Mmp‐8 −/− mice after IAV infection. Because each group and time point was represented by a single pooled RNA sample without independent biological replicates, the array data were analyzed descriptively without inferential statistical testing. A greater than three‐fold change was used as an exploratory screening threshold to identify genes showing the largest expression differences for subsequent evaluation.

RT‐PCR Measurements of Eif2ak2 (Pkr), Ifn‐α2, Ifn‐α4, Ifn‐β, Ifn‐γ, Receptor‐Interacting Serine/Threonine‐Protein Kinase 1 (Rip1) and Rip3

WT and Mmp‐8 −/− mice were infected with 103 PFU H1N1 by the intranasal route and euthanized at Days 1, 3 and 7 p.i. Uninfected mice were used as controls. Total RNA was isolated from lung samples using a SurePrep TrueTotal RNA Purification Kit (Fisher Scientific, Fair Lawn, NJ), following the manufacturer's instructions, and 1 μg of RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA). SYBR green‐based real‐time RT‐PCR was used to measure Eif2ak2 (Pkr), Ifn‐α2, Ifn‐α4, Ifn‐β, Ifn‐γ, Rip1 and Rip3 gene expression using primers from Invitrogen (Charlestown, MA), Ppia and/or β‐actin as the housekeeping genes (see Table S1 for primer sequences), the comparative threshold method, and an AriaMx Real‐time PCR machine (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) [22].

Double Immunofluorescence Staining of Murine Lung Sections for Readouts of Necroptosis (Phospho‐RIP3 [p‐RIP3] and Phospho‐Mixed Lineage Kinase Domain Like Pseudokinase [p‐MLKL])

Formalin‐fixed lung sections from mice were double immunostained for p‐RIP3 and markers of airway epithelial cells (pancytokeratin; Pan‐CK). Briefly, lung sections were immunostained with rabbit anti‐murine p‐RIP3‐Ser232 IgG (Abcam, Cambridge, MA) followed by goat anti‐rabbit F(ab′)2 conjugated to Alexa‐488. Lung sections were also immunostained with the following mouse anti‐murine pancytokeratin IgG2a (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) followed by goat anti‐murine F(ab′)2 conjugated to Alexa‐546. All secondary antibodies were obtained from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA). Isotype‐matched non‐immune rabbit and murine IgGs were used as control primary antibodies. Lung sections were counterstained with 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI). Images of the immuno‐stained lung sections were captured and analyzed using a confocal microscope (Olympus Corporation, Center Valley, PA). For each lung section, immunostaining in epithelial cells was quantified in 10 randomly acquired images using MetaMorph software (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA), and the staining signal was normalized to the unit area of bronchial wall evaluated.

Quantification of MLKL and RIP3 Activation (Phosphorylation) in Lung Samples

WT and Mmp‐8 −/− mice were infected with 103 PFU H1N1 by the intranasal route and were euthanized on Day 3 p.i. Lungs were removed and homogenized in RIPA buffer containing 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonylfluoride, 1 mM 1,10‐phenanthroline, 1% Sigma Mammalian Protease Inhibitor Cocktail and PhosSTOP (Sigma, St. Louis, MO). The total protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo‐Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL). Protein lysates were separated on 12% SDS‐PAGE gels, transferred to PVDF membranes, and then incubated with rabbit anti‐MLKL IgG (Abcam, Cambridge, MA), rabbit anti‐Ser345 phospho‐MLKL IgG (Abcam, Cambridge, MA), rabbit anti‐RIP3 IgG (Abcam, Cambridge, MA), rabbit anti‐p‐RIP3‐Ser232 IgG (Abcam, Cambridge, MA), or mouse anti‐α‐Tubulin IgG (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). Membranes were then incubated with goat anti‐rabbit IgG (H + L) conjugated to HRP or goat anti‐murine IgG (H + L) conjugated to HRP (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA), and signals were developed using a chemiluminescence kit. Western blot images were captured using a ChemiDoc imaging system and analyzed by densitometry (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA). Phosphorylated MLKL (p‐MLKL‐Ser345) and phosphorylated RIP3 (p‐RIP3‐Ser232) levels were normalized to total MLKL or RIP3 levels, respectively.

Cleavage Assays

Human recombinant MMP‐8 (rhMMP‐8) was activated as described previously [8, 27]. Human IFN‐α2 (1000 nM), IFN‐β (1000 nM), IL‐6 (900 nM), CCL‐2 (2000 nM), CCL‐5 (2000 nM), IL‐8 (2750 nM), or IL‐1β (1100 nM) were incubated in a total volume of 20 μL of assay buffer (50 mM Tris, 10 mM CaCl2, 150 mM NaCl, 0.05% (w/v) Brij35, pH 7.5) with or without 0.3 μM active rhMMP‐8 either with or without 1 mM 1,10‐phenanthroline (a non‐selective inhibitor of metalloproteinases that efficiently inhibits MMP‐8) for 18 h at 37°C. Reaction products were separated on Tris‐tricine gels and visualized using a silver staining kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Fair Lawn, NJ).

Primary Airway Epithelial Cell Isolation and Recombinant Human IFN‐β (rhIFN‐β) Treatment

Primary airway epithelial cells were isolated from WT and Mmp‐8 −/− mice as previously described [28]. Primary cells were cultured in Airway Epithelial Cell Basal Medium (ATCC, Manassas, VA). Upon reaching the appropriate confluence, primary airway epithelial cells were treated with 20 ng/mL rhIFN‐β (Peprotech, Rocky Hill, NJ) for 24 h. Following treatment, Eif2ak2 (Pkr) mRNA expression was quantified by RT‐qPCR. To evaluate necroptotic signaling, immunofluorescence staining was performed to detect phosphorylated RIP3 (p‐RIP3) and phosphorylated MLKL (p‐MLKL).

Statistics

SigmaPlot (Systat Software, San Jose, CA) was used for statistical analyses. Data that are normally distributed are presented as mean ± SD or SEM and analyzed using one‐way ANOVA tests followed by pair‐wise comparisons using two‐tailed Student's t‐tests. Non‐normally distributed data are presented as box plots, with boxes showing the medians and 25th and 75th percentiles and whiskers showing the 10th and 90th percentiles. These data were analyzed using one‐way ANOVA followed by pairwise Mann–Whitney U tests. p ≤ 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. To assess the association between plasma MMP‐8 levels and the PaO2/FiO2 ratio, multivariable linear regression analyses were conducted exclusively among influenza‐infected subjects. Healthy controls were not included because PaO2/FiO2 measurements were not available for this group. The adjusted analyses were conducted using complete cases, defined as subjects with available plasma MMP‐8, PaO2/FiO2, obesity, and influenza vaccination data. Of the 72 infected subjects with plasma MMP‐8 and PaO2/FiO2 measurements, 64 had complete covariate data and were included in the adjusted analysis. Because plasma MMP‐8 levels were not normally distributed, a sensitivity analysis was performed using log10‐transformed plasma MMP‐8 levels. No global adjustment was applied across different inflammatory mediators and time points because these analyses were exploratory and each mediator was treated as a distinct biological outcome. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating.

Results

Plasma MMP‐8 Levels Are Increased in IAV‐Infected Human Subjects

Table 1 shows the demographic and clinical characteristics of the human cohort. Human subjects with laboratory‐confirmed seasonal IAV or A/California/07/2009 H1N1 infection had ~30‐fold higher plasma MMP‐8 levels than age‐ and sex‐matched healthy controls (Figure 1A). Because the groups differed markedly in obesity prevalence and influenza vaccination status, this between‐group comparison was interpreted as descriptive. In unadjusted analysis, plasma MMP‐8 levels were inversely related to the severity of lung injury as assessed by the PaO2/FiO2 ratio (Pearson r = −0.384, p = 0.0009, n = 72) (Figure 1B). In a complete‐case analysis of 64 infected subjects with available covariate data, plasma MMP‐8 levels remained significantly associated with lower PaO2/FiO2 ratios (β = −1.138, 95% CI: −1.817 to −0.459, p = 0.001, n = 64). Similar results were obtained when log10‐transformed plasma MMP‐8 levels were used in the model (β = −74.18, 95% CI: −119.14 to −29.22, p = 0.002, n = 64). These findings support an association between plasma MMP‐8 and lung injury severity but do not establish its independent prognostic performance.

Mmp‐8 Is Upregulated in the Lungs of H1N1‐IAV‐Infected WT Mice

When C57BL/6 WT mice were infected with 103 PFU A/California/04/2009 H1N1 IAV, steady‐state Mmp‐8 mRNA and MMP‐8 protein levels in lung tissues were both strikingly upregulated on Day 3 p.i., and levels increased further on Days 7 and 10 p.i. (Figure 1C,D). Double immunostaining experiments localized the cellular sources of MMP‐8 in murine lungs. As expected, uninfected WT mice had minimal or no MMP‐8 staining in their lungs (Figure 2). Infected WT mice had increased MMP‐8 staining in airway epithelial cells on Days 3, 7, and 10 p.i. (Figure 2A,B). PMNs were not present in the airways of uninfected mice, but MMP‐8 staining was detected in PMNs recruited to the airways of IAV‐infected mice (Figure 2C,D). The percentage of macrophages staining positively for MMP‐8 in the lungs of WT mice increased over Days 3–10 after H1N1 infection (Figure 2E,F).

H1N1 IAV‐Infected Mmp‐8 −/− Mice Have Greater Pulmonary Necroptosis Than WT Mice

The expression of 84 genes involved in TLR‐mediated signal transduction and innate immunity was evaluated using pooled RNA from murine whole‐lung samples. Among these genes, six showed greater than three‐fold upregulation in pooled lung RNA from infected Mmp‐8 −/− mice relative to WT mice at one or more time points and were selected for descriptive presentation in Table 2. Because the array was performed using pooled RNA without independent biological replicates, these findings were interpreted descriptively. The complete heatmap containing all 84 genes, including genes with increased or decreased expression, is provided in Figure 4A. Among the genes meeting the descriptive screening threshold, Eif2ak2 (Pkr) showed the largest increase and was subsequently evaluated by qRT‐PCR using individual lung samples with independent biological replicates (Figure 4B). PKR is an RNA‐responsive kinase involved in IFN‐α/β‐induced necroptosis [29]. Necroptosis is a programmed form of pro‐inflammatory cell death in which virus‐infected cells undergo caspase‐independent “cellular suicide” in the presence of viral inhibitors of intracellular caspases. The release of cellular contents by infected cells triggers inflammation and an immune response to the virus that limits viral spreading [30, 31]. Necroptosis can be induced by Type I IFN signaling [29]. IFN‐α/β signaling upregulates Pkr expression, which stimulates receptor‐interacting protein kinases‐1 and ‐3 (RIP1‐RIP3) complex formation, leading to the formation of the “necrosome” complex, which induces cellular lysis. This process releases pro‐inflammatory damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and exposes viral particles to the immune system, leading to an adaptive immune response to the virus [30, 31, 32].

Type I IFNs serve as a major host defense pathway against IAV infection [33]. IAV RNA is recognized as foreign by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), which trigger the secretion of Type I IFNs [34]. Type I IFNs limit further viral growth and spread to uninfected host cells by inducing necroptosis of infected cells [29] and inducing the expression of antiviral proteins by cells susceptible to viral infection [35]. Infected Mmp‐8 −/− mice had higher lung protein levels of IFN‐α on Day 3 p.i. (Figure 6A), IFN‐β on Days 3 and 7 p.i. MMP‐8 Cleaves Some Pro‐Inflammatory Mediators In Vitro

MMP‐8 has an active metalloproteinase domain and cleaves and inactivates CCL‐3 during ALI in mice [6]. MMP‐9 proteolytically inactivates IFN‐β in vitro [37]. Thus, we investigated whether MMP‐8 degrades Type I IFNs and cytokines that were elevated in the lungs of H1N1 IAV‐infected Mmp‐8 −/− mice. When tested in vitro, active human MMP‐8 produced only modest cleavage of IFN‐α2 and IFN‐β, as assessed by the appearance of a small quantity of a lower molecular mass cleavage product. Generation of this product was blocked when a small molecule metalloproteinase inhibitor was added to the reactions, confirming that the active site of MMP‐8 is required for these activities (Figure 9A,B). Thus, the higher IFN‐α/β‐mediated antiviral immunity in H1N1 IAV‐infected Mmp‐8 −/− lungs is unlikely to be due to the lack of Mmp‐8‐mediated cleavage of IFN‐α/β.

The reaction products were separated on Tris‐tricine gels and visualized with silver staining. The arrows indicate the bands corresponding to rhMMP‐8 and the intact cytokines. The generation of IL‐1β by the inflammasome promotes host defense against IAV infection [38]. Active human MMP‐8 completely degraded IL‐1β (Figure 9C) and substantially degraded IL‐6 (Figure 9D) in vitro. MMP‐8‐mediated degradation of IL‐1β and IL‐6 was completely inhibited by adding a metalloproteinase inhibitor to the reaction mixtures. Thus, the biochemical capacity of MMP‐8 to cleave IL‐1β and IL‐6 in vitro raises the possibility that loss of this activity contributes to their elevated lung levels in infected Mmp‐8 −/− mice; however, this mechanism was not established in vivo. In contrast, active human MMP‐8 did not degrade human CCL‐2, CCL‐5, or IL‐8 (the human ortholog of Cxcl‐1) (data not shown). Thus, the increased levels of several pro‐inflammatory mediators may contribute to the enhanced immune responses observed in infected Mmp‐8 −/− mice, although the mechanisms responsible for their accumulation remain uncertain.

Discussion

Our data identify MMP‐8 as a host‐response regulator during IAV infections. We report for the first time that plasma MMP‐8 levels are substantially increased in human subjects infected with pandemic H1N1 and seasonal IAV, and that levels inversely correlate with the PaO2/FiO2 ratio, a clinical indicator of ALI. MMP‐8 expression was significantly elevated in airway epithelial cells, macrophages, and PMNs following H1N1 infection in WT mice. Notably, H1N1‐infected Mmp‐8 −/− mice exhibited lower mortality and lung viral loads compared with WT controls, despite experiencing more severe lung injury.

The reduced viral burdens in Mmp‐8 −/− mice were accompanied by increased pulmonary Type I IFN levels, enhanced necroptotic signaling in airway epithelial cells, and augmented adaptive immune responses. These pathways may contribute to more efficient viral clearance, although their relative contributions remain uncertain. However, the mechanism linking Mmp‐8 deficiency to increased pulmonary Type I IFN protein levels remains unclear and represents a key unresolved question of this study. The increased pulmonary adaptive immune response in IAV‐infected Mmp‐8 −/− mice may be driven, at least in part, by increased necroptosis, which can promote extracellular release of viral particles and DAMPs that enhance immune activation [30, 31, 32]. In addition, H1N1‐infected Mmp‐8 −/− mice exhibited higher pulmonary levels of the pro‐inflammatory cytokines IL‐1β and IL‐6, both of which have been implicated in promoting effective host immune responses to IAV [39, 40]. Our in vitro findings raise the possibility that the increased levels of IL‐1β and IL‐6 in infected Mmp‐8 −/− mice may partly reflect the loss of MMP‐8‐mediated proteolytic processing. However, this possibility was not directly tested in vivo.

Prior studies report that the expression of MMP‐14, ‐9, and ‐13 is increased in IAV‐infected cells and that antibody‐mediated inhibition of MMP‐14 or deficiency of Mmp‐9 protects mice from IAV‐mediated disease [18, 19, 41, 42]. However, the contribution of MMP‐8 to IAV‐induced disease pathogenesis has not been investigated previously. We report that plasma MMP‐8 levels were > 30‐fold higher in human subjects infected with H1N1 and seasonal IAV than in uninfected controls. Elevated plasma MMP‐8 levels likely reflect increased activation and degranulation of circulating PMNs, as MMP‐8 is predominantly expressed by PMNs [6], and circulating PMNs are activated in humans infected with IAV [21, 43]. Furthermore, the inverse association between plasma MMP‐8 levels and the PaO2/FiO2 ratio suggests that plasma MMP‐8 may have potential value as a candidate marker of lung injury severity during IAV infection. However, its clinical and prognostic value requires validation in larger prospective cohorts with longitudinal outcomes.

In the mouse model, pulmonary Mmp‐8 mRNA and MMP‐8 protein levels were increased as early as Day 3 p.i., with airway PMNs, macrophages, and epithelial cells serving as the main cellular sources of MMP‐8. The early rise in MMP‐8 likely results from the rapid recruitment of PMNs to the infected lung [8], whereas the sustained increase observed on Days 7–10 p.i. is likely driven by enhanced Mmp‐8 expression in activated airway macrophages and epithelial cells [9, 10].

IAV is tropic for airway epithelial cells, and replication of the virus within these cells causes epithelial death and pulmonary inflammation. The lower lung viral burdens of IAV‐infected Mmp‐8 −/− versus WT mice are likely due to their higher lung levels of Type I IFNs, as signaling via the Type I IFN receptor limits further viral growth and spreading by increasing cellular expression of antiviral proteins, which reduce viral replication [35]. In addition, Type I IFNs also upregulate Pkr expression, which promotes RIP1/RIP3 complex formation and the induction of necroptosis [29]. Therefore, the increased pulmonary Type I IFN levels in IAV‐infected Mmp‐8 −/− mice may contribute to the enhanced necroptotic signaling observed in airway epithelial cells. However, the present data do not establish Type I IFNs as the sole cause of enhanced necroptosis or determine the extent to which this pathway contributes to reduced viral replication [29]. The increased necroptosis in IAV‐infected Mmp‐8 −/− mice may also have contributed to their greater pulmonary adaptive immune responses. Necroptotic epithelial cells release DAMPs and viral particles, which promote the activation of innate immune cells and enhance subsequent adaptive immune responses.

Consistent with this mechanism, IFN‐β induced Pkr expression and increased RIP3 and MLKL phosphorylation in primary airway epithelial cells. However, these cells from WT and Mmp‐8 −/− mice responded similarly to IFN‐β stimulation, indicating that Mmp‐8 deficiency does not intrinsically alter epithelial responsiveness to Type I IFNs. Thus, the enhanced necroptotic signaling observed in vivo is associated with increased pulmonary Type I IFN levels and may depend on factors within the lung microenvironment, rather than an intrinsic difference in epithelial responsiveness to IFN‐β.

We initially hypothesized that MMP‐8 cleaves and degrades IFN‐α/β, as MMP‐8 cleaves several cytokines in other disease models [6]. Under this model, the absence of Mmp‐8‐mediated cleavage would be expected to increase pulmonary IFN‐α/β levels in infected Mmp‐8 −/− mice. Surprisingly, human MMP‐8 only modestly cleaved IFN‐α2 and IFN‐β, indicating that the enhanced IFN‐α/β‐mediated antiviral immunity in H1N1 IAV‐infected Mmp‐8 −/− lungs is unlikely to result from the loss of direct MMP‐8‐dependent cleavage of IFN‐α/β. The mechanism linking MMP‐8 to pulmonary Type I IFN protein levels remains unresolved. Future studies examining the cellular sources of Type I IFNs, upstream IFN‐regulatory pathways, and microenvironment‐dependent mechanisms will be required to define this relationship.

IAV‐infected Mmp‐8 −/− mice had a greater immune response to IAV as assessed by higher total lymphocyte and CD4+ and CD8+ T cell counts and enhanced markers of macrophage activation. The augmented adaptive immune response may be driven by increased airway epithelial necroptosis as well as elevated lung levels of CCL‐2, ‐3, and ‐5 and IL‐1α, ‐1β, ‐6, and ‐12. CCL‐2, ‐3, and ‐5 promote the recruitment and activation of monocytes and lymphocytes in the lung, whereas IL‐1α, ‐1β, ‐6, and ‐12 enhance T cell activation. Notably, IL‐1β and IL‐6 have established protective roles during IAV infection through their ability to stimulate immune responses [34, 40, 44]. IL‐1β promotes dendritic cell (DC) CC‐chemokine receptor‐7 expression and migration to draining lymph nodes, thereby enhancing T cell priming [34]. It also directly promotes T cell activation by increasing Il‐2 receptor expression and, together with IL‐6, induces the production of IL‐2 and other T cell growth factors. In addition, IL‐1β activates B cells by inducing IL‐4 expression [44]. IL‐6 further contributes to antiviral immunity by activating CD4+ T cells and promoting the proliferation, differentiation, and cytotoxic activity of CD8+ T cells during IAV infection [34, 40].

MMP‐8 has been implicated in several inflammatory pulmonary diseases through its ability to cleave and regulate the activity of pro‐inflammatory mediators [5, 6, 7]. Our study demonstrates that MMP‐8 has the biochemical capacity to cleave IL‐1β and IL‐6 in vitro. This finding raises the possibility that loss of MMP‐8‐mediated processing contributes to the elevated lung levels of these cytokines in infected Mmp‐8 −/− mice. However, the cleavage assays used recombinant proteins at relatively high concentrations and an extended incubation time and did not reproduce the dynamic, protease‐rich environment of the infected lung. Moreover, we did not measure local active MMP‐8 concentrations or detect intact cytokines and MMP‐8‐generated cleavage products in lung tissues. Therefore, the physiological relevance of this cleavage activity remains to be established in vivo. Several inflammatory mediators were elevated in Mmp‐8 −/− lungs primarily during the early phase of infection, on Days 1 and/or 3, and many returned toward WT levels at later time points. In contrast, CCL‐5 was elevated on Days 7 and 10, suggesting that the inflammatory consequences of Mmp‐8 deficiency vary over the course of infection. However, the mechanisms and biological consequences of these temporal patterns require further investigation.

Although H1N1‐infected Mmp‐8 −/− mice had lower IAV‐induced mortality and lung viral burdens, they paradoxically developed more severe ALI. The increased epithelial necroptosis in IAV‐infected Mmp‐8 −/− mice likely contributed to this heightened lung injury, as necroptotic cell death is a well‐established driver of tissue damage and organ injury [32, 45]. In addition, the augmented immune responses in infected Mmp‐8 −/− mice may have further exacerbated ALI, given the recognized role of immune cell‐mediated inflammation in the pathogenesis of IAV‐induced ALI [21, 46]. Thus, while the enhanced antiviral and immune responses associated with Mmp‐8 deficiency improve viral control and survival, they may simultaneously promote immunopathology and lung tissue damage.

The phenotype of H1N1‐infected Mmp‐8 −/− mice differs substantially from that of Mmp‐9 −/− mice reported in our previous study [19]. Mmp‐9 deficiency protected mice from severe H1N1 IAV infection and was associated with reduced mortality, lower viral burdens, reduced E‐cadherin shedding, attenuated myeloid inflammation, and enhanced adaptive immune responses [19]. In contrast, Mmp‐8 deficiency reduced lung viral burdens and body weight loss but exacerbated lung injury. These divergent phenotypes suggest that MMP‐8 and MMP‐9 perform distinct, nonredundant functions during IAV infection. Mechanistically, MMP‐9 promotes epithelial barrier disruption, at least in part through E‐cadherin shedding, whereas MMP‐8 may influence inflammatory cytokine bioavailability, based on its capacity to cleave IL‐1β and IL‐6 in vitro [19]. Consequently, loss of Mmp‐8 enhances antiviral immunity but also amplifies inflammatory responses and necroptosis‐associated tissue injury. Distinct effects on immune regulation may further contribute to these differences. Whereas Mmp‐9 deficiency was associated with reduced Type I IFN responses and enhanced IFN‐γ‐driven adaptive immunity [19], Mmp‐8 deficiency resulted in increased Type I IFN levels and augmented PKR/RIP3/MLKL‐mediated necroptosis signaling. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that MMP‐8 and MMP‐9 regulate distinct host defense and immunopathologic pathways during IAV infection.

Current antiviral therapies for IAV primarily target viral proteins such as neuraminidase. In contrast, our data identify MMP‐8 as a host‐derived protease that plays a key role in regulating both viral clearance and immunopathology during IAV infection. Future studies will define the cell‐specific contributions of MMP‐8 and evaluate whether selective modulation of its activity can improve viral control without exacerbating lung injury.

Although broad‐spectrum active site‐directed MMP inhibitors have generally proven unsuitable for the long‐term treatment of chronic diseases because of their limited selectivity among MMP family members, poor oral bioavailability, and dose‐limiting toxicities, these limitations may be less problematic in the setting of acute viral infection that requires only short‐term treatment [47, 48]. Accordingly, the therapeutic potential of transient or cell‐selective MMP‐8 modulation warrants further investigation in severe IAV infection.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the human studies were conducted in relatively small cohorts of subjects with seasonal and H1N1 influenza, which may have limited our ability to account for all potential confounding variables. One limitation of the human cohort is that obesity was more prevalent in influenza‐infected subjects than in healthy controls. Because obesity is a recognized risk factor for severe influenza, it could potentially confound associations between plasma MMP‐8 levels and clinical measures of disease severity. To address this concern, we performed multivariable analyses adjusting for obesity and influenza vaccination status and found that elevated plasma MMP‐8 levels remained significantly associated with lower PaO2/FiO2 ratios. However, the number of vaccinated subjects was small, limiting statistical power to assess the independent contribution of vaccination status. Taken together, the human data suggest that plasma MMP‐8 may have potential value as a candidate marker of lung injury severity during influenza infection; however, this association requires validation in larger prospective cohorts with balanced enrollment, complete covariate data, standardized sampling times, and longitudinal clinical outcomes.

Second, although Mmp‐8 deficiency was associated with increased pulmonary Type I IFN protein levels during IAV infection, the mechanism underlying this increase remains unclear and represents a key unresolved question of this study. Type I IFN transcript levels were similar in infected WT and Mmp‐8 −/− lungs, and MMP‐8 showed only limited cleavage of IFN‐α2 and IFN‐β in vitro. Therefore, the elevated IFN protein levels cannot currently be attributed to increased transcription or loss of direct MMP‐8‐mediated proteolysis. They may instead reflect differences in cellular sources, secretion, extracellular retention, compartmentalization, or the pulmonary microenvironment. These possibilities require direct experimental investigation, and our findings establish an association rather than a direct causal mechanism. In addition, IFN‐β‐induced p‐RIP3 and p‐MLKL signals in primary airway epithelial cells were quantified by immunofluorescence rather than Western blotting; therefore, further validation of these findings will be required in future studies.

Third, although MMP‐8 cleaved IL‐1β and IL‐6 in vitro, the physiological relevance of this activity remains uncertain. The assays used recombinant proteins at relatively high concentrations and an extended incubation time, and we did not measure active MMP‐8 concentrations or detect MMP‐8‐generated cytokine cleavage products in infected lungs. Therefore, these findings raise the possibility that loss of MMP‐8‐mediated cytokine processing contributes to increased pulmonary IL‐1β and IL‐6 levels in Mmp‐8 −/− mice but do not establish this mechanism in vivo.

Fourth, the use of global Mmp‐8 knockout mice does not distinguish the relative contributions of MMP‐8 derived from airway epithelial cells, macrophages, and PMNs to viral clearance and lung immunopathology. Therefore, the present findings apply to global Mmp‐8 deficiency and do not identify the cellular source responsible for the observed phenotype. Future studies using epithelial‐ and myeloid‐specific Mmp‐8 knockout models will be required to define the cell‐specific functions of MMP‐8 and determine whether selective targeting of particular cellular sources can enhance viral control without exacerbating lung injury.

Finally, the relative contributions of enhanced Type I IFN signaling, increased necroptosis, and augmented adaptive immune responses to the improved viral clearance observed in IAV‐infected Mmp‐8 −/− mice remain uncertain. Although our data suggest that these pathways may contribute to the observed phenotype, the present study does not establish a complete linear causal pathway from Mmp‐8 deficiency to increased Type I IFN levels, enhanced necroptosis, and altered viral clearance or lung injury. The extent to which these responses act independently or cooperatively also remains unclear. Future studies employing genetic and pharmacologic approaches will be needed to define the specific mechanisms by which MMP‐8 regulates antiviral immunity and immunopathology during influenza infection. Additionally, the inflammatory mediator analyses involved multiple outcomes and time points without global adjustment across all comparisons, which may increase the risk of Type I error. These findings should therefore be considered exploratory and require independent validation.

Collectively, our findings identify MMP‐8 as a regulator of host responses to IAV infection and a potential host‐response target for severe influenza. Mmp‐8 deficiency was associated with enhanced Type I IFN‐associated necroptotic signaling and antiviral immune responses, although the upstream mechanism linking MMP‐8 to increased pulmonary Type I IFN protein levels remains unresolved. Therapeutic strategies targeting MMP‐8 will therefore need to balance potential improvements in viral control against the risk of increased immune‐mediated lung injury.
