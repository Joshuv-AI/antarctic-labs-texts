Testing the capacity of Bard and ChatGPT for writing essays on ethical dilemmas: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Mariano Kaliterna, Marija Franka Žuljević, Luka Ursić, Jakov Krka, Darko Duplančić
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) have led to concerns about its potential misuse in education. As large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT and Bard can generate human-like text, researchers and educators noted the potential redundancy of tasking students with writing academic essays. We aimed to explore if the two LLMs could generate unstructured essays on medical students’ personal experiences of challenges and ethical dilemmas that are indistinguishable from human-written texts. We collected 47 original student-written essays from which we extracted keywords to develop prompts for the LLMs. We then used these prompts to generate an equivalent number of essays using ChatGPT and Bard. We analysed the essays using the Language Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) 22 software, extracting the main LIWC summary measures and variables related to social and psychological processes. We also conducted sub-analyses for sixteen student essays that were presumably written entirely or in part by AI, according to two AI detectors. We found that AI-written essays used more language related to affect, authenticity, and analytical thinking compared to student-written essays after we removed AI-co-written student essays from the analysis. We observed that, despite the differences in language characteristics compared to student-written essays, both LLMs are highly effective in generating essays on students’ personal experiences and opinions regarding real-life ethical dilemmas.Subject terms: Medical research, Clinical trial design

Introduction

The emergence of large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT (OpenAI, San Francisco, CA, USA) and Bard (Google LLC, Mountain View, CA, USA) in the public domain allowed a wider audience to access artificial intelligence (AI) tools that can generate human-like text based on user-defined questions or tasks. For example, ChatGPT can generate essay ideas or full essays, answer questions, and perform similar tasks which the user can refine through further input or “prompting”. However, this general availability also caused concerns of misuse and malpractice in various fields, but especially education1,2.

In response, the European University Association called all stakeholders to implement guidance and policies on the use of AI in education3. Open Universities Australia has offered students guidelines for the ethical and acceptable use of ChatGPT4, while the University of Toronto advised instructors to specify which digital tools are allowed for assignments, while cautioning them against using unapproved AI tools to grade student work5. Despite these specific cases, educational institutions have yet to develop stances on the application of AI; as of June 2023, over 60% of the UK’s top 100 universities lacked explicit policies or guidelines regarding AI writing tools6. A United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO) survey carried out in 2023 found that, among 450 schools and universities globally, “fewer than 10% have developed institutional policies and/or formal guidance concerning the use of generative AI applications”7, indicating that institutions have yet to catch up with the spread of AI.

In practice, concern about academic integrity violations arose regarding official examinations, such as when ChatGPT demonstrated capacity in passing law and medical exams in the USA8,9, but also in academic essay writing10. Among its many uses, the capability of LLMs such as ChatGPT to generate essays will likely become critical, especially if AI detectors (which are still being developed) are unable to recognise essays students manually edit after generation10,11. In a survey conducted on over 6300 students in Germany, more than two-thirds reported using AI-based tools in their studies, with almost 40% using them for text analysis or creation12. Another study on 245 Indonesian students found that they have positive stances towards the use of AI in essay writing, as it improved their efficacy and abilities; yet they also expressed concerns about impacts on creativity, critical thinking, and ethics13. However, in analysing academic essays written with the assistance of ChatGPT, Bašić et al.14 found that it did not accelerate essay writing or affect essay scores, as marked by independent educators. Conversely, in a large-scale comparison of ChatGPT-generated versus student-written academic, argumentative essays, Herbold et al.15 found the former to receive better scores, while also exhibiting more rigid, formulaic structures than human-written ones. Since LLMs undoubtedly offer a potential for novel and innovative applications in academia and higher education16–18, it is important to explore their potential in generating various types of textual material intended for different purposes. However, studies on this topic are yet to be conducted.

To our knowledge, existing research has either compared LLM-generated essays to non-specific, human-written ones extracted from large databases or focussed on other, non-psychometric properties of the analysed texts. By collecting a sample of thematically focussed essays written by human subjects in a controlled environment, we wanted to see how LLMs would address writing about specific and curated topics and real-life scenarios. Specifically, we aimed to examine whether ChatGPT and Bard can produce unstructured essays on medical students’ personal experiences of challenges and ethical dilemmas they faced during studies that are indistinguishable from human-written texts. Despite differences in the quality of the generated texts and the training datasets, both LLMs have been found to be highly capable of generating human-like essays19,20. Our initial hypothesis was that human- and AI-generated text would have identifiable differences in language, especially related to affect and cognition.

Results

Out of 69 students who attended the courses where these essays were given as final tasks, 47 (68%) agreed to participate in the study; we thus included their essays and generated an equivalent number of partial and full keyword essays using ChatGPT and Bard (now known as Gemini), respectively. Based on the check-up by AI detectors, 16 (34%) essays were likely partially or fully generated by AI. Main analysis

Both groups of AI-generated essays had significantly higher values in “Affect” than student-written essays, with the difference resulting from higher values in the “Positive tone” and “Emotion” sub-variables. Conversely, student-written essays usually had higher scores for “Words per sentence” and “Cognition” variables (specifically, the “Cognitive processes” and “All-or-none” sub-variables within “Cognition”), although they only marginally differed from the ChatGPT-generated essays for the former and from Bard-generated essays for the latter variable. The differences for the remaining variables varied; for example, ChatGPT-generated essays had higher scores on the “Analytical thinking”, “Authentic”, and “Big words” variables than either essay group. Meanwhile, while we found statistically significant differences between ChatGPT- and student-written essays in the “Authentic”, “Tone”, “Big words”, “Drives”, and “Dictionary words” variables (with varying directions), they were absent in the comparison of Bard-generated versus student-written essays. Overall, we found no statistically significant differences for the “Clout” variable (Table 1 and Table S1 in Supplementary file 1).

Sub-analysis – partial versus full keyword essays

We found no differences in comparing partial versus full keyword prompt-based ChatGPT-generated essays. However, in comparing Bard-generated essays, we found statistically significant differences in the “Analytical thinking”, “Words per sentence”, “Dictionary words” and “Social processes” variables. Although these discrepancies disappeared after correcting for multiple comparisons, we conducted a sub-analysis comparing the partial keyword essays to original student essays. Our findings remained identical to the main analysis, suggesting that the initially identified differences between the two types of Bard essays were likely due to chance (Tables S2 and S3 in Supplementary file 1).

Sub-analysis – potentially AI-generated student essays

Sixteen student essays were likely fully or partially AI-written according to the AI detectors; this subgroup had LIWC scores more similar to those of the AI-generated essays, while still differing from the “true” student-written ones. For example, we observed higher scores for this subgroup compared to “true” student-written essays in variables such as “Affect” and “Analytical thinking”, in line with the main analysis, yet lower scores on “Authenticity” and no statistically significant differences for “Tone” (Table S4 in Supplementary file 1). However, potentially AI-co-written essays handed in by students were more similar to the AI essays, as the initial differences in the main analysis for some variables such as “Tone”, “Social processes”, and “Affect” were no longer statistically significant. Likewise, we observed no statistically significant difference in “Analytical thinking” score between student-written and ChatGPT-generated essays (Table S5 in Supplementary file 1). These discrepancies were again present when comparing “true” student-written essays with AI-generates ones (Fig. 1 and Table S6 in Supplementary file 1).

Discussion

In this study, we compared the psychometric linguistic characteristics of student-written essays on personal ethical dilemmas with those of equivalent AI essays generated by prompts designed with keywords from the original essays. We found that the latter group usually had more affect-related words, specifically those exhibiting positive emotion. Conversely, student-written essays had more cognitive-related language and more words per sentence. The two LLMs used to generate essays (Bard and ChatGPT) also differed from one another, with the former generating more human-like text, and the latter generating essays with more complex words frequently related to analytical thinking and often presenting with more authenticity. However, we found that a third of the essays handed in by students were already fully or partially written by an LLM. This was confirmed by additional analyses, where we observed fewer or less significant differences between said essays and fully AI-generated ones than between “true” student-written essays and AI ones, indicating further similarity. Moreover, as AI-co-written essays had lower authenticity, but higher analytical thinking scores and higher prevalence of “big words”, it is likely that these were, in fact, AI-generated, but subsequently edited to some extent. Furthermore, we confirmed that AI-written essays used more language related to affect, authenticity, and analytical thinking compared to fully student-written essays after removing AI-co-written student essays from the analysis.

As far as we know, our study is the first to quantitatively compare the psychometric characteristics of human- and AI-generated essays on medical students’ personal experiences with a real-life ethical dilemma they faced within an educational or professional context. Other studies that compared human-written and LLM-generated texts focussed either on argumentative or reflective texts on a diverse set of topics15,21 or the ability of LLMs to write in certain personality styles, irrespective of a specific topic22.

For example, Jiang et al.22 demonstrated that ChatGPT can successfully emulate certain personality traits within the Big Five personality model, which may partially explain why LLMs appeared to be highly competent in writing about nuanced, emotional topics, as in our study. Otherwise, our finding that AI-written essays had more analytic language is not surprising, as Herbold et al.15 previously found that ChatGPT-written essays outperformed human-written essays insofar as they used a more academically desirable structure and narrative. Likewise, in comparing human dialogue to a ChatGPT-generated one, Sandler et al.23 found that the LLM also had higher levels of analytical thinking. Although we did not conduct qualitative or quantitative analyses to explore this, we noted that AI-generated essays had a formulaic structure, probably influenced by the prompt instructing the LLM to write “an essay”, similar to what Herbold et al.15 encountered (although they focussed on more argumentative, structured essays). This included, among other things, the use of fixed phrases such as “During my medical studies” to open essays and “In conclusion” to close them (Supplementary file 2). If individuals evaluating essays in educational contexts encounter such formulaic language, it could indicate that the authors used some type of AI- or LLM-based tool. This finding is also similar to what Li et al.21 identified in their study, where they investigated the ability of LLMs to write reflective texts on a wide range of topics. The authors noticed that reflections generated by ChatGPT had lower variability in terms of the number of sentences, unique words, and their overall number of words compared to student-written ones.

Meanwhile, the finding that AI-written essays tend to have more emotionally positive and authentic language compared to student-written essays is somewhat unintuitive. This could be interpreted based on the results of a prior experiment where an online emotional support chat service using GPT-3 was perceived as more emotionally supportive than human responses. However, once participants learned that these responses were not generated by humans, any benefits they had gained from these services disappeared24. This is in line with what Jiang et al. observed, as well22. We hypothesise (although we cannot confirm) that the students were more reserved in expressing their emotions and stances due to the ethical ambiguity of their real-life situations, resulting in lower scores for authenticity and affect/tone. While Sandler et al.23 did not observe a difference in affect between human and ChatGTP-generated dialogue, they did observe that the latter had higher values for positive emotional tone and social processes. This is in line with our results, supporting their findings that LLMs might be perceived as ‘more human than human’23. This positive tone we observed for AI-written essays might suggest that they provided idealised “best-case scenarios” and more morally desirable responses, with generally more positive outcomes to ethical dilemmas. Finally, the linguistic differences between Bard and ChatGPT observed in our study, especially the higher authenticity and higher frequency of complex words observed in the LIWC scores of ChatGPT-generated essays may be explained by past comparisons of these two LLMs, where ChatGPT appeared to perform better than Bard in adapting to different contexts and performing more complex and intensive tasks, while also having overall higher capabilities in drafting text25–29. However, our observation that ChatGPT-generated essays have higher values for authenticity than human-written ones is in contrast with the findings of Sandler et al.23, although the difference we observed was marginally significant in the full analysis and non-existent after we compared true student-written essays to their AI-generated equivalents.

Our findings indicate that AI-based tools may be quite efficient at producing human-like essays related to ethics and writing on personal experiences and opinions, which is in line with the observations by Sandler and colleagues23. The observed psychometric differences between AI and student-written essays became much more nuanced in our sub-analyses of AI-co-written essays, indicating that these were likely modified by students after they were generated by an LLM. As higher education institutions and global initiatives move towards implementing regulations for the use of AI-based writing tools30,31, implementing effective safeguards will be a top priority25. Using detection software is another possibility, but it may not fully guarantee that sufficiently modified AI-generated essays will be successfully identified. It may also be likely that humans are capable of recognising AI-written essays by themselves32 and that detection software may simply be a complementary tool to enhance or confirm their observations. This, for example, is how we used it in our study, as it allowed us to confirm our assumptions that some of the student-written essays were AI-generated. This issue could be examined in future research, where blinded human evaluators could check if less formal, non-academic essays were written by an AI or a human, and where their performance could be compared to available AI detecting tools. To otherwise address the challenges brought on by AI to the essay-based tasks, it may be necessary to reintroduce hand-written essay tasks to ensure students truly apply their own critical thinking skills without relying on AI-assisted writing. Regardless of whether AI will be fully integrated as a part of essay-based tasks and examinations or whether more traditional formats will remain in place, clear rules for the use of AI in the setting of education should be adopted and integrated with all these factors taken in consideration, as well as a clear definition on which practices constitute plagiarism in the context of AI26.

The main strength of our study is the use of LIWC software, which has been extensively validated in previous research. This allowed us to quantitatively compare different types of essays. Another significant strength is that we used a curated dataset with a specific topic. Specifically, we collected a comparatively small, but topically focussed set of essays (with a high response rate from students) produced in a real-life context, rather than using ones from existing databases or online sources. This meant that our finding reflects both how our study participants write in practice and how they might use AI tools to generate essays. However, our study also has some limitations. The students who wrote the essays were not native English speakers; however, they were attending a fully English language-based medical programme during which they had also submitted essay-based tasks within each previous study year, meaning their experience with and knowledge of the language were sufficient. We also used ChatGPT 3.5 and Bard, although newer, fee-based versions were available at the time, such as ChatGPT 4.0. This choice was guided by the fact that they were both easily accessible to students for free; using paid versions of the LLMs might not be reflective of real-life situations, as we thought it unlikely that students would pay the fees needed to access the more advanced models. Furthermore, the two LLMs do have some inherent differences which have been highlighted in several previous studies. For example, a recent study found that ChatGPT generates essays with more complex sentences and wordings, while Bard generates more cohesive texts19. Other research prompting the two LLMs using a standardised set of closed- and open-ended questions found Bard generates lengthier, yet more accurate responses, which is seemingly in contrast with the study by Borji and Mohammadian, who found that ChatGPT generally outperforms Bard in terms of correctness of responses to a diverse set of questions29,33. Yet despite these divergences, researchers generally agree that both LLMs are highly capable of text and content generation, especially in the context of education19,20,29,33. Finally, although we were able to identify a certain number of AI-generated essays via AI detectors, we are unable to determine whether there were any false positives/negatives, or whether other students sufficiently tampered with AI-generated essays to make them undetectable. To minimise the impact of this limitation, we used two different types of AI detection software to identify such texts, while all student-written essays had also been previously independently checked by two human assessors.

Conclusions

AI shows a significant potential for application in writing personal, introspective essays on ethical dilemmas, despite the observed differences in language characteristics compared to student-written essays. This is especially true if subsequent modifications mask them and thus make AI detection software redundant or potentially ineffective. This has several implications for essay-based tasks; if the use of AI writing tools in higher education is prohibited or limited in the future, hand-written essays could be considered if educators aim to keep existing learning outcomes regarding writing skills and creativity. Further research could explore whether the linguistic differences identified in our study are consistent with assessments by human observers.

Methods

Data collection

We collected essays written by 5th and 6th year medical students at the University of Split School of Medicine (USSM) in 2023, enrolled in the Medicine in English programme. As part of a mandatory, graded task during the Medical Humanities V – Clinical Ethics IV (5th year) and Medical Humanities VI – Medical Ethics V (6th year) courses, the students were required to write an essay in which they reflected on an ethical, professional, or moral dilemma they encountered during their studies and clinical rotations at the USSM or during any clinical practice they may have had before enrolling at the USSM. The essays had to be 400–500 words in length, without headings or subheadings; they had to contain the students’ reflection on an ethical dilemma, and should not have contained any personal names or other identifiers for the persons involved in the described situation (Supplementary file 2).

One researcher (MK) introduced the students to the study during in-person teaching, informing them of the study goals and that their decision regarding participation would not affect their grades or studies in any way. They were also informed that the essays would be fully anonymised and analysed only at a group level. This information was reiterated within the informed consent form which they were encouraged to fill out at the point of submitting their graded task via the Merlin e-learning system, version 2022/2023 (Srce, Zagreb, Croatia). Reminders were sent via e-mail to each study year to fill out the form a day before the essays were due and again a week after the essay submission deadline. We excluded essays of students who did not provide informed consent or declined to participate.

Keyword extraction and prompt development

Two researchers (MK, MFŽ) independently extracted up to 14 keywords from each essay, after which they discussed discrepancies and jointly selected keywords (range = 12–14) that would be used to generate the prompts for ChatGPT and Bard. Two other researchers (LU, JK) then used the keywords to independently develop two levels of prompts: full prompts, which included all the keywords extracted in the first stage, and partial prompts, which contained half of those keywords. Aside from these keywords, all prompts contained the exact instructions that were given to the students within their task (Supplementary file 2). If there was initially an uneven number of keywords (e.g., n = 13), we rounded down the number of keywords for the partial prompt (e.g., n = 6).

After creating full and partial keyword prompts, the two researchers discussed discrepancies and selected the final versions of prompts for generating essays via ChatGPT, version 3.5, August 3, 2023 and Bard, version 2023.08.13 (labelled as AI-generated essays in this manuscript). We accessed the LLMs via their openly accessible user interface rather than their application programming interface to simulate how a student might access it in real life. After using each prompt, we deleted the previous chat history to avoid influence with prior prompts or generated essays. We continued this process until we generated a number of essays equal to that of student-written essays.

Detection of AI-generated essays

We used the publicly available, but paid versions of Originality.AI, version 2.0 (Originality.AI, Ontario, Canada) and GPTZero, version 2023 (Tian E and Cui A, GPTZero) software to detect if the student-written essays were either partially or fully written by AI software. We declared the student-written essays as being AI-written only if both AI detection software categorised them as being ≥ 50% AI-generated.

Analysis

We analysed the essays using the Language Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) 22 software, version 1.4.0 (Pennebaker Conglomerates, Austin, TX, USA), extracting the main LIWC summary measures and variables related to social and psychological processes (as detailed in the LIWC manual)34. We then compared these LIWC values between student-written and full-keyword AI-generated essays. We also conducted sub-analyses comparing the full-keyword AI-generated essays and student-written ones flagged as being potentially AI-cowritten; the “true” student-written essays (i.e. not flagged as AI-cowritten) with their AI-generated counterparts; and the “true” student-written essays with the potentially AI-cowritten student-written ones.

We presented categorical data as frequencies and percentages and the LIWC variables as medians with interquartile ranges. We used the Kruskal–Wallis test to compare the overall LIWC scores and conducted the Dwass-Steel-Critchlow-Fligner test for pairwise comparisons between each essay group. We used the Mann–Whitney U test in comparing any two groups and applied the post hoc Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

Ethical aspects

Basic information about the study and what participation entails was verbally presented by one of the study authors (MK) at the beginning of the courses during which the essays were given as a task to participants. We emphasised that study participation would in no way impact their overall course grade or our assessment of their essays. Students were once again presented with this information, along with a survey where they could either give or withhold their informed consent, on the University of Split School of Medicine’s Merlin e-learning platform, where they submitted their essay tasks. Only authors who were also course teachers and graded the essays (MK, MFŽ) had access to the non-anonymised essays; other authors who performed the analysis (LU, JK) accessed and used only the anonymised versions. All study methods were carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.
