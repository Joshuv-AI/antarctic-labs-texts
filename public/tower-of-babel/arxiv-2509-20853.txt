On the representation type of a finite tensor category
Petter Andreas Bergh, Karin Erdmann, Julia Yael Plavnik, Sarah Witherspoon

Abstract
. Given an exact module category over a finite tensor category with finitely generated cohomology, we show that if there exists an object of complexity at least three, then the category is of wild representation type. In particular, if the Krull dimension of the cohomology ring of the finite tensor category is at least three, then this category is wild.

16G60, 16T05, 18M05, 18M15

1.
Introduction

In 1990, Rickard
[
29
]
showed that representation type can be discernible via homological information, for example in finite group algebras. Almost twenty years later, Farnsteiner
[
19
]
realized this connection for finite dimensional cocommutative Hopf algebras using the support variety theory that had just been developed for these algebras. Specifically, he proved that such a Hopf algebra has wild representation type if its cohomology has Krull dimension at least three, or equivalently if it has a module of complexity at least three. The proof relies heavily on prior work of Drozd
[
12
]
and Crawley-Boevey
[
11
]
, in particular the key role played by the Auslander-Reiten translate. Farnsteiner’s techniques apply more generally to settings where cohomology is finitely generated and there is a well behaved support variety theory. Other authors used these techniques successfully for finite dimensional self-injective algebras
[
9
,
22
]
and braided finite dimensional Hopf algebras
[
21
]
. These three papers require cohomological finiteness conditions to ensure a robust support variety theory. In
[
9
]
, such a finiteness hypothesis is placed on the cohomology of a module, in
[
21
]
on Hopf algebra cohomology, and in
[
22
]
on Hochschild cohomology. In these papers, the results are applied to certain small quantum groups, Hecke algebras, Nichols algebras, and more. The theory has been further adapted to prove representation type results for Lie superalgebras
[
24
]
.

In this paper, we transport these methods to finite tensor categories and their finite module categories, applying the support variety theory developed in
[
6
,
8
]
. Our main result is Theorem
3.1
, stating that under a cohomological finite generation condition, such a module category is of wild representation type if it has an object of complexity at least three. Here, we define wild representation type in Section
2
as it is defined for categories of modules; each such category is indeed equivalent to a category of modules for a finite dimensional algebra. The proof of the theorem follows the plan of earlier proofs: support varieties are used to identify a one parameter family of nonisomorphic indecomposable objects (of some fixed length), each of complexity at least two. Among the key ingredients are objects generalizing Carlson’s
L_{\zeta}
-modules, their action on the module category, and an application of Crawley-Boevey’s results to our category setting (Lemma
2.3
).

As mentioned above, finite tensor categories and their finite module categories are known to be equivalent to categories of modules for finite dimensional algebras. We recall the needed details in Remark
2.1
(3) below and use this fact in Lemma
2.3
. One distinction between our work here and past results on finite dimensional algebras is in the hypotheses; finiteness conditions here are imposed on the cohomology of the acting category. Any of its finite module categories then enjoys the representation type consequences of that action.

Several classes of examples are included to illustrate the theory: In Example
2.4
, we work in positive characteristic
p
and explore some local algebras, focusing particularly on some connected Hopf algebras of dimension
p^{3}
. In Examples
3.3
and
3.4
, we describe how the theory applies to higher Verlinde categories and crossed product categories.

2.
Representation type and complexity

Throughout this section, we fix an algebraically closed field
k
. We start by recalling the notion of representation type of a finite dimensional
k
-algebra
A
. Unless otherwise specified, whenever we speak of modules, we mean finitely generated left modules. The category of such
A
-modules is denoted by
\operatorname{mod}A
.

The algebra
A
has
finite representation type
if it has only finitely many isomorphism classes of indecomposable modules. In this case, then, one can classify all the indecomposable modules by simply listing them. If
A
does not have finite representation type, then it is of
infinite representation type
. A classification of the indecomposable modules may still be possible, if
A
has
tame representation type
. This means that the representation type is infinite, but for each integer
d\geq 1
the following holds: almost all the indecomposable modules of dimension
d
belong to one of a finite number of one-parameter families. Specifically, for each
d\geq 1
there exists a finite set
B_{1},\dots,B_{n_{d}}
of
A
-
k[x]
-bimodules satisfying the following: each
B_{i}
is free of rank
d
as a module over
k[x]
, and every
A
-module of dimension
d
– except possibly finitely many – is isomorphic to
B_{i}\otimes_{k[x]}k[x]/(x-\alpha)
for some
i
and
\alpha\in k
. Note that since
k
is algebraically closed, the
k[x]
-modules of the form
k[x]/(x-\alpha)
are precisely the simple ones.

If
A
is of infinite representation type but not tame, then a classification of indecomposable modules is beyond reach. Denote by
k\langle x,y\rangle
the free algebra generated by two non-commuting variables
x
and
y
. The algebra
A
has
wild representation type
if there exists an exact embedding from the category of finite dimensional
k\langle x,y\rangle
-modules into
\operatorname{mod}A
, in such a way that the image of an indecomposable
k\langle x,y\rangle
-module is indecomposable in
\operatorname{mod}A
. It is well-known (see
[
31
, Theorem XIX.1.11]
) that this is equivalent to the following: for
every
finite dimensional
k
-algebra
B
, there exists such an embedding from
\operatorname{mod}B
into
\operatorname{mod}A
. In other words, a finite dimensional
k
-algebra is wild if its module category contains the module category of every finite dimensional
k
-algebra.

It turns out that the two kinds of algebras of infinite representation type that we have discussed are actually all there are. This is a highly nontrivial fact, and was proved by Drozd (see
[
12
]
and
[
11
]
). Specifically, he proved that if a finite dimensional algebra over an algebraically closed field is of infinite representation type, then it is either tame or wild, and these are mutually exclusive.

We now turn to the categories that are of interest to us: finite tensor categories and their module categories. For a detailed explanation of the terminology and concepts that follow, we refer to
[
16
]
. A finite tensor category over
k
is a triple
\left(\mathscr{C},\otimes,\mathbf{1}\right)
, where
\mathscr{C}
is a locally finite
k
-linear abelian category having finitely many isomorphism classes of simple objects, and each of these has a projective cover. Moreover, the tensor product
\otimes
is a bifunctor
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{C}\times\mathscr{C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{C}}
\otimes

which is bilinear on morphisms and associative up to functorial isomorphisms. Furthermore, the object
\mathbf{1}\in\mathscr{C}
is a unit object with respect to the tensor product, and the triple
\left(\mathscr{C},\otimes,\mathbf{1}\right)
is a monoidal category. Finally, the unit object is simple, and
\mathscr{C}
is rigid in the sense that every object has a left and a right dual. A whole range of important properties follow from the latter, and here are three of them: the category
\mathscr{C}
is quasi-Frobenius (that is, the projective and the injective objects coincide), the projective objects form a two-sided ideal, and the tensor product is bi-exact (see
[
16
, Proposition 6.1.3, Proposition 4.2.12, Proposition 4.2.1]
).

Now let
\left(\mathscr{C},\otimes,\mathbf{1}\right)
be such a finite tensor category, and
\left(\mathscr{M},\ast\right)
a left module category over
\mathscr{C}
. Thus
\mathscr{M}
is a locally finite
k
-linear abelian category, together with a bifunctor (the module product)
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{C}\times\mathscr{M}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{M}}
\ast

which, as with
\otimes
, is bilinear on morphisms and associative up to functorial isomorphisms:
(A\otimes B)\ast M\simeq A\ast(B\ast M)
for
A,B\in\mathscr{C}
and
M\in\mathscr{M}
. Furthermore, the unit object
\mathbf{1}\in\mathscr{C}
is a unit object also for the module product, and this product is exact in the first argument. It follows from
[
16
, Proposition 7.1.6]
that the module product is also exact in the second argument, and therefore bi-exact. Moreover, it follows from
loc. cit
. that if
P
is a projective object in
\mathscr{M}
, then
A\ast P
is projective for every
A\in\mathscr{C}
. The module category is called
exact
if the latter holds with opposite arguments: if
Q
is a projective object in
\mathscr{C}
, then
Q\ast M
is projective for every
M\in\mathscr{M}
. When this holds, then
\mathscr{M}
is quasi-Frobenius, by
[
16
, Corollary 7.6.4]
, and it also has enough projective objects, by
[
16
, Lemma 7.6.1]
. Finally, we say that
\mathscr{M}
is
finite
if it has finitely many isomorphism classes of simple objects.

The typical example of a finite tensor category is
\operatorname{mod}H
, where
H
is a finite-dimensional Hopf algebra over
k
. The tensor product is then not necessarily commutative. However, if
G
is a finite group, then the tensor product in
\operatorname{mod}kG
is commutative up to natural isomorphism, and in a strong sense, making this a
symmetric
finite tensor category (that is, the natural isomorphism squares to the identity). Note that every finite tensor category is trivially a finite exact module category over itself, with the tensor product as module product.

In the following remark, we collect some facts that will be of use to us throughout. Let us therefore fix a finite tensor category
\left(\mathscr{C},\otimes,\mathbf{1}\right)
, together with a finite exact
\mathscr{C}
-module category
\left(\mathscr{M},\ast\right)
.

Remark 2.1
.

(1) One of the defining properties of
\left(\mathscr{C},\otimes,\mathbf{1}\right)
is that every simple object

has a projective cover. This is defined in

as an epimorphism

is an epimorphism with

. One can show that every object admits such a cover, and that the cover map is right minimal (see, e.g.

To see this, take any object

, and let

be an epimorphism with

, there exists an object

, together with a commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces N}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}

g

in which the morphism

is right minimal. This morphism must be an epimorphism, since

is and

. Moreover, by the right minimality of

(2) Let

be a complete set of representatives for the isomorphism classes of simple objects in

must be indecomposable, and these are all the indecomposable projective objects in

is a direct sum of (some of) the

(3) By the above, the module category

has projective generators, that is, projective objects

with the property that every projective object in

is a direct summand of a direct sum of copies of

is one such generator. Denote the finite dimensional

are projective generators in

In light of the last remark, we make the following definition.

Definition
.

Let
\left(\mathscr{C},\otimes,\mathbf{1}\right)
be a finite tensor category over an algebraically closed field
k
, and
\left(\mathscr{M},\ast\right)
a finite exact
\mathscr{C}
-module category. The
representation type
of
\mathscr{M}
is the representation type of the finite dimensional
k
-algebra
\mbox{\rm End\,}_{\mathscr{M}}(P)^{\operatorname{op}}
, where
P
is a projective generator in
\mathscr{M}
.

Thus
\mathscr{M}
has finite representation type if the number of isomorphism classes of indecomposable objects is finite; a typical example is a fusion category. If
\mathscr{M}
is tame, then by definition almost all the indecomposable
\mbox{\rm End\,}_{\mathscr{M}}(P)^{\operatorname{op}}
-modules of any fixed dimension belong to a finite number of one-parameter families (with
P
a projective generator in
\mathscr{M}
). For every
\mbox{\rm End\,}_{\mathscr{M}}(P)^{\operatorname{op}}
-module
X
, there are inequalities

\ell(X)\leq\dim_{k}X\leq m\ell(X)

where
\ell(X)
denotes the length, and
m
is the maximum dimension of the simple modules. Therefore, for the category
\mathscr{M}
itself, tameness means the following: for any fixed length
d
, there is a finite collection

\{M^{1}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in k},\dots,\{M^{n_{d}}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in k}

of one-parameter families of objects – not all of which are necessarily of length
d
– such that almost all the objects of
\mathscr{M}
of length
d
belong to these families (up to isomorphism). Namely, just take all the one-parameter families of
\mbox{\rm End\,}_{\mathscr{M}}(P)^{\operatorname{op}}
-modules of dimension at most
dm
, and consider their images in
\mathscr{M}
under the equivalence.

If
\mathscr{M}
is wild, then for every finite dimensional
k
-algebra
B
, there exists an exact embedding
\operatorname{mod}B\longrightarrow\operatorname{mod}\mbox{\rm End\,}_{\mathscr{M}}(P)^{\operatorname{op}}
that preserves indecomposability. Since
\operatorname{mod}\mbox{\rm End\,}_{\mathscr{M}}(P)^{\operatorname{op}}
is equivalent to
\mathscr{M}
, we obtain such an embedding
\operatorname{mod}B\longrightarrow\mathscr{M}
as well. In particular, this means that given any finite exact module category
\mathscr{M}^{\prime}
over any finite tensor
k
-category
\mathscr{C}^{\prime}
, there exists such an embedding
\mathscr{M}^{\prime}\longrightarrow\mathscr{M}
, since
\mathscr{M}^{\prime}
is equivalent to the category of modules over
\mbox{\rm End\,}_{\mathscr{M}^{\prime}}(P^{\prime})^{\operatorname{op}}
, for some (equivalently, every) projective generator
P^{\prime}\in\mathscr{M}^{\prime}
. Therefore, when the module category
\mathscr{M}
is wild, then it contains every finite exact module category.

Remark 2.2
.

When
H
is a finite dimensional Hopf algebra over
k
, then the representation type of the finite tensor category
\operatorname{mod}H
is the same as the representation type of
H
as a

is a projective generator in
\operatorname{mod}H
.

Next, we recall a concept which will play a key role in the main result. As recalled in Remark

admits a projective cover, and therefore also a minimal projective resolution
(P_{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}},d_{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}})

. The

; we denote it by

\operatorname{cx}_{\mathscr{M}}(M)=\inf\{c\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\mid\exists b\in\mathbb{R}\text{ and }m\in{\mathbb{N}}\text{ with }\ell_{\mathscr{M}}(P_{n})\leq bn^{c-1}\text{ for all }n\geq m\}

where

denotes the length of objects in

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field,
\left(\mathscr{C},\otimes,\mathbf{1}\right)
a finite tensor category over
k
, and
\left(\mathscr{M},\ast\right)
a finite exact
\mathscr{C}
-module category. Suppose there exists an integer
d
for which the following holds: there exist infinitely many non-isomorphic indecomposable objects
M\in\mathscr{M}
of length
d
with
\operatorname{cx}_{\mathscr{M}}(M)\geq 2
. Then
\mathscr{M}
is of wild representation type.

Proof.

Let
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}
be a complete set of representatives of the isomorphism classes of indecomposable projective objects in
\mathscr{M}
, and denote the finite dimensional
k
-algebra
\mbox{\rm End\,}_{\mathscr{M}}(P_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus P_{n})^{\operatorname{op}}
by
\Gamma
. By Remark
2.1
(3), the category
\operatorname{mod}\Gamma
is equivalent to
\mathscr{M}
, hence
\Gamma
is selfinjective since
\mathscr{M}
is quasi-Frobenius. Moreover, as
P_{i}\nsimeq P_{j}
when
i\neq j
, the algebra
\Gamma
is basic, and therefore a Frobenius algebra by
[
32
, Proposition IV.3.9]
. Denote by
\nu
its Nakayama automorphism, which is unique up to composition with an inner automorphism. By
[
32
, Proposition IV.3.13 and Theorem IV.8.5]
, the Auslander-Reiten translate
\tau
is naturally isomorphic to
\Omega_{\Gamma}^{2}({{}_{\nu}(-)})
as functors on the stable module category
\operatorname{\underline{mod}}\Gamma
, where the latter is the functor that maps a module
N
to
\Omega_{\Gamma}^{2}({{}_{\nu}N})
. Here
{{}_{\nu}N}
is the twisted
\Gamma
-module that equals
N
as a
k
-vector space, but with module operation
x\cdot m=\nu(x)m
for
x\in\Gamma
and
m\in N
.

Since
\mathscr{M}
is equivalent to
\operatorname{mod}\Gamma
, the assumptions in the statement imply that there exists an integer
d
for which the following holds: there exist infinitely many non-isomorphic indecomposable
\Gamma
-modules
N
of length
d
, with
\operatorname{cx}_{\Gamma}(N)\geq 2
. Let

be the maximum of the dimensions (as

-vector spaces) of the simple
\Gamma
-modules. Then
\dim_{k}N\leq m\ell_{\Gamma}(N)
for every

, so there must exist an integer

for which the following holds: there exist infinitely many non-isomorphic indecomposable
\Gamma
-modules

with
\dim_{k}N=d^{\prime}
, and with
\operatorname{cx}_{\Gamma}(N)\geq 2
. For each such module

, necessarily

. If not, then
\Omega_{\Gamma}^{2}({{}_{\nu}N})
and

are isomorphic in

, and since projective covers commute with automorphism twistings of any kind, it follows that
\Omega_{\Gamma}^{2}(N)\simeq{{}_{\nu}N}
. Then by induction

, and consequently

. By

We end this section with a rather long example illustrating the lemma. For finite dimensional Hopf algebras other than group algebras of finite groups, it is not generally understood which ones are of tame representation type. Even for restricted enveloping algebras of Lie algebras, there is at present no general classification; although for example
[
18
]
and
[
20
]
give classifications in many settings, it is usually assumed that the characteristic is not two. Of the known cases, most tame Hopf algebras are special biserial, for example blocks of restricted enveloping algebras of
\mathfrak{sl}(2)
, or Drinfeld doubles of Taft algebras.

Example 2.4
.

Assume that the characteristic of the (algebraically closed) field
k
is
p\geq 2
, and let
A
be a finite dimensional local
k
-algebra with two independent generators
x
and
y
. That is,
x
and
y
are in the Jacobson radical
\operatorname{rad}(A)
and are linearly independent modulo
\operatorname{rad}(A)^{2}
. There is a one-parameter family
\{M_{\lambda}\mid\lambda\in k\}
of two dimensional
A
-modules, with
x
and
y
acting on
M_{\lambda}
via the two matrices

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\lambda\\
0&0\end{array}\right)\hskip 8.53581pt\text{and}\hskip 8.53581pt\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
0&0\end{array}\right)

respectively. These modules are indecomposable since, for example, the latter is an indecomposable Jordan block. One also checks that
M_{\lambda}\nsimeq M_{\mu}
when
\lambda\neq\mu
. Consequently, if
A
is a Frobenius algebra, and
\operatorname{cx}_{A}(M_{\lambda})\geq 2
for each (or infinitely many)

, then
A
is of wild representation type, as shown in the proof of Lemma
2.3
.

Suppose, for example, that
A=kG
, the group algebra of a finite group

. If
\operatorname{cx}_{A}(M_{\lambda})=1
, then
M_{\lambda}
must be a periodic module; see
[
3
, Theorem 5.10.4]
. Since
M_{\lambda}
is then also periodic when restricted to a subgroup, in particular an elementary abelian subgroup of rank one, the dimension of
M_{\lambda}
must be divisible by
p
. This cannot happen when

, and so in this case
\operatorname{cx}_{A}(M_{\lambda})\geq 2
for all
\lambda\in k
. The algebra
A
is therefore wild when

. More generally, for such

, a group algebra

is wild whenever

has a factor group isomorphic to
C_{p}\times C_{p}
. Namely, the group algebra of the latter is isomorphic to
k[x,y]/(x^{p},y^{p})
, and an algebra having a wild factor algebra is itself wild.

Assume now that

. The modules constructed above may then have complexity one. For example, consider the algebra

k\langle x,y\rangle/(x^{2},y^{2},xyxy-yxyx)

which is isomorphic to the group algebra of the dihedral group of order

. This algebra is tame (see
[
14
, Chapter III]
), and in this case, the modules

are periodic, of period two (see
[

, Theorem in Appendix]
).

For our local algebra

, we now construct a new one-parameter family
\{N_{\lambda}\mid\lambda\in k\}
of

-modules, this time of dimension three. Note first that both the generators

and

are nilpotent; let us restrict ourselves to the case

. If also
x^{2}=0
, then
A
is special biserial, and therefore tame; see
[
34
, Corollary 2.4]
. Assume therefore that
x
has nilpotency index

, and also that
x
and
y
commute. Now define
N_{\lambda}
by letting

and
y
act via the two matrices

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&\lambda&0\\
0&0&\lambda\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right)\hskip 8.53581pt\text{and}\hskip 8.53581pt\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&0&1\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right)

respectively. When restricted to the subalgebra generated by

, this module is indecomposable, hence it is also indecomposable over

. Moreover, as for the modules

, it can be shown that

when

. Finally, it can be shown that, since

, these modules are not periodic, and that our assumptions on the algebra

then forces them to be of complexity at least two. The algebra is therefore wild. Note that

is isomorphic to a factor algebra of the group algebra of any abelian

, and hence such group algebras are also wild.

As a concrete application, let us look at connected Hopf algebras of dimension

. An almost complete classification of these algebras was given in

, the exception being those for which the primitive space is a two dimensional abelian restricted Lie algebra. We shall determine the representation type of those algebras from the classification that are isomorphic to enveloping algebras of restricted Lie algebras; they are the ones labelled by C5, C6 and C15 in

]
, see

. In each case, the Lie algebra

.

For the algebra C5, the element

is central in

-map is trivial. Then
A=U^{[p]}(L)
has the presentation

k\langle x,y\rangle/(x^{p},y^{p},(xy-yx)^{p},xy-yx\text{ is central})

Let
\mathfrak{a}
be the ideal of
A
generated by the element
xy-yx
. Then
A/\mathfrak{a}
is isomorphic to
k[x,y]/(x^{p},y^{p})
, which again is isomorphic to the group algebra of
C_{p}\times C_{p}
. When
p\geq 3
, this algebra is wild, and then so is
A
. When
p=2
, then
A
is isomorphic to the group algebra of the dihedral group of order
8
, and therefore tame.

For the algebra C6, the commutator
[x,y]
is again central. In this case, the algebra
A=U^{[p]}(L)
has the presentation

k\langle x,y\rangle/(y^{p},x^{p}-(xy-yx),(xy-yx)^{p},xy-yx\text{ is central})

As in the previous case, with
\mathfrak{a}
the ideal of
A
generated by the element
xy-yx
, the algebra
A/\mathfrak{a}
is isomorphic to
k[x,y]/(x^{p},y^{p})
. Thus
A
is wild when
p\geq 3
. When
p=2
, then by changing the generators from
x,y
to
x+y,y
we see that also here
A
is isomorphic to the group algebra of the dihedral group of order
8
, and therefore tame.

Finally, for the algebra C15, the Lie algebra is
\mathfrak{sl}(2)
, and its restricted enveloping algebra is tame. It has one simple block, and all other blocks are Morita equivalent to Kronecker algebras; see for example
[
28
]
or
[
23
]
.

3.
The main result

As in the previous section, we fix an algebraically closed field
k
. Furthermore, let us fix a finite tensor category
\left(\mathscr{C},\otimes,\mathbf{1}\right)
over
k
, together with a finite exact
\mathscr{C}
-module category
\left(\mathscr{M},\ast\right)
.

For objects
M,N\in\mathscr{M}
, we shall denote the graded
k
-vector space
\oplus_{n=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathscr{M}}^{n}(M,N)
by
\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathscr{M}}^{*}(M,N)
, and similarly for objects in
\mathscr{C}
. The
cohomology ring
of
\mathscr{C}
is
\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathscr{C}}^{*}(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{1})
, which we denote by
\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{*}(\mathscr{C})
. Since the module product is exact in the first variable, there is a homomorphism
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{*}(\mathscr{C})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathscr{M}}^{*}(M,M)}
\varphi_{M}

of graded
k
-algebras, induced by
-\ast M
. This makes
\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathscr{M}}^{*}(M,N)
into a right
\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{*}(\mathscr{C})
-module, via
\varphi_{M}
followed by Yoneda composition. Similarly, it becomes a left
\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{*}(\mathscr{C})
-module via
\varphi_{N}
. By
[
8
, Corollary 2.3]
, the left and the right module structures coincide up to a sign for homogeneous elements. In particular, by taking
\mathscr{M}=\mathscr{C}
and
M=N=\mathbf{1}
, we see that the cohomology ring
\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{*}(\mathscr{C})
is graded-commutative; this was proved in a more general setting in
[
33
, Theorem 1.7]
. It was conjectured by Etingof and Ostrik in
[
17
]
that
\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{*}(\mathscr{C})
is always finitely generated as a
k
-algebra, and that
\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathscr{C}}^{*}(X,X)
is a finitely generated
\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{*}(\mathscr{C})
-module for every object
X\in\mathscr{C}
. This conjecture is still open, and so we therefore make the following definition.

Definition
.

The finite tensor category
\left(\mathscr{C},\otimes,\mathbf{1}\right)
satisfies the
finiteness condition

Fg
if
\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{*}(\mathscr{C})
is finitely generated, and
\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathscr{C}}^{*}(X,X)
is a finitely generated
\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{*}(\mathscr{C})
-module for every object
X\in\mathscr{C}
.

Note that this finiteness condition only involves the objects of
\mathscr{C}
. A priori, then, it has nothing to do with the module category
\mathscr{M}
and the cohomology of its objects. However, by
[
26
, Proposition 3.5]
, when
Fg
holds for
\mathscr{C}
, then
\mathscr{M}
satisfies a finiteness property on cohomology as well: for every object
M\in\mathscr{M}
, the
\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{*}(\mathscr{C})
-module
\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathscr{M}}^{*}(M,M)
is finitely generated as a module.

When
Fg
holds for
\mathscr{C}
, then we obtain a quite powerful theory of support varieties for the objects of
\mathscr{M}
, as explored in
[
6
]
and
[
8
]
. In the proof of the main result below, we shall make use of these varieties, so we recall here some definitions.

Definition
.

(1) We define

\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{*}(\mathscr{C})&\text{if the characteristic of $k$ is two,}\\
\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{2*}(\mathscr{C})&\text{if not.}\end{array}\right.

Furthermore, we define
\mathfrak{m}_{0}
to be
\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{+}(\mathscr{C})
, that is, the ideal in
\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})
generated by the homogeneous elements of positive degrees.

(2) For an ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})
, we denote by
Z(\mathfrak{a})
the set of all maximal ideals in
\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})
containing
\mathfrak{a}
.

(3) For an object
M\in\mathscr{M}
, we define
\mathfrak{a}_{M}
to be the annihilator ideal of
\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathscr{M}}^{*}(M,M)
in
\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})
, and the
support variety

V_{\mathscr{M}}(M)
to be
\{\mathfrak{m}_{0}\}\cup Z(\mathfrak{a}_{M})
.

Some comments are in order here. First, note that
\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})
is commutative in the ordinary sense, since
\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{*}(\mathscr{C})
is graded-commutative. The latter also implies that when the characteristic of
k
is not
2
, then the homogeneous elements of odd degrees square to zero. So when passing from
\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{*}(\mathscr{C})
to
\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})
, we gain ordinary commutativity and get rid of only nilpotent elements. We do not lose anything in relation to the support variety theory; note that the definition of the finiteness condition
Fg
is equivalent to the version where we replace
\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{*}(\mathscr{C})
by
\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})
.

Next, note that since
k
is algebraically closed, the degree zero part of
\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})
, which is
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathscr{C}}(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{1})
, is just
k
, because the unit object is simple. Therefore
\mathfrak{m}_{0}
is a maximal ideal in
\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})
, and it is the unique graded maximal ideal. Now given any object
M\in\mathscr{M}
, the annihilator ideal
\mathfrak{a}_{M}
is a graded ideal of
\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})
, and is therefore contained in
\mathfrak{m}_{0}
as long as
\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathscr{M}}^{*}(M,M)
is nonzero. The latter is the case if and only if
M
itself is nonzero, and so we see that the explicit inclusion of
\mathfrak{m}_{0}
in the definition of the support variety
V_{\mathscr{M}}(M)
only matters when
M=0
.

Finally, note that when the assumption
Fg
holds, then the complexity of the objects of
\mathscr{M}
is an invariant with several nice properties, as shown in
[
8
, Theorem 2.7]
. For example, the complexity of an object
M\in\mathscr{M}
is finite and equal to the dimension of the support variety
V_{\mathscr{M}}(M)
, which by definition is
\operatorname{Kdim}\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})/\mathfrak{a}_{M}
(here
\operatorname{Kdim}
denotes the Krull dimension). In particular,
\operatorname{cx}_{\mathscr{M}}(M)
is at most
\operatorname{Kdim}\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})
.

Now to the main result. It states that if
Fg
holds and
\mathscr{M}
contains an object of complexity three or more, then
\mathscr{M}
is of wild representation type. Compare with
[
9
, Theorem 4.1]
for selfinjective algebras: If
\Gamma
is a finite dimensional selfinjective algebra over
k
that has a module
M
with complexity at least three, and
\operatorname{Ext}^{*}_{\Gamma}(M,M)
contains a finitely generated subalgebra over which it is a finitely generated module, then
\Gamma
has wild representation type. Compare also with
[
22
, Theorem 2.1]
, an analogous result for categories of modules of selfinjective algebras satisfying instead a finiteness condition on Hochschild cohomology.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field,
\left(\mathscr{C},\otimes,\mathbf{1}\right)
a finite tensor category over
k
, and
\left(\mathscr{M},\ast\right)
a finite exact
\mathscr{C}
-module category. If
Fg
holds, and there exists an object
M\in\mathscr{M}
with
\operatorname{cx}_{\mathscr{M}}(M)\geq 3
, then the category
\mathscr{M}
is of wild representation type.

Proof.

Suppose
Fg
holds and there is an object of complexity at least 3 in
\mathscr{M}
. By the reduction of complexity result
[
8
, Theorem 2.7(6)]
, there exists an object
M\in\mathscr{M}
with
\operatorname{cx}_{\mathscr{M}}(M)=3
. Furthermore, as explained in the paragraph following
[
8
, Corollary 3.2]
, the set of minimal primes lying over the annihilator ideal
\mathfrak{a}_{M}
in
\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})
is finite, and they are all graded; let us denote them by
\mathfrak{r}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{r}_{n}
. Then
V_{\mathscr{M}}(M)=Z(\mathfrak{r}_{1})\cup\cdots\cup Z(\mathfrak{r}_{n})
, and since
\operatorname{Kdim}\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})/\mathfrak{a}_{M}=3
, one of these primes, say
\mathfrak{r}_{1}
, is of coheight three.

Since
\mathfrak{r}_{1}
is graded, it is of the form
(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{t})
for some homogeneous nonzero elements
\zeta_{i}\in\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})
, say of positive degrees
n_{i}
. Each
\zeta_{i}
corresponds to a nonzero morphism
\Omega_{\mathscr{C}}^{n_{i}}(\mathbf{1})\longrightarrow\mathbf{1}
, which must necessarily be an epimorphism since
\mathbf{1}
is a simple object. Denote the kernel of this morphism by
L_{\zeta_{i}}
. By
[
8
, Theorem 2.7(3)]
, there are equalities

\displaystyle V_{\mathscr{M}}\left((L_{\zeta_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes L_{\zeta_{t}})\ast M\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Z(\zeta_{1})\cap\cdots\cap Z(\zeta_{t})\cap V_{\mathscr{M}}(M)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Z(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{t})\cap V_{\mathscr{M}}(M)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Z(\mathfrak{r}_{1})\cap V_{\mathscr{M}}(M)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Z(\mathfrak{r}_{1})

and so we see that the object
(L_{\zeta_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes L_{\zeta_{t}})\ast M
is also of complexity
3
. By replacing
M
with this object, we may without loss of generality suppose that
V_{\mathscr{M}}(M)
is irreducible, that is, there is a unique minimal (graded) prime
\mathfrak{p}
lying over
\mathfrak{a}_{M}
in
\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})
.

Consider the ring
\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})/\mathfrak{p}
. Since its Krull dimension is three, the Noether normalization lemma applies: There is a polynomial ring
S=k[w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}]\subseteq\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})/\mathfrak{p}
over which
\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})/\mathfrak{p}
is a finitely generated module. Moreover, we may suppose that the elements
w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}
are homogeneous, and have positive degrees. The ideal
\mathfrak{p}
is contained in the ideal
\mathfrak{m}_{0}
generated by all homogeneous elements of positive degree, and so the degree zero part of
\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})/\mathfrak{p}
is
k
. Now from standard commutative algebra (see, for example,
[
1
, Chapter 5]
), we obtain some important properties. First of all, since the ring extension is integral, the going-up property holds, and the contraction map
\operatorname{Spec}\nolimits\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})/\mathfrak{p}\longrightarrow\operatorname{Spec}\nolimits S
is surjective. Moreover, since both
S
and
\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})/\mathfrak{p}
are integral domains, and
S
is integrally closed in its field of fractions (it is a unique factorization domain), the going-down property holds as well.

Let
\mathfrak{q}_{1}
be a prime ideal in
\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})/\mathfrak{p}
with
\mathfrak{q}_{1}\cap S=(w_{1})
. By the going-up property, there is a chain of primes
\mathfrak{q}_{1}\subset\mathfrak{q}_{2}\subset\mathfrak{q}_{3}
in
\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})/\mathfrak{p}
with
\mathfrak{q}_{2}\cap S=(w_{1},w_{2})
and
\mathfrak{q}_{3}\cap S=(w_{1},w_{2},w_{3})
. Each
\mathfrak{q}_{i}
is of the form
\mathfrak{p}_{i}/\mathfrak{p}
for a prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}_{i}\subseteq\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})
, and so we obtain a chain

\mathfrak{p}\subset\mathfrak{p}_{1}\subset\mathfrak{p}_{2}\subset\mathfrak{p}_{3}

in
\operatorname{Spec}\nolimits\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})
. Now, as elements of
\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})/\mathfrak{p}
, write each of
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
as
w_{i}=\eta_{i}+\mathfrak{p}
for some homogeneous element
\eta_{i}\in\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})
of positive degree. Then since
w_{1}\in\mathfrak{q}_{1}\setminus\{0\}
and
w_{2}\in\mathfrak{q}_{2}\setminus\mathfrak{q}_{1}
, we see that
\eta_{1}\in\mathfrak{p}_{1}\setminus\mathfrak{p}
and
\eta_{2}\in\mathfrak{p}_{2}\setminus\mathfrak{p}_{1}
. Choose positive integers
s,t
such that the homogeneous elements
\eta_{1}^{s},\eta_{2}^{t}
are of the same degree, and let
\alpha\in k

\eta=\eta_{1}^{s}+\alpha\eta_{2}^{t}
and consider the object

M_{\alpha}=L_{\eta}\ast M

where

is defined in the same way as the objects
L_{\zeta_{i}}
from the beginning of the proof. We shall prove that
\operatorname{cx}_{\mathscr{M}}(M_{\alpha})=2
for every
\alpha\in k

First note that there are equalities

(
\dagger

Z(\mathfrak{a}_{M_{\alpha}})=V_{\mathscr{M}}(M_{\alpha})=Z(\eta)\cap V_{\mathscr{M}}(M)=Z(\eta)\cap Z(\mathfrak{a}_{M})=Z\left(\mathfrak{a}_{M}+(\eta)\right),

In general, the complexity of a direct sum of objects equals the maximum of the complexities of the summands. Therefore, the indecomposable summands of

Z(\mathfrak{a}_{N_{\alpha}})=V_{\mathscr{M}}(N_{\alpha})=V_{\mathscr{M}}(N_{\beta})=Z(\mathfrak{a}_{N_{\beta}}),

as a consequence of Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz. Choose a chain

\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{\prime}\subset\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{\prime}\subset\mathfrak{q}_{3}^{\prime}
in
\operatorname{Spec}\nolimits\left(\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})/\mathfrak{p}\right)
, with
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{\prime}
since
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in\mathfrak{p}_{1}^{\prime}
. Then
\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{\prime}\cap S
is a prime ideal in
S
containing both
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
, and so there is a chain
0\subset(w_{1})\subset(\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{\prime}\cap S)
in
\operatorname{Spec}\nolimits S
. By the going-down property, there is a corresponding chain
0\subset\mathfrak{q}_{0}^{\prime}\subset q_{1}^{\prime}
in
\operatorname{Spec}\nolimits\left(\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})/\mathfrak{p}\right)
, with
\mathfrak{q}_{0}^{\prime}\cap S=(w_{1})
. This gives a chain

0\subset\mathfrak{q}_{0}^{\prime}\subset q_{1}^{\prime}\subset\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{\prime}\subset\mathfrak{q}_{3}^{\prime}

in
\operatorname{Spec}\nolimits\left(\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})/\mathfrak{p}\right)
, so that
\operatorname{Kdim}\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})/\mathfrak{p}\geq 4
. However, the Krull dimension of
\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})/\mathfrak{p}
is three, and so we have reached a contradiction. This shows that
N_{\alpha}\nsimeq N_{\beta}
when
\alpha\neq\beta
.

Finally, consider the length of each object
N_{\alpha}
. First of all, since this object is a summand of
M_{\alpha}
, we obtain the inequality
\ell_{\mathscr{M}}(N_{\alpha})\leq\ell_{\mathscr{M}}(M_{\alpha})
. Now recall that
M_{\alpha}=L_{\eta_{1}^{s}+\alpha\eta_{2}^{t}}\ast M
, where
L_{\eta_{1}^{s}+\alpha\eta_{2}^{t}}
is the kernel of an epimorphism
\Omega_{\mathscr{C}}^{n}(\mathbf{1})\longrightarrow\mathbf{1}
in
\mathscr{C}
, with
n
the degree of
\eta_{1}^{s}+\alpha\eta_{2}^{t}
. The module product
-\ast M
is exact, so from the exact sequence
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L_{\eta_{1}^{s}+\alpha\eta_{2}^{t}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega_{\mathscr{C}}^{n}(\mathbf{1})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}

in
\mathscr{C}
, we obtain an exact sequence
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M_{\alpha}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega_{\mathscr{C}}^{n}(\mathbf{1})\ast M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}

in
\mathscr{M}
. Consequently, we see that
\ell_{\mathscr{M}}(N_{\alpha})\leq\ell_{\mathscr{M}}(\Omega_{\mathscr{C}}^{n}(\mathbf{1})\ast M)
for every
\alpha\in k
. As
k
is infinite, this implies that there exists an integer
d
with the property that
\ell_{\mathscr{M}}(N_{\alpha})=d
for infinitely many elements
\alpha\in k
. Therefore, by Lemma
2.3
, the module category
\mathscr{M}
is of wild representation type.
∎

When
Fg
holds, by
[
6
, Theorem 4.1]
, the complexity of the unit object
\mathbf{1}\in\mathscr{C}
equals the Krull dimension of
\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})
, and
\operatorname{cx}_{\mathscr{C}}(X)\leq\operatorname{cx}_{\mathscr{C}}(\mathbf{1})
for every object
X\in\mathscr{C}
. Consequently, we see that
\operatorname{Kdim}\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})\geq 3
if, and only if, there exists an object
X\in\mathscr{C}
with
\operatorname{cx}_{\mathscr{C}}(X)\geq 3
. We therefore obtain the following corollary of Theorem
3.1
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field and
\left(\mathscr{C},\otimes,\mathbf{1}\right)
a finite tensor category over
k
. If
Fg
holds, and
\operatorname{Kdim}\operatorname{\operatorname{H}\nolimits^{\begin{picture}(1.0,1.0)\put(2.0,3.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}}\nolimits(\mathscr{C})\geq 3
, then the category
\mathscr{C}
is of wild representation type.

We end by illustrating the above theorem and corollary with examples of higher Verlinde categories and crossed product categories.

Example 3.3
.

(Higher Verlinde categories.)
Let
k
be an algebraically closed field of positive characteristic
p
,
and let
n
be a positive integer.
Let
\mathscr{C}=\Ver_{p^{n}}
be a Verlinde category (see for example
[
4
,
10
]
for
definitions).
These are incompressible finite tensor categories of moderate growth.
Their importance is due to the result of
Coulembier, Etingof, and Ostrik
[
10
]
that every pretannakian category
of moderate growth has a tensor functor to an incompressible category of
moderate growth. In positive characteristic, the latter are conjectured to be
precisely the subcategories of these Verlinde categories (including
\Ver_{p^{\infty}}
)
as classified in
[
5
, Corollary 4.6]
.
The
n=1
case
\Ver_{p}
is semisimple for all
p
, being the semisimplification of the
category of representations of
{\mathbb{Z}}/p{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Benson and Etingof
[
4
]
proved generally that
\operatorname{H}^{*}(\Ver_{p^{n}})

is finitely generated of Krull dimension
n-1
.
By our Corollary
3.2
then,
\Ver_{p^{n}}
is of wild representation type
for all
p
and all
n\geq 4
.
Likewise, by Theorem
3.1
, any finite exact
\Ver_{p^{n}}
-module category having an object of complexity at least 3 is of wild representation type, as long as
n\geq 4
.
The corollary does not apply to the nonsemisimple
Verlinde categories
\Ver_{p^{2}}
and
\Ver_{p^{3}}
.

Example 3.4
.

(Crossed product categories.)
In
[
7
]
, we studied crossed product categories
\mathscr{C}\rtimes G
where

\mathscr{C}
is a finite tensor category and
G
is a finite group acting by tensor autoequivalences.
As abelian categories, these are equivalent to the Deligne products
\mathscr{C}\boxtimes\Vect_{G}
, where
\Vect_{G}
is the category of
G
-graded finite dimensional vector spaces over
k
.
The crossed product category
\mathscr{C}\rtimes G
is a finite exact
\mathscr{C}
-module category.
Thus Theorem
3.1
applies to
\mathscr{C}\rtimes G
.
