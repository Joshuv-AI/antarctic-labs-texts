Instabilities, thermal fluctuations, defects and dislocations in the crystal- R I R_{I} - R I ​ I R_{II} rotator phase transitions of n-alkanes
Soumya Kanti Ganguly, Prabir K. Mukherjee

Abstract
The theoretical study of instabilities, thermal fluctuations, and topological defects in the crystal-rotator-I-rotator-II ( X − R I − R I ​ I X-R_{I}-R_{II} ) phase transitions of n-alkanes has been conducted. First, we examine the nature of the R I − R I ​ I R_{I}-R_{II} phase transition in nanoconfined alkanes. We propose that under confined conditions, the presence of quenched random orientational disorder makes the R I R_{I} phase unstable. This disorder-mediated transition falls within the Imry-Ma universality class. Next, we discuss the role of thermal fluctuations in certain rotator phases, as well as the influence of dislocations on the X − R I X-R_{I} phase transition. Our findings indicate that the Herringbone order in the X X -phase and the Hexatic order in the R I ​ I R_{II} -phase exhibit quasi-long-range characteristics. Furthermore, we find that in two dimensions, the unbinding of dislocations does not result in a disordered liquid state.

Introduction

The crystalline form of n-alkanes exhibits a layered structure made up of bilayer stacks of lamellas.
When these n-alkanes are heated, they reach a point where the molecules are well-ordered in terms of
their translations but exhibit rotational disorder. This newly formed phase is referred to as the
rotator phase. To date, five distinct rotator phases have been identified in n-alkanes. Due to the
unique and unusual properties of these rotator phases, they have become a significant area of research
over the past few decades

Alkanes can be confined in materials such as Vycor glass, microcapsules, and Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) matrixes.
When alkanes are confined, their behavior is influenced by the competition between correlation lengths and the finite
size of the system. This confinement reduces the dimensionality of the system and alters the surface forces at play.
Research has shown that, in such constrained environments, the static or dynamic correlation lengths of alkanes are
limited by the size of the confining structure. This theoretical limitation can be analyzed by applying a unidirectional
field that is imposed by the porous solid.

The lattice distortion parameter, denoted as
\xi

\xi=(A^{2}-B^{2})/(A^{2}+B^{2})

\xi

is non-zero in the
R_{I}
phase, indicating a distortion, while it is zero in the
R_{II}
phase, indicating no distortion. The
Ginzburg-Landau energy functional in

H[\xi,\sigma]=\int d^{d}\bm{x}\bigg[\frac{c}{2}|\vec{\nabla}\xi(\bm{x})|^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\xi^{2}(\bm{x})-\frac{\beta}{3}\xi^{3}(\bm{x})+\frac{\gamma}{4}\xi^{4}(\bm{x})-\sigma(\bm{x})\xi(\bm{x})\bigg]

(1)

\sigma

The coefficients

We numerically solve the above equation to calculate the thermodynamic phase boundary and stability limits of the

The role of crystal defects in the

phase transition

In the previous section we looked at the effect of thermal fluctuations in the
different ordered phases of the crystalline n-alkane system. The order parameter
fields which characterize broken continuous symmetry phases are represented by
smooth continuous functions of
\bm{x}
. There can arise situations
e.g. mechanical, thermal stress, when these fields may suffer from discontinuities
which cannot be removed by any amount of smooth deformation. The presence of these
defects affects the topology of the system and hence they are known as topological
defects (TD). Such defects will have an inner core region where the order parameter
vanishes and a far field region where the field changes slowly. Depending upon the
nature of the broken symmetry, the TD’s can have different names. For example, in
crystalline solids where there are both broken translational and rotational symmetry,
the TDs are known as dislocations and disclinations respectively (FIG.
6
).
Similarly, if the Herringbone order in
X
-phase, or the Hexatic order in the

R_{II}
-phase can be identified with the
XY
-model order parameter then the TDs in
these phases will be vortices. In the present section we will focus on the role of
dislocations in inducing the
X-R_{I}
transition. For a dislocated crystal, the singular
part of the displacement vector fields give rise to TDs. The displacement vector fields
can be decomposed into a regular part
u_{i}^{(R)}
, and a singular part
u_{i}^{(S)}
.

u_{i}(\bm{x})=u_{i}^{(R)}(\bm{x})+u_{i}^{(S)}(\bm{x}).

(14)

Along any closed circuit
\Gamma

\oint_{\Gamma}du_{i}^{(R)}=0\quad\textrm{and}\quad\oint_{\Gamma}du_{i}^{(S)}=b_{i}.

(15)

Where

is the Burgers’ vector in the
i

dimensions has the following expression
[
36

\displaystyle\frac{H_{D}}{T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\tilde{\kappa}\sum_{\alpha<\alpha^{\prime}}b_{i}(\bm{x}_{\alpha})U_{ij}(\bm{x}_{\alpha}-\bm{x}_{\alpha^{\prime}})b_{j}(\bm{x}_{\alpha^{\prime}})+\frac{E_{c}}{T}\sum_{i,\alpha}b^{2}_{i}(\bm{x}_{\alpha}).

(16)

\displaystyle\textrm{Where,}\quad U_{ij}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\bigg[\delta_{ij}\ln\bigg(\frac{|\bm{x}|}{a_{c}}\bigg)-\frac{x_{i}x_{j}}{|\bm{x}|^{2}}\bigg],\quad\tilde{\kappa}a^{2}=\frac{2\mu(\mu+\lambda)}{(2\mu+\lambda)},

and

\sum_{\bm{x}}b_{i}(\bm{x})=0

Similar to (

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\hat{e}}_{k}.[\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{u}^{(S)}(\bm{x})]_{k}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\bm{b}(\bm{x}_{\alpha}).(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{\alpha})}{|\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{\alpha}|^{2}}

(17)

\displaystyle\textrm{Where,}\quad\bm{b}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\bm{b}(\bm{x}_{\alpha})\delta^{2}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{\alpha})

is the dislocation vector field. In the long wavelength limit, the r.m.s. fluctuations in
\tilde{\eta}

\lim_{\bm{k}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\langle|\tilde{\eta}(\bm{k})|^{2}\rangle\propto\frac{\xi^{2}}{|\bm{k}|^{2}}.

This is because, above the transition temperature, the unbinding dislocations interact via a screened Coulomb potential
(See

for details). Therefore, the thermal correlations in the Herringbone phase due to dislocations will read as

\langle\Phi(\bm{x})\Phi^{*}(\bm{0})\rangle\propto|\bm{x}|^{-n_{\Phi}},\quad n_{\Phi}=\frac{2}{\pi c\xi^{2}_{\Phi}}.

We observe that the correlations display algebraic decay (quasi-long-range order) rather than exponential decay.
This indicates that in a two-dimensional system, or a quasi-two-dimensional state (such as confinement), the
transition mediated by dislocations does not result in a completely disordered state. Instead, their unbinding
leads to the formation of an orientationally ordered phase, known as the Herringbone phase or the Hexatic phase.
The transition from either the Herringbone phase or the Hexatic phase to a completely disordered state can be
aided by vortices, and this transition is of second order. However, this holds only if the system is strictly
two-dimensional. In three dimensions, this transition is known to be first-order, as confirmed by experiments

[
20
]
.

VII
Conclusion

In the light of the Imry-Ma argument, we conclude that in dimension
d=2
, the confined

R_{I}
phase will be unstable in the presence of quenched static random disorder.
Furthermore, we find that thermal fluctuations in the Herringbone order in the
X
-phase,
and the Hexatic order in the
R_{II}
-phase result in a quasi long range order, rather than
true long range order in the rotator phases. Lastly, we observe that the unbinding of dislocations
results in the creation of an orientationally ordered Herringbone or Hexatic phases.

IX.1
The bulk and surface free energy of the
R_{I}-R_{II}
phases, and the density of defects

For a
d
-dimensional system, the area
\mathcal{A}\propto L^{(d-1)}
. Let
s
be the variable that
measures the spatial variation of the domain wall separating
R_{I}
and
R_{II}
. The direction of
s

is perpendicular to
\mathcal{A}
, and the
d
-dimensional volume measure
d^{d}\bm{x}=\mathcal{A}ds
.
To calculate the energy of this wall in
d
dimensions, we will consider the gradient term, also
known as the surface energy along with the confinement term in (
1
).

H_{w}[\xi,\sigma]=\int_{0}^{L_{\Delta}}d^{d}\bm{x}\bigg[\frac{c}{2}|\vec{\nabla}\xi(\bm{x})|^{2}-\sigma(\bm{x})\xi(\bm{x})\bigg]

(20)

If the value of the order parameter in the
R_{I}
phase is
\xi_{0}
, then

\xi(\bm{x})=\xi(x_{1},x_{2},..,x_{d-1},s)=\xi_{0}\Theta_{H}(s),

(21)

Where
\Theta_{H}(s)=1,(s>0)
, and zero otherwise, is the Heaviside or the step function defined in the regions
where the domain boundaries exist. And its derivative is

\vec{\nabla}\xi(\bm{x})=\xi_{0}\frac{d\Theta_{H}(s)}{ds}=\xi_{0}\delta(s).

(22)

a
\delta
-function defined in the small
\varepsilon
-neighbourhood of the domain boundary (
s\in[-\varepsilon/2,\varepsilon/2]
)
(see FIG.
3
). The surface energy of the wall is then given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{w}(L_{\Delta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c\xi_{0}^{2}L_{\Delta}^{d-1}}{2a^{2}}\int_{s-\varepsilon/2}^{s+\varepsilon/2}ds\delta(s)

(23)

\displaystyle\textrm{or,}\quad\mathcal{E}_{w}(L_{\Delta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c\xi^{2}_{0}}{2a^{2}}L_{\Delta}^{d-1}

Where
a
is the lattice constant. Similarly, the confinement or the volume energy is given by

\int_{0}^{L_{\Delta}}d^{d}\bm{x}\sigma(\bm{x})\xi(\bm{x})=\xi_{0}\sigma L_{\Delta}^{d},

(24)

and the corresponding r.m.s. fluctuations per domain wall is given by

\mathcal{E}_{b}(L_{\Delta})=-\xi_{0}\sqrt{\langle\sigma^{2}\rangle}L^{d/2}_{\Delta}.

(25)

This is the bulk energy of the system and the total energy is given by

\mathcal{E}(L_{\Delta})=\frac{1}{2}c\xi^{2}_{0}L^{d_{c}/2}_{\Delta}-\xi_{0}\sqrt{\langle\sigma^{2}\rangle}L^{d/2}_{\Delta}.

(26)

Where
d_{c}=2(d-1)
. To find the density of the defects when the system is in its lowest energy state, we differentiate
(
26
) w.r.t
L_{\Delta}
and equate it to zero and find the following expression for the density of defects

\frac{L_{\Delta}}{a}=\bigg(\frac{c\xi_{0}d_{c}}{2d\sqrt{\langle\sigma^{2}\rangle}}\bigg)^{\frac{2}{2-d_{c}}}

(27)

IX.2
Mean-field correlation length of domain walls

Figure 7:
(a) The double well potential where
\mathcal{V}[\xi=0]=\alpha\xi^{2}_{0}/4
, and
\mathcal{V}[\xi=\xi_{0}]=0
;
(b) Plots for the order-parameter (black), and its derivative (green) for the domain wall.

In the previous section, we assumed that the order parameter
\xi
had a spatial profile resembling a step function.
However, in general, the shape of the domain wall is determined by the system’s free energy. This free energy dictates
a characteristic length scale that is dependent on the parameters of the system. The typical size of the domain walls
is set by the mean-field correlation length
\zeta_{d}
. In order to obtain an expression for
\zeta_{d}
, we ignore
the quenched disorders (
\sigma=0
), and consider only the
\xi^{2}
and the
\xi^{4}
terms (
\beta=0)
in the
G-L functional in (
1
)(FIG.
7
).

\frac{H[\xi]}{\mathcal{A}}=\int ds\bigg[\frac{c}{2}\bigg(\frac{d\xi(s)}{ds}\bigg)^{2}+\mathcal{V}[\xi(s)]\bigg]

(28)

\textrm{Where}\quad\mathcal{V}[\xi]=\frac{\alpha(\xi^{2}-\xi_{0}^{2})^{2}}{4\xi^{2}_{0}},\quad\textrm{and}\quad\xi_{0}=\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{\gamma}}\quad(\xi\geq 0).

(29)

The static Euler-Lagrange equation is obtained by calculating the functional derivative of (
29
) w.r.t.
\xi

\frac{\delta}{\delta\xi}\bigg(\frac{H[\xi]}{\mathcal{A}}\bigg)=-c\frac{d^{2}\xi(s)}{ds^{2}}+\frac{d\mathcal{V}[\xi(s)]}{ds}=0

(30)

Integration w.r.t
s\in[s_{0},s]
gives us the following formal expression.

s-s_{0}=\int_{\mathcal{V}[\xi(s_{0})]}^{\mathcal{V}[\xi(s)]}\frac{d\xi}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{V}[\xi]/c}}

(31)

Substituting the R.H.S. of (
29
), and integrating w.r.t.
\xi
, we find the order-parameter (See FIG.
3
(b))

\xi(s)=\xi_{0}\tanh\bigg(\frac{s-s_{0}}{\sqrt{2}\zeta_{d}}\bigg),

(32)

and the integrand in (
28
), which is the energy density
\epsilon(s)
(See FIG.
3
(a)) is given by

\mathcal{E}(s)=\frac{\alpha\xi^{2}_{0}}{2}\sech^{4}\bigg(\frac{s-s_{0}}{\sqrt{2}\zeta_{d}}\bigg).

(33)

The quantity
\zeta_{d}\equiv\sqrt{c/\alpha}
, has the dimension of length, and is identified as the
mean-field correlation length.

IX.3
The bulk and surface free energy of Herringbone,
R_{II}
phases, and the density of defects

We have seen in
IV
, the order parameter for the Herringbone phase and the
R_{II}
phase is a general complex
scalar field
\Theta(\bm{x})=\Theta_{0}e^{i\theta(\bm{x})}
(N = 2). Following the arguments presented
in
IX.1
, the surface energy density is given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}c_{\Theta}|\vec{\nabla}\Theta(\bm{x})|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{|\bm{a}|\rightarrow 0}\quad\frac{1}{2}c_{\Theta}\Theta_{0}^{2}\bigg|\frac{e^{i\theta(\bm{x+a})}-e^{i\theta(\bm{x})}}{\bm{a}}\bigg|^{2}.

(34)

\displaystyle\textrm{Or,}\quad\frac{1}{2}c_{\Theta}|\vec{\nabla}\Theta(\bm{x})|^{2}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}c_{\Theta}\Theta_{0}^{2}|\vec{\nabla}\theta(\bm{x})|^{2}.

Where
c_{\Theta}
is the stiffness constant of the rotators. Let
\theta(\bm{x})
be the angle between the rotators and the external
field
h(\bm{x})
. Then the bulk energy density will be proportional to the real part of
\Theta(\bm{x})
, and read as

-\Theta_{0}h(\bm{x})\cos\theta(\bm{x}).

(35)

Combining (
34
) and (
35
), and assuming that the spatial variation of the wall is along

s\in[0,L_{\Delta}]
, the free energy of the domain wall in this case is given by

H[\theta]=\mathcal{A}\int_{0}^{L_{\Delta}}ds\bigg[\frac{\rho_{\Theta}}{2}\bigg(\frac{d\theta(s)}{ds}\bigg)^{2}-\Theta_{0}h(s)\cos\theta(s)\bigg]

(36)

Where
\mathcal{A}\propto L_{\Delta}^{d-1}
, and
\rho_{\Theta}=c_{\Theta}a^{2}\Theta_{0}^{2}
, is the stiffness constant.
To obtain an expression for the density of defects, let us assume that
\theta
varies linearly with
s
, or
\theta=2\pi s/L_{\Delta}
.
Then the surface energy will read as

\mathcal{E}_{s}(L_{\Delta})=\frac{\rho_{\Theta}}{2}L_{\Delta}^{d-1}\int_{0}^{L_{\Delta}}ds\bigg(\frac{2\pi}{L_{\Delta}}\bigg)^{2}=2\pi^{2}\rho_{\Theta}L_{\Delta}^{d-2}.

(37)

Secondly, if we replace
\cos\theta(s)
by its thermal average
\langle\cos\theta(s)\rangle_{T}=\kappa(T)
, which is a
temperature dependent dimensionless quantity, then the bulk energy per domain wall is given by

\mathcal{E}_{b}(L_{\Delta})=-\kappa(T)\Theta_{0}\sqrt{\langle h^{2}\rangle}L^{d/2}_{\Delta}.

(38)

Where
\sqrt{\langle h^{2}\rangle}
is the r.m.s. fluctuations in
h
. The total energy, which is the sum of (
37
)
and (
38
), will then read as

\mathcal{E}(L_{\Delta})=2\pi^{2}\rho_{\Theta}L^{d_{c}/2}_{\Delta}-\kappa(T)\Theta_{0}\sqrt{\langle h^{2}\rangle}L^{d/2}_{\Delta}.

(39)

In the present case
d_{c}=2(d-2)
, implying that the critical dimension is 4. Similar to (
27
), the density of the
defects in the system’s lowest energy state is given by

\frac{L_{\Delta}}{a}=\bigg(\frac{2\pi^{2}\rho_{\Theta}d_{c}}{\kappa(T)d\Theta_{0}\sqrt{\langle h^{2}\rangle}}\bigg)^{\frac{2}{2-d_{c}}}

(40)

Similar to (
29
) in
IX.2
, the spatial profile of these domain walls can be obtained by calculating the
functional derivative of (
36
) w.r.t.
\theta
. The resulting static Euler-Lagrange equation is given by

\frac{\delta}{\delta\theta}\bigg(\frac{H[\theta]}{\mathcal{A}}\bigg)=\frac{d^{2}\theta(s)}{ds^{2}}+\frac{\Theta_{0}h(s)}{\rho_{\Theta}}\sin\theta(s)=0

(41)

If
h(s)=h_{0}
, the amplitude value of the random field, then we can define a quantity
\lambda_{0}=\sqrt{\rho_{\Theta}/\Theta_{0}h_{0}}
,
that has the dimensions of length. As a result, the system will exhibit domain walls characterized by a length that depends on the strength
of the external random field. Equation (
41
) resembles the equation of motion of a real pendulum, and its solutions will be
described by Jacobi’s elliptic functions
[
37
]
.

IX.4
Elastic energy and thermal fluctuation calculations of crystalline solids

The strain tensor for crystalline solids in real and Fourier space in any dimensions is given by

\displaystyle u_{ij}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial u_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}\bigg)

(42)

\displaystyle u_{ij}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i}{2}(k_{j}u_{i}+k_{i}u_{j})

If
\mu
and
\lambda
be the two elastic constants, then the elastic energy is given by

\displaystyle\beta H

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int d^{2}\bm{x}[2\mu u_{ij}u_{ij}+\lambda u_{ii}u_{jj}]

(43)

\displaystyle\beta H

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}[2\mu k_{i}k_{i}u_{j}u_{j}+(\mu+\lambda)(k_{i}u_{i})^{2}]\quad(\textrm{Fourier space})

The transverse and longitudinal energy for the phonons are given by

\displaystyle\beta H_{T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{2}\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}k^{2}u^{2}_{T},

(44)

\displaystyle\textrm{and}\quad\beta H_{L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\mu+\lambda}{2}\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}k^{2}u^{2}_{L}

respectively. According to the
equipartition theorem
,

\displaystyle\langle H_{T}\rangle=\langle H_{L}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}k_{B}T.

(45)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\langle|u_{T}(\bm{k})|^{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{\mu k^{2}},\quad\textrm{and}\quad\langle|u_{L}(\bm{k})|^{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{(2\mu+\lambda)k^{2}}.

The displacement field correlation function will read

\langle u_{i}(\bm{k})u_{j}(\bm{k^{\prime}})\rangle=\frac{(2\pi)^{2}\delta(\bm{k}+\bm{k^{\prime}})}{\mu|\bm{k}|^{2}}\bigg[\delta_{ij}-\frac{\mu+\lambda}{2\mu+\lambda}\frac{k_{i}k_{j}}{|\bm{k}|^{2}}\bigg].

(46)

As a consequence of the above equation we will have the following relationship

\displaystyle\langle[u_{i}(\bm{x})-u_{i}(\bm{0})]^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\bigg(\frac{2-2\cos(\bm{k.x})}{\mu|\bm{k}|^{2}}\bigg)\bigg[\delta_{ii}-\frac{\mu+\lambda}{2\mu+\lambda}\frac{k_{i}k_{i}}{|\bm{k}|^{2}}\bigg],

(47)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3\mu+\lambda}{\mu(2\mu+\lambda)}\frac{\ln(|\bm{x}|/a)}{\pi}.

Density correlation function calculation of crystalline solids

The order parameter for the crystalline phase is the density function in the reciprocal space.
Namely
\rho_{\bm{G}}(\bm{x})
, where
\bm{G}
is any reciprocal
lattice vector. If
\bm{x}_{0}
be a lattice vector then
\bm{G.x_{0}}=2n\pi
,
where
n
is an integer. In two-dimensions or three-dimensions with stacked layers.
The density-density correlation function obeys a power law given below.

\displaystyle\langle\rho_{\bm{G}}(\bm{x})\rho^{*}_{\bm{G}}(\bm{0})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\exp[i\bm{G}.\{\bm{u}(\bm{x})-\bm{u}(\bm{0})\}]\rangle

(48)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\bigg[-\frac{G_{i}G_{j}}{2}\langle\{u_{i}(\bm{x})-u_{i}(\bm{0})\}\{u_{j}(\bm{x})-u_{j}(\bm{0})\}\rangle\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\bigg[-\frac{G_{i}G_{j}}{2}\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{k}d^{2}\bm{k^{\prime}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}(e^{i\bm{k.x}}-1)(e^{i\bm{k^{\prime}.x}}-1)\langle u_{i}(\bm{k})u_{j}(\bm{k^{\prime}})\rangle\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\bigg[-\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\bigg(\frac{2-2\cos(\bm{k.x})}{\mu|\bm{k}|^{2}}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{|\bm{G}|^{2}}{\mu}-\frac{\mu+\lambda}{2\mu+\lambda}\frac{(\bm{G.k})}{|\bm{k}|^{2}}\bigg)\bigg],

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\exp\bigg[-\frac{|\bm{G}|^{2}(3\mu+\lambda)}{2\mu(2\mu+\lambda)}\frac{\ln(|\bm{x}|/a)}{2\pi}\bigg]=\bigg(\frac{a}{|\bm{x}|}\bigg)^{n_{\bm{G}}}

\displaystyle\textrm{Where,}\quad n_{\bm{G}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|\bm{G}|^{2}(3\mu+\lambda)}{4\pi\mu(2\mu+\lambda)}

IX.7
Correlation function calculation of the dislocation vector fields and the corresponding Herringbone order in the
X
-phase

The singular part of the displacement vector fields can induce distortion in the Herringbone phase rotators, given by

\tilde{\eta}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int d^{2}\bm{x^{\prime}}\frac{\bm{b}(\bm{x^{\prime}}).(\bm{x}-\bm{x^{\prime}})}{|\bm{x}-\bm{x^{\prime}}|^{2}}.

(50)

In Fourier space,

\tilde{\eta}(\bm{k})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int d^{2}\bm{x}\int d^{2}\bm{x^{\prime}}e^{i\bm{k.x}}\frac{\bm{b}(\bm{x^{\prime}}).(\bm{x}-\bm{x^{\prime}})}{|\bm{x}-\bm{x^{\prime}}|^{2}}=i\frac{\bm{k}.\bm{b}(\bm{k})}{|\bm{k}|^{2}}.

(51)

The r.m.s. fluctuations in the phase is given by

\displaystyle\langle|\tilde{\eta}(\bm{k})|^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k_{i}k_{j}}{|\bm{k}|^{4}}\langle b_{i}(\bm{k})b_{j}(\bm{k})\rangle.

(52)

\displaystyle\textrm{Where}\quad\langle b_{i}(\bm{k})b_{j}(\bm{k})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{2}\bm{x}e^{i\bm{k.x}}\langle b_{i}(\bm{x})b_{j}(\bm{0})\rangle.

Above the transition temperature, if the unbinding dislocations interact via a screened Coulomb potential (
16
), then

\displaystyle\lim_{|\bm{x}|\rightarrow\infty}\langle b_{i}(\bm{x})b_{j}(\bm{0})\rangle

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{ij}\exp(-|\bm{x}|/\xi)}{|\bm{x}|}.

(53)

\displaystyle\textrm{Or,}\quad\langle b_{i}(\bm{k})b_{j}(\bm{k})\rangle

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{ij}}{|\bm{k}|^{2}+\xi^{-2}}.

\displaystyle\textrm{And,}\quad\lim_{|\bm{k}|\rightarrow 0}\langle b_{i}(\bm{k})b_{j}(\bm{k})\rangle

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\delta_{ij}\xi^{2}.

Substituting this in (
51
), we get (
18
)

\lim_{\bm{k}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\langle|\tilde{\eta}(\bm{k})|^{2}\rangle\propto\frac{\xi^{2}}{|\bm{k}|^{2}}.

(54)

Using this result, the thermal correlations in the Herringbone phase due to the dislocation fields will read as

\displaystyle\langle\Phi(\bm{x})\Phi^{*}(\bm{0})\rangle

\displaystyle\langle\exp(i2[\tilde{\eta}(\bm{x})-\tilde{\eta}(\bm{0})])\rangle

(55)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi c\xi^{2}_{\Phi}}
