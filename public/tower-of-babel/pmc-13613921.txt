Comparative Study of Enhanced CH4 Desorption in Real-Reservoir Coal Containing Trace O2 under Injection of Different Superheated Gases
Authors: Xiao Zhang, Jupeng Tang, Aiwen Wang, Lianpeng Dai, Changbao Jiang, Fei Wang, Xiao Zhang, Honghao Yu, Tianhong Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To enhance the extraction efficiency of deep, low-permeability coalbed methane, gas-injection techniques have attracted attention for their combined effects of permeability enhancement and production stimulation. Previous studies, however, lack systematic comparisons among different injected gases (N2, CO2, H2O) and often neglect trace O2 in coal, leading to discrepancies with actual reservoir conditions, while thermal coupling effects of superheated gas injection are rarely considered. In this work, Xiaolongtan lignite from Yunnan containing trace O2 was investigated via molecular dynamics simulations across a temperature range from 373.15 to 773.15 K (100–500 °C) to examine CH4 desorption behavior. For the first time, the Grand Canonical Monte Carlo method was employed to determine the lowest-energy adsorption configurations of CH4 and O2. Results show that the enhancement of CH4 desorption follows the order H2O > CO2 > N2. The results revealed that within the vacuum region, H2O injection produced the maximum CH4 average relative concentration, the largest diffusion coefficient of 3.454 × 10–20 m2·ps–1, and exhibited a diffusion activation energy of 0.101 kcal/mol, the lowest, indicating a reduction in the diffusion barrier for CH4. This study provides important parameters and a theoretical basis for the thermal-injection-enhanced CBM recovery.

Introduction

Due to the clean and environmentally friendly characteristics of coalbed methane (CBM), it has been used extensively instead of traditional energy sources in various countries. − As the mining depth increases, the permeability of the coal seam decreases gradually, and it is difficult for CBM to be extracted efficiently. In order to solve this problem, several permeability-enhancement methods have been proposed, including hydraulic fracturing , and hydraulic cavitation. However, hydraulic fracturing has poor controllability of fractures, and hydraulic cavitation cannot be applied to complex geological conditions. In order to solve this problem, gas-injection methods have also been developed. These methods may increase coal seam permeability, encourage CH4 desorption without great fracturing being needed, and can be applied to low-permeability or geologically complicated coal seams. Therefore, it is important to understand the CH4 behavior characteristics under gas injection conditions so as to reveal the optimizing CBM extraction strategy.

Numerous experimental tests and molecular simulations have systematically investigated the effects of injecting N2, CO2 and H2O on coalbed methane recovery, from three perspectives including adsorption-diffusion, coal matrix deformation and engineering application. For N2 injection: From the perspective of adsorption-diffusion, tensile and compressive strain of coal and molar fraction of gas alter the competitive adsorption-diffusion behavior between N2 and CH4. Although N2 injection can increase coal porosity, its efficiency for displacing desorbed CH4 is far lower than that of CO2. In terms of coal matrix deformation, fatigue fracturing induced by pulsed N2 causes periodic expansion and contraction of coal, promoting the conversion of micropores into mesopores and macropores and enhancing fracture connectivity. High-pressure pulsed N2 modifies the internal coal structure via multiple mechanisms such as pore pressure variation, adsorptive deformation and mechanical deterioration. , From an engineering application perspective, pressurized N2 increases the reservoir pressure gradient and dredges free CH4 in fractures. Deep learning algorithms including BP, LSTM and TCN can accurately predict dynamic gas production under N2 injection. For CO2 injection: From the perspective of adsorption-diffusion, CO2 preferentially occupies oxygen-containing functional group sites on coal surfaces to displace CH4 efficiently, and its adsorption capacity reaches 2.0–2.3 times that of CH4. Gas diffusion and displacement efficiency are jointly governed by coal rank, pore morphology, molar fraction of CO2, injection pressure and sequential gas injection modes. − Modification via N, S heteroatom doping enriches surface active functional groups and improves the chemical reactivity of coal matrix, thereby remarkably strengthening the CO2 capture capacity of coal. Moreover, alkali metal doping can also significantly boost the CO2 adsorption performance of coal. In terms of coal matrix deformation, CO2 adsorption induces swelling of coal matrix, compresses seepage channels, and reduces the compressive strength and elastic modulus of coal. High-pressure injection tends to trigger premature CO2 gas channeling, weakening the long-term extraction performance. From an engineering application perspective, theoretical calculations combined with underground in situ tests can determine the optimal injection pressure and volume of liquid CO2 for a single well. Sinusoidal variable-rate gas injection greatly elevates cumulative desorbed CH4 volume, while stepped gas injection improves the total CO2 sequestration capacity. For H2O injection: From the perspective of adsorption-diffusion, existing studies have explored the evolution law of CH4 probability density under H2O injection, and further analyzed the influence of H2O phase state on CH4 production performance. From an engineering application perspective, microwave heating technology has been adopted to reveal the effect of input power on coalbed methane recovery efficiency. N2 injection rarely causes coal matrix swelling and pore blockage, and can sustainably improve coal permeability, yet its weak CH4 displacement capacity restricts high-yield gas recovery. CO2 injection delivers high CH4 displacement efficiency and enables simultaneous geological CO2 sequestration; nevertheless, matrix swelling impairs reservoir permeability, and premature gas channeling readily occurs under high-pressure conditions. H2O injection facilitates thermal fracturing and permeability enhancement of coal, whereas prominent H2O lock effect inhibits CH4 migration.

Although the behavior of CH4 in these gas-injection scenarios has been investigated extensively, there are still some key unknowns: first, most current research is focused on the mechanism for one kind of injected gas, and systematic comparison of the behaviors of CH4 in CO2, N2, and H2O injection is still missing. Second, most numerical models consider an inert coal reservoir environment without accounting for the existence of trace O2 in coal, that can cause a mismatch between simulated and real field behavior; third, the influence of heat interaction due to superheated gas injection is not considered in existing works. High temperature gas carries high thermal energy, which may greatly change the coal microstructure and promote CH4 diffusion and desorption, thus playing a significant role in the actual CBM improvement activities. Hence, a systematic investigation is conducted on the effect of different superheated gases (N2, CO2, and H2O) on the CH4 behavior in O2-containing coal by numerical simulations. The studied coal is the Xiaolongtan lignite from Yunnan Province. Numerical simulations of CH4 desorption from an O2-containing lignite at 373.15–773.15 K have been performed. In particular, a lignite slit model was developed based on the GCMC method to determine its minimum energy structures for CH4 and O2 adsorption. N2, CO2, and H2O were then individually introduced into these lowest-energy configurations, and molecular dynamics simulations were carried out at 373.15–773.15 K to investigate CH4 desorption behavior. Finally, under identical temperature conditions, the three systems’ pore size distributions were analyzed. The results in the present work can be used as a basis to optimize the strategy of injecting superheated gases, which will lead to an increase in CBM recovery.

Simulation Method

Establishment of the Coal Slit Model

Coal exhibits complex structural characteristics and strong heterogeneity, which have motivated extensive research on coal molecular structure models in previous studies. − In this work, lignite from the Xiaolongtan mining area in Yunnan Province was selected as the research sample. Its molecular formula was determined to be C45H47O12N based on multiple characterization techniques, and the corresponding molecular structure model was subsequently constructed using Materials Studio software. The resulting model is listed in Figure . This model was established based on multiple characterization results of Xiaolongtan lignite, including elemental analysis, IR, and XPS, and retains the core carbon skeletons and oxygen-containing functional groups that dominate gas adsorption behavior. Although reasonable simplifications are applied to the intricate raw coal structure during model construction, the key structural features governing gas–solid adsorption interactions are well-preserved, which ensures the objectivity of the research results.

Slit model construction.

The schematic diagram of the lignite slit model’s construction is presented in Figure . First, molecular mechanics combined with annealing kinetics was used to carry out the optimization of the basic structural unit. Subsequently, a unit cell (Models A, B, and C) composed of two such units was constructed and further optimized, with the detailed optimization parameters provided in Table S1. A subsequent expansion operation was performed on this unit cell, resulting in the formation of a 1 × 2 × 2 supercell (Models D, E, and F). Finally, a 2 nm vacuum layer was constructed to obtain lignite slit model Models G, H, and I. Periodic boundary conditions were applied to the coal slit model. Without a vacuum layer, the upper and lower coal surfaces of adjacent periodic unit cells would generate unphysical intermolecular forces, which would distort the adsorption configurations and diffusion behaviors of CH4, O2 and injected gases. Therefore, a 2 nm vacuum layer was constructed to separate periodic coal slabs, eliminate cross-cell interlayer interference, and ensure the reliability of simulation results regarding gas adsorption–desorption behaviors.

Adsorption Structures with Minimum Energy for CH4 and O2 on Lignite, Along with the Structures of Injected Gases

Under conditions of 373.15–773.15 K and pressures from 1 to 10 MPa, the molar ratios of CH4 to O2 were set to 0.9:0.1, 0.7:0.3, and 0.5:0.5, respectively. Xiaolongtan lignite is a typical low-rank coal whose organic macromolecular skeleton contains abundant oxygen-containing functional groups such as hydroxyl and carboxyl groups. During long-term coalification, these functional groups slowly decompose to produce trace oxygen, which is stably stored within the coal pore system. The trace oxygen adopted in our model refers to this intrinsic component that naturally exists in coal seams prior to superheated gas injection. It can truly restore the original occurrence environment of coal seams before being disturbed by gas injection. The temperature range of 373.15–773.15 K was adopted because this work investigates CH4 desorption behavior in coal containing trace O2 under superheated gas injection. A temperature no lower than 373.15 K is required to ensure water exists in a superheated state. In addition, existing relevant studies commonly employ 373.15–673.15 K for simulations of superheated gas injection. Combining the above factors, the present temperature range was determined. The pressure range of 1–10 MPa adopted in this simulation is determined for two reasons. First, this interval matches the actual in situ reservoir pressure of coalbed methane in lignite formations, representing the real underground storage environment. Second, gas adsorption capacity is extremely low below 1 MPa with insufficient adsorbed molecules for analysis, while adsorption reaches saturation above 10 MPa and the adsorption amount no longer changes significantly. The GCMC method implemented in the Sorption and Adsorption Locator module was employed to determine the lowest-energy adsorption configurations of CH4 and O2 on lignite. The corresponding results are shown in Figure .

Variation of the total energy during CH4 and O2 adsorption on lignite with increasing temperature and pressure.

Figure shows the variation law of the total system energy after CH4 and O2 are adsorbed by the lignite system with temperature and pressure. The total energy of the adsorption system fluctuates obviously with the change of temperature and pressure conditions, which directly reflects the stability of gas adsorption configurations under different reservoir environments. When the pressure rises, the total energy of the system decreases continuously and gradually becomes stable, indicating that a high-pressure environment can strengthen the adsorption and binding effect of CH4 and O2 on the lignite surface; while the rise in temperature will increase the total energy of the system, weaken the adsorption force between gas molecules and the coal matrix, and be more conducive to gas desorption. Under all simulated working conditions of 373.15–773.15 K and 1–10 MPa, Model I corresponds to the lowest total system energy and has the optimal thermodynamic stability of the adsorption structure. Therefore, Model I is selected as the optimal adsorption configuration of lignite adsorbing CH4 and O2 in this paper to carry out subsequent molecular dynamics simulation calculations for the injection of different superheated gases.

At 373.15 K and 10 MPa, the number of adsorbed molecules of the three gases on the Coal/CH4/O2 was determined separately using the Sorption module. At 373.15 K, the gases exhibit the strongest adsorption capacity, and the number of adsorbed molecules is highest at 10 MPa; therefore, these conditions were selected for the study. The amounts of three gases injected were 34, 50, and 106, respectively. The three gases were each adsorbed onto Model I using the Adsorption Locator module. The resulting configuration is shown in Figure S1. The gas optimization process is depicted in Figure S2. Results and Discussion

Variation of Gas Relative Concentrations Across Different Systems at Varying Temperatures

An analysis of the relative concentration distribution curves was conducted to clarify the CH4 desorption behavior at 373.15–773.15 K. The results are shown in Figure .

Gas relative concentration profiles of different systems under varying thermal conditions.

The results for relative concentration are shown in Figure , and it can be seen that there is a symmetric shape, and it can be seen that the position from 0–20 and 40–60 Å corresponds to the range of the coal molecular layer; the middle 20–40 Å region is taken to be the vacuum layer. The symmetry can be attributed to the interaction between gas molecules and the coal surface, which modifies the adsorption characteristics and improves the homogeneity of gas molecule diffusion on the coal surface space. This increased molecular diffusion leads to a relatively more uniform gas concentration profile throughout the coal.

CH4 relative concentration within the vacuum region at temperatures of 373.15–773.15 K in the N2-injected system is calculated as 1.040, 1.104, 1.148, 1.059, and 1.126, respectively. At the same temperatures as above, the Coal/CH4/O2/CO2 system has values of 1.333, 1.356, 1.352, 1.361, and 1.410; at the same temperature range, the Coal/CH4/O2/H2O system has much higher CH4 mean relative concentration values of 1.596, 1.647, 1.657, 1.692, and 1.691. The CH4 relative concentration shows an increase with increasing thermal conditions. H2O has the most significant promotion in releasing CH4 among all tested gases; CO2 has a certain promotion of releasing CH4; but N2 plays a very small role in promoting the release of CH4. The adsorption capacity of H2O on the coal surface is stronger than that of other gases, resulting in an enhanced capability (relative to CO2 or N2) of displacing CH4 and therefore making it more easily released from the coal matrix, resulting in improvements in the recovery efficacy. By comparison, CO2 promotes CH4 release to a lesser extent than H2O. While such a contribution can still be observed, it seems much less efficient in enhancing CH4 desorption as compared to H2O. On the other hand, the least effect exerted by N2 on CH4 desorption might be attributed to its very small interaction strength with the coal, being unable to change the adsorption behavior greatly enough to cause significant CH4 desorption. The CH4 relative concentration in the CO2-injected system was found to be 1.18–1.29 times higher than that of the N2-injected system. These results are consistent with previous studies, including Jessen, and show agreement with observations reported by Bai, who documented a 1–2-fold relative concentration increase. The values obtained here (1.18–1.29) are fully contained within that interval, and the maximum deviation of 0.82 further confirms the credibility of the results.

At 373.15–773.15 K, in the N2-injected system, the O2 average relative concentration was 0.085, 0.073, 0.189, 0.165, and 0.232. At the same temperature, the CO2 injection case shows larger values of 0.280, 0.366, 0.439, 0.415, and 0.329. By contrast, the O2 concentration at the corresponding positions is significantly increased in the H2O-injected system, reaching values of 0.585, 0.488, 0.634, 0.683, and 0.780. Overall, O2 enrichment within the vacuum layer intensifies with rising temperature, and the relative concentration ranks as H2O-injected > CO2-injected > N2-injected. The underlying mechanism is illustrated in Figure , which reflects the differences in the competitive adsorption ability, physicochemical effects, and diffusion characteristics of the injected gases toward the O2 adsorption sites in lignite. N2 has the least adsorption capacity on the coal body; it can only occupy adsorption sites by physically displacing other molecules, leading to a lower driving force for O2 desorption and therefore the smallest relative concentration. CO2 has a higher adsorption strength and can compete for O2 adsorption sites through competitive adsorption, thus partly displacing O2 and resulting in a higher relative concentration compared with that of the N2 system. However, lacking pore-enlarging chemical effects, its promotion remains limited. In contrast, H2O shows the strongest adsorption affinity for the coal surface. It not only actively replaces O2 through strong competitive adsorption but also enhances O2 desorption and diffusion by facilitating hydration reactions and pore expansion in the coal. Consequently, O2 relative concentration is the highest in the H2O-injected system.

Desorption mechanisms of O2 and gases in different systems.

The relative concentrations of N2 in the N2-injected system vary between 0.770 and 0.799 at 373.15–773.15 K. At the same temperature, the CO2-injected system exhibits CO2 relative concentration values of 0.783, 0.805, 0.823, 0.803, and 0.829 within the same region. In comparison, H2O in the H2O-injected system has a noticeably higher relative concentration, increasing from 0.854 to 1.034 with increasing temperature. In general, the amount of gas in the vacuum region increases with rising temperature, and ranked as H2O > CO2 > N2. As shown in Figure , this trend can be attributed to the very low affinity of N2 for the coal matrix, which causes predominantly stochastic migration within pores and limits its accumulation in the vacuum region. On the other hand, CO2, which has a stronger affinity for the coal matrix and experiences a certain degree of directed migration, does not induce swelling nor structural damage that would significantly enhance diffusivity. By contrast, H2O exhibits both strong surface affinity and hydration-induced opening effects, thus widening the transport channels. As a result, H2O attains the highest relative concentration in the vacuum layer, followed by CO2, while N2 remains the least enriched.

As shown in Figure , for CH4, the ln D – 1/T slopes are −71.364, −63.670, and −50.770 × 10–20 m2·ps–1·K, respectively. The diffusion activation energies are 0.142, 0.127, and 0.101 kcal/mol, respectively. For CH4, the diffusion activation energy in the CO2-injected system is reduced to 0.127 kcal/mol, representing a decrease of 10.56% relative to the Coal/CH4/O2/N2 case. For the Coal/CH4/O2/H2O system, the activation energy of CH4 declines by 28.87% and 20.47% compared with the other two systems, respectively. CH4 shows the highest diffusion activation energy in the N2-injected system, suggesting that CH4 diffusion must overcome a larger energy barrier when N2 is injected, thereby hindering molecular transport. Conversely, for the other two systems, both the ln D – 1/T slopes and activation energies of CH4 are lower, indicating that CO2 or H2O injection reduces the diffusion barrier and promotes CH4 migration.

Gas diffusion activation energies for the individual systems.

The diffusion activation energies of O2 are 0.103, 0.090, and 0.069 kcal/mol. The interaction between different gases and the coal matrix differs. N2 can only interact with the coal matrix by weak physical occupancy, and the dense structure of coal pores results in high resistance to O2 diffusivity, leading to the largest activation energy. CO2 can moderately expand the pore structure by its high adsorption effect, which reduces the diffusion barrier of O2, thus giving rise to a moderate activation energy. On the contrary, since H2O has high adsorption strength and a large pore-expansion effect, which can open diffusion channels in the coal, it is therefore the lowest resistance (i.e., the smallest activation energy) that is experienced by O2 in the presence of H2O. N2, CO2, and H2O diffusion activation energies are 0.134, 0.110, and 0.079 kcal/mol. It was observed a clear ranking of the different systems, with H2O having the lowest barrier, CO2 showing an intermediate value, and N2 exhibiting the greatest hindrance effect on the diffusion process, which indicates that H2O plays the best role in promoting the migration of CH4 in the coal matrix. CO2 is conducive to the diffusion of CH4 as well, although it has less influence compared with that of H2O, while N2 shows the smallest promotion ability.

Pore Size Distribution

The adsorption of these injected superheated gases in the pores will cause some changes to the structure of the coal, which, in turn, strongly influences the CH4 desorption performance. It is thus important to understand the effect of superheated gas injection on the pores to enhance the CH4 extraction process. Figure shows the pore size distribution of the studied systems between 373.15 and 773.15 K. The pore size distributions for all coals were obtained with a probing particle insertion every 0.02 nm up to a maximum radius of 0.4 nm.

Pore size distribution profile.

The changes in the coal pore size distribution at gas injection are shown in Figure . The peak radius ranges from 1.0 Å at 373.15 K to 1.2 Å at high temperature for the N2-injected system. For the CO2-injected system, the peak radii are almost unchanged in this whole temperature range (approximate value: 1.2 Å). The case for the H2O-injected system shows no variation in peak radius values of about 1.2 Å at low temperatures, which is followed by a rise to 1.4 Å at 773.15 K. This indicates a larger growth of the peak radius with increasing temperature and the associated release of more CH4. The reason for this phenomenon may be as follows: (1) coal molecule thermal movement is strengthened, leading to the extension of pores and cracks, which open larger pathways for the diffusion of CH4; (2) the equilibrium between the adsorption and desorption processes in the coal at higher temperature moves toward desorption, weakening the adsorption of CH4 by the coal and making it easier to escape; also, CH4 molecules will have higher kinetic energy, enabling them to overcome the adsorption energy barrier; and (3) a higher temperature increases the gas molecules’ mean free path, reduces intermolecular interactions, enhances the mobility of molecules, and increases CH4 diffusivity. Overall, this results in a significant improvement in the CH4 desorption capacity.

At 373.15–773.15 K, peak radii y-values are 0.1122, 0.1275, 0.1290, 0.1305, and 0.1308 Å for N2 injection. The corresponding values are 0.1432, 0.1434, 0.1443, 0.1498, and 0.1521 Å for CO2, while H2O injection results in 0.1643, 0.1656, 0.1675, 0.1682, and 0.1733 Å. At each temperature, the corresponding y-values are always larger for H2O than those obtained with either N2 or CO2. It is considered that higher y-values indicate easier access to the pore site or better pore structure, which can facilitate the release of CH4 from the adsorbed to the free state, thus leading to a higher desorption capacity.

Conclusion

In this study, grand canonical Monte Carlo simulation and molecular dynamics simulation were used to investigate the CH4 desorption law and pore structure evolution of Xiaolongtan lignite containing trace O2 under the injection of N2, CO2 and H2O at 373.15–773.15 K. The main conclusions are as follows:(1)The promoting effect of the three gases on CH4 desorption follows the order: H2O > CO2 > N2. The average relative concentration of CH4 in the vacuum layer is the highest in the H2O system. The maximum diffusion coefficient reaches 3.454 × 10–20 m2·ps–1, and the diffusion activation energy is the lowest at 0.101 kcal/mol.(2)The desorption degree of O2 in coal also follows the order: H2O > CO2 > N2. H2O has the strongest competitive adsorption and displacement effect, which can effectively displace adsorbed O2 on the coal surface. CO2 can occupy only partial adsorption sites, while N2 has the weakest displacement effect on O2.(3)The peak pore radius reaches the maximum value of 1.4 Å under H2O injection. Superheated H2O exhibits the best pore modification effect and provides smooth migration channels for CH4.
