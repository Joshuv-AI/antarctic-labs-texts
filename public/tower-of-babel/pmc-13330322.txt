Unveiling the perfect workout: exercise modalities and dosages to combat visceral adipose tissue in individuals with overweight and obesity – a systematic review with pairwise, network, and dose-response meta-analyses
Authors: Yuanbo Chang, Lu Wang, Hai Wang, Enyan Zhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVisceral adipose tissue (VAT) plays a crucial role in obesity-related metabolic diseases, making its reduction an important target for obesity treatment. Although exercise has been shown to significantly reduce visceral fat, the specific mechanisms underlying the effects of different exercise modalities and exercise doses on VAT reduction remain unclear, particularly regarding the dose-response relationship, which has not been systematically explored.ObjectiveThis study aims to systematically compare the effects of different exercise modalities on visceral adipose tissue (VAT) reduction and to explore in depth the dose–response relationship between exercise dose and VAT decrease, thereby providing scientific evidence for optimizing individualized exercise prescriptions in obesity treatment.MethodsA systematic search of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) up to November 2024 was conducted (search updated in September 2025). Network meta-analysis (NMA) was employed to compare the effects of different exercise interventions on VAT, while Bayesian network meta-analysis (MBNMA) was used for dose-response analysis to evaluate the impact of exercise intensity, frequency, and duration on VAT reduction.ResultsThis study included 61 RCTs involving 4,136 participants. The NMA results indicated that all exercise interventions significantly reduced VAT compared to the control group. Among them, HIIT was the most effective in reducing VAT (SMD = -0.84, 95% CI [-1.08, -0.61], p < 0.0001, GRADE: Moderate; SUCRA = 99.3%, GRADE: High), followed by AE + RT (SMD = -0.54, 95% CI [-0.75, -0.32], p < 0.0001, GRADE: Moderate; SUCRA = 59.1%, GRADE: High), AE (SMD = -0.53, 95% CI [-0.68, -0.38], p < 0.001, GRADE: Low; SUCRA = 58.9%, GRADE: High), and RT (SMD = -0.41, 95% CI [-0.63, -0.20], p < 0.001, GRADE: Moderate; SUCRA = 32.7%, GRADE: High). The dose-response analysis showed a significant nonlinear dose-response relationship between total exercise volume and VAT, with the minimum effective dose being 730 METs min/week. Furthermore, the minimum effective doses for different exercise types varied. Specifically, HIIT significantly reduced VAT at a lower dose (400 METs min/week), while AE and AE + RT required a higher dose (1100 METs min/week) for significant reduction.ConclusionThis study reveals a nonlinear dose-response relationship between exercise dose and VAT reduction, demonstrating that HIIT is the most effective exercise modality for reducing VAT, with significant effects at low doses. AE and AE + RT are also effective but require higher doses (1100 METs min/week) to achieve significant VAT reduction. These findings provide specific dosage recommendations for exercise prescriptions for individuals with obesity, helping to optimize treatment plans and improve obesity management outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12986-026-01129-w.Keywords: Visceral adipose tissue, VAT, Exercise, Obesity, Exercise dosages, Meta-Analysis

Introduction

With the rising global obesity rates, visceral adipose tissue (VAT) has become a significant health risk factor and is a key contributor to various metabolic diseases, such as type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and certain types of cancer [1, 2]. Excessive accumulation of visceral fat, particularly in the abdominal area, has been shown to be closely associated with insulin resistance (IR), low-grade inflammation, and the onset of cardiovascular diseases [3–6]. Furthermore, visceral fat is considered a metabolically active and pro-inflammatory fat type, involved in energy metabolism, insulin signaling, and lipid metabolism regulation through the secretion of free fatty acids, cytokines, and hormones [7, 8]. Therefore, reducing visceral fat is considered a critical intervention target in addressing obesity and its associated complications.

Although pharmacological treatments, dietary interventions, and surgical methods have been used to address obesity and related diseases, exercise intervention remains the most feasible, sustainable, and cost-effective non-invasive solution [9]. Exercise has been proven to effectively reduce visceral fat and improve metabolic function, particularly in individuals with overweight and obesity [10]. AE and RT are the two most common forms of exercise, both of which have demonstrated potential in reducing visceral fat by improving aerobic capacity and increasing muscle strength, respectively. In recent years, combined exercise modalities, such as AE + RT and HIIT, have gained widespread attention due to their higher time efficiency and quicker health benefits [11–15–16].

However, despite existing research demonstrating the effects of these exercise modalities on body composition, the specific mechanisms and optimal intervention strategies for reducing visceral fat through different exercise modalities, doses, and intensities remain uncertain. In particular, current research lacks a standardized approach to the dosage of various exercise modalities (including frequency, intensity, and duration), with considerable heterogeneity observed across studies. Although some studies suggest that HIIT is more effective than traditional AE and RT in reducing visceral fat, these conclusions still lack systematic comparison and multi-level evidence support.

Therefore, this study aims to systematically evaluate and compare the effects of different exercise modalities—such as AE, RT, AE + RT, and HIIT—on reducing visceral fat through Pairwise, Network, and Dose-Response Meta-Analyses. Furthermore, we seek to explore the impact of exercise dosage and intensity on exercise outcomes. We plan to employ advanced network meta-analysis (NMA) methods, integrating data from RCTs of various exercise interventions to analyze the benefits of different exercise types in reducing visceral fat and to establish a dose-response model to provide recommendations for optimal exercise intervention dosages.

Methods

Registration

This study was performed according to the PRISMA guidelines [17]. The review is registered with PROSPERO under the ID: CRD42025635629.

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted across multiple databases, including PubMed, EBSCO, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and Embase, to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) examining the effects of exercise on visceral adipose tissue (VAT) in individuals with overweight or obesity. The search covered the period from database inception to November 2024 and was updated in September 2025. A combination of subject headings and free-text terms was used, and the search strategy was developed in accordance with the PICOS framework. The PICOS-based eligibility criteria and the complete database-specific search strategies are presented in Appendix 1 (Tables S1–S2). In addition, the reference lists of all included studies were manually screened to identify further potentially relevant articles.

Eligibility criteria

Studies that fulfilled the following inclusion criteria were deemed eligible: (1) RCTs; (2) Participants were individuals with overweight and/or obesity, where the European criteria defined overweight as a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² and obesity as BMI ≥ 30 kg/m², and the Asian criteria defined overweight as BMI ≥ 24 kg/m² and obesity as BMI ≥ 28 kg/m², with no age limitations; (3) The exercise intervention in the experimental group lasted a minimum of 8 weeks and included modalities such as AE, RT, AE + RT, or HIIT (specific intervention measures are listed in Table 1); (4) The control group comprised individuals who either did not engage in exercise or maintained their usual activity levels; (5) Visceral adipose tissue (VAT) levels were measured before and after the intervention, with no restrictions on the methods used for measurement; (6) Participants had no comorbid conditions.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Theses, conference proceedings, or abstracts; (2) Full text not available; (3) Unable to extract valid outcome data from the literature, and no response from the corresponding author; (4) Duplicate publications.

Two researchers (WH and BY) independently screened the titles and abstracts of the studies and further assessed the full texts based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved by consulting a third researcher (WL). The literature screening and management were performed using Endnote X9 (USA) software.

Data extraction

The literature was independently screened and data extracted by two researchers, who also cross-checked their results. Any discrepancies were addressed by consulting a third researcher for clarification. When data were missing, attempts were made to contact the authors for additional information. The screening process began with a review of titles and abstracts to exclude studies that were clearly irrelevant. Full texts of the remaining studies were then evaluated to determine their eligibility for data extraction. The data extraction process included details such as the first author, country, year of publication, participant characteristics (number of participants in both experimental and control groups, gender, age, BMI), intervention specifics (type of exercise, intensity, duration, frequency, and cycle), as well as the methods and units used for outcome measurement.

Data coding and management

The intensity of each exercise unit was calculated by multiplying the intensity of a specific exercise (measured in metabolic equivalents [METs]) by the duration of a single session, and then by the weekly frequency of exercise. The resulting value was expressed in MET-minutes per week (MET-min/week). The intensity for each exercise type was classified according to the 2024 Compendium of Physical Activities [20], which includes 821 activity-specific codes that encompass nearly all exercise forms. Exercise frequency was determined by the total number of sessions per week, including instances where multiple sessions were performed on a single day. Only the duration of the primary intervention exercise was considered in data extraction; if the duration was not explicitly stated, the average duration for that type of exercise across all studies was used. In cases where the duration increased gradually over several weeks, the average total duration of exercise was calculated.

To improve network connectivity and maintain model stability in the dose-response network meta-analysis, weekly exercise doses were categorized into six prespecified groups: 0 (control), 300, 600, 900, 1200, and 1500 MET-min/week. This strategy was adopted because direct comparisons were sparse across many exact dose values, making a fully continuous network structure less stable and less interpretable. Although this categorization was methodologically practical, it may have reduced dose resolution and introduced categorization bias.

Risk of bias and GRADE assessment

Two researchers independently evaluated the risk of bias (ROB) in the included studies using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool [21], which assesses seven distinct domains: (a) random sequence generation, (b) allocation concealment, (c) blinding of participants and personnel, (d) blinding of outcome assessment, (e) incomplete outcome data, (f) selective reporting of results, and (g) other potential sources of bias. Given the difficulty of blinding participants in exercise interventions, this aspect was not incorporated into the overall ROB rating. However, blinding of outcome assessors was still considered an important quality standard. The overall ROB for each study was classified as follows: studies with no domains rated as high ROB and three or fewer rated as unclear were categorized as low ROB; studies with one domain rated as high ROB or no high ROB domains but four or more unclear ratings were classified as medium ROB; all other cases were considered high ROB.

We assessed the certainty of evidence contributing to network estimates of the primary and secondary outcomes with the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework [22].

Data synthesis and statistical analyses

To reduce the influence of baseline variations, this study utilized the change in mean values and standard deviations before and after the intervention to calculate the effect sizes. The standard deviation for the change was determined using the formula outlined in the Cochrane Handbook(version 6.3) [23].

Direct pairwise meta-analyses

Considering the potential heterogeneity across interventions, this study employed both network meta-analysis and pairwise meta-analyses to further investigate variability among studies, utilizing Review Manager 5.3 (Nordic Cochrane, Denmark) [24]. For the pairwise meta-analysis in Review Manager 5.3, a random effects model was applied to calculate the SMD along with its 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Heterogeneity among the studies was assessed using the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test. Significant heterogeneity was indicated [25]. when the I² value exceeded 50% or the P-value from the Q test was ≤ 0.10. In addition, the presence of publication bias was assessed through funnel plot asymmetry analysis and Egger’s test [26].

Network meta-analysis

This study followed the PRISMA guidelines for network meta-analysis [27] and utilized a random effects model within a frequentist framework to combine effect sizes. Stata 16.0 software was employed to compute 95% confidence intervals (CIs) [28] to evaluate the effects of various exercise interventions on visceral adipose tissue (VAT). Since different measurement tools and units were used across the included studies, the standardized mean difference (SMD) was selected as the effect size metric to assess VAT changes.

A network evidence graph was created to illustrate the relationships between the different exercise interventions. The connections between nodes represented direct comparisons, with the line thickness reflecting the number of studies involved in each comparison and the node size corresponding to sample size. A network contribution plot was also generated to determine the relative contribution of each direct comparison.

To assess inconsistency within closed loops, the inconsistency factor and its 95% CI were calculated [29]. Overall inconsistency was tested using an inconsistency model, and if the P-value was > 0.05, a consistency model was used for further analysis [30]. Additionally, a node-splitting analysis was performed to detect local inconsistency, and if all P-values were greater than 0.05, the results were considered to have high reliability.

For ranking and comparing the effects of different exercise interventions, the SUCRA probability plot was used. This plot ranged from 0% to 100%, where 100% indicated the best intervention effect and 0% the worst [31, 32]. Lastly, funnel plots were employed to assess potential publication bias or small-sample effects in the studies included.

Dose–response NMAs

This study employed a Bayesian random-effects network meta-analysis (MBNMA) model to explore the dose-response relationship between exercise and visceral adipose tissue (VAT) [33]. The analysis results indicate that the key assumptions of the MBNMA did not show any abnormalities in this study, specifically including network transitivity [34], data consistency [35], and network connectivity [36] (see Appendix 10). Since the included studies used various tools and units to measure VAT, this study chose the SMD as the effect size measure and applied a 95% Credible Interval (CrI) to assess the confidence of the results.

In selecting the dose-response model, this study referenced several commonly recommended functions (such as Emax, restricted cubic splines, quadratic functions, and non-parametric functions) [37] and compared the fit indices of these functions, including the Deviance Information Criterion (DIC), between-study standard deviation, model parameters, and residual bias [38] (see Appendix 11). The results showed that the quadratic function model provided the best fit in all cases, and it was selected as the model for assessing the nonlinear dose-response relationship. The quadratic random-effects model converged well when handling nonlinear relationships and demonstrated higher accuracy in reflecting time-dependence and dose-dependence phenomena compared to the linear model [39].

In this model, the β coefficient was used to estimate the minimum exercise dose required to cause significant changes in visceral fat, and the interventions were ranked based on the likelihood of inducing changes according to different exercise types [33]. In dose-response analysis, results were considered statistically significant when the 95% credible interval for the effect size did not include zero. Finally, a 95% “prediction interval” was used to describe the potential variability in future study results, allowing for the estimation of the potential effects of implementing exercise interventions in the future [40]. All analyses were performed using the “MBNMAdose” package in R software (version 4.3.1), and dose-response curves and their visualizations were generated using the “ggplot2” package.

To further assess the potential impact of clinical heterogeneity on the transitivity assumption, exploratory univariable meta-regression analyses were conducted using mean age, baseline BMI, proportion of male participants, intervention duration (weeks), and exercise time per session as study-level moderators. Bubble plots were generated to visualize these associations, with effect size (SMD) as the dependent variable.

Results

Study identification and selection

Based on the inclusion criteria, the first search (November 2024) identified a total of 1,797 studies, and the second search (November 2024 to September 2025) identified 70 studies. At the initial screening stage, 1,732 duplicate and irrelevant studies were excluded by reading the titles and abstracts. Subsequently, the full texts of the remaining studies were assessed, and 74 studies that were not related to the research objectives were excluded. Finally, 61 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included. The study selection flow is shown in Fig. 1.

Literature screening process and results

Basic characteristics of included studies

This study included a total of 61 randomized controlled trials (RCTs), with study regions covering North America (22 studies), Asia (12 studies), Europe (11 studies), Oceania (9 studies), South America (6 studies), and Africa (1 study). These studies involved a total of 4,136 individuals with overweight and obesity, aged between 10 and 70 years, with 1,621 male participants, accounting for 39% of the total. The exercise intervention group included 2,903 individuals with overweight and obesity, including 1,432 individuals undergoing Aerobic exercise interventions, 569 individuals undergoing Resistance training interventions, 575 individuals undergoing Aerobic combined with resistance training interventions, and 327 individuals undergoing High-intensity interval training interventions. The control group included 1,226 individuals with overweight and obesity. Among the studies, 8 included only male participants, 24 included only female participants, 19 included both male and female participants, and 10 studies did not report gender information. The frequency of exercise interventions ranged from 1 to 7 times per week, with training durations ranging from 20 to 75 min per session. Detailed information can be found in Table 2.

Direct pairwise meta-analyses

This study first conducted a direct pairwise meta-analysis and provided subgroup analysis forest plots based on exercise types (Appendix 3). The results showed that, compared to the control group, AE (SMD = −0.60, P < 0.0001, 95% CI [−0.76, −0.44], I² = 57%), RT (SMD = −0.49, P = 0.002, 95% CI [−0.79, −0.18], I² = 67%), AE + RT (SMD = −0.41, P = 0.04, 95% CI [−0.80, −0.02], I² = 79%), and HIIT (SMD = −0.78, P < 0.0001, 95% CI [−1.11, −0.44], I² = 67%) all significantly reduced visceral adipose tissue (VAT). However, most studies exhibited high heterogeneity (I² > 50%). Funnel plot analysis and Egger’s test revealed significant publication bias only in the AE subgroup (P = 0.015), while no significant publication bias was observed in the other subgroups (Appendix 4).

Meta-regression analyses

Meta-regression analyses were conducted to examine whether age, baseline BMI, proportion of male participants, intervention duration, and exercise time per session influenced the intervention effect on VAT. No statistically significant moderating effect was observed for age (β = −0.002, 95% CI −0.0082 to 0.0046, P = 0.58), BMI (β = 0.0070, 95% CI −0.034 to 0.049, P = 0.74), exercise time per session (β = −0.0022, 95% CI −0.012 to 0.008, P = 0.66), proportion of male participants (β = −0.0027, 95% CI −0.006 to 0.001, P = 0.12), or intervention duration (β = 0.0073, 95% CI −0.003 to 0.018, P = 0.17). These findings provide no statistical evidence that these study-level characteristics materially modified the observed effects in the present dataset. The corresponding bubble plots are shown in Appendix 13 Fig. 9.

Dose–response NMAs

In the Bayesian dose–response NMA, assessment of network connectivity at both the treatment and dose levels showed no evidence of network disconnection. Consistency diagnostics also showed close agreement between the consistency and unrelated mean effects (UME) models (DIC: 905.9 vs. 906.3), supporting acceptable model consistency. Among the candidate nonlinear models, the quadratic random-effects model showed the best fit (DIC = 905.5). Detailed diagnostic results are provided in Appendices 10 and 11.

In the third part of this study, we employed a Bayesian-based network dose-response analysis method to explore the relationship between total exercise dose and VAT levels. The analysis revealed a negative nonlinear dose-response relationship between total exercise dose and VAT levels (Fig. 4). Discussion

In this NMA of randomized controlled trials, all exercise modalities were associated with significant reductions in VAT compared with the control condition. Among them, HIIT ranked highest and showed the largest relative effect. However, the broader dose-response findings suggest that the overall minimum effective exercise threshold for VAT reduction was approximately 730 MET-min/week. Importantly, the dose requirements differed across modalities: HIIT appeared to achieve significant VAT reduction at a lower weekly dose, whereas AE and AE + RT generally required higher volumes (approximately 1100 MET-min/week) to reach statistical significance. Therefore, our findings support not only the relative efficiency of HIIT, but also the broader importance of achieving a sufficient weekly exercise dose for VAT reduction.

Additionally, we identified a negative nonlinear dose-response relationship between total exercise volume and VAT levels. For practical interpretation, the estimated minimum effective threshold of 730 MET-min/week is approximately equivalent to 210 min of walking per week (3.5 METs), 185 min of cycling per week (4 METs), 145 min of resistance training per week (5 METs), 185 min of yoga per week (4 METs), or 245 min of Tai Chi per week (3 METs). Overall, higher exercise volume was generally associated with greater VAT reduction, suggesting that even beyond the upper limit of the World Health Organization (WHO) recommended physical activity range (1200 MET-min/week), continued exercise may still provide additional health benefits.

When examined by exercise modality, the dose-response patterns showed important differences. Specifically, AE and AE + RT showed a negative nonlinear dose-response relationship with VAT levels, whereas HIIT and RT demonstrated a U-shaped dose-response pattern. In particular, HIIT required a smaller dose (400 MET-min/week) to significantly improve VAT, and at a dose of 1000 MET-min/week, HIIT had the highest probability of producing the maximum effect. Although RT also showed a U-shaped relationship with VAT levels, this association did not reach statistical significance.

To further assess whether variation in participant and intervention characteristics may have influenced the indirect comparisons, we conducted exploratory meta-regression analyses using age, baseline BMI, proportion of male participants, intervention duration, and exercise time per session as study-level moderators. None of these variables showed a statistically significant association with effect size. This finding provides some support for the robustness of the main results at the study level; however, because these analyses were based on aggregate rather than individual participant data, residual heterogeneity and ecological bias cannot be fully excluded.

Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have confirmed that AE is an effective clinical intervention for reducing VAT [100–102]. Nevertheless, this finding should be interpreted with appropriate caution, as the certainty of evidence for AE was lower than that for some other intervention comparisons in the network. Further analysis indicated that the effect of AE may be influenced by exercise dose and intensity. Specifically, Bayesian dose-response analysis revealed a negative nonlinear dose-response relationship between AE volume and VAT levels. The minimum effective dose required for statistically significant results was approximately 1100 MET-min/week, equivalent to 315 min of walking per week (3.5 METs) or 275 min of cycling per week (4 METs). Furthermore, as exercise volume increased, the effect became more pronounced. Therefore, when interpreting the effects of AE, both exercise intensity and total weekly exercise dose should be taken into account.

RT in this study was also shown to significantly improve VAT (SMD = −0.41, 95% CI [−0.63, −0.20], p < 0.001), a finding consistent with previous meta-analysis results [103, 104]. The mechanism by which RT reduces VAT may partly be related to its effect on improving insulin sensitivity [105]. However, the benefits of RT extend beyond improving insulin sensitivity. Increased muscle mass and basal metabolic rate also play a crucial role in reducing VAT [106]. The increase in muscle mass can elevate resting metabolic rate, thereby increasing overall energy expenditure, which contributes to a reduction in fat storage [106].

Moreover, RT may promote lipolysis in adipocytes, improving the metabolic function of fat tissue and further reducing fat storage, particularly the accumulation of visceral fat [107, 108]. RT enhances the process of fat oxidation, which helps improve the distribution of body fat, particularly in reducing abdominal fat. Studies also suggest that RT can reduce inflammation associated with abdominal obesity, further optimizing fat metabolism [106]. These mechanisms collectively make RT an effective intervention, not only reducing VAT but also improving overall metabolic health.

Nevertheless, meta-analyses by Chang et al. [102] and Ismail et al. [101] failed to show a significant effect of RT on VAT reduction. This could be attributed to factors such as small sample sizes (7 and 14 studies, respectively), as well as differences in training intensity, sets, and repetitions. These factors may lead to significant variation in RT effects across different studies. For example, the intensity, frequency, and rest periods in RT may affect training outcomes, and these variables can vary considerably across studies. Additionally, the lack of subgroup analysis of training prescriptions (e.g., training volume, intensity, and work-rest ratios) within RT protocols further contributes to the inconsistency in intervention effects.

Although our study demonstrates that RT significantly reduces VAT in individuals with overweight and obesity, the overall effect size is relatively small (SMD = −0.41). Furthermore, SUCRA ranking results suggest that RT is likely the least effective intervention. This small effect size may be related to individual differences in responses to RT, particularly among individuals with different obesity types, ages, and health statuses, as RT effects may be influenced by a variety of factors. The effectiveness of RT may be more dependent on increasing muscle mass and improving basal metabolic rate, which are directly related to fat loss [109, 110]. However, for certain individuals, despite increases in muscle mass, reductions in abdominal fat may not be significant, especially in the absence of noticeable weight loss.

In the Bayesian-based network dose-response analysis, we found that although there was a U-shaped dose-response relationship between RT and VAT levels, this relationship did not reach statistical significance. This suggests that the effect of RT on visceral fat may be closely related to variations in training duration, intensity, and other training-related factors. This U-shaped relationship may imply that moderate-intensity RT is most likely to lead to significant reductions in visceral fat, while very high or low training intensities may fail to produce the expected results. Therefore, when designing RT programs for individuals with overweight and obesity, it is crucial to determine the appropriate training intensity and volume.

In conclusion, while the effectiveness of RT in reducing VAT has been validated, its significance and stability are still influenced by the training protocol and individual differences. Future studies should focus more on personalized RT prescriptions and long-term effects to define optimal training parameters and further explore the combined effects of RT with other interventions (e.g., aerobic exercise, dietary control).

Recent studies have demonstrated that AE + RT is one of the most effective approaches for optimizing body composition [18, 111] and metabolic health in individuals with overweight and obesity [112, 113]. In the present NMA, AE + RT significantly reduced VAT (SMD = −0.54, 95% CI [−0.75, −0.32], p < 0.0001) and ranked second in the SUCRA analysis. Compared with AE alone, the addition of RT may provide further benefits for preserving or increasing muscle mass while also contributing to VAT reduction. Previous studies have suggested that although AE can reduce VAT and other fat depots, combined aerobic and resistance training may offer broader benefits for body composition, including improvements in lean mass and bone-related outcomes [114].

Furthermore, multiple meta-analyses suggest that AE + RT may be particularly beneficial for improving obesity-related inflammatory markers [112, 114]. Our findings are consistent with this broader literature, as AE + RT showed favorable effects on VAT reduction and ranked highly among the included exercise modalities. Further Bayesian dose–response analysis revealed a negative nonlinear relationship between AE + RT volume and VAT levels. The minimum effective dose required to achieve statistically significant reductions was approximately 1100 METs-min/week, suggesting that AE + RT, similar to AE, generally requires a relatively higher weekly exercise volume to produce clear VAT reductions. This result is consistent with other studies, which suggest that high-intensity AE + RT training is more effective at reducing visceral fat. For example, previous studies have also found that sustained moderate-to-high intensity combined aerobic and resistance training significantly reduces VAT in individuals with overweight and obesity and plays an important role in improving cardiovascular risk [60, 115, 116].

Given these findings, when using AE + RT as an intervention to improve VAT levels, it is important to consider individual factors such as age, gender, and training levels to develop a personalized exercise program. It is recommended that the exercise volume should not be less than 1100 METs-min/week to ensure optimal intervention effects. Additionally, a personalized training program helps maximize the combined benefits of fat reduction, muscle growth, and bone health. Taken together, AE + RT appears to be a valuable intervention option for improving body composition, metabolic health, and inflammation in individuals with overweight and obesity.

HIIT has shown significant effects in reducing visceral adipose tissue (VAT) in individuals with overweight and obesity (SMD = −0.84, 95% CI [−1.08, −0.61], p < 0.0001). Compared to other interventions, HIIT has a more pronounced effect on reducing VAT. In the SUCRA probability ranking analysis, HIIT ranked highest among the included exercise modalities, suggesting that it may be a particularly efficient and time-efficient intervention strategy for reducing VAT in individuals with overweight and obesity. HIIT works by alternating short bursts of high-intensity exercise with brief recovery periods, activating the synergistic effect of multiple physiological mechanisms to effectively reduce VAT levels.

Recent studies have shown that high-intensity exercise can stimulate the secretion of interleukin-6, myokines, and irisin, which moderate-intensity aerobic exercise cannot produce, and this may be one of the key reasons why HIIT is most effective at reducing VAT [94, 117]. The increase in cytokines such as interleukin-6 improves the inflammatory state of adipose tissue and promotes fat breakdown. Additionally, HIIT can increase the resting metabolic rate in individuals with obesity, and the excess post-exercise oxygen consumption (EPOC) effect may be another mechanism through which HIIT induces VAT reduction [76, 118]. Studies suggest that HIIT, compared to moderate-intensity endurance training, shows a greater intensity-dependent enhancement of post-exercise oxygen consumption and fat utilization [119].

Moreover, HIIT has been found to be more effective than moderate-intensity endurance training in reducing postprandial insulin and triglycerides, as well as increasing morning fat oxidation the following day [120]. HIIT encourages the body to continue burning fat during the recovery phase (EPOC effect), improving fat oxidation rates and energy expenditure, which is particularly pronounced in reducing visceral fat. The metabolic boost following exercise, especially the sustained fat oxidation process after high-intensity training, further aids in reducing visceral fat [76, 118]. Studies also indicate that high-intensity training significantly enhances fat breakdown in individuals with obesity, especially visceral fat, due to a substantial increase in catecholamine release (such as adrenaline and noradrenaline), which stimulates lipolysis in visceral fat tissue via β-adrenergic receptors [121, 122].

Thus, HIIT appears to be a time-efficient and potentially dose-efficient intervention option that may improve VAT and broader metabolic health outcomes. Further Bayesian dose–response analysis revealed that, compared with AE and AE + RT, which generally required approximately 1100 METs-min/week to achieve statistically significant VAT reductions, HIIT appeared to produce significant effects at a lower dose of 400 MET-min/week. Additionally, at a dose of 1000 METs-min/week, HIIT has the highest probability of producing maximum effects. This suggests that HIIT not only generates significant results at lower doses but also has substantial potential for effect enhancement at higher doses, further highlighting its advantage as an efficient exercise intervention.

This characteristic of low dose, high benefit makes HIIT an ideal exercise modality for individuals with overweight and obesity, especially those with fat accumulation issues. Through HIIT, individuals can not only reduce visceral fat but also improve overall metabolic health, reduce obesity-related metabolic risk factors, and further enhance fat oxidation ability and overall metabolic rate. Therefore, HIIT may represent a particularly efficient exercise strategy for improving VAT, body composition, and metabolic health in individuals with overweight and obesity.

Clinical implications

This meta-analysis has several clinical implications. First, the findings indicate that exercise interventions can effectively reduce VAT in individuals with overweight and obesity, with HIIT showing the highest relative ranking among the included modalities. Second, the overall minimum effective exercise dose associated with significant VAT reduction was approximately 730 MET-min/week, suggesting that the World Health Organization (WHO) recommended minimum of 600 MET-min/week may be insufficient for achieving meaningful VAT reduction in some exercise modalities. Third, exercise prescription should consider both modality and dose: HIIT may achieve significant VAT reduction at a lower weekly dose, whereas AE and AE + RT generally require higher weekly exercise volumes to produce statistically significant effects. For individuals with lower exercise tolerance, older age, or limited capacity to perform HIIT, alternative modalities such as AE or AE + RT may still be effective, provided that sufficient weekly exercise volume is achieved.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it combined pairwise meta-analysis, network meta-analysis, and dose–response network meta-analysis to comprehensively evaluate the effects of different exercise modalities and doses on VAT. Second, the inclusion of multiple intervention types and a relatively large number of randomized controlled trials enabled broader comparisons across exercise modalities and improved the overall robustness of the findings. Third, by examining both comparative effectiveness and dose–response relationships, this study provides clinically relevant information for exercise prescription in individuals with overweight and obesity.

However, several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, although exploratory meta-regression analyses did not identify significant effect modification by age, baseline BMI, proportion of male participants, intervention duration, or exercise time per session, these analyses were based on study-level rather than individual-level data; therefore, residual clinical heterogeneity and ecological bias cannot be fully excluded. Second, VAT was assessed using multiple methods across studies, including DXA, MRI, CT, ultrasound, BIA-based devices, and formula-derived indices, which may differ in accuracy, sensitivity, and measurement precision. We carefully considered whether this issue could be addressed more directly by stratified analysis; however, because several assessment categories were represented by only a limited number of studies and were unevenly distributed across comparisons, such an analysis was not feasible in a sufficiently robust and interpretable manner within the current revision. Although standardized mean differences helped to accommodate differences in measurement scales, they could not fully eliminate heterogeneity related to differences in measurement sensitivity and validity across assessment methods. Third, some potentially important covariates, such as dietary co-intervention, energy deficit, adherence, and baseline fitness, were inconsistently reported and therefore could not be fully incorporated into the quantitative analyses. Finally, heterogeneity remained moderate to high in some pairwise comparisons, and female participants were overrepresented in part of the included literature, which may affect the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusions

In this pairwise, network, and dose–response meta-analysis, exercise interventions were associated with significant reductions in VAT in individuals with overweight and obesity, and a nonlinear dose–response relationship was identified. The overall minimum effective exercise dose associated with significant VAT reduction was approximately 730 MET-min/week. Among the included exercise modalities, HIIT ranked highest and appeared to achieve significant VAT reduction at lower doses, whereas AE and AE + RT were also effective but generally required higher weekly exercise volumes (approximately 1100 MET-min/week) to reach statistical significance. These findings suggest that both exercise modality and sufficient weekly exercise dose should be considered when prescribing exercise interventions for VAT reduction. Future large-scale and long-term trials are needed to further clarify optimal dose ranges and intervention parameters across different populations.
