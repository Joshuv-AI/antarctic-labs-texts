Quantifying medical device cybersecurity risk with CVSS BTE
Authors: Lorenzo Bracciale, Sasha Riozzi, Giorgia Panico, Emanuele Raso, Giuseppe Bianchi, Pierpaolo Loreti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Common Vulnerability Scoring System (CVSS) is the de facto standard for assessing the severity of cybersecurity vulnerabilities. Yet, in practice, it is often misused. Most stakeholders rely solely on the so called CVSS “Base Score”, a measure of technical severity, ignoring the contextual metrics introduced in the 4.0 version: “Threat” and “Environmental”. This narrow use results in poor prioritization, especially in safety-critical domains like healthcare. In this study, we perform the first large-scale application of CVSS 4.0’s full Base, Threat, Environmental (BTE) scoring to a comprehensive dataset of medical device vulnerabilities. We show that the Threat group can be partially automated using structured data sources, while meaningful environmental profiles (e.g., home vs hospital care) allow semi-automatic compilation of the Environmental metrics. Our results confirm that BTE scoring significantly changes vulnerability prioritization, yielding a representation of risk that is both more accurate and more actionable, especially when safety is involved.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In vulnerability management, a central challenge is prioritization. Organizations rarely have the resources to remediate every disclosed vulnerability immediately, so they must decide which ones demand urgent attention. Ideally, prioritization should be based on a rigorous assessment of risk. Yet risk is inherently complex, depending not only on technical factors but also on organizational context, attacker capabilities, and the value of the affected assets. Estimating it consistently across heterogeneous environments is therefore difficult, often impractical, and resource-intensive.

To cope with this complexity, most organizations rely on the CVSS, the de facto global standard for classifying vulnerability severity. Virtually every publicly known vulnerability is registered in the Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVE) catalog, and each CVE entry is typically accompanied by a CVSS score. This score is expressed as a numerical value from 0.0 to 10.0 and is designed to represent the technical severity of the vulnerability, not its actual risk. The widespread appeal of CVSS lies in its universality: scores are readily available, easy to compare, and consistent across domains.

However, CVSS, especially in its widely used Base Score, captures only the intrinsic technical properties of a vulnerability without regard to the context in which it occurs. This very simplicity, which makes the metric accessible and scalable, is also its main weakness. Scores are usually assigned by third-party analysts, such as the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA), who necessarily lack insight into the specific deployment environments. As a result, the CVSS Base Score provides a quick but context-blind assessment that can easily misrepresent the real-world risk posed by a vulnerability. This limitation has been repeatedly highlighted in the literature: the scoring formula does not rest on a solid mathematical foundation1, the documentation is inconsistent, and, crucially, there is no proven correlation between high scores and actual exploitation in the wild2,3.

The limitations of relying solely on CVSS scores are not abstract, they manifest with concrete consequences. A recent example is |CVE-2024-11053|4, disclosed in January 2025, which affected cURL, a widely used utility embedded in millions of systems. The vulnerability initially received a CVSS score of 9.1, classified as “Critical.” Automated tools immediately flagged it, the media amplified the alert, and security teams worldwide rushed to apply mitigations. However, the maintainers of cURL themselves rated it as low severity, emphasizing that the conditions required for exploitation were highly specific and unlikely. In practice, it posed negligible risk. This disconnect between the CVSS score and the developers’ assessment triggered a broad debate, culminating in a public statement by the project’s creator pointedly titled: “CVSS is dead for us”5.

This case illustrates a structural weakness in the use of CVSS: the persistent confusion between severity and risk. A high technical score does not necessarily translate into high risk, since risk also depends on the likelihood of exploitation and, critically, on the potential consequences of a successful attack.

The distinction between severity and risk is crucial. CVSS scores measure the intrinsic technical severity of a vulnerability, but in practice they are often interpreted as if they reflected actual risk2,6. This conceptual overlap leads to misguided prioritization decisions, operational inefficiencies, and in some cases unjustified alarmism. The organization that developed CVSS, the Forum of Incident Response and Security Teams (FIRST), has repeatedly stressed this point. The official guide to CVSS v3.1 explicitly states: “The Base Score measures severity, not risk.”

Risk is inherently contextual. It depends not only on the exploitability of a vulnerability, but also on the value of the affected asset, the attacker model considered, and above all the potential consequences of a successful attack. Unlike severity, risk is dynamic, it changes over time, and it is to some extent subjective. For this reason it has been described as an almost “religious” concept7, and it cannot be reliably captured by a single static score.

This misalignment becomes even more critical in safety-critical domains, where the consequences of a vulnerability extend far beyond data loss or service disruption1. A prime example is medical devices: embedded systems that interact directly with patients. In this context, vulnerabilities do not merely threaten confidentiality, integrity, or availability, but can directly affect patient safety and, in extreme cases, human life.

Historically, CVSS has struggled to capture these real-world risks. Its Base Score, the most widely used component, is detached from deployment context and ignores factors such as physical safety or system exposure. As a result, it can produce counter-intuitive comparisons. For instance, |CVE-2019-10964|8 describes a flaw in an insulin pump, where an unauthenticated attacker within radio range could alter dosage data and take control of insulin delivery, with potentially fatal consequences. Yet it received a lower score than |CVE-2025-27677|9, a vulnerability affecting a printer process. The discrepancy arises from the fact that CVSS, until recently, included no explicit notion of safety or impact on human life.

In response to these limitations, alternative scoring models have been proposed. One example is the Healthcare Vulnerability Scoring System (HVSS), a variant of CVSS v3.1 specifically adapted to medical contexts. However, these approaches remain niche and have not displaced CVSS, which continues to be by far the most widely adopted metric across the cybersecurity landscape.

This does not mean that CVSS is useless for estimating medical risk. Rather, it requires going beyond the simple Base Score. The CVSS framework actually defines two additional groups of metrics: Threat (T) and Environmental (E). These components have received considerably less attention in the literature and remain relatively unknown even among practitioners. When combined with the Base Score (B), they yield the CVSS-BTE score, a single value still ranging from 0.0 to 10.0, but designed to capture severity within a context. The Threat metric reflects the current likelihood of exploitation, based on factors such as exploit availability or observed activity in the wild. The Environmental metric captures the specific deployment conditions of the affected system. Notably, in CVSS v4.0 the Environmental group has been expanded to include a Safety metric, explicitly designed to account for potential physical harm to humans, a critical factor in domains such as healthcare, automotive, or industrial control systems.

According to FIRST, the full BTE score provides a better approximation of real-world risk than previous CVSS versions10, and in some cases may even outperform commercial “risk ratings”7. This view is supported by recent studies11, which highlight the enhanced adaptability of CVSS v4.0 to specific contexts, representing a step forward in contextualized risk assessment, although opportunities for further improvement remain.

Despite these theoretical advances, a critical gap remains between the potential of CVSS v4.0 and its use in practice. Base Scores are readily available through public repositories such as the National Vulnerability Database (NVD)12, while the Threat and Environmental components are rarely computed and almost never reported. Their applicability therefore remains uncertain. Can they be derived reliably without expert input, or do they reintroduce the same limitations of traditional risk assessments? And if they can be computed, do they meaningfully change how vulnerabilities are prioritized? Most importantly, how do the new CVSS v4.0 metrics alter prioritization when safety becomes a factor?

This work addresses these questions by focusing on a well-defined domain: vulnerabilities affecting medical devices. In this context, where software flaws can translate directly into risks for patient safety, the shortcomings of relying solely on the Base Score become particularly evident. By applying CVSS v4.0 in its full BTE formulation, we aim to evaluate whether contextual metrics can be leveraged in practice to produce more accurate and actionable prioritization of vulnerabilities in safety-critical environments.

Results

How CVSS 4.0 alters the base scores of medical device vulnerabilities

This study is based on the new CVSS standard, version 4.0, released on November 1, 2023. As of today, the National Vulnerability Database (NVD) includes CVSS 4.0 scores only for recently disclosed vulnerabilities. To address this gap, we manually computed CVSS 4.0 Base scores for all the 470 vulnerabilities affecting medical devices, following the procedure described in the method sections. As a first step, we compared the CVSS 4.0 Base Score with its CVSS 3.1 counterpart to assess differences in severity evaluation across versions.

As illustrated in Fig. 1a , which displays the score differences across the evaluated cases, we observed that CVSS 4.0 Base Scores are, on average, higher than those obtained using CVSS 3.1. Specifically, the mean increase was approximately 0.77 points, despite evaluating the same set of vulnerabilities under both scoring systems. This can be explained by the modified metrics introduced in the latest version of CVSS10. In particular, the removal of the Scope parameter, replaced with a more structured evaluation that considers both the vulnerable system and the Subsequent System, results in a higher severity score being assigned to individual CVEs.

(a) Difference between CVSS v4.0 Base and v3.1 Base. (b) CVE distribution by Year.

However, this is not always the case, as we can see by the case CVE-2020-1622213. This vulnerability concerns the Patient Information Center iX system14, which fails to perform strict or complete checks to confirm that the entity requesting access is who they claim to be. The CVSS v3.1 Base vector assigned to this vulnerability is AV:A/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:H/A:H, resulting in a Base Score of 8.8. When evaluated using the CVSS v4.0 Base vector AV:A/AC:H/AT:P/PR:N/UI:N/VC:L/VI:L/VA:L/SC:L/SI:L/SA:L, the resulting score is 2.3. This reflects a decrease of 6.5 points, indicating a significantly lower severity according to the CVSS 4.0 standard. In this case, the introduction of the additional Attack Requirements (AT) metric in CVSS v4.0-B, indicating that the attack depends on specific conditions (e.g., partial authentication), contributes to a reduction in the overall score.

Is this trend also observed in non-medical devices? To address this question, we compiled a dataset of vulnerabilities for a representative class of non-medical devices, namely printers. Using the same methodology applied to medical devices, we identified 368 CVEs, each annotated with its CVSS v3.1 vector, and converted them to CVSS v4.0-B scores. The average severity increased from 7.67 (CVSS v3.1) to 8.24 (CVSS v4.0), corresponding to a mean increment of 0.57. This increase, comparable to the 0.77 observed for medical devices, demonstrates that the upward shift in severity introduced by CVSS 4.0 is systematic and not confined to the medical domain.

Toward semi-automated estimation of CVSS v4.0 BTE scores for medical devices

As discussed earlier, the CVSS Base Score alone is a poor proxy for actual risk, as it does not consider crucial factors such as the likelihood of an attack. Nonetheless, it remains widely used due to its simplicity and its immediate availability in public vulnerability databases. In contrast, the CVSS-BTE score offers a more nuanced and realistic estimation by incorporating both threat and environmental considerations. But is it just as straightforward to compute? To explore this question, we attempted to partially automate the calculation of the additional components of the CVSS 4.0, focusing in particular on the Threat and Environmental metric groups.

Automatically populate the Threat information

The Threat metric group consists of just one metric, the Exploit Maturity, which can take one of the following values: i) Attacked (A), indicating confirmed exploitation in the wild; ii) Proof-of-Concept (P) , where public exploit code exists but no attacks have been observed; or iii) Unreported (U), for vulnerabilities with no known exploits or exploitation attempts.

To enable large-scale and consistent risk evaluation, we first explored the possibility of automating the assignment of CVSS Threat metrics using a predictive metric released by FIRST: the Exploit Prediction Scoring System (EPSS)15. The EPSS provides a daily-updated, data-driven score estimating the real-world likelihood of exploitation for each CVE. Moreover, it is publicly available via API, making it an ideal candidate for automatically populating the Threat metrics.

We hypothesized that Exploit Maturity classes could be inferred directly from EPSS scores, thus enabling fast and scalable enrichment of CVSS vectors. To validate this approach, we implemented two techniques: grid search and kernel density estimation (KDE), mapping EPSS values to maturity levels. As ground truth for training and validation, we conducted an extensive crawling of major exploit and vulnerability databases. Evidence of active exploitation (A) was collected from the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) Catalog16, Google Project Zero17, and the Zero Day Initiative (ZDI)18. For confirming the presence of public proof-of-concept code (P), we considered Metasploit19, GitHub repositories20, and Exploit-DB21. Our goal was to establish decision thresholds on the EPSS score distribution that can automatically assign Exploit Maturity levels with acceptable confidence and alignment to observed exploit availability.

Both methods used to determine thresholds on EPSS scores for inferring Exploit Maturity proved effective on the dataset under study. However, we observed that the resulting thresholds are highly dataset-dependent and may vary even when modifying the dataset itself, despite it still containing vulnerabilities related to medical devices. To validate this observation, we attempted to generalize our findings to other types of vulnerabilities. As illustrated in Fig. 2b , we applied the same classification strategy to a set of vulnerabilities unrelated to the medical field, specifically targeting printer devices and generic CVEs. The performance drops significantly, with accuracy deteriorating sharply, demonstrating that the threat behavior captured by EPSS is highly dataset-dependent. This finding highlights the importance of contextual calibration when leveraging data-driven threat estimation methods for CVSS enrichment.

(a) Distribution of Exploit Maturity classifications across the dataset. (b) Accuracy drop when applying domain-specific EPSS thresholds to unrelated domains.

Therefore, the most reliable approach for automating the Threat metric remains the direct integration of public vulnerability databases that report either the presence of proof-of-concept exploits20–22 or documented evidence of active exploitation16–18. This analysis can be performed at both global and local levels, with a substantial impact on the resulting values, as presented below.

Practical impact of Threat metrics on severity scoring

Introducing the Threat metric reduces the score compared to the Base Score alone, as shown in Fig. 3. This reduction occurs because, in the absence of threat information, the default assumption corresponds to the worst-case scenario, namely that the vulnerability is actively exploited.

Comparison of CVSS v4.0 Base (B) and Base + Threat (BT) score distributions, showing the effect of incorporating exploitability evidence.

In practice, the opposite often holds: for many vulnerabilities, there is neither evidence of exploitation nor the availability of public proof-of-concept code. This imbalance is highlighted in Fig. 2a , where the U class (Unreported) dominates over the other two categories. This distribution indicates that most analyzed vulnerabilities are unlikely to be exploited, given the absence of publicly available exploit code or records of real-world attacks. Incorporating the Threat metric thus deprioritizes the majority of vulnerabilities lacking evidence of exploitation, yielding severity scores that more accurately capture their real-world exploitability.

Approximating environmental metrics using deployment profiles

As the next step in automating the CVSS-BTE score, we turned to the Environmental metric group. This component presents a significant challenge for automation, as it is intended to capture characteristics specific to the deployment environment. In the case of medical devices, however, we identified two meaningful and representative usage scenarios: the hospital environment and the home environment.

The hospital setting assumes that the device operates in a regulated and controlled environment, where trained healthcare professionals are present, network infrastructure is protected, and technical support is readily available. While the consequences of a security breach can be severe, the likelihood of attack propagation is generally lower. In this context, availability is typically prioritized, whereas confidentiality may be less critical, except in specific use cases. In contrast, the home environment assumes that the device is used directly by patients or caregivers, often without supervision, and typically connected to less secure networks. Under these conditions, device safety and autonomous operation become even more critical. These two scenarios affect the parameters of the Environmental metric in different ways, as detailed in Section Methods.

Our analysis shows that, with our assumptions, vulnerabilities evaluated in the home environment yield higher CVSS-BTE scores than those assessed in the hospital context as shown in Fig. 4. This result is consistent with expectations: clinical settings are typically equipped with more effective mitigation mechanisms, including emergency management infrastructure and the presence of trained personnel.

Distribution of CVSS 4.0 BTE scores computed under the two environmental profiles: hospital and home.

The inclusion of environmental metrics can increase or decrease the overall CVSS-BTE score, depending on the deployment context and the specific values assigned to the Safety and Security Requirements parameters. In our analysis, the score computed for the home environment was found to be up to 2.3 points higher than the score obtained for the same vulnerability assessed in a hospital setting. An illustrative example is vulnerability CVE-2017-965423, affecting the Philips DoseWise Portal application14, where user credentials are stored in plaintext, potentially exposing sensitive data. The CVSS v4.0-BTE vector for the hospital context is AV:N/AC:L/AT:P/PR:L/UI:N/VC:H/VI:N/VA:N/SC:H/SI:N/SA:N/E:U/CR:L/IR:M/AR:M, yielding a score of 1.9. For the home environment, where confidentiality, integrity, and availability requirements are all set to high, the vector becomes AV:N/AC:L/AT:P/PR:L/UI:N/VC:H/VI:N/VA:N/SC:H/SI:N/SA:N/E:U/CR:H/IR:H/AR:H, resulting in a final score of 3.8.

Since this vulnerability exclusively affects data confidentiality, the Safety metric is not considered in either of the two evaluated contexts, as it is only applied when the vulnerability impacts the integrity or availability of the device. Moreover, an examination of the two vector strings reveals that the only difference lies in the values assigned to the Security Requirements metrics. This result further supports the earlier observation that the presence of mitigation measures in clinical settings leads to lower severity scores compared to scenarios where such protections cannot be guaranteed.

The observed increase of +1.9 points underscores the critical role of deployment context in medical device risk evaluation, highlighting how environmental assumptions can substantially alter perceived severity.

Impact of Threat and Environment metrics on qualitative ratings

After analyzing the role of contextual factors in severity scoring, we compared scores obtained by integrating the Threat and Environmental metrics (BTE) with those derived solely from the Base metrics (B), which served as the reference model. Figure 5 shows how the distribution of BTE scores diverges from both CVSS v4.0 Base and CVSS v3.1 Base scores. The inclusion of Threat and Environmental metrics generally lowers the overall score, indicating that real-world exploitability and deployment context tend to moderate severity assessments.

Distribution and boxplot comparison of CVSS v3.1 Base (B), CVSS v4.0 Base (B), Base + Threat (BT), and Base + Threat + Environmental (BTE) scores, computed under both hospital and home deployment profiles.

We then examined how CVSS v4.0 BTE affects the assignment of severity classes, or Qualitative Ratings (None, Low, Medium, High, Critical). Figure 6a reports the class transitions for medical device vulnerabilities (82 CVEs) under hospital deployment assumptions, while Fig. 6b shows the corresponding results for home deployment (48 CVEs).

Transition matrices from CVSS v3.1 B to CVSS v4.0 BTE under two environmental profiles. Each matrix includes marginal histograms showing the distribution of severity classes before and after the transition.

The results reveal substantial shifts in class assignments. This has important implications: if severity classes are used as the basis for prioritizing remediation actions, the change introduced by CVSS v4.0 BTE may significantly alter the perceived urgency of addressing certain vulnerabilities, with specifically 36.8% of the vulnerabilities moved from critical/high to medium/low in the hospital profile and 20% in the home profile.

Discussion

One of the main challenges in vulnerability assessment is bridging the gap between theoretical risk models and practical implementation. While expert analysis provides the most accurate results, time and resource constraints often push organizations to rely on simplified metrics such as CVSS Base Scores, which are context-blind and externally assigned.

This study explores a middle ground between these two extremes. By leveraging the new CVSS v4.0 BTE framework, we demonstrate that it is possible to improve risk prioritization through structured, context-aware metrics, without requiring full-scale expert evaluation for every individual vulnerability. The BTE model offers a standardized and well-documented structure that better aligns severity scoring with real-world risk, while preserving the advantages of consistency, scalability, and partial automation.

Our results suggest that computing the full BTE vector is partially feasible in practice, with some components amenable to automation and others requiring contextual assumptions. Specifically, the Threat group can be reliably derived through automation, thanks to structured datasets, while the Environmental metrics are inherently more complex, as they depend on the specific operational context. However, in domain-specific settings like healthcare, it is possible to define scenario-based profiles (e.g., hospital vs. home use) that allow consistent and semi-automated assignment of these values.

Even with simplified assumptions, the impact on prioritization is substantial. In both hospital and home environments, severity scores decreased when moving from Base to BTE. Notably, 36.8% of vulnerabilities shifted from critical/high to medium/low under the hospital profile, and 20% under the home profile. These results suggest that many vulnerabilities labeled “high” or “critical” by CVSS v3.1 Base may warrant lower priority once contextual factors are considered.

Given that CVSS v3.1 Base remains the de facto standard in many vulnerability management workflows, our findings highlight the need for a shift toward richer, more contextualized models like CVSS v4.0 BTE. By combining contextual risk factors and safety considerations, the BTE approach enables healthcare organizations to focus mitigation efforts on what truly matters: vulnerabilities that pose real threats to patient safety or the reliable functioning of medical devices.

In conclusion, adopting the full CVSS v4.0 BTE framework substantially alters the prioritization of medical device vulnerabilities compared to relying on the Base score alone. Its operationalization is feasible by integrating NVD data for Base metrics, public Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI) feeds for Threat metrics, and asset management information for Environmental factors7. Future work will extend this approach to larger datasets and additional domains, with the goal of enhancing automation while preserving the contextual accuracy required for effective risk-based prioritization.

Methods

Background: the CVE ecosystem

Cybersecurity research and practice rely on a set of common standards that together form what can be called the CVE ecosystem. At the center of this ecosystem lies the CVE identifier, a universal label for publicly disclosed vulnerabilities. Around it, complementary taxonomies and scoring systems, such as CWE, CPE, and CVSS, enrich each entry with additional layers of information that support classification, prioritization, and remediation. In this study, we provide an overview of these elements with particular focus on CVSS, which is the core subject of our analysis.

Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVE) The Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVE) system provides a standardized method to uniquely identify vulnerabilities in information technology products. Each entry in the CVE catalog consists of a CVE identifier (CVE-ID) and a brief description of the issue. By design, CVE entries do not contain detailed technical data, exploit code, or remediation guidance. Instead, they serve as a minimal reference point that can be enriched by other databases and tools.

The CVE program has grown rapidly in scale. As of early 2025, more than 286,000 CVEs have been assigned, with 40,077 disclosed in 2024 alone, representing a 617% increase compared to 201512. The current trend indicates that more than 12,000 new vulnerabilities were published in the first quarter of 2025, suggesting further acceleration. Each CVE can be linked to one or more weakness types, described through CWE identifiers, and to the affected platforms, described through CPE names.

Common Weakness Enumeration (CWE) The Common Weakness Enumeration (CWE)24 is a taxonomy that classifies recurring patterns of software and hardware weaknesses. Each CWE entry corresponds to a specific type of defect, ranging from broad categories such as buffer overflows to highly specialized flaws in code or design. The catalog is maintained by MITRE and provides a hierarchical structure that allows vulnerabilities to be described at different levels of granularity. In practice, the National Vulnerability Database (NVD) enriches CVE records by assigning one or more CWE identifiers, chosen from this hierarchy. This mapping enables vulnerabilities to be grouped and analyzed not only individually but also as classes of similar weaknesses, supporting both fine-grained and high-level assessments25.

Common Platform Enumeration (CPE) The Common Platform Enumeration (CPE)26 provides a structured naming scheme for identifying IT products, including hardware, operating systems, and applications. Based on the syntax of Uniform Resource Identifiers (URIs), CPE names ensure that vulnerabilities can be consistently linked to the specific platforms they affect. The CPE dictionary is curated by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) and is publicly accessible. Its use in vulnerability management is widespread, as it allows organizations to quickly determine whether a given vulnerability applies to their systems.

Common Vulnerability Scoring System (CVSS) The Common Vulnerability Scoring System (CVSS) is the de facto global standard for quantifying the severity of vulnerabilities. Originally introduced by the National Infrastructure Advisory Council (NIAC) and now maintained by FIRST (Forum of Incident Response and Security Teams), CVSS assigns a numerical score ranging from 0.0 to 10.0. This score is computed by selecting values for a set of predefined metrics, which are encoded in a vector string, a compact textual representation widely used in vulnerability databases.

CVSS has evolved over time. Version 2.0 (2007) achieved wide adoption but was criticized for its simplicity. Version 3.0 (2015) and its refinement 3.1 (2019) introduced more detailed metrics, such as Privileges Required and User Interaction. The most recent release, CVSS v4.0 (2023), introduces substantial innovations designed to improve contextualization and risk representation10.

In its current form, CVSS is structured around four metric groups:

Base (B): describes the intrinsic technical properties of the vulnerability, such as attack vector, attack complexity, privileges required, user interaction, and impacts on confidentiality, integrity, and availability;

Together, these groups form the CVSS-BTE score, which ranges from 0.0 to 10.0 and integrates both technical severity and contextual factors. The introduction of the Threat and Safety metrics marks a significant step toward aligning CVSS with real-world risk, especially in safety-critical domains such as healthcare. Governance and Institutional Roles The ecosystem described above involves several organizations, each with a specific role. The CVE program is managed by the MITRE Corporation, a private non-profit that operates Federally Funded Research and Development Centers (FFRDCs) on behalf of the U.S. government. MITRE coordinates the network of CVE Numbering Authorities (CNAs), assigns identifiers, and also maintains other widely used cybersecurity frameworks such as ATT&CK.

The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) maintains the National Vulnerability Database (NVD), which enriches CVE records with standardized attributes including CVSS scores, CWE identifiers, and CPE names. NVD has become the most widely used global repository for vulnerability data.

Given the accelerating growth in the number of vulnerabilities, NIST recently introduced the concept of Authorized Data Publishers (ADPs), external organizations allowed to contribute enrichment data. The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) is today the most active ADP, adding CVSS scores where missing, mapping CWE and CPE, and maintaining the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalog, which highlights vulnerabilities confirmed to be exploited in the wild.

Finally, the Common Vulnerability Scoring System (CVSS) itself is maintained by FIRST (Forum of Incident Response and Security Teams), an international non-profit association that develops open standards for incident response and vulnerability management. FIRST publishes the official specifications of CVSS, including calculators and documentation, and coordinates the evolution of the standard.

Complementary Approaches Although CVSS remains the dominant framework, other initiatives have emerged to complement or refine vulnerability assessment. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System (EPSS)15 estimates the probability that a vulnerability will be exploited within 30 days, using machine learning models trained on historical data. The Stakeholder-Specific Vulnerability Categorization (SSVC)27, developed by Carnegie Mellon University and CISA, provides a decision-tree framework that guides remediation actions based on exploitation status, mission impact, and safety considerations.

In the healthcare domain, Edwards Lifesciences proposed the Healthcare Vulnerability Scoring System (HVSS)28, which introduces metrics tailored to clinical environments, such as patient safety and hospital breach scenarios, and integrates AI-based assistants to support evaluation. Similarly, MITRE developed a rubric for applying CVSS to medical devices29, which incorporates patient privacy and safety impact into the evaluation process. However, these approaches have seen limited adoption and do not alter the official CVSS scoring system.

Together, these complementary systems highlight the limitations of relying exclusively on CVSS Base scores and underscore the need for more contextualized approaches. Nevertheless, CVSS remains the most widely adopted metric, and its evolution in version 4.0 provides an opportunity to re-examine how severity scoring can approximate real-world risk, particularly in safety-critical domains such as healthcare.

Semi-automatic risk estimation of medical devices

Dataset preparation

We began by constructing a dataset of vulnerabilities related to medical devices. The dataset was built from the Industrial Control Systems Medical Advisories (ICSMAs) issued by the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA), which report specific cybersecurity vulnerabilities affecting medical devices.

All medical-related CVEs published through ICSMA were collected via web scraping from CISA’s public GitHub30 repository. In addition, relevant advisories were downloaded from the official CISA website; CVE identifiers were extracted, and the corresponding details were retrieved directly from the NVD. To ensure data consistency and eliminate duplicates, the lists obtained from various sources were cross-validated and merged into a single unified dataset. Each entry contains the CVE identifier, the associated attack vector, the severity score according to CVSS v3.1, and the category of medical device affected by the vulnerability.

The final dataset includes 470 CVEs related to medical devices, disclosed between 1999 and 2025. Figure 1b shows the distribution of CVEs by year. Most of the vulnerabilities (approximately 94%) are relatively recent, having been discovered within the past ten years.

Base score estimation

The first step consisted of computing the CVSS v4.0 Base Score for all vulnerabilities in the dataset.

Since CVSS v4.0 is still recent, official databases such as the NVD do not yet provide Base vectors for older vulnerabilities. However, most CVEs already include a CVSS v3.1 vector, which can serve as a starting point.

We developed a conversion script based on the open-source CVSS CONVERTER project31, adapting it to align with the CVSS v4.0 specification10. Specifically:

the default mapping of Attack Requirements (AT) from Attack Complexity (AC) was removed, as the two metrics are independent in version 4.0;

the User Interaction (UI) metric was extended to support the new ACTIVE value, as introduced in CVSS v4.0.

All 470 CVEs were individually analyzed to derive the full Base vector. The evaluation was based on the vulnerability descriptions available in public advisories and followed the definitions provided in the official specification10.

Metric values were assigned using a set of heuristics. For example:

AT:PRESENT was used when exploitation required specific conditions (e.g., MITM scenarios);

AT:PRESENT was used when exploitation required specific conditions (e.g., MITM scenarios);

UI:ACTIVE was assigned when the attack required user interaction, such as clicking a link;

UI:ACTIVE was assigned when the attack required user interaction, such as clicking a link;

metrics related to the subsequent system were evaluated based on whether the exploit could affect the entire device or other connected components.

We adopted a conservative approach: if the description mentioned a potential impact (e.g., using “may”), we assigned LOW; if it clearly indicated full device compromise, we used HIGH; if no escalation was possible, we used NONE.

Once all vectors were defined, the corresponding Base Score was calculated for each CVE.

From an automation standpoint, this step is not a bottleneck. The process follows clear rules and can be supported by tools also supported by AI32,33. More importantly, in the near future, CVSS v4.0 Base Scores will be officially published in the NVD, just as CVSS v3.1 scores are today, making this step fully automatable.

Initially, we attempted to assign this metric based on thresholds derived statistically from EPSS values. However, this approach was ultimately discarded due to its high dependency on the specific dataset employed, which compromised the generalizability of the results.

Instead, we proceeded as follows for the creation of our dataset: the values for this metric were assigned based on a consultation of public databases that document the existence of known exploits or confirmed instances of exploitation in real-world attacks. To ensure methodological consistency, we limited our consultation to sources already referenced by the EPSS framework.

Environment score estimation

The third part of CVSS BTE refers to the Environment component. This group allows the risk to be contextualized within a specific operational environment, verifying the presence of any mitigation measures adopted by the target organization. Hence, by definition, the metrics in this group cannot be generalized but depends on the environment.

However, in the context of this study and with specific reference to medical devices, we argue that it is possible to make informed assumptions that can provide initial insights into the environment in which the device operates. To this end, we defined two broad categories of environments based on the location of the patient using the medical device:

the presence of the patient in a hospital environment;

the presence of the patient in a hospital environment;

a remote monitoring activity conducted at the patient’s home.

a remote monitoring activity conducted at the patient’s home.

Within the Environmental metric group, most elements mirror those in the Base group and were left unchanged in our analysis, with two exceptions:

(i)the Requirements metrics - Confidentiality Requirement (CR), Integrity Requirement (IR), and Availability Requirement (AR).

the Requirements metrics - Confidentiality Requirement (CR), Integrity Requirement (IR), and Availability Requirement (AR).

(ii)Modified Subsequent Integrity (MSI) and Modified Subsequent Availability (MSA), which can take the additional value S (Safety), a new option introduced in CVSS v4.0;

Modified Subsequent Integrity (MSI) and Modified Subsequent Availability (MSA), which can take the additional value S (Safety), a new option introduced in CVSS v4.0;

Regarding the Requirements metrics (CR, IR, and AR), we followed the official CVSS v4.0 specification10 and assigned values based on the operational context.

In the hospital profile, the Confidentiality Requirement (CR) was set to LOW(L), reflecting the presence of NIS2-compliant technical and organizational measures that limit the impact of data disclosure. Conversely, the Integrity (IR) and Availability (AR) requirements were rated as MEDIUM(M). While system failures may affect patient safety, hospitals benefit from mitigation factors such as controlled environments and the ability of medical staff to respond promptly, reducing the likelihood of catastrophic outcomes.

In the home care profile, such mitigation is absent. Devices operate in uncontrolled networks, without dedicated security measures or professional supervision. In this context, any compromise of confidentiality, integrity, or availability may pose a direct risk to the patient. For this reason, CR, IR, and AR were all assigned a value of HIGH(H).

Regarding the inclusion of the Safety (S) parameter, this metric was introduced to assess the potential impact of a vulnerability on human safety, i.e., the possible physical harm to patients or healthcare personnel that could result from the exploitation of the vulnerability. Specifically, the guidelines specify that the Integrity and Availability metrics should be assigned the maximum severity level (S) whenever the potential impact of an attack reaches at least a “Marginal” severity level, as defined by the IEC 61508 standard, an international standard for the functional safety of electrical, electronic, and programmable electronic safety-related systems which classifies impact levels based on potential risk to human life.

The severity levels defined by IEC 61508 are: (i) catastrophic: multiple fatalities; (ii) critical: a single fatality; (iii) marginal: serious injuries to one or more individuals; (iv) negligible: minor injuries in the worst-case scenario.

To incorporate the Safety (S) metric into the overall assessment, we leveraged the existing regulatory framework provided by Regulation (EU) 2017/745 on medical devices (MDR)34. Under this regulation, medical devices are already classified into risk classes based on their intended use and potential impact on patient health. This regulatory classification offers a reliable proxy for safety assessment and, importantly, can be retrieved automatically from publicly available sources, enabling systematic assignment of the Safety (S) metric across our dataset.

This classification, based on the intended use and the potential health impact in the event of malfunction, serves as a useful criterion for estimating the severity of outcomes resulting from a potential cyberattack.

Medical devices are classified as follows:

Class I - includes low-risk devices, such as non-invasive or transiently invasive devices like wheelchairs or bandages;

Class I - includes low-risk devices, such as non-invasive or transiently invasive devices like wheelchairs or bandages;

Class IIa - includes medium-risk devices, such as short-term corrective contact lenses or polymer film wound dressings;

Class IIa - includes medium-risk devices, such as short-term corrective contact lenses or polymer film wound dressings;

Class IIb - includes medium-high risk devices, such as those in contact with deep wounds or active devices with potentially hazardous interactions with the human body;

Class IIb - includes medium-high risk devices, such as those in contact with deep wounds or active devices with potentially hazardous interactions with the human body;

Class III - includes the highest-risk devices, such as active implantable devices or drug delivery systems.

Class III - includes the highest-risk devices, such as active implantable devices or drug delivery systems.

The logic used to assign the Safety (S) value to the Integrity and Availability metrics of the Subsequent System10 was based directly on the MDR classification. In particular, we considered the Safety (S) metric applicable whenever the device belonged to Class II or higher, since these categories imply a non-negligible potential for patient harm in case of malfunction or compromise.

To determine the appropriate risk class, we traced each analyzed CVE back to the associated proprietary device. This information was extracted either directly from the CVE description, when available, or inferred from the associated CPE (Common Platform Enumeration), which identifies the relevant product. The corresponding risk class was then assigned according to the Medical Device Regulation (MDR). Special attention was given to software components considered medical devices. For embedded software, the risk class of the host medical device was applied. In contrast, software considered as accessories with limited clinical impact was conservatively assigned to Class I.

Based on this assessment, we generated the full CVSS v4.0-BTE vector and assigned a severity score to each vulnerability in the dataset.

The effect of the additional metric groups differs in nature. The Threat metric generally lowers the score compared to the Base alone, since the default assumption of “actively exploited” is replaced by evidence that most vulnerabilities lack public exploits or observed attacks. The Environmental metrics, by contrast, can both decrease or increase the score depending on the deployment context. In hospital settings, where mitigation measures and professional supervision are present, the score often decreases. In home scenarios, or when Integrity and Availability are linked to the Safety parameter (MSI or MSA), the score can increase, sometimes significantly, reflecting the heightened risk for patient safety in the absence of strong contextual protections.
