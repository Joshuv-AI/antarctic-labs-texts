Comparative Evaluation of Microtissue-Engineered Root Dentin with Three Different Nanoparticles in Improving Fatigue Resistance of Endodontically Treated Teeth – An In Vitro Study
Authors: G Balavaishnavi, Dhanavel Chakravarthy, Shahana Shajahan, M Sornamalar, S Vijaya Raja
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:This study aims to analyze the fatigue resistance, surface mechanical properties, and biomechanical responsiveness of microtissue-engineered root dentin using cellulose nanofibers, hydroxyapatite nanocrystals, and walnut shell nanopowders.Methodology:The objectives are to evaluate the differences in fatigue resistance and biomechanics and surface mechanical characteristics by infiltrating these nanomaterials into the root canal of endodontically treated teeth. Forty human permanent single-rooted teeth were collected with the patient’s consent. Fatigue resistance was measured using cyclic loading, and surface roughness was measured by scanning electron microscopy (SEM).Results:The results showed a fatigue resistance of 36.81 ± 4.58 MPa, attributed to the availability of calcium ions at 9%, the highest compared to other groups. Non-microtissue-engineered root dentin exhibited a stress resistance of 12.3 ± 3.1 MPa and a strain resistance of 4.1 ± 1.1 MPa. Walnut shell nanopowder (WNP) exhibited higher fatigue resistance than other groups (P > 0.05).Conclusion:Root dentin engineered with HaNPs showed a fatigue resistance of 36.81 ± 4.58 MPa and surface roughness of 4.2 ± 0.5 MPa, attributed to the availability of calcium ions at 9%, the highest compared to other groups. Non-microtissue-engineered root dentin exhibited a fatigue resistance of 12.3 ± 3.1 MPa, while microtissue-engineered root dentin showed 36.81 ± 4.58 MPa, which is three times higher.

INTRODUCTION

Although millions of teeth are saved through root canal treatment, some still need to be sacrificed. Endodontically treated teeth are more prone to fracture due to various factors, such as reduced hydration, loss of coronal dentin architecture, diminished structural integrity of radicular dentin, dentin loss during canal preparation, and changes in dentin collagen. The most significant changes in tooth biomechanics are attributed to the loss and alteration of dentin collagen at both radicular and coronal levels during access cavity and canal preparation, possibly due to the chemical or structural changes induced by endodontic irrigants.[1]

Vertical root fractures (VRFs) occurring in teeth that have undergone root canal treatment are a matter of considerable concern within the field of dentistry.[2] Based on existing scholarly sources, it has been determined that vertical root fracture ranks as the third most prevalent cause for the removal of a tooth that has had endodontic treatment.[3] Microtissue engineering is the way of designing tissues to improve the biological and mechanical characteristics of structure, and it has the potential to significantly transform the approach of dental professionals in the field of endodontics when treating dental pulp-related disorders and enhancing the mechanical properties root canal-treated teeth.[4]

Researchers are developing innovative methods to enhance the biomechanical strength of teeth following endodontic procedures by integrating biopolymeric nanofillers into dentin matrix collagen. These engineered microtissues aim to improve the structural integrity of teeth, offering a more efficient approach to address the fatigue resistance of endodontically treated teeth. Studies have shown that nanomaterials exhibit superior qualities compared to their micro-sized counterparts. Allah SM et al. (2018)[5] reported that nanomaterials enable effective ionic mobility, ion exchange, and absorption capacities. Cellulose nanofibers (CNFs), hydroxyapatite nanoparticles (HANp), and walnut shell nanopowder (WNP) offer distinctive prospects for enhancing dentin properties, especially in root canal therapy. However, there is a research gap in investigating these nanomaterials for strengthening root dentin in teeth post-root canal treatment. This study aims to analyze the fatigue resistance, surface mechanical properties, and biomechanical responsiveness of microtissue-engineered root dentin using cellulose nanofibers, hydroxyapatite nanocrystals, and walnut shell nanopowders. The objectives are to evaluate the differences in fatigue resistance and biomechanical and surface mechanical properties by infiltrating these nanoparticles into the root dentin of endodontically treated teeth.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The in vitro randomized controlled study was conducted at Indira Gandhi Institute of Dental Sciences, Puducherry, in the Department of Conservative Dentistry and Endodontics, MGMCRI, Puducherry. The inclusion criteria for the study were non-carious teeth and teeth with mature apex. The exclusion criteria for the study were teeth with cracks or fractures, curved roots, resorption, and calcified canals.

Group I: Control group (MTA) Angelus, Brazil (n = 10), Group II: Cellulose nanofibers (CNFs) (n = 10), Group III: Hydroxyapatite nanocrystals (HANCs) (n = 10), Group IV: Walnut shell nanopowder (n = 10). Sample preparation

Forty extracted human permanent single-rooted teeth were collected with the patient’s consent. The teeth were transilluminated before testing to exclude the possibility of cracks or damages. Samples were subjected to radiography to confirm the geometry of the canal system. The samples were decoronated with a diamond disc, and the root length was standardized to 16 mm. Assessment of surface roughness and mechanical properties of root dentin, specimen, and material preparation was conducted. Evaluation of fatigue resistance by compression–compression fatigue test was performed by slicing samples corresponding to the middle third (8 mm from the apex), which were then mounted onto a cover glass. Samples were submitted to stepwise mechanical cycling with a frequency of 15 Hz and a stress ratio of 0.1 with a ball indenter in a 100% humidity universal testing machine[6]

Statistical analysis

In the statistical analysis of an RCT, rigorous procedures were used to evaluate the intervention’s effectiveness and minimize biases. Initially, descriptive statistics characterized the treatment and control groups for comparability. t tests and ANOVA were used for continuous outcomes, while Chi-square tests were used for categorical outcomes to determine statistical significance. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

DISCUSSION

Evaluation of surface roughness of these new experimental 3 nanomaterials was compared to MTA using SEM. SEM is utilized to evaluate the microstructure of dentin, how well the material incorporated and merged in dentin, and the surface roughness of dentinal walls. Fatigue resistance was evaluated using a universal testing machine (Instron, Germany). It is effective in determining the fatigue resistance of the tooth. The ability of a tooth to withstand the forces that are applied during chewing is critical factor for its longevity, particularly a tooth that has been treated with root canal therapy. Procedures for wear testing are widely utilized to simulate the influence that chewing and grinding pressures have on the surface of the dentin substance.

Fatigue loads generated cyclic stress/strain in a structure and contributed to crack initiation, crack propagation, and catastrophic failure with time. The cracks formed would behave as stress concentrators to initiate crack propagation when the stress magnitude is high enough to induce microscopic plastic deformation at the crack tip. According to Fennis et al.,[7] 2004 study, the average fatigue resistance of root canal dentin determined before microtissue engineering was 12.3 ± 3.1 MPa. In this study, microtensile testing was performed at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min. After, stereo microscopic analysis was conducted at 25x magnification to allow a proper vision of the fractured area of the specimen to determine the mixed area of failure. In the process of analyzing dentin in root canals, one of the most important mechanical characteristics that is investigated is the fracture toughness of the dentin.[8]

MTA showed fatigue resistance of 24.12 ± 2.23 MPa and surface roughness of 2.5 ± 0.5 MPa, which are lower than all three new naturally derived nanomaterials due to the calcium ion availability being around 3.14%, the lowest compared to other groups. HANPs exhibited increased fatigue resistance of 36.81 ± 4.58 MPa and surface roughness of 4.2 ± 0.5 MPa, with calcium ion availability at 9%, the highest among all groups. Cellulose nanofibers showed fatigue resistance of 25.78 ± 3.90 MPa and surface roughness of 2.8 ± 0.5 MPa, with calcium ion availability around 4.14%, which is the least among the three naturally derived nanomaterials but higher than the control group. Walnut shell nanopowder demonstrated fatigue resistance of 29.83 ± 1.4 MPa and surface roughness of 3 ± 0.5 MPa, with calcium ion availability around 6.9%, the second highest among the groups. All these naturally available nanomaterials showed better surface roughness and fatigue resistance because of the availability of calcium ions. All things considered, the incorporation of cellulose nanofibers, hydroxyapatite nanocrystals, and walnut shell nanopowder into the process of microtissue engineering provides a comprehensive approach to the enhancement of the fatigue resistance of endodontically treated teeth.[9]

CONCLUSION

Root dentin engineered with HaNPs showed a fatigue resistance of 36.81 ± 4.58 MPa and surface roughness of 4.2 ± 0.5 MPa, attributed to the availability of calcium ions at 9%, the highest compared to other groups. Non-microtissue-engineered root dentin exhibited a fatigue resistance of 12.3 ± 3.1 MPa, while microtissue-engineered root dentin showed 36.81 ± 4.58 MPa, which is three times higher. Researchers may leverage the unique properties of these nanomaterials in microtissue engineering to improve the surface roughness and fatigue resistance of endodontically treated teeth.
