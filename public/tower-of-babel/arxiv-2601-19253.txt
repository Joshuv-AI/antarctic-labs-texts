A property that characterizes the Enneper surface and helix surfaces
Pascual Lucas, José Antonio Ortega-Yagües

Abstract
The main goal of this paper is to show that helix surfaces and the Enneper surface are the only surfaces in the 3-dimensional Euclidean space ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} whose isogonal lines are generalized helices and pseudo-geodesic lines.



Keywords.
generalized helix; isogonal line; pseudo-geodesic line; helix surface.

2020 Mathematics subject classification.
Primary 53A04, 53A05.

1
Introduction

Let
\gamma(s)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
s\in I
, be an arc-length parametrized curve, with Frenet frame
\{T_{\gamma},N_{\gamma},B_{\gamma}\}
, satisfying the Frenet equations

T_{\gamma}^{\prime}=\kappa_{\gamma}\,N_{\gamma},\qquad N_{\gamma}^{\prime}=-\kappa_{\gamma}\,T_{\gamma}-\tau_{\gamma}\,B_{\gamma},\qquad B_{\gamma}^{\prime}=\tau_{\gamma}\,N_{\gamma},

(1)

where, as usual,
\kappa_{\gamma}>0
and
\tau_{\gamma}
stand for the
curvature
and
torsion
functions of
\gamma
.

When the curve
\gamma
lies on an orientable connected surface
M\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
, with Gauss map
N
, then along
\gamma
we also have the Darboux frame
\{T_{\gamma},JT_{\gamma},N\}
, where
J
denotes the positively oriented
\pi/2
-rotation in the tangent plane, which is defined by
JV=N\times V
. In this case, we have the following equations

T^{\prime}_{\gamma}=\kappa_{g}\,(JT_{\gamma})+\kappa_{n}\,N,\qquad(JT_{\gamma})^{\prime}=-\kappa_{g}\,T_{\gamma}-\tau_{g}\,N,\qquad N^{\prime}=-\kappa_{n}\,T_{\gamma}+\tau_{g}\,(JT_{\gamma}),

(2)

where the functions
\kappa_{g}
,
\kappa_{n}
and
\tau_{g}
are called the
geodesic curvature
,
normal curvature
and
geodesic torsion
of
\gamma
, respectively. From first equations of (
1
) and (
2
), we easily get the well-known relation
\kappa_{\gamma}^{2}=\kappa^{2}_{g}+\kappa^{2}_{n}
.

Let
\{E_{1},E_{2}\}
be a local orthonormal frame of principal directions of
M
, which is positively oriented, associated to the principal curvatures
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
, with
\kappa_{1}\leq\kappa_{2}
. The orthonormal frames
\{T_{\gamma},N_{\gamma},B_{\gamma}\}
and
\{E_{1},E_{2},N\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
are related along
\gamma
by

\displaystyle T_{\gamma}=

\displaystyle\cos\phi\,E_{1}+\sin\phi\,E_{2},

\displaystyle N_{\gamma}=

\displaystyle\sin\theta\,(-\sin\phi\,E_{1}+\cos\phi\,E_{2})+\cos\theta N,

(3)

\displaystyle B_{\gamma}=

\displaystyle-\cos\theta\,(-\sin\phi\,E_{1}+\cos\phi\,E_{2})+\sin\theta N,

for certain differentiable angle functions
\phi
and
\theta
. From the equations (
1
)–(
1
) it is not difficult to see that

\kappa_{g}=\sin\theta\,\kappa_{\gamma},\quad\kappa_{n}=\cos\theta\,\kappa_{\gamma}.

(4)

The following relations are also well-known (see
[
3
, pp. 145, 153]
):

\kappa_{n}=\kappa_{1}\cos^{2}\phi+\kappa_{2}\sin^{2}\phi,\qquad\tau_{g}=(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2})\cos\phi\sin\phi.

(5)

We recall the following definition introduced by Santaló in
[
15
]
.

Definition 1

A curve
\gamma
in a surface
M
is said to be an
isogonal line in
M
if the angle
\phi
between
T_{\gamma}
and
E_{1}
is constant along
\gamma
.

The lines of curvature and the loxodromes are trivial examples of isogonal lines. Note that any reparametrization of an isogonal line is also an isogonal line with the same angle. Therefore, and without loss of generality, we can assume that our isogonal lines have constant velocity.

Geodesics
\gamma
on a surface
M
are characterized by having their principal normal vector field
N_{\gamma}
parallel to the unit vector field
N
normal to the surface. This property was extended in a very natural way by Wunderlich in
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
.
He introduced the concept of pseudo-geodesic lines on a surface as the curves traced on the surface whose osculating planes form a fixed angle with the tangent plane of the surface; when this angle is a right angle, we get the proper geodesics and when it is equal to zero, the asymptotic lines. This property is equivalent to the following.

Definition 2

A curve
\gamma
in a surface
M
is said to be a
pseudo-geodesic line in
M
if the angle
\theta
between the normals
N_{\gamma}
and
N
is constant along
\gamma
.

It is easy to see that every curve in a plane is a pseudo-geodesic line in that plane, and for curves
\gamma
in the 2-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}(r)
,
\gamma
is a pseudo-geodesic line if and only it is a circle.
A link between pseudo-geodesic lines and helices has recently been found in
[
11
]
, where the generalized helix concept has been extended. It is shown in
[
11
]
that normal helices are precisely the pseudo-geodesic lines in a generalized cylinder.

Along this paper we will use the usual concept of generalized helix.

Definition 3

A curve
\gamma
is a
generalized helix
, or
cylindrical helix
, if its tangent vector makes a constant angle with a fixed direction
v
, called an axis of the generalized helix.

It is well-known (see, for example,
[
4
, p. 20]
or
[
3
, p. 26]
) that
\gamma
is a generalized helix if and only if
m\kappa_{\gamma}+n\tau_{\gamma}=0
, for certain constants
m
and
n
.

This paper is organized as follows. In Sect.
2
we obtain some results on isogonal lines, and their relation to several special families of surfaces (
CRPC–surfaces
and
CSkC–surfaces
). In Sect.
3
we study pseudo-geodesic lines and obtain the system of differential equations that characterizes them, which obviously is very similar to the system that characterizes the geodesic curves. In that section we also obtain a result analogous to the well-known Joachimsthal theorem (see Proposition
13
). Sect.
4
is devoted to prove the main result of the paper (see Theorem
15
):
Let
M
be a nonplanar connected surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Then the isogonal lines are pseudo-geodesic lines and generalized helices if and only if
M
is a helix surface or an open piece of the Enneper surface
. We end the paper by providing a family of curves on the Enneper surface with nice properties.

2
Some results on isogonal lines

Our first goal in this section is to prove that given a point
p
in a surface
M
and a tangent vector
v\in T_{p}M
, then there exists a unique isogonal line with initial conditions
(p,v)
.

Let
X(t,z)
be an orthogonal parametrization in a surface
M
, and consider
\gamma(s)=X(t(s),z(s))
an isogonal line with angle
\phi
satisfying
\gamma(0)=p
and
\gamma^{\prime}(0)=v
. Then its tangent vector is given by

\gamma^{\prime}(s)=t^{\prime}(s)X_{t}(t(s),z(s))+z^{\prime}(s)X_{z}(t(s),z(s)).

(6)

Let us write

X_{t}(t,z)=f_{1}(t,z)E_{1}(t,z)+f_{2}(t,z)E_{2}(t,z),\qquad X_{z}(t,z)=g_{1}(t,z)E_{1}(t,z)+g_{2}(t,z)E_{2}(t,z),

(7)

where
f_{1},f_{2},g_{1},g_{2}
are differentiable functions. Equations (
6
) and (
7
) leads to

t^{\prime}(s)f_{1}(s)+z^{\prime}(s)g_{1}(s)=|v|\cos\phi,\qquad t^{\prime}(s)f_{2}(s)+z^{\prime}(s)g_{2}(s)=|v|\sin\phi,

(8)

where we have written, for simplicity,
f_{1}(s)=f_{1}(t(s),z(s))
, and so on. It is not difficult to see that the differential equations (
8
) characterize the isogonal lines. So we have proved the following result.

Proposition 1

Given a point
p\in M
and a vector
v\in T_{p}M
,
v\neq 0
, there exist an
\varepsilon>0
and a unique isogonal line
\gamma:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to M
such that
\gamma(0)=p
,
\gamma^{\prime}(0)=v
and
|\gamma^{\prime}(t)|=|v|
for all
t\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
.

To indicate the dependence of this isogonal line of
(p,v)
, it is convenient to denote it by
\gamma(t,p,v)
.

Proposition 2

If the isogonal line
\gamma(t,p,v)
is defined for
t\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
, then the isogonal line
\gamma(t,p,\lambda v)
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
\lambda\neq 0
, is defined for
t\in(-\varepsilon/\lambda,\varepsilon/\lambda)
, and
\gamma(t,p,\lambda v)=\gamma(\lambda t,p,v)
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha:(-\varepsilon/\lambda,\varepsilon/\lambda)\to M
be the parametrized curve defined by
\alpha(t)=\gamma(\lambda t,p,v)
. Then
\alpha(t)
is an isogonal line such that
\alpha(0)=p
,
\alpha^{\prime}(0)=\lambda v
and
|\alpha^{\prime}(t)|=|\lambda v|
for all
t
, and so from Proposition
1
we have
\alpha(t)=\gamma(t,p,\lambda v)
.
∎

If
v\in T_{p}M
,
v\neq 0
, is such that
\gamma(1,p,v)
is defined, we set
\Phi_{p}(v)=\gamma(1,p,v)
and
\Phi_{p}(0)=p
. Then we can define a differentiable map
\Phi_{p}
in some neighborhood of
0\in T_{p}M
. This map will be called the
isogonal map
at
p
. More precisely,

Proposition 3

Given a point
p\in M
there exists an
\varepsilon>0
such that
\Phi_{p}
is defined and differentiable in the interior
B_{\varepsilon}
of a disk of
T_{p}M
, with center in the origin and of radius
\varepsilon
.

Proof.

It is clear that for every direction of
T_{p}M
it is possible, by Proposition
2
, to take
v
sufficiently small so that the interval of definition of
\gamma(t,p,v)
contains 1, and thus
\gamma(1,p,v)=\Phi_{p}(v)
is defined. To show that this reduction can be made uniformly in all directions, we need the theorem of the dependence of an isogonal line on its initial conditions, in the following form:
Given
p\in M
there exist numbers
\varepsilon_{1}>0
,
\varepsilon_{2}>0
and a differentiable map
\gamma:(-\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{2})\times B_{\varepsilon_{1}}\to M
such that, for every
v\in B_{\varepsilon_{1}}\subset T_{p}M
,
v\neq 0
,
t\in(-\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{2})
, the curve
\alpha(t)=\gamma(t,p,v)
is the isogonal line in
M
with
\alpha(0)=p
,
\alpha^{\prime}(0)=v
and
|\alpha^{\prime}(t)|=|v|
; and for
v=0
,
\gamma(t,p,0)=p
.

From this statement and Proposition
2
, our assertion follows. In fact, since
\gamma(t,p,v)
is defined for
|t|<\varepsilon_{2}
,
|v|<\varepsilon_{1}
, we obtain, by setting
\lambda=\varepsilon_{2}/2
in Proposition
2
, that
\gamma(t,p,(\varepsilon_{2}/2)v)
is defined for
|t|<2
,
|v|<\varepsilon_{1}
. Therefore, by taking a disk
B_{\varepsilon_{1}}\subset T_{p}M
, with center at the origin and radius
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}/2
, we have that
\gamma(1,p,v)=\Phi_{p}(v)
,
v\in B_{\varepsilon_{1}}
, is defined. The differentiability of
\Phi_{p}
in
B_{\varepsilon_{1}}
follows from the differentiability of
\gamma
.
∎

The differentiable map
\gamma(t,p,v)
will be called the
isogonal flow
.

Proposition 4

Given a point
p\in M
, the map
\Phi_{p}:B_{\varepsilon}\subset T_{p}M\to M
is a diffeomorphism in a neighborhood
U\subset B_{\varepsilon}
of the origin
0
of
T_{p}M
.

Proof.

We shall show that the differential
d(\Phi_{p})_{0}
is nonsingular at
0\in T_{p}M
. To do this, we identify as usual the space of tangent vectors to
T_{p}M
at
0
with
T_{p}M
itself. Then

d(\Phi_{p})_{0}(v)=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\Phi_{p}(tv)=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\gamma(t,p,v)=v.

It follows that
d(\Phi_{p})_{0}
is the identity map. By applying the inverse function theorem, we complete the proof of the proposition.
∎

A surface
M
with
constant ratio of principal curvatures
or
CRPC–surface
is a surface such that
a\kappa_{1}+b\kappa_{2}=0
for certain constants
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
with
a^{2}+b^{2}\neq 0
; if we assume
\kappa_{1}\neq 0
, then a
CRPC–surface
is a surface with constant
\kappa_{2}/\kappa_{1}
,
[
6
,
18
]
. In other words, a
CRPC–surface
is a surface with linearly dependent functions
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
. Trivial examples are spheres and minimal surfaces. Another known case is an ideal
Mylar balloon
(see, e.g.,
[
12
,
13
]
) which is obtained by gluing two equally sized discs of ﬂexible, but inextensible, foil along their common border, and blowing it up. Further surfaces of revolution of that sort, with positive and negative ratios of principal curvatures, have appeared in other contexts, e.g., in
[
5
,
9
,
6
]
.

Proposition 5

Let
\gamma
be an arclength parametrized curve in a surface
M
. If
\gamma
is an isogonal line (which is not a line of curvature), then the following conditions are equivalent:

C1)

\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
are linearly dependent functions along
\gamma
.

C2)

\tau_{g}
and
\kappa_{n}
are linearly dependent functions along
\gamma
.

Proof.

From equations (
5
), it is easy to see that, for any constants
a,b,c,d
and
\phi
, we have

\displaystyle\sin\phi\cos\phi(a+b)\kappa_{n}+(a\sin^{2}\phi-b\cos^{2}\phi)\tau_{g}

\displaystyle=\sin\phi\cos\phi(a\kappa_{1}+b\kappa_{2}).

(9)

\displaystyle(d\cos\phi+c\sin\phi)\cos\phi\kappa_{1}+(d\sin\phi-c\cos\phi)\sin\phi\kappa_{2}

\displaystyle=c\tau_{g}+d\kappa_{n}.

(10)

From these equations, it is not difficult to see that
C1
and
C2
are equivalent conditions.
∎

Proposition 6

Let
\gamma
be an arc-length parametrized curve in a surface
M
,
\gamma
without umbilical points. If
\gamma
satisfies conditions C1 and C2 of Proposition
5
, then
\gamma
is an isogonal line.

Proof.

It is an easy consequence of the equation (
10
).
∎

A surface
M
is said to be of
constant skew curvature
or
CSkC–surface
if the difference of principal curvatures
\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}=\lambda
is a constant,
[
17
]
. Note that this is equivalent with
H^{2}-K=\lambda^{2}/4
being constant, where

is the mean curvature and
K
is the Gaussian curvature of the surface.

Proposition 7

Let
M
be a connected surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
without umbilic points. Then

a)

M
is a CRPC–surface if and only if
\tau_{g}
and
\kappa_{n}
are linearly dependent functions along the isogonal lines.

b)

M
is a CSkC–surface if and only if
\tau_{g}
is constant along the isogonal lines.

Proof.

a
) From Proposition
5
, we only need to show the converse part. If
\gamma
is an isogonal line (not a line of curvature) such that
c\tau_{g}+d\kappa_{n}=0
, for certain constants
c
and
d
with
c^{2}+d^{2}\neq 0
, then
\gamma
satisfies condition
C1
of Proposition
5
. On the other hand, if two isogonal lines
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
in
M
(not lines of curvature) intersect at a point, then condition
C1
for
\gamma_{1}
is equal to the condition
C1
for
\gamma_{2}
. Using the isogonal flow and a standard continuity argument, the same condition
C1
is valid over the whole surface, and so
M
is an
CRPC–surface
.

b
) This can be proved in a similar way to
a
).
∎

3
Pseudo-geodesic lines

Given a curve
\gamma
in a surface
M
, the following facts are well known: 1) if
\gamma
is geodesic and line of curvature, then
\gamma
is a plane curve; 2) if
\gamma
is geodesic and plane, then
\gamma
is a line of curvature,
[
16
, pp. 211–212]
. However, if
\gamma
is plane and line of curvature, then
\gamma
is not necessarily a geodesic.
The following result, that can be seen as an extension of Lemma 15 in
[
16
, p. 212]
, is an easy consequence from the equation (
4
) and the relation (see
[
3
, p. 153]
):

\tau_{\gamma}=\tau_{g}+\theta^{\prime}.

(11)

Proposition 8

Let
\gamma
be an arc-length parametrized curve in
M
. If any two of the following conditions are satisfied then the third condition is also satisified:

a)

\gamma
is a plane curve.

b)

\gamma
is a line of curvature.

c)

\gamma
is a pseudo-geodesic line.

In the following we present several results connecting the concepts of isogonal line, pseudo-geodesic line and generalized helix.

Proposition 9

Let
\gamma
be a pseudo-geodesic line in a surface
M
, and assume that it is not an asymptotic line. Then
\gamma
is a generalized helix if and only if
\kappa_{n}
and
\tau_{g}
are linearly dependent functions along
\gamma
.

Proof.

Note that on a pseudo-geodesic line
\gamma
(
\theta
is constant) we have from (
11
) and (
4
) that
\tau_{g}=\tau_{\gamma}
and
\kappa_{n}=\cos\theta\kappa_{\gamma}
. Therefore,
\kappa_{\gamma}
and
\tau_{\gamma}
are linearly dependent functions along
\gamma
(i.e.
\gamma
is a generalized helix) if and only if
\kappa_{n}
and
\tau_{g}
are linearly dependent functions along
\gamma
.
∎

Proposition 10

Let
\gamma
be an isogonal line in a surface
M
which also is a pseudo-geodesic line, and assume that it is not a line of curvature nor an asymptotic line. Then
\gamma
is a generalized helix if and only if
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
are linearly dependent functions along
\gamma
.

Proof.

If
\gamma
is a generalized helix then
m\kappa_{\gamma}+n\tau_{\gamma}=0
for certain constants
m
and
n
, and so
m\sec\theta\kappa_{n}+n\tau_{g}=0
. Then from Proposition
5
we get that
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
are linearly dependent functions along
\gamma
.

To proof the converse, let us assume that
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
are linearly dependent functions along
\gamma
. From Proposition
5
we get that
\kappa_{n}
and
\tau_{g}
are linearly dependent functions along
\gamma
, and so from Proposition
9
we deduce
\gamma
is a generalized helix.
∎

Proposition 11

Let
\gamma
be a pseudo-geodesic line in a surface
M
,
\gamma
without umbilical points. If
\gamma
is a generalized helix and the curvatures
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
are linearly dependent along
\gamma
, then
\gamma
is an isogonal line in
M
.

Proof.

If
\gamma
is not an asymptotic line, then the result follows from Proposition
6
. In the case where
\gamma
is an asymptotic line, then along
\gamma
we have

\cos^{2}\phi\ \kappa_{1}+\sin^{2}\phi\ \kappa_{2}=0,\quad a\kappa_{1}+b\kappa_{2}=0,

for certain constants
a
and
b
, with
a^{2}+b^{2}\neq 0
. Then
\phi
is necessarily a constant.
∎

Proposition 12

Let
\gamma
be a generalized helix, with axis
V_{\gamma}
, in a surface
M
. Then

\gamma
is a pseudo-geodesic line in
M
if and only if
\left<V_{\gamma},N|_{\gamma}\right>
is constant.

Proof.

If
\gamma
is a generalized helix with axis
V_{\gamma}=\cos\psi\,T_{\gamma}+\sin\psi\,B_{\gamma}
, then the quotient
\tau_{\gamma}/\kappa_{\gamma}=-\cot\psi
is constant. On the other hand, from (
1
) we have
\left<V_{\gamma},N|_{\gamma}\right>=\sin\psi\,\sin\theta
, which leads to the result.
∎

Let
M
and
\overline{M}
be two regular surfaces that intersect transversally (i.e. whenever
p\in M\cap\overline{M}
we have
T_{p}M\neq T_{p}\overline{M}
), then it is well known that
C=M\cap\overline{M}
is a regular curve. Let
\gamma(s)
be an arc-length parametrization of
C
and denote by
\xi(s)\in(0,\pi)
the angle between
N(\gamma(s))
and
\overline{N}(\gamma(s))
, where, as usual, a bar over a function or vector field denotes that we are considering the curve within the surface
\overline{M}
. A classical result states that if
M
and
\overline{M}
intersect with constant angle along a line of curvature of
M
, then the curve of intersection is also a line of curvature of
\overline{M}
. In 1846, Joachimsthal proved a special case of this theorem,
[
7
]
, while the general case was presented by Bonnet in 1853,
[
1
]
.

It is easy to see that

\xi(s)=\varepsilon(\bar{\theta}(s)-\theta(s)),\quad\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\},

(12)

where
\theta(s)
(resp.
\bar{\theta}(s)
) is the angle between the vectors
N_{\gamma}(s)
and
N(\gamma(s))
(resp.
\overline{N}(\gamma(s))
). From here, we have
\xi^{\prime}(s)=\varepsilon(\tau_{g}(s)-\overline{\tau}_{g}(s))
.
If we assume that
\gamma
is of constant geodesic torsion
\tau_{g}
in
M
, then
\gamma
is of constant geodesic torsion
\overline{\tau}_{g}
in
\overline{M}
(with
\tau_{g}=\overline{\tau}_{g}
) if and only if
M
and
\overline{M}
intersect with constant angle.

We finish with a result of same kind as Joachimsthal theorem, which is a direct consequence of (
12
).

Proposition 13

Let
\gamma(s)
be an arc-length parametrization of the intersection curve of two surfaces
M
and
\overline{M}
that intersect transversally, and assume that
\gamma
is a pseudo-geodesic line in
M
. Then
\gamma
is a pseudo-geodesic line in
\overline{M}
if and only if
M
and
\overline{M}
intersect with constant angle.

3.1
Differential equations of pseudo-geodesic lines

In this subsection, the angle
\theta
between
N
and
N_{\gamma}
is not necessarily a constant. Let
X(t,z)
be an orthogonal parametrization in a surface
M
, and denote by
\{E,F=0,G\}
and
\{e,f,g\}
the coefficients of the first and second fundamental forms, respectively.
Let
\gamma(s)=X(t(s),z(s))
be an arc-length parametrized curve, then its unit tangent
T_{\gamma}=t^{\prime}X_{t}+z^{\prime}X_{z}
.
By taking derivative here we deduce the following ODE system

\displaystyle t^{\prime\prime}+\Gamma_{11}^{1}\,(t^{\prime})^{2}+2\Gamma_{12}^{1}\,t^{\prime}z^{\prime}+\Gamma_{22}^{1}\,(z^{\prime})^{2}=

\displaystyle-\sin\theta\,z^{\prime}\sqrt{G/E}\,\kappa_{\gamma},

\displaystyle z^{\prime\prime}+\Gamma_{11}^{2}\,(t^{\prime})^{2}+2\Gamma_{12}^{2}\,t^{\prime}z^{\prime}+\Gamma_{22}^{2}\,(z^{\prime})^{2}=

\displaystyle\sin\theta\,t^{\prime}\sqrt{E/G}\,\kappa_{\gamma},

\displaystyle e(t^{\prime})^{2}+2ft^{\prime}z^{\prime}+g(z^{\prime})^{2}=

\displaystyle\cos\theta\,\kappa_{\gamma},

where as usual the
\Gamma_{ij}^{k}
’s denote the Christoffel symbols. If
\theta=\theta(s)\neq\pm\pi/2
, then the above equations lead to

\displaystyle t^{\prime\prime}+

\displaystyle(t^{\prime})^{2}\,(\Gamma_{11}^{1}+\tan\theta\,z^{\prime}\sqrt{G/E}\,e)+2t^{\prime}z^{\prime}\,(\Gamma_{12}^{1}+\tan\theta\,z^{\prime}\sqrt{G/E}\,f)+

\displaystyle+(z^{\prime})^{2}\,(\Gamma_{22}^{1}+\tan\theta\,z^{\prime}\sqrt{G/E}\,g)=0,

(13)

\displaystyle z^{\prime\prime}+

\displaystyle(t^{\prime})^{2}\,(\Gamma_{11}^{2}-\tan\theta\,t^{\prime}\sqrt{E/G}\,e)+2t^{\prime}z^{\prime}\,(\Gamma_{12}^{2}-\tan\theta\,t^{\prime}\sqrt{E/G}\,f)+

\displaystyle+(z^{\prime})^{2}\,(\Gamma_{22}^{2}-\tan\theta\,t^{\prime}\sqrt{E/G}\,g)=0.

(14)

Conversely, if
\gamma(s)=X(t(s),z(s))
satisfies equations (

) and (
14
) for a function
\bar{\theta}
, define the function
\bar{\kappa}=\sec\bar{\theta}\,(e(t^{\prime})^{2}+2ft^{\prime}z^{\prime}+g(z^{\prime})^{2})

\displaystyle t^{\prime\prime}+\Gamma_{11}^{1}\,(t^{\prime})^{2}+2\Gamma_{12}^{1}\,t^{\prime}z^{\prime}+\Gamma_{22}^{1}\,(z^{\prime})^{2}=

\displaystyle-\sin\bar{\theta}\,z^{\prime}\sqrt{G/E}\,\bar{\kappa},

\displaystyle z^{\prime\prime}+\Gamma_{11}^{2}\,(t^{\prime})^{2}+2\Gamma_{12}^{2}\,t^{\prime}z^{\prime}+\Gamma_{22}^{2}\,(z^{\prime})^{2}=

\displaystyle\sin\bar{\theta}\,t^{\prime}\sqrt{E/G}\,\bar{\kappa}.

By derivating

we get
\kappa_{\gamma}\,N_{\gamma}=t^{\prime\prime}X_{t}+z^{\prime\prime}X_{z}+t^{\prime 2}X_{tt}+2t^{\prime}z^{\prime}X_{tz}+z^{\prime 2}X_{zz}
, and then we deduce

\displaystyle\kappa_{\gamma}\left<N_{\gamma},X_{t}\right>=

\displaystyle(t^{\prime\prime}+\Gamma_{11}^{1}\,(t^{\prime})^{2}+2\Gamma_{12}^{1}\,t^{\prime}z^{\prime}+\Gamma_{22}^{1}\,(z^{\prime})^{2})\,E,

\displaystyle\kappa_{\gamma}\left<N_{\gamma},X_{z}\right>=

\displaystyle(z^{\prime\prime}+\Gamma_{11}^{2}\,(t^{\prime})^{2}+2\Gamma_{12}^{2}\,t^{\prime}z^{\prime}+\Gamma_{22}^{2}\,(z^{\prime})^{2})\,G,

\displaystyle e(t^{\prime})^{2}+2ft^{\prime}z^{\prime}+g(z^{\prime})^{2}.

From these three equations, and bearing (

4
The main result

The goal of this section is to show that helix surfaces and the Enneper surface are the only ones whose isogonal lines are generalized helices and pseudo-geodesic lines. First, we will see that helix surfaces and the Enneper surface verify that property.

Before starting with the examples, we recall the well-known formula of Liouville (see
[
3
, p. 253]
):

\kappa_{g}=\phi^{\prime}+\cos\phi\,\kappa_{g_{1}}+\sin\phi\,\kappa_{g_{2}},

(15)

where
\kappa_{g_{1}}
and
\kappa_{g_{2}}
denote the geodesic curvatures of the lines of curvature.

Example 1

A surface
M
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a
helix surface
if there exists an unitary vector
V
such that for each point
q\in M
the angle between
V
and
T_{q}M
is constant,
[
2
]
. A helix surface can be locally parametrized as follows (
[
2
,
10
]
),

X(t,z)=\beta(t)+z\left(\cos\varphi\,N_{\beta}(t)+\sin\varphi\,V\right),

(16)

where
\varphi
is a constant angle and
\beta
a curve in a plane orthogonal to
V
. The geodesics of helix surfaces are slant helices, and they are characterized by the fact that their unit normal vector field makes a constant angle with the vector
V
. The coordinate curves of
X(t,z)
are lines of curvature, and we have the following formulas

\kappa_{g_{1}}=\frac{\cos\varphi\;\kappa_{\beta}}{1-z\cos\varphi\;\kappa_{\beta}},\qquad\kappa_{g_{2}}=0,\qquad\kappa_{1}=\frac{-\sin\varphi\;\kappa_{\beta}}{1-z\cos\varphi\;\kappa_{\beta}},\qquad\kappa_{2}=0.

If
\gamma
is an isogonal line, from (
4
), (
5
), (
15
) and Proposition
10
we easily get that
\gamma
is a generalized helix and a pseudo-geodesic line.

Moreover, cylinders are the only helix surfaces whose isogonal lines are generalized helices and geodesics.

\Box

Example 2

The usual parametrization of the
Enneper surface
,
[
3
, p. 205]
, whose coordinate curves are lines of curvature, is given by

X(t,z)=\Big(t-\frac{t^{3}}{3}+tz^{2},z-\frac{z^{3}}{3}+zt^{2},t^{2}-z^{2}\Big).

(17)

A straightforward computation yields

\kappa_{g_{1}}=\frac{-2z}{(1+t^{2}+z^{2})^{2}},\quad\kappa_{g_{2}}=\frac{2t}{(1+t^{2}+z^{2})^{2}},\quad\kappa_{1}=\frac{2}{(1+t^{2}+z^{2})^{2}},\quad\kappa_{2}=\frac{-2}{(1+t^{2}+z^{2})^{2}}.

A curve
\gamma(s)=X(t(s),z(s))
is an isogonal line if and only if
t^{\prime}(s)\sqrt{E}=\cos\phi
and
z^{\prime}(s)\sqrt{G}=\sin\phi
, for a constant
\phi
, or equivalently
z^{\prime}(s)=\tan\phi\,t^{\prime}(s)
. Hence, the isogonal lines are given by
\gamma(s)=X(t(s),m\,t(s)+n)
, for certain constants
m=\tan\phi
and
n
. In this case, from (
4
), (
5
) and (
15
) we get

\tan\theta=\frac{-n\cos\phi}{\cos^{2}\phi-\sin^{2}\phi},

showing that
\gamma
is a pseudo-geodesic line (without loss of generality we can assume that
\phi\neq\pi/4
; otherwise,
\gamma
is a line of curvature). Furthermore, from Proposition
10
we also obtain that
\gamma(s)
is a generalized helix.

In summary, if
\gamma(s)
is an isogonal line in the Enneper surface, then
\gamma
satisfies the following properties: (i)
\gamma
is a pseudo-geodesic line; (ii)
\gamma
is a generalized helix; (iii)
\gamma(s)=X(\alpha(s))
, where
\alpha
is a straight line in the plane.

\Box

In the following two examples we show surfaces which do not verify the above properties, but which will be used in the proof of the theorem.

Example 3

It is shown in
[
9
, p. 96]
that a revolution surface which is a CRPC–surface can be parametrized as

X(t,z)=(t\,\cos z,t\,\sin z,\varepsilon\int_{1}^{t}u^{c}(1-u^{2c})^{-1/2}du),

where
\varepsilon=\pm 1
and
\kappa_{1}=c\kappa_{2}\neq 0
.
The principal directions and the unit normal are given by

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle=(\sqrt{1-t^{2c}}\,\cos z,\sqrt{1-t^{2c}}\,\sin z,\varepsilon t^{c})

\displaystyle E_{2}

\displaystyle=(-\sin z,\cos z,0),

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=(-\varepsilon t^{c}\,\cos z,-\varepsilon t^{c}\,\sin z,\sqrt{1-t^{2c}}).

Let
\gamma(s)=X(t(s),z(s))
be an isogonal line which is not a line of curvature. Then we have

t^{\prime}=\cos\phi\sqrt{1-t^{2c}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad z^{\prime}\,t=\sin\phi,

(18)

and therefore

T_{\gamma}=(\cos\phi\,\sqrt{1-t^{2c}}\,\cos z-\sin\phi\,\sin z,\cos\phi\,\sqrt{1-t^{2c}}\,\sin z+\sin\phi\,\cos z,\varepsilon\cos\phi\,t^{c}).

From here and (
18
) we get

\kappa^{2}_{\gamma}=\frac{1}{t^{2}}\left(t^{2c}(c^{2}\cos^{4}\phi+(2c-1)\cos^{2}\phi\sin^{2}\phi)+\sin^{2}\phi\right).

(19)

If
\gamma
were a pseudo-geodesic line, then there would exist a constant
\theta
such that

\kappa_{\gamma}\cos\theta=\left<\kappa_{\gamma}N_{\gamma},N\right>=\varepsilon(\sin^{2}\phi+c\cos^{2}\phi)t^{c-1}.

(20)

Then from (
19
) and (
20
) we deduce that either
\gamma
is a line of curvature or
t(s)
is a constant function, which is a contradiction. In conclusion, we have proved that on this surface there exist isogonal lines which are not pseudo-geodesic lines.
\Box

Example 4

According to
[
14
, pp. 164–166]
(see also
[
4
, p. 308]
), the catenoid, the Enneper surface and the Bonnet surfaces are the only nonplanar minimal surfaces with plane lines of curvature. The usual parametrization of a Bonnet surface, whose coordinate curves are lines of curvature, is given by

X(t,z)=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-a^{2}}}(at+\sin t\cosh z),\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-a^{2}}}(z+a\cos t\sinh z),\cos t\cosh z\right),

(21)

for
0<a<1
. It is not difficult to see that

\kappa_{g_{1}}=\frac{-\sqrt{1-a^{2}}\sinh z}{(a\cos t+\cosh z)^{2}},\quad\kappa_{g_{2}}=\frac{-a\sqrt{1-a^{2}}\sin t}{(a\cos t+\cosh z)^{2}},\quad\kappa_{2}=-\kappa_{1}=\frac{1-a^{2}}{(a\cos t+\cosh z)^{2}}.

Let us suppose that
\gamma
is an isogonal line and a pseudo-geodesic line. From (
4
), (
5
) and (
15
), and reasoning as in Example
2
, we can write

A\,\sinh z+B\,\sin t+C=0,

for certain constants
A
,
B
and
C
. Derivating twice here, and taking into account that
z^{\prime}=\tan\phi\;t^{\prime}
, we get a contradiction. In conclusion, as in the above example, we have proved that on this surface there exist isogonal lines which are not pseudo-geodesic lines.

\Box

Now we can state and prove the main result.

Theorem 15

Let
M
be a nonplanar connected surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Then the isogonal lines are generalized helices and pseudo-geodesic lines if and only if
M
is a helix surface or an open piece of the Enneper surface.

Proof.

Bearing examples
1
and
2
in mind, we only need to show the direct part.

Let
M
be a connected surface such that any isogonal line is a generalized helix and a pseudo-geodesic line. From Proposition
8
,
M
has plane lines of curvature. On the other hand, if
\gamma
is a pseudo-geodesic line and a generalized helix, then
\kappa_{n}
and
\tau_{g}
are linearly dependent functions along
\gamma
, and from Proposition
7
we deduce that
M
is a
CRPC
-surface.

In
[
8
]
, the authors study surfaces with
k_{2}=ak_{1}+b
and plane lines of curvature, and show that (i) if
M
is a flat surface then
M
is a helix surface (also called a slant cylinder); (ii) if
M
is nonflat then
M
is a minimal surface or a surface of revolution. In case (ii), and bearing examples
3
and
4
in mind, we get that
M
is an open piece of the Enneper surface. This concludes the proof.
∎

Appendix: A special family of geodesics in the Enneper surface

Another interesting property of the Enneper surface

and
p_{2}
in

Indeed, let
p_{1}=X(t_{1},z_{1})

p_{2}=X(t_{2},z_{2})

, two points in

\gamma
is an isogonal line in

and so it is a generalized helix. In the case
t_{1}=t_{2}
, the points
p_{1}
and

are connected by a planar line of curvature. Some generalized helices are plotted in figure

.

It is easy to see that
