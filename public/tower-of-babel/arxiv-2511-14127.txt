Symmetric Distributions from Shallow Circuits
Daniel M. Kane, Anthony Ostuni, Kewen Wu

Abstract
We characterize the symmetric distributions that can be (approximately) generated by shallow Boolean circuits. More precisely, let f : { 0 , 1 } m → { 0 , 1 } n f\colon\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n} be a Boolean function where each output bit depends on at most d d input bits. Suppose the output distribution of f f evaluated on uniformly random input bits is close in total variation distance to a symmetric distribution 𝒟 \mathcal{D} over { 0 , 1 } n \{0,1\}^{n} . Then 𝒟 \mathcal{D} must be close to a mixture of the uniform distribution over n n -bit strings of even Hamming weight, the uniform distribution over n n -bit strings of odd Hamming weight, and γ \gamma -biased product distributions for γ \gamma an integer multiple of 2 − d 2^{-d} . Moreover, the mixing weights are determined by low-degree, sparse 𝔽 2 \mathbb{F}_{2} -polynomials. This extends the previous classification for generating symmetric distributions that are also uniform over their support.

2.1
Proof Overview of Theorem

We begin with a useful observation from

(1)

Lemma

3.4
).

By assumption, we know

is
\varepsilon
-close to a symmetric distribution
\mathcal{D}

Thus,

1

\left\|f(\mathcal{U}^{m})-f(\mathcal{U}^{m})_{\mathrm{sym}}\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}=O(\varepsilon)

Hence, it suffices to prove a weaker version of

1.2

4.26

More precisely, we want to show that for some

Step 1: Removing Large Influences.

In an extremely ideal setting, we might wish that all of

To progress toward this dream scenario, we follow in the footsteps of many prior works (e.g.,

The goal is now to argue that the function resulting from each conditioning produces a distribution which is roughly of the form we originally sought.

We start with a result from

At a high level, the proof uses the second-moment method.

Finally, we combine these deductions to prove that for each conditioning

Step 2: Kolmogorov Distance.

We now group the restricted functions according to their biases, defining

Since this difference in probability mass is convex over mixtures, it suffices to consider the individual distributions

Observe that we can associate any function
g\colon\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
to a hypergraph on the vertex set

with an edge for each input bit

containing all of the output bits that depend on
b
.
In the case that

is
d

d
.
We follow standard hypergraph terminology in defining the

of a vertex

to be the set of vertices sharing an edge with
v
.
We additionally call two neighborhoods
N_{1},N_{2}

connected
if there exist two adjacent (i.e., contained in the same edge) vertices
v_{1}\in N_{1},v_{2}\in N_{2}
.

By
[
29
, Corollary 4.11]
, we can find a collection of
r=\Omega_{d}(n)
non-connected neighborhoods in
\mathcal{H}
of size
O_{d}(1)
by only removing
O_{d}(n)
(with a small implicit constant) edges.
Translating the result to

, we find that there exists a not-too-large set
B\subseteq[m]\setminus S
of input bits such that any conditioning
\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{B}
yields a sub-function
f_{\rho,\sigma}\colon\{0,1\}^{[m]\setminus(S\cup B)}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
with many small, pairwise independent collections of output bits.
We will define
E
and
W
according to the behavior of these neighborhoods, similarly to the arguments in
[
29
,
30
,
24
]
.

Suppose for at least
r/2
of the
r
non-connected neighborhoods
N_{1},\dots,N_{r}
, we have that
f_{\rho,\sigma}(\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus(S\cup A)})
restricted to the neighborhood
N_{i}
has a marginal distribution which differs from the
\gamma
-biased product distribution over
N_{i}
, denoted
\mathcal{U}_{\gamma}^{N_{i}}
.
Since
\mathcal{U}_{\gamma}^{N_{i}}
is pointwise close to the uniform distribution over strings of Hamming weight around
\gamma n
, the marginal distributions of these neighborhoods (in
f_{\rho,\sigma}
) must also differ from the marginal distributions of the symmetric distribution
\mathcal{D}
.
We can then accumulate these errors via concentration bounds to show that
f_{\rho,\sigma}(\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus(S\cup B)})
is far from
\mathcal{D}
(see
Lemma

).

We now set
E
to be the mixture over all conditions of the bits in
B
where most resulting neighborhoods differ from the corresponding
\gamma
-biased product distribution, and set
W
to be the mixture over the remaining conditionings.
Since each sub-distribution in the mixture

is far from
\mathcal{D}
by the previous paragraph, a union bound argument implies

\mathcal{D}
(see
Lemma

3.3
).
It remains to show the continuity property for

.

Suppose the
r
non-connected neighborhoods are generated by
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{r}\in[n]
in the sense that all bits in the
i
-th neighborhood
N_{i}
are either
v_{i}
or in an edge that also contains
v_{i}
.
We further condition on all input bits that do not affect any of
v_{1},\dots,v_{r}
, so that the value of every output bit outside of
N_{1}\cup\cdots\cup N_{r}
is fixed.
In this way, the output weight of
f_{\rho,\sigma}(\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus(S\cup B)})
becomes a fixed integer (corresponding to the fixed bits outside of the neighborhoods) plus the sum of the neighborhoods’ output weights.
Since we are in the case where most neighborhoods are extremely structured, we can show that for each
2\leq\ell\leq t
, the output weight distribution modulo
\ell
of
N_{i}
is not constant for a constant fraction of the
N_{i}
’s with high probability (see
4.22
).
Finally, we can obtain our desired continuity result through known density comparison theorems for sums of independent, non-constant integer random variables, such as
[
30
, Theorem A.1]
, which relies on classical anticoncentration tools.

There is one important exception to the above analysis: the case of
\ell=2
and
\gamma=1/2
.
Here, we are not guaranteed to typically get many non-connected neighborhoods with variable output weight modulo 2 (even if most of them have the “correct” marginal distribution).
To better understand where our reasoning breaks down, consider one of the most surprising examples in this area of work.
Define
h\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
to be

h(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=(x_{1}\oplus x_{2},x_{2}\oplus x_{3},\dots,x_{n-1}\oplus x_{n},x_{n}\oplus x_{1}),

so that
h(\mathcal{U}^{n})
is the uniform distribution over
n
-bit strings of even Hamming weight.
Observe that
h
is extremely simple, only requiring two bits of locality.
Additionally, the marginal distribution onto any
k\leq n-1
coordinates is exactly the product distribution
\mathcal{U}_{1/2}^{k}
.

Let us focus on a particular output bit
y_{i}=x_{i}\oplus x_{i+1}
.
(For simplicity, assume
i
is not too close to 1 or
n
.)
Its neighborhood is
y_{i-1}=x_{i-1}\oplus x_{i}
,
y_{i}
, and
y_{i+1}=x_{i+1}\oplus x_{i+2}
, so its Hamming weight modulo 2 is

(x_{i-1}\oplus x_{i})\oplus(x_{i}\oplus x_{i+1})\oplus(x_{i+1}\oplus x_{i+2})=x_{i-1}\oplus x_{i+2}.

If we follow our earlier analysis and fix the value of the input bits that do not affect
y_{i}
(i.e.,
x_{i-1}
and
x_{i+2}
), the neighborhood’s Hamming weight modulo two is fixed, regardless of the values of
x_{i}
and
x_{i+1}
.

At a high level, the reason for this exceptional case is that it is the only setting of
\ell
and
\gamma
for which the binomial distribution
\mathsf{Bin}(t,\gamma)\bmod\ell
can equal
\mathsf{Bin}(t-1,\gamma)\bmod\ell
.
In other words, the weight distribution modulo
\ell
of
\mathcal{U}_{\gamma}^{k}
conditioned on the first bit being 0 is different than that distribution conditioned on the first bit being 1, unless
\ell=2
and
\gamma=1/2
.
This difference means that in the case of
\ell=2
and
\gamma=1/2
, we can only derive a continuity result for weights that are an even distance apart.
Hence, we must be cognizant of the output weight’s parity throughout much of our analysis, which explains the required parity version of our Kolmogorov distance result mentioned earlier in the proof overview.

Step 4: Putting It Together.

At this point, all that remains is to combine the pieces.
Recall we have already conditioned on high degree (i.e., larger than
n/O_{d,\varepsilon}(1)
) input bits to find sub-functions
\left\{f_{\rho}\right\}_{\rho}
, each producing a distribution with some approximate bias
\gamma_{\rho}
.
Additionally, the mixtures
F_{\gamma}(\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S})
obtained by grouping the sub-functions around their biases each satisfy a Kolmogorov distance bound and approximate continuity.

We partition
\left\{0,1,\dots,n\right\}
into consecutive intervals of length
c\sqrt{n}
for some small
c>0
, and restrict our attention to the
O(\log(1/\alpha))
of them that contain all but
O(\alpha)
of the mass.
By our Kolmogorov distance result (
Lemma

4.15
), we have that
|F_{\gamma}(\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S})|
and the binomial distribution
\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)
assign similar mass to each of these intervals.
Moreover, our continuity result (
Proposition

4.21
) implies the mass in a fixed interval is almost uniformly distributed.
Thus for most weights
w
,
|F_{\gamma}(\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S})|
assigns roughly the same mass to
w
as
\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)
does.
Summing over all weights provides an upper bound on the total variation distance between the two weight distributions.
We remark that there is a slight complication in the case of
\gamma=1/2
, as there
Proposition

4.21
only lets us compare weights that are an even distance apart.
However, by further subdividing each interval we consider into its even and odd parts, we are able to carry out a similar argument as before.

Finally, we recall that
f(\mathcal{U}^{m})
is a mixture of the distributions
F_{\gamma}(\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S})
.
Since the weight distribution of each
F_{\gamma}(\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S})
is close to the weight distribution corresponding to one from
Theorem

1.2
, their mixture
|f(\mathcal{U}^{m})|
naturally is close to a mixture of those weight distributions (see
Lemma

4.24
).
We conclude by recalling that
Equation

1
implies it was sufficient to classify the output weight distribution.

Mixing Weights.

The above argument shows that
f(\mathcal{U}^{m})
is close to a mixture
\mathcal{M}
of the form specified in
Theorem

1.2
, but without the additional control on the mixing weights.
The reason for this shortcoming is that the threshold
A
at which we consider an input bit “high degree” depends not just on
d
, but also on
\varepsilon
, and so the mixing weights in the obtained mixture also depend on
\varepsilon
.
If we instead chose a threshold that only depended on
d
, we would not have been able to obtain an effective error bound in the Kolmogorov distance step (i.e.,
Proposition

4.16
) when
\gamma=1/2
.

The key observation is that the arguments of
Step 1
only require conditioning on input bits of degree larger than
n/O_{d}(1)
.
In other words, if we perform some different conditioning
\kappa
on the set of input bits
S^{\prime}\subseteq[m]
above the weaker threshold
n/O_{d}(1)
, the distributions generated by the restricted functions will still have output weights which strongly concentrate around some integer multiple of
n/2^{d}
.
Moreover, the mixing weights in this setting
are
integer multiples of
2^{-O_{d}(1)}
.

It remains to argue that the mixing weights on
\mathcal{M}
, the mixture we proved
f(\mathcal{U}^{m})
is close to by the stronger conditioning
\rho
, correspond to the mixing weights derived from the weaker conditioning
\kappa
(see
Lemma

4.26
).
Since both conditionings produce mixtures of the same distribution, we have

\sum_{\gamma}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{\rho}\left[\gamma_{\rho}=\gamma\right]\cdot\E_{\rho:\gamma_{\rho}=\gamma}f_{\rho}(\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S})=f(\mathcal{U}^{m})=\sum_{\gamma}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{\kappa}\left[\gamma_{\kappa}=\gamma\right]\cdot\E_{\kappa:\gamma_{\kappa}=\gamma}f_{\kappa}(\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S^{\prime}}).

By standard concentration bounds, the probability that
x\sim f(\mathcal{U}^{m})
has Hamming weight close to
\gamma n
is almost entirely determined by the mass assigned to the distributions whose output weight concentrates around
\gamma
.
In other words, we know
\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{\rho}\left[\gamma_{\rho}=\gamma\right]\approx\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{\kappa}\left[\gamma_{\kappa}=\gamma\right]
for each
\gamma
.
By our earlier analysis and the assumption that
f(\mathcal{U}^{m})
is close to
\mathcal{D}
, it must be that the mixing weight on
\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)
in
\mathcal{M}
is roughly
\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{\rho}\left[\gamma_{\rho}=\gamma\right]
, so it must also be close to
\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{\kappa}\left[\gamma_{\kappa}=\gamma\right]
, which has the form we sought.

The analysis in the case of
\gamma=1/2
is similar, but as always, we need to keep track of the output weight’s parity.
Here, the analogous equivalence is

\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{\rho}\left[\gamma_{\rho}=1/2\land|f(\mathcal{U}^{m})|\text{ is even}\right]\approx\frac{1}{2^{|S^{\prime}|}}\sum_{\kappa:\gamma_{\kappa}=1/2}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S^{\prime}}}\left[|f_{\kappa}(x)|\text{ is even}\right].

(2)

Since
f
is
d
-local with
n
output bits, the parity of
|f_{\kappa}(x)|
can be represented as a degree-
d

\mathbb{F}_{2}
-polynomial with
O_{d}(n)
monomials.
Thus, the mixing weight for the uniform distribution over
n
-bit strings of even Hamming weight in
\mathcal{M}
must be close to the right-hand side of
Equation

2
, which is the precise sense in which the mixing weights are “determined by low-degree
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-polynomials”.
The odd parity case is essentially identical.
This concludes the proof overview of
Theorem

1.2
.

2.2
Technical Comparison to Prior Works

We now briefly survey the approaches of related prior works.
To avoid an overly verbose digression, we attempt to focus only on the most relevant papers and do not hope to be exhaustive.
Similarly, we restrict our attention within the mentioned works to the results and techniques most pertinent to our own; most, if not all, of the papers discussed contain a number of other interesting results.

The study of the complexity of sampling goes back to at least the 1980s in the influential work of Jerrum, Valiant, and Vazirani
[
28
]
.
In the context of shallow circuits, several clever sampling constructions (including the one in the introduction) were devised in
[
4
,
9
,
27
]
, while the first serious treatment of the complexity of sampling (with shallow circuits) appeared in
[
39
]
.
There, Viola proved an assortment of sampling-related results, but we will confine our attention to two on the hardness of approximately generating
\mathcal{D}_{n/2}
, the uniform distribution over
n
-bit strings of Hamming weight
n/2
.
(This specific choice is purely for simplicity, and both results also apply to the setting of
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha n}
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
.)

The first result we will mention
[
39
, Theorem 1.6]
is an unconditional lower bound of
2^{-O(d)}-O(1/n)
on the distance between
f(\mathcal{U}^{m})
, where
f\colon\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
is a
d
-local
4
4

4

In fact, the result is proven for an adaptive version of locality.
function, and
\mathcal{D}_{n/2}
.
The proof, much like our own (see
Step 2
), relies on a Kolmogorov distance bound obtained from a
k
-wise independence assumption
[
18
,
23
]
.
If the output distribution
f(\mathcal{U}^{m})
is
k
-wise independent for some large integer
k
, then
[
23
, Theorem I.5]
implies it has the wrong Hamming weight with constant probability, and we are done.
Otherwise, there exists a
k
-tuple
T\subseteq[n]
of output bits that are not uniformly distributed over
\{0,1\}^{k}
.
In this case, one observes that the probability assigned to any element of
\{0,1\}^{k}
is an integer multiple of
2^{-kd}
, so the marginal distribution over
T
must be at least
2^{-kd}
-far from uniform.
The proof concludes by noting that the marginal distribution of
\mathcal{D}_{n/2}
onto
T
is very close to uniform.
Note that we use similar granularity ideas in the proof of
Proposition

4.5
(in
Step 1
).

The second result
[
39
, Theorem 1.3]
provides a much stronger lower bound of
1-1/\mathsf{poly}(n)
for the distance between
f(\mathcal{U}^{m})
and
\mathcal{D}_{n/2}
for any
O(\log n)
-local function
f
, but is
conditional
on the input size
m
not substantially exceeding the information-theoretic minimum required to generate the target distribution.
The proof begins by partitioning the input bits
u
of
f
as
u=(x,y)
and expressing

f(u)=f(x,y)=h(y)\circ g_{1}(x_{1},y)\circ\dots\circ g_{s}(x_{s},y),

where each
g_{i}
may depend arbitrarily on
y
but only on the single bit
x_{i}
of
x
.
Additionally, each
g_{i}(x_{i},y)\in\{0,1\}^{O(d)}
.
By a greedy approach, this can be accomplished with
s\geq\Omega(n/d^{2})=n/\mathsf{polylog}(n)
.
The argument proceeds by conditioning on certain input bits (in this case
y
) and splitting into two scenarios depending on the amount of concentration; this strategy has been employed by several later works, including this one.

In the “concentrated” case where at least
\sqrt{n}
many
g_{i}
’s become fixed, the output distribution of
f(x,y)
has small support.
Even taking the union over all choices of such
y
, one finds that many elements in the support of
\mathcal{D}_{n/2}
cannot be obtained.
Note that this is where the input length restriction originates.
Alternatively, at least
s-\sqrt{n}=n/\mathsf{polylog}(n)
of the
g_{i}
’s take multiple values.
One would like to apply standard anticoncentration results to argue the output weight is too spread out, but as noted earlier in the proof overview (see
Step 3
), it may be that the Hamming weight of a
g_{i}
is fixed, even if the output is not.
Viola circumvents this issue by adding an additional “test” to determine whether at most
2\sqrt{n}
many
g_{i}
’s can output the all zeros string; in this case, taking two or more values implies the weight cannot be fixed, and thus anticoncentration inequalities can be applied.

We now turn to the recent work of Filmus, Leigh, Riazanov, and Sokolov
[
22
]
, who addressed the case of
\mathcal{D}_{k}
for
k=o(n)
.
They proved
[
22
, Theorem 1.2]
that
\widetilde{\Omega}(\log(n/k))
bits of locality (even adaptively chosen) are required to generate
\mathcal{D}_{k}
to constant error.
Moreover, the same result holds for the uniform distribution over strings whose Hamming weight lies in a set
S
, where
\max_{s\in S}s=k
.
Their proof first reduces to the case of considering
\mathcal{D}_{1}
, and then proceeds via a hitting set vs independent set dichotomy.
This can again be viewed as fitting into the concentration vs anticoncentration paradigm.
If there are
O(d2^{d})
input bits that together affect every output bit, then one can afford to condition on them and induct on the remaining
(d-1)
-local sub-distributions.
Bounds from these distributions can eventually be recombined to deduce bounds on the full distribution using some version of a union bound, such as
Lemma

3.3
.
Otherwise, there are
\Omega(2^{d})
independent output bits, and one can argue it is very likely that two of these bits evaluate to 1, since each (nonzero) output bit is 1 with probability at least
2^{-d}
.
Interestingly, the authors are able to use the
robust sunflower lemma

[
34
,
3
]
from extremal combinatorics to improve their quantitative bounds; we refer the reader to their original paper for details.

Also recently, strong unconditional locality lower bounds for sampling
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha n}
where
\alpha
is a non-dyadic rational were proven independently in
[
42
,
29
]
.
At a very high level, both proofs follow from the observation (recorded in
[
37
]
) that the marginal distribution on any output bit of a
d
-local function
f
must have probabilities that are integer multiples of
2^{-d}
, which cannot accurately approximate (say)
1/3
, corresponding to the marginal distribution for
\mathcal{D}_{n/3}
.
We begin by describing the approach of Viola
[
42
, Theorem 25]
, continuing to focus on the case of
\alpha=1/3
for simplicity.
First assume by contradiction
\left\|f(\mathcal{U}^{m})-\mathcal{D}_{n/3}\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq 1-\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon
to be determined.
The proof proceeds by arguing that there must exist a large subset
R\subseteq\{0,1\}^{m}
of the inputs, such that
f(\mathcal{U}(R))
,
f
evaluated on inputs drawn uniformly at random from
R
, lands entirely in the support of
\mathcal{D}_{n/3}
and has almost full min-entropy.
5
5

5

Recall the
min-entropy
of a random variable
X
is
\log(1/\max_{x\in\mathsf{supp}\left(X\right)}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}[X=x])
.

The so-called
fixed-set lemma
of Grinberg, Shaltiel, and Viola
[
25
]
then implies that
R
can be further restricted to a large subset
R^{\prime}\subseteq R
whose uniform distribution is nearly indistinguishable by
d
-local functions from a product distribution where each bit is either fixed or uniform.
Then the large min-entropy of
f(\mathcal{U}(R))
(and thus
f(\mathcal{U}(R^{\prime}))
) combined with the fact that the distribution is contained in the support of
\mathcal{D}_{n/3}
implies there must be at least one output bit whose marginal distribution is extremely close to
1/3
.
This, however, contradicts the fact that
1/3
cannot accurately be approximated by an integer multiple of
2^{-d}
.

The proof in
[
29
]
instead operates very similarly to the details of
Step 3
.
Through a graph-theoretic lemma
[
29
, Corollary 4.8]
, they show that there must be a set of at most
r/2^{d}
many input bits upon whose conditioning results in
r
many independent output bits for
r\approx n/2^{d^{2}}
.
As previously noted, each of these output bits inevitably incurs some error from mismatched marginal distributions, and these errors can be aggregated via standard concentration inequalities (as in
Lemma

3.2
).
Bounds from the individual sub-distributions can once again be recombined via a union bound result like
Lemma

3.3
.

The work
[
29
]
also contains locality bounds for
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha n}
when
\alpha
is a dyadic rational.
The proof has strong similarities to the non-dyadic case, except that now the marginal distributions on output bits of
f
do not necessarily disagree with those of the target distribution.
The authors overcome this issue by turning to the familiar concentration vs. anticoncentration paradigm.
The details have almost entirely been spelled out already in
Step 3
, as our analysis is nearly identical at that part.
We briefly note that the quantitative dependencies here are much worse than in the non-dyadic case, essentially for the reason that one needs to obtain a much richer structure than simply independent output bits, and so the analogous graph-theoretic lemma in this case has poor bounds.
6
6

6

Moreover, the bounds in this graph-theoretic lemma are essentially best possible; see
[
29
, Appendix A]
.

Finally, let us mention the predecessor of this work
[
30
]
, which classifies what uniform symmetric distributions can be sampled by functions of bounded locality, confirming a conjecture of
[
22
]
.
In broad strokes, it follows similarly to the proof overview, so we will be brief.
Through a strategic conditioning of input bits, one may reduce to the case where no input bits have large degree and almost all the output bits are
k
-wise independent.
Then one can show the resulting Hamming weight distribution is close in Kolmogorov distance to the binomial distribution, and it satisfies a continuity-type property.
Combining these steps together, one classifies the Hamming weight distribution, which is enough to classify the actual distribution, since we assume
f(\mathcal{U}^{m})
is close to a uniform symmetric distribution.
Much of the present work is an extension of the ideas in
[
30
]
to the general symmetric case, although there are several parts (e.g., much of the analysis in
Step 1
and the finer control on the mixing weights) that require substantially new ideas.

We conclude by noting that several works
[

10

There is a reasonable overlap in the techniques used with those discussed above, but the proofs of all these results rely on the special properties of certain pseudorandom objects like good codes
[
33
,

,
10
]
or extractors
[

Preliminaries

For a positive integer
n
, we use
[n]
to denote the set
\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}
.
We use
\mathbb{R}
to denote the set of real numbers, use
\mathbb{N}=\left\{0,1,2,\ldots\right\}
to denote the set of natural numbers, and use
\mathbb{Z}
to denote the set of integers.
For a binary string
x
, we use
|x|
to denote its Hamming weight.
We use
\log(x)
and
\ln(x)
to denote the logarithm with base
2
and
e
respectively.
For
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
, we use
a\pm b
to shorthand a number in the interval
[a-b,a+b]
.

Asymptotics.

We use the standard
O(\cdot),\Omega(\cdot),\Theta(\cdot)
notation, and emphasize that in this paper they only hide universal positive constants that do not depend on any parameter.
Occasionally we will use subscripts to suppress a dependence on particular variable (e.g.,
O_{d}(1)
).
The notation
\mathsf{poly}(\cdot)
is also sometimes used to denote a quantity that is polynomial with an unspecified exponent.
That is,
\mathsf{poly}(n)=\Theta(n^{c})
for some
c>0
.

Locality and Hypergraphs.

Let
f\colon\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
. We say
f
is a
d
-local function if each output bit
i\in[n]
depends on at most
d
input bits. Unless otherwise stated,
n,m,d
are positive integers.

We sometimes take an alternative view, using hypergraphs to model the dependency relations in
f
.
Let
G=(V,E)
be an (undirected) hypergraph.
For each
i\in V
, we use
I_{G}(i)\subseteq E
to denote the set of edges that are incident to
i
.
We say
G
has maximum degree
d
if
|I_{G}(i)|\leq d
holds for all
i\in V
.
Define
N_{G}(i)=\left\{i^{\prime}\in V\colon I_{G}(i)\cap I_{G}(i^{\prime})\neq\emptyset\right\}
to be the neighborhood of
i
. We visualize the input-output dependencies of a function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
as a hypergraph on the output bits
[n]
with an edge for each input bit containing all of the output
bits that depend on it.
Note that a
d
-local function corresponds to a hypergraph with maximum degree
d
.

Probability.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a (discrete) distribution. We use
x\sim\mathcal{P}
to denote a random sample
x
drawn from the distribution
\mathcal{P}
.
If
\mathcal{P}
is a distribution over a product space, then we say
\mathcal{P}
is a product distribution if its coordinates are independent.
In addition, let
S
be a non-empty set. If
S
indexes
\mathcal{P}
, we use
\mathcal{P}[S]
to denote the marginal distribution of
\mathcal{P}
on coordinates in
S
.
We reserve
\mathcal{U}
to denote the uniform distribution over

f_{\rho,\sigma}\colon\{0,1\}^{[m]\setminus(S\cup B)}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
by
f_{\rho,\sigma}(x)=f(x,\rho,\sigma)
, so that

f_{\rho}=\E_{\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{B}}\left[f_{\rho,\sigma}\right].

Observe that the value of every output bit in
[n]\setminus\left(N(1)\cup\cdots\cup N(r^{\prime})\right)
is fixed for all
f_{\rho,\sigma}
, and the weight of the output bits of each neighborhood becomes a random variable

X_{\sigma,i}\coloneqq\sum_{j\in N(i)}f_{\rho,\sigma}(\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus(S\cup B)})[\left\{j\right\}].

In particular, the total weight of the output of
f_{\rho,\sigma}(\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus(S\cup B)})
is some constant plus the sum of the
X_{\sigma,i}
’s, which are independent.
We would like to claim that
Lemma

4.19
can be applied to this situation with high probability.

From here, we proceed similarly to
[
29
, Claims 5.16 & 5.23]
.
For each integer
s\geq 2
, define

p_{\sigma,s,i}=\max_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[X_{\sigma,i}\equiv x\ (\mathrm{mod}\ s)\,\middle|\,\rho,\sigma\right],

where recall we previously fixed a good
\rho
.

Claim 4.22
.

For any
i\in[r^{\prime}]
and
s\geq 3
, there exists some
\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{B}
satisfying
p_{\sigma,s,i}<1
(i.e.,
X_{\sigma,i}
is not constant modulo
s
). Moreover if
\gamma\neq 1/2
, the same is true for
s=2
.

Proof.

Consider the neighborhood
N\coloneqq N(i)
of size
t_{i}\leq t
, and let
\mathcal{I}
be the set of input bits that the output bit
i
depends on.
Note
|\mathcal{I}|\leq d
.
We randomly sample
\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{B}
and two independent
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{I}}
.
Since
f_{\rho}[N]
only depends on the bits in
B\cup\mathcal{I}
, we can define

(V,Z)=f_{\rho}((\sigma,\lambda))
and
(V^{\prime},Z^{\prime})=f_{\rho}((\sigma,\lambda^{\prime}))
, where
V,V^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}
are the values of
N
’s center and
Z,Z^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{t_{i}-1}
are the values of the remaining bits in
N
.
Observe that
V
is independent from
(V^{\prime},Z^{\prime})
and likewise
V^{\prime}
is independent from
(V,Z)
, so we may apply
Lemma

4.20
to conclude

\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[V+|Z|\equiv V^{\prime}+|Z^{\prime}|\ (\mathrm{mod}\ s)\right]<1.

In particular, there must exist some
\sigma
where
X_{\sigma,i}
is not constant modulo
s
.
∎

For our fixed good
\rho
and some fixed
2\leq s\leq t
(or
3\leq s\leq t
if
\gamma=1/2
), let
\mathcal{E}_{i}
be the event that
X_{\sigma,i}
is at least
2^{-d}
-far from constant modulo
s
.
Since
X_{\sigma,i}
depends on at most
d
input bits (namely, the bits
i
depends on), if it is not constant modulo
s
, it must be at least
2^{-d}
-far from constant.
Furthermore, the bits in the neighborhood
N(i)
depend on at most
dt
input bits, so

\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{B}}\left[\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]\geq 2^{-dt}.

Recall that the neighborhoods are non-adjacent (after removing the edges in
S
), so the extraneous bits used to determine
X_{\sigma,i}
are disjoint from those used to determine
X_{\sigma,j}
for
i\neq j
.
Thus, whether or not
X_{i}
is constant modulo
s
is independent of whether
X_{j}
is.
Applying Chernoff’s inequality (
3.9
) with
\delta=1/2
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{B}}\left[\sum_{i\in[r^{\prime}]}\mathcal{E}_{i}\leq 2^{-dt-1}r^{\prime}\right]\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{r^{\prime}}{8}\right\}\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{r}{16}\right\}=\exp\left\{-\Omega_{d}(n)\right\}.

Now call a
\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{B}

fluid
if
\sum_{i}\mathcal{E}_{i}\geq 2^{-dt-1}r^{\prime}
for
every

2\leq s\leq t
(or
3\leq s\leq t
if
\gamma=1/2
).
By a union bound and the fact that
t=O_{d}(1)
, we find

\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{B}}\left[\sigma\text{ is fluid}\right]=1-\exp\left\{-\Omega_{d}(n)\right\}.

(22)

Note any fluid
\sigma
satisfies

\sum_{i\in[r^{\prime}]}(1-p_{\sigma,s,i})\geq 2^{-dt-1}\cdot r^{\prime}\cdot 2^{-d}\geq 2^{-d(t+1)-1}\cdot r\geq\Omega_{d}(n),

so we can apply
Lemma

4.19
to find for any
w\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\Delta\in\mathbb{Z}
(or even
\Delta\in\mathbb{Z}
if
\gamma=1/2
) that

\left|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus(S\cup B)}}\left[|f_{\rho,\sigma}(x)|=w\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus(S\cup B)}}\left[|f_{\rho,\sigma}(x)|=w+\Delta\right]\right|\leq O_{d}\left(\frac{|\Delta|}{n}\right).

(23)

Taking the mixture over all such subfunctions, we have

\displaystyle\>\bigg|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W|=w\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W|=w+\Delta\right]\bigg|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\>\bigg|\E_{\text{good }\rho}\left[\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S}}\left[|f_{\rho}(x)|=w\right]\right]-\E_{\text{good }\rho}\left[\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S}}\left[|f_{\rho}(x)|=w+\Delta\right]\right]\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\>\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{good }\rho\\
\sigma\end{subarray}}\bigg|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus(S\cup B)}}\left[|f_{\rho,\sigma}(x)|=w\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus(S\cup B)}}\left[|f_{\rho,\sigma}(x)|=w+\Delta\right]\bigg|

(by triangle inequality)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\>e^{-\Omega_{d}(n)}+\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{good }\rho\\
\text{fluid }\sigma\end{subarray}}\bigg|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus(S\cup B)}}\left[|f_{\rho,\sigma}(x)|=w\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus(S\cup B)}}\left[|f_{\rho,\sigma}(x)|=w+\Delta\right]\bigg|

(by
Equation

22
)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\>e^{-\Omega_{d}(n)}+O_{d}\left(\frac{|\Delta|}{n}\right).

(by
Equation

23
)

If
\Delta=0
, then
\bigg|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W|=w\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W|=w+\Delta\right]\bigg|
is trivially at most
O_{d}\left(\frac{|\Delta|}{n}\right)
.
Hence we may assume
|\Delta|\geq 1
, in which case
e^{-\Omega_{d}(n)}+O_{d}\left(\frac{|\Delta|}{n}\right)\leq O_{d}\left(\frac{|\Delta|}{n}\right)
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

4.4
Putting It Together

In this final subsection, we combine our earlier results to prove
Theorem

4.1
.
Recall
Lemma

3.4
gives that the distance between
f(\mathcal{U}^{m})
and any symmetric distribution
\mathcal{P}
is

\left\|f(\mathcal{U}^{m})-\mathcal{P}\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}=\Theta\left(\left\||f(\mathcal{U}^{m})|-|\mathcal{P}|\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}+\left\|f(\mathcal{U}^{m})-f(\mathcal{U}^{m})_{\mathrm{sym}}\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\right),

(24)

where the symmetrization
f(\mathcal{U}^{m})_{\mathrm{sym}}
is the distribution resulting from randomly permuting the coordinates of a string
x\sim f(\mathcal{U}^{m})
.
Under the assumption
f(\mathcal{U}^{m})
is
\varepsilon
-close to a symmetric distribution
\mathcal{D}
,
Equation

24
implies
\left\|f(\mathcal{U}^{m})-f(\mathcal{U}^{m})_{\mathrm{sym}}\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq O(\varepsilon)
.
Thus it remains to show that the weight distribution of
f(\mathcal{U}^{m})
is close to the weight distribution of a mixture of the form specified in
Theorem

4.1
.
We first prove the simpler case where no input bit of
f
affects many output bits, the output distribution of
f
restricted to (almost) any small set of output bits looks like a
\gamma
-biased product distribution, and the symmetric distribution
\mathcal{D}
is supported on weights around
\gamma n
.

Lemma 4.23
.

Let
k\geq 2
and
\ell\geq 1
be integers, and let
f_{1},\dots,f_{\ell}\colon\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
be
d
-local functions.
For each
f_{i}
, suppose no input bit affects more than
n/A
output bits, and suppose there exists a subset
T_{i}\subseteq[n]
of size
|T_{i}|\leq O_{d,k,A}(1)
such that every
k
-tuple of output bits in
[n]\setminus T_{i}
has distribution
\mathcal{U}_{\gamma}^{k}
, where
\gamma=a/2^{d}
for some fixed integer
0\leq a\leq 2^{d}
.

Furthermore, assume there is some mixture
F
of the
f_{i}
’s such that
F(\mathcal{U}^{m})
is
\varepsilon
-close to a symmetric distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\{0,1\}^{n}
which is supported on strings of weight
\gamma n\pm n^{2/3}
.
Then if
\gamma\neq 1/2
and
n
is sufficiently large in terms of
d
,
k
,
A
, and
\varepsilon
, we have

\left\||F(\mathcal{U}^{m})|-\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq O_{d}(\varepsilon+k^{-1/5}).

Moreover if
\gamma=1/2
, then there exists a mixture
\mathcal{Q}=\eta|\mathtt{evens}|+(1-\eta)|\mathtt{odds}|
satisfying

\left\||F(\mathcal{U}^{m})|-\mathcal{Q}\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq O_{d}\left(\varepsilon+k^{-1/5}+\log(k)\sqrt{\frac{\log(A)^{O(d)^{d}}}{A^{1/3}}+\frac{\mathsf{polylog}(k)}{\sqrt{k}}}\right).

Much of the following proof overlaps with
[
30
, Section 5.3]
.
The overall idea is to use
Proposition

4.21
to argue that the output weight of (most of)
f(\mathcal{U}^{m})
satisfies a continuity property: weights at distance
\Delta
apart are assigned mass that differs by at most
O_{d}(\Delta/n)
.
Since
Proposition

4.16
shows any contiguous weight interval is given roughly the same probability as a binomial distribution, our continuity property implies that the output weight of
f(\mathcal{U}^{m})
behaves similarly to a binomial distribution pointwise.
In the case of
\gamma=1/2
, we can combine the precise control
Proposition

4.16
provides on the probability of being in a fixed interval and even/odd with the parity continuity of
Proposition

4.21
to carry out a similar argument.

Proof.

We first address the case of
\gamma\in\{0,1\}
, where several of the tools we have developed (e.g.,
Proposition

4.16
and
Proposition

4.21
) do not apply.
We will show the argument for
\gamma=0
; the case of
\gamma=1
is essentially identical.
Let
T=\bigcup_{i}T_{i}
, and observe that by assumption, all output bits in
[n]\setminus T
are identically zero.
Thus,

\left\||F(\mathcal{U}^{m})|-\mathsf{Bin}(n,0)\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}=\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[F(\mathcal{U}^{m})[T]\neq 0^{T}\right].

(25)

Let us briefly consider the symmetrized distribution
F(\mathcal{U}^{m})_{\mathrm{sym}}
.
For clarity, let
t=|T|
.
Since any string
x\sim F(\mathcal{U}^{m})
has Hamming weight
|x|\leq t\leq\ell\cdot O_{d,k,A}(1)
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[F(\mathcal{U}^{m})_{\mathrm{sym}}[T]\neq 0^{T}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 1-\frac{\binom{n-t}{t}}{\binom{n}{t}}=1-\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}\frac{n-t-i}{n-i}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\left(1-\frac{t}{n-t}\right)^{t}\leq 1-\exp\left\{-\frac{2t^{2}}{n-t}\right\}\leq\varepsilon,

(26)

since
n
is sufficiently large in terms of
d
,
k
,
\ell
,
A
, and
\varepsilon
.
By
Lemma

3.4
and our initial assumption, we know that

\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[F(\mathcal{U}^{m})[T]\neq 0^{T}\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[F(\mathcal{U}^{m})_{\mathrm{sym}}[T]\neq 0^{T}\right]\leq\left\|F(\mathcal{U}^{m})-F(\mathcal{U}^{m})_{\mathrm{sym}}\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}=O(\varepsilon).

Combining with
Equation

25
and
Equation

26
yields the desired upper bound of

\left\||F(\mathcal{U}^{m})|-\mathsf{Bin}(n,0)\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[F(\mathcal{U}^{m})_{\mathrm{sym}}[T]\neq 0^{T}\right]+O(\varepsilon)=O(\varepsilon).

We now turn to the case of
\gamma\not\in\left\{0,1/2,1\right\}
, assuming for notational convenience that
\gamma\leq 1/2
.
Afterwards, we will describe the necessary modifications to handle the remaining
\gamma=1/2
case.
Define
\kappa\coloneqq\sqrt{\mathsf{polylog}(k)/(\sqrt{k}\cdot\mathsf{poly}(\gamma))}
with the exponents on the
\mathsf{polylog}(k)
and
\mathsf{poly}(\gamma)
corresponding to those in
Proposition

4.16
, and partition
\left\{0,1,\dots,n\right\}
into (consecutive) intervals of length
\Theta(\kappa\sqrt{n})
.
Observe that for any such interval
\mathcal{I}
,
Proposition

4.16
implies

\left|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|F(\mathcal{U}^{m})|\in\mathcal{I}\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)\in\mathcal{I}\right]\right|=O(\kappa^{2}).

(27)

Next, we apply
Proposition

4.21
to each
f_{i}
to write
f_{i}(\mathcal{U}^{m})=\lambda_{i}E_{i}+(1-\lambda_{i})W_{i}
, where

1.

\left\|E_{i}-\mathcal{D}\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\geq 1-\exp\left\{-\Omega_{d}(n)\right\}
, and

2.

For all
w\in\left\{0,1,\dots,n\right\}
and
\Delta\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

\bigg|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W_{i}|=w\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W_{i}|=w+\Delta\right]\bigg|\leq O_{d}\left(\frac{|\Delta|}{n}\right).

(28)

Since
F
is a mixture of the
f_{i}
’s, there exist
c_{1},\dots,c_{\ell}
such that

F(\mathcal{U}^{m})=\sum_{i}c_{i}\lambda_{i}E+\left(1-\sum_{i}c_{i}\lambda_{i}\right)W,

where

E=\frac{1}{\sum_{i}c_{i}\lambda_{i}}\cdot\sum_{i}c_{i}\lambda_{i}E_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad W=\frac{1}{1-\sum_{i}c_{i}\lambda_{i}}\cdot\sum_{i}c_{i}(1-\lambda_{i})W_{i}.

For each
i\in[\ell]
, our distance bound between
E_{i}
and
\mathcal{D}
(
Item

1
) guarantees an event
\mathcal{E}_{i}
with mass at least
1-\exp\left\{-\Omega_{d}(n)\right\}
in
E_{i}
, but mass at most
\exp\left\{-\Omega_{d}(n)\right\}
in
D
.
Thus if we define
\mathcal{E}=\cup\mathcal{E}_{i}
, then

\varepsilon\geq\left\|F(\mathcal{U}^{m})-\mathcal{D}\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\geq\sum_{i}c_{i}\lambda_{i}(1-\exp\left\{-\Omega_{d}(n)\right\})-\ell\cdot\exp\left\{-\Omega_{d}(n)\right\}.

In particular,
\sum_{i}c_{i}\lambda_{i}\leq O(\varepsilon)
, and

\left\|W-F(\mathcal{U}^{m})\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq O(\varepsilon).

(29)

By expanding the definition of total variation distance, we find

\displaystyle\left\||W|-\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{w=0}^{n}\left|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W|=w\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)=w\right]\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathcal{I}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{I}}\left|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W|=w\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)=w\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{I}\\
w\in\mathcal{I}\end{subarray}}\left|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W|=w\right]-\frac{\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W|\in\mathcal{I}\right]}{|\mathcal{I}|}\right|+\left|\frac{\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W|\in\mathcal{I}\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)\in\mathcal{I}\right]}{|\mathcal{I}|}\right|

\displaystyle\qquad+\left|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)=w\right]-\frac{\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)\in\mathcal{I}\right]}{|\mathcal{I}|}\right|.

For any
\delta\in(0,1)
,
3.8
implies that all but
O(\delta)
of the mass of
\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)
is supported on
O(\log(1/\delta)/\kappa)
intervals, which we call
big
(and the remaining intervals
small
).
Moreover,
|W|
also only assigns
C\cdot(\varepsilon+\kappa\log(1/\delta)+\delta)
mass to small intervals for some sufficiently large constant
C>0
, as otherwise we obtain the contradiction

\displaystyle C\cdot(\varepsilon+\kappa\log(1/\delta))

\displaystyle\leq C\cdot(\varepsilon+\kappa\log(1/\delta)+\delta)-O(\delta)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\text{small }\mathcal{I}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{I}}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W|=w\right]-\sum_{\text{small }\mathcal{I}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{I}}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)=w\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\text{big }\mathcal{I}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{I}}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)=w\right]-\sum_{\text{big }\mathcal{I}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{I}}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W|=w\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\text{big }\mathcal{I}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{I}}\left|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)=w\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W|=w\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\text{big }\mathcal{I}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{I}}\Big(\left|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)=w\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|F(\mathcal{U}^{m})|=w\right]\right|

\displaystyle\qquad+\left|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|F(\mathcal{U}^{m})|=w\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W|=w\right]\right|\Big)

\displaystyle\leq O(\kappa\log(1/\delta))+2\left\||F(\mathcal{U}^{m})|-|W|\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq O(\varepsilon+\kappa\log(1/\delta)),

where the final two inequalities follow from
Equation

27
and
Equation

29
, respectively.
Hence,

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}\left\||W|-\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}

\displaystyle\leq O(\varepsilon+\kappa\log(1/\delta)+\delta)+\sum_{\text{big }\mathcal{I}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{I}}\left|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W|=w\right]-\frac{\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W|\in\mathcal{I}\right]}{|\mathcal{I}|}\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\>+\left|\frac{\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W|\in\mathcal{I}\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)\in\mathcal{I}\right]}{|\mathcal{I}|}\right|+\left|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)=w\right]-\frac{\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)\in\mathcal{I}\right]}{|\mathcal{I}|}\right|.

(30)

Clearly,

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{I}}\left|\frac{\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W|\in\mathcal{I}\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)\in\mathcal{I}\right]}{|\mathcal{I}|}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W|\in\mathcal{I}\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)\in\mathcal{I}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W|\in\mathcal{I}\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|F(\mathcal{U}^{m})|\in\mathcal{I}\right]\right|+\left|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|F(\mathcal{U}^{m})|\in\mathcal{I}\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)\in\mathcal{I}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W|\in\mathcal{I}\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|F(\mathcal{U}^{m})|\in\mathcal{I}\right]\right|+O(\kappa^{2}).

(by
Equation

27
)

Summing over all big intervals, the first term is at most
2\left\|W-F(\mathcal{U}^{m})\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq O(\varepsilon)
by
Equation

29
, and the second term is at most
O\left(\kappa\log(1/\delta)\right)
.

Additionally, note that
Equation

28
and the triangle inequality implies

\left|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W|=w\right]-\frac{\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W|\in\mathcal{I}\right]}{|\mathcal{I}|}\right|\leq\max_{y\in\mathcal{I}}\left|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W|=w\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|W|=y\right]\right|\leq O_{d}\left(\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Summing over all
w\in\mathcal{I}
gives an upper bound of
O_{d}(\kappa^{2})
, and further summing over big intervals gives
O_{d}(\kappa\log(1/\delta))
.
The sum of the

\left|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)=w\right]-\frac{\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)\in\mathcal{I}\right]}{|\mathcal{I}|}\right|

terms can be bounded similarly, since

\max_{y\in\mathcal{I}}\left|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)=w\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)=y\right]\right|\leq O\left(\frac{\kappa\sqrt{n}}{\gamma(1-\gamma)n}\right)=O_{d}\left(\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Combining these inequalities together with
Equation

30
, we find

\left\||W|-\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq O_{d}(\varepsilon+\delta+\kappa\log(1/\delta)).

Set
\delta=k^{-1/5}
and recall
\kappa=\sqrt{\mathsf{polylog}(k)/(\sqrt{k}\cdot\mathsf{poly}(\gamma))}
.
Again applying
Equation

29
, we conclude

\left\||F(\mathcal{U}^{m})|-\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq\left\||F(\mathcal{U}^{m})|-|W|\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}+\left\||W|-\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}=O_{d}(\varepsilon+k^{-1/5}).

We now consider the case of
\gamma=1/2
.
The proof is almost identical to the previous case, so we only highlight the relevant differences.
In this setting,
Proposition

4.21
only provides a continuity guarantee on weights differing by an even integer.
Hence, we refine the previously considered intervals into their even and odd parts.
Note this only changes the number of intervals in our analysis by a factor of two.
Moreover,
Proposition

4.16
(applied with
\delta=A^{-1/3}
) now provides a bound on the Kolmogorov distance between
|f(\mathcal{U}^{m})|
and a mixture
\mathcal{M}=\eta|\mathtt{evens}|+(1-\eta)|\mathtt{odds}|
of

O\left(\frac{\log(A)^{O(d)^{d}}}{A^{1/3}}+\frac{\mathsf{polylog}(k)}{\sqrt{k}}\right)\text{\reflectbox{$\coloneqq$}~}\kappa^{2}.

Carrying out the remaining steps with
\delta
set to
k^{-1/5}
as before, we conclude

\displaystyle\left\||F(\mathcal{U}^{m})|-\mathcal{M}\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\||F(\mathcal{U}^{m})|-|W|\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}+\left\||W|-\mathcal{M}\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}

\displaystyle=O_{d}(\varepsilon+\delta+\kappa\log(1/\delta))

\displaystyle=O_{d}\left(\varepsilon+k^{-1/5}+\log(k)\sqrt{\frac{\log(A)^{O(d)^{d}}}{A^{1/3}}+\frac{\mathsf{polylog}(k)}{\sqrt{k}}}\right).\qed

We now use
Lemma

4.23
to prove the general case.
To obtain that lemma’s required assumptions, we first condition on all input bits of large degree.
This will certainly result in the setting where no input bit affects many output bits, but using
Proposition

4.5
, it also lets us conclude the restricted functions generate distributions which resemble
\gamma
-biased product distributions.
For the final condition of
Lemma

4.26
, we need the symmetric distribution
\mathcal{D}
to be supported on weights around
\gamma n
.
Through a somewhat laborious but straightforward calculation, we show in
4.25
that the restrictions of
f(\mathcal{U}^{m})
with bias roughly
\gamma
are relatively close to
\mathcal{D}
conditioned on its output weight being
\gamma n\pm n^{2/3}
, as desired.

Lemma 4.24
.

Let
f\colon\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
be a
d
-local function.
Assume
f(\mathcal{U}^{m})
is
\varepsilon
-close to a symmetric distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\{0,1\}^{n}
where
n
is sufficiently large in terms of
d
and
\varepsilon
, and
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small in terms of
d
.
Then the distribution over the Hamming weight of
f(\mathcal{U}^{m})
is
O_{d}\left(\frac{1}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/5}
-close to a convex combination of the form

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in[0,2^{d}]\cap\mathbb{Z}\\
a\neq 2^{d-1}\end{subarray}}c_{a}\cdot\mathsf{Bin}(n,a/2^{d})+c_{e}\cdot|\mathtt{evens}|+c_{o}\cdot|\mathtt{odds}|.

Proof.

Let
k
be the largest even integer less than
\log(1/\varepsilon)/C_{d}
, where
C_{d}\geq 1
is a sufficiently large constant depending only on
d
.
By choosing
\varepsilon
small enough, we may assume
k\geq 4
(in order to later apply
3.11
).
Define
S\subseteq[m]
to be the set of input bits with degree at least
n/k
.
Note that by the locality assumption,
|S|\leq dk
.
For each conditioning
\rho\in\{0,1\}^{S}
on the bits in
S
,
Proposition

4.5
guarantees a subset
T_{\rho}\subseteq[n]
of size
|T_{\rho}|\leq O_{d,k}(1)
and a bias
\gamma_{\rho}=a_{\rho}/2^{d}
, where
0\leq a_{\rho}\leq 2^{d}
is an integer, such that every
k
-tuple of output bits in
[n]\setminus T_{\rho}
has distribution
\mathcal{U}_{\gamma_{\rho}}^{k}
.

We proceed by grouping the restricted functions according to their biases.
More formally, we write
f(\mathcal{U}^{m})
as the mixture

f(\mathcal{U}^{m})=\sum_{\gamma}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{\rho}\left[\gamma_{\rho}=\gamma\right]\cdot f_{\gamma}(\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S})\quad\text{where}\quad f_{\gamma}(\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S})\coloneqq\E_{\rho}\left[f(\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S},\rho)\,\middle|\,\gamma_{\rho}=\gamma\right].

Similarly, we let
\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}
denote the distribution
\mathcal{D}
conditioned on the Hamming weight being
\gamma n\pm n^{2/3}
.
Since
f(\mathcal{U}^{m})
is close to
\mathcal{D}
by assumption,
f_{\gamma}(\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S})
should be close to
\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}
.
We formalize this intuition in the following claim.

Claim 4.25
.

If
\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{\rho}\left[\gamma_{\rho}=\gamma\right]>0
, then
\left\|f_{\gamma}(\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S})-\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq O_{d}(\varepsilon)
.

The proof of
4.25
is routine but rather tedious, so we defer the details to
Appendix

C
.

For each
\gamma
, we combine
4.25
and
Lemma

4.23

to deduce

\displaystyle\left\||f_{\gamma}(\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S})|-\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}

\displaystyle\leq O_{d}\left(\varepsilon+k^{-1/5}+\log(k)\sqrt{\frac{(\log(k))^{O(d)^{d}}}{k^{1/3}}+\frac{\mathsf{polylog}(k)}{\sqrt{k}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq O_{d}\left(\varepsilon+\left(\frac{1}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/5}\right)

(since
k=\Theta_{d}(\log(1/\varepsilon))
)

\displaystyle\leq O_{d}\left(\frac{1}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/5},

(31)

where

\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}=\begin{cases}\mathsf{Bin}(n,\gamma)&\text{ if }\gamma\neq 1/2\\
\eta|\mathtt{evens}|+(1-\eta)|\mathtt{odds}|&\text{ if }\gamma=1/2\end{cases}

for some
\eta\in[0,1]
.
Define the mixture
\mathcal{P}\coloneqq\sum_{\gamma}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{\rho}\left[\gamma_{\rho}=\gamma\right]\cdot\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}
.
Writing the output weight of
f
as a convex combination of the conditionings, we find

\displaystyle\left\||f(\mathcal{U}^{m})|-\mathcal{P}\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{\gamma}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{\rho}\left[\gamma_{\rho}=\gamma\right]\cdot|f_{\gamma}(\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus(S\cup R)})|-\sum_{\gamma}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{\rho}\left[\gamma_{\rho}=\gamma\right]\cdot\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\gamma}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{\rho}\left[\gamma_{\rho}=\gamma\right]\left\||f_{\gamma}(\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus(S\cup R)})|-\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}

(by triangle inequality)

\displaystyle\leq O_{d}\left(\frac{1}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/5}.

(by
Equation

31
)

This completes the proof of
Lemma

4.24
.
∎

Lemma

4.24
states that the weight distribution of
f(\mathcal{U}^{m})
must be close to a mixture of specific distributions.
The following lemma provides additional information about this mixture by describing the structure of the mixing weights.

Lemma 4.26
.

Let
f\colon\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
be a
d
-local function.
Assume
f(\mathcal{U}^{m})
is
\varepsilon
-close to a symmetric distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\{0,1\}^{n}
where
n
is sufficiently large in terms of
d
and
\varepsilon
, and
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small in terms of
d
.
Then the distribution over the Hamming weight of
f(\mathcal{U}^{m})
is
O_{d}\left(\frac{1}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/5}
-close to a convex combination of the form

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in[0,2^{d}]\cap\mathbb{Z}\\
a\neq 2^{d-1}\end{subarray}}c_{a}\cdot\mathsf{Bin}(n,a/2^{d})+c_{e}\cdot|\mathtt{evens}|+c_{o}\cdot|\mathtt{odds}|,

where each
c_{a}=c_{a}^{\prime}/2^{C}
for some integer
0\leq c_{a}^{\prime}\leq 2^{C}
and a fixed integer
C=O_{d}(1)
.
Moreover, there exist at most
2^{C}
many degree-
d

\mathbb{F}_{2}
-polynomials
\{p_{i}\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}\}
, each with
O_{d}(n)
monomials, such that

c_{e}=\frac{1}{2^{C}}\cdot\sum_{i}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}^{m}}\left[p_{i}(x)=0\right]\quad\text{and}\quad c_{o}=\frac{1}{2^{C}}\cdot\sum_{i}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}^{m}}\left[p_{i}(x)=1\right].

Proof.

We know from
Lemma

4.24
that
f(\mathcal{U}^{m})
’s weight distribution is
O_{d}\left(\frac{1}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/5}
-close to a convex combination of the form

\mathcal{P}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in[0,2^{d}]\cap\mathbb{Z}\\
a\neq 2^{d-1}\end{subarray}}c_{a}\cdot\mathsf{Bin}(n,a/2^{d})+c_{e}\cdot|\mathtt{evens}|+c_{o}\cdot|\mathtt{odds}|,

so it remains to reason about the mixing weights.
To this end, let
S\subseteq[m]
be the set of input bits with degree at least
n/2^{100d}
.
Observe that this is a smaller set than the
S
used in the proof of
Lemma

4.24
; this will ultimately provide stronger control over the mixing weights.

For each conditioning
\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{S}
on the bits in
S
,
Lemma

4.9
guarantees an integer
0\leq a_{\sigma}\leq 2^{d}
such that

\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S}}\left[\left|\frac{\left|f(x,\sigma)\right|}{n}-\frac{a_{\sigma}}{2^{d}}\right|\geq\frac{1}{n^{1/(800d)}}\right]\leq\mathsf{poly}(\varepsilon).

(32)

This also implies for any integer
b\neq a_{\sigma}
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S}}\left[\left|\frac{\left|f(x,\sigma)\right|}{n}-\frac{b}{2^{d}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{n^{1/(800d)}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S}}\left[\left|\frac{\left|f(x,\sigma)\right|}{n}-\frac{a_{\sigma}}{2^{d}}\right|\geq\frac{1}{2^{d}}-\frac{1}{n^{1/(800d)}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S}}\left[\left|\frac{\left|f(x,\sigma)\right|}{n}-\frac{a_{\sigma}}{2^{d}}\right|\geq\frac{1}{n^{1/(800d)}}\right]\leq\mathsf{poly}(\varepsilon).

(33)

Define
p_{\sigma}\colon\{0,1\}^{[m]\setminus S}\to\{0,1\}
to be
|f(x,\sigma)|\bmod 2
.
Note that
p_{\sigma}
is a sum (modulo 2) of
n
output bits, each of which depends on at most
d
input bits.
Hence,
p_{\sigma}
can be expressed as a degree-
d

\mathbb{F}_{2}
-polynomial with
O_{d}(n)
monomials.
We will show that the conclusion of
Lemma

4.26
is satisfied by

\mathcal{Q}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in[0,2^{d}]\cap\mathbb{Z}\\
a\neq 2^{d-1}\end{subarray}}\frac{c_{a}^{\prime}}{2^{|S|}}\cdot\mathsf{Bin}(n,a/2^{d})+\frac{c_{e}^{\prime}}{2^{|S|}}\cdot|\mathtt{evens}|+\frac{c_{o}^{\prime}}{2^{|S|}}\cdot|\mathtt{odds}|,

(34)

where

c_{a}^{\prime}=\#\left\{\sigma:a_{\sigma}=a\right\},\quad c_{e}^{\prime}=\sum_{\sigma:a_{\sigma}=2^{d-1}}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S}}\left[p_{\sigma}(x)=0\right],\ \text{and}\ c_{o}^{\prime}=\sum_{\sigma:a_{\sigma}=2^{d-1}}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S}}\left[p_{\sigma}(x)=1\right].

The proof will proceed in two steps.
First, we will show that
c_{a}
is essentially the probability that
|f(\mathcal{U}^{m})|/n
is close to
a/2^{d}
.
Second, we will show that this probability is closely approximated by the fraction of conditionings which concentrate around
a
.
(As usual, there are some additional considerations in the
a=2^{d-1}
case.)

For clarity, define
c_{a}=c_{e}+c_{o}
and

•

\delta_{a}=\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}^{m}}\left[\left|\frac{|f(x)|}{n}-\frac{a}{2^{d}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{n^{1/(800d)}}\right]
,

•

\delta_{e}=\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}^{m}}\left[\left|\frac{|f(x)|}{n}-\frac{1}{2}\right|\leq\frac{1}{n^{1/(800d)}}\land|f(x)|\text{ is even}\right]
,

•

\delta_{o}=\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}^{m}}\left[\left|\frac{|f(x)|}{n}-\frac{1}{2}\right|\leq\frac{1}{n^{1/(800d)}}\land|f(x)|\text{ is odd}\right]
.

Claim 4.27
.

For any
0\leq a\leq 2^{d}
, we have

\left|c_{a}-\delta_{a}\right|\leq O_{d}\left(\frac{1}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/5}
.
Moreover, the same upper bound holds on
\left|c_{e}-\delta_{e}\right|
and
\left|c_{o}-\delta_{o}\right|
.

Proof of
4.27
.

We first record a number of consequences of Hoeffding’s inequality (
3.8
) for sufficiently large
n
.
We have

\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{w\sim\mathsf{Bin}(n,a/2^{d})}\left[\left|\frac{w}{n}-\frac{a}{2^{d}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{n^{1/(800d)}}\right]\geq 1-\exp\left\{-2n^{1-(1/(400d))}\right\}\geq 1-e^{-\sqrt{n}}

and

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{w\sim\mathsf{Bin}(n,1/2)}\left[\left|\frac{w}{n}-\frac{1}{2}\right|\leq\frac{1}{n^{1/(800d)}}\,\middle|\,w\text{ is even}\right]

\displaystyle=1-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{w\sim\mathsf{Bin}(n,1/2)}\left[\left|\frac{w}{n}-\frac{1}{2}\right|>\frac{1}{n^{1/(800d)}}\,\middle|\,w\text{ is even}\right]

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{w\sim\mathsf{Bin}(n,1/2)}\left[\left|\frac{w}{n}-\frac{1}{2}\right|>\frac{1}{n^{1/(800d)}}\right]}{\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{w\sim\mathsf{Bin}(n,1/2)}\left[w\text{ is even}\right]}

\displaystyle\geq 1-2\exp\left\{-2n^{1-(1/(400d))}\right\}\geq 1-e^{-\sqrt{n}}.

Furthermore for
b\neq a
,

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{w\sim\mathsf{Bin}(n,b/2^{d})}\left[\left|\frac{w}{n}-\frac{a}{2^{d}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{n^{1/(800d)}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{w\sim\mathsf{Bin}(n,b/2^{d})}\left[\left|\frac{w}{n}-\frac{b}{2^{d}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2^{d}}-\frac{1}{n^{1/(800d)}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{-2n\left(\frac{1}{2^{d}}-\frac{1}{n^{1/(800d)}}\right)^{2}\right\}\leq e^{-\sqrt{n}}.

Thus for any
0\leq a\leq 2^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\delta_{a}^{\prime}\coloneqq\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{w\sim\mathcal{P}}\left[\left|\frac{w}{n}-\frac{a}{2^{d}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{n^{1/(800d)}}\right]

\displaystyle=c_{a}\cdot\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{w\sim\mathsf{Bin}(n,a/2^{d})}\left[\left|\frac{w}{n}-\frac{a}{2^{d}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{n^{1/(800d)}}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{b\neq a}c_{b}\cdot\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{w\sim\mathsf{Bin}(n,b/2^{d})}\left[\left|\frac{w}{n}-\frac{a}{2^{d}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{n^{1/(800d)}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq c_{a}+e^{-\sqrt{n}}.

Similarly,
\delta_{a}^{\prime}\geq c_{a}-e^{-\sqrt{n}}
.
Combining, we find that

\displaystyle\left|c_{a}-\delta_{a}\right|\leq\left|c_{a}-\delta_{a}^{\prime}\right|+\left|\delta_{a}^{\prime}-\delta_{a}\right|

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\sqrt{n}}+\left\|\mathcal{P}-|f(\mathcal{U}^{m})|\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq O_{d}\left(\frac{1}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/5}.

For the case of
\delta_{e}
, we have

\displaystyle\delta_{e}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{w\sim\mathsf{Bin}(n,1/2)}\left[\left|\frac{w}{n}-\frac{1}{2}\right|\leq\frac{1}{n^{1/(800d)}}\land w\text{ is even}\right]

\displaystyle=c_{e}\cdot\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{w\sim\mathsf{Bin}(n,1/2)}\left[\left|\frac{w}{n}-\frac{1}{2}\right|\leq\frac{1}{n^{1/(800d)}}\,\middle|\,w\text{ is even}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{b\neq 2^{d-1}}c_{b}\cdot\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{w\sim\mathsf{Bin}(n,b/2^{d})}\left[\left|\frac{w}{n}-\frac{1}{2}\right|\leq\frac{1}{n^{1/(800d)}}\land w\text{ is even}\right]

\displaystyle\leq c_{e}+\sum_{b\neq 2^{d-1}}c_{b}\cdot\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{w\sim\mathsf{Bin}(n,b/2^{d})}\left[\left|\frac{w}{n}-\frac{1}{2}\right|\leq\frac{1}{n^{1/(800d)}}\right]\leq c_{e}+e^{-\sqrt{n}}

and

\displaystyle\delta_{e}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\geq c_{e}\cdot\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{w\sim\mathsf{Bin}(n,1/2)}\left[\left|\frac{w}{n}-\frac{1}{2}\right|\leq\frac{1}{n^{1/(800d)}}\,\middle|\,w\text{ is even}\right]\geq c_{e}-e^{-\sqrt{n}}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\left|c_{e}-\delta_{e}\right|\leq\left|c_{e}-\delta_{e}^{\prime}\right|+\left|\delta_{e}^{\prime}-\delta_{e}\right|

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\sqrt{n}}+\left\|\mathcal{P}-|f(\mathcal{U}^{m})|\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq O_{d}\left(\frac{1}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/5}.

The analysis of
\delta_{o}
is essentially identical.
∎

Recall
p_{\sigma}(x)=|f(x,\sigma)|\bmod 2
.

Claim 4.28
.

For any
0\leq a\leq 2^{d}
, we have
\left|\delta_{a}-\frac{\#\left\{\sigma:a_{\sigma}=a\right\}}{2^{|S|}}\right|\leq\mathsf{poly}(\varepsilon).

Moreover, the same upper bound holds on

Proof of
4.28
.

We can express
\delta_{a}
as

.

For the case of

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2^{|S|}}\sum_{\sigma:a_{\sigma}=2^{d-1}}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S}}\left[p_{\sigma}(x)=0\right]+\mathsf{poly}(\varepsilon)

and

\displaystyle\delta_{e}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2^{|S|}}\sum_{\sigma:a_{\sigma}=2^{d-1}}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S}}\left[\left|\frac{|f(x,\sigma)|}{n}-\frac{1}{2}\right|\leq\frac{1}{n^{1/(800d)}}\land|f(x,\sigma)|\text{ is even}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2^{|S|}}\sum_{\sigma:a_{\sigma}=2^{d-1}}\left(\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S}}\left[|f(x,\sigma)|\text{ is even}\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S}}\left[\left|\frac{|f(x,\sigma)|}{n}-\frac{1}{2}\right|>\frac{1}{n^{1/(800d)}}\right]\right)

Combining

, and

.

Proof.

(35)

where
\mathcal{Q}
is a distribution of the form in the theorem statement, and

.

\left\|f(\mathcal{U}^{m})-f(\mathcal{U}^{m})_{\mathrm{sym}}\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}=O(\varepsilon)
.

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in[0,2^{d}]\cap\mathbb{Z}\\

Let

Again applying

\mathcal{P}

Proof.

Claim A.2

\mathcal{P}
can be sampled exactly by a 3-local function.

.

)

Recall for each restricted function
f_{\rho}(\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S})
there exists some subset
T_{\rho}\subseteq[n]
of size
|T_{\rho}|\leq O_{d,k}(1)
such that every
k
-tuple of bits in
[n]\setminus T_{\rho}
sampled from
f_{\rho}(\mathcal{U}^{[m]\setminus S})
has distribution
\mathcal{U}_{\gamma_{\rho}}^{k}
(i.e., is
k
-wise independent for some even
k
).
Define
T
to be the union over all
T_{\rho}
in the mixture
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
, and note that
|T|\leq\ell\cdot O_{d,k}(1)
.
Let
\overline{x}
denote the restriction of
x
to the bits in
[n]\setminus T
.
Then we may continue the previous chain of inequalities by

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}}\left[\mathcal{E}^{*}(x)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}}\left[\big||\overline{x}|-\alpha n\big|\geq 2^{-d-1}n-|T|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}}\left[\big||\overline{x}|-\alpha(n-|T|)\big|\geq 2^{-d-1}n-(1+\alpha)|T|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}}\left[\big||\overline{x}|-\E[\overline{x}]\big|\geq 2^{-d-2}n\right]

(since
n\gg d,k,\ell
)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\frac{nk}{\left(2^{-d-2}n\right)^{2}}\right)^{k/2}

(by
3.11
)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{2^{2d+5}\cdot k}{n}\right)^{k/2}.

(45)

Now consider
p_{\gamma}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}^{*}(x)\right]
.
We know by assumption that
p_{\gamma}>0
, so there must exist some setting
\rho
of the bits in
S
such that
\gamma_{\rho}=\gamma
.
Hence, we in fact have the stronger lower bound

p_{\gamma}\geq 2^{-|S|}.

(46)

For the remaining factor, we find

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}^{*}(x)\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}}\left[\big||x|-\gamma n\big|\leq n^{2/3}\right]

\displaystyle\geq 1-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}}\left[\big||\overline{x}|-\gamma(n-|T|)\big|>n^{2/3}-(1+\gamma)|T|\right]

\displaystyle\geq 1-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}}\left[\big||\overline{x}|-\E[\overline{x}]\big|>\frac{n^{2/3}}{2}\right]

(since
n\gg d,k
)

\displaystyle\geq 1-2\left(\frac{nk}{(n^{2/3}/2)^{2}}\right)^{k/2}

(by
3.11
)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\left(\frac{8k}{n^{1/3}}\right)^{k/2}.

(47)

Finally, we consider
p_{\gamma}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\land\mathcal{E}^{*}(x)\right]
.
We can again use
Equation

46
to lower bound
p_{\gamma}
.
Additionally,

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\land\mathcal{E}^{*}(x)\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\land\neg\mathcal{E}^{*}(x)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\right]-\left(1-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}^{*}(x)\right]\right)

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\right]-\left(\frac{8k}{n^{1/3}}\right)^{k/2},

(48)

where the final inequality uses
Equation

47
.
Substituting
Equation

45
,
Equation

46
,
Equation

47
, and
Equation

48
into
Equation

44
, we find that

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{\gamma}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\land\mathcal{E}^{*}(x)\right]+\sum_{\alpha\neq\gamma}p_{\alpha}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\land\mathcal{E}^{*}(x)\right]}{p_{\gamma}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}^{*}(x)\right]+\sum_{\alpha\neq\gamma}p_{\alpha}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}}\left[\mathcal{E}^{*}(x)\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\right]+\frac{1}{p_{\gamma}}\max_{\alpha\neq\gamma}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}}\left[\mathcal{E}^{*}(x)\right]}{\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}^{*}(x)\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\right]+2^{|S|}\left(\frac{2^{2d+5}\cdot k}{n}\right)^{k/2}}{1-\left(\frac{8k}{n^{1/3}}\right)^{k/2}}.

Rearranging gives

\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\right]\leq\left(\frac{8k}{n^{1/3}}\right)^{k/2}+2^{|S|}\left(\frac{2^{2d+5}\cdot k}{n}\right)^{k/2}\leq n^{-k/10},

(49)

since
n
is sufficiently large in terms of
d,k,
and
\varepsilon
.
Similarly, we find that

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{\gamma}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\land\mathcal{E}^{*}(x)\right]+\sum_{\alpha\neq\gamma}p_{\alpha}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\land\mathcal{E}^{*}(x)\right]}{p_{\gamma}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}^{*}(x)\right]+\sum_{\alpha\neq\gamma}p_{\alpha}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}}\left[\mathcal{E}^{*}(x)\right]}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\land\mathcal{E}^{*}(x)\right]}{1+\frac{1}{p_{\gamma}}\max_{\alpha\neq\gamma}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}}\left[\mathcal{E}^{*}(x)\right]}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\right]-\left(\frac{8k}{n^{1/3}}\right)^{k/2}}{1+2^{|S|}\left(\frac{2^{2d+5}\cdot k}{n}\right)^{k/2}},

or equivalently

\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\right]\leq\left(\frac{8k}{n^{1/3}}\right)^{k/2}+2^{|S|}\left(\frac{2^{2d+5}\cdot k}{n}\right)^{k/2}\leq n^{-k/10}.

(50)

Combining
Equation

49
and
Equation

50
yields

\left\|\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}-\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq n^{-k/10}.

(51)

We now compare
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}
.
For clarity, define

\frac{A}{B}\coloneqq\frac{\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim f(\mathcal{U}^{m})}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\land\mathcal{E}^{*}(x)\right]}{\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim f(\mathcal{U}^{m})}\left[\mathcal{E}^{*}(x)\right]}=\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\right]

and

\frac{A^{\prime}}{B^{\prime}}\coloneqq\frac{\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{y\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathcal{E}(y)\land\mathcal{E}^{*}(y)\right]}{\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{y\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathcal{E}^{*}(y)\right]}=\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\right].

By our initial assumption, we know

\max\left\{|A-A^{\prime}|,|B-B^{\prime}|\right\}\leq\left\|f(\mathcal{U}^{m})-\mathcal{D}\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq\varepsilon.

(52)

Thus,

\displaystyle\left|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\right]\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{\left|AB^{\prime}-A^{\prime}B\right|}{BB^{\prime}}\leq\frac{A+B}{B(B-\varepsilon)}\cdot\varepsilon

(by
Equation

52
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2B}{B(B-\varepsilon)}\cdot\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon\cdot\left(\frac{1}{p_{\gamma}\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}^{*}(x)\right]-\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2^{-|S|}\left(1-\left(\frac{8k}{n^{1/3}}\right)^{k/2}\right)-\varepsilon}\right)

(by
Equation

46
&
Equation

47
)

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2^{-2|S|}-\varepsilon}\right).

(since
n
large in terms of
k,\varepsilon
)

Recall that
|S|\leq dk
, where
k\leq\log(1/\varepsilon)/C_{d}
for some sufficiently large constant
C_{d}>0
depending only on
d
.
Hence,

\left|\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\right]-\operatorname*{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)\right]\right|\leq O_{d}(\varepsilon).

(53)

Combining
Equation

51
and
Equation

53
, we conclude

\left\|\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}-\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq\left\|\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}-\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}+\left\|\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}-\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}\right\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq n^{-k/10}+O_{d}(\varepsilon)\leq O_{d}(\varepsilon)

for large enough
n
.
∎
