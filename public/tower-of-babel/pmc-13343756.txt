Hope and fear in AI-based language teaching: opportunities, challenges, and emotions
Authors: Eda Tekin, Yusuf Karakahraman, Durmuş Barış Kır
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into second and foreign language (L2) education has increased significantly; however, its emotional impact on teachers remains underexplored, particularly in less commonly taught languages (LCTL) such as Turkish. This qualitative exploratory case study examines the perceptions, experiences, and emotional responses of 17 Turkish as a Foreign Language (TFL) teachers. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews and analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis assisted by MAXQDA 24 software, grounded in Pekrun’s Control-Value Theory (CVT). Findings reveal a nuanced landscape where AI acts as a “pedagogical co-instructor.” Teachers reported predominantly positive emotional responses, including feeling motivational (42.31%) and developer/beneficial (34.62%), while a minority expressed fear (7.69%) and suspicion (3.85%). These emotions are linked to perceived pedagogical control and the “English-centric” bias of current AI tools. The study concludes that successful AI integration in TFL requires balancing technological efficiency with human-centered cultural nuances.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04754-5.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) tools and their impact on education have recently gained significant attention [5, 8, 12, 46, 68, 79, 84]. AI is a powerful technology that can improve teaching, support personalized learning, and help with complex tasks in education. In language teaching, it allows teachers to customize lessons to meet the needs and abilities of each student. AI tools can give instant feedback, guide students step by step, and handle repetitive teaching tasks, making learning more efficient and engaging. They also help teachers track student progress and make better teaching decisions based on data. By analyzing this data, teachers can identify learners’ strengths and areas for improvement, allowing for more targeted instruction. Such insights create a foundation for tailoring learning experiences to individual needs. Therefore, AI tools have begun to be used more widely by teachers and students in language teaching [48, 54, 58, 71]. These generative AI tools, including chatbots, have demonstrated the capacity to engage learners in meaningful language interactions, simulate authentic communicative scenarios, and support the development of higher-order language skills. They also provide teachers with opportunities for innovative assessment methods, such as automated formative feedback and adaptive evaluation, which can complement traditional classroom assessment techniques.

However, while extensive research has been conducted on the effects of AI in enhancing language skills and facilitating learning in widely taught languages such as English [19, 36, 37, 70, 80], there is a lack of in-depth studies on the opportunities and challenges of AI for less commonly taught languages (LCTL) like Turkish. This gap is not merely geographical or linguistic but conceptual; the “English-centric” nature of AI [2, 44, 57] development creates a tension for teachers of languages with different morphological structures and lower digital resource availability. In such contexts, AI tools are often not fully aligned with the linguistic and pedagogical needs of the target language, requiring teachers to adapt, reinterpret, or compensate for these limitations. This situation places teachers in a position where they must continuously evaluate the usefulness, reliability, and appropriateness of AI tools within their specific teaching contexts [69]. Such ongoing evaluation is not only a cognitive process but also an affective one. As teachers engage with AI tools under conditions of uncertainty and adaptation, they may experience a range of emotional responses, from motivation and perceived professional growth to hesitation, concern, or skepticism. These emotional responses emerge from the interaction between technological possibilities and pedagogical realities, shaping how teachers interpret and integrate AI into their practices [22, 33, 60]. This dynamic aligns with recent frameworks on AI-mediated environments, which demonstrate that the effectiveness of AI integration is rarely determined by technological affordances alone, but rather by how internal self-guides and control-value appraisals generate active achievement emotions [38, 65, 86]. Consequently, the integration of AI in Turkish as a foreign language (TFL) is not a simple adoption process but a complex negotiation of pedagogical agency and emotional adaptation.

Understanding teachers’ perceptions, experiences, and emotional responses is critical, as these directly influence instructional practices and learner outcomes. This study moves beyond a descriptive account of AI use by framing teachers’ experiences within Pekrun’s [61] Control Value Theory (CVT). From this perspective, teachers’ emotional responses to AI can be understood in terms of their perceived control over instructional processes and the value they attribute to AI in supporting pedagogical goals. Positive emotions such as motivation and perceived usefulness reflect higher levels of control and value, whereas negative emotions such as fear and suspicion may arise when teachers perceive limited control or potential threats to professional autonomy and human-centered instruction.

As AI systems are increasingly integrated into classrooms, teachers experience a wide spectrum of these emotions. Positive emotions, such as excitement, enjoyment, and hope, frequently emerge when teachers perceive AI as a valuable tool that automates routine tasks, provides superior scaffolding, and enhances personalized learning [41, 51, 63]. Conversely, educators also encounter negative emotions, including anxiety, frustration, and fear of being replaced or losing their pedagogical authority due to technological complexities [51, 63, 72, 81]. Ultimately, recognizing and systematically addressing both the positive and negative emotional states of teachers is of paramount importance, as these emotions directly dictate their technology acceptance, instructional effectiveness, and professional well-being in an AI-enhanced educational landscape [28, 51, 81].

This study aims to explore Turkish teachers’ experiences and emotions regarding AI, as well as the contributions and challenges of these technologies in teaching. By documenting these experiences, the study provides insights into both the pedagogical benefits and potential limitations of AI in TFL instruction, offering practical guidance for educators and informing future research. Examining teachers’ motivations to use AI, the impact on instructional processes, and encountered challenges can help fill existing gaps and guide policy development for more effective and inclusive use of AI-based technologies in language education. By investigating the AI perceptions of teachers within the context of TFL, this research contributes to the global discourse on AI-assisted language education. It explains how future educators in morphologically complex and digitally less dominant language contexts navigate the technical challenges and emotional changes brought by rapid AI integration.

Literature Review

AI, L2 and Teachers

AI is increasingly affecting second language (L2) teaching and learning, offering new ways to improve instruction and overcome common challenges. AI can transform L2 education by enabling personalized learning [78, 85], providing timely feedback [27, 35], and supporting language skill development through innovative technologies [45]. After the COVID-19 pandemic, the use of AI tools in L2 education increased, encouraging educators to adopt digital technologies for remote and blended learning [52, 55]. Tools like chatbots and AI tutors can create realistic language practice situations and make learning more engaging [3].

However, relying too much on AI may reduce real human interaction, and issues like data privacy, inaccurate feedback, or limited access to technology can create challenges. When used alongside traditional teaching, AI can still greatly support both teachers and learners in language classrooms. Teachers are on the front lines of integrating AI in education, and their understanding of AI, as users and educators, is crucial for the success of AI-enhanced learning [76]. AI technology is creating new roles and responsibilities for teachers. Traditional teaching roles are changing as AI takes over routine tasks such as assessment and content delivery. Teachers are increasingly focusing on guidance, mentoring, and designing creative learning environments. This shift requires educators to collaborate with AI to enhance their pedagogical practices. In language teaching, developing digital literacy skills related to AI use is especially critical for teachers to keep pace with this transformation. However, the growing role of AI in L2 teaching also raises concerns. Teachers may feel anxious about job security and professional competence, which can lead to resistance toward these technologies [66, 87]. Excessive use of AI tools in education could reduce human interaction between teachers and students.

This study is grounded in Pekrun’s [61] CVT in order to make sense of participants’ emotional responses to AI tools. According to this theory, achievement- and activity-related emotions are primarily shaped by two cognitive appraisals: control (the individual’s perceived influence over actions and outcomes) and value (the intrinsic and extrinsic importance of the activity or its outcomes for the individual). Therefore, it is important for teachers to balance the advantages of AI with pedagogical values to maintain a human-centered approach to language learning. AI can facilitate teaching and transform learning experiences, but its effective and sustainable use requires developing teachers’ digital skills [39, 83], enhancing awareness of pedagogical roles, and preserving human interaction. In summary, while AI offers transformative efficiency, the teacher’s role is shifting toward a critical mediator who must balance digital literacy with pedagogical values.

The exploration of teachers’ emotional responses to technology is vital, as emotions significantly influence pedagogical practices. Drawing on foundational frameworks [10, 31], teacher emotions are understood not merely as internal psychological states but as socially and politically constructed responses to their educational environments. In the context of technology-supported language learning, students can experience a wide range of emotions ranging from anxiety to enjoyment [21]. These emotions are a fundamental determinant of the classroom environment and directly impact instructional effectiveness/learning success [25, 32]. Similarly, in their study, Wang et al., [74] identified three types of learner beliefs toward AI-mediated language learning optimistic, critical, and hesitant indicating that learners hold both positive and cautious attitudes toward AI use. Therefore, understanding both the emotional states and the associated cognitive and motivational evaluations is critical when introducing new AI technologies. These dimensions can influence how teachers perceive the usefulness and trustworthiness of AI tools, as well as their willingness to integrate them into their instructional practices. Ultimately, understanding the social and cognitive construction of teacher emotions is vital for predicting the long-term sustainability of AI integration in language classrooms.

Despite the growing body of work on AI in L2, the specific context of Turkish language with complex agglutinative morphology remains largely absent from these global discussions. This makes understanding teachers’ experiences, including the opportunities, challenges, and emotional responses associated with AI, particularly important. To address this gap, the present qualitative study aims to explore teachers’ perspectives on the use of AI tools in teaching TFL. Specifically, the study examines the role of AI tools in Turkish language instruction, how teachers utilize these tools, the challenges they encounter, and their emotional experiences throughout the process. The main goal of the study is to provide recommendations for the more effective use of AI tools in Turkish language teaching. The study seeks to answer the following research questions:

Method

The recruitment and data collection period for this study started on 17/03/2025 and ended on 28/03/2025. This study was reviewed and approved by the Yıldız Technical University Ethics Committee (Approval No: 20250204338; Verification Code: acc31; Date: 05/02/2025) prior to the commencement of data collection. All participants provided written informed consent before participating in the study.

Research Design

The study was designed as a qualitative exploratory case study aiming to gain an in-depth understanding of teachers’ perceptions, experiences, and emotional responses regarding the use of AI tools in teaching TFL. Case studies are a strategy of inquiry in which the researcher explores in depth a program, event, activity, process, or one or more individuals [67]. This design was preferred in the present study as the use of AI in teaching TFL is an emerging and under-researched area, requiring a detailed exploration of teachers’ experiences, perceptions, and emotional responses in a natural instructional context.

Participants

In this study, teachers using AI tools in the field of TFL were selected through purposive sampling. Purposive sampling is a non-probability sampling technique in which participants are intentionally selected based on specific characteristics that are relevant to the research purpose, enabling the researcher to obtain rich and information-rich data [59]. This method is particularly appropriate in qualitative research when the aim is to explore a phenomenon in depth rather than to generalize findings to a larger population. In line with this approach, two main criteria guided the selection of participants: first, participants voluntarily agreed to take part in the study after being informed about its aims and procedures; second, they had prior experience in using AI tools in language teaching. These criteria ensured that the participants could provide meaningful insights into the research topic.

Participants were recruited through professional TFL networks and social media groups dedicated to educational technology. The sample size of 17 was determined based on the principle of “theoretical saturation.” Following Guest et al., [26], saturation in qualitative interviews often occurs within the first 12–15 interviews; in this study, no new dimensions or emotional categories emerged after the 14th interview, confirming that 17 participants provided sufficient depth. There were 4 male and 13 female participants, ranging in age from 23 to 40. Their academic backgrounds included Turkish language teaching (9), Turkish language and literature (2), and teaching Turkish to foreigners (6). The participants’ teaching experience was categorized as 1–2 years (3), 2–4 years (6), and over 5 years (8). Their academic qualifications were distributed as follows: Master’s degree (11) and PhD degree (6). Detailed information on all participants is presented in the tables below:

Instrument

Semi-Structured Interview

​​To understand teachers’ perceptions of using AI technologies in teaching TFL, as well as the opportunities these technologies offer and the challenges they encounter, the researchers employed a semi-structured interview method. This method was chosen because it allows for a detailed exploration of teachers’ perceptions and experiences. The interviews were conducted in Turkish, and after collecting demographic information, six open-ended questions were asked. The interviews were conducted face-to-face, and audio recordings were made for subsequent thematic analyses. To ensure environmental consistency, interviews were conducted in quiet, neutral settings such as university faculty lounges or private offices at the participants’ respective institutions across six different universities. All 17 interviews were conducted by the primary researcher to maintain stylistic consistency and rapport. Each interview lasted approximately 20–25 min. Participants were encouraged to elaborate on their responses, and the interviews were conducted in a friendly and relaxed atmosphere to ensure that participants could express their ideas freely.

The semi-structured interview protocol was specifically developed to align with the conceptual focus of the study, grounded in Pekrun’s [61] CVT. To ensure instrument conceptual alignment, the questions were designed to capture the three core pillars of the theory:

Operational Context (Q1): The first question identifies the AI tools and their purposes, establishing the baseline for the participants’ technological and pedagogical agency.Value Appraisals (Q2 & Q4): Questions 2 and 4 focus on the perceived value of AI. They explore the pedagogical opportunities and contributions to student learning, reflecting the intrinsic and extrinsic importance teachers assign to AI integration.Control Appraisals (Q3): Questions 3 examines the control dimension. It addresses challenges (technical/pedagogical barriers), and introduces a comparative linguistic perspective (Turkish vs. English). This comparison specifically probes the “perceived control” teachers feel when using AI for a morphologically complex language like Turkish compared to a digitally dominant one like English.Emotional Outcomes (Q5): The final question directly operationalizes the affective domain. It prompts teachers to synthesize their experiences into specific emotional responses (e.g., Hope vs. Fear) and links these emotions to their professional motivation, as suggested by the CVT framework.

The initial version of the interview questions was reviewed by two experts in teaching TFL and educational technology to ensure content relevance and clarity. Following expert feedback, minor revisions were made, and one item was removed. The revised interview protocol was pilot-tested with three instructors to confirm its appropriateness and comprehensibility. The final English version of the interview questions is provided as Appendix A.

Data Collection Procedure

The data for this qualitative study were collected through face-to-face semi-structured interviews with a sample of 17 university instructors teaching TFL. At the outset of the research, a set of interview questions was developed and reviewed by two experts for relevance and content validity. The experts suggested some revisions, and one irrelevant item was removed. Subsequently, a pilot study was conducted with three TFL instructors representing the study population. After confirming the appropriateness of the interview questions, participants were invited to take part in the data collection process through face-to-face interviews. The interviews were conducted outside participants’ working hours at pre-arranged convenient times. With participants’ consent, interviews were audio-recorded and later transcribed and prepared for analysis. The researchers adhered strictly to ethical considerations, including confidentiality, protection of participants’ information, and the freedom to participate or withdraw from the study, and the purpose of the study was clearly explained to all participants. The collected data were carefully organized and prepared for the analysis process.

Data Analysis

In this study, the interview data were carefully transcribed by the researchers prior to analysis. The audio recordings and transcripts were reviewed for accuracy and consistency. A thematic analysis was then conducted to identify the perceived positive and negative aspects of AI-supported TFL instruction, as well as the challenges encountered. The data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis [14], moving beyond a simple descriptive summary toward an interpretive synthesis. The analysis followed an inductive approach, allowing themes to emerge directly from the data without being forced into pre-existing categories, although the final interpretation was informed by CVT. The process followed six phases: (1) Familiarization with data, (2) Generating 48 initial codes (e.g., “grammar inaccuracy,” “time efficiency,” “loss of human touch”), (3) Searching for themes, (4) Reviewing potential themes, (5) Defining and naming themes, and (6) Producing the report. To ensure a systematic and transparent analytical process, the entire coding and thematic mapping procedure was managed using MAXQDA 24 qualitative data analysis software.

The reliability of the findings was ensured through a multi-layered verification process. First, inter-coder reliability was established with an independent expert, yielding a Cohen’s Kappa coefficient of .76, which indicates substantial agreement [47]. Following this independent coding phase, the researchers conducted "negotiated agreement" sessions. During these meetings, all researchers collectively reviewed the codes and themes to resolve any interpretive discrepancies through peer debriefing. This collaborative process functioned as "researcher triangulation", ensuring that the final themes especially the nuanced emotional categories were the result of a collective consensus rather than individual bias. Furthermore, member checking was performed by providing five participants with the extracted themes to confirm the "interpretive validity" of the findings. Finally, an independent researcher audited the entire process to ensure methodological transparency.

In qualitative research, the researcher is the primary instrument of data collection and analysis. The authors acknowledge their positionality as academics within the field of TFL with a professional interest in educational technology. To mitigate potential researcher bias and ensure that the findings reflect the participants’ true experiences rather than the authors’ preconceptions, a collaborative reflexive approach was adopted. Instead of a single-researcher interpretation, the authors engaged in researcher triangulation. During the coding and theme-generation phases, the researchers held regular peer debriefing sessions to discuss emerging findings. Any discrepancies in the interpretation of emotional responses (e.g., distinguishing between "Fear" and "Suspicion") were resolved through negotiated agreement. This collective deliberation process served as a check against individual biases, ensuring a balanced representation of both the “Hope” and “Fear” dimensions in the data.

As shown in Table 11, teachers’ responses to AI tools in TFL reflect a range of emotional reactions. The findings indicate that positive emotions are more prominent than negative ones. A large proportion of participants reported feeling motivational (42.31%) and developmental/beneficial (34.62%), suggesting that AI tools are perceived as encouraging and professionally enriching. Additionally, some teachers described AI as supportive (7.69%), further highlighting its positive contribution to teaching practices. At the same time, a smaller group of participants expressed negative emotions such as fear (7.69%) and suspicion (3.85%), indicating that AI integration may still evoke concerns related to uncertainty, reliability, or over-reliance on technology. A limited number of participants also reported feeling relaxed (3.85%), suggesting that AI tools can, for some teachers, reduce instructional pressure and facilitate the teaching process. Overall, the findings suggest that teachers’ emotional responses toward AI in TFL are predominantly positive, while still including certain reservations. This distribution reflects a nuanced emotional landscape in which enthusiasm and perceived benefits coexist with cautious attitudes. As reflected in the participants’ statements, teachers emphasize the importance of maintaining a balance between traditional teaching methods and AI-supported practices. For instance, Participant 5 stated, “It positively influenced my motivation to teach Turkish. However, it feels like relying solely on AI in teaching, forgetting to try methods other than technology in language teaching, is not a good approach. Maintaining a balance between traditional teaching methods and newly introduced teaching methods is very important.” Similarly, Participant 14 noted, “Thanks to AI, I believe that both my students’ and my own interest and motivation levels have increased because I can create a more diverse learning environment. I think it takes the monotony out of the lesson and extends the attention span.”

The participants’ previous professional experience significantly influenced their pedagogical and emotional engagement with AI. Novice teachers (1–2 years of experience) primarily viewed AI as a “substitute” for routine tasks like material preparation, which contributed to high “Motivational” (42.31%) ratings due to increased instructional efficiency. In contrast, experienced educators (over 5 years) tended to perceive AI as a tool for “augmentation,” often expressing more “Suspicion” (3.85%) or “Fear” (7.69%) regarding the potential loss of cultural nuance and human-centered instruction. As noted by Participant 5, these veteran teachers prioritize a balance between traditional and AI-supported methods, suggesting that while novices find “hope” in AI’s functional utility, experienced teachers evaluate the technology based on its ability to preserve the pedagogical integrity of the TFL context.

Discussion

Addressing the aim of the study, the findings indicate that AI integration in TFL involves both significant opportunities and notable challenges. Drawing on Pekrun’s [61] CVT, this study examines how teachers interpret these opportunities and challenges. The findings suggest that AI integration is not merely a technical implementation process, but a deeply emotional and linguistically situated negotiation shaped by teachers’ perceptions of control and value. This establishes the theoretical contribution of the present study.

While previous research has highlighted the multidimensional nature of AI use in education [9, 29, 75], our data extends this literature by proving that in LCTL contexts, teachers’ emotional responses are uniquely shaped by the alignment between AI capabilities and target language morphology. This suggests that AI adoption must be understood not only technologically but also affectively.

In response to RQ1, teachers can integrate educational technology to promote learners’ independent skill development, provided that they possess the necessary knowledge and expertise to use it effectively. Participants reported that AI tools facilitated teaching by easing the preparation of instructional materials, supporting assessment processes, and enhancing learner engagement. For example, platforms such as ChatGPT, Duolingo, and Gemini were highlighted as frequently used tools, consistent with previous studies emphasizing the role of AI in facilitating educational tasks and fostering interactive learning environments [1, 17]. However, interpreting these findings reveals a significant epistemological shift: AI is no longer merely an external administrative support tool but has become a “pedagogical co-instructor.” This pattern emerges because generative AI fulfills a critical gap in foreign language instruction by addressing the need for dynamic and personalized content that standard textbooks frequently lack [16, 43, 49]. This shift gives teachers newfound creative autonomy, requiring them to make ongoing judgments about when and how AI-generated content aligns with their instructional goals [4, 7, 34, 50]. Therefore, the significance of RQ1 lies in understanding how teachers negotiate their professional agency. AI integration is reshaping not only classroom practices but the very nature of teaching itself, transforming teachers from passive consumers of curricula into active, dynamic knowledge creators.

Regarding RQ2, the findings indicate that the use of AI tools in TFL offers significant opportunities in instructional contexts. The importance of integrating technologies into L2 education has garnered increasing attention, highlighting the need for educators to become digitally literate ([23]; Wang, [73]). These findings confirm that AI integration is a pedagogical transformation requiring active teacher engagement [15]. AI reduces the rigidity of pre-planned instruction, allowing teachers to engage in rapid, context-sensitive decision-making [13, 42]. The motivating emotions observed in participants stem from this enhanced pedagogical control. However, this increased flexibility simultaneously introduces a demand for higher levels of pedagogical awareness, as teachers should critically evaluate the relevance, accuracy, and appropriateness of AI-generated content.

Technical difficulties, English-dominant applications, occasional inaccuracies in AI responses, and limitations in assessing writing skills were among the most frequently cited issues. Another notable challenge was the transmission of cultural elements, which is particularly significant for TFL instruction where language and culture are inextricably linked. AI tools sometimes failed to adequately convey Turkish cultural nuances. Teachers’ emotional responses reflect an ongoing recalibration of control and value judgments in response to evolving technological affordances.

In relation to RQ3, the findings reveal that AI functions as a complex pedagogical tool presenting specific, language-bound challenges. According to Derakhshan and Ghiasvand [20], the use of ChatGPT in L2 education involves both benefits and limitations. The specific challenges reported in our study such as the failure to convey Turkish cultural nuances and grammatical errors suggest that AI tools carry an “English-centric bias.” For TFL teachers, this creates a “linguistically-conditioned digital divide” not present in dominant languages. This finding nuances and extends the work of Octavio et al., [56], demonstrating that standard prompt-engineering skills are insufficient in LCTLs; TFL teachers could exercise continuous linguistic-cultural editing to mitigate AI errors. Although AI integration has been widely studied, this research shows that its effects differ in Turkish because of its language structure. Therefore, AI-based teaching approaches need to be adapted rather than directly transferred across languages.

Addressing RQ4, evaluating how AI contributes to students’ language learning, Pokrivčáková [62] noted that AI technologies can enhance personalization, flexibility, and inclusivity. Participants reported that students’ listening, speaking, reading, and writing skills improved through the use of AI-mediated exercises, personalized feedback, and interactive activities. These findings align with research suggesting that AI-based language applications enhance learner autonomy and provide individualized learning pathways [53, 85]. Moreover, the capacity of AI to create engaging, fun, and interactive learning experiences was seen as motivating both for students and instructors in language classrooms [18]. Furthermore, incorporating AI-mediated interactions effectively enhances EFL learners’ willingness to communicate [24, 30, 40, 82]. Our findings confirm these insights, but we argue that achieving these positive outcomes in TFL is heavily mediated by the teacher’s ability to filter out the cultural discrepancies identified in RQ3.

With respect to RQ5, this study explicitly links emotional dimensions, control-value appraisals, and TFL. While most participants described AI as motivating (“Hope”), some expressed that it can be scary and suspicious (“Fear”). Grounding the results in Pekrun’s [61] CVT, we interpret the high level of “Motivation” (42.31%) as a reflection of high control-value appraisals: when teachers save time and execute creative tasks effortlessly, they perceive higher control, triggering “Hope.” Conversely, the “Scary” (7.69%) and “Suspicion” (3.85%) findings are analytically linked to the loss of control experienced when English-centric AI models hallucinate Turkish grammar or miss cultural subtleties. These findings offer a core theoretical contribution by demonstrating that perceived control in AI-mediated language teaching is not solely a matter of digital literacy but is instead strongly linguistically conditioned. In low-resource language contexts such as Turkish, teachers’ sense of control is directly shaped by the alignment or misalignment between AI systems and the morphological structure of the target language. Consequently, this perspective challenges studies that frame AI integration merely through the lens of general technology acceptance [6, 11, 64] by demonstrating that for TFL educators, perceived linguistic accuracy rather than perceived ease of use is the primary driver of emotional appraisals.

This suggests that both “Hope” and “Fear” function as rational and evaluative responses to specific technological limitations rather than being abstract psychological states. The emotional landscape identified in this research indicates that teachers’ motivation is not a product of uninformed tech-optimism; instead, the reported fear and suspicion reflect a critical awareness of AI’s current lack of linguistic fidelity. Ultimately, these findings demonstrate that AI integration in TFL is an emotionally and linguistically situated negotiation that requires analytical agency to balance traditional human-centered instruction with AI-mediated efficiency.

While earlier studies emphasized the need for digital literacy to reduce anxiety [66, 77], our findings suggest that AI-pedagogical integrity the ability to maintain human-centered instruction. The findings show that teacher motivation is not just about accepting technology, but also about how well AI fits the language and cultural characteristics of Turkish. Ultimately, the successful future of AI in TFL depends on shifting from seeing AI as a replacement to viewing it as a pedagogical affordance that requires constant human oversight, linguistic sensitivity, and a high-level analytical mindset.

Conclusion

This study explored TFL teachers’ use of AI by focusing on the opportunities, challenges, and emotional responses identified in the findings. The results show that AI is used as a supportive tool in instructional processes, particularly in material preparation, learner engagement, and skill development. At the same time, several challenges were identified, including linguistic inaccuracies, limitations in reflecting cultural elements, and the dominance of English-based systems. In addition, teachers reported a range of emotional responses, from motivation and professional growth to fear and suspicion, reflecting how they interpret and manage AI within their teaching contexts.

Despite these contributions, the study has certain limitations. This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the sample was limited to 17 instructors working in universities in Istanbul, which may restrict the generalizability of the results to other regions or educational contexts. Second, the study focused solely on teachers’ perspectives, without including student experiences or learning outcomes, which limits the understanding of the full impact of AI tools on language acquisition. Third, the use of self-reported data through interviews may be subject to social desirability or recall bias. Finally, the study examined a specific set of AI tools and applications, so findings may not fully reflect experiences with emerging or alternative technologies.

Future research could expand this line of inquiry by including larger and more diverse samples, incorporating classroom-based observations, and examining student perspectives. Further studies may also explore how different AI tools function in LCTL and how teachers can be better supported in adapting AI to linguistically and culturally specific contexts.

Implications

The findings of this study highlight several important implications for integrating AI into the teaching of LCTL, with Turkish as a representative case.

Professional development should extend beyond basic technical skills and also address teachers’ emotional and cognitive needs. The tension between optimism and concern toward AI needs to be acknowledged and managed. When teachers understand how AI functions within frameworks such as control-value theory [61] they are more likely to feel confident and maintain a sense of control, especially when using tools that are not fully aligned with the structural features of their language.

Due to the predominantly English-centered nature of current AI systems, the use of AI in Turkish and other LCTL contexts requires active teacher mediation. Rather than functioning as an independent source of instruction, AI should be positioned as a supportive tool that is guided and monitored by the teacher.

In morphologically complex languages like Turkish, AI use should emphasize strategic enhancement rather than direct substitution. Its value lies in supporting higher-order learning processes, such as generating personalized vocabulary activities or simulating authentic communication contexts, while teachers remain critically aware of its limitations in low-resource language settings.

There is also a clear need for stronger collaboration between language educators and AI developers. Insights from teachers working in LCTL contexts can contribute to the development of more inclusive and linguistically responsive AI systems. Such collaboration is essential to reduce the digital gap and to ensure that AI-supported language education serves not only dominant languages but also the wider diversity of global language learning contexts.
