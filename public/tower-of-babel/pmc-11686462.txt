From Dielectric to Electro-Responsive Thermoplastics: An Approach Based on Electro-Thermal Reorientation and Charged Gas Activation
Authors: Merve Gul, Adrian Fontana-Escartín, Marc Arnau, Jordi Sans, Sonia Lanzalaco, Elaine Armelin, Enrica Chiesa, Ida Genta, Maria M. Pérez-Madrigal, Carlos Alemán
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The transition from insulator to electro-responsive has been successfully achieved by earlier studies for some inorganic materials by applying external stimuli that modify their 3D and/or electronic structures. In the case of insulating polymers, this transition is frequently accomplished by mixing them with other electroactive materials, even though a few physical treatments that induce suitable chemical modifications have also been reported. In this work, a smart approach based on the application of an electro-thermal reorientation process followed by a charged gas activation treatment has been developed for transforming insulating 3D printed polymers into electro-responsive materials. First, the developed procedure has been exhaustively investigated for 3D printed poly(lactic acid) (PLA) and subsequently has been extended to 3D printed polypropylene (PP) and poly(ethylene terephthalate glycol) (PETG) specimens. FTIR and Raman spectroscopies, X-ray diffraction, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, scanning electron microscopy, and water contact angle measurements confirmed that, while the electro-thermal reorientation mainly promotes the crystallinity of the samples, the charged gas activation oxidizes the C–O bonds at the surface and consequently modifies the surface morphology and wettability. Furthermore, cyclic voltammetry assays demonstrated that treated PLA, PP, and PETG are electro-responsive, even though the electrochemical activity was much higher for oxygen-containing polymers (PLA and PETG) than for the oxygen-free one (PP). Finally, as a proof of concept, treated 3D printed PLA specimens have been used as electrochemical sensors to detect dopamine (DA), an important neurotransmitter, in a concentration interval ranging from 50 to 1000 μM. The peak associated with the oxidation from DA to dopaminoquinone and the linearity of the calibration plot, which was constructed using the anodic peak current, proved that treated PLA is not only electro-responsive but also able to electrocatalyze the oxidation of DA.

Introduction

The transition of insulating materials to electro-responsive ones is becoming a topic of increasing interest.1−7 Persistent changes in the electric behavior of several inorganic materials, such as vanadium dioxide (VO2),1,2 gallium trioxide (Ga2O3),3 hydroxyapatite,4−6 and metal–polymer composites,7 have been obtained applying external thermal, electrical, or light stimuli, or even a combination of them. Although in general the resistance of such materials was tuned from 2 to 4 orders of magnitude,1−6 in some cases the change in the resistance extended up to 8 orders of magnitude,7 which is impressive.

Different strategies have also been followed to obtain electro-responsive systems using insulating polymers. For example, electro-responsive polymer composites are frequently obtained combining insulating polymers with intrinsically conducting fillers, for example carbon nanotubes,8−10 graphene oxide,11−13 metallic nanoparticles,14,15 and MXene (2D titanium carbide nanosheets).16−18 Besides, the combination of insulating polymers with intrinsically conducting polymers has produced electro-responsive systems, for example multilayered films,19−21 semi-interpenetrated hydrogels,22−24 and fibers.25−27

In addition to the strategies previously mentioned to produce electro-responsive polymer composites and blends,8−27 some physical treatments have been also reported. In particular, the application of atmospheric corona discharge plasma was found to transform insulating polyethylene28−30 and polypropylene30 into electro-responsive conducting materials able to act as mediators in the detection of glucose and dopamine. In other examples, 3D printed poly(lactic acid) (PLA), polypropylene, thermoplastic polyurethane, and polyethylene terephthalate glycol were transformed into conducting materials by applying a low pressure oxygen plasma using very controlled operational conditions.31−33

In this work, we propose a smart procedure that combines electro-thermal reorientation with charged gas activation for transforming dielectric polymers processed by 3D printing into electro-responsive materials. It is worth mentioning that the choice of 3D printing is due not only to the increasing use of this manufacturing technique in the industrial sector but also to the detrimental effect on the mechanical properties exerted by treatments based on the application of activated of charged gases (e.g., atmospheric plasma and low-pressure plasma) on micro and nanometric samples (e.g., nano and microfilms).28−31 Thus, 3D printing allows users to obtain not only identical and reproducible samples with dimensions adapted to the measurement setup but also samples with sufficient entity to receive an aggressive surface treatment without suffering any damage. The proposed transformative process has been studied in detail for PLA, which is an insulating and therefore non-electro-responsive biopolymer derived from nontoxic renewable feedstock.34 The choice of this specific thermoplastic was also motivated by its well-known high biocompatibility, useful for biomedical applications.35−37 However, to demonstrate the extrapolation of our approach to other dielectrics, the transformation from insulator to conductor of polypropylene (PP) and polyethylene terephthalate glycol (PETG) has also been studied, as will be demonstrated by electrochemical assays. Finally, the potential of the obtained electro-responsive materials has been proven using one of them as an electrochemical biosensor to detect dopamine (DA).

Methods

Materials

Filaments of thermoplastics, which were purchased from BCN3D (Spain), included PLA (Mn = 133,000; Mw/Mn = 1.94; supplied by MCPP Netherlands BV, Helmond, The Netherlands), PP (Mn = 40,000; Mw/Mn = 8.6; Ultrafuse PP, supplied by BASF 3D Printing Solutions BV, Heidelberg, Germany), and PETG (Mn = 40,000; Mw/Mn = 2.1; supplied by MCPP Netherlands BV). Dopamine (DA) neurotransmitter and phosphate buffered saline (PBS) solution, which was used as an electrolyte for electrochemical assays, were also purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

3D Printing

PLA samples were printed with a BCN3D SIGMAX R19 3D printer device (BCN3D, Barcelona, Spain) by using PLA filaments. A brass nozzle with an inner tip diameter of 0.3 mm was used for all of the samples, while the printing parameters were set as follows: printing temperature between 220 and 240 °C, depending on the filament; printing speed, 30 mm/s; filling density, 100%. The samples were designed with Cura Software, and PLA filaments were shaped into discs with a diameter and a thickness of 13 and 0.5 mm, respectively. All printed samples were cleaned with deionized water three times for removing the microresidues before any further treatment.

Electro-Thermal Reorientation

The electro-thermal reorientation treatment was applied using a furnace and a power voltage supply simultaneously. PLA samples were placed between two stainless steel (AISI 304) electrodes placed in a ceramic holder inside the furnace (Carbolite ELF11/6B/301). The steel electrodes were connected through Pt wires to an external power voltage supply, which applied a voltage to the samples heated at a predefined temperature. Considering the glass transition temperature, the melting temperature, and the decomposition temperature of PLA (Tg = 60 °C, Tm = 150 °C, and Td = 290–380 °C, respectively), the furnace was preheated at 100 °C for 10 min. Then, a voltage of 500 V was applied for 1 h, maintaining the temperature at 100 °C. Finally, samples were cooled for 10 min at room temperature. The same operational parameters were applied when the technology was extended to PP and PETG samples.

Charged Gas Activation

For the charged gas activation treatment, PLA, PP, or PETG samples were placed within a vacuum-purged chamber that was first evacuated and subsequently filled with oxygen until reaching a pressure of 0.8 mbar. After that, an electric discharge was applied for 2 min using a power of 100 W.

Surface and Chemical Characterization

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) studies were performed in a Focused Ion Beam Zeiss Neon 40 microscope equipped with a SEM GEMINI column with Shottky field emission and an EDX spectroscopy system. Samples were sputter-coated with a thin layer of carbon to prevent sample charging problems and were immobilized over metallic pins with carbon based stickers.

Water contact angle (WCA) measurements were performed by using a Kruss GmbH device. The sessile drop method was employed for all samples at room temperature and humidity. Raw data was processed using the software ADVANCE. For reproducibility, the average WCA was obtained by considering at least 10 independent samples for each material.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) intensities of untreated and treated PLA samples were collected using a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer with a one-dimensional Lynx Eye detector and monochromatized Cu–Kα radiation. The 2θ measurement ranged from 5° to 60°.

FTIR spectra were recorded on an FTIR Jasco 4100 spectrophotometer through an attenuated total reflection accessory (top-plate) with a diamond crystal (Specac model MKII Golden Gate Heated Single Reflection Diamond ATR). Samples were evaluated using spectra manager software, and for each sample, 64 scans were performed between 4000 and 600 cm–1 with a resolution of 4 cm–1. FTIR spectra were normalized with respect to the most intense band (C=O stretching).

Raman spectra were obtained using the inVia Qontor confocal Raman microscope (Renishaw) equipped with a Renishaw Centrus 2957T2 detector and a 785 nm laser. The exposure time was 0.5 s, the laser power was adjusted to 1% of its nominal output power, and each spectrum was collected with six accumulations. Raman spectra were normalized with respect to the most intense band (874 nm).

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses were performed on a SPECS Surface Nano Analysis system (GMbH, Berlin, Germany). The spectrometer was equipped with a high-intensity twin-anode X-ray source XR50 of Mg/Al (1253 eV/1487 eV) operating with the Al anode at 150 W, placed perpendicular to the analyzer axis, and using a Phoibos 150 MCD-9 XP detector. The position of the stage was digitally controlled to ensure that the spot was in the same exact place during the whole treatment. The pass energy of the hemispherical analyzer was set at 25 eV, while the energy step of high resolution spectra was set at 0.1 eV. The pressure in the analysis chamber was always below 10–7 Pa, and binding energy (BE) values were referred to the C 1s peak at 284.5 eV. Data were processed with CasaXPS software (Casa Software, Ltd., UK).

Electrochemical Characterization

For electrochemical characterization assays, 3D printed PLA specimens consisted of discs 13 mm in diameter and 0.5 mm in thickness. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) assays were performed with an Autolab VIONIC (Metrohm) potentiostat–galvanostat, which was controlled with Intello software. A standard three-electrode cell was used to evaluate the electrochemical response of the studied samples. The untreated and treated 3D printed samples were employed as the working electrode (WE), while the counter electrode (CE) and reference electrode (RE) were a platinum wire and Ag | AgCl (KCl, 3 M) electrode, respectively. The electrochemical cell was filled with 5 mL of a phosphate buffered saline solution (PBS, pH 7.4), which was used as an electrolytic medium. The initial and final potentials were −0.90 V, while the reverse potential was +0.90 V. In general, the scan rate was fixed at 100 mV/s, even though some specific experiments were performed using other scan rates, as is explicitly indicated in the next section. For the observation of the reproducibility of results, three independent samples of each specimen were tested. All of the assays were performed at room temperature.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was carried out to evaluate the bulk resistance changes of PLA 3D printed discs. For it, three sets of materials were approached: (i) untreated PLA (control); (ii) samples treated applying charged gas activation and electro-thermal reorientation; and (iii) samples treated applying electro-thermal reorientation and charged gas activation (i.e., reverse treatment with respect to (ii)). The experiments were conducted in a three-electrode cell configuration using a VIONIC (Metrohm) potentiostat–galvanostat equipped with INTELLO software (Autolab). The electrolyte was the same as that used in CV assays. The operating conditions were fixed to a sinusoidal voltage of 10 mV of amplitude within a frequency range between 100 MHz and 0.1 Hz. PLA 3D printed discs (untreated or treated), with reduced height (0.5 mm), were used as the WE (area of 1.33 cm2) and were previously sputtered with a conductive layer of Au on the back side before analysis. Pt wire was employed as the CE, whereas Ag | AgCl (KCl 3M) was used as the RE. After data collection, EIS results were then processed and fit to an electrical equivalent circuit (EEC) by using the INTELLO software. At least 3–4 replicates for each sample were tested to ensure reproducibility.

Electrochemical Detection of Dopamine

The electrochemical detection of DA was studied by CV using a three-electrode cell and an Autolab VIONIC potentiostat–galvanostat. In this case, 3D printed PLA specimens consisted of rectangular parallelepipeds of size 7.0 × 2.0 × 2.0 mm3 with one embedded well of 7.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm3 printed on a face with the smallest area (2.0 × 2.0 mm2) and a small protuberance, also with rectangular parallelepiped shape (1.5 × 1.5 × 1.0 mm3), at the opposite face (Figure 1a). After the corresponding treatment(s) (i.e., electro-thermal reorientation and/or charged gas activation), one of the faces with the largest area (7.0 × 2.0 mm2) was coated with a Kapton tape disc (13 mm in diameter and 0.5 mm in thickness) to improve the voltammetric signal (Figure 1b). Finally, the potentiostat–galvanostat was connected to the WE by using a glassy carbon rod, which was introduced in the cavity of the printed specimen (Figure 1c). The glassy carbon rod acted as a simple electrical connector and was never in contact with the electrolytic medium (i.e., the printed protuberance avoided contact between the connector and the electrolytic medium), as is sketched in Figure 1d. The CE and RE were identical to those described above for electrochemical characterization.

Results and Discussion

The procedures used to modify PLA filaments are summarized in Figure 1e, while details are displayed in the Methods section. 3D printed discs were subjected to two different approaches that only differ in the order in which we apply the two processes to transform the dielectric material into a conductor. In the first one, the discs were subjected to the electrothermal (ET) reorientation, and subsequently, the charged gas activation (CA) treatment was applied to the resulting material. Hereafter, discs obtained by using this approach are denoted PLA(ET-CA). In the second approach, the charged gas activation, which was applied first, was followed by electro-thermal reorientation, the resulting discs being denoted PLA(CA-ET).

Surface and Chemical Characterization of PLA Treated Discs

Morphological characterization of PLA, PLA(ET-CA), and PLA(CA-ET) surfaces was performed using SEM (Figure 2). As it can be seen, the homogeneous and smooth surface of PLA (Figure 2a) was converted into a heterogeneous rough surface in PLA(ET-CA) (Figure 2b), while the surface of PLA(CA-ET) (Figure 2c) was similar to that of untreated PLA. These features suggest that the charged gas activation treatment has a higher impact on the morphology of PLA than the electro-thermal reorientation process, as is confirmed in Figures S1a and S1b, which display the surface of morphology of PLA discs subjected to charged gas activation and electro-thermal reorientation alone (hereafter those specimens are denoted PLA(CA) and PLA(ET), respectively). In fact, results suggest that both treatments affect the surface roughness. The charged gas activation increases the surface roughness, while the electro-thermal reorientation decreases it, although the effect of the latter treatment is more pronounced than that for former ones.

Surface morphology of (a) PLA, (b) PLA(ET-CA), and (c) PLA(CA-ET) discs.

In addition to the changes in surface morphology, the electro-thermal and charged gas activation processes affected the surface wettability. Comparison of the WCA values determined for untreated PLA, which is a nearly hydrophobic material with WCA = 79.2° ± 3.3°, PLA(ET-CA), PLA(CA-ET), PLA(CA), and PLA(ET) discs are displayed in Figure 3a (numerical values are listed in Table S1). As can be seen, the hydrophilicity of the PLA(CA-ET), and especially PLA(ET-CA), is higher than that of pristine PLA, the WCA decreasing by around 18% and 49%, respectively. This feature is consistent with the morphological changes observed in Figure 2, which were significantly higher for PLA(ET-CA) than for PLA(CA-ET). Furthermore, comparison of the WCA values obtained for PLA(CA) and PLA(ET) confirmed that the effects of the charged gas activation were higher than the ones caused by the electro-thermal process (Table S1).

(a) WCA values of untreated PLA (control), PLA(ET-CA), PLA(CA-ET), PLA(ET), and PLA(CA). (b) XRD patterns of untreated PLA, PLA(ET-CA), and PLA(CA-ET).

The X-ray diffraction pattern of untreated PLA, which is a semicrystalline thermoplastic, exhibits a diffuse hump centered at 2θ ≈ 18° that corresponds to the amorphous phase and a small sharp peak at 2θ = 28.0° that corresponds to the β-crystalline form (Figure 3b).38,39 However, both PLA(ET-CA) and PLA(CA-ET) specimens display intense sharp peaks at 2θ = 16.8° and 19.2°, which have been associated with the diffraction planes (110)/(200) and (203), respectively, which are characteristic of the α-crystalline form. Interestingly, such peaks are slightly more intense for PLA(CA-ET) than for PLA(ET-CA);40 hence the electro-thermal treatment played a crucial role in such structural conversion. This hypothesis was confirmed by the X-ray diffraction patterns of the PLA(ET) and PLA(CA) samples (Figure S2). While PLA(CA) samples display a diffuse hump centered practically identical to that obtained for untreated PLA, the hump disappears in PLA(ET) discs, which indicates the conversion of PLA to the α-crystalline phase.

Normalized FTIR spectra for untreated PLA, PLA(ET-CA), and PLA(CA-ET) are displayed in Figure 4a. All samples exhibit the characteristic peaks of PLA at 2998 and 2948 cm–1 (asymmetric and symmetric C–H stretching of −CH3, respectively), 1749 cm–1 (C=O stretching), 1451 and 1361 cm–1 (−CH3 asymmetric and symmetric bending, respectively), 1182 cm–1 (C–O stretching), 1079 cm–1 (−C–O–C stretching in esters), and 867 and 750 cm–1 (C–C stretching of amorphous and crystalline phases, respectively).41,42 It is worth noting that the intensities of the C–C stretching peaks ascribed to the amorphous and crystalline phases (indicated by green lines in Figure 4a) are similar for untreated PLA, whereas the latter is significantly more intense than the former for PLA(ET-CA) and PLA(CA-ET). This feature indicates that the charged gas activation, and especially the electro-thermal reorientation, promote the crystallinity of the samples, as is proven in the FTIR spectra recorded for PLA(CA) and PLA(ET) (Figure S3). Furthermore, the small shoulder at 1265 cm–1 (brown line in Figure 4a), which is weak for untreated PLA, becomes more intense for PLA(ET-CA) and PLA(CA-ET), suggesting that the charged gas activation process oxidizes the C–O bonds at the surface (Figure S3).

Figure 4b compares the Raman spectra obtained for untreated PLA, PLA(ET-CA), and PLA(CA-ET) discs. Untreated PLA samples show characteristic peaks at 3001–2882 cm–1 (C–H stretching modes of CH3), 1788 cm–1 (C=O groups), 1454 and 1383 cm–1 (asymmetric and symmetric CH3 deformation modes, respectively), 1128 cm–1 (CH3 asymmetric groups), 1094 cm–1 (symmetric C–O–C), 1044 cm–1 (C–CH3 stretching), 874 cm–1 (C–COO vibration), and 742 cm–1 (O–C=O in plane bending).43 The Raman spectra of PLA(ET-CA) and PLA(CA-ET) display additional peaks at 1212 and 916 cm–1 (indicated by purple lines in Figure 4b), which are attributed to changes at the surface of PLA. Inspection of the Raman spectra recorded for PLA(ET) and PLA(CA) indicates that the origin of such peaks is caused by the electro-thermal treatment (Figure S4). As previously investigated,44 the peak located around 1212 cm–1 appears for polarized α′-forms of PLA, illustrating changes in the amorphous and crystalline orientation as a function of processing conditions.

In order to better understand the effects of the charged gas activation and the electro-thermal reorientation on the chemical composition of PLA, XPS analyses were performed for untreated PLA, PLA(ET-CA), PLA(CA-ET), PLA(ET), and PLA(CA) specimens. Table 1 reports the atomic percentage of C and O elements detected by XPS for all of the samples investigated, before and after treatments. The carbon-to-oxygen ratio (C/O) provides a measure of the changes in the composition induced by the different processes. The charged gas activation caused a drastic decrease in the C/O ratio from 1.6 for the untreated PLA to 0.7 for PLA(CA), while this variation was slightly lower for PLA(ET) with a C/O ratio of 0.9. Interestingly, the latter value is preserved for PLA(CA-ET), indicating that the effects of electro-thermal reorientation, which is applied last, prevail over those induced by the charged gas activation treatment, which is applied first. Instead, the C/O ratio decreases to 0.5 for PLA(ET-CA), evidencing the synergistic effects in terms of induced chemical transformation between the electro-thermal reorientation process and the charged gas activation when applied in this order.

In all cases, deconvolution of the C 1s signal led to three contributing curves centered at 284.5, 286.3, and 288.8 eV that were attributed to C–C/C–H bonds, C–O single bonds, and O–C=O bonds, respectively (Figures 5a and S5).

While the contribution of the C–C/C–H peak is much higher than the contributions of the C–O and O–C=O peaks for untreated PLA samples (Table S2), it becomes significantly weaker for PLA(CA-ET) and especially PLA(ET-CA) specimens (i.e., its contribution decreases by 17.5% and 44.1%, respectively). Instead, the contribution of the O–C=O peak, which was the weakest for untreated PLA (6.1%), experienced the largest relative increment after treatment, increasing 3.2 and 5.2 times for PLA(CA-ET) and PLA(ET-CA), respectively. The contribution of the C–O peak also experienced an increase upon treatment, even though it was moderate with respect to that observed for the O–C=O peak (i.e., 1.2 and 1.9 times those for PLA(CA-ET) and PLA(ET-CA), respectively). After evaluation of the results obtained for PLA(ET) and PLA(CA), changes in the chemical composition were confirmed to be greater for the charged gas activation than for the electrothermal reorientation (Figure S5).

Deconvolution of the O 1s signal led to two contributing curves centered at 532.1 and 533.3 eV, which were ascribed to the −C=O bonds and O–C single bonds, respectively (Figures 5b and S6). While the differences in contribution of these two deconvoluted peaks were relatively similar for untreated PLA and PLA(ET) (Table S2), the O–C=O peak was clearly predominant for the rest of the samples (i.e., 3:1 ratio), supporting previous discussion about the greater impact of the charged gas activation on the chemical composition.

Electrochemical Characterization

The electrochemical behavior of untreated PLA, PLA(ET-CA), PLA(CA-ET), PLA(ET), and PLA(CA) was investigated by CV using PBS as the electrolytic medium. The voltammograms recorded at a scan rate of 100 mV/s, which are shown in Figure 6a, indicate that PLA(ET-CA) and PLA(CA) are the only specimens with electrochemical response, even though the electroactivity (i.e., the area of the voltammogram) is much higher for the former than for the latter. In the case of PLA(ET-CA), the current density increased from −5.1 to +53.4 μA/cm2 when the potential was scanned from −0.90 to +0.90 V, while the current density increased from −0.3 to +1.6 μA/cm2 for PLA(CA) when the same potential interval was swept (Figure 6a, right). Hence, although the electro-thermal reorientation process does not provide electrochemical response, it becomes essential to enhance the electrochemical activity given by the charged gas activation treatment. Accordingly, the latter is very effective when the α-crystalline phase appears (probably at the surface) as a consequence of the previous electro-thermal reorientation step. In contrast, this synergistic effect was not detected in PLA(CA-ET) samples, in which the two processes were applied in opposite order. In this case, the electro-thermal reorientation inhibited the electrochemical response imparted by the charged gas activation.

(b) Cyclic voltammograms for PLA(ET-CA) recorded in PBS at different scan rates. (c) Variation of the current density at the reversal potential against the scan rate for PLA(ET-CA).

Cyclic voltammograms recorded for PLA(ET-CA) at different scan rates (from 10 to 100 mV/s) in PBS show that the current increases with the scan rate (Figure 6b). Since the current at the reversal potential does not vary linearly against the square root of the scan rate (Figure S7), the charge transfer processes do not involve freely diffusing redox species. However, its linear variation against the scan rate (Figure 6c) indicates that the charge transfer can be ascribed to species present on the electrode surface.

As EIS is an electrochemical technique useful for exploring material interfaces, this method was chosen to confirm the surface changes observed in XPS analyses and that of bulk polymer reflected by CV assays after the different samples’ treatments. As can be seen in Figure 7a, after the 3D printing, the back side of the discs was Au-sputtered to ensure electrical contact in the whole WE area and connectors. The front side was left exposed to the electrolyte solution (PBS) in the three-electrode cell arrangement, described in the Methods section.

(a) Procedure used to prepare the samples for the EIS assays. (b) Nyquist and (c and d) Bode plots of untreated PLA, PLA(ET-CA), and PLA(CA-ET). (e) EECs obtained for the studied samples.

The Nyquist plots of PLA (control), PLA(ET-CA), and PLA(CA-ET) presented varied semicircle behaviors with different time constants, as displayed in Figure 7b. The electro-thermal reorientation treatment, followed by the oxygen-gas activation, seems to have the greatest effect on the insulating PLA material, drastically decreasing the bulk resistance (Rb) to 201.0 ± 1.1 kΩ·cm2, when compared to untreated PLA discs (48.3 ± 5.5 MΩ·cm2) (Table S3). Instead, the reverse treatment (i.e., first charged gas activation followed by electro-thermal reorientation) almost doubles the resistance value of the material when evaluating similar thicknesses (0.5 mm). This has been attributed to the well-known and proven PLA crystallinity phase changes when this thermoplastic undergoes thermal, electrical, or even mechanical induced transformations. Al-Rubaiai et al.45 reported thermal and electrical measurements on conductive PLA filaments under heating and cooling conditions. At values close to the glass transition temperature of PLA (Tg ≈ 55–60 °C), the polymer resistance suffered a subtle inversion in the Rb values, decreasing immediately after overpassing the Tg and increasing again when the temperature was raised from ∼65 to 100 °C. However, the resistance reduction was accounted to be rather discrete (60 Ω) in comparison to the results of this work since their commercial plastic contained carbon black additive, which is a semiconducting material. The most remarkable difference with respect to the present study is that PLA 3D printed discs, without any additive or plastic treatment, behave as insulators, being converted into semiconducting polymers by applying the proposed treatment. This represents a challenge for the future preparation of plastic sensors because optimization of the method conditions could offer even lower Rb values.

Electrical conductivity is also directly affected by the charge transfer resistance (Rct) of nanometer-sized layers or roughness created at the material surface. This can be identified by the electrolyte diffusion to the plastic surface when nonporous materials are used. Therefore, the Rct values of PLA(CA-ET) and untreated PLA are very similar (Table S3), showing that samples in which the last treatment is the electro-thermal reorientation do not influence significantly the liquid–solid interface, as demonstrated previously by SEM images and WCA measurements. In contrast, PLA(CA-ET) does not exhibit the same behavior that PLA(ET-CA) does. This has been attributed to the fact that the charged gas activation process does not influence the polymer crystallinity, even though it is strong enough to create conducting layers through charged oxygen-containing species. As a consequence, this interface has a lower Rct than those obtained for PLA and PLA(ET-CA) specimens.

Exploring the changes in the constant phase elements (CPEs), no substantial differences were observed since the solid samples were not able to store charge, as was observed in CV experiments (Figure 6), where narrow voltammetric curves were obtained. This was an expected behavior since either the charged gas activation or the electro-thermal reorientation were not able to create ions or ionic charges in the bulk. Bode plots (Figures 7c and 7d) confirmed the most resistive response of PLA(CA-ET) and untreated PLA. Data obtained from the electrical equivalent circuits (EEC, Figure 7e) are summarized in Table S3. Overall, EIS results are fully consistent with the outcomes provided by SEM, XRD, XPS, and CV techniques.

Extension to Other Polymers and Validation of the Methodology

As a proof of concept, we extrapolated our approach to other dielectrics. For this purpose, two thermoplastics, which differ mainly from PLA in the content of oxygen, were selected: polyethylene terephthalate glycol (PETG) and polypropylene (PP). PETG is a glycol-modified class of poly(ethylene terephthalate) (PET) that is largely used in manufacturing plastic beverage bottles and food products. Besides, it is polar and degradable to an extent until specific conditions. PP is a nonpolar, biocompatible, and nonbiodegradable polymer with high impact strength and flexibility. While PP is an oxygen-free material, the C/O ratio in PETG (2.5) is higher than that in PLA (1.5).

PETG and PP discs of dimensions identical with those of PLA were produced by 3D printing. Then, the electro-thermal reorientation and subsequently the charged gas activation processes were applied to half of the printed discs using the operational conditions optimized for PLA(ET-CA), the materials obtained using this approach being denoted PETG(ET-CA) and PP(ET-CA). Representative and reproducible CV curves for untreated and treated discs, which were recorded in PBS at scan rates of 50 and 100 mV/s, are compared in Figure 8. While PETG and PP behaved as dielectrics without showing any electrochemical activity, the treated polymers clearly exhibited an electrical response. Specifically, the current density increased from −8.1 μA/cm2 at the initial potential to 20.2 μA/cm2 at the reversal potential for PETG(ET-CA) and from −3.0 to 13.3 μA/cm2 for PP(ET-CA). The effect of the scan rate was significant for PETG(ET-CA), as was observed for PLA(ET-CA), while it practically did not exist for PP(ET-CA). This feature confirms that oxygen atoms play a crucial role in the formation of the species involved in charge transfer processes at the electrode surface.

Cyclic voltammograms recorded in PBS at different scan rates for (a) untreated PETG and PETG(ET-CA) and (b) untreated PP and PP(ET-CA) specimens.

Overall, the application of the electro-thermal reorientation followed by the charged gas activation allows the activation of the electrochemical response of any dielectric polymer. However, the efficacy of such activation depends on the presence of oxygen and, in turn, on the kind of oxygenated species created during the activation process. Consistently, the electrochemical response of PP(ET-CA) was much lower than those of PLA(ET-CA) and PETG(ET-CA). As the only oxygen atoms in PP are those associated with the thermal oxidation of the own material during the processing of the discs, the formation of electro-responsive species during the treatment is more difficult. Instead, the formation of electrochemically active species in PLA and PETG is favored by the presence of oxygen-containing functional groups. On the other hand, it is worth noting that this treatment was engineered for semicrystalline polymers since in intrinsically amorphous polymers the effect of thermal reorientation will be null and they will only be activated by the charged gas treatment.

Detection of Dopamine

Finally, as a proof of concept, the electrochemical detection of DA was performed by using a PLA(ET-CA) electrode. DA is an important catecholamine neurotransmitter (Scheme S1), whose electrochemical detection has been widely investigated because of its important functions in the human body (e.g., human metabolism, cardiovascular, central nervous, renal, and hormonal systems).46−48 Thus, DA is critical for signal transmissions to the brain since an inadequate level of DA is related to severe neurological diseases/disorders, for example schizophrenia, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, and Parkinson’s disease. The electrochemical detection of DA has been afforded using many types of materials, such as conducting polymers, metal oxides, noble metals, bimetallic nanoparticles, carbonaceous materials (i.e., carbon nanotubes and graphene), metal organic frameworks, and MXene,49−55 which electrocatalyze its oxidation to dopaminoquinone (DQ).

The voltammograms recorded in PBS with increasing additions of DA are displayed in Figure 9a. The oxidation peak was well resolved at PLA(ET-CA) electrodes with peak potentials at ∼0.3 V at low DA concentrations and ∼0.4 V at high DA concentrations. The shift in the peak potential to higher values with increasing DA concentration is caused by the participation of protons in the electrode reaction (i.e., the pH decreases with increasing DA concentration).56 On the other hand, the anodic peak current increases with the concentration of DA, which indicates that (i) PLA(ET-CA) shows electrocatalytic activity in the oxidation of DA and (ii) the adsorption of oxidized DA molecules on the surface of the electrode is a favored process. In contrast, the effect is much less pronounced for the cathodic current associated with the reduction peak at ∼0.1 V.

(a) CV curves for PLA(ET-CA) in PBS solutions with an increasing DA concentration from 0 to 1000 μM. (b) Calibration plot in PBS (i.e., peak current vs DA concentration) for a DA concentration interval ranging from 50 to 1000 μM.

The calibration curve was built in the range of 50–1000 μM (R2 = 0.9902) using the anodic peak current (Figure 9b). The variation of the anodic peak current with the DA concentration was linear (R2 = 0.9822) for the whole interval. The limit of detection, which was calculated according to the formula (3·σ)/m, where m is the slope of the calibration curve and σ is the standard deviation of the blank signal, was 6.3 μM. This preliminary study proves the success of the ET + CA approach to transform oxygen-containing insulating plastics into electrochemically responsive materials with great potential for biosensing applications. Based on these results, in a near future, we will conduct a specific study to propose PLA(ET-CA) and other modified electrodes as selective multiplexed sensors for neurotransmitters. For this purpose, the electrochemical response of such electrodes will be examined in PBS solutions containing both DA and serotonin with concentrations comprised between 1 nM and 5 μM in the presence of interferents (i.e., uric acid and ascorbic acid) as well as in real biological fluids.

Conclusions

The electro-thermal reorientation combined with the charged gas activation is a successful approach to convert insulating thermoplastics into electro-responsive materials. The synergistic behavior of such processes when applied following the said order has been demonstrated by comparing the properties of PLA(ET+CA) with those of PLA(CA+ET), PLA(ET), and PLA(CA). The proposed approach is a useful tool to easily fabricate 3D printed electrodes for biosensing from insulating thermoplastic filaments without adding expensive conducting materials such as carbon-based structures (e.g., carbon nanotubes and graphene), metallic nanoparticles (e.g., Au and Pt nanoparticles), or even conducting polymers (e.g., poly(3,4-ethyelenedioxythiophene).

The proposed approach has been exhaustively studied using 3D printed PLA specimens, even though its effectiveness has been evaluated by using other polymers (PP and PETG). As it was expected, after treatment, the electroresponse of oxygen-containing polymers, PLA and PETG, is greater than that of the oxygen-free polymer, PP. Finally, as a proof of concept, we demonstrate the electrochemical detection of DA, an important neurotransmitter related to a wide variety of neurological diseases, using 3D printed PLA(ET-CA) electrodes. Results show that PLA(ET-CA) electrocatalyzes the oxidation of DA to DQ and allows its detection over a wide interval of concentrations (from 50 to 1000 μM). Overall, these developments pave the way for the easy fabrication of innovative electrodes for electrochemical biosensing. The next work will focus on the specific analysis of the performance of PLA(ET-CA) and other treated electrodes when used as electrochemical sensors of biomolecules using different detection techniques and physiological media as well as varying the concentration of the analytes of interest.
