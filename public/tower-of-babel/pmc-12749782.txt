Numerical simulation study of the sonic detection and imaging methods for fish top in downhole environments
Authors: Zhongzhi Hu, Dingrui Yang, Wenxing Wu, Xiang Gao, Lei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo address the difficulty and low efficiency of fishing and docking fractured drill tools in deep and ultra-deep wells, a line-array scanning method using acoustic transducers was employed to generate images of the fish top. Based on borehole acoustic theory, a casing-fish annulus space model was established. The acoustic wave propagation characteristics and one-dimensional linear array receiving properties were studied, with reflection echoes serving as the evaluation index. Key parameters such as sound source frequency, detection distance, fish wall thickness, and array spacing were analyzed in detail. Effective strategies to enhance sound wave transmission distance and image quality were discussed. Acoustic imaging results for a Ø244.5 mm casing and Ø127.0 mm drill pipe indicated that the horizontal section image error relative to the true center of the fish was 11.7%, while the error between the image center and the true center of the fish top was 2%. For inclined sections, The image of the fish revealed the inclined orientation. The findings indicated that acoustic imaging of the fish top was achieved using fewer acoustic transducers, providing a novel solution for enhancing fishing efficiency and serving as a valuable reference for future research on downhole tools and systems.Keywords: Fractured drilling tools, Fishing operations, Ultrasound imaging, Fish top imaging, Numerical simulationSubject terms: Mechanical engineering, Petrol

Introduction

Drilling tool fractures in deep and ultra-deep wells are a significant form of failure. When a drilling tool fractures, salvage operations must be implemented swiftly to avoid the collapse of open-hole sections, tool burial, or even well abandonment1. Conventional fishing technologies struggle to obtain the top position of the dropped drill tool, and the success of the docking between the fishing tool and the dropped drill tool depends on changes in drill weight and pump pressure signal. This process is both difficult and inefficient. Additionally, as well depth increases, the overhang and pump pressure signals weaken during the fishing process, thereby escalating the difficulty of retrieving the drilling tool2. Therefore, real-time monitoring of the relative position between the fishing tool and the fish top is essential to enhance the success rate and efficiency of fishing operations.

After the drill pipe breaks, it is common to infer the cause of the fall based on the shape of the drill pipe at the drop junction. If downhole conditions are determined, appropriate fishing tools can be selected3. However, incorrect tool selection may exacerbate the issue, leading to further crack expansion, thereby complicating the accident. In cases where predicting the fall of the fish through the drilling interface is infeasible, traditional fall detection technology involves determining the size, shape, and depth of the fallen fish in the well. This is achieved by pressing a lead mold against the top of the fallen fish or the blocked position4. Lead molds have chemical stability in complex downhole fluids, but the marks they leave are often insufficiently specific for accurate assessment of the fish top. As a result, the state of the fish top is often assessed based on experience. In addition to lead printing, active magnetic detection, electromagnetic wave detection, downhole TV, and acoustic detection technologies are also used for downhole fish detection. Bin Zhong Zhou et al.5 employed traditional radar to create images of falling objects in the well by analyzing reflected and scattered electromagnetic signals. He used guided waves to detect targets along the well axis, thereby achieving excellent results. However, the main radiation direction of the system remains radial, with only a small part of the energy propagating along the shaft. In comparison with downhole TV imaging, electromagnetic imaging technology is more limited in the application of falling object detection. It is primarily used to identify object types, detect underground environments, and prevent collisions. Downhole TV imaging6 is a logging technology that directly observes downhole conditions in open holes and cased wells. This includes visible downhole TV, short-wave infrared downhole TV, and Visuray downhole TV. However, it is constrained by the well-fluid environment, which can include mud, sediment, and oil–water mixtures, thereby requiring washing for clear water visibility. Short-wave infrared TV can penetrate crude oil but not water7. While X-ray scattering imaging is unaffected by well fluid8, its resolution and visual quality are significantly inferior to visible downhole TV. Miniaturization X-ray detection equipment remains a challenge.

Recently, ultrasonic imaging for fish location detection has gained attention. In industrial and medical ultrasound applications, phased array ultrasound has achieved notable advancements in both imaging algorithms and hardware9,10. In medical ultrasound testing, rectangular array elements (acoustic transducers) are commonly used for three-dimensional (3D) imaging. Matrix array enables arbitrary focus in 3D space and direct image voxels11. In industry applications, line arrays, due to their simple structure, strong directivity, and wide frequency range, are more commonly used for 3D imaging through mechanical scanning12. Annular arrays, spherical surfaces, and concave surfaces also have successful cases13,14, but their limitations in production cost, circuit complexity, and processing technology hinder their widespread use. The 3D imaging capabilities of linear arrays are sufficient to fuse discrete ultrasonic data into 3D comprehensive images.

Given the complex downhole environmental conditions during drilling, replacing drilling fluid in the wellbore with a transparent medium is impractical. Acoustic detection technology, leveraging the characteristics of acoustic imaging, holds significant potential for guiding the alignment of downhole tools with fish tops in the retrieval of fractured drilling tools. This study investigated the feasibility and influencing factors of acoustic imaging in a drilling wellbore, especially under the condition of a limited array arrangement of acoustic transducers, using numerical simulation methods. Initially, factors affecting the detection of echoes from falling fish were analyzed, including the influences of distance, falling fish wall thickness, transducer excitation frequency, and array spacing on echo amplitude and signal extraction. Furthermore, the optimal detection range, frequency limits, and ideal array spacing were determined. Given these data, a numerical model was established, and a 3D imaging method utilizing a linear array was established. Finally, the imaging results were compared with the model to evaluate accuracy, thereby confirming the feasibility and reliability of the method. Thus, considering the stringent size limitations of downhole tools and instruments, this study introduced an innovative approach for imaging the fish on top of a fractured drill pipe using a one-dimensional line array. It also defined the relationship between the number and density of transducers and the imaging quality, thereby offering valuable insights for the subsequent research on related methods and technologies.

Principles

Full matrix imaging is an ultrasonic array post-processing technology for image reconstruction based on data captured from the full matrix. It enables focusing at any point in the detection area, thereby offering excellent resolution across the entire image. This overcomes the limitation of conventional acoustic detection, which only achieves high resolution within the focused region15. The method consists of two primary tasks: reflected wave collection and data processing. The assembled offset reflection points delineate the reflection interface, which accurately defines its position and size. Given a limited amount of scanned data, the method enhances image contrast and applies interpolation to improve resolution, thereby achieving a resolution approximately equal to the wavelength of the reflected wave.

Full matrix imaging

The full matrix imaging algorithm divides the two-dimensional (2D) imaging plane into grids. Upon utilizing the full matrix synthesis algorithm, the beam was focused on any grid, and the amplitudes of each array combination were superimposed under this condition to obtain the image amplitude for that grid16. For an array with N2 elements (acoustic transducers, hereafter referred to as transducers), the grid image amplitudes were derived by superimposing the amplitude of group A-scan time-domain signals. Furthermore, by traversing all the grids in the imaging region, a 2D image was drawn according to the image amplitude at each grid. Full matrix imaging is thus achieved focusing on any grid within the imaging target region during the post-processing.

A linear array was employed to achieve faster 3D ultrasound imaging, where the speed of 2D imaging plays a crucial role. Sampling points were arranged in a linear array at a fixed interval, and the linear array probe was systematically scanned to obtain 2D images. These discrete 2D data points were mapped to cylindrical coordinates, thereby reconstructing a 3D ultrasonic image of the fish top.

Numerical simulation scheme

The actual propagation distance of the acoustic signal was affected by the environment and its properties. When sound waves propagate in the wellbore environment, many factors affect the reception of reflected waves, such as the sound source frequency, propagation distance, fish wall thickness, and the receiving position, properties of the medium, as well as other physical parameters. In this study, the effects of source frequency, fish wall thickness, detection distance, and array distance on the reception of reflected wave signal were primarily considered. The array size was 3 mm, and the number of arrays was selected as 16. The detection distance was initially set to 1 m, the filling medium in the wellbore was water, and the sound speed was 1500 m/s. Given variations in water temperature, pressure, and other factors17, the sound propagation speed changed, thereby affecting the acquisition of imaging echoes. Furthermore, considering various factors such as computing time, resources, and grid independence, the spatial dimension was selected according to the center of gravity for the research problem. The spatial dimension is selected as a 2D two-dimensional axisymmetric model when studying detection distance, sound source frequency, and fish wall thickness; and as a 3D model when studying array spacing.

Boundary conditions

The casing and drill pipes were equivalent to hollow cylinders, with the end face of the broken drill pipe serving as the starting end face and the z-axis as the center axis of the wellbore. A transducer was arranged at the L position of the drill pipe end face. The perfectly matched layer (PML) was the 20 mm end of the geometric model, which extended along the Z-axis.

The simulated process of the model is as follows: the pulse sound excited by the transducer scattered at the bottom of the well and the casing wall, which was received by the transducer. In the constructed model, the transducer material was PZT-5 H, the filling material in the well was water, and the acoustic velocity in the medium was 1500 m/s. Subsequently, considering the large impedance difference between the water and the well wall, the boundary of the hard sound field was assumed around the water area. The fish at the bottom of the well was set as linear elastic material with isotropy. The PML simulated an infinitely long bottom-hole environment, thereby absorbing excess top and bottom model reflections. Furthermore, according to the simulation process, all solid surfaces except the transducer surface were subjected to fixed constraints.

The falling fish and the transducer were solid mechanical physical fields, while the electrostatic field involved only the transducer through circuit excitation. The structural mechanics and pressure acoustics were coupled at the contact surface between fish and water, and the piezoelectric effect was confined inside the transducer, thereby forming a physical field of acoustic-solid-electric coupling.

Interpretation of result

The influence of distance on echo reception

Based on the theory of acoustic wave propagation, defining the superior detection distance was conducive to compensating echoes, and distinguishing the reflected waves of the casing wall from the multiple mode waves. Therefore, to analyze the correlation between the amplitude of the echo wave and the propagation distance, and to clarify the nature of acoustic wave axial propagation in the borehole, the reference value modeling was used. Thus, L increased from 100 mm to 500 mm for comparative analysis in the free field and casing respectively.

Figures 5(a–b) show the sound pressure distribution of reflected sound waves at different distances under two boundary conditions, respectively. It was observed that the borehole reflected amplitude negatively correlated with distance as in the free field. This was attributable to the propagation path of the reflected wave received under borehole constraint, which was not a straight round trip from the transducer to the falling fish (also known as waveguide). In the process of sound transmission and reception, the reflected wave reflected several times at the wall of the well, which affected the extraction of the reflected wave from the falling fish.

Reflected echoes at different distances between the free field and borehole (a) in the borehole (b) in the free field.

The free field of fish reflection echo at 200–400 mm corresponded to that in the borehole, which served as an advantageous range for detection. The reflected echo was easily separated from multiple reflections on the borehole wall, and the reflected echo did not need additional filtering. For a single transducer with a coaxial line arrangement within 100 mm, the influence of fish shape on sound was investigated, and the sound reflected multiple times under borehole constraints, thereby forming a detection blind spot. Thus, sound waves in borehole confinement exhibited an enhanced effect, decaying more slowly than free fields, thereby enabling longer-range detection. Furthermore, it is necessary to analyze the waveform characteristics of different mode waves in long-distance detection. In the actual detection, different detection methods or fishing tools were used to eliminate the influence of detection blind spots.

Effect of wall thickness on echo reception

In the detection of falling objects, the accurate identification of the size of falling objects is an important factor in determining the salvage strategy. To observe the size effect of the falling object on the reflected wave, the distance between the falling object and the probe was kept at 200 mm, the probe excited at 100 kHz, and the wall thickness of the cylindrical falling object changed, thereby observing the change of the reflected wave.

Figure 6 shows the sound pressure echo distribution corresponding to the Ø127 mm drill pipe thickness (t) of 9.19 mm, 7.52 mm, 6.43 mm, and 5.59 mm. The results indicated that the reflected wave amplitude increased with thickness. Therefore, no significant change was observed in the reflected amplitude with increasing or decreasing wall thickness of 1 mm. Simultaneously, the increase in wall thickness led to a variety of mode waves, which necessitated filtering by setting the energy threshold.

The influence of frequency on echo reception

Acoustic frequency significantly impacts imaging resolution and beam attenuation rate, which largely determines the effectiveness and accuracy of acoustic detection of falling objects. Higher frequencies enhance resolution, thereby enabling more detailed detection. However, excessively high detection frequencies require suppression of side lobes, and the increased sound wave attenuation hampers detection performance. Therefore, it is necessary to select the appropriate acoustic wave transducer emission frequency to detect the shape of the fish top.

Figure 7 shows the distribution of echo sound pressure in the time domain at different frequencies over a fixed detection distance, which is determined through numerical simulation. At a detection distance of 200 mm, the reflected wave gradually became more obvious with the increased probe excitation frequency. The reflected amplitudes at 300 kHz and 400 kHz excitation frequencies were similar. When the excitation frequency was 200 kHz, the reflected wave appeared clearer than at 100 kHz, thereby facilitating subsequent recognition and extraction of the reflected wave.

Pulse echoes of different sound source frequencies.

Figure 8 shows the response amplitude-frequency graph for different excitation frequencies obtained through Fourier transform. At a transmitting frequency of 100 kHz, the sound main lobe was relatively wide. As the frequency increased to 200 kHz, the main lobe narrows, thereby resulting in improved directivity. Higher frequencies not only enhanced directivity but also increased side lobe intensity and decreased lateral resolution. In the Ø244.5 mm casing (inner diameter 222.6 mm), the sound source exhibited consistent spectral characteristics between 200 kHz and 400 kHz. At 200 kHz, side lobe intensity was relatively low, which contributed to improved resolution.

The distance between array elements and echo reception

High frequencies not only narrow the sound beam but also lead to larger attenuation amplitude during sound wave propagation. Therefore, the imaging system did not increase the ultrasonic frequency without limitation to pursue the resolution, thereby focusing on sound beam accuracy. Optimizing the related parameters of the phased bursts is also an important way to improve the horizontal resolution of the detection system. Furthermore, the more the number of elements, the clearer the imaging effect. However, considering the limited size of the pipeline and circuit complexity reduction, 16 arrays were selected with only the array space varying from 1 mm to 11 mm.

Discussion

In this study, when the sound source was close, the incidence angle of each transducer varied, and the emission angle was calculated geometrically. Conversely, when the sound source is far away, the large radius of the spherical wave results in nearly parallel wave propagation paths. In such cases, the beam angles were equal, and the relationship between the path length and array distance followed the cosine function. The dividing line between the near field and the far field was determined by the array (D). For a linear array (d). To distinguish the shape of the top of the fish, it is necessary to arrange the transducer array within the near field. The transducer transmitted sound waves at the highest possible frequency, and the array width was maximized. In free space, the theoretical boundary between near field and far field of an array width of 108 mm and center frequency of 100 kHz was ~ 1.5 m. However, in pipeline detection, to ensure effective detection, the actual detection distance was relatively shorter.

Accurate performance of the algorithm relied on selecting array elements with high receiving sensitivity and exceptional consistency18. Enhancements to imaging were achieved by increasing the number of array elements, array width, and array spacing within a reasonable range. However, practical constraints, such as the complexity of data acquisition, signal transmission, and hole size, make extending the detection range challenging. Therefore, the optimization focus for transducer arrays was to maximize the data collected using a finite number of elements.

In downhole imaging, the ideal echo waveform only reflected the peak corresponding to the top or part of a specific part of the fish surface, thereby enabling clear visualization of the surface contour. However, artifacts often appear in the image due to the reflection of the acoustic beam and the characteristics of the reflective interface. Some artifacts were removed through prior estimation of the falling fish. In theory, the first peak of reflection represented the signal reflected from the top interface of the fish, which corresponded to the initial surface first contour encountered by the sound beam. Therefore, removing data after the first peak effectively eliminated removable artifacts. However, issues arose when the amplitude defined as the peak value of the pseudo-signal exceeded that defined as the peak value of the real signal, and the amplitude of the wave collected at different angles was significantly different. Therefore, when using threshold filtering, the energy threshold was defined as 1.6 times the mean threshold of the entire scan line. The first point exceeding this threshold was regarded as the starting point of the first peak signal, and waveform data beyond this point was retained for further processing.

Limitations and future work

This study ignored changes in bottom-hole temperature and irregularities in borehole walls such as open holes. However, borehole temperature significantly impacted sound velocity, particularly with increasing detection depth. Follow-up experiments were conducted to calibrate mud temperature and test mud sound velocity. Further exploration is needed to investigate the effects of irregular borehole walls, including open holes. In practical applications, the soft and hard properties, as well as the formation porosity, can generate a variety of complex waves that interfere with the echo. Thus, the direct wave reflected by the tube and fish can not be separated by analyzing only the dominant detection distance. A more effective approach will be to study the behavior of multiple reflected waves to enhance the signal, thereby enhancing the detection distance. Additionally, it is necessary to analyze the attenuation factors in sound propagation to compensate for energy lost due to attenuation in the reflected waves. For multi-group or multi-line array imaging with fishing tools in downhole exploration, the line array must be arranged sparsely to maximize imaging resolution while ensuring adequate coverage. The probe should be developed to match existing fishing tools, and the anchor structure, such as the hook and slip, may require custom design.

However, the use of a threshold of 1.6 times the mean across the scan line can effectively distinguish the first peak. In practice, empirically setting the threshold size and selecting waveform data may not be entirely accurate. Adaptive threshold algorithms based on statistical methods or physical principles may be necessary to improve this process.

In actual downhole exploration, the transmission medium was water or downhole mud. The goal is to detect fish or objects that have fallen into the well, which may include rocks or irregular well walls. Some rocks may be removed through cleaning processes. For large objects, the condition of the fish top or objects should be determined according to the reflected acoustic information, with intervention measures taken afterward. Non-metallic rock chunks in open holes may be the result of wall collapse and may not have a salvage value.

Conclusion

This study proposed a method based on a one-dimensional linear array of acoustic transducers to image the top of a fractured drill pipe at the bottom of the well. Numerical simulations and full-matrix acoustic imaging methods were employed to achieve 3D imaging of the fracture pipe top section model. The simulation results indicated the feasibility of using acoustic imaging to visualize the top of the fractured drill pipe. The method shows promising potential for further research, particularly in enhancing imaging quality, underground guided fishing technology, and related tools development. Key insights from this research include:

The energy of the sound wave reflected by the falling fish negatively correlated with the distance. The amplitude of the reflected waves was enhanced by the well wall, with larger falling objects generating stronger reflection waves. While the amplitude changes of the reflection waves from thicker drill pipes were less obvious, more waves of different modes were generated. Additionally, the frequency of the sound wave was proportional to the amplitude of the reflected wave. As the frequency increased, the primary lobe of the reflected wave became more concentrated with an increasing number of side lobes.
