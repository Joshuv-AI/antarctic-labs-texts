The linearized Floer equation in a chart
Urs Frauenfelder, Joa Weber, UNICAMP

Abstract
In this article we are considering the Hessian of the area functional in a non-Darboux chart. This does not seem to have been considered before and leads to an interesting new mathematical structure which we introduce in this article and refer to as almost extendable weak Hessian field . Our main result is a Fredholm theorem for Robbin-Salamon operators associated to non-continuous Hessians which we prove by taking advantage of this new structure.



1
Introduction

In a Darboux chart the Hessian of the area functional is the
constant operator
A_{0}=i{\partial}_{t}
.
In this note we compute the Hessian of the area functional in a
non-Darboux chart.
To the best of our knowledge this was never done before.
Here, in particular, an additional summand shows up
whose discontinuity challenges the previous methods.

We discover an interesting structure from the point of view of scale
geometry. We think that the structure is of crucial importance in
order to understand the structure of Floer theory in general.
Therefore we give this structure a name and refer to it as
an
almost extendable weak Hessian field
.
We then study the Robbin-Salamon operator

{\mathbb{D}}={\partial}_{s}+A

associated to a connecting path in an almost extendable weak Hessian
field
A
.

Our main result is that this Robbin-Salamon operator is Fredholm.
The difficulty in proving this result is that
the Hessian is not necessarily continuous.
Therefore the improvement, due to Rabier
[
Rab04
]
, of the classical
Robbin-Salamon theorem
[
RS95
]
is not necessarily
applicable to the situation at hand.
However, the new notion of almost extendability discovered in this article
allows to decompose the Hessian in the sum of two operators
where one is still continuous while the other one is of lower order,
in symbols

A=F+C.

In contrast to the Hessian itself the two summands are not necessarily
symmetric any more.
Luckily the theorem of Rabier can as well deal with non-symmetric
situations as long as continuity is guaranteed.
We check that the conditions of Rabier apply to the operator
associated to the first summand in our situation

{\mathbb{F}}={\partial}_{s}+F.

Hence
{\mathbb{F}}
is a Fredholm operator.
Since the second summand
C
is of lower order we show that it
gives rise to a compact multiplication operator. This then proves our
main result, because the Fredholm property is invariant under compact
perturbation.

This article is part of our endeavor to understand the basic structure
lying behind Floer theory
in order to make Floer theory applicable to a broader class of
problems involving Hamiltonian delay equation
as explained in
[
FW24a
, §1.2]
.

On

Expressing the area functional with the help of the

\begin{split}&\left.\tfrac{d}{d\rho}\tfrac{d}{dr}\right|_{(r,\rho)=(0,0)}\left(u_{r,\rho}^{*}\lambda\right)\\

&=\sum_{i,j,k}{\partial}_{k}{\partial}_{j}\lambda_{i}(u)\eta_{k}\xi_{j}\dot{u}_{i}\,dt+\sum_{i,j}{\partial}_{j}\lambda_{i}(u)\left(\xi_{j}\dot{\eta}_{i}+\eta_{j}\dot{\xi}_{i}\right)dt\end{split}

(4.11)

evaluated pointwise at
t
.
In the following calculation
we use the definition (
4.5
) of the action functional
in step 2, and (
4.11
) in step 3, to obtain

\begin{split}&d^{2}{\mathcal{A}}_{0}(u)\left(\xi,\eta\right)\\
&=\left.\tfrac{d}{d\rho}\tfrac{d}{dr}\right|_{(r,\rho)=(0,0)}{\mathcal{A}}_{0}(u_{r,\rho})\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{=}}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{1}}\left.\tfrac{d}{d\rho}\tfrac{d}{dr}\right|_{(r,\rho)=(0,0)}{u_{r,\rho}}^{*}\lambda\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{=}}\int_{0}^{1}\biggl(\sum_{i,j,k}{\partial}_{k}{\partial}_{j}\lambda_{i}(u)\eta_{k}\xi_{j}\dot{u}_{i}\,dt+\sum_{i,j}{\partial}_{j}\lambda_{i}(u)\bigl(\xi_{j}\dot{\eta}_{i}+\eta_{j}\dot{\xi}_{i}\bigr)\biggr)dt\\
\end{split}

\begin{split}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 4}}{{=}}\int_{0}^{1}\biggl(\sum_{i,j,k}\underbrace{{\partial}_{k}{\partial}_{j}\lambda_{i}(u)}_{=:\Lambda_{kji}(u)}\eta_{k}\xi_{j}\dot{u}_{i}\,dt+\sum_{i,j}\underbrace{{\partial}_{j}\lambda_{i}(u)\bigl(\xi_{j}\dot{\eta}_{i}-\dot{\eta}_{j}\xi_{i}\bigr)}_{=\omega_{ji}(u)\xi_{j}\dot{\eta}_{i}}\biggr)dt\\
&\quad-\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i,j,k}{\partial}_{k}{\partial}_{j}\lambda_{i}(u)\dot{u}_{k}\eta_{j}\xi_{i}\,dt\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}\omega_{u}(\xi,\dot{\eta})\,dt+\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i,j,k}\bigl(\underbrace{\Lambda_{kji}(u)-\Lambda_{ikj}(u)}_{=:L_{kji}(u)}\bigr)\eta_{k}\xi_{j}\dot{u}_{i}\,dt\end{split}

where step 4 is by integration by parts
(boundary terms vanish by periodicity).
Symmetry holds by the Theorem of Schwarz.
This proves Lemma
4.5
.
∎

Hypothesis 5.5

Theorem 5.6

The operators

\begin{split}{\mathbb{D}}^{u}&={\partial}_{s}+A^{u}\colon W^{1,2}_{H_{0}}\cap L^{2}_{H_{1}}\to L^{2}_{H_{0}}\\

Theorem

Main difficulties and how to overcome them

Before introducing the abstract setup in Section
6

below which we use to prove
Theorem
5.6
we first explain the main difficulties
and the main ideas how to overcome these difficulties.

The fact that
{\mathbb{D}}^{u}
is Fredholm almost directly follows from
Rabier’s Theorem which itself generalizes previous work by
Robbin and Salamon.
In particular, Rabier does not need any differentiability assumption
on
s\mapsto A(s)
, but requires continuity.
However, since
u
as a map
{\mathbb{R}}\to U_{2}
,
s\mapsto u_{s}
,
is only of class
L^{2}_{\rm loc}
, it is not necessarily continuous.
In particular, the map
{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathcal{L}}(H_{2},H_{1})
,
s\mapsto A^{u_{s}}_{2}
,
is not necessarily of class
C^{0}
.
Thus the improvement of the Robbin-Salamon Fredholm
theorem
[
RS95
]
from
C^{1}
to
C^{0}

by Rabier
[
Rab04
]
cannot be applied to
{\mathbb{D}}^{u}_{2}
.

Decomposition.

In order to deal with this difficulty we decompose the operators

{\mathbb{D}}^{u}
and
{\mathbb{D}}^{u}_{2}
into two parts.
To this end we introduce the notation

A^{u_{s}}=\underbrace{B_{u_{s}}^{-1}{\partial}_{t}-a^{u_{s}}}_{=:F^{u_{s}}}+\underbrace{\bar{L}_{u_{s}}(\cdot,\dot{u}_{s})}_{=:C^{\dot{u}_{s}}}=F^{u_{s}}+C^{\dot{u}_{s}}

and

{\mathbb{D}}^{u}\xi=\underbrace{({\partial}_{s}+F^{u})}_{=:{\mathbb{F}}^{u}}\xi+\underbrace{\left[s\mapsto C^{\dot{u}_{s}}\xi_{s}\right]}_{=:M_{C^{\dot{u}}}\xi}={\mathbb{F}}^{u}\xi+M_{C^{\dot{u}}}\xi.

(5.20)

The advantage of this decomposition is the following.
The map

{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathcal{L}}(H_{2},H_{1}),\quad s\mapsto F^{u_{s}}_{2}=B_{u_{s}}^{-1}{\partial}_{t}|_{H_{2}}-a^{u_{s}}_{2}

is continuous since it avoids
\dot{u}_{s}
, while the non-continuous map

{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathcal{L}}(H_{2},H_{1}),\quad s\mapsto C^{\dot{u}_{s}}_{2}=\bar{L}_{u_{s}}(\cdot,\dot{u}_{s})|_{H_{2}}

is of lower order since it avoids
{\partial}_{t}
.

Since
C^{\dot{u}}
is of lower order we show, maybe after an
asymptotic correction, with the help of
Theorem
B.3

that the multiplication operator
M_{C^{\dot{u}}}
as a map

W^{1,2}_{H_{1}}\cap L^{2}_{H_{2}}\to L^{2}_{H_{1}}
is compact.
But Fredholm property and index are stable under compact perturbation.
Thus to show that

\begin{split}{\mathbb{D}}^{u}_{2}\colon W^{1,2}_{H_{1}}\cap L^{2}_{H_{2}}\to L^{2}_{H_{1}},\quad\xi\mapsto{\partial}_{s}\xi+A^{u}_{2}\xi\end{split}

(5.21)

is Fredholm of the same Fredholm index as
{\mathbb{D}}^{u}

is equivalent to showing this for

\begin{split}{\mathbb{F}}^{u}_{2}={\partial}_{s}+F^{u}\colon W^{1,2}_{H_{1}}\cap L^{2}_{H_{2}}\to L^{2}_{H_{1}},\quad\xi\mapsto{\partial}_{s}\xi+\underbrace{B_{u}^{-1}{\partial}_{t}\xi-a^{u}\xi}_{=:F^{u}\xi}.\end{split}

The fact that
{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathcal{L}}(H_{2},H_{1})
,
s\mapsto F^{u_{s}}
, is continuous,
while
s\mapsto A^{u_{s}}
is not, makes it easier to prove
the Fredholm property for
{\mathbb{F}}^{u}_{2}
(Rabier’s theorem applies)
than than for
{\mathbb{D}}^{u}_{2}
(it doesn’t apply by non-continuity of

s\mapsto C^{\dot{u}_{s}}
).
The disadvantage of
F^{u_{s}}
when compared to
A^{u_{s}}
is that, while

A^{u_{s}}
is
H_{0}
-symmetric for every
s
,
necessarily not so is
F^{u_{s}}
.
However, Rabier’s theorem can as well deal with non-symmetric
operators.

6
Almost extendability

Consider a
Hilbert space pair

(H_{0},H_{1})
,
that is
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
are both infinite dimensional Hilbert spaces
such that as sets
H_{1}\subset H_{0}
and inclusion
\iota\colon H_{1}\hookrightarrow H_{0}
is
compact and dense.

Then, as we explain in
[
FW24a
, §2]
,
there exists an unbounded monotone function

h=h(H_{0},H_{1})\colon\mathbb{N}\to(0,\infty),

called
pair growth function
, such that the pair
(H_{0},H_{1})

is isometric to the pair
(\ell^{2},\ell^{2}_{h})
,
see
[
FW24a
, App. A]
, and from now on we identify
the pairs

(H_{0},H_{1})=(\ell^{2},\ell^{2}_{h}),\qquad h=h(H_{0},H_{1}).

Here
\ell^{2}_{h}
is defined as follows. In general,
for
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to(0,\infty)
unbounded monotone

\ell^{2}_{f}
is the space of all sequences
x=(x_{\nu})_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}}
with

\sum_{\nu=1}^{\infty}f(\nu)x_{\nu}^{2}<\infty
.
The space
\ell^{2}_{f}
becomes a Hilbert space if we endow it
with the inner product

\langle x,y\rangle_{f}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{\infty}f(\nu)x_{\nu}y_{\nu},\qquad\left\|x\right\|_{f}:=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle_{f}}.

Therefore we can define for every real number
r
a Hilbert space

H_{r}:=\ell^{2}_{f^{r}}
.
For each
s<r
the inclusion
H_{r}\subset H_{s}

is compact and dense.

The resulting triple of Hilbert spaces
H_{0}
,
H_{1}
, and
H_{r=2}

is called a
Hilbert space triple
, notation
(H_{0},H_{1},H_{2})
.
Pick a Hilbert space triple
(H_{0},H_{1},H_{2})
.

6.1

Fix a Hilbert space triple
(H_{0},H_{1},H_{2})
.
Let

be open. Set
U_{2}:=U_{1}\cap H_{2}
.

Definition 6.1

(Weak Hessian
[
FW24a
]
)

An element

of the Banach space
3
3

3

{\mathcal{L}}(H_{1},H_{0})\cap{\mathcal{L}}(H_{2},H_{1})

\max\{\mathopen{\|}\cdot\mathclose{\|}_{{\mathcal{L}}(H_{1},H_{0})},\mathopen{\|}\cdot\mathclose{\|}_{{\mathcal{L}}(H_{2},H_{1})}\}

{\mathcal{L}}(H_{1},H_{0})\cap{\mathcal{L}}(H_{2},H_{1})
is said a

if it satisfies these two axioms:

(symmetry)

\forall x,y\in H_{1}\colon

\left\langle Ax,y\right\rangle_{0}=\left\langle x,Ay\right\rangle_{0}

-symmetry
.

(fredholm)

A\colon H_{1}\to H_{0}
,

A_{2}:=A|_{H_{2}}\colon H_{2}\to H_{1}

are Fredholm of index zero.

We are interested not in a single weak Hessian, but in fields of weak Hessians.

Definition 6.2

(Weak Hessian field)
.

A

on
U_{1}
is a continuous map

A\in C^{0}(U_{1},{\mathcal{L}}(H_{1},H_{0}))\cap C^{0}(U_{2},{\mathcal{L}}(H_{2},H_{1}))

(Symmetry)

\forall x,y\in H_{1}\colon\quad\left\langle A^{u}x,y\right\rangle_{0}=\left\langle x,A^{u}y\right\rangle_{0}.

(6.22)

(Fredholm)

\forall u\in U_{1}\colon

A^{u}\colon H_{1}\to H_{0}
is Fredholm of index zero.

\forall u\in U_{2}\colon

A^{u}_{2}\colon H_{2}\to H_{1}
is Fredholm of index zero.

.

Given a weak Hessian field
A
, then along level two, i.e. at every point

We say that a weak Hessian field

A weak Hessian field

(F)

\forall u\in\underline{\,U_{1}}\colon

\forall u\in U_{1}

(ii) If

Lemma 6.8

Assume that a weak Hessian field

Proof.

We write

Since

of a bounded and a compact operator. Hence

is compact.
By the stability of the Fredholm property and the index

∎

6.2
Main theorem

Let
(H_{0},H_{1},H_{2})
be a Hilbert space triple and
U_{1}\subset H_{1}
an
open subset. Write

W^{1,2}_{H_{j}}:=W^{1,2}({\mathbb{R}},H_{j}),\quad L^{2}_{H_{j}}:=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},H_{j}).

To avoid constants
maybe after replacing the norms by equivalent norm we
assume in the following that

\mathopen{|}\cdot\mathclose{|}_{H_{0}}\leq\mathopen{|}\cdot\mathclose{|}_{H_{1}}\leq\mathopen{|}\cdot\mathclose{|}_{H_{2}}.

Definition 6.9

(Connecting paths)
.

Fix two points
u_{-},u_{+}\in U_{2}:=U_{1}\cap H_{2}
.
Fix a
basic path

\hat{u}
from
u_{-}
to
u_{+}

(see
[
FW25a
]
),
i.e.
\hat{u}\in C^{2}({\mathbb{R}},U_{2})
with the property that there exists
T>0

such that
\hat{u}(s)=u_{-}
whenever
s\leq-T
and

\hat{u}(s)=u_{+}
whenever
s\geq T
.

A
connecting path from
u_{-}
to
u_{+}

is a continuous map
u\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to U_{1}

such that the difference
u-\hat{u}
lies in the intersection
Hilbert space
W^{1,2}_{H_{1}}\cap L^{2}_{H_{2}}
, i.e.

u\in C^{0}({\mathbb{R}},U_{1}),\qquad u-\hat{u}\in W^{1,2}_{H_{1}}\cap L^{2}_{H_{2}}.

(6.26)

Remark 6.10

(Independence of choice of basic path)
.

The notion of connecting path does not depend on the choice of
the basic path
\hat{u}
.
Indeed suppose
\hat{v}
is another basic path, then

u-\hat{v}=u-\hat{u}+\hat{u}-\hat{v}

where
u-\hat{u}\in W^{1,2}_{H_{1}}\cap L^{2}_{H_{2}}
by assumption
and the other difference is
C^{2}
and of compact support, namely

\hat{u}-\hat{v}\in C^{2}_{\rm c}({\mathbb{R}},H_{2})\subset W^{1,2}_{H_{1}}\cap L^{2}_{H_{2}}
.

Theorem 6.11
.

Let
A
be an almost extendable weak Hessian field on

.
Consider two points
u_{-},u_{+}\in U_{2}
and a connecting path
u
.
Assume that both asymptotic operators

are isomorphisms as maps

.
Then the operators

\begin{split}{\mathbb{D}}^{u}={\partial}_{s}+A^{u}\colon W^{1,2}_{H_{0}}\cap L^{2}_{H_{1}}\to L^{2}_{H_{0}}\end{split}

(6.27)

and

\begin{split}{\mathbb{D}}^{u}_{2}={\partial}_{s}+A^{u}_{2}\colon W^{1,2}_{H_{1}}\cap L^{2}_{H_{2}}\to L^{2}_{H_{1}}\end{split}

are both Fredholm operators of the same Fredholm index

\mathop{\mathrm{index}}{\mathbb{D}}^{u}=\mathop{\mathrm{index}}{\mathbb{D}}^{u}_{2}.

Remark 6.12
.

That

is almost extendable is only used to show that
{\mathbb{D}}^{u}_{2}
is a

That

is a Fredholm operator follows for every weak Hessian field
on

.

Lemma 6.13

Suppose that

Since

.

and

(F)

for

(C)

.
To this end, by the triangle inequality,

4
4

4

The unparametrized Rabier condition (H4)

In this section we do not deal with operator families

Definition A.2
.

A bounded linear operator

satisfies the

, more precisely

Symmetric Fredholm operators of index zero

Lemma A.3
.

Suppose
A\in{\mathcal{L}}(W,H)
is a)
H
-symmetric
and b) Fredholm of index zero.
Then for every real number
\alpha\not=0
there is the estimate

\mathopen{\|}\alpha(A-i\alpha)^{-1}\mathclose{\|}_{{\mathcal{L}}(H)}\leq 1.

In particular,
A
satisfies the Rabier condition (H4)
for
C_{0}=1
and every
r_{0}>0
.

Proof.

By a) and b) the spectrum of
A
is real
and there exists an ONB
\{e_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of
H

and
\{a_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
such that
Ae_{n}=a_{n}e_{n}

for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
; see e.g.
[
FW24b
, App. E]
.
Let
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
, then

\alpha(A-i\alpha)^{-1}e_{n}=\frac{\alpha}{a_{n}-i\alpha}e_{n}.

Let
\xi\in H
and write it in the form

for unique real numbers
\xi_{n}
.
Suppose that
\xi
is of unit norm
\mathopen{|}\xi\mathclose{|}_{H}^{2}=\sum_{n}\xi_{n}^{2}=1
.
Then

\alpha(A-i\alpha)^{-1}\xi=\sum_{n}\frac{\alpha\xi_{n}}{a_{n}-i\alpha}e_{n}

and we estimate

\begin{split}\mathopen{|}\alpha(A-i\alpha)^{-1}\xi\mathclose{|}_{H}^{2}&=\sum_{n}\frac{\alpha^{2}\xi_{n}^{2}}{\mathopen{|}a_{n}-i\alpha\mathclose{|}_{{\mathbb{C}}}^{2}}\\
&=\sum_{n}\frac{\alpha^{2}}{a_{n}^{2}+\alpha^{2}}\xi_{n}^{2}\\
&\leq\sum_{n}\xi_{n}^{2}\\
&=1.\end{split}

This proves Lemma
A.3

Stability under compact perturbation

The following proposition is a version of
[
Rab03
, Thm. 3.5]
.

Proposition A.7

(Compact perturbation)
.

Let
A\colon W\to H
be a bounded linear operator
satisfying the Rabier condition (H4)
and
K\colon W\to H
a compact linear operator.
Then
A+K\colon W\to H

satisfies the Rabier condition (H4) as well.

In the proof we utilize the following notions.
Let
A\colon W\to H
be a bounded linear operator.
A bounded linear operator
T\colon W\to H
is called

A
-bounded
if
there exist constants
a\geq 0
and
b(a)\geq 0
such that

\mathopen{|}T\xi\mathclose{|}_{H}\leq a\mathopen{|}A\xi\mathclose{|}_{H}+b(a)\mathopen{|}\xi\mathclose{|}_{H}

(A.38)

for every
\xi\in W
.
The
A
-bound
of
T

is the infimum of all possible values
a\geq 0
, in symbols

\inf\{a\geq 0\mid\text{$\exists b(a)$ such that~(\ref{eq:A-bounded})
holds $\forall\xi\in W$}\}.

A theorem of Hess
[
Hes69
, Satz 1]

says that, given
A\in{\mathcal{L}}(W,H)
with non-empty resolvent set,
compact operators
K\colon W\to H
have vanishing
A
-bound.

Proof.

Let
A
satisfy (H4). Then
A
satisfies (i,ii) in
Lemma
A.5
with constants
r_{0},C_{0}
.
It suffices to check conditions (i,ii)
for
A+K
for some constants
r_{1},C_{1}
.
As
K\colon W\to H
is compact,
it holds that
A+K
is Fredholm and

\mathop{\mathrm{index}}(A+K)=\mathop{\mathrm{index}}A=0
This proves (i).
To prove (ii) we define

{\varepsilon}:=\min\{\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{C_{0}}{4}\},\qquad r_{1}:=\max\{r_{0},\tfrac{1}{2}+4\tfrac{b({\varepsilon})}{C_{0}}\},\qquad C_{1}:=\tfrac{C_{0}}{8}

and for
\mathopen{|}\alpha\mathclose{|}\geq r_{1}\geq r_{0}
we estimate

\begin{split}\mathopen{|}(A+K-i\alpha)\xi\mathclose{|}_{H}&\geq\mathopen{|}(A-i\alpha)\xi\mathclose{|}_{H}-\mathopen{|}K\xi\mathclose{|}_{H}\\
&\geq\mathopen{|}(A-i\alpha)\xi\mathclose{|}_{H}-{\varepsilon}\mathopen{|}(A{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\,-i\alpha+i\alpha})\xi\mathclose{|}_{H}-b({\varepsilon})\mathopen{|}\xi\mathclose{|}_{H}\\
&\geq(1-{\varepsilon})\mathopen{|}(A-i\alpha)\xi\mathclose{|}_{H}-\left({\varepsilon}\mathopen{|}\alpha\mathclose{|}+b({\varepsilon})\right)\mathopen{|}\xi\mathclose{|}_{H}\\
&\geq\left((1-{\varepsilon})C_{0}\mathopen{|}\alpha\mathclose{|}-{\varepsilon}\mathopen{|}\alpha\mathclose{|}-b({\varepsilon})\right)\mathopen{|}\xi\mathclose{|}_{H}\\
&\geq\left(\tfrac{C_{0}}{4}\mathopen{|}\alpha\mathclose{|}-b({\varepsilon})\right)\mathopen{|}\xi\mathclose{|}_{H}\\
&\geq\tfrac{C_{0}}{8}\mathopen{|}\xi\mathclose{|}_{H}.\end{split}

In inequality 2 we used (
A.38
) and then added zero

-i\alpha+i\alpha
.
Inequality 3 is by the triangle inequality.
Inequality 4 is by the hypothesis (
A.36
) on

which applies since
\mathopen{|}\alpha\mathclose{|}\geq r_{0}
.
Inequality 5 is by choice of
{\varepsilon}
.
The final inequality 6 uses that
\mathopen{|}\alpha\mathclose{|}\geq r_{1}

and the choice of
r_{1}
.
This proves Proposition
A.7
.

\Phi_{1}\in{\rm GL}(n,{\mathbb{C}})
such that

\tilde{\Psi}(1)=\tilde{\Psi}(0)\Phi_{1}=\Phi_{1}
.
Since
{\rm GL}(n,{\mathbb{C}})
is connected
there exists a smooth path
\Phi\colon[0,1]\to{\rm GL}(n,{\mathbb{C}})

from
\Phi(0)={{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}}
to
\Phi(1)=\Phi_{1}
.
The path defined for
t\in[0,1]
by

\Psi(t):=\tilde{\Psi}(t)\Phi(t)^{-1}

has the following properties.
Firstly, it is a loop in
{\rm GL}^{+}(2n,{\mathbb{R}})
since

\Psi(0):=\tilde{\Psi}(0)\Phi(0)^{-1}={{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}}

and

\Psi(1):=\tilde{\Psi}(1)\Phi(1)^{-1}=\Phi(1)\Phi(1)^{-1}={{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}}
.
Secondly, it is of class
W^{1,2}
since

\tilde{\Psi}
is of class
W^{1,2}
and
\Phi
is smooth.
Thirdly, it is a lift of
\psi
since

[\Psi(t)]=[\tilde{\Psi}(t)\Phi(t)^{-1}]=[\tilde{\Psi}(t)]=\psi(t)
.
Therefore (
C.46
) holds by (
C.47
).
∎

This proves Lemma
C.5
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
C.1

We show Theorem
C.1
for
k=2
, the case
k=1

is analogous. To this end,
given a loop
\omega\in W^{1,2}({\mathbb{S}}^{1},\Omega({\mathbb{R}}^{2n}))
,
we get a
W^{1,2}
-loop
B=B^{\omega}
in
{\rm GL}^{+}(2n,{\mathbb{R}})
.
Consider the operator

F_{B^{-1}}=B^{-1}{\partial}_{t}\colon W^{2,2}({\mathbb{S}}^{1},{\mathbb{R}}^{2n})\to W^{1,2}({\mathbb{S}}^{1},{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}).

By Lemma
C.2
this is a semi-Fredholm operator.
By (
C.45
) the loop
t\mapsto B(t)
is homotopic in

{\rm GL}^{+}(2n,{\mathbb{R}})
to the loop
t\mapsto J_{B(t)}
of almost complex structures.
Therefore the loop
B^{-1}
is homotopic to the loop
(J_{B})^{-1}=-J_{B}
.

Since the semi-Fredholm index is a homotopy invariant,
see e.g.
[
Mül07
, §18 Cor. 3]
,
we have

\mathop{\mathrm{index}}F_{B^{-1}}=\mathop{\mathrm{index}}F_{-J_{B}}
.
By Lemma
C.5
the loop
J_{B}
is conjugated in

W^{1,2}({\mathbb{S}}^{1},{\rm GL}^{+}(2n,{\mathbb{R}}))
to the constant loop
J(0)=J_{B(0)}
.
And therefore by Lemma
C.3

\mathop{\mathrm{index}}F_{-J_{B}}=\mathop{\mathrm{index}}F_{-J(0)}
.
Since
F_{-J(0)}=-J(0){\partial}_{t}
is symmetric for the
W^{1,2}

inner product it holds

\mathop{\mathrm{index}}F_{-J(0)}=0
.
Summarizing

\mathop{\mathrm{index}}F_{B^{-1}}=\mathop{\mathrm{index}}F_{-J_{B}}=\mathop{\mathrm{index}}F_{-J(0)}=0.

Since the index of the semi-Fredholm operator

F_{B^{-1}}
is zero the operator
F_{B^{-1}}
is actually Fredholm.
This proves Theorem
C.1
.

D.1.1
Heron iteration

Lemma D.1

(Heron method)
.

Let
q>0
be a positive real number.
Pick a positive real number
r_{1}>0
.
Define a sequence
r_{n}
recursively by the requirement

r_{1}>0,\hskip 22.99988ptr_{n+1}:=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(r_{n}+r_{n}^{-1}q\right),\hskip 11.49994ptn\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(D.49)

Then the following is true.

(i)

The sequence
(r_{n})_{n\geq 2}
is monotone decreasing and bounded
below by
\sqrt{q}
.

(ii)

The limit
r:=\lim_{n\to\infty}r_{n}
exists and
r^{2}=q
.

Proof of Lemma
D.1
.

(i). We first show that

a)
r_{n}>0
,

b)
r_{n}\geq\sqrt{q}
,

whenever
n\geq 2
.
While a) is obvious from (
D.49
), to see b) note that

\text{$a:=\sqrt{\tfrac{r_{n-1}}{2}}$,
$b\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{a)}}}{{:=}}\sqrt{\tfrac{q}{2r_{n-1}}}$},\hskip 11.49994pt\Rightarrow\hskip 11.49994pt\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{q}=ab\leq\tfrac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\tfrac{r_{n-1}}{2}+\tfrac{q}{2r_{n-1}}\right)=\tfrac{r_{n}}{2}.

We show that the sequence is monotone decreasing.
Indeed by a) and b) we get

r_{n}-r_{n+1}=r_{n}-\tfrac{1}{2}\left(r_{n}+r_{n}^{-1}q\right)=\tfrac{1}{2}\frac{r_{n}^{2}-q}{r_{n}}\geq 0

whenever
n\geq 2
.

(ii) follows from (i).
By the Monotone Convergence Theorem
the sequence
r_{n}
has a limit
r\geq\sqrt{q}
.
On the other hand, by the recursion formula (
D.49
) the
limit
r
satisfies

r=\frac{1}{2}\left(r+r^{-1}q\right)
,
equivalently
r^{2}=q
.
∎

D.1.2

To determine the square root of a positive real number

q>0
is equivalent to show that the function

f(r)=r^{2}-q
has a unique positive zero
r_{*}>0
.
For the latter Newton-Picard iteration serves.
Choose a point
r_{n}>0
and consider the tangent
line to the graph of
f
at the point
(r_{n},f(r_{n}))
.
If the slope
f^{\prime}(r_{n})\not=0
is non-zero
the tangent line intersects the
x
-axis at a point denoted and given by

r_{n+1}=r_{n}-\frac{f(r_{n})}{f^{\prime}(r_{n})}

as illustrated by Figure
3
.
Since
f(r_{n})=r_{n}^{2}-q
and
f^{\prime}(r_{n})=2r_{n}

Newton-Picard iteration for the function
f(r)=r^{2}-q

reproduces the Heron method, indeed

r_{1}>0,\hskip 22.99988ptr_{n+1}=r_{n}-\frac{r_{n}^{2}-q}{2r_{n}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(r_{n}+\tfrac{q}{r_{n}}\right),\hskip 11.49994ptn\in{\mathbb{N}}.

D.2
Matrix values

Lemma D.2

(Heron method)
.

Let
Q\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k\times k}
be a symmetric positive definite matrix.
Define a sequence
R_{n}
of matrices recursively by the requirement

R_{1}:={{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}},\hskip 22.99988ptR_{n+1}:=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(R_{n}+R_{n}^{-1}Q\right),\hskip 11.49994ptn\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(D.50)

Then the following is true.

(i)

Each matrix
R_{n}
commutes with
Q
and is symmetric positive definite.

(ii)

The limit
R:=\lim_{n\to\infty}R_{n}
exists and is symmetric.

(iii)

R^{2}=Q
and
R
is positive definite.

Remark D.3
.

The symmetric positive definite matrix
R
is called the (positive)

square root
of
Q
and denoted by
\sqrt{Q}:=R
.

Corollary D.4
.

Let
Q\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k\times k}
be a symmetric positive definite matrix.
Then any matrix
B
that commutes with
Q
also commutes with
\sqrt{Q}
.

Proof of Corollary
D.4
.

Let
(R_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be the sequence of positive definite symmetric
matrices defined by (
D.50
).
In particular
\lim_{n\to\infty}R_{n}=R=:\sqrt{Q}
.
Clearly
B
commutes with
R_{1}={{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}}
.
We show inductively that
B
commutes with
R_{n+1}
whenever
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
To this end assume that
[B,R_{n}]=0
.
Therefore
[B,R_{n}^{-1}]=0
.
Together with the corollary hypothesis
[B,Q]=0
, we obtain that

[B,R_{n}^{-1}Q]=0
.
Hence by the recursion formula (
D.50
)
we get that
[B,R_{n+1}]=\frac{1}{2}\left([B,R_{n}]+[B,R_{n}^{-1}Q]\right)=0
.
This finishes the induction.
Hence
[B,R]=\lim_{n\to\infty}[B,R_{n}]=0
.
This proves Corollary
D.4
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
D.2
.

(i) The proof is by induction on
n
. For
n=1
this is true.
Suppose (i) holds for
R_{n}
. In particular
R_{n}^{-1}
commutes with
Q
.
Then

R_{n+1}Q=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(R_{n}Q+R_{n}^{-1}QQ\right)=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(QR_{n}+QR_{n}^{-1}Q\right)=QR_{n+1}

and the transpose satisfies

{R_{n+1}}^{T}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{=}}\tfrac{1}{2}\left({R_{n}}^{T}+(QR_{n}^{-1})^{T}\right)=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(R_{n}+(R_{n}^{-1})^{T}Q^{T}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{=}}R_{n+1}

where in step 1 we used that
R_{n}^{-1}Q=QR_{n}^{-1}

and in step 3 that transpose of inverse is inverse of transpose.
That
R_{n+1}
defined by (
D.50
)
is positive definite is true since positive definiteness
is preserved under composition of commuting symmetric matrices
and under sum of symmetric matrices.

(ii)
We follow
[
Hig86
]
.
Since
Q
is symmetric positive definite it is diagonalizable, that is
there exists an orthogonal matrix
P
such that

PQP^{-1}={\rm diag}(q^{(1)},\dots,q^{(k)})=:\Lambda

where

q^{(1)},\dots,q^{(k)}\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
are the eigenvalues of
Q
.
Now the iterations

PR_{n}P^{-1}={\rm diag}(r_{n}^{(1)},\dots,r_{n}^{(k)})=:D_{n}

are diagonal as well, as follows by induction on
n
.
For
n=1
this is true since
D_{1}:=PR_{1}P^{-1}={{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}}
.
Suppose it is true for
n
, then using (
D.50
) in step two

\begin{split}D_{n+1}:&=PR_{n+1}P^{-1}\\
&=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(PR_{n}P^{-1}+PR_{n}^{-1}P^{-1}PQP^{-1}\right)\\
&=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(D_{n}+D_{n}^{-1}\Lambda\right)\end{split}

is indeed a diagonal matrix.
But now each diagonal position corresponds to a real-valued Heron
iteration (
D.49
). This proves that the limit
R
exists.

Symmetry: Given
\xi,\eta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
, then

\left\langle R_{n}\xi,\eta\right\rangle=\left\langle\xi,R_{n}\eta\right\rangle
by symmetry of
R_{n}
.
In the limit, as
n\to\infty
, we get

\left\langle R\xi,\eta\right\rangle=\left\langle\xi,R\eta\right\rangle
.

(iii) By (ii) the limit
R
exists and by the recursion
formula (
D.50
) it satisfies

R=\frac{1}{2}\left(R+R^{-1}Q\right)
,
equivalently
R^{2}=Q
.

Positive definite:
Since the
R_{n}
are symmetric positive definite,
their eigenvalues are real and
\geq 0
.
Since
R=\lim_{n\to\infty}R_{n}
is symmetric
its eigenvalues are real. Since eigenvalues depend continuously
on the matrix the eigenvalues of
R
are
\geq 0
.
Let
\rho
be the smallest eigenvalue of
R
.
Then
\rho\geq 0
and
\rho^{2}
is eigenvalue of
R^{2}=Q
.
Since
Q
is positive definite all eigenvalues are
strictly positive, in particular
\rho^{2}>0
, hence
\rho>0
.
Thus
R
is positive definite.

This concludes the proof of Lemma
D.2
.
∎

If in Corollary
D.4

one assumes in addition symmetric and positive definite for the matrix

B
, then the proof reduces essentially to the existence of a basis
composed of common eigenvectors of
B
and
Q
.

Lemma D.5
.

Let
Q\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k\times k}
be a symmetric positive definite matrix.
Then a symmetric positive definite matrix
B
that commutes with
Q

commutes with
\sqrt{Q}
.

Proof.

Linear algebra tells the following:
Two symmetric positive definite
k\times k
matrices, in the case at
hand
Q
and
B
, commute iff there exists an orthonormal basis

{\mathcal{X}}=\{\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{k}\}
whose elements are eigenvectors of both
matrices, say
Q\xi_{i}=\rho_{i}\xi
and
B\xi_{i}=\lambda_{i}\xi_{i}
for
some
\rho_{i},\lambda_{i}>0
and
i=1,\dots,k
.
The positive square root of
Q
, notation
\sqrt{Q}=:R
, is defined
by
R\xi_{i}:=\sqrt{\rho_{i}}\xi_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,k
.
It is an exercise to check that
R
is symmetric positive definite;
here pairwise orthogonality of the
\xi_{i}
enters.
But the ON basis
{\mathcal{X}}
is composed of eigenvectors of both

R
and
B
, hence both matrices commute by the linear algebra
cited in the beginning of this proof.
This proves Lemma
D.5
.
∎
