Multiple-layer mesh from electrospinnable amyloid‑like protein and their application in treating oral ulcer
Authors: Juanhua Tian, Yujia Zhang, Yan Li, Yongchun Liu, Rundong Song, Shuo Wei, Ziyu Cao, Li Xue, Tie Chong, Peng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oral ulcers significantly impair quality of life due to prolonged severe pain caused by delayed healing. The moist, dynamic, microorganism‑rich environment hinders natural scab formation and ordinary patch adhesion, limiting therapeutic outcomes. Inspired by bioadhesion, we propose an unfolding‑stabilization strategy to overcome globular protein electrospinning limitations and construct a multifunctional patch. Water triggers a conformational transition to an amyloid‑like β‑sheet interface that disrupts the hydration layer for initial wet adhesion, while Carbomer-mucin interactions further enhance retention. This synergy gives adhesion strength 6-50 times that of commercial products, enabling sustained attachment in complex oral environments for effective ulcer protection. Glycyrrhetinic acid provides local antibacterial and anti‑inflammatory effects. In a rat model, the patch improves healing by 75% over the best commercial control. It also matches the mucosal modulus, while being steroid‑free, biodegradable, and biocompatible. By integrating electrospinning with amyloid‑mediated adhesion, this work offers a scalable strategy for engineering multifunctional wet biointerfaces.

Introduction

Oral ulcers affect over 25% of the global population1. Due to the oral mucosa’s inability to form a stable protective scab like skin, the ulcer is continuously exposed to physical friction, chemical stimuli, and microorganisms, resulting in slow natural healing with intense pain and requiring up to two weeks for complete resolution2. Moreover, pain-related difficulties in eating, sleep disturbance, and psychological stress may further lead to immunosuppression, ultimately forming a vicious cycle of impaired healing and potential recurrence3.

Topical therapy serves as the primary intervention for oral ulcers. Although steroids are widely used for symptom relief, their potential adverse effects cannot be overlooked, including mucosal thinning, immunosuppression, secondary infections, and delayed wound healing4. Furthermore, conventional formulations such as mouthwashes and pastes demonstrate limited clinical efficacy, as they fail to establish the necessary physical barrier required to compensate for the compromised natural protection mechanism. In this context, film-forming patches capable of covering the wound and providing isolation protection have gradually emerged as a research hotspot. However, their adhesion stability remains poor in the oral cavity, where the surface hydration layer blocks direct contact between adhesive functional groups and the mucosa. In addition, hydrolysis, swelling, and mechanical disturbances such as chewing, tongue motion, and saliva flow collectively accelerate structural degradation, leading to detachment or adhesive failure within 2 h5,6. Furthermore, many patch formulations suffer from issues such as the requirements for pre-pressing and a tendency for interfacial delamination, which further limit their clinical applicability and patient compliance7. Although emerging strategies, including light-activated gels, dry-film technology, and dopamine-based chemical modifications, have been explored to enhance mucosal adhesion1,8–10. However, these methods still exhibit notable limitations: polyethylene glycol -based materials suffer reduced adhesion due to excessive water absorption and swelling11; dopamine-based adhesives are sensitive to the oral pH and oxidative environment, resulting in unstable performance12; and some technologies face industrialization challenges such as complex manufacturing processes and high costs9,13,14. Thus, a novel material system that combines stable wet adhesion, biocompatibility, and antibacterial/anti‑inflammatory functions is urgently needed for oral ulcer treatment.

In recent years, electrohydrodynamic atomization techniques, including electrospinning and electrospraying, have shown broad potential in drug delivery and biointerface engineering15,16. Electrospinning has advanced from single-fluid to complex multi-fluid configurations (e.g., coaxial, side-by-side, and triaxial), enabling precise structural and functional control17–22. Nevertheless, these advances largely rely on synthetic polymers with good spinnability. In contrast, protein-based materials, despite their inherent biocompatibility and biofunctionality, are constrained by low chain flexibility, insufficient molecular entanglement, unstable rheological behavior, and restricted solvent and interfacial conditions23. Consequently, achieving stable electrospinning of proteins without carrier polymers remains challenging.

To address these limitations, this study proposes a class of wet-adhesive materials based on amyloid-like proteins with structural features analogous to barnacle adhesive proteins. In nature, the strong underwater adhesion of barnacles arises from the formation of amyloid-like fibrillar adhesive proteins24. However, compared with conventional globular proteins, amyloid proteins present greater challenges in electrospinning processing. Their strong intermolecular interactions promote rapid and irreversible aggregation, while their highly ordered β-sheet-rich structures reduce chain deformability and entanglement capability, thereby hindering the formation of a stable Taylor cone and a continuous electrospinning jet25,26. To overcome this challenge, we employ a disulfide bond reductant, tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP) and the amphiphilic solvent 2,2,2-trifluoroethanol (TFE) to molecularly unfold a model globular protein, bovine serum albumin (BSA) and maintain its extended conformation, thereby imparting it with spinnability. The resulting pure BSA nanofibrous mesh (BFM) exhibits a Young’s modulus comparable to that of the oral mucosa, which helps prevent detachment and discomfort caused by modulus mismatch. Upon wetting, BFM undergoes a conformational transition to form β‑sheet‑rich amyloid‑like structures with exposed hydrophobic regions. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) force spectroscopy shows that this transition increases the interfacial adhesion force of BFM to 2.7-fold of its original value, enabling reliable initial adhesion on the mucosal surface. Meanwhile, the Carbomer in the middle layer facilitates BFM in disrupting the hydration layer, and its swollen chains diffuse toward the BFM surface, thereby anchoring the patch while simultaneously forming specific molecular interactions with mucin to enhance adhesion. This synergistic effect ultimately enables robust and durable adhesion of the BFM-C (BFM composite with Carbomer) patch to the oral mucosa via multiple mechanisms, including physical interpenetration, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic interactions. The adhesion strength of BFM‑C is 6–50 times higher than that of commercial products, enabling effective resistance against fluid rinsing, cyclic mechanical loading, oral muscle movement, and chewing friction. Furthermore, functional integration is accomplished by co-electrospinning glycyrrhetinic acid (GA), an herbal extract with antibacterial and anti-inflammatory properties, into the BFM. The multifunctional wet-adhesive patch demonstrates pronounced efficacy in the rat model, attaining 96% ulcer closure (based on the proportion of the initial ulcer area covered by regenerated tissue), which signifies a 75% improvement over the best-performing commercial product (55%). Additional validation in a larger rabbit model further confirms its efficacy and biosafety, highlighting its broad applicability and promising potential for clinical translation. (Fig. 1). The principal innovations of this study lie in the creative integration of a biomimetic wet adhesion mechanism with electrospinning technology, thereby overcoming the scalability constraint in producing macroscopic amyloid-like protein materials. The resulting adhesive patch significantly accelerates oral ulcer healing and improves structural repair integrity. Results

Preparation and characterization of BFM

The electrospinning of BSA first requires the disruption of the intramolecular disulfide bonds that stabilize its native globular conformation. Based on our previous research, we employed TCEP as a reducing agent to cleave these disulfide bonds, thereby inducing protein unfolding and subsequent exposure of previously buried intermolecular interaction sites (Fig. 2a)27. However, the unfolded BSA chains alone tended to aggregate rapidly in aqueous environments (Supplementary Fig. 1a), completely losing spinnability. To address this challenge, an amphiphilic fluoroalcohol solvent was introduced to maintain proteins in an extended conformation and facilitate intermolecular entanglement. Among commonly used fluoroalcohols, TFE provides a favorable balance of conformational control, process stability, safety, and cost. In contrast, hexafluoroisopropanol may induce excessive protein aggregation, and its low boiling point can lead to nozzle clogging, whereas trifluoroacetic acid is highly acidic and corrosive28–31. Therefore, TFE was selected as the spinning solvent and combined with a disulfide-bond reducing agent to enhance protein conformational flexibility and intermolecular entanglement. As a result, the viscosity of the TCEP-treated BSA/TFE system was markedly higher than that of native BSA dissolved in TFE alone over the concentration range of 40–140 mg/mL (Fig. 2b). The importance of this synergistic effect was confirmed by control experiments. Although native BSA could dissolve in TFE without TCEP treatment, insufficient chain entanglement resulted only in micron-sized particles on the collector (Supplementary Fig. 1b, c). In contrast, without TFE, the unfolded proteins underwent irreversible aggregation, leading to spinning failure (Supplementary Fig. 1a). By combining protein unfolding with conformational stabilization, BSA chains could be effectively stretched and aligned under the electric field and subsequently solidified into a uniform nanofibrous mesh upon solvent evaporation (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 1d, e). This strategy overcame the inherently poor spinnability of globular BSA and enabled the fabrication of pure protein nanofibrous meshes without the incorporation of synthetic polymers. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) further confirmed the effective removal of residual solvent, as no detectable F 1 s signal was observed at ~686 eV in the wide-scan spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 2)32. Consequently, concerns regarding fluorinated-solvent residues were minimized. The fabricated BFM exhibits tunable structural and performance characteristics, demonstrating its adaptability for diverse biomedical applications34. Specifically, the fiber diameter demonstrated a positive correlation with the BSA concentration in the spinning solution. Furthermore, by adjusting the pH of TCEP to control the reaction extent between BSA and TCEP27, the fiber diameter could be tuned within a range of approximately 100–250 nm (Fig. 2d–f, Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 4). Mechanical characterization through tensile testing demonstrated that the BFM exhibited a breaking strength of 1.5 MPa, ~10% elongation at break, and a Young’s modulus of 19.2 ± 7.0 kPa (Fig. 2g). Compared with rigid polymeric materials, its Young’s modulus is closer to that of oral mucosa (1–10 kPa), which may enhance patient comfort and minimize interfacial instability arising from mechanical mismatch35. In addition, the use of a high-speed rotating collector enables fiber alignment, increasing the tensile strength to 6 MPa (approximately fourfold higher than 1.5 MPa) (Fig. 2g, h). Furthermore, the versatility of this approach was demonstrated by successfully fabricating nanofibrous meshes with similar morphology under varied conditions, including hybridization with other proteins (e.g., lysozyme, fibrinogen, and collagen; Supplementary Fig. 5) or replacing TCEP with other reducing agents such as cysteine (Cys) or glutathione (GSH; Supplementary Fig. 6). Taken together, the integration of the unfolding-stabilization strategy with electrospinning establishes a scalable and adaptable platform towards protein electrospinning. The proposed methodology enables precise tuning of BFM properties while leveraging the natural abundance and inherent biocompatibility of BSA, offering a versatile platform for sustainable and safe biomedical materials in tissue engineering and drug delivery36.

Water-driven conformational transition and enhanced wet adhesion

The demand for wet adhesion materials has become increasingly prominent across various biomedical fields, particularly in tissue repair and biosensing applications. In this context, barnacle cement, a naturally occurring adhesive, has attracted considerable attention, with its abundant amyloid structure being identified as a key factor responsible for the exceptional wet adhesion property. These structures provide an exceptional mechanical support for wet adhesion by forming stable intermolecular hydrogen-bond networks and enhancing interfacial interactions37. Similarly, in our previously studied instantly adhesive protein-based nanofilms, β-sheet-driven conformational transitions were also demonstrated to offer interfacial adaptability and superior adhesive performance38,39. These findings suggest that similar mechanisms may be applicable to amyloid-like protein-based electrospinning systems to achieve reliable wet adhesion. To validate this hypothesis, it is necessary to investigate the dynamic conformational evolution of BSA during electrospinning and subsequent adhesion, as well as the distribution of surface functional groups, thereby elucidating the interfacial interaction mechanisms of BFM and enabling further optimization of its adhesive performance.

In the spinning solution, the gradual increase in fluorescence intensity of N-(1-pyrenyl)maleimide (NPM) indicated a rise in free thiol groups, confirming that TCEP effectively reduced disulfide bonds in BSA, thereby inducing protein unfolding (Fig. 3a)40. Meanwhile, the amphiphilic solvent TFE stabilized this unfolded state. Accordingly, attenuated total reflection infrared (ATR-IR) spectroscopy showed that the secondary structure of BFM closely resembled that of native BSA and BSA in the spinning solution, with no significant differences in the deconvoluted amide I bands, and the β-sheet content remained below 33% (Fig. 3b). Notably, this conformational stabilization by TFE was independent of the reducing agent used (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). Upon exposure to water, the unfolded BSA chains within the BFM regained conformational freedom and rapidly underwent a structural transition toward β-sheet–rich amyloid-like assemblies (Fig. 3b), resembling the adhesive motifs found in barnacle cement proteins41. This conformational transition was supported by ATR-IR spectroscopy, as evidenced by a red shift of the amide I band from 1650 cm⁻1 to 1639 cm⁻1 (Fig. 3b) and a marked increase in β-sheet content, reaching 50.6% according to quantitative deconvolution (Fig. 3c). Furthermore, Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) directly visualized nanocrystalline structures with a lattice spacing of 0.24 nm in the water-treated BFM, corresponding to half the hydrogen-bonding distance in face-to-face staggered β-sheet stacking, whereas such ordered nanostructures were largely absent in the untreated sample (Fig. 3d, e)42. Wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS) of BFM showed characteristic diffraction peaks at 4.5 Å and 9.5–10 Å, corresponding to the intramolecular hydrogen-bond periodicity and intersheet stacking distances of β-sheets, respectively39. Upon water treatment, both the intensity and sharpness of these two peaks increased significantly (Fig. 3f), indicating increased β-sheet content and improved structural ordering. The binding of Congo red and Thioflavin T (ThT) to the β-sheet domains (Supplementary Fig. 8) further validated the formation of the resulting amyloid-like protein structures. Concomitant with this water-induced conformational transition, the surface functional groups of BFM also underwent substantial rearrangement. Raman spectroscopy indicated that the stabilizing effect of TFE resulted in surface chemical characteristics of untreated BFM that closely resembled those of native BSA43. However, after water treatment, disulfide bond content decreased to 46% of the untreated state, while hydrophobic phenylalanine (Phe) residues increased by 1.5-fold; in contrast, hydrophilic residues such as cysteine (Cys), histidine (His), and aspartic acid (Asp) decreased to 24%, 48%, and 89%, respectively (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 9). High-resolution XPS C 1 s spectra revealed an increase in the O = C–O component, alongside a decrease in O = C–N, C–S, and C–N signals (Supplementary Fig. 10), indicating a water-driven redistribution of surface functionalities44. Additionally, exposure of various surface functional groups, including aliphatic carbon, hydroxyl, and aromatic moieties (Supplementary Fig. 10), could further facilitate multiple binding interactions with the substrate45.

In summary, water effectively triggers the reorganization of unfolded BSA chains in BFM into β‑sheet‑rich structures, accompanied by the exposure of hydrophobic residues, thereby disrupting the hydration layer on wet surfaces41,46,47. Subsequently, the exposed abundant functional groups and β‑sheet domains on the BFM surface can reach an interaction distance with the substrate and form stable binding with the mucosal surface through hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and van der Waals forces48. As shown by AFM force spectroscopy, water treatment significantly enhanced the interaction force between BFM and a mucin‑modified probe (Supplementary Fig. 11), increasing the normalized adhesion force from 45.7 mN/m to 121.8 mN/m, approximately 2.7 times that of the untreated sample (Fig. 3h, i). Overall, these results indicate that water acts as a triggering factor that simultaneously induced β-sheet-dominated structural reorganization and surface chemical remodeling in BFM, thereby synergistically enhancing its interfacial adhesion performance under wet conditions.

To further enhance the wet adhesion of electrospun mesh, we incorporated Carbomer (C3H4O2)n—an FDA-approved bioadhesive polymer widely used in oral drug delivery systems—between two layers of BFM to form a sandwich-like structure (BFM-C) (Fig. 4a)49. The incorporation of Carbomer did not hinder the water-driven conformational transition of BFM. As revealed by ATR-IR, XPS, and Raman spectroscopy, the hydrated BFM-C still underwent a secondary structural transition from α-helix to β-sheet, accompanied by further surface enrichment of carboxyl groups and hydrophobic amino acids (tyrosine, Tyr, and Phe) (Supplementary Fig. 12). In contrast to the water-treated BFM, the wetted BFM-C exhibited distinct inter-fiber connections, indicating that the free chains of the swollen Carbomer diffused to the material–biological interface and integrated with the surfaces of BFM fibers (Fig. 4b). This spatial distribution is conducive to the interaction of Carbomer with mucosal glycoproteins through hydrogen bonding and physical penetration, thereby enhancing the bioadhesion effect of the patch9. To validate this hypothesis, confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, Fig. 4c) was employed to track the spatial distribution of Cyanine 5 (Cy5)-labeled Carbomer (red) within the BFM matrix fabricated with FITC-conjugated BSA (green) during the wetting process. In the dry state, Carbomer was concentrated in the interlayer of BFM-C, whereas upon wetting the bottom side, Carbomer diffused downward, as evidenced by red fluorescence permeating the lower portion of BFM-C (Fig. 4c). This observation was further corroborated by ATR-IR analysis. Compared with dry BFM-C, the surface layer of hydrated BFM-C exhibited significantly enhanced peak intensities at 3276 cm−1 (OH stretching), 2938 cm−1 (–CH2 stretching), 1444 cm−1 (–CH2 bending), 1241 cm−1 (C–O stretching), and 827 cm−1 (C–H out of plane bending vibrations) (Fig. 4d), which correspond to characteristic absorption bands of Carbomer50,51. Notably, the diffused Carbomer molecules formed intermolecular hydrogen bonds with polar groups (–CONH–, –OH/–NH2) in BFM. This interaction altered the electron cloud density distribution of relevant chemical bonds, consequently modifying their infrared absorption characteristics52. Specifically, water treatment caused significant changes in the IR of BFM-C: the amide II band (representing C-N deformation vibrations) shifted from 1537 cm−1 to 1525 cm−1, while the peak corresponding to –OH/–NH2 groups shifted from 3288 cm−1 to 3276 cm−1 with concomitant peak broadening (Fig. 4d). These intermolecular hydrogen bonds are critical for firmly anchoring the Carbomer onto BFM-C, limiting its mass loss in aqueous environments and thereby ensuring sustained interactions with the mucosa. As shown in Fig. 4e, free powdered Carbomer lost over 40% mass within 5 min and drops below 10% after 1 h in water, while BFM-C retained ~96% even after 8 h, ensuring prolonged adhesion. On the other hand, the hydrophilic nature of Carbomer endowed the composite material with remarkable water absorption properties. As reflected by Fig. 4f, within 5 s of contact with water, BFM-C swelled to 15 times its original mass, with a final swelling ratio exceeding 18 times that of BFM (which exhibited a maximum swelling ratio below 150%). Notably, this high‑swelling core/low‑swelling shell design confines the loss of cohesive strength to the middle layer, thereby leaving the interfacial adhesion strength largely unaffected. Additionally, differential swelling could enhance adhesion either by generating compressive stress or by promoting conformal contact with three‑dimensional tissues53,54. Thus, the sandwich structure of BFM‑C provides a rational architectural basis for reconciling high swelling capacity with robust adhesion. Based on the above concept, we propose a mechanism that integrates biomimetic design with synergistic adhesion. First, upon water activation, the molecular chains in BFM gain conformational freedom and undergo rearrangement into β-sheet-rich amyloid-like structures, accompanied by exposure of hydrophobic residues, thereby disrupting the interfacial hydration layer and mimicking the wet adhesion strategy of barnacles. The abundant functional groups and β-sheet domains on BFM mediate initial substrate attachment via hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and van der Waals forces, while its low-swelling nature stabilizes the interface. Upon water absorption, Carbomer chains diffuse toward the interface, fuse with the BFM fibrous network, and interact with mucin through hydrogen bonding and physical interpenetration, further reinforcing adhesion. Ultimately, BFM and Carbomer synergistically construct a multivalent network, combining hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, electrostatic forces, and physical interpenetration, thereby achieving stable adhesion to wet biological surfaces (Fig. 4g).

Adhesion performances of the BFM-C mucoadhesive patch on wet tissue

Based on the proposed wet adhesion mechanism, we further investigated the specific interactions between the bioadhesive polymer Carbomer and mucosa, as well as the contribution of the sandwich architecture to the overall performance. To distinguish the swelling behavior of carbomer from its specific interfacial interactions with mucin, starch with swelling capability but negligible adhesive properties was selected as a control, and its low-molecular-weight hydrolysis product dextrin was used as a soluble model compound to probe polymer–mucin interactions at the molecular level. Meanwhile, poly(acrylic acid) (PAA) was employed as a linear soluble model of the poly(acrylic acid) backbone of Carbomer, enabling a comparable assessment of interaction and interfacial behavior in solution. First, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was used to analyze the intermolecular interactions between PAA or dextrin and mucin. Compared with pure mucin, the mixture of PAA and mucin exhibited a noticeable overlap between the amide I band and the carbonyl absorption of PAA. In addition, a significant increase in absorption intensity was observed in the O–H stretching region (3000–3700 cm−1), accompanied by a distinct shoulder peak at approximately 3400 cm⁻1. These spectral changes indicate the formation of an intermolecular hydrogen-bonding network between PAA and mucin (Fig. 5a)55. In contrast, no such pronounced spectral variations were observed for the dextrin–mucin mixture under the same conditions, suggesting weak intermolecular interactions and the absence of stable specific binding. To further determine whether these intermolecular interactions can be translated into effective interfacial binding, a dissipative quartz crystal microbalance (QCM-D) was employed to investigate their adsorption behavior on mucin‑modified surfaces. Under identical concentration and flow conditions, PAA induced a significantly larger frequency shift (Δf) than dextrin. After rinsing with ultrapure water to remove unbound polymers, the adsorbed mass of PAA on the mucin surface remained approximately 110 ng/cm2, whereas no measurable increase in adsorbed mass was observed for dextrin. From a macroscopic perspective, the BFM-C exhibited a high adhesion strength of 22.67 kPa, whereas single-layer BFM, BFM/PAA blended meshes, and starch-interlayer sandwich meshes showed markedly lower values of 2.73, 0.83, and 3.13 kPa, respectively (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 13). Consistently, the interfacial fracture energy of BFM-C reached 127.4 kJ/m2, which is 12.3-, 35.3-, and 76.4-fold higher than that of the above corresponding controls. Additionally, compared with previously reported electrospun and non-electrospun adhesive patches, BFM-C exhibits a leading level of adhesive strength (Fig. 5d). These results indicate that neither relying solely on the bioadhesive properties of Carbomer molecules nor a simple sandwich structure with a swellable polymer interlayer was sufficient to achieve the superior performance of BFM-C. Instead, its key advantage arose from the synergistic interplay between Carbomer-mucin molecular interactions and the sandwich architecture. Notably, the increase in fracture energy was significantly greater than that in adhesion strength, suggesting the presence of substantial energy dissipation during debonding. To elucidate this mechanism, the BFM-C patch and the adhered tissue surface were examined after the pull-off test. The results show that BFM-C detached from the tissue surface as an intact film without visible residues, indicating interfacial failure as the dominant failure mode. However, in comparison with water-wetted but non-contacted samples, pronounced cracks were observed on the surface of the detached BFM-C, indicating irreversible deformation and partial fracture of the fibrous network during debonding (Fig. 5e). Moreover, the dissociation of intermolecular interactions between BFM-C and the mucosal surface also significantly contributed to energy dissipation. Therefore, the high interfacial toughness of BFM-C primarily arose from multiple synergistic energy dissipation mechanisms rather than cohesive failure.

Building on these findings, we further evaluated the adhesion stability of BFM-C under dynamic conditions. BFM-C remained firmly attached to tissue surfaces under stretching, bending, and torsional deformation (Fig. 5f). After 200 cyclic loading cycles, it retained 89.6% of its initial adhesion strength on porcine oral mucosa (Fig. 5g). In simulated saliva at 37 °C under continuous shaking (100 rpm), BFM-C retained 84.0%, 74.4%, and 61.2% of its initial adhesion strength after 3, 12, and 24 h, respectively. Although a decreasing trend was observed over time, even after 24 h, its absolute adhesion strength remained significantly higher than the initial values of commercial products (0.45–3.89 kPa), thereby contributing to prolonged effective adhesion on mucosal surfaces (Fig. 5h). To further evaluate its durability under fluid shear stress conditions, a rotating disk test (300 rpm) was conducted. All commercial patches, as well as single-layer BFM, BFM-starch meshes, and BFM/PAA blended meshes, detached or disintegrated within 10 min to 3 h, whereas BFM-C maintained stable adhesion for over 24 h, corresponding to at least an eight-fold increase in durability (Fig. 5i). Furthermore, in a Sprague-Dawley rat oral model, the patches were subjected to food abrasion, tongue movement, and masticatory stress. Both the Z-Film and the three experimental control groups completely dissolved within 5 min, while other commercial products detached within 60–70 min. In contrast, BFM-C remained firmly adhered to the buccal mucosa throughout the 4 h observation period (Fig. 5j). Moreover, BFM-C exhibited reliable adhesion on various wet biological surfaces (skin, heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 14), further highlighting its expandability and versatility. In summary, the strong interfacial interaction between Carbomer and mucin, together with the synergistic sandwich design, constructs a wet adhesive interface with both high adhesion strength and long-term stability. This system enables durable adhesion under complex dynamic physiological environments, providing a promising local delivery platform for oral ulcer treatment.

Filtration turned the suspension from turbid (OD600 = 0.813) to clear (OD600 = 0.005), corresponding to >99% bacterial removal. To further enhance bioactivity and explore its potential as a drug-loading and controlled-release platform, glycyrrhetinic acid (GA) was selected as the model drug. GA is a natural bioactive compound derived from licorice, with multiple activities, including anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and mucosal healing effects. Previous studies have demonstrated that GA enhances epithelial cell adhesion and maintains oral mucosal barrier integrity, while its safety and clinical efficacy have also been validated in commercial oral care formulations such as Gelclair®57,58. Accordingly, GA serves as a model drug to evaluate BFM for local drug delivery and mucosal repair. To this end, glycyrrhetinic acid (GA) was dissolved in the electrospinning solution to prepare GA‑loaded BFM, denoted as BFM(GA). achieving an encapsulation efficiency of 86.6%, and was further composited with Carbomer to form the BFM(GA)-C patch (Fig. 6c). The successful GA loading was confirmed by FTIR (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 15). Characteristic GA peaks at 1664 cm⁻1 (ketone C = O) and 1706 cm⁻1 (carboxylic C = O) overlapped with BFM’s amide I (1540 cm⁻1) and II (1651 cm⁻1) bands, forming a wide double-hump peak in BFM(GA), with amide peaks shifted to 1596 and 1632 cm⁻1 59. Notably, BFM(GA) showed enhanced O-H stretching vibration (3000–3600 cm⁻1) versus individual components. These spectral features confirm that GA interacts with protein polar groups through intermolecular hydrogen bonds (O–H···O = C/N–H), which not only facilitate GA incorporation into the fibrous network but also provide a foundation for sustained release. On this basis, we evaluated the antibacterial activity as a function of GA concentration in the electrospinning solution and found that concentrations above 10 mg/mL produced significant antibacterial effects (Supplementary Fig. 16). In vitro static diffusion assays demonstrated that BFM(GA)-C completely inhibited the growth of E. coli, Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus, associated with secondary oral mucosal infection and aggravated inflammation), Streptococcus mutans (S. mutans, capable of inducing immune-inflammatory responses and exacerbating ulcer severity), and Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis, reported to inhibit oral epithelial cell migration and delay wound healing), with no visible colonies observed in the contact region60,61. This was in sharp contrast to the blank control, BFM, and BFM-C groups (Fig. 6e), demonstrating that BFM(GA)-C exhibits broad-spectrum antibacterial activity against both aerobic/facultative anaerobic and obligate anaerobic bacteria, as well as Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. The in vitro release experiment demonstrated that the release profile of GA from BFM(GA)-C in simulated saliva followed a biphasic pattern, being well-fitted by two segmented zero-order kinetic equations (R2 ≥ 0.95). During the initial release phase (within 4 h), approximately 60% of GA was released, followed by a slower release profile in the next 6 h, with the total cumulative release reaching approximately 80% (Fig. 6f). This biphasic release behavior is advantageous for rapidly and efficiently inhibiting pathogen growth and invasion in the early stages, while the slower release in the later stage provides prolonged therapeutic effects to offer sustained support for ulcer repair. Considering both its barrier function and antibacterial performance, BFM(GA)-C demonstrates potential as an advanced functional patch for oral ulcer treatment.

Nevertheless, for oral ulcer repair materials, biocompatibility and biodegradability are as essential as their functionality, which is the core consideration driving our commitment to developing protein‑based materials. To validate these, the effects of the three BFM‑based materials (BFM, BFM‑C, and BFM(GA)‑C) on L929 fibroblast proliferation were first assessed via an extraction method. Using complete culture medium as a control, no significant cytotoxicity was observed at 24, 48, and 72 h (Fig. 6g and Supplementary Fig. 17). Furthermore, a hemolysis ratio of less than 1.5% (below the 5% threshold defined by ASTM F756-08, Supplementary Fig. 18) confirmed the blood compatibility of the BFM-C and BFM(GA)-C in vitro. To further evaluate in vivo tissue compatibility, oral mucosal irritation tests were conducted in SD rats. Macroscopically, the buccal mucosa showed no signs of irritation, including congestion, swelling, and erosion. Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining further confirmed that neither BFM‑C nor BFM(GA)‑C induced pathological changes such as inflammation, necrosis, or keratinization (Fig. 6h). Despite the good biocompatibility, the potential risk of accidental ingestion must be addressed for an oral patch. Therefore, the degradation behavior of these materials in the digestive system was investigated. As shown in Fig. 6i, all materials degraded completely in simulated gastric fluid (SGF) within only 20 min, leaving no significant solid residues. Moreover, Matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) analysis of the digestive products confirmed the complete enzymatic digestion of BSA molecules, as evidenced by the absence of the characteristic 66 kDa peak (the molecular weight of BSA) (Fig. 6j). These results indicate that accidental ingestion of BFM(GA)-C is unlikely to cause physical obstruction or other gastrointestinal injuries, mitigating the safety concerns associated with non-degradable biomaterials. The combination of biocompatibility and desired gastric degradability ensures safe application, even during sleep or in vulnerable populations such as the elderly and infants, supporting its potential for clinical translation.

Therapeutic efficacy of the BFM(GA)-C patch in vivo

Thereafter, the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of the BFM(GA)-C patch on oral ulcers was investigated. Standardized acid‑induced injury was used to create mucosal defects on the buccal mucosa of SD rats as a model of oral ulcers (Supplementary Fig. 19)9. Untreated ulcers served as the negative control, while Z-Film, Y-Patch (steroid-containing), and M-Spray were used as commercial controls, representing film, tablet, and spray formulations, respectively. BFM(GA)-C patches were used as the experimental treatment group. Ulcer size changes in each group were continuously recorded over 7 days (Fig. 7a). On day 1 post-treatment, H&E staining showed epithelial layer necrosis across all groups, manifested as cell membrane rupture, nuclear fragmentation, dissolution, and even mucosal layer shedding, presenting a typical ulcer morphology. Notably, CD11b-positive cells (a leukocyte marker) were confined to the lamina propria in the BFM(GA)-C group, whereas extensive leukocyte infiltration into the lamina propria and underlying submucosa was observed in the untreated and commercial product groups (Fig. 7b)62. This suggested that early intervention with BFM(GA)-C patches could effectively mitigate local inflammatory responses. After 7 days of treatment, the untreated and commercial product groups exhibited limited efficacy, with the degree of ulcer closure ranging from 49–55%. In contrast, the BFM(GA)-C patch group achieved near-complete healing (approximately 96%), corresponding to a healing acceleration of at least 75% (Fig. 7c, d). Histological analysis further supported these observations. H&E staining revealed incomplete epithelialization and unclosed ulcers in the commercial product groups and the untreated group, accompanied by infiltration of cells with a high nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio, indicating the presence of the inflammatory stage in these groups. Conversely, the BFM(GA)-C group showed complete coverage by regenerated epithelium and abundant underlying connective tissue (Fig. 7c). Immunohistochemical staining for CK5 (expressed in the basal layer where proliferating cells are located) and CK13 (expressed in the intermediate/parabasal layer) further confirmed tissue regeneration: in the BFM(GA)-C group, the ulcers were completely covered by newly formed epithelium with clear stratification (superficial, intermediate, and basal layers from top to bottom), resembling normal buccal mucosa, whereas neither the untreated nor the commercial product groups achieved complete basal cell coverage (Fig. 7c)1. Routine blood tests at the end of the intervention revealed that white blood cells (WBC), monocytes (Mon), and granulocytes (Gran) were elevated to varying degrees in the commercial and untreated groups compared with baseline, with the total WBC count in the control group exceeding twice the baseline level. In contrast, these inflammatory cell counts in the BFM(GA)-C group remained near baseline (Fig. 7e), demonstrating its potent anti-inflammatory efficacy. Next, we evaluated the biosafety of the BFM(GA)-C patch from multiple aspects. H&E staining of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) revealed no pathological changes in animals treated with BFM(GA)-C (Supplementary Fig. 20). Serum biochemical indicators including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatinine (CREA), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) also supported its favorable biosafety profile (Fig. 7f and Supplementary Fig. 21). Notably, Y‑Patch treatment elevated ALT to more than three times the baseline level (Fig. 7f), accompanied by hepatocyte ballooning and damage (Supplementary Fig. 21), indicating severe liver dysfunction. Changes in body weight among the treatment groups not only reflected biosafety but also indicated pain relief efficacy, as severe oral ulcers can impair food intake and lead to weight loss. As shown in Fig. 7f, rats in the BFM(GA)-C group gained 20% in body weight steadily throughout the study, outperforming the commercial and untreated groups, while the Y‑Patch group showed a 24% loss from baseline. The adverse effects of Y‑Patch were attributed to its poor mucosal adhesion, leading to accidental ingestion and systemic steroid exposure, hence its contraindication for children and pregnant women. In contrast, our steroid‑free BFM(GA)-C patch is comfortable and highly safe, making it suitable for a wide range of patients, including children, pregnant women, and those with hepatic or renal dysfunction63.

To further validate the cross-species generalizability of this patch, we conducted a parallel study in rabbits using untreated animals as the control group. On day 5 of treatment, the average ulcer closure degree in the BFM(GA)-C group reached 85.7%, which was 2.3 times that of the control group (Fig. 7g, h). H&E and Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS, binds to basal layer glycoproteins) staining revealed that the ulcer surface in the BFM(GA)-C group was covered by a newly formed, intact basal layer, with no obvious local aggregation of CD45‑positive cells, indicating mild inflammation. In contrast, the ulcers in the control group remained far from complete healing and displayed severe inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 7g, i). Histological examination of major organs and serum biochemical parameters once again consistently confirmed the reliable biosafety of BFM(GA)-C (Fig. 7j and Supplementary Fig. 22). Collectively, these cross‑species data are highly consistent, demonstrating the universal applicability and high clinical translational potential of BFM(GA)-C.

Discussion

In this study, BSA was used as a representative protein to establish a conformational programming strategy by precisely regulating the unfolding-stabilization state of globular proteins, enabling direct electrospinning of protein nanofibers without synthetic polymers. The resulting nanofibrous mesh BFM exhibits a Young’s modulus matching that of oral mucosa and undergoes rapid water-triggered conformational rearrangement into a β-sheet-rich structure with exposed hydrophobic residues, thereby mimicking the adhesion mechanism of natural amyloids at wet interfaces. This transition enhances interfacial adhesion strength by 2.7-fold. Furthermore, by integrating BFM with the bioadhesive polymer Carbomer in a sandwich architecture, the composite BFM-C synergistically disrupts the interfacial hydration layer and achieves robust wet adhesion through multivalent noncovalent interactions. The adhesion strength is 6–50 times higher than that of commercial products, while maintaining stability under dynamic oral conditions. After further loading with GA, the BFM(GA)-C patch integrates barrier protection, antibacterial, and anti‑inflammatory functions. achieving a 96% wound closure rate in a rat oral ulcer model and accelerating healing by 75% compared with the best-performing commercial control. Consistent outcomes were further validated in a rabbit model, indicating good cross-species reproducibility and translational potential.

At the conceptual level, this work introduces two key advances. Firstly, the unfolding-stabilization strategy balances chain extensibility with intermolecular aggregation propensity, overcoming the intrinsic difficulty of electrospinning amyloid-like proteins arising from highly ordered β-sheet structures and strong intermolecular interactions. Unlike conventional approaches relying on blending with spinnable polymers, core–shell architectures, or exogenous additives, this strategy enables intrinsic regulation of spinnability through protein conformational states. Secondly, this study employs a water-triggered amyloid-like conformational transition as the central driving force for wet adhesion. This transition disrupts the interfacial hydration layer and exposes adhesive functional domains, thereby activating wet adhesion through protein-intrinsic conformational responsiveness.

Compared with existing wet adhesion systems that rely on synthetic polymers or exogenous chemical crosslinkers, this protein-based platform offers inherent advantages in biocompatibility, biodegradability, and processability. Combined with the scalability and structural tunability of electrospinning, this approach provides a generalizable paradigm for designing adhesive materials tailored to wet biological interfaces. This strategy is potentially applicable to diverse globular proteins and reducing conditions, with further promise for broader wound healing, mucosal drug delivery, and mucosal immunotherapy.

Materials

Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) and glutathione (GSH) were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (China). Tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP) and 2,2,2-trifluoroethanol (TFE) were purchased from TCI. Cysteine (Cys) and branched starch were purchased from Aladdin. N-(1-pyrenyl) maleimide (NPM) and Thioflavin T (ThT) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Glycyrrhetinic acid (GA) was purchased from RHAWN. Carbomer (Mw = 450000) was purchased from Beijing Vokai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Poly(acrylic acid) (PAA) was purchased from Adamas-beta. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), ethanol, sodium hydroxide (NaOH), hydrochloric acid (HCl), sodium chloride (NaCl), acetic acid, and Tween 80 were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Staphylococcus aureus (S. au, ATCC 6538), Escherichia coli (E. coli, ATCC 25922), Streptococcus mutans (S. mutans, ATCC 25175), and Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis, ATCC 33277) were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (USA). Simulated saliva was purchased from Leagene (Beijing, China). Paraformaldehyde, Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), Mueller–Hinton broth (MHB), Mueller–Hinton agar (MHA), Brain Heart Infusion (BHI), trypsin-EDTA solution, pepsin, basic cell culture media and 3‑(4,5‑dimethylthiazol‑2‑yl)‑2,5‑diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) were purchased from Solarbio. Isoflurane was purchased from Rayward (Shenzhen, China).

Preparation of spinning solution

BSA was completely dissolved in 9 mL of TFE, and then 1 mL of TCEP aqueous solution (250 mM, pH = 9 unless otherwise specified) was added. After mixing evenly, the mixture was reacted at 25 °C for 3 h to form a viscous, translucent and slightly yellow spinning solution. Spinning solution systems using Cys or GSH as reducing agents were prepared under identical conditions, with the exception that 1 mL of un-pH-adjusted Cys aqueous solution (250 mM) or 1 mL of 250 mM GSH aqueous solution (pH 7) was used to replace TCEP in the aforementioned formulation.

Preparation of BFM, BFM-C and BFM(GA)-C by electrospinning

Unless otherwise specified, all BFMs mentioned in this article were prepared using TCEP as the reducing agent. The spinning solution, after reaction for 3 h, was loaded into a 5 mL syringe fitted with a 20 G needle (inner diameter 0.6 mm). Electrospinning was carried out using a high-voltage electrospinning apparatus (ET-2535, Ucalery, China) under the following conditions: positive voltage of 20 kV, negative voltage of 3 kV, tip-to-collector distance of 8 cm, and feeding rate of 5 mL/h. Depending on the application, either a stationary flat collector or a high-speed rotating collector was used to obtain randomly oriented or aligned BFMs, respectively. After spinning, a single layer of BFM could be peeled off from the tin foil covering the collector.

For the fabrication of BFM-C, a base layer of BFM was first prepared following the aforementioned electrospinning conditions. Subsequently, Carbomer powder was then uniformly deposited onto the BFM surface using a dry powder sieving method. Briefly, the powder was placed on a 400-mesh sieve covering the entire substrate, and gently tapped to achieve even distribution. The BFM substrate was positioned on an analytical balance during deposition to monitor mass changes in real time, and the deposited amount was controlled by normalizing the mass increase to the substrate area, yielding a deposition density of 0.5 mg/cm2. Finally, a second layer of BFM was electrospun directly onto the Carbomer-coated surface, resulting in a composite-structured BFM-C material.

For BFM(GA)-C, 1.2 g of BSA and different masses of GA were dissolved in 9 mL of TFE, and 1 mL of TCEP aqueous solution was added and mixed evenly to obtain a spinning solution. After reacting at 25 °C for 3 h, BFM(GA) was spun using an electrospinning machine at a positive high voltage of 20 kV, negative voltage of 3 kV, and a receiving distance of 8 cm. Subsequently, BFM(GA)-C was obtained by sandwiching Carbomer (0.5 mg/cm2) between two layers of BFM(GA).

Mechanical properties of BFM

The non-oriented and oriented BFM were cut into strips with a length of 20 mm and a width of 10 mm. The samples were fixed between the clamps of a universal material testing machine, ensuring that the samples were in a naturally extended state without any applied force. The initial distance between the two clamps was adjusted to 10 mm. During the tensile test, the displacement speed of the clamps was set to 10 mm/min, and the force-displacement curve was recorded. The Young’s modulus is determined as the slope of the linear fit applied to the initial elastic region of the stress-strain curve.

Wide‑angle X‑ray scattering (WAXS) characterization of BFM before and after water treatment

WAXS measurements of untreated BFM and BFM after water treatment followed by vacuum freeze-drying were conducted on a Xenocs system (France). The experimental parameters were set as follows: X-ray wavelength of 0.154 nm, detector pixel size of 75 × 75 μm, and integration time of 300 s.

Determination of swelling ratio of BFM and BFM-C

BFM and BFM-C were cut into several 20 mm × 20 mm square pieces. After weighing the initial mass of each sample, they were immersed in water and taken out after immersion for 5 s, 15 s, 30 s, 60 s, 300 s, and 600 s, respectively. The surface water was immediately absorbed by filter paper, and the mass was weighed and recorded to calculate the swelling ratio corresponding to each time point.

Residual mass ratio in liquid environments

Freshly spun BFM-C was cut into several 20 mm × 20 mm squares. After weighing the mass of each sample, it was immersed in water and taken out at 5 min, 1 h, 2 h, 3 h, 5 h, and 7 h, and then freeze-dried in a freeze dryer to remove water. The mass of each sample was weighed again and recorded as the residual mass. The ratio of the residual mass to the initial mass is the residual mass ratio. Carbomer powder was subjected to the same process as a control.

Mucin–polymer interactions were investigated using quartz crystal microbalance with dissipation monitoring (QCM-D)

Experiments were performed on a Q-Sense E1 system (Biolin Scientific, Sweden) by monitoring the changes in resonance frequency (ΔF) and dissipation (ΔD) at the fundamental frequency (4.95 MHz) and the 3rd, 5th, 7th, 9th, and 11th overtones. A cleaned gold chip sensor was mounted in the flow chamber and equilibrated with Milli-Q water. Subsequently, a 1 mg/mL mucin solution was introduced into the chamber. After approximately 45 min, when ΔF and ΔD reached equilibrium, the chamber was rinsed with Milli-Q water to remove unbound mucin, yielding a stable mucin layer. Then, a 1 mg/mL solution of either poly(acrylic acid) (PAA) or dextrin was introduced for about 40 min, followed by a final rinse with Milli‑Q water to remove non‑adsorbed polymer. All measurements were conducted at 25 °C with a flow rate of 200 μL/min. The adsorbed mass was calculated using the Dfind SmartFit model implemented in QSense Dfind software.

Preparation of control patches (BFM-Starch and BFM/PAA blended meshes

The preparation procedure of BFM-Starch was identical to that of BFM-C, except that Carbomer in the middle layer was replaced with branched starch, yielding a BFM–starch–BFM sandwich-structured patch.

For the BFM/PAA blended mesh, poly(acrylic acid) (PAA) was first dissolved in TCEP aqueous solution, while BSA was dissolved in TFE separately. The two solutions were then mixed to achieve a final PAA concentration of 5 mg/mL in the resulting spinning solution. The spinning solution was electrospun under the same conditions as BFM to fabricate PAA‑blended BFM nanofibrous meshes. This mesh exhibited a single-layer structure, in which linear PAA (the precursor of Carbomer) was distributed within the BFM fibrous network, enabling structural comparison of the effect of the sandwich architecture on adhesion performance.

Characterization of adhesion performance

For AFM force measurements, commercially available AFM probes (nominal tip radius of 2.13 μm) were surface-modified by immersion in a 1 mg/mL mucin solution for 24 h. Water-treated BFM samples were freeze-dried to preserve their conformation, while untreated BFM served as a control. Force–distance curves were acquired using a contact model, with the aid of an optical microscope integrated into the Cypher VRS system for positioning.

For the rotating disk method, three square BFM‑C films were adhered to fresh ex vivo porcine liver, alongside control samples and commercial products. The liver was fixed to the wall of a beaker containing PBS buffer and stirred at 1000 rpm to apply fluid shear forces. Adhesion of each sample was recorded at set time intervals.

For the pull-off test, the porcine oral mucosa was fixed to one side clamp of a universal testing machine (UTM2103, Shenzhen Suns Technology STOCK Co., Ltd.) using bio-glue, while BFM-C or other control patches adhered to the porcine’s oral mucosa were fixed to the other side clamp. The clamp on one side was moved at a loading speed of 10 mm/min until the sample was completely peeled off, and the force-displacement curve was recorded throughout the process. The interfacial fracture energy was calculated by integrating the area under the force–displacement curve during peeling and normalizing it to the area of the adhesive interface.

For oral adhesion assessment in rats, 6-week-old male Sprague Dawley rats (200 ± 40 g) were fully anesthetized with isoflurane. Control patches or BFM-C were applied to the same location on the buccal mucosa, and their adhesion was recorded using optical photography at specific time points.

Shielding effect against bacteria

BFM was cut into circles with a diameter of 50 mm as filter membranes, and E. coli suspended in PBS was filtered using a suction filtration device. The filtered mesh was then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde solution, and the samples were dehydrated with 50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, and 90% ethanol in sequence. The morphology of the upper and lower surfaces of the filter membrane and the distribution of bacteria were observed using SEM.

Evaluation of the bactericidal effect of BFM(GA)

Logarithmic-phase bacterial suspensions (107 CFU/mL, 50 μL) of Escherichia coli (E. coli), Staphylococcus aureus (S. au), Streptococcus mutans (S. mutans), and Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis) were spread onto the surface of corresponding solid agar plates. Test samples were then attached to the inoculated agar surfaces. After incubation at 37 °C under appropriate conditions (aerobic for E. coli, S. aureus, and S. mutans; anaerobic for P. gingivalis) for a suitable period, the plates were inverted, and bacterial colonies were observed and photographed.

In vitro release of GA

30 mg of BFM(GA)-C was completely immersed in 20 mL of simulated saliva containing 2% Tween 80 for in vitro release and incubated on a shaker at 37 °C. At specific intervals, 1 mL of the solution was aspirated as a sample to determine the concentration of GA in the release medium, and then 1 mL of fresh release medium was added. The ultraviolet absorbance of the sample at 264 nm was further detected by an ultraviolet spectrophotometer, and the concentration and cumulative release amount of GA in the release medium were obtained by referring to the standard curve, thereby plotting an in vitro release curve of GA.

Cytotoxicity and in vivo mucosal compatibility

The toxicity of the samples to mouse fibroblast L929 (ATCC, CCL 1) was evaluated by the extraction method (ISO 10993-5:2009). First, the UV-sterilized samples were immersed in DMEM complete medium containing 10% FBS at a ratio of 6 cm2/mL. The samples were placed in a cell culture incubator and cultured for 24 h to obtain extracts. Complete medium was treated in the same way for 24 h as the control group. After L929 cells were completely attached, the complete medium in the well was replaced with a mixture of 200 μL of the extract and DMEM complete medium (the extract accounted for 1/2, 1/4, and 1/8 of the total volume, respectively). After continuing to culture for 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h, the medium was replaced with a solution of MTT in DMEM (0.5 mg/mL) and incubated for another 3 h. Finally, the MTT/DMEM solution was replaced with DMSO for color development, and the absorbance of each well at 490 nm was measured using a UV/vis spectrum.

To assess mucosal compatibility, BFM-C and BFM(GA)-C were tested according to the irritation and skin sensitization tests in the biological evaluation criteria for medical devices specified in ISO 10993-10:2017. The BFM-C or BFM(GA)-C patch was attached to the buccal mucosal surface of male Sprague Dawley rats (6 weeks old, weighing 200 ± 40 g). After 4 h, the rats were anesthetized with isoflurane, and the buccal mucosa was photographed and recorded to observe whether the mucosa was irritated, damaged, congested, swollen, eroded, and ulcerated, and compared with the buccal mucosa of healthy rats. Subsequently, all rats were sacrificed to collect the buccal mucosal area with the attached patch. The tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution and stained with H&E to examine the mucosal structure under a microscope.

In vitro SGF degradation experiment

First, SGF was prepared according to the following formula: 3.84 mL of concentrated hydrochloric acid was diluted to about 800 mL with water, and 10 g of pepsin was added to it, and the volume was fixed to 1000 mL to obtain SGF. In order to verify the degradation of the electrospun meshes in SGF, 20 mg of BFM, BFM-C and BFM(GA)-C were respectively placed in glass bottles containing 20 mL of SGF. The samples were incubated in a thermostatic oscillator controlled at 37 °C, and photos of each group of samples in SGF were taken at 0 min, 10 min, and 20 min. Finally, after the solid completely disappeared, the remaining liquid in the bottle was used for MALDI-TOF to determine the molecular weight of the solute in the solution, using sinapinic acid as the matrix compound.

In vivo therapeutic efficiency and biosafety analyses

Forty‑eight male Sprague Dawley rats (6 weeks old, 200 ± 40 g) were anesthetized sequentially. Oral ulcers were induced by placing a circular filter paper (5 mm in diameter) soaked in acetic acid (70%) on the buccal mucosa for 1 min. All rats were randomly divided into 5 groups (n = 8), and their initial body weights were measured. In the following 7 days, Z-Film, Y-Patch, M-Spray and BFM(GA)-C patches were applied to the buccal mucosal ulcers twice a day, and rats without any treatment were set as the control group. The body weight and corresponding ulcer changes of rats in each group were recorded. Half of the animals in each group were euthanized on Days 1 and 7 after treatment, respectively. The mucosa surrounding the ulcers was collected for further H&E staining and immunohistochemical analysis. At the end of the experiment, blood was collected for routine blood tests and biochemical analysis (including ALT, AST, CREA and BUN). Additionally, major organs (including heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney) were also harvested for further histological evaluation. To further validate cross‑species applicability, a parallel study was conducted in rabbits. Eight male New Zealand white rabbits, aged 4–6 months, with body weights of approximately 2.5 kg, were assigned to untreated control and BFM(GA)-C treatment groups.

Immunohistochemistry

All paraffin-embedded tissues were cut into 5 µm thick continuous sections. Tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, antigen retrieval, and quenched in 3% hydrogen peroxide. After blocking with 5% BSA, the slides were incubated with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight, and then incubated with secondary horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled rabbit anti-goat IgG antibodies (Zhongshan Corp.). The sections were visualized with a 3,3’-diaminobenzidine kit (DAB, Zhongshan Corp.) and counterstained with haematoxylin. Primary antibodies used in the staining include CD11b Rabbit monoclonal antibody (abcam, ab133357, 1/20000), CK5 Rabbit monoclonal antibody (abcam, ab52635, 1/200) and CK13 Rabbit monoclonal antibody (Lifespan, LS-C400440-20, 1/100).

Characterizations

Attenuated total reflectance infrared spectroscopy (ATR-IR) spectra were obtained between 400 and 4000 cm−1 with a resolution of 1 cm−1 using an Alpha-T spectrometer (Bruker Tensor 27, Germany). The freeze-dried powder of sample was tableted with KBr for testing. Peak Fit (version 4) was used to analyze FTIR spectra. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was obtained with AXIS ULTRA from Kratos Analytical Ltd., and the binding energies were calibrated by setting C1s peak at 284.6 eV. Field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) observation was conducted on SU8220 (Hitachi, Japan). The UV/vis spectrum was collected by U-3900/3900H (Hitachi). The Raman spectrum was collected using an in Via Reflex (Renishaw) with 785 nm laser excitation. BFM was stained with ThT (0.1 mg/mL) for 1 min and then observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus FV1200, Japan). Relevant data were collected using FV1000 Viewer (version 3.1). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed using a Tecnai G2 F20 transmission electron microscope. Biological samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and dehydrated before observation.

Statistics and reproducibility

All data are mean± S.D. Statistical tests and exact P‑values are provided in the corresponding figures and legends. Statistics were calculated using GraphPad Prism 9. The experiments in Figs. 2g, h, 3d, e, 4b, c, 5e, 6b, h and 7b were repeated three times independently with similar results.
