Arbitrary Spectral Edge of Regular Graphs
Dingding Dong, Theo McKenzie

Abstract
We prove that for each d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 and k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 , the set of limit points of the first k k eigenvalues of sequences of d d -regular graphs is { ( μ 1 , … , μ k ) : d = μ 1 ≥ ⋯ ≥ μ k ≥ 2 ​ d − 1 } . \{(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}):d=\mu_{1}\geq\dots\geq\mu_{k}\geq 2\sqrt{d-1}\}. The result for k = 2 k=2 was obtained by Alon and Wei, and our result confirms a conjecture of theirs. Our proof uses an infinite random graph sampled from a distribution that generalizes the random regular graph distribution. To control the spectral behavior of this infinite object, we show that Huang and Yau’s proof of Friedman’s theorem bounding the second eigenvalue of a random regular graph generalizes to this model. We also bound the trace of the non-backtracking operator, as was done in Bordenave’s separate proof of Friedman’s theorem.

Introduction

For a graph
\mathcal{G}
on
N
vertices, we denote by
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{G})\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{N}(\mathcal{G})
the eigenvalues of the adjacency operator on
\mathcal{G}
. Given a family of graphs
\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2},\ldots
, all of bounded degree
d
for fixed
d\geq 3
, a natural question asks what are the possible limit points of the
k
-dimensional vectors
(\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{G}_{i}),\ldots,\lambda_{k}(\mathcal{G}_{i}))
. This question is a variant of the inverse spectral problem—as opposed to the classical problem asking whether we can recover a graph up to isomorphism from the first few eigenvalues, this question asks which spectral edges are in fact possible.

In terms of what is possible, a consequence of the Perron–Frobenius theorem is that
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{G}_{i})\leq d
for all
i
. If we further assume that the graphs are
d
-regular, the Alon-Boppana bound
[
1
,
38
]
gives that
\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{G})\geq 2\sqrt{d-1}-o_{N}(1)
. More generally (stated for example in
[
16
,
20
,
39
]
)
for every fixed
k\geq 1
, as
i\to\infty
, we have
\lambda_{k}(\mathcal{G}_{i})\geq 2\sqrt{d-1}-o_{N}(1)
. Therefore, for regular graphs, the question reduces to asking the following.

Question 1.1
.

Fix
d\geq 3
and
k\geq 2
. For which vectors in

\{(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}):d=\mu_{1}\geq\dots\geq\mu_{k}\geq 2\sqrt{d-1}\}

(1.1)

does there exist an infinite family of
d
-regular graphs
\{\mathcal{G}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\lim_{i\to\infty}\lambda_{j}(\mathcal{G}_{i})=\mu_{j}
for every

Alon and Wei
[
3
]

1.1
and showed that when

, the answer was

pairs of numbers

that all vectors in the slightly smaller set

) as

) exists as limit points if we relax the requirement of being
d

Alon and Wei conjectured that every point in (
1.1

d
-regular graphs
[
3

Theorem 1.2

Fix

and

The

The authors were able to show that, under appropriate conditions, the top

1.1
Proof techiniques

Our proof of
Theorem

1.3
uses several ideas in spectral theory. To show concentration of
\lambda_{1}(T^{\infty}\mathcal{G}_{0})
, we use the Ihara–Bass formula for regular graphs
[
5
,
7
]
that gives a correspondence between eigenvalues of
T^{\infty}\mathcal{G}_{0}
and eigenvalues of
B
, the nonbacktracking walk operator of
T^{\infty}\mathcal{G}_{0}
(
Definition

2.9
). We then give probabilistic bounds on the number of cycles in
\mathcal{G}_{0}
that show concentration of
\lambda_{1}(B)
, which leads to the desired result for
\lambda_{1}(T^{\infty}\mathcal{G}_{0})
. These types of ideas were used in Bordenave’s proof of Friedman’s theorem, which states that for any
d\geq 3
a randomly sampled
d
-regular graph
\mathcal{H}
on
N
vertices has, with high probability,
\max\{\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{H}),|\lambda_{N}(\mathcal{H})|\}=2\sqrt{d-1}+o_{N}(1)

[
10
,
21
]
.

A strong statement is true about the eigenvector of the nonbacktracking operator that corresponds to any eigenvalue greater than
\sqrt{d-1}
, in that it is not supported on edges in the tree extension directed towards
\mathcal{G}_{0}
. This means that to estimate large eigenvalues of the nonbacktracking operator of
T^{\infty}\mathcal{G}_{0}
, it suffices to look at the operator on
\mathcal{G}_{0}
. This eigenvector can be approximated by using the tree neighborhood of most vertices, and realizing that the neighborhood of most vertices approximates a random tree branching process
[
11
]
. We will reason about the combinatorial statistics of these neighborhoods through the Kesten-Stigum theorem, which states that the distribution of the sizes of neighborhoods in this process converge to a deterministic limit
[
29
]
.

To upper bound
\lambda_{2}(T^{\infty}\mathcal{G}_{0})
, we will prove a mild generalization of Friedman’s theorem to the random infinite graph model described above. A challenge in generalizing Friedman’s theorem to our model is that previous proofs
[
21
,
10
,
26
,
15
]
make use of two natural facts concerning finite regular graphs: (1) they are finite, which allows us to bound the second eigenvalue by bounding a normalized trace; (2) we have explicit knowledge about the top eigenvalue and eigenvector, which allows us to project away the dependence on this eigenvalue when applying a trace method or performing Green’s function analysis. Neither of these two points is true in our model.

We found that the proof that was easiest to generalize to our model was that of Huang and Yau, which gives a local law for the random regular graph distribution tight enough to imply Friedman’s theorem
[
26
]
. The local law states that the
Green’s function

G(z)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(\frac{A}{\sqrt{d-1}}-z\mathbb{I})^{-1}
approximates that of the infinite regular tree. This proof generalizes nicely to our setting, as much of it is dedicated to showing that the contribution of certain subgraphs to the Green’s function is approximately that given by replacing the subgraph with an infinite tree. Our model is identical, except some subgraphs are
actually
infinite trees, meaning that many parts of the proof immediately generalize to our model (namely, the true random regular graph is the most difficult version to analyze).

Another advantage here is that the Green’s function allows us to analyze the infinite tree extension
T^{\infty}\mathcal{G}_{0}
through the original graph
\mathcal{G}_{0}
. The Green’s function of
T^{\infty}\mathcal{G}_{0}
on
\mathcal{G}_{0}
is equal to that of a finite graph where each connection to an infinite tree is replaced by a weighted loop. This “finitization” of the Green’s function is possible through the Schur complement formula, and has been crucial in proving sharp spectral statistics in random regular graphs
[
8
,
25
,
26
]
. Moreover, we will show that any eigenvalue
\lambda>2\sqrt{d-1}
has an
Anderson localized
eigenvector, in that the eigenvector is exponentially decaying away from
\mathcal{G}_{0}
. This allows us to bound
\lambda_{2}(T^{\infty}\mathcal{G}_{0})
by bounding the normalized projected trace, where the projection is on the finite vertex set of
\mathcal{G}_{0}
.

Nevertheless, Huang and Yau still use explicit knowledge of the top eigenvalue and eigenvector in
[
25
]
. Having calculated the Green’s function, they show that after subtracting the contribution of the
d
eigenvalue we achieve a sufficiently tight local law. As noted above, we cannot do this, as we do not know our top eigenvector; even if we could show that it is almost deterministic, the contribution of this top eigenspace in our model becomes quite complicated.
To avoid this, we perform our second eigenvalue bound in two steps. The first step is to show that in fact, our trace bound is strong enough to show that
\lambda_{1}(T^{\infty}\mathcal{G}_{0})-\lambda_{2}(T^{\infty}\mathcal{G}_{0})>\epsilon
, for some constant
\epsilon>0
, using an argument similar to Broder and Shamir’s proof that a random regular graph has second eigenvalue
O((d-1)^{3/4})

[
14
]
.
This allows us to ignore the structure of the top eigenvector, and in the second step we can reduce to analyzing our Green’s function for
z
away from
\lambda_{1}(T^{\infty}\mathcal{G}_{0})
. This simplifies our analysis, as we can show that in this region Green’s function of the graph sharply approximates that of the infinite tree, rather than having to consider the error term coming from the top eigenvalue explicitly.

1.2
Related work

The infinite graph we use has the form of a finite graph extended with regular trees; the properties of such a graph, such as the fact that the absolutely continuous spectrum is that of the infinite tree, were studied in
[
17
]
. Our method of preserving spectrum when passing from disjoint infinite graphs to a large graph with fixed
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})
follows a number of works asking whether graphs with optimal spectral expansion can preserve this property after undergoing some small perturbation.
Kahale used this general idea of taking a small gadget graph and implanting it within a larger, optimally expanding graph to show a tight relationship between spectral expansion and vertex expansion
[
28
]
. Implanted gadget graphs into large Ramanujan graphs have been used to relate spectral gap with combinatorial properties
[
36
]
, as well as to relate spectral gap with eigenvector localization
[
2
,
22
]
.

In order to give tight bounds on
\lambda_{1}(T^{\infty}\mathcal{G})
, we must consider the percolated graph. The spectrum of percolated graphs has been well studied
[
13
,
11
]
, as has the spectrum for branching processes
[
6
]
.
Moreover, we see this as closely related to the study of the nonbacktracking operator for Erdős-Rényi (where the local limit is a branching process), where we see similar structure of the eigenvector of the nonbacktracking operator
[
12
, Theorem 3]
.

Other versions of the inverse spectral problem have also been of interest. Kollár and Sarnak studied possible gaps in the spectrum for cubic graphs
[
30
]
. Magee showed a version of the theorem of Alon and Wei for hyperbolic surfaces by showing that the set of limit points of the smallest nontrivial eigenvalue of an arithmetic hyperbolic surface is the interval
[0,1/4]

[
34
]
(note that there is an Alon-Boppana type bound for hyperbolic surfaces at
1/4
, meaning this region is the largest possible).
The related problem of studying the the spectral edge of hyperbolic surfaces is also of much interest
[
24
,
4
]
. Specifically, the methods of
[
4
]
include proving analogous results to those of Friedman’s original theorem. Moreover, similar ideas to the eigenvalue multiplicity problem on graphs from
[
27
]
and
[
37
]
have been used to upper and lower bound the multiplicity of eigenvalues of negatively curved surfaces
[
31
]
. As our method applies a generalization of Friedman’s theorem, and Green’s function analysis can deduce spectral behavior in both settings, we are curious as to whether our result can generalize in such a way that it can prove an arbitrary spectral edge for hyperbolic surfaces.

2.1
Tree extension and eigenvector localization

In this section, we give formal definition of the tree extension operator
T^{L}
mentioned in the introduction, and show that tree extensions have localized eigenvectors for large eigenvalues.

Definition 2.1

(
d
-augmentation)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a graph with maximum degree at most
d
. The
d
-augmentation of
\mathcal{H}
, written as
T\mathcal{H}
, is obtained by adding
d-\deg(v)
distinct new neighbors to every
v\in V(\mathcal{H})
. In particular, all vertices in
T\mathcal{H}
have degree
d
or 1. If
\mathcal{H}
is
d
-regular, then we have
T\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}
.

Definition 2.2

(Tree extension)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a graph with maximum degree at most
d
, and
L\geq 1
. The depth-
L
tree extension
T^{L}\mathcal{H}
is defined by

T^{L}\mathcal{H}=\underbrace{T\cdots T}_{\text{$L$ times}}\mathcal{H}.

Given a tree extension
T^{L}\mathcal{H}
, for every
\ell\geq 0
, we define
V_{\ell}
to be the set of vertices in
T^{L}\mathcal{H}
that have distance exactly
\ell
from
\mathcal{H}
.

Lemma 2.3

(Eigenvector localization)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{G}=T^{\infty}\mathcal{F}
for some
\mathcal{F}
. Then
\sigma(\mathcal{G})
has no continuous spectrum in the
region
(2\sqrt{d-1},\infty)
, and for any eigenvalue
\lambda>2\sqrt{d-1}
of
\mathcal{G}
with unit eigenvector
\psi
, the projection of
\psi
onto
V_{0}
, denoted as
\psi_{0}
, satisfies
\|\psi_{0}\|>\frac{\lambda-2\sqrt{d-1}}{2d}
.

Proof.

Let
A
be the adjacency operator of
\mathcal{G}
. For any normalized vector
\psi
, let
\psi_{0}
denote the projection of
\psi
onto
V_{0}
and
\psi_{\geq 1}
denote the projection of
\psi
onto
V\setminus V_{0}
.
Since
\mathcal{G}\setminus V_{0}
is a disjoint union of infinite rooted
(d-1)
-ary trees, the spectral radius of which are
2\sqrt{d-1}
, we have
\psi_{\geq 1}^{*}A\psi_{\geq 1}\leq 2\sqrt{d-1}
. Therefore there are finitely many eigenvalues greater than
2\sqrt{d-1}
, and they form pure point spectrum.

Suppose
\lambda>2\sqrt{d-1}
is an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{G}
with unit eigenvector
\psi
. Then we have

\lambda=\psi^{*}A\psi=\psi_{0}^{*}A\psi_{0}+2\psi^{*}_{0}A\psi_{1}+\psi_{\geq 1}^{*}A\psi_{\geq 1}\leq d\|\psi_{0}\|^{2}+2\|\psi_{0}\|\sqrt{1-\|\psi_{0}\|^{2}}+2\sqrt{d-1}(1-\|\psi_{0}\|^{2}).

Solving for
\|\psi_{0}\|
, we recover that
\|\psi_{0}\|>\frac{\lambda-2\sqrt{d-1}}{2d}
.
∎

The following lemma shows that eigenvectors of
T^{\infty}\mathcal{F}
with large eigenvalues have exponentially decaying eigenvectors at some quantifiable rate down the tree.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose
\mathcal{G}=T^{\infty}\mathcal{F}
has eigenvalue
\lambda=(\zeta+\frac{1}{\zeta})\sqrt{d-1}
for some
\zeta>1
with normalized eigenvector
\psi
. Then for all
\ell\geq 0
, we have
\sum_{u\in V_{\ell}}|\psi(u)|^{2}\leq\zeta^{-2\ell+2}
.

Proof.

Fix any
v\in V_{1}
. Let
W
denote the set of
v
and its descendants in
\mathcal{G}
. We create a new graph
\mathcal{G}_{v}
by taking the infinite rooted
(d-1)
-ary tree
\mathcal{G}[W]
and adding a loop of weight
\lambda-\sum_{u\sim v,u\in W}\psi(u)/\psi(v)
onto
v
. Then
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{G}_{v}
with eigenvector
\psi_{W}
.

Deleting the root vertex from
\mathcal{G}_{v}
will leave a graph with spectral radius
2\sqrt{d-1}
. Therefore, by Cauchy interlacing,
\lambda
is the unique eigenvalue of
\mathcal{G}_{v}
that is greater than
2\sqrt{d-1}
. We now claim that the loop has weight
\zeta\sqrt{d-1}
. Indeed, assume the loop is of weight
\zeta^{\prime}\sqrt{d-1}
for
\zeta^{\prime}>1
. Define the vector
\phi\in\mathbb{R}^{|W|}
such that for every
u\in W
,

\phi(u)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\psi(v)(\zeta^{\prime})^{-\ell}(d-1)^{-\ell/2}

where
\ell
is the distance between
u
and
v
. One can check that
\mathcal{G}_{v}
has eigenvalue
\lambda=(\zeta^{\prime}+1/\zeta^{\prime})\sqrt{d-1}
with eigenvector
\phi
. Moreover, by the Perron-Frobenius theorem, the spectral radius is monotonic in the weight of the loop, meaning that to have eigenvalue
\lambda
, we must have
\zeta^{\prime}=\zeta
. Therefore
\phi
is a constant multiple of
\psi_{W}
.

Squaring and summing, we get that

\sum_{u\in V_{\ell+1}}|\psi(u)|^{2}\leq\sum_{v\in V_{1}}(d-1)^{\ell}\cdot|\psi(v)|^{2}\zeta^{-2\ell}(d-1)^{-\ell}\leq\zeta^{-2\ell}.

∎

Due to the localization of this eigenvector, trees of finite depth will approximately have the same large eigenvalues.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a finite graph with maximum degree at most

. Then for any
\lambda>2\sqrt{d-1}
in

such that for all

.

Definition 2.6

(

.

Fix

. Let
\mathcal{F}_{0}
be a

-regular graph with girth at least

2.2
Nonbacktracking walk matrix

In this section, we introduce the notion of a nonbacktracking walk matrix.

Definition 2.8
.

For a graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
and

Definition 2.9

(Nonbacktracking walk matrix)
.

For a graph

is the

matrix

B_{f,e}=\mathbf{1}(v=x,f\neq\hat{e})\qquad\text{for all $e=(u,v),\,f=(x,y)\in\vec{E}$.}

Definition 2.10

Let

be a graph and

in

is defined to be a sequence of directed edges

such that

•

e_{i}\in\vec{E}
for all

,

•

for all

e_{i}=(u,v)
and
e_{i+1}=(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})
, we have
v=u^{\prime}
and
e_{i}\neq\hat{e}_{i+1}
.

We say that a nonbacktracking walk

starts at

and ends at

if
e_{1}=e
and
e_{\ell+1}=f
. In particular, for every

,

equals the number of length-

\mathcal{G}
that start and end at the same edge.

We now state the Ihara–Bass formula that gives a correspondence between eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
\mathcal{G}
and its nonbacktracking walk matrix

Let

-regular graph. For every eigenvalue

For every

2.3
Green’s function preliminaries

Let
\mathbb{C}^{+}
denote the set of complex numbers
z
with
\Im[z]>0
. We define the
Green’s function
of the normalized adjacency operator
H\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=A_{\mathcal{G}}/\sqrt{d-1}
of a
d
-regular graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
by

G(\mathcal{G},z)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(H-z\mathbb{I})^{-1},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}.

When

is finite-dimensional, we have the spectral decomposition

G(\mathcal{G},z)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\psi_{i}\psi_{i}^{\top}}{\lambda_{i}-z}

where
\psi_{i}
is the unit eigenvector for
\lambda_{i}(\mathcal{G})
.
We will drop the dependence on
\mathcal{G}
and

when it is clear from the context.

Note that the Green’s function has the interpretation as a walk generating function, as

G=-\frac{1}{z}-\frac{A_{\mathcal{G}}}{z^{2}\sqrt{d-1}}-\frac{A_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}}{z^{3}(d-1)}-\cdots.

Although we will consider
z
that lie within the radius of convergence, this interpretation will remain useful in our analysis.

Let
V_{0}
be a vertex subset of
\mathcal{G}
.
We denote the
V_{0}
-
Stieltjes transform
of
H
by

m_{N}(z)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in V_{0}}G_{ii}(z).

(2.1)

Therefore, rather than taking the entire spectral density, we have reweighted our spectral density by the norm of the projection onto
V_{0}
. Therefore for the spectral density
\varrho(x)
of
H
,

m_{N}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{P_{V_{0}}(x)\varrho(x)}{x-z}{\rm d}x,

where
P_{V_{0}}(x)
is the spectral projector onto
V_{0}
at eigenvalue
x
. In fact, to bound the largest eigenvalues, it is sufficient to consider
m_{N}(z)
as we know by
Lemma

2.3
that a constant fraction of all eigenvector mass for large eigenvalue is contained in
\psi_{0}
. Projecting to this finite set allows us to use the combinatorial properties of the Green’s function.

We define the
Kesten-McKay measure
to be the spectral density of the infinite tree

\displaystyle\varrho_{d}(x):=\mathbf{1}_{x\in[-2,2]}\left(1+\frac{1}{d-1}-\frac{x^{2}}{d}\right)^{-1}\frac{\sqrt{4-x^{2}}}{2\pi}.

(2.2)

Our goal will be to approximate
m_{N}(z)
with
m_{d}(z)
,
the Stieltjes transform of the Kesten–McKay law

\displaystyle m_{d}(z):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\varrho_{d}(x){\rm d}x}{x-z},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}.

We also recall the
semi-circle distribution

\varrho_{\rm sc}(x)
, its Stieltjes transform
m_{sc}(z)
, and the quadratic equation satisfied by
m_{sc}(z)
:

\displaystyle\varrho_{\rm sc}(x):=\mathbf{1}_{x\in[-2,2]}\frac{\sqrt{4-x^{2}}}{2\pi},\quad m_{sc}(z):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\varrho_{\rm sc}(x){\rm d}x}{x-z}=\frac{-z+\sqrt{z^{2}-4}}{2},\quad m_{sc}(z)^{2}+zm_{sc}(z)+1=0.

(2.3)

The Stieltjes transform of the Kesten–McKay law
m_{d}(z)
can be expressed in terms of the Stieltjes transform
m_{sc}(z)
through a Schur complement,

\displaystyle m_{d}(z)=\frac{1}{-z-\frac{d}{d-1}m_{sc}(z)}.

(2.4)

Note that the Kesten-McKay law is absolutely continuous with respect to the semicircular law.

As with previous arguments
[
25
,
26
]
, we will deduce
m_{N}\approx m_{d}
by considering a function
Q
that approximates the semicircular law
m_{sc}
, and showing that
Q\approx m_{sc}
implies
m_{N}\approx m_{d}
.
To this end, for any
\mathbb{V}\subseteq V
, we define
G^{(\mathbb{V})}
to be the Green’s function of the graph
\mathcal{G}^{(\mathbb{V})}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathbb{V}
. Let
\vec{E}_{0}\subseteq\vec{E}
be the set of directed edges supported on
V_{0}
, and let

Q(\mathcal{G},z):=\frac{1}{|\vec{E}_{0}|}\sum_{(o,i)\in\vec{E}_{0}}G_{oo}^{(i)}.

(2.5)

Note that

is not uniform over

and depends on the degree of
o
in
\mathcal{G}_{0}
. We will show in
Section

6
that

is small enough to bound
\lambda_{2}(T^{\infty}\mathcal{G})
.

\mu:=\max\{\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{F}_{0}),\lambda_{2}(T^{L}\mathcal{F}_{1}),\dots,\lambda_{2}(T^{L}\mathcal{F}_{k-1})\}<2\sqrt{d-1}+\epsilon.

Up to reordering
T^{L}\mathcal{F}_{1},\dots,T^{L}\mathcal{F}_{k-1}
so that their first eignvalues have descending order, we have

\lambda_{1}(T^{L}\mathcal{F}_{1})\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{1}(T^{L}\mathcal{F}_{k-1})\geq 2\sqrt{d-1}+8\epsilon\geq\max\{2\sqrt{d-1},\mu\}.

Therefore by
Lemma

2.7
, we have

|\lambda_{i}(P)-\lambda_{1}(T^{L}\mathcal{F}_{i-1})|<\max\left\{\frac{\sqrt{d-1}}{R},\frac{2d^{3}\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}|V(T^{L}\mathcal{F}_{i})|}{|V(\mathcal{F}_{0})|}\right\}\leq\epsilon\qquad\text{ for all $2\leq i\leq k$,}

where the second inequality follows from our choices of

and
\mathcal{F}_{0}
. Altogether, we get that

|\lambda_{i}(P)-\mu_{i}|\leq|\lambda_{i}(P)-\lambda_{1}(T^{L}\mathcal{F}_{i-1})|+|\lambda_{1}(T^{L}\mathcal{F}_{i-1})-\mu_{i}|=3\epsilon\qquad\text{ for all $2\leq i\leq k$.}

Taking

, we can obtain a sequence of
d
-regular graphs that certifies
Theorem

1.2
.
∎

Test eigenvector

Lemma 4.1

With probability

We will do this by showing a vector obtained via the power method is close to satisfying the eigenvector equation with our desired eigenvalue. Define

We now show that

Let

. Then

We first show how this concentration is enough to find

.

Proof of

As

Proof of
Lemma

4.2
.

To do this, we will apply

2.18
with the functions

\displaystyle\tau_{1}(G,v)

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\left|(A\psi_{t})(v)-(p(d-1)+p^{-1})\psi_{t}(v)\right|^{2},1\right\},

\displaystyle\tau_{2}(G,v)

(t+2)

By

Applying

\left|\sum_{v\in S}\tau_{1}(H,v)-\mathbb{E}\sum_{v\in S}\tau_{1}(H,v)\right|,\,\left|\sum_{v\in S}\tau_{2}(H,v)-\mathbb{E}\sum_{v\in S}\tau_{2}(H,v)\right|\leq N^{3/4}.

(4.3)

We now compute the expectation
\mathbb{E}\sum_{v\in S}\tau_{1}(H,v)
. For every

, let

denote the number of neighbors of

in
V_{0}
. For every

\displaystyle\sum_{x\sim v}\psi_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p^{t}(d-1)^{t}}\left(\sum_{x\sim v,\,x\in V_{0}}Z_{t}(x)+(d-d_{v})\frac{Z_{t}(v)}{p(d-1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p^{t}(d-1)^{t}}\left(\sum_{x\sim v,\,x\in V_{0}}\left(Z_{t-1}^{(x)}(v)+\sum_{y\sim x,\,y\in V_{0}\setminus\{v\}}Z_{t-1}^{(x)}(y)\right)+(d-d_{v})\frac{Z_{t}(v)}{p(d-1)}\right).

Observe that

\sum_{x\sim v,\,x\in V_{0}}Z_{t-1}^{(x)}(v)=(d_{v}-1)Z_{t-1}(v)

\sum_{x\sim v,\,x\in V_{0}}\sum_{y\sim x,\,y\in V_{0}\setminus\{v\}}Z_{t-1}^{(x)}(y)=Z_{t+1}(v).

Thus we get that

(A\psi_{t})(v)=\sum_{x\sim v}\psi_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{p^{t}(d-1)^{t}}\left(Z_{t+1}(v)+(d_{v}-1)Z_{t-1}(v)+(d-d_{v})\frac{Z_{t}(v)}{p(d-1)}\right),

\displaystyle(A\psi_{t})(v)-(p(d-1)+p^{-1})\psi_{t}(v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p^{t}(d-1)^{t}}\left(Z_{t+1}(v)+(d_{v}-1)Z_{t-1}(v)+(d-d_{v})\frac{Z_{t}(v)}{p(d-1)}-p(d-1)Z_{t}(v)-p^{-1}Z_{t}(v)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p^{t}(d-1)^{t}}\left(\left(Z_{t+1}(v)-p(d-1)Z_{t}(v)\right)-\frac{d_{v}-1}{p(d-1)}\left(Z_{t}(v)-p(d-1)Z_{t-1}(v)\right)\right).

By (
4.3

Combining this with (

Proof of

So far we have shown that

We start with the following observation. Recall that

is the nonbacktracking walk operator of

(

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose
\theta>\sqrt{d-1}
is an eigenvalue of
B
with unit eigenvector
\phi
. Let
B_{0}
denote the
\vec{E}_{0}\times\vec{E}_{0}
submatrix of
B
(with
\vec{E}_{0}
defined in
Section

\phi_{0}
denote the projection of
\phi
onto
\vec{E}_{0}
. Then
\theta
is also an eigenvalue of
B_{0}
with eigenvector
\phi_{0}

Proof.

Let
(u,v)
be a directed edge in
\vec{E}\setminus\vec{E}_{0}
that points towards
V_{0}
(i.e.,
\text{dist}(v,V_{0})=\text{dist}(u,V_{0})-1
). Then for all
k\geq 1
, we have

\theta^{k}\phi(u,v)=(B^{k}\phi)(u,v)=\sum_{(a,b)\in C_{k}(u,v)}\phi(a,b),

where
C_{k}(u,v)
is the set of distance-
k
children of
(u,v)
. Since
|C_{k}(u,v)|=(d-1)^{k}
, we have

\sum_{(a,b)\in C_{k}(u,v)}|\phi(a,b)|^{2}\geq(d-1)^{k}\left(\sum_{(a,b)\in C_{k}(u,v)}\frac{|\phi(a,b)|}{(d-1)^{k}}\right)^{2}\geq(d-1)^{k}\left(\frac{\theta^{k}|\phi(u,v)|}{(d-1)^{k}}\right)^{2}=\left(\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{d-1}}\right)^{2k}|\phi(u,v)|^{2}.

If
\phi(u,v)\neq 0
, then this diverges as
k\rightarrow\infty
, contradicting the fact that
||\phi||=1
. Therefore we must have
\phi(u,v)=0
.

Observe that for all
(u,v)\in\vec{E}_{0}
, if
B_{(u,v)(a,b)}=1
for some
(a,b)\in\vec{E}
, then either (1)
(a,b)\in\vec{E}_{0}
, or (2)
(a,b)\in\vec{E}\setminus\vec{E}_{0}
is pointed towards
V_{0}
, in which case
\phi(a,b)=0
. For all
(u,v)\in\vec{E}_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle B_{0}\phi_{0}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(B_{0})_{(u,v)(a,b)}=1}\phi_{0}(a,b)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B_{(u,v)(a,b)}=1\\
(a,b)\in\vec{E}_{0}\end{subarray}}\phi(a,b)

\displaystyle=B\phi(u,v)=\theta\phi(u,v)=\theta\phi_{0}(u,v),

so we get that
\theta
is also an eigenvalue of
B_{0}
with eigenvector
\phi_{0}
.
∎

Since
\mathcal{G}
has an eigenvalue
p(d-1)+p^{-1}+o_{N}(1)>2\sqrt{d-1}
, by

4.3
, we know that
B
has an eigenvalue greater than
\sqrt{d-1}
. Thus by
Lemma

4.3
, the first eigenvalues of

and

agree.

We now proceed to estimate the first eigenvalue of
B_{0}
using a trace method.
We start by estimating the number of
k
-cycles in
\mathcal{H}
, the random
d
-regular graph on
V_{0}
. For convenience in counting, in the remainder of this section we assume that every
k
-cycle is an
ordered

k
-tuple of edges (so

and

are treated as distinct cycles).

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a random
d
-regular graph on
N
vertices. Let
k
be a subpolynomial function of
N
with
k\geq 10\log_{d-1}N
, and let
U_{\mathcal{H}}
denote the set of
k
-cycles in
\mathcal{H}
.
Then with high probability we have
|U_{\mathcal{H}}|=(1+\widetilde{O}(N^{-1}))(d-1)^{k}
.

Proof.

We first compute
\mathbb{E}|U_{\mathcal{H}}|
. Observe that in a random matching on
Nd
half-edges, the probability that
k
explicit pairs get matched equals

\displaystyle\frac{(dN-2k)!!}{(dN)!!}=\frac{1}{(dN-1)(dN-3)\cdots(dN-2k+1)}=(1+\widetilde{O}(N^{-1}))\frac{1}{(dN)^{k}}.

(4.8)

Observe that
|U_{\mathcal{H}}|
is at least the number of
k
-cycles in
H
that occupies
k
distinct vertices. Thus, letting
(N)_{k}:=N(N-1)\cdots(N-k+1)
, we can explicitly compute that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|U_{\mathcal{H}}|]

\displaystyle\geq(1+\widetilde{O}(N^{-1}))\frac{(N)_{k}d^{k}(d-1)^{k}}{(dN)^{k}}=(1+\widetilde{O}(N^{-1}))(d-1)^{k}\cdot\frac{(N)_{k}}{N^{k}}=(1+\widetilde{O}(N^{-1}))(d-1)^{k}.

On the other hand, by choosing non-distinct vertices, (
4.8
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|U_{\mathcal{H}}|]

\displaystyle\leq(1+\widetilde{O}(N^{-1}))\frac{N^{k}d^{k}(d-1)^{k}}{(dN)^{k}}=(1+\widetilde{O}(N^{-1}))(d-1)^{k}.

Combining the two, we get that
\mathbb{E}[|U_{\mathcal{H}}|]=(1+\widetilde{O}(N^{-1}))(d-1)^{k}
. The result then follows from
Lemma

2.16
.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a random
d
-regular graph on
N
vertices. Let
k
be a subpolynomial function of
N
with
k\geq 10\log_{d-1}N
. For every
1\leq i\leq k
, let
U_{H,i}
be the set of pairs of
k
-cycles in
H
that intersect in exactly
i
edges. Then

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{k}p^{2k-i}|U_{H,i}|\right]=\widetilde{O}(N^{-1}p^{2k}\mathbb{E}[|U_{\mathcal{H}}|]^{2}).

Proof.

For
i=1,\dots,k-1
, if two
k
-cycles intersect in
i
edges, then their intersection must be a union of
s
disjoint paths for some
1\leq s\leq i
; thus, their union occupies
2k-(i+s)
vertices and
2k-i
edges. For every
s
, there are
\binom{i-1}{s-1}
ways to partition
i
edges into
s
paths and
\binom{k-i-1}{s-1}
ways to partition
k-i
edges into
s
paths. Thus there are
\binom{i-1}{s-1}\binom{k-i-1}{s-1}^{2}
possible underlying graphs of the union of these two cycles. Finally, given the underlying graph, there are
k^{2}
ways to order the vertices in each cycle. Thus, we have the upper bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|U_{\mathcal{H},i}|]

\displaystyle\leq(1+o_{N}(1))\sum_{s=1}^{i}\binom{i-1}{s-1}\binom{k-i-1}{s-1}^{2}\frac{N^{2k-(i+s)}d^{2k-i}(d-1)^{2k-i}}{(dN)^{2k-i}}\cdot k^{2}

\displaystyle\leq k^{2}\sum_{s=1}^{i}\binom{i-1}{s-1}\binom{k-i-1}{s-1}^{2}\frac{(d-1)^{2k-i}}{N^{s}}\leq k^{2}(d-1)^{2k-i}\sum_{s=1}^{i}\frac{k^{3s}}{N^{s}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2k^{5}}{N}\cdot(d-1)^{2k-i}.

Finally, when
i=k
, it is clear that
\mathbb{E}[|U_{\mathcal{H},k}|]=k\mathbb{E}[|U_{\mathcal{H}}|]
.
This gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{k}p^{2k-i}|U_{\mathcal{H},i}|\right]

\displaystyle\leq k\mathbb{E}[|U_{\mathcal{H}}|]\cdot p^{k}+\frac{2k^{5}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(d-1)^{2k-i}\cdot p^{2k-i}

\displaystyle=k\mathbb{E}[|U_{\mathcal{H}}|]\cdot p^{k}+\frac{2k^{5}}{N}\sum_{i=k+1}^{2k-1}(d-1)^{i}p^{i}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(2k)^{5}}{N}(p^{k}\mathbb{E}[|U_{\mathcal{H}}|])^{2}=\widetilde{O}(N^{-1}p^{2k}\mathbb{E}[|U_{\mathcal{H}}|]^{2}).

∎

This immediately gives strong concentration of
\textnormal{Tr}(B_{0})
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
k=c\log N
, with
c\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\max\left\{10,\frac{3}{\log(p\sqrt{d-1})}\right\}
.
Then with probability

, we have

\textnormal{Tr}(B_{0}^{k})=(1+\widetilde{O}(N^{-1}))(p(d-1))^{k}.

Proof.

Note that by our choice of
c
, we have

\displaystyle k\geq 10\log_{d-1}N,\qquad N^{2}d

\displaystyle\leq\left(p\sqrt{d-1}\right)^{k}.

Recall that
\mathcal{G}_{0}
is obtained from
\mathcal{H}
by including every edge independently with probability
p
. Let
U_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}
denote the set of
k
-cycles in
\mathcal{G}_{0}
.
With high probability,
\mathcal{H}
satisfies
Proposition

4.4
and
Lemma

4.5
. Under this assumption, we have

\mathbb{E}[|U_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}|]=p^{k}|U_{\mathcal{H}}|=(1+\widetilde{O}(N^{-1}))p^{k}(d-1)^{k}.

For the variance, we have

\mathbb{E}[|U_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}|^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[|U_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}|]^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[p^{2k-i}|U_{\mathcal{H},i}|\right]=\widetilde{O}(N^{-1}p^{2k}(d-1)^{2k}).

Therefore, by a Chebyshev inequality, with high probability we have

|U_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}|=(1+\widetilde{O}(N^{-1}))p^{k}(d-1)^{k}.

Finally, observe that
\textnormal{Tr}(B_{0}^{k})
equals the number of length-
k
nonbacktracking walks in
\mathcal{G}_{0}
(
Definition

2.10
) that start and end at the same directed edge.

By
Lemma

2.17
, with high probability the number of such walks in
\mathcal{H}
is

(d-1)^{k}+O(Nd(d-1)^{k/2}),

meaning that the number of such walks that are not
k
-cycles in
\mathcal{H}
(and thus, not
k
-cycles in
\mathcal{G}_{0}
) is
O(Nd(d-1)^{k/2})=O(N^{-1}p^{k}(d-1)^{k})
. Thus, we have that with high probability

\textnormal{Tr}(B_{0}^{k})=(1+\widetilde{O}(N^{-1}))p^{k}(d-1)^{k}+O(Nd(d-1)^{k/2})=(1+\widetilde{O}(N^{-1}))p^{k}(d-1)^{k}.

∎

We are now ready to prove
Proposition

3.3
.

Proof of
Proposition

3.3
.

Take
k=c\log N
as in

4.6
. We know that with high probability

, and

(4.9)

Note that

.
Using

and there exists

5.2
Switching dynamics

In this section, we recall the local resampling and its properties as introduced in

Recall that for graph

For a subset

Definition 5.8
.

A (simple) switching is encoded by two oriented edges

.
Then the switching consists of

.
We denote the graph after the switching by

.

\vec{E}_{0}
. We fix a vertex

to be the center of the resampling. We denote by
\mathcal{T}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{B}_{\ell}(o,\mathcal{G}_{f})

\ell

in
\mathcal{G}
and

\mathcal{T}
.

We enumerate the edges of
\partial_{E}\mathcal{T}

and

. Thus

is the number of vertices at distance

to

in

. We orient the edges

.
We note that

.
To perform our local resampling, we choose

within

(5.14)

The sets

will be called the

. We remark that repetitions are allowed in the resampling data
(b_{1},c_{1}),(b_{2},c_{2}),\cdots,(b_{\mu},c_{\mu})
.
We define an indicator that will be crucial to the definition of switch.

Definition 5.9
.

Recall the definition of
\mathcal{G}^{(\mathbb{V})}
in

2.3
.
For

,

.

Lemma 5.11

Fix

In the following lemma we show that typically

Assuming

We only prove the first statement, as the second is analogous. By the assumption that

Under the assumptions of

5.4
Initial bounds

Our starting point to show
Proposition

5.14
will be the combinatorial bounds of Section 5 of
[
26
]
. Fundamental to these ideas is that of the Ward identity.

Lemma 5.16

(Ward identity for
V_{0}
)
.

For any vertex
i
and truncation function
f(\cdot)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=f_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}(\cdot)
as in (
5.7
), we have

\Im[G_{ii}]=\eta\sum_{j\in V_{0}}|G_{ij}|^{2}+\frac{\Im[m_{sc}]}{d-1}\sum_{j\in V_{0}}f(j)|G_{ij}|^{2}.

(5.19)

Proof.

Let
H_{0}
be the normalized adjacency matrix of
\mathcal{G}_{0}
. By the resolvent identity (
A.1
), we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Im[G_{ii}]&=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}}\left(\frac{1}{-z+H_{0}-\frac{m_{sc}}{d-1}\sum_{j}f(j)e_{j}e_{j}^{*}}-\left(\frac{1}{-z+H_{0}-\frac{m_{sc}}{d-1}\sum_{j}f(j)e_{j}e_{j}^{*}}\right)^{*}\right)_{ii}\\
&=\left(G\left(\Im[z]+\frac{\Im[m_{sc}]}{d-1}\sum_{j}f(j)e_{j}e_{j}^{*}\right)G^{*}\right)_{ii}\\
&=\eta\sum_{j\in V_{0}}|G_{ij}|^{2}+\frac{\Im[m_{sc}]}{d-1}\sum_{j\in V_{0}}f(j)|G_{ij}|^{2}.\end{split}

(5.20)

∎

In particular, we have

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\in V_{0}}|G_{ij}|^{2}\leq\frac{\Im[G_{ii}]}{N\eta}.

(5.21)

Note that this is stronger than the bound given for finite graphs, in which case (
5.21
) is an equality.

There is a series of propositions in
[
26
, Section 5]
that bound the change of Green’s function under adding or removing vertices. These bounds all come from the following properties of random regular graphs:

1.

Distance bound: randomly selected vertices are typically far from each other in graph distance. This expresses itself, for example, through
Lemma

2.14
.

2.

Green’s function bound: by the Ward identity (
Lemma

5.16
), most pairs of vertices have Green’s function entry at most
\varphi
, for
\varphi
as defined in (
5.5
).

In our model, both of these statements have only improved, as distance has only increased, and using the Ward identity to bound the Green’s function now uses an inequality rather than an equality. Specifically, by the definition of
\Omega(z)
,
Proposition

5.7
, and (
5.21
), we have

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\in V_{0}}|G_{ij}|^{2}\leq\frac{\Im[G_{ii}]}{N\eta}\lesssim\frac{\Im[m_{d}(z)]+\varepsilon^{\prime}+\varepsilon/\sqrt{\kappa+\eta+\varepsilon}}{N\eta}.

This means that we can apply the preliminary bounds in
[

A.1

The following well-known identity is referred as resolvent identity:
for two invertible matrices
A
and
B
of the same size, we have

A^{-1}-B^{-1}=A^{-1}(B-A)B^{-1}=B^{-1}(B-A)A^{-1}.

(A.1)

A.2
Schur complement formula

Given an
N\times N
matrix
M
and index set
\mathbb{V}\subseteq[{N}]
, up to rearrangement of indices,
M
can be written in the block form

M=\begin{bmatrix}A&B^{*}\\
B&D\end{bmatrix}.

The Schur complement formula asserts that the inverse of
M
satisfies

M^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}(A-B^{*}D^{-1}B)^{-1}&-(A-B^{*}D^{-1}B)^{-1}B^{*}D^{-1}\\
-D^{-1}B(A-B^{*}D^{-1}B)^{-1}&D^{-1}+D^{-1}B(A-B^{*}D^{-1}B)^{-1}B^{*}D^{-1}\end{bmatrix}.

Taking
M=H-z
where
H

\mathcal{G}
, we know that
M^{-1}=G
is the Green’s function of
\mathcal{G}
, and
D^{-1}=G^{(\mathbb{V})}

\mathcal{G}^{(\mathbb{V})}
.

Let

G|_{\mathbb{V}^{\complement}\mathbb{V}}&G|_{\mathbb{V}^{\complement}}\end{bmatrix}.

Then by the Schur complement formula, with

\displaystyle G|_{\mathbb{V}}

\displaystyle=(H-z-B^{*}G^{(\mathbb{V})}B)^{-1}

and

\displaystyle G|_{\mathbb{V}\mathbb{V}^{\complement}}

\displaystyle=-G|_{\mathbb{V}}B^{*}G^{(\mathbb{V})}.
