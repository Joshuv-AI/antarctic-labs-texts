Analytic weak-signal approximation of the Bayes factor for continuous gravitational waves
Reinhard Prix

Abstract
We generalize the targeted ℬ \mathcal{B} -statistic for continuous gravitational waves by modeling the h 0 h_{0} -prior as a half-Gaussian distribution with scale parameter H H . This approach retains analytic tractability for two of the four amplitude marginalization integrals and recovers the standard ℬ \mathcal{B} -statistic in the strong-signal limit ( H → ∞ H\rightarrow\infty ). By Taylor-expanding the weak-signal regime ( H → 0 H\rightarrow 0 ), the new prior enables fully analytic amplitude marginalization, resulting in a simple, explicit statistic that is as computationally efficient as the maximum-likelihood ℱ \mathcal{F} -statistic, but significantly more robust. Numerical tests show that for day-long coherent searches, the weak-signal Bayes factor achieves sensitivities comparable to the ℱ \mathcal{F} -statistic, though marginally lower than the standard ℬ \mathcal{B} -statistic (and the Bero-Whelan approximation). In semi-coherent searches over short (compared to a day) segments, this approximation matches or outperforms the weighted dominant-response ℱ ^ ABw \widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{ABw}} -statistic and returns to the sensitivity of the (weighted) ℱ ^ w \widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{w}} -statistic for longer segments. Overall the new Bayes-factor approximation demonstrates state-of-the-art or improved sensitivity across a wide range of segment lengths we tested (from 900 s 900\text{\,}\mathrm{s} to 10 days 10\text{\,}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{y}\mathrm{s} ). Introduction

Continuous gravitational waves (CWs), expected to be emitted by spinning non-axisymmetric neutron
stars in our galaxy, are one of the most anticipated but still undetected types of gravitational
waves.
Finding CWs will mark the culmination of decades of research aimed at refining search methods
and applying them to data from ground-based detectors, such as LIGO Hanford (H1), Livingston (L1)

[
1
]
, and Virgo (V1)
[
4
]
.
For recent reviews, see, for example,
[
28
,
35
]
.

CW signals manifest in the detector data as amplitude-modulated quasi-sinusoidal waveforms with
slowly varying (generally decreasing) frequency. These are long-lasting but extremely weak signals
compared to the detector noise, and will therefore require combining months to years of data to become
detectable.
The signals are characterized by four
amplitude
parameters: the overall amplitude

h_{0}
, two polarization angles
\iota
and
\psi
, and the initial phase
\phi_{0}
.
An additional set of
phase-evolution
parameters, such as the frequency (and its time
derivatives), sky position and binary-orbital parameters, is required to fully determine the signal,
depending on the search space considered.

A key research focus for detecting CWs is the development of statistics that maximize the detection
probability at a fixed false-alarm level, while being as computationally efficient as possible.
A significant milestone in this area was the seminal work by Jaranowski, Królak, and Schutz

[
21
]
(henceforth “JKS”), demonstrating that the likelihood ratio can be analytically
maximized over the four unknown amplitude parameters after a coordinate transformation.
This results in the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic, which only requires explicit searches over phase-evolution
parameters, thereby substantially reducing the computing cost.
Another closely-related coherent statistic was constructed by different arguments within the 5-vector
formalism
[
6
]
, equally dispensing with the amplitude parameters and resulting
in very similar detection power (see
D
for the detailed relation).

Searle pointed out in
[
31
]
that for composite signal hypotheses (i.e.,
those with unknown parameters), the Neyman-Pearson lemma shows that the optimal statistic is the

marginalized
(rather than maximized) likelihood ratio, also known as the Bayes factor.
This was illustrated in the CW context in
[
26
]
, showing that marginalizing
with more physical priors over the amplitude parameters resulted in a (slightly) more powerful
statistic (referred to as the
\mathcal{B}
-statistic) than the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic.
However, this statistic entails a substantially higher computing cost, given that only two of the
four marginalization integrals have been solved analytically
[
36
]
, while the
remaining two need to be performed numerically.
There have been a number of attempts

[
14
,
20
,
15
,
34
]

to find usable solutions and approximations to these integrals, with the most successful
fully-analytic approximation to date provided by Bero & Whelan
[
7
]
.

On the other hand, recent work
[
9
]
on semi-coherent all-sky searches for
neutron stars in unknown binary systems has revealed some unexpected weaknesses of the

\mathcal{F}
-statistic:
the extreme computational cost of these searches requires using very short segments, of order

\mathcal{O}\left($100\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$\right)
.
Surprisingly, the
\mathcal{F}
-statistics turns out to be quite sub-optimal for such short segments, and an
empirically-constructed
dominant-response
statistic
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{AB}}
, effectively dropping two of
the four amplitude degrees of freedom, was shown in
[
11
]
to beat the

\mathcal{F}
-statistic sensitivity by up to
\sim$19\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}$
in the short-segment limit.
Furthermore, when signal power varies over segments, which can happen both due to varying
(i) antenna-pattern response, and (ii) noise floors and duty factors, then an explicit
segment-weighting scheme was shown in
[
10
]
to improve sensitivity over
the conventional directly-summed semi-coherent
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}
- (or
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{AB}}
) statistics.

These empirical findings raise the question of how to understand and leverage them within the
Bayesian framework.
Given that three of the four amplitude parameters (namely
\iota,\psi,\phi_{0}
) have well-defined
ignorance priors
[
26
]
, the only remaining freedom left is the choice of

h_{0}
-prior. Previous works on the CW Bayes factor have all used a uniform
h_{0}
-prior, for
simplicity and because it enables analytical marginalization.
However, this is intrinsically a “strong-signal” prior, in the sense that it puts more probability
weight on larger orders of magnitude compared to smaller ones.

Here we explore a new
h_{0}
-prior in the form of a half-Gaussian with scale parameter
H
, which is
more “physical” in the sense of giving more weight to smaller
h_{0}
amplitudes compared to larger
ones.
This contains the uniform prior in the asymptotic strong-signal limit (
H\rightarrow\infty
), while
preserving the ability for analytic
h_{0}
integration.
In the “weak-signal” limit (
H\rightarrow 0
), we can Taylor-expand in small
H
and obtain a
fully analytic solution for all four Bayes- factor integrals.
Used as a semi-coherent statistic, this performs as well and better than the weighted

\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{ABw}}
statistic for short segments and the weighted
\mathcal{F}
-statistic
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{w}}

for long segments.

The plan of this paper is as follows: introduction to the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic formalism in
Sec.
2
, followed by a recap of the corresponding Bayesian formalism in
Sec.
3
with
\mathcal{B}
-statistic and Bero&Whelan approximation. Derivation of the
new generalized
\mathcal{B}
-statistic and analytic weak-signal approximation in
Sec.
4
. Derivation of the weak-signal statistic as a generalized

\chi^{2}
-distribution in Sec.
5
, followed by numerical tests and results
presented in Sec.
6
, with conclusions in Sec.
7
.

A
shows an alternative way to derive the weak-signal statistic,

B
gives the expressions for the general multi-detector
non-stationary scalar product,
C
provides the explicit likelihood terms
in physical coordinates, and
D
shows the detailed translation to the
5-vector framework and weak-signal statistic equivalence.

2
The
\mathcal{F}
-statistic formalism

2.1
Signal model

The signal depends on amplitude parameters
\mathcal{A}\equiv\{h_{0},\eta,\psi,\phi_{0}\}
, where
h_{0}
is
the overall amplitude,
\eta\equiv\cos\iota
quantifies the degree of circular polarization in
terms of the inclination angle
\iota
of the neutron-star spin axis to the line of sight,
\psi

describes the polarization in terms of its rotation angle on the sky, and
\phi_{0}
is the signal
phase at a reference time (see
[
36
]
for a more detailed discussion of the
geometry). We refer to this parametrization as the
physical
amplitude coordinates.

The seminal paper
[
21
]
introduced a new set of amplitude coordinates

\{\mathcal{A}^{\mu}\}
, referred to as JKS coordinates, which are defined as

\eqalign{\mathcal{A}^{1}&\equiv\hskip 6.99997ptA_{+}\cos\phi_{0}\cos 2\psi-A_{\times}\sin\phi_{0}\sin 2\psi\,,\\
\mathcal{A}^{2}&\equiv\hskip 6.99997ptA_{+}\cos\phi_{0}\sin 2\psi+A_{\times}\sin\phi_{0}\cos 2\psi\,,\\
\mathcal{A}^{3}&\equiv-A_{+}\sin\phi_{0}\cos 2\psi-A_{\times}\cos\phi_{0}\sin 2\psi\,,\\
\mathcal{A}^{4}&\equiv-A_{+}\sin\phi_{0}\sin 2\psi+A_{\times}\cos\phi_{0}\cos 2\psi\,,}

(1)

in terms of the polarization amplitudes
A_{+}\equiv h_{0}\,(1+\eta^{2})/2
and
A_{\times}\equiv h_{0}\,\eta
.
Using these coordinates, the signal
h(t)
in the detector frame takes the linear form

h(t;\mathcal{A},\lambda)=\mathcal{A}^{\mu}\,h_{\mu}(t;\lambda),

(2)

with automatic summation over repeated amplitude indices
\mu=1,\ldots,4
,
and with four matched-filter basis functions
h_{\mu}(t;\lambda)
defined as

\eqalign{h_{1}(t)&\equiv a(t)\,\cos\phi(t),\quad h_{2}(t)\equiv b(t)\,\cos\phi(t),\\
h_{3}(t)&\equiv a(t)\,\sin\phi(t),\quad h_{4}(t)\equiv b(t)\,\sin\phi(t),}

(3)

in terms of the signal phase
\phi(t;\lambda)
at the detector with sky-direction dependent
antenna-pattern functions
a(t;\vec{n}),\,b(t;\vec{n})
(cf.
[
21
,
27
]
for explicit
expressions).

The
phase-evolution parameters

\lambda
include the remaining signal parameters, namely the
sky-position
\vec{n}
, frequency
f
and higher-order derivatives
\dot{f},\ddot{f},\ldots
at some
given reference time, describing the slowly-changing intrinsic signal frequency. For CW sources in
binary systems
\lambda
also includes the binary orbital parameters. The sky position
\vec{n}
is most
commonly specified in terms of the equatorial longitude
\alpha
and latitude
\delta
, measured in
radians.

2.2
Likelihood ratio

We want to construct the
optimal
detection statistic for distinguishing between the
Gaussian-noise hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{G}}:x(t)=n(t)
, when the data
x
contains only Gaussian noise
n
,
and the signal hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}}:x(t)=n(t)+h(t;\mathcal{A},\lambda)
, when there is an additional signal

h(t;\mathcal{A},\lambda)
of the form (
2
).

Optimality
of a statistic
S(x)
is defined in terms of the Neyman-Pearson criterion of
maximizing the detection probability
p_{\mathrm{det}}=P\left(S>S_{\mathrm{thr}}|\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}}\right)
at a fixed false-alarm
probability
p_{\mathrm{fa}}\equiv P\left(S>S_{\mathrm{thr}}|\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{G}}\right)
, parametrized in terms of a detection
threshold
S_{\mathrm{thr}}
.

For two
simple
hypothesis, i.e., with no unknown parameters, the classic Neyman-Pearson lemma

[
32
]
proves that the optimal statistic is the likelihood ratio, namely

\mathcal{L}(x;\mathcal{A},\lambda)\equiv\frac{P\left(x|\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}},\mathcal{A},\lambda\right)}{P\left(x|\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{G}}\right)}=\exp\left[\left(x|h\right)-\frac{1}{2}\left(h|h\right)\right],

(4)

where the last expression holds for the specific hypotheses
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{G}},\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}}
being considered here, and
we introduced the “matched-filter” scalar product
\left(x|y\right)
, see
[
18
]
.

The scalar product for narrow-band signals (such as CWs) in a single detector with stationary noise
floor and no gaps in the data, would simply be

\left(x|y\right)\equiv 2\mathcal{S}^{-1}\int_{0}^{T_{\mathrm{data}}}x(t)\,y(t)\,dt
,
in terms of the (single-sided) noise power spectral density
\mathcal{S}(f)
around the signal frequency
f

and data duration
T_{\mathrm{data}}
.
One can generalize this to several detectors, allowing for gaps in the data and non-stationary
noise-floors (see
B
for the full expression), and still write it in
the form

\left(x|y\right)\equiv 2\gamma\,\langle{x\,y}\rangle\,,

(5)

in terms of a (weighted) multi-detector average
\langle{\ldots}\rangle
and a dimensionless
data factor

\gamma

[
27
,
11
]
, which we define as

\gamma\equiv\mathcal{S}^{-1}\,T_{\mathrm{data}},

(6)

which can be understood as the product of the data “quality” (i.e.,
\mathcal{S}^{-1}
) and “quantity”,

T_{\mathrm{data}}
.
Note that this involves the non-stationary generalization of the overall noise floor
\mathcal{S}
given in
(
95
).

Substituting the JKS signal form (
2
), we can express the log-likelihood ratio as

\ln\mathcal{L}(x;\mathcal{A},\lambda)=\mathcal{A}^{\mu}x_{\mu}-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{A}^{\mu}\mathcal{M}_{\mu\nu}\mathcal{A}^{\nu}\,,

(7)

where we defined

x_{\mu}\equiv\left(x|h_{\mu}\right),\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{M}_{\mu\nu}\equiv\left(h_{\mu}|h_{\nu}\right).

(8)

The four numbers
x_{\mu}
are the scalar products of the data
x
matched against the basis functions

h_{\mu}
of (
3
), while the
detector-response matrix

\mathcal{M}_{\mu\nu}
can be
expressed more explicitly
1
1

1

Assuming the long-wavelength limit for ground-based
detectors.
as

\mathcal{M}=\gamma M=\gamma\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}m&0\\
0&m\\
\end{array}\right),\quad\text{with}\quad m\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A&C\\
C&B\\
\end{array}\right),

(9)

where
A=\langle{a^{2}}\rangle
,
B=\langle{b^{2}}\rangle
, and
C=\langle{a\,b}\rangle
are the (sky-position
dependent) averaged antenna-pattern coefficients.
This expression shows two contributions to the detector response, namely the data factor
\gamma

and a geometric
antenna-pattern matrix

M
, encoding the detector sensitivity to a particular
sky direction (integrated over the available observation time).
We can express the determinant as

\det\mathcal{M}=\gamma^{4}\,D^{2}\,\quad\text{with}\quad D\equiv\det m=AB-C^{2}\,.

(10)

In practice the amplitude parameters
\mathcal{A}
are (generally) unknown, and depending on the
type of search, also some or all of the phase-evolution parameters.
The signal hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}}
is therefore always a
composite
hypothesis involving unknown
parameters.
Therefore the likelihood ratio (
4
) is a function of these unknown
parameters and cannot be directly used as a detection statistic.

2.3
The
\mathcal{F}
-statistic

A classic method for dealing with composite hypotheses is the maximum-likelihood approach, which
consists of using the maximized likelihood ratio as a detection statistic. Applying this approach
to (
7
), one can analytically maximize
\mathcal{L}
over the JKS amplitude parameters
\mathcal{A}^{\mu}

and obtain

\mathcal{F}(x;\lambda)\equiv\max_{\{\mathcal{A}^{\mu}\}}\ln\mathcal{L}(x;\mathcal{A},\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}x_{\mu}\left(\mathcal{M}^{-1}\right)^{\mu\nu}x_{\nu},

(11)

defining the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic
[
21
]
, with the corresponding maximum-likelihood estimators

\hat{\mathcal{A}}^{\mu}
for the amplitude parameters given by

\hat{\mathcal{A}}^{\mu}\equiv\left(\mathcal{M}^{-1}\right)^{\mu\nu}x_{\nu}\,.

(12)

This statistic can be shown
[
21
]
to follow a
\chi^{2}
-distribution with
four

degrees of freedom, and noncentrality parameter

\rho^{2}\equiv\left(h|h\right)=\mathcal{A}^{\mu}\mathcal{M}_{\mu\nu}\mathcal{A}^{\nu},

(13)

which we refer to as the
signal power
, also commonly known as the squared signal-to-noise
ratio (SNR) in this context.

3
Bayesian detection framework

3.1
Bayes factor

Unlike the
ad-hoc
maximum-likelihood approach discussed earlier, the Bayesian framework
offers a natural and unique method for handling unknown parameters, grounded in the three
fundamental laws of probability
[
22
]
.

The likelihood for a composite signal hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}}
can be directly obtained as

P\left(x|\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}}\right)=\int P\left(x|\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}},\mathcal{A},\lambda\right)\,P\left(\mathcal{A},\lambda|\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}}\right)\,d\mathcal{A}\,d\lambda,

(14)

also referred to as the
marginalized
likelihood, which requires an explicit specification of
the prior probabilities for the unknown signal parameters
\{\mathcal{A},\lambda\}
.
Assuming for simplicity a Gaussian-noise hypothesis with known noise floor
\mathcal{S}
, this further leads
to the marginalized likelihood ratio

B_{\mathrm{{\mathrm{S}}/{\mathrm{G}}}}(x)\equiv\frac{P\left(x|\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}}\right)}{P\left(x|\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{G}}\right)}=\int\mathcal{L}(x;\mathcal{A},\lambda)\,P\left(\mathcal{A},\lambda|\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}}\right)\,d\mathcal{A}\,d\lambda,

(15)

commonly known as the
Bayes factor
between the signal and the Gaussian-noise hypothesis.

Here we focus exclusively on the marginalization over amplitude parameters
\mathcal{A}
, and therefore
consider only a single phase-evolution point
\lambda
in the following.
This could be interpreted as a “targeted” search scenario, but could equally represent simply a
single template step in a wide-parameter space search.

3.2
Rediscovering the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic

Building on the pioneering work
[
29
,
30
]
in burst
searches,
[
26
]
demonstrated that the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic can also be derived as
a Bayes factor, assuming a (somewhat arbitrary) prior that is uniform in
\mathcal{A}^{\mu}
-space.
Specifically,
P\left(\{\mathcal{A}^{\mu}\}|\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}}\right)=k
, leading to a Gaussian integral that yields

B_{\mathrm{{\mathrm{S}}/{\mathrm{G}}}}(x;\lambda)=k\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\sqrt{\det\mathcal{M}}}e^{\mathcal{F}(x;\lambda)}\,.

(16)

The Jacobian for the coordinate transformation (
1
) between
\mathcal{A}^{\mu}
and physical
amplitude parameters can be obtained
[
26
,
36
]
as

P\left(h_{0},\eta,\psi,\phi_{0}|\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}}\right)=\frac{1}{4}h_{0}^{3}\left(1-\eta^{2}\right)^{3}P\left(\{\mathcal{A}^{\mu}\}|\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}}\right)\,,

(17)

therefore a constant-
\mathcal{A}^{\mu}
prior favors signals with large
h_{0}
and linear polarization
(
\eta\sim 0
) over circular polarization
\eta\sim\pm 1
, both of which are unphysical.

In order to be normalizable, this prior needs to be artificially truncated at some large-
\mathcal{A}^{\mu}

surface. One can also use this to (arbitrarily) remove the prefactor by choosing an
\mathcal{M}
-dependent
truncation surface, as discussed in
[
25
]
, resulting in a
\mathcal{F}
-statistic Bayes
factor of the form
B_{\mathrm{{\mathrm{S}}/{\mathrm{G}}}}(x;\lambda)=k^{\prime}\,e^{\mathcal{F}(x;\lambda)}
instead, where
the prefactor
k^{\prime}
is now independent of the detector-response matrix
\mathcal{M}
.
Empirically this form was found to result in better performance on transient-CW signals

[
25
]
, and was also used as the basis for constructing extended “line-robust”
Bayes factors in
[
23
,
24
]
.

3.3
Neyman-Pearson-Searle optimality

As Searle noted in
[
31
]
, the Neyman-Pearson proof for the optimal
detection statistic only requires the likelihoods for the two competing hypotheses. Since the
Bayesian framework uniquely provides a marginalized likelihood (
14
) for the composite
signal hypothesis, it follows that the marginalized likelihood ratio (
7
) (i.e., the Bayes
factor)
is
the Neyman-Pearson optimal statistic, assuming the unknown signal parameters are
drawn from the priors.

Thus, the somewhat unphysical prior of Sec.
3.2
implies that the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic
is not optimal, and using more appropriate priors can lead to a more powerful detection
statistic. This was demonstrated in
[
26
]
, where an isotropic prior on the
neutron star’s spin orientation (reflecting our ignorance of the spin axis) was used instead,
namely

P\left(h_{0},\eta,\psi,\phi_{0}|\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}}\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\,P\left(h_{0}|\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}}\right)\,,

(18)

which is uniform in
\eta\in[-1,1]
,
\psi\in[-\pi/4,\pi/4]
and
\phi_{0}\in[0,2\pi]
.
We can therefore write the general form of the optimal Bayes factor as

B_{\mathrm{{\mathrm{S}}/{\mathrm{G}}}}(x;\lambda)\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int\mathcal{L}(x;\lambda,\mathcal{A})\,P\left(h_{0}\right)\,dh_{0}\,d\eta\,d\psi\,d\phi_{0}\,.

(19)

The correct choice for an
h_{0}
-prior is less obvious and would ultimately have to be constructed
from astrophysical arguments about the expected distance, rotation rate and deformation of neutron
stars. Generally speaking, however, we expect a “physical”
h_{0}
-prior to favor weaker signals
over stronger ones, contrary to the implicit
\mathcal{F}
-statistic prior
\propto h_{0}^{3}
of (
17
).

Likelihood ratio in physical coordinates

2\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{A}}
and
2\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}
are
\chi^{2}
-distributed
with two degrees of freedom and non-centrality parameters
\rho^{2}_{\mathrm{A}}
and
\rho^{2}_{\mathrm{B}}
,
respectively.

3.5
The
\mathcal{B}
-statistic

With the likelihood ratio expressed in physical coordinates (
20
), the Bayes-factor integral
(
19
) now takes the form

B_{\mathrm{{\mathrm{S}}/{\mathrm{G}}}}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int dh_{0}d\eta d\psi\,P\left(h_{0}\right)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}h_{*}^{2}g^{2}}\int e^{h_{*}q\cos(\phi_{0}-\varphi_{0})}\,d\phi_{0}\,.

(31)

The
\phi_{0}
-integral can be performed analytically
[
36
]
using the Jacobi-Anger
expansion, resulting in
\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{x\,\cos\phi}\,d\phi=2\pi\,I_{0}(x)
in terms of the
modified Bessel function of the first kind
I_{0}(x)
, which yields

B_{\mathrm{{\mathrm{S}}/{\mathrm{G}}}}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}h_{*}^{2}g^{2}}\,I_{0}\left(h_{*}q\right)\,P\left(h_{0}\right)\,dh_{0}\,d\eta\,d\psi.

(32)

In
[
26
]
and all subsequent analyses of this Bayes factor, only the uniform

h_{0}
prior,
P\left(h_{0}\right)=\kappa
(for
h_{0}\in[0,h_{\max{}}]
), has been used.
This simple choice allows for analytic integration over
h_{0}
, serving as a proof of principle by
demonstrating that the resulting Bayes factor is more sensitive than the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic.
The prior leads to a known integral
[
36
]
for
h_{0}
, specifically using
Eq. 11.4.31 in Abramowitz & Stegun
[
3
]
:

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-a^{2}\,t^{2}}\,I_{0}(b\,t)\,dt=\frac{\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}}{2a}\,e^{\frac{b^{2}}{8a^{2}}}\,I_{0}\left(\frac{b^{2}}{8a^{2}}\right)\,,

(33)

and so we are left with a two-dimensional integral

\eqalign{\mathcal{B}(x)&\equiv\frac{\kappa}{\pi}\int\frac{1}{g}\,e^{\Theta}\,I_{0}(\Theta)\,d\eta\,d\psi,\quad\text{with}\\
\Theta(x;\eta,\psi)&\equiv\frac{q^{2}(x;\eta,\psi)}{4g^{2}(\eta,\psi)},}

(34)

defining the “
\mathcal{B}
-statistic”.

Several studies have investigated this statistic and its integral, exploring different amplitude
coordinate systems and testing various approaches to derive useful analytical approximations

[
14
,
36
,
20
,
15
,
7
]
.
Recently, a novel geometric approach to expressing the likelihood ratio and deriving the

\mathcal{F}
-statistic through marginalization was explored in
[
34
]
.
In the next section we will discuss the approach of
[
7
]
, which
is the most effective analytic approximation to (
34
) found so far.

3.6
The Bero-Whelan approximation
\mathcal{B}{{}_{\mathrm{BW}}}(x)

This approximation is expressed using circular-polarization-factored (CPF) amplitude coordinates

\mathcal{A}^{\check{\mu}}
, introduced in
[
36
]
as the linear combinations

\eqalign{\mathcal{A}^{\check{1}}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{A}^{1}+\mathcal{A}^{4}\right),&\quad\mathcal{A}^{\check{2}}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{A}^{2}-\mathcal{A}^{3}\right)\,,\\
\mathcal{A}^{\check{3}}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{A}^{1}-\mathcal{A}^{4}\right),&\quad\mathcal{A}^{\check{4}}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left(-\mathcal{A}^{2}-\mathcal{A}^{3}\right)\,,}

(35)

with corresponding transformation of the detector-response matrix (
9
)
into
3
3

3

This definition differs from
[
36
,
7

by
factoring out the data factor
\gamma
.

\mathcal{M}_{\check{\mu}\check{\nu}}=\gamma\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}I&0&L&-K\\
0&I&K&L\\
L&K&J&0\\
-K&L&0&J\end{array}\right),

(36)

in terms of the CPF antenna-pattern coefficients

I=J=A+B,\quad K=2C,\quad L=A-B\,,

(37)

where we assumed the long-wavelength limit.

The Bayes-factor approximation follows from assuming small off-diagonal terms
K/I=2C/(A+B)
and

L/I=(A-B)/(A+B)
, and Taylor-expanding to first order around a diagonal CPF antenna-pattern matrix,
which eventually can be shown to result in the explicit solution

\displaystyle\ln\frac{\mathcal{B}{{}_{\mathrm{BW}}}(x)}{\mathcal{B}(0)}\equiv\ln b_{0}(\hat{y}_{\mathrm{R}})+\ln b_{0}(\hat{y}_{\mathrm{L}})+\hat{A}_{\mathrm{R}}\hat{A}_{\mathrm{L}}\gamma\left[K\sin 4\hat{\psi}+L\cos 4\hat{\psi}\right]

\displaystyle\times\left[\frac{1}{4}\frac{b_{1}(\hat{y}_{\mathrm{R}})}{b_{0}(\hat{y}_{\mathrm{R}})}+\frac{1}{4}\frac{b_{1}(\hat{y}_{\mathrm{L}})}{b_{0}(\hat{y}_{\mathrm{L}})}-\frac{1}{16}\frac{b_{1}(\hat{y}_{\mathrm{R}})}{b_{0}(\hat{y}_{\mathrm{R}})}\frac{b_{1}(\hat{y}_{\mathrm{L}})}{b_{0}(\hat{y}_{\mathrm{L}})}\right],

(38)

in terms of the functions

\eqalign{\ln b_{0}(y)\equiv\ln{}_{1}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{4},1,y\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle y\gg 1}}{{\sim}}-\ln\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)+y-\frac{3}{4}\ln y,\\
\ln b_{1}(y)\equiv\ln{}_{1}F_{1}\left(\frac{5}{4},2,y\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle y\gg 1}}{{\sim}}-\ln\Gamma\left(\frac{5}{4}\right)+y-\frac{3}{4}\ln y,}

(39)

where the asymptotic forms
[

are used for

We further defined the shortcuts

\hat{y}_{\mathrm{R}}\equiv\frac{\gamma I\hat{A}_{\mathrm{R}}^{2}}{2},\quad\hat{y}_{\mathrm{L}}\equiv\frac{\gamma J\hat{A}_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}}{2}\,,

(40)

and the above expressions are evaluated at the maximum-likelihood amplitude estimators

\hat{\mathcal{A}}^{\mu}
of (
12
), translated to polar CPF coordinates
[
36
]

A_{\mathrm{R}}=\frac{A_{+}+A_{\times}}{2},\quad A_{\mathrm{L}}=\frac{A_{+}-A_{\times}}{2},

(41)

and polarization angle
\psi
, which can be obtained by inverting (

The normalization
\mathcal{B}(0)\approx\frac{\kappa\Gamma(1/4)^{2}}{2^{5/2}(\gamma^{2}IJ)^{1/4}}
represents a
(sky-dependent) offset and does not affect the detection power of the statistic.

4
Generalized
\mathcal{B}
-statistic and weak-signal approximation

4.1
Motivation

Recent work
[
11
]
has revealed some surprising shortcomings of the

\mathcal{F}
-statistic for short coherence times
T\lesssim$1\text{\,}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{y}$
. In fact, it was explicitly shown
that an empirically-constructed “dominant-response” statistic
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{AB}}
, defined as

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{AB}}(x)\equiv\left\{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{A}}(x)\quad\text{if}\;A\geq B,\quad\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}(x)\;\text{otherwise}\right\},

(42)

using the partial
\mathcal{F}
-statistics
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{A}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}
of (
26
), can

substantially
improve upon the sensitivity of the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic for short coherence times,
by up to
19\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
.

In the Bayesian framework there is no handle for encoding “short segments”, and in fact the only
remaining freedom in the
\mathcal{B}
-statistic definition (
32
) is in the choice of
h_{0}
-prior,
P\left(h_{0}\right)
.
However, considering that shorter coherence times will correspond to less signal power (
24
),
motivates the idea of exploring an
h_{0}
-prior that allows for describing “weak signals”.

4.2
New
h_{0}
prior: introducing an amplitude scale
H

We consider a half-Gaussian prior on
h_{0}
with scale parameter
H
, namely

P\left(h_{0}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}\,H}\,e^{-\frac{h_{0}^{2}}{2H^{2}}},\quad\text{for }h_{0}\geq 0.

(43)

This prior preserves the functional form of the integrand (
32
) and therefore still allows
for analytic
h_{0}
integration via (
33
) as with the uniform prior, resulting in the
generalized
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{H}}
-statistic:

\eqalign{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{H}}(x)&\equiv\frac{1}{\pi}\int d\eta\,d\psi\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+H_{*}^{2}g^{2}}}\,e^{\Theta_{\mathrm{H}}}\,I_{0}(\Theta_{\mathrm{H}})\,,\quad\text{with}\\
\Theta_{\mathrm{H}}(x;\eta,\psi)&\equiv\frac{H_{*}^{2}\,q^{2}}{4(1+H_{*}^{2}\,g^{2})}\,,}

(44)

where we defined the
relative
scale
H_{*}
in analogy to (
21
), as

H_{*}\equiv H\,\sqrt{\gamma}\,.

(45)

In the strong-signal limit (
H\rightarrow\infty
) this prior converges to a uniform prior, and we
can also recover the standard
\mathcal{B}
-statistic of (
34
) in that limit, namely

\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{H}}(x)\binrel@{\longrightarrow}\binrel@@{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{H\rightarrow\infty}}\mathcal{B}(x)
.
Furthermore, the half-Gaussian prior is more “physical” than the uniform one in two respects:
(i) it favors weaker signals over stronger ones, as would be physically expected, and
(ii) it is normalizable and therefore does not rely on an arbitrary cutoff at some large
h_{0}
value.

From a practical point of view, the new prior does not help with analytically solving the remaining
two marginalization integrals, and is therefore as computationally impractical as the

\mathcal{B}
-statistic.
It does, however, open up a new limiting regime of
weak signals
, when
H\rightarrow 0
, which
we will explore next.

4.3
Weak-signal approximation
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{weak}}(x)

Assuming the weak-signal limit
H\rightarrow 0
of the new
h_{0}
-prior (
43
), we can
Taylor-expand the integrand (
44
) in terms of small
H_{*}\ll 1
, namely

\eqalign{\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+H_{*}^{2}g^{2}}}&=1-\frac{1}{2}H_{*}^{2}g^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(H_{*}^{4}\right),\\
\Theta_{\mathrm{H}}(x;\eta,\psi)&=\frac{1}{4}H_{*}^{2}q^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(H_{*}^{4}\right),\\
e^{\Theta_{\mathrm{H}}}&=1+\frac{1}{4}H_{*}^{2}q^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(H_{*}^{4}\right),\\
I_{0}(\Theta_{\mathrm{H}})&=1+\mathcal{O}\left(H_{*}^{4}\right),}

(46)

and to leading order in
H_{*}
the
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{H}}
-statistic now takes the form

\eqalign{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{H}}(x)&=\frac{1}{\pi}\int d\eta\,d\psi\left(1+\frac{1}{4}H_{*}^{2}\left(q^{2}-2g^{2}\right)\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(H_{*}^{4}\right)\\
&=1+\frac{H_{*}^{2}}{4}\langle{q^{2}-2g^{2}}\rangle_{\eta,\psi}+\mathcal{O}\left(H_{*}^{4}\right).}

(47)

Using the known
\eta,\psi
-averages of (
23
), namely

\langle{\alpha_{1}}\rangle_{\eta,\psi}=\langle{\alpha_{2}}\rangle_{\eta,\psi}=\frac{2}{5}
and
\langle{\alpha_{3}}\rangle_{\eta,\psi}=0
,
applied to (
22
) and (
25
), we find

\langle{g^{2}}\rangle_{\eta,\psi}=\frac{2}{5}\left(A+B\right),\quad\langle{q^{2}}\rangle_{\eta,\psi}=\frac{2}{5}\left(2\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{A}}(x)\,A+2\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}(x)\,B\right),

(48)

resulting in the weak-signal Bayes-factor
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{weak}}
defined as

\eqalign{\ln\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{weak}}(x)&\equiv\frac{H^{2}}{10}\left[\gamma A\,\left(2\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{A}}(x)-2\right)+\gamma B\,\left(2\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}(x)-2\right)\right]\\
&=\frac{H^{2}}{10}\left[\boldsymbol{x}^{2}-\mathrm{Tr}\mathcal{M}\right]\,,}

(49)

where boldface denotes a (column-) four-vector in
amplitude space, i.e.,
\{\boldsymbol{x}\}_{\mu}=x_{\mu}
, and
\boldsymbol{x}^{2}\equiv\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{x}=\sum_{\mu}x_{\mu}^{2}
.
It will be useful to define a simpler but equivalent
\beta
-statistic as

\eqalign{\beta(\boldsymbol{x})&\equiv\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^{2}}{\gamma}=A\,2\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{A}}(\boldsymbol{x})+B\,2\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}(\boldsymbol{x}),}

(50)

which is of order
\beta\sim\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
in the absence of a signal and is independent of the prior
scale
H
.
It is interesting to compare this to the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic expressions of (
27
) and
(
11
), and we can further also write it as

2\mathcal{F}(x)=\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathrm{T}}M^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}}{\gamma}.

(51)

In terms of the
\beta
-statistic, the weak-signal Bayes factor (
49
) is now just

\ln\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{weak}}(x)=\frac{H_{*}^{2}}{10}\,\left(\beta(\boldsymbol{x})-\mathrm{Tr}M\right)\,,

(52)

which is a monotonic function of
\beta
and therefore an equivalent statistic.

We further point out that the coherent
\beta
-statistic is equivalent to the (initial)
5-vector-method statistic constructed in
[
6
]
, which is further analyzed (in
terms of its noise distribution) in
[
5
]
. As noted in

[
6
]
and more recently discussed in greater detail in
[
17
]
,
a different choice of weights in that framework can also lead to the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic instead.
The details of the translation of the notation and formalism to see this equivalence are given in

D
.

4.4
Semi-coherent generalization

A semi-coherent Bayes factor can be derived
[
25
]
by relaxing the
signal hypothesis to allow for independent amplitude parameters
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}
for ever segment

\ell=1,\ldots,N_{\mathrm{seg}}
.
The resulting semi-coherent Bayes factor is then given by the product of per-segment coherent Bayes
factors, each marginalized over its per-segment amplitude parameters
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}
independently.
The resulting semi-coherent weak-signal Bayes factor is therefore

\eqalign{\ln\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathrm{weak}}(x)&\equiv\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{\mathrm{seg}}}\ln\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{weak}}{}_{,\ell}(x)\\
&=\frac{H^{2}}{10}\sum_{\ell}\left[\gamma_{\ell}A_{\ell}\left(2\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{A,\ell}}(x)-2\right)+\gamma_{\ell}B_{\ell}\left(2\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B,\ell}}(x)-2\right)\right].}

(53)

This expression shows that each
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{A,\ell}}(x)
and
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B,\ell}}(x)
contribution is
naturally summed over segments with respective
segment weights

(\gamma A)_{\ell}
and

(\gamma B)_{\ell}
, which account for varying data-factors (i.e., different quality and/or quantity
of available data) and antenna-pattern sensitivity (to a particular sky-direction
\boldsymbol{n}
) over
segments.
Only recently
[
10
]
has shown that applying segment weights of the form

\propto\gamma(A+B)
to the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic (and similarly for the dominant-response statistic

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{AB}}
) improves their detection power, defining the weighted semi-coherent statistics as:

\eqalign{\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{w}}(x)&\equiv\sum_{\ell}w^{\prime}_{\ell}\,\mathcal{F}_{\ell}(x)\,,\quad\text{with}\quad w^{\prime}_{\ell}\equiv K^{\prime}\gamma_{\ell}(A_{\ell}+B_{\ell})\,,\\
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{AB,w}}(x)&\equiv\sum_{\ell}w^{\prime\prime}_{\ell}\,\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{AB,\ell}}(x)\quad\text{with}\quad w^{\prime\prime}_{\ell}\equiv K^{\prime\prime}\gamma_{\ell}\left(Q_{\ell}+\frac{C_{\ell}}{Q_{\ell}}\right),}

(54)

where
Q\equiv\max(A,B)
and
K^{\prime},K^{\prime\prime}
are weight normalizations, typically chosen to obtain unit
mean over segments, i.e.,
\langle{w}\rangle_{\ell}=1
.

Defining per-segment
data weights
as

w^{\ell}\equiv\frac{\gamma_{\ell}}{\overline{\gamma}},\quad\text{with}\quad\overline{\gamma}\equiv\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{seg}}}\sum_{\ell}\gamma_{\ell}\,,

(55)

in terms of the average data factor
\overline{\gamma}
over segments, such that

\sum_{\ell}w^{\ell}=N_{\mathrm{seg}}
, we can introduce a semi-coherent
\widehat{\beta}
-statistic as

\widehat{\beta}(\boldsymbol{x})\equiv\sum_{\ell}w^{\ell}\,\beta_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\ell}\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}^{2}}{\overline{\gamma}},

(56)

and with a corresponding average relative scale defined as
\overline{H_{*}^{2}}\equiv H^{2}\overline{\gamma}
,
the semi-coherent Bayes factor now takes the form

\ln\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathrm{weak}}(x)=\frac{\overline{H_{*}^{2}}}{10}\,\left(\widehat{\beta}(\boldsymbol{x})-\mathrm{Tr}\widehat{M}\right),

(57)

in terms of the semi-coherent antenna-pattern matrix

\widehat{M}=\sum_{\ell}w^{\ell}M_{\ell}=\frac{\sum_{\ell}\mathcal{M}_{\ell}}{\overline{\gamma}}.

(58)

4.5
Do detectable signals falls into the weak-signal regime?

Using the simple (albeit slightly biased
[
33
,
16
]
) sensitivity
estimate of
[
2
]
, we can express the weakest detectable
signal amplitude as
h_{0}^{\prime}\sim 11.4\sqrt{\mathcal{S}/T_{\mathrm{data}}}
at a false-alarm probability of
p_{\mathrm{fa}}=1\%

and a detection probability of
p_{\mathrm{det}}=90\%
. This corresponds to a relative amplitude of

h_{*}^{\prime}=11.4
for a typical well-detectable signal.
Even for semi-coherent searches, assuming a rough
N_{\mathrm{seg}}^{1/4}
sensitivity scaling in terms of the
number of segments, this would only reach the weak-signal threshold of
h_{*}\sim 1
over a single
segment at around
N_{\mathrm{seg}}\sim\mathcal{O}\left(${10}^{4}$\right)
.

This shows that for most typical searches, detectable signals would not actually be expected to fall
into the “weak signal” regime
h_{*}\ll 1
. On the other hand, the half-Gaussian prior has no
hard cutoff and therefore also covers strong signals. We also see that for the resulting
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{weak}}

and
\beta
statistics, increased signal power always translates into higher detection-statistic
values.

The weak-signal prior is sensible, however, exactly because any detectable CW signal is expected to
come from the tail of the actual
h_{0}
distribution of existing astrophysical CW signals in the
data, most of which are in fact undetectably weak.

5
Distribution of the
\beta
-statistic

5.1
Decorrelating
\{x_{\mu}\}

We see in (
49
) that
\beta(\boldsymbol{x})
is a quadratic function of the four matched-filter scalar
products
\{\boldsymbol{x}\}_{\mu}\equiv\left(x|h\right)
, similar to the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic (
11
).
The four
x_{\mu}
are Gaussian distributed with mean and covariance obtained from (
28
) as:

E\left[\boldsymbol{x}\right]=\boldsymbol{s}\,,\qquad\mathrm{cov}\left[\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}\right]=\mathcal{M}\,,

(59)

We can decorrelate these variables by diagonalizing the detector response matrix
\mathcal{M}
of
(
9
) in the form

\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{R}\,\mathcal{W}\,\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{T}},

(60)

in terms of the orthogonal rotation matrix

\mathcal{R}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}r&0\\
0&r\\
\end{array}\right),\quad\text{with}\quad r\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\\
\end{array}\right),

(61)

with the rotation angle
\theta
given by

\tan 2\theta=c,\quad\text{with}\quad c\equiv\frac{2C}{A-B}\,.

(62)

The resulting diagonal matrix is
\mathcal{W}=\gamma\,\mathrm{diag}\left(w^{+},w^{\times},w^{+},w^{\times}\right)
in terms of
the two unique eigenvalues

w^{+,\times}=\frac{1}{2}\left(A+B\pm(A-B)\sqrt{1+c^{2}}\right)\,.

(63)

The action of
\mathcal{R}
on the amplitude-vector
\mathcal{A}^{\mu}
can be shown to be

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\equiv\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}=\left.\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\right|_{2\psi\rightarrow 2\psi-\theta},

(64)

namely a rotation of the polarization axes in the sky plane by
\theta
, defining a new polarization
angle
2\psi^{\prime}\equiv 2\psi-\theta
, defining search-specific “
{+}
” and “
{\times}
” polarizations.
It is further useful to define the matrix

\mathcal{Q}\equiv\mathcal{R}\sqrt{\mathcal{W}},

(65)

such that

\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}^{\mathrm{T}}=\mathcal{M},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{Q}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{W}\,.

(66)

This allows us to define the transformed matched-filter quantities
z_{\mu}=\{\boldsymbol{z}\}_{\mu}
as

\boldsymbol{z}\equiv\mathcal{Q}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x},

(67)

with mean and covariance

\eqalign{E\left[\boldsymbol{z}\right]&=\mathcal{Q}^{-1}\boldsymbol{s}=\mathcal{Q}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}=\sqrt{\mathcal{W}}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime},\\
\mathrm{cov}\left[\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{T}}\right]&=\mathcal{Q}^{-1}\mathcal{M}\mathcal{Q}^{-1}{}^{\mathrm{T}}=\mathbb{I}\,,}

(68)

i.e., the four
z_{\mu}
are uncorrelated unit normal variates.

5.2
Coherent statistics

The
\beta
-statistic of (
50
) now reads as

\eqalign{\beta(\boldsymbol{x})&\equiv\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{x}}{\gamma}=\frac{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathcal{W}\boldsymbol{z}}{\gamma}=w^{+}\,2\mathcal{F}_{+}(\boldsymbol{x})+w^{\times}\,2\mathcal{F}_{\times}(\boldsymbol{x})\,,}

(69)

where we defined

2\mathcal{F}_{+}(\boldsymbol{x})\equiv z_{1}^{2}+z_{3}^{2}\,\qquad 2\mathcal{F}_{\times}(\boldsymbol{x})\equiv z_{2}^{2}+z_{4}^{2}\,,

(70)

which are two
uncorrelated

\chi^{2}
-distributed statistics with two degrees of freedom and
non-centrality parameters

\eqalign{\rho^{2}_{+}&\equiv\gamma w^{+}\left[\left(\mathcal{A}^{\prime 1}\right)^{2}+\left(\mathcal{A}^{\prime 3}\right)^{2}\right]=h_{*}^{2}\,w^{+}\,\alpha_{1}(\eta,\psi^{\prime}),\\
\rho^{2}_{\times}&\equiv\gamma w^{\times}\left[\left(\mathcal{A}^{\prime 2}\right)^{2}+\left(\mathcal{A}^{\prime 4}\right)^{2}\right]=h_{*}^{2}\,w^{\times}\,\alpha_{2}(\eta,\psi^{\prime}),}

(71)

respectively, in terms of the intrinsic (rotated) polarization angle
\psi^{\prime}
introduced in the
previous section.
Thus
\beta(\boldsymbol{x})
follows a
generalized

\tilde{\chi}^{2}
-distribution

[
13
,
12
]
, which we denote as

\beta\sim\tilde{\chi}_{\vec{w},\vec{2},\vec{\rho^{2}}}
, with vectors of weights

\vec{w}=(w^{+},w^{\times})
, degrees of freedom
\vec{2}=(2,2)
and non-centrality parameters

\vec{\rho^{2}}=(\rho^{2}_{+},\rho^{2}_{\times})
.
This distribution has known mean and variance

\eqalign{E\left[\beta\right]&=\sum_{p=+,\times}w^{p}\left(2+\rho^{2}_{p}\right),\\
\mathrm{var}\left[\beta\right]&=2\sum_{p=+,\times}(w^{p})^{2}\left(2+2\rho^{2}_{p}\right)\,.}

(72)

It is interesting to note that the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic (
11
), which in these variables reads as

2\mathcal{F}(x)=\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathcal{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{z}=z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}+z_{3}^{2}+z_{4}^{2}\,,

(73)

and therefore follows a
\chi^{2}
-distribution with four degrees of freedom as first shown in

[
21
]
, is simply an
unweighted
sum of the two partial statistics

2\mathcal{F}_{{+,\times}}
, namely

2\mathcal{F}(x)=2\mathcal{F}_{+}(\boldsymbol{x})+2\mathcal{F}_{\times}(\boldsymbol{x})\,,

(74)

with corresponding mean and variance

\eqalign{E\left[2\mathcal{F}\right]=4+\rho^{2}\,,\quad\mathrm{var}\left[2\mathcal{F}\right]=2(4+2\rho^{2})\,,}

(75)

in terms of the total signal power

\rho^{2}=\rho_{+}^{2}+\rho_{\times}^{2}\,.

(76)

This is effectively the unweighted special case
w^{+}=w^{\times}=1
of the
\beta
-statistic.

5.3
Semicoherent
\widehat{\beta}
-statistic

The semi-coherent generalization of the
\beta
-statistic (
56
) can now be expressed as

\widehat{\beta}(x)\equiv\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{\mathrm{seg}}}w^{\ell}\beta_{\ell}(x)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{\mathrm{seg}}}\sum_{p=+,\times}w^{\ell{}p}\,2\mathcal{F}_{\ell{}p}(x),

(77)

in terms of
2N_{\mathrm{seg}}
independent
\chi^{2}
-distributed statistics
2\mathcal{F}_{\ell{}p}
with two degrees of
freedom and non-centrality parameters
\rho^{2}_{\ell{}p}
, respectively, and per-segment weights

w^{\ell{}p}
defined as

w^{\ell{}p}\equiv w^{\ell}w^{p}_{(\ell)}\,,

(78)

in terms of the polarization weights
w^{p}_{(\ell)}
of (
63
) for segment
\ell
and the
data-weights
w^{\ell}
of (
55
).
Therefore we see that
\widehat{\beta}
follows a generalized
\tilde{\chi}^{2}
-distribution

\tilde{\chi}^{2}_{\vec{w},\vec{2},\vec{\rho^{2}}}
, with length-
2N_{\mathrm{seg}}
vectors

\eqalign{\vec{w}&=(w^{1{+}},w^{1{\times}},\ldots,w^{N_{\mathrm{seg}}{+}},w^{N_{\mathrm{seg}}{\times}}),\\
\vec{2}&=(2,2,\ldots,2,2),\\
\vec{\rho^{2}}&=(\rho^{2}_{1{+}},\rho^{2}_{1{\times}},\ldots,\rho^{2}_{N_{\mathrm{seg}}{+}},\rho^{2}_{N_{\mathrm{seg}}{\times}}),}

(79)

with resulting mean and variance

\eqalign{E\left[\widehat{\beta}\right]&=\sum_{\ell{}p}w^{\ell{}p}\left(2+\rho^{2}_{\ell{}p}\right),\\
\mathrm{var}\left[\widehat{\beta}\right]&=2\sum_{\ell{}p}(w^{\ell{}p})^{2}\,\left(2+2\rho_{\ell{}p}^{2}\right).}

(80)

5.4
Noise distribution and false-alarm probability

The mean and variance (
80
) for
\widehat{\beta}
in the noise case can be made more explicit as

\eqalign{\left.E\left[\widehat{\beta}\right]\right._{h_{0}=0}&=2\sum_{\ell{}p}w^{\ell{}p}=\mathrm{Tr}\widehat{M},\\
\left.\mathrm{var}\left[\widehat{\beta}\right]\right._{h_{0}=0}&=4\sum_{\ell{}p}(w^{\ell{}p})^{2}=\frac{4}{\overline{\gamma}^{2}}\sum_{\ell}\gamma_{\ell}^{2}\left(A_{\ell}^{2}+B_{\ell}^{2}+2C_{\ell}^{2}\right),}

(81)

where we used the eigenvalue expressions of (
63
).
Although the generalized
\tilde{\chi}^{2}
-distribution has a known characteristic function, obtaining a
probability density function (pdf) or threshold-crossing probabilities generally requires either
direct numerical approaches (e.g., see
[
13
,
12
]
) or
Monte-Carlo simulation.

Some more analytical progress can be made in the noise case
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{G}}:h_{0}=0
:
the per-segment quantities
Z_{\ell{+}}\equiv\sqrt{w^{\ell{+}}}(z_{\ell 1}-iz_{\ell 3})
and

Z_{\ell{\times}}\equiv\sqrt{w^{\ell{\times}}}(z_{\ell 2}-iz_{\ell 4})
are then circularly-symmetric
(centered) complex Gaussians with zero mean,
E\left[Z_{\ell{}p}\right]=0
, and variances

E\left[|Z_{\ell{}p}|^{2}\right]=2w^{\ell{}p}
, where
p\in\{+,\times\}
.
We can write the semi-coherent
\widehat{\beta}
-statistic in these variables as

\widehat{\beta}(x)=\sum_{\ell{}p}|Z_{\ell{}p}|^{2},

(82)

and one can then show (cf. Eq. (19) in
[
19
]
) that the noise pdf is

P\left(\widehat{\beta}|\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{G}}\right)=\sum_{\ell{}p}\frac{e^{-\frac{\widehat{\beta}}{2w^{\ell{}p}}}}{2w^{\ell{}p}\prod_{\ell^{\prime}p^{\prime}\not=\ell{}p}\left(1-\frac{w^{\ell^{\prime}p^{\prime}}}{w^{\ell{}p}}\right)}\,,

(83)

provided that all weights
w^{\ell{}p}
are unique.
In practice this expression will be problematic for numerical reasons, as any weights being too
close to each other will lead to the denominator approaching zero.

Alternatively, in the limit of many segments,
N_{\mathrm{seg}}\gg 1
one could use the central-limit Gaussian
distribution with given mean and variance (
80
), which will likely yield a feasible
approximation for many use cases, but testing and quantifying this approach left to future work.

In the coherent case we can write this as

P\left(\beta|\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{G}}\right)=\frac{1}{2(w^{+}-w^{\times})}\left[e^{-\frac{\beta}{2w^{+}}}-e^{-\frac{\beta}{2w^{\times}}}\right],

(84)

and analytically integrate for the false-alarm probability

\eqalign{p_{\mathrm{fa}}(\beta_{\mathrm{thr}})&\equiv P\left(\beta>\beta_{\mathrm{thr}}|\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{G}}\right)=\int_{\beta_{\mathrm{thr}}}^{\infty}P\left(\beta|\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{G}}\right)\,d\beta\\
&=\frac{1}{w^{+}-w^{\times}}\left[w^{+}e^{-\frac{\beta_{\mathrm{thr}}}{2w^{+}}}-w^{\times}e^{-\frac{\beta_{\mathrm{thr}}}{2w^{\times}}}\right],}

(85)

in terms of the false-alarm threshold
\beta_{\mathrm{thr}}
.
This result agrees with the analysis in
[
5
]
(see Eqs.(34) and (35)) for the
classic 5-vector statistic, which is equivalent to
\beta
-statistic as mentioned in
Sec.
4.3
and shown explicitly in
D
.

Contrary to the
\chi^{2}
-distributed statistics such as
2\mathcal{F}
or the dominant-response statistics

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{AB}}
, the noise distribution (and therefore false-alarm probabilities) of the

\beta
-statistic depends on the sky position due to the antenna-pattern weights
w^{+,\times}
.
The same is true for the weighted
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{w}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ABw}}
statistics of (
54
).
This sky-dependent false-alarm probability complicates the application of this statistic to all-sky
searches and we postpone this topic to future work, focusing instead on searches in single
phase-evolution points
\lambda
instead. We have verified the qualitative robustness of all
subsequent results by testing in different sky points (not shown).

The agreement between the theoretical noise distribution (
84
) and the measured distribution
for
\beta
will be tested in the next section.

7
Conclusions

In this paper we have generalized the Bayesian
\mathcal{B}
-statistic by using a half-Gaussian

h_{0}
-prior instead of a uniform prior.
This reduces to the
\mathcal{B}
-statistic in the strong-signal limit (
H\rightarrow\infty
) but yields a new analytic
approximation in the weak-signal limit (
H\rightarrow 0
), defining the new weak-signal statistic

\beta(x)
.
This statistic is shown to follow a generalized
\tilde{\chi}^{2}
-distribution with
(2,2)
degrees of
freedom per segment, with weights determined by the eigenvalues of the detector-response matrix

\mathcal{M}_{\mu\nu}
and non-centrality parameters given by the corresponding signal-power components.

The sensitivity of
\beta
is found to be comparable to
\mathcal{F}
for day-long coherent searches, making
it slightly less sensitive than the
\mathcal{B}
-statistic (and the Bero-Whelan approximation).
However, for semi-coherent searches, it matches or outperforms both
(i) the best short-segment statistic (i.e., the weighted dominant-response statistic

\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{ABw}}
), and
(ii) the best practical long-segment statistic, i.e., the weighted
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{w}}
-statistic.

Overall, the weak-signal
\beta
-statistic appears to be a powerful and practical detection
statistic across a wide range of segment lengths that we have tested, namely from

900\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
to
10\text{\,}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{y}\mathrm{s}
.
Further testing is needed across various real-world scenarios and different false-alarm
regimes beyond those considered here.

One interesting practical question is how to correctly perform an all-sky search with a statistic
(such as
\beta
) that has a sky-position-dependent false-alarm distribution.
This issue is not new and already affects any antenna-pattern-weighted segment statistics.
A common practical solution is to use a “noise-normalized” critical-ratio statistic for the
candidate ranking.

Another potentially interesting application of this new statistic is the line-robust framework of

[
23
,
24
]
.
The prior scaling parameter
H
, which governs the new Bayes factor, has a more direct physical
interpretation compared to the unphysical prior cutoff parameter in the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic-prior, which
complicates the correct tuning of the line-robust
B_{\mathrm{S/GL}}
statistics.

I am very grateful to Pep B. Covas for numerous insightful discussions on short-segment statistics
that ultimately motivated this work.
I thank Luca D’Onofrio for very helpful feedback on the 5-vector framework translation.

Appendix A
Alternative derivation of
\beta
from weak-signal
\mathcal{F}
-statistic prior

Similar to the idea of using a (half-) Gaussian
h_{0}
-prior (
43
),
we can use a Gaussian
\mathcal{A}^{\mu}
prior centered on zero with scale
H
, namely

P\left(\mathcal{A}|\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}}\right)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}H^{4}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{A}^{\mu}C_{\mu\nu}\mathcal{A}^{\nu}}\quad\text{with}\quad C_{\mu\nu}\equiv\frac{1}{H^{2}}\delta_{\mu\nu}\,,

(86)

which reduces to the original uniform-
\mathcal{A}^{\mu}

\mathcal{F}
-statistic prior of Sec.
3.2

in the strong-signal limit
H\rightarrow\infty
.
This prior preserves the functional form of the likelihood ratio (
7
) and we can write the
resulting Bayes-factor integral in analogy to (
16
) as

B_{\mathrm{{\mathrm{S}}/{\mathrm{G}}}}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{H}}}}e^{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{H}}(x)}\,,

(87)

with modified detector-response matrix

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{H}}{}_{\mu\nu}\equiv\mathcal{M}_{\mu\nu}+\frac{1}{H^{2}}\delta_{\mu\nu}=\gamma\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}m_{\mathrm{H}}&0\\
0&m_{\mathrm{H}}\end{array}\right),

(88)

where

m_{\mathrm{H}}\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A+\frac{1}{\gamma H^{2}}&C\\
C&B+\frac{1}{\gamma H^{2}}\end{array}\right)

(89)

and we defined the modified
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{H}}
-statistic as

2\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{H}}(x)\equiv x_{\mu}\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{H}}^{-1}\right)^{\mu\nu}x_{\nu}\,.

(90)

Considering
\det\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{H}}=\gamma^{4}D_{\mathrm{H}}^{2}
with the sub-determinant

D_{\mathrm{H}}\equiv\det m_{\mathrm{H}}=D+\frac{A+B}{\gamma H^{2}}+\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}H^{4}}\,,

(91)

in the weak-signal limit
H_{*}^{2}\ll 1
we obtain the limiting expressions

D_{\mathrm{H}}\binrel@{\longrightarrow}\binrel@@{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{}H\rightarrow 0}}\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}{}H^{4}}
,

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{H}}\rightarrow\frac{1}{{}H^{2}}\mathbb{I}
, and
\sqrt{\det\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{H}}}\rightarrow H^{-4}
, and finally

2\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{H}}(x)\binrel@{\longrightarrow}\binrel@@{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{{}H\rightarrow 0}}\gamma{}H^{2}\beta(x),

(92)

which yields the resulting Bayes factor as

B_{\mathrm{S/G}}\rightarrow H^{4}e^{\frac{1}{2}H_{*}^{2}\,\beta(x)}\,,

(93)

in terms of the weak-signal
\beta
-statistic of (
50
).
While normalized differently, this is otherwise equivalent to the weak-signal Bayes factor obtained
in (
52
).

Appendix B
General matched-filter scalar product
\left(x|y\right)

The scalar product
\left(x|y\right)
of (
5
) can be fully generalized

[
27
,
36
]
to several detectors
X=1,\ldots,N_{\mathrm{d}}
