Associations of meteorological variability with sesame yield in semi-arid Bundelkhand region of India
Authors: RN Singh, Sonam Sah, Santosha Rathod, B. Das, Yogeshwar Singh, A. K. Singh, Anil Kumar, K. S. Reddy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding long-term changes in weather variables and their impact on crop productivity is essential for climate resilient agriculture in semi-arid regions of India. This study assessed the district wise long-term trends in temperature and rainfall and examined their linkages with sesame (Sesamum indicum L.) yield across Bundelkhand region of India. Long-term monthly temperature and seasonal rainfall trends were analyzed using simple linear regression, Mann-Kendall, modified Mann-Kendall and Sen’s slope methods. District-wise crop weather relations between sesame yield and weather variables were evaluated using correlation analysis and stepwise linear regression models. The long-term trends revealed a consistent warming across the region (0.9–2.2 °C/100 years), particularly during the late monsoon and post-monsoon months, whereas monsoon as well as annual rainfall showed a declining trend at the rate of -1.03 to -1.51 mm/year. Correlation results indicated that sesame yield was positively associated with temperature and heat accumulation indices (r = 0.28 to 0.48) especially during August–September, reflecting favourable thermal conditions for crop growth. In contrast, rainfall-related variables, including rainfall amount, number of rainy days, heavy rainfall events, and prolonged wet spells showed negative correlations with sesame yield (r = -0.26 to -0.47), with August rainfall exerting the strongest adverse influence. Regression models (R2 = 0.29 to 0.78) identified monthly temperature indices (Tmin, Tmax, Tavg, DTR, and GDD) and rainfall distribution and intensity indices (RainyDays, R10_days, R20_days, CWD, SDII, and CDD) as more important predictors than seasonal averages. The findings revealed that moderate warming may benefit sesame production, whereas excess and poorly distributed rainfall remains an important climatic constraint. Overall, this biometeorological assessment offers a strong scientific basis for formulating climate-resilient sesame production strategies that effectively address long-term climate trends as well as short-term weather variability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55293-3.

Introduction

Atmospheric stresses arising due to changing rainfall and temperature patterns are among the most critical factors governing crop productivity in rainfed agricultural systems, particularly in semi-arid environments1,2. Crops in these regions are often exposed to multiple abiotic stressors like heat or cold stress, moisture excess/deficit, erratic rainfall, prolonged dry/wet spells etc. With increasing temperatures and declining rainfall in most parts of the India, the frequency of these atmospheric stresses affecting agricultural systems has increased substantially3–5. Long-term analyses of meteorological variables like rainfall and temperature provides a robust basis for quantifying abiotic stresses, understanding their gradual shifts and their potential impacts on crop performance.

In addition to long-term changes in climate, increasing attention is being given to short term weather variability (monthly and seasonal), as agricultural systems often respond strongly to short-duration extreme weather events. Weather variables and stress indices derived on monthly and seasonal scales from daily temperature and rainfall data, such as growing degree days, diurnal temperature range, heat and moisture stress indicators, rainfall intensity, consecutive wet and dry days etc. provide valuable information into crop-weather interactions6,7. These indices capture the timings, magnitude, and persistence of atmospheric stress events that directly influence crop growth and yield within short period of time. These indices are particularly well suited to represent the complex, non-linear responses of crops to abiotic stresses, particularly under changing climatic conditions with increased weather variability. Consequently, analyzing crop weather relations with short- term weather variability has also become increasingly important for accurately assessing climate risks to crop production systems.

Bundelkhand represents a typical semi-arid agro-ecological system characterized by irregular rainfall, frequent dry spells, and recurrent droughts8–11. Agriculture in this region is predominantly rainfed and is highly vulnerable to both short-term weather variability and long-term changes in meteorological variables, especially rainfall and temperature. Recent studies showed notable changes in rainfall4(amount, intensity and distribution), temperature5 and enhanced variability in weather variables including drought12 across Bundelkhand, posing a serious challenge to crop productivity in the region13. Kuriachen et al.14 reported that a 1 °C increase in minimum temperature results in yield reductions of approximately 5.6% in wheat, 4.5% in chickpea, 9.6% in mustard and 6.9% in linseed. In contrast, crop yields showed a positive association with rainfall and irrigation, which was found to partially offset the negative impacts of warming particularly for wheat and chickpea in Bundelkhand region15–17. Given the region’s socio-economic vulnerability and climate-sensitive agriculture, a detailed analysis linking long-term trends and short-term weather variability with crop yields is essential to understand the impact of these changes on agricultural production system.

Sesame (Sesamum indicum L.), also known as queen of oilseed crops, is one of the most important kharif oilseed crops grown in semi-arid Bundelkhand region. Sesame is valued for its high oil content, rich antioxidants, and adaptability to marginal environments, making it an important crop in rainfed regions and low-input agricultural systems18–20. At the regional scale, sesame, along with pigeonpea and millets, accounts for nearly one-quarter (~ 24%) of the total kharif cropped area in Bundelkhand, comparable to major pulse - oilseed systems and exceeding the area under irrigated cereals in marginal landscapes. The area under sesame expanded substantially from approximately 27,500 ha in 1989 to over 2,19,000 ha in 2019 showing a compounded annual growth rate of 7.2%, reflecting a strong farmer preference for this crop under increasing climate uncertainty21. Despite this expansion, sesame productivity in the region has remained low and highly variable, underscoring its pronounced sensitivity to atmospheric stresses. The dominance of sesame in rainfed, low-input systems and its documented responsiveness to rainfall distribution, wet spells, and extreme temperature variations make it an appropriate crop for investigating biometeorological controls on yield variability in Bundelkhand region of India.

Although several studies have analyzed long term climate trends and crop weather relationships in isolation, integrated analyses that combine long-term climatic trends, weather stress indices, and crop yield responses at a district level remain limited, particularly for sesame in semi-arid Bundelkhand region13,14,22–24. Such integrated assessments are essential for quantifying the climate change, identifying key climatic drivers of yield variability and for developing climate resilient management strategies. Therefore, the present study aims to (i) analyze long-term trends in temperature and rainfall across all districts of the Bundelkhand region, (ii) evaluate the relationships between sesame yield and multiple temperature and rainfall based weather indices using correlation analysis, and (iii) identify key meteorological predictors of yield variability through regression modelling. This study is novel in integrating long-term climate trends with growing season weather variability and a comprehensive set of weather variables and extreme weather indices at the district scale. Unlike previous studies in the region, it quantifies the relative influence of these variables, including extremes on sesame yield variability, providing a more comprehensive understanding of climate- yield interactions. The findings are expected to provide insights into climate-induced risks to sesame production and support informed adaptation strategies in semi-arid agro-ecosystems.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study focused on the semi-arid Bundelkhand region of India, covering 7 districts of southern Uttar Pradesh and 6 districts of northern Madhya Pradesh (Fig. 1). The Bundelkhand region is part of central India, and is situated between latitudes 23°20’ N and 26°20’ N and longitudes 78°20’ E and 81°40’ E. The area in Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh that spans over 7.08 million hectares (Mha) and is distinguished by hard rocks, undulating topography, and variable slopes. The region is characterized by high climatic variability, recurrent droughts, and frequent thermal extremes during the monsoon season. Sesame, a climate-sensitive oilseed crop, is predominantly cultivated under rainfed conditions in this region.

Meteorological data

Gridded daily meteorological data were obtained from the India Meteorological Department (IMD). Temperature data (maximum and minimum temperature) were available for the period 1951–2023, while rainfall data spanned 1901–2023. The IMD gridded datasets were extracted for all districts of the Bundelkhand region and aggregated to the district scale using R software version 4.5.225 (https://www.r-project.org/).

Trend analysis

To assess long-term changes in climatic conditions, trend analyses were performed separately for temperature and rainfall variables using R software version 4.5.225. Monthly trends were analyzed for maximum and minimum temperatures, while for rainfall seasonal (winter, pre-monsoon, monsoon, post-monsoon) and annual trends were analyzed. The Mann–Kendall (MK) test26,27 which is a rank-based non-parametric test, was used to detect significant trend in meteorological time series data28. The positive and negative values of standardized MK test statistic indicate increasing and decreasing trend, respectively. Whereas, modified Mann–Kendall (mMK) test was employed to analyze serially correlated data using the Hamed and Rao29 variance correction approach. The autocorrelation in the rainfall time series data was calculated using the expressions of Shahin et al.30 and Haan31. Anderson’s test is used to calculate the upper and lower critical values of autocorrelation function32. Lag-1 autocorrelation coefficient in the data series was calculated and tested against the 5% confidence limits. If the lag-1 autocorrelation coefficient was found to be significant, mMK was applied to detect the trend33. A positive value of the standardized mMK test statistic (Zmmk) represents an increasing trend and a negative value of Zmmk represents a decreasing trend in the time series data. The null hypothesis (no trend) and alternate hypothesis (trend) are tested at 5% significance levels. The magnitude of trends was estimated using both non-parametric Sen’s slope estimator, and parametric simple linear regression (SLR). Spatial patterns of detected trends were mapped at the district level to visualize regional heterogeneity across Bundelkhand using ArcGIS Desktop v 10.134 (https://www.esri.com/en-us/home) .

Crop yield data

District wise sesame yield data for the period 1966–2021 were compiled from two sources. First is the International Crops Research Institute for the Semi-Arid Tropics35 and second is the Directorate of Economics and Statistics, Department of Agriculture and Farmers Welfare36, Government of India. We restricted our analysis to only those districts where at least 40 years of crop data was available. To remove long-term trends associated with technological progress and management improvements, the yield data was detrended. For each district, a linear regression of yield on time was fitted as: Yt = α + βt + εt, where Yt is the observed yield in year t, α is the intercept, βt represents the long-term trend component and εt is the residual term. The detrended yield was defined as the residuals (εt), representing deviations from the fitted trend37,38.

Crop weather relations

For the crop yield and weather relation analysis (1966–2021), daily meteorological data were used to compute a comprehensive set of growing season monthly (June, July, August, September and October) and seasonal (kharif) weather variables and stress indices using R statistical software version 4.5.025. The details of temperature and rainfall variables used are presented in Table 1. The total 20 indices computed on monthly (5 kharif season months) and seasonal (kharif) scales resulted in 120 meteorological variables. The relationships between sesame yield and meteorological variables were examined using a combination of Pearson’s correlation analysis and stepwise linear regression in SAS software version 9.339 (https://support.sas.com/software/93/). Initially, correlation analysis was conducted to identify dominant weather variables influencing sesame yield. Subsequently, stepwise regression analysis was performed to identify the most influential predictors among explanatory variables.

Model selection was based on partial R², F-statistics, and significance levels, with entry and retention criteria set at conventional thresholds. Multicollinearity among the selected variables was evaluated using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF)40,41. The normality of residuals was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test to verify the assumptions of the regression models42.

Results

Weather and yield summary

Temperature and rainfall of the whole Bundelkhand region of India showed pronounced seasonal variability (Table 2.). The mean monthly maximum temperatures ranged from 23.5 ± 1.3 °C (January) to 41.7 ± 1.2 °C (May), while mean minimum temperatures varied from 8.3 ± 1.1 °C (January) to 27.2 ± 1.0 °C (June). Rainfall showed a highly variable seasonal patterns with highest amount of 846.7 ± 186.0 mm occurring in the monsoon months (June–September). The highest mean monthly rainfall in the region was observed in month of August (304.5 ± 107.7 mm). Winter, pre-monsoon and post-monsoon seasons were relatively dry with mean seasonal rainfall of 27.7 ± 24.9 mm, 19.2 ± 17.7 mm and 44.1 ± 45.1 mm, respectively. The CV of rainfall depicted high rainfall variability reaching as high as 225% in November.

Trends of monthly temperatures

The month-wise trend analysis of mean temperature (Tavg) across Bundelkhand stations revealed pronounced temporal variability (Figs. 3 and 4), with consistent warming in all months except January, May, and June, when a weak declining trend was observed. January exhibited predominantly declining trends, with significant cooling observed at Chitrakoot (SS = -1.062 °C/100 year; m = -1.247 °C/100 year), Jalaun (SS = -1.172 °C/100 year; m = -1.253 °C/100 year), and Banda (SS = -0.999 °C/100 year). In contrast, February and March showed weak warming tendencies across the region, though these trends were non-significant. April showed clear warming, with statistically significant positive trends at Datia (SS = 1.383 °C/100 year; m = 1.430 °C/100 year), Sagar (SS = 1.265 °C/100 year; m = 1.299 °C/100 year), Tikamgarh (SS = 1.182 °C/100 year; m = 1.285 °C/100 year), and Lalitpur (SS = 1.329 °C/100 year; m = 1.440 °C/100 year), indicating an intensification of pre-monsoon temperatures. May and June were dominated by mild cooling at most stations, which was statistically non-significant, except for Chitrakoot in May (SS = -1.080 °C/100 year; m = -1.196 °C/100 year), which showed a significant decline. July showed a shift towards weakly increasing, non-significant temperature trends (SS = 0.533 to 0.890 °C/100 year; m = 0.539 to 0.799 °C/100 year). Strong and statistically significant warming across almost all districts was evident from August onwards. In August, significant positive trends were observed across all stations, with Sen’s slope values between 0.919 and 1.138 °C/100 year and m ranging from 0.829 to 1.142 °C/100 year, including the regional mean for Bundelkhand (1.0 °C/100 year). The strongest increases were observed in Jalaun (SS = 1.138 °C/100 year, m = 1.124 °C/100 year) and Chitrakoot (SS = 1.120 °C/100 year, m = 1.142 °C/100 year). This intensification continued in September (SS values between 1.098 and 1.499 °C/100 year; m between 1.234 and 1.525 °C/100 year), October (SS values ranged from 1.079 to 1.927 °C/100 year; m from 0.983 to 1.836 °C/100 year), and November (SS values ranging from 1.576 to 2.213 °C/100 year; m from 1.452 to 2.158 °C/100 year), where again all districts recorded significant positive trends.

Trends of seasonal and annual rainfall

The seasonal analysis of rainfall across Bundelkhand revealed variable temporal trends, as presented in Fig. 5. Winter rainfall exhibited a weak and non-significant declining trend at all stations, with Mann–Kendall (SS) values ranging from − 0.02 to -0.12 mm/year and SLR (m) values ranging from 0.00 to -0.12 mm/year. Pre-monsoon rainfall showed mixed trends across the region, while most stations exhibited non-significant trends in SLR (m). Statistically significant increase in pre-monsoon rainfall were observed at Hamirpur (SS = 0.07 mm/year) and Mahoba (SS = 0.07 mm/year), whereas a significant decrease was recorded at Panna (SS = -0.07 mm/year). Monsoon rainfall demonstrated a pronounced decreasing trends in all districts, with SS values ranging from − 0.16 to -1.75 mm/year and m values from − 0.03 to -1.52 mm/year.

Relatively higher decreases (> 1 mm/year) were observed at Banda (SS = -1.43 mm/year; m = -1.28 mm/year), Chitrakoot (SS = -1.59 mm/year; m = -1.47 mm/year), Hamirpur (SS = -1.31 mm/year; m = -1.14 mm/year), Jalaun (SS = -1.75 mm/year; m = -1.52 mm/year), and Jhansi (SS = -1.30 mm/year; m = -1.04 mm/year), indicating a substantial weakening of monsoon rainfall in these districts. Post-monsoon rainfall exhibited non-significant variations, with SS values ranging between − 0.16 and 0.05 mm/year and m values between − 0.21 and 0.05 mm/year, indicating weakly declining to relatively stable rainfall during this season. Annual rainfall showed declining trends at all stations, with statistically significant decreases observed at Chhatarpur (SS = -1.51 mm/year), Panna (SS = -1.66 mm/year; m = -1.32 mm/year), Banda (SS = -1.52 mm/year; m = -1.32 mm/year), Chitrakoot (SS = -1.85 mm/year; m = -1.60 mm/year), Hamirpur (SS = -1.29 mm/year; m = -1.12 mm/year), Jalaun (SS = -1.71 mm/year; m = -1.51 mm/year), Jhansi (SS = -1.44 mm/year), and Mahoba (SS = -1.03 mm/year). Overall, monsoon rainfall contributed most to the observed annual declines, highlighting a significant reduction in the primary rainy season across the region.

At the regional scale, Bundelkhand exhibited predominantly declining rainfall trends during all seasons except pre-monsoon. However, the decline was statistically significant only during the monsoon season as indicated by SS (-1.09 mm/year). Annual rainfall also demonstrated a statistically significant decreasing trend in Bundelkhand (SS = -1.30 mm/year; m = -1.00 mm/year), confirming an overall reduction in precipitation over the region.

Regression analysis

Stepwise linear regression models revealed that different climate variables were important in different districts. The variable selected in the models were not uniform in the districts (Table 5). The VIF values for all selected variables were found to be less than 10, indicating acceptable levels of multicollinearity, considering weather variables43. The Shapiro–Wilk test results were non-significant for all models, confirming that the residuals followed a normal distribution and that model assumptions were satisfied. The regression models explained substantial amount of yield variability in different districts with final model R² values ranging from 0.29 to 0.79, indicating strong but spatially heterogeneous climate and yield relationships. In Jhansi, the final regression model was able to explain 65.1% of yield variability. The SU_days_Oct showed a strong negative effect (PE = -220.78, p < 0.0001), followed by SDII_Oct (PE = -3.51, p < 0.001) and CDD_Kharif (PE = -4.67, p = 0.005). R20_days_Aug also showed a negative effect (PE =-5.61, p = 0.050). In contrast, TR_days_Kharif (PE = 26.39, p < 0.001) and Tmin_mean_Aug (PE = 69.44, p = 0.001) showed positive impacts on yield. In Hamirpur, the final regression model was able to explain 41.2% of yield variability. Tmin_mean_Sep (PE = 37.95, p < 0.001 and SDII_Jun (PE = 26.63, p < 0.001) were having significant positive impact on sesame yield in Hamirpur district. In contrast PRCPTOT_Jul (PE = -1.16, p = 0.002), CDD_Kharif (PE = -3.68, p = 0.014), and SDII_Jun (PE = -4.15, p = 0.006) have negative effects on yield. In Damoh, the regression model exhibited the highest model performance among all districts, with the final regression model explaining 78.9% of total yield variability. CWD_Aug (PE = -12.09, p < 0.0001) and R10_days_Jul (PE = -22.67, p = 0.003) were major negative yield predictors. CDD_Jul, SU_days_Oct, HWDI_Oct, R20_days_Oct and Rainfall_Sep were also having significant negative impacts. Positive effects on yield were observed for Tmin_mean_Sep (PE = 67.70, p = 0.006), Tavg_mean_Oct (PE = 84.45, p < 0.001), Tmax_mean_Jul (PE = 76.14, p < 0.0001), and PRCPTOT_Jul (PE = 0.88, p < 0.0001). In Banda, the final model explained 61.3% of yield variability. Tmin_mean_Sep was the strongest positive predictor (PE = 82.81, p < 0.0001), whereas HeatStressDays_Aug showed highest negative effect (PE = -14.55, p < 0.0001). Variables related to high rainfall including R20_days_Sep (PE = 21.08, p < 0.0001) and R20_days_Jun (PE = 33.68, p = 0.005) showed positive effects, while RainyDays_Kharif (PE = -3.11, p = 0.002) showed negative effect on sesame yield. In Datia, the model accounted for 74.0% of the total yield variability. RainyDays_Aug (PE = -38.50, p < 0.0001), CDD_Jul (PE = -65.4, p < 0.0001) and DTR_mean_Oct (PE = -79.3, p < 0.0001) showed strong negative impacts on yield. CDD_Oct (PE = 16.6, p < 0.0001) and Tmin_mean_Jul (PE = 88.35, p = 0.010) were among variables showing major positive impacts on yield. Several other temperature and rainfall variables were retained in the model. In Panna, the final regression model was able to explain 39.9% of yield variability and has only 3 variables. Tavg_mean_Aug was single most important positive predictor (PE = 146.51, p < 0.0001), followed by Tmax_mean_Sep (PE = 78.27, p = 0.002). In contrast, only HeatStressDays_Sep (PE = -24.85, p < 0.001) showed significantly negative association with yield. In Chhatarpur, the final regression model showed lowest explanatory power among all districts with R² = 0.29. The CWD_Aug was the only statistically significant parameter retained, showing a negative effect on yield (PE = -7.19, p = 0.005). In Sagar, the final regression model explained 67.2% of the total yield variability. Discussion

This study provides an integrated assessment of meteorological variability and their associations with sesame yield across the semi-arid Bundelkhand region. By combining long-term trend analysis with correlation and regression approaches, the results highlight the differential roles of temperature and rainfall related variables in shaping yield variability during the crop growing period. The findings emphasize that both gradual climatic shifts and short term weather variability are critical in determining sesame productivity in rainfed environments. The long-term temperature trend analysis revealed significant warming across Bundelkhand, which is more pronounced in late monsoon and post monsoon months (August-November). Across all districts both MK and SLR tests detected significant increase in temperature from August to November, with highest warming rates in November (> 1.5°/100 year). However, January and June showed declining temperature trends, though they are not consistent statistically. Similarly, warming trends of February and March were also not statistically significant. In April, however, the warming trend were significant in few districts, which shows the warming trends are not uniform throughout the year and the thermal stress period is seasonally distributed. Increasing trends of temperature in districts of Bundelkhand were also reported by several researchers44,45. Singh et al.5 reported similar findings for maximum and minimum temperatures in agro-climatic zones of India (upper gangetic plains and central plateau and hills), which overlaps with the Bundelkhand region. The seasonal rainfall trends in the region were more heterogeneous with no significant trends in winter and post monsoon season and very few significant increasing trends detected by MK/m-MK in pre-monsoon season. The monsoon season rainfall as well as annual rainfall in all districts showed declining trends in many districts, though in the lower part of the region the rainfall trends were not significant. Similar declining rainfall trends of seasonal and annual rainfall over Bundelkhand region have been reported using MK test, SS estimator and SLR tests4,46. The significant warming in the late kharif season (August –November) and declining rainfall trends in the peak monsoon season, is particularly relevant to thermo-sensitive and short duration oilseed sesame crop as this period coincides with critical phenophases including flower initiation, capsule development, and seed filling47.

District wise correlation analysis revealed consistent positive and significant correlations between sesame yield and temperature variables including Tmax_mean_Aug, Tmin_mean_Aug, Tavg_mean_Aug, Tmin_mean_Sep, Tavg_mean_Sep, Tavg_mean_Oct, Tmin_mean_Kharif, GDD_Aug, GDD_Sep and GDD_Oct. The positive correlations ranging between 0.28 and 0.47 indicate that the sesame yield variability in Bundelkhand region is moderately but systematically controlled by thermal conditions. The stepwise regression analysis further supported these associations by identifying several temperature variables like Tmax_mean_Jul, Tmin_mean_Jul, Tmin_mean_Aug, Tavg_mean_Aug, Tmax_mean_Sep, Tmin_mean_Sep and Tavg_mean_Oct as dominant predictors of yield in several districts of the region. Despite positive temperature-yield correlations, the regression results reveal that the warming beyond optimal thresholds can negatively impact yield, as evident by negative parameter estimates of frequently selected heat stress indices (HeatStressDays, SU_days and HWDI) in regression equations. This suggests that the temperature response in sesame is nonlinear, crop-stage and threshold-dependent. The positive associations between temperature variables and sesame yield are consistent with the crop’s tolerance to moderately high temperatures during the vegetative phase48,49. Sesame shows optimal yield near 25 °C, while reproductive development is more temperature-sensitive, with yield declines reported above 32–33 °C. This temperature-dependent response explains the beneficial impact of warming on yield, while also indicating potential risks under extreme heat stress events (T > 35 °C)50.

Unlike temperature, the rainfall variables including Rainfall_Aug, RainyDays_Aug, R10_days_Aug, R20_days_Aug, R10_days_Kharif, R20_days_Kharif, PRCPTOT_Aug, CWD_Aug and CWD_Kharif showed consistent negative correlations with sesame yield across different districts. Among the monthly and seasonal data, the rainfall variables of August month were found to have more negative impact on yield as compared to others. This indicates that the yield variability is driven by rainfall distribution and intensity (event characteristic) rather than the total amount of rainfall. The regression models also indicated the importance of rainfall intensity, as evident from selection of intensity linked rainfall variables (RainyDays, R10_days, R20_days, CDD, SDII and CWD) as significant negative predictors in districts with declining rainfall trends. Excess or poor rainfall distribution during the crop growing season can adversely impact the sesame yield by disrupting the crop physiology and soil moisture balance51. As the crop is sensitive to water logging and prolonged wet spells, particularly during reproductive phase, when excess moisture can restrict aeration and nutrient uptake by crop. High rainfall intensity (R10 and R20 days) can also increase surface runoff and soil erosion, limiting effective water availability and consequently reducing yield. The negative association of CWD and RainyDays with yield further suggests that extended wet spells may impose stress by increasing disease incidence, lodging risk, and floral abortion in sesame47,52.

Similar findings have been reported for other oilseed crops grown in semi-arid environments like Bundelkhand. For example, in rapeseed and mustard crops high temperatures during flowering and siliqua formation have been reported to reduce pollen viability and seed set resulting in significant yield loss53. In groundnut, heat stress during the reproductive stage adversely affects peg penetration, pod formation and kernel filling resulting in reduced yield54. In soybean also, high temperatures during the reproductive stage reduced flower retention and seed filling duration, thereby lowering yield in the crop55,56. Similarly, negative impacts of excess rainfall and poor drainage conditions on oilseed crops through root zone hypoxia, impaired nutrient uptake and increased disease incidence are well documented9,57. In groundnut, also prolonged wet spells have been associated with higher disease pressure and reduced biomass accumulation during reproductive stages58. These physiological responses across oilseed crops are consistent with findings of the present study.

Our results suggests that the sesame cultivation in Bundelkhand is currently a temperature favored and moisture constrained system. The moderate warming is favorable for higher yield but the declining rainfall with increasing extreme rainfall episodes makes the crop vulnerable to yield loss. Adaptation strategies should therefore focus on moisture conservation, improving drainage and adjustment of sowing dates to synchronize the crop phenology with changing climate regime. However, the findings should be interpreted considering certain limitations. The regression analysis was based on relatively limited district-wise annual observations (~ 55–60 years), and the use of stepwise regression may introduce some degree of variable-selection instability and overfitting. Therefore, the identified meteorological predictors should be interpreted primarily as exploratory associations useful for understanding climate and yield relationships, rather than definitive predictive relationships.

Conclusions

The present study provides an analysis of district wise long-term temperature and rainfall variations and a comprehensive evaluation of the impacts of weather variability on sesame yield in the semi-arid Bundelkhand region. The trend analysis revealed a consistent warming during the late monsoon and post-monsoon months across all the districts with rates exceeding 1.5 °C/100 years in some districts, while the monsoon as well as annual rainfall exhibited declining trends (-1.03 to -1.51 mm/year) in most of the districts. Spatial mapping highlighted district level variability, indicating localized climate issues risks. The analysis of crop weather relationships revealed that the temperature linked variables exhibited exclusively positive correlations (r = 0.28–0.48), whereas rainfall-related variables consistently showed negative correlations (r = -0.26 to -0.47). The moderate warming observed during months of August-September has generally benefited sesame yield in the region. In contrast, the rainfall linked variables, especially those describing rainfall intensity and wet spells, showed strong negative influences on sesame yield across districts. Regression analysis confirmed that the distribution of rainfall and event characteristics, rather than total amount of rainfall, are the primary drivers of sesame yield variability in this region. These findings indicate that sesame cultivation in Bundelkhand is temperature favored but moisture constrained, highlighting the need for targeted adaptation strategies. Practical measures such as adjustment of sowing dates to avoid peak rainfall periods, improvement of field drainage to reduce waterlogging and adoption of soil moisture conservation practices can help mitigate yield losses. The findings underscore the importance of integrating long-term climate trends, short-term weather variability and crop specific climate-yield modeling to support region specific adaptation strategies and informed management decisions, thereby enhancing the resilience of rainfed oilseed production under increasing climate variability.
