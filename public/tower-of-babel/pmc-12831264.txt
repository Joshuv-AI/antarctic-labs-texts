Comparative study of endocuff assisted colonoscopy and artificial intelligence in colonoscopy in adenoma and polyp detection: a randomized, back-to-back study
Authors: Patarapong Kamalaporn, Supphamat Chirnaksorn, Taya Kitiyakara, Sasivimol Rattanasiri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAn endocuff is attached at the tip of colonoscope to improve mucosal visualization. An artificial intelligence (AI) assisted colonoscopy is a computer system designed to enhance detection and differentiation polyps. The aim of this study was to evaluate the endocuff assisted colonoscopy (E) and the AI assisted colonoscopy (A) in the detection of adenomas and polyps, in a randomized, back-to-back study.MethodsThis study was done in a single tertiary center. Patients were randomly assigned to either the EA group, which underwent endocuff-assisted colonoscopy (E) followed by AI-assisted colonoscopy (A), or the AE group, which underwent AI-assisted colonoscopy followed by endocuff-assisted colonoscopy. All polyps were removed as appropriate during the colonoscopy in which they were detected.ResultsThree hundred sixty patients were randomly assigned to either the EA group or the AE group, with 180 patients in each group. The adenoma detection rates (ADR) of the first pass were 64.44% in the EA group and 65% in the AE group, p = 0.912. The median number of adenomas per patient was 2 and the most common size of an adenoma was 1–4 mm in both groups. In the second pass, the adenoma missed rates (ADR of the second pass) were 34.44% in the EA group and 42.78% in the AE group, p = 0.104. The median number of adenomas per patient was 1 and the most common size of an adenoma was 1–4 mm in both groups. There was no complication during the study.ConclusionsThere was no significant difference between endocuff assisted colonoscopy and AI assisted colonoscopy in the detection of adenomas. Both systems demonstrated high ADRs but were also associated with high adenoma missed rates. As a high ADRs in this study, the post hoc analysis indicated that the larger sample size may detect a significant difference of ADR between two systems. This study was registered with Thai Clinical Trial Registry; identification number TCTR 20,240,904,003. (https://www.thaiclinicaltrials.org/show/TCTR20240904003).

Introduction

Colonoscopy is an effective method for screening colonic polyps and colonic cancer. It is recognized as a safe procedure with serious complications occurring less than 0.2%, such as colonic perforation, bleeding and infection [1]. The aim of colonoscopy is to detect and remove adenomas, before they progress to cancer. Acceptable adenoma detection rates (ADR) are 30% in men and 20% in women. Colonic cancer detection rate is usually 3–5% in screening colonoscopy, depending on the population screened [2]. The adenoma missed rate for 1–9 millimeters (mm) colonic polyps is 13–27% with standard colonoscopy [3]. There are many methods to increase the ADR and decrease the adenoma missed rate, for example, using attached devices to improve mucosal visualization, light technology to improve mucosal visualization and microvasculature, and artificial intelligence assisted colonoscopy.

An endocuff is a cylindrical core device with a row of soft, flexible projections which can be attached to the tip of colonoscope to improve mucosal visualization. One meta-analysis and systematic review showed that endocuff assisted colonoscopy could increase the ADR from 34.2% to 41.3%, Relative Risk (RR) 1.2, compared with standard colonoscopy. It did not increase the detection rates of advanced adenoma and colon cancer. No complications were reported with the use of this device [4].

An artificial intelligence (AI) assisted colonoscopy is a computer system designed to help the endoscopist detect and differentiate polyps. The aims of the system are to increase the ADR and reduce the adenoma missed rate. One meta-analysis and systematic review showed that AI assisted colonoscopy increased the ADR from 19.3% to 29.6%, RR 1.52, compared with standard colonoscopy [5]. Another large meta-analysis and systematic review, which included 27,404 patients, reported that AI assisted colonoscopy increased the ADR (RR, 1.242; 95% CI, 1.159–1.332) and decreased the adenoma missed rate (RR, 0.495; 95% CI, 0.390–0.627) [6].

The aim of this study was to evaluate the endocuff assisted colonoscopy and the AI assisted colonoscopy in detection of adenomas and polyps, in a randomized, back to back study. By this method, this study can minimize the confounding effects of inter-patient variability.

Methods

Study design and patients

This was a single tertiary center, randomized controlled, prospective, comparative (back-to-back) study. The study was conducted at Ramathibodi hospital (government hospital), Mahidol university, Bangkok, Thailand. Between September 14, 2022 to September 10, 2024, 360 patients who came for screening, surveillance and diagnostic colonoscopy were recruited in this study. Patients with age < 40 years old, age > 80 years old, inflammatory bowel disease, colonic stricture, family history of polyposis syndrome and known case of large colonic lesions were excluded from this study. All patients were informed about risks and benefits of colonoscopy and study protocol. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of Ramathibodi Hospital. The study was conducted in compliance with the principles of Good Clinical Practice and Declaration of Helsinki. This study was registered with Thai Clinical Trial Registry; identification number TCTR 20,240,904,003 without intention to conceal the study protocol and result of the study. All recruited subjects provided written informed consent prior to any study-related procedures. All patient characteristics were recorded. The authors declare that they have no conflicts of interest regarding this study. The datasets generated and/or analyzed during the current study are not publicly available due to hospital regulation and ethics committee regulation but are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Participants were randomized in a cross-over design in blocks of four (sealed envelope) to undergo back-to-back colonoscopies, either with an endocuff assisted colonoscopy first followed by artificial intelligence assisted colonoscopy (EA group), or with an artificial intelligence assisted colonoscopy first followed by endocuff assisted colonoscopy (AE group).

Endoscopic procedures

All participants used split doses of Polyethylene glycol solution for bowel preparation. Colonoscopies were done under light-moderate sedation, with intravenous midazolam, either in combination with intravenous meperidine or intravenous fentanyl. Four experienced endoscopists performed the colonoscopies with each having performed more than 1,000 standard colonoscopies over 10 years. Each participant underwent back-to-back colonoscopies, performed by two different endoscopists, under standard guideline. All colonoscopies were done using an Olympus CF-EZ1500DL/I video colonoscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). An endocuff vision (Arc Medical Design Ltd., Leeds, United Kingdom) was applied to tip of colonoscope during the endocuff assisted colonoscopy. For artificial intelligence assisted colonoscopy, the AI system (the EndoBRAIN-EYE; Cybernet. Systems, Tokyo, Japan) was used to help the endoscopist for polyp detection during the procedure. The polyps were removed during insertion or withdrawal and recorded. The withdrawal time in this study was recorded. The time used for polyp removal was not included in the insertion time or the withdrawal time. The second endoscopist was blinded to the findings of the first procedure. This was ensured by prohibiting verbal communication about findings among the first endoscopist, the second endoscopist and room staff, and the second endoscopist did not have access to the procedure report or patient chart from the first pass. If a polyp was found during the second pass colonoscopy, the polyp was removed and recorded as a missed lesion (from the first pass colonoscopy) and used to calculate the missed rate of adenomas and polyps. The types of polyps were classified by histopathologists. The quality of bowel preparation was graded by the Boston Bowel Preparation Scale. Carbon dioxide was used during all colonoscopies to reduce discomfort of patient. Vital signs and symptoms of patients were monitored pre, during and 2-hours post procedure. The cecal intubation rate, size of polyp, location of polyp, advanced adenoma, colonic cancer, complication and false positive (false alarm) of AI system were also recorded.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were the adenoma detection rates (rate of detection at least one adenoma in each patient) and the adenoma missed rates (ADR of the second pass) for the endocuff assisted colonoscopy and AI assisted colonoscopy. The secondary outcomes were the polyp detection rate, the polyp missed rate, the insertion time and complications for the endocuff assisted colonoscopy and AI assisted colonoscopy.

Statistical analysis and sample size

For continuous data, the mean and standard deviation or median and range were used to describe the data, while frequency and percentage were used for categorical data. Patients’ characteristics were compared between two groups of interventions for categorical data using the chi-square test (or Fisher’s exact test) and for continuous data using the student t test (or quantile regression). In order to compare the detection and missed rates across the two intervention groups (AE and EA) in first pass and second pass colonoscopy, mixed-effects Poisson regression with random-intercept and fixed-slope was applied to estimate the risk ratio of detection and miss rates between two procedures. All analyses were performed using STATA version 18.0. (Stata Corp., College Station, TX, USA).

The sample size was calculated for two independent proportions, comparative groups (1:1) between EA and AE groups. Form previous studies, an endocuff assisted colonoscopy increased an ADR to 41.3% [4] and AI assisted colonoscopy increased an ADR to 29.6% [5]. An endocuff assisted colonoscopy may increase an ADR by 1.5 times when compare with AI assisted colonoscopy. Based on type I error 0.05 and type II error 0.2, the calculated sample size was at least 171 patients for each group [7].

This study was blinded to participants, but this study was not blinded to endoscopists, who recorded the data. The data was blinded to the data analyst.

Results

Study population

From September 14, 2022 to September 10, 2024, a total of 8540 patients came to our endoscopy unit for colonoscopy. After risks and benefits of participation in this study were discussed, 360 patients met the inclusion criteria and consented to participate the study. Then they were randomized into EA group, 180 patients and AE group, 180 patients. All patients, completed the colonoscopy, as a consort diagram [8] (Fig. 1). The patient characteristics are shown in Table 1. The age, sex, underlying disease, medications of patient in each group were similar, except dyslipidemia was higher in AE group (98 patients vs. 120 patients, p = 0.018) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) patients were higher in EA group (10 patients vs. 2 patients, p = 0.019). Colon screening was main indication of colonoscopy in both groups (60.56% vs. 55%). (Table 1).

Patient characteristics

Quality of colonoscopy

The quality of bowel preparation in the two groups during the first pass and the second pass were similar (1st pass; mean of Boston bowel scale 7.8 vs. 7.78; p 0.674 and 2nd pass; mean of Boston bowel scale 7.9 vs. 7.82; p 0.18). There was 100% cecal intubation in both groups without any complication. The first pass cecal intubation time was shorter in EA group with median 222 (81, 1030) seconds compared with median 248 (73, 1517) seconds in AE group. P = 0.05. The second pass cecal intubation times were similar in both groups, EA group: median 186.5 (51,874) seconds vs. AE group: 192.5 (60, 660) seconds, p = 0.633. The withdrawal time, for both the first pass and the second pass colonoscopy, was more than 360 s/ 6 min. It was slightly longer in the EA group compared with the AE group during the second pass (417.5 s vs. 398.8 s, p = 0.014) (Table 2). There was no complication in this study.

Adenoma detection rates and adenoma missed rates

In the EA group, the first pass colonoscopy detected adenomas in 116 patients from 180 patients (ADR 64.4%) and in the AE group, the first pass colonoscopy detected adenomas in 117 patients from 180 patients (ADR 65%). The ADRs were similar between the two groups, p value = 0.948. In the first pass colonoscopy, the median number of adenomas per patient was 2 and the commonest size of an adenoma was 1–4 mm in both groups. Adenomas were commonly detected in the sigmoid and descending colon. EA group had higher adenoma detection in transverse colon with statistical significance (35 vs. 18, p = 0.007) (Table 3).

Findings from first pass of colonoscopy: adenoma by size and location

For the second pass colonoscopy, adenomas were detected in 62 patients from 180 patients in the EA group (adenoma missed rate 34.4%) and 77 patients from 180 patients in the AE group (adenoma missed rate 42.7%). The adenoma missed rates were not statistically different between the two groups, p value = 0.203. The median number of adenomas per patient was 1 in both groups. The median size of polyps was similar in both groups, but there were slightly but significantly more polyps in the 5–8 mm size in AE group compared to the EA group, with median numbers of polyps in this size of (0 (0,1) vs. 0 (0,2), p value 0.042). The common sites for missed adenomas in EA group were the transverse colon (35.48%), descending colon (25.81%) and the sigmoid (20.97%), respectively. For the AE group, the common sites for missed adenomas were the sigmoid (37.66%), descending colon (24.68%) and ascending colon (24.68%), respectively. AE group had higher adenoma detection in ascending colon (7 vs. 19, p = 0.044) and sigmoid (13 vs. 29, p = 0.033). (Table 4)

Hyperplastic polyp detection

In EA group, the first pass colonoscopy detected hyperplastic polyps in 34.44% (62/180) and the second pass colonoscopy showed a missed rate of 19.44% (35/180). In AE group, the first pass colonoscopy detected hyperplastic polyps in 27.78% (50/180) and the second pass of colonoscopy showed a missed rate of 15% (27/180).

For the first pass colonoscopy, the median number of hyperplastic polyps per patient was 2 in EA group and 1 in AE group. The commonest size of hyperplastic polyps was 1–4 mm in both groups. In the second pass colonoscopy, the median number of hyperplastic polyps per patient was 1 and the commonest size of hyperplastic polyps was 1–4 mm in both groups.

Advanced adenomas and colonic cancer

In this study, one advanced adenoma (a small focus of adenocarcinoma in a large polyp) in the sigmoid, one circumferential adenocarcinoma in the sigmoid and one circumferential adenocarcinoma at the hepatic flexure were detected. Complete colonoscopy could be performed in all of these cases.

False positive of AI assisted colonoscopy

The total number of false positives (false alarm) from AI assisted colonoscopy was 451 from both groups. The median number of false alarms was 1 (0,6). Common causes of false positive were colonic folds 80.04% (361/451), blood clot from previous polypectomies 18.18% (82/451) and fecal content 1.7% (8/451).

Discussion

This was a comparative, randomized, cross-over study between device assisted colonoscopy (endocuff) and AI assisted colonoscopy, and included 360 participants. For patient characteristics, dyslipidemia was higher in AE group and chronic kidney disease (CKD) patients were higher in EA group with statistically significance. This distribution happened by chance but these factors might not have strong relation to the development of an adenoma. However, it may be one of limitation in this study. The study was designed as a back-to-back randomized study because we preferred to compare both methods in the same patient rather than comparing them with the standard colonoscopy. This methodology has a significant strength to minimize the confounding effects of inter-patient variability in anatomy, bowel preparation, and underlying polyp. As all detected polyps are removed in the first pass and calculate as the ADR, the second pass (back-to-back colonoscopy) also allow us to remove the detected polyps and calculate as the adenoma missed rate. The ADRs from the first pass of colonoscopy were similar between EA group and AE group, 64.44% vs. 65%, p = 0.912 and the adenoma missed rates from the second pass of colonoscopy were slightly different without being statistically significant, 34.44% vs. 42.78%, p = 0.104. For the risk ratios (95%CI) of detection rates between the EA and AE groups were 1.01 (0.75, 1.27) for the first pass and 0.81 (0.54, 1.07) for the second pass. It implied that the AE group had a lower adenoma detection rate in the second pass than the EA group without statistically difference.

The median number and commonest size of adenoma were quite similar, 2 adenomas, and size 1–4 mm for the first pass, and 1 adenoma, and size 1–4 mm in the second pass of colonoscopy.

The possible explanation of the high ADR in our study, 2 times higher than the standard guideline [2], could have been from a combination of reasons. The patients in our study included patients with other indications for colonoscopy other than cancer screening. Certain indications such as rectal bleeding, anemia and follow-up for previous polyps are known to have higher rates of polyp detection. In addition, each experienced endoscopist may have been meticulous in their mucosal inspection, knowing that a second endoscopist would be doing a colonoscopy to check for missed polyps. Even tiny polyps were removed and included in this study. Lastly, the addition of the endocuff and AI assistance with at least 6-minute withdrawal time [9] may have increased the detection of polyps compared to standard colonoscopy. Nevertheless, despite the high ADR in the first pass of colonoscopy, the missed polyp rate was still high, at around 35% and 43%. Fortunately, the missed adenomas were in the majority small, 1–4 mm. As the observed ADRs were substantially higher than those assumed in the original calculation, a post-hoc recalculation indicated that approximately 1,375 participants per group would be required to detect a 5% difference between Endocuff assisted and AI assisted colonoscopy. Therefore, the present study may have been underpowered to detect small differences in ADR between the two interventions.

The common locations for the detection of adenomas in the first pass were similar in both groups, in the sigmoid, descending colon, ascending colon and transverse colon respectively. However, in the first pass EA group had higher adenoma detection in transverse colon (35 vs. 18, p = 0.007). In the second pass, AE group had higher adenoma detection in ascending colon (7 vs. 19, p = 0.044) and sigmoid (13 vs. 29, p = 0.033). It is unclear if these findings were related to the performance of the equipment, or a chance finding as although there were slightly higher missed adenomas in the AE group, this reached statistical significance. From the large randomized, multicenter trial in the United Kingdom, where 1,772 patients underwent colonoscopy with or without endocuff, Endocuff assisted colonoscopy significantly increased ADR from 36.2% to 40.9% (P = 0.02). The detection of adenoma increased significantly in left colon, 22.2% to 26.1% (P = 0.03), but not for the right colon, 24.8% to 27.5% (P = 0.1) [10]. So, with the current data, there does not seem to be a specific location where endo-cuff consistently increases polyp detection.

For hyperplastic polyps, this study showed that the detection rates were 34.44% and 27.78%, p = 0.172 and hyperplastic polyp missed rate were 19.44% and 15%, p = 0.329. in EA group and AE group, respectively.

From the previous studies, a systematic review and meta-analysis showed that the endocuff assisted colonoscopy increased the ADR by RR 1.2 when compared with a standard colonoscopy [4]. In the previous study of EndoBrain-Eye, AI assisted colonoscopy identified colonic lesions from 100 patients with sensitivity 96.9% (95% CI, 95.8%–97.8%), specificity 100% (95% CI, 99.6%–100%) and accuracy 98% (95% CI, 97.3%–98.6%) [11]. Systematic review and meta-analysis studies showed that AI assisted colonoscopy increased the ADR by RR 1.24–1.52.5,6 In a randomized 3-arm trial, comparing endocuff with AI assisted colonoscopy, AI assisted colonoscopy, and standard colonoscopy in 682 patients, the ADRs were 58.7%, 53.8% and 46.3%, respectively. The combination of endocuff with AI assisted colonoscopy improved ADR significantly when compared with the other two arms, ADR difference 4.9%, p = 0.03 and 12.4%, p < 0.01, respectively [12]. In the study both the endocuff and AI improved the ADR, either as a single device or a combination of both devices.

This present study showed that the endocuff and AI were similar in ADR, but the adenoma missed rate was slightly higher in AI assisted colonoscopy, without reaching statistically significance. The endocuff improves mucosal visualization and slows down the withdrawal maneuver. The attached endocuff increases the diameter of the scope tip, so it can potentially cause some difficulties in scope intubation especially in patient with difficult colon. In this study, the endocuff did not prolong the cecal intubation, and in fact, the first pass cecal intubation time in EA group was shorter than the first pass cecal intubation time in AE group. P = 0.05. The second pass cecal intubation times were not statistically different. The benefit of an endocuff was inconsistency. In some circumstances, the flexible projections of an endocuff may flatten colonic folds which make the scope could reach the cecum faster. From the meta analysis [4], the endocuff did not impact the cecal intubation rate and the procedure time.

AI is thought to help in distinguishing small polyps from the surrounding mucosa. However, with the current technology there is still a significant number of false alerts (median number was 1 (0,6) and common cause was colonic fold 80.04%), and in certain conditions such as poor bowel preparation, redundant colon and having blood clot, the accuracy of AI to differentiate the polyp from the other findings can be limited. With this finding, AI may increase the procedure time, the unnecessary biopsies and distracted endoscopist’s attention.

The limitations of this study were that it was a single center study and colonoscopy was performed by experienced endoscopists, so the results may not apply to all populations, patients with complex lesions and less experienced endoscopists. The larger sample size may detect a small difference of ADR between two systems which did not show in this study. By using of single AI system (EndoBRAIN-EYE), the result may not reflect performance of other systems. Newer versions of AI may improve the detection rate and decrease the false positive alarms. It may also be able to differentiate the type of polyp seen accurately.

Endocuff assisted colonoscopy and artificial intelligence in colonoscopy had high adenoma detection rate and acceptable adenoma missed rates, which was mainly for small adenomas. Both systems could be an option for increasing the quality of colonoscopy.
