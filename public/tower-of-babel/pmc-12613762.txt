Analysis of the economic benefits and feasibility of the same-city delivery model for donor human milk (SCDM-DHM)
Authors: Dingding Yin, Yang Yi, Kangmei Huang, Yijun Chen, Huali Zheng, Ping Zhou, Lian Zhang, Zhangbin Yu, Quanfu Zhang, Junhong Cai, Ruirui Xing, Kanghua Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhen mother’s own milk (MOM) is insufficient, feeding very preterm infants with donor human milk (DHM) reduces the incidence of multiple severe complications and provides greater benefits than infant formula. Background

For very preterm infants (VPIs), donor human milk (DHM) from human milk banks serves as the second-best feeding option after mother’s own milk (MOM), demonstrating superior benefits over infant formula [1–3]. The benefits of breastfeeding are well established in the medical literature [4]. However, VPIs face significant challenges in achieving early and sustained access to MOM [5, 6]. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), the European Society for Paediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition (ESPGHAN), and the World Health Organization (WHO) unanimously endorse DHM as the optimal nutritional alternative for VPIs when MOM is unavailable [7]. DHM feeding for VPIs significantly improved both short-term and long-term health outcomes, including reduced mortality rates and other outcomes. DHM feeding effectively prevents major neonatal complications, such as necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), late-onset sepsis (LOS), and bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD). Furthermore, longitudinal studies have demonstrated superior neurodevelopmental outcomes and improved cardiovascular health in these infants during later life stages [1, 8–12].

Human milk banks are responsible for the collection, processing, and distribution of DHM and implement rigorous protocols to ensure both quality and safety [7]. Global estimates indicate that approximately 800 human milk banks will operate worldwide by 2025; only 33 facilities are maintained in China, with only 2 in Shenzhen. However, Shenzhen currently has more than 30 neonatal intensive care units (NICUs), but each human milk bank can only serve a single NICU [13]. Expanding access to DHM for VPIs across all NICUs in Shenzhen requires solutions beyond the establishment of 34 individual human milk banks, which are an impractical approach given resource constraints. The establishment and operation of a human milk bank requires an initial investment of several hundred thousand dollars or more, but all such facilities operate as nonprofit organizations [14–16]. Current reports have described centralized or regionalized systems approaches to DHM provision in many countries (e.g., Europe, North America, and Vietnam). Such systems include milk bank satellite centers [17, 18], the Donor Human Milk Bank–Blood Bank model [19–21], and informal human milk-sharing networks [22]. However, these systems have limited comparative literature assessing their cost-effectiveness or real-world feasibility following deployment.

To address the supply and demand imbalance of DHM in Shenzhen, we implemented a same-city delivery model for donor human milk (SCDM-DHM) that enables the cold-chain transportation of DHM from a centralized human milk bank to NICUs without onsite milk bank facilities. The SCDM-DHM was developed through iterative deliberations by a multidisciplinary expert panel that consisted of neonatologists and lactation specialists. The SCDM-DHM adheres to international safety and quality standards while specifically addressing environmental barriers. In this case study, we describe the implementation of this SCDM-DHM designed to ensure safe, efficient, and sustainable DHM provision that meets regional patient needs. Concurrently, we analyze [1] the cost-effectiveness of SCDM-DHM compared to traditional milk banking and [2] the safety and efficacy of DHM utilization in recipient NICUs.

Methods

Study design

We retrospectively analyzed the medical records of very preterm infants (VPIs) admitted to Shenzhen People’s Hospital between June 2023 and May 2025. The aims of this study were to evaluate both the cost-effectiveness of the SCDM-DHM and its impact on clinical outcomes in VPIs. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) gestational age < 32 weeks and (2) parental consent for SCDM-DHM participation. Eligible VPIs were those cleared for enteral feeding by attending neonatologists. The exclusion criteria were as follows: VPIs who could not receive food because of critical illness, mortality, or hospital transfer. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committees of Shenzhen People’s Hospital (IEC No. LLSC-2023-04-01-06-KS) and Shenzhen Bao’an Maternal and Child Health Hospital (IEC No. LL-KY-2024100-01).

Group assignment

The SCDM-DHM was implemented in July 2024, with VPIs born in June 2024 becoming the first cohort to receive donor human milk (DHM) through this program. Study participants were retrospectively allocated into two cohorts based on one-year periods before and after SCDM-DHM implementation: the control group (VPIs admitted June 2023–June 2024) received either mother’s own milk or formula feeding, whereas the SCDM-DHM group (VPIs admitted June 2024–May 2025) received mother’s own milk, donor human milk (MOM + DHM), or formula feeding.

Donor human milk delivery model implementation protocol

Established in 2015, the human milk bank at Shenzhen Bao’an Maternal and Child Health Hospital is distinguished as Shenzhen’s first and China’s ninth such facility. Operating under the principle of free donation and nonprofit use, the bank has collected more than 10,000 L of donor human milk (DHM) from nearly 2,000 altruistic mothers during its 8-year operation, benefiting approximately 7,000 neonates. The human milk bank (HMB) is equipped with dedicated facilities that include a milk expression room and preparation room, hospital-grade breast pumps, pasteurization equipment (temperature-controlled water bath), milk storage containers, medical refrigerators (standard and ultralow temperature models), a Miris human milk analyzer (HMA), and computers with integrated information management systems. All the operational procedures, from organizational guidelines and infrastructure management to donor screening, and the collection, processing, storage, and distribution of donor human milk strictly adhere to standardized protocols. The NICU at Shenzhen People’s Hospital maintains 30 beds, admitting approximately 100 very preterm infants annually; it is a level 3 NICU. Shenzhen People’s Hospital is equipped with a separate milk dispensing room, medical refrigerators and breast milk heating devices.

The SCDM-DHM represents a same-city distribution system for processed donor human milk (DHM), in which the human milk bank at Shenzhen Bao’an Maternal and Child Health Hospital coordinates the cold-chain delivery of pasteurized DHM to the NICU at Shenzhen People’s Hospital.

The operational process at the Bao’an human milk bank begins with comprehensive donor screening, including voluntary blood testing of lactating mothers to ensure donor eligibility. Qualified donor milk is then collected, properly stored, and subjected to standardized processing protocols featuring holder pasteurization (62.5 °C for 30 min) and nutritional composition analysis before distribution.

The recipient hospital, Shenzhen People’s Hospital, assigns neonatologists in the NICU to evaluate very preterm infants (VPIs) and obtain informed consent from families of VPIs needing DHM for participation in the SCDM-DHM program. The NICU team regularly communicates DHM demand forecasts to the human milk bank. Upon preparation of the requested DHM, the milk bank coordinates with designated transporters, documenting both the dispatch time and temperature conditions of the transport containers throughout delivery. The DHM is transported to the recipient hospital NICU via dedicated vehicles equipped with temperature-controlled containers. During the approximately one-hour delivery process, trained transporters monitor and document the container temperature in real time. Upon arrival at the recipient NICU, the transporter and NICU nurse jointly verify the DHM quantity, check the temperature records, and inspect the product integrity before the nurses store the DHM according to standardized protocols.

The transport containers comply fully with China’s National Food Safety Standards regarding exterior design, interior lining, construction materials, and thermal insulation performance. Powered by rechargeable batteries, these temperature-controlled units maintain a strict − 20 °C to 0 °C range throughout transit. The integrated cold-chain system enables real-time automated temperature regulation, digital display, and continuous data logging. Specially trained transporters certified through competency assessments prior to assignment execute weekly scheduled deliveries, with additional ad hoc transports arranged as clinically needed.

Upon arrival at the receiving hospital, the NICU physicians prescribe DHM for eligible VPIs on the basis of clinical assessment. The NICU nurse then carefully warms and aliquots the DHM according to medical orders, administering it to VPIs as prescribed. All procedures, including feeding volumes and infant responses, are meticulously documented in the nursing records (Fig. 1).

SCDM-DHM process

We have established an intelligent information system utilizing modern technologies, such as the Internet of Things, big data analytics, and cloud computing, to develop a comprehensive delivery management platform. This system enables real-time data uploads and tracking of all critical parameters: donor milk batches, milk bank operations, recipient NICUs, distribution routes, vehicle status, handover processes, and staff credentials. The platform facilitates seamless communication between the human milk bank and recipient hospital NICUs, ensuring the efficient operation of the SCDM-DHM.

Data collection

During the study, we collected comprehensive demographic and clinical characteristics of VPIs, including sex, gestational age, birth weight, maternal age, singleton or multiple birth status, delivery mode, and small for gestational age (SGA) status. The primary outcome measures were defined as follows: (1) breastfeeding rate (exclusive/mixed) and (2) necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) incidence (Bell’s stage ≥ II). We compared these outcomes in recipient hospitals before and after the implementation of donor human milk (DHM) provision. Breastfeeding included MOM feeding, DHM feeding, or mixed breastfeeding. MOM feeding was defined as exclusive feeding with the MOM during hospitalization, DHM feeding was defined as exclusively feeding with DHM during hospitalization, and mixed breastfeeding refers to receiving both MOM and DHM during hospitalization. During NICU hospitalization, VPIs received enteral nutrition through direct breastfeeding, nasogastric tube feeding, or syringe feeding, with the method tailored to individual digestive tolerance. All VPIs were maintained on parenteral nutritional support until full enteral feeding was achieved. The department’s discharge criteria remained consistent throughout the study period.

A VPI was defined as an infant whose gestational age was less than 32 weeks. SGA was defined as < P10 (10th percentile) of the average birth weight of infants of identical gestational age and sex [23]. NEC was defined as having both clinical and radiological criteria, based on the modified Bell’s criteria ≥ 2 [24].

We also gathered data on the economic expenditures of the human milk bank that provided DHM and the NICU of the recipient hospital that received DHM under the SCDM-DHM (¥1 = US$0.14). We recorded the costs incurred by the human milk bank of Shenzhen Bao’an District Maternal and Child Health Hospital for collecting DHM, the equipment costs for maintaining DHM operations, the sterilization costs, and the manpower cost costs for operating the human milk bank. The equipment backup costs and labor costs incurred by the NICU of Shenzhen People’s Hospital when DHM is used, as well as the costs of delivering DHM, were also collected. The cost allocation for the equipment was calculated using the straight-line depreciation method, with an assumed useful life of 10 years.

Statistical analysis methods

All the statistical analyses were performed using Stata 18.0 software. Continuous variables are expressed as the mean with standard deviation or median with interquartile range as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages.

For comparisons of continuous variables between groups, Student’s t test was used for normally distributed data, whereas the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied for nonnormally distributed data. The chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was employed for categorical variable comparisons as appropriate.

A two-sided p value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance for all tests. Covariate balance between the comparison groups was assessed by standardized mean differences (SMDs), with SMDs ≥ 0.2 indicating a significant difference between groups and SMDs of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 indicating small, medium, and large differences, respectively.

Clinical characteristics of the population

In this study, 116 VPIs with a mean birth weight of 1,181.7 g and a mean gestational age of 29.0 weeks were included. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were well balanced between the SCDM-DHM group and the control group (p > 0.05), with no statistically significant differences observed in terms of maternal age, birth weight, gestational age, number of males, delivery mode, SGA status, or length of hospital stay; a decreasing trend in the number of single births was observed in the SCDM-DHM group (p = 0.006). Discussion

This study demonstrates how the SCDM-DHM can overcome common challenges and barriers in human milk bank implementation. It establishes the feasibility and effectiveness of this innovative operational approach within the context of Shenzhen, China, while simultaneously offering promising opportunities for adaptation and scalability in other settings. Launched in 2024, the SCDM-DHM program addressed the critical gap in donor human milk access at the Shenzhen People’s Hospital NICU through an innovative distribution model.

The economic benefits of the SCDM-DHM

This study revealed that the average production cost of DHM at this milk bank over 8 years of operation was $44.94 per liter, which aligns closely with reported costs from other established milk banking systems globally [15, 16, 25–29]. After implementing the SCDM-DHM, the total expenditure for DHM in recipient hospitals was only $279.58, which was significantly lower than the cost of operating a standalone milk bank. The current cost per liter of DHM ($25.34) is much lower than that of centralized milk banks ($44.94). Following the introduction of the DHM program, recipient hospitals demonstrated significantly lower operational costs than standalone human milk banks did.

The primary expenditure for establishing a milk bank lies in fixed assets, particularly equipment procurement costs [17], whereas secondary costs include DHM pasteurization, quality testing, and staffing expenses [15]. The SCDM-DHM demonstrates superior cost efficiency by centralizing the most substantial expenses associated with DHM utilization equipment procurement, pasteurization, and quality testing within the milk bank infrastructure. This approach eliminates the need for recipient hospitals to invest heavily in specialized devices or processing facilities, requiring only minimal expenditures for cold-chain transport and storage. The streamlined workflow of the SCDM-DHM requires recipient hospital staff to master only DHM storage and aliquot administration skills fully compatible with existing MOM handling protocols. This integration yields dual advantages in clinical practice and cost containment.

Analysis of the feasibility of the SCDM-DHM

The State Council of the People’s Republic of China approved a development plan for cold-chain logistics spanning the 14th Five-Year Plan period (2021–2025), according to a circular issued by the General Office of the State Council on December 12 [30]. This directive urged the establishment of a green and smart cold-chain logistics system with safety and efficiency while increasing the quality of logistics services based on China’s national conditions, including the production, circulation and consumption of cold-chain products. Infrastructure involves smart sorting equipment, logistics robots and temperature monitors, while cold-chain technologies and equipment cover big data, 5G, artificial intelligence and other smart technologies. By 2024, smooth cold-chain logistics could ensure the safety of food and pharmaceutical products in transportation. Our model is precisely built upon this cold-chain logistics.

By establishing only one centralized human milk bank, the SCDM-DHM can successfully address the critical need for DHM across multiple NICUs lacking their own milk banking facilities. Shenzhen has established a fully operational cold-chain distribution network capable of covering the entire municipality and extending to neighboring cities. Recipient hospitals require only minimal infrastructure, specifically, storage equipment for frozen DHM and trained personnel for thawing and administration, to participate in this system. This innovative approach demonstrates how strategic resource allocation and existing logistics capabilities can dramatically expand access to lifesaving DHM without the prohibitive costs of building multiple human milk banks. The efficiency of the model stems from centralized processing (including standardized pasteurization and quality testing) coupled with decentralized utilization, creating a sustainable framework for regional neonatal nutritional support.Shenzhen has distinctive resources and infrastructure, but these conditions may not be representative of less-developed regions in China.

All NICUs in Shenzhen possess the necessary infrastructure and trained personnel for administering MOM, with existing staff equally capable of handling DHM feeding without the need for additional hires. This operational efficiency enables seamless scaling of the SCDM-DHM across Shenzhen and neighboring municipalities. Several countries, including Spain and Vietnam [17, 18], have successfully implemented milk bank satellite centers; currently, detailed literature outlining the specific implementation processes for satellite human milk banks is lacking. Existing studies indicate that satellite hospitals must first collect donor milk, transport it to a central milk bank for pasteurization, and then redistribute it back to satellite centers. This multistep transportation process introduces significant risks of temperature control failures, which may compromise the quality and safety of donor milk. Given these logistical challenges, the satellite model is not suitable for implementation in Shenzhen. Several nations, such as Switzerland, Australia, Canada, the United States, Spain and Germany, have reported collaborative blood bank and human milk bank (HMB) operational models [19–21]. The successful operation of the integrated Donor Human Milk Bank-Blood Bank model requires close coordination between two distinct medical units—human milk banks (typically under maternal–child health departments) and blood centers (usually managed by transfusion services). This cross-departmental collaboration presents significant challenges that currently limit its feasibility in the context of Shenzhen. Some studies have also reported informal human milk-sharing networks, which operate outside of medical or profit-driven institutions [22]. These models lack regulatory oversight, making it impossible to ensure the quality and safety of donor milk. Additionally, they often incur high operational costs, rendering them unsuitable for widespread adoption [31].

Clinical effectiveness of the SCDM-DHM

Comparisons before and after the recipient hospital used donated breast milk revealed that the breastfeeding rate increased, which was consistent with the results reported in previous studies. However, the incidence of severe NEC did not decrease. Because the sample size was relatively small (n = 116), the study included very few NEC cases, which limits the statistical power to detect differences. Multiple studies have shown that compared with formula, DHM can reduce the incidence of diseases, such as NEC and LOS, as well as future medical expenses [29, 32]. A meta-analysis revealed that DHM is more effective at reducing the incidence of NEC and length of hospital stay [33, 34]. A study by Johnson et al. also revealed that compared with infants receiving MOM + Formula, infants receiving MOM + DHM had a lower incidence of NEC [14].

Limitations

The focus of our research is on introducing the SCDM-DHM process. In evaluating the economic benefits of the SCDM-DHM, a dedicated and professional economic benefit model was not adopted, and the economic benefits of the entire NICU were not included for comparison. We did not adjust for confounders because our research was only a single-center study with a small sample size. Due to the absence of adjustment for potential confounders, the observed differences in outcomes cannot be attributed solely to the intervention. However, the SCDM-DHM operated for a short period and involved one NICU. We plan to continue operating this model, expand the number of NICUs with benefits, increase the sample size, and conduct further research in the future. Another shortcoming of our study is that the feeding regimens of VPIs were not further grouped for comparison. This drawback should also be addressed by expanding the sample size in the future.

Conclusions

The SCDM-DHM is an adaptation of existing international approaches to the unique infrastructure and policy environment in China. This model provides mutual assistance between the breast milk bank and NICUs. This study confirmed the clinical effectiveness and feasibility of the SCDM-DHM and confirmed that this model can reduce costs. This study indicates that this model can improve exclusive breastfeeding, but more research is needed to confirm whether the SCDM-DHM can be promoted among NICUs in Shenzhen and other regions as well.
