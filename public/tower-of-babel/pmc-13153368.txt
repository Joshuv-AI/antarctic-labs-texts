Tribolium flour beetles are strongly attracted to decomposing wood, their putative historical habitat, over flour
Authors: Derek W. Rosenberger, Hannah E. Chung, Samuel D. Elsen, Charles W. Leys, Isabelle J. Pastoor, James S. Petno, Lela R. Plankeel, Alison R. Gerken
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tribolium castaneum and T. confusum are important stored grain pests and model organisms, yet little is known about the life history of these beetles outside the stored grain environment. While the historical habitat is thought to be rotting logs, current affinity and use of such habitat is unknown despite its potential role in mediating dynamics between natural landscape and stored product facilities. Here we assessed attraction to three different species of decomposing wood, sex-mediated differences in attraction, and survival and reproduction on wood versus flour substrates. We found that both Tribolium species demonstrated significantly higher attraction to decomposing wood than to wheat flour (2.5–7.25x), and reproduction studies with T. castaneum showed reduced survivorship and no reproduction on a decomposing wood substrate. Evidently, attraction to decomposing wood is conserved across these two prominent stored product pests, yet wood alone may not fulfill dietary or reproductive requirements. Wheat flour also elicits a significant olfactory response but also provides high reproductive capacity, which may contribute to Tribiolium’s utilization of this novel anthropogenic resource. This study contributes to an important yet understudied area of inquiry into the role natural microhabitats play in supporting these economically important species and the origin of stored grain associations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45364-w.

Introduction

Many insects can eat a variety of diets, depending on their nutritional needs at any moment, a dietary self-selecting or optimal foraging strategy1. This continuous switching provides insects with proper nutrition and oviposition sites throughout their life span, while allowing them to adapt to new diets when faced with environmental changes2. Many factors impact the selection of food sources, including, but not limited to, chemical cues associated with conspecifics, quality of the diet or refugia, mating status, and protection from predators3–5. In addition, human influences can drive many insects to different environments, prompting new preferences for diet and other resources6.

Tribolium castaneum (Herbst) and T. confusum Jacquelin du Val (collectively hereafter Tribolium) (Coleoptera: Tenebrionidae) are important stored product pests, causing significant damage to various types of flour and regularly infesting grain mills7,8. Remains of these beetles found in ancient Egyptian tombs suggests an association with human food since the inception of grain storage some 4500 + years ago9. Since then, these insect pests have become cosmopolitan, infesting grain storage and processing facilities around the world8,10.

Although Tribolium are ubiquitous to locations with grain, they do show varying attraction to different types of flour, suggesting that some olfactory components can drive their infestation potential and lead to a differentiation in risk level for various commodities11,12. Tribolium castaneum and T. confusum also have varying levels of attraction to damaged grain, with T. confusum often showing a higher attraction to split wheat kernels than T. castaneum13. In addition, studies of kairomone-based lures for monitoring and trapping beetles have shown varying responses between the two species and across different strains of T. castaneum14,15 suggesting differences in preference for grain-based chemical cues, and potentially alternative attractants for these insect pests. However, due to a paucity of direct comparison studies, little more is known about relative attraction between species.

While much work has been done on Tribolium to better control populations and limit reinfestations, little is known about how these insects disperse or survive outside of stored products. Ningombam et al. 16 recently found T. confusum in tunnels within fungal infested logs of tree bean trees (Parkia roxburghii) in India. Good7 reported that other Tribolium species (e.g. T. madens Charp and T. indicum Blair17,18) live under bark or in rotten logs, suggesting they may be saproxylic, but also asserts that the adaptations to stored products are only recent and all Tribolium species likely originally lived in decaying woody vegetation as T. castaneum and T. confusum are also occasionally found in these environments. In fact, the Tenebrionidae family is likely underrepresented in the literature on saproxylic species19, despite including many scavengers20 and the ability of many beetles in this family to digest cellulose and hemicellulose from plant material21,22. In Tribolium, a generalist saproxylic lifestyle is supported by studies indicating that Tribolium have developed olfactory capabilities for navigating complex environments23, can be predatory on other insects24, and can feed and complete development on fungal mycelium alone25. Of particular note, Hori et al.26 demonstrated that T. confusum is similarly attracted to ground wood and wood volatiles as to wheat flour, indicating wood may remain a part of their life history outside of pest spaces. However, while Hori et al.26 demonstrated T. confusum can be attracted to wood, they used commercial wood and not the decomposing wood inhabited by saproxylic organisms. This attraction to wood in species now adapted to stored product areas is understudied, as is their continued use of such habitats, representing a critical gap in our understanding of these significant stored product pests.

Understanding the historical and potentially contemporary affinity for a saproxylic or possibly even saprophagous lifestyle in Tribolium could provide important linkages between landscape and stored product facilities, including opportunities for management. Other important stored product pests, the buprestids, Rhyzopertha deminica (F.) and Prostephanus truncatus (Horn) have been shown to be highly attracted to, and able to reproduce in wild plant material, suggesting ecosystem and landscape factors could contribute to stored pest population dynamics27,28. Species-specific differences in preferences and the ability to reproduce on wood are also critical components to understand how Tribolium exist in a broader environmental space. By examining behavioral responses to widespread wood species, alternative attractants could also be used for low-risk attract-and-kill techniques to help manage populations. Here we assess T. castaneum and T. confusum attraction to three different species of decomposing wood, as well as survival and reproductive output by T. castaneum in woody substrates.

Results

Choice assays

In olfactometer choice assays, a total of 3000 T. castaneum and 3000 T. confusum were assessed for attraction to three species of ground decomposing wood, wheat flour, and an empty control. Of the beetles tested, 39.2% of T. castaneum and 51.9% of T. confusum made a choice over the hour in the assay. Model results indicated significant effects of substrate (X2 2, N = 180 = 1495.14, p < 0.0001) and beetle species (X2 2, N = 180 = 5.68, p = 0.017) but not wood species (X2 2, N = 180 = 2.34, p = 0.311) on choice.

Because there was no effect of wood species on beetle choice, data were pooled for further analysis for each beetle species. Wald Chi-Square tests indicated that there was a significant effect of substrate choice in our generalized linear mixed effects models for both T. castaneum (X2 2, N = 90 = 842.85, p < 0.0001) and T. confusum (X2 2, N = 90 = 653.57, p < 0.0001; Fig. 1A). Tukey HSD post-hoc means comparison tests further indicated a significant difference between each treatment at the p < 0001 level for all comparisons except for control and flour in the T. castaneum analysis (p = 0.0026; Fig. 1A). Beetles were approximately 1.5x (T. castaneum) and 6.25x (T. confusum) more attracted to wheat flour than to an empty control. Sex Choice Assay

We analyzed the separate sex choice assay with a Pearson’s Chi-square to determine if sex had an effect on treatment choice for T. castaneum. No effect of sex was observed (X2 2, N = 515 = 1.00, p = 0.61) (Fig. 1B).

Survival and Reproduction Experiment

Beetle survival at 31ºC was assessed every five days until 100% mortality was observed in all treatment groups except the positive control (wheat flour). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis indicated a significant effect of substrate on survival according to our global log-rank test (X2 3, N = 80 = 63.6, p = 1 × 10−13). Beetles in wheat flour showed complete survival over 20 days, while those in ground wood, wood chunks and empty substrates died by the end of the experiment. Pairwise log-rank tests showed all substrates having significantly greater mortality than wheat flour, but no significant differences between the other substrates: empty vs. ground wood (p = 0.12), empty vs. wood chunks (p = 0.33), and ground wood vs. wood chunks (p = 0.33). Reproduction assessments were conducted after 38 days to provide extended time for any possible larval development. While beetles were present in substrates for at least 10 days before 100% mortality occurred, providing ample opportunity for mating and egg laying, no reproduction was observed in any of the treatments besides flour (Fig. 3B).

Discussion

An underlying olfactory perception to prefer decomposing wood over flour by Tribolium flour beetles suggests an important ecological relationship between the natural landscape and human-made food facilities where they are pests, yet this has been studied little to date. Overwhelmingly, both T. castaneum and T. confusum preferred the decomposing wood tested here when given a choice, even when wheat flour was an option (Fig. 1A). This attraction is primarily based on the treatment volatiles, as the visual cues were not available for the insects to use. Conspecific cues were also likely not involved, due to the short amount of time to make a decision (1 h), further suggesting that the volatile cues from the wood were driving the choices for these insects. The lack of sex differences in preference (Fig. 1B) points to a pervasive saproxylic preference, where the preference for the wood is not necessarily driven by oviposition choice or mate finding, but by an inherent primary attraction for that option of substrate.

While the attraction to decaying dead wood was high, and even greater than previously shown for T. confusum26 on commercial wood, the survival on wood tended to be similar or lower than an empty vial (Fig. 3A), pointing to a possible mismatch in preference for the substrate and the overall benefit from choosing it. In white mulberry, the complete lack of offspring on ground and chunked wood suggests no reproductive value of wood alone for these insects (Fig. 3B). In fact, mortality occurred in white mulberry wood at a slightly faster rate than in the empty control, indicating wood could reduce survival. Similar results have been observed in black cherry and eastern white pine in a related study (Rosenberger, unpublished data). This finding deserves further study, particularly if the physical features of the woody environment, such as cuticular abrasion by the wood, make it less suitable than flour. The unsuitability of wood alone is supported further by anecdotal evidence by Good7 indicating Tribolium were unable to subsist on cork and slippery elm alone, despite being found tunneling in both. This may indicate that Tribolium are historically saproxylic—dependent upon rotting logs, but not necessarily saprophytic—consuming woody material19. However, at the family level, Tenebrionids are prevalent in woody areas of shrub-steppe regions20 and the mealworm Tenbrio molitor carries digestive enzymes capable of digesting plant cells21 suggesting that there may be capabilities in the Tenebrionidae family for xylophagy. For Tribolium however, while there are some genes for cell wall degradation, the genes for hemicellulose and pectin digestion are missing29. Gut microbiome symbionts able to aid in the digestion of cereal grains however, are typically present, and acquisition of others needed for woody material may be possible30,31. The microbiome is important in digestion in many beetles and the variety of endosymbionts has dramatically driven the diversification of these beetles over time32.

The expansion in related species from survival in woody ecosystems to stored product environments could also be partially driven by ecological trade-offs between reproduction and predation avoidance behaviors. As evidenced, while Tribolium may be unable to survive and reproduce on wood alone, they reproduce very well on wheat flour (Fig. 3). Other research has demonstrated low attraction to kairomone-based (food related) lures but high affinity for the aggregation pheromone14,33–35. Behaviorally, Tribolium may be initially attracted to alternative cues associated with food processing and storage facilities, such as temperature and cover36. Once these beetles locate the food and oviposition resources, the real attraction to stored product environments may come from the pheromone cues of conspecifics. However, the underlying attraction to the historical living spaces in woody environments still remains strong, and preferred, in these insects, especially T. castaneum (Fig. 1A). Interestingly, attraction to flour seems to be much stronger in T. confusum compared to T. castaneum, suggesting possible difference in primary attraction. T. castaneum may also require additional attraction cues such as pheromones, direct contact with a substrate, or optimal temperatures to more actively seek out cereal grains. Additional work comparing pheromone attraction to wood may help elucidate more of these preferences for both Tribolium species.

As humans developed grain storage, the pressure from predation in grain versus more trophically diverse woody material may have lessened significantly enough that Tribolium beetles faced different selective pressures related to grain consumption and aggregation 30,37,38. Aggregation pheromones can be important for insects utilizing scarce resources for food or reproduction, and may have been repurposed from decaying trees to grain caches39. Orienting to grains may have originally been an adaptation to stored grain resources cached by rodents, and supported by the unusually large olfactory receptors found among scavenger beetles, and Tribolium in particular23,40. Competition in woody habitats may have also driven Tribolium to invade food-related spaces and subsequently utilize human-mediated dispersal between them41–43. Even as evidenced here, the closely related T. castaneum and T. confusum show differences in the degree of attraction to flour and decaying wood, suggesting that differentiation in primary attraction to substrates is still ongoing between species (Fig. 1A). Competition between these two species in particular often drives niche-space distributions, with T. confusum often preferring and inhabiting previously infested flour44 and showing more attraction to damaged goods in general13. This proposed differential attraction and niche-space separation is also found readily in mills and warehouses as these two species have often been found coexisting15 but in different areas45, whereas in laboratory settings, one will readily outcompete the other46.

The surprisingly high attraction to wood in Tribolium could be harnessed in monitoring and management as a low-risk solution to driving insects out of stored product environments. Results here suggest that regardless of wood species, even distantly related Angiosperms and Gymnosperms were attractive to Tribolium (Fig. 2). Hori et al.26 also showed attraction to all the wood species assayed, with Zelkova serrata, a large deciduous tree, having the highest attraction. More recently, Tribolium has been reported from rotting logs of this species in proximity to fungal mycelia47. Putative compounds for attraction were also assessed, and mellein, a compound found in many fungi, was identified as an important chemical peak of attraction and confirmed as behaviorally attractive in further assays26. Mellein is also produced by Aspergillus spp.48, a common fungal microbe present in stored products49,50, which may provide the connection of attraction to rotting wood and stored products. However, in experiments assessing attraction by T. castaneum to whole wheat with active fungal cultures including Aspergillus and eight other fungal species, no attraction was observed51. Analysis of microbial produced volatile organic compounds did not detect mellein in the bouquets, supporting this chemical as the attractant compound. However, it is also possible that the variety of chemical volatiles produced by nine different fungal species may have shielded attractive effects. Additional analyses of the three wood species assayed here could also provide potential attractive components, including those components that overlap among all woody species tested so far.

This study lays the groundwork for future work on Tribolium attraction and reproduction, which could further demonstrate the link between natural environments and stored product facilities. Beetles in the Ulominae subfamily, such as Tribolium, are typically associated with bark or rotting trees7,52. Investigating whether the chemical cues from these trees could pull insects away from stored product facilities is warranted. Further analyses of insect presence on live trees versus fallen debris, including in favorable versus unfavorable (colder seasons) conditions, are also needed to understand how best to utilize hedgerows and other trees around food facilities, while also balancing the activities of other stored product pests, such as mammals8. The level of decay in rotting wood and wood structure (such as size or abrasiveness) on the survival of Tribolium can also expand the baseline of what these insects are consuming in these woody ecosystems. Grinding the wood as done here and by Hori et al.26 to control for treatment structure may be removing or obliterating necessary bacteria, fungi or abiotic conditions associated with the wood in nature that may further promote beetle success53–55. Wood as a substrate for fungal food should also be investigated, as Tribolium are able to survive and reproduce on fungi alone25, and the Tribolium species used in this study were often seen on or in proximity to mycelial mats on a log kept under observation within a terrarium (D. Rosenberger, unpublished). As scavengers, Tribolium may also be living on other small insect eggs, larvae, or pupae associated with the wood in nature as they have often been known to cannibalize their own progeny under challenging conditions54. All of these components including bacteria, fungi, other insects, material, habitat and more, may be necessary for survival on wood. However, maintenance of the attraction to wood is clearly maintained.

Conclusions. Both T. castaneum and T. confusum showed primary attraction to both flour and decomposing wood, but significantly greater preference to wood compared to flour in this choice assay (Fig. 1A). However, in T. castaneum survival and reproduction studies with white mulberry, no progeny developed on ground or chunked wood and survivorship was similar to adults held in an empty vial (Fig. 3). The attraction to decaying wood is likely a conserved behavior in Tribolium species as found in the distantly related T. castaneum and T. confusum. This primary attraction may be important to historical and perhaps understudied contemporary habitats of these beetles as closely related Tenebrionidae continue to survive in woody habitats. For these two Tribolium flour beetles, some trade-off in reproductive capacity with predation avoidance has likely enhanced survival on cereal grains compared to more trophically diverse woody debris. Additional tests with intact sticks containing other insects, mycelial mats, fungi, or bacteria that may be specific to wood could provide clues into how Tenebrionidae are able to use these natural landscapes and if additional tree coverage could be used to attract these insects away from stored grain commodities. This research advances our understanding of how these insects have adapted to different environments and diets, while also identifying potential avenues for their management.

Methods

Choice assays

Beetles used in this experiment were reared according to standard methods in an incubator at 30ºC and 40% humidity. T. castaneum were reared from stock obtained from Carolina Biological Supplies and T. confusum were obtained from the USDA-ARS Center for Grain and Animal Health Research (Manhattan, KS). Beetle colonies were reared in a 95% whole wheat flour (King Arthur Baking Company, Vermont) and 5% brewer’s yeast (Mommy Knows Best, NYC, New York) substrate in 32 oz glass jars with filter paper lids. Colonies were subcultured every 30–45 days by removing approximately 100–150 mixed-sex adults to a new jar, where they were allowed to lay eggs for approximately 1 week before being removed to allow the new generation to develop. During the experiment, beetles were chosen at random from five maintained colonies.

Wood material was collected in hedgerows in Northern Illinois from the ground under focal tree species in late January. Material consisted of fallen limbs approximately 0.5 m in length and demonstrating similar levels of decay (loose or peeling bark, softened or punky wood). Material was collected from 10 different trees of each of the three species, namely black cherry (Prunus serotina) (Rosaceae), white mulberry (Morus alba) (Moraceae) and eastern white pine (Pinus strobus) (Pinaceae). Prunus serotina and M. alba in particular are common in hedgerows in the American Midwest, thus approximating resources available around grain mills. P. strobes, while also found in such environments to some degree, provides a distantly related gymnosperm to contrast with the two angiosperms. Gymnosperms and angiosperms also generally support differing decay fungal communities56. Limbs were allowed to dry indoors at room temperature (25 °C ± 5 °C) for at least two weeks before use. When needed for experiments, wood material was extracted via a drill from various parts of the limb, ground in a metal mortar and pestle and allowed to equilibrate for 24 h at room temperature.

A six-hole pitfall-trap olfactometer, similar to that used by Hori et al.26, was used to assess beetle preference. The olfactometer consisted of six holes, 5 mm in diameter, arranged evenly around a 12.5 cm diameter circular stage. The stage consisted of cardstock paper, which provided traction. Plastic tape on the underside of the stage through which the holes were punched reduced the beetle’s ability to walk on the underside. The stage was enclosed by a plastic ring with a screen placed over the top, allowing ventilation, and placed atop six Erlenmeyer flasks (25 mL). Each wood was tested independently of the other wood species but always with a control (empty) flask as a negative control and whole wheat flour (King Arthur Baking Company, Vermont) as a positive control for a total of three treatments per individual assay. Two vials were used for each of the three treatments, for a total of six vials per assay, each containing 2 g of material (or empty for the negative control), and treatments were alternated around the circle so that each treatment was always between the other two treatments.

Assays were initiated by placing 100 mixed-sex adult beetles that had eclosed 2–6 weeks previously and starved for 24 h, into the center of the platform. Assays occurred at room temperature in a dark room, and beetles were given one hour to make a choice. At the end of an hour, the beetles that had fallen into each treatment were counted. The same procedure was repeated for 10 replicates for each species of wood and for both T. castaneum and T. confusum.

The sex of beetles making choices were not determined for the primary experiment in this study. However, to obtain an understanding as to whether one sex was preferentially choosing one of the treatments, we analyzed the results of a separate experiment using T. castaneum that was completed in the same way, but with one log from one tree species (white mulberry) and eight replicate experiments. Beetles were sexed after choices were made57.

Survival and reproduction

To determine if beetles can survive and reproduce in a wood substrate, we conducted survival and reproduction experiments using female T. castaneum and white mulberry wood. White mulberry was chosen as the model tree for these assays as it was the medially attractive wood in the attraction surveys. White mulberry is also the most cosmopolitan of the species tested, being native to China but now common worldwide. Each of four different treatment containers received one female beetle having eclosed 2–4 weeks previously from the colony population. Treatments included ground wood, chunked wood (small broken pieces of the rotting logs used in the choice experiment, ranging from 1–2 cm in diameter), wheat flour (positive control), and an empty container (negative control) with only a Kim Wipe (Kimberly-Clark, Texas) placed at the bottom to reduce beetles becoming stuck on their backs and expending additional energy. Treatments were repeated 20 times each, with 10 in mini petri dishes and 10 in three-dram vials, all kept in an incubator at 31ºC and approximately 40% humidity. Survival of each beetle (n = 80) was checked every five days, and the proportion surviving was recorded over 20 days, after which they were removed. Total reproduction was assessed at 38 days by counting all life stages present.

Statistics

Choice data were analyzed using generalized linear mixed effects models with a Poisson distribution (lme4 package) in R (vs. 4.5.0) with each assay included as a random effect to account for non-independence within assays. Wood species, Tribolium species, and treatment were the main effects in the model. Wald Chi-Square tests (car package) were used to test whether predictors significantly contributed to the model. Means were separated with a Tukey HSD post-hoc test (multcomp package) with α < 0.05. Comparisons between species were conducted with independent t-tests, with assumptions checked using residual plots to check for heteroskedasticity and normality of errors and square root transformations applied to fit data when needed. We used Kaplan–Meier analysis (survival and survminer packages in R) to assess survival among the four substrates in the survival experiment. We assessed differences between survival curves using a global log-rank test and pairwise log-rank comparisons between substrates with a Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing. No test statistics were needed for reproduction due to the lack of reproduction in all but one treatment.
