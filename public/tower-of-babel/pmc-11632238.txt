Critical ablation targets of ventricular tachycardia revealed by activation mapping with both last deflection and peak frequency annotations in a patient of cardiac sarcoidosis
Authors: Shinya Yamada, Takashi Kaneshiro, Minoru Nodera, Sadahiro Murota, Yasuchika Takeishi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Different rotation activation pattern (RAP) sites with good pacemap score were identified by activation mapping with both last deflection and peak frequency annotations. These two RAP sites were considered to be the critical ablation targets of ventricular tachycardia in cardiac sarcoidosis, which led to successful ablation without an extensive lesion.

Isochronal late activation mapping (ILAM) with last deflection annotation has been reported as an effective technique for identifying critical isthmuses of the ventricular tachycardia (VT) circuit. 1 A novel peak frequency annotation software may provide useful information on ablation targets different from those identified by the ILAM. Herein, we present a case of cardiac sarcoidosis with drug‐refractory VT in which two different ablation targets were identified by activation mapping with last deflection and peak frequency annotations.

A 75‐year‐old female patient with cardiac sarcoidosis was admitted to our hospital for catheter ablation of ventricular tachycardia (VT), for which frequent implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator therapies had been delivered, although the patient was administered steroids and antiarrhythmic drugs.

First, left ventriculography was performed, and a giant ventricular aneurysm was found in the postero‐inferior area of the left ventricular (LV) wall (Figure 1A). Next, endocardial LV voltage mapping was performed during right ventricular pacing rhythm, using a high‐density mapping catheter (Advisor™ HD Grid Mapping Catheter; Abbott, St. Paul, MN) with the EnSite X™ EP system (Abbott, St. Paul, MN). During this mapping, hemodynamically unstable VT with right bundle branch block configuration and superior axis was repeatedly induced (Figure 1B). The voltage map revealed extensive low‐voltage areas and large areas of dense scar tissue within the giant LV aneurysm (Figure 2A). Next, local potentials, collected during right ventricular pacing, were automatically annotated at the last deflection using the Ensite X system, and an isochronal late activation mapping (ILAM) was constructed as described previously. 1 Activation was visualized based on isochronal analysis with the mapping window divided into eight equal isochrones. Deceleration zones 1 were observed extensively in the giant LV aneurysm (Figure 2B). In addition, an activation map constructed with last deflection annotation (last deflection map) revealed a line of conduction block 1 with split potentials with isoelectric segments of ≥20 ms and a rotation activation pattern (RAP) 2 pivoting around the conduction block line in deceleration zones (Figure 2C and Video S1). We also constructed an activation map of potentials, collected during right ventricular pacing, using peak frequency annotation on the EnSite OT Near Field™ (near‐field map). Interestingly, the near‐field map demonstrated the line of conduction block and the RAP at a different part from that of the last deflection map (Figure 2D and Video S2). Subsequently, pace mapping was performed at the RAP site on the last deflection map and near‐field map. During the pace mapping on the last deflection map (Figure 3A), three morphologies with different stimulus latencies were observed during pace mapping, which suggest the presence of multiple exit sites, 3 and the third morphology closely matched to a targeted VT (stimulus‐QRS interval: 184 ms). On the near‐field map (Figure 3B), a close correspondence with a targeted VT was found during pace mapping (stimulus‐QRS interval: 82 ms). A high‐density mapping catheter was placed at the RAP site on the near‐field map. VT induction was performed, and early systolic potentials were observed during VT at the RAP site on the near‐field map (Figure 3C). Accordingly, these two RAP sites were considered to be the critical targets of ablation, which led to successful VT ablation without an extensive lesion. One year after ablation, the patient was free of recurrence.

The direction of ventricular wavefront is very important to evaluate activation pattern and detect critical isthmuses in the VT circuit. Therefore, substrate mapping during sinus rhythm and/or ventricular pacing from different sites is frequently performed in hemodynamically unstable VT, and abnormal electrograms within a scar or a scar border zone are identified as ablation targets. 4 However, it is still difficult to accurately identify abnormal electrograms related to VT circuits, because near‐field (the endocardial surface) and far‐field (the intramural myocardium) electrograms may be present and merge together within the scar or the scar border zone. A peak frequency annotation software has been recently developed that is capable of distinguishing near‐field signals from mixed signals of intracardiac electrograms and identifying abnormal electrograms in the endocardium. 5 However, in cases where a VT circuit forms in the intramural myocardium, abnormal electrograms may not be clearly displayed using peak frequency annotation from the endocardial sites. In those cases, an activation map with last deflection annotation may be useful in the identification of abnormal electrograms in the intramural myocardium. Therefore, the efficacy of peak frequency annotation and/or last deflection annotation depends on the location of critical isthmuses in the VT circuit.

In the present case, the line of conduction block and RAP were in different locations on both the near‐field and last deflection maps. It has been reported that the RAPs with slow conduction are located at the isthmuses of the VT circuit (at the exit in 71% of the reported cases and at the central proximal region in 29% of the reported cases). 2 At the RAP site on the near‐field map, good pace mapping with a shorter stimulus‐QRS interval was obtained and early systolic potentials were observed during VT, indicating that an exit of the VT circuit isthmus was located at the endocardium. Whereas, at the RAP site on the last deflection map, multiple exit sites and good pace mapping with a longer stimulus‐QRS interval were found during pace mapping, suggesting that the central proximal region of the VT circuit isthmus was located in the intramural myocardium. The re‐entrant circuit could not be entirely identified due to the unmappable VT. However, the presence of a three‐dimensional re‐entrant circuit with the isthmus from the intramural myocardium to the endocardial surface was suspected from the findings in the near‐field and last deflection maps. Accordingly, we performed the ablation of core elements for the VT circuit that included the two different RAP sites without extensive ablation for low‐voltage areas.

In conclusion, we identified critical ablation targets of VT by activation mapping with both the last deflection and peak frequency annotations in cardiac sarcoidosis with a giant LV aneurysm. Determination of ablation targets requires the use of a near‐field map, a last deflection map, or both, depending on the location of critical isthmuses in the VT circuit.
