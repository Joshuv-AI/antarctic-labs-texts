Prevalence and Trends in Global Retractions Explored Through a Topic Lens
Zhengyi Zhou, Ying Lou, Zhesi Shen, Menghui Li

Abstract
Scientific publications form the cornerstone of innovation and have maintained a stable growth trend over the years. However, in recent years, there has been a significant surge in retractions, driven largely by the proliferation of low-quality and fraudulent papers. This study aims to examine retractions and their evolving trends through a topic lens. Our analysis of global retraction data reveals that the numbers of retraction have remained alarmingly high in recent years, with the growth rate of retracted papers significantly outpacing that of overall global publications. While retractions are observed across various fields, their distribution is not uniform. In disciplines characterized by high retraction rates, certain topics may only encounter minor issues, whereas in fields with lower retraction rates, some topics can experience substantial challenges. Moreover, an unexpected surge in publications has been observed in specific topics that also display abnormally high retraction rates. This study underscores several indicators that can assist the scientific community in pinpointing key fields that require rigorous scrutiny for potential low-quality and fraudulent research. Ultimately, our findings could serve as a benchmark for examining scientific integrity across diverse topics and offer crucial insights for developing tailored governance policies to enhance research integrity in each field. Keywords: Retraction; Systematic Fraud; Research Integrity; Relative Retraction Rate ( R 3 R^{3} ); Growth Rate;

Materials and Methods

2.1
Retractions

We collected retracted papers from Amend platform
[
5
]
, which consolidates information on retractions from official websites of journals. This is primarily achieved by collecting the Digital Object Identifiers (DOIs) of both the retraction notices and the corresponding retracted papers, along with the content of retraction notices. As of the end of August 2025, this corpus has comprised 55,000 unique retracted papers, mainly consisting of research articles and reviews, while excluding conference abstracts.

2.2
Publications and Citation Topics

We retrieved the citation topics for each retracted paper using their DOIs from Clarivate’s Web of Science (WoS). The citation topics are organized into a three-level hierarchy, consisting of 10 macro topics, 326 meso topics, and 2,478 micro topics, with each article assigned a topic at every level. Ultimately, out of the 55,000 retracted papers, about 46,000 were assigned topics. Additionally, we collected data on the number of publications (both article and review) and retracted papers for each topic from 2000 to 2024 using WoS.

2.3
Retraction Rate

The retraction rate is an index used to assess the frequency of retracted articles within a collection of publications
[
24
]
. It is defined as the number of retracted papers published between year

j
and year
i
, multiplied by 10,000, and then divided by the total number of publications during the same period. The calculation formula is as follows:

r=\frac{\sum^{i}_{j}R_{y}}{\sum^{i}_{j}P_{y}}*10000,

(1)

where
R_{y}
denotes the number of retracted papers, and
P_{y}
represents the number of publications published in year
y
.

2.4
Compound Annual Growth Rate

The Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) is a valuable metric used to measure the mean annual growth rate of publications over a specified time period as following
[
27
]
,

\beta=((\frac{P_{i}}{P_{j}})^{\frac{1}{i-j}}-1)*100,

(2)

where
\beta
represents the Compound Annual Growth Rate (in percentage),
P_{i}
denotes the number of publications in the final year
i
, and
P_{j}
indicates the number of publications in the initial year
j
. The analysis of CAGR reveals the evolving trends in annual publications over time.

By calculating the CAGR of a field and comparing it to the global CAGR, one can assess whether the field is growing faster than the global average. This can be represented using the following formula:

\Delta CAGR=\beta^{f}-\beta^{g},

(3)

where
\beta^{f}
and
\beta^{g}
represents the CAGR of a specific field and global publications, respectively. Due to the differing peak years for annual publication volumes and retraction counts across various topics (Fig.
7
and
8
), we select the year in which retraction counts peak as the final year
i
for each specific topic. Then, we take
i-n
as the initial year and calculate the growth rates and retraction rates accordingly.

3
Results

3.1
Global annual retractions remain high

In recent years, the number of retractions has surged dramatically, with 2023 setting a record of over 14,000 retraction notices being issued. Concurrently, nearly 12,000 articles published in 2022 have been retracted, further establishing a new record. Following this trend, 2024 witnessed more than 9,000 additional retractions. As of the end of August, the total number of retractions has surpassed 5,000 in 2025 (Fig.
1
). The ongoing high volume of retractions poses unprecedented challenges for the governance of research integrity in the future.

The number of retractions is not only in constant flux, but the reasons behind them are also evolving. In 2023, the majority of retractions were linked to fraudulent peer reviews, with retraction notices highlighting issues such as “concerns that the peer review process has been compromised” and “systematic manipulation of the publication and peer-review process”. This trend continued into 2024 and 2025, with over 6,400 retractions attributed to fake peer reviews.

Moreover, there were 2,100 retractions associated with AI-generated content (AIGC), with retraction notices citing issues like “tortured phrases”, “nonstandard phrases”, and “generated by a large language model (LLM)”. Additionally, 2,300 cases involved paper mills, with retraction notices including “substantial changes in authorship”, “unauthorized authorship changes”, and “suspicious email addresses linked to different researcher accounts”.

Furthermore, there were 1,800 instances of citation manipulation, pointing to factors such as “significant increases in citations”, “citations irrelevant to the research article”, “citations included in the paper benefited”, and “excessive level of self-citation”. These issues often occur simultaneously within the same paper, highlighting that low-quality fraudulent research has become a systematic problem. This presents new challenges for the governance of research integrity.

In the past two years, the number of retractions remains high, with systematic fraud continuing to be the primary reason. This raises an important question: how many fraudulent articles have yet to be discovered? Therefore, it is essential to strengthen post-publication review processes to identify potential fraudulent papers and prevent them from continuing to impact subsequent research.

3.2
Retractions are increasing much faster than publications

Above results indicate a surge in retractions in recent years. We then examined these retractions from a topic perspective, focusing initially on ten macro topics: Clinical & Life Sciences (Clin & Life Sci), Chemistry, Agriculture, Environment & Ecology (Agri Env & Ecol), Electrical Engineering, Electronics & Computer Science (EE & Comp Sci), Physics, Social Sciences, Engineering & Materials Science (Eng & Mat Sci), Earth Sciences, Mathematics, Arts & Humanities.

According to the Incites database from the Web of Science, between 2000 and 2024, journals indexed by SCI, SSCI, and ESCI published a total of nearly 42 million articles, of which over 46,000 were retracted for various reasons. This translates to an retraction rate of approximately 11.04 per 10,000 articles. However, retraction rates vary significantly across disciplines, ranging from 3.25 to 31.97. For instance, the number of retracted papers has exceeded 9,700 in EE & Comp Sci, yielding a retraction rate of 31.97 per 10,000 - ten times higher than that of Physics. Additionally, Clin & Life Sci, a discipline known for its retraction challenges, has seen over 21,000 retracted articles, with a retraction rate of 13.85 (Fig.
2
). These two disciplines, characterized by a substantial number of retractions and a notably high retraction rate, have emerged as significant hotspots for retractions. This suggests a varying prevalence of retractions among different disciplines.

These findings reveal a concerning trend of increasing proportions of questionable research outputs over time.

3.3
Retractions are common across topics, but their numbers vary significantly

There is a consistent trend in retractions across all disciplines. Next, we will analyze the scale of retractions across over 320 meso topics, including Micro & Long Noncoding RNA (mlncRNA), Artificial Intelligence & Machine Learning (AI & ML), Human-Computer Interaction(H Comp Interact), among others.

At the meso-topic level, it has been confirmed that at least one instance of retraction is found in almost meso-topics. This global representation effectively highlights the meso-topics affected by retractions (Fig.

Notably, the number of retracted papers in Human-Computer Interaction ranks second, just after Micro & Long Noncoding RNA. Consequently, fields related to topics with a high number of retractions may also become hotspots for retractions, necessitating more rigorous reviews of papers associated with these fields.

3.4
Prevalence of retractions differs across various topic levels

The science map of retractions provides an overview of retraction cases, but it does not account for the significant differences in publication volumes across various topics. Beyond these fields with a substantial number of retractions, many topics may report few retractions, yet their overall publication numbers are also relatively low. In fact, these fields are also facing challenges related to retractions. Consequently, it is insufficient to rely solely on retraction counts to reflect the severity of the issues at hand. To gain a more accurate assessment of retraction severity, it is essential to consider both the number or proportion of retractions in relation to total publications.

It is found that certain topics exhibit a disproportionately high proportion of retractions relative to their publication output. For example, the topic of mlncRNA (H Comp Interact) accounts for 6.8% (4.73%) of retractions, despite representing only 0.39% (0.06%) of publications. Therefore, a retraction index known as Relative Retraction Rate (
R^{3}
) is defined, drawing inspiration from the relationship between the percentage of retractions and the percentage of publications, as follows,

R^{3}_{i}=\frac{R_{i}/\sum_{j}R_{j}}{P_{i}/\sum_{j}P_{j}},

(4)

where
R_{i}
(or
P_{i}
) denotes the number of retracted papers (or publications) in a particular entity
i
, respectively. The Relative Retraction Rate can be used to assess the severity of retractions across specific entities, such as topics, journals, and institutions, regardless of the scale of the retractions or the number of articles involved. Furthermore, annual
R^{3}
s for each entity can be calculated to facilitate longitudinal comparisons.

The
R^{3}
for all meso-topics offers a broad perspective on the severity of retractions across various disciplines or meso topics, as shown in Figure
5
(B). The baseline reflects the overall level of retractions, corresponding to the global average retraction rate. If the
R^{3}
is greater than 1, it indicates a higher severity of retractions in comparison to the overall baseline. Conversely, a value less than 1 suggests lower severity. Notably, H Comp Interact has faced the most severe retraction issue, with a peak
R^{3}
of 74.32. This indicates that its retraction rate is 74 times higher than the global average of 11.04, equating to approximately 820 retracted papers per 10,000 publications. This suggests that the prevalence of retractions in this topic is quite high. Furthermore, the
R^{3}
s exceed 1 for the majority of topics within the discipline of EE & Comp Sci. In the discipline of Clin & Life Sci, around half of the topics also have
R^{3}
s greater than 1. In contrast, nearly all topics within the disciplines of Physics and Mathematics exhibit a
R^{3}
of less than 1, indicating a lower prevalence of retractions in these topics.

The overall level of research integrity fails to adequately reflect the status of individual topics, as the severity of retractions at the macro level does not ensure the integrity of all research areas within that field. This variation is instrumental in understanding research integrity issues and in effectively addressing the prevalence of unreliable papers.

4
Conclusions and Discussions

Due to the pressure of “publish or perish”, researchers may engage in problematic research practices, such as fabrication, falsification and plagiarism, or choose popular topics that have a high annual publication volume, making them more susceptible to systematic fraud
[
39
,
34
]
. In contrast, these hot topics are susceptible to systematic fraud due to numerous unresolved challenges. For instance, under investigated non-coding RNAs have emerged as targets for systematic fraudulent research
[
2
,
18
]
. Thus, the rapid increase in publication volume within a field may contribute to a rise in large-scale scientific fraud. Conversely, the proliferation of low quality and fraudulent papers can further accelerate the growth of that field.

The increasing prevalence and trends in retractions pose significant risks for the scientific community. In the past two years, a significant number of articles have been retracted (Fig.
1
), primarily due to systematic fraud, including practices such as paper mills, AI-generated content (AIGC), and fake peer reviews. The growth rate of retractions is increasing at a pace surpassing that of regular publications (Fig.
3
). While the number of retractions varies, they can occur across a wide range of topics (Fig.
