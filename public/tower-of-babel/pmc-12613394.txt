How childhood maltreatment influences aggression in juvenile offenders: a serial mediation model involving executive function and alexithymia
Authors: Xue Zhang, Tailei Gong, Xiaodi Wang, Runchu Zhang, Weijie Meng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildhood maltreatment is prevalent among juvenile offenders and constitutes a significant risk factor for aggression, which is highly associated with violent crimes in this population. However, the underlying serial mediation mechanism involving executive function and alexithymia in the relationship between childhood maltreatment and aggression requires further investigation.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted with 135 juvenile offenders from a detention center, of which 103 valid participants were retained (62.1% male; aged 15 – 18 years). Participants completed validated scales: The Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ), the Adult Executive Function Inventory-Self (ASEXI), the Perth Alexithymia Questionnaire (PAQ), and the Buss-Perry Aggression Questionnaire (BPAQ). Serial mediation analysis was performed using Hayes’ PROCESS macro (Model 6).Results(1) Childhood maltreatment was directly and positively associated with aggression (β = 0.49, t = 2.96, p < 0.01); (2) Significant independent mediating effects were found for executive function (β = 0.12, SE = 0.06, 95% CI [0.01, 0.25]) and alexithymia (β = 0.10, SE = 0.07, 95% CI [0.001, 0.25]); (3) A significant serial mediation pathway emerged: childhood maltreatment → executive function → alexithymia → aggression (β = 0.05, SE = 0.03, 95% CI [0.002, 0.12]), accounting for 6.59% of the total effect.ConclusionThis study confirms dual pathways linking childhood maltreatment to aggression in juvenile offenders: direct effects and serial mediation through executive dysfunction and alexithymia. Findings underscore the need for trauma-informed interventions targeting cognitive and affective regulation skills to reduce violence in this high-risk group.Keywords: Juvenile offenders, Childhood maltreatment, Aggression, Executive function, Alexithymia, Juvenile delinquency prevention

Introduction

Juvenile delinquency represents a critical global social challenge characterized by distinctive offender profiles, diverse crime typologies, and multifaceted etiological pathways. Recent data from China's Supreme People’s Procuratorate [51] reveals an escalating trend in youth crime, with notable increases in violent offenses such as gang fights, robbery, and disorderly conduct. This pattern is particularly concerning given that adolescent violence not only inflicts immediate harm on victims and perpetrators but also poses long-term risks: juvenile violent behavior predicts adult criminality [14], threatening institutional safety and social stability. Understanding the mechanisms driving aggression in juvenile offenders is, therefore, imperative for effective intervention.

Childhood maltreatment and aggression

Aggression and violence exist on a behavioral continuum, with violence representing the most severe manifestation of aggression. And aggression is operationally defined as any intentional act causing physical or psychological harm to others or oneself [4]. During adolescence — a critical developmental period characterized by neurocognitive immaturity and limited self-regulatory capacity — aggressive behaviors are particularly prevalent. Empirical evidence consistently identifies childhood maltreatment (encompassing physical, emotional, or sexual abuse/neglect causing actual or potential harm) as a robust distal factor influencing aggression [34, 45]. Neurodevelopmental research confirms that early maltreatment alters stress-response systems (e.g., HPA-axis dysregulation), predisposing individuals to aggressive behavior [24, 30, 52]. Social learning theory further posits that maltreated youth may internalize violent behavioral scripts as maladaptive coping strategies [5, 36, 46]. Consistent with these theoretical accounts, Koolschijn et al. [29] found that higher levels of childhood maltreatment were associated with higher scores on general aggression and increased clinical risks. This association appears especially pronounced in legal-involved youth, who experience more severe maltreatment and display aggressive behavior more frequently than their peers in the general population [3, 6, 37, 40]. Consequently, investigating the maltreatment–aggression link in this vulnerable group is both scientifically and clinically imperative.

Executive dysfunction as a mediator

Indeed, early life experiences constitute a significant factor influencing aggressive behavior. However, adolescent physical and psychological development results from the complex interaction of multiple factors. Only through a comprehensive examination of these interacting elements can we thoroughly analyze and interpret aggressive behaviors in juvenile offenders. Executive function, as a higher-order cognitive ability, plays a crucial role in the manifestation of aggressive behavior. It refers to a top-down psychological process through which individuals consciously regulate their thoughts and actions in goal-directed behavior [19]. Empirical studies have demonstrated that the level of executive function significantly influences both the initiation and inhibition of aggressive behaviors [55]. The General Aggression Model (GAM) posits that situational factors (e.g., maltreatment, frustration) contribute to aggressive behavior by shaping an individual's cognitive, affective, and arousal processes [2]. Specifically, adverse childhood experiences may lead to executive dysfunction, fostering negative cognitive schemas and impairing an individual's ability to flexibly respond to environmental cues, thereby increasing the likelihood of aggressive responses. Emerging research indicates that adolescence represents a critical period for executive function development, during which early adverse experiences can exert long-term effects on executive functioning into late adolescence and early adulthood [16]. These developmental disruptions may further be associated with the emergence of aggressive behaviors in youth [22]. Consequently, this study hypothesizes that the effect of childhood maltreatment on aggressive behavior is mediated by executive function.

Alexithymia as a mediator

Adolescence represents a critical period for emotional development, during which deficits in emotional awareness can have far-reaching behavioral consequences. One such deficit is alexithymia, characterized by difficulties in identifying and describing one’s own emotions, challenges in distinguishing emotions from the bodily sensations they elicit, and a tendency toward externally oriented thinking [38, 47, 52]. Research has linked alexithymia to interpersonal difficulties and externalizing problem behaviors, including aggression [43, 56]. According to the social mechanism perspective of alexithymia [57], its development is influenced by adverse social factors, such as family violence and childhood maltreatment. Empirical evidence suggests that early adverse experiences constitute a significant risk factor for alexithymia [1]. Specifically, an unfavorable social environment during childhood may fail to meet an individual’s emotional needs, impairing their ability to recognize and communicate emotions effectively. This deficit, in turn, may contribute to the development of maladaptive behaviors [27]. Research further indicates that alexithymia, often arising as a consequence of social trauma, frequently serves as a mediating factor in the development of both internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors [3]. Juvenile offenders represent a distinct population with a higher prevalence and complexity of childhood adversities compared to the general adolescent population. Given the established links between early adversity, alexithymia, and aggression, a critical question emerges: Does childhood maltreatment in juvenile offenders contribute to alexithymia, thereby increasing the likelihood of aggressive behavior?​.

Executive function-alexithymia pathways

Crucially, a significant association exists between executive function and alexithymia. As a higher-order cognitive process, executive function plays a pivotal role in information processing and behavioral regulation. Impairments in executive function can adversely affect emotional expression and emotion regulation, thereby exacerbating alexithymia [20]. Empirical evidence suggests that individuals with lower executive function exhibit poorer emotional processing and higher levels of alexithymia [18]. Furthermore, childhood maltreatment and neglect are associated with cognitive processing deficits, which may impair emotion regulation and intensify alexithymia. This cascade of cognitive-emotional dysfunction may, in turn, increase susceptibility to aggressive behaviors.

Current study

Integrating GAM principles, we investigate serial mediation pathways linking childhood maltreatment (situational factor) to aggression in juvenile offenders through executive function and alexithymia. Neurocognitive evidence further supports this theoretical framework, demonstrating that childhood maltreatment reduces hippocampal-prefrontal connectivity [12, 21], while impairing both cognitive control and emotional processing—this dual deficit collectively contributes to aggression.

This study aims to examine whether (1) aggressive behavior in juvenile offenders is significantly associated with childhood maltreatment and (2) executive function and alexithymia play mediating roles in this association. The findings may provide empirical evidence for developing prevention and intervention strategies targeting aggressive behavior in this vulnerable population.

Methods

Participants

A cross-sectional survey was conducted with 135 juvenile offenders recruited from a women's prison and juvenile detention center in F Province, China. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Ludong University (Approval No. 202411001) and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and custodial authorities.

Data were collected in person under the supervision of correctional officers. The survey was administered in two sessions (males recruited from a juvenile detention center and females from the juvenile section of a women’s prison). Participants were escorted by correctional officers to an activity room, where researchers distributed questionnaires, read standardized instructions, and collected the questionnaires upon completion. Completion of the study battery required approximately 20 min. However, given that participants were still serving sentences and their daily activities were institutionally regulated, no financial reimbursement was provided.

After excluding invalid responses — based on either incomplete answers, extremely low response rates, or failure on attention-check items — 103 valid responses were retained (76.30% valid response rate). 62.1% of participants were male and aged 15-18 years, with a mean incarceration duration of 9.00 ± 7.68 months. As participants may have engaged in more than one type of offense, crime categories were coded non-mutually exclusively: ① Violent offenses (robbery, assault, affray, school bullying): 50.49%; ② Sexual crimes (rape, prostitution, solicitation): 39.81%; ③ Other offenses (such as theft, cyber fraud, et al.): 38.83%. To ensure the adequacy of the sample size for the study analyses, power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1. With a medium effect size of f2 = 0.15, α = 0.05, and three predictor variables, the statistical power for a sample size of 103 was 0.91, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.80 [17].

Measures

Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ)

The questionnaire was developed by Bernstein et al. [8] and the Chinese revised version was used in this study. This 28-item scale assesses five dimensions of childhood maltreatment: physical abuse, emotional abuse, sexual abuse, physical neglect, and emotional neglect. Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater severity of maltreatment. In the current study, the scale demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.84).

Adult executive functioning inventory-self (ADEXI)

The ADEXI was developed by Holst and Thorell [26], and validated for adolescent populations by Thorell et al. [53]. This 14-item instrument evaluates working memory and inhibitory control. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores reflecting better executive function (e.g., “I have difficulty remembering lengthy instructions”; “I have a tendency to do things without first thinking about what could happen”). The scale exhibited excellent reliability in our sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.96).

Perth Alexithymia Questionnaire (PAQ)

The PAQ was developed by Preece et al. [44] and adapted for adolescents in China by Niu et al. [41]. This 24-item scale includes five sub-scales: negative-difficulty identifying feelings (N-DIF), positive-difficulty identifying feelings (P-DIF), negative-difficulty describing feelings (N-DDF), positive-difficulty describing feelings (P-DDF), and general-externally oriented thinking (G-EOT). Seven-point scoring was used, with higher scores indicating greater alexithymia severity. The scale showed excellent internal consistency in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.97).

Buss-Perry Aggression Questionnaire (BPAQ)

The BPAQ was developed and validated by Buss and Perry [11] and used to measure aggressive behavior widely. This 20-item revision measures four dimensions: physical aggression, hostility, anger, and verbal aggression via 5-point Likert scales. Higher scores indicate greater aggression, and the scale demonstrated excellent reliability in our sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.96).

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 and PROCESS macro v3.1. First, SPSS 26.0 was used to test the correlations between childhood maltreatment, executive function, alexithymia and aggression. Subsequently, we examined key regression assumptions, including normality of residuals, linearity, and multicollinearity. Then, a serial mediation analysis was conducted using Model 6 of the PROCESS macro. Furthermore, the potential common method bias effect was examined using Harman’s single factor test for all research items [59]. The results revealed 15 distinct factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, and the first factor accounted for 28.52% of the total variance, which is below the threshold level of 40%. Therefore, common method deviation was not obvious in this study.

A chain mediation analysis

This study employed a structural equation modeling approach to examine the multiple mediating effects [23]. Childhood maltreatment was used as the independent variable, executive function and alexithymia as mediating variables, and aggression as the dependent variable. First, regression assumptions were carefully examined. Multicollinearity was not a concern, as all tolerance values were greater than 0.75 and all VIF values were well below the critical threshold of 10 (Tolerance range = 0.75-0.91; VIF range = 1.10-1.33). Normality and linearity of residuals were further supported by the P–P plots and histogram of standardized residuals, which approximated a normal distribution (mean ≈ 0, SD ≈ 1). These results indicate that the regression assumptions were adequately met.

Next, PROCESS 3.1 was used to test the mediating effect. The results indicated that the path coefficient from childhood maltreatment to executive function was significant and negative (β = −0.52, t = −3.13, p < 0.01), while the paths to alexithymia (β = 0.51, t = 2.51, p < 0.05) and aggression (β = 0.49, t = 2.96, p < 0.01) were significant and positive. Executive Function was significantly and negatively associated with alexithymia (β = −0.49, t = −4.18, p < 0.01) and aggression (β = −0.22, t = −2.22, p < 0.05), and alexithymia was significantly and positively associated with aggression (β = 0.20, t = 2.49, p < 0.05).

The Bootstrap method was used to calculate the 95% confidence interval by repeating the sample for 5000 times to further investigate the mediating effect. The results showed that (Table 2), executive function (β = 0.12, 95% CI [0.01, 0.25]) and alexithymia mediated (β = 0.10, 95% CI [0.001, 0.25]) relationship between childhood maltreatment and aggression separately, and the serial mediation pathway is also significant (β = 0.05, 95% CI [0.002, 0.12]).

Discussion

The present study examined the effects of early adverse experiences on aggressive behavior in juvenile offenders, with particular attention to the mediating roles of executive function and alexithymia. Our findings supported the hypothesized model, demonstrating that childhood maltreatment may impair executive function development in juvenile offenders, subsequently exacerbating alexithymia and increasing the risk of aggressive behavior.

The serial mediating effect of executive function and alexithymia

Consistent with previous research [33, 34, 54], childhood maltreatment emerged as a significant positive predictor of aggressive behavior. This finding aligns with established literature on the intergenerational transmission of violent behavior [35], suggesting that individuals exposed to early maltreatment or neglect may acquire and replicate aggressive behaviors as maladaptive coping strategies. Furthermore, most juvenile offenders are in adolescence—a critical developmental period characterized by cognitive immaturity and behavioral impulsivity [60]. The absence of supportive family environments appears to render these individuals particularly vulnerable to employing aggression as a means of emotional regulation. Therefore, childhood abuse constitutes a key risk factor for juvenile delinquents' aggressive behavior. We should pay attention to family education, create a good childhood environment for adolescents, and reduce the experience of early abuse and neglect. In school settings, trauma-informed educational approaches may help to disrupt the developmental trajectory from childhood adversity to delinquent behavior, such as structured school-based support programs and teacher-focused psychoeducation initiatives.

Our results revealed that executive function mediated the relationship between childhood maltreatment and aggressive behavior. The social information processing model provides a theoretical framework for understanding this association [32], positing that environmental factors influence cognitive processing and subsequent behavioral responses. From a neurobiological perspective, early adverse experiences may disrupt cortical development, leading to executive dysfunction [10, 12]. As executive function serves as a central component in adolescent developmental tasks, its impairment has been consistently linked to increased aggression [49]. For juvenile offenders, their family environment and atmosphere are often poor, and the problem of early abuse and neglect is more prominent, which affects the development of their executive function. When they face external environmental stimuli, they are more likely to lose control and produce aggressive behavior. These findings underscore the importance of cognitive enhancement interventions (e.g., working memory training and executive function interventions) in prevention and treatment programs for juvenile offenders.

The study also demonstrated that alexithymia mediated the association between childhood maltreatment and aggressive behavior. Family, as a primary socialization context, plays an important role in individual growth and development; early adverse experiences in family environments not only affect cognitive development but also impair emotional processing capacity [39]. Individuals with higher levels of alexithymia exhibit greater difficulty in emotional identification and regulation, consequently displaying increased aggressive tendencies [15]. For juvenile offenders, their early negative experiences have multiple effects on themselves. In terms of emotion, it increases the risk of emotional expression disorder, which has no negligible consequences for adolescents in the critical stage of development and will increase the occurrence of various internal and external problems. These results suggest that interventions targeting emotional competence, such as positive psychology approaches or specialized group therapy, may effectively reduce alexithymia and associated behavioral problems.

Notably, our findings supported a serial mediation model wherein executive function and alexithymia sequentially mediated the relationship between childhood maltreatment and aggressive behavior. This pattern aligns with the GAM [28], which conceptualizes aggressive behavior as resulting from the interaction of situational factors and internal states. In this study, childhood abuse, as an important situational variable, may influence internal states, including cognitive, emotional, and arousal processes, and executive function and alexithymia, representing key cognitive and emotional components, in turn, contribute to social functioning impairments and the emergence of aggressive behavior. Neurocognitive evidence indicates overlapping deficits in attention, inhibition, and working memory among individuals with both executive dysfunction and alexithymia [57, 58]. As executive function represents a core component of higher-order cognition, its impairment may directly compromise emotional expression and processing, contributing to alexithymia development [20, 42], which in turn leads to a variety of externalizing problem behaviors, such as aggression [3]. Juvenile offenders constitute a clinically vulnerable adolescent population characterized by significant emotional and safety needs deficits stemming from early adverse experiences of abuse and neglect. These developmental disruptions frequently result in neurocognitive impairments that compromise emotional processing capacities, including affecting recognition and self-regulation mechanisms. Such deficits predispose this population to employ dysfunctional coping strategies when confronted with environmental stressors, thereby substantially elevating their risk for maladaptive behavioral outcomes, particularly aggression and externalizing behaviors. The lack of cognitive development affects the development of their emotional ability, and they are more likely to adopt negative coping styles when facing external environmental stimuli, increasing the risk of aggressive behavior.

Moreover, although the present study demonstrated that executive function and alexithymia sequentially mediated the relationship between childhood maltreatment and aggression in juvenile offenders, some of the individual pathways were relatively weak (P < 0.05). First, the association between childhood maltreatment and alexithymia was modest, which may be partly explained by its stronger links to the negative emotion dimensions of the PAQ (e.g., N-DIF, N-DDF) [7], while its influence on other dimensions appears relatively limited. When total scores are employed in analysis, such dimensional differences may be averaged out, thereby reducing the overall effect size. In addition, adolescents are in a critical period of emotional development, during which emotional recognition and expression skills are not yet fully matured [48]. Thus, the impact of childhood maltreatment on alexithymia may not be fully manifested and may also be masked by contextual factors such as family and peer environments. Second, the effects of the “executive function → aggression” and “alexithymia → aggression” pathways were relatively weak, possibly because their associations are influenced by moderating variables such as self-control or impulsivity [9, 25], which may attenuate their predictive power for aggression. Additionally, different dimensions of aggression rely on cognitive and emotional mechanisms to varying degrees. For example, the physical and verbal aggression represent overt behavioral manifestations and may be more strongly influenced by social context or external motivations [13, 50], whereas anger and hostility represent emotional and cognitive components of aggression and are more closely tied to maladaptive emotion regulation [31]. When these components are combined into a total score, the specific effects of executive function or alexithymia on particular aggression dimensions may be diluted, which could explain the relatively weak overall effects observed in the model. Nevertheless, although some effects were relatively weak, this study offers meaningful insights into the interplay between childhood maltreatment, executive function, alexithymia, and aggression, with all measurement indices meeting psychometric standards ensuring both theoretical relevance and practical applicability. Therefore, in the prevention and intervention of aggressive behavior among juvenile offenders, it is essential to adopt a comprehensive approach that considers these multiple influences, such as Multisystemic Therapy (MST). Strengthening family support, promoting collaboration in school settings, and engaging community resources can foster the development of executive function and emotional regulation, which in turn may reduce aggressive tendencies and improve psychosocial outcomes.

Limitations and future directions

This study examined the role of executive function and alexithymia in the relationship between childhood maltreatment and aggressive behavior among juvenile offenders. However, as a cross-sectional study relying on self-reported data, which may increase social desirability bias and pose potential challenges to data quality, as adolescents may underreport experiences of maltreatment or downplay aggressive tendencies. Future studies should adopt longitudinal or experimental designs that would establish causal relationships, and incorporate additional data sources (e.g., judicial records, institutional files) to cross-validate self-reported information, thereby enhancing the reliability and validity of findings. Additionally, the study focused exclusively on juvenile offenders from F Province and did not include a control group of non-delinquent adolescents, which limits the generalizability and external validity of the findings. Future research should adopt multi-regional and multi-group designs and include both delinquent and non-delinquent populations, in order to validate the mechanisms through which childhood maltreatment influences aggressive behavior. While executive function and alexithymia were selected as mediating variables, other psychological factors (e.g., impulsivity, emotion regulation, or social cognition) were not included. Further studies should explore the complex mechanisms between multiple psychological variables and childhood maltreatment to inform more comprehensive prevention and intervention strategies for adolescent aggression. Moreover, this study did not control for potential confounding variables, such as crime type, educational background, or family socioeconomic status, which may influence the associations between childhood maltreatment, executive function, alexithymia, and aggression. Future research should incorporate more comprehensive demographic and psychological measures to refine theoretical models and enhance their predictive validity.

Conclusion

The present study highlights the complex mediation of cognitive and emotional factors in the relationship between childhood maltreatment and aggressive behavior among juvenile offenders. Specifically, our findings demonstrate that executive dysfunction and alexithymia serve as critical mediating mechanisms underlying this association. These results have important implications for developing targeted interventions to reduce recidivism in juvenile offenders and prevent at-risk adolescents from engaging in delinquent behavior.
