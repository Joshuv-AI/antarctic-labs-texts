Protocol for preparation and execution of collagen plug contraction assay to measure fibroblast contractility induced by growth factors
Authors: Sahi Nandini Wuppukondur, Jinal C. Patel, Vivien J. Coulson-Thomas, Tarsis F. Gesteira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present an in vitro assay for quantifying fibroblast-mediated contractile dynamics using a three-dimensional collagen plug. We describe steps for suspending fibroblasts in neutralized collagen type I, polymerizing to form uniform collagen plugs, and stimulating contraction by growth factors to induce myofibroblast activation. We detail procedures for measuring contraction of the collagen plug by quantifying its area and assessing matrix remodeling. This protocol allows for the screening and assessment of potential therapeutic targets for preventing fibrosis.

Before you begin

Fibroblasts-mediated contraction of the extracellular matrix (ECM) is a hallmark of wound healing, fibrosis, and 3D collagen plug systems. The 3D collagen plug system was first established by Bell et al.1 and later refined by Ehrlich and Moyer, 2013 and Ngo et al., 2006.2,3 Hasegawa et al. 2005 demoSnstrated that blocking TGFβ signaling with small-molecule inhibitors such as SB-4315424 reduces TGFβ1-induced collagen gel contraction; however, those findings were generated using receptor-level inhibition rather than direct ligand neutralization.

In our assay, TGFβ1 (1 ng/mL) induced a 75% reduction in plug area, reflecting a stronger contractile response than previously reported, where 10 ng/mL TGFβ1 produced only ∼65% contraction. Furthermore, to our knowledge, no prior study has evaluated Fresolimumab in a 3D collagen contraction system. While our TGFβ1-induced contraction values are consistent with established reports, our dataset provides the first quantitative benchmark for ligand-level TGFβ neutralization in a 3D collagen plug assay. Here, we present a detailed protocol for a three-dimensional (3D) collagen contraction plug assay using NIH3T3 fibroblasts. Building on these foundational approaches, the protocol incorporates a pre-assay NaOH titration step to normalize collagen stiffness, standardized preparation of collagen and cell suspensions, and defined serum conditions to ensure reproducibility across replicates. The workflow further integrates an optimized digital image acquisition setup with a quantitative ImageJ/Fiji analysis pipeline, enabling reproducible and interpretable measurements of fibroblast-mediated contraction in a controlled 3D environment.

Innovation

Traditional collagen contraction assays often show substantial variability due to inconsistent collagen neutralization, non-uniform collagen concentration across replicates, and mechanical disturbances that alter the plug shape. These limitations compromise the reproducibility of measurements of fibroblast-mediated extracellular matrix remodeling and hinder comparison across independent experiments. Existing 3D approaches, such as scratch-wound or monolayer-contraction assays, provide only limited insight into cell-matrix interactions and do not capture the biomechanical constraints of a 3D environment. This protocol overcomes these limitations through a customized pre-assay NaOH titration that standardizes collagen stiffness and through strict normalization of collagen concentration, cell suspension preparation, and serum conditions, enabling precise experimental control and reproducibility.

Step-by-step method details

This protocol was optimized using rat-tail type I collagen (EMD Millipore, SKU: C3867). Collagen source and species can influence fibril assembly kinetics, viscosity, and polymerization behavior. Therefore, rat-tail collagen I is considered a non-substitutable reagent for this workflow unless users independently validate alternative collagen sources for equivalent pH, viscosity, and polymerization characteristics.

Expected outcomes

This Protocol is expected to generate a time-resolved dataset documenting the contraction of the fibroblast-seeded collagen plugs under defined culture conditions. Anticipate a measurable reduction in plug surface area over the course of the assay, with pronounced changes typically occurring within the first 48 h. Collagen plugs maintained in the control condition, generally retain their initial size with the minimal spontaneous contraction, whereas plugs exposed to induction factors are expected to show a clear, quantifiable reduction in plug area. When test variables are included, their effects can be visualized as either attenuation, enhancement, or no change in contraction relative to induced controls. Images at each time points (0 h, 24 h, 48 h) should provide sufficient contrast to allow reliable segmentation and area quantification. Across independent experiments (n=3), the assay generally produces consistent trends, with SEM values typically within 5–12% of the mean, depending on the treatment condition. The final dataset usually consists of series of standardized images and corresponding quantitative measurements of plug area over time, enabling comparison across experimental conditions and supporting downstream analysis.

Limitations

Environmental and mechanical factors should be controlled as much as possible to ensure reliable comparison across wells and time points. Variations in incubator conditions—including temperature stability, humidity, and CO2 levels—can influence cell behavior and alter contraction kinetics. Mechanical disturbances, such as tilting or uneven placement of the plate, may shift or deform collagen plugs, leading to inconsistent measurements. Handling steps involving media removal, reagent addition, or plate transfer must be performed gently, as collagen plugs are highly sensitive to shear forces. Consistency in imaging is also essential; maintaining a constant zoom level during image acquisition and applying a uniform ImageJ scale before measuring plug area helps minimize measurement variability.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Inconsistent or incomplete plug polymerization (Related to step 1–5, steps 11–13).

Collagen plug polymerization can vary when the stock material is old, nearing expiration, or has been stored for an extended period after opening, which reduces its ability to form a stable gel. Inconsistent pH adjustment—often due to degraded or expired NaOH—can further impair polymerization efficiency. These factors introduce variability in plug structure and stiffness, ultimately affecting downstream contraction measurements and comparisons across wells. To ensure reliable and reproducible plug formation, consider the following solutions.

Potential solution

•Use fresh collagen stock: Collagen polymerization efficiency declines with age. Avoid repeated freeze–thaw cycles and replace stocks that have been stored for prolonged periods after opening.•Track collagen age after opening: Record the date each collagen vial is opened and establish a defined usage window to prevent relying on degraded material.•Prepare NaOH fresh: Degraded or expired NaOH leads to inconsistent pH neutralization. Prepare NaOH immediately before use when possible and verify that the neutralization step is performed consistently.•Maintain cold conditions during preparation: Keep all components on ice throughout the dilution and mixing steps to prevent premature polymerization, which can lead to uneven or incomplete plug formation.

Problem 2

Variability in contraction across wells (Related to the step-by-step method details section, steps 10–14).

Variability in collagen plug contraction can arise when the final collagen concentration differs across wells due to pipetting inaccuracies or incorrect dilution. Differences in media composition—particularly serum concentration—can further alter cell behavior and contraction kinetics. In addition, uneven distribution of cells within the collagen mixture can lead to inconsistent plug density and variable contraction profiles. These factors, taken together, reduce reproducibility and complicate comparisons across wells and time points. To ensure consistent, interpretable contraction measurements, consider the following solutions.

Potential solution

•Improve pipetting accuracy: When working with viscous collagen solutions, use a positive-displacement pipette to minimize volume errors and reduce well-to-well variability.•Verify collagen dilution volumes: Prepare a clear concentration-dilution plan and confirm all volumes before assembling the collagen mixture to ensure consistent final concentrations.•Ensure uniform cell distribution: Mix the collagen–cell suspension gently but thoroughly to achieve an even distribution of cells across all wells, which supports uniform plug formation and contraction behavior.
