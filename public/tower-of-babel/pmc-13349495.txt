Outpatient Guidance and 1-Year Physical Function After Mitral Valve Transcatheter Edge-to-Edge Repair
Authors: Yuri Kamijo, Yoshifumi Abe, Shiori Mori, Takuya Miyahara, Hideki Tanaka, Mitsutoshi Akiho, Masahiko Kimura, Masahiko Asami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEvidence for structured post-discharge programs to improve long-term physical function and activities of daily living (ADL) after transcatheter mitral valve edge-to-edge repair is limited.Methods and ResultsIn a single-center cohort, patients receiving outpatient guidance at 3 and 6 months (n=34) were compared with those receiving none (n=11). At 1 year, the guidance group had higher SPPB (12 vs. 8, P=0.034), higher Barthel Index (100 vs. 95, P=0.008), and shorter 5-STS time (8.1 vs. 14.5 s, P=0.002).ConclusionsGuidance was associated with better 1-year function/ADL, but findings are hypothesis-generating.

Central Figure

Exercise therapy and cardiac rehabilitation improve outcomes in cardiovascular disease,1 and maintaining function is particularly important in older heart failure patients.2 Transcatheter mitral valve edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER) is increasingly used for severe mitral regurgitation (MR) in older, comorbid patients.3 Because hospitalization is short after M-TEER, sustained exercise and self-management after discharge may be important to maintain functional activities of daily living (ADL), yet evidence for structured post-discharge programs remains limited.

We hypothesized that standardized outpatient guidance at 3 and 6 months would be associated with better 1-year physical function and ADL. We performed an exploratory single-center retrospective cohort study of consecutive patients undergoing M-TEER with MitraClip between August 2019 and January 2023. Of 108 patients, 45 with assessments at discharge and 1 year were analyzed. Patients were classified as guidance (both sessions, n=34) or non-guidance (neither, n=11). Allocation reflected operational factors (including temporary COVID-19 service suspension) and patient preference (Figure A). Guidance was delivered by physiotherapists specialized in cardiovascular rehabilitation using a standardized framework: feedback on functional assessments, individualized exercise instruction, and lifestyle/self-care counseling, supported by written materials. Sessions were held in the rehabilitation room (30–40 min). Exercise advice followed cardiovascular rehabilitation guidance and typically combined balance, resistance, and aerobic training tailored to functional status.4

The primary endpoint was 1-year Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) total score; secondary endpoints were 1-year Barthel Index (BI) and Five-Times Sit-to-Stand (5-STS) time. Exploratory endpoints included falls and selected strength/mobility measures. Functional/ADL measures were summarized preprocedure (where available), and at discharge, and 1 year (Figure B); falls were ascertained at 1 year. Continuous variables were compared with the Mann-Whitney U test and categorical variables with chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test; P values were two-sided and nominal without multiplicity adjustment.

Among the analyzed patients (median age 81 years; 56% male), secondary MR predominated (91.1%), and the median Clinical Frailty Scale score was 3. Available clinical background factors were broadly similar between the analyzed (n=45) and excluded patients (n=63), although functional status could not be assessed in the excluded patients. Between groups, clinical characteristics were broadly similar; the guidance group had a shorter preprocedure 5-STS time (10.1 [7.8–11.1] vs. 10.9 [10.7–12.5] s, P=0.029) and tended to have lower stroke volume index and milder residual MR after the procedure (not statistically significant).

At 1 year, the guidance group showed higher SPPB (12 [11–12] vs. 8 [4–9], P=0.034), higher BI (100 [100–100] vs. 95 [93–100], P=0.008), and shorter 5-STS time (8.1 [8.0–10.0] vs. 14.5 [11.3–16.8] s, P=0.002). Grip strength, knee extension strength, and gait speed did not differ significantly, and falls were numerically less frequent in the guidance group (Figure B).

Two outpatient sessions may help maintain function after M-TEER. Scheduled follow-up with objective feedback may reinforce self-care and exercise. However, causal inference is limited by the non-random allocation, baseline functional imbalance, and exclusions (selection/survivor bias); residual confounding also cannot be excluded. We lacked objective data on exercise adherence/physical activity, and the small non-guidance group limits precision; P values are nominal without multiplicity adjustment. Potential ceiling effects and the single-center design limit generalizability. If confirmed, brief guidance sessions could be a pragmatic addition to routine follow-up after M-TEER.

In conclusion, outpatient disease-management guidance at 3 and 6 months after M-TEER was associated with better 1-year physical function and ADL, but prospective confirmation is required.
