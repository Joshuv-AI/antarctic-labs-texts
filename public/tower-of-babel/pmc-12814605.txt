The SERCA–PLN–DWORF axis in cardiometabolic disease: mechanisms and therapeutic perspectives
Authors: Ok-Hee Kim, Seung Wan Noh, Jun-Su Choi, YunJae Jung, Byung-Chul Oh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntracellular calcium (Ca2+) homeostasis is a central determinant of cardiometabolic physiology, integrating excitation–contraction coupling, metabolic signaling, and stress adaptation across multiple organs. The sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+-ATPase (SERCA), regulated by the micropeptides phospholamban (PLN) and dwarf open reading frame (DWORF), governs ER/SR Ca2+ reuptake and thereby shapes Ca2+-dependent signaling dynamics. Dysregulation of the SERCA–PLN–DWORF axis is increasingly recognized as a shared pathogenic mechanism in type 2 diabetes–related complications, including diabetic cardiomyopathy and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), where reduced SERCA2a activity prolongs diastolic Ca2+ clearance and promotes calcineurin–NFAT activation and mitochondrial Ca2⁺ overload. In the liver, loss of SERCA2b activity promotes chronic ER stress, Ca2+–phosphoinositide complex formation, insulin resistance, and fibrotic activation, thereby linking Ca2+ dysregulation to progressive metabolic liver injury in metabolic dysfunction–associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) and steatohepatitis (MASH). These observations position Ca2+ dysregulation as a unifying mechanism across the cardiometabolic disease continuum, spanning myocardial dysfunction, systemic insulin resistance, and progressive fatty liver disease. Therapeutic strategies targeting the SERCA–PLN–DWORF axis, including SERCA activators, PLN-directed antisense oligonucleotides, DWORF gene therapy, and CRISPR-based modulation, have demonstrated efficacy in preclinical models by improving Ca2⁺ handling and alleviating metabolic or contractile stress. Further studies are required to determine the translational feasibility, long-term safety, and optimal patient subsets for SERCA-targeted interventions in cardiometabolic disease.

Introduction

Intracellular calcium (Ca2⁺) is a universal second messenger that regulates diverse cellular processes, including contraction, secretion, gene expression, and metabolic regulation. Precise regulation of Ca2⁺ dynamics is essential for proper organ function, particularly in excitable tissues such as cardiac and skeletal muscle, and is increasingly recognized as critical in metabolically active non-excitable cells like hepatocytes [1, 2]. Central to this regulation is the sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2⁺-ATPase (SERCA), which actively sequesters cytosolic Ca2⁺ into the endoplasmic or sarcoplasmic reticulum (ER/SR), thereby terminating cytosolic Ca2⁺ signals and refilling luminal stores [3–8].

SERCA function is finely modulated by small transmembrane peptides, known as regulins, including phospholamban (PLN), and the recently discovered dwarf open reading frame (DWORF) [9–11]. These micropeptides modify SERCA’s affinity for Ca2⁺ and its enzymatic kinetics in a context-dependent manner, enabling tissues to dynamically adjust Ca2⁺ reuptake in response to physiological demands or stress signals [12–15]. PLN, predominantly expressed in cardiac and slow-twitch skeletal muscle, binds and inhibits SERCA2a when dephosphorylated, while phosphorylation at serine 16 (Ser16) or threonine 17 (Thr17) relieves this inhibition during β-adrenergic stimulation [9, 12, 16, 17]. Conversely, DWORF acts as an endogenous activator by displacing PLN and stabilizing SERCA in its active conformation, enhancing Ca2⁺ transport independent of phosphorylation status [10, 14, 18].

Dysregulation of this micropeptide-mediated SERCA control is a shared pathogenic feature in multiple diseases. In heart failure, elevated PLN expression combined with reduced phosphorylation impairs SERCA2a function, leading to delayed cytosolic Ca2⁺ clearance, decreased contractility, and arrhythmogenesis. Concurrently, DWORF expression is downregulated, worsening this functional imbalance [13, 19–21]. In non-contractile tissues such as the liver, SERCA2b is the predominant isoform. Although PLN is minimally expressed under physiological conditions, recent studies implicate PLN and PLN-like peptides (such as endoregulin [ELN] and another-regulin [ALN]) in modulating ER Ca2⁺ handling in hepatic stellate cells and hepatocytes, particularly under metabolic stress and fibrotic remodeling [22–28].

Loss of SERCA2b expression or function in the liver leads to ER Ca2⁺ depletion, chronic unfolded protein response (UPR) activation, mitochondrial Ca2⁺ overload, and inflammatory signaling cascades, driving progression of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) and fibrosis [3, 29–33]. Analogous defects in the cardiac SERCA2a and PLN balance are central to heart failure pathogenesis, where emerging gene therapies and small-molecule interventions targeting this axis show promising preclinical and early clinical results [34–38].

Given the conserved yet tissue-specific roles of the SERCA–PLN–DWORF system, a comprehensive understanding of its molecular architecture, regulatory dynamics, and pathophysiological consequences is essential. This review aimed to integrate mechanistic insights from muscle physiology, liver metabolism, and disease models to elucidate the central role of this axis in Ca2⁺ homeostasis. Furthermore, it aimed to discuss current and emerging therapeutic approaches—ranging from small-molecule activators and gene therapy to micropeptide modulation—that harness this knowledge to restore Ca2⁺ balance and mitigate organ dysfunction in cardiometabolic disease.

Overview of intracellular Ca2⁺ signaling and regulatory networks

Intracellular Ca2⁺ signaling plays a fundamental role in regulating diverse physiological processes, including muscle contraction, neurotransmission, hormone secretion, metabolism, immune responses, and gene expression [5, 39, 40]. The steep gradient between extracellular (approximately 1–2 mM) and intracellular (approximately 50–100 nM) Ca2⁺ concentrations provides a rapid and tightly regulated signaling mechanism, enabling cells to respond to external stimuli with precision and temporal specificity [3, 41, 42]. This homeostasis is maintained by an elaborate network of Ca2⁺ transport systems distributed across the plasma membrane, ER, mitochondria, and Golgi apparatus [3, 4]. Key regulators include plasma membrane Ca2⁺ ATPases (PMCAs), SERCAs, and the mitochondrial Na⁺/Ca2⁺/Li⁺ exchanger (NCLX), which extrude Ca2⁺ from the cytosol to restore basal Ca2⁺ levels following activation [22, 39, 42, 43]. Within the ER, Ca2⁺ depletion is detected by stromal interaction molecules STIM1 and STIM2, which translocate to ER–plasma membrane junctions and activate ORAI1 channels and Transient Receptor Potential Canonical channels. This process mediates store-operated Ca2⁺ entry (SOCE), a key mechanism for replenishing ER Ca2⁺ stores (Fig. 1, schematic of intracellular Ca2⁺ signaling and inter-organelle communication) [44–47].

Intracellular Ca2⁺ signaling is typically initiated by the activation of G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) in response to hormones such as glucagon, epinephrine, or norepinephrine. Gq-coupled receptors primarily mediate Ca2⁺ release by activating phospholipase C (PLC), which hydrolyzes phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PI(4,5)P2) into diacylglycerol (DAG) and inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP3). IP3 then binds to IP3Rs on the ER membrane, triggering Ca2⁺ efflux into the cytosol [48]. In contrast, Gαs signaling regulates cAMP/PKA pathways that indirectly modulate Ca2⁺ handling—for example, by phosphorylating Ca2⁺ channels or sensitizing downstream effectors—rather than serving as a primary IP3 generator [40, 49, 50]. In addition, IP₃ can be sequentially phosphorylated into various inositol phosphates (IPs) and pyrophosphates (PP-IPs), which provide additional layers of metabolic and stress-related regulation, although their contribution to direct Ca2⁺ signaling is secondary [51]. The spatial and temporal patterns of Ca2⁺ signals—ranging from localized “puffs” to global cytosolic waves—are shaped by stimulus strength and the availability of phosphoinositide cofactors such as PIP2, which primarily regulate PLC activity and certain TRP channels, indirectly influencing Ca2⁺ signaling dynamics rather than directly modulating IP3R sensitivity [52–54].

Elevated cytosolic Ca2⁺ levels activate various Ca2-sensitive signaling proteins, notably calmodulin (CaM), which undergoes conformational change upon Ca2⁺ binding to its EF-hand domains and subsequently activates a cascade of kinases and phosphatases [55, 56]. This includes Ca2⁺/calmodulin-dependent kinases (CaMKs), protein kinase C (PKC), and the phosphatase calcineurin, each of which modulates specific transcriptional programs. As an example of tissue-specific Ca2⁺-dependent feedback regulation, phospholamban (PLN) in cardiac muscle binds to the transmembrane domain of SERCA and inhibits its activity in the unphosphorylated state. This inhibition is relieved upon phosphorylation by PKA (Ser16) or Ca2⁺/calmodulin-dependent kinase II (CaMKII; Thr17), thereby enhancing SERCA-mediated Ca2⁺ reuptake and promoting efficient cardiac contractility and relaxation during β-adrenergic stimulation [57–61]. For instance, CaMKs phosphorylate transcription factors such as Forkhead box O (FOXO), cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB), p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38 MAPK), nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells, and c-Jun N-terminal kinase to influence metabolic, inflammatory, and survival pathways [55, 62]. Simultaneously, calcineurin dephosphorylates transcription factors including CREB-regulated transcription coactivator 2 (CRTC2), myocyte enhancer factor-2 (MEF2), nuclear factor of activated T cells c, and transcription factor EB, enabling their nuclear translocation and activation of genes involved in metabolism, autophagy, lysosomal biogenesis, and immunity [63–65]. In addition, PKC activation is triggered by the concurrent rise in Ca2⁺ and DAG, which bind to its C2 and C1 domains, respectively, promoting translocation to the plasma membrane and subsequent phosphorylation of substrates, including MAPKs, IκB, vitamin D receptor, and calpain [39, 66, 67]. Thus, intracellular Ca2⁺ functions as a versatile and highly integrated second messenger that governs numerous aspects of cellular physiology and inter-organelle communication. In this review, we focus specifically on the functional regulation and pathological dysregulation of the SERCA–PLN–DWORF axis across tissues, highlighting its central role in Ca2⁺ reuptake and its therapeutic relevance in metabolic, cardiovascular, and fibrotic diseases.

Tissue-specific SERCA regulation

Three major isoforms of Ca2⁺-ATPase (SERCA) are encoded by distinct genes (ATP2A1–3), and exhibit tissue-specific expression patterns critical for Ca2⁺ handling in diverse cellular contexts [68–73]. SERCA2a is the predominant isoform in cardiac muscle and slow-twitch skeletal fibers, optimized for rhythmic Ca2⁺ cycling required during contraction–relaxation. The ubiquitously expressed SERCA2b variant, derived from the same gene as SERCA2a but possessing a longer luminal C-terminal tail, is the principal isoform in non-excitable tissues such as liver, where it maintains ER Ca2⁺ levels critical for metabolic and secretory functions (Fig. 2A) [74, 75]. All SERCA isoforms share a conserved molecular structure comprising cytosolic nucleotide-binding, phosphorylation, and actuator domains linked to a transmembrane channel that mediates Ca2⁺ transport [9, 76, 77].

SERCA function is fine-tuned by small transmembrane micropeptides termed regulins, including PLN, and DWORF, which exhibit tissue- and isoform-specific expression patterns [78]. PLN, a 52-residue protein, is highly expressed in ventricular myocardium and slow oxidative skeletal fibers, where it regulates SERCA2a [28]. DWORF, a 35-residue muscle-specific peptide, is detectable in cardiac and slow skeletal muscle and functions as a direct SERCA activator. In cardiac tissue, PLN is expressed at high levels, while DWORF is expressed at lower levels but may be upregulated under stress or via gene therapy [13, 79]. In non-excitable tissues like the liver, SERCA2b is the dominant isoform. PLN and DWORF are not robustly expressed under physiological conditions; however, PLN messenger RNA (mRNA) has been detected in hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), reported in some datasets but with functional significance remaining unclear [24], and DWORF expression is limited to trace transcriptomic signals without confirmed protein expression or functional relevance. Other SERCA-interacting regulins, such as ELN and ALN, have been identified in endothelial and epithelial cells and may functionally substitute for PLN in non-muscle tissues (Fig. 2A) [13, 22, 25, 78].

PLN modulates SERCA2a activity through direct interaction with its transmembrane domain, primarily M3. In its unphosphorylated state, PLN binds to SERCA and stabilizes the E2 conformation, reducing the pump’s apparent affinity for Ca2⁺ and thereby slowing Ca2⁺ uptake into the SR [9, 80, 81]. X-ray crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy studies confirm that PLN binding traps SERCA in the Ca2⁺-unbound state, effectively acting as a brake on cardiac relaxation (Fig. 2B) [10]. This inhibition is reversed upon phosphorylation of PLN at Ser16 [82] by PKA [82] or Thr17 by CaMKII, [57, 58], which shifts PLN into a dynamic or dissociated conformation, freeing SERCA to bind and transport Ca2⁺ more efficiently [12, 16, 83]. This regulatory phosphorylation is integral to the β-adrenergic response, enhancing both inotropy and lusitropy during sympathetic stimulation.

In contrast to these inhibitory peptides, DWORF acts as an endogenous activator of SERCA by occupying the same binding groove on the transmembrane domain and stabilizing the Ca2⁺-bound E1 conformation [10, 13, 14, 84]. This single-pass α-helical peptide with a helix–linker–helix structure, stabilizes the Ca2-bound E1 conformation of SERCA, facilitating enhanced turnover and increased Ca2⁺ reuptake (Fig. 2A). DWORF not only displaces PLN from SERCA but also appears to exert allosteric effects that further enhance SERCA pump kinetics [10, 13]. Unlike its inhibitory counterparts, DWORF remains monomeric in the membrane and is the only known endogenous micropeptide that stimulates SERCA activity. DWORF is part of a growing family of small transmembrane peptides (collectively termed “regulins”) that modulate SERCA. Other members include MLN, ELN, and ALN, each predominantly expressed in specific tissues [13, 14, 27, 78, 85]. MLN, for instance, is expressed in skeletal muscle and acts as an inhibitor analogous to PLN, while ELN and ALN are found in non-muscle tissues (such as endothelial cells) and appear to have PLN-like functions. Uniquely, DWORF is the only known endogenous peptide that activates SERCA (Fig. 2A) [11, 14]. Its ability to enhance Ca2⁺ transport has made it a compelling target for therapeutic exploration.

The functional consequences of this regulatory balance are tissue-dependent and pathophysiologically relevant. In cardiac and skeletal muscle, phosphorylation of PLN or the presence of DWORF promotes rapid Ca2⁺ sequestration, enabling efficient relaxation and contractile performance [10, 12]. In contrast, excess PLN activity delays Ca2⁺ reuptake, predisposing to diastolic dysfunction, arrhythmogenic Ca2⁺ waves [13, 86]. In hepatocytes, high SERCA2b activity sustains luminal ER Ca2⁺ concentrations essential for protein folding and secretory capacity; its inhibition by PLN, ER stress, or lipotoxic conditions leads to UPR activation, mitochondrial Ca2⁺ overload, and hepatocellular injury [29, 87–90]. PKA signaling has been associated with reduced fibrogenic activation, potentially through Ca2⁺-dependent mechanisms rather than direct PLN modulation [91, 92]. Overall, the balance between SERCA inhibition and activation, whether through PLN, SLN, DWORF, or other regulins, emerges as a central determinant of Ca2⁺ signaling fidelity and organ function, providing a framework for therapeutic targeting in metabolic, cardiovascular, and fibrotic diseases (Fig. 2B).

Dysregulation of the SERCA2a–PLN–DWORF signaling axis in heart failure (HF)

HF is a clinical syndrome of inadequate cardiac output due to impaired pump function, often following chronic stress such as hypertension, myocardial infarction, or genetic cardiomyopathies. A defining characteristic of the failing myocardium is impaired intracellular Ca2⁺ handling, especially during excitation–contraction coupling, in contrast to the tightly regulated and efficient Ca2⁺ cycling observed in healthy myocardium (Fig. 3) [9, 12, 16, 86, 93]. In cardiomyocytes from failing hearts, studies have consistently found reduced SERCA2a expression and activity, along with elevated PLN levels or activity, shifting the balance toward suppressed Ca2⁺ reuptake [17, 37, 38, 43, 86, 94]. The result is a slower and incomplete removal of Ca2⁺ from the cytosol during diastole, leading to diastolic dysfunction (impaired relaxation) and a lower SR Ca2⁺ store for subsequent systoles, which contributes to reduced contractile force (systolic dysfunction) (Fig. 3B) [9].

Mechanistically, in many forms of HF (especially non-ischemic or genetic cardiomyopathies), PLN remains in a largely dephosphorylated (active inhibitory) state under baseline conditions, because β-adrenergic signaling is blunted and phosphatase activity is often elevated [12, 95]. This means the inhibition of PLN on SERCA2a is not adequately released, even when the failing heart attempts to increase output. Supporting this, failing human hearts have shown not only a higher PLN/SERCA protein ratio but also lower PLN phosphorylation at Ser16 [12, 37, 38, 91]. The net effect is chronic depression of SERCA2a function, contributing to prolonged Ca2⁺ overload, secondary activation of Na⁺/Ca2⁺ exchanger (which can promote arrhythmias), and energetic inefficiency (Fig. 3B) [96]. A dramatic example of the pathological role of PLN is the hereditary PLN R14del mutation, which produces a mutant PLN that cannot be properly phosphorylated and tends to form toxic aggregates [19, 20, 34, 97, 98]. Patients with this mutation develop lethal dilated cardiomyopathy at a young age, underscoring how overactive PLN inhibition of SERCA2a (and associated ER stress from PLN aggregation) can alone drive HF. Mouse models of PLN R14del and other PLN overexpression similarly exhibit severe contractile dysfunction, arrhythmias, and ventricular dilation [19]. Conversely, partial reduction of PLN (haploinsufficiency) or PKA-mediated hyperphosphorylation of PLN can ameliorate contractile deficits in various HF models [16, 93].

DWORF’s role in the heart has also gained attention. In healthy myocardium, DWORF modulates SERCA2a subtly under resting conditions. However, during heightened adrenergic drive (when PLN is phosphorylated), DWORF may bind SERCA2a and further accelerate Ca2⁺ uptake [28, 99, 100]. In HF, DWORF expression has been reported to be downregulated in several failing human heart samples and mouse HF models (Fig. 3B) [13, 21]. The loss of DWORF removes an important counterbalance to PLN. Reduced DWORF in HF may reflect aspects of fetal gene reprogramming (since DWORF is higher in adult heart and lower in fetal heart), which unfortunately leaves SERCA2a unopposed by inhibitors [13]. Indeed, transgenic DWORF overexpression in mice has been protective: when engineered mice with high cardiac DWORF were subjected to pressure overload or myocardial injury, they showed preserved ejection fraction and less hypertrophy/fibrosis compared to controls [11, 13]. DWORF presumably helped maintain SERCA activity in those stressed hearts, preventing the typical Ca2⁺ mishandling and pathologic remodeling of HF.

Another contributor to Ca2⁺ dysregulation in HF is hyperactivation of kinases such as CaMKII, which can phosphorylate not only PLN (at Thr17) [57, 58] but also the ryanodine receptor (RyR2) [83, 101], leading to SR Ca2⁺ leak. In the context of HF, chronic hyperphosphorylation of RyR2 renders the channel leaky, leading to SR Ca2⁺ depletion even in the setting of impaired Ca2⁺ reuptake. This pathological state creates a vicious cycle wherein SERCA2a must function against two opposing forces: the inhibitory brake imposed by PLN and continuous Ca2⁺ leak through destabilized RyR2 channels (Fig. 3B). Experimental PLN ablation in knockout mice substantially enhances myocardial contractility, underscoring the critical role of PLN in limiting SERCA2a efficiency; however, in the absence of RyR2 stabilization, such interventions may provoke spontaneous Ca2⁺ waves and arrhythmias due to unrestrained SR Ca2⁺ release [20]. Therefore, successful therapeutic strategies must balance improvements in Ca2⁺ reuptake with stabilization of SR Ca2⁺ release. Nonetheless, the net effect in PLN knockout mice is improved cardiac performance, and remarkably, mice completely lacking PLN are viable and have supernormal cardiac function with no HF, although in humans, complete PLN deficiency has been linked to cardiomyopathy, suggesting species differences and possible compensatory mechanisms in mice [98, 102]. These findings underscore that SERCA regulation and micropeptide function exhibit species-dependent variability, as rodents display higher Ca2⁺ cycling tolerance and compensatory reserve compared to humans. Therefore, translational extrapolation from murine to human systems requires careful validation using large-animal or humanized models. In summary, HF is fundamentally characterized by impaired intracellular Ca2⁺ cycling, driven by reduced SERCA2a expression or activity and excessive inhibition by PLN, particularly in its hypophosphorylated state. This dysfunction limits Ca2⁺ reuptake into the SR, resulting in depleted SR Ca2⁺ stores, weakened contractile force, and diastolic dysfunction. Moreover, insufficient SR Ca2⁺ buffering contributes to pro-arrhythmic spontaneous Ca2⁺ release events and activates pathological signaling cascades, including the calcineurin–nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT) axis and hypertrophic transcriptional programs. A critical contributor to this pathological state is the imbalance within the SERCA2a–PLN–DWORF regulatory axis: elevated PLN activity inhibits SERCA2a, while the counter-regulatory action of DWORF is often reduced or insufficient to restore pump function. Accordingly, emerging preclinical strategies have focused on rebalancing this axis, either by silencing or inhibiting PLN or by augmenting DWORF expression or mimetics, to reactivate SERCA2a, improve SR Ca2⁺ sequestration, and normalize cardiac Ca2⁺ handling and contractility.

Hepatic SERCA2b–PLN signaling axis in health and disease

In healthy hepatocytes, SERCA2b is the predominant SERCA isoform and plays a central role in maintaining intracellular Ca2⁺ homeostasis by actively transporting Ca2⁺ from the cytosol into the ER lumen [30, 31, 103]. This process supports protein folding, lipid metabolism, and metabolic signaling by preserving luminal Ca2⁺ levels. Under basal physiological conditions, the expression of PLN, a well-known SERCA inhibitor in cardiac and slow-twitch skeletal muscle [9], is low in the liver [104]. Comparative protein analyses have revealed that hepatic PLN expression is approximately 100-fold lower than in cardiac muscle [104], and Northern blot confirmation of PLN mRNA in the liver remains limited. However, single-cell RNA sequencing has reported PLN transcripts in subsets of HSCs, but the functional significance of this expression remains unconfirmed [24]. HSCs, as liver-resident pericytes, regulate extracellular matrix production and contractility in response to injury, processes that are highly dependent on transient cytosolic Ca2⁺ elevations [105]. SERCA2b in these cells is thought to buffer cytosolic Ca2⁺ following G protein-coupled agonist stimulation (such as endothelin-1, angiotensin II) and may attenuate persistent activation. Although the regulatory function of PLN in liver cells remains unconfirmed, other SERCA-inhibiting micropeptides such as ELN and ALN have been identified in non-muscle cells [22, 23, 25, 106]. These peptides are believed to interact with SERCA2b to modulate ER Ca2⁺ reuptake, suggesting a conserved micropeptide-mediated regulatory system for ER Ca2⁺ pumps in epithelial cells, including hepatocytes and HSCs (Fig. 4A).

In pathological conditions such as metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) and its progressive inflammatory form, metabolic-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), disruption of hepatic ER Ca2⁺ homeostasis emerges as a central pathophysiological mechanism [87, 107, 108]. Across diverse experimental models—including ob/ob mice, high-fat/high-sugar diet-fed mice, and the methionine/choline-deficient (MCD) diet-induced steatohepatitis model—a consistent and marked downregulation of SERCA2b expression and activity has been reported in hepatocytes [3, 31, 87, 109]. These alterations are accompanied by inactivation of key metabolic signaling pathways, particularly protein kinase B (AKT) and PKA, which are essential for insulin signaling and hepatic metabolic regulation [3, 4, 59, 109–111]. Catalucci et al. [112] demonstrated that Akt directly phosphorylates phospholamban (PLN) at Thr17, relieving its inhibition of SERCA2a and thereby enhancing Ca2⁺ reuptake and contractility. In transgenic mice expressing constitutively active Akt (Akt-E40K), improved Ca2⁺ handling occurred independently of CaMKII signaling, confirming a direct Akt–PLN interaction. This study provides key evidence linking insulin/IGF-1 signaling to SERCA2a-mediated Ca2⁺ cycling in the heart, supporting the concept that Akt inactivation may impair PLN phosphorylation and SERCA function in hepatocytes under lipotoxic stress. Mechanistically, chronic cytosolic Ca2⁺ overload, a hallmark of SERCA2b dysfunction, has been proposed to induce direct interaction of Ca2⁺ with phosphorylated headgroups of phosphoinositides (PIPs) such as phosphatidylinositol 3,4-bisphosphate (PI[3,4]P2), phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PI[4,5]P2), and phosphatidylinositol 3,4,5-trisphosphate (PI[3,4,5]P3), resulting in the formation of stable Ca2⁺–PIP complexes, a mechanism supported by experimental and modeling studies [110]. These complexes sequester PIPs into membrane microdomains and impair membrane recruitment of pleckstrin homology (PH) domain-containing proteins, including AKT, phospholipase C delta, and insulin receptor substrate 1, thereby disrupting PIP-dependent insulin signaling [4, 110]. This defect prevents the phosphorylation of PLN, allowing it to remain in its inhibitory state and chronically suppresses SERCA2b activity (Fig. 4B). The resulting impairment in ER Ca2⁺ reuptake leads to sustained cytosolic Ca2⁺ elevation and progressive ER Ca2⁺ depletion, disrupting intracellular Ca2⁺ dynamics and ER homeostasis [3, 4, 113].

As ER Ca2⁺ stores decline, the UPR is chronically activated, with upregulation of stress markers such as glucose-regulated protein 78/binding immunoglobulin protein (GRP78/BiP) and C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP), eventually triggering hepatocyte apoptosis and progressive hepatic injury [29, 30, 114, 115]. This maladaptive ER stress is particularly evident at mitochondria-associated ER membranes (MAMs), where SERCA2b levels are significantly reduced in fibrotic livers [29, 30, 116]. Loss of SERCA2b at MAMs disrupts ER-mitochondrial Ca2⁺ coupling, promotes mitochondrial Ca2⁺ overload, enhances reactive oxygen species production, and accelerates cell death (Fig. 4B) [31, 32, 117, 118]. In parallel, chronic activation of three UPR branches—protein kinase R–like ER kinase (PERK), inositol-requiring enzyme 1 (IRE1), and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6)—shifts from adaptive to deleterious, amplifying hepatocellular dysfunction under sustained metabolic stress [119–121]. Damaged hepatocytes release damage-associated molecular patterns [122, 123], which activate Kupffer cells and recruit pro-inflammatory immune cells, further exacerbating hepatic inflammation. Moreover, reduced ER luminal Ca2⁺ compromises lipid metabolic enzyme activity and impairs insulin signaling, thereby aggravating steatosis and promoting hepatic insulin resistance [3, 109, 110]. Thus, the sequential events triggered by SERCA2b dysfunction—Ca2⁺-PIP complex formation, insulin signaling disruption, prolonged cytosolic Ca2⁺ elevation, and UPR-mediated cellular injury—represent a unified mechanism linking Ca2⁺ dysregulation to the pathogenesis of MAFLD and MASH, particularly at the interface of ER stress, lipid accumulation, and inflammation (Fig. 4B).

Upon liver injury or stimulation by pro-fibrotic agonists such as serotonin (5-HT), endothelin-1, angiotensin II, and platelet-derived growth factor, HSCs undergo rapid and sustained elevations in cytosolic Ca2⁺ concentration [124]. Although quiescent HSCs display only transient and modest Ca2⁺ responses, activated HSCs, transdifferentiated into myofibroblast-like cells, acquire the capacity to generate prolonged Ca2⁺ transients essential for maintaining the fibrogenic phenotype. This transformation involves upregulation of Ca2⁺ signaling machinery, including GPCRs (such as 5-HT2A and 5-HT2B), ER IP3Rs, SERCA isoforms for Ca2⁺ reuptake, and Ca2-binding chaperones such as calreticulin and calnexin [125, 126]. Elevated cytosolic Ca2+ acts as a central intracellular signal that both initiates and sustains expression of fibrogenic genes, including those encoding extracellular matrix (ECM) components such as collagen type I [125, 127]. This process is tightly linked to Ca2-dependent activation of the TGF-β signaling, particularly via SMAD-mediated transcription, which promotes pathological ECM deposition and liver fibrosis [128].

In this context, SERCA2b plays a critical role in terminating cytosolic Ca2⁺ signals by resequestering Ca2⁺ into the ER. However, its function is susceptible to inhibition by PLN. Although PLN expression in hepatocytes is negligible under physiological conditions [104],], its possible expression in HSCs raises the hypothesis that PLN may theoretically inhibit SERCA2b and sustain cytosolic Ca2⁺ elevation, but this remains to be experimentally validated [125, 126]. Supporting this hypothesis, activation of the cAMP/PKA pathway has been shown to suppress HSC activation and fibrogenesis, potentially through mechanisms involving Ca2⁺ handling rather than direct PLN modulation (Fig. 4B) [91]. Further evidence comes from fibrotic liver models, where HSCs exhibit persistent SOCE and enhanced STIM1 activation, reflective of chronic ER Ca2⁺ depletion [129, 130]. Moreover, CISD2-deficient mice, lacking a SERCA2b-stabilizing factor, develop age-dependent non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) and liver fibrosis, underscoring the causal role of impaired SERCA2b function in hepatic fibrogenesis [131]. Notably, the consequences of hepatic Ca2⁺ dysregulation extend beyond the liver. In patients with cirrhosis, systemic manifestations such as cirrhotic cardiomyopathy are associated with abnormal myocardial PLN phosphorylation, suggesting a possible link whereby liver-derived inflammatory or endocrine mediators may influence SERCA–PLN signaling in the heart [132].

Disruption of ER Ca2⁺ homeostasis is also implicated in drug-induced liver injury. Pharmacological agents such as sorafenib, acetaminophen, and volatile anesthetics can impair ER Ca2⁺ balance either by directly inhibiting SERCA2b or inducing Ca2⁺ leakage, contributing to hepatocellular stress and death [133]. Similarly, herbal compounds like saikosaponins, known SERCA inhibitors, have been linked to hepatotoxicity and cardiotoxicity [30]. Importantly, restoring or enhancing SERCA2b function shows therapeutic potential. In MCD diet-fed mice, the augmenter of liver regeneration restored SERCA2b expression, preserved ER Ca2⁺ stores, and mitigated hepatic steatosis and apoptosis [30]. Likewise, the natural compound matrine increased SERCA2b expression in palmitate-challenged hepatocytes, reducing cytosolic Ca2⁺ overload and UPR activation [134]. Conversely, hepatic Serca2b knockdown in healthy mice induced steatosis, glucose intolerance, and ER stress, highlighting its essential role in hepatic metabolic integrity [135]. Sustained cytosolic Ca2⁺ overload in HSCs—driven by receptor activation, ER Ca2⁺ leakage, and SERCA2b inhibition—thus represents a central mechanism promoting HSC activation, ECM production, and fibrosis. SERCA2b dysfunction, whether through direct inhibition, regulatory suppression by PLN, or loss of stabilizing factors like CISD2, integrates ER stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and pro-fibrotic signaling. Therefore, restoring SERCA2b expression or activity, potentially alongside inhibition of negative regulators such as PLN, offers a compelling therapeutic strategy against fibrogenesis and progression of metabolic liver disease.

Targeting SERCA regulation: pharmacologic activation, PLN suppression, and gene-based upregulation

Therapeutic interventions targeting the PLN–SERCA–DWORF axis focus on restoring Ca2⁺ homeostasis through three principal strategies: direct pharmacologic activation of SERCA, suppression of PLN-mediated inhibition, and upregulation of SERCA expression (Fig. 5). Small-molecule SERCA activators, such as CDN1163, have demonstrated efficacy in hepatic models of fatty liver disease by restoring SERCA2b function, reducing ER stress, improving redox balance, and ameliorating systemic metabolic dysfunction [74, 87]. In the cardiac setting, istaroxime has shown similar benefit by directly activating SERCA2a independent of PLN phosphorylation, improving diastolic relaxation and cardiac output in patients with acute HF without inducing arrhythmias [36, 136]. Indirect pharmacological approaches, including atractylenolide II, have been reported to upregulate SERCA2b transcription via FXR activation, thereby reducing hepatic steatosis [29]. Complementing these therapeutic strategies, PLN inhibition has emerged as an effective approach to relieve SERCA2a suppression, particularly in hereditary cardiomyopathies caused by the pathogenic R14del mutation. The R14del variant results in the deletion of a conserved arginine residue adjacent to the cytoplasmic phosphorylation sites of PLN, abolishing its ability to be phosphorylated at Ser16 and Thr17. This renders PLN constitutively inhibitory toward SERCA2a and promotes the formation of aberrant pentamers and toxic oligomeric aggregates within the SR membrane, which exert dominant-negative effects on Ca2⁺ handling and protein homeostasis [19, 20]. Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) targeting PLN mRNA reduce the expression of both wild-type and mutant PLN, thereby resolving toxic aggregates, alleviating SERCA2a inhibition, and restoring physiological Ca2⁺ cycling—collectively leading to improved cardiac function and survival in preclinical mouse models of PLN-R14del cardiomyopathy. In parallel, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated allele-specific editing of the mutant PLN offers a conceptually curative genomic strategy that has shown efficacy in preclinical systems but has not yet reached clinical translation (Fig. 5) [34]. Additional methods—such as phosphomimetic PLN peptides [93], PP1 inhibition to sustain PLN phosphorylation, and monoclonal antibodies or intrabodies to disrupt SERCA–PLNbinding—are also under development. Although gene therapy with AAV1/SERCA2a in the CUPID trial demonstrated safety and suggested potential benefit rather than definitive efficacy due to vector delivery limitations, it confirmed the feasibility of SERCA overexpression in human HF [35]. In parallel, hepatic overexpression of SERCA2b has proven effective in alleviating ER stress and metabolic derangements in MAFLD models [30, 31], supporting the feasibility of liver-targeted SERCA gene therapy.

In contrast to strategies that activate or disinhibit SERCA, augmentation of DWORF represents a novel and mechanistically distinct approach. As a micropeptide that competes with PLN for SERCA binding, DWORF overexpression has been shown in cardiac models to enhance contractile function, suppress ventricular remodeling, and provide protection against HF progression in settings such as dilated cardiomyopathy and Duchenne muscular dystrophy [13, 84]. Its action is independent of β-adrenergic or kinase signaling, making it particularly attractive for end-stage or genetic cardiomyopathies where upstream signaling is compromised. Although DWORF therapy has not yet been explored in hepatic models, it remains a hypothetical approach that requires experimental validation; AAV-mediated DWORF delivery could conceivably enhance SERCA2b function and attenuate hepatic fibrosis if proven feasible. Peptide-based delivery remains technically challenging due to the transmembrane structure of DWORF; however, mRNA–lipid nanoparticle formulations are under investigation to enable transient, titratable expression. Importantly, DWORF overexpression in vivo has exhibited minimal off-target or pro-arrhythmic effects [34] (Fig. 5).

To complement the direct targeting of the SERCA–PLN–DWORF axis, several adjunctive strategies have been developed to further stabilize intracellular Ca2⁺ handling. Stabilization of RyR2 with rycal compounds reduces pathological SR Ca2⁺ leak and preserves SR Ca2⁺ stores, thereby potentiating the effects of SERCA activators. Other strategies, such as administration of exogenous Ca2⁺ buffers like parvalbumin or upregulation of endogenous chaperones including calreticulin, aim to reduce cytosolic Ca2⁺ overload, though they must be carefully balanced to avoid disrupting physiological signaling [20]. In hepatic stellate cells, blockade of SOCE via STIM1 or ORAI1 inhibition reduces Ca2⁺ influx and attenuates fibrogenic activation [131]. Particularly in HF, chronic overactivation of CaMKII leads to excessive phosphorylation of PLN at Thr17 and RyR2 at Ser2814, driving SR Ca2⁺ leak, reduced Ca2⁺ content, and impaired relaxation. Targeted inhibition of CaMKII—either genetically or pharmacologically—ameliorates these maladaptive changes by decreasing pathological phosphorylation events and stabilizing Ca2⁺ homeostasis (Fig. 5) [59, 101, 137]. Together, these convergent strategies offer a multifaceted therapeutic framework to restore efficient Ca2⁺ cycling and protect against metabolic and contractile dysfunction across multiple organ systems.

Experimental models and translational evidence

A wide array of experimental systems has delineated the physiological and pathological roles of the SERCA–PLN–DWORF axis and its translational potential. In vitro cellular models, such as rodent and human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes, have reveal that PLN knockdown or DWORF overexpression enhances SERCA activity and Ca2⁺ reuptake into the SR, improving contractile function, though the magnitude of effect in human cells remains modest and requires further validation [13]. Hepatocyte cultures exposed to fatty acids (such as palmitate) replicate MAFLD-like stress responses, where SERCA2b overexpression or treatment with activators such as CDN1163 restores ER Ca2⁺ homeostasis and reduces UPR markers and cell death [29]. LX-2 stellate cells, while not yet tested for PLN/DWORF expression, exhibit reduced collagen output upon SERCA modulation independent of confirmed PLN/DWORF expression [91]. In vivo, transgenic and knockout mice have confirmed the protective roles of DWORF and the detrimental consequences of PLN overexpression in cardiac models of stress and HF [9, 138]. In the liver, dietary models such as HFD, MCD, and Western Diet consistently show decreased Serca2b levels and increased ER stress, which are ameliorated by SERCA activators, ALR overexpression, or in CISD2-deficient models that spontaneously develop liver injury [3, 30, 74, 87, 131]. Organ-level assays, such as Langendorff-perfused hearts and isolated liver perfusions, have provided mechanistic insights into how SERCA modulation alters Ca2⁺ dynamics and function under hormonal or pharmacological stimuli [139]. MAM assays in NASH liver models have revealed aberrant ER-mitochondria Ca2⁺ flux, normalized by SERCA reactivation [30]. Human biopsy studies, reported in several datasets, support these findings: failing hearts display reduced SERCA2a and DWORF mRNA with increased PLN levels [13, 86], and NASH liver tissue exhibits ER stress consistent with impaired Ca2⁺ sequestration. Safety studies using large animals (such as PLN knockout pigs or planned/early-stage AAV-DWORF studies in primates) and off-target screening for SERCA activators are essential for clinical translation, as SERCA shares structural homology with other P-type ATPases. Collectively, these integrated models validate the SERCA–PLN–DWORF axis as a modifiable regulator of Ca2-dependent pathology and provide a rational basis for ongoing therapeutic development.

Clinical translation of the SERCA–PLN–DWORF signaling axis: opportunities, challenges, and future directions in cardiometabolic disease

The PLN–SERCA–DWORF signaling axis has emerged as a central regulator of intracellular Ca2⁺ homeostasis, with direct implications for cardiac, hepatic, and skeletal muscle pathophysiology. Preclinical studies have established its relevance in disease contexts characterized by SR/ER Ca2⁺ dysregulation, including HF, MASH, and muscle-based metabolic disorders. Translating these insights into clinical therapies is now an active frontier, particularly in cardiology, with expanding potential in hepatology and skeletal muscle disease.

In cardiovascular medicine, the most advanced clinical efforts focus on SERCA2a gene therapy, particularly in the context of HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF). Human trials such as CUPID [140] and MUSIC-HFrEF (ClinicalTrials.gov ID NCT04703842) have established proof-of-concept for AAV1-SERCA2a delivery, with ongoing refinements in vector design, targeted myocardial delivery, and patient stratification based on baseline SERCA activity [35]. Despite promising preclinical efficacy, significant translational challenges remain, including immune responses to viral vectors, limited transduction efficiency in diseased tissue, and potential off-target Ca2⁺ modulation. Long-term safety and dosage optimization will therefore be critical before clinical implementation. For HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), characterized by diastolic dysfunction, SERCA2a augmentation may enhance active relaxation and improve compliance. Parallel to this, DWORF gene therapy (AAV9-DWORF), designed to displace PLN from SERCA2a, has shown promising preclinical efficacy and is nearing first-in-human testing, with considerations for optimal dosing, cardiac-specific expression, and re-dosing potential [13, 21]. In addition to gene therapy, pharmacologic agents targeting the SERCA axis are in development. Istaroxime, a dual Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase inhibitor and SERCA2a activator, has demonstrated hemodynamic improvement in acute HF and may be reformulated for chronic use [36]. In genetic cardiomyopathies such as those caused by the PLN-R14del mutation, ASOs and CRISPR-derived base editing technologies offer the potential to specifically silence pathogenic PLN variants, restore SERCA function, and reverse disease phenotypes (Fig. 6) [34].

In hepatology, while direct clinical application remains in its early stages, a growing body of mechanistic evidence supports SERCA2b as a therapeutic target. FXR agonists [141, 142], such as obeticholic acid, developed for NASH, have been shown to upregulate SERCA2b expression and reduce ER stress. GLP-1 analogs and emerging antifibrotics may indirectly support SERCA2b function by alleviating lipotoxicity and fibrogenic signaling. Notably, SERCA activators such as CDN1163 could be integrated into combination regimens with metabolic or anti-inflammatory agents to enhance efficacy in NASH [87, 143]. Although liver-targeted SERCA gene therapy [144] has not yet reached the clinic, it is technically feasible due to the hepatotropic properties of AAV serotypes, raising the possibility of AAV-SERCA2b or AAV-DWORF delivery for conditions such as acute liver failure, ER stress-driven hepatic injury, or Ca2⁺ dysregulation syndromes. Biomarker-based patient selection, using serum indicators (such as BiP and FGF21) or biopsy-derived ER stress markers (such as CHOP), may enable precision targeting in future trials (Fig. 6). Beyond the PLN–SERCA–DWORF axis itself, genetic and functional abnormalities in SERCA isoforms have been directly linked to human diseases, underscoring the translational importance of Ca2⁺ pump regulation. Pathogenic mutations in ATP2A1 (SERCA1) cause Brody disease [145–147], an autosomal recessive myopathy; ATP2A2 (SERCA2) mutations underlie Darier disease [71, 148–150]; and reduced ATP2A2/ATP2A3 expression has been implicated in heart failure [151–153], MAFLD/MASH, and type 2 diabetes [154, 155]. These findings collectively demonstrate that both genetic and acquired SERCA dysfunctions contribute to the pathogenesis of cardiometabolic disorders (Table 1).

However, the lack of tissue-specific delivery systems remains a barrier, especially given the risk of off-target cardiac effects, including arrhythmias from excessive SERCA activation in the heart. Given the multifaceted roles of this axis, combination therapies are expected to be necessary. In HF, SERCA-based interventions may complement β-blockers, renin–angiotensin system inhibitors, or sodium–glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors, though care must be taken with β-agonist co-administration due to possible pro-arrhythmic synergy. In MASH, concurrent use of GLP-1 receptor agonists, PPAR agonists, or FXR modulators may optimize metabolic and anti-inflammatory outcomes. Patient stratification will be critical: individuals with high PLN expression, low SERCA2a activity, or impaired SR Ca2⁺ cycling may be ideal candidates for cardiac applications, while those with elevated ER stress markers in hepatic tissue (such as CHOP) may benefit most in the MASH setting (Fig. 6). Collectively, modulation of the SERCA–PLN–DWORF axis represents a paradigm shift in cardiometabolic therapy, offering disease-modifying potential by restoring intracellular Ca2⁺ homeostasis. Future success will depend on the development of tissue-specific delivery platforms, rigorous safety profiling, and precision medicine strategies integrating biomarkers, combination regimens, and patient-tailored intervention protocols.

Conclusion and perspectives

The discovery and functional elucidation of the SERCA–PLN–DWORF axis has substantially advanced our understanding of how intracellular Ca2⁺ homeostasis is precisely regulated across diverse tissue types. At the core of this system, SERCA pumps act as key effectors of Ca2⁺ sequestration into the ER/SR, maintaining Ca2⁺ gradients essential for muscle contractility, metabolic flux, and protein folding. Micropeptides such as PLN and DWORF are highly specialized regulators that fine-tune SERCA activity in response to physiological stimuli, developmental stages, and pathophysiological stressors [12–14].

In cardiac tissue, the balance between PLN-mediated inhibition and DWORF-driven activation of SERCA2a dictates the amplitude and timing of Ca2⁺ cycling critical for contractile performance. HF exemplifies how disruption of this balance—marked by increased PLN activity and diminished DWORF expression—leads to defective relaxation, Ca2⁺ overload, and contractile dysfunction [19, 20]. Emerging evidence indicates that SERCA2b and its micropeptide regulators in hepatocytes and hepatic stellate cells play similarly important roles in maintaining ER Ca2⁺ homeostasis, suppressing ER stress, and preventing fibrotic remodeling under metabolic or inflammatory stress [3, 4, 24, 30]. Although the cardiac, skeletal muscle, and hepatic systems exhibit distinct SERCA isoforms and micropeptide profiles, they share a conserved regulatory logic based on phosphorylation dynamics, Ca2⁺-dependent feedback, and stress-induced remodeling. This cross-organ comparison highlights the SERCA–PLN–DWORF axis as a unifying Ca2⁺ control module with context-specific adaptations.

Targeting the SERCA–PLN–DWORF axis is being explored as a potential strategy for treating Ca2-dependent diseases. SERCA activators (such as CDN1163, istaroxime), PLN inhibitors (antisense oligonucleotides, CRISPR-based editing), and DWORF overexpression have demonstrated encouraging preclinical effects in preclinical studies in improving contractile function, reducing ER stress, and restoring metabolic balance in models of HF, MAFLD/MASH, and skeletal muscle dysfunction [13, 34, 87]. However, several challenges remain for clinical translation. The tissue-specific expression and isoform diversity of SERCA and its regulators necessitate precision-targeted delivery to avoid off-target effects, particularly in excitable tissues. Moreover, the long-term safety and dosage optimization of SERCA activators and gene therapies must be rigorously evaluated, especially in chronic disease contexts where prolonged Ca2⁺ modulation could inadvertently trigger maladaptive signaling or arrhythmias.

Parallel to this, DWORF gene therapy (AAV9-DWORF), designed to displace PLN from SERCA2a, has shown preclinical efficacy and early translational potential, with considerations for optimal dosing, cardiac-specific expression, and re-dosing potential. Human trials such as CUPID and MUSIC-HFrEF have demonstrated safety and suggested modest benefit for AAV1-SERCA2a delivery, with ongoing refinements in vector design, targeted myocardial delivery, and patient stratification based on baseline SERCA activity. In genetic cardiomyopathies such as those caused by the PLN-R14del mutation, ASOs and CRISPR-derived base editing technologies have shown preliminary potential to specifically silence pathogenic PLN variants, restore SERCA function, and reverse disease phenotypes in preclinical models. Although liver-targeted SERCA gene therapy has not yet reached the clinic, it is technically feasible due to the hepatotropic properties of AAV serotypes, though immunogenicity and dosing remain significant challenges for clinical translation. Collectively, these translational efforts underscore the therapeutic potential of SERCA activation and PLN suppression in cardiometabolic contexts while highlighting the need for precision targeting and long-term safety validation before human applications.

Future research should focus on three major directions. First, expanding the characterization of novel regulins across tissue types—including hypothesized micropeptides in the liver and pancreas predicted by transcriptomic studies—to determine whether tissue-specific brakes on SERCA exist and can be therapeutically modulated. Second, structural and kinetic studies of SERCA and its micropeptide complexes are critical for designing selective small molecules and peptides that enhance SERCA activity without disrupting physiological regulation. Third, developing integrative therapeutic strategies combining SERCA modulation with other metabolic or anti-inflammatory agents (such as GLP-1 receptor agonists or FXR modulators) represents a promising but largely unexplored approach, particularly in complex diseases like NASH or HFpEF.

Although the present review consolidates the current mechanistic and translational knowledge of the SERCA–PLN–DWORF axis, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, much of the evidence is derived from preclinical and animal models, and interspecies variability in SERCA isoform expression, PLN phosphorylation dynamics, and DWORF regulation limits direct extrapolation to humans. Second, the lack of comprehensive quantitative data on hepatic and skeletal muscle micropeptide interactions precludes definitive cross-organ comparisons. Third, therapeutic studies remain largely proof-of-concept, with long-term safety, delivery efficiency, and off-target Ca2⁺ modulation remaining unresolved. Finally, as this study is a narrative review rather than a meta-analysis, potential publication bias and incomplete inclusion of negative findings cannot be excluded. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the translational potential of SERCA-targeted interventions.

In conclusion, the SERCA–PLN–DWORF axis represents a key regulatory system for Ca2⁺ homeostasis across excitable and metabolic tissues. Modulating this axis may help improve Ca2⁺ handling and mitigate cellular stress responses relevant to cardiometabolic disease, but further validation in human models is needed to clarify its therapeutic feasibility and long-term safety.
