Perioperative and short-term outcomes of total arch reconstruction using a novel frozen elephant trunk stent
Authors: Xiaoqin Tang, Sanjiu Yu, Hongduan Liu, Chaojun Yan, Lingfeng Tang, Jun Li, Ping He, Wei Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTotal arch reconstruction (TAR) for thoracic aortic dilatation increasingly incorporates frozen elephant trunk (FET) techniques. However, comparative data on the novel FET stent Fontus™ versus traditional designs like CRONUS™ (conventional CRONUS™ and modified CRONUS™) remain limited. This study aimed to compare perioperative and short-term outcomes of TAR using the Fontus™ FET stent with two CRONUS™ FET-based strategies.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 267 consecutive patients with thoracic aortic dilatation/aneurysms who underwent surgical procedures at our department between January 2019 and December 2024, of whom 76 were excluded (e.g., concomitant severe comorbidities, without TAR). The remaining 191 patients were grouped by stent type: C–C group (TAR with conventional CRONUS™ Stent, n = 62), C–F group (TAR with Fontus™ Stent, n = 54), and M–C group (TAR with modified CRONUS™ Stent, n = 75). Data on patient characteristics, surgical details, and the occurrence of postoperative complications were collected.ResultsNo statistical intergroup differences were found in baseline patient characteristics. All patients recovered well with no intraoperative mortality, and no significant differences in the rate of concomitant procedures and 6-month follow-up outcomes were found in the three groups (p > 0.05). However, the operation time, CPB time, hypothermic circulation time, and SCP time were significantly longer in the C–C group, followed by the M–C group, and finally in the C–F group (p < 0.05). In addition, the C–C group exhibited the highest intraoperative blood loss and intraoperative blood transfusion, followed by the C–F group, with the M–C group showing the lowest levels. Pairwise comparisons showed these differences were statistically significant for groups C–C versus M–C and groups C–C versus C–F. Multivariate binary logistic regression showed that the operative time, CPB time, aortic cross-clamp time, and hypothermic circulatory arrest time were risk factors for postoperative pneumonia.ConclusionsThe Fontus™ stent significantly reduced operative time and was not associated with increased postoperative complication rates compared with the traditional CRONUS™ stent. Introduction

Thoracic aortic aneurysm (TAA) is often referred to as a ‘silent killer,’ because most patients do not have symptoms in its early stages [1, 2]. The management of TAA relies on a triad of strategies: risk factor control, endovascular therapy, and open surgical reconstruction [2–4]. However, complex TAA pathologies involving the aortic arch require incorporation of the aortic arch branch vessel origins and are anatomically unsuitable for standard endovascular treatment. Open surgical aortic replacement remains the gold standard for more proximal arch involvement in suitable candidates [5, 6]. Open aortic arch replacement involving all 3 supra-aortic branches without the adjunct of either elephant trunk (ET) repair or in combination with the frozen elephant trunk (FET) technique has become rare [6–8].

Early FET devices, such as the CRONUS™ stent, still present limitations: their multi-branch design necessitates extensive dissection of the left subclavian artery, increasing the risk of nerve injury, and their complex anastomotic requirements prolong operative time. Our team previously introduced a simplified TAR with a modified CRONUS™ stent with favorable results [9]. While promising, this approach retains the need for intraoperative stent modification (e.g., in situ fenestration), which may still complicate procedures in high-risk patients. Recently, the Fontus™ Branched Intraoperative Stent (a novel FET), as an upgrade of the CRONUS™ Intraoperative Stent (classic FET), has been marketed and applied in clinical practice. Its single-branch design (the branch can directly cover the left subclavian artery origin, requiring only anastomosis between the branch and the graft at the proximal aortic arch) effectively avoids the deep vacular mobilization and anastomosis of the left subclavian artery, which significantly simplifies the surgical operation and avoids the damage to the surrounding organs and nerves [10]. Despite these design advantages, direct comparisons of novel FET stents Fontus™ versus traditional designs CRONUS™ (conventional and modified CRONUS™) are lacking, such as the clinical outcomes and clinical efficacy.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the efficacy of the Fontus™ FET stent with two CRONUS™ FET-based strategies for aortic arch surgery. By analyzing perioperative parameters, complication rates, and short-term survival, we sought to clarify the relative efficacy of Fontus™ and provide evidence to guide surgical decision-making in aortic arch reconstruction.

Methods

Study design

This single-center retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Army Medical University (Institutional Review Board Document KY2021058). All procedures were performed by a cardiac surgery team led by a principal surgeon. This surgeon possesses over 10 years of experience in the field of large vessel surgery. The primary surgeon had performed the described procedure numerous times prior to inclusion in this study. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (Revised 2013) to observe the efficacy of the novel FET (Fontus™ Stent) compared with previous aortic arch surgery.

Patients

Consecutive patients diagnosed with thoracic aortic dilatation/aneurysms who underwent FET-based TAR at our department between January 2019 and December 2024 were retrospectively enrolled. Inclusion criteria were met if patients satisfied one of the following [11, 12]: (1) the diameter of the ascending aorta was > 55 mm, and the diameter of the aortic arch in Region II was > 35 mm; (2) isolated aortic arch diameter > 55 mm; (3) aortic arch dilatation < 55 mm with concomitant presence of symptoms attributable to the aneurysm (hoarseness, dyspnea, and dysphagia may be caused by the mass effect of the aneurysm on the nerves, trachea, or esophagus, respectively) in low operative risk patients. (4) aortic arch > 45 mm in patients with Marfan syndrome or Loeys–Dietz syndrome; and (5) the ascending or descending aorta is definitely in need of replacement with concomitant arch abnormalities or high-risk anatomy; Exclusion criteria were met if patients satisfied one of the following: (1) serious complications, including massive cerebral hemorrhage, severe coagulopathy, multiple organ dysfunction syndrome, and superior mesenteric artery occlusion; (2) missing baseline data; (3) loss to follow-up; (4) acute aortic arch rupture (≤ 24 h from onset) or arch aneurysms with contiguous involvement of the innominate artery origin (> 15 mm). (4) Patients who have previously undergone any aortic-related surgery, including open or endovascular repair/replacement of the ascending aorta, aortic arch, or descending aorta, see Fig. 1 for details.

Flow diagram of patient inclusion and exclusion

Patients were categorized into three groups according to the type of FET: the C–C group (TAR with CRONUS™ Stent), the M–C group (TAR with a modified CRONUS™ Stent), the C–F group (TAR with Fontus™ Stent).

Preoperative management

All patients completed a detailed routine preoperative examination and imaging tests such as CTA and enhanced CT to confirm the diagnosis of thoracic aortic dilatation/aneurysms. Finally, all patients were evaluated for laboratory test results to rule out contraindications for surgery, determine the surgical method, and sign the informed consent for surgery.

Surgical procedures

The procedures were performed under general anesthesia via standard median sternotomy. Extracorporeal circulation was established through femoral artery cannulation as arterial perfusion and venous drainage by placing superior vena cava and inferior vena cava cannulas in the right atrium, respectively. After the start of the extracorporeal circulation, the temperature cooled uniformly, and when the nasopharyngeal temperature cooled to 30 °C, the ascending aorta was clamped, and coronary artery openings and ice chips were placed in the pericardial cavity to cool and protect the myocardium. Circulation was stopped when the nasopharyngeal temperature cooled to 28 °C.

The ascending aorta was blocked, and ascending aortic replacement and aortic root treatment were accomplished under cardiac arrest while gradually cooling to 28 °C. The main treatments include sinuplasty, Bentall’s procedure, or ascending aorta replacement, which were determined for each patient based on individual circumstances, CTA results, intraoperative exploration, and the patient’s workup.

Selective cerebral perfusion (SCP) was performed via cannulation of the innominate artery at a flow rate of 8–10 mL/(kg.min). When intraoperative testing revealed a left cerebral oxygen saturation of < 50%, conversion from unilateral to bilateral SCP was initiated by insertion of the left common carotid artery. The cooling cap was placed around the head. Following release of the ascending aorta cross-clamp, the aortic arch was opened, and its branch vessels were transected. The recurrent laryngeal nerve and phrenic nerve were carefully protected. Subsequently, the patient underwent different approaches to aortic arch management, i.e., conventional total arch replacement with a CRONUS™ Stent, simplified total arch reconstruction with a modified CRONUS™ Stent, and conventional total arch replacement with a Fontus™ Stent.

The data, including the patient characteristics, surgical details, and the occurrence of postoperative complications, were collected.

The C–C group procedure

The steps of the Sun’s procedure were similar to those previously reported [13, 14]. Briefly, the three supra-aortic branches were extensively dissected and occluded and transected individually. A CRONUS stent was then placed in the descending thoracic aorta through the aortic lumen of the descending aorta distal to the arch and implanted in the descending aorta at the appropriate location to close the tear site. The proximal end of the bifurcated vessel and the CRONUS stent were closed with 4–0 prolene sutures, and the left subclavian, left common carotid, and innominate arteries were securely anastomosed to the collateral branches of the bifurcated vessel with a 5-0 prolene suture, and the cross-clamp was removed for perfusion after completion of the anastomosis (Fig. 2). Rewarming was started immediately after the left common carotid artery anastomosis. The remaining procedures were performed in a routine manner.

Simplified surgical procedure diagram of C–C, M–C and C–F group. C–C group: TAR with CRONUS™ stent; M–C group: TAR with modified CRONUS™ stent; and C–F group: TAR with Fontus™ stent

The M–C group procedure

The in situ stent graft fenestration was similar to those previously reported [15]. Briefly, after replacement of the ascending aorta, the ascending aorta cross-clamp was opened, circulation was stopped, and a stent (CRONUS™) was implanted proximally through the aorta to cover the openings of the three supra-aortic branches, which could be adjusted as needed; the stent was later clamped and visualized to reassess the size and distance of the openings of the three supra-aortic branches, and then windowing to match the size of the branch openings was performed using surgical scissors. After confirming that the positioning of the overlying stent and the window openings was accurate, anastomosis between the overlying stent window openings and the autologous aortic arch was performed using 5-0 prolene sutures, and after completion of the anastomosis, a double-lumen ureteral catheter or a balloon occlusion catheter was carefully inserted into the stent graft to restore lower body circulation through the femoral artery. Other sutures were performed simultaneously.

The C–F group procedure

Briefly, the three supra-aortic branches were extensively dissected and occluded and transected individually. When the aortic arch was opened and its branch vessels were transected, a Fontus™ branch stent was inserted through the distal end of the arch into the descending aorta and the left subclavian artery (Fig. 2). The vessel and left subclavian artery are then dilated with a balloon. The remaining procedures were performed as described above by Sun’s procedure.

Stent graft: a brief introduction

CRONUS™ intraoperative stent

As previously described, the china CRONUS™ Stent Graft Device is manufactured by Shanghai Microtronic Medical (Shanghai) Co. Ltd. Briefly, this stent-graft consists of a polyester vascular graft and a self-expanding metal stent (in various sizes). The stent covers a diameter of 24–46 mm (for aortic applications) and a length of 50–150 mm, with a superelastic structure to accommodate vascular curvature and a coated layer to provide blood flow isolation. This stent was used in this study for groups Sun and ISSF.

Fontus™ branched intraoperative stent

China The Fontus™ Branching Stent Graft Device is a new generation of stent products after the CRONUS™ Stent Graft Device by Shanghai MicroStroke Medical (Shanghai) Co. Ltd. The stent system utilizes a dual modular structure, with the main stent consisting of a superelastic Nitinol skeleton with an expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE) overlay, and the branching stents are made of the same high-quality combination of materials to ensure the consistency of the system’s overall performance. The diameter of the main bracket ranges from 26 to 42 mm, the diameter of the branch bracket is 10–14 mm, and the length can be customized according to the anatomical needs (50–150 mm for the main body and 20–60 mm for the branches).

Data collection and follow-up

Data were collected from electronic medical records and a dedicated institutional database, including: baseline patient characteristics, surgical details (e.g., operative time, CPB duration), and postoperative outcomes (e.g., complications, length of stay). All patients were followed up at 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively via outpatient visits or telephone interviews. Follow-up assessments included clinical examinations, laboratory tests, and imaging (CTA) to evaluate aortic remodeling and complications (e.g., endoleak, anastomotic stenosis).

Study endpoint

The primary endpoint was hospitalized all-cause mortality. Secondary endpoints included time to operative duration, CPB duration, aortic occlusion duration, intraoperative blood transfusion, postoperative intensive care unit (ICU), postoperative hospitalization, complication rates (pericardial effusion, cerebrovascular accidents, paraplegia, and acute kidney injury), as well as the results of aortic remodeling.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies (%), and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test (for small expected frequencies). Continuous variables are reported as mean ± SD or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on whether the data were distributed normally or not. The normal distribution of data was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous outcomes were analyzed using a one-way analysis of variance or Kruskal–Wallis test. When an overall intervention effect was detected (P < 0.05), the Bonferroni correction for multiple pairwise comparisons was performed. In addition, in models adjusted for type of surgery, age, BMI, history of smoking, alcohol consumption, comorbidities, concomitant surgery, intraoperative bleeding, intraoperative blood transfusion, and mechanical ventilation, binary logistic regression models were used to determine the association between surgery-related time and postoperative pneumonia. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and all p values reported were two-sided.

Among them, 62 in C–C group, 54 in C–F group, and 75 in M–C group. Intraoperative outcomes

All 191 patients successfully underwent total arch reconstruction. None of the patients died during the operation, and no significant differences in the rate of concomitant procedures were found in the three groups (p > 0.05). In addition, intraoperative bleeding and intraoperative infusions also differed significantly among groups (p < 0.05). The C–C group exhibited the highest levels of both intraoperative blood loss and intraoperative blood transfusion, followed by the M–C group, with the C–F group having the lowest values. Short-term follow-up results

All patients were referred to our outpatient clinic for follow-up or were contacted via telephone interviews, with CTA examinations conducted at 6-month post-surgery. No patients were lost during the 6-month follow-up period. The overall survival rate was 96.77% in the C–C group, 96.00% in the M–C group, and 96.30% in the M–C group. A total of 2 aortic-related reinterventions were performed in all three groups, mainly dealing with distal vascular lesions. In addition, the C–C group was readmitted for medication adjustment in month 2 for paroxysmal dizziness. Furthermore, 6-month CTA findings indicated no stent displacement or significant aortic arch dilatation in any group; however, anastomotic stenosis was observed in three cases within the C–C group and two cases within the C–F group. Other results

To determine whether there is a correlation between pneumonia and operative time. We divided the patients into two groups according to whether they developed pneumonia postoperatively. The baseline data of the two groups are compared in Supplementary Table S1. The median operation time, CPB time, aortic cross-clamp time, and hypothermic circulatory arrest time were significantly longer in the pneumonia group than in the non-pneumonia group. In addition, the pneumonia group exhibited higher levels of both intraoperative blood loss and intraoperative blood transfusion than the non-pneumonia group. Further multivariate binary logistic regression revealed that the median operative time, CPB time, aortic cross-clamp time, and hypothermic circulatory arrest time are risk factors for pneumonia in the fully adjusted model (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Sun et al. proposed total arch replacement combined with FET implantation as a new “standard” treatment for aortic arch repair [16–18]. However, comparative data on the novel FET stent Fontus™ versus traditional designs CRONUS™ (conventional vs. modified CRONUS™) remain limited. This retrospective analysis evaluates the efficacy of the Fontus™ FET stent with two CRONUS™ FET-based strategies for aortic arch reconstruction at our department. It was found that there was no difference in 6-month follow-up outcomes and postoperative complications among the three groups. However, the operation time, CPB time, hypothermic circulation time, and SCP time were significantly longer in the C–C group, followed by the M–C group, and finally in the C–F group (p < 0.05). In addition, the C–C group exhibited the highest levels of both intraoperative blood loss and intraoperative blood transfusion, followed by the M–C group, with the C–F group. Therefore, this study emphasizes that for patients with thoracic aortic dilatation/aneurysms requiring aortic arch reconstruction, the new FET (Fontus™ stent) implantation may serve as a viable and effective alternative approach for total arch reconstruction surgery.

Open aortic arch replacement involving all three supra-aortic branches without the adjunct of either elephant trunk (ET) repair or in combination with the frozen elephant trunk (FET) technique has become rare [19, 20]. Because of the specific anatomical structure and hemodynamic characteristics of the aortic arch, this not only makes aortic arch reconstruction time-consuming in the surgery but also may be highly fatal and associated with multiple complications [21, 22]. Therefore, based on the concept of Sun’s technique, improved methods and materials are constantly being developed and can further shorten the operative time and the occurrence of complications [23]. In the present study, the modified CRONUS™ stent has a shorter operative time and CPB time compared to the conventional CRONUS™ stent, which is consistent with previous reports. This further demonstrates that the modified CRONUS™ stent compensates for the shortcomings of conventional procedures, such as achieving proximity of the distal anastomosis and eliminating the need to reconstruct one or more supra-aortic vessels. We also found that in thoracic aortic dilatation/aneurysm patients, the novel Fontus™ stent significantly reduced operative time, CPB time, hypothermic circulation arrest time, intraoperative bleeding, and intraoperative blood transfusion compared with the conventional CRONUS™ stent for total arch replacement. This may be because the single-branch design of the new FONTUSTM stent effectively eliminates the need for deep subclavian artery dissection and anastomosis and significantly simplifies surgery. However, there was no significant difference between the modified CRONUS™ stent and the novel Fontus™ stent in terms of procedural time.

Research indicates a positive correlation between operative duration and postoperative complications [24]. However, this study found no statistically significant difference in the incidence of postoperative complications (pneumonia, recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, chylothorax, and internal leakage). This warrants careful consideration. Indeed, the modified CRONUS™ stent graft eliminates the need for resection or extensive dissection of the three superior vena cava branches, thereby preventing damage to the thoracic duct and recurrent laryngeal nerve. Concurrently, the single-piece design of the novel Fontus™ graft effectively eliminates the need for deep dissection and anastomosis of the left subclavian artery, significantly simplifying the surgical procedure and preventing damage to surrounding organs and nerves. These factors collectively suggest that, compared to the traditional CRONUS™ graft, the incidence of recurrent laryngeal nerve injury and chylothorax should be reduced in the other two groups. This aligns with the findings of this study and previous research [25]. Regarding pneumonia, multiple studies have demonstrated a positive correlation with operative time, whereas we observed no significant difference in this study [26]. Therefore, we conducted a binary logistic regression analysis for pneumonia and operative time. This further indicates that prolonged operative time constitutes a risk factor for postoperative pneumonia. This finding aligns with both the present study and previous investigations [26]. The lack of statistical significance in this study may be attributed to low event rates, small sample size, and insufficient test power. In addition, it is noteworthy that the modified CRONUS™ stent graft exhibited a higher incidence of endoleak compared to the other two groups. This may be related to the positioning of the covered stent graft and its proximal aortic anchoring. In summary, the findings indicate a correlation between surgical duration and certain postoperative complications. However, the absence of significant postoperative complications observed in this study may be attributable to the small sample size or the lack of pronounced early clinical outcomes. The long-term effects remain unclear and warrant further investigation.

Fontus™ and CRONUS™ stents have other concerns. For instance, the modified CRONUS™ stent technique is not suitable for patients with a diameter of the aortic arch greater than 4 cm, as this may result in less secure stent fixation. In addition, in patients with greater curvature of the aortic arch, caution is needed when using this technique to prevent twisting of the stent graft. Concurrently, internal endoleaks are most common in modified CRONUS™ stents and least frequent in the conventional surgery, so the use of this technique requires constant attention to the occurrence of internal leakage. Although this study demonstrates that the novel Fontus™ stent offers a viable and effective alternative for thoracic aortic reconstruction, it is noteworthy that the Fontus™ stent is comparatively more expensive than the CRONUS™ stent. Consequently, surgeons should select the optimal approach based on each patient’s condition to achieve satisfactory outcomes.

Limitations

There are several limitations in this study: (1) the lack of randomization in assigning patients to Fontus™ and CRONUS™ stents (conventional vs. modified CRONUS™), and the anatomic applicability of the aorta may introduce selection bias in this study. Thus, rigorous control of exclusion criteria in subsequent studies would be valuable in future studies, particularly regarding the specific pathological and imaging status of patients. (2) Longer follow-up and long-term correlative imaging data are needed to confirm whether the modified CRONUS™ stent technique and the novel Fontus™ stent technique provide similar or better long-term benefits than conventional surgery and the clinical treatment differences between the modified CRONUS™ stent technique and the novel Fontus™ stent technique.

Conclusions

In total, arch reconstruction surgery, there were no differences in complications or 6-month follow-up outcomes between the novel FET stent Fontus™ and traditional designs CRONUS™ (conventional vs. modified CRONUS™). However, the Fontus™ stent significantly reduced operative time and had no postoperative complications compared with the conventional CRONUS™ stent. Consequently, the novel Fontus™ stent may serve as a safe and effective alternative approach for thoracic aortic reconstruction surgery, offering potential advantages in operative efficiency without compromising short-term outcomes.
