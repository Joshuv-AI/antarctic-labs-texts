Drowning deaths in West Bengal, India: a statewide community-based survey
Authors: Jagnoor Jagnoor, Sujoy Roy, Suman Das, Soumyadeep Bhaumik, Medhavi Gupta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDrowning burdens in low- and middle-income contexts are underestimated, as current data collection systems gather inadequate data. We conducted one of the largest community-based surveys to date capturing fatal drowning data across age, gender and rurality, focusing on the state of West Bengal, India.MethodsA resource-efficient community knowledge approach was used to conduct an observational community-based survey where meetings were held with community residents and key informants to identify drowning deaths in the population. Identified deaths were verified at the victim’s household through a structured survey, inquiring on the circumstances around the drowning death.ResultsA fatal drowning rate of 9.17/100 000 was found across the state. Drownings were highest in children 1–4 years old (60.02/100 000), followed by 5–9 years (19.20/100 000). Males faced higher rates across all ages (11.82/100 000) compared with females (6.33/100 000). Rural areas faced significantly higher drowning rates than urban areas (12.52/100 000 and 1.19/100 000, respectively). About half of the events occurred in waterbodies near the victim’s home. Most events occurred between 10:00 a.m. and 2:00 p.m. and were over-represented during the monsoon months of June to August. A minority of cases were administered appropriate resuscitation techniques or taken to formal healthcare providers, and only 16.1% of events were issued death certificates.ConclusionsThe results illustrated how routine data collection systems in low-income settings grossly underestimate drowning events, emphasising the importance of community-based surveys in capturing true drowning burdens in India and low- and middle-income country contexts more broadly.Keywords: Child health, Community-based survey, Injury

Introduction

Despite advancements in global development and the promotion of targeted interventions, drowning remains a significant cause of injury-related mortality in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), carrying 92% of the burden.1 While the previous decade had seen a 36% decline in global drowning rates, this reduction has not been equitably represented in LMIC contexts. This disproportionate result has been attributed to multiple factors, including lower investment in interventions and lower availability of quality data that could guide the targeting of these interventions within high-risk populations.1 2 Community-based analysis of the burden and contributing factors is essential to effectively address drowning risk in LMICs. India is estimated to carry 18% of the global drowning fatalities, implying dedicated action within this country could enable significant progress towards global Sustainable Development Goals for better health and well-being.1 However, these estimates are likely an under-representation of the true burden. Previous drowning burden surveys conducted in India found less than 1% of drowning deaths were administered a death certificate.3 4

The limited studies on drowning burden in LMICs miss key knowledge required to effectively address drowning risk. First, a variety of contexts have not been assessed in LMICs, including in India. While it is understood that rates of drowning are higher in rural areas, the factors and circumstances related to drowning may differ between rural and urban regions.5 Greater population density, differing water safety-related infrastructure and economic conditions may affect contributors and intervention mechanisms. Second, existing studies in India have primarily focused on childhood drowning, leaving adult drowning risk largely unexplored.3 4 Poor workplace safety protocols, lack of protective infrastructure and disaster risk may all contribute to adult drowning in LMICs.1

The current study focused on West Bengal, a state with high-risk geographies, and where previous studies have found an under-reporting of drowning events.3 Assessing statewide drowning risk will develop understanding of risk across contexts and provide a strong rationale for action and policy influence at a state-government level.6 We aim to identify fatal drowning rates across ages and context, including rural and urban regions, to better quantify and understand how drowning rates may differ in LMICs by these factors.

Methods

Aims

The aim of this study was to understand the fatal drowning burden across the state of West Bengal, India, including the following:

The rates of drowning across demographic characteristics: age and gender.

The rates of drowning across demographic characteristics: age and gender.

The rate of drowning across rural and urban contexts.

The rate of drowning across rural and urban contexts.

Context

Our survey covered the state of West Bengal, India. West Bengal is a state with a diverse topography and developmental levels and a population of approximately 99 million. The state is situated in the east of India and has five divisions, four of which are classified as coastal, deltaic or riverine, and likely experience high risks for drowning. With similar environments across the state of West Bengal, it is likely that high drowning rates exist, and these are being under-reported in official data sources. One study in the Sundarbans region, located in the southern part of the state, found some of the highest rates of drowning in children globally.3 West Bengal also faces some of the highest poverty rates in India.

Community knowledge approach

Collecting household-level incidence data is resource-intensive, which can limit opportunities for data collection in LMICs. The community knowledge approach (CKA) was developed and validated as an alternative method to identify maternal deaths at the community level using community connections.7 The approach has since been applied to LMICs such as India, Bangladesh and Pakistan.3 8

Data collection applied the CKA and followed a three-step process, where data collections (1) drew a community map of the sample unit area, (2) conducted a group interview in a community hub with key informants to list drowning events in their knowledge and (3) verified identified drowning events at the household level (see online supplemental file 1 for data collection process diagram).

The survey methodology and reporting was guided by the relevant components of the Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (CROSS)9 (see online supplemental file 2 for checklist).

Sample size

West Bengal is comprised of five administrative divisions and 19 districts divided within these. A total of 250 units were sampled in each of the 19 districts, covering an estimated population of 1 million per district, which allowed the estimation of drowning event prevalence of 0.00001 (10/1 000 000 population) to within ±0.00001. Rural and urban units were randomly selected as proportional to the rural/urban population ratio of the district as per the 2011 census, adjusted for population growth as estimated by the Unique Identification Authority of India in the Government of India, which tracks the population through the issuance of an identification card (Aadhar) held by 99% of Indians. In total, 4631 units were sampled (250 from each district, except 131 in the city of Kolkata, which did not have rural units). A total estimated population of 17.9 million was covered.

Data collection

For rural areas, one community group meeting per unit of 1000 residents was held. Due to higher population densities and a lower ratio of available key informants to the population, the unit size for urban areas was adjusted to 3000 residents. Data collection occurred over a period of 8 months from March to October 2024.

Data collection teams consisted of two data collectors. Data collectors had a minimum qualification of university-level graduation and received field training. Data collectors visited each unit and located a central area such as a tea shop or community centre where a group of 8–15 key informants were interviewed, involving lay individuals and key individuals such as community leaders and health workers. They were asked to identify cases of drowning in their community. Data collectors then asked the group to identify community leaders such as local doctors and health workers who would have oversight over drowning events, such as a Panchayat member (elected local government representative), Anganwadi worker (government-run nutrition programme provider) or teacher. These key informants were also engaged to identify drowning events in their community. All community participants were required to be residents of the community for at least the last 2 years and over the age of 18 years (online supplemental file 3 provides the tool used to conduct these interviews).

Drowning events identified by community members and key informants in each unit were collated for household survey administration. The drowning events identified by the community were validated by visiting the household of the victims. The household survey was developed and administered on the REDCap software and verified the drowning event, captured demographic characteristics of the victim and household and circumstances around the drowning event (online supplemental file 4). The household survey was piloted and verified with known cases of drowning to assess for clarity, variable adequacy and logical flows in the survey.

Household survey respondents were required to be an adult over the age of 18 years, who had been living in the household at the time of the drowning event. The participation information statement clearly stated that participation was voluntary, and refusal would not affect their relationship with the research team. If a participant refused to participate or was not available, the data collectors returned at a later suitable time or requested another adult from the household to participate.

Internal validity range checks, alphanumeric variables and mandatory variables checks were conducted to ensure data accuracy and completeness. Field supervisors monitored data at the end of each day, and standard reports were generated on REDCap on a fortnightly basis and monitored for correctness. Data quality was ensured through 5% back checks of surveys throughout data collection, and frequent random cross-checks of data collection activities.

Analysis

Drowning was defined as inclusive of disaster and water transport-related drownings. Findings were reported as deaths per 100 000 population. The estimated population (denominator) was derived from the total estimated population within each population unit, and the annual rates are weighted to represent the entire population of West Bengal. The drowning mortality rate was calculated using the STATA software package.

The weights were generated based on the inverse of the probability of selected units. The weights of rural units were based on sample probability of subdistricts (0.5 because half of the subdistricts were sampled initially) and villages (no. of villages taken/no. of villages available). The weights for urban units selected from subdistricts were generated similarly as villages, and those which were out of subdistricts were generated with the inverse probability of selected town (0.5 because half of the towns in each district were selected) and then selected wards within the towns (no. of wards taken/no. of wards available). The weights were then multiplied with the number of events within each district and then divided by the total projected population of West Bengal in 2021.10

Proportional data on the circumstances around the drowning events were also reported.

A total of 15 962 community interviews were conducted across 4631 units to identify drowning deaths using key informants. From these, 2439 household surveys were conducted based on events identified in community interviews (see online supplemental file 5 for all demographic information of respondents). Less than 2% of data was missing.

Drowning mortality rates by rurality, age and gender

Drowning rates by age, gender, rurality and division

As age increased, the risk of drowning gradually declined, reaching its lowest point in the 25–29 years group where the drowning rate was 3.21/100 000. However, the data revealed a resurgence in drowning risk among the elderly individuals aged 65 and above, where the rate exceeded 9/100 000 (table 1).

The highest proportion of fatal incidents occurred in the 1–4 age group (33.2%), followed by the 5–9 age group (14.6%). The proportion of incidents generally declined with increasing age from the 5–9 age group onwards, with a slight increase observed in the 70+ age group (4.3%).

The data revealed that males were disproportionately affected by drowning incidents (11.82/100 000 males, 6.33/100 000 females) (table 1). A small number of drowning events were reported for individuals identifying as Other, but due to the lack of estimated population data for this group, rates could not be calculated.

Age and gender analysis of rates found that fatal drowning cases were significantly higher in males across all age groups compared with females. This difference was higher in younger age groups (see online supplemental file 6 for table of rates by age and gender).

Rural areas experienced significantly higher drowning rates compared with urban areas, with rates of 12.52/100 000 vs 1.19/100 000, respectively (table 1). A higher proportion of drowning events occurred in rural areas compared with urban areas (86.4% vs 13.6%).

The division of Medinipur had the highest fatal drowning rate at 139.9/100 000, indicating a high-risk area requiring focused intervention. Jalpaiguri had the lowest fatal rate at 2.82/100 000, suggesting better safety measures or lower exposure. Presidency had the highest number of fatal cases but a moderate fatality rate at 7.61/100 000 (see online supplemental file 7 for table of rates by division).

A χ2 test on age and division was significant (p<0.001), showing that there were differences in the proportion of age-wise events across divisions (see online supplemental file 8 for table of proportions by age group and division). Jalpaiguri, a mountainous division, experienced relatively similar proportions of cases across age groups which peaked in ages 10–14 years. The remaining four divisions all experienced the highest proportion of cases in children aged 1–4. Presidency and Malda divisions experienced more than one-third of cases in children aged 1–4 years, a greater proportion for this age group than others.

Person characteristics of drowning victims

Educational attainment for people over the age of 6 years old (n=1085) also showed disparities, with the greatest proportion of drowning victims being uneducated (29.6%, n=321). Occupational trends in victims 14 years and older (as per legal working age, n=843) indicated that students comprised a substantial proportion of drowning cases (23.5%, n=198), followed by individuals engaged in agriculture (16.6%, n=141). Notably, very few drowning cases were reported among fishermen (1.8%, n=15) (table 2).

Playing/swimming was the most common activity of the victim before drowning, accounting for 47.7% (n=879) of cases, followed by bathing at 25.4% (n=477). For children under 18 (n=1137), an adult was accompanying the child in only 4.8% (n=550) of cases. The mother/usual caretaker was most often at home doing household chores at the time (82.5%, n=938) (table 2).

Pre-existing health conditions were uncommon among victims, with 86.7% (n=1629) reported to have no prior health issues. However, 7.2% (n=136) had epilepsy or another seizure disorder and 2.9% (n=54) had a diagnosed mental health condition (table 2).

Alcohol consumption at the time of drowning was identified in 14.1% (n=102) of cases involving adults over 18. The majority (81.3%, n=587) were not under the influence of alcohol, while 4.6% (n=33) of respondents were unsure.

Place and time of drowning events

Temporal patterns showed that drowning incidents peaked between late morning and early afternoon, with the highest occurrence in the 12:00–1:59 p.m. timeframe (32.9%, n=618; figure 1).

Time of day for fatal drowning events.

Fatal drowning cases peaked in July (12.3%, n=232) and August (11.9%, n=224). The numbers rose gradually from March, aligning with the onset of pre-monsoon activities and increasing water exposure (online supplemental file 9).

Drowning events predominantly occurred outdoors (97.3%, n=1829), with ponds being the most common drowning sites (69.4%, n=1271), followed by rivers (17.0%, n=311). Water bodies largely did not have a physical barrier surrounding them (95.1%, n=1743). Among outdoor incidents, most took place beside walkways/roads (54.2%, n=992), followed by yards/verandas (21.1%, n=386). See online supplemental file 10 for full data table.

Indoor drowning events were rare (2.7%, n=50), but when they occurred, household water sources, such as buckets, tubs and reservoirs, were involved (online supplemental file 10).

More than half of drowning incidents occurred in water bodies located more than 100 m away from home (50.7%, n=926). However, a significant proportion (39.1%, n=715) occurred within 50 m of home (online supplemental file 10).

A majority (90.5%, n=1701) of deaths occurred at the site of the drowning event. A notable proportion of deaths occurred during the journey to a medical facility (4.5%, n=85), and 2.2% victims (n=42) died at the hospital (online supplemental file 10).

Post-drowning responses

First-aid practices varied widely, with common methods including applying pressure to the abdomen to induce vomiting (43.6%, n=819), spinning the victim over the head (38.5%, n=723) and massaging with oil (24.1%, n=453). Medically appropriate interventions, such as mouth-to-mouth and chest compressions, were performed in only 10.6% (n=199) of cases. Some individuals were taken immediately to a local doctor or hospital (28.2%, n=529), while a significant number were taken to quack doctors (18.3%, n=343) (table 3).

10.9% (n=205) of individuals were admitted for over 12 hours at a health facility, while the majority (87.5%, n=1644) were not admitted at all. Those admitted to a hospital (n=209) were primarily taken to block hospitals (44.5%, n=93), district hospitals (18.7%, n=39) or primary health centres (16.7%, n=35) (table 3).

Transportation to the hospital for all those taken (n=209) was mainly by private vehicles (38.3%, n=80), motorcycles (13.9%, n=29) or ambulances (23.9%, n=50) (table 3). Travel time to the hospital was mostly within 30 min (79.4%, n=166), although some took between 30 min and 1 hour (16.3%, n=34), and a percentage took over an hour (1.4%, n=3). Delays in transportation were primarily due to distance from the hospital or challenges in organising transportation.

In addition to hospital admissions, drowning victims received consultations without admission from a registered medical practitioner (2.4%, n=46), a community-based health worker like ASHA (government-employed Accredited Social Health Activist) or Anganwadi worker (0.9%, n=16), or a quack doctor (1.5%, n=28). Consultations for fatal events often took place in the community (7.2%, n=135) or at a health centre (2.3%, n=44).

Reporting and compensation for fatal drowning

Of all fatal drownings, only 18.0% (n=339) had been issued a death certificate, of which n=299 respondents (15.9% of events) were aware of what was reported on the death certificate. In over half of the cases with known death certificate causes (57.24%, n=171), drowning itself was explicitly stated as the cause of death (online supplemental file 11). However, a significant proportion of cases had alternative or unclear attributions.

In 16.5% of fatal cases with a death certificate with known reported causes (n=299), no specific cause of death was mentioned. Cardiac or heart conditions were cited in 6.06% (n=18) of cases, while epilepsy—a known risk factor for drowning—was recorded in 4.71% (n=14) of deaths. Additionally, 11.79% (n=35) of cases listed respiratory and cardiac arrest as the primary cause of death, a term that, while medically valid, does not specify drowning as the triggering event.

Postmortem examinations were conducted in less than a quarter of fatal cases (24.9%, n=467). A small percentage (1.7%, n=32) were uncertain about whether a postmortem examination had been conducted (data not shown).

Financial impact of fatal drowning events

Among fatal drowning cases issued a death certificate (18.0%; n=339), compensation for the death was rarely received, with only 2.4% (n=8) of families reporting any form of compensation, while 95.0% (n=322) did not receive any, and 3.5% (n=12) were unsure (data not shown).

8.5% (n=159) of the fatal event victims were the sole earning members of their households, while 10.6% (n=199) were major contributors to household income, and 5.5% (n=104) were minor contributors. The majority (71.8%, n=1350) were non-earning members, and 3.6% (n=67) either did not know or chose not to answer (data not shown).

Families who lost an earning member (n=462) adopted various coping mechanisms. Common responses included using savings (n=178, 38.3%), borrowing from relatives or friends (n=78, 16.9%), taking loans (n=23, 5.0%) and selling assets (n=14, 3.0%). Others reduced food consumption (n=17, 3.7%), cut educational expenses (n=7, 1.5%) or limited spending on basic needs such as clothing and shelter (n=58, 12.6%). A few received government support (n=10, 2.2%), while many relied on unspecified means (n=130, 28.1%) (data not shown).

Health insurance coverage among drowning victims was low; 69.8% (n=1312) had no coverage. Of the insured, 29.1% were enrolled in Swasthya Sathi, and 0.9% had coverage under State health insurance schemes or private insurance (0.3%) (data not shown).

Discussion

This study conducted a state-wide survey to identify fatal drowning rates across all ages, gender and rurality in the state of West Bengal, India, and built an understanding of the circumstances around these events. State-wide drowning rates were found to be 9.17/100 000. Global Burden of Disease 2021 data suggested much lower rates of drowning in West Bengal of 3.91/100 000 population.11 The study highlighted the pressing need for community-level data collection to identify true burdens in high-risk regions where underestimation is likely, especially in young children aged 1–9 years old who accounted for 47.8% of cases.

A key reason for underestimations of drowning rates in LMICs is demonstrated in our results—many drowning cases go unreported or are reported as other causes. Only 18% of the deaths identified in our data were issued a death certificate, and only 57% of these mentioned drowning as a cause of death. These findings emphasise the challenges in accurately reporting drowning-related fatalities and suggest a need for improved documentation and standardised coding of drowning deaths. Strengthening the accuracy of cause-of-death reporting could enhance data reliability and inform targeted drowning prevention strategies.

As posited, rural areas experienced significantly higher drowning rates compared with urban areas. This disparity demonstrates that factors such as accessibility to water bodies, swimming skills, emergency response times and health system accessibility play a crucial role in drowning risks. In rural West Bengal, emergency response services are essentially non-existent, and opportunities to train community-based first responders in safe rescue and resuscitation are scarce.12 Previous pathway analyses in rural West Bengal have shown that multiple delays occur post-drowning event due to low availability of trained first responders and healthcare workers, as well as poor accessibility to secondary and tertiary healthcare centres.13 Primary health centres in rural regions are usually ill-equipped to manage drowning cases, with limited oxygen-administration equipment and inconsistent availability of trained staff,14 and hospitals are often too far to access in a timely manner and without the expense of hiring private transportation, often unaffordable for rural families.13 Strengthening of emergency response provision, as well as primary, secondary and tertiary healthcare system components, is essential to addressing drowning burden in rural regions of LMICs. Increasing the presence of primary health centres with resuscitation and oxygen equipment for treatment may encourage families to seek formal treatment rather than administering incorrect resuscitation techniques or seeking a quack doctor.

Overall, drowning rates were higher in males across all age groups, highlighting the need for targeted prevention strategies that address this disparity. These data are different from our findings of child drowning in the Sundarbans region of West Bengal, where we found no gender differences in drowning rates in children aged 1–9 years old,3 and add to the often-contradictory data on gender differences in drowning. Where adult male drowning events can be explained by greater occupational risk, higher risk-taking behaviour and greater alcohol consumption,15 it is still not clear why differences occur in younger children.16 Further research is required to understand if gender differences in behaviour or exposure of young boys need to be catered differently to young girls.

Although we did not directly measure socioeconomic status, we posit that drowning may be correlated with poverty and economic disadvantage in LMICs. Drowning events were overall more prevalent in rural regions, where the poverty rate is 32.5%, compared with urban areas where it is 18.1%.17 Medinipur and Malda Divisions, which face higher rates of rurality and poverty compared with the other three Divisions, experienced the highest rates of drowning. We also found that those who had completed higher levels of education were less frequently represented. Low socioeconomic status impedes families from building safety infrastructure in their homes, forces them to live in remote communities away from health infrastructure, may reduce their ability to provide supervision to children and excludes them from swim training opportunities.18 19 Drowning is an equity issue, and in order to protect vulnerable communities, it must be addressed.

Our data provided insights into interventions that may target factors around drowning and decrease risk. For children under 18 years, 93% of victims did not have an accompanying adult at the time, indicating that supervision may prevent mortality in drowning events. Interventions that prevent children’s access to water and improve supervision, such as fencing of ponds and supervised childcare during at-risk hours, have already been trialled in LMIC contexts and found to be acceptable and feasible.1 13 20 For older children above 6 years, swim training is recommended. However, successful population-level implementation remains highly context-dependent, even within resource-limited settings. The findings also highlighted the critical role of immediate responders, particularly immediate family, neighbours and other caregivers who form the large majority of rescuers in preventing drowning fatalities, and emphasised the importance of timely intervention in such incidents. This can be improved by community-level training in appropriate post-event care, such as empowering parents, potential bystanders and community health workers in safe water rescue and the administration of cardiopulmonary resuscitation, as recommended by the WHO implementation guide for drowning prevention interventions.21

The lowest rates of drowning were in working-age individuals, with notably very few drowning cases reported among fishermen or boatmen, perhaps due to greater capability to avoid water bodies or familiarity with water. These drowning events may be prevented through improved workplace safety conditions, prompt rescue and resuscitation, provision and enforcement of life jacket use, and swim training.1 22 The data also suggested a protective effect of marriage—while the 2011 census estimated 17% of adults were single, widowed or divorced in West Bengal, this population represented 36% of drowning victims in our sample, highlighting the importance of nearby family to respond to events. Concerningly, risk increased as adults aged, particularly in those 70+ years old. This may be due to worsening mobility and health, and possibly even isolation preventing fast rescue. There has been limited research on how elderly drowning may be reduced in LMIC settings, but research in high-income regions suggests educating groups with debilitating medical conditions to avoid water, building safety infrastructure around water bodies (eg, non-slip platforms and rails), physical training to build strength and supervision may be effective if they can be adapted to lower-resource contexts.22 23

Methodological insights and limitations

Due to challenges in identifying enough key informants in urban regions, and difficulties in defining units in more population-dense regions, we increased the urban unit size to 3000 people, as opposed to 1000 people for rural regions. The larger unit size may increase the chance of missing drowning events during the group interview stage, especially if urban communities are less socially connected than rural communities. A validation study would provide insight into the appropriateness of the CKA in capturing drowning events in an urban setting.

Conclusions

The current study provided crucial insights into drowning rates in an LMIC context—the state of West Bengal, India. Data on drowning in LMICs are scarce due to poor data collection, poor reporting of drowning events and inadequate classifications of injuries and deaths. Our study revealed drowning rates in the state are almost three times those estimated by empirical sources, underscoring the need for similar studies to be conducted in other regions with high-risk geographies. Once true burdens are identified, targeted action can be taken to reduce the risk in these vulnerable and underserved populations.
