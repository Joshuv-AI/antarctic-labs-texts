PUFAs-enriched oil bodies as natural anti-osteoarthritis drug delivery platforms
Authors: Lin Shen, Junyan Li, Xinyu Zhang, Quanzhou Wu, Hongkai Qi, Chao Lou, Zhang Chen, Lingfeng Wu, Xiaoxiao Chen, Fengkai Qiu, Minjiang Chen, Xiaokun Li, Zhouguang Wang, Ye Zhu, Dengwei He, Jiansong Ji, Gaofeng Shu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteoarthritis (OA) is a prevalent degenerative condition that leads to cartilage degradation, joint pain, and functional impairment, with few effective treatment options available. Curcumin (Cur), a polyphenol with potential therapeutic benefits for OA, is constrained by its poor solubility and bioavailability. In this study, we developed an innovative Cur delivery platform utilizing flaxseed-derived oil bodies (OBs), which are naturally enriched with ω-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) that exhibit significant anti-inflammatory and antioxidant characteristics. We demonstrated that OBs could efficiently encapsulate Cur via a pH-driven method, significantly improving its aqueous dispersibility and bioavailability. The Cur-loaded OBs (Cur/OBs) exhibited superior anti-inflammatory and antioxidative effects by reducing intracellular ROS, alleviating inflammation, and preventing chondrocyte apoptosis. Additionally, Cur/OBs promoted the polarization of M1 pro-inflammatory macrophages to M2 anti-inflammatory macrophages through the PPARγ/NF-κB signaling pathway. In an experimental rat OA model, Cur/OBs significantly mitigated the progression of OA, outperforming free Cur and OBs alone. These findings suggest that PUFAs-enriched OBs are an effective carrier for Cur, offering a synergistic therapeutic strategy for OA.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Curcumin, Oil bodies, Macrophage polarization, Synergistic treatment

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA), characterized by a gradual degradation of articular cartilage, is a common degenerative joint disease predominantly affecting the elderly population [1,2]. OA frequently results in the persistent joint pain, inflammation and physical disability, which is also referred to as the incurable but non-fatal disease [2]. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are commonly used in conservative treatments to alleviate symptoms of OA [3]. However, NSAIDs only provide temporary pain relief and may lead to adverse effects, including the development of peptic ulcers [4]. In the advanced stages of OA, joint replacement is recognized as a viable treatment option, but it comes with surgical risks and considerable financial costs [5]. Therefore, it is crucial to explore a novel avenue for effective treatment of OA.

It has been well established that the imbalance of M1/M2-type synovial macrophages plays a crucial role in the regulation of joint inflammation [6]. M1-type macrophages display a pro-inflammatory phenotype that could secrete various inflammatory cytokines including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), which contribute to the joint cartilage damage [7,8]. In addition, M1-type macrophages produce excessive levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and their long-term presence can lead to hyper-peroxidation, protein carbonylation and DNA damage, which in turn disrupt chondrocyte function [9,10]. In contrast, M2-type macrophages exhibit an anti-inflammatory phenotype that could secrete anti-inflammatory factors such as interleukin-10 (IL-10), which are essential for vascular remodeling and tissue repair [[11], [12], [13]]. Therefore, the application of biocompatible therapeutic agents with strong ability to shift M1-type macrophages into M2 phenotype and reduce the intracellular ROS levels presents a highly promising approach for treating OA.

Curcumin (Cur) is a naturally occurring polyphenolic compound derived from turmeric (Curcuma longa) [14]. Due to the presence of phenolic and methoxy groups, Cur exhibits a powerful antioxidant activity, even showing a greater capability than vitamin E in scavenging free radicals [15,16]. Moreover, Cur can polarize M1-type macrophages into M2 phenotype by regulating a variety of signaling pathways, thus effectively inhibiting the release of inflammatory factors, making it useful for the treatment of many inflammatory diseases [17,18].Despite its promising therapeutic potential, Cur has been studied in various clinical trials with limited success, and its poor water solubility and low bioavailability are major factors contributing to its inefficiency [19,20]. Hence, it is crucial to design suitable delivery systems to overcome the aforementioned limitations of Cur.

Oil bodies (OBs) is a specialized organelle abundantly found in plant seeds at nanoscale and microscale, primarily responsible for lipid storage [21,22].OBs consists of a central core filled with triacylglycerol, which is surrounded by a monolayer composed of phospholipids and proteins [23,24]. As a natural carrier, OBs has the ability to efficiently encapsulate lipophilic active compounds without the need for additional surfactants during preparation. Notably, the OBs derived from some types of plant seeds (e.g. flaxseed and perilla seeds) are rich in Omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (ω-3 PUFAs), which also display potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities [[25], [26], [27]]. Moreover, evidences have supported the notion that PUFAs could act as effective modulators in preventing or slowing the onset and progression of OA [[28], [29], [30]].Therefore, we proposed that OBs rich in PUFAs may not only serve as an efficient carrier for Cur delivery, but also amplify its therapeutic efficacy in treating OA through a synergistic effect.

In this study, flaxseed-derived OBs enriched with ω-3 PUFAs were developed as a natural delivery system for Cur, aiming to provide a synergistic therapeutic effect for OA (Scheme 1). pH-driven method is a green, simple and low-energy-consuming encapsulation method for phenolic compounds [31,32]. Relevant literature has confirmed that the pH-driven method can effectively encapsulate Cur into OBs [22,33], which could therefore significantly enhance its aqueous dispersibility and bioavailability. In addition, the synergistic effect between the drug and carrier resulted in Cur/OBs showing a considerably stronger ability to eliminate intracellular ROS, alleviate the inflammatory response, and protect chondrocytes from apoptosis compared to free Cur and bare OBs. In-depth exploration demonstrated that Cur/OBs facilitated the synergistic polarization of M1-type macrophages into the M2 phenotype, primarily by regulating the PPARγ/NF-κB signaling pathway. As a result, Cur/OBs exhibited significant therapeutic potential in alleviating the progression of OA in a SD rat model, offering a greater efficacy than either free Cur or OBs. Overall, this is the first study to propose OBs as a drug carrier for Cur, enhancing its therapeutic potential in treating inflammatory diseases.

Results and discussion

α-Linolenic acid favors macrophages polarization from M1 to M2 phenotype

The polarization of pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype has become a widely recognized strategy for the effective treatment of OA [34]. Among various anti-inflammatory agents, α-linolenic acid (ALA), a vital omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFAs), has shown promising anti-inflammatory properties, making it a potential candidate for OA therapy [35].Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the potential of ALA inducing the polarization of M1-type macrophages to the M2 phenotype in vitro. In this study, RAW264.7 cells, which serve as an established model of macrophages (designated as M0 macrophages), were first polarized into M1-type macrophages using lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), known potent inducers of M1 polarization [36]. After the polarization, these M1 macrophages were incubated with varying concentrations of ALA for 24 h to assess its effect on macrophage polarization. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that ALA treatment significantly reduced the proportion of M1-type macrophages, as indicated by the decrease in CD86+ cells (Fig. 1A). Conversely, the treatment of ALA resulted in an increase in the proportion of M2-type macrophages, marked by the higher presence of CD206+ cells (Fig. 1A). These findings clearly demonstrate that ALA induces the transformation from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. Furthermore, a dose-dependent manner of ALA on macrophage polarization was observed, with higher concentrations of ALA promoting a greater shift towards the M2 phenotype, as evidenced by the increased percentage of CD206+ cells and the reduced percentage of CD86+ cells (Fig. 1D-1E). To further confirm the polarization, the inflammatory macrophages with different treatment were stained with antibodies targeting iNOS (a specific marker for M1 macrophages) and Arg-1 (a marker for M2 macrophages). Confocal microscopy analysis revealed a marked decrease in the fluorescence intensity of iNOS in macrophages treated with higher ALA concentrations, while a corresponding increase in Arg-1 fluorescence intensity was observed, further supporting the shift towards the M2 phenotype (Fig. 1B-1C). The quantitative analysis results further confirmed that as the concentration of ALA treatment increased, the fluorescence intensity of iNOS weakened, while that of Arg-1 increased (Fig. 1F-1G). Additionally, western blot analysis confirmed the altered expression of iNOS and Arg-1 proteins in response to ALA treatment, with a reduction in iNOS and an elevation in Arg-1 levels in the treated groups, in accordance with the confocal imaging results (Fig. S1). Overall, these results suggest that ALA has the ability to polarize M1-type macrophages into the M2 phenotype, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic option for OA.

Preparation and characterization of Cur/OBs

Flaxseed oil is widely acknowledged as a leading source of ALA, with its content exceeding 50 wt% [37]. Hence, oil bodies (OBs) were first extracted from flaxseed by using aqueous extraction method, which subsequently serve as a natural carrier for delivering Cur (Fig. 2A). The initial OBs paste was comprised of 56.46 ± 1.67 % oil, 42.04 ± 0.83 % water, and 1.5 ± 0.12 % proteins and phospholipids. Afterwards, the characteristics of the prepared OBs were evaluated after a certain dilution. It was found that the resulting OBs have a spherical shape with an average droplet diameter of around 0.94 μm and a surface charge of −41.2 mV, respectively (Fig. 2C–2D, S2A). Then, the macroscopic structure of OBs was examined using confocal microscopy, with the lipids, phospholipids, and proteins specifically labeled using the fluorescent dyes Nile red, Rhodamine DHPE and FITC, respectively [38]. As shown in Fig. S2B, the as-prepared OBs clearly exhibit a central core filled with lipids, which is encased by a monolayer composed of phospholipids and proteins. Furthermore, to analyze the protein composition of OBs, SDS-PAGE analysis was performed (Fig. S2C). The protein bands identified in the OBs were primarily observed at 17 kDa (oleosin), 22 kDa (globulin), and 33 kDa (globulin), respectively. These findings are in accordance with previous reports [22,39].

Subsequently, the encapsulation of Cur within OBs was proceeded by a pH-driven method [22]. It was observed that the resultant Cur-loaded OBs (Cur/OBs) visibly presented a yellow appearance, reflecting the inherent color of Cur (Fig. 2A). The microstructure images of OBs and Cur/OBs were collected by cryo-scanning electron microscopy (cryo-SEM), and the results are shown in Fig. 2B. It could be observed from the cryo-SEM images that OBs and Cur/OBs exhibit spherical morphology, with a distinct network-like structure. This might be due to flaxseed gum covering the surface of flaxseed-derived OBs, which could contribute to the stability of the particles. The different particle size distributions and potential distributions of OBs and Cur/OBs were measured by using a dynamic light scattering (DLS) instrument. The results showed that the droplet size, morphology and ζ-potential of Cur/OBs were measured to be comparable with those of OBs (Fig. 2C-2D), demonstrating that the Cur loading had little impact on the physical characteristics of lipid droplets. The encapsulation efficiency of Cur within Cur/OBs was calculated to be 87.44 % (data not shown), indicating that OBs could be employed as natural carrier for effectively delivering Cur. We found that Cur/OBs displayed a UV-Vis absorbance spectrum with a peak at 425 nm, which was the characteristic peak of Cur (Fig. 2E) [40]. The molecular interaction is determined by Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy [41,42]. FT-IR spectroscopy of Cur, OBs and Cur/OBs were shown in Fig. 2F, the conjugated C=O/C=C vibration band of Cur in the 1666-1682 cm−1 rang showed a significant shift towards lower wavenumbers and broadens after being loaded into the OBs. In addition, the phenolic hydroxyl O-H absorption of Cur (∼3535-3540 cm−1) showed band shape alteration (mild broadening/intensity adjustment) in Cur/OBs, while the C-O/C-O-C stretching band in the fingerprint region (∼1300-1100 cm−1) also has a slight deviation. These changes indicate that Cur is stabilized within the OBs by hydrogen bonding together with hydrophobic/van der Waals interactions. Moreover, confocal microscopy images could clearly observe that OBs emitted fluorescence signals indicative of Cur, which was evenly distributed within the oil droplets (Fig. 2G). These results collectively suggest that Cur/OBs was successfully formulated by a pH-driven method in our study.

After confirming the successful encapsulation of Cur within OBs, the drug release behavior was assessed using a dialysis bag method. As shown in Fig. 2H, the cumulative release profiles of Cur/OBs at pH 7.4 and pH 6.5 are highly similar, with no significant difference in release rate. This might be because OBs is stabilized by a monolayer of phospholipids and oil proteins, and do not contain acid-labile or pH-responsive groups. Moreover, we found that Cur/OBs was quite stable without significant change in droplet sizes and Cur concentration after 21 days of storage at 4 °C (Fig. 2I-2J). In addition, to evaluate the reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging potential of Cur/OBs, we employed DPPH radical and ABTS radical scavenging experiments to assess their ability to neutralize ROS. The experimental results demonstrate that Cur/OBs significantly enhance the ROS scavenging capacity of both Cur and OBs, effectively reducing DPPH radical and ABTS radical levels in vitro (Fig. 2K-2L). These findings suggest that the incorporation of Cur into OBs may synergistically improve the antioxidant properties of both components, thereby improving their collective ability to combat oxidative stress.

Cellular uptake, ROS scavenging ability, and protect effect on chondrocytes in vitro

Before evaluating the cellular uptake behavior, the in vitro cytotoxicity of Cur/OBs against RAW264.7 cells was tested via the MTT assay. As illustrated in Fig. S3, free Cur showed an obvious toxicity towards the tested cells in a dose-dependent manner, with the IC50 value calculated to be 7.247 μg/mL. Compared to free Cur, Cur/OBs demonstrate a significantly improved IC50 value of 36.08 μg/mL, highlighting the potential of OBs as a delivery vehicle to significantly enhance the safety profile of Cur. Afterwards, the cellular uptake of Cur/OBs by RAW264.7 cells over time was evaluated, with free Cur serving as a control group. Both confocal microscopy and flow cytometry revealed a time-dependent reduction in the fluorescence intensity of Cur within cells treated with free Cur (Fig. 3A-3B). In line with the findings of this study, previous studies have documented the rapid cellular uptake of free Cur, followed by a decrease in fluorescence intensity over time, likely resulting from the degradation of unprotected Cur [43,44]. In contrast, the fluorescence intensity of Cur in Cur/OBs-treated cells increased progressively and stabilized after 4 h (Fig. 3A-3B), suggesting that OBs could considerably improve the intracellular retention of Cur.

Then, the ROS scavenging ability of Cur/OBs was investigated by measuring intracellular ROS levels in macrophages using DCFH-DA. DCFH-DA, a probe selective for ROS, is oxidized by cellular ROS to form fluorescent DCF, with the fluorescence intensity serving as an inverse indicator of intracellular ROS levels [45]. Confocal microscopy analysis revealed a remarkable higher green fluorescence intensity in M1-type macrophages compared to M0-type macrophages, confirming a notable upregulation of intracellular ROS levels in M1-type macrophages. Both free Cur and OBs demonstrated the ability to scavenge ROS, as reflected by the notably diminished green fluorescence in M1-type macrophages treated with these agents, compared to the untreated M1-type macrophages (Fig. 3C). Notably, the most pronounced reduction in intracellular green fluorescence intensity was observed in M1-type macrophages treated with Cur/OBs, demonstrating its superior ROS scavenging capability compared to the other treatment groups (Fig. 3C). The intracellular ROS level of macrophages after different treatments was further quantified by flow cytometry (Fig. 3D). In agreement with the confocal microscopy results, flow cytometry analysis indicated that M1-type macrophages treated with Cur/OBs exhibited the lowest intracellular ROS levels, further highlighting its superior antioxidant activity.

The protective effect of Cur/OBs on chondrocyte activity was subsequently assessed. In this experiment, the conditioned medium (CM) from M1-type macrophages treated with various treatments were collected and incubated with chondrocyte cells for 24 h (Fig. 3E). As shown in Fig. 3F, the CM collected from untreated M1 macrophages caused substantial damage to chondrocyte cells, resulting in an activity level of approximately 48.77 ± 2.08 %. In contrast, the CM collected from M1+Cur group, M1+OBs group and M1+Cur/OBs group resulted in a cell viability of 73.16 ± 4.08 %, 77.57 ± 0.76 % and 85.63 ± 1.94 %, respectively. This result suggests that Cur/OBs effectively protect chondrocytes from inflammation-induced death, providing superior protection compared to free Cur and OBs. Additionally, the apoptotic percentages of ATDC5 chondrocytes following different treatments were assessed using flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 3G and S4, 59.62 ± 0.36 % of apoptotic cells were observed in the M1 group. However, this value was reduced to 46.27 ± 1.12 %, 51.93 ± 1.02 % and 35.37 ± 1.36 % after treatment with free Cur, OBs, and Cur/OBs, respectively. These findings further reinforce the potential of Cur/OBs in protecting ATDC5 chondrocytes from the damaging effects induced by inflammatory agents.

Cur/OBs regulates macrophage polarization via PPARγ/NF-κB signaling pathway

Given the excellent ability of Cur/OBs to alleviate the intracellular oxidative stress in M1-type macrophages, we proceeded to evaluate its role in regulating the polarization of RAW264.7 cell-differentiated macrophages. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that the proportion of M1-type macrophages decreased from 88.57 ± 0.72 % in the control group to 47.20 ± 1.75 %, 42.13 ± 1.72 %, and 36.47 ± 0.84 % after treatment with free Cur, OBs, and Cur/OBs, respectively (Fig. S5A–S5B). In contrast, the proportion of M2-type macrophages increased from 31.43 ± 3.65 % to 59.80 ± 1.81 %, 59.40 ± 0.80 %, and 72.70 ± 1.10 % following the same treatments (Fig. S5A–S5C). These results indicate that Cur/OBs not only markedly suppress M1 macrophage polarization but also effectively promote the repolarization toward the M2 phenotype. To further confirm the immunoregulatory effects observed in RAW264.7 macrophages, the anti-inflammatory activity of Cur/OBs was validated in primary bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) (Fig. S6). Consistent with the previous findings, Cur/OBs also markedly suppressed the inflammatory response in BMDMs, further supporting their potent ability to modulate macrophage polarization. In addition, the immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 4A) and Western blot analysis (Fig. S7) of the M1 marker iNOS and the M2 marker Arg-1 further corroborated the flow cytometry results, demonstrating a consistent shift from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype following Cur/OBs treatment. These findings suggest that Cur/OBs promotes the polarization of M1-type macrophages into the M2 phenotype more effectively than either free Cur or OBs. To further assess the state of macrophage polarization, cytokine secretion profiles were analyzed by using ELISA kits, with particular focus on TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 released by M1-type macrophages [7,8]. It was observed that both free Cur and OBs could prevent M1-type macrophages from secreting TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, but their efficiency was significantly lower than that of Cur/OBs (Fig. 4C-4E). Additionally, the mRNA expression levels of inflammatory and anti-inflammatory factors were further assessed. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis showed that Cur/OBs significantly suppressed the mRNA expression of pro-inflammatory factors IL-1β and TNF-α, while significantly promoting the mRNA expression of the anti-inflammatory factor IL-4 (Fig. S5D–S5F). Collectively, these results further reinforce that the combination of Cur and OBs could synergistically promote the conversion of M1-type macrophages into the M2 phenotype, thereby effectively reducing the harmful inflammatory response.

To further explore the potential anti-inflammatory mechanism of Cur/OBs, RNA-seq analysis was conducted on M1-type macrophages in the control and Cur/OBs-treated group. The volcano plots demonstrated that Cur/OBs treatment induced 2657 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), with 1332 genes being upregulated and 1325 genes downregulated (Fig. 4F). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis indicated that the anti-inflammatory effects of Cur/OBs were associated with various signaling pathways including PPAR signaling pathway, fatty acid metabolism, TNF signaling pathway, HIF-1 signaling pathway and so on (Fig. 4G). It is well accepted that the PPARγ/NF-κB axis is a critical target for treating inflammatory diseases, where PPARγ activation promotes anti-inflammatory M2 polarization by suppressing NF-κB-driven pro-inflammatory M1 polarization [[46], [47], [48]]. Notably, PUFAs are natural ligands for PPARγ activation [49], and both PUFAs and Cur have been shown to attenuate inflammation through the inhibition of the NF-κB signaling pathway [[50], [51], [52]].Therefore, we proposed that Cur/OBs reduce the inflammatory response through a mechanism associated with the regulation of the PPARγ/NF-κB signaling pathway. To verify our hypothesis, western blot analysis was therefore performed to examine the expression levels of PPARγ, NF-κB p65, and Phospho-NF-κB p65 proteins in M1-type macrophages treated with different conditions. It was found that both free Cur and OBs treatment significantly enhanced PPARγ expression while reducing Phospho-NF-κB p65 levels in M1-type macrophages, however, their efficiency was substantially less than that of Cur/OBs (Fig. 4H). These results confirm that the combination of Cur and OBs equips Cur/OBs with a dual ability to activate the PPARγ signaling pathway and inhibit the NF-κB signaling pathway (Fig. 4I), thereby significantly promoting macrophage polarization from the M1 to the M2 phenotype and reducing the inflammatory response.

Cur/OBs delays the progress of OA in vivo

Given the outstanding in vitro anti-inflammatory and antioxidant capabilities of Cur/OBs, an in vivo study was carried out to examine its therapeutic potential in a rat model of OA induced by destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) [53]. The rats were randomly divided into 5 groups on the 14th day post-surgery, with the sham-operated rats used as the healthy controls. The DMM-induced OA rats were received intra-articular injections of saline, free Cur, OBs, and Cur/OBs quintic, with a 7-day interval between each injection. On day 56, the joints from rats in each experimental group were harvested for subsequent analysis (Fig. 5A).

As a chronic degenerative joint disease, OA typically manifests pathological features such as subarticular cartilage destruction and subchondral osteosclerosis [54]. We therefore employed microcomputed tomography (Micro CT) imaging to examine the effects of Cur/OBs on blocking bone damage. The three-dimensional (3D) reconstructions of knee joint images from the Saline group clearly demonstrated the typical OA-related alterations, including the formation of osteophytes and joint deformities (Fig. 5B). In contrast, the treatment with free Cur and OBs alone showed a positive effect in delaying the formation of osteophytes and joint deformities. Notably, the Cur/OBs treatment group exhibited minimal morphological alterations, revealing a stronger protective effect on joint integrity (Fig. 5B). The micro-CT-based quantitative analysis further validated the excellent therapeutic efficacy of Cur/OBs in the OA. The microstructural changes in the subchondral bone were further assessed by measuring bone volume percentage (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular number (Tb.N), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) across all groups (Fig. 5C-5F). Compared to the sham group, the DMM-induced OA rat model exhibited significantly increased BV/TV, Tb.N, and Tb.Th, while Tb.Sp was notably decreased. However, treatment with Cur/OBs effectively reduced certain bone microstructure parameters (BV/TV, Tb.N, and Tb.Th) and increased others (Tb.Sp) in the DMM-induced OA model. These findings suggest that Cur/OBs treatment could effectively alleviate OA by inhibiting joint space narrowing and mitigating subchondral osteosclerosis. Taking together, these results suggest that Cur/OBs is capable of effectively preserving cartilage and inhibiting the progression of OA.

To further assess the therapeutic effects of Cur/OBs, the knee joints from various experimental groups were subjected to histological analysis using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and safranine O-fast green (S&F) staining. As illustrated in Fig. 6A, the OA group exhibited abnormal cartilage layers with substantial wear, indicating severe joint damage. In contrast, treatment with Cur, OBs, and Cur/OBs demonstrated protective effects by slowing the progression of cartilage degradation in OA. Remarkably, The Cur/OBs treatment group displayed a well-organized chondrocyte arrangement and minimal cartilage damage, closely resembling the Sham group. Among all treatment groups, we found that Cur/OBs treatment resulted in a lower Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) score, which serves as a key indicator for evaluating the histological features associated with OA progression (Fig. 5G). These results further support that the combination of Cur and OBs significantly alleviates joint inflammation and offers enhanced protection against cartilage degradation in OA.

Subsequently, the polarization of macrophages in the joint during Cur/OBs treatment was assessed using immunofluorescence staining for M1 and M2 markers. As shown in Fig. 6A, the OA group exhibited a significant increase in the fluorescence intensity of iNOS, reflecting a predominance of M1 macrophages. As compared to free Cur and OBs, Cur/OBs treatment resulted in a significant reduction in iNOS fluorescence intensity, along with a marked increase in Arg-1 fluorescence intensity (Fig. 6A). In addition, the levels of IL-6 and IL-1β expression in the joint were assessed after Cur/OBs treatment. The results clearly showed that Cur/OBs treatment was more effective in reducing the expression levels of IL-6 and IL-1β in the joint than either free Cur or OBs (Fig. 6A). Immunofluorescence analysis of joint tissue sections revealed that treatment with Cur, OBs, and Cur/OBs resulted in an increased fluorescence intensity of PPARγ and a decreased fluorescence intensity of Phospho-NF-κB p65. Notably, the Cur/OBs group exhibited the most pronounced regulatory effect. These results further confirm that the anti-inflammatory activity of Cur/OBs is mediated by activation of the PPARγ signaling pathway and inhibition of the NF-κB pathway (Fig. S8). The quantitative results are consistent with the above results (Fig. 6B). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that Cur/OBs induces a transition from pro-inflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages in the OA synovium, offering a promising strategy to mitigate inflammation in osteoarthritis.

In vivo distribution and biosafety of Cur/OBs

Firstly, an in vivo distribution study was performed using OBs encapsulated with DiR, a near-infrared fluorescent dye, to track the retention of Cur/OBs. The DiR/OBs were carefully administered into the knee joint cavity of the C57BL/6 mice, followed by tracing the time-dependent fluorescence signals using an IVIS imaging system. As depicted in Fig. 7A-7B, the fluorescence intensity of DiR/OBs gradually diminished over time. By day 7, a faint fluorescence signal of DiR/OBs was detectable in the knee joint of the OA rat. Additionally, the fluorescence intensity of DiR/OBs was not detected in the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidneys and intestine) at 48h after the intra-articular injection (Fig. S7). These findings suggest that Cur/OBs exhibited a long-term retention in the knee joint cavity without causing obvious impact on the other major organs. To assess the in vivo safety of Cur/OBs, histopathological examination of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) was conducted following treatment with various formulations using H&E staining. No notable pathological alterations were detected in the tissues from the Cur, OBs or Cur/OBs treatment groups when compared to the sham group (Fig. 7C), suggesting that Cur/OBs exhibited an outstanding biocompatibility in vivo.

Conclusion

In this study, we engineered a novel natural delivery platform by utilizing flaxseed-derived OBs enriched with ω-3 PUFAs for Cur delivery, aiming for an enhanced treatment of OA. The Cur was efficiently encapsulated within the OBs by using a pH-driven method, effectively overcoming its limitations related to solubility and bioavailability. The prepared Cur/OBs demonstrated a superior therapeutic efficacy by reducing intracellular ROS, alleviating inflammation, and promoting M1-to-M2 macrophage polarization via the PPARγ/NF-κB pathway. In vivo evaluations using a rat OA model demonstrated that Cur/OBs administration markedly attenuated OA progression, showing superior therapeutic outcomes compared to both free Cur and bare OBs. Although only a single dose was used in the current in vivo studies, future research will include dose gradient experiments to further determine the treatment window and clinical relevance. Overall, this study not only establishes OBs as a promising natural carrier for Cur delivery but also presents a synergistic and biocompatible therapeutic strategy for OA intervention. The findings provide substantial evidence for the potential application of natural delivery systems in the management of inflammatory disorders, opening new avenues for further research in this field.

Materials and methods

Materials

The flaxseed was sourced from a local market in Lishui City, China. Curcumin (C1386) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5 diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) was purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (HY-D1056), DiR (HY-D1048) and DCFH-DA (HY-D0940) were purchased from MedChemExpress (Shanghai, China). The interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) (AF-315-05) was purchased from Thermo fisher Scientific (USA). The β-actin (AC004), HRP-conjugated Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (AS014), and HRP-conjugated Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) (AS003) were purchased from ABclonal (Wuhan, China). The CD86 antibody (159204), CD206 antibody (141708) were purchased from Biolegend (USA). The Arg-1 antibody (16001-1-AP) were purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). The iNOS antibody (ab178945) was purchased from Abcam (USA). The PPAR-γ antibody (2443T) and Phospho-NF-κB p65 antibody (3033T) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (USA). The NF-κB p65 antibody (AF5006) was purchased from Affinity (Liyang, China). All other reagents used in this study were of analytical grade and were employed without any additional purification.

Cell lines and animals

RAW264.7 were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) (Manassas, VA, USA). ATDC5 cells was purchased from IMMOCELL (Xiamen, China). RAW264.7 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin, and incubated at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. To induce M1-type macrophage differentiation, RAW264.7 cells were treated with 100 ng/mL LPS and 20 ng/mL IFN-γ for 24 h. ATDC5 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin, and maintained at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. To promote chondrocyte differentiation, ATDC5 cells were treated with insulin-transferrin-selenium (ITS) medium for 7 days.

C57BL/6 male mice (4–6 weeks) and Sprague-Dawley (SD) male rats (200–250 g) were purchased from procured from Shanghai Silaike Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The animal procedures were carried out in compliance with Lishui University's Guidelines for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee at Lishui University.

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated from the femur and tibia of 6-8-week-old C57BL/6 mice. Cells were rinsed with PBS, filtered, and cultured in a complete medium supplemented with 20 ng/mL M-CSF in a 5 % CO2 humidified incubator at 37 °C for 7 days to differentiate into BMDMs. On the 3rd and 5th days, half of the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing M-CSF. The cells to be differentiated adhere and are used for subsequent experiments.

Preparation of Cur-loaded oil bodies (Cur/OBs)

First, ω-3 PUFAs-enriched OBs were extracted from flaxseed using an aqueous extraction approach. In brief, 20 g of flaxseed was immersed in 140 mL of Milli-Q water and allowed to soak overnight at 4 °C. Following this, the mixture was centrifuged, and the supernatant was replaced with an equal volume of water. The soaked seeds were then processed using a commercial juicer (Joyoung, China) for 90 s. The resultant slurry was filtered through a filter membrane to remove solid residue. The filtrate was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 30 min, and the upper creamy layer was carefully collected. The crude OBs was redispersed in distilled water and centrifuged again to remove free water-soluble substances. After repeated washing 3 times, the purified OBs were collected and re-dispersed in water for further studies.

Subsequently, the flaxseed OBs were utilized as a natural carrier for Cur loading through a pH-driven method, following the procedure outlined previously with minor modifications [22]. Briefly, 2 mg of Cur was dissolved in 5 mL of an aqueous solution containing 10 wt% OBs. The pH of the mixture was then adjusted to 11.0, followed by continuous stirring for 2 min to ensure complete dissolution of Cur in the aqueous phase. Subsequently, the pH of the mixture was then rapidly adjusted back to 7.0. The resulting mixture was stirred at room temperature for 15 min in the dark. After stirring, the mixture was centrifuged and the upper yellow phase containing the Cur loaded OBs (Cur/OBs) was collected. To determine the drug encapsulation efficiency, Cur was extracted from Cur/OBs by ethanol. The mixture was treated by ultrasonic for 30 min and then centrifuged for 10 min at 5000 rpm to obtain the upper solution. The concentration of Cur in OBs was measured by ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometer (UV-1900i, Shimadzu, Japan) under 425 nm. Characterization of Cur/OBs

The droplet size, size distribution, and zeta potential of OBs and Cur/OBs were measured using a dynamic light scattering instrument (Zetasizer Lab ZSU3100, Malvern, UK). The macroscopic structure of OBs was examined using a confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, Leica STELLARIS 5, Germany), in which 1 mL of OBs was labeled with 10 μL of Nile red (1 mg/mL in ethanol), 5 μL FITC (20 mg/mL in ethanol) and rhodamine DHPE (1 mg/mL in ethanol), respectively. To assess the stability of Cur/OBs, the change in droplet size was monitored after 28 days of storage at 4 °C. The protein bands of OBs were analyzed using the SDS-PAGE method, following the protocol outlined in a previous study [22].

In vitro drug release behavior of Cur/OBs

The in vitro release curve of Cur/OBs was determined using a dialysis method. briefly, the Cur/OBs was placed in a dialysis bag (MWCO: 3500 Da) and immersed in a 20 mL of PBS containing 1 % Tween 80. The dialysis bag was placed in an oscillator (ZHICHENG, Shanghai, China) and then shaken at 200 rpm (37 °C). At a predetermined time point, 1 mL of the dissolved solution was withdrawn, and an equal volume of fresh medium was supplemented. The concentration of Cur in the samples was measured using a microplate reader (TECAN, Switzerland).

In vitro antioxidant activity of Cur/OBs

The scavenging ability of Cur/OBs against DPPH and ABTS free radicals was assessed using a DPPH free radical scavenging kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China) and an ABTS free radical scavenging kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China), respectively. Briefly, free Cur, OBs, and Cur/OBs were incubated separately with DPPH and ABTS solutions at room temperature. The absorbance was then measured at 515 nm and 734 nm, respectively, using microplate reader. The free radical scavenging percentage was calculated according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Cellular uptake study

The RAW264.7 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h under standard culture conditions. After culture medium replacement, the cells were treated with either free Cur or Cur/OBs at an equivalent drug concentration. Following incubation at designated time points, the cells were washed with PBS three times. The internalization efficiency of free Cur and Cur/OBs was evaluated by two methods: (1) CLSM imaging for subcellular localization, using DAPI to counterstain nuclei, and (2) fluorescence intensity quantification, analyzed by flow cytometry (BD FACSCanto II).

Cytotoxicity evaluation

The cytotoxicity of Cur/OBs on RAW264.7 cells was evaluated using the MTT assay. Briefly, RAW264.7 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h under standard culture conditions. Following adherence, the cells were incubated with fresh medium containing various concentrations of free Cur, OBs, or Cur/OBs for 24 h. After the incubation period, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing MTT (0.5 mg/mL), and the cells were incubated for an additional 4 h at 37 °C. After removing the medium, DMSO was added to dissolve the formazan crystals, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader to quantify the cell viability.

Cellular ROS measurement

The cellular ROS scavenging ability of Cur/OBs was evaluated using the fluorescent DCFH-DA probe. Briefly, RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well and polarized to M1-type macrophages using LPS and IFN-γ. After polarization, the M1-type macrophages were treated with free Cur, OBs, or Cur/OBs at an equivalent Cur concentration (5 μg/mL) for 24 h. Following treatment, the cells were washed with PBS to remove excess reagents and incubated with DCFH-DA at a final concentration of 2 μM for 30 min in the dark at 37 °C. The cells were then thoroughly washed to remove any unincorporated DCFH-DA. Finally, the fluorescence intensity, which correlates with intracellular ROS levels, was measured using a fluorescence microscope or flow cytometry.

Macrophage polarization assessment

RAW264.7 cells or BMDMs were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well and polarized into M1-type macrophages using LPS and IFN-γ. After polarization, the M1-type macrophages were treated with free Cur, OBs, or Cur/OBs at an equivalent Cur concentration (5 μg/mL) for 24 h. Following treatment, the cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.3 % Triton X-100 to enable antibody penetration. To block non-specific binding, the cells were incubated with 2 % BSA. Subsequently, the cells were stained with anti-CD86 and anti-CD206 antibodies, which are markers for M1 and M2 macrophages, respectively. After primary antibody incubation, fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies were applied, along with DAPI, a nuclear-specific stain, for counterstaining. The stained cells were then observed using confocal microscopy to visualize the expression of M1 and M2 markers. Additionally, the proportions of M1 (CD86+) and M2 (CD206+) macrophages within the samples were analyzed quantitatively by flow cytometry.

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed to assess the expression of iNOS, Arg-1, PPAR-γ, NF-κB p65, and Phospho-NF-κB p65 proteins. Cell lysates were prepared by adding a lysis buffer (RIPA) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors in a ratio of 100:1:1. The protein concentration was quantified using the Coomassie Brilliant Blue method. An appropriate separation gel was selected based on the molecular weights of the target proteins. A total of 10 μg of protein from each sample was loaded into each lane and separated by SDS-PAGE. Following electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane, which was then blocked with 5 % skim milk for 1 h. The membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (β-actin, iNOS, Arg-1, PPAR-γ, NF-κB p65 and Phospho-NF-κB p65) diluted at 1:1000. After washing, a secondary antibody was applied at room temperature for 2 h. Western blot images were visualized using a gel imaging system (Invitrogen iBright, USA).

ELISA detection of cytokines

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well and polarized into M1-type macrophages using LPS and IFN-γ. After polarization, the M1-type macrophages were treated with free Cur, OBs, or Cur/OBs at an equivalent Cur concentration (5 μg/mL) for 24 h. Following treatment, the supernatant of culture medium was collected, and the levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β were quantified using commercially available ELISA kits (MeiMian, Yancheng, China).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well and polarized into M1-type macrophages using LPS and IFN-γ. After polarization, the M1-type macrophages were treated with free Cur, OBs, or Cur/OBs at an equivalent Cur concentration (5 μg/mL) for 24 h. After, the total RNA was extracted from the cells using RNA Rapid Extraction kit (ES SCIENCE, Shanghai, China). cDNA was synthesized using a cDNA Synthesis Kit (Yeasen, Shanghai, China). qRT-PCR was conducted to amplify the cDNA using a 7500 Real-timedetection system (Thermo fisher Scientific, USA). The primers used in this work were designed by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Macrophages conditioned medium (CM) collection

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well and polarized into M1-type macrophages using LPS and IFN-γ. After polarization, the M1-type macrophages were treated with free Cur, OBs, or Cur/OBs at an equivalent Cur concentration (5 μg/mL) for 24 h. Following treatment, the supernatant of culture medium was collected by centrifugation and then stored at −80 °C for further experiments. The CM from macrophages was then mixed with an equal volume of serum-free DMEM and then introduced into the culture dish for chondrocyte culture.

Apoptosis detection in ATDC5 chondrocytes

The cell apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V-FITC apoptosis detection kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Briefly, the ATDC5 chondrocytes were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well and incubated overnight. Subsequently, the cells were treated with CM of various treatment groups for 24 h. After incubation, the cells were collected, centrifuged at 1000 g for 5 min, and resuspended in Annexin V/PI binding solution for 15 min at room temperature. The cells were then washed with PBS, and apoptosis analysis was performed using flow cytometry.

In vivo biodistribution

The biodistribution of Cur/OBs was evaluated using an IVIS Spectrum imaging system (IVIS Lumina Series III, PerkinElmer, USA). Prior to imaging, OBs were labeled with DiR, a near-infrared fluorescent dye. The DiR-labeled OBs (DiR/OBs) were then injected into the knee joint of mice. At predetermined time points, the distribution of DiR/OBs was tracked using the IVIS imaging system. In a separate experiment, the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney and intestine) along with the knee joint were harvested at 48h and imaged to observe fluorescence signals under the same imaging parameters.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy

The OA rat model was established using the destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) method, following a previously described protocol [53]. Briefly, the rats were anesthetized, and a 1 cm longitudinal incision was made along the right knee. The joint capsule was carefully opened to expose the medial meniscotibial ligament (MMTL), which attaches the anterior horn of the medial meniscus to the tibial plateau. The MMTL was meticulously transected, resulting in the destabilization of the medial meniscus. In the sham-operated group, the joint capsule was opened, and the incision was immediately sutured without further intervention. Two weeks after the DMM model was established, the rats were intra-articularly injected with saline, free Cur, OBs, or Cur/OBs at weeks 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6. The administration dose of curcumin is 0.4 mg/kg, and the corresponding dose of the carrier OBs is 114 mg/kg. At week 8, the rats were sacrificed, and their knee joints were harvested for subsequent evaluation.

Histopathological examination and micro-CT analysis

After euthanizing the rats at the study endpoint, the OA joints and major organs were harvested and immediately fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde. The keen joint from each group were scanned using a small animal micro-computed tomography system (SKYSCAN 1276, Bruker, Germany). Moreover, the keen joints of each group were decalcified in 10 % EDTA decalcification solution for 8 weeks before being embedded in paraffin. Histological sections were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and Safranin O-Fast Green (S&F) to assess tissue morphology. The histological changes of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidneys) in each group were assessed using H&E staining.

Histological and immunohistochemical analyses

At the end of the 6-week treatment period, the rats were euthanized, and their knee joints were collected and fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde. The samples were then decalcified in 10 % EDTA (pH 7.4) for eight weeks before being embedded in paraffin wax. Sagittal sections of the medial compartment of the knee joint were cut to a thickness of 4 μm. For immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis, antibodies targeting IL-6 and IL-1β were applied. Immunofluorescence (IF) assays were performed using antibodies against iNOS, Arg-1, PPARγ and Phospho-NF-κB p65.

Statistical analysis

The data were expressed as mean ± standard Error of the Mean (SEM). Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Inc., La Jolla, USA; version 10.0). Student's t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.
