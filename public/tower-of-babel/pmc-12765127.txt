A multiplex PCR for rapid detection of vancomycin and linezolid resistance genes in clinical and environmental enterococci: A one health diagnostic approach
Authors: Yusuf Wada, Nabiha A Bouafia, Basim R Al shammari, Hafeez A Afolabi, Fatimah SM Aljebaly, Mohammed S Almogbel, Mohammed Garout, Muhammad A Halwani, Zainab H Almansour, Ameen SS Alwashmi, Nawal A AlKaabi, Maha F Al-Subaie, Ali A Rabaan, Chan Y Yean, Zaidah Abdul Rahman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) in Enterococcus, particularly vancomycin-resistance (VRE) and linezolid-resistance (LZRE) strains, presents a rising threat to public health globally.MethodsWe designed an assay targeting vanA, vanB, vanC, vanD, optrA, poxtA, cfr and validated it across human and animal Enterococcus isolates. One hundred eighty-six (186) clinical isolates of Enterococcus were retrieved from Microbiology & Parasitology Laboratory, while 300 environmental isolates were obtained from poultry farms. Analytical sensitivity was assessed using successive dilutions of synthetic DNA constructs and bacterial lysates. The diagnostic performance was benchmarked against the automated system for testing of antimicrobial susceptibility, VITEK II.ResultsThe assay demonstrated high analytical sensitivity, detecting synthetic DNA as low as 1 pg and lysates down to 105 CFU/mL. Among 30 clinical VRE isolates, the assay identified VRE genes with a 71.8 % sensitivity, 100 % specificity and an overall 83.6 % accuracy. For LZRE detection, the assay showed 88.9 % sensitivity, 100 % specificity, and 97.1 % accuracy. In environmental isolates, LZRE genes were detected in 3.0 % of poultry samples.ConclusionsThis assay supports integrated AMR surveillance within a One Health framework.Keywords: Antimicrobial resistance, Vancomycin-resistant enterococci, Linezolid resistance, Multiplex PCR, One health, Poultry, Clinical microbiology

Background

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) constitutes a significant danger to global health security which jeopardizes the efficient management of diseases in human, animals and the environment [1,2]. Recognizing this, the World Health Organization (WHO), Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) advocate a One Health approach to address AMR through coordinated surveillance, stewardship, and policy implementation. Their joint action plans emphasize the interconnectedness of health sectors and the need for integrated responses to prevent resistant pathogens from spreading across ecosystems.

Vancomycin- and linezolid-resistant enterococcal species, especially E. faecalis and E. faecium, are among the main causes of this crisis. Often linked to bloodstream infections, urinary tract infections, and intra-abdominal sepsis [3], these Gram-positive microbes have emerged as significant nosocomial agents, particularly among hospitalized and immunocompromised individuals [4,5].

Long known for their clinical and epidemiological effects, vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE) are caused mainly through the acquisition of vanA and vanB gene clusters, which change peptidoglycan synthesis and decrease drug binding[6]. The emergence of vancomycin- and linezolid-resistant Enterococcus is driven by diverse resistance genes with important clinical and environmental implications. VanA and vanB confer high-level vancomycin resistance and are the most clinically significant, commonly linked to hospital outbreaks due to their plasmid- and transposon-mediated transfer. vanC, found intrinsically in E. gallinarum and E. casseliflavus, produces low-level resistance but can still complicate treatment in immunocompromised hosts. vanD, though less common, confers moderate to high-level resistance and has been reported in both clinical and animal isolates, emphasizing zoonotic potential.

Given that linezolid is an essential last-resort treatment for Gram-positive infections which are multidrug-resistant, the emergence of linezolid-resistant enterococci (LZRE) in recent years has raised concerns. 23S rRNA chromosomal changes or the development of transferrable resistance genes, such as poxtA, optrA, and cfr, which provide cross-resistance to several antibiotic classes, are the main causes of linezolid resistance [7,8,9].

Beyond hospitals, VRE and LZRE present a challenge. Increasingly, enterococci have been recovered from food–producing animals, where the overuse or abuse of antibiotics can favour resistant strains that can spread through the environment, the food chain, or direct human contact [10,11]. The interconnection of human, animal and environmental sources represents One Health framework, which stresses that effective AMR mitigation must require coordinated surveillance and control activities across sectors and disciplines [12,13]. Resistant Enterococcus species persist in the environment through contamination from hospitals, agriculture, and wastewater. They survive harsh conditions and disseminate resistance genes, including van and optrA, to other bacteria. Their presence in soil, water, and animal waste highlights environmental reservoirs as critical pathways in the One Health spread of antimicrobial resistance.

Currently, phenotypic and culture-based susceptibility testing are frequently used in the surveillance of resistant Enterococcus strains. Despite being useful, these techniques are time-consuming, difficult, and may miss some resistance genes because of phenotypic heterogeneity or delayed growth [14,15]. On the other hand, molecular diagnostics like polymerase chain reaction (PCR) provide quicker and more precise options. They may identify resistant genes straight from environmental or clinical samples, frequently in a matter of hours. Molecular diagnostics enhance integrated surveillance by enabling rapid, sensitive, and specific detection of pathogens and resistance genes across human, animal, and environmental sectors. They allow for real-time data sharing, genetic linkage of outbreaks, and early identification of emerging resistance mechanisms. This strengthens the One Health framework, promoting coordinated responses, evidence-based interventions, and timely containment of antimicrobial resistance. For AMR surveillance and outbreak response, multiplex PCR is a high-throughput, cost-effective method that enables the concurrent detection of several resistant genes [16,17,18].

This study evaluates the effectiveness of a multiplex PCR technique developed to identify vancomycin- and linezolid- resistance genes in enterococcal species acquired from human clinical specimens and poultry farm environments. This research advances the creation of diagnostic tests compatible with One Health principles, for early identification plus containment of antimicrobial resistance across the human–animal–environment interface by combining clinical microbiology with environmental surveillance.

Materials and methods

The aim of the current investigation was to evaluate the assay's performance in conjunction with the retrospective surveillance of enterococcal genes resistant to linezolid and vancomycin at a poultry farm and a teaching hospital in Kelantan, Malaysia. The study included a retrospective phase in which the assay was utilized to re-identify previously identified storage isolates and quality control strains. Clinical isolates of VRE and farm isolates of LZRE were assessed using the multiplex PCR assay in the prospective phase concurrently with the VITEK II system, the laboratory's standard testing procedure.

Retrospective study

The full protocol for the Optimization of this multiplex PCR assay showing key reaction details and primer sequences has been reported by Wada et al. [17].

Assessment of sensitivity and specificity of multiplex PCR

Using 11 reference strains, the single-tube multiplex PCR (mPCR) sensitivity was assessed (Supplementary Table 1). The multiplex PCR specificity was also assessed by utilising 26 non-target isolates already identified and confirmed (Supplementary Table 2). These clinical isolates used for the sensitivity and specificity evaluation have already been confirmed by the VITEK II Systems (BioMerieux, France) and culture in a diagnostic laboratory in the Department of Medical Microbiology and Parasitology, Universiti Sains Malaysia, Health Campus. A total of 37 bacteria strains were therefore included for the analytical evaluation of the specificity and sensitivity of the mPCR. A 2 μl DNA template from these bacteria strains was used in the evaluation of their analytical performances.

Assessment of analytical sensitivity of reference strains through multiplex PCR at both genomic and bacterial levels

Limit of detection is the lowest concentration detected in ≥95 % of replicates. The analytical sensitivity of the multiplex PCR on reference strains at the genomic level involved the extraction of genomic DNA from the reference strains utilising Qiagen DNA Mini Kits (Qiagen, Germany). The concentration was then adjusted to 10 ng to prepare a 10-fold serial dilution from 10 ng - 1 pg. After which, 2 μl of each serially diluted concentration was used as a template in the assay. Each target was run in triplicates and repeat runs, and the amount of genomic DNA detected was observed and recorded.

At the bacterial level, Reference strains were cultivated in BHI broth overnight at 37 °C. Stock cultures based on the value of OD600 (1 × 109) were prepared. To prepare bacterial lysates consisting of 109–101 CFU/ml, the bacteria were diluted into 10 fold with 900 μl of BHI broth. A 100 μl of each diluted culture was cultured on BHI agar using the spread-plate technique and incubated at 37 °C to ascertain the amount of bacteria in each dilution. However, only culture plates with approximately 50–300 colonies were considered. Further, the supernatant of the serially diluted cultures were discarded and the pellet was rinsed with normal saline after which the boiling method was employed to extract the DNA, which was then used as template. The lowest detection limit of the DNA concentration was therefore observed and recorded.

Prospective study

Sample size calculation for the prospective study

The sample size for evaluating the diagnostic performance of this multiplex PCR assay was determined using Buderer's formula [19] with the assistance of Sample Size Calculator Version 2.0 from Microsoft Excel, which can be accessed at http://wnarifin.github.io [20]. Considering the prevalence rates of patients with VRE at 12.4 % [21] and LZRE at 22.7 % [4] it was determined that a minimum of 186 clinical specimens and 300 environmental samples, respectively, would be required, accounting for a 10 % dropout rate.

Collection of specimens and cultivation of microorganisms

The study's specimens came from poultry farms in the Kelantan area as well as the Department of Medical Microbiology and Parasitology and Hospital Pakar Universiti Sains Malaysia (HPUSM), Kelantan, Malaysia. Three farms were selected using purposive sampling and were designated as C1, C2 and C3 and samples were collected following farmers permission. Variety of samples were received from patients with a range of infection who visited Hospital Pakar USM. The laboratory technologists had first performed regular microbial culture on all of the specimens, which included microscopic and macroscopic examination, and antibiotic susceptibility testing using the VITEK II system. To prevent biases in diagnostic accuracy, the specimen culture status was not known at the time of sampling. Samples were assigned coded identifiers to conceal their origin, ensuring objective analysis and minimizing operator bias. Laboratory activities followed a one-way workflow, progressing unidirectionally from clean to contaminated zones, reagent preparation, extraction, amplification, and detection. Dedicated pipettes, consumables, and personal protective equipment were used in each area, and reverse movement was strictly prohibited. This structured approach prevented cross-contamination, maintained biosafety standards, and ensured the accuracy and reproducibility of molecular diagnostic results. Every obtained specimen was handled as though it were contagious and proceeded through the same process for the blinded multiplex PCR assay examination. Following microbial growth, an aliquot of at least 500 μl to 1 ml from each sample specimen (contingent on the remaining volume) was collected in a 1.5 ml tube for subsequent DNA extraction. All laboratory procedures involving specimens were conducted within a level 2 biological safety cabinet. This research received approval from the Institutional Review Board of the Human Research Ethics Committee at Universiti Sains Malaysia (Reference number: USM (USM/JEPeM/19050308).

Preparing DNA templates and processing samples

Before the DNA extraction technique, each material was subjected to a liquefying solution comprising NaOH and NaLC in a 1:1 volume ratio. The liquefaction process was allowed to proceed for 15 min at ambient temperature. A sample matrix was obtained from 1 ml of liquefied specimen using centrifugation at 13,000 xg for 5 min. For excessively viscous specimens, further mixing with 1 ml of liquefying solution was required to achieve a homogeneous solution. Following the decantation of the supernatant, the specimen pellet was washed with 1 ml of normal saline and subsequently re-centrifuged to get the matrix for the DNA extraction method. DNA extraction from sputum was performed utilising the QIAamp DNA Mini kit (Qiagen, Germany) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions for bacterial DNA purification from blood or bodily fluids. For poultry swabs, following microbial growth, an aliquot of at least 500 μl to 1 ml was collected in a 1.5 ml tube for subsequent DNA extraction using either the boiling method or the QIAamp DNA Mini kit (Qiagen, Germany) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The extracted DNA samples were preserved at −20 °C.

Multiplex polymerase chain reaction evaluation

DNA from samples underwent amplification by PCR utilising an mPCR assay [17]. This test focusses on the 990 bp16S rRNA of the Enterococcus genus, as well as the vancomycin resistance genes; 850 bp vanA, 380 bp vanB, 220 bp vanC, and 120 bp vanD, along with the 550 bp cfr a methyltransferase, 700 bp optrA, and 450 bp poxtA which are an adenosine triphosphate-binding cassette (ABC) transporter for linezolid resistance genes. A 300 bp Vibrio cholerae ctxA was built-in as an internal amplification control.

The assay included optimized concentrations of primers, magnesium chloride, and additional PCR components [17]. The thermocycling parameters for the multiplex PCR assay included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, primer annealing at 64.5 °C for 30 s, and elongation at 72 °C for 30 s, concluding with a final elongation at 72 °C for 5 min. In addition to the multiplex PCR, 16S rRNA PCR was conducted to validate the presence of doubtful bacteria using the following primer pair: 5′-AGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG-3′ (forward) and 5′-GGACTACHAGGGTATCTAAT-3′ (reverse) [22]. The annealing temperature for the 16S rRNA primer was established at 58 °C, adhering to the thermocycling parameters employed for mPCR. PCR amplification products were examined using 2.0 % agarose gel electrophoresis.

The gel comb tray was immersed in 0.5× TBE running buffer in an electrophoresis tank. Following that, PCR product of 8.0 μl was combined with 2 μl of 6× Loading Dye (Thermo Scientific, USA). The mixture was then placed into the gel sample wells, with the first well receiving 8.0 μl of Gene Ruler 100 bp DNA ladder (Thermo Scientific, USA). After that, the tank lid was closed and a current of 100 V (v) was supplied for 90 min. The DNA bands were seen under UV light of an image analyser (ChemilmagerTM 5500, USA) and shot by a gel image documentation system after gel electrophoresis (ChemilmagerTM 5500, USA). By matching the size of the DNA band to marker, the size of the DNA band was estimated.

Statistical evaluation

Sociodemographic data and the prevalence of resistance genes were inputted into a spreadsheet utilising Microsoft Excel 2013. The correlation between patient features and microbiological isolates was examined utilising Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, Chicago) Version 24.0. The Pearson chi-square test and Fisher's exact test were employed, with a significance threshold set at a p-value of less than 0.05. Where p value is significant, a posthoc pairwise comparisons adjusted with Bonferroni was carried to find out the source of the significance. The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and accuracy were assessed for the diagnostic efficacy of the multiplex PCR assay and compared to the VITEK II system, regarded as the gold standard.

Results

Retrospective study phase

In the retrospective phase, a total of 11 reference strains for VRE and LZRE genes were utilized for the sensitivity evaluation which were all correctly detected by the multiplex PCR assay. These target strains included a 550 bp cfr and 450 bp poxtA synthetic dsDNA. Synthetic dsDNA was utilized because of the unavailability of the cfr and poxtA reference strains.

The result on Supplementary Fig. 1 show the amplification of all 11 target reference strains as expected. 26 non intended targets were utilized for the specificity evaluation and the multiplex PCR did not amplify targets as expected (Supplementary Fig. 2, 3)

Following the evaluation of the sensitivity and the specificity of the multiplex PCR, a 100 % sensitivity (95 % CI) result was observed in the target organisms (n = 11) and 100 % specificity (95 % CI) was observed in the non-intended targets (n = 26) (Supplementary table 3)

Subsequently, the result in Supplementary Figs. 4, 5 affirmed the presence of the 16S rRNA in all the isolates utilized in this study.

Limit of Detection (LOD) of the multiplex PCR assay

Following the evaluation of the sensitivity and specificity of the multiplex PCR assay on reference strains, the limit of detection was also evaluated. Also referred to as analytical sensitivity, LOD is the lowest amount of DNA of bacteria that can be detected using a 10-fold serial dilution in a PCR reaction. LOD was evaluated at both the genomic and bacteria level

LOD at genomic level

At the genomic level, the concentration of the representative bacteria strain was then adjusted to 10 ng to prepare a 10-fold serial dilution from 10 ng - 1 pg.

All the genes evaluated showed an LOD of 100 pg with the exception of cfr and poxtA which showed an LOD of 1 pg (Supplementary Fig. 2, 3). As stated earlier, no reference strain was available for cfr and poxtA, so, a synthetic dsDNA was used. The results are shown in Supplementary Figs. 6–13

LOD at bacterial level

At the bacterial level, the lysate of a ten-fold serially diluted overnight culture of the reference strains was used to evaluate the LOD. Only 5 of the reference strains were used as they contain one or two of the target genes with the exception of cfr and poxtA (no reference strains).

The result showed that the LOD of Enterococcus 16S rRNA, vanA and vanD was 109 CFU/ml (Supplementary Figs. 14, 18) while those of optrA, vanB and vanC were 107 CFU/ml, 106 CFU/ml and 105 CFU/ml respectively (Supplementary Figs. 15–17).

A concise summary of the LOD at both genomic and bacterial level is shown in Supplementary Table 4.

Discussion

This study has set out to evaluate the performance of a developed mPCR assay for the detection of vancomycin- and linezolid – resistant genes in Enterococcus and also conduct a retrospective surveillance of vancomycin - and linezolid - resistant genes in Enterococcus at a teaching hospital and poultry farms respectively in Kelantan, Malaysia.

The specificity and sensitivity of the optimized mPCR assay was assessed and found to be sensitive and specific. The LOD or analytical sensitivity was evaluated both at the genomic and bacteria level. In this study, utilising a 10 –fold serial dilution, the LOD at the genomic level of isolates that expresses the genes of interest were evaluated to be 100 pg of the DNA. Reference strains for cfr and poxtA were unavailable and so, a synthetic dsDNA was used instead of a bacterial reference strain. A LOD of 1 pg was observed for both cfr and poxtA. Due to the intensity of their bands, the LOD could have gone lower should a lower range for the evaluation of LOD (Range in this study; 1 ng – 1 pg) was utilized. Synthetic dsDNA are designed to possess high purity and high yield integrity and this could explain the LOD observed for cfr and poxtA. Yean et al. [23] reported a lower LOD of 1 pg in their study which is higher than ours. Following the evaluation of LOD at the genomic level, the LOD at the bacteria level was also evaluated. Since it involves bacteria reference strains, cfr and poxtA were exempted because of the unavailability of bacteria strain. Therefore, only 5 bacteria reference strains which expresses the genes of interest were utilized. An LOD in the range 109 CFU/ml – 105 CFU/ml was observed in this study. This is in disagreement to the study of Yean et al. [23] where they reported a LOD of 105 CFU/ml for all isolates. The LOD of some isolates possessing vanA and Enterococcus 16S rRNA gene was observed to be high (109 CFU/ml) in this study and the possible reason could be the nature of the isolates utilized especially as regards their growth during the overnight culture.

The limit of detection (LOD) for some of the isolates, particularly at the bacterial level, was found to be relatively high. This is likely attributable to the low number of bacterial colonies available for DNA extraction, which becomes further diminished during serial dilution. While this does not substantially affect the performance of the multiplex PCR assay in detecting vancomycin- and linezolid-resistant genes at either the bacterial or genomic level, it does highlight a practical limitation when considering its direct application to clinical specimens such as blood and urine. In such specimens, bacterial loads are often low, especially in the early stages of infection or in patients already receiving antimicrobial therapy. The presence of host DNA, PCR inhibitors in blood or urine, and the complex background can further compromise assay sensitivity. Without prior culture enrichment, the reduced bacterial density may fall below the LOD of the assay, increasing the risk of false negatives.

This multiplex PCR assay demonstrated strong diagnostic performance for both VRE and LZRE gene detection, highlighting its potential utility in clinical microbiology laboratories. For VRE gene detection, the assay achieved a sensitivity of 71.80 % and specificity of 100 %, with an overall accuracy of 83.60 %. The positive predictive value (PPV) was 100 %, indicating that all PCR-positive cases were true positives, while the negative predictive value (NPV) stood at 71.80 %. These values suggest that while the assay is highly reliable in confirming the presence of VRE genes, it may miss some true positive cases likely due to limitations in DNA extraction, primer binding efficiency, or sub-threshold bacterial loads in certain samples. The false negatives (n = 11) observed could be attributed to several biological and technical factors. First, heterogeneous gene expression or mutations in primer target regions may compromise amplification. Second, inhibitory substances in clinical samples or suboptimal template concentrations could reduce sensitivity [24]. To mitigate these issues, incorporating multiplexed primer sets targeting conserved genomic regions can reduce the impact of sequence variation. Optimizing sample preparation can enhance template quality. Including internal amplification controls helps detect reaction failure early. Regular quality assurance, reagent validation, and operator training can further minimize technical variability. Clinically, these strategies improve diagnostic reliability and reduce the risk of missed infections in high-burden settings. Nonetheless, the specificity of 100 % ensures that a positive result is highly credible, reducing the risk of unnecessary isolation or treatment.

On the other hand, the assay's performance for LZRE genes was even better. The multiplex PCR produced results with a PPV of 100 %, NPV of 96.30 %, sensitivity of 88.89 %, specificity of 100 %, and accuracy of 97.14 %. These results put the assay in the top tier of molecular diagnostics, on the same level with commercially available gold-standard systems like BD MAX and GeneXpert, which also show excellent accuracy in detecting resistance genes [25,26]. The assay's resilience in identifying uncommon but clinically significant resistance mechanisms, such as linezolid resistance, which is generally linked to mutations in the 23S rRNA gene or the presence of the optrA, poxtA, or cfr genes, is highlighted by the extremely low false negative rate (n = 1) [27].

Compared to conventional phenotypic susceptibility testing, such as broth microdilution or disc diffusion, the multiplex PCR offers superior turnaround time, typically producing results within 3–5 h from sample preparations. This enables faster implementation of targeted therapy and infection control measures, which is critical in settings where VRE or LZRE outbreaks can escalate rapidly. Moreover, the ability to detect multiple resistance genes simultaneously enhances laboratory efficiency and reduces reagent costs [18].

Phenotypic assays are also limited by slow-growing organisms, intermediate resistance profiles, and varying resistance, which can lead to ambiguous results. In contrast, molecular methods such as PCR directly detect the presence of resistance genes, irrespective of their phenotypic expression, allowing early identification even before full resistance is phenotypically evident [28,29].

The high specificity and PPV of the assay make it an excellent rule-in tool for resistance surveillance and infection control. In high-risk environments such as ICUs or oncology wards, early detection of carriers can inform isolation practices and prevent nosocomial spread. The strong performance in detecting LZRE genes is particularly noteworthy, given the emergence of linezolid resistance in both clinical and animal reservoirs, threatening the utility of this last-resort antibiotic [30,31]. Given the rising global prevalence of multidrug-resistant enterococci, the deployment of accurate and rapid molecular diagnostics such as this multiplex PCR is a timely and strategic public health intervention. Its cost-effectiveness, scalability, and diagnostic yield make it suitable for both resource-rich and resource-limited settings when integrated into laboratory workflows.

This study also investigated the distribution and determinants of VRE and LZRE genes across clinical and environmental sources. VanA dominated in clinical isolates while vanD and optrA appeared in poultry farm samples underscoring possible ecological differences. Resistant Enterococcus species persist in diverse environmental matrices, including hospital wastewater, soil, surface water, and animal manure, where they can survive harsh physicochemical conditions and maintain viable resistance genes. These environmental reservoirs act as critical interfaces for horizontal gene transfer, enabling the spread of resistance determinants to other commensal and pathogenic bacteria through mobile genetic elements such as plasmids and transposons. The presence of van and optrA genes beyond clinical boundaries reflects the ecological complexity of antimicrobial resistance and supports the concept of environmental persistence as a driver of resistance evolution. From a One Health perspective, the findings reinforce the need for integrated surveillance systems linking human, animal, and environmental sectors. Coordinated monitoring and molecular characterization of Enterococcus isolates from these compartments are essential to trace transmission routes, identify emerging resistance determinants, and guide effective antimicrobial stewardship policies aimed at curbing the spread of vancomycin- and linezolid-resistant Enterococcus species.

LZRE in poultry is rare but emerging due to veterinary antibiotic use [32,33]. In the environmental context, LZRE gene detection across three poultry farms did not differ significantly (p = 0.851), indicating a relatively uniform distribution. This contrasts with studies such as Wegener et al. [34]; Ma et al. [35]; Wada et al. [36], which demonstrated that antimicrobial resistance prevalence in livestock is influenced by farm-level antibiotic practices. The low overall prevalence of LZRE in poultry (3.0 %) in this study may reflect improved antimicrobial stewardship or sample homogeneity.

The co-detection of vanD and optrA resistance genes in poultry samples is fascinating and it underscores the critical importance of a One Health approach to antimicrobial resistance. These genes, conferring resistance to vancomycin and linezolid, highlight the interconnectedness of animal, human, and environmental health. In this study, poultry serves as both a reservoir and a potential transmission route for resistant enterococci, with risks of spillover to humans through direct contact, food chains, or environmental contamination. In Kelantan, where small- and medium-scale poultry farms are often located close to residential areas and frequently rely on open-environment waste management, the potential for community exposure is substantial. Farm workers, nearby households, and consumers of inadequately handled poultry products face heightened risk of acquiring these highly resistant strains. Integrating surveillance across veterinary, medical, and environmental sectors is therefore essential to curb dissemination, safeguard antibiotic efficacy, and meet global AMR containment goals under the One Health framework.

Despite its high performance, the mPCR assay is not without limitations. This mPCR assay only utilized 4 VRE genes. The rise of other resistant determinants has been reported and their impact cannot be underestimated. A case in point is the recent detection of a novel vanP resistant gene in E. faecium which discovery was due to the negative PCR observed for all known van genes but still expressed vancomycin resistance phenotypically. It is highly recommended to develop a mPCR assay that considers all known VRE genes and linezolid mutations. This would guarantee that during standard laboratory diagnosis, no resistance genes are overlooked.

The LOD for some of the isolates especially at the bacterial level was observed to be high and a routine laboratory DNA extraction method with a little enrichment can improve the LOD and diagnostic accuracy of this multiplex PCR assay.

Sequencing was not done to resolve discordant results from susceptibility testing using the VITEK II system.

The lower sensitivity for VRE (71.80 %) suggests a need for primer optimization or the inclusion of additional gene targets to improve coverage. Additionally, implementing internal amplification controls can help monitor PCR inhibition and improve reliability across diverse sample types. Additionally, the 95 % CI for sensitivity, especially for LZRE (51.75 % - 99.72 %) are very wide due to the low number of positives (n = 9). For future studies, a larger sample size for environmental and clinical studies would improve the confidence. The limited sample size and prevalence rates in most hospital divisions and sample types likely reduced the power to detect statistically significant differences. While poultry farms were included, other relevant environmental sources like wastewater, soil, livestock were not assessed, which could provide a broader One Health surveillance picture.

A further limitation is that while PCR detects the presence of resistance genes, it does not quantify gene expression levels or differentiate between colonization and active infection. Some bacteria strains may exhibit sensitive drug susceptibility phenotypes but do not carry specific resistance – determining genes or the carried resistant genes are not expressed. Therefore, integration with clinical data and culture-based confirmation remains essential, particularly in surveillance or outbreak investigations.

The retrospective nature of part of the study may limit generalizability as the study is geographically limited to Kelantan, Malaysia. As such, generalization to other regions may not hold.

Conclusion

In summary, the multiplex PCR assay developed in this study exhibited excellent diagnostic accuracy, particularly for LZRE detection, and high specificity for both targets. These findings support its use as a reliable, rapid, and high-throughput tool for the molecular diagnosis of VRE and LZRE infections. Overall, this study has shown the critical role of specimen source in resistance detection and highlights the need for comprehensive surveillance strategies that integrate clinical, demographic, and environmental data. The findings support targeted screening of high-risk specimens, particularly blood, for early detection of resistant pathogens. Larger multicenter studies are recommended to explore the demographic and risk factors with greater statistical power. The assay showed excellent specificity and predictive values, with a moderate-to-high sensitivity. While the limit of detection at the bacterial level was higher for certain genes, this limitation can be mitigated by sample enrichment or improved DNA extraction protocols. Given the growing concern about horizontal gene transfer of resistance determinants to other Gram-positive pathogens, this assay represents a valuable tool for infection control programs and epidemiological surveillance. Future versions of the assay should incorporate fluorescent staining – based multiplex method and emerging resistance markers, such as the vanP gene, to ensure comprehensive detection and as electrophoretic analysis are gradually losing acceptance in routine testing due to their insufficient convenience. Broader clinical validation in diverse geographic settings is recommended to further establish its utility in routine diagnostics.
