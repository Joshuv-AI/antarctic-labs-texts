Algebraicity of Hodge classes on some Generalized Prym Varieties
Deepam Patel, Yilong Zhang

Abstract
. In this article, we revisit the construction of some algebraic cycles due to Schoen ( [ 11 ] ) on certain Prym Varieties. More precisely, we show that these cycles arise naturally from (unramified) geometric class field theory, and apply it to prove the algebraicity of certain Hodge classes on some generalized Prym Varieties .

1.
Introduction

We work over the field of complex numbers, and all varieties will be considered over this field. Given a curve
C
, let
J(C)
denote the Jacobian variaty of
C
and
\Sym^{k}(C)
denote its
k
-symmetric power. We shall assume that a point
c_{0}\in C
is fixed, and consider the corresponding Abel–Jacobi maps (recalled below).

As an easy consequence of basic covering space theory, one obtains that every abelian cover of smooth projective curves
C\to C^{\prime}
arises from base change of finite covers of Jacobian varieties. In particular, there is a Cartesian diagram

(1.0.1)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces W}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Sym^{d}(C^{\prime})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces J(C^{\prime}),}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Delta_{d}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces AJ_{d}}

where the bottom left arrow is the ‘diagonal map’,
AJ_{d}
is the degree
d
Abel–Jacobi map (note that this depends on the fixed point
c^{\prime}\in C^{\prime}
),
A\to J(C^{\prime})
is an isogeny of abelian varieties, and the diagram is
G
-equivariant. We will investigate how this diagram produces non-trivial algebraic cycles for appropriate
d
. The existence of the diagram above can also be viewed as an instance of unramified geometric class field theory for curves, and is valid over arbitrary algebraically closed fields. From this perspective, one has analogous assertions in the
ramified
setting where one considers instead Jacobians with modulus. However, we do not discuss the ramified setting in this article.

It’s classical (for example, see
[
1
]
) that for
d
sufficiently large (
d\geq 2g-1
, where
g=g(C^{\prime})
), the Abel–Jacobi map

AJ_{d}:\Sym^{d}(C^{\prime})\rightarrow J(C^{\prime})

is a projective bundle. On the other hand, if
d=2g-2
then the Abel–Jacobi map is a
\mathbb{P}^{g-2}
-bundle over the complement of a point and the fiber over this point is
\mathbb{P}^{g-1}\cong|K_{C^{\prime}}|
coming from the canonical divisor class on
C^{\prime}
. The cycle
\mathbb{P}^{g-1}
defines a class in the middle-dimensional cohomology, and then the pullback of
\mathbb{P}^{g-1}
to
\Sym^{2g-2}(C)
produces interesting algebraic cycles. It turns out that these cycles factor through
W
, an abelian cover of
\Sym^{2g-2}(C^{\prime})
.

1.1.
Cohomology on
G
-cover

The following result describes the cohomology of the abelian cover
W
of the symmetric product of a curve, and in particular shows the algebraicity of certain Hodge classes.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
C\to C^{\prime}
be an étale
G
-cover of smooth projective curves, where the Galois group
G
is a finite abelian group. Let
h=2g(C^{\prime})-2
and suppose
g(C^{\prime})\geq 2
.

(1)

There is a natural isomorphism of Hodge structures

(1.1.1)

{\rm H}^{n}(W,{\mathbb{Q}})\cong\begin{cases}{\rm H}^{h}(\Sym^{h}(C^{\prime}),{\mathbb{Q}})\oplus U(-\frac{h}{2}),\ \textup{when}\ n=h;\\
{\rm H}^{n}(\Sym^{h}(C^{\prime}),{\mathbb{Q}}),\ \textup{when}\ n\neq h.\\
\end{cases}

(2)

The summand
U(-\frac{h}{2})
is generated by algebraic classes, and has dimension
|G|-1
.

(3)

The isomorphism above is also
G
-equivariant, where the
G
-action on
{\rm H}^{*}(\Sym^{h}(C^{\prime}))
is trivial, and the action on
U
is isomorphic to complement of the trivial factor in the regular
G
-representation. In particular, its complexification decomposes as a sum of
\chi
-isotypic components

U\otimes{\mathbb{C}}\cong\bigoplus_{\chi\neq 0}{\rm H}^{h}(W,{\mathbb{C}})_{\chi},

where each summand is one-dimensional and
\chi
ranges over all non-trivial characters.

On the other hand, the finite cover
C\to C^{\prime}
induces the Norm map on Jacobian varieties
J(C)\to J(C^{\prime})
. A connected component
B
of its kernel is a subabelian variety of
J(C)
and is called the
Prym variety
. There is an isogeny of abelian varieties

J(C)\sim J(C^{\prime})\times B.

At the cohomological level, this corresponds to the decomposition

{\rm H}^{1}(J(C),{\mathbb{Q}})\cong{\rm H}^{1}(J(C^{\prime}),{\mathbb{Q}})\oplus{\rm H}^{1}(J(C),{\mathbb{Q}})_{nt},

where the summand
{\rm H}^{1}(J(C),{\mathbb{Q}})_{nt}
is naturally identified with
H^{1}(B,{\mathbb{Q}})
and has the property that its complexification is a sum of
\chi
-isotypic components ranging over all non-trivial characters
\chi
of
G
.

1.2.
Algebraicity of Weil Hodge classes

Since
G
is abelian, the group algebra
{\mathbb{Q}}[G]
is semi-simple and is a product of fields
F_{1}\times\cdots\times F_{r}
, where each field
F_{i}
corresponds to a rational irreducible representation of
G
. In particular, there is a decomposition of rings

{\mathbb{Q}}[G]\cong{\mathbb{Q}}\times{\mathbb{Q}}[G]_{nt}
,
where the ring
{\mathbb{Q}}[G]_{nt}
is the product of fields corresponding to non-trivial representations.

It follows that the first cohomology group of the Prym variety
{\rm H}^{1}(B,{\mathbb{Q}})
is naturally an
{\mathbb{Q}}[G]_{nt}
-module. In fact, since each nontrivial character will appear exactly
h
-times,
{\rm H}^{1}(B,{\mathbb{Q}})
is a free
{\mathbb{Q}}[G]_{nt}
-module with rank
h
, and the resulting top exterior power

(1.1.2)

U_{Weil}:=\bigwedge^{h}_{{\mathbb{Q}}[G]_{nt}}{\rm H}^{1}(B,{\mathbb{Q}})\subset{\rm H}^{h}(B,{\mathbb{Q}})

consists of Hodge classes (see Section
2
for the proof of these statements). This is analogous to the construction of Weil Hodge classes in the setting of Weil abelian varieties. Note that this is a subspace of dimension
|G|-1
, and is in general not generated by the divisor classes. Our second result is

Theorem 1.2
.

The Hodge classes
U_{Weil}
(
1.1.2
) are represented by algebraic cycles.

As Hodge substructures,
U_{Weil}
is identified with
U
from (
1.1.1
) by pulling back to
\Sym^{h}(C)
, applying Abel–Jacobi map, and powers of the Lefschetz operator on
H^{*}(B,{\mathbb{Q}})
. The algebraicity follows from the same chain of maps, together with Theorem
1.1
and the standard conjecture on abelian varieties
[
4
]
.

In fact, one can associate sub-abelian subvarieties
J(C)_{V}
to any rational representation
V
of
G
, and consider an analogous space of Hodge classes. Theorem
1.2
also holds in this setting with essentially the same proof.

1.3.
Relation to Schoen’s work

In the case of cyclic étale covers, Theorem
1.2
is due to Chad Schoen
[
11
]
.
In that setting, Chad Schoen showed that there is a canonical abelian subvariety of
B
corresponding to primitive characters of
{\mathbb{Z}}/m{\mathbb{Z}}
. When
m=3
(resp.
4
), these give rise to Weil abelian fourfolds with CM field
{\mathbb{Q}}(\mu_{3})
(resp.
{\mathbb{Q}}(i)
) with trivial discriminant. Schoen proved the Hodge conjecture for such fourfolds by specializing to the ones arising from the Prym construction as above. Another proof for
{\mathbb{Q}}(i)
case was given by van Geemen
[
15
]
. Note that based on the work of Faber and Koike
[
2
,
5
]
, Schoen’s result
[
11
,
12
]
shows the Hodge conjecture for Weil abelian fourfolds with CM field
{\mathbb{Q}}(\mu_{3})
and
{\mathbb{Q}}(i)
and arbitrary discriminant. By recent work of Markman
[
8
,
9
]
, the Hodge conjecture is now known for all Weil abelian fourfolds with CM in other quadratic fields.

The main goal of this paper is to generalize Schoen’s method of obtaining algebraic cycles to the case of an abelian cover, i.e., étale cover with Galois group being abelian. The results above follow closely Schoen’s original method of proof. The main contribution here is to put forth the viewpoint that the algebraic cycles in question come from (unramified) geometric class field theory. Specifically, the relevant statement is Theorem
1.1
(in so far as the algebricity of Hodge classes is concerned); and this in turn follows easily from the geometry of the Abel–Jacobi map.

While this result recovers Schoen’s result in the cyclic group case, it should be noted that it’s likely that Schoen’s result can also be used to prove (in a formal way) the general case of non-cyclic abelian covers. The main goal here is to provide an alternate viewpoint that slightly generalizes and simplifies Schoen’s construction of algebraic cycles. Moreover, the Class Field Theory viewpoint allows one to easily generalize many of the results in this article to the ramified setting, where one works systematically with Jacobians with modulus. This approach also partially generalizes to surfaces (and
{\rm H}^{2}(S,{\mathbb{Q}})
where
S
is a surface).

We do not consider the ramified or higher-dimensional cases here, and plan to discuss these in a future paper. Finally, we note that the proofs here adapt easily to the étale setting and Tate classes (though we stick to the complex setting below).

2.1.
Geometric Class Field Theory

In this section, we recall a basic result from Geometric Class Field Theory, which will be used in the following. We refer to
[
14
]
for details (where far more general results are proved). In fact, in this article, we shall only be concerned with the unramified setting, where the results we need are a simple consequence of covering space theory. These results are certainly well known, and are only recalled here for the convenience of the reader.

Let
C^{\prime}
be a smooth projective curve of genus
g
over a perfect field
k
. Let
\Sym^{d}(C^{\prime})
denote the
d
-th symmetric power of
C^{\prime}
, and
C^{\prime d}\xrightarrow{\pi_{d}}\Sym^{d}(C^{\prime})
denote the natural projection. Finally, let
{\rm Pic}^{0}(C^{\prime})
denote the usual Picard scheme of degree

P\in C^{\prime}(k)
is fixed. With this notation, we have the standard morphisms:

C^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\Delta_{d}}\Sym^{d}(C^{\prime})\xrightarrow{AJ_{d}}{\rm Pic^{0}}(C^{\prime}),

where the first arrow is given by composing
\pi_{d}
with the diagonal map, and the second is the degree
d
Abel–Jacobi map, which depends on the fixed point
P
. With this notation, one has the following:

Theorem 2.1
.

(1)

Both
\Delta_{d}
and
AJ_{d}
induce isomorphisms on the abelianizations of geometric étale fundamental groups (resp. topological fundamental groups when
k={\mathbb{C}}
). Note that both
{\rm Sym}^{d}(C^{\prime})
(
d\geq 2
) and
J(C^{\prime})
have abelian étale (resp, topological) fundamental groups.

(2)

In particular, every étale covering
C\rightarrow C^{\prime}
fits into a commutative Cartesian diagram:

(2.1.1)

\textstyle{C\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{W\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{A\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{C^{\prime}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\Sym^{d}(C^{\prime})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{{\rm Pic^{0}}(C^{\prime}),}

where the vertical maps are all finite étale covers, and both
W
(resp.
A
) are unique up to an isomorphism.

Proof.

For the first part, note that this is by construction in the case of Abel–Jacobi map. We refer to
[
3
]
for the analogous result for
AJ_{d}
(
d\geq 2
). The second part follows from the first as an application of standard covering space theory. Specifically, we have a bijection between subgroups of the abelianization of the fundamental group and abelian covers.
∎

We also record the following basic fact about the Abel–Jacobi morphism.

Theorem 2.2
.

(cf.
[
13
]
) Let
C^{\prime}
be a smooth projective curve over an algebraically closed field
k
.

(1)

For
d\geq 2g-1
, the Abel–Jacobi map
AJ_{d}
is a projective bundle.

(2)

If
d=2g-2
, then
AJ_{d}
is a projective bundle with fibers
{\mathbb{P}}^{g-2}
over an open subset
\mathcal{U}
, where the complement
Z=J(C^{\prime})\setminus\mathcal{U}
is a point. Over this point, the fiber is
\mathbb{P}^{g-1}
, corresponding to the canonical divisor class.

Note that set-theoretically, this follows from Abel’s theorem and the Riemann–Roch theorem: the fiber over
AJ_{d}(D-dP)
is the projectization of the linear system
|D|
with dimension
\dim|D|-1=d-g-h^{1}(D)
. Then by Serre duality
h^{1}(D)=h^{0}(K_{C^{\prime}}-D)
, which is zero when
\textup{deg}(D)\geq 2g-1
; when
\textup{deg}(D)=2g-2
, it vanishes if and only if

is the canonical divisor.

For the rest of the paper, we work with
k={\mathbb{C}}
.

2.2.
A Prym type construction

Given a finite group
G
, a rational representation
V
of
G
, and a homomorphism
G\rightarrow{\rm End}_{0}(A):={\rm End}(A)\otimes{\mathbb{Q}}
, one can associate an abelian sub-variety
A_{V}\subset A
. We briefly recall this construction following (
[
6
]
).

With
G
as above, consider the corresponding group rings
{\mathbb{Q}}[G]
and
{\mathbb{C}}[G]
. Since
{\mathbb{Q}}[G]
is semi-simple, we may write

{\mathbb{Q}}[G]=\oplus_{i=1}^{s}{\mathbb{Q}}[G]e_{V_{i}}

as a direct sum of simple modules where
e_{V_{i}}
are central idempotents and
V_{1},\ldots,V_{s}
are the irreducible rational representations of
G
. Note that the action of
G
on
{\mathbb{Q}}[G]e_{V_{i}}
is given by some multiple of
V_{i}
(more precisely, by
m_{i}
where
m_{i}=\frac{\dim(V^{\prime}_{i})}{s_{i}}
,
V^{\prime}_{i}
a complex irreducible representation associated with
V_{i}
, and
s_{i}
is the Schur index). One has a similar description of
{\mathbb{C}}[G]
in terms of irreducible complex representations. Note that if

is abelian, then
{\mathbb{C}}[G]
is a sum of characters, and

.

Given
\alpha\in{\rm End_{0}}(A)
,
m\alpha\in{\rm End}(A)
for some integer
m
, and we define
A_{\alpha}:=\textup{Im}(m\alpha)
for a minimal such

. In particular, we have abelian subvarieties
A_{V_{i}}\subset A
associated with each
V_{i}
.

Definition 2.3
.

If
V
is a rational representation of
G
, then we may write
V=\Sigma_{i}n_{i}V_{i}
as a sum of irreducible representations, and we set

A_{V}:=\prod_{n_{i}>0}A_{V_{i}}.

Theorem 2.4
.

(
[
6
]
, 2.9.1)

(1)

Each
A_{V_{i}}
is
G

A_{V_{i}}
are pairwise orthogonal, and the addition map induces a
G
-equivariant isogeny
\prod A_{V_{i}}\rightarrow A
.

(2)

The
G
-action on
A_{V_{i}}
is through a multiple of the rational representation
V_{i}
.

In particular, for any rational representation
V
of
G
we obtain a
G
-equivariant isogeny
A_{V^{c}}\times A_{V}\rightarrow A
where
V^{c}
is the sum of the irreducible rational representations not appearing in

. The following two examples will be useful in the following.

Example 2.5
.

(1)

Let
V_{nt}=\oplus V_{i}
where the sum is over all the non-trivial rational representations. Let
A_{nt}\subset A
denote the corresponding variety. On cohomology, we have

{\rm H}^{1}(A,{\mathbb{Q}})={\rm H}^{1}(A,{\mathbb{Q}})^{G}\oplus{\rm H}^{1}(A_{nt},{\mathbb{Q}}),

where
{\rm H}^{1}(A_{nt},{\mathbb{C}})
decomposes as direct sums of
\chi
-isotopyic components of
{\rm H}^{1}(A,{\mathbb{Q}})
, where
\chi\neq 0
.

(2)

If

is cyclic of prime order and we consider

to the rational representation whose complexification is the sum of the

-isotypic components where

We will investigate these two cases in Sections
2.6
and
5

2.3.
Weil type classes

Suppose
A
has the action of a finite group
G
, and let
\rho:{\mathbb{Q}}[G]\rightarrow{\rm End}(A)_{{\mathbb{Q}}}
denote the corresponding rational representation as above. Then the simple algebras (indexed by the irreducible rational representations
V_{i}
) appearing in the decomposition of
{\mathbb{Q}}
are all matrix algebras over division algebras. We denote by
F_{i}
the centers of these division algebras (corresponding to the representations
V_{i}
). In the current setting, this is a number field. Let
R=F_{1}\times\cdots\times F_{k}
and
R\rightarrow{\rm End}(A)_{\mathbb{Q}}
be the induced morphism. It follows that
V:={\rm H}^{1}(A,{\mathbb{Q}})
is a finitely generated
R
-module. We assume that it is a free
R
-module of rank
r
. Let
V_{R}:=\Lambda^{r}_{R}V
the resulting rank
1
-module; it is an
\dim_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(R)
-dimensional
{\mathbb{Q}}
-vector space. Note that one has a natural inclusion

V_{R}\xhookrightarrow{}{\rm H}^{r}(A,{\mathbb{Q}})=\Lambda_{{\mathbb{Q}}}^{r}V.

Let
\Sigma_{F}:={\rm Hom}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(R,\mathbb{C})=\Pi_{i}{\rm Hom}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(F_{i},\mathbb{C})
, and for
\sigma\in\Sigma_{F}
let
n_{\sigma}
denote the multiplicity of
\sigma
in the
R
-action on
V_{\mathbb{C}}^{(1,0)}
. If
R=F
is a field (i.e.

) then one has the following standard criterion for when

consists of Hodge classes.

Lemma 2.6
.

If
n_{\sigma}=n_{\bar{\sigma}}
for all
\sigma\in\Sigma_{R}
, then

consists entirely of Hodge classes.

Proof.

If
R
is a field, this is well-known (
[
10
]
). That argument also applies to a more general setting where

is a product of fields as we considered here.
∎

In the following, we shall be concerned with the setting where

is abelian. In that case, each

is a subfield of the cyclotomic field. Moreover, since

2.4.
Local systems on finite étale covers

Let
\pi:X\rightarrow X^{\prime}
be a finite (Galois) étale cover of a complex algebraic variety with Galois group
G
. Then we have the standard short exact sequence of fundamental groups (we ignore base points for ease of notation):

1\rightarrow\pi_{1}(X)\rightarrow\pi_{1}(X^{\prime})\rightarrow G\rightarrow 1.

A local system
{\mathcal{G}}
(of
k
-vector spaces, with
k
a field) on
X
is equivalent to giving a finite-dimensional
\pi_{1}(X)
-representation in a
k
-vector space. Let
\rho_{{\mathcal{G}}}
denote this representation. Then the pushforward
\pi_{*}{\mathcal{G}}
corresponds to the induced representation

{\rm Ind}_{\pi_{1}(X)}^{\pi_{1}(X^{\prime})}\rho_{{\mathcal{G}}}
.

A finite-dimensional representation of
G
gives rise to a local system on
X^{\prime}
; in particular, every (complex) character
\chi
of
G
gives a (rank one) local system
{\mathcal{L}}_{\chi}
on
X^{\prime}
. We may also express these constructions in the language of group rings. More precisely, a finite-dimensional
k
-representation
V
of
\pi_{1}(X)
is equivalent to a
k[\pi_{1}(X)]
-module. We sometimes use the same notation
V
to denote the corresponding module. For example, the constant local system
k
is given by the quotient
k[\pi_{1}(X)]/I
where
I
is the augmentation ideal. The induced representation is then given by the tensor product

k[\pi_{1}(X^{\prime})]\otimes_{k[\pi_{1}(X)]}V.

We have the following standard computation.

Lemma 2.7
.

With the same notation as above, assume moreover
G
is abelian.

(1)

One has a natural isomorphism of local systems on
X^{\prime}

(2.7.1)

\pi_{*}(\mathbb{C})\cong\oplus_{\chi}{\mathcal{L}}_{\chi},

where the sum is over all characters of
G
.

(2)

Taking cohomology, there is a decomposition

{\rm H}^{i}(X,\mathbb{C})\cong\bigoplus_{\chi}{\rm H}^{i}(X,\mathbb{C})_{\chi},

where
{\rm H}^{i}(X,\mathbb{C})_{\chi}\cong{\rm H}^{i}(X^{\prime},{\mathcal{L}}_{\chi})
is the
\chi
-isotypic component of the
G
-representation
{\rm H}^{i}(X,\mathbb{C})
.

Proof.

The push-forward corresponds to the module
k[\pi_{1}(X^{\prime})]\otimes_{k[\pi_{1}(X)]}k[\pi_{1}(X)]/I.
On the other hand, the latter is easily seen to be isomorphic to
k[G]
(as a
k[\pi_{1}(X^{\prime})]
-module). Finally, note that
k[G]
(as a
k[G]
-module) is a direct sum of the characters of
G
when
k=\mathbb{C}
. This follows from the standard fact that the regular representation of a finite abelian group is the direct sum of all the one-dimensional representations, each appearing with multiplicity one. The second part is a direct consequence of the first part, since the
\chi
-isotypic component is precisely
{\rm H}^{i}(X,{\mathcal{L}}_{\chi}).

∎

2.5.
The group action on a curve

We specialize to the case of and étale abelian cover of curves
\pi:C\rightarrow C^{\prime}:=C/G
. As an application of the results of the previous section, we have

{\rm H}^{1}(C,{\mathbb{C}})=\bigoplus_{\chi}{\rm H}^{1}(C^{\prime},{\mathcal{L}}_{\chi}),

where
{\rm H}^{1}(C^{\prime},{\mathcal{L}}_{\chi})
is the
\chi
-isotypic component (cf. Lemma
2.7
). We are interested in determining the multiplicity of this component, i.e. the multiplicity of
\chi
in the
G
-representation
{\rm H}^{1}(C,\mathbb{C})
. It is well known (see
[
11
, Lemma 1.5]
) that this is precisely
h:=2g-2
where
g
is the genus of
C^{\prime}
. Here we give a simple direct proof (without recourse to the Lefschetz fixed point formula as in
[
11
, Lemma 1.5]
).

Lemma 2.9
.

With notation as above,
\dim{\rm H}^{1}(C^{\prime},{\mathcal{L}}_{\chi})=2g-2
, if
\chi
is not trivial.

Proof.

First, it follows from standard topology that the Euler characteristic of a complex local system
{\mathcal{L}}_{\chi}
with rank one is the same as the topological Euler characteristic
\chi(C^{\prime},L_{\chi})=\chi(C^{\prime})=2-2g
. Then since
{\mathcal{L}}_{\chi}
has nontrivial monodromy,
\dim{\rm H}^{0}(C^{\prime},{\mathcal{L}}_{\chi})=\dim{\rm H}^{2}(C^{\prime},{\mathcal{L}}_{\chi})=0
, hence the result follows.
∎

Now, we may split the local system into
\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}={\mathbb{Q}}\oplus{\mathcal{L}}_{nt}
. Recall that the local system
{\mathcal{L}}_{nt}
corresponds to the sum of the non-trivial irreducible rational representations of
G
, and each of these has a field
F_{i}
associated with it. Let
{\mathbb{Q}}[G]_{nt}
denote the product of these fields. Then
{\mathcal{L}}_{nt}
has an action of
{\mathbb{Q}}[G]_{nt}
, and an induced action on

. The cohomology group

is naturally a

-module.

As a consequence of Lemma

, we have

Corollary 2.10
.

With notation as above,
{\rm H}^{1}(C,{\mathbb{Q}})_{nt}
is a free
{\mathbb{Q}}[G]_{nt}
-module of rank
h
.

Remark 2.11
.

In fact, the analog of the previous lemma is true for any rational

-representation. More precisely, let

denote the

-isotypic component. Then

is a sum of a collection of irreducible rational representations, and therefore one has a ring

associated to

(i.e. the product of fields corresponding to the irreducible summands appearing in

is a free

-module of rank

.

2.6.

•

3.
Cohomology on abelian cover of symmetric product

3.1.
Set-up

Recall that we work with an unramified Galois cover

of smooth projective curves with Galois group
G

G
-cover of
\Sym^{h}(C^{\prime})
, where
h=2g(C^{\prime})-2
is the degree of the canonical divisor, and also is the multiplicity in
{\rm H}^{1}(C,{\mathbb{C}})
of each nontrivial character of
G
(cf. Lemma
2.9
).

By Geometric Class Field Theory (cf. Theorem
2.1
), any étale
G
-cover of
\Sym^{h}(C^{\prime})
comes from a Cartesian square:

(3.0.1)

\textstyle{W\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\tilde{\pi}}
\textstyle{A\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi}
\textstyle{{\rm Sym}^{h}(C^{\prime})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{AJ_{h}}
\textstyle{J(C^{\prime}).}

Note that

is an abelian variety isogeny to

, therefore its cohomology (over
{\mathbb{Q}}
) will be isomorphic to that of
J(C^{\prime})
.

The rest of the section is devoted to computing the cohomology of

and proving the first main theorem.

3.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

We split the proof into two propositions. We assume that the base curve has genus
g(C^{\prime})\geq 2
, since there is no nontrivial unramified cover of a genus zero curve; and an unramified cover of a genus one curve still has genus one, hence the Prym variety is trivial.

Proposition 3.1
.

(cf. Theorem
1.1
, (1) )
There is a natural isomorphism of Hodge structures

(3.1.1)

{\rm H}^{n}(W,{\mathbb{Q}})\cong\begin{cases}{\rm H}^{h}(\Sym^{h}(C^{\prime}),{\mathbb{Q}})\oplus U(-\frac{h}{2}),\ \textup{when}\ n=h;\\
{\rm H}^{n}(\Sym^{h}(C^{\prime}),{\mathbb{Q}}),\ \textup{when}\ n\neq h,\\
\end{cases}

where
U
consists of Hodge classes.

Proof.

We break up the proof in a few steps.

(1)

The direct image local system

\pi_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}
is a polarized variation of Hodge structure (of weight zero). We have a decomposition of this into a direct sum of local systems on
J(C^{\prime})
indexed by the irreducible rational representations of
G
. We may write this as
{\mathbb{Q}}\oplus{\mathcal{L}}_{nt}
. (Note that this decomposition underlies variations of Hodge structures.) Here
{\mathcal{L}}_{nt}
is the rational local system which corresponds to the complement of the trivial representation. We have a similar decomposition
\tilde{\pi}_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}\cong{\mathbb{Q}}\oplus\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{nt}
on
\Sym^{h}(C^{\prime})
. Moreover, by naturality, the pullback of
{\mathcal{L}}_{nt}
along
AJ_{h}
is the corresponding local system on
{\rm Sym}^{h}(C^{\prime})
, i.e.,
AJ_{h}^{*}\mathcal{L}_{nt}=\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{nt}
. Hence, we have an isomorphism

(3.1.2)

R\Gamma(W,{\mathbb{Q}})\cong R\Gamma({\rm Sym}^{h}(C^{\prime}),{\mathbb{Q}})\oplus R\Gamma({\rm Sym}^{h}(C^{\prime}),AJ_{h}^{*}{\mathcal{L}}_{nt})

in the (derived) category of mixed Hodge modules.

(2)

We are interested in computing the second factor of (
3.1.2
). By the projection formula, we have

R\Gamma({\rm Sym}^{h}(C^{\prime}),AJ_{h}^{*}{\mathcal{L}}_{nt})\cong R\Gamma(J(C^{\prime}),(AJ_{h})_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}\otimes{\mathcal{L}}_{nt}).

(3)

Let
\mathcal{U}\xhookrightarrow{j}J(C^{\prime})
be the complement of the point
Z\xhookrightarrow{i}J(C^{\prime})
(as in Theorem
2.2
). In particular; over
Z
the morphism
AJ_{h}
has fiber
{\mathbb{P}}^{g-1}={\mathbb{P}}^{\frac{h}{2}}
and over
\mathcal{U}
it is a projective bundle of rank
g-2
. Let
{\mathcal{G}}:=(AJ_{h})_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}
, then we have the distinguished triangle:

Rj_{!}{\mathcal{G}}|_{\mathcal{U}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{G}}\rightarrow i_{*}i^{*}{\mathcal{G}}\xrightarrow{[1]}

By proper base change and the projective bundle formula,
{\mathcal{G}}|_{\mathcal{U}}=\oplus_{j=1}^{g-2}{\mathbb{Q}}|_{\mathcal{U}}[-2j](-j).

Similarly, we have
{\mathcal{G}}|_{Z}=\oplus_{j=1}^{g-1}{\mathbb{Q}}|_{Z}[-2j](-j).

Since both
j_{!}
and
i^{*}
are exact functors, taking the long exact sequence in homology for the above triangle gives short exact sequences:

0\rightarrow j_{!}{\mathbb{Q}}|_{\mathcal{U}}(-k)\rightarrow R^{2k}(AJ_{h})_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{Q}}|_{Z}(-k)\rightarrow 0,

for
0\leq k\leq g-2
. The higher direct images vanish in odd degrees. In the top degree, one has

R^{2(g-1)}(AJ_{h})_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}\cong i_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}|_{Z}(-(g-1)).

(4)

Combining everything, we have

(3.1.3)

R^{2k}(AJ_{h})_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}\cong\begin{cases}{\mathbb{Q}}(-k),\ \textup{for}\ 0\leq k\leq g-2,\\
i_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}|_{Z}(-(g-1)),\ \textup{for}\ k=g-1,\end{cases}

and zero in other degrees.

(5)

Note that

(3.1.4)

{\rm H}^{k}(J(C^{\prime}),{\mathcal{L}}_{nt})=0,\ \textup{for all}\ k.

This is because after tensoring with
\mathbb{C}
,
{\mathcal{L}}_{nt}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}
splits as a sum of rank one local systems corresponding to the non-trivial characters
G
. Each of these has finite monodromy given by roots of unity. On a torus, such local systems have vanishing cohomology. (Note this is consistent with the fact
\pi
is an isogeny, and hence an isomorphism on cohomology.)

(6)

We have the Leray spectral sequence converging to
{\rm H}^{k}({\rm Sym}^{h}(C^{\prime}),\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{nt})
, with

{\rm E}_{2}^{p,q}={\rm H}^{p}(J(C^{\prime}),R^{q}(AJ_{h})_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}\otimes{\mathcal{L}}_{nt}).

By (
3.1.3
) and (
3.1.4
), all
{\rm E}_{2}^{p,q}
terms vanish except for

and

. It is precisely

{\rm H}^{0}(J(C^{\prime}),i_{*}{\mathbb{Q}}|_{Z}(-(g-1)\otimes{\mathcal{L}}_{nt}))=:U(-(g-1))
. It follows that the second term of (
3.1.2
) is

(3.1.5)

{\rm H}^{h}({\rm Sym}^{h}(C^{\prime}),\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{nt})\cong U(-(g-1)),

and the cohomology vanishes at other degrees. Note that the isomorphism preserves the
G
-action. This completes the proof of the first part of Theorem
1.1
.

∎

Proposition 3.2

The Hodge substructure

Proof.

By the proof of Proposition
