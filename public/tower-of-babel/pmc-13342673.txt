Exploring the antibacterial mechanism and application of lavender essential oil based on network pharmacology and molecular docking
Authors: Lei Wang, Hanlu Hu, Xiaoxia Sun, Ziwei Jiao, Hao Zhang, Weikang Luo, Xiaode Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, essential oil was extracted from lavender using steam distillation, and its chemical components were identified by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS). The active ingredients of lavender essential oil (LEO) and their corresponding targets were obtained from the STP, SEA, and TCMSP databases. The antibacterial activities of the active components in LEO were investigated against Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus subtilis, and Candida albicans. The antibacterial efficacy was evaluated by measuring the diameter of the inhibition zone and the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC). The results showed that 156 common targets were identified by intersecting the component targets of LEO and the host targets related to antibacterial activity. Topological analysis was performed using Cytoscape software, and 21 potential core targets were finally obtained through two rounds of screening based on topological indexes. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that these targets were mainly involved in biological processes such as transcriptional regulation, while Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis indicated their participation in 169 signaling pathways. Molecular docking results demonstrated that the core targets exhibited strong binding affinity with the four key components. In vitro antibacterial experiments verified that linalool, linalyl acetate, 1,8-cineole, and caryophyllene all showed favorable antibacterial activity. Combining network pharmacology, molecular docking, and in vitro antibacterial assays, this study clearly distinguished the direct antibacterial effect of lavender essential oil from its indirect anti-infectious role via host immune regulation. This work clarified the material basis for direct bacteriostasis and the auxiliary mechanism at the host level, providing an important reference for the potential application of lavender essential oil in the field of antibacterial treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52170-x.

Introduction

Lavender (Lavandula angustifolia Mill.) is a small perennial shrub of the Labiatae family1. China is one of the top three lavender-producing countries globally, with its cultivation primarily concentrated in the Ili River Valley of Xinjiang, accounting for 98% of the nation’s total planting area. Lavandula angustifolia, Lavandula latifolia, and Lavandula intermedia represent the three most commercially available lavender species2. Lavender exhibits strong adaptability, rapid growth, and a high essential oil yield, positioning it among the top 20 essential oil plants globally. With significant economic and ornamental value, its products are widely utilized in various industries, including healthcare, cosmetics, food, and landscaping3–6.

Natural plant essential oils are gaining recognition in the food industry for their natural derivation, aromatic characteristics, and safety7–9. A growing body of evidence indicates that essential oils and their chemical constituents possess broad-spectrum antibacterial, antifungal, and antiviral activities, making them promising candidates against drug-resistant microbial pathogens10,11. Specifically, recent comprehensive reviews have highlighted their potential mechanisms of action against various viruses, including influenza and coronaviruses12. Lavender essential oil (LEO) is considered a “universal remedy” due to its diverse chemical composition, which primarily includes linalool, linalyl acetate, lavandulol, lavandulyl acetate,eucalyptol,1,8-cineole, and camphor13. It exhibits a wide range of pharmacological properties, such as promoting wound healing, alleviating pain, inducing sedation and sleep, and exerting anti-anxiety effects14–17. Network pharmacology is an interdisciplinary field that integrates systems biology and computational technologies18. The continuous advancement of artificial intelligence has led to its extensive application in network pharmacology analysis. The unique characteristics of traditional Chinese medicine, such as its multi-component and multi-target nature, as well as the complex relationships between drug molecules and various biomolecules in the body, offer considerable advantages for advanced big data analysis19. Network pharmacology plays a pivotal role in deciphering the components of traditional Chinese medicine and their mechanisms of action. Hu Zhang et al.20 explored the mechanism of action of Zhishi Daozhi decoction in treating diarrhea using network pharmacology and molecular docking. Wei Zhou et al. 21explore the potential mechanism of Danggui Buxue Decoction in treating the iron deficiency anemia (IDA) based on network pharmacology and molecular docking technology. Similarly, molecular docking studies have become a cornerstone in identifying potential lead compounds from natural sources for drug discovery against challenging pathogens like coronaviruses22. Network pharmacology methodologies can be employed to construct a comprehensive network model, incorporating various molecules and targets, as well as the relationships between drugs, active compounds, and their corresponding targets23. Through big data analysis, candidate pathways and target genes can be effectively screened. Characterized by systematicness, relevance, and predictability24, this approach enables accurate prediction of potential targets and signaling pathways of natural plant extracts in host organisms. Linalool has a broad-spectrum antibacterial capability and can inhibit the growth of various bacteria and fungi. Its action mechanism includes disrupting the lipid structures of cell membranes and enhancing their permeability to monoterpenes25,26.

This study represents the first integration of the active component database of LEO with potential antibacterial-related target information. Based on the research strategy of the “LEO-compound-genes-pathway” network and combined with direct in vitro antibacterial assays, we systematically elucidated and verified the direct bacteriostatic effects of LEO and its auxiliary mechanisms at the host level. LEO was extracted by steam distillation, and its chemical constituents were analyzed by GC–MS. In vitro antibacterial assays were performed to evaluate the direct bacteriostatic activities of linalool, linalyl acetate, 1,8-cineole, and caryophyllene. Meanwhile, network pharmacology and molecular docking were applied to predict host immune regulation-related targets and pathways. This work provides a data reference for the application of lavender essential oil in antibacterial-related fields.

Materials and methods

Materials

The lavender (Lavandula angustifolia) was provided by Yili Perilla Beauty Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Yili, China). The Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus subtilis, and Candida albicans were supplied by the Nanjing Institute for the Comprehensive Utilization of Wild Plants. Linalool, linalyl acetate, eucalyptol, caryophyllene, ciprofloxacin and nystatin were all purchased from Aladdin Technology Co. Ltd.

Extraction of the LEO

The LEO was extracted using steam distillation. Dried lavender flower samples and distilled water were added to a rotary evaporator at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:12 and allowed to stand27. Then, the essential oil extractor and reflux condenser were connected sequentially, and the mixture was distilled for 4 h, followed by another resting period to allow for natural essential oil and water separation. The oil layer was collected, dried with anhydrous sodium sulfate, and sealed for storage.

Analysis of the LEO via GC–MS

The LEO components were analyzed via gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) using an HP-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm). The GC/MS analytical conditions were selected according to the established protocols for essential oil component identification and quantification28,29. The initial column temperature was set at 50 °C, held for 5 min, increased to 70 °C at 10 °C/min, maintained for 2 min, increased to 125 °C at 5 °C /min, held for 2 min, increased to 210 °C at 10 °C/min, and held for 2 min. Finally, the temperature was increased to 280 °C at 10 °C/min and maintained for 5 min. Additional parameters included an injector temperature of 240 °C, helium (He) as a carrier gas at a flow rate of 1 mL/min, a split ratio of 50:1, and an injection volume of 1 μL. The EI ion source employed an ion source temperature of 230 °C, an MS quadrupole temperature of 150 °C, an interface temperature of 200 °C, an emission current of 200 μA, and a scan range between 33 and 650 amu with a solvent delay of 3 min. The NIST11.L standard spectral library was used for retrieval, while the relative content percentage of each component was calculated via peak area normalization.

Network pharmacology analysis

Active ingredient screening

The literature on the chemical components of lavender was retrieved from the CNKI (http://www.cnki.net), WanFang Data (http://www.wanfangdate.com.cn/index.html), and PubMed (http://www.pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) databases. Then, the SMILES codes for each component were obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/).

LEO effective component screening and target acquisition

In the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/), the keyword “Antibacterial” was inputted to collect relevant genes. All obtained targets were integrated into Excel for deduplication and merging. The compound-target network was constructed using Cytoscape V3.7.2 (http://www.cytoscape.org) to analyze the relationships between active components and potential targets.

Construction of the protein–protein interaction network for LEO to antibacterial activity

To investigate the potential target proteins through which lavender essential oil modulates host immunity and exerts an indirect adjuvant anti-infective effect, the data of 156 common host targets screened in Section “LEO effective component screening and target acquisition” were imported into the STRING database(https://string-db.org/, version 11.0), with the species designated as Homo sapiens. To ensure data reliability, the protein–protein interaction (PPI) confidence score threshold was set to ≥ 0.400, and disconnected nodes were excluded. The TSV file obtained from the STRING database was then imported into Cytoscape V3.7.2 to construct the PPI network. The network was evaluated using the cytoHubba network topology analysis plugin. The core target proteins for LEO to inhibit bacterial activity were identified based on the Degree value. To clarify the roles and significance of core targets in the regulation of host immune and inflammatory responses.

GO and KEGG enrichment analysis

To further elucidate the mechanism behind the inhibitory effect of LEO on bacterial activity, the potential target proteins were entered into the STRING database for Gene Ontology (GO) biological and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses. The top 20 results with significance thresholds of P < 0.05 were extracted and visualized.

Construction of the compound-target-pathway networks

Based on the KEGG enrichment analysis results, the top 60 signaling pathways with significance thresholds of P < 0.05, the antibacterial targets, and the core components of the LEO were imported into Cytoscape V3.7.2 to construct a compound-target-pathway network. This network was used to further analyze the interactions among the targets, active compounds, and pathways. The cytoHubba network topology analysis plugin was employed to highlight the core targets30. The intersection of these core targets with those identified in the PPI network was determined, revealing the key targets for LEO to inhibit bacterial activity.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking technology was employed to explore the interaction between the screened key targets and core components. The 3D co-crystal structures of the protein receptors were downloaded from the PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org), while the small-molecule ligand structures were obtained from the TCMSP database. The Pymol 2.3.4 software was used to remove the water molecules and original ligands from the macromolecular receptors. The docking parameter model was validated via self-docking with a root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) threshold of < 2 Å31. Next, the active molecular ligands were docked with the protein receptors using AutoDock Vina in a Linux environment32. The docking results were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL 2.3.4.

Preparation of the bacterial suspension

The sterilized liquid medium, which had been autoclaved and cooled, was poured into sterile test tubes in a laminar flow hood. Approximately one-third of each tube was filled, and the mouth of the tube and the stopper were flame-sterilized using the outer flame of an alcohol burner before being securely capped and placed on a test tube rack. The inoculation loop was sterilized by heating it in the outer flame of the alcohol burner until it turned red and then allowed to cool. A portion of the bacterial culture was aseptically transferred into the liquid medium using the loop. The concentration of the bacterial suspension was determined by the plate count method, and then it was diluted to a bacterial suspension with a bacterial content of 1 × 104 CFU/mL for standby.

Measurement of the inhibition zone diameter

The diameter of the inhibition zone provides a direct visual assessment of the antimicrobial efficacy of the test substances. The K-B paper disc diffusion method33 was employed to compare the antibacterial effect between compounds in this experiment. The mother solutions of linalool, linalyl acetate, eucalyptol, caryophyllene, ciprofloxacin and nystatin at 2 mg/mL were prepared respectively. The bacterial suspension was evenly spread on the surface of the culture medium, and then 6 mm sterile filter paper discs were pasted on the surface of the culture medium with forceps, gently pressed to adhere tightly, followed by dropping 25 μL of the test drug. Bacterial strains were incubated in a constant temperature incubator at 37 °C for 24 h, fungal strains were incubated for 48 h, and the diameter of the inhibition zone was measured. The inhibition zone diameter is defined as extremely sensitive when it is greater than 20 mm, highly sensitive at 15–19 mm, moderately sensitive at 10–14 mm, and low sensitivity when below 10 mm26.

Determine the minimum inhibitory concentration

The MIC is defined as the lowest concentration of the substance required to completely inhibit bacterial growth. The MICs of the linalool, linalyl acetate, eucalyptol and caryophyllene were determined by preparing dilutions in LB broth at different concentrations (100.00, 50.00, 25.00, 12.50, 6.25, 3.125, 1.562, 0.781, 0.390, 0.195 µg/mL).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate. Experimental data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (x ± s). Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 26.0 software. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons among multiple groups.

A comparison between the 1,167 antibacterial-related targets and the target proteins of the LEO components identified 156 overlapping targets. The results are presented in Fig. 2. PPI network analysis

A total of 156 common host targets were imported into the STRING database with the confidence score threshold set at ≥ 0.400, and a total of 1248 qualified PPI pairs were screened out. The PPI network was constructed using Cytoscape V3.7.2 software (Fig. 3), which consisted of 156 nodes representing host targets and 1248 edges corresponding to interaction pairs. In the network diagram, each node stands for a specific protein with its label indicating the protein name, and the pattern within the node represents the three-dimensional structure of the protein24. Two interacting proteins were connected by a line, and the color of the line reflected the type of interaction25, including experimentally verified or predicted interactions, as well as direct physical interaction, co-expression, gene fusion and other relationships.Fig. 3PPI network analysis.

GO and KEGG enrichment analysis

The GO enrichment analysis yielded 751 entries, of which 366 were filtered based on a significance threshold of p < 0.05. These included 197 biological processes (BP), 53 molecular functions (MF), and 116 cellular components (CC). The top 10 most significantly enriched terms in each category were selected for visualization (Fig. 5). Since antibacterial was used as the keyword to mine targets in the GeneCards database in this study, with a focus on screening targets related to host immunity and inflammatory regulation, the results of GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were inevitably biased toward immune- and inflammation-related pathways, which is consistent with the core purpose of exploring the host immunomodulatory mechanism of LEO. The BPs primarily included positive RNA polymerase II transcription regulation, signal transduction, inflammatory response, positive DNA-templated transcription regulation, protein phosphorylation, apoptosis, negative RNA polymerase II transcription regulation, positive gene expression regulation, xenobiotic stimulus response, and negative apoptosis regulation. The active targets associated with these processes were mainly related to inflammatory factors, such as CXCL8, C5AR1, PTAFR, NOD1, and TNF. The MFs mostly involved cytoplasmic, cytosol, plasma membrane, nucleus, nucleoplasmic, extracellular region, extracellular exosome, extracellular space, and mitochondrial processes. The associated targets included TOP2A, PREP, CHRM, VCP, and CXCL8. The CCs primarily included protein, identical protein, metal ion, ATP, and enzyme binding, with potential action targets mainly located in the cytosol, nucleus, cytoplasm, nucleoplasm, and extracellular space. Additional activities included zinc ion binding, protein homodimerization activity, RNA polymerase II cis-regulatory region sequence-specific DNA binding, DNA-binding transcription factor activity, RNA polymerase II-specific, and protein serine/threonine kinase activity. The associated targets included PRF1, ABAT, ADRA1D, THRβ, NR1I3, and XIAP.Fig. 5The GO functional enrichment analysis of the LEO.

The GO functional enrichment analysis of the LEO.

The KEGG analysis identified 156 potential targets predominantly associated with 169 pathways (p < 0.05), including the TNF, Toll-like receptor, NOD-like receptor, and IL-17 signaling pathways. Figure 6 shows the top 20 pathways. The NOD-like receptor signaling pathway was mainly related to 23 targets, including JUN, HSP90AA1, CXCL8, CHUK, PRKCD, XIAP, NOD1, TYK2, and MAPK14. The TNF signaling pathway correlated with 19 targets, including JUN, CHUK, XIAP, PTGS2, MAPK14, SELE, TNF, and MMP9. The Toll-like receptor signaling pathway was primarily mediated by 17 targets, including JUN, CXCL8, CHUK, TYK2, MAPK14, TNF, NFKB1, and MAPK10. The IL-17 signaling pathway corresponded to 17 targets, including JUN, HSP90AA1, CXCL8, CHUK, MMP1, PTGS2, MAPK14, TNF, and MMP9. These findings indicated that the antibacterial activity of the LEO involved a multi-target, multi-pathway process. Notably, all KEGG pathways screened in this study were host immune- and inflammation-related pathways mainly involved in the host response to microbial infection, rather than metabolic or structural pathways of bacteria themselves. These results illustrate the mechanism by which LEO exerts indirect adjuvant anti-infective effects via regulating host immune and inflammatory pathways.Fig. 6The KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the LEO.

LEO-compound-genes-pathway network analysis

The “LEO-compound-genes-pathway” network was constructed using Cytoscape 3.7.2 (Fig. 8). This network incorporated 20 core active components, 156 intersecting gene targets, and 20 key KEGG enrichment pathways (P < 0.05), with a total of 154 nodes. Node size was positively correlated with degree value, reflecting the strength of network connectivity. Network analysis revealed complex multidirectional regulatory relationships among the active components of lavender essential oil, the intersecting gene targets, and the signaling pathways. Each active component could regulate multiple gene targets, and each individual target was involved in the mediation of multiple pathways. Among them, core active components including linalyl acetate, linalool, eucalyptol, α-terpineol, and caryophyllene showed the strongest connections with core targets such as MAPK1, MAPK8, NFKB1, JUN, MAPK10, MAPK14, and TNF. These targets further mediated antibacterial-related pathways including NOD-like receptor and Toll-like receptor signaling pathways. These results directly verified the synergistic antibacterial characteristics of lavender essential oil via multiple components, multiple targets, and multiple pathways, and clarified the molecular regulatory network in which its active components regulate downstream pathways by acting on core intersecting targets.Fig. 8LEO-compound-genes-pathway Network.

Molecular docking

Autodock Vina 1.5.6 was used to dock the four key targets (TNF: 1a8m, JUN: 1fos, IL6: 1alu, and CASP3: 1cp3) of the LEO with its four active components (linalool: M1, linalyl acetate: M2, eucalyptol: M3, and caryophyllene: M4) to explore their potential interactions. The relevant detailed information is listed in Table 3 and the visual results of molecular docking are shown in Fig. 9 (taking the target TNF as an example, others are presented in Figure S1, S2 and S3). The docking results were used to preliminarily predict the binding potential between the core components of LEO and host targets, providing reference for further exploration of the host immunomodulatory mechanism. It is generally believed that if the binding energy obtained from molecular docking is less than − 5 kcal·mol⁻1, it suggests that the target and the compound have a certain binding activity. The lower the binding energy, the more stable the binding between the two38. According to the energy after molecular docking, the docking score of M4 on all key targets is the lowest, suggesting that its potential binding ability to these targets may be the strongest, showing certain potential as a potential drug candidate. TNF has good affinity with 4 compounds in lavender essential oil. As shown in the figure, TNF and two amino acid residues ALA-134 (2.2 Å) and TRP-28 (2.3 Å) on the M1 receptor protein form a stable docking model through H-bonds (Fig. 9a). TNF and two amino acid residues GLN-125 (2.6 Å) and ARG-82 (2.3 Å, 2.0 Å) on the M2 receptor protein form stable H-bonds (Fig. 9b), which play an important role in stabilizing small molecule compounds. Determination of the MIC

The MIC reflects the lowest concentration at which a compound inhibits the growth of microorganisms; a smaller value indicates stronger antibacterial activity43. As shown in Table 5, the positive control exhibits extremely low MICs against bacteria or fungi, verifying the stability of the experimental system and the sensitivity of the strains. The MIC values of linalyl acetate against Staphylococcus aureus and Bacillus subtilis (50.00 μg/mL) are relatively high, and its antibacterial activity is weak. This might be attributed to the fact that the lipophilic ester structure can penetrate the cell membrane at high concentrations. The MIC values of eucalyptol against Escherichia coli and Candida albicans are as low as 3.125 μg/mL, and the MIC value against Staphylococcus aureus is 1.562 μg/mL, showing broad—spectrum activity against both bacteria and fungi. The MIC of caryophyllene against Escherichia coli and Bacillus subtilis is at a medium level, but the MIC value against Candida albicans is only 1.562 μg/mL. The MIC of linalool against most strains is in the range of 6.25–12.50 μg/mL. Although its activity is inferior to that of some compounds, its monoterpenol structure may inhibit key metabolic enzymes. In terms of strain specificity, although the MIC of linalyl acetate is relatively high, it may rapidly disrupt the peptidoglycan layer of Gram-positive bacteria at high concentrations; caryophyllene and linalool show moderate activity against Gram- positive bacteria and can be used as auxiliary components. Eucalyptol has the lowest MIC against Escherichia coli and may penetrate the outer membrane lipopolysaccharide layer, showing potential for developing natural inhibitors against Gram-negative bacteria. Conclusion

Advancements in modern science and technology have enabled comprehensive research on LEO, a natural plant extract that has received extensive attention due to its diverse biological activities including antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, soothing, and antioxidant effects. This study systematically investigated the antimicrobial mechanism of LEO by integrating network pharmacology, molecular docking, and in vitro antibacterial experiments, and found that LEO exerted antimicrobial effects via a multi-component, multi-target, and multi-modal mode achieved through the synergy of direct antibacterial activity and host immune regulation. Using “Antibacterial” as a keyword, host targets associated with bacterial infection were mined from the GeneCards database, verifying that LEO could bind to core host targets such as TNF, JUN, IL6, and CASP3 via its key components and modulate host immune and inflammatory pathways including NOD-like receptor, Toll-like receptor, TNF, and IL-17 signaling pathways, thereby regulating immune responses, alleviating excessive inflammatory injury, and exerting indirect adjuvant anti-infective effects. Meanwhile, in vitro antibacterial experiments were performed to determine the inhibition zone diameters and minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) of four active components, namely linalool, linalyl acetate, 1,8-cineole, and caryophyllene, confirming that these key active ingredients of LEO possess significant antibacterial activity, and these two lines of evidence complement each other to jointly illustrate the antimicrobial effects of LEO. This study clarifies the bioactive basis underlying the direct bacteriostatic effect of LEO and its auxiliary mechanism at the host level, which not only provides solid experimental data and theoretical support for the industrial application of LEO and its core active components in food preservation, skin antisepsis, medical bacteriostasis, and related fields, but also offers feasible strategies and technical references for further research on the multi-modal antimicrobial mechanisms of natural plant essential oils.
