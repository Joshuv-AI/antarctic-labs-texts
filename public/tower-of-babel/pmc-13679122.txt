Ultrasensitive serum levels of interleukin 13 in systemic lupus erythematosus patients: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Iván Ferraz-Amaro, Juan Carlos Quevedo-Abeledo, Beatriz Tejera-Segura, Enrique García-Barrera, Luisa María Villar, Javier Gonzalo Ocejo-Vinyals, Raquel Largo, Miguel Ángel González-Gay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSystemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is characterized by elevated levels of various cytokines. Interleukin (IL)-13 is a pleiotropic type 2 cytokine that plays a central role in allergic inflammation and tissue fibrosis. Until recently, serum measurement of IL-13 was not feasible because of its extremely low concentration. New highly sensitive techniques now allow its quantification at the femtogram level. In the present study, we aimed to evaluate the association between SLE-disease characteristics and ultrasensitive serum IL-13 levels.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, a comprehensive characterization of 313 patients with SLE was performed, including autoantibody profiles and indices of disease activity (SLE-DAS, SLEDAI-2 K, and LLDAS), damage (SLICC-DI), and remission (DORIS). In addition, the cohort was evaluated with complete hematological and lipid profiles, insulin resistance indices, and assessment of subclinical carotid atherosclerosis and carotid stiffness. Serum IL-13 levels were measured using the Simoa (Single Molecule Array) technique. Multivariable linear regression analyses were then conducted to examine the associations between these disease characteristics and circulating IL-13.ResultsIn multivariable regression analyses demographic features, disease activity indices (SLEDAI-2 K, SLE-DAS, DORIS), damage scores, overall disease duration, autoantibodies and interferon or complement levels were not associated with IL-13. However, in the adjusted models, neutrophil and platelet counts showed an inverse association with IL-13. It was also the case for metabolic factors, as higher levels of HDL-cholesterol and apolipoprotein A1 were independently associated with lower IL-13 levels, while a higher LDL: HDL ratio, a higher ApoB: ApoA1 ratio, and a higher atherogenic index were associated with superior IL-13 levels. In contrast, augmentation index was inversely and independently associated with IL-13.ConclusionsSerum IL-13 levels are not associated with key SLE-related features such as disease activity or the autoantibodies profile. Since IL-13 was associated with an adverse lipid profile, its inverse relationship with augmentation index suggests that its cardiovascular effects may be at least partially independent of circulating lipids. These apparently divergent associations likely reflect distinct but interconnected pathways through which IL-13 influences cardiovascular risk in SLE.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41927-026-00694-0.Keywords: Interleukin 13, Systemic lupus erythematosus, Cytopenias, Dyslipidemia

Introduction

IL-13 (interleukin-13) is a pleiotropic type 2 cytokine produced primarily by Th2 cells, ILC2s (group 2 innate lymphoid cells), mast cells, basophils, and eosinophils. It plays a central role in allergic inflammation, anti-helminth immunity, and tissue fibrosis [1]. In this regard, IL-13 is considered the central mediator of allergic asthma, where it regulates eosinophilic inflammation, goblet cell metaplasia with mucus hypersecretion, subepithelial fibrosis, smooth muscle hyperplasia, and airway hyperresponsiveness [2]. However, IL-13 has also anti-inflammatory effects on monocytes/macrophages. It inhibits the production of proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6, IL-8) and chemokines by activated monocytes and promotes alternative (M2) macrophage polarization [3]. It also has a role in endothelial activation upregulating VCAM-1 on endothelial cells, facilitating transmigration of eosinophils, T lymphocytes, monocytes, and basophils into tissues [4]. IL-13 signals through a complex receptor system involving two main receptor types. The type II receptor, composed of IL-4Rα and IL-13Rα1, is the functional signaling receptor shared with IL-4 and activates the JAK1/TYK2–STAT6 pathway. In contrast, IL-13Rα2 is a high-affinity decoy receptor that binds IL-13 but lacks an intracellular signaling domain, thereby acting as a negative regulator by sequestering IL-13 [5]. IL-13 is a major therapeutic target in type 2 inflammation, also referred to as the Th2 response. This immune pattern is closely associated with allergic responses and is primarily mediated by the cytokines IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13. Dupilumab (anti-IL-4Rα) blocks both IL-4 and IL-13 signaling and is approved for asthma, atopic dermatitis, chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps, and eosinophilic esophagitis. Other agents targeting IL-13 specifically (tralokinumab, lebrikizumab) have shown efficacy in atopic dermatitis and asthma [6].

IL-13 is elevated in patients with active systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and contributes to lupus pathogenesis through multiple mechanisms including B cell activation, autoantibody production, regulation of age-associated/autoimmune B cells (ABCs), and epigenetic dysregulation [7]. In this sense, some studies have confirmed that serum IL-13 levels are significantly elevated in SLE patients compared to healthy controls [8]. Besides, IL-13 stimulates B lymphocyte proliferation and differentiation, inducing synthesis of immunoglobulins contributing to polyclonal B cell activation and autoantibody production [9]. Remarkably, its receptor IL-13Rα1 has been described a potential link between the sex bias (female predominance) of lupus and autoimmune B cell biology [10].

However, these studies have not consistently measured IL-13 in serum, as they have sometimes focused on IL-13 at the cellular or tissue level instead. Previous studies assessing serum IL-13 levels have often relied on ELISA-based assays, whose sensitivity may be insufficient to accurately quantify the very low circulating concentrations of this cytokine. Due to this, methodological heterogeneity, particularly differences in assay sensitivity, may have contributed to the inconsistent findings reported in the literature. This limitation should be kept in mind when interpreting associations between IL-13 and specific disease manifestations, as methodological factors may partially explain discrepancies among studies. However, with the advent of ultrasensitive technologies such as Simoa (Single Molecule Array), it is now possible to measure very low ILs concentrations in the femtogram range, which was not previously feasible.

In the present study, we determined for the first time, ultrasensitive serum IL-13 levels in a large cohort of patients with SLE. These patients were extensively characterized not only in terms of disease-related features, but also with respect to cardiovascular comorbidity and detailed lipid and hematological profiles. Consequently, we present a study that comprehensively examines the relationship between these disease characteristics, metabolic features, and serum IL-13 concentrations in patients with SLE.

Materials and methods

Study design

We performed a cross-sectional analysis of 313 adults with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), recruited between September 2023 and February 2024. All participants were enrolled at four tertiary centers in Spain: Hospital Universitario de Canarias (approval CHUC-2023-48), Hospital Universitario Doctor Negrín (2021-023-1), Hospital Insular de Gran Canaria (2021-023-1), and Hospital Fundación Jiménez Díaz (PIC135-23). The protocol was reviewed and approved by the institutional ethics committees of all sites, and written informed consent was obtained from every participant prior to inclusion, in accordance with applicable ethical regulations and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study participants

All participants were adults (≥ 18 years) with a clinical diagnosis of SLE who fulfilled at least four American College of Rheumatology (ACR) classification criteria [11]. They had been diagnosed and managed by rheumatologists and were under regular follow-up in rheumatology outpatient clinics. Patients receiving glucocorticoids were eligible provided their prednisone-equivalent dose was ≤ 10 mg/day, reflecting standard therapeutic practice in SLE. The screening and enrollment workflow is summarized in Supplementary Fig. 1: of 400 preselected patients with a clinical diagnosis of SLE, 45 were excluded for not meeting ≥ 4 ACR criteria; of the remaining 355, 20 were excluded due to incomplete informed consent, and a further 10 were excluded because of inadequate or insufficient serum samples. The final study cohort therefore consisted of 313 patients. Clinical records were systematically reviewed to confirm specific diagnoses and current medications.

Data collection

SLE disease activity and cumulative damage were evaluated using the Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI‑2 K) [12] and the Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics/American College of Rheumatology (SLICC/ACR Damage Index -SDI-) [13], respectively. For analytical purposes, SLEDAI‑2 K scores were categorized as follows: none (0 points), mild (1–5), moderate (6–10), high (11–19), and very high activity (≥ 20), in line with previous reports [14]. Remission was defined according to the Definitions of Remission in SLE (DORIS) criteria, requiring a clinical SLEDAI‑2 K of 0 and a physician global assessment (PGA) < 0.5, while allowing treatment with antimalarials, low-dose glucocorticoids (prednisone ≤ 5 mg/day), and/or maintenance immunosuppressive therapy [15]. Likewise, the Lupus Low Disease Activity State (LLDAS) was defined by a SLEDAI‑2 K ≤ 4 with no activity in major organ systems, absence of new clinical manifestations compared with the prior visit, PGA ≤ 1, prednisone ≤ 7.5 mg/day, and stable use of antimalarials and immunosuppressants [16].

Laboratory assessments

Serum was processed using standardized procedures: samples were collected, promptly aliquoted, and stored at − 80 °C, and remained frozen at this temperature until analysis to safeguard the stability of IL-13 and ensure reliable quantification. Serum total cholesterol, triglycerides, and HDL cholesterol were measured using enzymatic colorimetric assays (Roche), and lipoproteins were quantified by immunoturbidimetric methods from the same manufacturer. The analytical ranges and intra-assay coefficients of variation were 0.08–20.7 mmol/L and 0.3% for total cholesterol, 4–1,000 mg/dL and 1.8% for triglycerides, and 3–120 mg/dL and 0.9% for HDL cholesterol. The atherogenic index was derived from the total cholesterol-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (Castelli index), and LDL cholesterol was estimated using the Friedewald equation. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) was determined using a validated high-sensitivity immunoassay.

Insulin resistance was assessed using the homeostatic model assessment (HOMA2). This model provides estimates of insulin sensitivity (%S) and β-cell function (%B) based on fasting plasma insulin, C‑peptide, and glucose concentrations. In the present study, we applied HOMA2, the updated computer-based version of the model [17]. This model can be applied to estimate insulin sensitivity and β‑cell function from paired fasting plasma glucose and specific insulin or C‑peptide concentrations, within a range of 1–2,200 pmol/L for insulin and 1–25 mmol/L for glucose. C‑peptide provides a better approximation of β‑cell function because it reflects insulin secretion, whereas insulin is preferable for calculating %S, since HOMA‑%S is derived from glucose disposal as a function of circulating insulin. In our study, insulin resistance and insulin sensitivity (%S) were calculated using serum insulin levels, while β‑cell function (%B) was derived from serum C‑peptide. The computer model outputs insulin sensitivity as HOMA2‑%S (with 100% representing normal sensitivity), and HOMA2‑IR (insulin resistance index) is defined as the reciprocal of %S. Insulin (Architect, Abbott, 2000i) and C‑peptide (Immulite 2000, Siemens) were measured using chemiluminescent immunometric assays.

The presence of metabolic syndrome was defined according to the National Cholesterol Education Program (NCEP/ATP III) criteria [18]. Under this definition, metabolic syndrome is diagnosed when at least three of the following five conditions are present: waist circumference > 102 cm in men or > 88 cm in women, blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg, fasting triglycerides > 150 mg/dL, fasting high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol < 40 mg/dL in men or < 50 mg/dL in women, and fasting plasma glucose > 100 mg/dL [18].

Cardiovascular risk was estimated using the Systematic Coronary Risk Evaluation‑2 (SCORE2) algorithm, calculated as previously described from age, sex, smoking status, systolic blood pressure, and non‑HDL cholesterol. SCORE2 provides 10‑year estimates of fatal and non‑fatal cardiovascular events in individuals aged 40–69 years, while the SCORE2‑OP (older persons) model yields 5‑ and 10‑year risk estimates for adults aged ≥ 70 years [19].

Blood cell counts were obtained using a Sysmex-XN automated hematology analyzer (Sysmex, Kobe, Japan), which calculates red cell distribution width from the standard deviation of the mean corpuscular volume distribution curve rather than from the coefficient of variation.

IFN-α was quantified in serum using the Simoa (Single Molecule Array) IFN-α MS Advantage PLUS KitTM (no. 103638, Quanterix, Billerica, MA, USA). IFN-γ was measured with the Cytokine 4-Plex C Advantage PLUS Reagent KitTM (no. 105066, Quanterix, Billerica, MA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. All samples were processed and read on an HD-X analyzer (Quanterix; software version 4.16.2307.14001). For IFN-α, the limit of detection (LOD) and lower limit of quantitation (LLOQ) were 0.003 and 0.016 pg/mL, respectively; for IFN-γ, the corresponding values were 0.012 pg/mL (LOD) and 0.075 pg/mL (LLOQ), as specified by the manufacturer. IL-13 was measured using the Simoa® Cytokine 4-Plex B (C4PB) Advantage PLUS Assay (product no. 104997), with an LOD of 0.001–0.007 pg/mL and an LLOQ of 0.136 pg/mL. In one patient, IL-13 was undetectable; for this case, the IL-13 value was imputed as LOD/√2, as previously recommended [20].

Carotid ultrasound assessment

A carotid ultrasound was performed to measure carotid intima–media thickness (cIMT) in the common carotid artery and to detect focal atherosclerotic plaques in the extracranial carotid tree. Examinations were carried out using an Esaote MyLab 70 ultrasound system (Genova, Italy) equipped with a 7–12 MHz linear transducer and the Quality Intima Media Thickness (QIMT) real-time, radiofrequency-based automated software, developed by Esaote (Maastricht, The Netherlands). The assessment process followed the guidelines established by the Mannheim Consensus [21], which establishes criteria for identifying plaques within the accessible extracranial carotid arteries A plaque was defined as a focal protrusion into the arterial lumen with cIMT > 1.5 mm, and either an increase of at least 50% compared with the adjacent wall segment or a lumen encroachment > 0.5 mm.

Carotid stiffness was evaluated using high‑resolution ultrasound with radiofrequency‑based echo‑tracking on an Esaote® system, which automatically derived pulse wave velocity (PWV), augmentation index (AIx), distensibility coefficient, arterial compliance, and the β‑stiffness index from synchronized measurements of arterial diameter and brachial blood pressure. All recordings were gated to the electrocardiogram to ensure precise alignment with systolic and diastolic phases, enabling reproducible characterization of early vascular changes related to cardiovascular risk [22].

Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical characteristics of SLE patients were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed continuous variables and as percentages for categorical variables, while non-normally distributed continuous variables were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR). Missing data were handled by systematically checking all variables for completeness before analysis; cases with missing key clinical or laboratory information were excluded from the corresponding analyses to minimize potential bias, and the overall proportion of missing data was low and explicitly documented. The distribution of IL-13 concentrations was examined using histograms and normal Q–Q plots in both the original and log-transformed scales. Because raw IL-13 values displayed pronounced right-skewness and clear departures from normality, subsequent parametric analyses were conducted using log-transformed IL-13, which yielded a more symmetric distribution and a closer approximation to normality (Supplementary Fig. 2). Associations between log-transformed circulating IL-13 and clinical characteristics in SLE patients were examined using multivariable linear regression. For interpretability on the original scale, beta coefficients were back‑transformed by calculating exp(beta), with percentage change estimated as (exp(β) − 1)×100. Potential confounders were selected using a change‑in‑estimate strategy, whereby a variable was retained in the multivariable model if its inclusion altered the beta coefficient of the main exposure by at least 10%. This process was applied iteratively to balance adequate control of confounding with model parsimony, while age, sex, and body mass index were designated a priori confounders based on clinical considerations, and smoking was additionally evaluated given its known links to both exposure and outcome. Model assumptions were checked by assessing the normality of residuals through visual inspection of histograms and normal Q‑Q plots, complemented by the Shapiro–Wilk test. All statistical tests were two‑sided with a 5% significance level, analyses were conducted using Stata 17/SE (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA), and data visualizations were generated in Python 3.x via Julius AI (Julius AI Inc., San Francisco, CA, USA; https://julius.ai).

Results

Most participants were women (91%), with a mean age of 52 ± 12 years. Traditional cardiovascular risk factors were frequent: 18% were current smokers, 38% had hypertension, and 28% were obese. In regression analyses with log-transformed IL-13 as the dependent variable, most demographic and cardiovascular risk factors, including age, sex, body mass index, waist circumference, hypertension, obesity, and metabolic syndrome, showed no significant associations with IL-13 levels. Regarding SLE-related variables, overall disease duration, global disease activity indices (SLEDAI-2 K, SLE-DAS, DORIS, and LLDAS), and damage scores (SLICC-DI and SLICC-DI ≥ 1) were not significantly associated with IL-13. In contrast, the presence of anti-ENA and anti-nucleosome antibodies demonstrated significant higher IL-13 levels in univariable analysis, but only the association with anti-nucleosome autoantibodies remained statistically significant after adjustment. The presence of antiphospholipid antibodies or antiphospholipid syndrome showed no significant relationship with IL-13. Among treatments, higher daily prednisone dose was independently associated with increased IL-13, whereas the use of individual immunosuppressants or biologic agents were not related to circulating IL-13. Moreover, the use of intensive immunosuppressive therapy (disease-modifying antirheumatic drug or biologics versus the intake of hydroxychloroquine alone) was not associated with IL-13 values (Table 2).

Hematological profiles relation to circulating IL-13

In analyses evaluating the relationship between hematologic parameters and IL-13, several red blood cell indices showed no significant association with log-transformed IL-13 levels, including hemoglobin, mean corpuscular volume, mean corpuscular hemoglobin, mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration, and red blood cell count (Table 3). Hematocrit displayed a trend toward an inverse association with IL-13 that approached significance after multivariable adjustment. In contrast, higher red cell distribution width was consistently associated with higher IL-13 levels in both univariable and adjusted models (Table 3).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to measure IL-13 using an ultrasensitive assay in patients with SLE, which provides greater robustness to the findings. According to our results, IL-13 is not associated with key disease features such as global disease activity or cumulative damage, autoantibodies or interferon alpha and complement levels. However, IL-13 appears to be related to lower neutrophil and platelet counts, and to a more adverse lipid profile.

Serum IL-13 concentrations in healthy individuals from the general population are extremely low, typically below approximately 1–4 pg/mL and frequently under the detection limit of conventional ELISA-based assays. For example, in a study of 30 blood donors IL-13 had a concentration range of 0–3.9 pg/mL, with a median of 0 pg/mL, indicating that IL-13 was frequently undetectable [23]. In another study using an ELISA-based assay, IL-13 concentrations in human plasma ranged from 0.59 to 68.4 pg/mL [24]. Two additional studies using single-molecule counting reported substantially lower IL-13 concentrations. In one study of 60 healthy individuals, IL-13 levels ranged from < 0.07 to 1.02 pg/mL; the lower limit of quantification was 0.07 pg/mL, and IL-13 was detectable in 98% of samples [25]. In another report including 23 healthy donors, the mean IL-13 concentration was approximately 0.155 pg/mL [26]. In our study, IL-13 concentrations were 103 (63–151) fg/mL, indicating that values were in the sub-pg/mL range in most participants. To our knowledge, no previous study has used the Simoa platform to quantify IL-13 in healthy controls. Therefore, direct comparison of IL-13 concentrations in patients with SLE with those reported using the same technology is not currently possible. Although our observed concentrations appear lower than those reported in the aforementioned studies of healthy populations, no firm conclusion can be drawn without appropriately designed comparative studies using the same assay platform and pre-analytical conditions.

The relationship between IL-13 and SLE activity is complex and remains inconclusive. Some studies have reported a positive association between IL-13 and disease activity, whereas others have not confirmed this link. Consequently, until today, IL-13, has not emerged as a reliable biomarker of global SLE activity. For example, it has been showed that patients with active SLE had significantly higher serum IL-13 levels, but the association was stronger with specific clinical patterns than with SLEDAI itself: a pure Th2 profile (IL-13 plus IL-6) was linked to nephritis, whereas a mixed Th2/Th1 profile (IL-13 plus IFN-γ) was associated with hemolytic anemia and pulmonary involvement [8]. Similarly, another study in 15 patients with SLE showed that IL-13 transcription levels in CD4 + T cells correlated with disease activity [27]. However, two studies found no relationship between IL-13 and disease activity [9, 28]. Our study extends previous ones in terms of the number of recruited patients and, consequently, the possibility of performing multivariable analyses. Based on our findings, serum IL-13 was not associated with disease activity or cumulative damage over time. However, since our findings refer to serum IL-13 levels, we cannot rule out that it may still play a role in disease activity at the tissue or molecular level.

IL-13 has been reported to contribute to autoantibody production through several interconnected mechanisms, including basophil activation, enhanced survival of plasmacytoid dendritic cells, and direct promotion of B-cell differentiation. In a murine model, administration of recombinant IL-13 exacerbated disease, as evidenced by increased glomerular IgG deposition, whereas blockade of IL-13 with a monoclonal antibody significantly reduced anti-dsDNA autoantibody production [29]. In our study, we found only a positive and significant association between IL-13 and the presence of anti-nucleosome antibodies. Notably, no association was observed with other antinuclear autoantibodies, anti-DNA, or antiphospholipid antibodies. Therefore, a cross-sectional serum-level association between IL-13 and autoantibody production in SLE does not appear to be supported by our findings. It was also the case for the use of different therapies in the disease, which showed no association with IL-13 levels.

In our study, we observed an association between IL-13 and lower neutrophil and platelet counts in patients with SLE. To our knowledge, this relationship has not been previously described, except for a report linking IL-13 to hemolytic anemia in SLE [8]. Although the mechanisms underlying this association remain unclear, IL-13 is known to play a role in B-cell biology and myeloid cell regulation. Therefore, higher IL-13 concentrations may be associated with immune-mediated peripheral destruction or enhanced clearance of blood cells, processes that may contribute to neutropenia and thrombocytopenia in SLE. While causality cannot be inferred from our cross-sectional design, these findings suggest that IL-13 may serve as a marker of immune pathways involved in the development of cytopenias.

We observed a significant and independent association between IL-13 and an adverse lipid profile in patients with SLE, characterized by lower HDL-cholesterol and apolipoprotein A1 levels, as well as higher LDL: HDL and ApoB: ApoA1 ratios and an increased atherogenic index. However, no association was found with triglycerides. In contrast, previous studies in metabolically dysregulated populations have reported a different pattern, in which IL-13 is primarily linked to triglyceride levels rather than cholesterol-related parameters. In this regard, in a study of 92 individuals, a strong positive correlation between serum IL-13 and triglycerides was observed, whereas no association was found with total cholesterol. This relationship was mainly described in patients with type 2 diabetic nephropathy, overweight or obese women, and individuals with metabolic syndrome [30]. These differences may probably reflect distinct pathophysiological mechanisms underlying lipid abnormalities in metabolic diseases and SLE. In diabetes, dyslipidemia is largely driven by insulin resistance and altered metabolism of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins, making triglycerides the predominant lipid abnormality. In contrast, lipid disturbances in SLE are more closely linked to chronic immune activation and inflammation, which preferentially affect HDL metabolism, ApoA1 levels, and reverse cholesterol transport, resulting in a more atherogenic cholesterol-related profile. Mechanistically, IL-13 has been proposed to influence lipid metabolism through pathways involving increased hepatic proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) expression, leading to enhanced LDL receptor degradation, as well as through downregulation of LXRα-dependent ATP-binding cassette transporters such as ABCG5, thereby impairing cholesterol efflux [31]. These pathways may predominantly affect cholesterol homeostasis rather than triglyceride metabolism, consistent with the pattern observed in SLE. Taken together, our findings suggest that IL-13 may be linked to an immunometabolic phenotype in SLE characterized by a more atherogenic lipid profile and increased cardiovascular risk.

We also observed a negative association between IL-13 and AIx. This finding is noteworthy given that IL-13 has been broadly associated with a protective role in cardiovascular disease, including atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction, heart failure, and myocarditis [32]. Experimental studies have shown that IL-13 reduces macrophage content in atherosclerotic plaques, increases lesional collagen deposition, and decreases VCAM-1–mediated monocyte recruitment. In addition, IL-13 promotes macrophage polarization toward an M2 phenotype, which enhances clearance of oxidized LDL compared with M1 macrophages [33]. This is further supported by evidence that blockade of IL-13 signaling with dupilumab may increase the risk of post-ischemic heart failure.

Taken together, although IL-13 was associated with an adverse lipid profile, its inverse relationship with AIx suggests that its cardiovascular effects may be at least partially independent of circulating lipids. These apparently divergent associations likely reflect distinct but interconnected pathways through which IL-13 influences cardiovascular risk in SLE. Moreover, AIx has previously been associated with carotid intima–media thickness in patients with SLE [34], supporting the notion that hemodynamic abnormalities detected by AIx may be linked to the subsequent development of carotid atherosclerosis. Accordingly, an association between IL-13 and AIx in the absence of an association with carotid plaque may be biologically plausible, as AIx may capture functional vascular alterations that precede—or are not necessarily accompanied by—established focal atherosclerotic plaque. Specifically, IL-13 may contribute to systemic metabolic alterations that promote an atherogenic lipid profile, while simultaneously exerting anti-inflammatory and tissue-protective effects within the vascular wall, including reduced endothelial activation, decreased leukocyte recruitment, and M2 macrophage polarization. This compartment-specific activity may help explain the partial dissociation between lipid abnormalities and functional measures of vascular disease in SLE.

Besides, IL-13 may reflect a broader immune pathway associated with early functional vascular alterations, which may be partly independent of the autoantibody specificities assessed in the present study as autoantibodies positivity were not related to IL-13. Other humoral mechanisms, including unmeasured autoantibody specificities, immune-complex–mediated inflammation, qualitative antibody characteristics, or B-cell activation, may contribute to this association.

In addition, we observed an inverse association between IL-13 levels and neutrophil and platelet counts. Although this relationship has not been previously described, experimental evidence indicates that IL-13 can influence hematopoiesis, suggesting a potential regulatory role in myeloid cell production [35]. Nevertheless, this finding requires prospective confirmation, and the precise physiological mechanisms underlying this association warrant further investigation.

Our study has several limitations. First, we did not include a healthy control group. This reflects our primary objective, which was to examine how IL-13 relates to clinical manifestations of SLE, rather than to compare its levels with those of the general population, which has already been partially addressed in previous studies. In addition, due to the cross-sectional design, causal inferences cannot be drawn from our findings. Although serum IL-13 levels were not associated with disease activity, it cannot be excluded that IL-13 may still play a role at other levels (tissue, cellular, or transcriptomic) in the mechanisms driving disease activity or damage. Our cohort also had a high proportion of patients in low disease activity or remission, which likely reflects optimal clinical control rather than a selection bias. For this reason, we cannot rule out the possibility that IL-13 may be related to disease flares or that its levels may vary over time in response to changes in disease activity or specific treatments. Moreover, because multiple associations were examined without formal adjustment for multiplicity, the possibility of false-positive findings cannot be excluded. The reported p values are nominal, and the results should be interpreted according to the magnitude and precision of the effect estimates rather than solely on the conventional p < 0.05 threshold. Accordingly, the observed associations should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating until externally validated. B-cell activating factor (BAFF) has been implicated in both allergic/IL-13 responses and SLE pathogenesis. Therefore, as BAFF levels were not assessed in our study, we cannot determine whether BAFF-related mechanisms mediate or modify the association between IL-13 levels and disease-related features in SLE. This possibility should be investigated in future studies. In addition, although anti-nucleosome autoantibody positivity was associated with IL-13 levels, the sample size may have limited the power to detect differences in analyses stratified by individual autoantibody status. Accordingly, modest associations within specific autoantibody-defined subgroups cannot be excluded, and larger longitudinal studies are warranted. Finally, although disease-related treatments were not significantly associated with IL-13 levels in our analyses, residual confounding or treatment-related effects on IL-13 concentrations and the observed associations cannot be completely excluded.

On the other hand, several strengths of our study should be noted. These include the sample size, which allowed for a broad multivariable analysis, the comprehensive characterization of the cohort, which was not limited to disease-specific features but also included hematologic manifestations and cardiovascular disease-related variables, and the use of the Simoa technique, in which only one subject had undetectable IL-13 levels.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study indicates that serum IL-13 levels are not associated with hallmark SLE-related features, including disease activity or the autoantibody profile. In contrast, IL-13 is independently associated with lower neutrophil, and platelet counts and with an adverse lipid pattern in patients with SLE. These findings suggest that, while IL-13 may not play a central role in the classic immunological manifestations of SLE, it could contribute to hematologic abnormalities and metabolic dysregulation in this population.
