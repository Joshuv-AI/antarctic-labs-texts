On the Variability of Concept Activation Vectors
Julia Wenkmann, Damien Garreau

Abstract
One of the most pressing challenges in artificial intelligence is to make models more transparent to their users. Recently, explainable artificial intelligence has come up with numerous method to tackle this challenge. A promising avenue is to use concept-based explanations, that is, high-level concepts instead of plain feature importance score. Among this class of methods, Concept Activation vectors ( Cav s), Kim et al. 2018 ( Kim et al. 2018 ) stands out as one of the main protagonists. One interesting aspect of Cav s is that their computation requires sampling random examples in the train set. Therefore, the actual vectors obtained may vary from user to user depending on the randomness of this sampling. In this paper, we propose a fine-grained theoretical analysis of Cav s construction in order to quantify their variability. Our results, confirmed by experiments on several real-life datasets, point out towards an universal result: the variance of Cav s decreases as 1 / N 1/N , where N N is the number of random examples. Based on this we give practical recommendations for a resource-efficient application of the method.

Introduction

Explainable Artificial Intelligence (
Xai

) has rapidly ascended to the forefront of machine learning research, as the field grapples with the challenge of making deep neural networks more transparent and trustworthy.
Recently, explainable artificial intelligence has come up with numerous method to tackle this challenge.
A promising avenue is to use concept-based explanations, that is, high-level concepts instead of plain feature importance score
(
Poeta et al. 2023
)
.
One method, that has risen to prominence in Concept-based Explainable Artificial Intelligence (C-
Xai

), is
Testing with Concept Activation Vectors (
Tcav

), introduced by
Kim et al. 2018

(
Kim et al. 2018
)
.

Tcav

quantifies the influence of human-understandable concepts (
e.g.
, “stripes”) on a specific class (
e.g.
, “zebra”). The core of the method is the Concept Activation Vector (
Cav

).
This vector defines the direction of a concept in a latent layer as the normal vector of a linear boundary between the embeddings of concept examples and a set of randomly selected reference data, so-called random samples.
The resulting
Tcav

score measures the sensitivity of a prediction for this specific concept, as described in detail in Section
3
.

A problem of
Xai

methods is their stability (also referred to as consistency).
A method is considered stable if it provides identical or at least similar explanations for the same model and input when applied repeatedly
(
Alvarez-Melis and Jaakkola 2018
)
.
Failure to meet this standard can lower the trust users place in the explanation, a known issue for many
Xai

techniques
(
Krishna et al. 2024
)
.
Due to its reliance on random sampling,
Tcav

’s results exhibit high variability from one run to another.

Kim et al. 2018

(
Kim et al. 2018
)
acknowledged this issue and suggested running the method several times and reporting the average scores to mitigate the variability.
However, this solution is not perfect, as the average itself is still subject to variance. This leads to a fundamental question regarding computational resources: how can the variance of the result be minimized within a limited sampling budget? In other words, is it more effective to conduct a single run with a large number of samples, or to average the outcomes of multiple runs, each using a smaller sample set?

To answer this question, we conduct the (first) theoretical analysis of the influence of the number of random samples on the variance of
Tcav

.
Based on this, we derive practical recommendations for a resource-efficient application of the method.

Our contributions are as follows:

•

Asymptotic variability of CAVs.
In the infinitely imbalanced regime (fixed concept set,
N\!\to\!\infty
random references) we prove asymptotic normality of the penalized logistic CAV estimator (Thm.
1
). Under some reasonable assumptions, the covariance trace decays as
\mathcal{O}(1/N)
(Cor.
4.2
).

•

Sensitivity and
Tcav

.
The asymptotic normality transfers to sensitivity scores; Under the same assumptions, their variance scales as
\mathcal{O}(1/N)
, whereas
Tcav

may retain
\Theta(1)
variance due to borderline evaluation points.

•

Multi-run averaging.
Averaging
Tcav

over
s
independent runs reduces variance as
\mathcal{O}(1/s)
(Conj.
1
); we discuss the effect of mild dependence.

•

Guidelines.
To stabilize
Tcav

, prefer several modest-
N
runs; to stabilize the
Cav

direction for downstream use, increase
N
per run.

We provide the code for all experiments in the supplementary material and will publicly release it after publication.

Subsequent work has also focused on improving the accuracy of

Pattern-based

Finally, researchers such as

Pahde et al. 2022

, and
Bareeva et al. 2024

Bareeva et al. 2024

Prior Theoretical Analysis of XAI.

An important part of evaluating
Xai

the extent to which an explanation method provides deterministic explanations for the same inputs to be explained.
Such an analysis has already been carried out for some established

Although Concept Activation Vectors are widely used, their fundamental properties and limitations have been underexplored, with only few works systematically addressing this gap

Ramaswamy et al. 2023
,
Soni et al. 2020

Soni et al. 2020
).

In this paper, we extend the last point, the
sensitivity to data variations

s. To the best of our knowledge, there has been no throughout investigation of this topic to date.

3.1
Generating Concept Activation Vectors

We can understand how a model works by looking at the concepts it learns in its hidden layers. For instance, a network might identify “stripes” as an important concept when classifying an image as a “zebra”.
To formalize this, we split our model
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{K}
intended for the classification of
K
classes, into two parts
f\vcentcolon=g_{\ell}\circ h_{\ell}
, at a specific layer
\ell
.
Here
h_{\ell}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the model up to layer
\ell
, and
g_{\ell}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{K}
is the remaining part of the network, which transforms the embedding vectors in the latent space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
into class logits.
In other words, for a fixed class
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
, the function

g_{\ell,k}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}

maps a latent embedding
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to the logit (or score) of class
k
.
This is illustrated in Figure
1
.

To compute the
Cav

in layer
\ell
, we collect:

1.

Positive examples
of concept
C
,
i.e.
, a set of
n
inputs
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathcal{X}
that clearly exhibit the concept
C
. These examples can be sourced from annotated datasets, like the
Broden
dataset
(
Bau et al. 2017
)
, or be custom-curated for the specific concept under study.

2.

Negative (random) examples
,
i.e.
, a set of
N
inputs
\{z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}\subset\mathcal{X}
selected uniformly among the training set.

We then compute the latent embeddings of the concepts and the random examples.
Since raw feature vectors do not trivially encode the presence or absence of a concept
C
,
Tcav

trains a linear classifier to discriminate between positive
\bigl\{h_{\ell}(x_{i})\bigr\}_{i=1}^{n}
and negative
\bigl\{h_{\ell}(z_{j})\bigr\}_{j=1}^{N}
embeddings.
The normal vector to the decision boundary learned by the classifier, oriented towards the concept examples, is defined as the Concept Activation Vector
v_{C}^{\ell}
of concept
C
.
Note, that the
Cav

depends on both the random sampling and the stochastic nature of the classifier training process.
Several different classifiers can be employed:

•

The original
Tcav
implementation defaults to a linear Support Vector Machine (
SGDClassifier
with hinge loss
(
Pedregosa et al. 2011
)
), as detailed in Appendix
B.2
.

•

A simpler approach,
Difference of Means
, proposed by
Martin 2019

(
Martin 2019
)
, is adopted in Visual-
Tcav

(
Santis et al. 2024
)
. It calculates the
Cav
as the difference between the mean embeddings of the concept and random samples.
Santis et al. 2024
report that this estimator yields better
Cav
quality. This case is discussed in Appendix
B.3
.

Despite the differences in these models, all of those different classifiers exhibit the same asymptotic normality behaviour, as demonstrated experimentally in Appendix
A
.
However, in this paper, we focus on
LogisticRegression
with binary cross-entropy loss. We choose this method because it presents a more amenable mathematical analysis and is another prominent classifier available in the official
Tcav
libraries (
Kokhlikyan et al. 2020

Kokhlikyan et al. 2020
,
Kim et al. 2025

Kim et al. 2025
).

3.2
Calculating TCAV Scores

Once the
Cav

v_{C}^{\ell}
has been determined for the concept
C
in the layer
\ell
,
Tcav

then evaluates the relevance of this concept for a class
k
by comparing the gradient of
g_{\ell,k}
with the direction of
v_{C}^{\ell}
using the dot product (
\cdot
).
Specifically, for an input
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{k}
, that is, an input
\mathbf{x}
of class
k
,
Tcav

defines the
sensitivity score
of
\mathbf{x}
as

S_{C,k,\ell}(\mathbf{x})\;\vcentcolon=\;\nabla g_{\ell,k}\bigl(h_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})\bigr)\;\cdot\;v_{C}^{\ell}\,.

(1)

The score
S_{C,k,\ell}(\mathbf{x})
measures a concept’s influence on the classification of
\mathbf{x}
. A positive score means the concept pulls the prediction toward class
k
, while a negative score pushes it away.
To aggregate these local sensitivities, the
Tcav

score
is defined as the proportion of samples
\mathbf{x}
from the class
k
whose sensitivity
S_{C,k,\ell}(\mathbf{x})
is positive, that is,

\mathrm{\text{{Tcav} }}_{C,k,\ell}\;\vcentcolon=\;\frac{\bigl|\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{k}\mid S_{C,k,\ell}(\mathbf{x})>0\}\bigr|}{\bigl|\mathcal{X}_{k}\bigr|}\,.

(2)

A score close to
1
means concept
C
shifts the classification toward class
k
, a low score (close to
0
) indicates a strong negative effect, while a score near
0.5
implies no consistent effect.
Finally, a two-tailed
t
-test (see,
e.g.
,
(
Hogg, McKean, and Craig 2019
)
, Chapter 4.5) is performed on the sensitivity values to ensure that the effect captured by the
Tcav

score is not due to random variation.

4
Theoretical analysis

We now present our main theoretical results regarding the variability of
Cav

s obtained by logistic regression when the number of random examples goes to infinity.
This reflects the practice well: in applications, the number of annotated concepts is usually restricted (for instance, a typical concept class in the Broden dataset has on average
50
examples
(
Bau et al. 2017
)
), whereas the number of random examples is virtually unlimited (the ImageNet dataset has
10^{6}
images
(
Deng et al. 2009
)
).
Therefore, we analyse the stability of
Cav

s in this
(infinitely) imbalanced setting.

Let us now define the formal setting for our analysis.

4.1
Theoretical setting

We consider two sets of feature vectors:

•

n

fixed points
,
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, which represent our “concept” samples and are assigned the class label
Y=1
.

•

N

random points
,
\{z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
, which are drawn from the distribution
F_{0}
of training samples and assigned the class label
Y=0
.

For simplicity, we use
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\{z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
as shorthand for their respective latent representations,
\{h_{\ell}(x_{i})\}
and
\{h_{\ell}(z_{j})\}
in the definitions above as well as throughout the rest of this paper.
We denote the
intercept
by
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and the
coefficients
by
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Following
Owen 2007

(
Owen 2007
)
, for any input
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we write the logistic regression model as

\mathbb{P}(Y=1|X=w)=\sigma(\alpha+\beta^{\top}w)\,,

(3)

where
\sigma(u)\vcentcolon=(1+\mathrm{e}^{-u})^{-1}
, with
u\in\mathbb{R}
.
For any given
N
, we fit the logistic regression model Eq. (
3
) by maximizing the
L^{2}
-penalized log-likelihood

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{N}^{(\lambda)}(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\sigma(\alpha+\beta^{\top}x_{i})

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\log(1-\sigma(\alpha+\beta^{\top}z_{j}))-\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert^{2}\,,

(4)

where
\lambda
is the regularization parameter.
We denote by
(\alpha_{N},\beta_{N})
the unique maximizer of this function.
The theoretical coefficient
\beta_{N}
corresponds exactly to our empirical
Cav

v_{C}^{\ell}
calculated with
N
random samples.
Note, however, that our analysis does not consider any effects from the optimisation procedure.

4.2
Variability of Concept Activation Vectors

In this section, we prove asymptotic normality of
Cav

s in the special case of infinite imbalance.
This specific logistic regression setting, where the number of samples in one class grows infinitely large while the other is held fixed, was previously analyzed by
Owen 2007

(
Owen 2007
)
and
Goldman and Zhang 2022

(
Goldman and Zhang 2022
)
.
Our result, similar to that of
Owen 2007

(
Owen 2007
)
, is conditional upon the “Surrounded Mean” Assumption
1
. This is a weak condition, postulating that infinite random samples surround the concept’s mean
\bar{x}
in the latent space, which generally holds in practice (see Appendix
B.1
).

Assumption 1

(Surrounded Mean)
.

The distribution
F_{0}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
has the point
\bar{x}
“surrounded,” that is

\int_{(z-\bar{x})^{\top}\omega>\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}F_{0}(z)>\delta

(5)

holds for some
\varepsilon>0
, some
\delta>0
and all
\omega\in\Omega
where
\Omega=\{\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}|\omega^{\top}\omega=1\}
is the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\bar{x}
is the mean of the concept embeddings
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

This mild geometric condition says that, in every direction, the random references populate a non-trivial cap around
\bar{x}
. Our second assumption is a technical one. We assume that the limit of the Hessian of Eq. (
4
) is exists and invertible, which is a standard condition in the analysis of maximum likelihood estimators.

Assumption 2

(Limit Hessian)
.

Assume that the matrix
H_{0}
, defined as the limit in probability of the normalized Hessian of the loss function,

H_{0}:=\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{N}\nabla^{2}_{\beta}\mathcal{L}^{(\lambda)}(\alpha_{N},\beta_{N})\right)

exists and is invertible.

We can now state our main result:

Theorem 1

(Asymptotic variability of CAVs)
.

Assume that Assumptions
1
and
2
hold, that the limit
A_{0}=\lim_{N\to\infty}N\mathrm{e}^{\alpha_{N}}
exists and that
\beta_{N}\xrightarrow{p}\beta_{0}
, where
\xrightarrow{p}
denotes convergence in probability as
N\to\infty
.
Then
\beta_{N}
is asymptotically normal.
Namely,

\sqrt{N}(\beta_{N}-\beta_{0})\xrightarrow{D}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)\,,

(6)

where

\xrightarrow{D}
denotes convergence in distribution. A precise expression of
\Sigma
is given in Appendix
B.1
(Eq. (
18
)).

Mean variability of
Cav

s for
ResNet
at layer
‘layer2‘
as a function of the number of random examples per run (

\pm 1
standard deviation; the

.

Informally, this means that for large values of
N
,
\sqrt{N}(\beta_{N}-\beta_{0})
behaves as a multivariate normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
.
This finding is consistent with standard statistical theory. The
Cav

\beta_{N}

behaves like a logistic regression estimator
(
Goldman and Zhang 2022
)
, whose variance is known to scale inversely with the sample size.

Let us now return to our central topic: the variability of the
Cav

s. A direct consequence of Theorem
1
is that the
Cav

s become more stable with increasing number
N
of random samples used.
Coming back to variability, we choose the trace of the covariance matrix of the
Cav

s ,
i.e.
, the sum of the per-feature variance, as a variability measure. This measure will equal zero,
if and only if
the
Cav

s had no variability at all.

Definition 1

(Variance of CAVs)
.

We define the variance of
\beta_{N}
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\beta_{N})

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\sum_{j}\mathrm{Cov}(\beta_{N})_{jj}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\bigl(\beta_{N}-\mathbb{E}\left[\beta_{N}\right]\bigr)\bigl(\beta_{N}-\mathbb{E}\left[\beta_{N}\right]\bigr)^{\top}\Bigr]_{jj}\;\,.

Using this definition, we are now able to examine the variability of
Cav

s .

Asymptotic Behavior of the Covariance Trace

Theorem
1
gives
\sqrt{N}(\hat{\beta}_{N}-\beta_{0})\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
.
Assuming, that the sequence
\{\sqrt{N}(\hat{\beta}_{N}-\beta_{0})\}_{N\geq 1}
is uniformly integrable, this entails

\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\beta}_{N})(\hat{\beta}_{N})\big)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{1}{N}\right).

See Appendix
B.1
, Corollary
4.2
for a precise statement and proof.
Essentially, this means that as the random dataset size (
N
) grows, the variability in the direction of the Concept Activation Vector (
\beta_{N}
) shrinks at a rate proportional to
1/N
. For instance, multiplying the number of random examples
N
by ten reduces the variance of the estimator by a factor of ten.
This is in accordance with our experimental results presented in Figure
2
. Additional experimental findings for different datasets and models are presented in Section
5
.

Sketch of the proof of Theorem
1
.

The proof uses and adapts methods from
(
Owen 2007
)
and
(
Goldman and Zhang 2022
)
. The main difference with their work is that we take the
L^{2}
penalization of the objective (
4
) into account, thus extending their results.
This leads to a different, but still asymptotically normal convergence behaviour in
\mathcal{O}(N^{1/N})
.
We proceed as follows: First, we perform a
Taylor-expansion
on the gradient of our loss function (
4
) at the optimum, where
\nabla_{\beta}\mathcal{L}^{(\lambda)}(\beta_{N})=0
, around the true parameter
\beta_{0}
. This allows us to express
\sqrt{N}(\beta_{N}-\beta_{0})
as the product of the inverse Hessian and the scaled score. Then, the normalized Hessian converges to a constant by the Law of Large Numbers. Vitally, the scaled score converges to a zero-mean normal distribution via the Central Limit Theorem, as the term from the
L^{2}
-penalty is perfectly cancelled by the score’s expectation.
Slutzky 1937
’s Theorem
(
Slutzky 1937
)
then combines these results to establish the asymptotic normality of
\beta_{N}
. The full proof is provided in Appendix
B.1
.

4.3
Variance of Sensitivity Scores

We now analyze whether the asymptotic normality of
Cav

s transfers to the sensitivity scores. We restate them as

S(\mathbf{x},{\beta}_{N})\;:=\;\nabla g_{\ell,k}\bigl(h_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})\bigr)\;\cdot\;{\beta}_{N}\,.

(7)

The following corollary shows that we can transfer the asymptotic convergence of the
Cav

s. The variance of the sensitivity score decreases at the rate
\mathcal{O}(1/N)
, like the variance of
{\beta}_{N}
, as demonstrated in Figure
3
.

N
of random samples used for the class “zebra” in the Imagenet classification setting for one input. The

Corollary 1

(Asymptotic variance of the Sensitivity Scores)
.

Under the conditions specified in Theorem
1
, the sensitivity score
S(\mathbf{x},{\beta}_{N})
satisfies

Z_{N}:=\sqrt{N}\left(S(\mathbf{x},\beta_{N})-S(\mathbf{x},\beta_{0})\right)\xrightarrow{D}\mathcal{N}\left(0,V(\mathbf{x})\right),

(8)

where the asymptotic variance
V(\mathbf{x})
is given by

V(\mathbf{x}):=\nabla g_{\ell,k}\bigl(h_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})\bigr)^{\top}\cdot\Sigma\cdot\nabla g_{\ell,k}\bigl(h_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})\bigr)\,.

(9)

This corollary is a direct consequence of Theorem
1
and the properties of multivariate normal distributions. Since the sensitivity score
S(\mathbf{x},\beta_{N})
is a linear transformation (specifically, a dot product) of the random vector
\beta_{N}
, the asymptotic normality of
\sqrt{N}(\beta_{N}-\beta_{0})
transfers directly to the scaled sensitivity scores. The variance of the sensitivity score therefore diminishes at a rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/N)
, confirming that a larger sample size also yields more stable and trustworthy sensitivity scores, besides improved
Cav

s.

Our intuition for this quite surprising fact is, that across the embeddings of samples from a specific class, over which we calculate the
Tcav

score, a subset of them may lie
on
or
near
the decision boundary. This makes their classification highly sensitive to small changes in the model. Those samples, which we call “borderline points”, therefore still contribute to the variance of the
Tcav

score. This holds even when the variance of the
Cav

s vanishes asymptotically. For any other “non-borderline” point, the classification is asymptotically stable, and its contribution to the variance is negligible.
The covariance between any two borderline points, on the other side, is a constant value,
\mathcal{O}(1)
, that does not decrease as the
Cav

estimate improves. Therefore, the total variance is dominated by the sum over the pairs of “borderline points” and
\operatorname{Var}(\mathrm{\textsc{Tcav}})=\mathcal{O}(1).
More examples of this behavior are provided in Appendix
A
.
Based on our empirical and theoretical observations we can now give concrete recommendations for action.

4.5
Recommendations for practice

Kim et al. 2018

(
Kim et al. 2018
)
already incorporated into their official implementation the method of running the
Tcav

algorithm multiple times with
s
different random sets and then calculating the mean value of the obtained
Tcav

scores.
Precisely, the multi-run approach partitions a set of
R
random samples into
s
disjoint subsets, each of size
N=R/s
. For each subset
\in[1,\dots,s]
, a separate logistic regression is trained to find a
Cav

\beta_{N}^{(j)}
. This yields
s
individual
Tcav

scores

T_{j}:=\mathrm{TCAV}\bigl(\beta_{N}^{(j)}\bigr)=\frac{\bigl|\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{k}\mid S(\mathbf{x},\beta_{N}^{(j)})>0\}\bigr|}{\bigl|\mathcal{X}_{k}\bigr|}\;.

The final
multi-run
Tcav

score
,
T_{\mathrm{multi}}
, is the average of these individual scores
T_{\mathrm{multi}}\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{j=1}^{s}T_{j}.

Based on our observations, averaging
Tcav

scores over multiple runs is indeed the most favourable method. Our analysis shows that as
s
increases, the variance decreases as
\mathcal{O}(1/s)
(see Figure
5
).
This empirical finding aligns well with our theoretical expectations, as described below in Conjecture
1
.

Conjecture 1
.

Assuming
\operatorname{Var}(\mathrm{\textsc{Tcav}})=\mathcal{O}(1)
, the variance of the multi-run score
T_{\mathrm{multi}}
scales inversely with the number of subsets
s
:

\operatorname{Var}\,\bigl(T_{\mathrm{multi}}\bigr)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{s}\right).

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we analyze the stability of the
Tcav

method. To this end, we introduce a mathematical framework to theoretically analyze variability in the limit of infinitely imbalanced logistic regression, which applies beyond the scope of this work. Building on this analysis and extensive experiments, we provide practical recommendations on how many random samples to use and how to allocate them for optimal stability in
Tcav

. We further present a thorough exploration of the compute-consistency trade-off, quantifying how a fixed budget is best split between (i) more independent runs with fewer random samples per run and (ii) fewer runs with larger per-run sample sizes.
Crucially, this trade-off is method- and implementation-dependent
: it differs for
Tcav

scores versus
Cav

s and varies with the logistic-regression solver, regularization, feature normalization, and stopping criteria.
We demonstrate that, for stable
Tcav

scores, a modest number of random samples distributed across multiple independent runs is sufficient and compute-efficient. By contrast, applications that require stable
Cav

s, such as bias mitigation or feature steering, benefit more from larger per-run sample sizes than from additional runs. We emphasize again that there is no single best setting: the optimal compute allocation should be re-evaluated for the specific method and implementation at hand.

Building on our findings, we identify two primary directions for future work. First, the nature of the trade-off between compute time and stability is highly implementation-dependent. We therefore propose investigating this trade-off in more detail to provide implementation-specific advice. Second, since our analysis assumed perfect optimization, an important goal for future work is to investigate the role of the optimizer. In particular, it would be interesting to examine the interaction between its convergence properties and the statistical stability of the resulting explanation.

Lemma 2

(Bounding

(Lemma 7 in

Assume that Assumption

Proof.

Let the regularized log-likelihood be
\mathcal{L}^{(\lambda)}(\alpha,\beta)=\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta)-\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert^{2}
.
We analyze the behavior of the objective function by comparing its value at an arbitrary
\beta

\beta=0

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{(\lambda)}(\alpha,0)-\mathcal{L}^{(\lambda)}(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathcal{L}(\alpha,0)-\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert 0\right\rVert^{2}\right)-\left(\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta)-\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(\alpha,0)-\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert^{2}.

For the unregularized part,
\mathcal{L}(\alpha,0)-\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta)
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\alpha,0)-\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=-(n+N)\log(1+e^{\alpha})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left(1+e^{\alpha+(x_{i}-\bar{x})^{\prime}\beta}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+N\int\log\left(1+e^{\alpha+(x-\bar{x})^{\prime}\beta}\right)dF_{0}(x).

Since
\log(1+e^{y})>0

\mathcal{L}(\alpha,0)-\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta)>-(n+N)\log(1+e^{\alpha})\,.

Using the inequality

. Thus,

\mathcal{L}(\alpha,0)-\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta)>-(n+N)e^{\alpha}.

Substituting this into the expression for the regularized difference, we obtain

\mathcal{L}^{(\lambda)}(\alpha,0)-\mathcal{L}^{(\lambda)}(\alpha,\beta)>\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert^{2}-(n+N)e^{\alpha}.

The objective function
\mathcal{L}^{(\lambda)}(\alpha,\beta)
must be less than
\mathcal{L}^{(\lambda)}(\alpha,0)

\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert^{2}>(n+N)e^{\alpha}.

As in the original problem, we let
\mathrm{e}^{\alpha}=A/N

The condition becomes

\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert^{2}>\frac{2(n+N)e^{\alpha}}{\lambda}=\frac{2(n+N)A/N}{\lambda}=\frac{2A}{\lambda}\left(1+\frac{n}{N}\right).

\beta_{N}

\beta_{N}
must be bounded as follows

\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{N}^{2}\leq\frac{2A}{\lambda}\left(1+\frac{n}{N}\right).

We still rely on Lemma

To extend this result to the real asymptotic convergence behaviour of our Concept Activation Vectors, we need the notation of

(

Definition 2

(Uniform Integrability)
.

A sequence of random variables
\{X_{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
is uniformly integrable if

\lim_{K\to\infty}\sup_{N\geq 1}E\left[|X_{N}|\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{|X_{N}|>K\}}\right]=0.

Now we can prove the following corollary about the asymptotic behavior of the covariance trace.

Corollary 4

(Asymptotic Behavior of the Covariance Trace)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1
and
uniform integrability
, the asymptotic covariance matrix of the estimator
\beta_{N}
is proportional to
\frac{1}{N}\Sigma
.
Consequently, the trace of the covariance matrix
\mathrm{Var}(\beta_{N})
is of order
\mathcal{O}(N^{-1})
.

Proof.

Theorem
1
states that:

\sqrt{N}(\beta_{N}-\beta_{0})\xrightarrow{D}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)

Uniform integrability also ensures the convergence of the relevant moments (
Vaart 2007

Vaart 2007
, Theorem 2.20). Therefore, the covariance matrix of the sequence of random variables converges to the covariance matrix of the limiting distribution. This allows us to write the exact limit:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathrm{Cov}(\sqrt{N}(\beta_{N}-\beta_{0}))=\Sigma

(19)

Using the properties of the covariance operator, where
\beta_{0}
is a constant vector and
N
is a scalar, we have the identity:

\mathrm{Cov}(\sqrt{N}(\beta_{N}-\beta_{0}))=N\cdot\mathrm{Cov}(\beta_{N})

Substituting this identity into the limit expression from (

) yields:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(N\cdot\mathrm{Cov}(\beta_{N})\right)=\Sigma

This equation formally states that the asymptotic covariance matrix of the scaled estimator
\sqrt{N}\beta_{N}
is
\Sigma
. For a finite but large
N
, this implies that
\mathrm{Cov}(\beta_{N})
is well-approximated by
\frac{1}{N}\Sigma
. This justifies the corollary’s statement that the covariance is proportional to
\frac{1}{N}\Sigma
.

The variance is defined as the trace of the covariance matrix:
\mathrm{Var}(\beta_{N})\vcentcolon=\mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{Cov}(\beta_{N}))
. We apply the trace operator to the limit we derived in Part 1. Since the trace is a continuous linear map on the space of matrices, it commutes with the limit operator:

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left(\lim_{N\to\infty}N\cdot\mathrm{Cov}(\beta_{N})\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathrm{tr}\left(N\cdot\mathrm{Cov}(\beta_{N})\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(N\cdot\mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{Cov}(\beta_{N}))\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(N\cdot\mathrm{Var}({\beta_{N}})\right)

So we have the precise limit:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(N\cdot\mathrm{Var}({\beta_{N}})\right)=\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma)

Since
\Sigma
is a constant matrix, its trace
\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma)
is a finite constant. Setting
a_{N}=\mathrm{Var}({\beta_{N}})
and
f(N)=N^{-1}
, our limit shows:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{Var}({\beta_{N}})}{N^{-1}}=\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma)

This directly implies that
\mathrm{Var}({\beta_{N}})=\mathcal{O}(N^{-1})
, which concludes the proof.
∎

B.2
Asymptotic Normality with Hinge Loss

By default, the TCAV implementations in both TensorFlow and PyTorch use

SGDClassifier

(
Pedregosa et al. 2011
)
, which operates by minimizing the hinge loss function. It achieves this by penalizing predictions that are not only incorrect but also those that are correct but fall within a specified “margin” around the decision boundary.

For a dataset of
N+n

\{(u_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N+n}
with true labels
y_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
, the final loss
J_{N}^{(\lambda)}(\beta,\alpha)
minimized is defined as the sum of the average hinge loss and an
L^{2}
regularization term:

J^{(\lambda)}_{N}(\beta,\alpha)=\left(\frac{1}{N+n}\sum_{i=1}^{N+n}\max[0,1-y_{i}(\beta^{\top}u_{i}+\alpha)]\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\beta\|^{2}.

Here,
\lambda
represents the regularization strength hyperparameter,
\beta
is the weight vector, and
\alpha
is the intercept term.
Again we consider our known setting. The only difference is that we use a different labeling system.

The first class consists of
n
fixed points,
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, referred to as “concept” samples with label
y=1
.
The second class consists of
N
random points,
\{z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, drawn independently and identically from a distribution
F_{0}
with label
y=-1
.

For our main theorem of asymptotic normality to hold, we require the following assumptions:

1.

The distribution
F_{0}
of the controls must be continuous.

Specifically, the projection
\beta_{0}^{\top}Z
must have a continuous probability density function,
f_{\beta_{0}^{\top}Z}(\cdot)
, in a neighbourhood of
-1
.

2.

The distribution
F_{0}
must have finite second moments, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim F_{0}}[\|Z\|^{2}]<\infty
.

Given these assumptions we can now state the theorem.

Theorem 1

(Asymptotic Normality with Hinge Loss)
.

Let
\beta_{N}
be the minimizer of the objective function
J_{N}(\beta)
. Let
\beta_{0}
be the unique minimizer of the limiting objective function

\lim_{N\to\infty}J_{N}^{(\lambda)}(\beta,\alpha)=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim F_{0}}[\max(0,1+\beta^{T}Z+\alpha)]+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\beta\|^{2}.

(20)

Under the assumptions listed above in Section
B.2
, as
N\to\infty
with
n
fixed, the estimator is asymptotically normal:

\sqrt{N}(\beta_{N}-\beta_{0})\xrightarrow{D}\mathcal{N}(0,M^{-1}\Sigma_{Z}M^{-1}),

(21)

where
M=\lambda I+\mathbb{E}[ZZ^{\top}|\beta_{0}^{\top}Z=-1]f_{\beta_{0}^{\top}Z}(-1)
, and
\Sigma_{Z}=\text{Var}\left(Z\cdot I(\beta_{0}^{\top}Z>-1)\right)
.

Proof.

As
N\to\infty
, the objective function
J_{N}(\beta)
converges pointwise in probability to
\lim_{N\to\infty}J_{N}^{(\lambda)}(\beta)
. Under standard M-estimation arguments, the minimizer
\beta_{N}
of
J_{N}(\beta)
converges in probability to the minimizer
\beta_{0}
of
\lim_{N\to\infty}J_{N}^{(\lambda)}(\beta)
.

We first perform a Taylor expansion of the gradient of the objective function around
\beta_{0}
, and set
\nabla J_{N}(\beta_{N})=0
:

0=\nabla_{\beta}J_{N}(\beta_{0})+\nabla^{2}_{\beta}J_{N}(\beta_{0})(\beta_{N}-\beta_{0})+o_{p}(1).

(22)

Rearranging gives

\sqrt{N}(\beta_{N}-\beta_{0})=-[\nabla^{2}_{\beta}J_{N}(\beta_{0})]^{-1}\sqrt{N}\nabla_{\beta}J_{N}(\beta_{0})+o_{p}(1).

We analyze the two terms on the right.
The optimality of
\beta_{0}
for the limiting problem implies
\lim_{N\to\infty}\nabla_{\beta}J^{(\lambda)}(\beta)=0
.
This gives the condition

\mathbb{E}[Z\cdot I(\beta_{0}^{T}Z>-1)]+\lambda\beta_{0}=0.

Let now
\mu_{0}=\mathbb{E}[Z\cdot I(\beta_{0}^{\top}Z>-1)].

The gradient of the finite-sample objective at
\beta_{0}
is

\displaystyle\nabla_{\beta}J_{N}(\beta_{0})={}

\displaystyle\frac{N}{n+N}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{j}I(\beta_{0}^{\top}z_{j}>-1)-\mu_{0}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{n}{n+N}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}I(\beta_{0}^{\top}x_{i}<1)+\mu_{0}\right).

When scaled by
\sqrt{N}
, the second term vanishes as
N\to\infty
. By the Central Limit Theorem and our assumption on
\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim F_{0}}[\|Z\|^{2}]<\infty
, the first term converges in distribution. Thus, the scaled score has a normal limit

\sqrt{N}\nabla_{\beta}J_{N}(\beta_{0})\xrightarrow{D}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{Z})

(23)

where
\Sigma_{Z}=\text{Var}\left(Z\cdot I(\beta_{0}^{\top}Z>-1)\right)
.
The Hessian matrix of the finite-sample objective,
H_{N}(\beta)=\nabla^{2}_{\beta}J_{N}(\beta)
, converges in probability to the Hessian of the limiting objective

H:=\lim_{N\to\infty}\nabla^{2}_{\beta}J_{N}(\beta_{0})=\lambda I+\mathbb{E}[ZZ^{\top}|\beta_{0}^{\top}Z=-1]f_{\beta_{0}^{\top}Z}(-1).

(24)

Slutsky’s Theorem
(
Slutzky 1937
)
and the assumed probability density function,
f_{\beta_{0}^{\top}Z}(\cdot)
give us the asymptotic distribution of the estimator

\sqrt{N}(\beta_{N}-\beta_{0})\xrightarrow{D}\mathcal{N}(0,H^{-1}\Sigma_{Z}H^{-1}).

This completes the proof.
∎

B.3
Asymptotic Normality of Difference of Means

The Difference of Means (DoM)
(
Martin 2019
)
method identifies a concept’s direction within a model’s activation space by simply taking the vector difference between the average activation for concept examples and the average activation for random examples. As this method was also adapted for calculating
Cav

s
(
Santis et al. 2024
)
, we analyze the stability of the direction vector
\beta_{N}=\bar{x}-\bar{z}
, where
\bar{x}
is the mean of the
n
fixed concept samples and
\bar{z}
is the mean of
N
random samples (from an independent distribution
F_{0}
with covariance
\Sigma_{z}
).

Theorem 2

(Asymptotic Variance of the Difference of Means Vector)
.

Let
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be
n
fixed points, and let
\{z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
be
N
random samples drawn i.i.d. from an independent distribution
F_{0}
with finite covariance
\Sigma_{z}
. Define the direction vector
\beta_{N}=\bar{x}-\bar{z}
as the difference of the respective means.

Then, the total variance of
\beta_{N}
, is given by:

\operatorname{tr}(\mathrm{Cov}(\beta_{N}))=\frac{1}{N}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{z})

(25)

Proof.

The variance of
\beta_{N}
, measured by
\operatorname{tr}(\mathrm{Cov}(\beta_{N}))
, is derived as follows. Since the set
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is fixed, their mean
\bar{x}
is a deterministic constant vector, and thus
\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{x})=0
. The variance of
\beta_{N}
is therefore determined exclusively by the random component
\bar{z}
.

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(\mathrm{Cov}(\beta_{N}))

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}(\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{x}-\bar{z}))

(26)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}(\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{z}))\quad(\text{since }\bar{x}\text{ is a fixed constant})

(27)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathrm{Cov}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{j}\right)\right)

(28)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{Cov}(z_{j})\right)\quad(\text{by i.i.d. assumption})

(29)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}}(N\cdot\Sigma_{z})\right)

(30)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{1}{N}\Sigma_{z}\right)

(31)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{z})

(32)

Since
\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{z})
is a fixed constant, the variance of
\beta_{N}
declines at a rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/N)
as the number of random samples
N
increases.

∎

As the number of random samples
N\to\infty
(while
n
remains fixed), the variance of
\beta_{N}
converges to zero at a rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/N)
. The
Difference of Means
-method thus exhibits the same convergence behaviour as the other two classifiers discussed.
