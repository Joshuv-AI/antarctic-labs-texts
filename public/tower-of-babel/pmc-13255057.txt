Introduction

The sciatic nerve is the largest in the human body. Proximal sciatic nerve transections cause significant morbidity and are difficult to treat. The surgeon must be prepared to address a gap between the proximal and distal stumps, as well as the long distance the regenerating axons must travel to reach the muscle end plate. Overall, the prognosis after sciatic nerve repair has been considered poor (Turkman et al., 2023), but favorable outcomes have been reported for repair of the tibial division (Kim et al., 2004). We present useful functional outcomes of early nerve repair following proximal sciatic nerve transection in two cases resulting from similar skiing accidents.

Presentation of cases

Case 1

The patient is a 20-year-old male who suffered a skiing accident in the French Alps two years ago, when the edge of a ski inflicted a deep, oblique cut on his left buttock (see Fig. 1). He was taken to the University Hospital in Grenoble, where paralysis of all muscles innervated by the left sciatic nerve was found. With the patient in the prone position, a surgical exploration was performed on the same day. The sciatic nerve and the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve were confirmed to be sharply transected. The tibial and peroneal divisions of the sciatic nerve were identified; particular attention was given to maintaining intra-neural orientation to ensure fascicular alignment at each nerve stump (Panagopoulos et al., 2017). The nerve stumps were trimmed. After flexing the knee, no gap remained between the two stumps. The sciatic nerve was sutured under a microscope using interrupted epi-perineural stitches with Ethilon 8.0 (Ethicon, GA, USA). Distant epineural sutures using PDS 5.0 (Ethicon, GA, USA) were placed to relieve tension across the suture line. An absorbable biological nonautogenous type I collagen nerve conduit (Neuragen®, Integra, NJ, USA) was applied around the suture area and secured with PDS 6.0 (Ethicon, GA, USA) to stimulate nerve recovery (Braga Silva et al., 2017). The posterior femoral cutaneous nerve was sutured in a similar fashion. Several lacerated muscles were also sutured. The wound was closed in a standard fashion with submuscular and subcutaneous drains. Postoperatively, the patient was confined to bed, with hip flexion restricted to a maximum of 20° for 3 weeks. He was then transferred to a Danish hospital and followed up in our neurosurgical outpatient clinic. Over time, he gradually regained motor function (see Fig. 2). He has a persistent loss of sensation limited to the sole and back of his left foot, and in his toes. He went on to graduate from high school. Case 2

The patient is a 21.5-year-old male University student who fell onto his own ski 2 years and 3 months before (see Fig. 1). A primary repair of his left sciatic nerve was carried out in Grenoble, France, on the same day in a similar fashion as in case 1. The postoperative regimen was also similar to that in case 1. He returned to the UK and was followed up in the Peripheral Nerve Unit in London. Over time, he gradually regained motor function (see Fig. 3). At the latest follow-up, the calf muscle bulk measured 36.5 cm and 38 cm on the left and right sides, respectively, 30 cm above the medial malleolus. His passive range of movement of his foot and ankle was to the neutral position. A tendon transfer of tibialis posterior to extensor hallucis longus and extensor digitorum longus is currently considered. He has been back to running, wearing a brace, and recently completed the Cambridge half-marathon in 2 h and 56 min. More recently, he felt pain in the calf, in the foot, and in the big toe. Discussion

Sciatic nerve lesions can result from a variety of causes, including fracture-dislocations, gunshot wounds, and iatrogenic injury, e.g., buttock injection and hip surgery (Kim et al., 2004). We present two cases of young adult male patients who achieved useful function after acute direct repair of proximal sciatic nerve transection. The two cases are unusual because they arose from similar recreational skiing accidents; the cuts were most likely caused by the sharp edges of carving skis.

Untreated complete lesions will invariably result in complete loss of function. The prognosis after proximal sciatic nerve repair is less favorable than after lesions at the thigh level (Kim et al., 2004; Kline et al., 1998). Several factors may explain this: Firstly, the nerve's diameter is large, increasing the risk that an individual axon will not find its way to its target. Secondly, the regenerating axons must travel a long distance to reach the muscle end plate and may not reach it in time. Thirdly, the surgeon must be ready to deal with a gap between the proximal and distal stumps. Nerve sutures with tension produce a fibrous reaction and poor results (Millesi et al., 1972). Due to progressive retraction of nerve ends, early exploration is key to success. Both cases underwent nerve repair on the day of the accident, in accordance with the rule of 3, which calls for immediate surgery within 3 days for clean, sharp injuries (Martins et al., 2013). Direct repair—if possible—is preferable to nerve grafting, in which the axons have to pass an anastomosis site. Direct repair is feasible, as presented here, if the gap between the proximal and distal stumps is less than 2.5-2 cm. Coaptation of the nerve stumps can be obtained with the patient in a prone position with the knees and hips flexed to approximately 20° and by dissection up to 6 cm along each stump. Distant epineural 6/0 resorbable Tsuge sutures can be used to neutralize tension at the anastomosis site (Mathieu et al., 2020). In the postoperative period, the knee must be immobilized at 30° of flexion for 3 weeks to prevent traction on the suture line (Mathieu et al., 2020). Larger defects - up to 8 cm - can be managed by progressive flexion of the knee to 90° for 6 weeks, bearing in mind that younger patients can tolerate this better than elderly patients (Mathieu et al., 2020). However, if tension-free direct repair is not feasible, nerve grafting is the best option; autografts yield better results than allografts. Protective sensation of the sole is partially preserved by the saphenous nerve. As reinnervation is not always accomplished, one may consider harvesting the sural nerve from the contralateral leg, not to impede sole sensation even further. Graft length is also related to prognosis, and for defects larger than 8 cm, a vascularised nerve graft consisting of the peroneal division of the sciatic nerve may be considered (Mathieu et al., 2020). In a large series of surgically treated sciatic nerve lesions, three patients sustained sharp transections of the buttock and underwent end-to-end suture repairs. In two of three tibial division repairs, the patients had a grade 3 or better functional outcome, whereas only one of three with peroneal division repairs attained this outcome (Kim et al., 2004). In our two cases, recovery of motor function in tibial-innervated muscles was superior to that in peroneal-innervated muscles, but both achieved grade 3 for ankle dorsiflexion. The role of nerve conduits, in addition to nerve suture, in stimulating nerve recovery, as used here, has its proponents (Braga Silva et al., 2017), but has not been finally settled.

Conclusions

Early direct repair of proximal sciatic nerve transection can result in relatively good functional outcomes, particularly for tibial-innervated muscles.
