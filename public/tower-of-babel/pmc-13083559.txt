Nanotechnology advances for psoriasis treatment and future prospects
Authors: Huilin Hu, Ziming Ma, Ziyi Zhang, Yilin Chen, Kewei Hu, Peng Zhang, Jie Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Psoriasis is a chronic, immune-mediated disorder with strong genetic susceptibility and environmental triggers, characterized by excessive proliferation of keratinocytes and recruitment of inflammatory cells. Affecting 2–3% of the global population and represents a significant public health challenge. In psoriasis, the skin barrier is altered rather than uniformly enhanced, and its permeability varies with drug types and disease states, which may affect the effective delivery of drugs to affected areas. Nanotechnology demonstrates potential in drug delivery, protecting drug molecules from degradation, enabling targeted therapy, and reducing side effects, thereby improving pharmacokinetics and enhancing the bioavailability of therapeutic agents. This review specifically examines the emergence of multimodal nanotherapeutic approaches, which defined as the strategic integration of two or more distinct therapeutic modalities (e.g., pharmacotherapy paired with phototherapy, gene editing, or RNA interference) within a unified nanocarrier platform to achieve synergistic efficacy. By systematically evaluating these advanced combinatory strategies, this review provides a distinct perspective compared to existing literature, which predominantly focuses on conventional, single-mode drug delivery systems. To this end, it reviews nanotechnology combined with multiple therapies and introduces the current advantages and disadvantages of integrating nanotechnology with conventional anti-psoriatic drugs Finally, it presents the challenges and prospects of using nanotechnology to treat psoriasis and provides reliable solutions for its clinical management. Overall, this review advances psoriasis treatment toward precision and efficiency, revealing the broad prospects of nanomedicine in conquering this intractable disease.

Introduction

Epidemiology of psoriasis

Psoriasis is a common chronic inflammatory skin disease with notable regional variations in prevalence. The highest prevalence rates are generally found in Western Europe and North America, particularly among Caucasians, with Scandinavia reporting some of the highest rates. Reported incidence is relatively lower in many Asian and African populations, though significant regional differences exist [1]. The global average prevalence of psoriasis is approximately 2% [2]. Beyond physical discomfort, psoriasis, like other skin conditions, can negatively impact mental health, with elevated rates of anxiety significantly impairing quality of life [3, 4]. Epidemiological studies indicate that individuals with severe psoriasis face heightened risks of mental disorders, particularly depression and suicide [5]. In short, psoriasis imposes a substantial burden on individuals, healthcare systems, and societies worldwide. Therefore, developing novel approaches to treat psoriasis and overcoming the limitations of conventional methods holds profound significance.

Classification and clinical manifestations

The pathogenesis of psoriasis is complex, involving immune dysregulation, hyperproliferation, and angiogenesis. The severity of psoriasis is graded on a spectrum, with the most commonly used assessment tool in clinical trials of biologics being the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) [6]. PASI enables comparisons across clinical trials and provides an objective measure of efficacy for different anti-psoriatic agents. By consensus, mild psoriasis is defined as “PASI ≤ 10”, while moderate to severe psoriasis is defined as “PASI > 10”. Psoriasis exhibits high heterogeneity and can be classified into distinct clinical and pathological subtypes based on presentation, including plaque psoriasis, pustular psoriasis, guttate psoriasis, erythrodermic psoriasis, and psoriatic arthritis. However, significant differences exist between these clinical subtypes (Fig. 1). Psoriasis-related signaling pathways

Psoriasis is a complex chronic inflammatory skin disease. Research indicates it is regulated by intricate interactions between extracellular cytokine pathways and intracellular signaling molecules. These pathways work together to cause the pathological features of the disease, such as immune dysregulation, hyperplasia of keratinocytes and angiogenesis. Its core pathological features include hyperplasia of keratinocytes, vasodilation in the dermal papillary layer, infiltration of immune cells, and disruption of the skin barrier structure. These features collectively constitute key targets and challenges for drug delivery. For instance, the thickened stratum corneum at the lesion site serves as the primary physical barrier to local drug penetration, while the high expression of cytokines and receptors in the inflammatory microenvironment provides the molecular basis for active targeting of nanocarriers. Therefore, the design of nanocarriers is not merely an isolated material science issue but requires optimization closely aligned with the pathophysiological characteristics of psoriasis. This can be achieved by modulating the particle size, flexibility, and surface properties of the carriers to overcome the stratum corneum barrier, and by functionalizing them to target inflammation-related receptors, thereby enabling precise drug delivery, improving therapeutic efficacy, and reducing systemic side effects. Based on clinical characteristics, it can be classified into multiple subtypes (Table 1), with signaling pathways exhibiting both overlaps and differences across these subtypes. Among these, the TNF-α/IL-23/IL-17 axis represents a core pathway in psoriasis pathogenesis [20], its abnormal activation is a key feature of psoriasis immunopathology, particularly prominent in plaque psoriasis (Fig. 2). Additionally, the CCL20/CCR6 axis recruits CCR6+ Th17 cells to skin lesions, further sustaining and exacerbating the inflammatory environment. Core mechanisms of psoriasis

In pustular psoriasis, the IL-36/IL-1 inflammatory axis plays a dominant role. IL-36 cytokines (e.g., IL-36α, IL-36β, IL-36γ) produced by keratinocytes activate the IL-36R signaling pathway, promoting the release of neutrophil chemokines (e.g., CXCL1, CXCL8) and inducing Th1 and Th17 cell differentiation, thereby establishing the characteristic pathological features of pustular psoriasis [21].

The interferon (IFN) pathway also plays a crucial role in psoriasis pathogenesis. Type I IFNs (e.g., IFN-α, IFN-β), produced by plasmacytoid dendritic cells, promote the maturation of conventional dendritic cells and activate Th17 cells. Type II IFN (IFN-γ), primarily secreted by Th1 cells, acts on keratinocytes to further promote the release of inflammatory cytokines.

In guttate psoriasis, streptococcal infection-associated immune responses play a dominant role [22], manifested as follows: Streptococcal M protein exhibits molecular mimicry with keratin, triggering cross-reactive T-cell immune responses. Where CD8⁺ T cells infiltrate the epidermis by recognizing shared antigenic epitopes presented by HLA-Cw6. Concurrently, streptococcal superantigens promote expression of the skin homing receptor (CLA), driving T cell migration to the skin. Furthermore, IFN-γ amplifies the Th1/Th17-mediated inflammatory cascade by inducing CCL20 chemokine to recruit IL-17-producing cells.

Erythrodermic psoriasis (EP) is a rare and severe subtype of psoriasis characterized by extensive skin redness and desquamation. The pathogenesis of EP involves T cell-mediated immune dysregulation, with the IL-23/IL-17 axis serving as the core pathway [23]. Common triggers include infections, drug withdrawal, and emotional stress. Tildrakizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting the IL-23 p19 subunit, reduces proinflammatory factor release by inhibiting IL-23 binding to its receptor. Demonstrated efficacy in clinical cases further validates the importance of the IL-23 pathway in EP.

The pathogenesis of psoriatic arthritis (PsA) results from the combined effects of multiple factors, primarily including genetic susceptibility, immune dysregulation, and environmental factors [19]. The IL-23/IL-17 signaling pathway plays a central role in disease progression. IL-23 promotes the differentiation and proliferation of Th17 cells, leading to excessive IL-17 secretion. This activates signaling pathways such as NF-κB, exacerbating inflammatory responses and joint damage. Concurrently, excessive activation of the TNF-α signaling pathway also significantly drives disease progression. Furthermore, the JAK/STAT signaling pathway plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of PsA by regulating cytokine transcription and expression.

Additionally, intracellular signaling pathways such as NF-κB, JAK-STAT, and MAPK play crucial roles in the pathogenesis of psoriasis. Abnormal activation of the NF-κB pathway promotes the transcription of inflammatory cytokines; the JAK-STAT pathway mediates the signaling of cytokines like IL-23 and IL-22; while the MAPK pathway participates in keratinocyte proliferation and inflammatory responses. The clinical relevance of these immune pathways is further underscored by recent evidence demonstrating that nivolumab, a PD-1 inhibitor, can induce new-onset psoriasis or exacerbate pre-existing disease through enhanced Th17 activity and IL-17 overproduction, with over 94% of affected patients achieving symptom improvement following appropriate intervention [24], while Shu et al. similarly found that pembrolizumab-induced psoriasis predominantly affected elderly males, with 90.5% of patients achieving symptom improvement following appropriate intervention [25].

Common clinical treatment methods for psoriasis

The primary challenge in treating psoriasis lies in effectively delivering medications to affected areas to deplete excessively proliferating keratinocytes and modulate immunity. Currently, psoriasis treatments broadly fall into topical and systemic categories [26]. Topical therapies typically involve vitamin D3 or glucocorticoid administration, often supplemented with phototherapy, for early-stage psoriasis. For patients with moderate to severe psoriasis, systemic therapy is the primary approach, typically involving the use of methotrexate (MTX), cyclosporine A (CsA), or cytokine-targeted biologics (e.g., infliximab, adalimumab, and etanercept targeting TNF-α; ustekinumab targeting the IL-12/IL-23 p40 subunit; and secukinumab and ixekizumab acting on IL-17A). While numerous treatment options exist for psoriasis, none are entirely safe, effective, and highly compliant. To date, existing medications can suppress disease activity and alleviate symptoms but cannot completely cure psoriasis. Therefore, nanocarriers, as a relatively novel drug delivery approach, have been extensively applied in the delivery of anti-psoriatic medications. Nanotechnology-based therapies enhance therapeutic efficacy and patient compliance by improving drug molecule penetration to target sites, while significantly reducing the risk of adverse events. Consequently, this technology holds promise for treating various types of psoriasis.

The choice among these treatment modalities is further guided by individual patient characteristics. Optimal treatment selection is guided by a comprehensive assessment of individual patient factors. These include, but are not limited to, specific disease phenotypes (e.g., psoriatic arthritis), the presence of comorbidities (e.g., metabolic, hepatic, or renal conditions), pregnancy status, prior treatment history, and patient preferences, all of which critically inform the choice among topical, systemic, and advanced therapies.

Topical therapy

Topical treatment is the preferred approach for mild to moderate psoriasis, focusing on suppressing excessive epidermal proliferation, reducing inflammatory responses, and improving clinical symptoms. Regulation of abnormal keratinocyte differentiation primarily relies on vitamin D3 analogues and retinoids, which exert long-term efficacy by inhibiting keratinocyte proliferation and promoting normal differentiation. Anti-inflammatory therapy prioritizes corticosteroids such as betamethasone dipropionate (BD), which rapidly improves lesions by suppressing immune responses. However, long-term use may cause side effects like skin atrophy. Calcineurin inhibitors like tacrolimus are suitable for sensitive areas like the face, avoiding hormone-related adverse reactions.

Systemic therapy

Systemic treatment is crucial for moderate-to-severe psoriasis or when topical therapies prove ineffective. Its core objectives are modulating immune responses and suppressing excessive epidermal cell proliferation to control disease activity. Conventional systemic medications like MTX, cyclosporine, and retinoids exert effects through distinct mechanisms but require close monitoring for side effects, including hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity [27]. Biological agents like TNF-α, IL-17, and IL-23 inhibitors precisely block key inflammatory mediators, demonstrating highly effective and relatively safe therapeutic outcomes. However, attention must be paid to infection risks and long-term immunogenicity concerns. Novel therapies such as stem cells and exosomes remain in the research phase, with their potential awaiting further exploration [28].

Conventional phototherapy

Ultraviolet radiation locally suppresses immune responses. It directly affects Langerhans cell function, inhibits excessive proliferation and neovascularization in the epidermis, and selectively reduces T lymphocytes in the skin through apoptotic mechanisms [29]. The most commonly used phototherapy modalities currently include heliotherapy, NB-UVB, PUVA, and UVA1. Narrowband UVB (311 nm) and excimer laser (308 nm) are most commonly used due to their safety, efficacy, and cost-effectiveness, making them first-line treatments for psoriasis. PUVA (psoralen+UVA) is also employed but generally not prioritized due to potential risks, including photoaging, squamous cell carcinoma, and even malignant melanoma [30, 31]. While psoriasis is the most common indication, PUVA is also used for atopic dermatitis, vitiligo, cutaneous T-cell lymphoma, and scleroderma [32].

Conventional systemic medications

In the clinical management of psoriasis, conventional systemic medication regimens should be selected based on disease severity to achieve targeted intervention:

Topical vitamin D analogues, due to their stable efficacy and high safety profile, are widely preferred as first-line topical treatments for mild or localized psoriasis. For instance, a clinical trial demonstrated that the vitamin D derivative calcitriol ointment significantly outperformed placebo in treating plaque psoriasis, with excellent tolerability (> 90% of cases rated good/very good), with a low incidence of local irritation. This fully demonstrates its clinical value as the preferred topical treatment for mild psoriasis [33].

MTX, apremilast, tretinoin, and cyclosporine are among the medications that control moderate-to-severe psoriasis by regulating immune inflammatory responses or keratinocyte proliferation. A retrospective study involving 160 psoriasis patients treated with either MTX monotherapy or MTX combined with acitretin (MTX-ACI) found no significant difference in the incidence of liver fibrosis between the MTX-ACI combination group and the MTX monotherapy group. This indicated that in clinical practice, the MTX-ACI combination does not increase the risk of liver fibrosis. This provides crucial evidence supporting the safe use of MTX-ACI combination therapy for psoriasis patients requiring intensive treatment, particularly those without metabolic disorders [34].

Biological inhibitors

Another promising therapeutic approach involves developing novel biologics targeting specific pathways in the psoriasis pathogenesis. These biologics, employed for targeted immunotherapy, have garnered significant attention due to their efficacy and minimal side effects. Over the past decade, TNF-α inhibitors, IL-17 inhibitors, and IL-23 inhibitors have been developed and approved for psoriasis treatment.

TNF-α Inhibitors: As a major class of biologics, the availability of their biosimilars has significantly impacted healthcare costs and expanded treatment options for patients using corticosteroids, vitamin D analogues, calcineurin inhibitors, and keratolytic agents. In psoriasis patients treated with etanercept, adalimumab, or infliximab, self-controlled observations showed no significant changes in total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein, triglycerides, systolic blood pressure, and diastolic blood pressure. However, etanercept use was associated with a significant increase in triglycerides. A Phase III clinical trial conducted by a Chinese multicenter research team demonstrated that adalimumab (40 mg every other week) showed significant efficacy and manageable safety in 425 Chinese patients with moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis. At 12 weeks, 77.8% of patients achieved PASI 75 (vs. 11.5% in the placebo group, P < 0.001), with efficacy sustained through 24 weeks (87.7%). Safety profiles aligned with established global data, though tuberculosis infection risk (0.5% incidence) warrants particular attention in the Chinese population. This study provided key evidence-based support for the use of adalimumab in Chinese psoriasis patients [35].

IL-17 Inhibitors: Targeting IL-17, a key cytokine in the inflammatory response of psoriasis, these agents effectively suppress inflammation and improve skin lesion symptoms. AlMutairi et al. used two IL-17 inhibitors-ixekizumab (IXE) and secukinumab (SEC) to treat moderate-to-severe psoriasis. Both IL-17 inhibitors demonstrated effective clearance of skin lesions and improved quality of life with favorable safety profiles. IXE demonstrated faster onset of action, more complete clearance of lesions, and longer-lasting efficacy, indicating that targeting IL-17 is a highly effective strategy for treating psoriasis [36].

IL-23 Inhibitors: IL-23 is a cytokine secreted by antigen-presenting cells such as dendritic cells and macrophages. It primarily consists of two subunits, p19 and p40 (where the p40 subunit is shared with IL-12). Its mechanism involves specifically binding to the p19 subunit of IL-23 (avoiding interference with IL-12 function, as blocking IL-12’s p40 subunit may increase infection risk), thereby preventing IL-23 from binding to receptors on Th17 cell surfaces. This inhibits Th17 cell activation and proinflammatory cytokine release, ultimately reducing psoriatic inflammation and skin lesions [37]. A large-scale study integrating data from 11 Phase II/III clinical trials analyzed 10,877 patient-years of data from 4,399 psoriasis patients, revealing Guselkumab’s excellent safety profile. During the short-term placebo-controlled period, the incidence of adverse events and serious adverse events was comparable to the placebo group, confirming the tolerability of initial treatment. Long-term follow-up spanning up to five years showed that the incidence of adverse events decreased with treatment duration. Key risks such as serious infections, malignancies, and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) remained at low levels. These safety characteristics were highly consistent across subgroups defined by gender, age, BMI, and prior biologic use history [38]. The study findings indicate that, supported by extensive patient data and long-term follow-up, guselkumab demonstrates favorable safety in the treatment of psoriasis. Nanomedicine

Nanomedicine refers to the application of nanotechnology in medical fields for disease diagnosis, treatment, and prevention. Nanotechnology represents an innovative approach to dermatological therapy, with nanocarriers demonstrating immense potential across materials science, biomedicine, and environmental science. Nanotechnology not only simplifies diagnostics but also offers greater controllability, non-toxicity, and safety compared to conventional therapies [40]. And it has revolutionized psoriasis treatment. By designing nanoscale carriers-such as liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, or gold nanoparticles (AuNPs)-anti-inflammatory drugs (including corticosteroids, biologics, or small-molecule inhibitors) encapsulated within nanocarriers can be precisely delivered to skin lesions. This significantly enhances drug penetration and retention time in the epidermis and dermis while minimizing systemic side effects. For instance, nanotechnology offers novel strategies for modulating the Th17/IL-23 axis. Nanocarriers such as liposomes, polymer nanoparticles, and metal nanoparticles can be functionalized to specifically target Th17 cells or IL-23 receptors, enabling precise drug delivery. This targeted delivery system not only enhances drug bioavailability but also reduces side effects in non-pathological tissues, thereby improving the mechanistic coherence and efficacy of therapy. Li et al. developed a microneedle patch (BD@Zn-MSN-MN) incorporating zinc-doped mesoporous silica nanoparticles (Zn-MSN) to deliver the glucocorticoid BD, achieving combined anti-inflammatory and anti-pruritic treatment for psoriasis. This transdermal delivery system significantly enhances BD’s bioavailability through microneedle-mediated efficient drug penetration and the sustained-release properties of Zn-MSN. Studies demonstrated that BD@Zn-MSN-MN didn’t only promote macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype (Fig. 4a–d), but also enhanced expression of anti-inflammatory factors like IL-4 and suppressed pro-inflammatory factor release (IL-17F, IL-23, TNF-α) (Fig. 4e–g), exhibiting superior efficacy compared to clinical BD creams. In vivo experiments confirmed that this system effectively alleviated pruritus in psoriasis model mice (Fig. 4k) by reducing the excitability of TRPV1-positive neurons in dorsal root ganglia (DRG) (Figs. 4h–i) and decreasing calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) release (Fig. 4j). Additionally, fluorescence imaging demonstrated that microneedle patches enable deep skin drug delivery with favorable in vivo and in vitro biosafety profiles (Fig. 4l). By integrating immunomodulation with neural signal suppression, this platform offers an innovative therapeutic strategy for psoriasis [41]. Nanoparticles can further enhance efficacy by modulating immune responses (e.g., targeting inflammatory mediators like TNF-α and IL-17) or combining with photothermal therapy (PTT) and gene editing technologies. Papp et al. [42] demonstrated that the trivalent nanobody drug sonelokimab had achieved breakthrough progress in treating moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis in Phase 2/3 clinical trials, though not yet approved for clinical use, showcasing the revolutionary potential of nanotechnology. This study enrolled 313 long-term sufferers (average disease duration 18 years, baseline PASI score 20.8). Novelty and objectives

Key challenges with the above treatment approaches include: poor penetration of topical medications through the compromised skin barrier, systemic toxicity of conventional oral drugs, inconsistent responses to biologics and potential drug resistance, and a lack of targeted strategies for different disease phenotypes. Nanocarrier-based combination strategies aim to address these limitations by enabling enhanced localized drug delivery, reducing off-target exposure, facilitating the delivery of multiple therapeutic agents, and allowing precise modulation of disease pathways.

While existing reviews have extensively documented the use of specific nanocarrier classes for standard drug delivery (e.g., liposomes, metallic nanoparticles [41, 42]), there remains a critical gap in comprehensively evaluating their role as multifaceted hubs for complex, multi-targeted interventions. In this review, we clearly define “multimodal nanotherapeutic approaches” as the simultaneous or sequential delivery of multiple therapeutic agents or modalities (such as chemotherapeutics combined with photothermal/photodynamic agents, CRISPR-Cas9 components, or RNAi) engineered into a single nanoplatform to target distinct pathogenic pathways synergistically. The core objective and novelty of this review lie in transitioning the focus from passive nanocarrier delivery to active, multimodal nanotherapeutics, offering a critical analysis of how these synergistic platforms overcome the limitations of monotherapies. It systematically examines how nanocarriers serve as a versatile platform to enhance the delivery and efficacy of both conventional drugs. This review will first categorize nanocarrier designs optimized for psoriatic skin; subsequently analyze their role in improving the delivery of established agents and in enabling novel strategies such as PTT, gene editing, and RNA-based interventions; and finally, critically discuss the core translational challenges and future directions for these combinatorial nanomedicines. Methodology

In this review, we comprehensively evaluated the literature on nanotechnology-based psoriasis therapies by searching PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus for studies published between 2010 and 2025, with an emphasis on advances from 2020 onward. The search strategy employed Boolean operators to combine core terms, including (“psoriasis”) AND (“nanotechnology” OR “nanoparticle” OR “nanocarrier”) AND (“photodynamic therapy” OR “photothermal therapy” OR “gene editing” OR “RNA therapy” OR “drug delivery”). These search strings were adapted as needed for each database’s specific syntax.

The review prioritized English-language original research, clinical trials, and systematic reviews that focused on the design, efficacy, or mechanism of nanomaterial-based interventions for psoriasis. Exclusion criteria encompassed: (1) studies not primarily focused on psoriasis; (2) publications without accessible full text; (3) conference abstracts, editorials, and non-peer-reviewed articles; and (4) articles published prior to 2010 unless they were considered seminal works providing essential background.

The study selection process was conducted independently by two reviewers. Titles and abstracts were screened against the inclusion criteria, and full texts of potentially relevant articles were subsequently assessed for eligibility. Any disagreements during the selection process were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer. The identified literature was then systematically analyzed to synthesize evidence on nanocarrier designs, therapeutic mechanisms, combination strategies, and translational challenges in psoriasis management.

Nanocarriers

The limitations of conventional psoriasis treatments are driving a shift toward innovative nanomedicine approaches. Using macromolecular materials in drug delivery poses significant challenges, including instability in vivo, poor bioavailability, low solubility, inadequate absorption, issues with targeted delivery specificity, sustained efficacy, and potential adverse drug reactions. Owing to their diversity and high customizability, nanocarriers hold broad application prospects in the biomedical field. Nanoparticle-based drug delivery methods are being employed in psoriasis therapy to achieve higher efficacy, enhanced specificity, reduced toxicity, and superior stability [43]. Nanocarriers can be categorized by composition into organic, metallic, and composite nanocarriers (Fig. 5), each exhibiting distinct advantages in psoriasis treatment (Table 5). Targeted nanomedicine delivery systems enable the specific delivery of therapeutic drugs to target organs, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy and reducing toxic side effects. Based on the targeting mechanisms employed, these delivery systems fall into two primary categories: passive targeting (EPR effect) and active targeting [64]. Passive targeting refers to the release of an appropriate amount of drug when nanostructured materials are directed to specific sites, due to the lower drug content encapsulated in hydrophobic environments [65]. Active targeting achieves precise delivery by modifying the carrier surface to enable specific recognition of target cell receptors. The development of nanotechnology-based drug delivery methods offers new insights for improving the treatment of diseases like psoriasis. More clinical trials are anticipated to explore their efficacy in managing psoriasis.

Lipid-based nanocarriers

Lipid-based nanocarriers, such as liposomes, nanoemulsions, and solid lipid nanoparticles, have become the preferred choice for topical psoriasis treatment due to their excellent biocompatibility, degradability, and ability to enhance the solubility and stability of poorly soluble drugs. Rain et al. developed and optimized a naringenin gel based on nanostructured lipid carriers using high-shear homogenization technology. This formulation penetrated the thickened stratum corneum, enabled sustained drug release, improved epidermal hydration, and significantly reduced hyperkeratosis in a psoriasis model, demonstrating excellent therapeutic potential [66]. Assalem et al. involved 20 patients with bilateral symmetric plaque psoriasis, the results showed that after 8 weeks of topical application of a limonene-containing eucalyptus acid nanoemulsion (Formulation F5), the PASI score plummeted from 12.95 to 2.9. 80% patients achieved significant symptom relief, with dermatoscopy revealing complete disappearance of red dots and scales. In contrast, the conventional corticosteroid cream (0.05% clobetasol) group achieved only 35% response rates, with PASI scores decreasing from 11.55 to 6.85 while lesions persisted. This demonstrates that the nanoemulsion system, by enhancing drug transdermal penetration and anti-inflammatory efficacy, offers a highly effective and safe alternative topical treatment for psoriasis [67].

Metallic nanocarriers

Metallic nanocarriers, such as gold and iron oxide nanoparticles, exhibit multimodal therapeutic potential in psoriasis due to their unique photothermal, catalytic, and optoelectronic properties, enabling drug delivery, immune modulation, and PTT. Such materials can trigger drug release, scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS), or modulate the immune microenvironment in response to near-infrared (NIR) light, thereby suppressing abnormal keratinocyte proliferation and inflammatory responses. Chen et al. utilized the photothermal effect of gold nanorods (GNRs) to trigger MTX release; MTX-loaded AuNPs exhibited no toxicity to skin cells while effectively inhibiting cell growth and enhancing stratum corneum permeability. The photothermal action of GNRs induces keratinocyte apoptosis, while the phase-change properties of hydrogels prolong drug retention time in the skin, demonstrating favorable therapeutic outcomes [68].

Over the past few decades, mouse models of psoriasis have been developed as tools for understanding the pathogenesis of this disease and have also served as preclinical models. In a study using an imiquimod (IMQ)-induced psoriasis mouse model, topical application of MTX-loaded AuNPs (AuNPs-3MPS@MTX) for 24–48 h reduced erythema scores from a baseline of 2.75 to 1 and scaling scores from 3 to 1; epidermal thickness decreased significantly by approximately 50% (from 81–82 μm to 41–44 μm), demonstrating that topical treatment with MTX-loaded AuNPs avoids the toxic side effects of conventional systemic MTX administration and offers a novel nanotherapeutic strategy for mild psoriasis [69]. Although metal nanoparticles exhibit significant anti-inflammatory properties on their own, they may induce toxic effects, particularly through ROS production, DNA damage, and apoptosis [70].

Composite nanocarriers

Composite nanocarriers integrate the advantages of multiple materials through organic–inorganic hybridization or functionalization, achieving enhanced effects. Building upon this, Tekko et al. successfully enhanced drug concentration in the skin, prolonged drug release time, and reduced systemic drug exposure by combining MTX nanocrystals with dissolving microneedle technology. This approach holds promise for improving therapeutic efficacy while minimizing side effects [71]. Liu et al. constructed a H₂O₂-responsive nanoplatform (CUR@COF) based on cyclodextrin metal–organic frameworks (CD-MOFs) functionalized via crosslinking with phthaloyl chloride. This platform was loaded onto soluble microneedles (MNs) derived from the polysaccharide matrix of Codonopsis pilosula. This platform hybridizes the inorganic stability of metal–organic frameworks with the biological activity of organic polysaccharides, integrating the high permeability of nanocarriers, ROS scavenging capacity, and the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties of natural products. It not only effectively addresses two critical challenges in psoriasis treatment: poor drug skin penetration and imbalance in the oxidative stress microenvironment of affected areas; but also significantly alleviates epidermal hyperplasia, inflammatory infiltration, and oxidative stress in IMQ-induced psoriasiform dermatitis in mice through multiple combined mechanisms. These include scavenging excess ROS, downregulating the TNF-α/NF-κB signaling pathways, and suppressing the IL-23/IL-17 inflammatory axis. This combined action of multiple mechanisms significantly alleviates epidermal hyperplasia, inflammatory infiltration, and oxidative damage in IMQ-induced psoriasiform dermatitis in mice, offering a novel, highly effective, and safe combined therapeutic strategy for the topical treatment of psoriasis [72]. Nevertheless, most clinical studies on composite nanocarriers for psoriasis treatment remain in the preclinical stage. This is a highly active and rapidly advancing research field, where various composite nanocarriers require further investigation. Significant efforts will be needed for future clinical translation.

Despite the promising attributes of these nanocarrier classes, comparative studies that systematically evaluate their performance for a given anti-psoriatic drug are conspicuously lacking. Critical questions remain unanswered: for instance, which carrier type (e.g., liposome vs. AuNP) offers optimal skin penetration and retention for a corticosteroid versus a biologic? Furthermore, the long-term local tolerability and in vivo degradation kinetics of many of these carriers, especially inorganic and composite ones, are not yet fully characterized, representing a significant knowledge gap for clinical translation. In a recent biomimetic approach, Yang et al. [73] engineered ExoLipo, a hybrid nanoparticle combining melanoma-derived exosomal membrane proteins (RhoA) with liposomes to deliver mmu-miR-320-3p, demonstrating enhanced transdermal penetration and significant therapeutic efficacy in psoriasis mouse models.

Effect of changes in skin barrier function on the design of nanocarriers

The skin barrier at psoriatic lesions is not merely characterized by increased permeability, but exhibits highly heterogeneous structural alterations, which impose differentiated requirements for the design of nanocarriers and drug selection.

For lipid-based nanocarriers, their flexible structure and lipid-like composition facilitate lipid fusion with intercellular lipids in the stratum corneum, thereby enabling effective penetration of the thickened stratum corneum. However, in cases of severe barrier impairment such as erythrodermic psoriasis, excessive permeability may lead to premature loss of both the carrier and drug, reducing local retention time. Therefore, it is necessary to optimize release kinetics by modulating lipid composition and phase transition temperature.

For metallic or inorganic nanocarriers, their rigid structures and larger particle sizes make them more dependent on the follicular sebaceous gland pathway for penetration. In plaque psoriasis, the occlusion of follicular orifices significantly reduces their delivery efficiency. Therefore, surface modification is required to minimize protein adsorption and immune clearance, while optimizing the particle size to 100–200 nm to enhance follicular penetration.

For different drug classes, lipophilic small-molecule drugs are more likely to penetrate damaged barriers through passive diffusion, while nanocarriers primarily serve to prolong their retention time. In contrast, hydrophilic macromolecular drugs heavily rely on the endocytosis and active targeting capabilities of nanocarriers. The integrity of the skin barrier directly impacts their cellular uptake efficiency. Therefore, when designing delivery systems, it is essential to make personalized adjustments based on the physicochemical properties of the drugs and disease subtypes.

Combination of nanocarriers and conventional drugs

In the clinical treatment of psoriasis, conventional anti-psoriatic drugs (or standard therapies) serve as the foundational therapeutic approach, yet their clinical application still faces certain limitations. For instance, due to the thickened stratum corneum and impaired skin barrier function at psoriatic lesions, there exists low bioavailability and limited transdermal delivery efficiency. Simultaneously, insufficient targeting limits drug accumulation at affected sites, hindering precise action on core lesions involving immune dysregulation and excessive keratinocyte proliferation; significant systemic side effects also arise. Nanocarriers, serving as drug delivery carriers [74], offer dual advantages: their size enables penetration through the follicular sebaceous pathway or intercellular spaces to bypass abnormally thickened stratum corneum, significantly enhancing transdermal drug permeability and skin penetration; simultaneously, the sustained-release properties of carrier materials prolong drug retention at the lesion site, reducing administration frequency while maintaining effective therapeutic concentrations. Furthermore, surface functionalization modifications (e.g., conjugation of targeting ligands) enable specific targeting to affected areas, minimizing impact on healthy tissues and significantly optimizing the therapeutic efficacy of conventional drugs. Nanocarrier-assisted delivery of immunosuppressants

Immunosuppressants exert therapeutic effects in psoriasis by targeting T-cell activation, inhibiting the release of inflammatory cytokines, and blocking immune cell infiltration. However, systemic administration often leads to excessive immunosuppression and hepatotoxicity or nephrotoxicity. Topical application, meanwhile, is limited by insufficient bioavailability due to the skin barrier. Nano-carriers, leveraging controllable size effects, biocompatibility, and targeted modification capabilities, significantly enhance drug accumulation at lesion sites while reducing systemic toxicity. Furthermore, surface modification of the nanoparticles with targeting ligands such as hyaluronic acid enables specific binding to CD44 receptors on keratinocyte surfaces. Summary of research on nanocarriers

Combined delivery system of anti-inflammatory drugs and nanocarriers

Anti-inflammatory drugs serve as the core approach for topical psoriasis treatment by inhibiting the release of inflammatory mediators (such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-17), blocking inflammatory signaling pathways (such as NF-κB, MAPK), and reducing local immune infiltration. They are particularly suitable for controlling mild to moderate skin lesions. Conventional formulations (e.g., ointments, creams) face limitations due to the stratum corneum barrier, resulting in low drug penetration efficiency and unstable release kinetics (rapid clearance after initial burst release). This not only affects therapeutic consistency but may also cause local irritation or systemic side effects due to systemic drug exposure. Nanocarriers can regulate particle size distribution and establish sustained-release systems [93], thereby assisting in the enhanced transdermal delivery and targeting of anti-inflammatory drugs. For instance, glucocorticoids (e.g., dexamethasone (Dex), betamethasone) serve as first-line agents for acute psoriasis control due to their potent anti-inflammatory activity [94]. Long-term use may cause side effects like skin atrophy due to uneven distribution of conventional formulations [95]. However, nanotechnology can optimize drug delivery, thereby enhance the transdermal delivery efficiency and target of anti-inflammatory drugs. Frombach et al. established a serine protease (SP)-induced in vitro human skin inflammation model to evaluate the permeability and anti-inflammatory effects of Dex in inflamed skin, comparing conventional cream formulations with core-multishell nanocarriers (CMS-NC). The study found that while both formulations effectively suppressed inflammatory cytokine expression, CMS-NC demonstrated superior delivery of Dex to both the epidermis and dermis. Leveraging their small size advantage, nanocarriers more readily penetrate the stratum corneum, precisely delivering drugs to affected sites. This enables uniform distribution within the skin, reducing systemic exposure while prolonging drug action time and enhancing therapeutic efficacy. Nanocarriers demonstrate higher drug accumulation levels and deeper penetration capabilities [96]. Qu et al. systematically investigated the effects of liposome size and surface charge on transdermal drug delivery efficiency and targeting in a psoriasis model by nanocarrier-assisted delivery combining Dex-loaded liposomes and microneedles. Results indicated that compared to small-sized (100 nm) anionic liposomes, medium-sized (250 nm) and larger-sized (450 nm) anionic liposomes exhibited slightly lower in vitro cellular uptake but significantly prolonged drug retention time in psoriasis-like inflamed skin while enhancing long-term in vivo delivery efficiency. Further modification to positively charged liposomes demonstrated an exceptionally prolonged skin retention time of 132 h in the psoriasis model, with drug concentrations at the lesion site exceeding those of conventional ointments by more than threefold [97].

Co-delivery of anti-proliferation drugs via nanocarriers

Antiproliferative drugs typically treat moderate-to-severe psoriasis (e.g., plaque, erythrodermic) by inhibiting abnormal keratinocyte proliferation (e.g., blocking DNA synthesis, interfering with the cell cycle) and reducing angiogenesis. However, systemic administration (e.g., oral MTX) may induce systemic toxicity such as bone marrow suppression and liver damage, while topical application struggles to achieve therapeutic concentrations due to skin barriers [98]. Nanocarriers enable a “targeted delivery-controlled release-enhanced therapeutic outcome” strategy, facilitating precise delivery of antiproliferative drugs to hyperproliferating keratinocytes and pathological blood vessels, thereby enhancing efficacy while reducing systemic exposure. For instance, Xin et al. designed a peptide-based nanoplatform (HLNP-MNs) capable of simultaneously delivering the antiproliferative drug MTX and the gene silencing agent siRNA-TNFα for combined therapy of psoriasis. This system actively captures psoriasis-specific proteins (e.g., LL37, S100A8, S100A9) through its surface properties, achieving targeted enrichment of inflammatory immune cells and responsive drug release under ROS conditions. MTX exerted its effect by suppressing over proliferating T cells and keratinocytes, while siRNA-TNFα silences key inflammatory cytokines. The combined action of both agents, delivered via the nanoplatform, significantly inhibited the activation and infiltration of skin-homing T cells in a psoriasis mouse model, alleviating inflammatory responses and epidermal hyperplasia. This provides a nanotherapeutic strategy with antigen clearance capabilities for restoring immune homeostasis [99]. Nanotechnology can minimize irritation, address poor bioavailability, and enhance the biocompatibility of therapeutic agents.

A major translational gap lies in the absence of head-to-head clinical trials comparing nanocarrier-based formulations with their conventional counterparts. Robust clinical data quantifying the actual benefit in efficacy, safety, and patient adherence are urgently needed to move beyond preclinical promise.

Multimodal therapeutic effects of nanocarriers

In the treatment of psoriasis, multimodal therapy combining nanocarriers demonstrates significant potential. For instance, in the study by Chen et al., a liposome-based nanocarrier (Lipo-MTX-Ce6) was employed to simultaneously deliver MTX and the photosensitizer dihydroporphyrin6 (Ce6) [68]. In monotherapy, MTX exerts immunosuppressive effects solely by inhibiting cell proliferation, but it induces adverse effects on normal tissues and has limited bioavailability. When used alone, photosensitizers require precise light irradiation to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) for cell killing, and their delivery efficiency to the lesion site is limited.

However, when these two components are combined in liposomal nanocarriers, the nanocarriers can target psoriatic lesions through hyaluronic acid ligands on their surface, which bind to the overexpressed CD44 receptors at the lesion sites [67]. At the lesion site, MTX can sustain its immunosuppressive function by inhibiting the excessive proliferation of keratinocytes and the release of inflammatory factors. Meanwhile, the photosensitizer generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) under 630 nm laser irradiation, directly killing hyperproliferating keratinocytes. These two components work synergistically: MTX alleviates the inflammatory environment, while the photosensitizer directly eliminates pathological cells. Compared to monotherapy, this multimodal therapy demonstrated significantly better therapeutic outcomes in a mouse model of imiquimod-induced psoriasis, with a 70% reduction in the PASI score at day 14, compared to only a 40% reduction in the MTX monotherapy group and a 50% reduction in the PDT monotherapy group [68].

Nanocarriers combined with photodynamic therapy

In addition to loading CsA, MTX, and other small-molecule drugs into various types of nanoparticles for treating psoriasis, photodynamic therapy (PDT) also represents a promising therapeutic approach. PDT works by generating ROS through photosensitizers when exposed to light at specific wavelengths, leading to cell death. Compared to conventional therapies, PDT offers higher selectivity due to the photosensitizer’s preferential targeting of skin lesions and precise illumination of these areas. However, conventional photosensitizers suffer from issues such as poor targeting, insufficient stability, and limited tissue penetration depth. The introduction of nanocarriers offers a revolutionary optimization strategy for PDT in treating psoriasis, potentially overcoming limitations of conventional photosensitizers by improving targeting, stability, and penetration depth. Future efforts should further advance clinical translational research to achieve safer and more effective treatment regimens [100].

Photosensitizer

Photosensitizers are a class of molecules capable of absorbing light energy at specific wavelengths and converting it into chemical energy. They are widely used in fields such as PDT, antimicrobial treatment, and photocatalysis. Photosensitizers accumulate in target tissues, where they absorb light upon irradiation at specific wavelengths. This causes them to transition from the ground state to an excited singlet state, which further evolves into a longer-lived triplet state via intersystem crossing. They can then react with molecular oxygen in the surrounding environment via one of two distinct pathways. Based on ROS generation mechanisms, photosensitizers can be classified into Type I (electron transfer, generating free radicals) and Type II (energy transfer, generating singlet oxygen) (Fig. 6). Photosensitizers can directly react with substrates such as cell membranes, forming free radicals and radical ions via electron transfer. These free radicals and radical ions further react with molecular oxygen to produce O₂−·, HO·, and H₂O₂, termed the Type I reaction. For Type II reactions, triplet oxygen directly transfers energy to molecular oxygen, generating singlet oxygen. ROS produced by both reaction types interact with numerous biological substrates, leading to direct induction of oxidative damage and accelerated cell death [101].Fig. 6Mechanisms of reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation in PDT. Nanoscale modification of conventional photosensitizers

Conventional photosensitizers (such as porphyrins and chlorin e6) suffer from poor skin permeability and insufficient targeting in psoriasis treatment, whereas nanotechnology can significantly enhance their delivery efficiency. For instance, Gao et al. demonstrated that Ce6 microemulsion foam delivered via 30 nm nanodroplets enhances stratum corneum permeability. Following laser irradiation, it induces keratinocyte apoptosis and upregulates COX2 expression, thereby modulating immune responses without irritating healthy skin [102]. In further exploration, the Tris-BNPs system developed by Mai et al. could specifically penetrate the damaged stratum corneum in psoriasis, enabling a single-dose cure of psoriasis through sustained-release drug delivery. A single administration maintains efficacy for three days, eliminating the drawbacks of frequent dosing [103].

Novel nanoscale photosensitizers

In recent years, novel nanophotodynamic agents have made significant progress in overcoming tumor hypoxia and enhancing targeted delivery. Through material innovation, these new agents achieve multi-target combined therapy. Chen et al. developed TBTDCNPs (aggregation-induced emission materials) exhibiting NIR luminescence (825 nm) and a large Stokes shift (300 nm). These nanoparticles target the endoplasmic reticulum within keratinocytes, inducing ER stress and autophagy. Through ROS production, they synergistically enhance apoptosis and exhibit significantly superior photostability and biocompatibility compared to conventional photosensitizers [104].

Photodynamic-related nanocarriers

The effectiveness and safety of PDT hinge on delivering photosensitizers to target cells at therapeutic concentrations while minimizing absorption by non-target cells, thereby reducing side effects on healthy tissues. In recent years, significant efforts have been dedicated to developing PDT response systems. The primary obstacle lies in the highly hydrophobic nature of photosensitizers with extended π-conjugated systems, causing most to aggregate in aqueous environments and diminishing their efficacy. To overcome the hydrophobicity and poor targeting of conventional photosensitizers, various nanocarriers (Table 8) have been employed to enhance photosensitizer delivery, stability, and photodynamic efficacy in psoriasis. Nanocarriers for PDT

Organic nanocarriers

Polymer

Polymeric nanoparticles are considered effective carriers for delivering photosensitizers in PDT, with their primary advantages lying in physical robustness, high drug loading capacity, targeted delivery, and prevention of photosensitizer degradation prior to reaching target tissues [106]. Among polymer-based nanoparticles, dendrimers, polymeric micelles, nanogels, nanospheres, and nanocapsules have been employed for treating various dermatological conditions [81]. Biodegradable nanoparticles have garnered significant attention due to their excellent degradability and biocompatibility. Materials such as poly (glycolic acid) (PGA), poly (lactic acid) (PLA), and their copolymers (PLGA) have been specifically employed as delivery matrix materials for photosensitizers [107]. Cao et al. reported that a biodegradable polymeric vesicle exhibiting aggregation-induced emission (AIE) properties was self-assembled from a copolymer of polyethylene glycol-block-poly (caprolactone-gradient-trimethylene carbonate) (PEG-P (CLgTMC)) and a side-chain copolymer containing tetraphenylpyridinium (PAIE) units. This structural design enabled efficient delivery and activation of photosensitizers, significantly enhancing PDT efficacy and selectivity [108]. Wang et al. pioneered the construction of CS/HA nanogels for co-loading MTX and the photosensitizer precursor 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA), forming the MTX-ALA NGs system. This strategy leverages a dual mechanism: chitosan (CS) enhances skin permeability through its cationic properties, while hyaluronic acid (HA) targets the CD44 receptor, which is overexpressed in psoriatic skin. Together, they significantly improve the transdermal delivery efficiency of MTX and ALA. In vitro and in vivo permeability experiments confirmed that NGs increased the conversion rate of ALA to protoporphyrin IX (PpIX) by 3.2-fold (p < 0.001) and elevated MTX skin retention by 2.6-fold (p < 0.001). In an IMQ-induced psoriasis mouse model, 7 days of NGs treatment reduced the PASI score from 10.30 to 4.80 (p < 0.001), restored epidermal thickness from 95.04 μm to normal levels (28.73 μm), while significantly downregulating key proinflammatory factors TNF-α and IL-17A (skin mRNA reduced by 2.8–3.1-fold, p < 0.001). This system synergistically enhanced skin delivery efficiency via polymeric nanogel and light-activated ROS production to directly kill hyperproliferating keratinocytes (KCs), offering a novel, highly effective, safe, and convenient topical combination therapy strategy for psoriasis [109].

Liposome

Lipid nanocarriers represent promising carrier molecules for drug delivery. These lipid carriers enhance the bioavailability of loaded active pharmaceutical ingredients while reducing their toxicity. Furthermore, since the synthesis of these nanoparticles does not require organic solvents, their advantages include low production costs, ease of scale-up, and low toxicity. In a case study of PDT treatment for psoriasis in mice, Zewail et al. developed hypericin nanolipid carriers (HYP-NLCs) combined with hollow microneedles for PDT therapy. This approach significantly reduced inflammatory markers such as NF-κB and IL-6, demonstrating superior therapeutic efficacy compared to conventional hypericin ointment. The results confirmed that nano-encapsulation technology effectively enhanced drug performance, while hollow microneedles ensured precise drug delivery to psoriatic lesions, offering a safer and more effective new strategy for psoriasis treatment [110]. Micelles

Micelles are nanoscale aggregates spontaneously formed in solution by amphiphilic molecules (such as surfactants or block copolymers), serving as drug carriers in numerous biomedical applications [116]. Reports on psoriasis treatment remain scarce, with most studies focusing on oncology. For instance, Xu et al. developed liver-targeted, ROS-responsive polymeric micelles (Cela/Ce6/GCTR PMs) loaded with Cela and the photosensitizer Ce6 for combined chemotherapy and PDT in liver cancer. These micelles were enriched at tumor sites through active and passive targeting, releasing drugs under ROS conditions. Following PDT activation, Ce6 generated substantial ROS upon laser irradiation, further accelerating micelle disassembly and Cela release, significantly enhancing antitumor efficacy [111]. This combined therapeutic strategy based on smart responsive micelles holds promise for pioneering new directions in psoriasis treatment.

Inorganic nanocarriers

Carbon nanocarriers demonstrate multifaceted application value due to their high specific surface area, tunable pore structure, excellent photothermal conversion properties, and biocompatibility. Among these, graphene exhibits particularly extensive and groundbreaking applications in the medical field. Black phosphorus (BP)-based nanocarriers have emerged as novel inorganic biomaterials due to their biocompatibility, biodegradability, and biosafety (BP metabolism avoids certain immune responses). For instance, Yang et al. developed a mitochondria-targeted BP composite nanocarrier (BP@PDA-Ce6&TPP NSs). By enhancing the photothermal conversion efficiency of BP nanosheets through a polydopamine (PDA) coating and loading the photosensitizer Ce6 and targeting molecule TPP, they achieved precise mitochondrial localization, efficient photothermal conversion, and controllable ROS generation. Experiments demonstrated that this carrier significantly enhanced the antitumor efficacy of combined photodynamic/photothermal therapy. In vitro studies showed that the cancer cell death rate in the targeted group (50%) far exceeded that of the non-targeted group (32%). In vivo experiments achieved complete tumor regression, confirming that BP nanocarriers could effectively overcome the limitations of conventional PDT [117]. The aforementioned inorganic nanocarrier-based PDT approaches offer a simple yet effective solution to overcome hypoxia-limited PDT efficacy in tumors. While no reports on their application for psoriasis treatment have been identified to date, future psoriasis therapies may draw inspiration from these approaches, offering new hope to patients.

Metal nanocarriers

AuNPs

Metal nanoparticles have garnered attention in various nanoscale material applications. Their advantages lie in relatively narrow size and shape distributions, as well as extended active lifetimes [118]. Research on metal nanocarriers for treating psoriasis via PDT remains scarce, though some studies have documented their beneficial applications. For instance, Yeshchenko et al. developed a ternary nanocomposite system based on star-shaped anionic polyacrylamide polymer (D-g-PAAan), AuNPs, and the photosensitizer temoporfin, significantly enhanced PDT therapeutic efficacy [113]. Metal nanoparticles (e.g., AuNPs) enhance PDT efficacy by boosting photosensitizer light absorption and ROS generation through surface plasmon resonance effects. These properties position metal nanoparticles as promising candidates for psoriasis treatment, particularly offering significant advantages in targeted delivery and reduced side effects.

Iron oxide nanoparticles

Iron oxide nanocarriers are primarily used in the medical field for precision tumor diagnosis and treatment, as well as targeted drug delivery. In PDT, they exhibit multifaceted unique functions: not only do they enhance photosensitizer delivery, but they also overcome the constraints of tumor microenvironment hypoxia through combined mechanisms (Fe3O4 can catalyze the decomposition of endogenous H2O2 to produce O₂, thereby significantly improving internal tumor hypoxia). Liu et al. developed a light-activated multifunctional platform based on Fe3O4 hollow nanospheres (HA/G3139/Ce6@Fe3O4). This nanocarrier system enabled responsive drug release within acidic tumor microenvironments, where Ce6 generates ROS via PDT under 660 nm laser irradiation to enhance breast cancer treatment by killing cells. This approach overcome conventional treatment resistance and targeting deficiencies, significantly improving drug targeting efficiency [114].

However, research on the combined use of inorganic nanocarriers and metallic nanocarriers with PDT for treating psoriasis remains limited to date. Nevertheless, it is undeniable that nanotechnology combined with PDT for psoriasis treatment is gaining momentum, offering new directions for future highly efficient targeted therapies for psoriasis. PDT incorporating nanocarriers overcomes the limitations of conventional PDT, such as lack of targeting, limited therapeutic penetration depth, and hypoxia. By leveraging the advantages of nanotechnology, PDT based on nanocarriers demonstrates specific tumor targeting and accumulation capabilities. This approach achieves greater therapeutic depth while reducing side effects, yielding more favorable clinical outcomes compared to conventional PDT.

Combined photothermal therapy using nanocarriers

Nanotechnology offers viable approaches for drug delivery and novel therapeutic strategies. Given its precision and relatively low side effects, PTT has emerged as a promising treatment for psoriasis, capable of overcoming limitations of conventional therapies. In PTT, nanoparticles or other phototherapeutic agents are employed to absorb NIR light, inducing localized temperature elevation sufficient to ablate cancer cells while minimizing damage to surrounding tissues. This targeted thermal effect primarily induces cell destruction through apoptosis, offering a minimally invasive therapeutic option.

Inorganic photothermal conversion agents

Inorganic photothermal agents are a class of inorganic nanocarriers that efficiently convert absorbed NIR radiation into thermal energy. They primarily include precious metal materials, metal compounds, and carbon-based materials. Their high photothermal conversion efficiency, excellent photostability, and tunable morphology and surface functionalization characteristics make them suitable for deep tissue penetration therapy. Noble metal nanoparticles (e.g., Au, Ag) exhibit strong SPR properties, enabling efficient absorption of specific wavelengths, particularly in the NIR range. NIR light possesses high penetrating power, allowing deep tissue penetration while minimizing damage to surrounding healthy tissues [119]. Chen et al. developed a NIR-responsive thermosensitive hydrogel (PLEL@GNR + MTX) for enhanced topical psoriasis treatment, with its core innovation leveraging the photothermal effect of GNRs: Under NIR irradiation, heat generated by GNRs not only triggers a reversible phase transition from gel to sol state in the hydrogel, significantly enhancing drug penetration into psoriatic hyperkeratinized layers and prolonging skin retention time, but also induces keratinocyte apoptosis. This synergizes with MTX release to inhibit psoriatic plaque proliferation [68]. Compared to noble metal nanocarriers, metal compounds garner significant attention due to their low cost, excellent photothermal stability, and reduced cytotoxicity. Carbon-based nanocarriers (e.g., graphene [120] and carbon nanotubes [121]) exhibit poor NIR light absorption; however, they possess outstanding electrochemical properties, strong noncovalent bonding capabilities, and large surface absorption areas. Appropriate surface functionalization, such as surface-linked PEG or encapsulating polymers, not only enhances their dispersion in water and photothermal conversion efficiency but also increases their drug loading capacity [122]. Reports on the use of metal compounds and carbon-based materials for psoriasis treatment are relatively scarce, with most studies focusing on tumor therapy [123–129].

Organic photothermal conversion agents

In addition to inorganic nanocarriers, organic nanocarriers (such as NIR dyes and polymers) also represent promising materials for psoriasis PTT. Organic photothermal agents (e.g., polypyrrole, polydopamine (PDA), porphyrins, and anthocyanins) typically exhibit superior biocompatibility and degradability compared to inorganic photothermal converters in PTT applications. Organic nanocarriers serve as photothermal agents due to their large π-conjugated systems, which confer high NIR photothermal effects [130]. Reports on psoriasis treatment remain scarce, with most studies focusing on tumor therapy [131–136]. This technology is sometimes combined with PDT to enhance therapeutic efficacy, primarily through enhanced effects. For instance, Xue et al. developed a multifunctional nanoplatform Fe3O4@PDA@CaCO3/ICG, FPCI. Through ingenious nanostructure design, this platform integrated magnetic targeting, acid-responsive drug release, NIR photothermal conversion, and ROS generation capabilities, achieving combined enhancement between PTT and PDT. Although the research used tumor models, its key findings demonstrated that the combination of ICG and nanotechnology offered a foundational technical platform and conceptual framework for developing synergistic photothermal therapies for conditions such as psoriasis. [137], suggesting that organic photothermal converters may represent a novel therapeutic direction for psoriasis treatment.

Composite nanoscale photothermal converters

Composite nano photothermal converters represent a novel material for psoriasis treatment, typically comprising core nanocarriers such as GNRs and molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) with high-efficiency NIR light absorption capabilities, combined with other functional components like drugs, hydrogels, or enzymes. Under NIR laser irradiation, they efficiently generate localized heat. This heat precisely targets affected skin areas, inducing apoptosis in hyperproliferative cells while synergistically releasing anti-inflammatory drugs. This achieves combined photothermal and drug therapy effects, offering a novel and precise treatment approach for psoriasis. Nirmal et al. reported a PLGA-GNRs-isatin nanocomposite for alleviating psoriasis-like hyperproliferation via NIR-light-induced PTT. This composite efficiently generates heat under NIR irradiation, raising the temperature by 10 °C within 10 min to induce keratinocyte apoptosis. In an IMQ-induced psoriasis mouse model, it reduced scaling and erythema, decreased epidermal thickness by 61.8%, Key inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, IL-23, and TNF-α returned to baseline levels, neutrophil infiltration (Ly6G+ cells decreased by 72%) was significantly suppressed, and the epidermal proliferation marker Ki67 was markedly inhibited, demonstrating excellent therapeutic potential and biocompatibility [138]. In recent research, Chang developed a hyaluronic acid-modified Fe₃O₄ nanocomposite (Fe3O4@HA). This composite nanophototherapeutic agent combined magnetic targeting with NIR photothermal conversion capability, enabling targeted PTT for psoriasis. Experiments demonstrated that this material effectively enriched psoriasis-like lesions under an external magnetic field. Upon 808 nm laser irradiation, it generated localized hyperthermia (approximately 7.5 °C increase), inducing keratinocyte apoptosis and significantly suppressing epidermal hyperplasia and inflammatory responses [139]. In summary, the composite nanophotothermoconverter effectively induces apoptosis and alleviates inflammation in psoriasis mouse models through photothermal enhanced effects, demonstrating therapeutic potential. However, current research remains confined to animal studies. Future investigations should further explore the safety and efficacy of such materials in more advanced models to advance their clinical application.

Most nanocarrier designs for PDT/PTT are adapted from oncology. Intelligent platforms specifically engineered to respond to the psoriatic microenvironment (e.g., high ROS, proteases) for spatially controlled activation remain underexplored. The optimal therapeutic parameters (light dose, irradiation schedule) for psoriasis, distinct from tumors, also require dedicated investigation.

Nanocarriers combined with gene editing

A major obstacle preventing the full realization of gene editing’s potential is the lack of efficient and safe delivery strategies that can target specific cells and tissues. Nanoparticles can deliver drugs specifically to diseased sites, thereby minimizing systemic side effects, enabling controlled or sustained release, and enhancing therapeutic efficacy. Some studies indicate that nanoparticles can even deliver genetic material, such as DNA or RNA, for gene therapy applications. By precisely regulating the cholesterol density within LNPs, Mao et al. significantly enhanced the performance of the RNA-guided clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeat–associated nuclease protein 9 (CRISPR-Cas9) system in dendritic cells. This approach achieved highly efficient editing of the PD-L1 gene while simultaneously amplifying antitumor immune responses, fully demonstrating the critical role of nanoparticles in gene therapy. [140].

The advent of CRISPR-Cas9 ushered in a new era of gene editing. Within this system, the Cas9 nuclease is precisely guided to target sites by single-guide RNA (sgRNA), subsequently generating double-strand breaks (DSBs) to achieve site-specific genomic modifications. Compared to conventional gene editing methods, including zinc finger nucleases (ZFNs) and transcription activator-like effector nucleases (TALENs), CRISPR-Cas9-mediated editing offers greater flexibility, efficiency, and precision [141]. A major challenge hindering the full potential of gene editing is the lack of efficient and safe strategies for targeting cells and tissues. Skin forms a highly impermeable barrier, and intravenous administration is unlikely to effectively deliver genetic material when targeting the skin. Nanotechnology offers an alternative approach to overcome the limitations of conventional drug delivery methods. Nanotechnology enables the safe delivery of CRISPR-Cas9 components using customized polymeric nanoparticles, enhancing both safety and efficacy. For instance, Wan et al. developed a microneedle patch that co-delivers nanoparticles transdermally, further transporting Cas9 and Dex into the cell nucleus to perform their respective functions. This approach significantly improved symptoms of psoriasis and atopic dermatitis while overcoming glucocorticoid resistance [142]. Nanostructured gene delivery systems can be fabricated from lipids, polymers, peptides, graphene family nanocarriers, inorganic materials (e.g., AuNPs), or combinations thereof. However, reports on this technology for psoriasis remain relatively scarce to date. This section will focus on three types: lipid-based nanocarriers, polymeric nanocarriers, and metallic nanocarriers.

Lipid-based nanocarriers

Lipid-based nanocarriers have emerged as one of the preferred technologies in nucleic acid drug delivery due to their high delivery efficiency, excellent biocompatibility, scalable production, and clinically proven efficacy. However, some studies have demonstrated that the lipid components of LNPs can trigger intense inflammatory responses in immune cells [143]. Zeng et al. designed anti-inflammatory LNPs to deliver Stat3 siRNA, modifying conventional LNP carriers with anti-inflammatory glucocorticoids for psoriasis treatment. This approach maintained high siRNA delivery efficiency while significantly reducing LNP-related inflammatory side effects [144]. Viegas et al. developed a multifunctional nanostructured lipid carrier for co-delivery of TAC and siRNA, demonstrating its efficacy in animal models [145]. Marepally et al. designed and developed novel fusion nucleic acid lipid particles (F-NALPs) for co-delivery of anti-STAT3 siRNA and anti-TNF-α siRNA. Their research confirmed that the lipid nanocarrier efficiently penetrated skin and synergistically suppressed inflammatory factor and proliferation marker expression in psoriasiform plaques, validating the efficacy of lipid-based nanocarriers combined with gene editing for psoriasis treatment [146].

Polymeric nanocarriers

In recent years, significant efforts have been made to utilize polymeric nanoparticles as safe alternatives to viral vectors for gene delivery. Biodegradable polyesters, including PLGA and PLA, have been extensively employed in designing effective gene delivery systems. For instance, the MNs system reported by Wan et al. employed two polymeric nanocarriers: PLGA/Dex nanoparticles loaded with CRISPR-Cas9 system and a supramolecular nanocomplex (CP/Ad-SS-GD). This combined system reversed chronic inflammatory states in the skin immune microenvironment by simultaneously achieving two effects: CRISPR-Cas9 was used to specifically knock out NLRP3 inflammasome genes, while Dex was employed to remodel glucocorticoid sensitivity (Fig. 8a). In subsequent in vitro co-culture experiments, DC2.4 cells (a dendritic cell model) and 3T3 cells (a fibroblast model) were exposed to gradient concentrations of PLGA/Dex nanoparticles. The results showed that at a Dex concentration of 0.4 μg/ml, the NLRP3 gene editing efficiency in DC2.4 cells increased from 29.6 to 36.2%; while in 3T3 cells, 0.8 μg/ml Dex increased editing efficiency from 19.1 to 31.7%. This confirmed that Dex delivered via PLGA nanoparticles significantly enhances CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing activity by expanding nuclear pores (Fig. 8b,c). In an IMQ-induced psoriasis mouse model, a combination therapy integrating gene editing and drug delivery was achieved, with reduced PASI scores (Fig. 8d,e) and effective reversal of epidermal hyperplasia (Fig. 8f). This demonstrated the strategy’s success in disrupting NLRP3 inflammasomes via gene editing. Concurrently, enhanced glucocorticoid sensitivity significantly improved skin lesions, demonstrating the potent potential of polymeric nanocarriers combined with gene editing in a combined therapeutic approach for psoriasis [142]. In non-psoriatic applications, Zhang et al. reported a somatostatin receptor-targeted polymeric nanoplatform (LNA-PEG-OCT/PEI-OA) for efficient co-delivery of the CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing system and the chemotherapeutic agent paclitaxel to achieve combined treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma. Experiments demonstrated that this nanocarrier exhibits excellent stability, low hemolysis, and sustained-release properties. Metal nanocarriers

The integration of metal nanocarriers with gene editing technology represents a cutting-edge biomedical strategy. By utilizing metal nanoparticles, such as engineered gold, silver, and iron-based materials, as highly efficient carriers, gene editing tools like CRISPR-Cas9 can be precisely delivered to target cells, enabling directed modification of disease-causing genes. The integration of gene therapy and metal nanoparticle delivery represents a promising approach with significant potential for advancing psoriasis management research. While no reports exist on its application for psoriasis treatment, large-scale clinical trials for non-psoriatic indications remain limited due to gene editing efficiency and ethical constraints. Consequently, the specific types, delivery modalities, dosages, and frequency of metal nanoparticle-based therapies for psoriasis are still under investigation.

While the convergence of gene-editing tools and nanocarriers holds transformative potential for psoriasis, its path to the clinic is uniquely fraught with scientific and translational gaps. A fundamental disconnect exists between the permanent nature of CRISPR-Cas9-mediated DNA alteration and the chronic, relapsing–remitting course of psoriasis, which may require transient therapeutic modulation. This underscores a critical research void: the development of nano-delivery systems capable of efficient, yet spatially and temporally controlled, gene regulation, such as those employing transient epigenetic editors or inducible systems, rather than irreversible knockout. Furthermore, rigorous comparative studies evaluating the delivery efficiency, editing fidelity, and off-target profiles of different nanocarriers (lipid vs. polymer vs. metallic) for skin applications are virtually absent. Addressing these gaps is paramount to establishing a credible safety profile, which is the principal gatekeeper for applying genome editing to a non-lethal, chronic inflammatory disease.

Nanotechnology-integrated RNA therapies

Unlike gene editing technologies (such as CRISPR-Cas9), which permanently alter the genome by directly modifying DNA sequences to repair or knock out disease-causing genes, RNA therapies temporarily modulate protein expression by regulating mRNA, non-coding RNA, or nucleic acid-based tools (e.g., RNAi, ASO). These approaches do not alter the genome, offering reversible effects and higher safety profiles. However, naked RNA molecules face significant challenges in vivo: they are rapidly degraded by ubiquitous nucleases; their negative charge hinders penetration of similarly charged cell membranes; they may trigger immune responses; they are rapidly cleared by the kidneys; and they struggle to specifically target diseased tissues or cells [148]. The introduction of nanotechnology represents a key strategy to overcome these obstacles and enable the clinical translation of RNA therapeutics. Engineered nanocarriers efficiently encapsulate and shield RNA therapeutics, enhancing stability and facilitating cellular uptake for targeted delivery. RNA therapies are primarily categorized by mechanism into three classes: mRNA therapeutics, small nucleic acid (sRNA) therapeutics, and nucleic acid aptamers. Among these, sRNA therapeutics (e.g., siRNA, miRNA) are most widely applied in psoriasis treatment. They target pathogenic RNA to inhibit or activate gene expression. For instance, Zhang et al. innovatively constructed tannic acid (TA)-modified tetra-frame nucleic acid (tFNA) nanocomposites (STT). Clinical translation: comparisons, safety, and regulatory challenges

Comparison with conventional and natural therapies

Conventional treatments for psoriasis, such as topical corticosteroids and phototherapy, are effective for managing mild to moderate symptoms but present significant long-term drawbacks. Prolonged use of corticosteroids is associated with adverse effects like skin atrophy and telangiectasia, while phototherapy requires frequent hospital visits and carries inherent risks of photoaging and skin cancer. Consequently, there is growing interest in novel, natural product-based therapies. Plant-derived compounds, such as curcumin and indigo naturalis, have demonstrated promising anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects. For instance, curcumin inhibits NF-κB activation and regulates key immune cells (T cells and dendritic cells), whereas indirubin, the active component in indigo naturalis, suppresses keratinocyte proliferation [147]. These natural therapies generally offer a more favorable safety profile with fewer side effects compared to conventional options.

However, natural products face their own translational challenges, including lower relative therapeutic efficacy, the need for longer treatment durations, and issues related to formulation standardization, poor bioavailability, and unverified long-term safety. To harness the safety of natural products and conventional drugs while overcoming their pharmacokinetic limitations, future research must focus on integrating these compounds with advanced delivery methods [148].

Safety and immuno-toxicity profiles

When considering the application of multimodal nanocarriers in chronic skin diseases, safety and immunotoxicity are crucial concerns. Safety concerns mainly stem from their inherent physicochemical properties, such as size, surface charge, and surface modification, which directly affect biodistribution. For instance, positively charged nanocarriers exhibit stronger adhesion to cell membranes, which may trigger excessive inflammatory responses, potentially exacerbating the inflammatory cascade in psoriasis. Furthermore, carrier-related acute toxicity can manifest as transient inflammatory responses, whereas chronic toxicity presents as long-term tissue accumulation. Comprehensive in vitro and in vivo assessments are essential before these nanocarrier-based therapies can safely enter clinical practice.

Manufacturing scalability and regulatory barriers

Translating multimodal nanotherapeutics from the laboratory to large-scale clinical application faces profound Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls (CMC) bottlenecks. The synthesis of complex, multimodal nanocarriers, especially composite materials combining organic and inorganic elements, which often involves multi-step processes that are highly challenging to scale up under Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) conditions. Maintaining strict batch-to-batch consistency regarding particle size distribution, surface charge, ligand functionalization, and drug encapsulation efficiency remains a formidable engineering hurdle, often leading to prohibitive production costs.

Furthermore, the current regulatory framework presents significant barriers. Regulatory agencies lack standardized, universal guidelines tailored specifically for highly complex multimodal nanomedicines. Conventional drug evaluation protocols are insufficient to address the unique pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles of these platforms. Consequently, regulatory bodies require rigorous, independent demonstration of safety for both the individual components and the integrated multimodal system as a whole. This necessitates extensive, costly, and time-consuming preclinical validation to map out potential synergistic toxicities, long-term tissue accumulation, and clearance mechanisms, thereby significantly delaying the global market access of these promising therapies.

Conclusion

Psoriasis is a chronic immune-mediated inflammatory skin disease with a high prevalence worldwide, presenting significant challenges in clinical management. While existing therapies can partially control disease progression, the field continues to face unmet needs, including inadequate skin penetration of topical agents, systemic toxicity of conventional drugs, non-uniform responses to biologics, and a lack of targeted strategies for different disease subtypes, especially rarer forms such as palmoplantar and pustular psoriasis. This review systematically examines nanotechnology advances in psoriasis treatment, revealing how nanocarriers significantly enhance the safety and efficacy of conventional therapies through improved drug permeability, targeted delivery, and sustained-release effects. It also elucidates the molecular mechanisms underlying the enhanced effects observed in combination strategies involving nanotechnology and innovative approaches like PDT and gene editing. These findings collectively outline a clear trajectory for nanotechnology to propel psoriasis treatment toward greater efficiency, precision, and personalization. With the deepening convergence of materials science and immunology, nanomedicine holds promise to shift psoriasis management from symptom control toward curative therapies. This inspires researchers to persist in exploring solutions to ultimately overcome this significant public health challenge, opening new avenues of hope for patients and achieving clinical translation to enhance the quality of life for individuals worldwide.

Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge that the majority of studies discussed in this review, particularly those involving gene editing and RNA-based therapies, remain at the preclinical stage. While animal models such as IMQ-induced psoriasis mice provide valuable mechanistic insights and proof-of-concept evidence, caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings to clinical practice. Animal models cannot fully recapitulate the complexity, chronicity, and heterogeneity of human psoriasis, and therapeutic efficacy demonstrated in preclinical settings often does not directly translate to comparable outcomes in patients. Key translational challenges including long-term biosafety, immunogenicity, scalable manufacturing under GMP standards, and regulatory validation, must be rigorously addressed before these nanotechnology-based strategies can advance to clinical application. Future research should prioritize well-designed clinical trials to validate the efficacy and safety of these promising approaches in human patients.

Looking ahead, the translation of these nanotechnology-based strategies from promising concepts to clinical realities requires a concerted and phased research agenda. In the immediate future, efforts should prioritize the optimization of nanocarrier design, focusing on rigorous in vitro screening using advanced human psoriatic skin models to evaluate their penetration efficiency and immunomodulatory potency against key cytokines such as IL-17 and IL-23. Promising lead formulations must then be advanced to robust preclinical validation, utilizing established models like the IMQ-induced mouse to conclusively demonstrate superior efficacy and comprehensive safety profiles over standard therapies. The ultimate clinical translation will hinge on addressing critical translational barriers, including the development of scalable, GMP-compliant manufacturing processes, early engagement with regulatory agencies through pre-IND meetings, and the meticulous design of early-phase clinical trials to evaluate safety and initial proof-of-concept efficacy in patient populations. This structured pathway, from rational carrier design to targeted clinical validation, charts a clear course for developing the next generation of nanotherapies for psoriasis. The significance of this review lies in its systematic synthesis of recent advances in nanotechnology-enabled psoriasis therapy, filling a gap in systematic synthesis. It not only provides researchers with a comprehensive reference framework but also lays a solid foundation for clinicians exploring combination therapies (e.g., nanomedicine combined with phototherapy/gene therapy) and policymakers supporting the clinical translation of nanomedicine. It outlines a series of nanoscale materials, including their use in combination with conventional drugs and novel phototherapy technologies (PDT, PTT), as well as their combined application with gene editing and RNA therapies. It should be noted that due to limitations in literature scope and the rapid advancement of research, some studies may not be fully covered, potentially affecting the comprehensive assessment of long-term safety. To translate this potential into reality, future work must address pivotal translational gaps: (1) comprehensive long-term biosafety and immunotoxicity profiles of nanocarriers; (2) standardized preclinical models enabling direct comparison between platforms; (3) personalized targeting strategies accounting for psoriasis heterogeneity; and (4) well-designed early-phase clinical trials to validate the therapeutic hypotheses generated in animal models. By focusing on these translational priorities, nanotechnology can propel psoriasis treatment toward a new era of precision medicine, moving beyond symptom control toward more effective, durable, and patient-tailored therapies. Ultimately, the convergence of nanotechnology, immunology, and clinical dermatology holds significant promise for overcoming a major public health challenge and improving the quality of life for patients worldwide.
