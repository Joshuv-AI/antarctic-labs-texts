Immune cell remodelling and the peribiliary immune niche in primary biliary cholangitis: from initiation to chronicity
Authors: Jiaqi Xiong, Zeyi Zhang, Ziying Zhang, Mengqi Yuan, Kaidi Zhu, Fu-Sheng Wang, Lei Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary biliary cholangitis (PBC) is a chronic autoimmune cholestatic liver disease characterised by immune-mediated destruction of small intrahepatic bile ducts. Although ursodeoxycholic acid remains the standard first-line therapy and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor agonists have expanded treatment options, current therapies do not fully address the immune mechanisms that drive disease initiation and persistence. Recent work suggests that PBC cannot be explained by abnormalities in a single immune-cell population but instead reflects coordinated immune remodelling around injured bile ducts. In this review, we organise current evidence within an overlapping, process-oriented framework encompassing immune initiation, effector amplification and immune-regulatory dysfunction. Stressed biliary epithelial cells provide autoantigens, immune-sensing signals and inflammatory cues while myeloid cells and lymphocyte subsets cooperate to sustain tissue-resident cytotoxic, inflammatory and profibrogenic circuits. Disease chronicity is further promoted by defective regulatory mechanisms, including impaired Treg stability and dysfunction of non-conventional regulatory populations. We also discuss mechanism-guided therapeutic strategies targeting effector T cells, myeloid inflammation, B-cell activation, immune tolerance and the cholestatic microenvironment. Understanding the peribiliary immune niche may facilitate immune phenotype-guided stratification and precision therapy in PBC.

Introduction

Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) is a chronic autoimmune cholestatic liver disease characterised by immune-mediated destruction of the small intrahepatic bile ducts. Globally, the annual incidence of PBC is approximately 1.8 per 100 000 population with a prevalence of 18.1 per 100 000.1 The disease predominantly affects middle-aged women and is marked by antimitochondrial antibody positivity, non-suppurative destructive cholangitis and progressive cholestasis, ultimately leading to fibrosis, cirrhosis and liver failure.2 Genome-wide association studies have identified susceptibility loci within both human leucocyte antigen (HLA) and non-HLA regions, supporting a multifactorial pathogenesis involving genetic predisposition and environmental triggers.3 Although the precise initiating events remain incompletely understood, immune dysregulation is widely recognised as the central driver of disease development and progression.

Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) remains the standard first-line therapy for PBC45; however, approximately 25%–50% of patients exhibit an inadequate biochemical response.67 Recent approval of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) agonists, including elafibranor and seladelpar, has expanded therapeutic options for these patients.89 Current therapeutic strategies for patients with different immunophenotypes are still limited. The significant heterogeneity of disease progression and treatment response suggests that PBC is driven by a complex dynamic immune network rather than a single pathogenic pathway.

Historically, PBC has been viewed as a T helper type 1 (Th1) and Th17 dominant autoimmune disease associated with impaired regulatory T (Treg)-cell function.10 However, recent studies using single-cell technologies and immune profiling have revealed a far more intricate immunological landscape.1112 In addition to conventional adaptive immune responses, biliary epithelial cells (BECs), monocyte-macrophage populations, innate-like lymphocytes, tissue-resident immune cells and functionally heterogeneous B-cell subsets actively participate in disease initiation, amplification and persistence. These findings support a shift from a reductionist view of individual immune-cell abnormalities towards a systems-level understanding of multicellular immune interactions within the peribiliary microenvironment.

In this review, we integrate recent advances in PBC immunobiology into an interconnected mechanistic framework encompassing immune initiation, effector amplification and failure of immune regulation. We highlight the dynamic interactions among BECs, innate and adaptive immune populations and regulatory-cell subsets that collectively drive disease progression. Building on this mechanistic framework, we discuss evolving therapeutic strategies targeting different immunophenotypes. By linking pathogenic mechanisms with potential therapeutic interventions, this review aims to provide an integrated perspective on PBC immunopathogenesis and to identify opportunities for precision immunomodulatory approaches in future clinical practice.

Dynamic immune mechanisms of biliary injury in PBC

Immune initiation: biliary epithelial stress and innate immune sensing

BECs: from passive targets to active immune initiators

BECs account for only a small proportion of hepatic cells, yet they line the entire biliary tree—from the canals of Hering to the extrahepatic bile ducts—and are among the principal targets in PBC.13 The hallmark pathological features of PBC include non-suppurative inflammatory injury of portal bile ducts, infiltration of lymphocytes and plasma cells, disruption of the basement membrane, disorganisation of BEC architecture and cytoplasmic eosinophilia.814 Increasingly, BECs are recognised not simply as passive victims of autoimmune attack but as active participants that provide autoantigens, immune-sensing signals and chemotactic cues during early disease development. Analyses of human PBC liver specimens demonstrated that BECs within damaged bile ducts exhibit a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). Senescent BECs undergo irreversible proliferative arrest while remaining metabolically active and acquiring a persistent hypersecretory state.15 Complementary experiments using murine BECs showed that SASP-associated mediators, particularly CCL2 and CX3CL1, promote macrophage migration and may propagate paracrine senescence, thereby sustaining peribiliary inflammation.16 Telomere shortening and accumulated DNA damage in BECs within damaged bile ducts further reflect ongoing cellular senescence and may contribute to progressive bile duct loss in PBC.17

Another important initiating event is the abnormal handling of mitochondrial autoantigens by injured BECs. During apoptosis, BECs uniquely retain the mitochondrial autoantigen pyruvate dehydrogenase complex E2 subunit (PDC-E2) with apoptotic bodies, providing a structural basis for antimitochondrial antibody (AMA) recognition.18 This feature may help explain why immune responses in PBC are selectively directed towards the biliary epithelium despite the ubiquitous expression of mitochondrial antigens. In parallel, molecules involved in antigen presentation and innate immune signalling, including CD1d molecules and multiple Toll-like receptors (TLRs), are upregulated on BECs.19 These molecules are found in the cholangiocyte systems of mice and humans to present lipid antigens and activate natural killer (NK) T cells,20 thereby shaping the immune microenvironment within the portal tract.21 On bacterial exposure, BECs can activate mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells through antigen presentation, triggering the release of proinflammatory cytokines and promoting biliary inflammation.22 Together, these observations suggest that BECs may serve as both an autoantigen reservoir and a local immune-presenting platform.

BECs also amplify local inflammation through costimulatory and chemotactic pathways. Stimulation by liver mononuclear cells enhances the expression of CD40, HLA-DRα (human leukocyte antigen DRα) and chemokines on BECs.23 Upregulated CD40 interacts with CD154 on adjacent macrophages, activating downstream apoptotic pathways and inducing programmed cell death of BECs in PBC patients.24 BECs also actively recruit neutrophils, leading to the formation of ductular reaction-associated neutrophils, which release reactive oxygen species and neutrophil extracellular traps, further exacerbating epithelial injury.25 In addition, bile acids modulate immune responses through receptors such as G protein-coupled bile acid receptor 1 and farnesoid X receptor (FXR). Disruption of bile acid composition, microbiota dysbiosis or intrinsic BEC dysfunction impairs immune tolerance and promotes inflammation.2627 Moreover, other inflammatory genes like interferon-induced protein with tetratricopeptide repeats 3 are upregulated in senescent BECs, further supporting the role of BECs as active participants in inflammatory signalling.1628 Thus, BECs in PBC can be viewed as antigen reservoirs, immune-sensing platforms and inflammatory amplifiers that help establish a permissive microenvironment for downstream immune activation.

Dendritic cells and monocyte-macrophage populations: translating epithelial stress into immune activation

Dendritic cells (DCs) help bridge epithelial injury and adaptive immune activation in chronic cholangiopathies. Although evidence from primary sclerosing cholangitis suggests that conventional type 2 DCs can promote pathogenic Th17-cell responses,29 whether a comparable cDC2-Th17 axis operates in PBC remains unresolved. In PBC, DCs are enriched around damaged bile ducts,30 where circulating exosome stimulation enhances their antigen-presenting capacity, thereby activating T cells and amplifying autoimmune responses.31 Conventional type 1 DCs further contribute by cross-presenting autoantigens to CD8+ T cells, inducing proinflammatory cytokine production and exacerbating inflammation and fibrosis.32 DCs may therefore provide a functional link between biliary epithelial stress and antigen-specific lymphocyte activation.

The monocyte-macrophage compartment is similarly shaped by environmental cues and plays a central role in disease progression. In the inflamed portal tract, upregulation of monocyte chemoattractant proteins promotes the recruitment of bone marrow-derived Ly6Chi monocytes through C-C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CCR2).33 These cells accumulate in the portal tract and differentiate into monocyte-derived macrophages (MoMFs) in the 2OA-BSA mouse model, which progressively replace liver-resident Kupffer cells (KCs) as the dominant intrahepatic population.34 Activated by autoantigens such as PDC-E2, MoMFs secrete proinflammatory factors, including tumour necrosis factor-α and IFN-γ (IFN-γ), thereby worsening biliary epithelial injury.35 In addition, MoMF-derived transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) activates hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) and promotes fibrosis,34 and interleukin-23 (IL-23) selectively amplifies Th17-cell responses.36 In this sense, MoMFs are not merely inflammatory bystanders; they may serve as a bridge linking epithelial injury, effector T-cell polarisation and fibrogenesis.

The functional heterogeneity of macrophages deserves particular attention. Recent studies have identified upregulation of insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 4 in MoMFs in human blood samples, where it may exert protective effects on BECs and serve as a potential biomarker for PBC.37 KCs also undergo functional reprogramming in PBC. They exhibit enhanced proinflammatory gene expression alongside reduced expression of phagocytosis-related receptors such as TLR4. Through secretion of IL-1β, KCs activate HSCs via IL-1 receptor type 1, thereby promoting fibrosis.11 In both patients with PBC and dnTGFβRII mouse models, KCs display excessive activation and increased autophagic activity, leading to accelerated degradation of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), reduced nitric oxide production and diminished suppression of CD8+ T-cell proliferation.38 This ultimately contributes to sustained biliary epithelial damage. These observations indicate that myeloid cells in PBC may exert both pathogenic and potentially reparative functions depending on their origin, activation state and local microenvironmental cues.

Collectively, immune initiation in PBC appears to involve more than isolated epithelial damage. Stressed BECs provide autoantigens, immune-sensing signals and chemotactic cues, while DCs and monocyte-macrophage populations translate these epithelial signals into adaptive immune activation. This process may establish an early peribiliary immune-activating niche in which epithelial cells and innate immune cells cooperate to recruit, retain and prime pathogenic lymphocytes. At the same time, the protective or reparative functions of innate immune cells remain incompletely defined and should be considered in future mechanistic studies.

Effector amplification of biliary injury

Tissue-resident and effector T-cell responses in bile duct-directed injury

CD8+ T cells are significantly enriched in the liver of patients with PBC.39 Among adaptive immune cells, CD8+ tissue-resident memory T (TRM) cells have emerged as key mediators of persistent bile duct-directed injury. Recent studies have shown that nudix hydrolase 1 (NUDT 1) promotes the generation of TRM cells, which are positioned to sustain local cytotoxicity against BECs.40

Mechanistically, antigen stimulation induces CD8+ T cells to upregulate E-cadherin and integrin αE(CD103), facilitating adhesion to BECs, active invasion and even intraepithelial proliferation.41 Notably, E-cadherin is also expressed on BECs and can deliver inhibitory signals to CD8+ T cells. However, fragmented loss of E-cadherin in PBC disrupts this regulatory interaction, resulting in sustained CD8+ T cell activation and amplified inflammation.42 Experimental models further demonstrate that CD8+ T cells across various differentiation stages, including naïve, central memory and effector memory subsets, can all contribute to biliary inflammation. Deficiency of C-X-C chemokine receptor type 3 (CXCR3) skews CD8+ T cells toward terminal differentiation, enhancing proinflammatory cytokine production.43 Additionally, dysregulation of pathways controlling chemotaxis, adhesion and cytotoxicity, including inhibitors of the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) pathway and programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), further exacerbates liver injury.4445 These findings suggest that persistent bile duct injury depends not only on the presence of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells but also on their retention, positioning and functional state within the biliary epithelial niche.

CD4+ T-cell subsets also play diverse roles. Th1 and Th17 cells have traditionally been associated with the early and late stages of PBC, respectively. Early CD14lowCD16+ monocytes promote Th1 differentiation by upregulating IL-12,46 whereas IL-23 drives Th17-cell responses during later stages.26 Recent studies in PBC patients have identified a tissue-resident Th1 population (CD69+CXCR6+ZNF683+RBPJ+) within the portal tract, which depends on Janus kinase-signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (JAK–STAT1) signalling to produce proinflammatory cytokines, activate DCs and enhance cytotoxic T-cell responses.11 This tissue-resident Th1 programme provides another mechanism by which local inflammatory memory may be maintained around damaged bile ducts.

Follicular helper T (Tfh) cells provide a further link between cellular immunity and humoral autoimmunity. Tfh cells (CXCR5+CD4+) are expanded in the peripheral blood of patients with PBC and produce high levels of cytokines, particularly IL-21,47 which promotes differentiation of naïve B cells into CD27+ plasma cells and drives production of immunoglobulin (Ig)M, IgG and specific AMAs.4849 Follicular Treg cells (FoxP3+CXCR5+PD-1+CD4+) are also increased in parallel with Tfh cells.50 Whether this expansion represents a compensatory regulatory response or functionally impaired follicular regulation remains unresolved. Additionally, aberrant DNA hypermethylation in CD4+ T cells suggests a role for epigenetic dysregulation and potential therapeutic targeting.51 Together, these findings place the Tfh-B-cell axis at the interface between cellular immune activation and sustained humoral autoimmunity in PBC.

B cells: humoral autoimmunity and organisation of the peribiliary immune niche

B cells play a central role in PBC through sustained autoantibody production following activation by autoantigens.52 Recent studies of paired liver-infiltrating and circulating B cells in PBC patients have identified preferential usage of the immunoglobulin heavy variable 4 family, significantly reduced usage of the immunoglobulin heavy joining 6 gene and dynamic trafficking patterns between the liver and peripheral circulation, collectively amplifying immune responses.53 Beyond their established role in antibody production, intrahepatic CD19+ B cells exhibit potent proinflammatory activity. On CD40 ligand stimulation, they secrete cytokines, including IL-6 and IFN-γ, highlighting their direct contribution to inflammation. Elevated levels of B-cell activating factor (BAFF) in patient serum further disrupt immune tolerance by promoting Treg apoptosis and inhibiting regulatory B-cell function.54

More recent evidence suggests that B cells may also organise local immune-cell positioning within the peribiliary niche. A distinct B-cell subset can recruit CCR5+CD4+ TRM cells to the peribiliary region through secretion of C-C motif chemokine ligand 3, thereby linking B-cell activation with tissue-resident T-cell cytotoxicity and BEC death.55 This observation broadens the pathogenic role of B cells from autoantibody production to tissue organisation and local immune amplification. Accordingly, B-cell-directed therapies in PBC may need to consider not only humoral autoimmunity but also cytokine secretion, antigen presentation and lymphocyte recruitment.

Innate-like lymphocytes: local effector functions of MAIT cells and NK cells

MAIT cells contribute to immune responses in PBC by secreting proinflammatory cytokines. In vitro experiments have confirmed that IL-12 stimulates MAIT cells to upregulate IL-17A expression, thereby activating HSCs and promoting fibrosis. In addition, peripheral MAIT cells are recruited to the bile ducts and secrete IFN-γ in response to IL-18.56–58 Of particular relevance, intrahepatic CD69+CD103+ resident MAIT cells highly express the transcription factor forkhead box M1, endowing them with sustained proliferative and tissue-retention properties.57 Furthermore, accumulation of primary bile acids can activate the FXR-IL7 axis in the liver, which may provide persistent stimulation to MAIT cells and enhance their pathogenic potential.59 These findings indicate that MAIT cells may participate in both inflammatory and profibrogenic responses, although their precise contribution to PBC progression requires further definition.

NK cells are markedly expanded in PBC liver tissue and localise around damaged BECs. Previous studies have shown that TLR4 ligands and IFN-α can stimulate NK cells to directly kill BECs.60 Recent research indicates that the IL-12/signal transducer and activator of transcription 4 (STAT4) axis is upregulated and the biological effect of NK cell-derived IFN-γ is strikingly context-dependent. At a low NK/BEC ratio, IFN-γ protects BECs from injury; however, when the ratio increases, overactivated NK cells instead promote T-cell activation and worsen biliary damage.61 IL-15 converts NK cells into inflammation-related NK cells that promote CD4+ T-cell proliferation and effector-memory differentiation. Another subset of CD49a+ NK cells express high levels of the chemokine receptor CXCR6, mediating their migration to the biliary epithelium, where they release perforin and granzyme to directly lyse BECs.62 Beyond classical NK and T-cell subsets, invariant NK T cells and CD3+CD56+ αGalcer-CD1d tetramer- T cells are also expanded in both peripheral blood and liver tissue. These cells bridge innate and adaptive immunity, inducing HSCs to secrete TGF-β and upregulate α-smooth muscle actin, thereby directly contributing to liver fibrosis.63 These apparently divergent observations suggest that NK cells do not have a fixed pathogenic or protective role in PBC. Their function is likely shaped by disease stage, local cytokine milieu, NK/BEC ratio and the composition of intrahepatic NK-cell subsets. This context dependency may explain why NK-cell responses have been linked to both epithelial protection and bile duct injury.

Taken together, effector T cells, B cells, MAIT cells, NK cells and NKT-like populations appear to act cooperatively within the peribiliary microenvironment. Rather than operating as isolated lineages, these cells jointly sustain cytotoxic injury and inflammatory signalling, while also promoting fibrogenesis. Immune-regulatory dysfunction across PBC progression

Treg cells: numerical deficiency and proinflammatory conversion

Treg cells are essential for maintaining immune tolerance by suppressing effector T-cell responses in liver diseases. In PBC, immunosuppressive CD4+CD25+ Treg cells are significantly reduced in peripheral blood, leading to impaired control of autoreactive T cells. Additionally, CD8+ Treg cells show severely compromised differentiation, with less than 40% of the differentiation efficiency and only 20% of the suppressive activity observed in healthy controls.6566 However, Treg dysfunction in PBC should not be interpreted solely as a numerical defect. Lineage instability and inflammatory conversion may be equally important. Treg cells aberrantly express IL-12 receptor β2, rendering them highly sensitive to IL-12 and promoting STAT4-mediated conversion into Th1-like cells.67 In mouse models, Treg cells display downregulation of immunosuppressive transcription factors and upregulation of proinflammatory genes, further contributing to immune-mediated injury.68 Thus, Treg abnormalities in PBC involve both reduced availability and loss of suppressive stability. Such instability may help explain why an initially localised immune response evolves into persistent bile duct injury and why tolerance-restoring strategies remain attractive therapeutic approaches.

Myeloid-derived suppressor cells: compensatory expansion and functional impairment

Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) are a heterogeneous population of immature myeloid cells with potent immunomodulatory properties. In patients with PBC, MDSCs with a predominant HLA-DR-/lowCD33+CD11b+CD14+CD15- phenotype are expanded in the circulation and liver and suppress T-cell responses through an iNOS-dependent mechanism. This function is enhanced by cellular communication network factor 1 (CCN1) through integrin αMβ2-mediated STAT3 phosphorylation.69 Microbiota-derived butyrate can promote the expansion and suppressive activity of MDSCs through peroxisome proliferator activated receptor δ (PPARD)-dependent fatty acid β-oxidation. Patients with an inadequate response to UDCA exhibited reduced faecal butyrate levels and impaired suppressive function of MDSCs. In the 2-octynoic acid-bovine serum albumin (2OA-BSA) mouse model, butyrate supplementation or adoptive transfer of butyrate-conditioned MDSCs attenuated hepatic inflammation.70

NK cells and double-negative T cells

In addition to their pathogenic roles, NK cells can exert immunoregulatory effects in specific contexts. For example, NK cells can suppress CD4+ T-cell proliferation via the programmed death-ligand 1/PD-1 axis in mouse models.71 Additionally, under cholestatic conditions, such as bile-duct ligation, KC-mediated activation of NK cells promotes hepatoprotective effects through IFN-γ-dependent pathways, including increased IL-6 production and inhibition of STAT1 signalling. However, these regulatory functions appear to be context-dependent and may be impaired in PBC.72 Loss of such regulatory NK-cell activity could remove an important checkpoint on effector T-cell expansion within the inflamed portal tract.

Double-negative T (DN T) cells (CD4⁻CD8⁻) normally suppress autoreactive CD4+ and CD8+ effector T-cell proliferation via perforin-mediated mechanisms.73 In PBC, DN T cells exhibit functional impairment in the liver and peripheral blood. Notably, pathogenic concentrations of the bile acid lithocholic acid can specifically inhibit DN T-cell proliferation, removing a critical brake on peribiliary chronic inflammation and tissue injury.74 These findings suggest that immune regulation in PBC fails at multiple levels, involving not only classical Treg cells but also non-conventional regulatory populations that normally limit effector-cell expansion and tissue damage.

Overall, chronic biliary injury in PBC appears to be sustained by a dynamic imbalance between tissue-resident effector programmes and defective regulatory circuits within the peribiliary immune niche. BECs, myeloid cells, B cells, TRM cells, innate-like lymphocytes and regulatory populations do not act in isolation; rather, they form an interconnected network that links epithelial stress to chronic inflammation, fibrogenesis and therapeutic resistance. This model argues against uniform targeting of a single cytokine or cell type and instead supports treatment selection according to the dominant pathogenic pathway.

Therapeutic implications: targeting immune dysregulation and cholestatic microenvironment in PBC

The immunopathogenesis of PBC represents a dynamic and multilayered network process, initiated by active participation of the biliary epithelium, propagated through coordinated effector-cell responses and reinforced by immune-regulatory dysfunction within the cholestatic microenvironment. Accordingly, therapeutic strategies should be considered not simply according to drug class but according to the pathogenic circuits for intervention. Effector T-cell programmes

Effector T cells, particularly CD8+ T cells and Th1/Th17 subsets, serve as the principal mediators of biliary epithelial injury in PBC. Targeting these populations represents a rational therapeutic approach. Enoxacin has been shown to attenuate cholangitis progression in dnTGFβRII mouse models by downregulating IFN-γ and perforin expression in CD8+ T cells.75 This observation supports the concept that pathogenic cytotoxic programmes can be selectively dampened without necessarily inducing broader immunosuppression. However, enoxacin remains a proof-of-concept strategy, and its relevance to human PBC has not yet been established.

JAK inhibitors may be most relevant in immune-activated phenotypes characterised by tissue-resident Th1 programmes and cytokine-rich portal inflammation, although current support remains preclinical. The JAK1/2 inhibitor baricitinib reduces inflammatory-cell infiltration and BEC necrosis in experimental models.11 Similarly, ruxolitinib decreases the frequencies of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, reduces Tfh and germinal-centre B cells and promotes macrophage polarisation toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype.76 Compared with enoxacin, JAK inhibitors target multiple signalling pathways and may therefore better reflect the complex immunopathology of PBC. Nonetheless, their safety profiles—particularly risks of infection and thrombosis—require careful evaluation. Given the involvement of JAK–STAT signalling in tissue-resident Th1 responses, T-cell activation and cytokine-mediated myeloid crosstalk, this class of agents may be most relevant for immune-activated phenotypes but patient selection will be essential. Moreover, PD-1-targeting chimeric antigen receptor T cells have been shown to reduce PD-1high CD8+ TRM-cell populations and alleviate portal inflammation in murine models.4477 However, challenges related to specificity, safety and long-term effects remain.

Restoring immune regulation

In addition to targeting effector T cells, restoration of Treg-cell function has emerged as an important strategy. In PBC, Treg cells exhibit both quantitative deficiency and functional instability, including conversion toward a Th1-like phenotype. The DNA methyltransferase inhibitor decitabine can restore Treg function and rebalance the Treg/Th17 axis through epigenetic reprogramming.78 This approach aligns with the concept of ‘restoring immune tolerance’ rather than broadly suppressing immunity. However, potential off-target effects and long-term safety concerns of demethylating agents warrant further investigation.51 Conventional Treg infusion is limited by phenotypic instability, uncertain liver homing and lack of antigen specificity. Engineered immune-cell approaches may overcome some of these limitations, but their application in PBC remains at an early stage. Advances in engineered Treg technologies—including T-cell receptor-Treg and chimeric antigen receptor-Treg cells—may offer the potential for antigen-specific immune suppression and improved therapeutic precision.7980 DN T-cell therapy has demonstrated efficacy in preclinical models, significantly reducing serum alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase and AMA levels. Combined therapy with DN T cells and UDCA further improves liver inflammation, biliary injury and fibrosis.81

These cell-based strategies are conceptually attractive because they directly address the imbalance between tissue-resident effector programmes and defective regulatory circuits. Nevertheless, their clinical translation will require careful assessment of antigen specificity, off-target immune suppression, durability, manufacturing feasibility and the ability of infused or engineered cells to traffic to the peribiliary niche.

Myeloid and innate inflammatory pathways

Targeting innate immune pathways represents another promising direction, although current evidence highlights the limitations of single-target strategies. Ustekinumab, an anti–IL-12 monoclonal antibody, failed to significantly reduce alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels in a phase II trial, leading to discontinuation of its development for PBC.82 This outcome underscores the complexity and redundancy of the immune network, in which blockade of a single cytokine is unlikely to reverse established disease.

By contrast, interventions targeting chemokine pathways or intracellular signalling cascades show greater promise. The CCR2/CCR5 antagonist cenicriviroc has been shown in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis to reduce recruitment of Ly6C+ monocytes and ameliorate fibrosis,8384 suggesting potential applicability in PBC. Vitamin D₃ has also demonstrated immunomodulatory effects by inhibiting TLR4/NF-κB signalling and reducing M1 macrophage polarisation in experimental models.85

Overall, effective targeting of innate immunity in PBC may require simultaneous modulation of multiple signalling nodes rather than reliance on single-molecule inhibition. Future studies should also account for macrophage heterogeneity because indiscriminate suppression of myeloid cells may eliminate reparative as well as pathogenic subsets.

B-cell responses

B cells contribute to PBC pathogenesis through both autoantibody production and cytokine-mediated immune amplification. Rituximab, an anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody, can reduce AMA titres and ALP levels.86 However, it does not improve fatigue—a key clinical symptom—and has been associated with adverse effects, including the potential induction of inflammatory bowel disease.787 Moreover, the lack of correlation between AMA titers and disease severity suggests that B-cell depletion alone may be insufficient to reverse established immunopathology.

Similarly, belimumab, an anti-BAFF monoclonal antibody, reduced serum IgM levels in a small cohort but failed to significantly improve liver function. These findings highlight the functional heterogeneity of B cells in PBC, in which pathogenic effects extend beyond antibody production to include cytokine secretion and antigen presentation.88 They also suggest that therapeutic responses may depend on whether B cells are primarily driving humoral autoimmunity, inflammatory cytokine production or recruitment of tissue-resident effector T cells.

A more promising strategy involves dual targeting of CD20 and BAFF, which has achieved more complete B-cell depletion and significantly alleviated liver inflammation and biliary injury in preclinical models.89 This combinatorial approach may better address the multifaceted role of B cells and warrants further clinical investigation. However, future B-cell-directed trials should incorporate immune phenotyping rather than relying solely on AMA or IgM changes as pharmacodynamic readouts.

Cholestatic and metabolic microenvironment

FXR agonists have been extensively investigated as therapies for PBC. Obeticholic acid (OCA), an FXR agonist, was historically introduced as a second-line therapy for patients with an inadequate response to or intolerance of UDCA. Because of safety concerns and the failure of confirmatory data to verify clinical benefit, the regulatory status and clinical use of obeticholic acid have changed substantially, its EU marketing authorisation has been revoked and its US approval has been withdrawn. Accordingly, OCA should now be discussed primarily as a former second-line therapy and as a cautionary example for surrogate-endpoint-based approval.9091 Other FXR agonists, including tropifexor, have demonstrated improvements in biochemical indices and inflammation in phase II trials, and the effect of cilofexor in primary sclerosing cholangitis is being explored in phase III trials.92–94 Nevertheless, pruritus and safety concerns remain important limitations of this therapeutic class.

PPAR agonists have emerged as one of the most promising therapeutic classes. Unlike traditional immunosuppressants, PPAR agonists exert multitarget effects by simultaneously regulating bile acid metabolism, inflammation and immune-cell function. Seladelpar (PPAR-δ agonist) significantly reduces ALP levels and improves pruritus, potentially through modulation of IL-31 and bile acid pathways.9596 Elafibranor (dual PPAR-α/δ agonist) also improves biochemical and inflammatory parameters without exacerbating pruritus, possibly via fibroblast growth factor 21 signalling.9798 Both elafibranor and seladelpar have expanded the therapeutic landscape for patients with an inadequate response or intolerance to UDCA and have received Food and Drug Administration (FDA) accelerated approval.999100 However, their long-term effects on survival, liver transplantation and hepatic decompensation remain to be confirmed.

From an immunological perspective, PPAR agonists are particularly relevant because they may remodel the cholestatic inflammatory milieu rather than directly depleting immune cells. This distinction may partly explain their favourable position in PBC therapy compared with conventional immunosuppressive approaches. Pemafibrate, another PPAR-α agonist, has been reported to improve serological measures, including ALP and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), in retrospective and multicentre studies.101102 Saroglitazar (PPAR-α/γ agonist) has shown rapid biochemical improvement in phase II trials, although transient elevations in transaminases have been observed.103 Traditional PPAR-α agonists, such as bezafibrate and fenofibrate, also improve biochemical responses and liver stiffness when combined with UDCA, but adverse effects—including myalgia and gastrointestinal symptoms—require monitoring.4 PPAR-γ ligands have shown immunomodulatory effects in preclinical models, although their clinical relevance in PBC remains to be established.104105 Collectively, PPAR agonists illustrate how metabolic and immune pathways intersect in PBC and provide a clinically tractable means of modifying the cholestatic microenvironment.

Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) have also attracted considerable attention. Human umbilical cord-derived MSCs suppress Th1 and Th17 differentiation via a galectin-9-dependent pathway and inhibit CD4+ T-cell proliferation, thereby alleviating liver inflammation in experimental models.106 Bone marrow–derived MSCs inhibit HSC activation and attenuate fibrosis,107 and human placenta-derived MSCs delay BEC senescence.108 MSCs may additionally modulate multiple immune-cell populations, including NK cells and DCs, highlighting their broad immunoregulatory potential.109 Further studies are needed to define the optimal source, dosing and mechanisms of MSC therapy in PBC. Given their pleiotropic effects, MSC-based approaches should be interpreted as niche-modifying rather than cell-specific therapies. Their clinical development will require standardised products, well-designed trials and reproducible potency assays.

Additional therapeutic approaches include glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, which may indirectly improve PBC by reducing metabolic stress and systemic inflammation,110 and setanaxib, a nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase 1/4 inhibitor with potential antifibrotic effects via reduction of oxidative stress. Although initial phase II trials did not meet primary endpoints, further studies are ongoing.111112 These approaches are best viewed as adjunctive or phenotype-directed strategies rather than broadly applicable immune therapies at present.

Overall, the therapeutic landscape of PBC is shifting from non-specific immunosuppression towards multiple targeted combination therapy based on UDCA. Current evidence supports a central role for therapies that remodel bile acid and PPAR-dependent inflammatory pathways, whereas more direct immunological strategies remain largely investigational (table 1). A key challenge for future trials will be to define clinically meaningful patient subgroups based on the dominant pathogenic circuits operating within the peribiliary immune niche and to align therapeutic mechanisms with these integrated immune-epithelial-metabolic states rather than with any single immune-cell population alone. Such mechanism-based stratification may be necessary to translate immunobiological insights into durable clinical benefit.

Conclusions and challenges

PBC is a prototypical autoimmune cholestatic liver disease in which immune-mediated injury to small intrahepatic bile ducts evolves into chronic cholestasis, fibrosis and eventually cirrhosis in a subset of patients. Although UDCA remains the first-line therapy and recently approved PPAR agonists have expanded treatment options, current approaches still do not fully address the immune mechanisms that initiate and sustain bile duct injury. A central challenge is that PBC is not driven by a single immune-cell population or cytokine pathway, but by a dynamic epithelial-immune network operating within the peribiliary microenvironment.

In this review, we have organised recent advances in PBC immunobiology within an overlapping mechanistic framework encompassing immune initiation, effector amplification and immune-regulatory dysfunction. During immune initiation, stressed and senescent BECs act as antigen reservoirs, immune-sensing platforms and inflammatory amplifiers, while DCs and monocyte-macrophage populations translate epithelial stress into adaptive immune activation. During effector amplification, CD8+ TRM cells, tissue-resident Th1 programmes, Tfh–B-cell interactions, MAIT cells, NK cells and NKT-like populations cooperate to sustain cytotoxic, inflammatory and profibrogenic circuits around damaged bile ducts. Across these processes, impaired Treg stability and dysfunction of regulatory populations, including MDSCs, NK cells and DN T cells, weaken the counter-regulatory mechanisms that normally restrain tissue-destructive immunity. Available evidence increasingly supports the view that PBC is sustained by multicellular immune interactions within a peribiliary niche, with interpatient heterogeneity reflecting differences in the dominant circuits of this network rather than isolated abnormalities in any single immune-cell population.

Despite these advances, several key challenges remain. First, existing animal models recapitulate only partial features of PBC and fail to fully reproduce the complexity of human disease. The dnTGF-βRII mice spontaneously develop AMA-positive intrahepatic cholangitis but lack female predominance and advanced fibrosis.113 ARE-Del-/-mice display female-predominant cholangitis but also lupus-like autoimmunity.114 Non-obese diabetic c3c4 mice reproduce granulomatous biliary inflammation, although lesions preferentially involve the common bile duct.115 2-OA immunisation reliably induces AMA-positive portal inflammation and bile duct injury but rarely progresses to advanced disease.116 Many limitations constrain mechanistic validation and translational drug development.117 Compound models may lead to progress, such as combining a high-fat diet with 2OA treatment in Cyp2c70/Cyp2a12 double knockout mice.118 Future models should better capture the chronic, female-predominant, bile duct-selective and immune-regulatory features of human PBC, rather than reproducing only selected serological or inflammatory phenotypes. Second, substantial immune heterogeneity exists among PBC patients. Differences in immunophenotypes may underlie the inconsistent efficacy observed in clinical trials.119 Third, most mechanistic insights are derived from peripheral blood analyses, whereas high-resolution characterisation of intrahepatic immune dynamics remains limited, particularly with respect to spatial organisation and cell-cell interactions. This is especially important because the pathogenic events most relevant to bile duct injury occur in the portal tract and peribiliary niche, not necessarily in the circulation.

In terms of current treatment options, several directions may accelerate progress in both mechanistic research and clinical translation. Integration of single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomics will enable construction of high-resolution immune atlases of liver diseases, facilitating identification of pathogenic cellular subsets and in situ interaction networks within the peribiliary niche.120 These approaches should be combined with longitudinal sampling, clinical phenotyping and treatment-response data so that cellular signatures can be linked to disease stage, biochemical response, fibrosis progression and symptoms such as pruritus and fatigue. Development of serum-based and bile-based biomarker panels—including cytokine signatures and noncoding RNA profiles—may help identify immunophenotypes and guide clinical decision making. Precision immunotherapy guided by immune phenotyping should be prioritised. For example, patients characterised by Treg dysfunction may benefit from tolerance-restoring strategies such as epigenetic modulation with agents such as decitabine, whereas those with dominant Th1 polarisation may be more responsive to JAK inhibitors. Patients with severe pruritus or elevated IL-31 levels may preferentially benefit from PPAR agonists. Similarly, patients with prominent B-cell activation, myeloid-inflammatory signatures or fibrotic progression may require distinct therapeutic combinations rather than uniform second-line treatment. Furthermore, combination therapeutic strategies integrating anti-inflammatory, antifibrotic and metabolic-modulatory effects should be explored—for instance, UDCA combined with PPAR agonists or JAK inhibitors combined with B-cell-targeted therapies. However, combination therapy should be developed on the basis of mechanistic rationale and biomarker-defined patient selection, rather than empiric escalation. Finally, multicentre real-world studies and well-annotated biobank platforms will be essential for capturing treatment responses across diverse immune subtypes and advancing individualised therapeutic approaches.

In summary, the conceptual framework of PBC immunopathogenesis is moving from a reductionist model centred on single immune-cell populations or soluble mediators toward a systematic view of the peribiliary immune niche. This shift has important therapeutic implications. Future progress in PBC will depend less on identifying yet another immune-cell subset than on determining which pathogenic circuit—epithelial stress, tissue-resident effector immunity, B-cell activation, myeloid inflammation, regulatory failure, cholestatic injury or fibrosis—dominates in each patient and then matching that biology to a rational intervention. Continued integration of tissue-resolved immunology, biomarker discovery and mechanism-based clinical trial design will be essential for translating immune-cell biology into durable, individualised benefit for patients with PBC.
