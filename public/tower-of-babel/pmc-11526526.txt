The WE-RISE™ multi-domain intervention: a feasibility study for the potential reversal of cognitive frailty in Malaysian older persons of lower socioeconomic status
Authors: Resshaya Roobini Murukesu, Suzana Shahar, Ponnusamy Subramaniam, Hanif Farhan Mohd Rasdi, Amrizal Muhammad Nur, Devinder Kaur Ajit Singh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCognitive frailty (CF) is an emerging concern among older persons, particularly within lower socioeconomic statuses (SES) populations. Currently, there is limited evidence on the potential reversibility of CF through multi-domain interventions within this demographic. This study aimed to investigate the effectiveness of the WE-RISE™ multi-domain intervention in addressing the potential reversibility of CF among community-dwelling older persons of lower SES, the intervention effects on multiple health dimensions, and to determine if WE-RISE™ is of low cost.MethodsThis 24-week randomised controlled trial included 55 community-dwelling older persons of lower SES with CF, who were randomised into either the WE-RISE™ intervention group (n = 27), or the control group receiving usual care (n = 28). The WE-RISE™ intervention comprised of 12 weeks of instructor led, community-based intervention, followed by 12 weeks of WE-RISE™@Home. CF status, cognitive and physical function, nutrition, disability, health-related quality of life, exercise self-efficacy and depression were assessed at baseline, 12th and 24th weeks. Changes in CF status was analysed descriptively, while intervention effects were analyzed with split plot ANOVA. The cost of the WE-RISE™ intervention was calculated using activity-based costing.ResultsAt baseline, all participants in both the intervention and control groups (100%) were classified as CF. Following 12 weeks of the WE-RISE™ intervention, 74.1% of participants in the experimental group no longer met the criteria for cognitive frailty, with 63% maintaining this improvement at 24 weeks. In contrast, the CF status of participants in the control group remained largely unchanged, with only 10.7% showing improvement at 12 weeks and falling to 3.6% at 24 weeks. Significant intervention effects were observed for cognition, physical function, body composition, disability, health-related quality of life and self-perceived exercise self-efficacy (p < 0.05) following participation in WE-RISE™. The cost per intervention session was RM4.06 (≈ USD 0.90), and the cost per subject for 48 sessions was RM194.74 (≈ USD 43.39).ConclusionThe WE-RISE™ multi-domain intervention has demonstrated potential in reversing CF among older persons of lower SES, leading to significant improvements across multiple health dimensions at a low cost. WE-RISE™ shows promise to enrich the well-being of ageing societies, contribute to societal health, and ensure equitable access to healthcare, supporting broader implementation by enhancing equitable access to health programs and addressing the needs of older persons, WE-RISE™.Trial registrationThis trial was registered in the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ACTRN12619001055190) on the 29th of July 2019 – retrospectively registered.Keywords: Cognitive frailty, Aging, Multi-domain, Non-pharmacological intervention, Frailty, Dementia, Lower socioeconomic

Background

The global population is experiencing a significant increase in life expectancy and an increase in ageing societies [1]. In Malaysia, the percentage of older persons is expected to increase from 10.7% (≈ 3.5 million people) in 2020 to 15.3% (≈ 5.8 million people) by 2030, positioning Malaysia as an ‘aged nation’ [2]. While extended life expectancy and delayed mortality are encouraging, they present challenges for older individuals, including increased susceptibility to diseases, functional disabilities, and, consequently, a greater risk of dependency attributed to living longer [3].

In parallel, the simultaneous presence of physical frailty and cognitive impairment, a condition termed cognitive frailty (CF), has emerged as a prevalent geriatric syndrome among older persons [4–8]. CF was first defined in 2013 by the International Consensus Group from the International Academy of Nutrition and Aging (IANA) and the International Associations of Gerontology and Geriatrics (IAGG) [4]. CF is a multidimensional syndrome influenced by genetic and environmental factors, such as age-related DNA mutations and exposure to toxins, leading to metabolic dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammation [4, 9]. These processes deplete physiological reserves, increasing vulnerability to chronic diseases and frailty while reducing the body’s resilience to stressors [7]. Additionally, they contribute to cognitive decline, further worsened by physical frailty and linked to risk factors like cardiovascular disease, poor nutrition, hormonal imbalances, unhealthy lifestyle habits, and mental health issues [4].

CF has since gained recognition as an urgent public health priority due to its potential role in increasing dementia incidence [10, 11]. This issue is especially pressing, as approximately 60% of people living with dementia currently reside in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [12, 13]. This includes several economies within Southeast Asia, where health inequality is pervasive and physical frailty poses a significant challenge [14]. Older adults from lower SES are especially vulnerable to health declines, facing an increased risk of health inequalities and unmet healthcare needs, limited affordability, and access to diagnostics and treatments, all of which contribute to poorer prognostic outcomes [15, 16]. In Malaysia, older persons living with lower SES are at greater risk of frailty, cognitive impairment and dependency due to factors such as malnutrition, physical inactivity, poor health practices, and lower education level contributing to higher prevalence of CF within this population [8, 17, 18].

Cognitive impairment and physical frailty have been shown to exhibit a cyclic relationship, where deterioration in one domain exacerbates decline in the other, potentially resulting in a vicious cycle of worsening health in older adults [19]. Despite this observed interaction, the underlying mechanisms and exact directional relationship between physical frailty and cognitive impairment, as outlined in theoretical models of CF, remain poorly understood and require further investigation [19]. Nonetheless, it has been theorized that interventions coalescing the modifiable parameters of CF, namely physical activity, cognitive stimulation, healthy diet, and psychosocial support could potentially mitigate the risk factors of CF and its associated outcomes and may support the prevention and reversibility of this condition [4, 7].

While complete reversal of CF may or may not be achievable, implementing early prevention strategies could potentially delay its progression and extend the period of good health before the onset of chronic conditions or death, a concept known as ‘compression of morbidity’ [7, 20, 21]. Although CF has been emphasised as a novel target to preserve independence among older persons [7], the evidence on interventions specifically targeting the potential reversibility of CF remains limited. Existing interventions have primarily focused on addressing either cognitive impairment or physical frailty as distinct conditions, leaving a significant gap in the availability of integrated multidomain interventions targeting both syndromes as one compounded syndrome. However, the implementation of an integrated multi-domain interventional approach, as suggested by the FINGER trial has unearthed the possibility of addressing the potential reversibility of CF [22]. Additionally, there is a need for accessible, feasible, and scalable interventions for real-world delivery in low-resource settings.

The WE-RISE™ (Warga Emas – Resilient mInd and muScle Exercise) intervention was developed specifically to bridge these gaps. WE-RISE™ integrates physical, cognitive, nutritional, and psychosocial components to target the to address the potential reversibility of CF among older persons from lower SES and to be of low cost [23]. The development of this intervention addresses the multifaceted nature of CF and the identified predictors of CF within the Malaysian population, which include poor physical performance, heightened functional dependency, depression, lack of psychosocial support contributing to poor quality of life, and poor dietary intake [5, 8]. Thus, the objectives of this study were (1) to evaluate the effectiveness of a multidomain intervention targeting the potential reversibility of CF among community-dwelling older persons of lower SES, (2) to determine the intervention effects on the health dimensions of cognitive function, physical function, nutrition, disability, health-related quality of life (HRQoL), exercise self-efficacy, and depression, and finally (3) to determine if the multidomain intervention aimed at the potential reversibility of CF is of low cost.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was a two-armed randomized controlled trial conducted over a period of 24 weeks in Senior Citizen Activity Centers within the Klang Valley, Malaysia known locally as PAWE (Pusat Aktiviti Warga Emas). Participants were recruited via a purposive sampling method where informative invitations were distributed to as many registered members of the participating PAWEs. Recruitment of participants was carried out from January to February of 2019 and this trial was registered in the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry 29/07/2019, ACTRN12619001055190. The WE-RISE™ trial protocol has been previously published and is available online via open access (Murukesu et al., 2020). We used the CONSORT checklist when writing our report [24].

Lower socioeconomic status categorization

For the socioeconomic context, the PAWEs are located within the low-cost housing area under the ‘People Housing Program’ to provide affordable homes for the low-income group [25]. In Malaysia, the B40 category refers to the bottom 40% of households based on income levels. This group represents the lower-income segment of the population, characterized by monthly household incomes that fall below a government-defined threshold. The B40 classification is part of Malaysia’s broader income stratification system, which also includes the M40 (middle 40%) and T20 (top 20%) income groups. This categorization is used to identify households that may require targeted financial support and social assistance, as they are more likely to face economic and healthcare challenges. At the time of this study, households with a monthly income of less than RM4,840 (≈ USD 1,087.15) fall under the B40 category and were considered to be of lower SES [26].

Sample size calculation

The sample size calculated with GPower for each group was 28 to observe a significant difference between two different groups, with a power of 0.90, effect size of 0.2, and significance level (p < 0.05). In this study, an effect size of 0.2 was selected to represent a small yet clinically meaningful effect, frequently utilized in behavioural and health intervention research per Cohen’s established conventions [27]. Considering a 20% dropout rate, as informed by standard practice in similar intervention studies involving older persons where factors such as health deterioration and life events often contribute to participant attrition [28, 29], the total recommended sample size for recruitment was 34 participants per group.

Randomization

In total, 255 older persons were screened for eligibility, with 98 identified with CF. However, only 56 were eligible and successfully recruited in this RCT. Participants provided informed consent and were randomized into experimental and control groups using the Research Randomizer © online randomization software [30] and further pair matched by sex and age to ensure that both groups were equally comparable at baseline. Trained assessors involved in assessing outcome measures were blinded to intervention grouping.

Intervention design and delivery

The experimental group received the WE-RISE™ intervention over 24 weeks and comprised multicomponent exercise, cognitive stimulation, dietary counseling, and psychosocial support. The first 12 weeks consisted of a center-based, instructor-led, WE-RISE™ intervention conducted twice a week for a duration of 90 min per session. Multicomponent exercise and cognitive stimulation activities were carried out by a physiotherapist with a specialized background in geriatric rehabilitation and cognitive stimulation training under a clinical psychologist. The dietary counselling was carried out by a trained clinical dietician. Psychosocial support was provided within the group activity dynamics. This was followed by the home-based WE-RISE™@Home intervention carried out twice a week, with remote supervision via biweekly phone calls for the next 12 weeks. Participants received cognitive and exercise materials and an instructional guidebook for WE-RISE™@Home. Details of the WE-RISE™ intervention content and delivery methods are as reported earlier by Murukesu et al. and summarised in Fig. 1 [23]. Participants in the control group were an active control group who continued with the usual care received from their respective PAWEs. The usual activities conducted at PAWE include arts and craft, cooking activities, health screening every three months, and religious studies. Outcome measures

Participants’ sociodemographic information (sex, age, ethnicity, years of education, education level, marital status, living arrangement, employment status, household income, and smoking status) was collected. Next, the clinical characteristics of the participants were retrieved via self-reported medical history adapted from the Self-Administered Comorbidity Questionnaire [31]. Multimorbidity status was defined as the simultaneous presence of two or more chronic diseases [32].

The feasibility of the WE-RISE™ intervention in terms of retention, adherence, adverse events, intervention fidelity and program challenges. Retention and adherence of the participants was monitored via physical attendance and self-reported calendar and to instructor when contacted via phone calls during the WE-RISE™ center-based and WE-RISE™@Home sessions, respectively. Intervention fidelity of both instructor and participants was observed based on manipulation and compliance to the WE-RISE™ protocol [23].

To evaluate the effectiveness of the intervention, primary and secondary outcome measures were assessed at baseline, the 12th week, and the 24th week (Table 1). These specific time points were chosen based on their widespread use in interventional studies focused on cognitive impairment and frailty [22, 33]. Studies have demonstrated that physiological and cognitive changes are typically observable within a 12-week period, making it a reliable checkpoint for assessing intermediate outcomes [33]. Additionally, the 24-week endpoint allows for the evaluation of the sustainability of the intervention’s benefits. This timeframe is particularly valuable given the transition from an instructor-led to a home-based format in the WE-RISE™ program, enabling the assessment of whether improvements are maintained in a more autonomous setting. Details of the outcome measures can be found in the WE-RISE™ protocol [23].

Cost of WE-RISE™ intervention

Activity-based costing was employed to estimate the cost of the WE-RISE™ intervention whereby the resource consuming intervention elements were identified and translated into costs. For the purpose of this study, the costs incurred were logistics (meals and honorarium provided to participants), material costs (printed materials, stationary, cognitive stimulation activity tools, exercise equipment) and fieldworker costs (wages, transportation and telco charges). These calculations were performed for 27 participants for 48 sessions (twice a week for 24 weeks). The total cost per all sessions was calculated for 1296 sessions (27 participants x 48 sessions).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used for participants’ sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, feasibility outcomes and change in CF status (from baseline, 12th to 24th weeks). The intervention effects on cognitive, physical, functional, nutritional, disability, HRQoL, exercise self-efficacy and depression statuses were analyzed using the split plot ANOVA method. Post hoc analysis was then conducted using Bonferroni correction. Feasibility outcomes

The overall retention rate of the participants in the trial was 98.2% (only one drop-out for the experimental group after the first session). The adherence of the experimental group WE-RISE™ center-based (first 12 weeks) and WE-RISE™@Home intervention (following 12 weeks) was 99.4% and 96.6%, respectively. The total adherence rate for 24 weeks of intervention was 98.0%. Reasons for absence from sessions were unavoidable changes in hospital appointments and being away from home. At the 12-week follow-up, the majority of the experimental group participants 74.0% (n = 20) reported experiencing delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS) of the upper and lower limbs after the first few sessions of the intervention, which eventually ceased by the third week. No other adverse events were reported in this study. Next, no manipulation was executed by the instructor, and participants complied with the original intervention protocol, indicating a high level of intervention fidelity.

Changes in CF status

At baseline, 100% (n = 55) of participants in this trial presented with CF. After 12 weeks of the centre-based WE-RISE™ intervention, the percentage of participants with CF decreased to 25.9% (n = 7) and increased to 37.0% (n = 10) in the 24th week. This indicated that74.1% of participants in the experimental group were no longer cognitively frail, with 63% sustaining this improvement at 24 weeks after the WE-RISE@Home phase. The specific changes of participant states are as follows: At the 12th week, 11.1% (n = 3) of participants reverted to a robust state (absence of both physical frailty and MCI), 51.9% (n = 14) reverted to the physical frailty state (absence of MCI), and 11.1% (n = 3) reverted to the MCI state (absence of physical frailty). By the 24th week, only 7.4% (n = 2) remained in the robust state, while 51.9% (n = 14) continued to be in the physical frailty state, and 3.7% (n = 1) remained in the MCI state.

As for the control group, the percentage of participants with CF after receiving active usual care was 89.3% (n = 25) and 96.4% (n = 27) at the 12th and 24th weeks respectively. This indicated that 10.7% (n = 3) and 3.6% (n = 1) of the control group participants were no longer cognitively frail at 12th and 24th week of assessment. Specifically, at 12th week 7.14% (n = 2) reverted to the physical frailty state, and 3.6% (n = 1) reverted to the MCI state. While at 24th week, 3.6% (n = 1) remained in the MCI state.

Intervention effects

Cognitive function

Significant intervention effects were observed for the MMSE (p < 0.001, η2p = 0.237, power = 0.979), RAVLT Immediate Recall (p < 0.001, η2p= 0.212, power = 0.977), DS (p < 0.001, η2p= 0.193, power = 0.937) and TMT-A (p = 0.002, η2p= 0.122, power = 0.906) cognitive tests (Table 3). The percentage of mean change for MMSE, DS, and TMT-A showed significantly greater improvements in the experimental group than in the control group at the 12th and 24th weeks (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3). The experimental group exhibited a significantly higher percentage of mean change in RAVLT Immediate Recall at the 12th week of intervention than the control group (p < 0.05).

Nutritional status

In terms of body composition, significant intervention effects were observed for body composition in terms of fat percentage (%) (p = 0.014, η2p=0.077, power = 0.751), muscle mass (kg) (p = 0.002, η2p=0.110, power = 0.900), total body water percentage (%) (p = 0.012, η2p=0.081, power = 0.772), bone mass (kg) (p < 0.001, η2p=0.155, power = 0.978), basal metabolic rate (BMR) (kJ) (p < 0.001, η2p=0.135, power = 0.953), and metabolic age (years) (p < 0.001, η2p=0.355, power = 0.986) (Table 5). The experimental groups exhibited significantly more improved fat percentage, muscle mass, and bone mass at the 12th and 24th weeks than the control group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5). Total body water %, BMR kJ, and metabolic age were observed to be significantly more improved in the experimental group than in the control group at the 12th week (p < 0.05). Dietary intake measured with the Dietary History Questionnaire (DHQ) showed significant intervention effects for only pyridoxine (mg/d) (p = 0.024, η2p=0.071, power = 0.684) (Table 6).

Discussion

Our study findings showed that WE-RISE™ multi-domain intervention is feasible to conduct in community settings and thereafter continued as a home-based program among older persons with CF. Furthermore, as far as our knowledge extends, the WE-RISE™ multi-domain intervention has exhibited noteworthy achievements in capitalizing on the transitional characteristics of CF and its potential reversibility. This marks the first instance where such an intervention has effectively addressed the transitional nature of frailty, leading to positive outcomes.

Within the experimental group, 74.1% and 63% of the participants were no longer cognitively frail at the 12th and 24th weeks, respectively, compared to the control group, whereby only 10.7% and 3.6% of the participants exhibited reversal of CF. These findings support the theory that physical frailty and MCI are dynamic conditions, sharing common biological pathways that can respond positively to multi-domain interventions, demonstrating potential for reversal or improvement [34–36]. Our findings are also consistent with evidence showing that multicomponent interventions comprising nutritional, physical and cognitive interventions are more effective in reversing frailty and improving cognition than single-component interventions [37]. In addition, previous studies have shown that non-pharmacological interventions targeting physical frailty and cognitive impairment require a minimum duration of 10 weeks, with sessions conducted at least twice a week, to observe significant improvements in physical and cognitive function among older individuals living in the community [38, 39]. Even so, it is important to acknowledge that changes in physical frailty and cognitive status can occur spontaneously over time [40], which may support the observed, albeit minimal, change in CF status within the control group participants in our study.

WE-RISE™ was intentionally designed and developed as a versatile intervention so that it can be carried out in-group dynamics and individually as a home-based intervention without the requirement of expensive or specific facilities or equipment. Nevertheless, it must be pointed out that the reversal of CF to non-CF status, as well as intervention effects were more prominent following the supervised, instructed, group- and centre-based WE-RISE™ intervention at 12th week follow up. Supervised, group-based exercise programs have been established to be more effective than home-based interventions, especially with respect to physical and mental health among older persons [41, 42]. Moreover, older persons tend to prefer group-based, face-to-face interaction, as it is more encouraging and provides better psychosocial support [43]. This is further supported by a sub-study we conducted after the completion of the 24-week follow-up during the COVID-19 pandemic, which imposed a nationwide lockdown among the participants in this study. We found that the experimental group was more physically active and functionally independent and had better psychosocial well-being throughout social and physical isolation than the control group [44]. It was inferred that the WE-RISE™@Home performed after group- and center-based WE-RISE™ intervention may have empowered as well as enabled older persons to be more self-efficient and independent in carrying out the intervention.

The high retention and adherence rates indicate WE-RISE™ multi-domain intervention’s success in engaging older persons on a routine basis. This is higher than several multicomponent interventions incorporating exercise, cognitive, nutritional, psychological, and medical interventions targeted at frailty, which reported loss to follow-up ranging between 8% and 24% [37, 45–47]. Moreover, no serious adverse events were reported throughout the intervention. Several factors could have influenced these positive results. First, the location of the intervention, which was within the participants’ residential area, hence resolved the need for transportation and the cost it may incur. This implies that older persons are more likely to partake in regular health promotion programs should the circumstances be in their favor. Next, the intervention may have been perceived as more acceptable and approachable, as a codesign principle was undertaken in the development of WE-RISE™, taking the input from the need assessment conducted with the target population into account. Furthermore, the activities were simple to follow and administered in an inclusive, fun, motivating, and positive environment provided by the physiotherapist while also facilitating peer-group support. Intervention instructors have been recognized as a key social determinant in ensuring social cohesion, goal-setting, enjoyment, retention, and adherence to a program that shapes the experience of the participants [48]. Subsequently, WE-RISE™ is fluid in administration or delivery method, as it can be carried out in-group dynamics and can be carried out individually as a home-based intervention without the requirement of expensive or specific facilities or equipment.

To share a challenge faced in this study, there was a hesitancy in participation among males, whereas females were more forthcoming. It has been established that older males are less likely to participate in health promotion programs regardless of the benefits [49]. Another challenge was the participants’ poor access to technology and internet access due to techno-phobia and financial constraints. This limited supervision and communication during the WE- RISE@Home™ program to only phone calls. Digital literacy and financial restriction are obstacles to technology acceptance among older persons that require cost-effective interventions to overcome [50].

In line with the evidence, WE-RISE™ multi-domain intervention has shown encouraging results in addressing the multifaceted nature of CF within 24 weeks of engagement with improvements in cognitive and physical function, positive changes in body composition, improvement in self-perceived disability, HRQoL and exercise self-efficacy. The cognitive improvements in the experimental group, specifically global cognition, immediate recall of verbal memory, attention and working memory, and complex attention, can be attributed to the malleable state of brain function. According to the scaffolding theory of aging and cognition (STAC), interventions involving learning new tasks, social engagement, physical exercise, and cognitive training lead to improvements in cognition levels [51]. Moreover, multicomponent exercise conducted for 12 to 24 weeks has shown cognitive improvements in attention, verbal fluency and global cognition [52], which aligns with our present study findings. Cognitive gains observed in the experimental group, including improvements in immediate recall, attention, and global cognition, can be attributed to the mechanisms of synaptic plasticity and promote neurogenesis, particularly in areas of the brain associated with memory and executive function. However, the positive effects of physical exercise on global cognition, attention and delayed recall were observed only among older persons with MCI, emphasizing the importance of early intervention for cognitive improvement and delaying progression to dementia [53].

Next, the WE-RISE™ cognitive stimulation program focused on a tactile and hands-on approach to improve cognitive function and promote social engagement. The intervention also incorporated psychosocial components, creating a nurturing and friendly environment to encourage socialization and task completion within the group. Interactive and social group-based interventions that combine cognitive and physical activities while enhancing friendships may reduce the risk of cognitive decline and Alzheimer’s disease and potentially preserve or improve cognitive function in older individuals with cognitive deficits [54]. Psychological compensation induced by cognitive stimulation in a social setting has been associated with improvements in communication, social interaction skills and cognitive function [53]. It is also important to recognize that these improvements may be influenced by psychological shifts, fostering adaptive beliefs and behaviors that help compensate for cognitive deficits [51].

Regarding physical function, significant improvement was observed for aerobic endurance, lower body strength, and dynamic balance and mobility. The evidence supporting the improvement of physical function through adequate exercise in older persons with frailty is well established [38]. Furthermore, multicomponent exercises, as employed in the WE-RISE™ intervention, emphasises the importance of incorporating various forms of physical activity as a synergistic approach in enhancing physical function, mitigating the risk of falls, stimulating the release brain-derived neurotrophic factor for neuroplasticity, and promoting overall health [55, 56]. It is noteworthy that exercise in the WE-RISE™ intervention was progressive with increased challenge and was delivered in multiple forms, for example, dance aerobics, strengthening of the cores, upper and lower limbs, multitasking, and dynamic balance. The exercises were also conducted with music and game-like delivery to make exciting rather than routine and to encourage continuous participation.

With regard to nutritional status, there were several significant interaction effects observed for body composition in terms of fat percentage (%), muscle mass (kg), total body water percentage (TBW%), bone mass (kg), basal metabolic rate (BMR) (kJ) and metabolic age (years). The experimental group was observed to have lower fat %, increased muscle mass, increased TBW %, increased BMR and decreased metabolic age following participation in the WE-RISE™ intervention compared to the control group. At present, there is a lack of studies correlating the effects of CF interventions on anthropometric and body composition outcomes. Weight loss that occurs with changes in body composition (decrease in muscle mass and increase in fat mass) has been associated with malnutrition, higher risk of disability, higher mortality rates and higher frailty risk in older persons [57]. With this knowledge, the significant change in body composition over time observed in the experimental group could be interpreted as a positive outcome, as there was a significant reduction in fat % and increase in muscle mass. Furthermore, the findings of the present study imply that the progressive resistance training of the multicomponent exercise intervention may have brought upon osteogenic effects contributing to the significant increase in bone mass in the experimental group [58]. A refreshing finding from our study was that the experimental group participants in our study demonstrated a significant decrease in metabolic age. This indicates a positive impact on biological aging and suggests potential benefits for overall health and the risk of age-related conditions [59, 60].

In the present study, we found a reduction in nutrient intake for both the experimental and control groups at the 12th and 24th week follow-ups compared to baseline, as assessed using the DHQ. At baseline, the nutrient intake of all participants did not meet the recommended nutrient intake for older persons in Malaysia and the decline in nutrient intake raises concerns about possible underreporting or inaccurate reporting, potentially influenced by social desirability bias and a mindset of “less is better” [61]. More critically, the participants are of lower SES in an urban area and may face financial constraints that contribute to insufficient dietary intake patterns. A one-off, group-based dietary counseling may not be sufficient, and additional supplementation or more intensive interventions may be necessary, keeping in mind the affordability and sustainability for older persons of lower SES. However, the multi-domain nature of the intervention, combining exercise, cognitive stimulation, and psychosocial support, likely contributed to positive changes in body composition and improved nutritional profiles.

Favorable significant improvement in disability status, several domains of HRQoL and exercise self-efficacy of the participants of the experimental group compared to the control group following engagement in WE- RISE™ imply that they perceived to have experienced positive changes in their health and state of being. The physical activity and psychosocial components in the WE-RISE™ intervention are the most likely explanation for the significant improvement in self-perceived reduction in disability and improved social functioning. Considering that the WE- RISE™ intervention was designed to foster social interaction and engagement within the participants themselves and with the instructor, the improvements in social and societal domains of the disability assessments are justified. In addition, multidisciplinary exercise-based interventions have improved HRQoL domains, including mental health, perceived physical health, social relationships and leisure activities, among older persons with frailty [62]. Moreover, regular participation in the center-based intervention followed by a guided home-based intervention may have boosted their confidence and reduced apprehension in carrying out the exercises independently due to familiarity with the regime [63]. The center-based WE-RISE™ intervention may have provided a sense of belonging, companionship and feeling important when facilitating or assisting members of their peer group during sessions, as the activities were conducted in a safe space with no competitive elements [64]. Our findings support the observation that socially stimulating interventions improve psychological well-being among older persons and empower them to be active participants within the community [65].

Frailty and cognitive impairment impose significant economic burdens due to their high healthcare costs. However, the cost of the community-based, multi-domain WE-RISE™ intervention was found to be relatively low. With an estimated cost of RM4.06 (≈ USD 0.90) per session comprising exercise, cognitive stimulation, dietary counseling, and psychosocial support, the WE-RISE™ intervention is affordable for older persons from low SES households. Furthermore, the intervention was conducted in PAWEs located within walking distance of participants’ homes, making it easily accessible for the community. Moreover, since the PAWEs are funded by the Malaysian Department of Social Welfare, there were no additional costs for space rentals, administration, or utilities. In comparison, a multi-domain intervention for older individuals at risk of cognitive impairment in Singapore was reported to cost SGD 620 (≈ MYR1929; USD 458) per participant for 48 sessions conducted over 24 weeks. This cost did not include the expenses associated with manpower and equipment required for computerized cognitive training programs, suggesting that the total cost would be even higher [66]. Although the cost of intervention was not specified in other studies, non-pharmacological interventions associated with improvements in frailty and cognitive decline have been considered cost-effective, as they result in reduced healthcare expenditure for older individuals [67, 68].

One notable strength of WE-RISE™ is its progressive design, which allows participants to gradually transition from the center-based phase to the home-based phase. This approach helped participants build confidence and familiarity with the intervention in a structured environment, while the home-based phase empowered them to independently maintain these activities. Moreover, the intervention’s low-cost and accessible format, specifically designed for older adults from lower SES, makes it feasible for implementation in resource-constrained settings, increasing its potential scalability. WE-RISE™ incorporated a codesign principle, to meet the needs and preferences of the target population which may support the high retention and adherence rates observed. Finally, the intervention demonstrated no serious adverse events, highlighting its safety and appropriateness for older adults, even those with CF. While WE-RISE™ was observed to be feasible, effective, and low in cost, the findings of this feasibility study must be considered as a preliminary stepping stone for future interventional studies.

As for study limitations, the participants were urban community-dwelling older persons of lower SES. Hence, the findings may not be generalizable toward rural, community dwelling, noncommunity dwelling, or institutionalized populations. Moreover, the sustained effects of the intervention were not assessed after the completion of the 24-week intervention due to the unexpected COVID-19 pandemic. Another limitation is the slightly reduced sample size; although the target sample size was 56, data analysis included only 55 participants due to one dropout at the start of the intervention. As a recommendation for future research, the effectiveness of WE-RISE™ in addressing the potential reversibility of CF could be benchmarked for longitudinal interventional studies with similar aims, in more diverse populations, over extended follow-up periods and larger sample sizes.

Conclusion

To conclude, the 24-week WE-RISE™ multi-domain intervention may have the potential to address the reversible nature of CF in addition to leading to positive outcomes in cognition, physical function, body composition, disability, HRQoL and self-perceived exercise self-efficacy among older persons with CF from lower SES. To the best of our knowledge, WE-RISE™ is the first of its kind as a nonpharmacological, multi-domain intervention developed specifically for community dwelling older persons with CF from lower SES focused on ‘real-world’ dissemination. The scale-up of the WE-RISE™ multi-domain intervention holds great potential for older individuals, particularly those from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds, in terms of disease prevention and the preservation of functional independence within the community. By aligning with Sustainable Development Goal 3 set forth by the United Nations in 2020, the WE-RISE™ multi-domain intervention can contribute significantly to the overall well-being and quality of life of older persons, ensuring equitable access to healthcare and promoting healthy aging.
