Efficacy of Acupuncture for Insomnia After Chemotherapy in Breast Cancer: A Randomized Sham-Controlled Trial
Authors: Jie Li, Zhen Liu, Lianbo Li, Mingyue Xia, Xinyu Chen, Yuting Mei, Yijia Zhang, Yunfei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposePost-chemotherapy insomnia is a prevalent complication among breast cancer patients. No relevant sham-controlled studies have employed polysomnography to objectively verify acupuncture treatment effects. This single-blind randomized controlled trial assessed the short-term therapeutic effect and safety of acupuncture for insomnia occurring after breast cancer chemotherapy.Patients and MethodsThis single-center study recruited 76 patients diagnosed with stage I–III breast cancer. All participants completed chemotherapy treatment, fulfilled ICSD-3 chronic insomnia diagnostic standards, and conformed to the TCM syndrome of Yin deficiency with excessive fire. Subjects were allocated into acupuncture group and sham acupuncture group at a 1:1 ratio with sealed opaque envelope randomization. The research spanned 9 months, including 5 weeks of biweekly intervention with 10 treatment sessions, alongside follow-up visits conducted at one week and one month after treatment termination. Clinical assessments were carried out at baseline, treatment completion, 1-week and 1-month follow-up. The primary outcome was Insomnia Severity Index score. Secondary outcomes contained PSQI, polysomnographic parameters, BAI, BDI, BFI-C and TCMSRS. All 76 randomized participants (38 cases per group) were analyzed based on intention-to-treat approach via MMRM statistical model.ResultsBaseline clinical features showed no statistical difference between two groups (all p > 0.05). Significant group-time interaction was detected in ISI assessment (F = 2.80, p = 0.047). The acupuncture group exhibited more favorable symptom improvement compared with sham group. Between-group mean differences reached −1.61 (95% CI: −2.71 to −0.50, p = 0.005) after treatment, −1.75 (95% CI: −3.15 to −0.35, p = 0.015) at 1-week follow-up, and −1.77 (95% CI: −3.22 to −0.32, p = 0.017) at 1-month follow-up. Remarkable group-time interaction was also observed in PSQI (F = 4.67, p = 0.005), with acupuncture group presenting better sleep improvement at post-treatment, with mean difference of −1.18 (95% CI: −2.01 to −0.36, p = 0.006). Statistically significant interactive results were obtained in BFI-C (F = 4.07, p = 0.010), and acupuncture brought obvious fatigue relief with mean difference of −1.95 (95% CI: −3.22 to −0.68, p = 0.003). TCMSRS also showed notable interaction effect (F = 5.85, p = 0.001), and intergroup difference stood at −1.47 (95% CI: −2.34 to −0.60, p = 0.001) at 1-month follow-up. BDI scores presented significant group-time interaction (F = 3.00, p = 0.039), yet no evident pairwise difference was found in post-hoc comparison. No interactive variation existed in BAI evaluation (F = 1.69, p = 0.176). Polysomnography results demonstrated distinct advantages of acupuncture intervention. Between-group comparisons showed the treatment group obtained elevated sleep efficiency by 10.54% (95% CI: 0.75 to 20.33, p = 0.036) and increased N3 sleep proportion by 8.26% (95% CI: 3.09 to 13.43, p = 0.003). Wake after sleep onset time decreased by 32.00 minutes (95% CI: −57.50 to −0.50, p = 0.046). No severe adverse events emerged throughout the study.ConclusionAcupuncture improved insomnia severity, sleep quality, PSG-measured sleep architecture (increased sleep efficiency and N3, reduced WASO), fatigue, and TCM pattern symptoms in breast cancer patients with post-chemotherapy insomnia and the Yin deficiency with excessive fire syndrome; however, the between-group difference for the primary outcome did not reach the minimal clinically important difference; therefore, a clinically meaningful advantage over sham cannot be concluded from the current data.Clinical Trial RegistrationChina Clinical Trial Registry, https://www.chictr.org.cn/; registration number: ChiCTR2400082110.

In the breast cancer patient population, insomnia is observed in as many as 69.6% of patients.4 The etiology of cancer-associated insomnia is complex, but treatment for cancer, especially chemotherapy, is a prominent etiologic factor,5,6 with over 26.4% of breast cancer patients receiving chemo experience newly diagnosed clinical insomnia.7 The incidence of persistent insomnia is higher in breast cancer patients receiving chemotherapy (29.1%:3.3%).8 The percentage of breast cancer patients complaining of sleep disorders increases from 11.1% before chemotherapy to 36.1% after chemotherapy.9 Depression, anxiety, cancer-related fatigue and pain associated with breast cancer, and symptoms such as hot flashes that may arise during breast cancer treatment can all lead to insomnia or exacerbate its severity.10 Insomnia itself can have adverse effects on patients’ physical and mental health, leading to fatigue, exacerbating depressive and anxious moods,11 impairing treatment outcomes and quality of life, and even increasing the risk of cancer recurrence.

Currently, there is substantial evidence to support cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) and mind-body training programs as interventions for breast cancer-related insomnia. In fact, CBT-I has been recommended as a first-line treatment option for chronic insomnia.12 However, factors such as high cost, restrictive settings, complex procedures, and poor patient compliance may reduce the efficacy or accessibility of CBT-I.13 Moreover, long-term use of oral sleep medications can cause adverse effects such as dependence and morning drowsiness.11 A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis published by Khosravaninezhad et al analyzed 13 randomized controlled trials covering 806 cancer patients. The research confirmed that non-pharmacological approaches including acupuncture, acupressure, electroacupuncture and auricular acupressure can effectively boost sleep efficacy and total sleep time, and alleviate patient anxiety.14 Therefore, the academic community recommends cognitive behavioral therapy, acupuncture, and other non-pharmacological therapies as preferred treatments for insomnia in cancer patients.15–17

Acupuncture is widely suggested to relieve various symptoms triggered by cancer, such as insomnia, digestive discomfort, physical pain, hot flushes, persistent tiredness, anxious mood and depressive mood.18,19 In a recent systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Zhang and his team in 2026, researchers analyzed data from 27 randomized controlled trials involving 2025 participants. The outcome showed that acupuncture could obviously improve sleep quality in breast cancer patients.20 Most existing sham-controlled trials focusing on acupuncture therapy for insomnia among breast cancer patients mainly adopted subjective sleep assessment tools including ISI and PSQI, alongside actigraphy.21–24 The same 2026 meta-analysis also pointed out that according to GRADE assessment standards, the overall quality of relevant evidence was rated low or extremely low. Such limitations stemmed from inherent bias risks and high inter-study heterogeneity, along with the lack of standard sham intervention settings and objective evaluation indicators.20 This reveals an obvious research deficiency: there is still no sham-controlled trial applying PSG to verify the real therapeutic effect of acupuncture on this specific group of patients. Filling this research blank is of great clinical significance to help breast cancer patients achieve better quality of daily life.

Therefore, we designed this single-blind randomized sham-controlled trial with participant masking. It aims to explore the efficacy and safety of acupuncture for insomnia, mood disorders and fatigue among breast cancer patients who have finished chemotherapy. We recruited 30 subjects and equally divided them into two groups for PSG assessment, and applied polysomnography for objective sleep data collection. This approach makes up for the shortage of objective detection methods in former researches. We put forward the research hypothesis that real acupuncture can bring more obvious improvements in the primary indicator ISI score than sham acupuncture after intervention, and such favorable effects can still be maintained at the follow-up of one week and one month later.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study was designed as a single-blind randomized controlled trial. And sham acupuncture was adopted as the control measure. Qualified subjects were randomly split equally into two groups to receive authentic acupuncture or sham acupuncture intervention.

The research was conducted at the Yueyang Hospital of Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine, Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine. Enrollment work started in July 2023 and ended in January 2024. Patients were treated twice weekly, completing ten sessions over five weeks.

Relevant follow-up evaluations were conducted at one week and one month after treatment termination, and all follow-up work wrapped up in March 2024. The research scheme obtained ethical clearance from the institutional ethics committee (Approval No.: 2023–048), and the trial was registered on the China Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400082110). Every experimental procedure complied with the reporting specifications of CONSORT25 and STRICTA26 rigorously.

Participants

Participants were referred by physicians from the Acupuncture Department, Breast Surgery Department, and Oncology Department of the Yueyang Hospital of Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine, Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine. To facilitate recruitment, we conducted multi-channel promotion and recruitment through methods such as posting recruitment advertisements, online presentations, and offline free clinics. After the study process was fully explained, all participants signed informed consent forms. Assessment and intervention measures were implemented at the hospital.

To confirm participants conformed to the ICSD-3 diagnostic standards, two attending physicians with over five years of clinical experience in sleep disorders performed professional evaluations strictly based on relevant criteria. Apart from face-to-face interviews, all eligible candidates needed to keep a seven-day sleep diary in advance, so as to collect authentic data reflecting their actual sleep conditions.

Inclusion Criteria

(1) Women aged≥18 years and≤75 years; (2) Participants diagnosed with primary breast malignant tumors (ICD-10: C50), with clinical staging at stages I–III;27(3) Completion of the standard course of chemotherapy ≥2 weeks prior to enrollment; (4) Insomnia newly onset or significantly exacerbated after chemotherapy, and meeting the (ICSD-3) criteria for chronic insomnia (ICD-10: G47.01).28 (5) Participants with a Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) syndrome differentiation of Yin deficiency with excessive fire; (6) Participants with an Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) score > 7; (7) Participants taking sleep-related medications (barbiturates, chloral hydrate, trichloromethane, hydroxyzine, benzodiazepine sedative-hypnotics, zolpidem, zaleplon, or zopiclone) must not alter the dosage or switch medications during the study period. If adjustments to medications or sleep aids are necessary during the study, the investigator must be informed and detailed records kept; (8) Voluntarily participate in this study and sign the informed consent form.

TCM Syndrome Differentiation Protocol

All potential participants were diagnosed with Yin deficiency with excessive fire syndrome through a standardized syndrome differentiation process conducted by two senior TCM practitioners (with more than 10 years of clinical experience in oncology acupuncture). The two practitioners independently performed syndrome differentiation based on the “Chinese Medical Diagnosis” and the diagnostic criteria for insomnia in “Chinese Medical Diagnosis and Treatment Standards for Diseases and Syndromes”. If there was a disagreement in the syndrome differentiation results, a third senior TCM expert (with more than 15 years of clinical experience) was invited to conduct a final arbitral diagnosis, and the consistent results were used as the basis for inclusion.

Exclusion Criteria

(1) Presence of concurrent malignant tumors; (2) Severe cardiovascular, hepatic, renal, hematological, or respiratory system diseases; (3) Patients diagnosed with mental disorders according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM); (4) Other sleep disorders, including sleep apnea, restless legs syndrome, and shift work sleep disorder. Participants with a sleep apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) >15 and periodic limb movements during sleep (PLMS) >10 on polysomnography (PSG) were excluded; (5) Pregnant or breastfeeding women; (6) Infectious diseases; (7) Participants with breast skin ulcers that prevent acupuncture treatment; (8) Participants with a fear of needles or who faint at the sight of needles; (9) Participants who have participated in other studies within the past three months; (10) Participants with severe visual, auditory, or language impairments; or those with unhealthy lifestyle habits such as alcohol abuse or drug misuse.

Definition of eligible comorbidities: Participants with stable and well-managed chronic diseases such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes and mild osteoarthritis were permitted to enroll in this study.

Randomization, Blinding, and Allocation Concealment

Eligible participants were randomly assigned to the acupuncture group or sham acupuncture group at a 1:1 ratio, with random numbers generated by IBM SPSS 27.0. Sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes were used to ensure allocation concealment. The random sequence was produced by a researcher who took no part in participant recruitment and outcome evaluation. After participants met all inclusion criteria and finished baseline assessments, group allocation was determined by opening envelopes in numerical order. The acupuncturist confirmed the grouping information by opening the envelope before conducting treatment.

Participants were kept blind to their group assignment during the whole study. Outcome evaluators and data analysts remained unaware of group division and worked independently, without engaging in any intervention delivery.

Intervention

Both groups continued to receive standard sleep hygiene education to ensure ethical care and to isolate the specific effect of acupuncture, as both groups received identical concurrent education.

Participants continued to receive usual care and symptom management during the study period. Subjects completed a 7-day sleep diary for screening before PSG monitoring was scheduled. Subjects were required to complete all assessment scales as baseline data within 3 days prior to the start of treatment, and then entered a 5-week treatment period (2 sessions per week, 10 sessions in total). All scales and clinical information were collected from subjects again on the day after the end of treatment. Subjects were followed up at 7th days (1-week follow-up) and the 30th days (1-month follow-up) after the end of the 5-week (10-session) intervention. All assessment scales were completed at each follow-up time point.

Acupuncture Treatment Protocol

Select the following acupoints for acupuncture: Baihui (DU20), Yintang (GV29), Anmian (EX-HN1), Xinshu (BL15), Juque (CV14); Shenshu (BL23), Jingmen (GB25); Ganshu (BL18), Qimen (LR14). Acupoint localization was referred to the 2006 National Standard of the People’s Republic of China “Acupoint Names and Localization” (GB/T12346-2006).29 The locations of all acupoints and sham acupoints are shown in Figure 1 (head) and Figure 2 (trunk). Acupuncture was performed with participants in a sitting position. Bilateral acupoints were needled on both sides at each session. Midline acupoints were needled at every session. Needling operation: after the subjects were routinely sterilized using 75% medical alcohol, 0.25×40 mm (Andi, Guizhou, China) millimeter needles were used for needle insertion. Baihui and Yintang were punctured 1.5–2.0 cun flatly (cun as a unit of acupoint localization in Chinese medicine, 1 cun is 25 millimeters); Anmian was punctured 1–1.5 straight cun. Deqi (needle sensation) was obtained after insertion, and then flat reinforcing-reducing manipulation was applied. Xinshu, Shenshu, and Ganshu were inserted obliquely to a depth of 0.5–0.8 cun. Juque was inserted obliquely upward 0.5–1 cun (not deeply), and Jingmen obliquely 0.5–0.8 cun. Ximen was inserted obliquely 0.5–0.8 cun. For Xinshu, Ganshu, Juque, and Ximen were treated with the twirling reducing maneuver (heavy backward thumb rotation). Shenshu and Jingmen were treated with the twirling reinforcing maneuver (heavy forward thumb rotation). The needles were left in all points for 20 minutes, and the needle holes were pressed with sterilized cotton balls to prevent bleeding after the needles were activated. Sham Acupuncture Treatment Protocol

The sham acupuncture procedure followed the same protocol. The number and duration of treatments in the sham acupuncture group were consistent with those in the acupuncture group. The protocol employed the use of a Streitberger placebo needle (a blunt-tipped non-invasive placebo control. This device is now a widely reported and recognized means of sham acupuncture, which consists of a blunt-tipped needle that retracts upon skin contact, producing a tactile sensation similar to needle insertion without penetrating the skin,30 and selected non-acupoint points were administered. Sham acupuncture points were located 0.5–1 cun adjacent to the real acupoints, avoiding the meridians of the real acupoints. After routine sterilization with 75% medical alcohol, the sham device was applied. The needle was retained for 20 minutes and then removed. Treatment was given twice a week for 10 consecutive sessions.

Acupuncture treatments were administered by registered Chinese medicine practitioners (CMPs) with at least 5 years of clinical experience. They received uniform training on the treatment protocol before the study and were qualified to perform the procedures.

The PSG monitoring window was controlled to be performed within the first 3 days of the first acupuncture treatment and no more than 2 days after completion of the 10 acupuncture sessions. All PSG data interpretation and questionnaire assessment were done by an experienced PSG technician who was not aware of the subgroups.

Sample Size

According to our team’s previous research (published in the journal Sleep),31 the mean Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) score in the control group was 21.73±4.14. This study hypothesized that acupuncture using the “Back-Shu and Front-Mu acupoint combination” would reduce the ISI score by an additional 4.60 points relative to sham acupuncture in breast cancer patients with post-chemotherapy insomnia. The threshold for superiority testing was set at 1.4, with α set at 0.025 for a one-sided test, and 1-β = 0.85. Considering a 20% dropout rate, each group required 38 participants, totaling 76 participants across both groups.

Measure

Primary Outcome Measure

The primary outcome was the change in ISI32 score from baseline to post-treatment (the day after the final session of the 5-week intervention). The secondary endpoints were the ISI scores at 1-week follow-up (7 days after the end of treatment) and 1-month follow-up (30 days after the end of treatment). All time points were uniformly defined and recorded to ensure the consistency of outcome assessment.

Secondary Outcome Measure

Secondary outcome measures included sleep-related measures, depression and anxiety status, fatigue levels, and traditional Chinese medicine symptom scores.

(1) Sleep measurement: Using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI).33

(2) Polysomnography (PSG): PSG34 was used as the objective sleep assessment indicator in this study to evaluate the objective improvement in sleep quality before and after acupuncture treatment. The PSG device used in this study (NIHON KOHDEN, Japan) consists of multiple quantitative parameters, and the following parameters were selected to assess sleep quality: sleep latency (SOL), wake after sleep onset (WASO), sleep efficiency (SE), arousal, percentage of rapid eye movement (REM) sleep (REM%), N1 stage (N1%), N2 stage (N2%), and N3 stage (N3%). Sleep patterns were monitored for approximately 8 hours during the baseline period and post-treatment period (starting from the usual bedtime). Consecutive 2-night sleep monitoring was performed before and after treatment to exclude the “first-night effect.35 Participants with a respiratory apnea-hypopnea index (RAHI) >15 and an arousal index (AI) >10 in the PSG results were excluded. Sleep stages and parameters were manually interpreted according to the “Manual for the Interpretation of Sleep and Related Events: Rules, Terminology, and Technical Specifications (Version 2.3)” established by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine.

(3) Depression/anxiety states were assessed using the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI)36 and the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI).37

(4) Daytime status assessment: Brief Fatigue Inventory-Constructs (BFI-C).38

(5) The Traditional Chinese Medicine Syndrome Rating Scale (TCMSRS) is used to assess post-surgical insomnia in breast cancer patients with a pattern of yin deficiency and fire excess. This scale is based on the content related to yin deficiency with excessive fire syndrome in “Chinese Medical Diagnosis”, and references the diagnostic criteria for “insomnia” in the “Chinese Medical Diagnosis and Treatment Standards for Diseases and Syndromes” and the “Chinese Medical Diagnosis and Treatment Protocols for 22 Specialties and 95 Diseases” issued by the National Administration of Traditional Chinese Medicine. The scale consists of 11 Chinese medicine symptom syndromes, including 3 primary symptoms and 8 secondary symptoms, with the secondary symptoms including information on tongue coating and pulse patterns. Each item is scored on a four-point scale, ranging from mild to severe: “none” scores 0 points, “rarely occurs” scores 1 point, “occurs occasionally” scores 2 points, and “occurs frequently” scores 3 points. The higher the total score, the more severe the symptoms of yin deficiency with excessive fire.

The above scales were completed and assessed by the subjects during the assessment and screening period (before treatment), at the end of treatment, at 1 week of follow-up, and at 1 month of follow-up. The complete set of questionnaires (ISI, PSQI, BAI, BDI-II, BFI-C, and TCMSRS) required approximately 25–30 minutes to complete at each assessment time point.

Combination Drug Use

All enrolled participants either consistently and regularly use sedative hypnotic drugs, or have never taken such drugs before screening. Throughout the intervention and follow-up phases, participants strictly followed the original hypnotic medication regimen. Participants were not allowed to adjust the dosage or change the medication without authorization, and the daily medication situation has been recorded. If changing medication, it is necessary to report to the researcher.

During the trial period, it is prohibited to take any sedative dietary supplements. Medications prescribed for chronic diseases that do not affect the central nervous system, including hypoglycemic drugs, antihypertensive drugs, and lipid-lowering drugs, can still be used sustainably. All medication details are truthfully recorded in the case report form.

Adverse Events

Acupuncturists documented adverse events separately during each treatment session. Serious adverse events were defined as (1) death, (2) life-threatening conditions, (3) disability or permanent impairment, (4) need for medical intervention. The remaining adverse events were considered non-serious.

Criteria for determining that an adverse event was related to the intervention included: previous reports of the particular adverse event related to the intervention; a clear temporal association with the intervention and/or the presence of a physiologically plausible causal relationship; recurrence in the intervention group; and self-report by the patient. If a patient withdrew from the study due to an adverse event, this was recorded.

Data Management

Data were collected at four time points (baseline, post-treatment, 1-week follow-up, and 1-month follow-up), including questionnaire-based outcomes (ISI, PSQI, BAI, BDI, BFI-C, TCMSRS) and polysomnography (PSG) data before and after the intervention. Researchers received standardized training before data collection, and dedicated staff entered the data. Participant privacy was strictly protected.

Statistical Analysis Methods

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS 27.0. Continuous data that conforms to a normal distribution is represented as mean±standard deviation (SD), and independent sample t-test is used to examine inter group differences. Statistical analysis was conducted on data collected at four different time points (baseline, post-treatment, 1-week follow-up, and 1-month follow-up) using a repeated measures mixed effects model (MMRM). In this model, group allocation (real acupuncture and sham acupuncture), detection time and interaction between groups and time are set as fixed effects, and baseline scores are included in covariates. Apply an unstructured covariance matrix and define the unique ID of each participant as the subject variable. Missing data processing is based on the inherent assumption of random missing data in the MMRM method.

All statistical evaluations followed the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle. A total of 76 randomized participants were analyzed strictly in accordance with their initial group allocation, without any exclusion. Estimated marginal means and corresponding standard errors (SE) were adopted for result presentation. The least significant difference (LSD) method was utilized for post-hoc pairwise comparative analysis.

For continuous variables that failed to meet normal distribution criteria, nonparametric statistical tests were applied. Chi-square test and Mann–Whitney U-test were separately employed for the comparison of categorical indicators. For the polysomnography (PSG) subsample data, paired t-tests or Wilcoxon tests were used to assess intra-group changes before and after intervention, while independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U-tests were adopted for inter-group comparisons.

We calculated 95% confidence intervals and corresponding effect sizes for all outcome measures. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was regarded as the threshold for statistically significant differences. Baseline consistency between the two groups was verified prior to formal analysis, whereby independent samples t-tests were used for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables (Table 1). Random grouping design and baseline indicator adjustment in the MMRM model were jointly applied to control potential confounding factors in this trial.

Results

Participants Characteristics

A total of 76 eligible participants were randomly assigned to either the acupuncture group (n = 38) or the sham acupuncture group (n = 38). All 76 participants were included in the intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis using the MMRM model. Six participants (three in each group) discontinued the intervention prematurely due to personal reasons (acupuncture group: two moved out of Shanghai, one developed bone metastasis; sham acupuncture group: one moved out of Shanghai, two were preparing for pregnancy). The dropout rate was 7.89% (6/76), and 70 participants (92.1%) completed the full 5-week intervention and all follow-up assessments (Figure 3). Efficacy

Participant-Reported Sleep Assessments

As shown in Table 2, Figures 4 and 5, adjusting for MMRM with baseline values as covariates showed that all outcome measures improved significantly from baseline to post-treatment in both groups (p < 0.05). Adverse Events

Serious adverse events (SAEs) in the present trial were defined strictly in line with standard clinical trial specifications, covering six categories: patient death, life-threatening medical conditions, permanent or severe disability and functional impairment, new-onset hospitalization or extended hospital stay, congenital malformations or birth defects, and other clinically significant severe medical events requiring medical intervention.

All adverse events identified throughout the trial were closely monitored and followed up until full symptom resolution or stable physical conditions were achieved. In the acupuncture group, six cases of grade 1 pinpoint bleeding were observed during treatment. All bleeding symptoms ceased within one minute after acupuncture manipulation, and no recurrent abnormalities were detected during the one-month follow-up period. Additionally, 12 cases of mild acupuncture-related sensory reactions occurred in this group, all of which subsided completely during the treatment session, with no residual discomfort reported at any follow-up time point.

By contrast, no adverse events were documented in the sham acupuncture group throughout the entire intervention and follow-up phases. Furthermore, vital signs including blood pressure, heart rate and body temperature of all enrolled participants remained steady and within the normal reference range during the whole study period.

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the efficacy, safety, and feasibility of acupuncture for post-chemotherapy insomnia in patients with breast cancer. Sleep disturbances related to chemotherapy are commonly observed in cancer populations.39,40 Acupuncture is widely recognized as a safe and effective treatment for insomnia.41,42 There are currently no clinical trial reports based on polysomnography (PSG) regarding the efficacy and safety of acupuncture for post-chemotherapy insomnia in breast cancer patients.20 To our knowledge, this is the first study to use both questionnaire-based and PSG-based assessments, with sham acupuncture as control, to evaluate the short-term efficacy of acupuncture for post-chemotherapy insomnia in breast cancer patients.

Treatment is often individualized based on symptom patterns in TCM. For this trial, we standardized the acupoint protocol to ensure consistency. We selected a fixed acupoint protocol based on the yin deficiency and excessive fire symptoms: Baihui (DU20), Yintang (GV29), Anmian (EX-HN1), and the back-shu and front-mu pairings (BL15/CV14, BL23/GB25, BL18/LR14), which is a classic acupoint combination in TCM. By restricting enrollment to this specific syndrome, we reduced clinical heterogeneity and increased the likelihood of detecting a true treatment effect. The TCM pattern scale also improved after treatment in both groups, with greater improvement in the acupuncture group than the sham group, indicating that the acupoint selection protocol aligned with the TCM pattern of the disease.

Age was balanced between groups, and most participants were perimenopausal. And there were no significant differences between the two groups of subjects in terms of molecular typing of breast cancer, presence of underlying diseases, and allergies. However, due to the small sample size, a potential link between these factors and insomnia in breast cancer cannot be decisively stated.

The latest Cochrane systematic review, which focusing on the treatment of insomnia in cancer patients, has identified the minimum clinically significant difference (MCID) in the ISI: 4.7 point decrease indicates mild clinical improvement and 8.4 point decrease indicates moderate clinical improvement.43 As shown in the MMRM analysis results in Table 2, the between-group difference was 1.61 points (95% CI: −2.71 to −0.50, p = 0.005), which was statistically significant but falls below the 4.7-point MCID threshold. This result indicates that, compared with the sham acupuncture control group, the statistically significant advantage of acupuncture does not have clinical meaningfulness. Both groups showed substantial within-group improvements (acupuncture: −4.90 points; sham: −4.29 points), suggesting a notable placebo response. This finding highlights the importance of using sham controls in acupuncture trials to isolate the specific treatment effect.

Compared with the previous similar sham acupuncture control study results on breast cancer related insomnia after chemotherapy, the study results of Zhang et al21 showed that there was no significant difference in ISI between groups (average difference: −0.4, 95% CI: −1.8 to 1.1, p = 0.609). This discrepancy may be due to differences in the study populations: Zhang et al21 recruited patients who actively received chemotherapy, while our study included patients who completed chemotherapy for at least two weeks, which may potentially reduce acute chemotherapy related sleep disturbances. In addition, studies by Li et al22 and D’Alesandro et al23 also reported improvements in sleep outcomes, but neither of them used polysomnography (PSG) as an objective indicator.

The improvements in objective sleep parameters in this study were mainly as follows: after 10 sessions, the acupuncture group showed increased sleep efficiency (SE) and N3%, reduced wakefulness after sleep onset (WASO), and better outcomes than the sham acupuncture group. Previous evidence also suggests that acupuncture improves objective sleep parameters, including total sleep time and sleep efficiency.18 N3 (slow-wave sleep) is the deep sleep phase of NREM sleep and sleep has been shown to play a role in physical recovery and the clearance of daytime metabolic byproducts.44 In breast cancer patients receiving chemotherapy, these restorative processes are often impaired. Indeed, over 60% of breast cancer patients report clinically significant fatigue and sleep problems after chemotherapy.45 Whether the observed improvements in N3 and WASO contributed to the concomitant improvements in fatigue, mood, and insomnia symptoms cannot be determined from the current data, as this study was not designed to test mediation effects. Nevertheless, these findings generate the hypothesis that acupuncture-induced improvements in sleep architecture may be one pathway through which patients experience broader clinical benefits. Due to the small sample size of the PSG sub-study, these results should be interpreted as exploratory. Future studies with larger sample sizes and formal mediation analyses are needed to verify these findings and explore the underlying mechanisms.

Previous clinical trials have confirmed the efficacy of true acupuncture in alleviating anxiety and depression.46,47 Our MMRM analysis showed that both acupuncture and sham acupuncture significantly reduced participants’ BAI anxiety scores, with no significant group-by-time interaction (F = 1.69, p = 0.176) over time, this result indicates that the anti anxiety effects of these two interventions were comparable. For depression symptoms measured by BDI, a significant group-by-time interaction was observed (F = 3.00, p = 0.039), which indicates that the temporal changes in depression severity were different between groups. However, post hoc testing showed no significant inter group differences at any individual time point, indicating that the treatment effect of acupuncture on depressive symptoms unfolded gradually over time, rather than producing an immediate, detectable difference at a single assessment.

The BFI-C results showed that acupuncture significantly improved cancer-related fatigue (mean difference:-1.95, 95% CI: −3.22 to −0.68, p = 0.003), whereas no significant between-group differences were observed for BAI or BDI. One possible explanation is the strong placebo response in the sham acupuncture group for anxiety and depression (both groups showed substantial within-group improvements), which may have diminished between-group differences. Whether this differential effect reflects true differences in treatment mechanisms or statistical power requires further study.

The confidence intervals for some secondary outcome measures are relatively wide, such as the BAI score ranging from −1.44 to 0.65, indicating limited estimation accuracy. This statistical uncertainty is likely due to the moderate sample size of this experiment (n = 76). Considering the exploratory nature of these secondary analyses, the relevant findings should be interpreted with caution rather than being too general. No serious adverse events or clinically significant abnormalities occurred in either group during the study period. Consistent with previous studies,47,48 our research results further support the good safety and tolerance of acupuncture intervention. In future research, it is necessary to conduct larger scale clinical trials to generate more robust and accurate estimates of effects and confirm the preliminary findings of current studies.

Limitation

This study has several inherent limitations. No standardized tools like the James Blinding Index,49 were used to verify blinding effect. Therefore, the lack of a quantitative assessment of blinding remains a shortcoming, despite our use of Streitberger sham needles and blinded evaluators.

Polysomnography records were only gathered from 30 voluntary participants, that may bring potential selection bias. These objective sleep data can merely serve as exploratory references.

Participants continued their prescribed sleep medications at stable doses through the trial. Baseline medication status was comparable between groups. Only five cases adjustmented dosage (two in the acupuncture group, three in the sham group). Given the small number of cases and that dose adjustments were minor, no further statistical analysis was performed. This represents a potential limitation of our study, as the potential impact of these adjustments on the final outcomes cannot be fully ruled out.

As a single-center trial with 76 enrolled cases, the modest sample size restricts the external validity of outcomes and results in wide confidence intervals for certain secondary indicators.

Moreover, recruitment was confined to patients presenting yin deficiency and fire excess syndrome. Our conclusions cannot be extended to breast cancer patients with other TCM syndrome types.

Conclusion

In conclusion, acupuncture improved insomnia severity, sleep quality, PSG-measured sleep architecture (increased sleep efficiency and N3, reduced WASO), fatigue, and TCM pattern symptoms in breast cancer patients with post-chemotherapy insomnia and the Yin deficiency with excessive fire pattern, however, the between-group difference for the primary outcome did not reach the minimal clinically important difference; therefore, a clinically meaningful advantage over sham cannot be concluded from the current data.
