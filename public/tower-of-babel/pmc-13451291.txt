Push until it breaks: engineering is stronger when we know its limits
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Engineering and applied science papers too often stop at what succeeds. We ask authors to push their assumptions, methods, models, or devices until they falter, and to report where and why they break. Disclosed limitations are a mark of rigour, not a liability at review: they show the field where the next advance will have to come from.

At Nature Communications, we believe the most valuable engineering papers are those that show not only what works, but also what doesn’t. This builds on our long-standing interest in applied science1, and reflects our growing commitment to engineering across the Nature Portfolio, from the launch of dedicated journals to Nature’s own call to engineers2. Much has been written, including within our portfolio, on what makes a great engineering paper3. Solid results, robust methods, and reproducibility remain the non-negotiable cornerstones of high-quality research. But too often, articles stop at their achievements, staying silent about where they fail. Omitting failure misses what is perhaps the most intellectually honest and impactful dimension of engineering research: its limitations.

Don’t just demonstrate that your system performs under expected conditions. Test its boundaries! Push your assumptions, methods, models, or devices until they falter, and tell us where and why. We know this is not a trivial ask. Testing to failure takes real time and resources. But the points at which your work begins to fail are not weaknesses to be ignored or hidden; they are vital insights into how your contribution can be responsibly used, improved, and built upon in future research. Identifying and articulating limitations is a mark of rigour. By openly stating where your work breaks, you help define the frontier of engineering research, rather than merely contributing to it.

Be selective, though. Report the failures that bear on your central claims, and place peripheral analyses in the Supplementary Information, where they remain accessible without diluting your main message. A good way to anticipate which limitations matter most to the community is to engage with our transparent peer review files that accompany every published research article submitted to us since November 20224. These records are not just critiques; they are roadmaps. Pay attention to what reviewers highlight as missing or incomplete in similar work. Addressing those challenges head-on, before they are raised, signals maturity and command of your field.

There are practical payoffs, too. Clearly stated limitations define the scope of your contribution for editors and reviewers, allowing us to understand precisely what your work achieves and where the next advances may lie. You might worry that acknowledging them invites rejection, but disclosed limitations, on their own, are not held against a paper in our editorial assessment, and we ask reviewers to take the same view. Our guidance on applied science and engineering research sets out how we assess such work. Beyond the review process, engineering research is cumulative: sharing limitations invites the broader community to engage, replicate, and build upon your work, increasing its reach and potential impact.

“Disclosed limitations, on their own, are not held against a paper in our editorial assessment, and we ask reviewers to take the same view.”

So, we urge you: break your paper. Explore its edge cases, test its assumptions, and share what doesn’t work. In engineering, knowing where things fail is just as important as knowing how they succeed.
