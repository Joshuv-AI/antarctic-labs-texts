Combined Medial Unicompartmental Knee Arthroplasty and Revision Allograft Anterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction
Authors: Ryan J. Froom, Alexi J. Panos, Molly E. Kessenich, Nate Dickinson, Mikalyn T. DeFoor, Nathan Cafferky, Matthew T. Provencher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCombined medial unicompartmental knee arthroplasty and revision anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction with an Achilles tendon allograft offers a promising solution for young, active patients with isolated medial compartment osteoarthritis and a failed prior anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction. This technique restores knee stability and function through a joint‐preserving approach. Precise preoperative planning, anatomic tunnel placement and meticulous component alignment are critical steps for successful outcomes. This technique article outlines a single stage, joint‐preserving procedure for isolated, symptomatic medial compartment osteoarthritis with knee instability.

This technical note shows a single stage combined medial unicompartmental knee arthroplasty with revision anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) allograft reconstruction. A 41‐year‐old female presented with progressive medial knee pain and instability. Clinical examination revealed diffuse medial joint line tenderness and a 2B Lachman. Preoperative imaging revealed neutral coronal alignment and diffuse grade 4 arthritic changes isolated to the medial compartment and a complete rupture of the prior ACL graft without significant tunnel osteolysis. The tibial validation guide is used to confirm the appropriate cut and alignment. After the proximal tibia is sized, the medial extension gap is manually checked along with the overall alignment. Before trialing, the flexion and extension gaps are evaluated with the sizing spacing block. The medial meniscus is then resected along with any posterior osteophytes. Maintaining the knee in 90° of flexion, the appropriately sized distal femur cutting guide is attached, ensuring no overhang before the posterior and posterior chamfer cuts are made. After trialing, the bone is thoroughly irrigated, dried and drilled for final implantation and cement interdigitation. Alignment is then again verified with an external alignment drop rod. Knee range of motion is assessed along with varus and valgus stress to assess the flexion and extension gaps. The final femur and tibial implants are cemented into place with surrounding cement debris removed. A trial polyethylene liner is inserted, and after the cement hardens, final alignment, range of motion, stability, and patellar tracking are again assessed. The final highly cross‐linked medial fixed‐bearing polyethylene is inserted, and the joint is reduced. Knee arthroscopy is performed, beginning with an intra‐articular debridement. A cannulated, headed reamer is used to drill the tibial tunnel in an outside‐in fashion, as shown here from the anterolateral viewing portal on the left knee in supine position. A passing suture is again placed. The femoral socket and tibial tunnel are inspected for appropriate position. Isolated medial compartment osteoarthritis with concomitant anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) deficiency presents a complex clinical challenge, particularly in young, active patients seeking joint preservation surgery. Historically, ACL deficiency has been considered a contraindication for unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) due to elevated risks of tibial component loosening and implant failure. 1 However, increasing literature supports combined UKA with ACL reconstruction (ACLR) as a viable surgical treatment, offering a less invasive approach with preservation of native knee anatomy and kinematics compared to total knee arthroplasty (Table 1). 2 , 3 , 4 This technical note describes our surgical technique for combined medial UKA and revision ACLR using an Achilles tendon allograft, addressing both isolated medial compartment osteoarthritis and recurrent knee instability in a single‐stage procedure.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Positioning and Exposure

Critical evaluation of preoperative advanced imaging, to include full‐length standing alignment films, is critical in assessing potential factors contributing to a prior failed ACL graft and to confirm isolated arthritic changes (Figure 1 and 2). The patient is positioned supine on a regular operating room bed, and an examination under anesthesia is performed. A commercial knee holder is used throughout the case to assist with varying degrees of knee flexion (Figure 3). A longitudinal incision over the anterior knee is made with a standard medial parapatellar approach. A medial soft tissue release is performed to the mid‐coronal plane of the proximal tibial plateau, and the retropatellar fat pad is resected. The lateral and patellofemoral compartments are inspected to ensure preserved articular surfaces, confirming that the patient is a candidate to proceed with UKA.

Proximal Medial Tibial Plateau and Distal Medial Femoral Resection

The tibial sensor is placed with the knee in 90° of flexion, followed by attachment of the tibial cutting guide to the anterior proximal tibia. Care is taken to ensure the cutting guide is perpendicular to the mechanical axis and positioned to restore the native tibial slope. After securing the cutting guide, the proximal tibial cut is performed to minimize the posterior tibial slope, and the sagittal cut is aligned along the medial aspect of the medial tibial tubercle, along the medial ACL fibers. The soft tissue structures of the ACL and medial collateral ligament are protected. After the medial tibial plateau resection is performed, the tibial validation guide is used to confirm the appropriate cut and alignment. The proximal tibia is then sized, and the medial extension gap is manually checked along with the overall alignment.

Component Trialing and Final UKA Implantation

Maintaining the knee in 90° of flexion, the femur is sized, and the appropriately sized distal femur cutting guide is attached, ensuring no overhang before the posterior chamfer and posterior cuts are made. After the femoral cutting guide is removed, the medial meniscus is resected along with any posterior osteophytes. Before trial components are inserted, the flexion and extension gaps are evaluated with a sizing spacing block. With the knee in full extension, alignment is assessed and verified with an external alignment drop rod. The knee range of motion is then assessed, as well as a complete varus and valgus stress examination to assess the flexion and extension gaps. After trialing, the bone is thoroughly irrigated with pulse lavage, dried, and drilled for final implantation and cement interdigitation.

The final femur and tibial implants (Persona Partial Knee System; Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN) are cemented into place and cement debris removed. A trial polyethylene liner is inserted, and after the cement hardens, the alignment, range of motion, stability, and patellar tracking are again assessed. The final highly cross‐linked medial fixed‐bearing polyethylene (Persona Partial Knee System; Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN) is inserted, and the joint is reduced. The surgical wound is again irrigated and the retinacular layer is closed in a standard fashion.

Diagnostic Knee Arthroscopy, Notch Debridement, and Socket/Tunnel Drilling

Diagnostic knee arthroscopy is performed with standard anteromedial and anterolateral portals. After debridement of the remnant ACL graft, the notch and lateral femoral condyle are debrided for over‐the‐top visualization. Bone overgrowth within the notch is resected with a 4.0 mm bone cutting shaver (Bone Cutter; Arthrex, Naples, FL). The femoral sided ACL guide is placed within the anatomic footprint, ensuring a 1 mm posterior wall. Using a FlipCutter drill (Arthrex, Naples, FL), the femoral socket is made in a retrograde fashion (Figure 4A).

(ACLR, anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction AMFC, anteromedial femoral condyle; ATP, anterior tibial plateau; PCL, posterior cruciate ligament; PLFC, posterolateral femoral condyle.)

After debridement of the remnant tibial‐sided ACL footprint and removal of the prior fixation hardware, the tibial‐sided ACL guide is positioned medial to the anterior horn of the lateral meniscus at the anatomic footprint of the ACL (Figure 4B). A 10 mm cannulated, headed reamer (Arthrex, Naples, FL) is used to drill the tibial tunnel in an outside‐in fashion. Bony debris are removed from the joint with an arthroscopic shaver, and care should be taken to clear the tunnel apertures of soft tissue to allow for optimal graft passage. Passing sutures are placed through the femoral and tibial tunnels for graft passage.

Allograft Passage and Final ACL Fixation

An Achilles allograft is prepared on the back table and sized as a 9 mm all soft tissue graft. The allograft is passed in a retrograde fashion through the tibial tunnel into the femoral socket. The graft is secured on the femoral cortex (FiberTag TightRope; Arthrex, Naples, FL) and cycled under tension. The tibial side of the graft is then fixed in slight knee flexion with a posterior drawer applied utilizing a 11 × 30 mm FastThread biocomposite interference screw (Arthrex, Naples, FL).

The arthroscope is then introduced into the knee, with the knee taken through range of motion to confirm there is no impingement in the notch. The tibial‐sided fixation is then backed up with a 4.75 mm SwiveLock (Arthrex, Naples, FL) drilled into the anterior tibial cortex. The open surgical incisions are thoroughly irrigated and closed in a standard fashion.

Postoperatively, the knee is immobilized in a hinged knee brace locked in full extension for ambulation and unlocked for full range of motion when seated or supine. Immediate postoperative plain films are shown in Figure 5. The patient is allowed immediate weight bearing as tolerated with the hinged knee brace locked in full extension until return of appropriate quadriceps strength, typically for 6 weeks. Physical therapy is focused on quadriceps strengthening, closed‐chain exercises, and progressive range of motion, avoiding open‐chain knee extension for a minimum of 12 weeks.

UKA survivorship rates reach 92% to 98% at 10 years for fixed‐bearing UKA with significant functional improvements. 2 , 3 , 4 However, revision ACL reconstruction introduces unique challenges, including potential bone loss and hardware interference, necessitating meticulous preoperative planning and intraoperative precision. 7 Early outcomes support the efficacy of this approach, though long‐term studies are needed to validate implant survivorship and functional durability.
