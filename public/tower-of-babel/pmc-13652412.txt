Introduction

Splenosis refers to the autotransplantation of ectopic splenic tissue fragments, which usually occurs following traumatic splenic rupture or splenectomy[1]. It is a benign and often asymptomatic condition that is mostly detected incidentally. The main pathogenic mechanisms include direct implantation or hematogenous dissemination of splenic tissue during injury or operation[2]. Although splenosis can occur in multiple body regions[3], isolated presentation in the right retroperitoneum at the upper pole of the right kidney is extremely rare and easily misdiagnosed as a neoplasm. Here, we report a typical case and discuss the clinical, imaging, and pathological features to improve diagnostic accuracy. Case presentation

A 38-year-old woman was admitted to our hospital with a 3-hour history of acute right upper abdominal pain. An emergency abdominal ultrasound detected gallstones. She had undergone a total splenectomy for traumatic splenic rupture 6 years before admission.

Laboratory tests showed elevated liver enzymes and bilirubin: alanine aminotransferase 349 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase 248 U/L, total bilirubin 111.1 μmol/L, direct bilirubin 66.9 μmol/L. Tumor markers, including alpha-fetoprotein, carcinoembryonic antigen, and cancer antigen 19-9, were within normal limits. Biliary obstruction was highly suspected.

Abdominal MRI and MRCP confirmed multiple gallstones and a distal common bile duct stone. Meanwhile, a solid space-occupying lesion measuring 2.2 cm in maximum diameter was found in the hepatorenal space. The lesion was mildly hyperintense on T2-weighted imaging and showed restricted diffusion on DWI (Fig. 1), suggestive of a neoplastic process. Based on the anatomical location and MRI signal characteristics of the lesion, we established a detailed preoperative differential diagnosis, including primary retroperitoneal soft tissue sarcoma, right adrenal cortical adenocarcinoma, and retroperitoneal metastatic lymphadenopathy. All of the above candidate lesions present as retroperitoneal masses with restricted diffusion on DWI; therefore, based on the available clinical data, a definitive diagnosis cannot be established at this time. After a multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussion involving the Departments of Hepatobiliary Surgery, Urology, and Radiology, careful review of MRI images demonstrated a distinct, clear border separating the mass from the kidney, supporting our judgment that the lesion was merely adjacent to the kidney rather than invading renal tissue. The patient underwent laparoscopic cholecystectomy, common bile duct exploration, and primary closure. During surgery, a well-defined mass measuring 2.2 × 2.0 cm was found in the retroperitoneum at the upper pole of the right kidney, with a smooth capsule. The mass was completely removed using an ultrasonic scalpel (Fig. 2). Postoperative pathology confirmed the presence of normal splenic tissue with red and white pulp (Fig. 3). The patient’s abdominal pain resolved completely, and she recovered uneventfully. Discussion

Splenosis is a benign acquired change characterized by the autotransplantation and survival of ectopic splenic tissue, most often arising after traumatic splenic rupture or splenectomy[1,5]. The reported incidence ranges from 16% to 67% in patients with a history of splenic trauma, but most cases are asymptomatic and detected incidentally[3,6]. The main mechanisms include direct peritoneal seeding and occasional hematogenous spread of splenic fragments during injury or surgical manipulation[2,7]. Unlike congenital accessory spleens, which are usually located near the splenic hilum and fed by the splenic artery, splenosis implants can occur in almost any site and lack a typical vascular pedicle[1].

According to previous literature, splenic tissue can disseminate hematogenously to distant organs such as the thoracic cavity[8] or brain[9], or implant directly into the pelvis[10], liver[11], gastric fundus[12], or kidney[6]. However, isolated splenosis in the right retroperitoneum at the upper pole of the right kidney is extremely rare and can be readily misdiagnosed as a primary retroperitoneal tumor, metastatic cancer, or lymphoma. To our knowledge, only a small number of similar cases have been published, making the present case valuable for improving clinical recognition.

Imaging features of retroperitoneal splenosis are usually nonspecific and mimic those of malignant neoplasms. On MRI, splenosis typically presents as a well-circumscribed solid nodule with mild hyperintensity on T2-weighted imaging and restricted diffusion on DWI, consistent with our case[1,5]. Contrast-enhanced CT or MRI often shows moderate and homogeneous enhancement, further increasing the risk of misdiagnosis as a tumor[11]. Due to these overlapping imaging features, radiological examinations alone are often insufficient for a definitive diagnosis, especially in atypical locations such as the retroperitoneum.

For patients with a history of splenic trauma or splenectomy who present with retroperitoneal or intra-abdominal masses, splenosis should be routinely included in the differential diagnosis to avoid unnecessary invasive procedures. Recent studies have confirmed that 99mTc-labeled heat-damaged red blood cell scintigraphy (99mTc-DRBC) is the gold standard for noninvasive identification of functional splenic tissue, with high sensitivity and specificity[13]. The use of SPECT/CT fusion imaging further improves localization accuracy, especially for small or deeply located implants[14]. This functional imaging tool can help establish a preoperative diagnosis and avoid unnecessary resection in asymptomatic patients.

In conclusion, isolated retroperitoneal splenosis at the upper pole of the right kidney is an extremely rare condition that closely mimics retroperitoneal neoplasms. Given that this condition is highly prone to misdiagnosis, clinicians must routinely inquire about a history of splenic trauma or splenectomy for all patients with abdominal masses. For patients with a history of splenic rupture or splenectomy, routine contrast CT/MRI follow-up at 6–12 months postoperatively enables early detection of small splenosis lesions. If indeterminate soft nodules are found on follow-up scans, 99mTc-DRBC scintigraphy should be routinely ordered for confirmation before surgery to effectively reduce misdiagnosis and unnecessary operations.

Conclusion

Isolated splenosis confined to the retroperitoneal space at the upper pole of the right kidney represents an exceedingly rare clinical entity, with only a few similar cases documented in the current medical literature. This case report describes its MRI features, intraoperative laparoscopic dissection experience, and confirmatory pathological findings, which help frontline surgeons recognize this easily misdiagnosed lesion. For all patients with a definite history of splenic rupture or total splenectomy who develop incidental retroperitoneal soft-tissue masses, splenosis should be considered in the differential diagnosis workflow. Preoperative functional imaging with 99mTc-DRBC scintigraphy should be utilized to confirm functional ectopic splenic tissue, effectively lowering the incidence of misdiagnosis and eliminating avoidable invasive surgical interventions.
