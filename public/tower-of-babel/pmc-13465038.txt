Combined minimally invasive direct coronary artery bypass with video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery thymectomy via left anterolateral mini-thoracotomy
Authors: Leng Khoong Lee, Marco Lizwan, Kim Chai Chua, Cynthia Ming Li Chia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMinimally invasive surgery (MIS) has become increasingly prevalent across almost all surgical specialties, including cardiothoracic surgery. However, combined MIS for coronary artery bypass and thymectomy in a single sitting has not been previously documented. We report a case of a 61-year-old male with coronary artery disease and anterior mediastinal nodules who underwent both minimally invasive direct coronary artery bypass (MIDCAB) and video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) thymectomy via left anterolateral mini-thoracotomy. He was discharged on post-operative day 3. In well-selected patients, performing concomitant MIDCAB and VATS thymectomy through the same incisions is safe and feasible, and significantly reduces hospitalisation duration.Keywords: Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery, Thymectomy, Minimally invasive direct coronary artery bypass, Minimally invasive surgery, Concomitant surgery

Introduction

Over the past decades, minimally invasive surgery (MIS) has advanced considerably, enabled by evolutions in surgical techniques and developments in technology. MIS is increasingly adopted and accepted by surgeons and patients alike, offering reduced operative trauma with decreased post-operative pain, shorter hospitalisation and better cosmesis [1]. Today, it is common across almost all surgical specialties. Cardiothoracic surgery is no exception, where MIS is now considered standard of care for various thoracic procedures and is rapidly gaining traction in cardiac surgery, proving capable of replacing conventional open surgeries.

Typically, coronary revascularisation and anterior mediastinal mass excision are performed via median sternotomy or as staged procedures when indicated in the same patient, such as those requiring coronary artery bypass who also have thymomatous disease.

We report a case of using a minimally invasive approach through the same incisions for both minimally invasive direct coronary artery bypass and video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery thymectomy in a single combined surgery.

Case presentation

A 61-year-old male with background of hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, diabetes mellitus, and a smoking history, was found with electrocardiogram changes during pre-operative tests for parotidectomy. Further cardiac investigations confirmed single-vessel coronary artery disease involving chronic total occlusion of the mid-left anterior descending (LAD) artery (Fig. 1A). He was referred for minimally invasive direct coronary artery bypass (MIDCAB) and underwent computed tomography coronary angiogram as part of routine pre-operative planning, which incidentally found anterior mediastinal nodules, likely thymomas (Fig. 1B).

(A) Coronary angiogram findings of chronic total occlusion of the left anterior descending artery, with moderate diffuse disease in the right coronary and left circumflex arteries (B) Computed tomography coronary angiogram findings suggestive of thymoma measuring 1.9 cm by 1.6 cm (C) Healed operative incision (D) Thymic lesion overlying the pericardium and innominate vein (E) Excised specimen measuring 14 cm (F) After excision

The patient was scheduled for concomitant MIDCAB and video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) thymectomy by a team comprising a cardiac surgeon and a thoracic surgeon. The operation was performed under general anaesthesia with single lung ventilation on the right and the patient in slight right lateral decubitus position. After routine cleaning and draping, a small anterolateral thoracotomy was made through the left 5th intercostal space (ICS) (Fig. 1C) with a videoscopic port in the 2nd ICS at the anterior axillary line. The phrenic nerves and internal mammary artery were identified and preserved during dissection. An additional port was made at the 4th ICS along the anterior axillary line for VATS to proceed. The thymus, along with the mass, was mobilised (Fig. 1D) and excised (Fig. 1E) with remnant thymic tissue in the superior poles (Fig. 1F). No specimen bag was used for retrieval. The left internal mammary artery (LIMA) was then harvested and grafted to the LAD. The operation concluded with chest tube insertion and routine closure. Total operative time was 310 min.

The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit immediately after surgery without inotropic support. He was transferred to intermediate care on post-operative day (POD) 1 and general ward on POD 2. He was discharged on POD 3 following an uneventful course.

Discussion

Minimally Invasive Direct Coronary Artery Bypass (MIDCAB)

MIDCAB offers numerous advantages over median sternotomy, reporting lower blood loss and transfusion rate, shorter ventilation support and hospitalisation/intensive care unit stay, and earlier discharge [2]. Patients experience less pain, faster recovery, and sustained quality-of-life benefits [3]. Longer-term follow-up also suggests improved all-cause mortality [4]. Common indications for MIDCAB are isolated proximal left anterior descending (LAD) artery stenosis not amenable to percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or for multivessel disease either as a hybrid procedure with PCI or partial revascularisation in high-risk patients [5]. Although MIDCAB typically involve a single left internal mammary artery to LAD grafting, as surgical expertise continues to improve, some centres have the capability of offering multivessel grafting [6].

Video-Assisted Thoracoscopic Surgery (VATS) thymectomy

VATS thymectomy provides benefits akin to MIDCAB in decreasing blood loss and length of hospitalisation, in addition to shorter chest tube duration and reduced post-operative pain and complications [7]. Thymectomy is commonly performed for therapeutic or diagnostic purposes to manage anterior mediastinal masses, with consensus that surgical resection without histological confirmation is reasonable when thymoma is suspected [8]. A major concern with VATS is the thoroughness of surgery and its implications for disease recurrence, but the role of total thymectomy in patients without myasthenia gravis remains controversial [9]. In appropriately selected patients, VATS has demonstrated oncologic and survival outcomes comparable to open thymectomy [10].

Indications in present case

In this patient, revascularisation via MIDCAB was recommended as PCI was not feasible due to complexity of the chronic total occlusion (CTO) lesion, as is often not attempted in such cases since success rates fall short compared to non-CTO conditions [11]. Medical therapy alone was not preferable as he had left ventricular (LV) abnormalities on imaging, and studies describing improved clinical outcomes with CTO revascularisation [12].

VATS thymectomy was also a viable option for the anterior mediastinal lesion in this case, as there was no evidence of malignant features or local invasion, and anatomy was favourable with the primary nodule located in the thoracic portion of the thymus.

Considerations in combined surgery

There are pros and cons to concomitant versus staged procedures. Combined surgery spares the patient from the surgical stress and pain of a second operation and offers significant cost and resources saving. It also reduces treatment delay, avoiding the risk of interval progression of disease and alleviating symptoms and anxiety surrounding untreated conditions. While undergoing one less surgery eliminates an additional exposure to general anaesthesia, the prolonged duration of the single surgery may confer anaesthetic risk greater than the sum of its parts. The patient must also tolerate more extensive operative trauma when multiple procedures are completed in one sitting.

This patient was young and deemed fit despite his cardiovascular comorbidities. He had no symptoms or activity limitations, a normal left ventricular ejection fraction, and no conditions precluding minimally invasive surgery (MIS) such as poor pulmonary reserve or prior chest surgery. Since he was already planned for MIDCAB and with his low-risk profile, we decided to perform VATS thymectomy through the same incision, given the superior clinical outcomes and cosmetic benefits of MIS.

We note previous reports of combined elective surgery involving coronary artery bypass and thymectomy, all of which were through median sternotomy (Table 1). A key consideration for the equivalent via MIS is that the ideal surgical approaches for MIDCAB and VATS thymectomy differ. Generally, surgical access in VATS is more posterolateral than for MIDCAB. The third port not typical in MIDCAB was made at the position where the working port in VATS is usually located.

In our experience, achieving sufficient exposure and optimal access to the anterior mediastinum through the mini-thoracotomy presents a technical challenge due to limited manoeuvrability of thoracoscopic instruments. Ensuring adequate margin of resection avoids the need for re-operation, hence pre-operative assessment was essential to determine the extent of the lesion especially toward the superior poles of the thymus. Surgical expertise is also crucial as the mediastinum is primarily approached in an inferior-superior direction instead of latero-medially in standard VATS.

Conclusion

In conclusion, as we demonstrate in this combined MIS, it is safe and feasible to use the same incisions in MIDCAB for VATS thymectomy. Surgeons should consider concomitant procedures in well-selected patients with appropriate pre-operative planning and risk-benefit assessment.
