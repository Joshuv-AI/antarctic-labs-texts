Wrong-side imaging orders: automated detection using electronic health record data – a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jerard Z Kneifati-Hayek, Isaac Peabody, Charles Baillie, Jiheum Park, Tian Gu, Jonathan Elias, Keith Hentel, Stella Kang, Chunhua Weng, Rachel C Shelton, Joshua Weintraub, Michael Rinke, Baruch S. Fertel, Jason S Adelman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWrong-side diagnostic imaging order errors are preventable errors that can delay diagnosis and cause patient harm yet remain underdetected due to limitations in existing reporting systems.ObjectiveTo develop and validate an automated electronic health record (EHR)-based method for detecting potential wrong-side diagnostic imaging order errors using an adapted Retract-and-Reorder (RAR) approach and to identify associated risk factors.DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingSix-facility health system comprising inpatient, outpatient and emergency room sites.MethodsWe screened 355 000 imaging orders with side specified, placed during 2021 across our healthcare system. We adapted the RAR methodology, originally developed to detect near-miss medication errors, by extending detection windows to 24 hours and identifying any orders switching from one side to the contralateral side, accounting for multiprovider workflows inherent in imaging. We validated the method through chart review of 100 randomly selected RAR events, then applied the query across all imaging orders. Multivariate logistic regression was used to identify risk factors associated with RAR events.ResultsWe identified 1667 RAR events (4.70 per 1000 orders). Validation yielded a positive predictive value of 87% (95% CI 79.0% to 92.2%), estimating 4.09 confirmed wrong-side errors per 1000 orders. The odds of an RAR event were significantly higher in outpatient settings compared with inpatient settings (OR 4.53; 95% CI 3.80 to 5.42) and among administrative staff compared with attending physicians (OR 2.08; 95% CI 1.73 to 2.49). CT scans showed 79% higher odds of an RAR event compared with X-rays (OR 1.79; 95% CI 1.34 to 2.39).ConclusionThis validated approach offers a scalable solution for automated detection of potential wrong-side diagnostic imaging order errors. The methodology leverages commonly available EHR data to support continuous surveillance and intervention evaluation for improved diagnostic safety.

Introduction

Diagnostic errors occur frequently and cause significant harm to patients.14 Among the contributing factors, incorrectly ordered imaging plays a significant yet often under-recognised role.5 While many of these errors are preventable, they remain persistent across healthcare settings. Computerised physician order entry (CPOE) electronic health record (EHR) systems were implemented to improve ordering practices.6 Despite improvements over handwritten orders, other errors related to CPOE order issues arose.7 One frequent and particularly consequential error involves ordering imaging on the wrong side of the body.810

Researchers have used a range of methods to assess the prevalence of wrong-side diagnostic imaging order errors. In one study of voluntarily reported radiology errors, 247 wrong-side imaging errors were identified, including near misses and errors that reached the patient and caused direct harm.8 Recently, Sheehan et al found that wrong-sided errors occurred at a rate of 6.5/1000 orders.11 Their method used a combination of trigger tools and a manual checklist, but lacked the capacity for continuous monitoring. Similar prior approaches to detect wrong-side diagnostic imaging errors have relied on voluntary incident reports or semiautomated methods with manual components, limiting their scalability and real-time utility. For broader implementation, an automated approach to error detection is necessary.12 13

Patient safety experts recommend leveraging health information technology (IT) to develop novel methods to improve diagnostic safety.12 14 One such approach is the Retract-and-Reorder (RAR) method—the first validated health IT safety tool designed to automate the detection of near-miss, self-caught order errors.15 The conventional RAR method identifies events where a clinician places an order, retracts it within 10 min and then places a new order within the next 10 min. This approach has demonstrated high validity in previous applications and has successfully automated the detection of wrong-patient and medication prescribing order errors, achieving high positive predictive values (PPVs). The PPV of wrong-patient order errors was 76.2% (95% CI 70.6% to 81.9%) and the medication prescribing order error PPV was 85.0% (95% CI 75% to 91%).15 16

To date, the RAR method has not yet been applied to detect errors in diagnostic imaging, a domain that presents unique challenges that necessitate modifications to the standard RAR approach. After an order is placed, a radiologist typically reviews the study, and a technician performs the imaging, creating multiple opportunities for downstream providers to identify errors. Unlike medication orders, which the ordering provider typically catches, imaging orders may be intercepted later in the workflow by radiologists or technicians, requiring longer time frames for error detection.

To address this complexity, we developed a modified RAR approach tailored to imaging. We hypothesised that this adapted method could reliably detect potential wrong-side diagnostic imaging order errors by accounting for the longer detection window and involvement of multiple providers. In this study, we aimed to (1) develop an automated health IT tool based on the modified RAR framework, (2) validate that the method captures wrong-side diagnostic imaging order errors in a random sample and (3) identify risk factors associated with RAR events.

Methods

This retrospective study contained three phases (figure 1). First, the development of the adapted RAR method for automated detection of wrong-side diagnostic imaging order RAR events (defined below). Second, validation of identified RAR events to verify that they represent wrong-side diagnostic imaging order errors. Lastly, identification of risk factors associated with RAR events. The study used EHR data recorded from 1 January 2021 to 31 December 2021, across a six-facility health system comprising inpatient, outpatient and emergency room sites. All imaging orders placed by clinicians specifying a side (ie, right or left) were extracted from the data warehouse. Institutional review board approval was obtained from each participating site.

Flow diagram of three-phase study design. PPV, positive predictive value; RAR, Retract-and-Reorder.

Phase 1: development of the adapted RAR method

We created a 1-year database of imaging orders that specified a side (eg, right or left) and used the existing RAR method as the basis for developing our query.15 16 To define appropriate time parameters, we identified all orders that were retracted and had a subsequent reorder placed on the contralateral side without any time parameter restrictions. We found that reorders plateaued around 24 hours after initial order cancellation and captured a significant majority (84%) of events (online supplemental figure S1). Therefore, we expanded the detection window to 24 hours; we determined that extending beyond 24 hours would have diminishing returns.

Based on this evidence supporting a longer detection window and considering the involvement of clinicians other than the ordering clinician in correcting order errors (such as a radiologist or technician), we defined an RAR event as:

An imaging order placed on a patient specifying a side that is retracted within 24 hours of the original order placement.

An imaging order placed on a patient specifying a side that is retracted within 24 hours of the original order placement.

Followed by a subsequent reorder on the contralateral side, placed within 24 hours of retraction.

Followed by a subsequent reorder on the contralateral side, placed within 24 hours of retraction.

We executed this query retrospectively against the 1-year database to create a database consisting of these RAR events.

Phase 2: method validation

From the created database of RAR events, we took a random sample of 100 RAR events to validate whether a wrong-side diagnostic imaging order error occurred. We defined a confirmed wrong-side diagnostic imaging order error as discordance between the initial, retracted order and documentation in the EHR. Our random sample review size was determined based on established methodologies for validating detection tools in health informatics and provides an appropriate balance between statistical precision and feasibility of manual chart review.151720 With an anticipated PPV of approximately 85% based on a prior RAR study, a sample of 100 RAR events allows estimation of the PPV with a margin of error of approximately ±7% using the Wilson score method.16 21 22

To ensure rigorous validation, we developed a standardised chart review protocol with specific criteria for error determination. Criteria for a confirmed wrong-side diagnostic imaging order error were based on prior methods. Evidence of a discrepancy was identified through EHR documentation or the imaging order cancellation notes. If RAR events were not confirmed as wrong-side errors, reviewers assessed what type of order error occurred. A primary reviewer (JK-H, a physician with expertise in patient safety) reviewed all 100 charts using this protocol. For each case, the reviewer documented evidence of the error, the source of documentation confirming the error and categorised the error type. To assess inter-rater reliability, a second physician (CB, also with patient safety expertise) independently reviewed 20% of the cases, including all potential false positive RAR events. There was 100% agreement between reviewers.

The PPV was calculated by dividing the number of confirmed wrong-side diagnostic imaging order errors by the total number of RAR events reviewed. This PPV was then used to estimate the frequency of confirmed wrong-side diagnostic imaging order errors by multiplying the observed frequency of RAR events among all orders specifying sidedness by the PPV of the RAR measurement tool. Results for phase 2 were reported as rates, with RAR events and estimates of confirmed wrong-side diagnostic imaging order errors expressed per 1000 orders specifying sidedness. Results were further broken down by patient-level characteristics (patient age, sex and race/ethnicity), order-level characteristics (order type) and clinical-level characteristics (visit type, ordering user title).

Phase 3: risk factor identification

To determine the risk of an RAR event, individual feature-level significance for categorical features was tested using Fisher’s exact test, with a Benjamini-Hochberg False discovery rate correction run across all features within a given category to control for false discoveries. Category-level significance for numerical features was measured using a t-test.

To identify factors associated with RAR events, we performed multivariable logistic regression using RAR events (not chart-confirmed errors) as the outcome, examining patient demographics, clinical setting, imaging type and ordering provider type. Covariates were selected based on their established or plausible relevance to diagnostic imaging ordering errors, informed by prior literature and clinical practice. Patient demographics have been associated with variation in diagnostic processes and error risk.23 Clinical setting influences workflow, time pressure and cognitive load, which are known contributors to ordering errors.24 Imaging type captures differences in complexity and appropriateness criteria across modalities, which may affect error likelihood.25 26 Lastly, ordering provider types may reflect variation in ordering habits and workload. Reference groups were selected based on level prevalence within factors and to maximise interpretability. Model calibration was assessed via the Hosmer-Lemeshow χ² test, and model discrimination was assessed via the area under the receiver-operator characteristic curve. (online supplemental figure S2). We used the Huber-White-sandwich method for robust standard errors to adjust for ordering provider level clustering. Analysis was performed using R software V.4.4.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing).

Demographic and clinical characteristics associated with RAR events

Patients with orders specifying a side and those with RAR events had similar median ages (55 vs 52 years). Women represented 62.1% of all sided orders and 59.0% of RAR events, with a slightly higher RAR event rate for male patients. Among racial/ethnic groups, patients with unknown race/ethnicity (not reported at the time of data entry) had the highest RAR event rate (5.69 per 1000 orders), while other groups ranged from 4.66 (Asian/Pacific Islanders) to 4.07 (Other) per 1000 orders, showing minimal variation (table 1).

Clinical setting and imaging order characteristics of RAR events

When analysed by clinical setting, outpatient orders had the highest rate of RAR events (7.27 per 1000 orders), followed by ED orders (4.53 per 1000 orders) and inpatient orders (2.48 per 1000 orders) (table 1).

Among imaging order types, CT scans had the highest rate of RAR events (8.05 per 1000 orders), followed by X-ray (4.82 per 1000 orders), breast imaging (4.59 per 1000 orders), ultrasound (4.24 per 1000 orders), MRI (4.02 per 1000 orders) and nuclear medicine (0.68 per 1000 orders). Orders placed by administrative staff/clerks had the highest rate of RAR events (8.01 per 1000 orders), followed by advanced practice providers (5.37 per 1000 orders), other staff (4.63 per 1000 orders), students/residents (4.24 per 1000 orders), attending physicians (3.37 per 1000 orders) and nurses (2.04 per 1000 orders).

Risk factors for RAR events

In the multivariate logistic regression analysis comparing completed orders specifying sidedness with RAR events, several significant risk factors were identified (figure 2, online supplemental table S1).

Forest plot of multivariable logistic regression. Area under the receiver operator characteristic curve=0.68, Hosmer-Lemeshow χ² test=20.96 (df=8). ED, emergency department.

Clinical setting was strongly associated with RAR events. Compared with inpatient orders, outpatient orders had nearly five times higher odds of RAR events (OR: 4.53, 95% CI 3.80 to 5.42), and ED orders had over two times higher odds (OR: 2.18, 95% CI 1.77 to 2.68).

For imaging order types, CT scans (OR: 1.79, 95% CI 1.34 to 2.39) were associated with significantly higher odds of RAR events compared with X-rays, while MRI was associated with significantly lower odds of RAR events (OR: 0.71, 95% CI 0.56 to 0.91). Breast imaging, ultrasound and nuclear medicine did not show statistically significant differences.

Among ordering user titles, administrative clerks (OR: 2.08, 95% CI 1.73 to 2.49), advanced practice providers (OR: 1.46, 95% CI 1.25 to 1.71), staff categorised as ‘Other’ (OR: 2.17, 95% CI 1.63 to 2.89) and students/residents (OR: 1.31, 95% CI 1.07 to 1.60) had significantly higher odds of RAR events compared with attending physicians. Nurses did not show a significant difference.

Outpatient setting-specific analysis

Among 118 776 outpatient orders specifying sidedness, we identified 864 RAR events, yielding a rate of 7.27 RAR events per 1000 orders (table 2).

In the outpatient setting, patients with unknown race/ethnicity had the highest RAR event rate (9.52 per 1000 orders), substantially higher than other racial/ethnic groups. Among imaging order types, CT scans had the highest RAR event rate (10.43 per 1000 orders), followed by X-ray (8.94 per 1000 orders) and ultrasound (7.35 per 1000 orders).

Most notably, administrative staff/clerks and ‘Other’ staff had exceptionally high RAR event rates in the outpatient setting (12.01 and 18.47 per 1000 orders, respectively), while attending physicians and nurses had the lowest rates (3.91 and 3.80 per 1000 orders, respectively).

Emergency department-specific analysis

In the ED setting (table 3), we identified 479 RAR events among 105 639 orders specifying sidedness, yielding a rate of 4.53 RAR events per 1000 orders.

In the ED, female patients had a lower RAR event rate than the overall average (4.01 vs 4.53 per 1000 orders). Among racial/ethnic groups, Asian/Pacific Islanders had the highest rate (6.05 per 1000 orders), followed by non-Hispanic whites (5.52 per 1000 orders).

CT scans had the highest RAR event rate in the ED setting (7.05 per 1000 orders), followed by X-rays (4.95 per 1000 orders). Notably, there were no RAR events for breast imaging or nuclear medicine in the ED setting.

By ordering user title, advanced practice providers had the highest RAR event rate in the ED (5.40 per 1000 orders), followed by students/residents (4.39 per 1000 orders) and attending physicians (3.84 per 1000 orders). No RAR events were associated with orders placed by administrative clerks, nurses or other staff in the ED setting.

Inpatient setting-specific analysis

In the inpatient setting (online supplemental table S2), we identified 324 RAR events among 130 585 orders specifying sidedness, yielding a rate of 2.48 RAR events per 1000 orders.

In the inpatient setting, non-Hispanic black patients had the highest rate (3.46 per 1000 orders), followed by other (3.14 per 1000 orders). CT scans had the highest RAR event rate (7.89 per 1000 orders). Advanced practice providers had the highest RAR event rate (3.37 per 1000 orders) out of all clinicians.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated an adapted version of the RAR method to automate the detection of potential wrong-side diagnostic imaging order errors. Our PPV was high; nearly 9 in every 10 RAR events were confirmed as wrong-side diagnostic imaging order errors on chart review. An analysis of the false positive RAR events found that although these events were not wrong-side order errors, they were other types of CPOE order errors. This new health IT safety tool will enable healthcare systems to conduct surveillance of these potential errors and enable researchers and quality improvement specialists to conduct trials to prevent these errors. Together, these results show the applicability of the RAR method to diagnostic imaging to better understand patient safety risks and provide a foundation for future interventions targeting imaging order errors.

Our findings align with and extend the limited existing literature on wrong-side diagnostic imaging order errors. Sheehan et al reported a wrong-side error rate of 6.5 per 1000 imaging orders using a combination of trigger tools and manual checklists, with a PPV of 88.4%.11 Our rate of 4.09 confirmed errors per 1000 imaging orders (adjusted for the false positive rate) and PPV of 87% are consistent with these prior findings, providing important external validation of the RAR approach for imaging order error detection. The Pennsylvania Patient Safety Authority has documented the persistent nature of wrong radiology events, identifying 652 such events in 2009 and 993 events from 2016 to 2017, with wrong-side errors comprising approximately 15% of all wrong radiology events.8 27 Notably, prior research on radiology ordering errors has focused primarily on wrong-patient events or has relied on voluntary reporting systems,28 which are known to underestimate true error rates. Our study addresses this gap by providing an automated, scalable method specifically designed for wrong-side diagnostic imaging order errors that can be implemented using routinely collected EHR data. Importantly, our finding that outpatient settings have substantially higher RAR event rates than inpatient or ED settings represents a novel contribution, as prior studies have not systematically examined setting-specific variation in wrong-side diagnostic imaging order errors.

This method entirely relies on EHR log data and can be implemented in other healthcare systems that record healthcare log data. Additionally, the false positive rate was only 1 in 10 RAR events identified. These false positives were usually due to repetitively placing both right and left orders for the same anatomical site. While the false positive rate is low, it can likely be further reduced by requiring the use of bilateral orders in the EHR. Furthermore, the false positives, while not wrong-side errors, were found to indicate poor clinician workflows and other types of order errors.

ED RAR event rates were approximately 1.8 times higher than inpatient RAR event rates, highlighting the challenges of the ED environment where rapid decision-making under time pressure may increase error risk. However, it is notable that ED rates remained substantially lower than outpatient rates, suggesting that safety protocols in emergency settings may provide some protective effect.

Outpatient rates of RAR events were significantly elevated, with nearly five times higher odds compared with inpatient settings. This striking difference warrants particular attention, as outpatient care settings may have fewer built-in verification processes compared with inpatient units. Our findings suggest that the outpatient setting represents the highest risk environment for RAR events, which should inform targeted intervention efforts.

The increased risk for breast imaging and CT scan orders is particularly concerning. The higher RAR event rates for breast imaging likely relate to the frequency of these studies and their almost universal requirement for sidedness specification. For CT scans, the complexity of these orders and the potentially serious consequences of wrong-side diagnostic imaging order errors make this finding especially important for intervention planning.

Our finding that admin/clerks and ‘other’ staff had more than twice the odds of RAR events compared with attending physicians suggests that ordering authority and training significantly impact event rates. This may reflect issues in communication, frequency of placing orders or variations in oversight and verification processes. These disparities emphasise the importance of targeted training programmes and system-level interventions focused on high-risk user groups.

Automating detection of ordering errors has enabled intervention studies that were previously not possible.15 19 29 30 The wrong-patient RAR measure has been used in several intervention studies that would not have been possible without automated detection. The new health IT safety tool that we developed can similarly be used as an outcome measure in intervention studies.

Limitations

This study has multiple limitations. First, the RAR method is designed to detect near-miss order errors—instances where an error was caught and corrected before reaching the patient, not errors that reach the patient. As such, order errors that reach the patient are not directly included in our analyses. However, prior research has shown that for every 50 near-miss order errors, 1 error will reach the patient. Second, this study was conducted at a single healthcare system, which may limit the generalisability to unrelated healthcare systems. To enhance generalisability, our study integrated healthcare system data from six facilities spanning inpatient, outpatient and emergency care across academic and non-academic settings, and serves a demographically diverse patient population. Third, the regression analysis was based on RAR events (tool-flagged events), not chart-confirmed errors. As such, identified risk factors are risk factors for RAR events, and not necessarily wrong-side diagnostic imaging order errors. However, given the high PPV of 87%, RAR events serve as a reliable proxy for wrong-side diagnostic imaging order errors, and thus the risk factors identified for RAR events and the risk factors for wrong-side diagnostic imaging order errors likely overlap. Fourth, while we used the Hosmer-Lemeshow test to assess model calibration, this test can be overly sensitive in large samples. With our sample size of 355 000 orders, even clinically trivial differences between predicted and observed risk may reach statistical significance, and results should be interpreted with this caveat in mind. Fifth, we reviewed a small sample of RAR events to estimate the PPV; however, the resulting CI was narrow. Additionally, this methodology has been used by others to develop measures and electronic triggers.16 20 Finally, while our validation was based on manual chart review, the RAR method relies solely on routinely collected EHR log data, supporting its scalability and potential for implementation in other health systems.

Conclusion

We developed and validated an automated health IT safety tool to detect potential wrong-side diagnostic imaging order errors using routinely collected EHR data. This approach enables scalable surveillance and supports future intervention studies, offering a practical step toward safer, more reliable electronic ordering systems.
