Cardiorespiratory fitness is inversely associated with non-invasive markers of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis: a cross-sectional study of 39,197 UK adults
Authors: Scott A Willis, Kevin Deighton, Christopher Goodwin, Daniel J Cuthbertson, Jonathan G Stine, Shelley E Keating, Christopher J A Pugh, Gaël Ennequin, David J Stensel, Guruprasad P Aithal, James A King
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) is a powerful predictor of numerous health outcomes; however, its relationship with liver health remains unclear. This study examined the associations of CRF with non-invasive markers of liver steatosis and fibrosis in a large, health-screening cohort, and explored potential effect modification by demographic, cardiometabolic, and clinical factors.MethodsIn this independent healthcare-based cross-sectional study of 39,197 UK adults (32% female; mean ± SD, age 45.8 ± 9.0 years), CRF was objectively estimated as peak oxygen uptake (ml∙kg−1∙min−1) during submaximal treadmill and cycle ergometer tests. Hepatic steatosis and fibrosis were defined using validated non-invasive indices: Fatty Liver Index (FLI) and Hepatic Steatosis Index (HSI) for steatosis, and Fibrosis-4 Index (FIB-4) and NAFLD Fibrosis Score (NFS) for fibrosis. Multinomial logistic regression examined associations between CRF and low-, intermediate-, and high-risk categories for each index, adjusting for demographic, cardiometabolic, and clinical covariates (excluding component variables of the respective indices).ResultsFor FLI and HSI, each 1 ml∙kg−1∙min−1 higher CRF was associated with 3–5% lower odds of intermediate steatosis risk and 8–9% lower odds of high steatosis risk (P < 0.001). For FIB-4 and NFS, higher CRF was associated with 1–3% lower odds of intermediate fibrosis risk and 2–6% lower odds of high fibrosis risk (P ≤ 0.011). Associations were stronger in females and weaker in individuals with less favourable metabolic profiles.ConclusionsHigher CRF was associated with lower odds of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis across multiple validated non-invasive indices. These findings highlight CRF as an integrative biomarker of hepatic and cardiometabolic health, and modifiable target for steatotic liver disease prevention and management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05137-1.Keywords: Steatosis, MASLD, Fat, Stiffness, Exercise, Physical activity

Background

Steatotic liver disease (SLD) affects over a third (~ 38%) of adults worldwide and has become the leading cause of chronic liver disease, with metabolic dysfunction–associated SLD (MASLD) comprising the majority of cases [1]. SLD is characterised by excessive liver fat accumulation, hepatic steatosis, which can progress to inflammation, fibrosis, and cirrhosis, with hepatic complications including decompensation/liver failure and hepatocellular carcinoma [2]. Beyond the liver, SLD is strongly linked with elevated extrahepatic morbidity and mortality, particularly from cardiovascular disease and extrahepatic cancers, with hepatic fibrosis recognised as the most important determinant of prognosis [3]. Several recent studies in the UK Biobank cohort demonstrated the utility of non-invasive (blood- or imaging-based) markers of liver health in predicting future clinical, hepatic, or cardiovascular events [4, 5]. Identifying and addressing modifiable factors influencing hepatic steatosis and fibrosis through simple non-invasive tests is therefore essential for reducing the overall health impact of SLD.

Although some pharmacotherapies are now emerging, lifestyle modification, including the promotion of physical activity, remains central to the prevention and management of SLD across all disease stages, with benefits demonstrated for both hepatic steatosis and fibrosis [2, 6]. Cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) is an objective, modifiable clinical trait reflecting the integrated function of multiple physiological systems (cardiovascular, respiratory, and musculoskeletal), determined by both habitual physical activity levels and genetic factors [7, 8]. Importantly, compared with other traditional risk factors, low CRF has been identified as a powerful independent predictor of morbidity and premature mortality, including cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and certain cancers [7, 9]. Furthermore, a recent meta-analysis demonstrated that improvements in CRF are strongly associated with reductions in hepatic steatosis, providing support for a biological link between CRF and liver health [10]. Given the dual burden of hepatic and cardiometabolic complications in SLD, CRF may represent a potential target for preventive strategies.

Despite this rationale, large-scale evidence linking objectively assessed CRF with multiple markers of liver health remains limited. Existing studies have identified inverse associations between CRF and hepatic steatosis [11–16], and more recently with fibrosis [14, 17, 18], but these investigations have been restricted to small, clinically selected cohorts or relied on non-exercise predictive equations to derive CRF rather than exercise testing protocols (submaximal or maximal). Moreover, most prior research has focused on steatosis, with fewer studies examining advanced liver fibrosis despite its recognised importance in disease prognosis. A key reason for this has been the historical reliance on liver biopsy for diagnosis, which, while the reference standard for clinical trials, is invasive and unsuitable for epidemiological research. Multiple validated non-invasive tests are now available for assessing SLD risk, including the Fatty Liver Index (FLI) [19] and Hepatic Steatosis Index (HSI) [20] for steatosis, and the Fibrosis-4 Index (FIB-4) [21] and NAFLD Fibrosis Score (NFS) [22] for fibrosis, enabling associations to be examined in larger-scale, non-clinical cohorts, yet this opportunity has not been fully realised.

To address these limitations of existing research, this study investigated the associations between objectively assessed CRF and non-invasive indices of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis in 39,197 United Kingdom (UK) adults undergoing standardised health assessments. By utilising the largest cohort to date, we aimed to provide robust, population-level evidence on the relationship between CRF and markers of SLD, and as a secondary aim, to explore demographic, cardiometabolic, and clinical factors that may moderate these associations.

Methods

Data source and study population

This cross-sectional study involved secondary analysis of anonymised data collected from standardised health assessments conducted by a UK-based healthcare charity between 2006 and 2024. A total of 39,197 adults aged between 18 and 91 years attended assessments at hospitals, fitness centres, and corporate locations across the UK. These included assessments of anthropometry, CRF, and indicators of liver and cardiometabolic health. Health assessment attendance was voluntary and occurred for various reasons including for a general health check, medical concern, or occupational requirement. All assessment locations included a mixture of attendance reasons. Prior to attending, participants also completed a pre-assessment questionnaire to collect detailed demographic information. All participants consented for their anonymised data to be utilised in future research, and ethical approval was granted by the Loughborough University Ethics Review Sub-Committee (project ID: 15414). This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies [23].

Health assessment procedures

Participants underwent a standardised health assessment conducted by medical doctors and/or health and wellbeing physiologists. Height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using a stadiometer, while body weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using calibrated digital weighing scales. Body mass index (BMI, expressed as kg∙m−2) was subsequently calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). Waist circumference was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using an inelastic tape at the mid-point between the bottom of the ribs and the top of the iliac crest. Systolic (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were measured using an automated sphygmomanometer in accordance with the European Society of Hypertension guidance [24].

Assessment of cardiorespiratory fitness (exposure)

CRF was estimated as peak oxygen uptake (VO2peak, expressed as ml∙kg−1∙min−1) from a submaximal graded exercise test conducted on either a treadmill or cycle ergometer, selected based on participant preference or site availability. Standardised, validated protocols were used for both assessment modes in accordance with the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) guidelines [25]. For treadmill tests, the Bruce protocol was employed, and VO2peak was estimated using sex-specific equations based on total test duration [26]. For cycle ergometer tests, a multistage ramp protocol was utilised, with workload increases every two minutes; VO2peak was estimated using sex-specific equations based on final power output (watts) and body weight [25]. These methods have been widely used in clinical and epidemiological research to reliably estimate CRF in diverse populations and are described in further detail in the Supplementary Methods [27]. These protocols remained consistent over time during the periods of data collection.

Blood sampling and biochemical analyses

Fasting venous blood samples were obtained via venepuncture of an antecubital vein and collected into EDTA collection tubes. Samples were stored temporarily at 4 °C and analysed within 24 h of collection at local pathology laboratories. Biochemical analyses included liver enzymes (alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST], gamma-glutamyl transferase [GGT]), triacylglycerol [TAG], high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL-c], glucose, albumin and platelet count, as described previously [28]. Clinical pathology assays were subject to standard laboratory quality assurance and control procedures although some variation in assay techniques was apparent between sites and overtime.

Non-invasive indices of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis (outcomes)

Hepatic steatosis was assessed using the FLI and HSI. FLI was calculated from BMI, waist circumference, TAG and GGT using established equations [19]. Participants were categorised into low (< 30), intermediate (30–59), and high (≥ 60) risk of hepatic steatosis. HSI was derived from BMI, ALT/AST ratio, diabetes status, and sex in accordance with previous literature [20], with thresholds defining low (< 30), intermediate (30–36), and high (> 36) steatosis risk.

Hepatic fibrosis was evaluated using the FIB-4 index and NFS. FIB-4 was calculated from age, AST, ALT, and platelet count, with hepatic fibrosis risk categories defined as low (< 1.30), intermediate (1.30–2.67), and high (> 2.67) [21]. NFS incorporated age, BMI, presence of diabetes or impaired fasting glucose (IFG), AST/ALT ratio, platelet count, and albumin, categorised into low (< −1.455), intermediate (−1.455–0.676) and high (> 0.676) fibrosis risk [22].

The full equations for each of the non-invasive liver indices are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Covariates

Covariates included in the analysis were age, sex, socioeconomic status (SES), assessment year, UK region of assessment centre, mode of CRF assessment (treadmill or cycle ergometer), BMI, TAG, HDL-c, glycaemic status, and hypertension status. Age was calculated as the time between participants’ date of birth and the date of assessment. Sex was self-reported as male or female via the pre-assessment questionnaire.

SES was assessed using the national Index of Multiple Deprivation relevant to the participants’ country of residence: the English Index of Multiple Deprivation [29], the Scottish Index of Multiple Deprivation [30] or the Welsh Index of Multiple Deprivation [31]. To harmonise deprivation scores across UK nations, each participant was assigned a deprivation decile (ranging from 1 [most deprived] to 10 [least deprived]) based on their home postcode. UK region of assessment centre was represented as a categorical variable comprising 11 regions: nine regions in England, plus single regions for Scotland and Wales.

Glycaemic status was categorised using fasting glucose concentrations according to the American Diabetes Association criteria [32]: normoglycaemia (< 5.6 mmol∙L−1), impaired fasting glucose (5.6–6.9 mmol∙L−1), and type 2 diabetes (≥ 7.0 mmol∙L−1). Hypertension status (yes/no) was defined using the European Society for Hypertension guidelines [24], with hypertension defined as SBP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics v29 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois). The distribution of continuous variables was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and by visually inspecting histograms. Descriptive statistics are reported as means ± standard deviations (SD) for normally distributed variables or medians (interquartile ranges) for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables are summarised as counts [percentages].

The associations of CRF with risk categories of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis were examined using multinomial logistic regression across three sequential models, with the low-risk category specified as the reference group (i.e. odds ratio [OR] = 1.000). Multinomial logistic regression was selected due to the proportional odds assumption being violated for three of the four liver indices, and because it allowed intermediate- and high-risk categories to be modelled separately against the low-risk reference group. Model 1 was minimally adjusted for CRF assessment mode (binary). Model 2 was adjusted for Model 1 adjustments plus age (continuous), sex (binary), SES (ordinal), assessment year (continuous), and UK region of assessment centre (categorical). Model 3 was finally adjusted for Model 1 and 2 adjustments plus BMI (continuous), TAG (continuous), HDL-c (continuous), glycaemic status (categorical), and hypertension status (binary). To minimise the risk of collider bias and overadjustment, covariates used in the calculation of a given liver index were excluded from models where that index was the outcome. These models should therefore be interpreted as independent of non-overlapping covariates rather than demonstrating independence from all demographic, anthropometric, cardiometabolic, and clinical factors. As some cardiometabolic covariates included in Model 3 may plausibly lie partly on the causal pathway between CRF and liver health, Model 3 should be interpreted as estimating associations conditional on these factors, whereas Model 2 provides a less metabolically adjusted estimate. Model assumptions, including collinearity, were checked and verified.

Effect modification was assessed by including interaction terms individually for age, sex, SES, BMI, TAG, HDL-c, glycaemic status, and hypertension status in Model 3. Stratified analyses (median split for continuous variables) were then used to explore differences in the association between CRF and liver indices across subgroups. Due to low numbers of participants with type 2 diabetes, the impaired fasting glucose and type 2 diabetes categories were combined into a single ‘dysglycaemia’ category for interaction and stratified analyses to ensure adequate statistical power and maintain clinically meaningful comparisons. Where a covariate was not included in the main regression models, formal interactions terms were not tested with that variable to avoid collinearity, however, stratified analyses were still conducted to explore potential effect modification. Effect modification analyses were considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating; therefore, formal correction for multiple comparisons was not applied, and findings should be interpreted cautiously and in the context of the overall pattern of results.

Given that alcohol intake data were unavailable, and to reduce potential confounding, we conducted a sensitivity analysis whereby the main models and effect modification analyses were repeated after excluding participants with biochemical patterns suggestive of heavy alcohol consumption/alcohol-related liver injury: AST/ALT ratio ≥ 2.00 [33] or sex-specific GGT concentrations ≥ 97.5th percentile [34, 35]. The utility of the AST/ALT ratio in capturing potential alcohol-related hepatocellular injury, distinct from isolated metabolic fat accumulation, is further supported by recent preclinical models of combined fructose and ethanol exposure [36]. Percentiles were computed within sex from the full cohort, corresponding to 113 U∙L−1 for males and 63 U∙L−1 for females.

Analyses were conducted using a complete-case approach. All results are presented as ORs with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Exact P-values are reported, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05. In all models, ORs represent the relative odds of being in a higher liver risk category per 1 ml∙kg−1∙min−1 higher CRF, with values < 1.000 indicating lower odds.

The final analytic cohort was predominantly middle-aged, male, and from a high SES, with a generally favourable cardiometabolic profile: 15.9% had obesity, 14.0% had hypertension, 35.5% had impaired fasting glucose, and 1.3% had type 2 diabetes. For the non-invasive liver indices, hepatic steatosis was more common (high-risk prevalence: 25.1% for FLI and 37.0% for HSI), whereas hepatic fibrosis was rarer (high-risk prevalence: 0.9% for FIB-4 and 1.6% for NFS). Median VO2peak was 36.0 (IQR: 12.0) ml∙kg−1∙min−1, consistent with CRF levels expected in a generally healthy, working-age population.

Associations of cardiorespiratory fitness with non-invasive indices of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis

The results of the finally adjusted models (Model 3) are presented in Table 2, while the minimally (Model 1) and partially (Model 2) adjusted models can be found in Supplementary Table S7. For hepatic steatosis (FLI/HSI), each 1 ml∙kg−1∙min−1 higher CRF was associated with 2.7%–4.6% lower odds of being in the intermediate-risk category (P < 0.001), and 7.5%–9.4% lower odds of being in the high-risk category (P < 0.001; Table 2). Effect modification by demographic, cardiometabolic and clinical outcomes

The results of the interaction and stratified analyses for demographic outcomes and BMI are shown in Fig. 1. Across subgroups, the inverse association between CRF and liver risk categories was preserved. Interactions were evident for age (FLI: intermediate- and high-risk; HSI: high-risk; P < 0.001), sex (FLI: intermediate- and high-risk; FIB-4 and NFS: intermediate-risk; P ≤ 0.037), and SES (FLI: high-risk; FIB-4 and NFS: intermediate-risk; P ≤ 0.017). In stratified analyses, associations with steatosis indices were stronger in younger versus older adults and in females versus males. For example, among females, each 1 ml∙kg−1∙min−1 higher CRF corresponded to 3.7%–6.0% lower odds of intermediate steatosis risk and 9.2%–12.0% lower odds of high steatosis risk (FLI/HSI) compared to 2.9%–4.2% and 7.7%–8.9%, respectively, in males. For BMI, formal interaction testing was not undertaken for FLI, HSI, or NFS due to the inclusion of BMI in these indices; however, for HSI, the high-risk category showed a stronger association in adults with higher versus lower BMI. For fibrosis indices, associations were stronger in females versus males and in lower versus higher SES for the intermediate-risk category, while estimates were similar by age.

Sensitivity analysis

In our sensitivity analysis, n = 2,894 (7.4%) participants were excluded based on an AST/ALT ratio ≥ 2.00 or sex-specific GGT concentrations ≥ 97.5th percentile (males: 113 U∙L−1, females: 63 U∙L−1). Overall, the results of the main models were unchanged in the sensitivity cohort, with the inverse associations of CRF with steatosis and fibrosis risk categories being preserved in direction and magnitude (Supplementary Table S8). Furthermore, the results of the effect modification analyses were also preserved, with wider CIs consistent with reduced sample size (Supplementary Table S9). The only exceptions were the sex x CRF interaction for FIB-4 (intermediate-risk) and TAG x CRF interaction for NFS (high-risk) where statistical significance was no longer met (P ≥ 0.05), though stratum-specific estimates remained directionally similar.

Discussion

The primary findings of the present study are that higher CRF was consistently associated with lower odds of hepatic steatosis as well as fibrosis across multiple non-invasive indices. These relationships were consistently stronger in females and were generally weaker in those with a more adverse cardiometabolic profile. Collectively, these findings reinforce the role of CRF as a clinically important marker and potential intervention in preventing progressive SLD with the development of fibrosis.

This is the largest study to date to examine associations between objectively assessed CRF and hepatic steatosis at the population level, demonstrating consistent inverse associations across two validated non-invasive indices (FLI and HSI). Specifically, each 1 ml∙kg−1∙min−1 higher CRF was associated with 3–5% lower odds of intermediate steatosis risk, and 8–9% lower odds of high steatosis risk. These findings align with and extend previous research from smaller clinical and community-based cohorts, ranging from 72 to 15,781 individuals [11–16]. For example, Pälve et al. [15] reported an 8% lower risk of ultrasound-defined hepatic steatosis per 1 ml∙kg−1∙min−1 higher CRF in 463 adults in the Young Finns Study, a magnitude of association closely mirroring the present study. Importantly, our observed relationships were independent of key non-overlapping demographic, clinical, and cardiometabolic factors, suggesting that CRF could reflect a distinct, integrated dimension of cardiometabolic health that is strongly linked to susceptibility to hepatic steatosis. However, given that these indices include BMI in their calculation, and CRF was expressed relative to body mass, these associations may not be fully independent of adiposity, which could partly mediate or contribute to the observed relationships. Future prospective and interventional studies using imaging- or histology-based measures are needed to determine whether improvements in CRF influence hepatic steatosis and/or fibrosis independently of changes in body weight or adiposity.

The mechanisms underpinning the relationship between CRF and hepatic steatosis are likely multifactorial, encompassing physiological adaptations driven by both habitual physical activity and inherent genetic influences [7, 8]. Higher CRF is associated with improved peripheral insulin sensitivity and enhanced mitochondrial function in the liver and skeletal muscle, adaptations that promote increased lipid oxidation and reduced de novo lipogenesis [37, 38]. Contracting skeletal muscle also releases myokines and other exerkines that modulate hepatic and adipose metabolism, enhancing insulin signalling and attenuating inflammation [39]. In parallel, CRF is linked to reduced visceral adiposity, more favourable adipokine profiles, and lower systemic inflammation, collectively limiting hepatic fatty acid flux [38, 40]. While reverse causality cannot be discounted, these adaptations create a metabolic milieu less permissive to hepatic lipid accumulation, providing biological plausibility for the protective associations observed in the present study.

A novel aspect of this study was our comprehensive exploration of effect modification by key demographic, cardiometabolic, and clinical outcomes, which has not been feasible in previous studies with smaller sample sizes. Most consistently, the inverse associations between CRF and hepatic steatosis were stronger in younger adults and females. This could reflect greater metabolic flexibility and disease reversibility earlier in life [41], or sex-related differences in substrate utilisation, adipose distribution, and adipokine signalling [42]. Consistent with previous studies [11, 15], associations for the high steatosis risk category (HSI) were also stronger among individuals with higher BMI, suggesting that CRF may offset some adverse hepatic consequences of excess adiposity. By contrast, Florea et al. [14] recently reported no effect modification by age, sex, or BMI, which may reflect differences in cohort characteristics or limited statistical power for subgroup analyses in their study.

For the cardiometabolic and clinical outcomes, the associations of CRF with hepatic steatosis, particularly FLI, were broadly stronger in those with a more favourable health profile i.e. lower TAG, higher HDL-c, normoglycaemia, and normotension. While precise explanations are uncertain, preserved insulin sensitivity, efficient lipid trafficking, and greater metabolic flexibility in these individuals may strengthen the link between CRF and hepatic lipid handling through enhanced lipid oxidation, reduced de novo lipogenesis, and lower fatty acid flux to the liver [43]. Conversely, weaker relationships in dyslipidaemia, dysglycaemia and hypertension could reflect a ceiling effect, whereby advanced metabolic impairment and reduced flexibility limit the translation of CRF improvements into further reductions in hepatic lipids [44]. Together, these findings suggest that CRF acts synergistically with cardiometabolic health to protect against hepatic steatosis, while benefits may be attenuated in established clinical states.

We also found that CRF was inversely associated with hepatic fibrosis (FIB-4 and NFS), where each 1 ml∙kg−1∙min−1 higher CRF corresponded to 1–3% and 2–6% lower odds of intermediate- and high fibrosis risk, respectively. Compared with steatosis, existing evidence linking CRF and hepatic fibrosis is limited and somewhat inconsistent [14, 17, 18, 37]. In partial agreement, Florea et al. [14] reported an inverse association between CRF and liver stiffness measured by vibration-controlled transient elastography, although this was attenuated after BMI adjustment, while others observed lower CRF with higher histological fibrosis stage in MASLD [17, 18]. Conversely, Afolabi et al. [37] found no independent association in 97 patients with MASLD, underscoring the need for large-scale, non-clinical cohorts to capture the broader spectrum of disease risk before advanced clinical referral. As fibrosis is the principal determinant of disease progression and liver-related outcomes, and has also been linked to cardiovascular complications and mortality [3–5], our findings support the notion that higher CRF may protect against both hepatic and systemic consequences of SLD. This relationship likely reflects shared metabolic pathways between low CRF, metabolic dysfunction, and fibrosis severity, highlighting the need to clarify causal direction and underlying mechanisms.

The clinical interpretation of the fibrosis findings requires caution, as effect sizes were small, particularly for FIB-4, and should not be overinterpreted at the individual level. However, 1 ml∙kg−1∙min−1 represents a small unit of CRF and, when scaled to a 4 ml∙kg−1∙min−1 difference consistent with CRF improvements reported following 12 weeks of exercise training [45], the FIB-4 associations correspond to 2.4% and 6.4% lower odds of intermediate- and high-risk fibrosis, respectively. Although still modest, these associations may be relevant at the population-risk level, particularly in a relatively young, generally healthy cohort with low prevalence of high-risk fibrosis. Comparisons with higher-risk clinical cohorts with greater fibrosis burden are warranted.

Emerging evidence supports a complex interplay between metabolic and systemic mechanisms that may act in both causal directions [15, 17, 46]. Higher CRF may mitigate fibrogenesis through reductions in systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, improved endothelial and microvascular function, and enhanced mitochondrial efficiency [47–49], which in turn can attenuate hepatic stellate cell activation and extracellular matrix deposition [50]. Conversely, fibrosis progression may impair CRF by reducing skeletal muscle perfusion and mitochondrial capacity, promoting sarcopenia, a common feature of advanced liver disease, and contributing to cardiometabolic dysfunction [17, 51]. These reciprocal processes could explain a bi-directional relationship in which low CRF and hepatic fibrosis exacerbate one another. Given that hepatic fibrosis is a key prognostic determinant of both hepatic and extrahepatic outcomes [3], low CRF may therefore represent an important intermediary linking reduced functional capacity with adverse health outcomes in SLD.

For hepatic fibrosis, effect modification was more modest than for steatosis, though several notable associations were observed. Across both FIB-4 and NFS, inverse relationships between CRF and intermediate fibrosis risk were stronger in females and those with lower SES, while relationships for NFS were also stronger in individuals with lower TAG and normoglycaemia. This is consistent with our earlier notion that higher CRF may confer greater benefit in states of preserved metabolic flexibility and lower cardiometabolic burden. However, the comparatively fewer modifiers, particularly for high fibrosis risk, may partly reflect the low prevalence (1–2%) in our generally healthy population compared with high steatosis risk (25–37%). Our results partially align with previous work reporting no effect modification by age or BMI, although stronger associations were observed in males than females [14].

CRF is increasingly recognised as a vital sign that reflects the combined function of multiple organ systems and strongly predicts cardiovascular, metabolic, and all-cause morbidity and mortality [7]. Our findings extend this concept to the liver, highlighting CRF as both a marker of hepatic status, and a modifiable target for prevention and treatment in SLD. Importantly, some cardiometabolic factors in the finally adjusted models may lie partly on the causal pathway between CRF and liver health; therefore, this may provide conservative estimates of the overall relationship. However, given the persistence of the inverse associations with hepatic steatosis and fibrosis, independent of established risk factors, CRF may serve as a robust integrated measure of hepatic and cardiometabolic function not captured by traditional markers. Routine CRF assessment could complement existing tools to enhance risk stratification and early detection of hepatic complications. Notably, CRF can be enhanced through physical activity, with gains of only 1 metabolic equivalent (≈3.5 ml∙kg−1∙min−1) related to 10–20% reductions in cardiovascular events and overall mortality [52]. In people with MASLD, this magnitude of increase in CRF can be achieved in as little as four weeks [6]. Embedding fitness-based approaches into multidisciplinary SLD care may therefore offer additional prognostic and therapeutic value, though prospective and interventional studies are needed to confirm causality and long-term outcomes.

Major strengths of the present study include our large, well-characterised cohort, enabling assessment of effect modification across clinically relevant sub-groups and objective CRF measurement in a real-world healthcare setting. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and directionality, while residual confounding from unmeasured factors, such as alcohol intake and physical activity, cannot be excluded. However, our sensitivity analyses excluding participants with biochemical profiles suggestive of alcohol-related liver injury confirmed the robustness of our findings (recognising that our alcohol related biochemical criteria may not exclude moderate-to-heavy alcohol users without advanced alcoholic hepatitis), and previous work suggests that physical activity and sedentary behaviour exert minimal influence on the CRF–steatosis relationship [12, 13]. Second, the use of surrogate (indirect) liver scores rather than direct histological or imaging measures may have introduced some misclassification, although these indices are well validated for epidemiological research [19–22]. Third, participants were generally healthier and more affluent than the general UK population, reflected by the voluntary health-screening setting, which may have introduced potential self-selection bias and limited generalisability. Finally, the interaction and stratified analyses were exploratory, and formal correction for multiple comparisons was not applied, increasing the possibility of type I error. In addition, the low prevalence of high-risk fibrosis limited precision in these subgroup analyses, particularly for estimates involving the high-risk fibrosis categories. Consequently, effect modification findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require replication in cohorts with larger numbers of participants with clinically significant fibrosis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, higher CRF was associated with lower odds of both hepatic steatosis and fibrosis across multiple validated non-invasive indices in a large, health-screening cohort of UK adults. These inverse associations were most consistently stronger in females and weaker with more adverse metabolic profiles, suggesting greater CRF improvements may be required in more advanced disease states. Further prospective, interventional studies are needed to establish mechanisms and determine to what extent improving CRF can independently modify disease progression and reduce major adverse liver and cardiovascular outcomes.
