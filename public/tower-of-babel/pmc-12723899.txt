Mapping digital transformation and social impact in Italian healthcare: a holistic evaluation of organizational digital maturity
Authors: Caterina Galdiero, Rosario Marrapodi, Stefania Mele, Alessandro Scaletti, Marcello Martinez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextDigital transformation (DT) is a key driver of innovation in the healthcare sector, as it improves efficiency, quality, and patient outcomes. In the Italian public healthcare system, assessing digital organizational maturity is essential to evaluate the readiness and ability to effectively implement DT, particularly in chronic care management.MethodsThis study used a multi-case approach to assess the digital organizational maturity of four Italian healthcare cases: “Silver Age - Multiple Screening”, “Telemedicine for Chronic Disease Care”, “Diabetes Network” and “Digital Healthcare Ecosystem”. The assessment applied a holistic maturity model comprising four sequential stages: awareness, readiness, planning, and execution. Data collection included direct observations, semi-structured interviews with organizational stakeholders, and analysis of institutional documents.ResultsThe analysis revealed different levels of digital maturity across the cases. Notable strengths were observed in strategic alignment and innovation, particularly in the “Digital Healthcare Ecosystem”. However, challenges remain in terms of scalability, interoperability, and comprehensive data management. Cases such as “Silver Age - Multiple Screening” identified critical gaps in stakeholder alignment and process integration, while “Telemedicine for Chronic Disease Care” demonstrated significant results in reducing hospital admissions but encountered barriers to adoption by end users.ConclusionsThe findings highlight the critical role of structured digital maturity assessments in guiding healthcare organizations on their transformation journey. Addressing integration, workforce training, and strategic alignment is essential to achieving scalable and sustainable DT, especially in contexts involving chronic care management. This study underscores the value of a comprehensive maturity model for identifying gaps and providing targeted improvement strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13473-3.Keywords: Digital transformation, Digital organization maturity model, Information communication technology, Information processing view, Public health-care system

Introduction

In the healthcare sector, the recent focus on healthcare networks and integrated care highlights the importance of effectively supporting interdisciplinary cooperation alongside patient care processes. As the healthcare sector is heavily dependent on information and knowledge, information management is crucial in this context [1]. Numerous studies have demonstrated the positive impact of information systems in the healthcare sector (e.g., [16, 56]). These studies will serve as indispensable technology management tools for healthcare organizations to assess their social impact and continuous learning efforts and to help them create more effective roadmaps [36]. In fact, inadequate information management has been identified as one of the main causes of adverse events in medicine. Healthcare organizations need quality information to cope with environmental uncertainty and improve decision-making [8]. The development of information technology (IT) systems that facilitate a continuous flow of information through healthcare processes is complex due to the need to understand the factors that generate information during the process and address them in a coordinated manner through a holistic approach [4, 7]. Therefore, more than any other sector, healthcare requires a true digital transformation as there is a gap between the potential benefits of IT in healthcare and its actual use [5, 30]. However, identifying key challenges requires an assessment of the usefulness of IT and the effectiveness of current IT solutions, including both organizational processes (e.g., medical prescribing and communication of results) and medical treatment processes (e.g., diagnostic and therapeutic procedures for individual patients). By examining the challenges at these different levels, it becomes possible to analyze processes in a holistic and synergistic way. IT systems supporting processes, and in particular health information systems (HIS), are recognized as fundamental to improving both individual health and provider performance, resulting in improved quality, cost savings, and greater patient involvement in their own well-being [39]. The recent digital transformation process in the Italian healthcare sector is based on two main catalysts that are driving investment in IT systems: the growing burden of chronic diseases with a substantial increase in costs and the recognition of the need to significantly improve the quality and safety of healthcare [57]. These catalysts are fueling investment in HIS within the sector [15].

This article argues that digital transformation cases require integrated information management at various levels. In this study, we focus on four emblematic cases within the Italian public healthcare system—Silver Age - Multiple Screening, Telehealth for Chronic Care, Diabetes Network, and Digital Health Ecosystem—selected for their heterogeneity in geographic distribution (North, Center, and South Italy), care areas (chronic diseases, elderly care, and general services), and levels of technological advancement. These cases were not chosen randomly, but rather through purposive sampling based on their participation in a national ministerial evaluation process that recognized their relevance and innovation in advancing digital transformation pathways. Galbraith’s information processing theory (1974) helps explain why and how many cases of digital transformation in healthcare fail [9, 17, 40]. The objective of this study is to define and test an assessment model for the implementation of digital transformation by measuring the level of digital organizational maturity of organizations involved in a digital transformation process through a holistic approach [4, 7]. In light of these premises, the study sets out to examine whether the evaluation of digital organizational maturity can effectively facilitate the implementation of digital transformation in healthcare organizations. Building on these considerations, the study aims to investigate three interrelated dimensions of digital transformation in healthcare. First, it seeks to understand whether the assessment of digital organizational maturity can provide healthcare organizations with the necessary diagnostic insight to facilitate their transformation processes and reduce systemic misalignments. Second, it examines the extent to which the holistic digital maturity model (HDMM) proposed by Aras and Büyüközkan [4] can serve as a practical and strategic tool to guide organizations along their digital journey, highlighting areas of strength and priority for improvement. Third, it explores the specific context of chronic disease management, where the demand for continuity of care, integration of multiple providers, and patient engagement creates a particularly sensitive test-bed for evaluating the effectiveness of digital transformation initiatives. Through these lines of inquiry, the research sets out to demonstrate how maturity assessment is not only a theoretical construct, but also a catalyst for sustainable and patient-centered innovation in healthcare systems.

Backgrounds

The digital transformation: a pairing of information system and information processing view within organizations

Digital transformation (DT) in organizations can be interpreted as the result of integrating the information systems view—which conceives information systems as socio-technical infrastructures designed to acquire, store, share, and coordinate information flows—with Galbraith’s information processing view, which argues that organizations achieve high performance when there is a fit between information processing requirements and the available processing capacities [17, 18]. Such fit can be achieved either by reducing information needs through process simplification, the creation of more autonomous units, or the introduction of slack resources, or by increasing information processing capacity through the strengthening of vertical information systems and lateral relations that foster horizontal coordination [17]. The same logic can be found in complementary theoretical perspectives, all aimed at reducing organizational complexity by rationalizing information flows, such as bounded rationality [37], information-processing design [42], and lean thinking [52].

Building on these foundations, this study adopts Galbraith’s perspective to analyze the alignment between information processing requirements and HIS, assessing their effects on performance [17]. This analysis is conducted in the healthcare sector, a context characterized by high uncertainty and interdependence, where the demand for information is particularly intense. Understanding the factors that influence information processing requires a holistic approach that accounts for socio-technical dimensions, coordination mechanisms, roles, and the standardization of clinical and administrative processes, together with the digital maturity of the organization. The integrative view of information in organizations provides the relevant theoretical framework for this study. This perspective identifies three key concepts: information processing requirements, information processing capacities, and the degree of fit between the two as a condition for maximizing performance [7, 17]. Organizations require high-quality information to cope with environmental uncertainty, which arises from environmental complexity and dynamism or from the frequency of changes in key environmental variables [7]. One possible strategy is to redesign organizational processes and implement integrated information systems that streamline information flows and reduce uncertainty across organizational subunits [47]. In this perspective, maturity models—introduced over the past fifty years as guidelines and frameworks for the management of information systems—have been widely applied in healthcare, where they provide a reference point for addressing the challenges, complexities, and high implementation requirements associated with HIS. Moreover, recent studies highlight that maturity models are essential in guiding digital transformations. For instance, a nationwide analysis showed that digital maturity models enable hospitals to navigate digital transformation and strategically prioritize investments [19].

These models therefore provide healthcare organizations with structured pathways to progressively align information processing capacities with the increasing demands for efficiency, safety, and quality of care. By linking process redesign with maturity models, the healthcare context demonstrates that digital transformation is not merely a technological adoption, but rather a gradual and systemic process of organizational learning and adaptation.

Digital transformation is a challenge for the healthcare sector

Digital transformation is an important opportunity for organizations, particularly healthcare organizations, that are engaged in initiating a digital change process. Such transformation is seen as an opportunity for the application of new digital technologies to facilitate significant organizational improvements, leading to a better customer/patient experience and streamlined operations or the development of new business and care models [48]. It is a process that involves different areas of the organization, identifies organizational needs, designs new processes or redesigns existing ones using digital technologies to deliver value to customers/patients, businesses and other key stakeholders [44]. Typically, the transformation component involves substantial changes in the organization in terms of structure and strategies [33]; it can also be seen as a journey of organizations to create value by bringing together internal and external capabilities to achieve their goals with digital solutions. Therefore, DT involves social, technological, and managerial aspects at various levels of the organization, so the success of a digital transformation process in an organization should be managed from a holistic perspective [25, 43]. Here, we understand holistic to mean a systemic approach that encompasses strategy, governance, technology, organizational structure, and culture as interdependent dimensions [4]. DT is the last step in a process that begins with digitization and then transforms into digitalization [23, 43]. Digitization is defined as the encoding of analogue data into a digital format, which enables computers to store, process, and disseminate that information, whereas digitization involves organizational processes and refers to how information technology or digital technologies can be used to change existing business processes [43]. Digital transformation, in turn, extends beyond these stages by reshaping organizational structures, cultures, and business models to generate new forms of value [44]. IT is a primary driver of digitization because it offers new business opportunities by transforming processes such as distribution, communication, and business relationship management [23, 43]. DT, on the other hand, represents an even later step by redesigning critical processes to increase the business approach to value creation [20, 23]. Organizations face the challenge of identifying appropriate digital approaches and actions during their DT journey, as they must navigate both complex information and complex organizational structures. The lack of research on how organizations can systematically adopt DT is another critical factor in the digital transformation process, so organizations often face several problems, including cultural and managerial gaps as well as poor integration between IT and other organizational processes [6]. Successful DT requires organizations to retrain their human resources, adjust their culture, promote closer connections between IT and organizational processes, and meticulously measure digital value [2]. The success of DT depends not only on technology but also on the strategies implemented to change organizational processes [21, 24]. This digital transformation process varies depending on the level of maturity of the organization that must accommodate this change process. Therefore, the success of DT depends on the manager’s support from organizational processes, coordination systems from corporate strategy [29].

DT in healthcare sector is not merely a matter of introducing new technologies, but rather a profound reconfiguration of structures, processes, and mindsets. The sector, traditionally anchored to routines, protocols and professional logics that safeguard reliability and human care, now faces the need to integrate vast flows of data, interoperable platforms, and connected infrastructures. This passage is fraught with challenges: the sensitivity of patient information makes data protection and cybersecurity particularly critical, as healthcare is more vulnerable than other industries to the risks of breaches and cyber-attacks [10, 27]. At the same time, the costs associated with large-scale digital conversion and the difficulty of ensuring interoperability across fragmented systems slow down adoption, often colliding with a managerial culture focused on short-term returns rather than long-term efficiency gains [38]. Even where technological solutions are available, their effectiveness depends on organizational readiness and on the ability of professionals to accept and use new tools, overcoming resistance rooted in consolidated practices and a cautious approach to change [22]. In this sense, digitalization is not a neutral process of modernization, but a transformative challenge that touches upon ethical, cultural, and organizational dimensions, requiring investments in human capabilities, strategic vision, and a rethinking of the very meaning of healthcare in the twenty-first century.

The maturity model

Maturity models are based on the assumption that individuals, organizations, functional areas, and processes develop or grow, progressing through distinct levels of maturity [21, 24]. Digital maturity is closely related to DT and is defined by Gökalp and Martinez [20] as the state in which an organization’s digital technology has transformed its activities, involving skills and organizational structures. However, it is also defined as a process of change that has succeeded in resolving critical issues related to digital business factors [23]. Only later was the concept of maturity linked to that of DT, introducing the concept of digital maturity. Teichert [41] uses the term DT maturity to identify the link between DT and digital maturity, including technological and managerial components. Wagner and colleagues [45] define digital maturity as the level of organization in implementing digital technologies and processes to improve business performance and enable DT [45]. Based on these definitions, digital maturity can be summarized as a critical indicator that reveals the degree of maturity of adaptation to DT. Assessing organizational digital maturity is a process of evaluating its capabilities, readiness, and progress in implementing technologies to transform organizational processes and remain competitive. This assessment involves analyzing an organization in key areas, such as strategy, culture, processes, technology, and data analytics, using various frameworks, models, and tools to measure the organization’s level of digital maturity. According to [24, 28, 34, 49], assessing an organization’s digital maturity is critical to the growth and development of its business model. The process allows organizations to identify critical issues and provides them with detailed information on areas that need improvement to remain competitive. The organizational maturity analysis tool supports effective, scalable, and sustainable digital transformation in the healthcare sector by comprehensively assessing processes, information management, and knowledge management interventions at various levels of the healthcare system [45]. However, digital organizational maturity in chronic care has been insufficiently assessed [5]. This study assessed digital organizational maturity in patients with chronic diseases in Italy. The choice of this context is not accidental: chronic conditions, by their very nature, require continuity of care, integration between multiple providers, and active patient engagement over time. These features make chronic care a particularly critical field for testing digital maturity, since it is here that the capacity of information systems to support coordination, interoperability, and knowledge sharing is most visible. By linking the organizational challenges of chronic care with the maturity assessment model, the study demonstrates how digital transformation can serve as both a diagnostic tool and a driver of change, enabling healthcare organizations to identify systemic gaps and design targeted strategies for more sustainable and patient-centered care.

Research methodology: preliminary issues

The approach adopted was designed to respond to the need to manage information processes that simultaneously influence both information management processes and patient care processes in the evaluation of information system support. Thanks to its potential to foster common understanding and promote the modelling of digital transfer in the healthcare sector, it is possible to assess the organizational maturity of the information processes that IT is capable of supporting [32].

The methodology adopted for this work is that of a multi-case study. The careful, in-depth, and detailed study and examination of a topic, as well as the relevant contextual conditions [54], allows for the processing of information and the description of a phenomenon [14, 26, 31, 55].

By collecting data from four cases and using the digital organizational maturity model, it is possible to obtain more robust results [13, 14, 55]. Four independent cases were selected for the study. The purpose of this study is to test a digital organizational maturity assessment model proposed by Aras and Büyüközkan in 2023 [4], which is useful for the process of activating digital transformation in the healthcare context. These cases were selected based on a targeted sample representing northern, central, and southern Italy. These are healthcare organizations from a panel of the Italian Ministry of Health that underwent a ministerial evaluation process that activated digital transformation procedures to support the healthcare sector in improving the quality of patient services, with a significant impact on the quality of care provided by the Italian healthcare service.

These public companies were distributed throughout Italy, including the north, south, and centre of the country. However, each selected case is unique in terms of its operation, characteristics, and different levels of technological implementation. With regard to the level of adoption of information systems to support patient care, some public companies were at an advanced stage, while others are at a moderate or discreet level of implementation.

Data collection includes the following:

The research methodology integrates semi-structured interviews, direct observations, and document analysis within a mixed-methods design, enabling the triangulation of qualitative and quantitative evidence. This approach supports a holistic exploration of the phenomenon under study, capturing both the phenomenological perspectives of participants and the contextual dynamics of the case. By combining multiple data sources and analytical lenses, the study enhances the depth, validity, and interpretive richness of its findings [11].

In this study, we apply the “Holistic Digital Maturity Model” developed by Aras and Büyüközkan [4], which outlines a structured four-phase path—awareness, readiness, planning, and execution—for guiding organizations through digital transformation. The authors, after analysing an organization’s decision to initiate DT as part of a strategy to achieve its goals, applied a holistic model of organizational digital maturity that can reveal the extent and scope of DT, initiating a process of assessment of the level of organizational digitization to identify fit and misfit and identify areas for improvement that require new approaches to implement the intent digital transformation process.

The digital organizational maturity assessment model the authors used was selected and applied to answer the following research questions:

RQ1: Does the evaluation of digital organizational maturity in healthcare organizations facilitate digital transformation?

RQ2: Does the holistic model of digital organizational maturity developed by Aras and Büyüközkan promote and facilitate the activation of digital transformation in healthcare organizations?

RQ3: Does digital transformation influence healthcare systems for chronic and fragile patients?

To answer the research questions, authors use the model by Aras and Büyüközkan [4], which will be explained in the following section, and apply the model to four selected cases: “Silver Age - Multiple Screening”, “Telehealth for Chronic Care”, “Diabetes Network” and “Digital Health Ecosystem”, all of which are part of the Italian public healthcare sector.

This model features 6 dimensions and 24 subdimensions, which include not only all the dimensions and subdimensions of existing models but also all the issues that need to be addressed on a DT journey.

The Digital Strategy dimension assesses how corporate vision, strategic direction, and DT goals can create value. This encompasses a subdimension of “vision”, which explores the concept and understanding of DT; “strategies”, which focus on the strategic approach and potential competitive advantages; “business model”, which delves into the value propositions and expansion plans in existing offerings due to digitization; “operational model”, which investigates the impact on current operational frameworks; and “leadership”, which examines how digitization initiatives are adopted by organizational leaders.

The Digital Value dimension evaluates the impact of DT on the value delivered to customers and the differentiation in customer value processes. Its subdimensions include “creating value”, which looks at how digitization can foster new value for customers; “delivering value”, which explores the ability to introduce innovative products or services; and “capturing value”, which assesses the market reach and competitive edge gained through digital innovations.

Digital Processes, the third dimension, assess the extent of DT implementation within organizational processes. This includes “process architecture and business process management”, which considers how strategic planning shapes process digitization; “value chain”, which focuses on digitization’s effects on the value chain and interposes interactions; “core processes” which examines fundamental operational areas affected by digitization; and “management and support processes”, which evaluate changes in administrative and support functions due to digitization.

The Digital Technology & Data dimension addresses the sustainability of technologies and solutions that facilitate DT, including “data and analytics”, which scrutinizes how data-driven decision-making is transformed by digitization; “management information systems and applications”, which concern the selection and management of digital solutions; and “security and risk management”, which focuses on how digitization-associated risks are managed.

The Digital Work dimension examines the implications of DT for organizational work models and required employee competencies. It includes “work style”, analysing changes in how work is conducted; “skills and capabilities”, identifying necessary employee skills for digitization; “training and development”, focusing on employee development strategies; and “collaboration”, evaluating how digitization influences teamwork and technology integration.

Finally, the Digital Governance dimension explores the management of managerial and cultural shifts essential for DT success and sustainability. It encompasses “organization and roles” examining structural and role changes due to digitization; “program management” detailing the oversight of digitization efforts; “culture” assessing the cultural shifts driven by digitization; “ethics”, addressing the ethical considerations of digital initiatives; and “ecosystems”, focusing on managing external interactions and ensuring that these interactions add value. The inclusion of these six dimensions is particularly relevant for the present research, as they provide a comprehensive framework to capture the multifaceted nature of digital transformation in healthcare. Each dimension reflects a critical area in which healthcare organizations experience both opportunities and challenges: from defining strategic vision and creating value, to redesigning processes, adopting technologies, developing workforce skills, and ensuring effective governance.

Maturity level assessment

For each dimension, the object, subject, and information technology were identified and evaluated through a process analysis [11]. Interviews with process managers and analysis of internal documents related to the same processes provided the information needed for each dimension in order to carry out an assessment. Information from different levels and archive data were useful for cross-checking and verifying data reliability [55]. The interviews with process managers were conducted remotely, using a semi-structured format that allowed for both cross-case comparability and flexibility to capture context-specific insights. Each session lasted between 60 and 120 min and was carried out via secure video conferencing platforms. The interview guide used in the study was developed specifically for this study (see Appendix A). With participants’ consent, all interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed through qualitative content coding to ensure consistency and reliability across cases. The analysis of internal documents included strategic and operational materials such as organizational charters, ministerial reports, internal accounting data, project manuals, and process evaluation reports. The assessment assigned a value from 0 to 3 relating to the level of maturity (Table 1). The purpose of the interview contributed to the completion and validation of a maturity model for the implementation of healthcare information systems for the provision of care.

Level of digital organizational maturity

The assessment tool of Aras and Büyüközkan [4] was adapted by the authors for the assessment and measurement of the digital organizational maturity of the analysed healthcare organizations. Each maturity score (0–3) across the six dimensions was operationalized based on a structured evaluation grid. For example: in the “Digital Strategy” dimension, from interviews and analysis of company documents, the score of: “0” indicates the absence of any strategic digital vision; “1” reflects a loosely defined strategy not fully aligned with organizational goals; “2” represents a clearly articulated strategy embedded in governance structures; “3” corresponds to a system-wide strategic approach with measurable objectives and broad stakeholder alignment.

While, in the “Digital Work” dimension, based on interviews and analysis of company documents, the score of: “0” indicates the absence of any digital tool; “1” is assigned if some digital tools are adopted without workforce training, “2” if training is sporadic and limited to certain teams, “3” if an organization-wide training strategy exists and is monitored over time. Each interview and document analysis were matched against these benchmarks using qualitative content coding, ensuring consistency across the cases.

Case studies: “Silver Age–Multiple Screening”

The case aims to screen for frailty in the elderly via validated tools for multidimensional assessment, following a biopsychosocial approach, and to implement prevention and health promotion programs and telemonitoring with the help of IT tools.

Elderly individuals, after an outpatient visit for the prescription of adapted physical activity, are enrolled in a telemonitoring program with medical devices and smartwatches integrated with the hospital’s medical records and a shared care plan. Through a mobile app, patients can access their therapeutic plan and a personalized exercise program. Elderly people participate in health and psychological well-being promotion activities, with biweekly meetings on nutrition, mindfulness sessions, and fall prevention.

Patients are referred to the clinic by specialists responsible for the patient at the hospital-university structure. The program, which adopts an integrated approach supported by ICT and based on telemonitoring, aims to promote better home management of the clinical condition of patients over 65 years of age, providing specific individualized programs for nutrition, physical activity, and adherence to medical therapy.

Case studies: “Telehealth for chronic care”

“Telehealth for chronic care” is a case focused on integrated social and health care for the management of chronic diseases through the use of ICT tools. The central objective of the case is to conduct an experiment aimed at demonstrating the effectiveness of a structured remote telemonitoring model, combined with social and health care, with the goal of reducing the number of hospitalization days by creating a sustainable approach in terms of human and organizational resources.

The case is based on the use of a platform that allows the monitoring and transmission of vital patient data to a central database via innovative technologies to offer teleassistance and telemonitoring services. Specifically, medical equipment and wireless home detection sensors have been implemented and connected to a control unit in the patient’s home. These devices can include scales, blood pressure monitors, electrocardiographs, glucometers, pulse oximeters, and sensors for detecting smoke fires, flooding, and temperature. The data recorded by these devices are collected by the control unit and automatically transmitted to an operations center, which then forwards them to the general practitioner and the district team.

Additionally, videoconferencing systems have been made available to patients for remote communication with operators, portable devices with an emergency call button, and a wearable device for continuous movement monitoring and fall detection. Key elements of the case also include the empowerment of patients and their caregivers, promoting the interaction between individuals and technological resources to foster integrated social and health care supported by ICT platforms.

In conclusion, “Telehealth for Chronic Care” has demonstrated the success of an innovative care model based on advanced ICTs aimed at improving home care for elderly patients with chronic diseases, contributing to a reduction in hospitalization days and promoting greater autonomy and satisfaction among patients.

Case studies: “Diabetes Network”

The “Diabetes Network” is a case created to manage type 1 and type 2 diabetes in a coordinated and synergistic manner at the regional level. This initiative involves the establishment of 15 adult centers and 1 pediatric center, which are uniformly distributed throughout the region, easily accessible, and operate 5 or 6 days a week. The general objective is to standardize the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of diabetes across the territory, ensuring equity in both access to care and the level of assistance, as well as continuity of care and widespread coverage, respecting the principle of proximity and in line with the goals set by D.M. 77/2022.

The case has a strong digital component, which is its most valued aspect. It includes the creation of a shared diabetes medical record, updated by all diabetes centers in the region, and the use of a dedicated app by patients, available on a PC/tablet or smartphone. This app provides data summaries, alerts, glucometer data monitoring, diet and physical activity tracking, educational materials, and communication tools. The use of the app is also essential for encouraging patients’ responsibility in self-managing their condition and self-monitoring its progression, thereby promoting empowerment.

The case also includes the involvement of experienced patients in the practice pathways, who, in collaboration with general practitioners and diabetes specialists at the centers, can act as tutors for newly enrolled patients.

The practice employs multidisciplinary teams, consisting of various professional figures capable of providing comprehensive management of the disease: diabetologists, dietitians, diabetes nurses, podiatrists, and psychologists.

Specifically, to ensure the overall improvement of care and patient management, the case pursues the following specific objectives:

An integrated management model for the prevention and management of diabetes and its complications should be developed.

A network of diabetes centers connected by shared electronic medical records should be developed.

Provide person-centered care, taking into account the needs of patients and their families, culture/religion, capabilities, and adherence to various therapies through a multidisciplinary team.

Case studies: “Digital Health Ecosystem”

The platform, named the “Digital Health Ecosystem” is an innovative Italian digital health system designed to integrate and enhance the management of information and healthcare services at the regional level in a region of northern Italy. The platform may be considered a true ecosystem of applications and devices that support citizens in their daily health and care management and provide healthcare institutions with the necessary tools to develop advanced care models on the basis of e-health technologies. The applications, both web and mobile, serve as the platform’s front-end component and, operating across various devices, are connected through a common middleware platform. This middleware is designed to be “open,” exposing Application Programming Interfaces (APIs) that facilitate the development of third-party applications and improve interoperability among them. The underlying approach does not aim to create a single monolithic application managing every aspect but rather an integrated modular system composed of many small applications. These applications, which are part of a broader ecosystem, are distributed across various devices to integrate easily into the daily practices of both citizens and healthcare providers. Through its services, each individual can select the elements best suited to their current needs and change them as their health needs evolve. For example, a woman might typically use an application to manage routine exam reports and, if pregnant with gestational diabetes, might use a dedicated app to maintain a diabetes journal and receive remote monitoring from a specialist, along with an educational app to manage her diet. Additionally, owing to the “personal health diary” functionality, the platform serves as a crucial tool for implementing new care models for chronic patients, centered on the person and based on Diagnostic Therapeutic Care Pathways (DTCP) and the Chronic Care Model (CCM). These models, aligned with the latest national plans for chronicity management, view new technologies as enabling factors that promote patient empowerment, personalized care paths, and the activation of remote monitoring. Finally, the platform provides healthcare providers with a “digital environment,” named the “dashboard,” consisting of web and mobile applications, which allows for secure communication and data sharing of citizens’ health data.

Results – Emerging results on digital maturity

Through the interviews and document analysis for each case, a maturity level, described in Table 3, was assigned to each dimension. Following the holistic approach by Aras and Büyüközkan [4], a digital organizational maturity assessment was performed for each case.

Case 1: Digital maturity of “Silver Age - Multiple Screening”

Digital strategy (level 3)

Concerning the digital strategy for Silver Age - Multiple Screening was positively evaluated at level 3 for its clear vision and strategic direction, with a focus on improving the quality of life for the elderly through integrated care and telemonitoring. However, the interviewees highlighted difficulties in aligning the strategy among all stakeholders, as some may be affected by the digital divide. This gap has led to uneven implementation and variable success in different cases.

Digital value (level 1)

The value created through the Silver Age case is significant, particularly in terms of patient empowerment and preventive care. The interviewees noted that the telemonitoring program led to improvements in health outcomes and a reduction in hospital visits. However, there are challenges in capturing this value, especially in terms of cost savings and scalability.

Digital processes (level 1)

The integration of digital processes in Silver Age is moderate, with notable success in telemonitoring and health promotion activities. The interviewees indicated that although the core processes are well digitalized, internal communication and personnel assessment processes still rely heavily on fragmented information, hindering internal coordination. Additionally, the technological capabilities of the target patients limit overall efficiency.

Digital technology & data (level 2)

This case employs a range of digital technologies and ICT systems for telemonitoring and patient engagement. The interviewees praised the use of smartwatches and mobile apps but noted that data analysis capabilities are still under development. Better integration and data analysis could enhance decision-making and patient care.

Digital work (level 2)

Digital transformation has moderately impacted work models, with a focus on improving collaboration and interaction with patients. The interviewees noted that training and development initiatives are essential but currently underfunded, limiting the overall impact of digital work. An incentive policy could complement the introduction of digital systems to enhance personnel management.

Digital governance (level 2)

The governance practices for the Silver Age case are still in development, with interviewees noting the need for clearer long-term objectives and more consistent governance structures. Current governance, through better planning and more accurate management of ICT, could achieve better results over time.

Discussion: comparative analysis across case studies

The discussion of findings is grounded in the theoretical framework of digital maturity models, which have been widely recognized as essential tools for assessing and guiding digital transformation across organizations [4]. In the healthcare sector, digital transformation is particularly complex, as it involves not only technological change but also organizational, cultural, and relational shifts [35]. Chronic and fragile patients represent a critical testing ground for these transformations, given the high demand for care continuity, provider integration, and patient engagement [46].

The analysis of organizational digital maturity represented in the spider diagrams for each case has allowed us to better understand specific areas for improvement. The analysis of the four case studies, “Silver Age - Multiple Screening,” “Telehealth for Chronic Care,” “Diabetes Network,” and “Digital Health Ecosystem,” reveals significant variability in the digital maturity levels of Italian healthcare organizations. As shown by the spider diagram in Fig. 7, the differences in maturity levels clearly emerge.

Spider diagram overview of the case studies

The four case studies analyzed—Silver Age – Multiple Screening, Telehealth for Chronic Care, Diabetes Network, and Digital Health Ecosystem—offer a multifaceted and stratified portrait of digital maturity within Italian healthcare organizations. These cases not only highlight differing levels of advancement across the six dimensions of the Aras and Büyüközkan [4] model, but also narrate stories of distinct geographic, institutional, and cultural contexts, each with its own evolutionary trajectories, inertial resistances, and moments of acceleration.

The case that most clearly emerges as a benchmark of positive governance is the Digital Health Ecosystem, developed in a region of Northern Italy. Here, digital transformation has found fertile ground due to a virtuous combination of factors: a large population base (over 2,000,000 individuals), a consolidated healthcare infrastructure, a regional culture oriented toward innovation, and, most critically, a robust, multi-level governance capable of orchestrating actors and processes in a systemic fashion. What distinguishes this case from the others is not solely the breadth or sophistication of the adopted digital tools—from integrated apps to professional dashboards, from personal health diaries to a modular ecosystem of micro-apps—but rather the institutional coherence that underpins and sustains the entire architecture.

In the Digital Health Ecosystem, regional governance played a catalytic role, articulating a strategic vision that transcended the logic of temporary pilot projects to establish a structural, interoperable, and adaptable platform. The presence of a regional digital agency, with both operational mandate and coordinating authority, ensured continuity in investment, convergence of digital and healthcare policies, and a clear delineation of roles and responsibilities. A further enabler in this case was strong leadership commitment. Interviews with executives revealed that leadership at both the health authority and political levels consistently prioritized digital health, allocating stable funding and supporting adaptive models such as modular platform design. The system’s flexible architecture—built on open APIs and microservices—ensured that new functionalities, such as AI-powered predictive services and telemonitoring tools, could be seamlessly integrated over time, particularly in chronic disease management pathways. Political leadership, in turn, demonstrated both foresight and pragmatism, promoting digitalization as a strategic lever for sustainability and personalization of care, in alignment with the principles of the Chronic Care Model and the broader vision of community-based medicine.

The situation is markedly different in the Silver Age – Multiple Screening case, located in Southern Italy. Despite the potential outreach of more than one million citizens, the project faces structural, infrastructural, and cultural barriers. The digital divide is especially tangible in this context—not only among patients, who are mostly elderly and exhibit low digital literacy, but also among healthcare professionals, who often lack structured training or institutional support in adopting digital tools. Moreover, the absence of a unified regional framework means that governance is delegated to individual entities, resulting in weak coordination and fragmented vision. As confirmed by the interviews, the initiative—although well-conceived at the local level—struggles to evolve into a systemic model due to the lack of broader institutional momentum that could ensure replicability and scalability.

The Telehealth for Chronic Care case, also situated in Northern Italy and addressing a population base of over two million individuals, might reasonably be expected—based on geographic location alone—to exhibit a maturity level comparable to that of the Digital Health Ecosystem. However, a direct comparison between the two reveals a significant divergence. What is lacking in the Telehealth for Chronic Care case is a modular architectural design and a strong institutional governing body. Its trajectory was more functional than strategic: the project focused on specific outcomes, such as the reduction in hospitalization days, without engaging in a comprehensive redesign of underlying organizational processes. Technological adoption was sporadic, reactive, and frequently imposed in a top-down manner, without structured engagement of healthcare workers or patients. Despite the presence of technologically sophisticated solutions—such as environmental sensors, home control units, and wearable devices—the absence of a shared digital culture and a cohesive regulatory and policy framework limits the system’s integration and sustainability.

More balanced is the Diabetes Network case, developed in Central Italy, with a potential impact on more than one million individuals. This case presents a clear strategic vision supported by a network of sixteen adult and pediatric diabetes centers that collaborate through a shared electronic medical record and digital tools designed for therapeutic education and self-monitoring. The regional governance, although not as structured as in the Digital Health Ecosystem, played an important role in promoting the harmonization of care pathways and the standardization of operational processes. However, the high number of actors involved, the organizational heterogeneity across centers, and the persistence of bureaucratic barriers slow down uniform implementation. Technology adoption, though advanced, is not yet accompanied by systematic training programs for staff nor by a comprehensive communication strategy aimed at increasing patient involvement and digital empowerment.

From this comparative reading, several key lessons emerge. First, digital maturity cannot be measured solely in terms of technological tools implemented; rather, it depends decisively on each system’s ability to embed technology within organizational, cultural, and relational processes. Second, the regional context matters profoundly: regions with a history of strong governance, integrated health and digital policies, and a culture of evidence-based planning demonstrate greater ability to adopt systemic and sustainable approaches. Third, leadership—both political and administrative—is a critical driver: where leadership is visible, competent, and forward-looking, digital transformation initiatives tend to be more coherent, adaptive, and generative.

The comparative analysis confirms that maturity is not solely the result of technical sophistication, but rather emerges from a systemic alignment across ten critical dimensions (see Table 4).

Critical dimensions of organizational digital maturity

Ultimately, what distinguishes a digital project from a true process of digital transformation is the ability to redesign organizational architectures, rebuild alliances between professionals and citizens, and embed technology within a humanistic and sustainable vision of care. Digitalization, if guided by principles of equity, transparency, and participation, can become a tool of emancipation and social justice; otherwise, it risks amplifying preexisting inequalities and creating a two-tiered healthcare system.

The holistic model applied in this study has proven useful not only for measuring digital maturity but also for understanding its deeper determinants. It provides both a conceptual and operational map to guide healthcare organizations toward more advanced forms of technological and relational integration. Where applied rigorously and with a transformative spirit, it can assist public policies in constructing a future in which digital care is not a privilege for the few but a reliable, accessible, and universal right.

Conclusions

This study offers several empirical and practical implications for policymakers, healthcare administrators, and organizational designers engaged in digital transformation processes within the Italian public healthcare sector. The analysis was conducted in a field that is particularly emblematic of the challenges and opportunities of digitalization. Building on the holistic maturity model of Aras and Büyüközkan [4], the research sought to examine how maturity assessment can be used not only to evaluate the state of digital health but also to trigger processes of organizational learning and transformation. The model’s ability to map maturity across multiple dimensions (strategy, governance, digital work, and data integration) enables more targeted and evidence-based interventions, especially in chronic care settings where coordination and continuity are critical. In relation to RQ1, the findings indicate that the evaluation of digital organizational maturity serves as more than an assessment tool: it facilitates digital transformation by making visible the gaps and misalignments that hinder progress. For example, in the “Telehealth for Chronic Care” case, maturity assessment highlighted weaknesses in interoperability and professional training, which then became focal points for organizational reflection and improvement. Similarly, the “Diabetes Network” revealed that evaluation can expose fragmented governance structures and insufficient patient engagement, prompting initiatives aimed at reconfiguring care pathways.

RQ2 focused on the potential of the holistic maturity model developed by Aras and Büyüközkan [4] to promote and facilitate digital transformation. The results clearly suggest that the model functions not merely as a diagnostic framework but as a dynamic catalyst for change. In the “Silver Age – Multiple Screening” case, the model encouraged administrators to reconsider strategic funding choices and governance arrangements, thus initiating conversations that had long been absent. In the “Digital Health Ecosystem,” the model served as a common language across different stakeholders—clinicians, managers, policymakers—helping to structure dialogue around interoperability and inclusion. In this sense, the model provided a scaffolding for collective learning and organizational alignment. RQ3 examined whether digital transformation influences healthcare systems for chronic and fragile patients. The evidence strongly confirms that it does: digital tools, when properly integrated, enhance coordination among providers, support continuity of care, and empower patients to participate more actively in managing their conditions. In the “Diabetes Network,” digital platforms improved information sharing between primary care and specialist units, leading to more timely interventions. In the “Telehealth for Chronic Care” initiative, patients reported greater accessibility to follow-up care, reducing the burden of unnecessary hospital visits. Yet the findings also reveal challenges: in the “Digital Health Ecosystem,” digital divides risked excluding older or less technologically literate patients, raising concerns that digitalization may exacerbate inequalities if inclusion strategies are not adequately developed.

Taken together, these findings offer several theoretical contributions. First, they demonstrate that digital maturity models should be interpreted not as static measurement instruments but as dynamic devices that intertwine evaluation with organizational transformation. Second, they advance the literature on healthcare digitalization by showing that chronic care is a particularly sensitive lens through which systemic organizational weaknesses are revealed, thus providing valuable insights for broader reforms. Third, the research highlights the importance of viewing digital maturity as a socio-technical construct, where technological infrastructures must be aligned with governance mechanisms, professional practices, and patient engagement.

Limits and future research

Despite these contributions, the study presents several limitations. The multicase design, while rich in contextual insights, does not allow for statistical generalization across all Italian healthcare organizations. The selected cases, though diverse, were chosen through purposive sampling and may not fully capture the range of digital maturity levels present nationwide. Furthermore, the assessment relied heavily on qualitative data obtained through interviews and document analysis, which introduces potential subjectivity in the interpretation of maturity scores, despite the use of structured grids.

Another limitation concerns the temporal nature of digital transformation processes. The study offers a cross-sectional snapshot rather than a longitudinal understanding of how maturity evolves over time. Future research could benefit from longitudinal designs to assess how interventions in digital strategy or governance translate into improved maturity trajectories. Lastly, the model’s application is context-specific and may require adaptation for use in other health systems or non-European settings with differing institutional, cultural, or infrastructural conditions.

Overall, this study contributes to advancing the conceptual and operational understanding of digital maturity within healthcare organizations, offering a structured lens through which practitioners can interpret transformation processes and identify systemic levers for change. Nevertheless, to ensure that the proposed model transcends its current empirical boundaries and becomes a robust instrument for guiding strategic choices, further empirical replication in diverse healthcare contexts and continuous methodological refinement are essential. Only through such iterative validation can the model’s capacity to inform policy formulation and managerial decision-making be fully realized and sustained over time.

Future research should build on the insights of this study by expanding both the empirical scope and the conceptual depth of digital maturity assessment in healthcare. First, there is a need for longitudinal analyses that capture the temporal dynamics of digital transformation, since maturity is not a static condition but an evolving process [12]. Second, future studies should integrate emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence, digital twins, and big data into maturity frameworks, exploring their implications for healthcare governance and patient engagement [3, 53]. Fourth, more systematic attention should be devoted to patient and professional perspectives, especially with regard to digital inclusion, literacy, and motivational factors [50]. Finally, advancing mixed-method approaches that combine qualitative insights with quantitative generalizability could strengthen both theoretical contributions and practical applications in the field of digital health maturity [51].
