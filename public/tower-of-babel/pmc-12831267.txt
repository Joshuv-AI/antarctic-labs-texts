Epidemiology and risk factors of migraine and TTH in Sudanese health sciences students
Authors: Rashid Mohamed Hassan Nasr, Jaber Hamad Jaber Amin, Mustafa Mohamed Majdeldien Mustafa, Mohamedelmustafa Yahya Mohamed Eldouma, Majdy Jailany Alamin Abdelgadir, Ahmed Anwer Othman Atta Elfadil, Tarteel Abazer Musa Bakheit, Mohamed Elhassan Momin Mohamed Elhassan Noreldayem, Ahmed Adil Abdelrahman Mohamed, Shafee S. Almahi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeadache disorders, particularly migraine and tension-type headache (TTH), are common among university students and significantly affect academic performance and quality of life. However, data from Sudanese students remains few.MethodsA descriptive cross-sectional questionnaire-based study was conducted between November 2023 and February 2024 among undergraduate medical, dental, and pharmacy students across Sudan. Using International Classification of Headache Disorders-III (ICHD-3) criteria, we assessed prevalence, risk factors, headache characteristics, triggers, and management. Of 1789 respondents, 746 students with recurrent headaches were included in the analysis.ResultsThe overall prevalence of recurrent headaches was 41.7% (41.7%, 746/1789), comprising migraine (23.8%, 426/1789) and TTH (17.9%, 320/1789). Photophobia associated with 63% of migraine cases, and 18% were associated with nausea/vomiting, while 64% of TTH had no associated symptoms. Age and academic level were significantly associated with migraine with aura, with most cases aged (19–23 years) (p = 0.019) and third-year academic level (p = 0.045). Stress (54%) and sleep deprivation (50%) were the most common triggers, with stress strongly associated with TTH (65%) and sleep deprivation with migraine with aura (67%). Most students relied on over-the-counter analgesics (91%), with limited prescription use (6.3%) and low medical consultation rates (21%). Headaches negatively impacted academic performance, with 19% reporting missed classes or exams, and 27% had impacted mental health by migraine with aura.ConclusionMigraine and TTH are highly prevalent among Sudanese health sciences students, with stress and poor sleep as major modifiable triggers. The high burden of self-medication and low healthcare-seeking behaviors highlight a major care gap. We recommend campus health programs to raise awareness about these common disorders.

Introduction

Migraine and tension type headache (TTH) are common and concerning headache disorders. Globally, TTH is more prevalent than migraine with prevalence of 26% [1, 2], however in the Middle East region and according to the Head-MENAA study 2023 migraine without aura was the commonest primary headache (36.8%) [3] which is in line with a study done in Eastern Sudan [4].

Migraine is more common among the ages of 18 to 50, a time of high productivity and caregiving responsibilities [5] and is considered the seventh disabling disorder [6, 7].

Migraine and TTH have high prevalence among university students due to lifestyle changes that accompany studying, fatigue, anxiety, and stress peak at this level and jeopardize headache [8], with higher prevalence of both types among females [9] possibly due to hormonal changes and menstruation [10].

It was found that recurrent headaches increase the likelihood of future chronic headache and pain syndromes while also recurrent use of analgesics increase the risk of analgesic overuse headache and refractory headache, hence high percentages of student reported to use over the counter drugs without doctor prescription increasing the risk of drug errors, interaction, side effects and overuse [11–14].

The commonest triggers of migraine are sleeplessness, fatigue and smoking [15–17].

Other risk factors include: poor sleep, smoking, food and eating habits, weather and temperature changes, traveling, exercise, menstrual cycle and oral contraceptives [10, 15–17].

Migraine-related disability can be significant. Economic losses due to migraine are linked to missing days and decreased productivity during workdays [18], however TTH is usually less severe, less likely to be seen in healthcare settings and is associated with lower disability levels [19].

Studies were carried among the general population and occupational settings but fewer were carried among university students although they are the most commonly affected age group with most risk factors are found [12, 20–23].

The effects of headache among university students include: poor concentration and low performance, missing more school days, and low grades [18, 23–25]. The burden in student is complicated by the fact that many are underdiagnosed and undertreated compromising the overall performance [26].

In Sudan, data were few regarding primary headache disorders [27]. In addition, knowledge gap about migraine among medical students in Sudan was found, further increasing the burden of these disabling disorder [28].

We aim in this study to assess the prevalence, risk factors, and impact of migraine and tension type headache (TTH) among this high risk and overlooked population of undergraduate health science students in Sudan.

Methodology

This is an analytical cross-sectional questionnaire-based study that was conducted between November 2023 and February 2024. We aim to determine the prevalence of migraine and tension-type headache among Sudanese medical, dental, and pharmaceutical students and associated risk factors.

Study population and sampling

We included all undergraduate students enrolled in medical, dental, and pharmacy programs across universities in Sudan. A Google Forms-based online survey was distributed via e-mail and widely used social media platforms, which included WhatsApp, Telegram, LinkedIn, Messenger, and Facebook. These platforms were especially relevant during our ongoing conflict in Sudan, and have driven much of the education online. The minimum required sample size was calculated using the standard formula for a cross-sectional study with a large population:

A total of 1789 were identified after applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and of these, 746 participants had a headache, which exceeded the sample size.

Data collection tool and procedure

The questionnaire was structured based on the diagnostic framework of the International Classification of Headache Disorders-III (ICHD-3) [29], which was adapted from previously validated instruments used in similar epidemiological studies on headache. Before distribution, the questionnaire was pilot-tested on 20 students to ensure cultural relevance, clarity, and, lastly, the comprehensibility of the questions before the distribution. Their final feedbacks were used to refine the final version of the questionnaire.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

all undergraduate medical, dental, and pharmaceutical students from various universities in Sudan, students who completed the question ‘Did you have a headache two or more times in the last three months?’. While, the exclusion criteria include all student how did not match the criteria of migraine and tension type headache which would be unclassified as: trauma of head and neck, any history of neurological problem (stroke, transient ischemic attack, intracranial hemorrhage or aneurysm, brain tumor, multiple sclerosis, encephalitis, meningitis epilepsy, dementia) any cranial surgical operation, Allergic sinusitis or rhinitis, Acute systematic disorder, and pregnant students. Students whose headache characteristics did not meet the full ICHD-3 criteria for either migraine or TTH were considered to have unclassified headaches and were excluded from the headache subtype analysis.

Data collection tool

We designed the questionnaire in four parts; the first part contains the sociodemographic information (age, gender, faculty, academic year, marital status, residence, living condition, and smoking status). We assessed the headache at the end of this section, with a closed yes or no question: ‘Did you have a headache two or more times in the last three months?’. Students who answered yes were assumed to suffer from a headache by further questions (746 out of 1789). The second section includes questions to determine the type of headache according to the International Classification of Headache Disorders-III (ICHD-3) [29]. All students with headaches that did not match migraine and TTH criteria were considered to have unclassified headaches.

In the third section, we assessed the characteristics of their headache (type of pain, site of the headache, the duration, age at which the headache began, is there is any family history, pain in the neck, effect of headache on daily activities, and the associated trigger factors). In the fourth section, we assessed the management of headache according to student experience (requesting medical consultation, taking analgesics, and using preventive medications). In the last section, we assessed the psychological factors associated with headaches.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R programming (R version 4.4.3) utilizing Tidyverse and Tidyverse-associated packages. p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and a 95% confidence interval was calculated for precision assessment. Categorical variables were summarized using frequency percentages and compared with the type of headache using Pearson’s Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test when appropriate. Numerical variables were summarized using the median and interquartile range and compared using the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test. A multiple logistic regression model was used to evaluate the risk factors for the presence of headache.

Results

Of the 1789 students participated in the study, 746 fulfil the inclusion criteria. Majority of the students with headache aged 21–23 years (51%), female (75%), and medical students (85%). Most were single (94%), lived in urban areas (86%), and resided with family (81%). Smoking prevalence was low (4%) (Table 1).

Prevalence of headaches

746 students (41.7%) had headaches- 426 (23.8%) had migraine and 320 (17.9%) had tension-type headache. Additionally, considering the distribution of headache in the three colleges, 24% of medical students, 27% of pharmacy students, and 17% of pharmacy students had migraine. Similarly, 18%, 17%, and 24% had tension type headache respectively (Table 2; Fig. 1).

Headaches associations & regression analysis

Among participants with migraine, 55 (14.8%) had migraine with aura showed significant associations with age (p = 0.019)- migraine with aura was higher in 19–20-year-olds (35% vs. 17% for migraine without aura). Similarly, academic year showed significant association with migraine (p = 0.045), with third-year students reporting higher migraine-with-aura (22%). Furthermore, Migraine with aura was more common in rural residents (16% vs. 9.7% for migraine without aura, p = 0.13), though not statistically significant (Table 3). On the other hand, tension-type headaches lacked strong demographic associations- age (p = 0.5), gender (p = 0.2), and faculty (p = 0.2) showed no significant associations (Table 4).

In the model predicting overall headache presence, advanced academic years (3rd: OR 0.47, p = 0.006; 4th: OR 0.41, p = 0.002; 5th: OR 0.48, p = 0.011; 6th: OR 0.30, p < 0.001) were significantly associated with lower odds compared to the 1st year, as were married status (OR 0.52, p = 0.028), rural residence (OR 0.73, p = 0.023), and on-campus living (OR 0.66, p = 0.020). For migraine with aura, no variables reached statistical significance, though older age groups (21–23: OR 0.08, p = 0.087; 24–26: OR 0.04, p = 0.056) trended toward lower odds. In the ordinal model for Tension-type headache (TTH) frequency, pharmacy faculty had lower odds (OR 0.26, p = 0.006) and on-campus living to reduced frequency (OR 0.40, p = 0.045) compared to references, with male gender (OR 0.59, p = 0.077) and smoking (OR 2.50, p = 0.057) approaching significance for lower and higher frequencies, respectively (Table 5).

Triggers

Stress (54%, p < 0.001) and lack of sleep (50%, p < 0.001) were the most common triggers. Tension headaches associated strongly with stress (65% vs. 44% for migraine without aura). Migraine with aura was notably associated with lack of sleep (67%, p < 0.001) and hormonal changes (27%, p = 0.2) (Table 6; Fig. 2).

Management and severity

Participants reported higher daily activity disruption (median impact score: 5.00 vs. 3.00 for tension type headache, p < 0.001). Prescription medication use was higher in migraine with aura (13% vs. 1.9% for tension type headache, p < 0.001). However, 36% of students with migraine with aura found medications “completely effective” (vs. 61% for migraine without aura). Furthermore, mental health impact was higher in migraine with aura (27%, p = 0.04) (Table 7).

Discussion

This is a national cross-sectional study that assessed the prevalence of migraine and tension-type headache (TTH) among Sudanese medical, dental, and pharmacy students, determined the associated risk factors, and management patterns. Notably, our findings reveal a high burden of headache disorders in this population, with implications for academic performance, access to healthcare, and targeted public health interventions. Our study, with 746 participants, reported a high overall prevalence of headaches at 41.7% (95% CI: 39.4%-44.0%), which comprises 23.8% (95% CI: 21.8%-25.9%) for migraine (n = 426) and 17.9%% (95% CI: 16.1%-19.8%) for TTH (n = 320), diagnosed based on the ICHD-3 criteria using a self-report questionnaire.

Interestingly, the observed migraine prevalence of 23.8%, derived from self-reported ICHD-3 criteria, is greater than the global average of 14% [29] and regional figures from Saudi Arabia (17.6%) [30], and the African average (10.4%) [31], It is, however, comparable to results in other studies among medical students, such as 26.35% in the UAE [32], and 27.9% in Kuwait [33]. These differences may reflect the higher prevalence, which is typical of high-stress student groups, but in general it is acceptable, because self-report questionnaire-based studies lack the structured diagnostic interview of a clinician. So, this may lead to higher prevalence estimates compared to studies based on physician-diagnosed migraine [29]. However, when comparing our findings with similar epidemiological studies that applied self-report or screening tools among high-pressure health science students, our migraine prevalence (23.8%) is comparable to findings for examples 26.35% reported in the UAE [32], 23.2% among medical students in Saudi Arabia [34], and an ICHD-3 based finding of 22.09% among other Saudi health sciences students [35]. Our finding is higher than the 6.57% reported in medical students in China, which also used a preliminary screening tool [36], and 15.3% in Nepal [37]. Other factors besides the methodological differences, cultural variations in reporting symptoms or perception of pain, and levels or types of stress among students.

Conversely, our TTH prevalence rate of 17.9% is closer to findings in similar student populations, such as Turkey (20.35%) [38]and Nigeria (22.9%) [39]. Though it is lower relative to studies in Iraq (57.3%) [40] and Nepal (69.2%) [37]. Besides that, our investigation revealed prevalence differentials for headache by college (migraine: 24% medicine, 27% pharmacy, 17% dental; TTH: 18% medicine, 17% pharmacy, 24% dental) (Fig. 1). These can sensibly be attributed to discipline-based scholarly demands, curriculum structure, assessment practices, and concomitant psychosocial stressors, which align with Al-Hashel et al. findings regarding disparities across academic study fields [33].

Demographically, migraine with aura (14.8%) was strongly associated with younger age (19–20 years, AOR = 2.1, 95% CI: 1.2–3.8, p = 0.019) and third-year academic level (22%, AOR = 1.8, 95% CI: 1.1–3.1, p = 0.045). These findings indicate that certain stages in development or academic stress during the third year may trigger episodes of migraine with aura. This agrees with earlier studies highlighting age and academic year as risk factors for headaches among students [33, 41].

Regarding the prevalence of migraine with aura in our study (about 14.8% of migraineurs), it was lower than the global range of 20–40%. This finding may reflect under-reporting due to the transient nature of aura symptoms (e.g., visual disturbances) or Cultural factors, such as attributing neurological symptoms to stress or fatigue. Also lack of awareness among Sudanese students about transient neurological symptoms, as observed in other questionnaire-based studies [42, 43], so more education on the phenotypic spectrum of migraine is needed.

The relatively low prevalence of aura in our cohort compared to global estimates of 20–40% may reflect underreporting due to a lack of awareness of aura symptoms. Regional variations in migraine subtypes also have been documented; for example, studies in Saudi Arabia [35, 42] and Turkey [38] reported similarly low aura prevalence (11.6–13.7%). In Addition, the young age of our group and higher academic stress may predispose to migraine without aura, as reported in Ethiopian and Chinese students [36, 44].

Notably, TTH in our study did not have any significant association with gender, faculty, or age (p > 0.05). This lack of a robust gender association for TTH is particularly interesting in that it is in sharp contrast to a large number of studies that report uniformly a female preponderance in TTH [38, 42]. Although 75% of our overall sample were female, like the broad gender balance in health science courses, and as aligned with global trends toward increased headache occurrence among females [40, 41, 43, 45].

Clinically, Migraine without aura is most frequently reported with unilateral throbbing headache (59%), photophobia (63%), and nausea/vomiting (18%), as clinical features reported in previous studies [4, 9, 34]. In contrast, Migraine with aura was associated with greater visual disturbance (24%) and much longer duration (42% of 4–72 h), as expected to validate its disabling nature [9]. TTH, on the other hand, is characterized by bilaterally pressing pain (52%, p = 0.008) and had a smaller impact on activity. The prevalence of photophobia in 16% of TTH cases indicates several factors, first, potential phenotypic overlap between mild migraine and TTH in our population study, which led to misclassification, especially in self-reported studies without a confirmed clinical. also, the high-stress academic environment can trigger a headache phenotype with mixed features [35]. Supporting ours, a study conducted among Saudi students also reported photophobia in 36.6% of TTH cases [31]. This suggests the symptom may be common in student populations with tension headaches than previously recognized, possibly as a comorbid visual strain from prolonged studying [24]. Moreover, the intensity of pain also differed significantly, with 11% of migraineurs experiencing severe pain versus 3.4% of TTH patients (p < 0.001), corroborating the greater severity and potential incapacitation reported with migraine [24, 44].

Regarding triggers, stress (54%) and sleep deprivation (50%) were the most common self-reported precipitants for both migraine and TTH. The finding strongly aligns with many studies reporting the significant role of academic pressure and sleep deprivation in headache disorders among student populations in various environments [4, 5, 26, 46–48].

Specifically, studies from the Middle East and South Asia support this pattern, with sleep deprivation (74.7%) and stress (58%) being reported as primary triggers in the UAE [32] and high rates of anxiety/stress (75.4%) in Nepal [37]. The specific link of sleep deprivation with migraine with aura (67%) is intriguing, as is the strong connection between TTH and stress (65%), so they point to the differential impact of these modifiable variables on subtypes of headache [36]. These strong association findings are corroborated by other findings reporting academic and psychological stressors as the main TTH aggravating factors in a student [49].

The link between stress and both migraine and TTH is mediated by the body’s physiological stress response, so stress activates the autonomic nervous system, leads to increasing sympathetic outflow, and activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. Chronic stress can lead to allostatic load, sensitizing the central nervous system, including pain pathways, which can lower the threshold for a headache attack [50]. For TTH, high stress levels increase peri-cranial muscle tension and peripheral sensitization, which corroborates its robust association (65%) with stress in our cohort. For migraine, the relationship is more complex; chronic high-stress loads are risk factors for incidence and chronicity, while rapid decline in stress (the “let-down headache” phenomenon) may also precipitate attacks in some individuals [51].

The high prevalence of sleep deprivation (50% overall) as a trigger, especially in Migraine with Aura (67%), suggests an interaction between sleep/wake regulatory circuits and the trigeminal system. One of the hypotheses said that sleep deprivation induces oxidative stress within the brain [52]. This increase in reactive oxygen species can activate Transient Receptor Potential Ankyrin 1(TRPA1) receptors located on first-order trigeminal neurons. So, activation of these receptors leads to the release of pro-inflammatory neuropeptides, for example, Calcitonin Gene-Related Peptide (CGRP), which is central to the neurogenic inflammation underlying migraine pain [53]. besides, dysfunction in hypothalamic and brainstem nuclei responsible for sleep-wake cycles makes these patients more vulnerable to sleep-related disturbances [52].

The failure to distinguish between academic and personal stress is a limitation, but given the setting, the highly prevalent stress trigger strongly suggests that high-demand academic pressure contributes significantly to the headache burden. Future research is needed to quantify this distinction. The self-reported “stress” in this study likely encompasses the significant academic pressures prevalent in health science curricula [36, 49]; however, a formal distinction between academic and personal stress remains a valuable knowledge gap for future, highly granular research.

The management behavior of our study was heavily self-management oriented. A significant 91% of participants reported that they employed over-the-counter (OTC) analgesics (primarily paracetamol and ibuprofen) for the relief of headache. In contrast, the use of prescription medication was extremely low, being reported by only 6.3% overall (13% of migraineurs vs. 1.9% of TTH sufferers, p < 0.001). Similarly, the consultation of formal healthcare services was also low; only 21% had consulted a doctor regarding their headaches, and nearly half of these (46%) said that they had not considered their headaches to be severe enough to see a doctor.

This pattern of high self-medication, low physician consultation, and low use of prescription is highly consistent with the findings of other studies among students globally [2, 4–6, 12, 54, 55].

The high rate of self-medication, about 91% with low formal medical consultation (21%) represents a critical gap in headache management in Sudanese students. Also, this pattern, documented in some studies [34, 37]. which can lead to long-term risk of developing Medication-Overuse Headache (MOH), a refractory chronic headache that compounds disability. The underlying barriers to care-seeking behaviors are multifactorial, reflecting both systemic challenges in our health infrastructure and psychological barriers among the student population. The most frequent reasons for non-consultation behavior are that the headache was “not severe enough” (46%) or could be managed with OTCs (46%), suggesting a profound normalization and downplaying of headache severity, even the high disabling migraine attacks. This psychological barrier is likely reinforced by the intensely competitive and stressful environment of health sciences education. While a low percentage explicitly cited about cost concerns (2.7%) or Lack of time (7.5%), these figures are most likely due to underestimating the true, complex structural barriers in the Sudanese public health setting (especially during the study period). Specialized headache care accessibility is severely limited outside of major centers, and potential cost concerns which is a widespread issue in Sub-Saharan Africa [39]. In addition to the utilization of Herbal and traditional remedies (16% in TTH, 18% in Migraine with Aura) is highly significant due to deeply embedded cultural practices with traditional healing systems, even among highly educated medical students. Traditional remedies may be perceived as safer, more holistic, or simply more accessible and affordable than the formal orthodox healthcare pathway.

The high self-medication behavior pattern can be a risk for Medication-Overuse Headache (MOH). MOH is a secondary headache disorder defined by the ICHD-3 as a headache occurring on ≥ 15 days per month with a pre-existing primary headache disorder patient, that developed due to regular overuse of acute headache drugs for more than three months [1]. In our study, we revealed 17 students with chronic TTH (headache on ≥ 15 days/month). Among these students, 12 of them (70.6%) reported using OTC analgesics on 15 or more days per month, so meeting the criteria for MOH.

This behaviour translates to an MOH prevalence of 3.8% among all students with TTH and 1.6% of our total study population. This MOH is a common complication due to poorly managed primary headaches, especially in populations with easy access to analgesics and low consultation rates, and aligns with some studies [56, 57] This long-term use of medication in the chronic TTH subgroup suggests that most of their headache may be iatrogenic, due to over medications used, creating a cycle where headache frequency increases, leading to using more medications, which worsens the headache.

These headache disorders impacted the lives of these students. A significant proportion reported functional disability: 65% decreased ability to perform daily activities, 11% complete halt of activities 19% missed school or exams due to headaches, which directly threatens academic performance and grade attainment, a finding consistent with other student populations [21].

Regarding quantifying the impact on specific grades, while our study did not address it, the rate of absenteeism is an indicator of the educational burden caused by headaches. Our findings align with the many studies in the literature, which demonstrate the detrimental effects of headache disorders on academic life. One study conducted among medical students in Nepal revealed an even higher rate of absenteeism, with 35.4% missing school [37], while a Turkish study found that 12.4% of students with TTH experienced disabilities affecting their academic performance [38]. The impact of headache is also directly affecting academic achievement, a study conducted at Umm Al-Qura University in Saudi Arabia revealed a significant link between migraine headache and a lower GPA, which supports the conclusion that headaches can impair educational outcomes [58]. Furthermore, 27% of those with migraine with aura alone reported mental health effects due to headaches but no specific mental illness was identified by the questionnaire and we suggest further research to be done in this area. These findings are relevant to existing findings in the literature detailing the influence of migraine and TTH on academic progress, productivity, and overall quality of life within populations of students [4, 9, 27, 59].

Strengths and limitations

One of the key strengths of this study is its national nature, providing a window into a specific and understudied Sudanese population (health sciences students). The sample size is large compared with other studies among medical students in the African region and the Middle East [38–40, 42, 43, 49, 55]. The use of ICHD-3 criteria, even if via a self-administered questionnaire, adds diagnostic rigor compared to studies with less standardized definitions. However, Cross-sectional design permits no determination of causality between identified factors and headache development. Self-report use is subject to recall bias. The study population (health sciences students at specific universities) might not be generalizable to all Sudanese university students or the general Sudanese population. Also, the risk of misclassification from self-reported ICHD-3 diagnoses without clinical confirmation is a key limitation.

Implications and recommendations

The high prevalence of migraine and TTH, in addition to the strong association with stress and sleep, highlights the important of urgent need for interventions within Sudanese universities. We recommend implementing specific campus-based health initiatives, for example, a headache awareness campaign to educate regarding the diagnosis and management, workshops on techniques regarding stress management tailored to the academic calendar, and promoting healthy sleep hygiene. Also, all campaigns should aim to decrease the stigmatization of seeking help and to inform students about effective treatments, educate them about the risks of medication overuse, and the importance of early seeking care for recurrent headaches. University health services should consider establishing clearer pathways for headache consultation to encourage professional diagnosis and reduce reliance on self-medication.

Future research

Future research should build on these findings. Longitudinal studies are necessary to investigate causal links between identified risk factors (stress, sleep, academic pressures) and headache development, and incidence of medication-overuse headache in this population. Qualitative studies, for example in-depth interviews, to provide more understanding of student attitudes to the headache, the specific cultural and systemic barriers to seeking professional care, and the motivations to self-management choices. Interventional research to evaluate the effectiveness of campus-based programs, such as stress reduction and sleep hygiene interventions, on reducing headache frequency and severity and improving academic performance.

Conclusion

Our study found higher than global average prevalence of migraine and tension-type headache among Sudanese health sciences students. Stress and lack of sleep have been reported as the most frequent triggers. Despite the prevalence of headaches being high, most of the students utilized self-care involving over-the-counter analgesics, with very few seeking professional medical care. The impact extended to physical symptoms, mental health and academic productivity, as most of the students reported having missed lectures or having reduced productivity during occurrences of headaches. Migraine with aura was more frequently reported by rural residents and third-year students, suggesting potential associations with environmental and academic stress variables. These emphasize the need for institutional support, like stress management programs, besides improving healthcare access and awareness. Our findings emphasize the need for institutional support, including stress management programs and improved access to healthcare services.
