Impact of Maternal Vitamin D Levels on Offspring Bone Development: Systematic Review
Authors: Julian Dewantiningrum, Adriyan Pramono, Dwi Pudjonarko, Detty Siti Nurdiati, Wiku Andonotopo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMaternal vitamin D is essential for fetal calcium homeostasis and skeletal development. The long-term impact of maternal vitamin D status during pregnancy on offspring bone health remains unclear, especially in populations with high sun exposure yet persistent deficiency. This systematic review synthesizes cohort study on the association between maternal serum 25-hydroxy-vitamin D (25[OH]D) levels during pregnancy and bone outcomes in offspring.MethodsA total of 19 cohort studies, comprising 12,835 mother-offspring pairs across diverse geographic regions, were systematically reviewed. Offspring bone health indicators included bone mineral content (BMC), bone mineral density (BMD), bone turnover biomarkers, and fracture risk, assessed using dual energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), quantitative ultrasound (QUS), and biochemical markers. Heterogeneity in vitamin D thresholds, measurement timing, and follow-up duration was noted.ResultsThe findings were heterogeneous and often sex-specific. Some studies reported a positive association between higher maternal 25(OH)D levels and increased BMC/BMD, particularly in male offspring. Others showed no significant association after adjusting for maternal and neonatal confounders. Biomarker analyses generally supported an inverse relationship between maternal vitamin D deficiency and fetal bone turnover markers. However, evidence remains inconclusive regarding the benefits of prenatal vitamin D supplementation on long-term skeletal outcomes.ConclusionsMaternal vitamin D status appears to influence offspring bone development in a complex and multifactorial manner. Effects may be modified by child sex, timing of deficiency during gestation, and genetic factors. Future studies should prioritize standardized measurement protocols and expand cohort representation in under-researched regions, notably Southeast Asia, to inform public health guidelines and targeted interventions.

GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT

INTRODUCTION

Vitamin D plays a critical role in maintaining adequate calcium and phosphate concentrations essential for bone mineralization, muscle function, neural transmission, and cellular processes.[1] Maintaining optimal levels of circulating 25-hydroxy-vitamin D (25[OH]D) is particularly important during pregnancy to ensure adequate levels of the active form, 1,25-dihydroxy-vitamin D (1,25[OH]2D), for both maternal and fetal physiology.[2] Multiple studies have reported associations between maternal vitamin D status and fetal skeletal development,[3] with insufficient levels potentially contributing to neonatal hypocalcemia and rickets, conditions that may lead to long-term adverse health outcomes.[4]

Evidence from cohort studies, particularly in the United States and multi-ethnic populations, shows a positive correlation between maternal and umbilical cord 25(OH)D levels.[5–7] However, some studies also highlight variability in these levels based on seasonal fluctuations.[7] The Odense child cohort found an inverse association between cord 25(OH)D levels and sub-ischial leg length in boys aged 1.5 to 3 years, suggesting complex, sex-specific effects of maternal vitamin D.[8] These findings reinforce the notion that the fetus depends on transplacental transfer of maternal 25(OH)D,[9] which may stimulate fetal osteocalcin production through increased levels of 1,25(OH)2D.[10] The third trimester is particularly critical, as approximately 80% of fetal mineral accumulation and most bone mineralization occur during this period.[11]

Recent studies have explored whether maternal vitamin D status influences offspring bone health markers. For instance, higher maternal 25(OH)D levels have been associated with elevated bone turnover markers in cord blood [12] and improved neonatal bone mineral content (BMC), bone cross-sectional area (CSA), and bone mineral density (BMD).[13] A prospective cohort also found positive associations between maternal vitamin D status and offspring BMC and BMD at 20 years of age.[14] Similarly, in an Australian cohort, first-trimester vitamin D deficiency was associated with lower BMC and BMD in boys at age 11, but not in girls.[15] Another large study involving 9,901 children reported that maternal deficiency was associated with reduced BMC in offspring at six years.[16] Epigenetic modifications, particularly methylation of the retinoid X receptor α (RXRA) gene in umbilical cord DNA, may partially mediate these associations.[17]

However, findings across studies remain inconsistent. For example, a UK-based cohort found no association between maternal 25(OH)D levels and BMC in nine-month-old children,[18] and the Avon longitudinal study reported no association in late childhood.[19] The inconsistency may stem from variations in study design, measurement techniques, timing of vitamin D assessment, and population differences. Moreover, trials on vitamin D supplementation during pregnancy have yielded inconclusive results regarding its effects on offspring bone outcomes.[20,21] A systematic review by Thorne-Lyman and Fawzi [22] focused on broader perinatal outcomes, such as low birth weight, small-for-gestational-age status, and preterm birth, but not specifically on long-term skeletal health.

Despite substantial interest, there is no consensus on the critical gestational window during which vitamin D deficiency exerts the greatest influence on bone development in offspring. Therefore, this systematic review aims to assess the association between maternal serum 25(OH)D concentrations during pregnancy and offspring bone development across cohort studies.

METHODS

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The study protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD42021261886.

1. Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in four electronic databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, EBSCO, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library. The search covered all studies published up to 14 October 2025, with no restriction on study location and was limited to human subjects. Additional studies were identified by manually screening reference lists of relevant reviews and included articles. The search strategy used combinations of the following terms: “vitamin D,” “pregnancy,” “bone development,” and “cohort”. Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT) and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) were adapted to suit each database’s syntax and indexing system.

2. Study selection

Studies were selected based on predefined PICOS criteria:

• Participants: Pregnant women without complications and their offspring;

• Participants: Pregnant women without complications and their offspring;

• Exposure: Maternal serum 25(OH)D levels during pregnancy;

• Exposure: Maternal serum 25(OH)D levels during pregnancy;

• Comparison: Normal vitamin D levels vs. deficient or insufficient levels;

• Comparison: Normal vitamin D levels vs. deficient or insufficient levels;

• Outcomes: Offspring bone health indicators, including BMC, BMD, bone biomarkers, or fracture risk;

• Outcomes: Offspring bone health indicators, including BMC, BMD, bone biomarkers, or fracture risk;

• Study Design: Prospective or retrospective cohort studies.

• Study Design: Prospective or retrospective cohort studies.

Exclusion criteria included:

• High-risk pregnancies;

• High-risk pregnancies;

• Mothers with chronic or infectious diseases;

• Mothers with chronic or infectious diseases;

• Offspring born preterm, with low birth weight, congenital malformations, hematologic conditions, or chronic illnesses.

• Offspring born preterm, with low birth weight, congenital malformations, hematologic conditions, or chronic illnesses.

After duplicate removal, titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers JD and AP. Full-text eligibility was assessed using the same criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or adjudicated by a third reviewer DP.

3. Data extraction

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers using a standardized and pilot-tested data extraction form based on the Cochrane Handbook. Extracted data included study characteristics, population demographics, vitamin D measurement timing and thresholds, outcome measures, and statistical results. Data discrepancies were resolved through consensus.

4. Quality assessment and statistical analysis

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for cohort studies, applied independently and blindly by two reviewers (JD and AP). Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, and if necessary, by a third reviewer. Effect sizes were reported as β coefficients, mean differences, or hazard ratios (HRs), depending on the study. Where meta-analysis was not feasible due to heterogeneity, a narrative synthesis was conducted. As this research is a systematic review of previously published studies, ethical approval was not required.

DISCUSSION

This study exploring the association between maternal vitamin D status during pregnancy and offspring bone health, integrating data across 19 cohort studies and 12,835 mother–child pairs. While several studies suggest a positive correlation between adequate maternal 25(OH)D levels and BMC or BMD in offspring, results remain inconsistent, particularly after adjusting for confounding factors.

Geographically, the majority of studies originated from Europe, North America, and Australia, with only one study representing Asia, specifically China.[24] This underrepresentation is striking, especially considering that vitamin D deficiency is widespread in sun-abundant Southeast Asia, driven by cultural behaviors such as sun avoidance, use of sunblock, and covered clothing.[37–40] Despite a growing body of research on vitamin D deficiency in women and children in Southeast Asia, there remains a paucity of evidence connecting maternal vitamin D levels to offspring skeletal outcomes in this region.[41,42]

1. Biological plausibility and epigenetic mechanisms

Several mechanisms support a causal link between maternal vitamin D and fetal skeletal development. One hypothesis suggests that vitamin D influences bone growth through gene-environment interactions during intrauterine life. DNA methylation at loci such as RXRA and CDKN2A has been inversely associated with BMC in early childhood. [17] RXRA forms heterodimers with the vitamin D receptor (VDR), modulating transcription of genes involved in calcium absorption and bone mineralization.[43] Vitamin D deficiency during pregnancy may disrupt this process, reducing intestinal calcium absorption and impairing transplacental calcium transfer.[13,44]

2. Interpretation of heterogeneous findings

Despite plausible mechanisms, this review found probability of marked heterogeneity in outcomes, which may explain the inconsistency in study conclusions (Table 4–6). These differences are attributable to:

• The variability in the vitamin D cutoff thresholds used to define deficiency, ranging from 25 to 50 nmol/L,[16, 23,28,31,32] is a significant contributing factor.

• The variability in the vitamin D cutoff thresholds used to define deficiency, ranging from 25 to 50 nmol/L,[16, 23,28,31,32] is a significant contributing factor.

• The timing of serum 25(OH)D measurement varies, ranging from early to late gestation or from cord blood (Table 1).

• The timing of serum 25(OH)D measurement varies, ranging from early to late gestation or from cord blood (Table 1).

• The sensitivity and standardization of assessment methods like DXA, QUS, and biochemical markers vary (Table 3).

• The sensitivity and standardization of assessment methods like DXA, QUS, and biochemical markers vary (Table 3).

Some studies found sex-specific associations, particularly showing benefits in male offspring, possibly linked to different skeletal growth trajectories or hormone interactions. [15,28]

3. Global variation in vitamin D status

Maternal vitamin D levels are known to vary widely across regions, influenced by sunlight exposure, dietary intake, BMI, and genetic factors. A global review reported ranges from 13 to 130 nmol/L, with Southeast Asia averaging 20 to 52 nmol/L—near the lower end of sufficiency.[40] Furthermore, genetic polymorphisms can influence serum 25(OH)D concentrations and may contribute to population-level variability.[45] Currently, guidelines for optimal serum 25(OH)D during pregnancy remain inconclusive. The Institute of Medicine recommends ≥50 nmol/L (20 ng/mL),[46] while the Endocrine Society advocates for ≥75 nmol/L (30 ng/mL).[47] No consensus yet exists for the optimal threshold specific to fetal skeletal outcomes.[48,49]

4. Influence of maternal BMI and age

Maternal BMI and age also influence vitamin D status. Women with higher BMI often have lower circulating 25(OH)D, potentially due to volumetric dilution and sequestration in adipose tissue.[50] One study reported that each 1 kg/m2 increase in BMI reduced 25(OH)D by 0.23 ng/mL.[51] In the current review, most mothers were of reproductive age and had normal BMI (Table 1), which may partially explain the moderate levels of deficiency reported.

5. Bone assessment modalities

DXA remains the gold standard for assessing BMC and BMD but poses challenges in pediatric settings due to movement artifacts and radiation exposure. Biochemical markers, such as bALP and TRACP, offer less invasive alternatives and may serve as early indicators of bone turnover. This review found an inverse correlation between maternal vitamin D and bALP, consistent with vitamin D’s role in suppressing bone turnover.[13,23,30,36,52]

While some studies reported positive DXA-based associations,[14,15,30,32] others found no significant differences in BMC or BMD by maternal vitamin D status.[18,19,33,34] This variability supports the need for standardized outcome measures and consensus on clinically meaningful endpoints.

7. Strengths, limitations, and future directions

1) Strengths

This review comprehensively synthesizes evidence from 19 cohort studies involving over 12,800 mother–offspring pairs, providing a robust evaluation of the relationship between maternal vitamin D and skeletal outcomes across different ages and assessment methods. The inclusion of multiple assessment techniques, including DXA, QUS, and biochemical markers, enriches the understanding of both structural and functional aspects of bone health. Furthermore, the review adheres to PRISMA guidelines, was pre-registered with PROSPERO, and utilized a rigorous multi-database search strategy, minimizing the risk of selection and publication bias.

2) Limitations

Despite its strengths, this review has several limitations. First, substantial heterogeneity existed among included studies in terms of vitamin D cut-off values, timing of exposure assessment, and bone outcome measures, limiting direct comparability and precluding a meta-analysis. Second, there was a geographical imbalance, with most studies conducted in high-income Western countries and minimal representation from Southeast Asia, Africa, or Latin America. Third, individual participant data were unavailable from cohort studies, restricting subgroup analyses based on trimester-specific exposure, sex differences, or baseline nutritional status.

CONCLUSION

This systematic review demonstrates that the association between maternal vitamin D status during pregnancy and offspring bone development is inconsistent among several studies. Multifactorial factors such as child sex, gestational timing, and maternal or neonatal characteristics might be related to bone development. While several studies support a positive relationship between sufficient maternal 25(OH)D levels and favorable bone outcomes, particularly in males, others report null associations after adjusting for confounders.

Because of the heterogeneity, current evidence does not support a universal benefit of prenatal vitamin D sufficiency or supplementation for offspring bone health. However, consistent findings from biochemical markers and some imaging studies suggest a potential role for maternal vitamin D in fetal skeletal programming, which merits further investigation. To advance clinical practice and public health guidance, future research should focus on standardizing vitamin D assessments, clarifying critical exposure windows, and expanding cohort representation in regions with high deficiency prevalence, such as Southeast Asia. Until such data are available, routine vitamin D screening and tailored supplementation during pregnancy may be considered on an individual risk basis, particularly in populations known to be deficient.
