Investigating the Short‐Term Changes of the Electrocardiogram Indicators During Hospitalization in Patients With COVID‐19 Following the Use of Tocilizumab
Authors: Mahdi Zahedi, Golnaz Houshmand, Tabassom Hosseini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo evaluate short‐term changes in electrocardiographic (ECG) parameters after tocilizumab administration in COVID‐19 patients, considering the drug's role in treating severe cases and the involvement of IL‐6 in cardiovascular effects and ECG alterations.MethodsIn this retrospective study, we assessed cardiovascular and electrocardiographic parameters in PCR‐confirmed COVID‐19 patients who received tocilizumab. ECG variables were evaluated at two time points: 24 h before and 24 h after drug administration. All ECGs were verified by a supervisor, and demographic information was extracted from patient records.Results33 patients were included, of whom 36.4% were men. The patients' ages ranged from 37 to 79 years. The corrected QT interval (QTc) measured 24 h before (QTc1) and 24 h after (QTc2) tocilizumab administration showed no significant difference (QTc1: 390.3 ± 31.67 ms; QTc2: 392.73 ± 36.68 ms). No significant ECG changes were observed in the ST segment, ventricular fibrillation (VF), ventricular tachycardia (VT), or atrial fibrillation (AF). Four patients experienced cardiorespiratory arrest within 24 h after receiving tocilizumab.ConclusionTocilizumab administration in critically ill COVID‐19 patients was not associated with significant short‐term ECG changes or specific cardiovascular complications. Although previous studies in inflammatory diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis have reported QTc shortening following tocilizumab treatment, likely through suppression of IL‐6‐mediated systemic inflammation, no significant QTc reduction was observed in our cohort during the 24‐h follow‐up period. A few cases of cardiorespiratory arrest occurred; a causal relationship with tocilizumab could not be established due to the severity of illness and limited clinical data.

Introduction

COVID‐19 is a novel pneumonia caused by the SARS‐CoV‐2 virus (Liu et al. 2019). This strain is the third deadly human pathogenic virus, first identified in December 2019, following Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome (SARS) in 2003 and Middle East Respiratory Syndrome (MERS) in 2012, respectively. SARS‐CoV‐2 targets the lungs and possibly other organs by binding to the angiotensin‐converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptor, a cell surface protein highly expressed in the lungs, heart, and kidneys, leading to multi‐organ damage. The disease is transmitted through droplets during the incubation period and throughout the illness (Subramaniam et al. 2022). Between 3% and 20% of infected patients experience mild to severe symptoms such as fever, cough, and muscle pain, and 5% to 10% are admitted to the ICU (Dehghani et al. 2024). Male gender, older age, and underlying conditions like diabetes mellitus are risk factors identified for hospitalization and mortality in COVID‐19 patients (Tortonese et al. 2020).

The main pathogenic mechanism involved in the severe clinical manifestations of COVID‐19 is an inappropriate immune system response, leading to an excessive release of cytokines and chemokines, known as a cytokine storm (Zendehdel et al. 2024). Researchers have shown that cytokine storms occur in various diseases such as malignancy, rheumatological disease, and sepsis syndrome, and are concerning in COVID‐19 (Sarabia De Ardanaz et al. 2021).

Research indicates that elevated levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin IL‐6, tumor necrosis factor TNFα, and IL‐2 are linked to virus‐induced inflammation and lung damage (Zhou et al. 2020). Individuals infected with the virus have shown high levels of inflammatory factors like IL‐2, IL‐6, IL‐10, IFN, G‐CSF, and IP‐10 (Shakarami 2024). Among the inflammatory factors contributing to cytokine‐induced damage, IL‐6 is one of the key players (Kim et al. 2021).

Beyond its pro‐inflammatory effects, IL‐6 has been implicated in inflammation‐induced prolongation of ventricular repolarization and QTc interval prolongation. Previous studies in patients with rheumatoid arthritis have demonstrated that inhibition of IL‐6 signaling with tocilizumab can rapidly shorten prolonged QTc intervals, suggesting that reduction of systemic inflammation may favorably influence cardiac electrophysiology (Gerasimova et al. 2024).

Tocilizumab is a human monoclonal antibody against the IL‐6 receptor, and given the prominent role of IL‐6 in COVID‐19, it was the first drug used to treat inflammatory complications in patients with severe forms of the disease (Guillén et al. 2020). This drug is also used to treat rheumatoid arthritis (RA), juvenile idiopathic arthritis, and giant cell arteritis (GCA) (Tanaka et al. 2016).

Several studies indicate that Tocilizumab (TCZ) administered to patients with severe COVID‐19 can be an effective treatment for reducing mortality by neutralizing a key inflammatory agent in cytokine release syndrome (CRS) (Chen et al. 2020). This molecule may block the cytokine storm in the systemic inflammation phase and reduce disease severity (Lin et al. 2020).

Considering the crucial role of this drug in treating COVID‐19 patients, the importance of treating patients during the current pandemic, the significant role of IL‐6 in the disease's pathophysiology, its cardiovascular effects, associated electrocardiographic changes, and the relationship between the drug and IL‐6 levels, we decided to investigate the short‐term changes in electrocardiographic indicators after drug injection in this study.

Methods

Our study was a descriptive retrospective study that investigated the effect of tocilizumab on cardiovascular and electrocardiographic issues in COVID‐19 patients. Our study population consisted of 33 COVID‐19 patients, both male and female, admitted to Sayad Shirazi Hospital in Gorgan during the first 6 months of 2024. These patients were in a severe inflammatory phase and had received tocilizumab.

The prescription conditions for tocilizumab included high C‐reactive protein (CRP) (preferably above 70 mg/L) along with evidence of lung involvement. Treatment with tocilizumab in combination with low‐dose corticosteroids was suggested, and treatment with tocilizumab alone was not recommended.

Data for the investigated variables were obtained retrospectively by reviewing patient records. Inclusion criteria for the study consisted of patients aged over 30 years, a positive PCR for COVID‐19, and receipt of tocilizumab, while a history of Coronary Artery Bypass Graft (CABG) surgery served as the sole exclusion criterion.

Standard 12‐lead electrocardiograms (ECGs) were verified by the study supervisor and evaluated at two specific time points: 24 h before and 24 h after drug administration. To determine the heart rate‐corrected QT interval (QTc), the raw QT interval and preceding R‐R intervals were manually measured from the recorded ECG tracings and calculated using the Bazett correction formula. Beyond the QTc interval, other electrocardiographic parameters systematically evaluated at both time points included changes in the ST segment, VF, VT, and AF. Individual occurrences of arrhythmic events, such as paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (PSVT), and incidences of cardiorespiratory arrest were also monitored and recorded.

Data Analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS version 20 software. Quantitative variables (such as age and QTc intervals) were described using mean and standard deviation, while qualitative variables (such as gender, comorbidities, and the presence or absence of specific ECG abnormalities) were described using absolute and relative frequency distribution tables.

Study Limitations

The study has several limitations, including a limited sample size and the absence of a control group. Its retrospective nature, relying on historical patient medical records, may impact data quality and reliability. Furthermore, the research did not adequately explore the relationship between cardiovascular events and COVID‐19 severity, which could affect the observed prevalence and associations. Which appear to be an out‐of‐context point in this study focused on cardiovascular effects, which might be influenced by specific COVID‐19 treatment protocols, potentially distorting any observed associations.

This study is approved by the Ethics Committee of Vice Chancellor for Research & Technology of the Golestan University of Medical Sciences (IR.GOUMS.REC.1402.202).

Changes in QTC were observed in three cases: two cases showed an increase, and one case showed a decrease. One patient, a 64‐year‐old female with hypertension, developed PSVT after receiving the drug. She also experienced an increase in QTC (from 360 to 400). Another patient was a 57‐year‐old diabetic female who, after receiving the drug, experienced an increase in QTC (from 440 to 520). A 46‐year‐old male patient with no underlying disease showed a decrease in QTC (from 440 to 400) after receiving the drug.

Among the 33 patients who received tocilizumab, no electrocardiographic changes, including changes in ST segment, VF, VT, or AF, were observed from 24 h before to 24 h after drug administration. Additionally, four patients experienced cardiorespiratory arrest within 24 h after receiving tocilizumab (Table 1).

Discussion

We investigated the cardiac factors of 33 patients with severe COVID‐19 who received tocilizumab treatment. All patients were in the intensive care unit at the onset of tocilizumab administration. The mean age of our patients was 57.3 ± 10.48 years.

Given the established role of IL‐6 in inflammation‐associated QTc prolongation and the fact that tocilizumab inhibits IL‐6 signaling, we anticipated a reduction in QTc duration following treatment (Alsulaim et al. 2021). This expectation was based on previous studies in rheumatoid arthritis, where tocilizumab rapidly reversed QTc prolongation by reducing systemic inflammation. However, in our cohort of critically ill COVID‐19 patients, no significant overall change in QTc was observed, with shortening occurring in only one patient (3%). This discrepancy may reflect differences in disease pathophysiology, the severe clinical status of ICU patients, concomitant medications, and our relatively short 24‐h assessment period.

In contrast, a study by (Alattar et al. 2020), which aimed to investigate the effects of tocilizumab on severe coronavirus patients, showed that tocilizumab caused side effects such as QT interval prolongation (5.20%) and increased alanine aminotransferase. In terms of mean age, their study is consistent with ours. Regarding QT duration, we had one case of QTc reduction, which is inconsistent with the results of the Rand Alattar study.

In a case–control study conducted by (Kobayashi et al. 2018), which investigated QT segment size in rheumatoid arthritis patients and compared the effect of tocilizumab on this segment, a decrease in mean QTc was observed in RA patients compared to the control group.

In a study by (Lazzerini et al. 2020) aimed at providing recommendations for electrocardiographic/QTc monitoring along with a decision‐making guide to optimize the risk/benefit ratio when prescribing investigational COVID‐19 drugs, it was stated that the administration of targeted anti‐IL‐6 drugs (tocilizumab and sarilumab) in COVID‐19 is suggested not only in patients with symptoms of multi‐organ dysfunction, but also in patients with QTc > 500 ms, especially when IL‐6 ≥ 10. In active rheumatoid arthritis, tocilizumab rapidly reversed QTc prolongation by controlling systemic inflammation. It is suggested that QTc be checked in patients before and after treatment with tocilizumab (Desai et al. 2021).

According to a cohort study conducted by (Kim et al. 2017) on a population of rheumatoid arthritis patients receiving tocilizumab to assess its safety against TNF inhibitors in terms of cardiovascular events, it was observed that the incidence of cardiovascular events was 0.52% per year for tocilizumab recipients and 0.59% per year for TNF inhibitor recipients (Gaborit et al. 2020). Thus, no evidence of an increased risk of cardiovascular events was found in rheumatoid arthritis patients receiving tocilizumab compared to those receiving TNF inhibitors (Gaborit et al. 2020).

In our study, electrocardiogram changes were examined in patients who received tocilizumab, 24 h before and 24 h after drug administration. The results showed no changes in the ST segment, VF, VT, or AF. Unfortunately, no studies were found in the literature review that investigated cardiovascular factors in COVID‐19 patients, which would allow us to discuss differences in study results. Additionally, in our study, 4 patients experienced cardiorespiratory arrest within 24 h after receiving tocilizumab.

The first patient was a 71‐year‐old man with hypertension. After a COVID‐19 diagnosis, he received antiviral treatment (lopinavir/ritonavir along with hydroxychloroquine), but his fever persisted, and his shortness of breath continued to worsen (Fu et al. 2020). Ultimately, he received two doses of tocilizumab 12 h apart; his fever resolved after 3 days, and CRP was within the normal range after 2 days. Additionally, a repeat nasopharyngeal swab test for SARS‐CoV‐2 became negative.

The second patient was a 45‐year‐old man who, after being diagnosed with COVID‐19, began antiviral treatment. Despite initial improvement, with an increase in CRP (151 mg/L) and the onset of bilateral interstitial pneumonia on chest X‐ray, he received two doses of tocilizumab. His clinical condition improved with fever resolution after 48 h and a rapid decrease in CRP (13 mg/L on March 18).

The third patient was a 53‐year‐old man with hypertension whose chest X‐ray showed bilateral interstitial pneumonia. His CRP was 250 mg/L on admission. After diagnosis, antiviral treatment was immediately started. After 48 h, respiratory symptoms worsened, and he received tocilizumab in three doses. After 48 h, his CRP was 92 mg/L, and subsequent control was within the normal range. In this relevant report, “Off‐label use of tocilizumab in patients with SARS‐CoV‐2 infection”, similar to our study, no cases of cardiovascular complications were observed after tocilizumab use.

In a study by (Luo et al. 2020), which investigated the therapeutic response of TCZ treatment in COVID‐19 patients, the clinical outcome was retrospectively evaluated in 15 COVID‐19 patients. Two of them were moderate, six were severe, and seven were critically ill. TCZ was used in combination with methylprednisolone in eight patients. Five patients received TCZ injections twice or more. Although TCZ treatment rapidly improved elevated CRP in all patients, among the four critically ill patients who received only one dose of TCZ, three died, and the last patient, with an exacerbated clinical outcome, could not return to the normal range, while CRP levels were consistent in the others (Zhang et al. 2020).

In a study conducted by (Di Giambenedetto et al. 2020), they interpreted the outcomes of three patients hospitalized in an Italian hospital following a COVID‐19 diagnosis by real‐time PCR on oropharyngeal and nasopharyngeal swabs, who developed rapid respiratory failure and received tocilizumab after obtaining verbal consent.

Serum IL‐6 levels initially tended to increase further and then decreased after TCZ treatment in 10 patients (Klopfenstein et al. 2020). Persistent and significant increases in IL‐6 were observed in four patients who failed treatment (Xu, Han, et al. 2020a; Xu, Shi, et al. 2020b). It appears that TCZ is an effective therapeutic option in COVID‐19 patients at risk of cytokine storm, and for critically ill patients with high IL‐6, repeated doses of TCZ are recommended (Chan et al. 2021). Since our study was retrospective, unfortunately, we could not investigate information regarding IL‐6 and concomitant medications (Table 2) (Zhao et al. 2021; Guaraldi et al. 2020; Gabay et al. 2016).

As all patients were hospitalized in the ICU during the acute phase of the disease, and considering the average age of patients in our study and other studies, it seems that the population is older, with more comorbidities and a higher mortality rate compared to other studies conducted on COVID‐19 patients. This might be explained by the method of excluding patients without hospitalization criteria and without standard treatment to select critically ill patients and have a comparable population with the TCZ group (who were critically ill patients).

Conclusion

In our study, the use of tocilizumab in critically ill COVID‐19 patients hospitalized in the ICU did not show specific cardiovascular complications; only four cases of cardiac arrest were reported, which, due to the lack of sufficient patient information, cannot be attributed to tocilizumab use. While this research provides valuable findings on the effects of tocilizumab on cardiovascular events in COVID‐19, it still requires further investigation. Firstly, longitudinal studies are needed to determine the long‐term complications of cardiovascular events in patients with COVID‐19. Secondly, larger sample sizes and diverse populations should be included in future studies to increase the generalizability of the findings. This will contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the prevalence of cardiovascular events in different populations.
