Inhibition of either the sympathetic or parasympathetic nervous system increases tonic immobility duration in laying hens
Authors: I.C. von Seggern, H.L. Sutherland, M.A. Erasmus, G.S. Fraley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tonic immobility (TI) is a temporary state where a chicken remains immobile after restraint. It is a commonly used fear test in research, where a longer latency to right is equated with more fear. Despite its commonality, there is limited research done on the physiological systems that underlie TI. The objective of our study was to examine the roles of the sympathetic (SNS) and parasympathetic nervous systems (PNS) on TI. In experiment 1, sixteen Dekalb White hens (43 weeks of age) were given 0.1 mL/kg of either saline as a control (CON), labetalol (5 mg/mL) to inhibit the SNS (LAB), or glycopyrrolate (0.2 mg/mL) to inhibit the PNS (GLY). In experiment 2, eight Hy-Line Silver hens (20 weeks of age) were given either 0.2 mL/kg of saline as a control (CON2) or 0.2 mg/kg of a 1:1 glycopyrrolate and labetalol mixture (DRUG). For both experiments, latency to first leg movement, first head movement, and to right were measured, as well as thermal temperatures. In experiment 1, heart rate (HR) and respiratory rate (RR) were also measured. The MIXED procedure was used to analyze latencies for experiment 1. Experiment 2 utilized a Welch’s T-test. An ANOVA was used for all RR, HR, and thermal temperatures. Data were considered significant when P < 0.05 and trending when 0.05 < P < 0.10. For experiment 1, after injection and before induction, GLY had a lower respiratory rate (p = 0.019) compared to LAB and CON. This trend continued through the first minute (p = 0.020) and the average time under TI (p = 0.010). The CON group was quicker to right than the GLY (p = 0.007) and LAB (p = 0.0726). For experiment 2, the CON2 group was also quicker to right than DRUG (p = 0.052). These results indicate that both the PNS and SNS play a role in TI recovery, however further studies need to be undertaken to better understand the neurobiology of tonic immobility.

Introduction

Tonic immobility (TI) is characterized as an involuntary catatonic-like state induced by physical restraint, where the animal has a greatly reduced responsiveness to its surroundings, although, it can be abruptly ended by noxious stimuli. TI is a widely used as an indicator of welfare to determine how stressful or fearful the conditions are in which a bird lives (Tiemann et al., 2023). However, little is known about the physiology underlying the TI effect. Despite this gap in knowledge, it is known that tonic immobility can result in changes in heart rate (HR), respiration rate (RR), temperature, and peripheral vasodilation. Induction of TI results in a brief increase in HR and RR, which then decrease and reach their lowest at termination of TI. Core body temperature and shank temperature have both been reported to stay low during TI, while peripheral vasodilation decreases throughout (Nash and Gallup, 1976; Gentle et al., 1989). It is not clear if TI is related to, or is an avian expression, of the well characterized mammalian fear-freaze.

The fear freeze, has a well-described neural pathway (Roelofs, 2017). When a threat initially occurs, the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) plays an active role to initially react. However, with an intense novel stimulus multiple sensory modalities converge in the cortex of the brain and activate noradrenergic neurons in the locus coeruleus of the hindbrain leading to suppression of motor activity, but likely greater attentiveness (Roelofs, 2017). When the fear freeze is activated, the parasympathetic nervous system (PNS) then becomes the dominant system to lower heart rate, respiratory rate, and increase pupil dilation (Roelofs and Dayan, 2022). However, the roles of the SNS and PNS are not as well understood during tonic immobility.

The SNS is commonly known for preparing the body for the fight or flight response and plays a large role in physiological changes when an organism is facing a perceived threat. The SNS increases HR, RR, blood pressure, and core body temperature to allow for a quicker response to threats (Nakamura and Morrison, 2022; Alshak and Das, 2023). The short cholinergic preganglionic neurons of the SNS originate in the thoracic and lumbar regions of the spinal cord and innervate the long adrenergic postganglionic neurons, which release epinephrine (E) and norepinephrine (NE). E and NE act on the following adrenergic receptors: alpha-1, alpha-2, beta-1, and beta-2 (McCorry, 2007; Alshak and Das, 2023). The SNS aids to prepare an individual for fight or flight, but becomes less active during periods without stressors.

The PNS, known for preparing the body for a period of rest and digestion, plays a physiological role in maintaining homeostasis during periods of little or no threat. The PNS results in a decrease in HR, RR, blood pressure, and body temperature, as there is no need to prepare the body to react to a threat. The long cholinergic preganglionic neurons of the PNS originate in the brainstem and pelvic spinal cord and innervate short cholinergic postganglionic neurons that release acetylcholine (ACh), which acts on primarily visceral muscarinic receptors (McCorry, 2007).

Given that TI is so often used as a welfare test, it is important to understand the physiology underlying it. However, little research has been done to explore the role of the autonomic nervous system in TI. To do so, we conducted a study where adult laying hens were placed into TI following a pharmacological manipulation of the SNS, PNS, or both systems. The purpose of our study was to evaluate the impact of PNS and SNS inhibitory drugs on tonic immobility and, thereby, to understand further the roles of the two branches of the autonomic nervous system in TI. Our findings suggest that both the SNS and PNS play a role in TI duration.

Materials and methods

All procedures were approved by Purdue’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (PACUC # 0724002516), and Iowa State University’s IACUC (#IACUC-23-027).

Animals

Experiment 1. Sixteen Dekalb Whites (43 weeks of age) from Iowa State University’s Robert T. Hamilton Poultry Farm (Ames, IA) were used. Given that this type of procedure has never been done before, no power analyses on the sample size were possible. Therefore, we went with a large sample size. Birds were housed across 3 bird cages, and feed and lighting were in accordance with the Hendrix Dekalb White Management Guide (Dekalb). Hens were taken from their cage for approximately 3 hours each day and then returned to the same cage.

Experiment 2. Eight Hy-Line Silver Brown hens (20 weeks of age) from Purdue University’s Animal Sciences Research and Education Center (West Lafayette, IN) were used. A post-hoc power analysis was run on the data from experiment 1 and an n = 8 was found to be to provide > 90% Power. Hens were housed in a cage-free facility with feed and lighting in accordance with the Hy-Line Silver Brown Performance Guide (Hy-Line International, 2023). Hens were taken from their cage for approximately 3 hours and then returned to their environment.

Study design

Experiment 1. Hens were given 0.1 mL/kg of either saline (0.9% NaCl) as a control (CON), 0.1 mL/kg of labetalol (5 mg/mL) to inhibit the SNS (LAB), or 0.1 mL/kg of glycopyrrolate (0.2 mg/mL) to inhibit the PNS (GLY) intravenously via the brachial vein. Doses are those commonly used in veterinary medicine. Labetalol blocks the alpha-1, beta-1, and beta-2 adrenergic receptors so that E and NE are unable to elicit hormonal or neurotransmitter effects. Glycopyrrolate blocks muscarinic receptors in the peripheral nervous system so that ACh is unable act on them. Neither glycopyrrolate nor labetalol cross the blood-brain barrier and therefore, do not act on the central nervous system The study followed a crossover design for 3 days where each hen got a different drug each day (see Table 1). While the washout period for these drugs is unknown in birds, it is known that in mammals, the total body clearance of labetalol is 33 mL/min/kg (Hospira, 2021) and the half-life of glycopyrrolate is approximately 50 minutes (Gallanosa et al., 2025). Given that the metabolism of birds is approximately twice that of mammals, we can reason that 24 hours was more than a sufficient washout period for these drugs (Gavrilov et al., 2022). Immediately before and after injection of the drug, HR and RR were measured and a thermal image was taken of the hen that included the comb, beak, and open eye. The HR was measured using a stethoscope and RR was taken by counting the number of times the chest rose and fell. Thermal images were taken using a Fluke Ti480 PRO Infrared Camera (Fluke Corporation, Everett, WA). Tonic immobility was then induced and another thermal image was taken. The RR and HR were taken every minute until 10 minutes or the hen righted itself. Number of attempts to induce tonic immobility, latency to first leg movement, latency to first alert head movement, and latency to right were also recorded. HR was taken using a stethoscope and RR was taken by counting the number of times the chest rose and fell. Once the hen came out of tonic immobility, they were held upright for 5 minutes and then induced again. Experiment 2. Hens were given either 0.2 mL/kg of saline (CON2) or 0.1 mL/kg each of glycopyrrolate (5 mg/mL) and labetalol (0.2 mg/mL) (DRUG). These are the same doses as in Experiment 1. This was done to provide a complete autonomic block. Before injection, a thermal image was taken of the collective comb, beak, and eye. Birds were then injected and another thermal image was taken before they were induced into TI. After TI was induced, another thermal image was taken. Number of attempts to induce tonic immobility, latency to first leg movement, latency to first alert head movement, and latency to right were recorded.

Tonic immobility

Experiments 1 and 2. Tonic immobility was induced in a wooden cradle that followed the same design as in Jones and Faure (1981) with two microfiber cloths lining the cradle. Hens were restrained for 15 seconds (Gallup and Nash, 1971) and then released while simultaneously starting a timer. If the hen righted itself within 10 seconds, it was counted as an attempt, and the hen would be immediately induced again. If the hen righted within 10 seconds 3 times, it would be held upright for 5 minutes and then induced again. Observers were unaware of the drug treatment groups. Thermal images

Experiments 1 and 2. Thermal images were taken 30 centimeters from the head of the chicken. Ambient temperature was 21°C and the emissivity settings were set to 0.95. Images were taken at 3 time points: before the drugs were given, after the drugs were given and before TI was induced, and after TI was induced. Thermal images were uploaded to Fluke Connect (Fluke Corporation, Everett, WA), and the average comb, eye, and mandible thermal temperatures were measured. This was done by using the program to outline the comb, eye, and beak as shown in Fig. 2. Pictures with flipped combs and closed eyes were not utilized. Statistics

Experiment 1. The MIXED procedure (SAS 9.4) was used to analyze fixed effects on latency to right, latency to first head movement, latency to first leg movement, and number of attempts to induce TI. Fixed effects were drug (glycopyrrolate, labetalol, or saline), order (order in which drugs were given), trial (3 attempts to induce TI), and the interactions among them. Day was included as a random effect with individual bird as the subject. Least-square means and standard errors were calculated and post-hoc pairwise comparisons were completed using Tukey's test.

Statistics for HR, RR, and thermal temperature were run using MacJMP SAS (edition 18) using a repeated measures-ANOVA with time being the repeated measure and a Fishers LSD post-hoc test. Data were considered significant when P < 0.05 and trending when 0.05 < P < 0.10.

Experiment 2. TI variables and thermal temperature were run using MacJMP (edition 18). Latency to right, latency to first head movement, latency to first leg movement, and number of attempts to induce TI were analyzed using a Welch’s T-test as there were two treatment groups. Thermal temperatures were analyzed using repeated measures-ANOVA with time as the repeated measure and a Fishers LSD post-hoc test. Data were considered significant when p < 0.05 and trending when 0.05 < p < 0.10.

Discussion

Tonic immobility is often used as a welfare test in poultry science to determine the fearfulness of birds (Campo and Carnicer, 1994; Zhao et al., 2021), with a longer latency to right associated with more fearfulness (Forkmann et al., 2007). These studies determine the fearfulness of birds in their environment. This information can then be used by auditing companies to create tools that regularly impact commercial poultry welfare and management decisions. Therefore, it is important that the underlying physiology of TI is understood. The goal of our study was to better understand the roles that the PNS and SNS play in TI. To do so, we pharmacologically inhibited either the SNS or PNS or both systems and measured the effects on TI. Birds injected with glycopyrrolate, labetalol, or the combination took longer to right than those injected with saline. These results suggest that TI involves both PNS and SNS components. It should be noted that since the birds were pharmacologically manipulated, we cannot equate longer TI with more fear in this experiment.

Our results reveal yet another system in which the PNS and SNS are able to work synergistically; although most well-known interactions between these two systems are antagonistic. For example, the PNS decreases HR while the SNS increases it (Varzideh et al., 2026). Similarly, the SNS increases peripheral vasodilation to move blood to the muscles while the PNS works antagonistically to increase vasodilation to the gut (Sheng and Zhu, 2018). The SNS decreases blood flow to the gut and reduces gastric motility whereas the PNS stimulates blood flow to the GI tract and increases peristalsis (Raymond et al., 2024). However, there are occasions in which the PNS and SNS work synergistically. Such occasions include salivation, reproduction, and the fear freeze. The PNS is responsible for large volumes of watery saliva, while the SNS stimulates small amounts of protein-rich saliva (Ekström, 1989). In order for successful reproduction, the PNS causes an increase in blood flow to the penis and clitoris, and increases excitatory input, whereas the SNS plays a role in ejaculation and orgasm (Purves et al., 2001). Thus, there are several examples where the SNS and PNS act synergistically towards a physiological goal, and the fear freeze is yet another that may be related to TI.

The fear freeze is a behavior most notably described as a “deer in the headlights” or a “possum playing dead” (Roelofs and Dayan, 2022). This behavior involves both the SNS and PNS, and while they are both active, they vary in which system predominates during the sequence of events associated with the fear freeze. Upon exposure to an extreme stressor (eg. predator) the SNS is activated to increase in HR, arterial pressure, respiration, and muscle tone in response to first encounter with a predator. However, the actual freeze component is a well described sequence of central reflexes initiated by stimulation of the locus coeruleous in the hindbrain by cortical sensory efferents. Ultimately, this central reflex stimulates the PNS results during the freeze to decrease HR and respiration in order to calm and keep the animal quiet. The reflex also stimulates pupillary dilation in order to increase perception of the individual’s environment (Roelofs and Dayan, 2022). If the predator or aggressive stimulus continues, then the SNS reinitiates itself for the fight or flight response. Synergistically, these sequences of events result in an animal that is frozen but ready to move. The impact on HR and respiration depends on which branch of the autonomic nervous system is in control (Roelofs, 2017). In our experiment 1, both GLY and LAB groups had longer durations of TI than CON. In our experiment 2, DRUG had longer durations of TI than CON2. Taking these results into consideration, along with the knowledge that it is possible for the two nervous systems to work synergistically, our data suggest that both the PNS and SNS are involved in TI. However, future research is required to determine the exact sequence of events involved with autonomic control of TI.

The SNS is responsible for various changes in the body, such as airway dilation (lower RR), an increase in HR, and an increase in blood flow (McCorry, 2007; Brown et al., 2013; Edgar et al., 2013). Since labetalol inhibits the SNS from acting by blocking the alpha-1, beta-1, and beta-2 receptors, one would expect to see a higher RR, lower HR, and a decrease in blood flow/body temperature. While there were no changes in RR or HR for the LAB group. The lack of changes in HR is likely due to the technique used. Stethoscopes were utilized to listen to and count HR given there are not yet any better techniques. Counting bird HR can be highly variable due to how fast their hearts beat. However, there was a decrease in comb temperature from pre-injection to during TI. This provides evidence that the labetalol was effective in laying hens. The PNS is responsible for changes in the body, such as airway constriction (higher RR), a decrease in HR, and a decrease in peripheral blood flow (Kenney, 1985; McCorry, 2007; Tindle and Tadi, 2022). Glycopyrrolate inhibits the PNS by outcompeting acetylcholine for the muscarinic receptors so one would expect to see a decrease in RR, increase in HR, and increase in blood flow/body temperature. There were no changes in our study for HR or temperature for GLY, however, there was a change in RR suggesting, perhaps, an incomplete blockade of PNS activity, or alternatively, our inability to completely measure the blockade with our methodology. After injection, the GLY group continuously had lower RR than LAB and CON. We did not see that there was a reduction in RR, however it did not increase, which also suggests that there was a successful block of the PNS.

In conclusion, our data show that the PNS and SNS both play a role in at least the duration of TI. Our data differs slightly from previous research since in our study, glycopyrrolate increased the duration of TI. Previous studies done by Thompson et al. (1974), Hicks (1976), and Hughes (1982) found that scopolamine, a drug similar in its mechanism of action to glycopyrrolate, decreased the duration of TI in young leghorns between 7 and 21 DOA. However, scopolamine crosses the blood-brain barrier, whereas glycopyrrolate does not, thereby suggesting the involvement of the central nervous system in the TI response. Additionally, the birds in the aforementioned studies were much younger than the ones used in our study; we used early to peak lay adult hens while other research used chicks. Chicks (7 days of age) do not have as well-developed a blood-brain barrier as their older counterparts; this could show that the peripheral autonomic and central nervous systems have differing effects on TI. However, future research should address the possibly differing roles of the central and peripheral nervous systems in TI.
