Machine Learning as Iterated Belief Change à la Darwiche and Pearl
Theofanis Aravanis

Abstract
Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) are powerful machine-learning models capable of capturing intricate non-linear relationships. They are widely used nowadays across numerous scientific and engineering domains, driving advancements in both research and real-world applications. In our recent work [ 2 ] , we focused on the statics and dynamics of a particular subclass of ANNs, which we refer to as binary ANNs. A binary ANN is a feed-forward network in which both inputs and outputs are restricted to binary values, making it particularly suitable for a variety of practical use cases. Our previous study approached binary ANNs through the lens of belief-change theory, specifically the Alchourrón, Gärdenfors and Makinson (AGM) framework, yielding several key insights. Most notably, we demonstrated that the knowledge embodied in a binary ANN (expressed through its input-output behaviour) can be symbolically represented using a propositional logic language. Moreover, the process of modifying a belief set (through revision or contraction) was mapped onto a gradual transition through a series of intermediate belief sets. Analogously, the training of binary ANNs was conceptualized as a sequence of such belief-set transitions, which we showed can be formalized using full-meet AGM-style belief change. In the present article, we extend this line of investigation by addressing some critical limitations of our previous study. Specifically, we show that Dalal’s method for belief change naturally induces a structured, gradual evolution of states of belief. More importantly, given the known shortcomings of full-meet belief change, we demonstrate that the training dynamics of binary ANNs can be more effectively modelled using robust AGM-style change operations —namely, lexicographic revision and moderate contraction— that align with the Darwiche-Pearl framework for iterated belief change.



Keywords:
Machine Learning, Iterated Belief Change, Darwiche and Pearl’s Approach, Gradual Belief Transitions

Introduction

Belief change is the study of how an agent’s set of beliefs should be modified —typically through
revision
and/or
contraction
— when confronted with new evidence
[
11
,
28
,
10
]
. The foundations of this field were laid by the influential work of Alchourrón, Gärdenfors and Makinson
[
1
]
, who introduced a formal model for belief revision and contraction. This model, now known as the
AGM framework
, has since become a standard and widely accepted approach for analysing belief change.

Within the AGM framework, a logical theory
K
(also referred to as
belief set
) represents the belief corpus of an agent, while a sentence
\varphi
represents new evidential input. Belief-change operations are modelled as binary functions, mapping the pair
(K,\varphi)
to a modified theory, reflecting the result of incorporating or discarding information. The behaviour of these operations is governed by a set of well-accepted rationality postulates.

Since the foundational work of the AGM trio, several concrete belief-change operations have been proposed. One fundamental —albeit rather trivial— approach is
full-meet
belief change
[
1
]
. Although this method adheres to the AGM rationality postulates, it has been criticized for producing implausible outcomes
[
31
]
. A more refined alternative that also respects the AGM postulates is Dalal’s belief-change method, which is based on
Hamming distance

[
7
]
.

While the AGM postulates rigorously define the behaviour of
single-step
belief change —that is, the transition from an initial belief set
K
to its modified form— they offer little guidance on how to handle
sequences
of belief adjustments. This gap is addressed by the theory of
iterated
belief change, which focuses on the relationships between successive modifications and how earlier transformations influence subsequent ones
[
29
]
. One of the most influential proposals to iterated revision is the work of Darwiche and Pearl
(“DP” for short), which extends the AGM postulates with additional principles that regulate iterated revisions
[
8
,
9
]
. Darwiche and Pearl’s approach was later adapted to the context of belief contraction by Konieczny and Pino Pérez
[
21
]
. Among the most prominent DP-compliant concrete approaches are
lexicographic
revision
[
26
,
27
]
and
moderate
contraction
[
30
]
.

In a different vein, one may argue that the process of
machine learning
inherently involves a form of belief change. That is, a machine-learning model maintains a body of knowledge (some form of “beliefs”), which it modifies in response to incoming training data. This perspective underpins our recent study
[
2
]
, which examined the statics and dynamics of a certain class of Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) that we term
binary
ANNs. A binary ANN is a feed-forward ANN with all inputs and outputs consisting of
binary values
, and as such, it is well-suited for various practical applications, such as image processing and pattern recognition on datasets akin to the benchmark MNIST dataset
[
23
]
. Binary ANNs were extensively studied in
[
2
]
through the lens of belief-change theory, leading to several key contributions, including the following:

•

The knowledge of binary ANNs (expressed via their input-output relationship) can
symbolically
be represented in terms of a
propositional logic language
; specifically, by means of a collection of belief sets.

•

Within the belief-change framework, the transformation from an initial belief set to a modified one can be conceptualized as a
gradual
transition through intermediate states of belief, a perspective that aligns more closely with the cognitive behaviour of real-world agents
[
22
,
35
]
. Analogously, the
training
process of binary ANNs, implemented via the fundamental
backpropagation
algorithm
[
32
]
, can be interpreted as a sequence of
incremental transformations
between belief sets. Each step in this sequence can be quantitatively characterized by a
distance metric
that captures the degree of symbolic divergence between successive belief sets.

•

The aforementioned successive transitions of belief sets can be modelled by means of
full-meet
belief change.

In this article, we push further towards this line of research, establishing the following contributions:

•

We show that Dalal’s method for belief change inherently yields a gradual sequence of intermediate belief sets, where each transition is governed by a natural distance metric.

•

By means of a
refinement
of a distance metric between belief sets proposed in
[
2
]
, we demonstrate that the successive transitions of belief sets, followed by a binary ANN during training, can be modelled through
DP-compatible
AGM revision and contraction operations. In particular, we employ lexicographic revision and moderate contraction, which offer a more robust and well-behaved alternative to the full-meet belief change approach of
[
2
]
. This shift addresses several documented limitations of full-meet belief change and provides a more principled foundation for understanding belief dynamics in binary ANNs.

Evidently, this study is situated at the intersection of neuro-symbolic (hybrid) Artificial Intelligence (AI), with a particular emphasis on integrating AGM-style belief change into machine-learning systems — a direction that, while central to explainable and principled AI, remains largely under-explored. Although the broader neuro-symbolic field has gained significant attention in recent years
[
25
]
, the specific integration of belief-change theory into learning architectures is still in its early stages. To the best of our knowledge, the closest related works are those by Coste-Marquis and Marquis
[
6
]
, and Schwind
et al.

[
34
,
33
]
. The first two contributions
[
6
,
34
]
approach the incorporation of symbolic background knowledge into Boolean classifiers through the lens of belief revision, identifying the limitations of traditional belief-change operations and proposing specialized editing mechanisms to adapt classifiers with minimal disruption. The most recent work
[
33
]
is directly aligned with our study, introducing a learning framework grounded in
improvement operators

[
20
,
19
]
— a generalization of iterated belief change that permits gradual, noise-tolerant updates. Focusing on binary classification, Schwind
et al.
show that such improvement-based models perform competitively when benchmarked against standard machine-learning approaches. Our contribution builds upon this line of research by also leveraging iterated belief change, but rather than developing a belief-change-driven learning system, we focus on modelling the training dynamics of binary ANNs using well-established iterated belief-change operators.

The remainder of this article is structured as follows. The next section establishes the formal prerequisites necessary for our discussion. Section
3
presents the AGM framework, along with two notable belief-change operations, Dalal’s method and full-meet contraction. Section
4
explores belief change as a process involving gradual transitions through intermediate states of belief. Section
5
provides an overview of iterated belief change and its guiding principles. The subsequent sections examine the connection between machine learning and belief change. Specifically, Section
6
introduces the class of binary ANNs, while Section
7
demonstrates the compatibility of machine learning with the DP approach.

Belief Sets:

. As we shall subsequently introduce formal properties of revision and contraction functions, let us first define the simpler operation of

.

A

The AGM Framework

The task of modifying beliefs was formally addressed by Alchourrón, Gärdenfors and Makinson, who introduced a flexible and influential model for belief change, now widely known as the
AGM framework

[
1
]
. In this framework, an agent’s beliefs are captured by a logical theory
K
(also called a
belief set
), while new information (referred to as
epistemic input
) is expressed as a logical formula
\varphi
. The AGM framework focuses on two core types of belief modification:
belief revision
(or simply, revision) and
belief contraction
(or simply, contraction). The AGM trio provided an
axiomatic
characterization of both operations using a set of widely accepted
rationality postulates
. Later, Katsuno and Mendelzon proposed a characterization of belief revision in terms of possible worlds
[
18
]
. In this section, we outline the AGM framework’s axiomatic foundation (Subsection
3.1
), the possible-worlds semantics introduced by Katsuno and Mendelzon (Subsection
3.2
), and two specific AGM-style belief-change strategies: full-meet belief change
[
1
]
(Subsection
3.3
) and Dalal’s approach
[
7
]
(Subsection
3.4
).

3.1
Axiomatic Characterization

Within the AGM framework
[
1
]
, the process of belief revision is formalized through a
revision function
. A revision function
\ast
is a binary function that takes as input a belief set
K
and a formula
\varphi
, and produces a new belief set
K\ast\varphi
, which reflects the outcome of incorporating
\varphi
into
K
. A revision function
\ast
is considered an
AGM revision function
iff it satisfies postulates
(K\ast 1)
–
(K\ast 8)
.

\bf(K\ast 1)

K\ast\varphi
is a theory.

\bf(K\ast 2)

\varphi\in K\ast\varphi
.

\bf(K\ast 3)

K\ast\varphi\subseteq K+\varphi
.

\bf(K\ast 4)

If
\neg\varphi\notin K
, then
K+\varphi\subseteq K\ast\varphi
.

\bf(K\ast 5)

If
\varphi
is consistent, then
K\ast\varphi
is also consistent.

\bf(K\ast 6)

If
\varphi\equiv\psi
, then
K\ast\varphi=K\ast\psi
.

\bf(K\ast 7)

K\ast(\varphi\wedge\psi)\subseteq(K\ast\varphi)+\psi
.

\bf(K\ast 8)

If
\neg\psi\notin K\ast\varphi
, then
(K\ast\varphi)+\psi\subseteq K\ast(\varphi\wedge\psi)
.

.

When
\varphi

), it follows from postulates

and

(K\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\hss\cr$-$\crcr}}}1)

\bf(K\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\hss\cr$-$\crcr}}}2)

\bf(K\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\hss\cr$-$\crcr}}}4)

3.2
Semantic Characterization

Postulates
(K\ast 1)
–
(K\ast 8)
do
not uniquely
determine the revised belief set
K\ast\varphi
based solely on
K
and
\varphi
; rather, they delineate the range of all rationally acceptable ways to carry out belief revision. To precisely define the revised set
K\ast\varphi
, one must turn to
constructive models
of belief change — extra-logical mechanisms that encode specific strategies for modifying beliefs. A widely adopted approach of this kind is the model introduced by Katsuno and Mendelzon
[
18
]
, which relies on a particular class of total preorders over possible worlds known as
faithful preorders
.

Definition 3

(Faithful Preorder,
[
18
]
)
.

A total preorder
\preceq_{K}
over
\mathbb{M}
is faithful to a belief set
K
iff
[K]\neq\varnothing
entails
[K]=\min(\mathbb{M},\preceq_{K})
.

Intuitively, a faithful preorder
\preceq_{K}
over the set of possible worlds
\mathbb{M}
captures the
relative plausibility
of those worlds with respect to the belief set
K
. In this ordering, worlds that are considered more plausible given
K
are ranked lower.

Based on the notion of a faithful preorder, Katsuno and Mendelzon prove the next important result.

Theorem 4

(
[
18
]
)
.

A revision function
\ast
satisfies postulates
(K\ast 1)
–
(K\ast 8)
iff, for any theory
K
, there exists a total preorder
\preceq_{K}
over
\mathbb{M}
, faithful to
K
, such that, for any sentence
\varphi\in\mathbb{L}
:

(R)

[K\ast\varphi]=\min([\varphi],\preceq_{K})
.

Therefore, under condition (R), the revised belief set
K\ast\varphi
is defined in terms of the most plausible
\varphi
-worlds, with respect to
K
.

Hence, according to condition (R), the revised belief set
K\ast\varphi
is specified in terms of the most plausible
\varphi
-worlds relative to
K
.

Combining condition (R) with the well-known Levi and Harper Identities

14
]

)

:

(C)

[K\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\hss\cr$-$\crcr}}}\varphi]=[K]\cup\min([\neg\varphi],\preceq_{K})
.

Condition (C) indicates that the contracted belief set
K\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\hss\cr$-$\crcr}}}\varphi
is specified in terms of the set-theoretic union of the
K
-worlds and the most plausible
\neg\varphi
-worlds relative to
K
.

For an AGM revision function
\ast
and an AGM contraction function
\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\hss\cr$-$\crcr}}}
, we shall sometimes write
\preceq^{\ast}
and
\preceq^{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raise 3.01389pt\hbox{.}\hss\cr$-$\crcr}}}}
to denote faithful preorders over
\mathbb{M}
corresponding to
\ast
and
\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\hss\cr$-$\crcr}}}
, via conditions (R) and (C), respectively. These superscripts will be omitted whenever the context makes the intended operator clear.

3.3
Full-Meet Belief Change

A particular form of AGM-style belief change is known as
full-meet
revision and contraction, which was originally introduced in the foundational work by Alchourrón, Gärdenfors and Makinson
[
1
]
.

Definition 6

(Full-Meet Belief Change)
.

An AGM revision (resp., contraction) function
\ast
(resp.,
\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\hss\cr$-$\crcr}}}
) is the full-meet AGM revision (resp., contraction) function iff, for any theory
K
, it is specified —via (R) (resp., (C))— through a total preorder
\preceq_{K}
over
\mathbb{M}
, such that all worlds
r,r^{\prime}\in\big(\mathbb{M}\setminus[K]\big)
are equally plausible; that is,
r\approx_{K}r^{\prime}
.

The next observation follows immediately from Definition
6
.

Remark 7
.

Let
\ast
be the full-meet AGM revision function, and let
\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\hss\cr$-$\crcr}}}
be the full-meet AGM contraction function. Moreover, let
K
be a theory, and let
\varphi
be a sentence of
\mathbb{L}
. If
\neg\varphi\in K
, then
K\ast\varphi=Cn(\varphi)
. If
\varphi\in K
, then
K\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\hss\cr$-$\crcr}}}\varphi=K\cap Cn(\neg\varphi)
.

The above remark highlights that, although full-meet revision perfectly respects postulates
(K\ast 1)
–
(K\ast 8)
, it exhibits a radical behaviour by entirely discarding
all
prior beliefs when they conflict with new evidence. For this reason, Rott refers to this type of revision as
amnesic

[
31
]
. Similarly, full-meet contraction, while conforming to postulates
(K\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\hss\cr$-$\crcr}}}1)
–
(K\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\hss\cr$-$\crcr}}}8)
receiving praise from Hansson
[
13
]
, always produces the
smallest
theory —removing too much from the initial belief set— compared to other AGM-style contractions. As will be discussed in Section
5
(Remark
19
), full-meet belief change also performs poorly with respect to iterated belief revision
[
8
,
9
]
.

3.4
Dalal’s Revision Operator

A more fine-grained alternative to the full-meet approach to belief change is the Dalal’s method
[
7
]
. Given a belief set
K
, Dalal assigns plausibility to possible worlds relative to
K
using a total preorder
\preceq_{K}
over
\mathbb{M}
, which is faithful to
K
and defined according to a
Hamming-distance
metric between worlds. In the special case where
K
is inconsistent (i.e.,
[K]=\varnothing
), the revised belief set
K\ast\varphi
is simply defined as the logical closure of
\varphi
, that is,
Cn(\varphi)
. In the more typical case where
K
is consistent (i.e.,
[K]\neq\varnothing
), Dalal introduces the following formal definitions.

Definition 8

(Difference between Worlds)
.

The difference
Diff
(r,r^{\prime})
between two worlds
r
,
r^{\prime}
of
\mathbb{M}
is the set of propositional variables that have different truth values in the two worlds. That is,

Diff
(r,r^{\prime})=\Big(\big(r\setminus r^{\prime}\big)\cup\big(r^{\prime}\setminus r\big)\Big)\cap\mathcal{P}
.

Definition 9

(Distance between Belief Sets and Worlds,
[
7
]
)
.

The distance D
(K,r)
between a consistent belief set
K
and a world
r
is the cardinality-minimum difference between
r
and any
K
-world. That is,

D
(K,r)=\min\bigg(\Big\{\big|
Diff
(w,r)\big|:w\in[K]\Big\}\bigg)
.

Definition 10

(Dalal’s Preorder,
[
7
]
)
.

A Dalal’s preorder
\sqsubseteq_{K}
over
\mathbb{M}
is a preorder uniquely specified such that, for any
r,r^{\prime}\in\mathbb{M}
,

r\sqsubseteq_{K}r^{\prime}

iff

D
(K,r)

\leqslant
D
(K,r^{\prime})
.

Definition 11

(Dalal’s Revision Operator,
[
7
]
)
.

Dalal’s revision operator
\star
is the revision function induced, via condition (R), by the family of Dalal’s preorders
\{\sqsubseteq_{K}\}_{\forall\,K}
over
\mathbb{M}
.

Katsuno and Mendelzon pointed out that, for every (consistent) belief set
K
, the Dalal’s preorder
\sqsubseteq_{K}
is total and faithful to
K

[
18
, p. 269]
. Consequently, in view of Theorem
4
, Dalal’s revision operator satisfies postulates
(K\ast 1)
–
(K\ast 8)
, and thus qualifies as an AGM revision function.

4
Belief Change as a Gradual Transition of Beliefs

As previously outlined, the AGM framework provides a set of rationality postulates governing how an agent should transition from an initial belief set
K_{1}
to a revised or contracted belief set
K_{2}
. However, it remains silent on the inner workings of the belief-change process itself — it focuses
solely
on the initial and final states of belief, leaving the intermediate steps unaccounted for.

Motivated by this limitation, and acknowledging that humans typically resist sudden belief alterations due to cognitive and psychological constraints
[
22
,
17
,
35
,
36
]
, our previous work
[
2
]
formally investigated the internal structure of the
K_{1}
-to-
K_{2}
transition. Specifically, we proposed a model in which belief change unfolds as a
gradual
, stepwise process, represented by a sequence of intermediate belief sets
H_{1},\ldots,H_{n}
. These sets capture the agent’s evolving cognitive state during the transition from
K_{1}
to
K_{2}
, and we denote this progression compactly by
\langle K_{1},K_{2}\rangle=(H_{1},\ldots,H_{n})
.

The following belief-revision scenario illustrates the issue.

Example 12

(Stepwise Revision of Scientific Hypotheses)
.

Dr. Athena is a climate scientist studying the relationship between solar activity and regional temperature changes. Initially, based on earlier studies, she firmly believes that neither solar flares nor sunspot cycles influence regional climate. Let
a
denote the proposition “solar flares do not affect climate”, and
b
the proposition “sunspot cycles do not affect climate”. Therefore, her initial belief set is
K_{1}=Cn(a\wedge b)
.

Later, Athena receives new empirical data from a long-term satellite observation program, which suggests that both solar flares and sunspot cycles do, in fact, have a measurable effect on temperature patterns. This new information is expressed by the sentence
\varphi=\neg a\wedge\neg b
. Rather than immediately revising her beliefs to adopt
K_{2}=Cn(\neg a\wedge\neg b)
, Athena proceeds cautiously. Given the complexity of the phenomena and her commitment to scientific rigour, she considers and evaluates the new claims separately. Her belief revision unfolds through the following intermediate belief sets:

•

First, she entertains the possibility that only sunspot cycles are influential, reaching the intermediate belief set
H_{1}=Cn(a\wedge\neg b)
.

•

After conducting additional analyses, she begins to suspect that solar flares may also play a role, leading her to consider
H_{2}=Cn(\neg a\wedge\neg b)
.

•

Still uncertain, she briefly considers an alternative hypothesis —perhaps only solar flares are responsible— represented by
H_{3}=Cn(\neg a\wedge b)
.

•

Eventually, after multiple rounds of peer review and validation, she commits to the final revised belief set
K_{2}=Cn(\neg a\wedge\neg b)
.

This progression —from complete scepticism to full acceptance— models a gradual epistemic shift driven by evidence and critical analysis. It underscores the idea that belief change, particularly in scientific inquiry, often occurs through a sequence of cautious, intermediate steps, rather than through abrupt, wholesale revision.

In
[
2
]
, we emphasized that, in a formal setting, the sequence of intermediate belief sets
H_{1},\ldots,H_{n}
may be determined by several principled criteria. For instance, it may be guided by the structure of the faithful preorder
\preceq_{K_{1}}
associated with the agent’s initial beliefs, or alternatively, by a suitable
distance metric
that quantifies (dis)similarity between belief sets. In the present section, we revisit the distance-based approach introduced in
[
2
]
and extend these metrics (Subsection
4.1
), and show that Dalal’s method for belief change naturally induces a gradual, well-structured progression through intermediate belief sets (Subsection
4.2
).

4.1
Distance between Belief Sets

A
distance metric
between two belief sets
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
serves to formally capture the degree of difference between the bodies of knowledge they encode. In
[
2
]
, we introduced two intuitive notions of such distance, referred to as
type-A
and
type-B
distances, each offering a distinct perspective on how belief sets may diverge. These are defined below.

Definition 13

(Type-A Distance,
[
2
]
)
.

Let
K_{1}
,
K_{2}
be two consistent belief sets. The type-A distance
Dist
{}_{A}(K_{1},K_{2})
between
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
is the cardinality-minimum difference between the
K_{1}
-worlds and the
K_{2}
-worlds. That is,

Dist
{}_{A}\big(K_{1},K_{2}\big)=\min\bigg(\Big\{\big|
Diff
(w,w^{\prime})\big|:w\in[K_{1}]\
and
\ w^{\prime}\in[K_{2}]\Big\}\bigg)
.

Definition 14

(Type-B Distance,
[
2
]
)
.

Let
K_{1}
,
K_{2}
be two belief sets. The type-B distance
Dist
{}_{B}(K_{1},K_{2})
between
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
is the symmetric difference between
[K_{1}]
and
[K_{2}]
. That is,

Dist
{}_{B}\big(K_{1},K_{2}\big)=\Big([K_{1}]\setminus[K_{2}]\Big)\cup\Big([K_{2}]\setminus[K_{1}]\Big)
.

Observe that type-A distance is a Hamming-based metric representing a non-negative integer, whereas type-B distance corresponds to a set of possible worlds. Example
15
concretely illustrates the usage of both type-A and type-B distances.

Example 15

(Distances between Belief Sets)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{a,b\}
, and let
K_{1}=Cn(\neg a\wedge b)
and
K_{2}=Cn(a\leftrightarrow b)
be two belief sets. Obviously then,
[K_{1}]=\{\overline{a}b\}
and
[K_{2}]=\{ab,\overline{a}\overline{b}\}
. In view of Definitions
13
and
14
, we conclude that Dist
{}_{A}\big(K_{1},K_{2}\big)=1
and
Dist
{}_{B}\big(K_{1},K_{2}\big)=\big\{ab,\overline{a}b,\overline{a}\overline{b}\big\}
, respectively.

Having formally specified the notion of distance between belief sets, let us consider the sequence
\big(T_{1},\ldots,T_{l}\big)=\big(K_{1},H_{1},\ldots,H_{n},K_{2}\big)
, representing the full trajectory of belief change from the initial set
K_{1}
to the final set
K_{2}
through intermediate states
H_{1},\ldots,H_{n}
. Within this sequence, certain natural and meaningful relationships among belief sets can be articulated via the following conditions, for any indices
i,j,m\in\{1,\ldots,l\}
such that
i<j<m
:

(DA)

Dist
{}_{A}\big(T_{i},T_{m}\big)\geqslant

Dist
{}_{A}\big(T_{j},T_{m}\big)
.

(DB)

\big|
Dist
{}_{B}\big(T_{i},T_{m}\big)\big|\geqslant\big|
Dist
{}_{B}\big(T_{j},T_{m}\big)\big|
.

(SD)

Dist
{}_{B}\big(T_{i},T_{m}\big)\supseteq

Dist
{}_{B}\big(T_{j},T_{m}\big)
.

Conditions (DA) and (DB), originally proposed in
[
2
]
, are both based on Hamming-type distance metrics, which quantify the number of differing entries between belief sets. In contrast, condition (SD), introduced herein for the first time, constitutes a
strict inclusion-based
refinement of (DB). Rather than measuring only the cardinality of type-B distances, it posits a stronger structural constraint, according to which the type-B distance between
T_{i}
and
T_{m}
must fully contain the type-B distance between
T_{j}
and
T_{m}
. As such, (SD) captures a more fine-grained and hierarchical view of belief progression, reflecting a deepening convergence toward the final state of belief.

4.2
Gradual Transition of Beliefs via Dalal’s Method

In this subsection, we demonstrate that the belief-change method proposed by Dalal
[
7
]
inherently supports a gradual and well-ordered transition through intermediate belief sets. In particular, we show that belief change carried out via Dalal’s preorders can satisfy the progressivity condition (DA), reflecting a structured reduction in informational distance as the agent progresses from an initial to a final belief set. To that end, consider Theorem

5
Iterated Belief Change: The DP Approach

The AGM framework, as originally introduced by Alchourrón, Gärdenfors and Makinson in
[
1
]
and presented in Section
3
, examines only
one-step
changes (revisions and/or contractions) and lacks guidelines for
iterated
belief change. Perhaps the most influential work addressing the problem of multiple-steps revisions is the approach of Darwiche and Pearl (DP)
[
9
]
. To regulate the process of iterated belief revision, Darwiche and Pearl proposed the subsequent postulates (DP1)–(DP4), where
\ast
denotes an arbitrary AGM revision function.

(DP1)

If
\varphi\models\psi
, then
(K\ast\psi)\ast\varphi=K\ast\varphi
.

(DP2)

If
\varphi\models\neg\psi
, then
(K\ast\psi)\ast\varphi=K\ast\varphi
.

(DP3)

If
\psi\in K\ast\varphi
, then
\psi\in(K\ast\psi)\ast\varphi
.

(DP4)

If
\neg\psi\not\in K\ast\varphi
, then
\neg\psi\not\in(K\ast\psi)\ast\varphi
.

We refer to any AGM revision function that satisfies postulates (DP1)–(DP4) as a
DP revision function
. A discussion on postulates (DP1)–(DP4) can be found in the survey of Peppas
[
29
, Section 7]
. Darwiche and Pearl proved that the following constraints (R1)–(R4) correspond to the possible-worlds characterization of postulates (DP1)–(DP4), respectively
[
9
]
.

(R1)

If
r,r^{\prime}\in[\varphi]
, then
r\preceq_{K}r^{\prime}
iff
r\preceq_{K\ast\varphi}r^{\prime}
.

(R2)

If
r,r^{\prime}\in[\neg\varphi]
, then
r\preceq_{K}r^{\prime}
iff
r\preceq_{K\ast\varphi}r^{\prime}
.

(R3)

If
r\in[\varphi]
and
r^{\prime}\in[\neg\varphi]
, then
r\prec_{K}r^{\prime}
implies
r\prec_{K\ast\varphi}r^{\prime}
.

(R4)

If
r\in[\varphi]
and
r^{\prime}\in[\neg\varphi]
, then
r\preceq_{K}r^{\prime}
implies
r\preceq_{K\ast\varphi}r^{\prime}
.

In a subsequent work
[
21
]
, Konieczny and Pino Pérez proposed the following postulates
(DPC1)–(DPC4)
as counterparts to (DP1)–(DP4) for contraction, where
\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\hss\cr$-$\crcr}}}
is an arbitrary AGM contraction function.

(DPC1)

If
\neg\varphi\models\chi
, then
K\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\hss\cr$-$\crcr}}}(\varphi\vee\psi)\models K\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\hss\cr$-$\crcr}}}\varphi
iff
(K\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\hss\cr$-$\crcr}}}\chi)\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\hss\cr$-$\crcr}}}(\varphi\vee\psi)\models(K\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\hss\cr$-$\crcr}}}\chi)\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\hss\cr$-$\crcr}}}\varphi
.

(DPC2)

If
\chi\models\varphi
, then

Remark 19
.

Let
\ast

\ast

Proof.

Let

\preceq_{K}

\mathbb{M}

Now, let

First, note that a binary ANN with a single binary output

. That is,

of

can be represented as a propositional formula
\psi
of
\mathbb{L}
, in the sense that
f
and
\psi
yield
identical truth tables
. To illustrate this correspondence, assume that the set of propositional variables
\mathcal{P}
contains exactly one variable for each input of the ANN; i.e.,
|\mathcal{P}|=n
. Moreover, let
w
be a possible world of
\mathbb{M}
such that, for any
p_{i}\in\mathcal{P}
with

,
p_{i}\in w
whenever
X_{i}=1
, and
\neg p_{i}\in w
whenever
X_{i}=0
. Clearly then, there exists a
one-to-one correspondence
between the propositional variables in
\mathcal{P}
and the binary inputs
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
. Accordingly, the following equivalence holds for the binary output
Y
:

f\big(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\big)=1

iff

w\in[\psi]
.

That is, the possible world
w
satisfies the formula
\psi
iff the Boolean function
f
of
Y
maps to
1
the truth values of the propositional variables assigned in
w
. Moreover, since for the belief set
K=Cn(\psi)
it holds that
[K]=[\psi]
, the Boolean function
f
of the binary output

can, equivalently, be represented by the belief set
K
.

6.2
Training through Backpropagation

In standard ANNs, each neuron produces an output by applying a non-linear activation function to a weighted sum of its inputs, where the weights and biases are trainable parameters corresponding to connections between successive layers. Training involves
forward propagation
, where outputs are computed sequentially from the input to the output layer using the current parameters, followed by
backpropagation
, which computes the gradient of a
loss function

\mathcal{L}
—quantifying prediction error— with respect to each parameter via the chain rule. These gradients are used in the
parameter-update
step, where weights and biases are adjusted in the direction that minimizes
\mathcal{L}
. Repeating this process over multiple epochs leads to progressive minimization of
\mathcal{L}
and improved predictive performance
[
32
]
.

As demonstrated in
[
2
]
, the notion of intermediate states of belief within the context of belief change can also be meaningfully applied to the training process of binary ANNs. Specifically, for a single-output binary ANN with output
Y
, one can identify a sequence of belief sets
K_{1},\ldots,K_{n}
such that each pair
K_{i}
and
K_{i+1}
(for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
) correspond to the Boolean functions representing
Y
immediately
before
and
after
the
i
-th update of the network parameters, respectively. In this view,
K_{1}
and
K_{n}
denote the belief sets before and after the entire training process, while
K_{2}
through
K_{n-1}
represent the transitional belief sets throughout training.

Importantly, under plausible assumptions ensuring consistent loss reduction and alignment with prediction error, it was shown in
[
2
]
that, for any
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
i<j
, it holds that

\big|
Dist
{}_{B}\big(K_{i},K_{n}\big)\big|\geqslant\big|
Dist
{}_{B}\big(K_{j},K_{n}\big)\big|
.

This relationship reflects the structure outlined in condition (DB) of Subsection
4.1
, and establishes a natural type-B distance-based ordering among the belief sets
K_{1},\ldots,K_{n}
induced by the training process of a single-output binary ANN. Specifically, it implies that, as training progresses, the internal representation of the ANN —in terms of its logical behaviour— becomes
increasingly aligned
with the final learned configuration, as determined by the training labels (targets). That is, each training update moves the model closer to (or at least not further from) the Boolean behaviour it ultimately converges to.

In a similar vein, one may posit an even
stronger
structural relationship among the belief sets
K_{1},\ldots,K_{n}
by invoking condition (SD) of Subsection
4.1
, formulated in this work. This condition asserts that, for any
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
i<j
,

Dist
{}_{B}\big(K_{i},K_{n}\big)\supseteq

Dist
{}_{B}\big(K_{j},K_{n}\big)
.

Intuitively, this means that the discrepancies (in terms of type-B distance) between earlier belief sets and the final one not only remain greater in magnitude, but also contain the discrepancies of later belief sets as subsets. Clearly, this stronger inclusion-based condition implies the inequality of cardinalities mentioned above, i.e.,
Dist
{}_{B}\big(K_{i},K_{n}\big)\supseteq

Dist
{}_{B}\big(K_{j},K_{n}\big)
entails
\big|
Dist
{}_{B}\big(K_{i},K_{n}\big)\big|\geqslant\big|
Dist
{}_{B}\big(K_{j},K_{n}\big)\big|
.

6.3
Illustrative Examples

This aforementioned hierarchical relationship among the belief sets
K_{1},\ldots,K_{n}
is not merely a theoretical construct, but rather a
descriptive regularity
often observed in the training dynamics of practical binary ANNs. While not guaranteed by design, the structure imposed by condition (SD) tends to emerge naturally in many real-world models, often aided by modern training practices such as adaptive optimizers, learning-rate schedules, momentum, and regularization
[
12
]
. These methods implicitly encourage smoother, more directed convergence, thereby reinforcing the subset-based alignment of belief sets over time. In this subsection, we illustrate this phenomenon through two case studies involving standard binary ANNs, each demonstrating how the training trajectory consistently conforms to the progression prescribed by condition (SD).

7
Machine Learning is DP-Compatible

As outlined in Subsection
6.2
, the backpropagation algorithm is a fundamental gradient-based method for training feed-forward ANNs. Through iterative adjustment of network’s parameters, it aims to reduce the loss function
\mathcal{L}
to a satisfactory level. Throughout this training process, a single-output binary ANN evolves through a sequence of belief sets, each representing a distinct Boolean function corresponding to the current state of its binary output. Building on this interpretation, Theorem
26
was formulated in
[
2
]
, which establishes that the entire progression of belief-set transitions in such a network can be effectively modelled using the full-meet AGM revision function in conjunction with the full-meet AGM contraction function.

Theorem 26

(
[
2
]
)
.

Let

be the binary output of a single-output binary ANN. Let

,
K_{2}
be belief sets that represent, respectively, the Boolean functions of

be the full-meet AGM revision function, and let
\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\hss\cr$-$\crcr}}}

\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in\mathbb{L}

K_{2}=\big(K_{1}\ast\varphi_{1}\big)\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\hss\cr$-$\crcr}}}\varphi_{2}

While Theorem

Conclusion

This article is a continuation of the research initiated in our earlier work
[
2
]
, where the foundational connection between belief-change theory and the training dynamics of binary Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) was introduced. In that work, we proposed a symbolic framework in which the knowledge encoded by binary ANNs is represented via propositional logic, and the training process is interpreted as a sequence of belief-set transitions, modelled through full-meet belief change.

In the present study, we extended that framework by addressing key conceptual and technical limitations. First, we showed that Dalal’s method for belief change
[
7
]
inherently enables a structured and gradual modification of belief sets, progressing through intermediate states of belief. More significantly, we replaced the restrictive full-meet operation with well-behaved belief-change operators —namely, lexicographic revision
[
26
,
27
]
and moderate contraction
[
30
]
— that are consistent with the Darwiche-Pearl approach for iterated belief change
[
9
]
. This refinement allows for a more expressive and robust modelling of the dynamics underlying binary ANN training.

Taken together, our results strengthen the theoretical foundations of symbolic reasoning in machine learning, and pave the way for future work that explores the interpretability, robustness, and epistemic characterization of ANN behaviour through the lens of formal logic and belief dynamics.
