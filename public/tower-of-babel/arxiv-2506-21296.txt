Abstract
. In the context of random regular graphs, the size of the maximum cut is probably the second most studied graph parameter after the independence ratio. Zdeborová and Boettcher used the cavity method, a non-rigorous statistical physics technique, to predict one-step replica symmetry breaking (1-RSB) formulas. Coja-Ohglan et al. confirmed these predictions as rigorous upper bounds using the interpolation method. While these upper bounds were not expected to be exact, they may be very close to the true values. In this paper, we establish 2-RSB upper bounds and fine-tune their parameters to beat the aforementioned 1-RSB bounds.



1.
Introduction

This paper is concerned with the size of the
maximum cut
of
random regular graphs
.

Recall that a
cut
of a graph
G
is a partition of the vertex set
V(G)
into two sets:
V_{1}\sqcup V_{2}=V(G)
. Let
e[V_{1},V_{2}]
denote the
size of the cut
, that is, the number of edges crossing the cut (i.e., going between
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
). We will write
\gamma(G)
for the size of the maximum cut normalized by the total number of edges in
G
:

(1)

\gamma(G)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\max_{V_{1}\sqcup V_{2}=V(G)}\frac{e[V_{1},V_{2}]}{e(G)}.

Furthermore, a graph is said to be
regular
if each vertex has the same degree. For a fixed degree
d
, let
\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
be a uniform random
d
-regular graph on
N
vertices. This paper provides improved upper bounds on
\gamma\big(\mathbb{G}_{N,d}\big)
that hold asymptotically almost surely as
N\to\infty
.

1.1.
Ising model

A well-established approach for estimating the size of the maximum cut is to study the free energy of the Ising model. On a finite graph
G
, the Ising model is defined as follows. The
Hamiltonian
of a
spin configuration

\tau=\big(\tau_{v}\big)_{v\in V(G)}\in\{-1,+1\}^{V(G)}
is

\mathcal{H}_{G}(\tau)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{uv\in E(G)}\frac{1+\tau_{u}\tau_{v}}{2};

i.e.,
\mathcal{H}_{G}(\tau)
is simply the number of edges connecting vertices of the same spin. If
G
is
d
-regular and has
N
vertices, then we have

\mathcal{H}_{G}(\tau)=\frac{Nd}{2}-e[V_{\tau}^{-},V_{\tau}^{+}]\text{, where }V_{\tau}^{-}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\{v\,:\,\tau_{v}=-1\}\text{ and }V_{\tau}^{+}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\{v\,:\,\tau_{v}=+1\}.

Here
e[V_{\tau}^{-},V_{\tau}^{+}]
is the size of the cut corresponding to
\tau
. Consequently, on regular graphs, minimizing the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{G}
of the Ising model is equivalent to finding the maximum cut.

Given a real parameter
\beta
, the partition function of the model is

Z_{G,\beta}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{\tau\in\{\pm 1\}^{V(G)}}\exp\big(-\beta\mathcal{H}_{G}(\tau)\big).

In the antiferromagnetic regime
\beta>0
, the corresponding Boltzmann distribution is biased towards opposite spins on neighboring vertices. In fact, we will be interested in the limit
\beta\to\infty
, where the Boltzmann distribution concentrates on configurations corresponding to maximum cuts of
G
.

A simple but key observation is that for
\beta>0
we have

Z_{G,\beta}\geq\exp\bigg(-\beta\min_{\tau}H_{G}(\tau)\bigg)=\exp\left(-\beta\big(1-\gamma(G)\big)\frac{Nd}{2}\right).

Equivalently,

(2)

\gamma(G)\leq 1+\frac{\log Z_{G,\beta}}{\beta Nd/2}.

If we consider the random
d
-regular graph
\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
on
N
vertices, then the following limit is known
[
2
]
to exist for every
d,\beta
:

(3)

\Phi_{d}(\beta)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}\log Z_{\mathbb{G},\beta}}{N}.

Furthermore, let

\Phi^{\ast}_{d}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\liminf_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{\Phi_{d}(\beta)}{\beta}.

The following asymptotic bound follows easily from (
2
) and the well-known fact that
\gamma(\mathbb{G}_{N,d})
is tightly concentrated around its mean; see Section
2
for details.

Lemma 1.1
.

It holds with probability
1-o_{N}(1)
that

\gamma\big(\mathbb{G}_{N,d}\big)\leq 1+\frac{2}{d}\Phi^{\ast}_{d}+o_{N}(1).

Consequently, any upper bound on
\Phi^{\ast}_{d}
immediately yields an upper bound for the
MaxCut
problem over random
d
-regular graphs.

1.2.
Interpolation method

The interpolation method is a rigorous technique for bounding
\mathbb{E}\log Z
in various models. It is based on an “interpolating” family, which continuously transforms the original model into a “decoupled” one in such a way that
\mathbb{E}\log Z
increases along the way. It has been used with great success to confirm a number of statistical physics predictions (non-rigorously) derived from the cavity method. In the context of maximum cuts, Coja-Oghlan et al.
[
3
]
used the interpolation method to rigorously prove upper bounds predicted by Zdeborová and Boettcher
[
11
]
.

Originally, Franz and Leone established the interpolation method in sparse random graphs for a family of spin systems (including the Ising model) in
[
4
]
. This was later adapted to random regular graphs as well. A detailed exposition can be found in
[
9
, Appendix E]
. The parameters of the general RSB bound include a
functional order parameter
which can be thought of as a measure. In
[
3
]
the authors used a simple and natural choice for this measure before taking the limit
\beta\to\infty
. The resulting formula is far more manageable as it only has two free variables, see (
6
).

In this paper we consider 2-RSB formulas. At the “deepest layer” of the RSB hierarchy we employ a similar parameter choice as Coja-Oghlan et al. before taking the limit
\beta\to\infty
. The resulting bound still has a measure parameter. Interestingly, even purely atomic measures with a small number of atoms yield good results. The key is that both the size and the location of each atom are treated as variables. These variables can be tuned to a great precision due to the relatively small number of variables.

See Theorem
1.2
below for the discrete 2-RSB formula we obtained. Table
1
shows the best bounds we found via numerical optimization for specific values of
d
. While the improvements may seem small, we actually expect the true values to be fairly close to these upper bounds.

1.3.
Discrete 2-RSB formula

Our 2-RSB upper bound includes a number of parameters: real numbers
z,m\in(0,1)
and probabilities
p_{i}
along with three-dimensional vectors
\bm{q}_{i}
. Given a vector
\bm{a}\in[0,1]^{3}
, we will use the following notation for its coordinates:

\bm{a}=\big(a^{-},a^{0},a^{+}\big).

We will always assume that the three coordinates add up to one:
a^{-}+a^{0}+a^{+}=1
.

The formula involves two functions (
F^{\mathrm{edge}}_{z}
and
F^{\mathrm{star}}_{z}
) whose arguments are three-dimensional vectors. Specifically,

(4)

F^{\mathrm{edge}}_{z}(\bm{a}_{1},\bm{a}_{2})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=1+(z-1)\big(a_{1}^{-}a_{2}^{-}+a_{1}^{+}a_{2}^{+}\big).

To define the other function, we need the following notation. Given a sequence
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{d})
of signs
\{-,0,+\}
, let
k^{-}(\sigma)
,
k^{0}(\sigma)
, and
k^{+}(\sigma)
denote the number of
-
’s,
0
’s, and
+
’s in
\sigma
, respectively. Furthermore, let
k(\sigma)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\min\big(k^{-}(\sigma),k^{+}(\sigma)\big)
. Then we define
F^{\mathrm{star}}_{z}
as follows:

(5)

F^{\mathrm{star}}_{z}(\bm{a}_{1},\ldots,\bm{a}_{d})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{\sigma\in\{-,0,+\}^{d}}a_{1}^{\sigma_{1}}\cdots a_{d}^{\sigma_{d}}z^{k(\sigma)}.

Now we can state our upper bound.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
z,m\in(0,1)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose that
1
1

1

The condition
p_{1}+\cdots+p_{n}=1
may be omitted because multiplying each
p_{i}
with the same constant does not change the value of the upper bound formula.

p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\in[0,1]\text{ with }p_{1}+\cdots+p_{n}=1,

and that for each
i=1,\ldots,n
we have

\bm{q}_{i}=\big(q_{i}^{-},q_{i}^{0},q_{i}^{+}\big)\in[0,1]^{3}\text{ with }q_{i}^{-}+q_{i}^{0}+q_{i}^{+}=1.

Then

\displaystyle\Phi^{\ast}_{d}\,m\log(1/z)

\displaystyle\leq\log\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n}\cdots\sum_{i_{d}=1}^{n}\,\bigg(\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}p_{i_{\ell}}\bigg)\bigg(F^{\mathrm{star}}_{z}\big(\bm{q}_{i_{1}},\ldots,\bm{q}_{i_{d}}\big)\bigg)^{m}

\displaystyle-\frac{d}{2}\log\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{n}p_{i_{1}}p_{i_{2}}\bigg(F^{\mathrm{edge}}_{z}\big(\bm{q}_{i_{1}},\bm{q}_{i_{2}}\big)\bigg)^{m}.

Let us write up
F^{\mathrm{star}}
more explicitly. We may partition the sequences
\sigma
according to the value
k(\sigma)
: for

let

S_{d,k}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\bigg\{\sigma\in\{-,0,+\}^{d}\,:\,k(\sigma)=k\bigg\},

and define the corresponding polynomials:

P_{d,k}(\bm{a}_{1},\ldots,\bm{a}_{d})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{d,k}}a_{1}^{\sigma_{1}}\cdots a_{d}^{\sigma_{d}}.

Then
F^{\mathrm{star}}_{z}
can be written as

F^{\mathrm{star}}_{z}(\bm{a}_{1},\ldots,\bm{a}_{d})=\sum_{k}P_{d,k}(\bm{a}_{1},\ldots,\bm{a}_{d})z^{k}.

Note that each
P_{d,k}
is a homogeneous multivariate polynomial (in
3d
variables) of degree
d
. Adding them up for all

gives
1
:

\sum_{k}P_{d,k}(\bm{a}_{1},\ldots,\bm{a}_{d})=\sum_{\sigma\in\{-,0,+\}^{d}}a_{1}^{\sigma_{1}}\cdots a_{d}^{\sigma_{d}}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\big(a_{i}^{-}+a_{i}^{0}+a_{i}^{+}\big)=1^{d}=1.

For a specific example, consider the smallest case
d=3
, when
k(\sigma)
has two possible values (
0
and
1
), and it is easy to see that
S_{3,1}
consists of the following
12
triples:

•

(-,-,+)
and its permutations (
3

•

(-,+,+)
and its permutations (
3
triples);

•

(-,\,0,\,+)
and its permutations (
6

Since
P_{3,0}(\bm{a}_{1},\bm{a}_{2},\bm{a}_{3})+P_{3,1}(\bm{a}_{1},\bm{a}_{2},\bm{a}_{3})=1
, it follows that

F^{\mathrm{star}}_{z}(\bm{a}_{1},\bm{a}_{2},\bm{a}_{3})=1+(z-1)P_{3,1}(\bm{a}_{1},\bm{a}_{2},\bm{a}_{3})=1+(z-1)\sum_{\sigma\in S_{3,1}}a_{1}^{\sigma_{1}}a_{2}^{\sigma_{2}}a_{3}^{\sigma_{3}}.

1.4.
Tuning the parameters

Note that in Theorem
1.2
we get an upper bound for
any

z,m,p_{i},\bm{q}_{i}
. One can use a computer to find good substitutions for these parameters. It turns out that the function to optimize is very “rugged”: it has a large number of local minima with values close to the global minimum. Therefore, basic (local) optimization techniques are not sufficient. Our approach was to first use a Particle Swarm optimization algorithm (attempting to explore this rugged landscape), then, starting from the best point found by the Particle Swarm, to perform a local optimization in order to fine-tune the parameters.

n=4

Comparison with 1-RSB

, while

is a multivariate polynomial in

using (
5
).

1.6.
1-RSB revisited

In fact, it is possible to improve upon the best previous bounds from
[
3
]
even within the framework of one-step replica symmetry breaking by making a more elaborate choice in the parameters before taking the limit
\beta\to\infty
. We will refer to these bounds as
1^{+}
-RSB bounds; see Section
4
for details.

Although the bounds we get this way are inferior to the ones obtained by 2-RSB, there are a smaller number of free variables to optimize. Consequently, one may be able to perform the necessary numerical optimization even for those values of
d
that are too large for the 2-RSB approach.

2.
Configuration model and concentration

This short section contains some standard and well-known facts about random regular graphs and their maximum cut.

Recall that we defined
\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
as a uniform random graph among all
d
-regular simple graphs on the vertex set
\{1,\ldots,N\}
. As usual, we will work with the closely related random graphs produced by the
configuration model
instead. Given
N
vertices, each with
d
“half-edges”, the configuration model picks a random pairing of these
Nd
half-edges, producing
Nd/2
edges. The resulting graph is
d
-regular but it may have loops and/or multiple edges. We denote this random multigraph by
\mathbb{G}^{\mathrm{conf}}_{N,d}
. A well-known fact is that if
\mathbb{G}^{\mathrm{conf}}_{N,d}
is conditioned to be simple, then we get back
\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
. Moreover, for any
d
, the probability that
\mathbb{G}^{\mathrm{conf}}_{N,d}
is simple converges to a positive
p_{d}
as
N\to\infty
.

We say that a property holds
asymptotically almost surely (a.a.s.)
as
N\to\infty
if it holds with probability
1-o_{N}(1)
. If
\mathbb{G}^{\mathrm{conf}}_{N,d}
a.a.s. has a certain property, then so does
\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
. Therefore, it suffices to prove our a.a.s. results for
\mathbb{G}^{\mathrm{conf}}_{N,d}
.

In order to prove Lemma
1.1
we need an Azuma-type concentration result for
\gamma(\mathbb{G}_{N,d})
. One may derive one from the following general result from Wormald’s survey
[
10
]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

[
10
, Theorem 2.19]

Let
\varphi
be a real-valued graph parameter defined for
N
-vertex,
d
-regular multigraphs. A
simple swithching
is replacing a pair of edges
v_{1}v_{2}
,
v_{3}v_{4}
of a multigraph with the pair
v_{1}v_{3}
,
v_{2}v_{4}
. Suppose that
|\varphi(G^{\prime})-\varphi(G)|\leq c
holds whenever
G^{\prime}
can be obtained from
G
using a simple switching. Then for any
t>0
it holds for
\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{G}^{\mathrm{conf}}_{N,d}
that

\mathbb{P}\bigg(|\varphi(\mathbb{G})-\mathbb{E}\varphi(\mathbb{G})|\geq t\bigg)\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}}{Ndc^{2}}\right).

Proof of Lemma
1.1
.

Since a simple switching changes the size of the maximum cut by at most
2
, we can apply Lemma
2.1
for
\varphi=\gamma
and
c=4/Nd
. Setting
t=(\log N)/\sqrt{N}
we get that it holds with probability
1-o_{N}(1)
that

\gamma(\mathbb{G})\leq\mathbb{E}\gamma(\mathbb{G})+\frac{\log N}{\sqrt{N}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:gamma_bound}}}{{\leq}}1+\frac{\mathbb{E}\log Z_{\mathbb{G},\beta}}{\beta Nd/2}+\frac{\log N}{\sqrt{N}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:Phi_def}}}{{=}}1+\frac{2}{d}\frac{\Phi_{d}(\beta)}{\beta}+o_{N}(1)

for any given
\beta>0
. Then the same is true if we replace
\Phi_{d}(\beta)/\beta
with
\Phi^{\ast}_{d}=\liminf\Phi_{d}(\beta)/\beta
.
∎

3.
Replica formulas

Originally the cavity method and belief propagation were non-rigorous techniques in statistical physics to predict the free energy of various models. They inspired a large body of rigorous work, and over the years several predictions were confirmed. In particular, the so-called
interpolation method
has been used with great success to establish rigorous upper bounds on the free energy.

Building on the pioneering works
[
4
,
5
,
8
]
, there is a rigorous exposition of the interpolation method in the context of random regular graphs in
[
9
, Appendix E]
. It is carried out for a general class of models. They mention two chief examples, one of them being the Potts model (a generalization of the Ising model with
q\geq 2
spin states). A similar exposition had been given in
[
7
]
. Other sources explaining the interpolation method for specific models include
[
1
]
and
[
6
, Section 5]
.

3.1.
The general replica bound

Here we present the general
r
-step RSB bound for the Ising model/
MaxCut
problem. For a topological space
\Omega
let
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
denote the space of Borel probability measures on
\Omega
equipped with the weak topology. We define
\mathcal{P}^{k}(\Omega)
recursively as

\mathcal{P}^{k}(\Omega)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{P}(\Omega)&\text{if }k=1;\\
\mathcal{P}\big(\mathcal{P}^{k-1}(\Omega)\big)&\text{if }k\geq 2.\end{cases}

The general bound will have the following parameters:

•

\beta>0
;

•

0<m_{1},\ldots,m_{r}<1
corresponding to the so-called Parisi parameters;

•

a measure
\eta^{(r)}\in\mathcal{P}^{r}\big([-1,1]\big)
.

Definition 3.1
.

Given a fixed
\eta^{(r)}\in\mathcal{P}^{r}
, we choose (recursively for
k=r-1,r-2,\ldots,1
) a random
\eta^{(k)}\in\mathcal{P}^{k}
with distribution
\eta^{(k+1)}
. Finally, given
\eta^{(1)}
we choose a random
x\in[0,1]
with distribution
\eta^{(1)}
. In fact, we will need
d
independent copies of this random sequence, indexed by
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. Schematically:

\eta^{(r)}\,\to\,\eta_{\ell}^{(r-1)}\,\to\,\cdots\,\to\,\eta_{\ell}^{(1)}\,\to\,x_{\ell}\quad(\ell=1,\ldots,d).

For
1\leq k\leq r
we define
\mathcal{F}_{k}
as the
\sigma
-algebra generated by

\eta_{\ell}^{(r-1)},\ldots,\eta_{\ell}^{(k)}
,
\ell=1,\ldots,d
, and by
\mathbb{E}_{k}
we denote the conditional expectation w.r.t.
\mathcal{F}_{k}
. Note that
\mathcal{F}_{r}
is the trivial
\sigma
-algebra and hence
\mathbb{E}_{r}
is simply
\mathbb{E}
.

Given a random variable
V
(depending on the variables
\eta_{\ell}^{(k)},x_{\ell}
), let us perform the following procedure: raise it to power
m_{1}
, then apply
\mathbb{E}_{1}
, raise the result to power
m_{2}
, then apply
\mathbb{E}_{2}
, and so on. In formula, let
T_{0}V\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=V
and recursively for
k=1,\ldots,r
set

T_{k}V\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathbb{E}_{k}(T_{k-1}V)^{m_{k}}.

In this scenario, applying
\mathbb{E}_{k}
means that, given
\eta_{\ell}^{(k)}
,
\ell=1,\ldots,d
, we take expectation in
\eta_{\ell}^{(k-1)}
,
\ell=1,\ldots,d
(or in
x_{\ell}
if
k=1
).

Now we are ready to state the
r
-RSB bound given by the interpolation method.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
r\geq 1
be a positive integer and
\beta,m_{1},\ldots,m_{r},\eta^{(r)}
parameters as described above. Let
x_{\ell}
(
\ell=1,\ldots,d
) denote the random variables obtained from
\eta^{(r)}
via the procedure in Definition
3.1
.

Furthermore, let

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}_{\beta}^{\mathrm{edge}}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{1-x_{1}x_{2}}{2}+\frac{1+x_{1}x_{2}}{2}\exp(-\beta);

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}_{\beta}^{\mathrm{star}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=f_{\beta}^{-}(x_{1})\cdots f_{\beta}^{-}(x_{d})+f_{\beta}^{+}(x_{1})\cdots f_{\beta}^{+}(x_{d})\text{, where}

\displaystyle f_{\beta}^{-}(x)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{1+x}{2}+\frac{1-x}{2}\exp(-\beta);

\displaystyle f_{\beta}^{+}(x)

It is possible to improve the previous best bounds even within the framework of the
