Centralised management of Barrett’s oesophagus surveillance: assessing progression risk and management
Authors: Nicola B Raftery, Cian Muldoon, Ciara Ryan, Jacintha O’Sullivan, Dermot O’Toole, John V Reynolds, Jessie A Elliott, the National Barrett’s Oesophagus Registry and Scientific Network
Published: 2026

We are grateful to Dr. Vissapagada, Professor Watson and colleagues from the Flinders Health and Medical Research Institute for their thoughtful commentary (1) on our study examining progression risk within Ireland’s National Barrett’s Esophagus Registry and Bioresource surveillance network (2). We appreciate their positive comments on the novelty of this study, and the potential value of such a centralised quality-assured programme with high data capture, and specialist endoscopy and pathology. Their discussion provides important context regarding the implications of centralized Barrett’s surveillance systems and highlights several key considerations surrounding progression risk estimation, the diagnosis of dysplasia, and quality-assured surveillance pathways. Their comments echo our own analysis as to whether our data may be selectively biased towards the higher risk end of the Barrett’s spectrum compared with population registries. Notwithstanding, they concur that our data highlights real progression risk in the relatively higher risk end of the Barrett’s spectrum that presents an important consideration in cancer prevention health care policy.

As highlighted in the commentary, the progression rates observed within our cohort were notably higher than many previously published registry and population-based-study estimates among patients across all stages including non-dysplastic Barrett’s esophagus (NDBE), indefinite for dysplasia (IND), and low-grade dysplasia (LGD) (3,4). We agree that these findings require careful contextual interpretation. Importantly, we believe these observations likely reflect the unique characteristics of a centralized national referral network with rigorous pathological and endoscopic quality assurance and support the strength of such a network, rather than fundamental biological differences in Barrett’s esophagus within the Irish population.

The National Barrett’s Oesophagus Registry includes several large academic university teaching hospitals with specialist upper gastrointestinal surgical and Barrett’s esophagus surveillance services, some of whom manage tertiary referrals for dysplastic and complex Barrett’s esophagus and have established radiofrequency ablation (RFA) and endoscopic surgical, with a large centre at the National Centre for Early Mucosal Neoplasia at the Trinity St James Cancer Institute. Although all regions across the island of Ireland were engaged in the development of this network, participation and onward referral was not mandated at policy level, but rather established through community engagement, education and outreach. As such, it remains a possibility that some lower risk patients were managed in the community setting, or in the private sector. It is also possible, therefore, that the surveillance cohort within the Registry may be enriched for patients with longer-segment, or otherwise higher-risk Barrett’s oesophagus referred for specialist assessment and management. Although this may partially explain the elevated progression rates observed relative to population-based cohorts overall, it does not explain the increased progression rates seen in each diagnostic category, particularly for bona fide IND and LGD diagnosed by expert pathologists. With respect to progression of NDBE, we have also highlighted that entry into the Registry required a histological diagnosis of specialized intestinal metaplasia (SIM), with columnar metaplasia alone insufficient, in contrast to other population-based studies, and also the exclusion of suspected Barrett’s with no pathological corroboration.

At this time, we are working within the framework of an All Ireland Cancer Network (AllCaN). Funded by AllCaN, the study will compare progression within the Northern Ireland population registry with our National Registry in the Republic of Ireland which evolved from a collaboration of academic specialist centers. These findings will be of particular relevance as population level data, potentially enriched for patients without SIM, begin to inform policy decisions and debate regarding the cost-effectiveness of NDBE surveillance, with current studies evaluating the impact of surveillance de-escalation for low-risk patients in the Netherlands (BLISS study, NCT06803147). We hope that the AllCaN study will clarify and inform this debate relating to the impact of registry entry criteria on progression rates, cancer prevention and cost-effectiveness of surveillance.

There is complete consensus on the central importance of expert gastrointestinal pathology review as a critical determinant of progression risk estimation, as emphasised in the commentary. All dysplastic diagnoses within our Network undergo dual expert gastrointestinal pathological assessment. This distinction is particularly important in indefinite cases (IND), where inflammatory atypia and reactive epithelial changes may substantially overlap with low-grade dysplasia (LGD). Previous studies from the Netherlands by Curvers et al. and Duits et al. demonstrated that progression risk increases substantially with increasing expert pathological consensus, with the highest neoplastic progression rates observed among patients in whom LGD was confirmed by multiple expert gastrointestinal pathologists (5,6). These findings support the concept that expert-confirmed persistent LGD represents a biologically distinct high-risk entity, whereas heterogeneous community-diagnosed LGD cohorts may include patients without bona-fide dysplastic disease, thereby artificially lowering observed progression rates. In this context, the comparatively elevated progression rates observed within our cohort may reflect greater diagnostic specificity for biologically meaningful dysplasia rather than fundamental biological differences in Barrett’s esophagus itself.

We also strongly agree with the authors regarding the importance of prevalent disease in interpreting progression estimates. In our study, exclusion of progression events occurring within the first surveillance year substantially reduced annual progression rates across Barrett’s subgroups, supporting the likelihood that a proportion of early progression events represented prevalent neoplasia present but unrecognized at index endoscopy. We specifically performed these analyses to avoid overestimation of true incident progression risk and to provide a more accurate estimate of longitudinal neoplastic evolution within the surveillance cohort. These findings underscore the value of detailed assessment by experienced upper gastrointestinal endoscopists at specialist centres using Seattle protocol mapping, and the diagnostic-yield differential relative to the community endoscopy setting. The differential progression rates observed when accounting for likely prevalent disease also highlight the need for continued outreach and training initiatives in the community setting, where the majority of index endoscopies are performed, to ensure high-quality endoscopic assessment and protocolized sampling is standardized at a national level, and this represents a key focus area for systemic quality improvement into the future.

The observations relating to IND are also particularly noteworthy. Historically, IND has represented a diagnostically challenging category with significant interobserver variability. However, the elevated progression rates observed within our cohort suggest that at least a subset of IND patients harbour biologically significant neoplastic potential warranting closer surveillance and expert reassessment. In the context of our registry, patients with IND are subject to dual expert pathology review similar to the dysplasia cohort. As suggested by Professor Watson and colleagues, further characterization of patients progressing from IND may help refine future pathological and molecular risk stratification approaches. The current data support centralized assessment, not just of patients with established dysplasia, but also those with IND, which represents a high-risk diagnosis if confirmed at tertiary referral endoscopy. We agree that a further study characterizing progression within the cohort defined as IND within the Registry may be of particular interest in helping to further stratify this population.

Finally, we would like to thank the authors for their comments in relation to the significant impact of the National Barrett’s Oesophagus Network in terms of cancer prevention Ireland. Our findings support the concept that coordinated national surveillance pathways incorporating expert endoscopy, specialist pathology review, standardized surveillance protocols, and multidisciplinary management may facilitate earlier neoplasia detection, and improved risk stratification, and hence reduce oesophageal cancer specific mortality. It is also noteworthy, and particularly meaningful, that the National Barrett’s Oesophagus Network in Ireland is co-funded by the Oesophageal Cancer Fund (OCF), a registered charity run by people with a lived experience of oesophageal cancer, who have identified prevention through this national initiative as a key priority for investment. While direct implementation of centralized models may vary across healthcare systems, we believe several underlying principles—particularly quality-assured dysplasia confirmation and specialist review of IND/LGD cases, are broadly transferable and may improve surveillance internationally.

We again thank the authors for their insightful commentary and for advancing discussion regarding optimal Barrett’s esophagus surveillance strategies. Continued discussion and international collaboration between Barrett’s registries and networks will be essential to further refine risk prediction, dysplasia characterization, and individualized surveillance approaches for patients with Barrett’s-associated neoplasia.
