On the Trap Space Semantics of Normal Logic Programs
Van-Giang Trinh Sylvain Soliman François Fages, Belaid Benhamou

Abstract
The logical semantics of normal logic programs has traditionally been based on the notions of Clark’s completion and two-valued or three-valued canonical models, including supported, stable, regular, and well-founded models. Two-valued interpretations can also be seen as states evolving under a program’s update operator, producing a transition graph whose fixed points and cycles capture stable and oscillatory behaviors, respectively. We refer to this view as dynamical semantics since it characterizes the program’s meaning in terms of state‐space trajectories, as first introduced in the stable (supported) class semantics. Recently, we have established a formal connection between Datalog ¬ programs (i.e., normal logic programs without function symbols) and Boolean networks, leading to the introduction of the trap space concept for Datalog ¬ programs. In this paper, we generalize the trap space concept to arbitrary normal logic programs, introducing trap space semantics as a new approach to their interpretation. This new semantics admits both model-theoretic and dynamical characterizations, providing a comprehensive approach to understanding program behavior. We establish the foundational properties of the trap space semantics and systematically relate it to the established model-theoretic semantics, including the stable (supported), stable (supported) partial, regular, and L-stable model semantics, as well as to the dynamical stable (supported) class semantics. Our results demonstrate that the trap space semantics offers a unified and precise framework for proving the existence of supported classes, strict stable (supported) classes, and regular models, in addition to uncovering and formalizing deeper relationships among the existing semantics of normal logic programs.



keywords
logic programming, normal logic program, Clark’s completion, model-theoretic semantics, dynamical semantics, trap space, Boolean network, infinite structure

1
Introduction

The meaning of a Normal Logic Program (
NLP
)—its declarative semantics—is typically defined by a class of models of a logical formula associated with the program, such as the Herbrand models of the
Clark’s completion
of the program
[
14
]
.
This was later shown to be equivalent to the
supported model semantics

[
3
]
.
For
NLPs
with negation, the Clark’s completion may have no model, or several minimal models; therefore, there is generally no unique least Herbrand model.
In this setting, the
stable model semantics
was introduced by
[
25
]
to provide a canonical notion of two-valued models, defined by an elegant fixed‑point definition based on the least model of a
reduct
transformation.
Stable models were later characterized as
well-supported models

[
22
]
, i.e., models with circuit-free finite justifications—which led to graphical conditions for the existence of stable models
[
21
,
2
]
, and equivalence results with other forms of non-monotonic logics
[
44
,
32
,
33
,
8
]
.

A three-valued logic approach was introduced, where the third value denotes ignorance
[
24
,
34
,
36
]
.
Subsequent research expanded the semantic landscape of
NLPs
through additional three-valued logic transformations
[
4
]
.
The
stable (supported) partial model semantics
—also known as the three-valued stable (supported) model semantics—extends the two-valued stable (supported) model semantics
[
35
,
20
,
34
]
.
Under this framework, all models are minimal, and every
NLP
possesses at least one stable (supported) partial model.
Notably, two specific types of stable partial semantics are distinguished by their informational content: the
well-founded partial model semantics

[
23
,
34
]
, representing the unique model with minimal information, and the
regular model semantics

[
43
]
, encompassing models with maximal information.
It is worth noting that these three-valued semantics (e.g., stable partial models, regular models) in normal logic programs have been shown to correspond to several extension-based semantics (e.g., complete extensions, preferred extensions, respectively) in Dung’s frameworks
[
18
,
42
]
and assumption-based argumentation
[
11
]
, which are two prominent formalisms in symbolic AI.

Alternatively, an
NLP
can be viewed as a state-transition system in which each rule in the program represents how a component (here an atom) is affected by other components
[
5
,
9
]
.
The dynamical behaviors of the program, including its oscillating behavior
[
28
]
, are characterized by a directed graph called the (stable or supported)
transition graph
whose vertices are two-valued interpretations and whose arcs are transitions between interpretations.
This led to the
stable class semantics

[
5
]
and
supported class semantics

[
28
]
.
If a stable (resp. supported) class is of size one, it coincides with a stable (resp. supported) model and exhibits a stable behavior.
Otherwise, it exhibits an oscillating behavior.

The aforementioned semantics capture various aspects of program behavior.
Despite their success, they often diverge in interpretive principles, and many lack a unifying framework that integrates both model-theoretic and dynamical behaviors coherently.
Recently, we have established a formal connection between Datalog
¬
programs (i.e.,
NLPs
without function symbols—thus the Herbrand base is finite) and Boolean networks, leading to the introduction of the concept of
trap space
for Datalog
¬
programs, inspired by the notion of trap space in Boolean networks
[
29
,
40
]
.
This concept offers a bridge between model-theoretic and dynamical aspects, but applies only to finite structures
[
41
]
.

In this paper, we generalize the trap space concept to arbitrary
NLPs
, introducing
trap space semantics
as a new approach to their interpretation.
This new semantics admits both model-theoretic and dynamical characterizations, providing a comprehensive approach to understanding program behavior.
We establish foundational properties of the trap space semantics and systematically relate it to established model-theoretic and dynamical semantics—including the stable (supported), stable (supported) partial, regular, and L-stable model semantics—as well as to dynamical semantics such as the stable (supported) class semantics.
Our results demonstrate that the trap space semantics offers a unified framework (along with a proper use of set-theoretic axioms) for proving the existence of supported classes, strict supported classes, strict stable classes, or regular models—proofs that have been unfortunately either missing or imprecise in prior literature
[
5
,
43
,
28
]
.
Furthermore, by leveraging the concept of stable or supported trap set underlying the trap space semantics, we correct a flaw in the existing alternative characterization of strict stable or supported classes established in
[
28
]
.
Finally, the trap space semantics emerges as a powerful tool for uncovering and formalizing deeper relationships among various existing semantics of
NLPs
, for instance, the stable (resp. supported) class semantics vs. the stable (resp. supported) partial model semantics, and the regular model semantics vs. the stable class semantics and the L-stable model semantics.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows:
in Section
2
, we review the necessary background on
NLPs
and set theory.
Section
3
presents the formal definitions of the trap space semantics, as well as its fundamental properties.
Section
4
shows the relationships between the trap space semantics and other semantics.
In Section
5
, we present the existence results.
Section
6
offers concluding remarks and future research directions.

Preliminaries

2.1
Normal Logic Programs

We assume that the reader is familiar with the logic programming theory and stable model semantics.
Unless specifically stated,
NLP
means normal logic program.
In addition, we consider the Boolean domain
\mathbb{B}=\{0,1\}
, the three-valued domain
\mathbb{B}_{\star}=\{0,1,\star\}
.
The logical connectives used in this paper are
\land
(conjunction),
\lor
(disjunction),
\neg
(negation), and
\leftrightarrow
(equivalence).

We consider a first-order language built over an infinite alphabet of variables,
and finite alphabets of constant, function, and predicate symbols.
The set of first-order
terms
is the least set containing variables and constants, and closed by application of function symbols.
An
atom
is a formula of the form
p(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})
where
p
is a predicate symbol and each
t_{i}
is a term.
A
normal logic program

P
is a
finite
set of
rules
of the form
p\leftarrow p_{1},\dots,p_{m},{{\sim}p_{m+1}},\dots,{{\sim}p_{k}}

where
p
and
p_{i}
are atoms,
k\geq m\geq 0
, and
\sim
denotes default negation.
A fact is a rule with
k=0
.
For any rule
r
of the above form,
\mathsf{h}({r})=p
is called the
head
of
r
,
\mathsf{b}^{+}({r})=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{m}\}
is called the
positive body
of
r
,
\mathsf{b}^{-}({r})=\{p_{m+1},\dots,p_{k}\}
is called the
negative body
of
r
, and
\mathsf{b}({r})=\mathsf{b}^{+}({r})\cup\mathsf{b}^{-}({r})
is called the
body
of
r
.
For convenience, we denote
\mathsf{bf}({r})=\bigwedge_{v\in\mathsf{b}^{+}({r})}v\wedge\bigwedge_{v\in\mathsf{b}^{-}({r})}\neg v
as the
body formula
of
r
; if
k=0
, then
\mathsf{bf}({r})=1
.
If
\mathsf{b}^{-}({r})=\emptyset
forall
r\in P
, then
P
is called a
positive

NLP
.
If
\mathsf{b}^{+}({r})=\emptyset
forall
r\in P
, then
P
is called a
negative

NLP
.
The program
P
is called
internal-variable-free
if for any rule in
P
, the variables in the body also appear in the head
[
37
]
.

A term, an atom, or an
NLP
is
ground
if it contains no variables.
The
Herbrand base
of an
NLP

P
(denoted by
\mathsf{HB}_{P}
) is the set of ground atoms formed over the alphabet of
P
.
It is finite in the absence of function symbols, which is the case of
Datalog
¬

programs
[
13
]
.
The
ground instantiation
of an
NLP

P
(denoted by
\mathsf{gr}({P})
) is the set of the ground instances of all rules in
P
.
An
NLP

P
is called
uni-rule
if every atom in
\mathsf{HB}_{P}
appears in the head of at most one rule in
\mathsf{gr}({P})

[
38
]
.

We shall use the
least fixpoint
transformation of
NLPs

[
19
]
to prove many new results in the next sections.
To be self-contained, we briefly recall the definition of the
least fixpoint
of an
NLP

P
as follows.
The reader can refer
[
19
]
for more details.
Let
r
be the rule
p\leftarrow{{\sim}p_{1}},\dots,{{\sim}p_{k}},q_{1},\dots,q_{j}
in
\mathsf{gr}({P})
and let
r_{i}
be the rules
q_{i}\leftarrow{{\sim}q^{1}_{i}},\dots,{{\sim}q^{l_{i}}_{i}}
in
\mathsf{gr}({P})
where
1\leq i\leq j
and
l_{i}\geq 0
.
Then
\Pi_{{r}}{(\{r_{1},\dots,r_{j}\})}
is the following rule

p\leftarrow{{\sim}p_{1}},\dots,{{\sim}p_{k}},{{\sim}q_{1}^{1}},\dots,{{\sim}q_{1}^{l_{1}}},\dots,{{\sim}q_{j}^{1}},\dots,{{\sim}q_{j}^{l_{j}}},

which means that each atom
q_{j}
in the positive body of
r
is substituted with the body of the rule
r_{j}
.
Then
\Pi_{{P}}{}
is the transformation on negative normal logic programs:

\Pi_{{P}}{(Q)}=\{\Pi_{{r}}{(\{r_{1},\dots,r_{j}\})}\mid r\in\mathsf{gr}({P}),r_{i}\in Q,1\leq i\leq j\}.

Let
\mathsf{lfp}({P})_{i}=(\Pi_{{P}}{(\emptyset}))^{i}=\Pi_{{P}}{(\Pi_{{P}}{(\dots\Pi_{{P}}{(\emptyset)}))}}
, then
\mathsf{lfp}({P})=\bigcup_{i\geq 1}\mathsf{lfp}({P})_{i}
is the least fixpoint of the
NLP

P
.

2.1.1
Stable and Supported Partial Models

A
three-valued interpretation

I
of an
NLP

P
is a mapping
I\colon\mathsf{HB}_{P}\to\mathbb{B}_{\star}
.
If
I(a)\neq\star
for all
a\in\mathsf{HB}_{P}
, then
I
is a
two-valued interpretation
of
P
.
Usually, a two-valued interpretation is written as the set of atoms that are true.
A three-valued interpretation
I
characterizes the set of two-valued interpretations denoted by
\mathcal{S}({I})
as
\mathcal{S}({I})=\{J\mid J\in 2^{\mathsf{HB}_{P}},\forall a\in\mathsf{HB}_{P},I(a)\neq\star\Rightarrow J(a)=I(a)\}
.
For example, if
I=\{p=1,q=0,r=\star\}
, then
\mathcal{S}({I})=\{\{p\},\{p,r\}\}
.
We consider three orders on three-valued interpretations.
The truth order
\leq_{t}
is given by
0<_{t}\star<_{t}1
.
Then,
I_{1}\leq_{t}I_{2}

iff

I_{1}(a)\leq_{t}I_{2}(a)
forall
a\in\mathsf{HB}_{P}
.
The order
\leq_{s}
is given by
0<_{s}\star
,
1<_{s}\star
; no other relations hold.
Then,
I_{1}\leq_{s}I_{2}

iff

I_{1}(a)\leq_{s}I_{2}(a)
forall
a\in\mathsf{HB}_{P}
.
In addition,
I_{1}\leq_{s}I_{2}

iff

\mathcal{S}({I_{1}})\subseteq\mathcal{S}({I_{2}})
, i.e.,
\leq_{s}
is identical to the subset partial order.
The information order
\leq_{i}
is given by
\star<_{i}0
,
\star<_{i}1
; no other relations hold.
Then,
I_{1}\leq_{i}I_{2}

iff

I_{1}(a)\leq_{i}I_{2}(a)
forall
a\in\mathsf{HB}_{P}
.
It is easy to see that
\leq_{i}
is the complement of
\leq_{s}
, i.e.,
I_{1}\leq_{s}I_{2}

iff

I_{2}\leq_{i}I_{1}
.

Let
e
be a propositional formula on
\mathsf{HB}_{P}
, using only the
\neg
,
\land
, and
\lor
connectives.
Then the valuation of
e
under a three-valued interpretation
I
(denoted by
I(e)
) following the three-valued logic is defined recursively as follows:
I(e)=e
if
e\in\mathbb{B}_{\star}
;
I(e)=I(a)
if
e=a,a\in\mathsf{HB}_{P}
;
I(e)=\neg I(e_{1})
if
e=\neg e_{1}
;
I(e)=\text{min}_{\leq_{t}}(I(e_{1}),I(e_{2}))
if
e=e_{1}\land e_{2}
; and
I(e)=\text{max}_{\leq_{t}}(I(e_{1}),I(e_{2}))
if
e=e_{1}\lor e_{2}

where
\neg 1=0,\neg 0=1,\neg\star=\star
, and
\text{min}_{\leq_{t}}
(resp.
\text{max}_{\leq_{t}}
) is the function to get the minimum (resp. maximum) value of two values w.r.t. the order
\leq_{t}
.
We say three-valued interpretation
I
is a
three-valued model
of an
NLP

P
if for each rule
r\in\mathsf{gr}({P})
,
I(\mathsf{bf}({r}))\leq_{t}I(\mathsf{h}({r}))
.

Given a three-valued interpretation
I
of an
NLP

P
, the
reduct
of
P
w.r.t.
I
(denoted by
P^{I}
) is defined as: remove any rule
a\leftarrow a_{1},\dots,a_{m},{{\sim}b_{1}},\dots,{{\sim}b_{k}}\in\mathsf{gr}({P})
if
I(b_{i})=1
for some
1\leq i\leq k
; afterwards, remove any occurrence of
{{\sim}b_{i}}
from
\mathsf{gr}({P})
such that
I(b_{i})=0
; then, replace any occurrence of
{{\sim}b_{i}}
left by a special atom
u
such that
\textbf{u}\not\in\mathsf{HB}_{P}
and it always receives the value
\star
.
The
NLP

P^{I}
is positive and has a unique
\leq_{t}
-least three-valued model.
See
[
34
]
for the method for computing this
\leq_{t}
-least model.
Then
I
is a
stable partial model
of
P
if
I
is equal to the
\leq_{t}
-least three-valued model of
P^{I}
.
A stable partial model
I
is an
M-stable model
if it is
\leq_{s}
-minimal
[
36
]
.
In this paper, we define a
regular model
as an M-stable model as they are equivalent
[
20
]
.
In the two-valued setting,
P^{I}
is identical to the reduct defined by
[
25
]
;
I
is a
stable model
of
P
if
I
is equal to the
\leq_{t}
-least two-valued model of
P^{I}
.
It is easy to derive that a stable model is a two-valued regular model, as well as a two-valued stable partial model
[
4
]
.
We define
I^{\textbf{u}}
as the set of ground atoms that are assigned to
\star
in
I
, i.e.,
I^{\textbf{u}}=\{v\mid v\in\mathsf{HB}_{P},I(v)=\star\}
.
Then
I
is a
L-stable model
of
P
if
I
is a stable partial model of
P
and there is no stable partial model
J
such that
J^{\textbf{u}}\subset I^{\textbf{u}}

[
36
]
.

The (propositional)
Clark’s completion
of an
NLP

P
(denoted by
\mathsf{comp}({P})
) consists of the following equivalences:
p\leftrightarrow\bigvee_{r\in\mathsf{gr}({P}),\mathsf{h}({r})=p}\mathsf{bf}({r})
, for each
p\in\mathsf{HB}_{P}
; if there is no rule whose head is
p
, then the equivalence is
p\leftrightarrow 0
.
Let
\mathsf{rhs}_{P}({a})
denote the right-hand side of the equivalence of a ground atom
a\in\mathsf{HB}_{P}
in
\mathsf{comp}({P})
.
A three-valued interpretation
I
is a three-valued model of
\mathsf{comp}({P})

iff
for every
a\in\mathsf{HB}_{P}
,
I(a)=I(\mathsf{rhs}_{P}({a}))
.
In this work, we define a
supported partial model
of
P
as a three-valued model of
\mathsf{comp}({P})
, and a
supported model
of
P
as a two-valued model of
\mathsf{comp}({P})
.
Clearly, a supported model is a (two-valued) partial supported model.
It has been pointed out that a stable partial model (resp. stable model) is a supported partial model (resp. supported model), but the reverse may be not true
[
15
]
.

2.1.2
Stable and Supported Classes

Let
P
be an NLP and let
I
be any two-valued interpretation of
P
.
We have that
P^{I}
is positive, and has a unique
\leq_{t}
-least two-valued model (say
J
).
We define the operator
F_{P}
as
F_{P}({I})=J
.
In contrast, we define the operator
T_{P}
as
T_{P}({I})=J
where
J
is a two-valued interpretation such that for every
a\in\mathsf{HB}_{P}
,
J(a)=I(\mathsf{rhs}_{P}({a}))
.

Definition 2.1
.

A non-empty set
S
of two-valued interpretations is a
stable class
of an
NLP

P

iff
it holds that
S=\{F_{P}({I})\mid I\in S\}

[
5
]
.
Similarly,
S
is a
supported class
of an
NLP

P

iff
it holds that
S=\{T_{P}({I})\mid I\in S\}

[
28
]
.
A stable (resp. supported) class
S
of
P
is
strict

iff
no proper subset of
S
is a stable (resp. supported) class of
P
.

Example 2.1
.

Consider the
NLP

where the symbol “;” is used to separate program rules.
Since
P
is positive, for any two-valued interpretation

P^{I}=P
.
It follows that
F_{P}({I})=\emptyset
for all

It is easy to derive that

On the other hand, we have that

,
T_{P}({\{b\}})=\{a\}
, and

It is easy to derive that

\{\emptyset\}
,
\{\{a\},\{b\}\}
, and
\{\{a,b\}\}

There is a “strengthened” version that is equivalent to Zorn’s lemma:

(

)

3
Trap Space Semantics

The concept of stable (resp. supported) trap set is obtained by relaxing the equality in Definition
2.1
of stable (resp. supported) class, as follows:

Definition 3.1
.

A non-empty set
S
of two-valued interpretations of an
NLP

P
is called a
stable trap set
(resp.
supported trap set
) of
P

iff

\{F_{P}({I})\mid I\in S\}\subseteq S
(resp.
\{T_{P}({I})\mid I\in S\}\subseteq S
).

Note that a stable (resp. supported) class is clearly a stable (resp. supported) trap set, but the reverse may not be true.
Indeed, given the
NLP

P
of Example
2.2
,
\{\emptyset\}
is a stable class and also a stable trap set, whereas
\{\emptyset,a_{0}\}
is a stable trap set but not a stable class.
Similarly,
\{\emptyset,\mathsf{HB}_{P}\}
is a supported class and also a supported trap set, whereas
\{\emptyset,a_{0},a_{1}\}
is a supported trap set but not a supported class.
By adding the condition on three-valued interpretations, we obtain the concept of stable or supported trap space:

Definition 3.2
.

A three-valued interpretation
I
of an

5
Existence Results

Whether a given semantics is consistent (i.e., it contains at least one respective object) is a main property of interest
[
37
,
39
]
.
For Datalog
¬
programs, proving or refuting this property is mostly straightforward
[
7
]
.
However, for arbitrary
NLPs
with an infinite Herbrand base, conducting such proofs or refutations is more intricate.
It often needs a precise use of set-theoretic axioms or equivalent statements.
We here show that previous arguments for the existence of supported classes, strict supported (stable) classes, and regular models are either missing or imprecise; and that the trap space semantics can offer a unified and precise framework to prove the model or class existence.
Furthermore, we reveal problems in the proof of
[
36
]
for the existence of L-stable models and correct it for uni-rule programs only (see more details in Section
C
).

We first recall a well-known result:

Theorem 5.1

(Theorem 1 of
[
5
]
)
.

Any NLP
P
has a stable class.

The proof of Theorem
5.1
relies on the anti-monotonicity of
F_{P}
and the monotonicity of
F_{P}^{2}
to construct two two-valued interpretations that form a stable class.
However, this approach may not be directly applicable to supported classes, as
T_{P}
may be neither monotonic nor anti-monotonic.
In
[
28

Appendix B
Revisit of the Characterizations of Strict Stable and Supported Classes

We first revisit the basic definitions and results of
[
28
]
on stable and supported classes.

Definition B.1

(
[
28
]
)
.

Consider an
NLP

P
and a two-valued interpretation
I
of
P
.
A sequence of applications of an operator on the set of two-valued interpretations of
P
is called an
orbit
.
The orbit of
I
w.r.t.
T_{P}
is the sequence
\langle T_{P}^{k}(I)\rangle_{k\in\omega}
, where
T_{P}^{0}(I)=I
and
T_{P}^{k+1}(I)=T_{P}(T_{P}^{k}(I))
.
Similarly, we have the definition for the orbit of
I
w.r.t.
F_{P}
.

Definition B.2

(
[
28
]
)
.

Consider an
NLP

P
and a two-valued interpretation
I
of
P
.
Let
\mathcal{T}_{P}(I)
be the set of two-valued interpretations defined as:

\mathcal{T}_{P}(I)=\{T_{P}^{k}(I)\mid k\in\omega\}.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{P}(I)
be the set of two-valued interpretations defined as:

\mathcal{F}_{P}(I)=\{F_{P}^{k}(I)\mid k\in\omega\}.

Theorem B.1

(
[
28
]
)
.

Given an
NLP

P
, a non-empty set
S
of two-valued interpretations is a strict stable (resp. supported) class of
P

iff

\mathcal{T}_{P}(I)=S
(resp.
\mathcal{F}_{P}(I)=S
) for every
I\in S
.

Theorem
B.1
provides an alternative characterization for strict stable (or supported) classes.
This theorem mainly relies on the definitions of stable and supported orbits (see Definition
B.1
).
This may be a problem here, as these definitions only limit the length of the sequence to
\omega
(the first infinite limit ordinal).
Since the sequence can traverse through the set of all two-valued interpretations, which may be an infinite set, thus the length can be a bigger ordinal.
With Definition
B.1
, it seems that Theorem
B.1
only consider Datalog
¬
programs rather than any
NLPs
.
We fix the above problem as follows.

First, we recursively define the stable or supported reachable closure of a two-valued interpretation over all ordinals.

Definition B.3
.

Given an
NLP

P
, let
I
is a two-valued interpretation of
P
.
The
supported reachable closure
of
I
is defined as:

\displaystyle\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow 0

\displaystyle=\{I\}

\displaystyle\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha+1

\displaystyle=\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha\cup\bigcup_{J\in\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha}\{T_{P}({J})\}.

If
\beta
is a limit ordinal, then by construction
\{\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha\colon\alpha<\beta\}
is a
\subseteq
chain.
Define
\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\beta=\text{sup}_{\subseteq}\{\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha\colon\alpha<\beta\}
, where
\text{sup}_{\subseteq}
denotes the least upper bound w.r.t.
\subseteq
.
By replacing
T_{P}({J})
and
\Pi^{I}_{P}
with
F_{P}({J})
and
\Sigma^{I}_{P}
respectively, we obtain the definition of the
stable reachable closure
of
I
.

Then the two following propositions show that the stable or supported reachable closure eventually reaches a constant
w.r.t.
any two-valued interpretation.
The key to their proofs is the use of Hartogs’ lemma (see Lemma
2.3
).

Proposition B.1
.

Consider an
NLP

P
.
For any two-valued interpretation
I
of
P
, there is the unique least ordinal
\alpha_{I}
such that
\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha_{I}+1=\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha_{I}
.

Proof.

We have
\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow
is an increasing function from the ordinals into
2^{\mathsf{HB}_{P}}
.
It cannot be strictly increasing, as if it was, then we would have an injection from the ordinals into
2^{\mathsf{HB}_{P}}
, which violates Lemma
2.3
.
Hence,
\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow
must be eventually constant.
It follows that for some ordinal
\alpha
,
\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha+1=\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha
.
Let
\alpha_{I}
be the least ordinal such that
\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha_{I}+1=\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha_{I}
.
Since the ordinals are ordered,
\alpha_{I}
is unique.
∎

Proposition B.2
.

Consider an
NLP

P
.
For any two-valued interpretation
I
of
P
, there is the unique least ordinal
\alpha_{I}
such that
\Sigma^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha_{I}+1=\Sigma^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha_{I}
.

Proof.

Replace
\Pi^{I}_{P}
with
\Sigma^{I}_{P}
in the proof of Proposition
B.1
.
∎

By using transfinite induction (see more details in
[
31
]
), we prove that the supported reachable closure is closed under the supported trap set property.

Lemma B.1
.

Consider an
NLP

P
.
Let
S
be a supported trap set of
P
.
Then for all
I\in S
,
\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha\subseteq S
for all ordinal
\alpha
.

Proof.

We prove this by using transfinite induction.

Base case:
\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow 0=\{I\}\subseteq S
.

Successor case:
\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha+1=\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha\cup\bigcup_{J\in\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha}\{T_{P}({J})\}\subseteq S\cup\bigcup_{J\in S}\{T_{P}({J})\}
because
\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha\subseteq S
by the induction hypothesis.
Then
\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha+1\subseteq S\cup S=S
because
S
is a supported trap set.

Limit case:
\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha=\text{sup}_{\subseteq}\{\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\beta\colon\beta<\alpha\}\subseteq S
because
\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\beta\subseteq S
for all
\beta<\alpha
by the induction hypothesis.
∎

On the other hand, we prove that the constant that the supported reachable closure reaches is a supported trap set
w.r.t.
any two-valued interpretation.

Lemma B.2
.

Given an
NLP

P
, for any two-valued interpretation
I
of
P
,
\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha_{I}
is a supported trap set of
P
.

Proof.

By definition of
\Pi^{I}_{P}
, we have
\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha_{I}+1=\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha_{I}\cup\bigcup_{J\in\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha_{I}}\{T_{P}({J})\}
.
By definition of
\alpha_{I}
, we have
\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha_{I}+1=\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha_{I}
.
Set
S=\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha_{I}
.
It follows that
S=S\cup\bigcup_{J\in S}\{T_{P}({J})\}
.
Then
\bigcup_{J\in S}\{T_{P}({J})\}\subseteq S
, implying that
S
is a supported trap set of
P
.
∎

Combining Lemma
B.1
and Lemma
B.2
, we now obtain a new characterization for
\subseteq
-minimal supported trap sets of an
NLP
:

Theorem B.2
.

Given an
NLP

P
,
S
is a
\subseteq
-minimal supported trap set of
P

iff

\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha_{I}=S
for all
I\in S
.

Proof.

“
\Rightarrow
” Assume that
S
is a
\subseteq
-minimal supported trap set of
P
.
Suppose that there exists
I\in S
such that
\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha_{I}\neq S
.
By Lemma
B.1
,
\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha_{I}\subseteq S
since
S
is a supported trap set.
Then
\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha_{I}\subset S
.
By Lemma
B.2
,
\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha_{I}
is a supported trap set, which contradicts the
\subseteq
-minimality of
S
.
Hence,
\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha_{I}=S
for all
I\in S
.

“
\Leftarrow
” Assume that
\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha_{I}=S
for all
I\in S
.
We have
\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha_{I}+1=S=S\cup\bigcup_{J\in S}\{T_{P}({J})\}
.
This implies that
\bigcup_{J\in S}\{T_{P}({J})\}\subseteq S
, thus
S
is a supported trap set of
P
.
Suppose that
S
is not
\subseteq
-minimal.
Then there is a supported trap set
S^{\prime}
of
P
such that
S^{\prime}\subset S
.
Consider
J\in S^{\prime}
.
By Lemma
B.1
,
\Pi^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha_{J}\subseteq S^{\prime}\subset S
, which is a contradiction because
J\in S
.
Hence,
S
is a
\subseteq
-minimal supported trap set of
P
.
∎

This is similar for stable trap sets by replacing
\Pi^{I}_{P}
by
\Sigma^{I}_{P}
in the proof of Theorem
B.2
:

Theorem B.3
.

Given an
NLP

P
,
S
is a
\subseteq
-minimal stable trap set of
P

iff

\Sigma^{I}_{P}\uparrow\alpha_{I}=S
for all
I\in S
.

Proof.

Replace
\Pi^{I}_{P}
by
\Sigma^{I}_{P}
in the proof of Theorem

.
∎

Theorem

and Theorem

are the corrected version of Theorem

in the case of

, as we show that the
\subseteq

).
In the case of Datalog

Appendix C
Revisit of the Existence of L-stable Models

The existence of L-stable models in an
NLP
is stated and proved in
[
36
, Proposition 5.11]
.
The main idea of the proof is similar to what is used for proving the existence of M-stable models in an
NLP
(see
[
36
, Theorem 5.3]
).
However, this may pose a problem here because the case of L-stable models is actually not the same as the case of M-stable models.
More specifically, the union of all the partial stable models in a maximal chain w.r.t.
\subseteq
-minimal
I^{\textbf{u}}
might be not a partial stable model (even the union does not exist, see Example
C.1
), whereas the union of all the stable partial models in a
\leq_{i}
-chain is a stable partial model as shown in the proof of
[
36
, Theorem 5.3]
.
This problem is more justified by the bijection between the L-stable models of an
NLP
and the semi-stable extensions of an Abstract Argumentation Framework (
AF
)
[
10
]
.
It has been shown that there is an infinite
AF
without a semi-stable extension
[
7
]
.

Example C.1
.

Consider the
NLP

P=\{a\leftarrow{{\sim}b};b\leftarrow{{\sim}a};c\leftarrow{{\sim}c}\}
.
The program
P
has three stable partial models:
I_{1}=\{a=\star,b=\star,c=\star\}
,
I_{2}=\{a=0,b=1,c=\star\}
, and
I_{3}=\{a=1,b=0,c=\star\}
.
Using the notations of
[
36

Theorem C.1

Consider an

If

Proof.

Since

If
\mathsf{lfp}({P})=\emptyset
, then it trivially has a L-stable model
I^{*}
where

for every
a\in\mathsf{HB}_{\mathsf{lfp}({P})}
.
We then only consider the case
\mathsf{lfp}({P})\neq\emptyset
.
If there is an atom
a\in\mathsf{HB}_{\mathsf{lfp}({P})}
not being the head of any rule in
\mathsf{lfp}({P})

0

\mathsf{lfp}({P})

Since

also does.
There is a (possibly infinite)

\mathsf{HB}_{P^{\prime}}
and each atom in the body of a rule whose head is

a
in
\mathcal{A}

Since the body of a rule is finite by definition,
\mathcal{A}
is finitary, i.e., every argument has finitely many attackers
[
7
]
.

A labelling of an

NLP
.
An extension of an

There is a one-to-one correspondence between the set of labellings and the set of extensions of a given type

[

A complete labelling

\mathsf{lfp}({P})
