Reframing evidence on the BCG vaccine: What emerging systematic reviews and meta-analyses reveal about efficacy, safety, and heterogeneity
Authors: Paulo R Z Antas
Published: 2026

Despite more than a century of widespread use, the Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) vaccine continues to challenge straightforward interpretation, with evidence of its effects repeatedly being reframed across diverse transdisciplinary contexts.1,2 Attempts to define its protective profile have therefore evolved alongside the evidence itself, reflecting not only biological variability but also shifts in how data are synthesized and understood. As the only licensed vaccine against tuberculosis (TB), the BCG vaccine remains both indispensable and enigmatic.

Importantly, uncertainty persists not only regarding whether the BCG vaccine prevents primary Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection, but also its effectiveness against severe manifestations such as tuberculous meningitis, miliary/disseminated disease, and adult pulmonary TB.1,2 This ambiguity becomes even more relevant when considering revaccination strategies and their application in vulnerable populations.

Recent articles published in Human Vaccines & Immunotherapeutics issues have contributed to advancing the discussion on BCG (re)vaccination by presenting updated evidence and providing a platform for critical evaluation of current knowledge. In particular, the systematic reviews and meta-analyses by Ali and colleagues3 and Choi and colleagues4 provide complementary perspectives, addressing both the clinical and immunological effects of the BCG vaccine and the occurrence and variability of adverse events (AEs) following BCG administration. Together, these revisited studies integrate clinical outcomes with safety considerations, providing a more comprehensive context to interpret protection, immunogenicity, and reactogenicity across populations and study settings. In doing so, these contributions do not merely aggregate data; they reframe it.

Intrinsically, systematic reviews and meta-analyses serve not merely as tools of aggregation, but as interpretative frameworks.5,6 Like a prism that refracts scattered beams of evidence into a coherent spectrum, those studies reveal both convergent findings and underlying heterogeneity, enabling a more nuanced understanding of BCG vaccine performance across populations, study designs, and biological contexts. Rather than fully resolving uncertainty, they illuminate its structure, showing how methodological, immunological, and epidemiological differences shape the conclusions that can be drawn from this century-old vaccine. What arises is structured complexity, not simple clarity.

A central theme emerging from these analyses is the persistent disconnect between immune responses and measurable clinical protection, particularly in the context of BCG revaccination strategies and their uncertain role across different age groups and risk profiles. Notably, this conclusion emerges despite a substantial body of evidence, comprising around 100 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) conducted in this field, when studies on the intravesical use of BCG vaccine in bladder cancer were specifically excluded. In a retrospective study of RCTs evaluating BCG revaccination, Ali and colleagues3 report no significant reduction in M. tuberculosis infection, as assessed by ex vivo latent TB conversion (IGRA) outcomes. The pooled risk ratios for both initial and sustained conversion suggest minimal to modest efficacy, reinforcing long-standing uncertainty about the value of BCG revaccination in adolescents and adults,2 a doubt that continues to evolve as new evidence arises. Therefore, more data has not been translated into clearer answers. Formerly, early signals from a phase 2 RCT in South African adolescents suggested that BCG revaccination might reduce sustained M. tuberculosis infection, raising cautious optimism.7 However, this expectation was not confirmed in a subsequent large phase 2b RCT conducted in a comparable setting, which demonstrated no protection against TB infection despite robust immunogenicity and nearly identical infection rates between vaccine and placebo groups.8 Concurrently, that same study handled by Ali and colleagues3 indicates that BCG revaccination enhances specific components of the immune response, particularly the adaptive immunity, including increases in the CD8+ T-cell compartment and cytokine-producing CD4+ T cells. Immunity seems to be boosted by BCG revaccination, yet protection remains elusive. This divergence between no reduction in TB infection vs. a surge in specific components of the immune response, underscores a major limitation in TB vaccine research, namely the absence of validated correlates of protection linking immunogenicity to clinical outcomes.9,10 These findings further reinforce that revaccination, especially among adolescents and high-risk populations, remains a biologically plausible yet clinically unresolved strategy.7,8,11

An additional perspective is provided by another retrospective study conducted by Choi and colleagues,4 which focuses on AEs following primary BCG vaccination in children. Importantly, BCG-associated AEs encompass a broader clinical spectrum, including local reactions such as lymphadenopathy and lymphadenitis, delayed ulcer healing, and scar formation, as well as more severe complications such as osteomyelitis, BCGitis, and BCGosis, particularly in immunocompromised hosts.11,12 Across more than 50 studies and nearly 2 million individuals, the pooled incidence of lymphadenitis showed substantial heterogeneity, with wide variability throughout settings and study designs. Importantly, incidence estimates differed by surveillance methodology, with active approaches identifying higher AE rates than passive systems.4 And consequently, measurement shapes perception. These findings suggest that routine pharmacovigilance frameworks may underestimate not only the incidence of lymphadenitis but also the broader spectrum of BCG-related AEs, particularly in large-scale immunization programs relying on passive reporting,11 consistent with prior editorial perspectives emphasizing global heterogeneity in BCG-associated AEs.13

Safety must also be considered in the context of BCG revaccination. In the general practice clinic, lymphadenopathy mostly refers to non-suppurative enlargement of regional lymph nodes, while lymphadenitis denotes inflammation that may progress to suppuration. Although often benign and self-limited, these reactions reflect local immune activation. In contrast, complications such as osteomyelitis and BCGosis represent rare but severe manifestations, typically associated with underlying immune deficiencies. Additional reactions, including persistent ulceration and abnormal scar formation, may also signal altered host immune responses.12,14 Hence, the retrospective study by Ali and colleagues3 identifies an increased risk of local and systemic AEs, including injection-site reactions, erythema, and pyrexia, although most were mild to moderate. Together with findings from primary BCG immunization studies, this evidence reinforces that the immunizer maintains a generally favorable safety profile but is not devoid of clinically relevant reactogenicity, particularly when re-administered.11 Safety is reassuring, but not negligible. Hence, the balance between potential benefits and risks remains central to policy discussions.15 This consideration is particularly important in populations with increased susceptibility to adverse outcomes, such as neonates with low birth weight or individuals with underlying immunodeficiencies.12

As depicted below, perhaps the most striking cohesion between these retrospective studies is the pervasive heterogeneity that characterizes the evidence base. In the Choi and colleagues’ analysis of lymphadenitis,4 variability was influenced by surveillance method, study design, BCG vaccine strain, and temporal factors. Differences among BCG vaccine strains, notably Danish, Pasteur, and Japan, further highlight the biological variability inherent to BCG formulations,16 reinforcing the concept that BCG represents not a single immunogen but a heterogeneous family of substrains with distinct genetic and phenotypic profiles.17 Likewise, in the Ali and colleagues’ retrospective evaluation,3 heterogeneity reflects differences in populations, prior exposure, and protocols, a pattern illustrated by contrasting outcomes across successive RCTs in South Africans.7,8 Thus, context shapes outcomes.

Despite these limitations, the potential for agnostic, heterologous effects of the BCG vaccine continues to attract attention. Once again, Ali and colleagues3 pointed out that a reduction in upper respiratory tract infections was observed, supporting the concept of trained immunity.18 This aligns with broader evidence indicating that BCG vaccination might modulate innate immune responses and confer non-specific, off-target resistance against unrelated infections,19 making protection beyond TB plausible, yet formally unproven. However, these potential benefits must be weighed against the lack of measurable protection against M. tuberculosis infection and the increased incidence of AEs reported across studies.

Taken together, both Ali and colleagues3 and Choi and colleagues4 findings carry important implications for TB vaccine research and policy: (i) There is a need to refine endpoints in TB vaccine trials, moving beyond surrogate markers toward outcomes that better capture disease prevention;20 (ii) Standardization of surveillance systems is essential for accurate safety assessment;15 and (iii) systematic evaluation of BCG vaccine strain-specific effects is required.16,17,21 Above all, identifying correlates of protection after BCG vaccination remains critical to bridging the gap between immunogenicity and clinical effectiveness.9,10 Until then, interpretation will remain as complex as the BCG vaccine itself. As such, the evaluation of BCG vaccination must consider not only its uncertain protective efficacy across disease stages but also its context-dependent safety profile, which varies according to host characteristics, BCG vaccine strain, and surveillance practices.2,11,16

In this broader context, these recent contributions published in Human Vaccines & Immunotherapeutics do not resolve the uncertainties surrounding BCG vaccination; rather, they bring into sharper focus the complexity that defines it. By reframing accumulated evidence, they reveal not only what is known, but also how it is known, highlighting the interplay between data, methodology, and biological variability. Understanding the BCG vaccine now requires understanding the evidence itself. As the field advances, such integrative approaches will be essential for clarifying the role of BCG (re)vaccination and guiding the development of more effective immunization strategies for TB and beyond.
