On Tightness of the Figiel-Lindenstrauss-Milman Inequality
Tomer Milo

Abstract
This note aims to explore the tightness of the classical Figiel-Lindenstrauss-Milman inequality, which states that there exists some constant c > 0 c>0 such that for any dimension n n and any symmetric polytope P ⊂ ℝ n P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n} : log ⁡ | V | ⋅ log ⁡ | ℱ | ≥ c ​ n \log|V|\cdot\log|\mathcal{F}|\geq cn , where V V and ℱ \mathcal{F} denote the sets of vertices and facets of P P , respectively. We show that this inequality is asymptotically tight in almost all possible cases, except for a special case which was previously studied by Barvinok.



The Figiel-Lindenstrauss-Milman (FLM) inequality, originally proved in
[
2
]
(see also
[
4
]
for a short and elementary proof), states the following:

Theorem 1
.

There exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and for every convex polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
which is symmetric about the origin (that is,
P=-P
) we have:

\log|V|\cdot\log|\mathcal{F}|\geq cn

Where
V
and
\mathcal{F}
are the sets of vertices (
0
-dimensional) and facets (
(n-1)
-dimensional facets) of
P
, respectively. Moreover, the same estimate holds for a general polytope
P
satisfying
B_{2}^{n}\subset P\subset\sqrt{n}B_{2}^{n}
, where
B_{2}^{n}
is the Euclidean unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The aim of this note is to explore how tight this inequality is. As a starting point, we observe that for the cube
P=B_{\infty}^{n}:=[-1,1]^{n}
we have
|V|=2^{n}
and
|\mathcal{F}|=2n
and so
\log|V|\cdot\log|\mathcal{F}|\sim n\log n
(here and throughout,
\sim
means ‘equivalent up to absolute constants’).

The paper is organized as follows: first, we show that in some dimensions we can construct polytopes
P_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\log|V_{n}|\sim\log|\mathcal{F}_{n}|\sim\sqrt{n}
and in particular
\log|V_{n}|\cdot\log|\mathcal{F}_{n}|\sim n
. Here
V_{n}
and
\mathcal{F}_{n}
denote the sets of vertices and facets of
P_{n}
, respectively. We then use this construction to produce other constructions which show that Theorem
1
is tight for almost all possible choices of the sizes of
|V_{n}|
and
|\mathcal{F}_{n}|
, complementing a result of Barvinok (see Theorem
3
below) which shows that there are certain cases in which Theorem
1
is in fact not tight.

1
Preliminaries and notations

We call a subset
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
a
polytope
if it is the convex hull of finitely many points and has a non-empty interior. We denote by
V_{P}
and
\mathcal{F}_{P}
the sets of vertices (
0
-dimensional faces) and facets (
(n-1)
-dimensional faces) respectively, and often just write
V
and
\mathcal{F}
when the polytope which we refer to is clear from the context. When we have a sequence of polytopes
P_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
we will also often write
V_{n}
instead of
V_{P_{n}}
, etc. For two quantities
A
and
B
, which may depend on the dimension, a polytope, and other parameters, we say that
A\lesssim B
if there exists some absolute constant
c>0
so that
A<cB
. We write
A\sim B
if
A\lesssim B
and
B\lesssim A
.

For a polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we denote by
P^{\circ}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\max_{y\in P}\langle x,y\rangle\leq 1\}
the
dual polytope
of
P
(this is indeed a polytope whenever
0\in{\rm int}(P)
). It is easy to verify that the dual operation switches between the numbers of vertices and facets of a polytope; that is,
|V_{P^{\circ}}|=|\mathcal{F}_{P}|
and
|\mathcal{F}_{P^{\circ}}|=|V_{P}|
, and that duality is an involution:
P^{\circ\circ}=P
. We will often use the following elementary facts: given an orthogonal decomposition
\mathbb{R}^{n+k}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\bigoplus\mathbb{R}^{k}
and polytopes
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
, then for the polytope
P\times Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+k}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\bigoplus\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we have
|V_{P\times Q}|=|V_{P}||V_{Q}|
and
|\mathcal{F}_{P\times Q}|=|\mathcal{F}_{P}|+|\mathcal{F}_{Q}|
. Finally, it is elementary to verify that
(P\times Q)^{\circ}={\rm conv}(P^{\circ},Q^{\circ})
, where the duals
P^{\circ}
and
Q^{\circ}
are computed in the respective subspaces
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{k}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n+k}
. All logarithms are to base
2
.

2
The constructions

We begin by constructing symmetric polytopes
P_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2^{n}}
such that
\log|V_{n}|\cdot\log|\mathcal{F}_{n}|\sim 2^{n}
. Actually, we will have
\log|V_{n}|\sim\log|\mathcal{F}_{n}|\sim 2^{\frac{n}{2}}
. This will show that Theorem
1
is generally asymptotically tight (up to a constant).

Proposition 2
.

For any dimension
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a polytope
P_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2^{n}}
such that

\log|V_{n}|\sim\log|\mathcal{F}_{n}|\sim 2^{\frac{n}{2}}
. So, writing
N=2^{n}
, we have:
P_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\log|V_{n}|\cdot\log|\mathcal{F}_{n}|\sim N
.

Proof.

We take Cartesian products and convex hulls iteratively to obtain a certain Hanner polytope
P_{n}
, starting with
P_{0}=[-1,1]\subset\mathbb{R}
, as follows: Assuming
P_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2^{n}}
is defined, we define

P_{n+1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2^{n+1}}=\mathbb{R}^{2^{n}}\bigoplus\mathbb{R}^{2^{n}}
as

P_{n+1}=\begin{cases}{\rm conv}(P_{n},P_{n}),\>n\>even\\
P_{n}\times P_{n},\>n\>odd\\
\end{cases}

Clearly
P_{n}
is centrally symmetric for all
n
, and

|V_{n+1}|=\begin{cases}2|V_{n}|,\>n\>even\\
|V_{n}|^{2},\>n\>odd\\
\end{cases}

and by duality,

|\mathcal{F}_{n+1}|=\begin{cases}2|\mathcal{F}_{n}|,\>n\>odd\\
|\mathcal{F}_{n}|^{2},\>n\>even\\
\end{cases}

It is now obvious that we have the following asymptotics for
P_{n}
:

\log|V_{n}|\sim\log|\mathcal{F}_{n}|\sim 2^{\frac{n}{2}}.

which is what we wanted to prove.
∎

Remark.
One can use the above construction to construct, for any dimension
N
, a polytope
P_{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
\log|V_{N}|\sim\log|\mathcal{F}_{N}|\sim\sqrt{N}
. This shows that Theorem
1
can’t even be strengthened for some special sequence of dimensions. Indeed, we may write
N
as
N=\sum_{i\in I}2^{r_{i}}
for some index set
I\subset[n]
and some natural numbers
r_{1}>..>r_{|I|}
, where
r_{1}=n=\lfloor\log N\rfloor
. Letting

P_{N}=P_{1}\times...\times P_{|I|}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N},

we have
\log|V_{N}|=\sum_{i\in I}2^{\frac{r_{i}}{2}}\sim 2^{\frac{\log N}{2}}=\sqrt{N}
, and similarly,
\log|\mathcal{F}_{N}|\sim\sqrt{N}
as well.

Our next goal is to check whether there are special cases for which Theorem
1
is actually not tight. In
[
3
]
, Barvinok provided an answer in the case where the number of facets is linear in
n
:

Theorem 3
.

For any
\alpha>0
there exists
\gamma>0
such that the following holds: for any dimension
n
and any centrally symmetric polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, if
\mathcal{|}{\mathcal{F}|}\leq\alpha n
then
|V|\geq e^{\gamma n}
. The same result holds when one assumes that
B_{2}^{n}\subset P\subset\sqrt{n}B_{2}^{n}
instead of the central-symmetry assumption.

In words: linearly many facets implies exponentially many vertices, in the dimension
n
, and vice versa by duality, which means that Theorem
1
is not always tight. We now prove that other than this particular case, Theorem
1
is (almost) always tight:

Proposition 4
.

Let
f(n)\to_{n\to\infty}\infty
be some arbitrary sequence of integers such that
f(n)<\sqrt{n}
. There exists polytopes
P_{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
where
N=N(n)\to_{n\to\infty}\infty
is another sequence such that:

|V_{N}|\sim 2^{\frac{cN}{f(N)}}\>\>and\>\>|\mathcal{F}_{N}|\sim N\frac{2^{c^{\prime}f(N)}}{f(N)^{2}}

Where
c,c^{\prime}>0
are absolute constants. In particular,

\log|V_{N}|\cdot\log|\mathcal{F}_{N}|\sim N\left(1+\frac{\log N}{f(N)}\right).

(1)

Remark.

Choosing
f(N)=\log N
gives polytopes
P_{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
\log|\mathcal{F}_{N}|\sim\log N
and

\log|V_{N}|\sim\frac{N}{\log N}
, showing that Barvinok’s theorem
3
cannot be extended to the polynomial case.
In fact, we now see that Theorem
1
is tight, up to a constant, for almost all possible choices of sizes of
|V_{N}|
and
|\mathcal{F}_{N}|
, in the following sense: whenever
f(N)
has at least a logarithmic type growth (that is, there exists some
{\varepsilon}>0
such that
f(N)>{\varepsilon}\log N
), we have
\log|V_{N}|\cdot\log|\mathcal{F}_{N}|\sim N
in (1). Noting that
f(N)={\varepsilon}\log N
corresponds to
|\mathcal{F}_{N}|\sim\frac{N^{1+c^{\prime}{\varepsilon}}}{{\varepsilon}^{2}\log^{2}N}
, we can conclude that whenever a sequence
a_{n}
has a type of growth of at least
n^{1+{\varepsilon}}
for some
{\varepsilon}>0
(and also
\log(a_{n})<\sqrt{n}
) one can generate polytopes
P_{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
for which
\log|\mathcal{F}_{N}|\sim\log a_{N}
and
\log|V_{N}|\sim\frac{N}{\log a_{N}}
, by writing
a_{N}=N\frac{2^{f(N)}}{cf(N)^{2}}
and inverting the relation to find the appropriate
f
to apply Proposition
4
to (note that
f(n)\sim\log a_{n}
). This settles the question of tightness of Theorem
1
, except for the regime of ‘between linear and polynomial’ - see section 3 for a discussion.

Proof of Proposition
4
.

Let
P_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2^{n}}
be the polytopes constructed in Proposition
2
.
Let
N
be given by
n=2\lfloor\log f(N)\rfloor
(here we use
f(n)<\sqrt{n}
). We orthogonally decompose
\mathbb{R}^{N}=\mathbb{R}^{2^{n}}\bigoplus....\bigoplus\mathbb{R}^{2^{n}}
, where there are
\frac{N}{2^{n}}
summands. Let
P_{N}=P_{n}\times...\times P_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
. We have, for some absolute constants
c_{,}c^{\prime}>0
:

|V_{N}|=|V_{n}|^{\frac{N}{2^{n}}}\sim 2^{\frac{cN}{f(N)}},

|\mathcal{F}_{N}|=\frac{N}{2^{n}}|\mathcal{F}_{n}|\sim N\frac{2^{c^{\prime}f(N)}}{f(N)^{2}}.

∎

3
Between Linear and Polynomial

The final ‘gap’ we should address is the one between linear and polynomial, as explained in the remark after Proposition
4
; we have seen that, given a sequence
a_{n}
such that
a_{n}>n^{1+{\varepsilon}}
(and also
\log(a_{n})<\sqrt{n}
) for some
{\varepsilon}>0
, we may find polytopes
P_{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
for some sequence
N=N(n)
such that
\log|\mathcal{F}_{N}|\sim a_{N}
and
\log|V_{N}|\sim\frac{N}{a_{N}}
, while for the linear behavior
a_{n}\sim n
, Theorem
3
shows that whenever
|\mathcal{F}_{n}|\sim n
we have
\log|V_{n}|\sim n
.

This leaves the following question: Let
P_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a sequence of polytopes such that

|\mathcal{F}_{n}|=n\phi(n)
, where
\phi(n)
tends to infinity slowly; to be precise,
\phi(n)=o(n^{\varepsilon})
for all
{\varepsilon}>0
. What can be said about the size of
|V_{n}|
? Barvinok’s theorem
3
actually extends to this case as well. In his paper, he proved that if
|\mathcal{F}|\leq n\phi(n)
, then for all
\rho>0
, we have the following inequality:

|V|\geq e^{\frac{n}{2\rho^{2}}}(1-e^{-\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}})^{n\phi(n)}

Choosing
\rho=\sqrt{4\log\phi(n)}
gives that for some constant
c>0
,

|V|\gtrsim e^{\frac{cn}{\log\phi(n)}}

and so,

\log|V|\cdot\log|\mathcal{F}|\gtrsim\frac{n}{\log\phi(n)}\left(\log n+\log\phi(n)\right)=n\left(1+\frac{\log n}{\log\phi(n)}\right)

(2)

To see how tight this bound is, we choose
f(N)=\log\phi(N)
in the construction presented in the proof of Proposition
4
. This gives polytopes
P_{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
where
N=N_{n}
is a sequence of dimensions which tends to
\infty
such that:

|V_{N}|\sim 2^{\frac{cN}{\log\phi(N)}}\>and\>|\mathcal{F}_{N}|\sim N\frac{\phi(N)^{c^{\prime}}}{\log^{2}\phi(N)}

for some absolute constants
c,c^{\prime}>0
. In particular,

\log|V_{N}|\cdot\log|\mathcal{F}_{N}|\sim N\left(1+\frac{\log N}{\log\phi(N)}\right)

So the bound in (2) is tight up to a constant. Note that this example shows that the exponential behavior of
|V_{N}|
from Theorem
3
can in fact ‘break’ whenever
|\mathcal{F}_{N}|
grows faster than linear, no matter how slowly.
