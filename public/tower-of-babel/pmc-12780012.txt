AI-powered models for overcrowding prediction at TUMS hospitals
Authors: Mohammad Javad Shaibani, Ali Akbar Fazaeli, Rajabali Daroudi, Maryam Radinmanesh, Sara Emamgholipour Sefiddashti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
When the emergency department (ED) of a hospital reaches its saturation capacity, adverse events such as increased waiting times, increased mortality, clinical mistakes, and a financial burden escalate. Accurate overcrowding forecasting renders a powerful alarming tool for increasing the operational efficiency of hospitals. This study provides a novel contribution by leveraging a time series dataset for 5 hospitals of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (TUMS) to predict daily ED-overcrowding. Firstly, we identified 121 possible features per ED. Then, in the attribute selection, we used a new time series features selection method using minimum redundancy and maximum relevance (mRMR) with mutual information selector, and elastic net, both implemented via time series cross validation with growing window (TSCVG). In time series modeling, we implement several base learners, namely linear regression, elastic net, neural network, long short-term memory (LSTM), support vector machine (SVM), random forest, least square boosting, and a convolutional neural network with Bayesian tuning upon TSCVG folds. These base learners are combined by using ensemble methods, namely weighted and quantile averaging, and variance-penalized Bayesian Model Averaging (VP-BMA). Results indicated that reducing the initial feature set using the feature selection method significantly improves the modeling performance for all EDs. It is observed that the binary calendar feature and the delays of the target are frequently selected in all EDs. According to the VP-BMA model component analysis, the SVM and LSTM showed the most reliable performance across all EDs. As a result, ED A indicated a stable performance across most overcrowding thresholds (ROC ≈ 0.95–0.98), EDs C and E a reasonable at moderate cutoffs, while EDs B and D showed poor performance in all overcrowding thresholds.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-30426-2.

Introduction

Health managers and policymakers have always faced various challenges, such as the shortage of skilled workers, limited facilities, and increasing demand. Meanwhile, due to reasons such as population growth, the demand for emergency departments (EDs) of hospitals has always been on the rise in the last decade1 such that in some areas, its growth rate has surpassed the population growth rate. Arising from the dynamic nature of the working environment and the high uncertainty in patient conditions, the workload in EDs is higher than in other divisions of hospitals. Also, doctors and nurses in EDs have a great responsibility in providing primary diagnostic, therapeutic, resuscitation, and stabilization services to patients, heightening workload strains2. Inability to satisfy ED demand during the peak times will precipitate the ED-overcrowding3. On the one hand, this phenomenon causes patient dissatisfaction, slashed service quality, and diseconomies of scale4, and on the other hand, prolonged latency can also be associated with detrimental effects, such as increased mortality rates in EDs. This has led to the identification of overcrowding in EDs as a pivotal concern for all medical care systems in the world5.

The overcrowding has a huge financial burden for healthcare systems and distorts the resource allocation. Because of the expanded treatment duration, intensified resource usage, and the need for more staff, the overcrowding will increase the operational costs of EDs6. As reported by a review6 on the one hand overcrowding increases outlays of EDs by US/$ 6.8 million for three years, and on the other hand, reducing waiting time by one hour per day will decrease financial burdens and improve income flow by US/$ 4.9 million (quoted from7–9. A survey also articulated that 6.5% of Ontario’s ED spending ($51 million) was assigned to patients deferred in EDs as a result of bed shortages10. It has also been pointed out that when care seekers are discharged prematurely as a consequence of an overcrowded ED, their symptoms will exacerbate and they return to the EDs with more serious conditions, which in turn increases the ED costs exponentially6,11. Studies have shown that the shortage of medical personnel cannot be the main reason for the prolonged waiting times and decline in care quality12. Rather, the lack of an effective staffing strategy during the overcrowding is the leading cause for the decline in ED-care quality. Therefore, using new methodologies for predicting ED demand will equip emergency managers with an indispensable tool for optimally allocating hospital resources and mitigating economic setbacks.

In the past two years, for the first time in Iran, Tehran University Medical Sciences (TUMS) used an integrated system to collect ED visitors’ data from its hospitals. Since then, almost two years of time series data have been accumulated. Within this research, for the first time in Iran, we adopted a raw dataset for 5 EDs of TUMS hospitals and pre-processed them to retrieve targets, the ED time series. We have ordered the research in the following fashion: first, we reviewed the related literature of ED predictive models, highlighting the novelties of our work. Following that, the ED dataset of 5 TUMS hospitals is explored statistically. In the research methodology part, we presented the methodological design of the study. This part is partitioned into multiple sub-sections: In the feature engineering, we explained possible predictors and built rolling features based on the EDs dataset. After that, we examined the Bayesian optimization strategy for tuning hyperparameters and explained the expanding cross-validation process. Then, in the attribute selection, we introduced a time series version of minimum redundancy and maximum relevance (mRMR) with a mutual information selector as the “filter phase” and then discussed the regularization phase built upon an elastic net. In the following, the base predictive models (support vector machine (SVM), elastic net, long short-term memory (LSTM), random forest, and …) and the methods of combining the “base predictions” are outlined. The methodology section ended by delivering some criteria for both time series forecasting and overcrowding prediction phases. Finally, in the results section, we addressed the feature selection and time series modeling results, model component analysis, overcrowding classification performance, and operational decision analysis of using models for all EDs.

Related studies

Model wisely, the predictive models of ED demand could be categorized into statistical regressions, time series models (univariate), time series-max models (combinations of regression and univariate models), and machine learning (ML) algorithms. Accordingly, below is a summarized review of the related works conducted in EDs:

The demand for a radiology department in a hospital in China was estimated using a Lasso regression model13. This lasso is a modified form of the OLS estimation in which an attempt is made to control the number of collinear predictors with a penalty. The predictors considered for the model can be categorized as temporal and calendar features including lagged values of the patient visits, time indicators such as day of the week, months, and public holiday. Statistical evaluation suggested that the model performs better than other models (ARIMA models, random forest, support vector regression, and Adaboost). For the outcome of forecasting the total radiology emergency visits, the LASSO model selected a parsimonious set of features from the above categories, leading to its status as the best-performing model with a Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) of 7.06%13. Another study used OLS regression estimation followed by a Monte Carlo simulation to predict regional demand for EDs in Poland14. The researcher estimated the demand with three independent variables: total population, percentage of population aged 0–4 years of the total population, and percentage of population aged 60 years and above of the total population, and then, using a Monte Carlo simulation, the demographic impact on emergency demand is investigated. In another related case study in a hospital in Boston, USA, the Logit regression model was used as the main model for predicting inpatient admissions in the ED based on the triage levels15. The logit-linear regression model was constructed using a set of features available at the time of the patient’s triage. The features included:

The outcome variable was a binary indicator of whether the patients were admitted to the hospital as an inpatient. This model, with an average error of 0.19 beds per day, an R2 value (squared correlation coefficient) of 58%, and an ROC-AUC 0f 0.887, performed better than other benchmark models, namely expert opinions and a conditional probability model. A generalized additive model (GAM) was applied for predicting ED arrivals over a 48-hour horizon in the UK16. GAMs are a class of statistical regression employed to capture nonlinear relationships between predictors while maintaining interpretability. The researchers employed GAM and leveraging exponential smoothing to generate hourly probabilistic forecasts. Using generalized linear modeling (GLM), researchers examined the demand for the ED of a hospital in São Paulo, Brazil17. The GLM method is a generalized form of the linear regression model that allows the use of the target using a link function. It is usually estimated using the maximum likelihood. In addition to past values ​​of the target, the researchers used other variables such as days of the year and holidays, and the average ambient temperature. This GLM showed better performance in contrast to baseline methods.

In statistical time series models, the target is usually considered as a function of its past values​, and then the errors are minimized using optimization algorithms. A study examined ED demand for some hospitals in Western Australia18. The researchers estimated a set of targets, namely ED demands, using a vector auto-regressive moving average model, VARMA. Using error indicators, the researchers compared the predictive power of VARMA with the simple ARMA method as well as smoothing models in different forecast horizons (windows). The ARIMA family was used to predict ED demand in France19 and China20 and showed a better performance than the GARCH (an autoregressive process of errors) model in the US21. Considering the impact of recurring seasonal patterns on ED demand, a number of studies have investigated this issue using seasonal ARIMA as the main model22,23. Unlike ARIMA, such models focus on past values ​​of the dependent variable in past seasons or periods. In Brazil, the demand for the triage unit of a Clinic was investigated using SARIMA, multivariate ARIMA (ARIMA MAX), seasonal exponential smoothing, and seasonal multiplicative Holt-Winters models24. In ARIMA MAX, like ARIMA, both autoregressive and moving average terms are used. The only difference is the usage of a predictor in the ARIMA MAX model. In Holt-Winters models, a time series is divided into mean, trend, and seasonal fluctuation parts, and the final forecast is done by smoothing the result of these three parts. The study showed that incorporating temperature and environmental variables into the ARIMA MAX model does not improve its performance24. A study additionally documented the superiority of the seasonal version of ARIMA MAX for the lowest out-of-sample prediction mean error among benchmarks25.

Another side of the spectrum of ED demand predictive models is AI algorithms. Typically, AI predictive models fall into two categories: machine learning and deep learning frameworks. In machine learning, an attempt is made to discover patterns in data with several methods. For this purpose, after processing the data, the model is trained (the process of minimizing the squared prediction error) and then tested with unseen data to determine its reliability. The word “learning” also means the self-adaptation of these algorithms in minimizing the error function26. Deep learning has also been developed and inspired by neural dynamics and the brain functionality of humans. Deep networks are a special type of artificial neural network (ANN). A shallow net like ANN comprises three distinct layers: input, hidden, and output. The word “deep” also corresponds to an abundant presence of hidden layers in these networks26. An important application of AI models in the ED serves to predict demand for streamlined operational planning. Using an autoregressive ANN and decomposition of ED demand into trend, seasonal pattern, and noise, researchers aimed to predict ED demand in Morocco27. ANNs are usually trained using gradient descent algorithms and provide a mapping from the feature space to the target space. In the aforementioned study, researchers analyzed weekly data to model each signal using an ANN, and then combined all the signals to provide a prediction of the ED demand. Compared to ARIMA, the proposed ANN performed better27. In another study, an LSTM was used to approximate the ED demand of a hospital in Brazil28. LSTM networks are a special type of deep ANNs that focus on the entire data sequence. Each LSTM is composed of a cell with input and output gates. In LSTM, past demand values ​​are remembered using a cellular memory, and a type of memory is constructed for the network through feedback loops. In their study, the target is explained using features such as weekends, holidays, football match days, and days before and after holidays. In other studies in France and Spain, LSTM was used to predict ED demand29,30. A related study also combined LSTM and support vector regression predictions for predicting ICU-ED demand for resources in China31. The support vector regression (SVR) attempts to estimate a regression in a limited space between two hyperplanes using a kernel (usually RBF). An SVR classifier seeks to find a hyperplane that separates all the patterns in the data. The margin separation is done using some data elements known as support vectors. In their paper, the authors train the SVR model with a metaheuristic algorithm and attain the predicted values, and then pass the errors (Yactual-Ypredicted) to the LSTM. According to their results, the combined predictions indicated a better prediction compared to the individual predictions. In another study, researchers from the Mayo Clinic predicted ED outpatient demand in two regions32. For this purpose, researchers used various statistical models such as ARIMA, moving average, linear regression, as well as XGBOOST, LSTM, and ANN, and decision tree regression models. XGBOOST is trained like a random forest (tree regressions). The deviation between both lies in the sequential training of XGBOOST. In other words, in boosting methods, each regression is first trained using the gradient boosting method, and its error is used to train the next branch, while in the random forest, all regressions are trained distinctly. In addition to the target lagged intervals, variables like days of the week and…, are used. The empirical data established that the XGBOOST outperformed other models. The XGBOOST demonstrated the lowest out-of-sample prediction error in another study as well33.

It is stated that when occupancy levels surpass 90% in an ED, a relationship with 10-day mortality is observable34. Then LightGBM, which is another gradient boosting method, is applied to predict mortalities related to that ED overcrowding. The authors used features like meteorological and calendar variables. An additional work successfully implemented LightGBM for the prediction of ED demand as well35. Random forest regression has also been reported to perform better in predicting daily ED visits, as observed in one study36. That is a random model in which there is a set of tree regressions that are trained for both classification and regression prediction, and finally, the output is established through the use of the average output of all trees. Another important issue for effective ED management is the length of stay of patients. In a study in Lille, France, researchers applied s generator & discriminator ANN to predict the length of stay of patients in the ED37. In this model, the equilibrium occurs where both the generating and discriminating parts reach their maximum efficiency in simulating the real data and distinguishing it from the fake data, respectively. Their proposed model consists of 12 features (date and time of arrival at the ED, age, gender, diagnostic tests, etc.) and one output variable, namely the patient’s length of stay. The results demonstrated that the proposed ANN performs better relative to baseline models.

Some studies investigated scholarly sources for the ED demand predictive models as well. A review analyzed the applications of statistical predictive models to EDs38. This review cataloged the forecasting paradigms applied across various ED processes. Likewise, a 2023 review explored the error performance of ED demand predictive models39. The best models were found to be strongly influenced by the ED dimension. The deep learning competence forecasting ED volume is investigated by a recent study applying LSTM40. The study found that LSTM and deep learning models provide strong predictive ability for ED patient influx, while the calendar and meteorological data sustained their function as key features.

ED dataset

Since 2023/03/21, TUMS started documenting the EDs dataset in two hospitals (two university hospitals, namely Ziaeian & Razi hospitals (ED B and ED E, respectively)). Following them, ED C (Yas hospital) and ED D (Shariati hospital), and ED A (Children’s hospital) started recording visitors’ data. We selected these EDs among the other TUMS EDs considering longitudinal data availability, codified documentation protocols, data integrity, and minimal data omissions. We counted each ED visit based on a union operator for all dates, attaining the time series daily targets for 5 EDs. For a few missing values, we replaced them with mean values from the corresponding time points on the seven previous days. The ethical approval for this study was obtained from the TUMS board, under the formally registered code number [IR.TUMS.SPH.REC.1403.031]. The dataset contained no labels or sensitive personal identifiers, and only anonymized records of patient arrivals were analyzed, without access to any information that could allow for patient identification.

Figure 1 exhibits the histograms of these EDs fitted with the normal distribution, rounded descriptive statistics of the targets, and the Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) stationarity test. The length of the dataset ranges from 721 days (ED B and ED E) to 609 days (ED A). Accounting for ED size, ED A and ED B have more visitors than their counterparts. An important point was the presence of outliers. In the initial inspection, almost all outliers were identified as top outliers. These “top outliers” reflect real-world anomalies, such as a disease outbreak or a mass-casualty event. We detected these points using the boxplot method but decided to retain them within the data so that the model is trained on a realistic representation of demand that includes essential high-visit cases. Therefore, omitting them obscures their carrying signal and undermines the effectiveness of early warning systems for the overcrowding. In other words, these outliers in EDs could be meaningful anomalies rather than a noise. Hence, their retention secures models that reflect real-world conditions and is useful for defining overcrowding days. Table 2A (in the appendix 1), provides more descriptive statistics for the target.

Stationarity of the time series dataset is another critical point and is a prerequisite for many parametric models. Figure 1 indicates the ADF statistics with their p-values. All p-values are less than 0.05, rejecting H₀ (non-stationary). Hence, the target is stationary, and no further transformation except the Max-Min normalization is needed.

Expanding window validation

Expanding window validation is a time series cross-validation strategy in which the training portion grows incrementally. It starts with an initial training set and then adds new samples while preserving past data with temporal order and validating on the subsequent steps. Stated differently, the test partition of the series is merged into the training part incrementally, and the prediction reference point is also enlarged in the training data41. We set the initial window = 365 days with a step size of 30 days. It captures annual cycles and ensures baseline stability against short-term anomalies since the seasonal pattern in ED demand (e.g., winter flu seasons) cycles annually. A 30-day step size retains computational efficiencies while retaining recent trends (balancing temporal proximity and computational cost). Figure 3 shows the validation strategy of our study.

To ensure the validity of the mRMR, it is applied by time series expanding windows. Final scores are calculated using an average operator across all folds. The ultimate feature set is a sorted list of features on the basis of MIQ scores. The pseudocode of this process is depicted in Fig. 5.

For selecting features in the ranked mRMR output we standardized raw mRMR scores to the unit interval and used the features up to the 90% cumulative-score value. We selected this cutoff as a defined, reproducible stopping rule for balancing information and the model complexity. The cumulative-score curve indicates a similar behavior to an explained-information curve, similar to explained variance in PCA which keeps just enough of the components to account for at least 90% of the total variance44 such that the retained predictors explain the majority of the mRMR signals, leaving out many low-impact features. Notably, since mRMR is based on MI, the ranking phase captures both linear and non-linear dependencies; hence the 90% cumulative rule is effective in including features that offer distinct information about the target to the selected information budget. The pipeline that we applied is two-stage non-linear information filtering with mRMR and then linear elastic-net regularized regression, which focuses downstream estimation on less redundant and informative features.

Regularization phase

As an embedded attribute selection approach, the elastic net combines both L1 (Lasso) and L2 (Ridge) penalties45. The elastic net is an ideal model when dealing with many attributes that may be collinear as well. ​ It explicitly offers coefficients for selected attributes, which are more interpretable relative to alternative models. The basic formula of the elastic net is defined as an optimization problem.

6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}min\\\:\beta\:\end{array}\left\{{ \parallel\:y-X\beta\:\parallel}^{2}+\lambda\:\left[\alpha\:\sum\:_{j=1}^{p}\left|{\beta\:}_{j}\right|+(1-\alpha\:)\sum\:_{j=1}^{p}{\beta\:}_{j}^{2}\right]\right\}$$\end{document}

Where y, X, β, λ, α are dependent variable, attribute matrix, coefficients, regularization parameter, and tuning parameters (L1 and L2 penalties), respectively. Lambda manages the magnitude of the penalties. Alpha ranges between 0 and 1. When alpha = 1, the sparsity is promoted by shrinking some attribute coefficients to zero (Lasso). If alpha = 0, we have Ridge regression by smoothening the coefficients of correlated attributes. If 0 < α < 1, the model is called an elastic net.

We trained an elastic net and used time series cross-validation with growing windows (hereafter TSCVG). Based on the cross-validation prediction errors, the optimal λ and α are found (for each ED). The final model is trained with the tuned parameters, and attributes with non-zero coefficients are selected. The pseudocode of the elastic net is presented in Fig. 6.

Overall our pipeline combined non-linear mRMR filtering followed by the linear elastic-net regression and ranked the features in a non-redundant and an informative manner. In other words, complementary strengths of the two-stage feature selection result in signal discovery, parsimonious estimation, and increase interpretability of the selected features. An informative set of features is selected by the mRMR algorithm which identifies that a purely linear filter can overlook. Elastic net then applies the L1/L2 regularization for creating sparse well-shrunken coefficients under cross-validation and multicollinearity. This division simplifies nested-CV and hyperparameter tuning by decreasing the computational cost. This design is a hybrid of both non-linear screening (to extract informative temporal changes and interactions) and the statistical stability, explicit sparsity management and interpretability of elastic net regularization.

Random forest (RF)

RF is an ensemble machine learning algorithm that builds multiple randomized decision trees throughout the training process (on the bootstrapped data) and computes the mean of the tree predictions49. Formally.

12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{y}=\frac{1}{B}\sum\:_{n=1}^{N}{f}_{n}\left(x\right)$$\end{document}

Where N and fn are number of trees and the learned regression by the nth tree, respectively. Key hyperparameters are number of trees (N), min leaf size, and number of features to sample (NFS). There is an inverse relation between N and the variance of the model. Nevertheless, the greater the N, the greater becomes the cost of computation. The minimum number of data points in the leaf node is also determined by the min leaf size. Trade-off in this case is that as the tree becomes deeper more intricate patterns are learned, but conversely there is an increased risk of overfitting as the tree gets deeper. NFS with smaller values enhance the heterogeneity of trees by adding more randomness, although important splits can be rejected. In simple terms, there exists a trade-off between bias and variance in this case. All these parameters were tuned by BO.

Least-Squares boosting regression (LSBoost)

LSBoost which belongs to the gradient boosting family uses incremental updates to integrate some weak learners into a stronger predictive learner while chronologically modifying some of them to reduce squared errors left by the current model50,51. The final output of the LSBoost can be expressed as follows.

13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{y}\left(x\right)={y}^{0}\left(x\right)+\alpha\:\sum\:_{r=1}^{r=R}{w}_{r}\left(x\right)$$\end{document}

Where y0 is the first naïve prediction, alpha points to learn/shrinkage rate, w and R are the weak learner and boosting rounds, respectively. In this study, the learn rate, min leaf size (akin to RF), and boosting rounds (number of cycles) are tuned by BO.

Ensemble predictions

The ensemble forecasts were generated with a stacking paradigm, where the out-of-fold predictions of different base learners inputted as input data for the meta-ensemble layer. In this study we used the following models:

Weighted-average predictions (WAVG)

If we denote the prediction errors of the ith base learner with Ei, the non-negative weight is.

21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{W}_{i}=\frac{f\left({E}_{i}\right)}{\sum\:_{j=1}^{N}{E}_{j}}\:,\sum\:_{n=1}^{N}{w}_{n}=1$$\end{document}

Where f(E) is selected as inverse-RMSE (root mean squared error), assigning more weights to learners with smaller errors. The ensemble prediction is a linear blend as follows:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{y}}_{t}=round\left(\sum\:_{n=1}^{N}{W}_{n}{X}_{t,n}\right)$$\end{document}

In this way, models that showed less validation error will have a greater impact on the ultimate prediction.

Quantile averaging (QAVG): Given n base-learners with prediction set [Xt,1, Xt,2,…Xt, n] we can compute: Q10​(t) = 10th percentile of [Xt,1, Xt,2,…Xt, n] and Q90​(t) = 90th percentile of [Xt,1, Xt,2,…Xt, n]. The quantile average forecaster is then defined:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{y}}_{t}=round\left(\frac{{Q}_{10\%}\left(t\right)+{Q}_{90\%}\left(t\right)}{2}\right)$$\end{document}

This method ignores the extreme predictions of base models (under Q10% & over Q90%). It is robust to outliers and overfitting, while it incorporates dispersion as well.

Variance-Penalized bayesian model averaging (VP-BMA)

The classic Bayesian averaging model computes posterior model weights contingent on the likelihood of the target55. The posterior probability can be computed as follows.

24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\omega\:}_{j}\propto\:P\left(D\:|{\:M}_{j}\right)=\prod\:_{t}f\left({y}_{t}\:\right|\:{\widehat{y}}_{j,t},{\sigma\:}_{j}^{2})$$\end{document}

where f represents the Gaussian likelihood, ω points to weight, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:P\left(D\:|{\:M}_{j}\right)$$\end{document} is posterior probability of model j, respectively. In the above Eq we defined σ2 as the variance of prediction to account for uncertainty.

If σ2 is not taken into consideration, ω can overweight highly variable models even though they represent each model explanatory power within a probabilistic framework. Through the penalization of predictive variance, the suggested ensemble VP-BMA expands upon the classical BMA:25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\omega\:}_{j}=\frac{exp(log(P\left(D\:|{\:M}_{j}\right)\left)\right)}{{\sigma\:}_{j}^{2}+\lambda\:}/\sum\:_{k}\frac{exp(log(P\left(D\:|{\:M}_{k}\right)\left)\right)}{{\sigma\:}_{k}^{2}+\lambda\:}$$\end{document}

Where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{j}^{2}$$\end{document} denotes the empirical variance of residuals of time-series cross-validation, and λ is a stabilizing constant. By favoring models that attain both high likelihood and low variance this formulation penalizes instability while rewarding accuracy.

The advantages of using VP-BMA can be categorized into two main aspects: (1) it converges the predictions toward the most accurate and reliable models (the classic bias-variance trade-off), and (2) it provides probabilistic weights for model selection. The common practice for the model selection is based on average error metrics. However, this method does not account for models’ variances. VP-BMA works well especially where predictive mean and uncertainty are important like in daily ED forecasting. By penalizing models with large variability, VP-BMA prevents the overfitting, resulting in more stable ensemble weights for its base-models. Hence, VP-BMA has the advantage of selecting not only the most accurate but also the most consistent base learners over the time.

Conceptual frameworks for overcrowding

As applied to the situation of ED demand prediction and operational classification, percentile-based thresholds provide an intuitive and efficient way to determine high demands. In the Lazio Region, Italy, the researchers relied on percentile-based measures to describe crowding, associating high volumes of ED operation with the short-term health outcomes56. They categorized ED count visits as reference (< 75th percentile), crowded (75th − 95th), and overcrowded (> 95th percentile). Another paper determined the comparative overcrowding scores with a percentile threshold to compare the performance of EDs57.An ED warning system for 2-day warnings is also introduced a percentile-based thresholds using the past demand58. The authors defined overcrowding by combining two demand-based approaches. In the first method, the overcrowding is defined by visit counts according to some historical medians: Green (Level 1) for < 5% increase, Yellow (Level 2) for 5–10%, and Red (Level 3) for ≥ 10%. Then, the authors used previous historical percentile thresholds, defining low demand as below the 25th percentile, medium demand between the 25th and 75th percentiles, high demand between the 75th and 90th percentiles, and very high demand as above the 91th percentile. Early warning and resource planning are supported by these frameworks which aid in the forecasting and classification of ED demand intensity58.

A progressive increase in the threshold for what is considered overcrowded is represented by raising the percentile from 0.50th to 0.95th according to the percentile-based definition of ED overcrowding in which the threshold is established by a particular percentile of the historical ED count distribution. Days with moderately high patient volumes correspond to lower percentiles such as the 50th or 60th which define the overcrowding as a relatively common occurrence. As the percentile increases to 0.75th and 0.85th, the threshold corresponds to the upper quartile or decile of the distribution, covering days with markedly high visits. Finally, when we set overcrowding at the highest percentiles (0.90th, 0.95th), we are defining extreme thresholds which only identifies the most severe surge events that strain the EDs to their operational limits. Thus, in this distribution, a higher percentile always adds more visits to anticipate an overcrowding event and hence flags a smaller number of days as overcrowded, but with a growing degree of severity in operations. In other words, by increasing the percentile cutoffs, we assume that the overcrowded days are more infrequent phenomenon.

Metrics and thresholds for overcrowding

Since it explicitly accounts for the overdispersion that usually results from day-of-week effects, seasonal epidemics and unobserved heterogeneity, the negative binomial (NB) is an ideal statistical distribution for modeling ED visit counts59. The Poisson distribution is not appropriate because of the overdispersion which is defined as the conditional variance in daily visits exceeding the mean60. In such cases the NB distribution delivers a stable threshold estimation due to its inherent dispersion parameter for the overdispersion60.

In our research, we determined the percentage threshold levels of the volumes on a daily basis by applying a parametric model to existing pre-test data. We utilized the Poisson and negative binomial models. The Poisson model assumes that the daily counts Y follow P(λ) as bellow:26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:P\left(Y=k\right)=\frac{{e}^{-\lambda\:}{\lambda\:}^{k}}{K!}$$\end{document}

which implies the equidispersion trait such that Var(Y) = E[Y]= \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\uplambda\:}$$\end{document}. In practice, the ED data frequently demonstrate the violation of equidispersion. Hence, the sample variance will exceed the sample mean (e.g., because of day-of-week variation, short-term epidemics), showing the overdispersion. In these circumstances, the negative binomial is preferred since it can account for the overdispersion. The NB has mean µ and a dispersion parameter such that r > 0, with the following variance:27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Var\left(Y\right)=\mu\:+\frac{{\mu\:}^{2}}{r}\:$$\end{document}

When r tends to ∞, the NB will collapse to Poisson; when r is diminutive the variance increases quicker compared to the µ. We used a robust strategy to select the cutoff points. First, we checked the sample mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{\mu\:}$$\end{document}​ and the variance i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{Var}$$\end{document}. If \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{Var}$$\end{document} approaches to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{\mu\:}$$\end{document}​ (with a small tolerance), a Poisson distribution is fitted; otherwise, we can estimate an NB distribution. For the NB case, we applied a method-of-moments fit for estimating the dispersion. Hence, a stable moment estimator for the size parameter could be presented as follows:28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{r}\approx\:\frac{{\widehat{\mu\:}}^{2}}{\widehat{Var}-\widehat{\mu\:}}\:$$\end{document}

Where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{r}$$\end{document}>0 is not numerically unstable. From the NB model, we can extract thresholds by inverting the CDF. For a target percentile Tq, the cutoff can be calculated as:29\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{T}_{q}={F}^{-1}\left(q\right)\:$$\end{document}

Where F− 1 is the Poisson or NB inverse-CDF. This two steps threshold modeling offers a smoother tail approximation. If the parametric fit fails, we will return to the empirical percentile (i.e., falling back the samples qth percentile), offering resilience against any misspecification.

We also calculated a fixed operational threshold (OT) which is independent of the NB/Poisson cutoffs for all EDs. The OT is defined based on the ED capacity. The OT includes converting the useable bed capacity and average patient length-of-stay (LOS) into a level of daily throughput capacity with a safety margin and then using this value to adapt a practical maximum per ED as follows:30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Capacity\left(daily\right)=\frac{Useable\:beds}{LOS}\:,\:\:OT=\alpha\:\times\:Capacity$$\end{document}

The safety factor as denoted by α, is defined to keep an operating level of 80%. The desire to operate below absolute maximum ED capacity is taken into consideration by this safety factor. For defining the LOS per ED, we used a rolling median of monthly LOS as an approximation using historical pre-test dataset. This approach is particularly applicable when daily LOS data is nonexistent. In addition to providing a benchmark indicative of the ED operational capacity, it can establish a stable threshold that mitigates daily volatility and takes seasonality into account. All in all, OT encodes the operational capacity whereas the NB/Poisson procedure provides statistically derived exceedance thresholds for the ED overcrowding.

Youden’s index and top-k application

In order to convert continuous daily predictions into binary overcrowding signals and reconcile the true positive rate (TPR) and true negative rate (FPR), we calculated the Youden’s index (with a top-k fallback if the index degenerates) using the validation set. The Youden threshold could be defined as a probability cut-off c* by maximizing the following “J(c) = TPR(c)-FPR(c)”61,62 which is derived from the ROC curve. In our context, the Youden index at the same time maximizes sensitivity and specificity, balancing omitted overcrowding incidents and false alarms. For the sake of a leak-free and fair comparison across all cutoff points we selected c* from the validation set and applied it to the test set. When the single class issue arises in the validation period for a specific threshold (for instance there is no overcrowded days for that cutoff), the Youdens index will be degenerated. Hence, we implemented a top-k falling back mechanism for labeling the highest k predicted days as positive, according to the validation overcrowding rate. This maintains decision comparability and class balance.

Calibration and decision analysis (DCA)

The final predicted ED demand will be some daily counts of ED visits, instead of binary probabilities. Therefore, the calibration concept needs to occur in the absolute count units for making threshold-based decisions about overcrowding. We examined three scenarios for the calibration of the selected models: (1) keeping the use of the raw predictions; (2) using a linear recalibration method with the ordinary least squares for scaling predictions; and (3) using an isotonic regression to fit a monotonic and piecewise-constant mapping. The preferred method is selected by minimizing the calibration error on the validation bins for each ED. Then, the best calibrator is applied on test data to avoid data leakage. These scenarios are consistent with the common practice in prediction models, where isotonic regression continues to develop as an optimal calibration method and both isotonic regression and linear scale are well-known alternatives to calibration methods in assessments63,64.

Since the objective of the decision analysis is to determine whether to act (e.g., redistributing staff members) on a particular day, we provided a net-benefit decision analytic framework that is built into decision curve analysis (DCA). In this setup, we defined a cost ratio (CR) such that the relative cost of a false positive (FP, acting in the presence of no overcrowding) vs. relative cost of a false negative (FN, not acting in the presence of overcrowding). The net benefit is expressed here simply as:31\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Net\:benefit=\frac{TP-CR\times\:FP}{N}\:$$\end{document}

where N is the number of days (i.e., in the test set) and TP and FP are the counts on the same set. This algebraic mode is similar ro the traditional DCA expression which applies the weight with respect to a decision threshold probability ρ (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\text{w}\text{e}\text{i}\text{g}\text{h}\text{t}=\frac{{{\uprho\:}}_{t}}{(1-{{\uprho\:}}_{t})}$$\end{document})65. Setting CR = weight makes both approaches comparable. We computed the net benefit for every threshold, and indicated where a model/threshold is useful for a given CR with respect to the following criteria: (1) net benefit should be greater than the baseline net benefit for treat-all (or exceeds the NB of the “all”/“none” options), and the net benefit must be a positive value.

In this work, by using a moving-block bootstrap (MBB), we Implemented the bootstrapped uncertainty for the net benefit. The MBB setup takes into consideration the temporal correlation of the ED time series. Specifically, after resampling consecutive blocks of days (with replacement) we reconstructed paired predicted/true binary outcomes for each bootstrap sample. Then, we recalculated the net benefit for the bootstrapped thresholds (CI). In this way, we created confidence intervals for the net benefits as well as other indicators, accounting for the temporal correlation structure of the time series.

In sum, by generalizing DCA to ED-count predictions, it will retain the original interpretive structure of net-benefit analysis. In this study, we framed DCA such that all thresholds correspond to a practical cutoff for the decision making. This interpretation maintains the overall axiom of the DCA, that of considering net benefits of model-based decisions compared to treat-all or treat-none approaches but in an operational rather than a clinical setting.

Results and discussions

Feature selection

In the filter phase, we used our time series version of mRMR with expanding TSCVG and used the average scores across all folds (the algorithm stages are portrayed in Fig. 5). The fsrmrmr function in MATLAB has been applied for the implementation of the mRMR algorithm. With respect to improving information while maintaining a balance of model parsimony and retaining informative variables, we adopted a clear and repeatable stopping rule of cumulative 90% mRMR scores. The Cumulative mRMR plots could also be analogous as explained-information plots in PCA. As previously highlighted, for removing temporal data leakage and verifying stability across folds, we incorporated the ranking mechanism into a rolling-origin cross-validation design (one-year initial window with 30-day step size). Hence, it can be guaranteed that all selected attributes have a unique information about the target. The scores are depicted in Fig. 8. In EDs A, B, C, D, and E, 58, 72, 64, 77, and 78 features from the original feature set (121 attributes per ED) are selected, correspondingly. Table 3A in appendix 2 presents mRMR cumulative scores as well as the orders of all features per ED.

Based on predictive likelihood and uncertainty, the VP-BMA weights show each model’s contribution such that models having low-volatility and accurate forecasts receive higher weights. The VP-BMA weights across EDs indicate that SVM models are dominant in EDs A and B, while LSTM models are dominant in EDs C, D and E. Therefore, we can conclude that VP-BMA is not only caring pointwise accuracy, but actually penalizing variance, through weights that care for models that do accurate forecasts and stable accuracy across folds. This creates a principled model-selection process and mechanism when basic performance metrics yield a tightly clustered set of candidates because VP-BMA balances statistical weights with temporal reliability and therefore, helps to discriminate among closely equivalent forecasting candidates in a time-series sensitive way.

The selection of ensemble models should take computational cost into consideration since they need to train each base learner which increases computational cost compared to choosing a single model. In cases where we have limited time and resources and the performance of ensembles is not indicates a significant improvement, by using a carefully selected subset of candidates or favoring a single high-weight model from VP-BMA, we can select the mots reliable and accurate models. Hence, we can select SVM for EDs A and B, and LSTM for EDs C, D and E.

The moving-block bootstrap (MBB) approach has been employed in calibrating prediction intervals for ED demand forecasting. The results are depicted in Fig. 11. Our MBB method of ED demand prediction maintains the residual auto temporal dependency, and acquires samples of consecutively correlated blocks with automatic length selections (through an analysis based on autocorrelation and a standard n^(1/3) heuristic). It has a constrained calibration procedure, which guarantees ≥ 95% out-of-fold coverage minimizes interval width by maximizing scaling factors and avoids data leakage in estimating confidence interval (CI). Results are depicted by Fig. 11 and showed reliable and consistent performance across all five EDs, as the validation coverages were similar to nominal 95% coverage (numbers ranged from 94.8% to 95.8%), which indicated that the bootstrapping process preserved temporal dependence of the time series. The outcomes for each ED indicate the method’s flexibility: for ED A, point accuracy was remarkable (i.e., MAPE = 9.99%, R = 0.922), validation coverage was near-nominal, but test coverage was mediocre on 87.6%, for which may the phenomenon of demand spikes occurring outside of the training residuals; EDs B and D reached test coverage of 100%, but had mediocre point accuracy because the scaling factors (i.e., 1.010 & 1.120) were quite large relative to those of EDs A or C and led to a higher scaling factor to properly broaden the forecast interval for the unexpected uncertainty in the test data; however, ED C showed nearly similar validation and test coverage (95.5% & 95.6% coverage and forecasting stability) and MAPE or R values (i.e., MAPE = 13.35% & R = 0.689). Finally, ED E was better from a convenience perspective, but nonetheless had a point forecasting error of MAPE = 52.65% and an R-value of 0.364; These results showcase that the calibration framework is methodologically sound and has good operational robustness; the smaller test-coverage that was exhibited is more likely reflective of the volatility in demand (such as an atypical surge, policy shift, or data-recording change) or absence demand spikes than of the MBB failing.

For studying cross-generalization across EDs we trained models on each ED and then tested it with other EDs dataset (Table 8). In doing so, we normalized lagged inputs and non-lagged predictors and used the same BO tuning of hyperparameters within the TSCVG folds, and an identical data partitioning for training SVM regressors for EDs A B and E and LSTM for EDs C and D (here, the models are selected based on performance metrics). It is evident from the cross-ED transfer results that there is a significant domain shift between some EDs while others generalize well. Successful transfers include those originating from ED E (SVM) and ED D (LSTM) with low NRMSEs and low MAPE scores transferring into other EDs (for example E→B NRMSE = 0.159, MAPE = 11.45%; D→B NRMSE = 0.176, MAPE = 12.47%; D→C RMSE = 14.11, NRMSE = 0.183, MAPE = 17.40%), which suggest similar temporal structure or scale between those sites. Models that were trained on A and C did not transfer well to some EDs, most notably to ED E in the case of A→E (RMSE = 159.99, NRMSE = 4.45, MAPE = 598.79%) and C→E (MAPE = 210.10%), along with B→E (MAPE = 145.50%). This indicates that ED E experiences substantially different demand distribution and variability than the sources used for training. Overall, it follows two useful outcomes: (1) external validation, or transfer, is successful when the sources and targets share similarities in time dynamics and temporal scale, and (2) when the transfer error is large the distribution has shifted due to seasonality, demand volatility, or scale differences. These cannot be addressed with simply cross training. As a result, practitioners ought to favor models that were trained on EDs that are closely related/matched.

EDs B and D depict an evaluation pathology that is motivated by both extreme sparsity and threshold sensitivity and not by a case of model failure. In ED B the Kolmogorov Smirnov test flagged a distributional difference between validation and test sets (KS p ≈ 2.27 × 10⁻²²) and in many rolling-percentiles cut-points gave no positives in the test set; which in turn invalidated AUC/ROC and bootstrap CI calculations. This tendency must not be construed as conclusive evidence of zero discriminative capacity: but the combination of (a) variation in test data structure, (b) thresholds pegged on prior-validation quantiles while the demand spike has not been occurred in the test period (though we applied NB percentile on a seasonal rolling base), and (c) sparse positive events breaks conventional binary-metric estimation. ED D presents the same working distribution shift (KS p ≈ 5.16964E-30) in which ROC is calculated in a moderate range (≈ 0.72–0.75), but the thresholds which were applied categorized virtually all test scores as negative events (the single class problem).

EDs C and E are in a middle ground. This points to some practical choices that are dependent on thresholds. ED C displays a high degree of discrimination in moderate thresholds: the patterns of ROC ranges between 0.90 and 0.92 relative to moderate percentile ranges (0.50–0.70), and performance declines at the extreme tail (0.85–0.95) where ROC is around 0.78 − 0.67; Nevertheless, for the OT variant and extreme-percentile options we created conditions where zero overcrowding had been observed or the PR-AUC value was below the 0.3 for the extreme thresholds, the top-k fallback for the extreme thresholds is activated. In ED E the ROC is gradually rising at the median, approximately 0.55, up to 0.76 at the 95th percentile as well; Operationally, ED E may be able to generate moderately good alerts (e.g., at 60–75th percentiles).

Briefly, percentiles sweep is a helpful sensitivity analysis, which demonstrates that (1) in case of consistent score distributions and prevalence (as in ED A), ranking and thresholding are trustworthy; (2) in low prevalence or test distribution bias (EDs B, D, and some points in C and E), extreme fixed-percentile thresholds may yield single-class test sets and ill-defined AUCs; and (3) moderate percentiles are always most reproducible ROC signal, whereas the tail extremum is drastically sample-sensitive and must be treated specially (e.g., top-k evaluation, or rare-event modeling).

Limitations

Despite careful design, this study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the analysis is restricted to TUMS EDs and, within some EDs (B and D), extreme event sparsity and distributional shift in the unseen data (test dataset) caused single-class issue that invalidated conventional binary metrics and forced pipeline to fallback for extreme cutoffs; these phenomena reflect limitations of sample size, data recording policy, differences in local documentation practices or system integration rather than the algorithmic failure. Second, our engineered features and preprocessing setups can encode implicit assumptions that may not generalize across all EDs. Third, the hyperparameter tuning with BO algorithm and model selection using the variance-penalized-BMA modeling (VP-BMA) improved fairness in hyperparameter comparison and model selection but at an extra computational cost especially for deep learning models, highlighting the extra computational resources needed for EDs to practically use the models. Finally, the study is retrospective: operational thresholds and decision matrix all were derived from historical distributions and require prospective validation, continuous recalibration, and local governance before deployment.

Conclusion

Taken together, by deliberately varying key pre-assumptions and failure modes the proposed pipeline demonstrates practical robustness when the data-generating regime is reasonably stable. The approach to methodology included two-stage feature selection (mRMR → elastic net), BO tuning, TSCVG, MBB CIs, threshold sensitivity analysis, VP-BMA model selection, and decision analysis; for having performance estimating independent of a single option but rather represented both accuracy and reproducible uncertainty. This approach yielded strong results for overcrowding prediction in ED A (ROC ≈ 0.95–0.98), a defensible regime for ED C at moderate thresholds, while EDs B and D showed data sparsity, and ED E indicated mid-threshold utility.

From the findings, we suggest ED-specific thresholding (preferably moderate percentiles if the historical data is used), top-k and rare-event models for tail alerts, and prospectively recalibrating with continuous monitoring. For future applications, we recommend an expansion in site coverage, increased reporting of rare-events or use hierarchical pooling.
