Universal crystallization kinetics of solution-processed semiconductors
Authors: Zhitian Ling, Mateusz Brzezinski, Shuanglong Wang, Konstantinos Bidinakis, Lei Gao, Chongyao Li, Xin Zhou, Hai I. Wang, Stefan A. L. Weber, Paul W. M. Blom, Wojciech Pisula, Tomasz Marszalek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heterogeneous crystallization kinetics critically govern the morphology and performance of solution-processed semiconductors, yet morphology control is often achieved through trial-and-error optimization. Quantitative understanding of crystallization is essential for precise morphology control but is limited by the simultaneous occurrence of heterogeneous nucleation and crystal growth. Here, a time-defined induced nucleation strategy has been developed to initiate crystallization at specific stages of solution processing. Systematic variation of nucleation timing reveals three distinct morphological regimes, whose emergence is explained by the concentration-dependent critical cluster radius in bulk, interfacial, and cusp environments. On this basis, a quantitative, dimensionless model is developed for heterogeneous crystallization kinetics across diverse processing conditions. The model demonstrates universality across perovskite and organic semiconductors. In situ studies reveal identical growth kinetics for induced and spontaneous nuclei, validating its generality. The resulting morphology control enables tuning of crystallinity and charge transport in solution-processed transistors.

Introduction

Solution-processed semiconductors have been widely exploited for scalable, low-cost, and flexible electronic devices, including solar cells, light-emitting diodes, and field-effect transistors (FETs)1–3. The morphology of the semiconductor film, particularly the size, orientation, and distribution of crystalline domains, plays a decisive role in determining charge transport and overall device performance4,5. While empirical strategies have enabled partial control over film morphology, the underlying crystallization kinetics remain not fully understood. A predictive and quantitative description of the collective impact of nucleation and growth on the microstructure is therefore critical for both advancing material functionality and deepening fundamental insight into thin-film formation.

Among the various physical processes that contribute to thin-film growth, heterogeneous nucleation plays an outsized role that governs the spatial and temporal onset of the crystallization, directly influencing size, density, and orientation of domains6,7. Unlike homogeneous nucleation, which occurs uniformly, heterogeneous nucleation is triggered by local perturbations or interfaces, turning it highly sensitive to processing conditions and consequently challenging to predict8–10. While many studies have attempted to control crystallization for improving the film quality and device performance through solvent, additive or processing engineering, the individual contributions of nucleation and growth are not taken into account11–13. Common models offer valuable insights into the overall crystallization, but fall short in resolving crystallization of individual domains to describe domain-specific growth kinetics under heterogeneous conditions. For instance, the Lamer model explains the homogeneous nucleation, but lacks the spatial and temporal resolution needed to accurately describe heterogeneous nucleation events14–16. The Avrami model captures the overall crystallization kinetics, but averages out domain-to-domain variability17–19. Models related to the late stage of the crystallization process like Ostwald ripening focus on grain coarsening and are not applicable to early-stage thin-film formation20–22. The main challenge in describing the crystallization of individual domains is the occurrence of parallel existing subprocesses: nucleation and growth of different domains occur in overlapping and unpredictable timeframes23,24. Consequently, systematically decoupling the nucleation time from the growth stage via experimental strategies is crucial for establishing a comprehensive model capable of quantitatively describing the heterogeneous crystallization, which is essential for both mechanistic insight and control over the film morphology25–28.

To address these challenges, we have established a time-defined induced nucleation (TDIN) strategy that enables deterministic control over the heterogeneous nucleation and allows a separate analysis of the nucleation and growth mechanisms. In this approach, artificial nucleation sites are mechanically induced into the film after defined spin-coating times, followed by thermal annealing to continue the subsequent crystal growth. The systematic variation of the coating time exposes three well-defined morphological regimes: large domains (LDs), unidirectional domains (UDs), and small domains (SDs), each emerging within a distinct spin-coating time. The transitions between these regimes occur as short as a few seconds, which is mechanistically understood based on the classical nucleation theory (CNT). To quantitatively describe the growth, we construct a piecewise model based on the mass transfer theory, capturing concentration changes during the coating and annealing stages. The model reliably describes the domain evolution for diverse materials (e.g., 2D/3D perovskites, organics, and salts), independent of specific processing conditions, and accurately predicts crystallization trajectories for both induced and spontaneous nucleation events. Based on the understanding of the crystallization mechanism, film growth controlled by induced nucleation exhibits nearly one order of magnitude higher charge carrier mobilities in field-effect transistors than the spontaneously crystallized ones. This result emphasizes the critical correlation between precise crystallization control and device functionality. While classical crystallization theories describe an ensemble-averaged behavior, the present work establishes a model to resolve and control the evolution of individual domains under heterogeneous conditions. The developed model quantitatively describing the crystallization process of solution processed semiconductors into different film morphologies provides a fundamental basis to control and optimize the film processing for specific electronic applications.

Results and discussion

In most solution-processed materials, nucleation remains a stochastic event, governed by complex and poorly defined interfacial and concentration conditions. To address this challenge, we have developed a TDIN strategy, which is based on induced heterogeneous nucleation at defined timepoints during solution processing, effectively decoupling nucleation from subsequent crystal growth (Fig. 1a). In this approach, a mechanical scratch is applied to the spin-coated film after a specific spin-coating time to artificially induce nuclei, followed by thermal annealing to initiate crystal growth. The exact moment that the nucleation is induced, termed the induced nucleation time (ts0), determines the crystallization pathway and final film morphology. Early-stage nucleation yields large domains (LD), intermediate nucleation generates unidirectional domains (UD), and late-stage nucleation results in small domains (SD). To understand the mechanistic basis of the TDIN strategy, we first discuss the formation of clusters that act as potential nucleation sites in a wet film during this event. The nucleation sites can be located in the bulk (homogeneous nucleation), the liquid/air or liquid/substrate interface (heterogeneous nucleation), where nucleation may preferentially occur depending on the material system, and the cusp-like regions (induced nucleation), illustrated in Fig. 1b. In classical nucleation theory (CNT), the change in Gibbs free energy (ΔG) for the cluster formation consists of two competing terms (Equation S51) graphically presented in Fig. 1c: a positive surface term (ΔGS) scaling with the square of the radius (r), and a negative volume term (ΔGV) scaling with the cube of r. A spherical approximation of the cluster is used for conceptual and analytical simplicity29,30. Although the actual crystal growth may deviate from an ideal spherical geometry, the key conclusions of the model rely on the relative evolution of the nucleation barrier and critical radius rather than the exact nucleus geometry. At very small cluster sizes, the ΔGS dominates, resulting in an increase in the overall ΔG. As the cluster grows, the ΔGV governs, lowering the total ΔG. The transition point, the critical radius (r*), marks the smallest cluster size at which further growth becomes energetically favorable, such that clusters can grow further to a site enabling nucleation. Compared to the bulk (solid line), clusters at the interface (dotted line) bear a reduced ΔGS, lowering ΔG and finally reducing the barrier to form larger clusters with subsequent nucleation. Clusters at the cusp (dashed line) show the lowest barrier due to the largest interfacial contact, turning nucleation most favorable in this region.

To understand the nucleation kinetics, it is important to realize that both the cluster size (r) and critical radius r* are related to the concentration (C) of the solute in the solution in a reciprocal manner, as shown in Fig. 1d. The critical radius decreases with concentration, as derived from Supplementary Fig. 1a and explained by Equation S52. The analysis in Fig. 1d provides three curves: bulk (red solid), interface (red dotted), and cusp (red dashed), each representing a different surface energy (ΔGS). In contrast, r in solution (blue curve) increases with C and intersects the three curves at key concentrations: critical concentration for mechanically induced nucleation (Ci), concentration for the spontaneous interfacial nucleation (Cn), and critical concentration for spontaneous bulk nucleation (Css). According to CNT, nucleation begins only when r exceeds r*. These thresholds directly map to the morphological regimes LD, UD, and SD in Fig. 1a, as discussed below.

The relation between C and ts0 in Fig. 1e follows a LaMer-like trend (detailed explanation is given by the description of Supplementary Fig. 1b). The concentration first increases during solvent evaporation, followed by a decrease as nucleation and growth consume solute, eventually returning to the solubility (Cs) limit31,32. Mapping Ci and Css from Fig. 1d into this curve divides the TDIN process into three time regimes, each with a distinct film morphology upon induced nucleation.

(1) Blue regime (C < Ci, short ts0)

At short times, not much solvent has evaporated yet, and the concentration of solute in the wet film is still low. In this regime, r (blue curve in Fig. 1d) is smaller than r* at the cusp (red dashed curve), and therefore nucleation cannot be mechanically induced. The formed small clusters of solutes at this low concentration will just disassemble back into the solution. During subsequent annealing, C gradually increases, and spontaneous interfacial nucleation is initiated only when r exceeds r* at the interface (red dotted curve at Cn). By this time, the scratch tip has left the wet film, and the continuous cusp line is no longer active for crystal growth. Consequently, the LD morphology is formed via delayed spontaneous nucleation.

(2) Red regime (Ci ≤ C <Css, intermediate ts0)

In this regime the concentration has already increased due to evaporation and r exceeds r* at the cusp, but remains below r* in the bulk (red solid curve). Scratching creates a continuous nucleation front promoting UD growth until C reaches Css at which bulk nucleation commences. LDs emerge later, once C reaches Cn, since ΔGS at the cusp is lower than at the air/liquid or liquid/substrate interface inducing an earlier UD nucleation as visualized in Supplementary Movie 1.

(3) Yellow regime (C ≥ Css, long ts0)

In this high concentration regime, r exceeds r* already in the bulk (red solid curve) resulting in the formation of numerous spontaneous bulk nuclei before nucleation is mechanically induced, so that the entire film crystallizes during spin-coating into a SD morphology identical to films without scratching.

We can conclude that by controlling the supersaturation via adjusting ts0 it is possible to effectively control the three film morphologies by TDIN, as summarized in Fig. 1a. Importantly, nucleation and growth are not intrinsically tied to specific processing stages, i.e., spin-coating or annealing, but are governed by the evolution of solute concentration. Depending on the supersaturation trajectory, nucleation and subsequent growth may occur during spin-coating, annealing, or both. The three regimes described above represent distinct concentration-dependent crystallization pathways rather than stage-specific processes. The different growth modes are explained by the concentration dependent r* trends for the nucleation in bulk, at interface, and at cusp, offering a mechanistic basis for the prediction of the film morphology. Importantly, the TDIN model does not exclude spontaneous nucleation, but instead exploits the temporal and spatial separation between induced and spontaneous nucleation pathways to control the crystallization behavior.

To verify the mechanistic framework established above, we performed TDIN experiments on PEA-based 2D perovskite films ((PEA)2SnI4) as model system, systematically varying the spin-coating time for the induced nucleation before annealing (Fig. 1f). The ts0 was adjusted from 34 s to 52 s for a 0.1 M precursor solution spin-coated at 2000 rpm. Mechanically inducing nucleation below 36 s does not lead to an interfacial nucleation event resulting in LD morphologies. At intermediate ts0 between 38 s and 48 s, the induced nucleation generates a continuous growth front yielding the UD morphology. When the induced nucleation was delayed beyond 50 s, the SD morphology is identical to the spontaneously nucleated film, indicating that bulk nucleation and film crystallization are fully presented during spin-coating. These experimentally defined time windows of TDIN correspond to the three morphological regimes introduced earlier.

Beyond (PEA)2SnI4, the TDIN strategy was further applied to other functional materials, including the 2D/3D perovskite (MeOPEA)1.8Cs0.1SnI3.9, 3D perovskite MAPbI3, organic semiconductor TIPS-pentacene, and inorganic salt NaH2PO4 (Fig. 1g). Scanning electron microscopy images further confirm that the distinct LD, UD, and SD morphologies are preserved at the microscale, in agreement with the macroscopic optical images (Supplementary Fig 2–6). We note that the absolute domain sizes associated with the LD, UD, and SD regimes can vary between materials while still preserving the characteristic morphology evolution. In all cases, the same LD-UD-SD morphological sequence is observed by varying ts0, demonstrating the universality of this approach across distinct crystalline materials. Detailed results of the morphology evaluation of each material upon induced nucleation are provided in the SI (Supplementary Fig. 7). In these datasets, LD domains are randomly distributed in both position and size, reflecting the stochastic nature of their nucleation events. This irregularity makes it challenging to isolate this domain growth for quantitative analysis. In contrast, UD domains exhibit a clear, systematic reduction in size with increasing ts0, which is a direct consequence of delaying the nucleation onset. This well-defined temporal evolution not only enables robust modeling of the crystal growth, but also serves as a controllable analog for the otherwise unpredictable timing of the spontaneous LD nucleation.

To quantitatively explain the systematic decrease in UD size with ts0, the relevant growth kinetics following nucleation must be considered. When nuclei are mechanically induced, crystallization starts due to mass transport resulting from the concentration difference between the wet film and the nuclei surface, as illustrated in Fig. 2a. The concentration at the surface of the nuclei is fixed at Cs, whereas the concentration in the wet film increases with the spin-coating and annealing times, defined as C(t). Once the solute reaches the nuclei surface, crystal growth proceeds and the UDs grow perpendicular to the induced nucleation line. The consistent agreement between experiment and model across distinct processing conditions and materials indicates that UD growth follows a universal kinetic mechanism. More importantly, the ability to define the concentration-dependent nucleation window provides a practical model for controlling the crystallization pathways and improving film uniformity in solution-processed semiconductor films, particularly for large-area processing.

To further examine the generality of the proposed model under more complex processing conditions, we extended the analysis to a two-step spin-coating process, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 12. Despite the staged spin-coating protocol, the crystallization behavior exhibits a similar morphological evolution from LD to UD and finally to SD, consistent with the one-step process. Notably, the onset of the UD formation occurs at an earlier time, accompanied by a compressed processing window Δts for the UD regime compared to the one-step process (Supplementary Fig. 9). This behavior is attributed to the increased solvent evaporation rate during the second high-speed spinning step, which accelerates the concentration increase in the wet film. As a result, the wet film reaches Ci earlier, and the transition from Ci to Css proceeds within a shorter time interval. These observations are fully consistent with the model, independent of the specific spin-coating protocol. This result further highlights the robustness of the proposed crystallization kinetics and its applicability to more realistic multi-step processing conditions.

As a summary, the proposed model consists of two key components: (i) a nucleation model based on CNT, which defines the critical concentrations (Ci and Css) governing the onset of induced and spontaneous nucleation, and (ii) a diffusion-controlled growth model that quantitatively describes domain evolution after nucleation. The applicability of this framework relies on systems where the concentration evolution is governed by solvent evaporation and crystal growth is limited by mass transport. Under these conditions, the crystallization pathway can be described by the parameter pair of R and SSi, which links nucleation and growth across different processing conditions.

Despite the different C0 values at the three nucleation times, the experimentally monitored growth closely follows the theoretical curves provided by the model. Additionally, to the previously discussed projection of the growth initiated at the onset of the growth window, this agreement confirms that the model quantitatively also describes the UD crystallization even when the nucleation occurs at arbitrary times and processing conditions within the operation window. Importantly, this analytical capability enables rational design of the processing conditions of an optimized film crystallization for various applications. By choosing a desired induced nucleation time, it is possible to anticipate the complete domain growth and thereby tailor the final film morphology to meet process and device constraints.

To assess whether the model also applies to spontaneously nucleated domains, the growth of LDs was investigated and compared to UD growth under identical conditions. As also shown in Fig. 3d, two LDs, marked as LD1 and LD2 and highlighted by red and blue boxes, successively nucleate at different times. LD1 appears simultaneously with the UD, while LD2 emerges 4.7 s later. The growth trajectories of both LDs were extracted from Supplementary Movie 4 and plotted in Supplementary Fig 16b. To enable direct comparison with the UD growth, the size of both LDs was defined as half of the diagonal length of the square domain. Using this metric, the growth of LD1 coincides with the UD during the same growth time, while LD2, once nucleated after 4.7 s, and also evolves along the same growth profile as the UD as shown in Fig. 3g. This confirms that each nucleus, once formed, grows according to the same universal, diffusion-controlled mechanism, regardless of whether nucleation is induced (for UD) or spontaneous (for LD).

Since these nucleation events occur at different annealing times, the local concentration at the moment of nucleation varies for each domain. As a result, although governed by the same growth law, each domain exhibits a distinct crystallization kinetics determined by the nucleation time. Notably, earlier nucleated LDs such as LD1 initially grow at reduced growth rate due to lower supersaturation, while later ones, like LD2, experience faster initial growth, as demonstrated in Supplementary Fig 16b, under steeper concentration gradient between wet film (C) and surface of the cluster (Cs). Thus, each domain crystallizes under a distinct driving force, leading to domain-specific growth kinetics that may affect spatial uniformity and overall film quality. These insights reinforce the significance of the model since it captures both the intrinsic kinetics shared by all domains and the extrinsic impact introduced by different nucleation times. Consequently, the model offers a unified framework for understanding and controlling crystallization in solution-processed films.

After systematically and quantitatively understanding the crystallization kinetics, the impact on the film quality was further studied for (PEA)2SnI4 as an illustrative model material. The three distinct morphologies were obtained from a solution with Cinit of 0.06 M by TDIN setting ts0 at 32 s for LD, 34 s for UD and 42 s for SD. To investigate the influence of distinct crystallization pathways on the resulting structural and electronic properties, representative LD, UD, and SD regions of the films were selectively characterized. Grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) was exploited to evaluate the crystal structure, and the results are shown in Fig. 4a. The highest out-of-plane intensity is attributed to the interlayer (002) reflection corresponding to a d-spacing of 16.3 Å that is in agreement with literature37,38. The higher number of the (00 l) reflections for the UD morphology indicates long-range crystals (l = 2, 4, 6, 8) in comparison to the LD film with large crystals (l = 2, 4, 6) and SD morphology with small crystals (l = 2, 4). The larger number of higher-order reflections indicates a well-ordered structure of the UD film among the three samples39,40. The out-of-plane (002) interlayer diffraction peak was further analyzed to determine the crystallinity. The coherence length (CL) increases from 373 Å for SD, to 414 Å and 421 Å for LD and UD, respectively. The larger CL for the UD film is associated with its higher crystallinity, confirming higher molecular order along the out-of-plane direction. To further confirm the film quality along the in-plane direction, optical and atomic force microscopy images in Supplementary Fig. 18 are analyzed. SD films consist of 2 µm domains in size surrounded by pinholes resulting in a root mean square roughness (RMS) of 9.6 nm. The LD films exhibit a larger domain size of around 300 µm and a lower RMS of 6.7 nm. The UD films reveal a pinhole-free surface and the lowest RMS of 1.9 nm among the studied series. Potential maps and corresponding profiles of a transistor channel for the three film morphologies were recorded by Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) as shown in Fig. 4b. The mapping of the local potential allows to evaluate the influence of the film morphology on the electrical properties. A configuration with biased electrodes was used to simulate operating conditions above the threshold voltage of the FET (Supplementary Fig. 19). For the SD film, the potential distribution shows intermittent voltage drops with strong fluctuations at the domain boundaries. These boundaries act as structural defects and trapping sites for carriers, thereby impeding transport and resulting in low effective charge carrier mobility41–43. A random orientation of long domain boundaries in the LD film creates irregular potential maps and resistive barriers across the channel, causing significant voltage drops despite relatively high local charge carrier mobility within each domain, as discussed below. In contrast, the UD film exhibits a nearly linear potential decline with only minor fluctuations, reflecting the reduced density of boundaries and indicating more efficient carrier transport, which underpins their superior device performance.

To assess these structural and electronic features on a macroscopic scale, the FET performance of the films was subsequently evaluated. The spatially differentiated morphologies enable a direct comparison between distinct crystallization pathways and their corresponding electronic properties. The device geometry with the three film morphologies is shown in Supplementary Fig. 20. The transfer characteristics were recorded at room temperature at drain-source voltages (Vds) of −60 V with gate-source voltage (Vg) scans in forward and backward directions. As shown in Fig. 4c, the devices based on UDs exhibit the highest FET mobility (μFET) of 2.6 cm2 V−1 s−1 which is significantly improved compared to LDs with 0.6 cm2 V−1 s−1 and SDs with 0.2 cm2 V−1 s−1, as calculated from the transfer curves in the forward direction. For comparison, the local charge carrier mobility determined by optical pump-terahertz probe spectroscopy measurements (μOPTP) is shown in Supplementary Fig. 21. The μOPTP of 8.9 and 8.4 cm2 V−1 s−1 for LD and UD films, respectively, are nearly identical. In contrast, the μOPTP for SD films decreases to 6.3 cm2 V−1 s−1. While the macroscopic μFET and local μOPTP charge carrier mobilities are on the same order of magnitude for the UD morphology, the device mobility for LDs and especially for SDs is decreased by more than one order of magnitude due to the reduced order and higher density of domain boundaries, as proven by the GIWAXS, AFM and KPFM results. Furthermore, transient absorption measurements in Supplementary Fig. 22 show comparable carrier relaxation dynamics among LD, UD, and SD films, suggesting that the excited-state carrier dynamics within the crystalline domains are broadly similar across the three morphologies. Additional time-resolved photoluminescence measurements in Supplementary Fig. 23 reveal similar decay dynamics for the LD and UD films, whereas the SD film exhibits a faster decay, indicating enhanced defect-assisted non-radiative recombination in the SD film. This observation is consistent with the photoconductivity dynamics shown in Supplementary Fig. 21, suggesting that the superior FET performance mainly arises from continuous transport pathways along the aligned domains with reduced density of boundaries rather than lower defect density. The FETs based on the UD morphology also show the highest channel current and on/off ratio (Ion/off) of 5 × 105, the smallest dual-sweep hysteresis (ΔV) of 10.2 V (at |Ids | = 10−6 A in both sweep directions), the lowest threshold voltage (VTH) of 6.1 V, and the lowest subthreshold swing (SS) of 5.0 V dec−1, indicating improved device performance compared to the two other film morphologies as summarized in Table S2. The enhanced device performance of the UD morphology is attributed to its anisotropic domain growth and continuous charge-transport pathways, which reduce domain-boundary as trapping sites across the device channel. The output characteristics for the corresponding devices with various morphologies are plotted in Supplementary Fig. 24a revealing the highest gate modulation of the current for the UD perovskite FET. Bias-stability measurements in Supplementary Fig. 25 further reveal that UD devices exhibit the slowest current degradation under continuous gate and drain bias stress, followed by LD and SD devices, indicating improved operational stability associated with the reduced domain boundary density in UD films.

To evaluate the generality of the three types of film morphologies beyond the model (PEA)₂SnI4 system, two chemically distinct semiconductors were additionally examined consisting of (MeOPEA)1.8Cs0.1SnI3.9 (Fig. 4d), and TIPS-pentacene (Fig. 4e). Although the specific spin-coating protocols and processing windows of TDIN differ between these semiconductors (details provided in the experimental section of SI), the characteristic morphological progression was consistently reproduced through fine-tuning of the nucleation time as shown in Figures S10 and S11. Both semiconductors show an increase in μFET for films with a larger domain size. For (MeOPEA)1.8Cs0.1SnI3.9, the mobility improves from 0.3 cm2 V−1 s−1 (SD) to 0.8 cm2 V−1 s−1 (LD), reaching 2.8 cm2 V−1 s−1 (UD). For TIPS-pentacene, despite intrinsically lower transport, μFET rises from 0.2 cm2 V−1 s−1 to 0.5 cm2 V−1 s−1 and finally to 0.7 cm2 V−1 s−1 across the same morphological sequence. Device parameters are summarized in Tables S3 and S4, with corresponding output characteristics shown in Figs. S24b and S24c. The statistical analysis of multiple devices further confirms the improved and reproducible performance of UD-based FETs compared to LD and SD devices. The overall trends are consistent with those obtained in the case of (PEA)2SnI4. The recurrence of this transport behavior across chemically and structurally different semiconductors underscores the universality of the crystallization mechanism and of the impact on the electronic properties.

In conclusion, by integrating a TDIN strategy, stochastic nucleation becomes programmable, enabling direct control of the film morphology. The emergence of distinct morphological regimes is rationalized by concentration-dependent critical cluster radii in different environments. Based on this strategy, we establish a universal model that captures heterogeneous crystallization kinetics across materials and processing conditions. The model is broadly applicable, as validated for perovskite and organic semiconductors which exhibit distinct morphological regimes and crystallization behaviors. In-situ monitoring confirms the predictive accuracy of the model, showing that all nuclei, whether induced or spontaneous, grow via the same diffusion-controlled mechanism despite distinct domain-specific driving forces. The developed model offers a general strategy for understanding and controlling crystallization kinetics and defect formation relevant for both lateral and vertical device architectures. Since grain boundaries and defect distribution influence charge transport and recombination in both lateral and vertical devices, this universal strategy bears a broad potential for precise tailoring and elevating the performance of solution-processed optoelectronic devices beyond FET architectures.

Methods

Materials

All chemical reagents and solvents were used without further purification. Lead (II) iodide (PbI2, 99.99%), tin (II) iodide (SnI2, 99.999%), methylammonium iodide (MAI), cesium iodide (CsI), 6,13-bis(triisopropylsilylethynyl)pentacene (TIPS-pentacene), sodium dihydrogen phosphate (NaH2PO4), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, anhydrous, 99.8%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, anhydrous, ≥99.9%) toluene (anhydrous, ≥99.9%), chlorobenzene (CB, anhydrous, ≥99.9%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Tetrahydronaphthalin (Tetralin, anhydrous, ≥99.9%) was purchased from TCI chemical. Silicon wafers and ultraflat glasses were purchased from Ossila. 2-(2-Methoxyphenethyl)ammonium iodide (MeOPEAI) was purchased from Greatcellsolar Materials. Phenethylammonium iodide (PEAI) was purchased from Xi’an Polymer Baolaite Photoelectric Technology Corporation.

Substrate preparation

Heavily p-doped bare Si/SiO2 wafers (1.5 × 2 cm) and ultraflat glass were used as substrates and cleaned in an ultrasonic bath by deionized water, acetone, and isopropyl alcohol and in the next step, blown dry with N2. The substrates were treated by oxygen plasma for 20 min to improve the hydrophilicity before used.

Thin film preparation for TDIN

2D (PEA)2SnI4 perovskite film

The precursor solutions were prepared in a stoichiometric ratio of PEAI and SnI2 2:1 by dissolving PEAI and SnI2 in mixed DMF/DMSO solvent (1:1). The solution of defined concentrations (varied from 0.06 M to 0.12 M) was stirred overnight at 40 °C in a glove box and cooled to room temperature before deposition. The as-prepared fresh precursor solution was spin-coated at 2000 rpm with the defined times (varied from 32–52 s) on the glass for the morphology characterization or the silicon wafer for the device measurement, and annealed at two different temperatures, first at 60 °C around 10 min for crystallization followed by 100 °C for 10 min to remove the residual solvent.

2D/3D (MeOPEA)1.8Cs0.1SnI3.9 perovskite film

The precursor solutions were prepared in a stoichiometric ratio of MeOPEAI, CsI and SnI2 1.8:0.1:1 by dissolving them in mixed DMF/DMSO solvent (1:1). The 0.1 M solution was stirred overnight at 40 °C in a glove box and cooled to room temperature before deposition. The as-prepared fresh precursor solution was spin-coated at 2000 rpm with the defined times (varied from 50–64 s) on the glass for the morphology characterization or the silicon wafer for the device measurement, and annealed at two different temperatures, first at 60 °C around 10 min for crystallization followed by 100 °C for 10 min to remove the residual solvent.

3D MAPbI3 perovskite film

The precursor solutions were prepared in a stoichiometric ratio of MAI and PbI2 2:1 by dissolving them in mixed DMF/DMSO solvent (1:1). The 0.25 M solution was stirred overnight at 40 °C in a glove box and cooled to room temperature before deposition. The as-prepared fresh precursor solution was spin-coated at 2000 rpm with the time varied from 36–54 s on the glass substrate and annealed at 100 °C for 5 min.

Organic TIPS-pentacene semiconductor film

The precursor solutions were prepared by dissolving TIPS-pentacene in mixed solvent (45% toluene + 45% CB + 10% tetralin). The 10 mg/ml solution was stirred overnight at 60 °C in a glove box and cooled to room temperature before deposition. The as-prepared fresh precursor solution was spin-coated at defined speeds (varied from 2000 rpm to 2500 rpm) with defined times (from 3 s to 14 s) on the silicon wafer and annealed at 100 °C for 10 min.

Inorganic NaH2PO4 salt film

The precursor solutions were prepared by dissolving NaH2PO4 in deionized water at the concentration of 600 mg/ml. The as-prepared fresh precursor solution was spin-coated at 2000 rpm on the glass substrate and annealed at 100 °C for 10 min in air.

Bottom-gate top-contact configuration was employed for the FET devices. The 300 nm SiO2 layer of the silicon wafers was adopted as a dielectric layer. After film preparation, the source and drain electrodes were deposited with a thickness of 50 nm by gold thermal evaporation through a shadow mask (from Ossila), yielding transistor channels with dimensions of 80 × 1000 μm (length × width) for perovskite devices and 30 × 1000 μm for TIPS-pentacene devices. Film characterization

Thin film morphology was characterized using a Leica POM and a Bruker Dimension Icon FS AFM in tapping mode at room temperature. GIWAXS measurements of (PEA)2SnI4 perovskite films were performed at the TU Dortmund DELTA Synchrotron. The beam size was 1.0 mm × 0.2 mm (width × height), and samples were irradiated just below the critical angle for total reflection with respect to the incoming X-ray beam (∼0.1°). For the film with unidirectional domains, the X-ray beam was positioned to overlap with the UD region, ensuring that the scattering signal is dominated by the aligned domains. The scattering intensity was detected on a two-dimensional image plate (MAR-345) with a pixel size of 150 μm (2300 × 2300 pixels) and the detector was placed 523 mm from the sample center. All X-ray scattering measurements were performed under vacuum (∼1 mbar) to reduce air scattering and beam damage to the sample. The interlayer distance was calculated from Bragg’s law: 2dsin θ = nλ, where θ = 5.46° and nn = 2, respectively46.

Movie recordings

Perovskite crystal growth was recorded in the glovebox using a WADEO microscope camera as MPEG-4/MOV (H.264, 8-bit RGB) movie with a resolution of 1920 × 1440 pixels and a frame rate of 30 fps. Movies were quantified using digital image processing techniques implemented in Python (OpenCV and NumPy-based workflow). Initially, movie footage of the crystallization process was used to isolate individual frames, which were converted to grayscale. To enhance the visibility and clarity of crystals within the frames, manual contrast and brightness adjustments were applied (linear rescaling of pixel intensities by a user-defined contrast factor and brightness offset), improving the contrast between the growing crystals and the background and enabling more robust boundary detection.

A region of interest (ROI) was then manually selected (user-defined coordinates) on the adjusted images to focus analysis on the specific area where crystal growth occurred. Within this ROI, intensity-based binary thresholding was applied using user-tuned threshold levels so that pixels corresponding to crystal features were separated from the background, generating binary images with crystal regions represented as foreground and the background suppressed. Continuous curves tracing crystal boundaries (contours) were then identified from the binary images using OpenCV contour detection. To minimize noise, very small detections were removed using a minimum feature-size (area) criterion, ensuring that only features consistent with crystal growth were retained.

For each remaining contour, a thickness metric was extracted and stored for subsequent analysis. To obtain spatially resolved growth information, the manually selected ROI was segmented into multiple smaller sub-regions (uniform slices across the ROI height), and the thresholding–contour detection–thickness calculation steps were repeated independently for each segment. Within each segment, the most representative feature was retained (the thickest detected line), reducing sensitivity to spurious edges and fragmented detections.

The resulting thickness values were further screened to prevent non-physical outliers from biasing statistics by excluding measurements that were much smaller or much larger than the median thickness within a frame (median-based outlier rejection using user-defined lower/upper multiplicative cutoffs). After filtering, for each frame and each ROI selection, summary statistics were computed, including the mean thickness and the standard deviation across segments (with the corresponding sample size).

Finally, the same movie was analyzed multiple times using different manually selected ROIs, and for each ROI the processing parameters (ROI placement, segmentation granularity, contrast/brightness adjustment, threshold levels, and outlier cutoffs) were fine-tuned to maximize signal-to-noise and provide the most stable quantification for that region. For presentation across repeated ROI selections, the reported central value at each time point corresponds to the mean of the per-ROI frame means. Error bars represent the standard error of the mean across ROI selections, calculated as the standard deviation of the per-ROI frame means divided by the number of ROI selections.

Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM)

The contact potential difference (CPD) between the tip and sample for the perovskite films of FETs was measured with KPFM, using an Asylum Research MFP3D microscope from Oxford Instruments and a lock-in amplifier (HF2LI-MOD by Zürich Instruments). Since perovskites deteriorate under ambient conditions, the experiment was carried out in an argon glovebox. The bias voltages to the source and drain electrodes were provided by the auxiliary output of the lock-in amplifier, whereas the gate voltage was provided by an external battery. The cantilevers were conductively coated Pt-Ir (SCM-PIT-V2 by Bruker) with first eigenmode ω0 of ∼75 kHz and a spring constant of ∼3 N/m. The experiment was conducted with a scan rate of 0.2 Hz. For heterodyne KPFM, we excited the cantilever mechanically at ω0 and electrically at ω1-ω0, where ω1 is the second oscillating eigenmode of the cantilever. Then, by frequency mixing of the mechanical and the electrical oscillations of the cantilever, we generated a signal that coincided with the second resonance frequency and was therefore enhanced and used as input for the KPFM loop. In order to minimize the effect of stray electrostatic fields and topographical anomalies on the KPFM signal, KPFM was used in the heterodyne frequency-modulation variation47. After locating an area of the perovskite film between the source and drain electrodes with the microscope camera, the cantilever was positioned, and the KPFM experiment was conducted. In order to perform the experiments with an activated conductive channel, the drain and gate were biased at −9 V and −44 V, respectively, with respect to the grounded source to ensure an open channel according to the transfer and output characteristics.

Transient absorption spectroscopy

Femtosecond transient absorption (fs-TA) spectra in the visible range were obtained using a Helios pump–probe setup (Ultrafast Systems) driven by an amplified 1030 nm laser system (Pharos, Light Conversion, 200 fs, 200 μJ, 1 kHz). The fundamental laser output was converted to 400 nm using an optical parametric amplifier (Orpheus-F, Light Conversion) and employed as the excitation pump. The visible broadband probe light was generated by directing a portion of the fundamental laser through an optical delay stage and focusing it onto a sapphire crystal. The 400 nm pump and broadband probe beams were spatially overlapped and focused onto the perovskite film samples. The transmitted probe light was collected and analyzed as a function of wavelength and pump–probe delay time.

Time-resolved photoluminescence

TRPL was measured by a 4Piocs gated-iCCD camera (Stanford Computer Optics) with a Ti-sapphire laser (Coherent, Astrella, 5 mJ, 800 nm, 35 fs, 1 kHz). The excitation pulses at 400 nm were generated using an optical parametric amplifier (Orpheus, Light Conversion). The transient PL decays of the perovskite films were recorded with a low excitation power of 5 μW at a repetition rate of 1 kHz.
