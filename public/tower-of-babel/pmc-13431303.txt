Novel endoscopic posterior fossa cyst fenestration via an occipital meningocele defect in neonatal Dandy-Walker malformation: illustrative case
Authors: Noel F. Manalil, Hanish Polavarapu, Christine E. Fuller, Kathleen E. Knudson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDDandy-Walker malformation (DWM) is characterized by cerebellar vermis hypoplasia, absence of the lateral apertures (of Luschka) and the median aperture (of Magendie), cystic dilation of the fourth ventricle, and posterior fossa enlargement. Occipital meningoceles (OMCs) involve an occipital skull defect with herniation of the meninges and CSF. Fewer than 40 cases of concurrent OMC and DWM (DWM-OMC) have been reported in the literature, with none managed with transmeningocele endoscopic fenestration.OBSERVATIONSA preterm female neonate, delivered via cesarean section, presented with an OMC. MRI revealed DWM with a concurrent OMC and hydrocephalus. Given that the OMC was not covered by skin and the presence of hydrocephalus, endoscopic cyst fenestration of the DWM-associated dilated fourth ventricle through the OMC defect into the posterior spinal cord CSF space was performed. Shunt placement was avoided given concern for infection from exposed dura.LESSONSFollowing surgery, the infant demonstrated normal neurological and developmental function. Follow-up MRI demonstrated an interval decrease in the size of the posterior fossa cyst and hydrocephalus, without complications. Endoscopic fenestration is a feasible option that facilitates CSF diversion despite distorted posterior fossa anatomy. Longer follow-up is required to determine durability and if this can be applied to other similar patients. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE26259

Dandy-Walker malformation (DWM) is a rare posterior fossa malformation characterized by cerebellar vermis hypoplasia, absence of the lateral apertures (of Luschka) and the median aperture (of Magendie), cystic dilation of the fourth ventricle, and posterior fossa enlargement. It has an estimated incidence of approximately 1 in 25,000–35,000 live births in the United States and about 6.79 per 100,000 births worldwide.1 An early proposed development mechanism of DWM involved congenital atresia of the foramina of Luschka and Magendie.2 Newer evidence suggests that the underlying mechanism is failed roof-plate development of the rhombencephalon with nonfenestration of Blake’s pouch,3 with both impairing adequate CSF drainage.2,4 The clinical manifestations of DWM may include cerebellar dysfunction with chronic hydrocephalus that results in macrocephaly and elevated intracranial pressure.5,6 Heterozygous deletions on chromosome 3q24 involving ZIC1 and ZIC4, mutations in FOXC1 on 6p25, and environmental prenatal exposures to teratogens have all been linked to DWM.3,7

Occipital meningocele (OMC) is an occipital skull defect with protrusion of the meninges (but no brain/neural tissues) and CSF through the defect.8,9 Its pathogenesis involves premature cranial neural tube closure and faulty ossification of the occipital bone, creating a posterior skull defect through which the meninges herniate.10 The overall incidence of meningocele is 0.8–3 per 10,000 live births and they predominate in the occipital region.11 Maternal folate deficiency, diabetes, and obesity are associated risk factors for the development of OMC.10 While encephaloceles coexisting with DWM are more frequently reported, with approximately 79 cases in the literature, only about 40 cases of DWM coexisting with OMC have been reported.12,13 Since they lack neural tissue involvement, OMCs are often asymptomatic if small, with many being discovered incidentally or spontaneously resolving.14 These lesions pose risks when they rupture or harbor an infection, and an associated DWM can further complicate the mass effect and CSF circulation.

Historically, hydrocephalus and cystic expansion in DWM patients have been treated with ventriculoperitoneal shunt (VPS) insertion in the lateral ventricles, cystoperitoneal shunt (CPS) placement in the posterior fossa cyst, or a combination of both. These modalities carry risks of shunt malfunction and infection.14,15 Alternatives such as posterior fossa craniectomy with cyst excision can be technically challenging and morbid.16 Neuroendoscopic techniques such as endoscopic third ventriculostomy (ETV) and direct endoscopic fenestration allow internal CSF diversion in DWM patients without the need for shunt hardware.17–20 We present an illustrative case of a neonate with DWM and a concomitant OMC, managed with endoscopic cyst fenestration through the OMC defect—the first reported application of an endoscopic approach in this dual pathology.

Illustrative Case

History and Imaging

A 1-day-old female neonate was born at 33 weeks 6 days’ gestation via cesarean section. The prenatal ultrasound demonstrated evidence of an OMC. On postpartum evaluation, the patient was found to have an approximately 4-cm-diameter OMC. The cyst was not covered with skin, and visualization through the translucent dysplastic dura did not appear to have any evidence of brain parenchyma within the meningocele. Postnatal MRI of the brain without contrast was performed on day of life 1, revealing an OMC associated with DWM (Fig. 1A) without brain tissues within the sac. The supratentorial ventricles were enlarged, consistent with hydrocephalus (Fig. 1B), with a preoperative frontal occipital horn ratio (FOHR) of 0.53, well above the accepted threshold of 0.37 for ventriculomegaly,21 and the posterior fossa cyst was associated with mass effect and anterior displacement of the brainstem (Fig. 1A). Although there was some limitation in visualization below the skull base on MRI, there was also concern that the cyst extended caudally into the upper cervical spinal canal with compression of the cervical cord (Fig. 1A). Given that the meningocele was not covered by skin, the patient underwent urgent repair to minimize the risk of infection. Operative Technique

The patient was positioned prone on a horseshoe headrest (Fig. 2A and B). A midline incision was made superior to the meningocele and carried out around the side of the defect, at the margin of apparently normal, follicle-bearing skin. This was then continued inferiorly below the meningocele in the midline. The pericranium around the defect was elevated in the subperiosteal space, to be used to cover the defect later. The skull defect was circumferentially exposed. The dysplastic dura of the exposed portion of the meningocele was opened, and a specimen was sent for histopathological analysis. Microscopic examination revealed hair-bearing skin with adjacent thin fibroconnective tissue membrane with a thin squamous covering and meningothelial internal lining (Fig. 3). A 30° 3-mm neuroendoscope (LOTTA system, Karl Storz SE & Co. KG) was placed through the occipital defect into the (fourth) ventricle/DWM posterior fossa cyst. The 30° angle of view of the neuroendoscope allowed visualization of the membrane without requiring a steep angle to hold the scope within the skull defect. This approach is schematically illustrated in Fig. 4A, which demonstrates the surgical approach, endoscopic trajectory, and relevant anatomical landmarks. There was clear visualization of the lateral cerebellar hemispheres and brainstem on the floor of the fourth ventricle. As expected, no evidence of a cerebellar vermis was visualized endoscopically. At the inferior aspect along the skull base, a cyst wall membrane was able to be directly visualized. At the lateral/inferior aspect of the cerebellar hemisphere, there was a thickened arachnoid membrane, where the foramen of Luschka would normally be located (Fig. 4B). Using a blunt tip grasper forceps through the endoscope working channel, we grasped the membrane to the patient’s right side in this area and created a small ostomy (Fig. 4C). Blunt tip forceps were used to enter this small ostomy and gradually widen the opening. This was repeated on the patient’s left side. After the opening was sufficiently large, we were able to visualize the skull base and vessels directly through the membrane using the endoscope. There was confirmation of billowing of the tissues around the ostomy sites, consistent with CSF flow through these ostomies. We then removed the endoscope from the fourth ventricle. The layer on the inner surface of the meningocele appeared to be a continuation of the intracranial dura and was closed primarily. The dura was then covered with a thin layer of fibrin sealant (Tisseel, Baxter Healthcare Corp.) with no CSF egress. The size of the skull defect was measured to be 10 × 22 mm. Using the KLS resorbable plating system (KLS Martin), we measured a 22-mm burr hole cover and molded it to fit over the skull defect. This was secured using KLS 3-mm sonic weld pins. The pericranium that was previously elevated was then reflected over the absorbable plate. The scalp was closed by the neurosurgeon in a z-plasty fashion.22 The plastic surgery team was not required for the skin closure. The patient returned to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) for further recovery after surgery.

Postoperative Course

The patient was discharged home from the NICU on postoperative day 24. Early outpatient general pediatrics follow-up showed appropriate weight gain, good feeding, no neurological deficits, and satisfactory healing of the surgical incision. At the first postoperative visit to the pediatric neurosurgery clinic, approximately 4 weeks after surgery, the infant demonstrated age-appropriate movement and head control, with normal feeding, urination, and bowel movements. Her parents reported a possible intermittent dysconjugate gaze, which resolved by 7 months of age. At her neurosurgery follow-up appointment 7 months after surgery, she continued to make appropriate developmental progress without seizures, vomiting, lethargy, irritability, wound concerns, infection, or meningitis. Her head circumference continued to track along the growth curve. A follow-up MRI study of the brain without contrast obtained 7 months postoperatively (Fig. 1C and D) showed expected postoperative changes with an interval decrease in FOHR from 0.53 preoperatively to 0.48 postoperatively (Fig. 1D). There was also improvement of the anterior displacement of the brainstem (Fig. 1C) with no further evidence of cervical cord compression. At 14 months postoperatively, the patient did not require a VPS or other CSF diversion. Long-term monitoring will still be required to determine if hydrocephalus develops in the future.

Discussion

Observations

This case adds to the existing body of literature on the surgical treatment of patients with concomitant DWM-OMC, as summarized in Table 1.1223–34 These reports demonstrate that patients with DWM-associated OMCs can also harbor other systemic abnormalities, including craniofacial defects such as cleft lip and palate, congenital cardiac anomalies such as ventricular septal defect, and limb abnormalities such as amniotic band syndrome or partial absence of digits.12,23,27 Notably, our patient had no such coexisting anomalies. Prognosis depended heavily on the presence of hydrocephalus combined with the previously described systemic abnormalities. Overall, patients with small, uncomplicated meningoceles consistently show better outcomes compared with patients with large, growing, or otherwise complicated meningoceles, which pose greater clinical and surgical challenges. In the case series described in Table 1, a combination of CSF diversion such as VPS and/or CPS insertion, alongside meningocele repair, was commonly performed, especially for large or symptomatic lesions.12,23–34 We found there no prior reporting of transmeningocele endoscopic fenestration of the DWM-OMC in the literature. Outcomes of Shunt-Related Strategies

From the 30-year experience of Osenbach and Menezes, symptomatic control of DWM traditionally has been managed with combined VPS and CPS placement, but there are limited data on revisions, complications, or longevity of the ventricular or cyst shunt hardware.35 In a later study with 17 patients with DWM from 1985 to 2006, complications reported after CPS and VPS insertion included shunt infection, upward transtentorial herniation, chronic downward transincisural cerebral herniation, CSF overdrainage and maladaptive pressure gradients, and neurodevelopmental morbidity.36 Postoperative adverse outcomes increase in variability when DWM is accompanied by an OMC, as shown in Table 1. VPS placement with OMC repair was generally associated with overall patient survival, but some patients required additional shunt revisions and showed delayed or incomplete neurological recovery. Adding external drainage to the VPS did not improve outcomes, as the reported case still required further intervention with a prolonged postoperative course.23,25,33 CPS insertion with OMC repair showed results ranging from good neurological recovery and resolution of hydrocephalus in isolated cases to significant complications, including CSF leakage, infection, neurological deterioration, and early postoperative death.26–28 In the largest reported series, CPS-based management with OMC repair was associated with high complication rates and inconsistent neurological outcomes.26 In contrast, CPS placement combined with temporary external drainage during OMC repair was associated with favorable postoperative neurological recovery and effective hydrocephalus control.30,34 Combined VPS and CPS insertion with OMC repair resulted in good outcomes, with effective management of both supratentorial and posterior fossa CSF compartments and satisfactory neurological recovery.24 Finally, OMC repair without permanent CSF diversion was associated with poor outcomes when performed without external drainage, whereas the use of temporary CSF diversion during OMC repair was associated with a good neurological outcome in a single case.29,31 It is important to highlight that the outcomes of these studies are difficult to compare due to heterogeneity and confounders in lesion size, associated anomalies, hydrocephalus severity, and follow-up duration, limitations that reflect the variability of the condition itself. In our patient, we attempted an alternative to VPS placement, due to the concern that the OMC was not covered by skin, putting the patient at increased risk of infection or meningitis.

Rationale for Endoscopic Fenestration

Endoscopic fenestration serves as a promising treatment modality for patients with DWM. Mohanty et al. reported 21 ETV-based endoscopic procedures in patients with DWM (16 ETV alone, 3 ETV with aqueductal stenting, and 2 ETV with membrane fenestration), with 5 of the 21 endoscopic cases later requiring VPS placement, providing a 76% shunt-free endoscopic success rate.17 Although endoscopic fenestration has been described for DWM-associated posterior fossa cysts, to our knowledge there is no prior report of this technique being successfully applied to a patient with DWM and concurrent OMC.17,18,37 The posterior fossa abnormality disrupts normal CSF distribution and promotes turbulent CSF flow into the meningocele sac, increasing the risk of rupture. Endoscopic fenestration adjusts the CSF hydrodynamics by safely redirecting flow and reducing compartmental pressure on the meningocele sac, without the risk of overdrainage associated with external solutions such as VPS and CPS placement.

However, it is important to highlight that not all neuroendoscopic techniques can yield similar success. In a study by Mohanty et al. of 72 patients, treatment failure occurred in 25 patients (35%).17 Failure rates were 50% for CPS insertion (12/24), 19% for VPS placement (4/21, all within 6 months), and 29% for the ETV cohort (6/21); 5 of the 6 patients in the ETV group with treatment failure subsequently required VPS placement.17 Another study detailing patients undergoing combined ETV and choroid plexus cauterization for DWM or Dandy-Walker variant demonstrated treatment failure in nearly one-quarter of cases (25% in DWM and 27% in Dandy-Walker variant), requiring shunt placement.20 Theologou et al. described a patient who initially improved after ETV with tentorial fenestration but developed recurrent hydrocephalus and subdural hygromas 1month later, requiring VPS placement.19 The 2021 Hydrocephalus Clinical Research Network study found similar durability and complication rates between shunt- and ETV-based treatments for DWS-related hydrocephalus, supporting endoscopy as a reasonable but unproven superior alternative.38 Despite selecting the endoscopic route even though our surgical treatment was fenestration of the DWM cyst directly, our experience warranted a better result in this patient. On preoperative imaging, it appeared that the ventricular system communicated with the dilated fourth ventricle, allowing endoscopic fenestration to restore CSF flow, reduce inflow to the meningocele, and avoid additional shunt placement.

Lessons

The combination of DWM and OMC is rare, and currently there is no standard treatment approach. The typical approach would involve a shunt implant with OMC repair. We reported a successful single neonatal case demonstrating a unique method of endoscopic fenestration for the management of this rare concurrent disease presentation. This approach eliminates the need for permanent VPS or CPS insertion for CSF diversion, which is particularly advantageous in the DWM population given their susceptibility to shunt complications from altered flow dynamics and anatomical distortion. Immediate postpartum intervention was chosen because the OMC was covered only by thin, translucent dura and exposed outside the cranium, so delaying intervention would have increased the risk of infection/meningitis, rupture, or CSF leak.

The main limitation is that this report demonstrates successful endoscopic fenestration in only a single case of a small, uncomplicated OMC with DWM. However, given the rarity of concomitant presentation of these conditions, larger case series are inherently difficult to assemble. Therefore, this case represents an initial experience with endoscopic fenestration in selected patients and supports further application of this technique in cases of similar or greater complexity with long-term follow-up to better define its safety and efficacy. Additionally, the success of this approach may depend heavily on favorable anatomy, including a sac free of neural tissue, direct posterior fossa cyst access, and anatomy permitting safe bilateral fenestration. In the future, lesion morphology and volumetrics can be further delineated using CSF dynamics studies and advanced 3D MRI techniques, often used for surgical planning and decision-making in other posterior fossa defects such as Chiari type I malformation.39 Long-term surveillance with follow-up imaging is essential for detecting possible recurrent cystic dilation or the formation of delayed hydrocephalus. Neurosurgeons should continue to adopt minimally invasive strategies like endoscopic fenestration to address posterior fossa malformations in the pediatric population.
