Enhancing classroom behavior analysis with multimodal data: a cross-attention fusion network approach
Authors: Bo Li, Qi Zhao, Mengmeng Wei, Xu Yang, Xiaofeng An
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Classroom behavior analysis plays a crucial role in understanding student engagement, learning effectiveness, and instructional quality in higher education.This study presents a multimodal deep learning framework for classifying students’ classroom behaviors by integrating visual and physiological data to support data-driven instruction. To address the limitations of existing approaches—namely complex classroom settings, large data volumes, and reliance on single modalities—we develop a Multi-Level Cross-Self Attention Fusion Network (MCSFN). The network comprises three branches for extracting spatial visual features, Restormer-enhanced video representations, and physiological signals modeled via a SiLU-activated multilayer perceptron. These heterogeneous features are fused through a multi-level cross-attention mechanism for robust representation learning. Evalua ted on a self-constructed dataset of classroom videos and wearable-derived signals, the proposed framework achieves 95.88% classification accuracy, surpassing single-modality baselines. Beyond improving behavioral recognition accuracy, this work highlights the potential of multimodal AI-driven analytics to inform instructional design, enable real-time monitoring, and enhance personalized learning interventions, offering a practical pathway toward data-informed and adaptive classroom practices.

Introduction

In higher education classrooms, students’ behavioral expressions and emotional states are critical indicators of engagement and instructional effectiveness, offering valuable insights for teaching evaluation and strategy refinement1. Traditional classroom observations, reliant on teachers’ subjective judgment and manual recording, are inefficient, limited in scope, and unable to provide comprehensive, real-time feedback. These challenges are particularly evident in large-class settings, where teachers often overlook subtle behaviors such as off-task attention, side conversations, or fatigue due to competing demands2. Furthermore, students’ individual differences in interests, abilities, and psychological states render standardized evaluations inadequate for supporting personalized instructional interventions.

Advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and educational digitalization have driven research toward automating classroom behavior recognition, enabling data-driven decision-making and adaptive learning environments3,4. Existing approaches, typically based on video monitoring or sensor technologies, have shown potential but face key limitations, including constrained camera angles, high deployment costs, and a lack of insight into students’ internal cognitive or emotional states5,6,7,8. These factors hinder their scalability and broader adoption in real-world teaching contexts.

Wearable smart bands provide a portable, low-cost, and energy-efficient alternative, capable of continuously collecting physiological signals such as heart rate, body temperature, and blood pressure. These data offer a quantifiable and objective perspective on students’ cognitive and emotional states, complementing visual behavior data to enable richer, multimodal insights into classroom dynamics9,10,11. By integrating these modalities, it is possible to overcome the limitations of single-source systems and more accurately capture the complex interplay between students’ external behaviors and internal states.

To address the challenges of complex classroom environments and limited data diversity in current research, this study proposes a multimodal classroom behavior classification framework that combines visual and physiological signals. Specifically, we design a Multi-Level Cross-Self Attention Fusion Network (MCSFN), a three-branch architecture that jointly extracts spatial features from classroom video, enhanced visual features using a Restormer-based branch, and non-linear physiological embeddings via a SiLU-MLP module. These features are deeply integrated through a hierarchical cross-modal attention mechanism, enabling the model to dynamically balance and leverage complementary cues from different modalities.

The contributions of this study are threefold

Multimodal dataset development

We constructed and annotated a synchronized dataset combining classroom behavior images with physiological signals (heart rate, temperature, and blood pressure), providing a foundation for multimodal behavior analysis in educational research.Although the dataset is not publicly released due to privacy and ethical constraints, the data collection protocol, annotation criteria, and model architecture are fully described, enabling methodological reproducibility. In addition, cross-context evaluation across vocational and university classrooms provides an initial indication of generalizability beyond a single classroom setting.We acknowledge that the current data were collected within geographically and culturally homogeneous settings. While the proposed framework is designed to be architecture-agnostic to teaching styles and classroom layouts, future work will involve multi-region and multi-age cohorts to further evaluate cross-cultural transferability.

Novel fusion architecture

The proposed MCSFN integrates dual visual branches (MobileNetV3-S and Restormer) with a SiLU-MLP physiological branch, employing multi-level cross-attention to enhance cross-modal feature interactions and improve classification accuracy.

Robust visual enhancement

By incorporating a frequency-domain enhancement module that combines Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) and Efficient Channel Attention (ECA), the framework strengthens feature representation in challenging conditions such as varied lighting, occlusion, and subtle posture changes.

Experimental results demonstrate that the MCSFN achieves 95.88% classification accuracy on our self-constructed dataset, substantially outperforming traditional single-modality and shallow fusion models. Beyond improving the technical accuracy of classroom behavior recognition, this study highlights the potential of multimodal sensing and attention-based modeling to advance intelligent classroom management, enable real-time instructional adaptation, and support data-driven, personalized education.

Related work

Vision-based classroom behavior analysis

Student engagement is widely recognized as a key indicator of educational quality, with classroom participation strongly correlated with academic achievement12,13. Observable behaviors such as attentiveness, interaction, and non-verbal cues have therefore become central to research on smart classrooms and intelligent learning systems.

Driven by advances in artificial intelligence and computer vision, numerous studies have explored video-based approaches for automated classroom behavior recognition. Common techniques include facial expression analysis, pose estimation, and head orientation tracking. For instance, Lin et al.14 combined skeleton-based pose features with human detection to extract spatiotemporal behavior patterns. Dong et al.15 enhanced SSD-based detection using K-means clustering and focal loss, achieving a 10.2% accuracy improvement across five behavior categories. Wang et al.16 integrated OpenPose with YOLOv3 to capture both global and local visual features, while Trabelsi et al.17 proposed a YOLOv5-based framework for real-time monitoring of student attention and emotion states. In addition, Chai et al.18 incorporated speech and facial features to investigate the influence of teacher behaviors on student attentiveness.

Despite promising results, vision-based methods are often sensitive to lighting variations, occlusion, and pose diversity, which limits their robustness in authentic classroom environments.

Physiological signal–based learning analytics

Physiological signals provide stable and implicit indicators of learners’ cognitive and emotional states, making them valuable complements to observable behavioral data19–21. Signals such as heart rate, skin conductance, and EEG have been widely used to model attention, fatigue, and stress in learning contexts.

Zuraini et al.22 clustered heart rate data to classify classroom states such as fatigue and arousal, while Li et al.23 employed wearable sensor data with Random Forest models to predict attentiveness, achieving an accuracy of 69%. Liu24 and Lai25 demonstrated improved attention recognition by integrating multiple physiological indicators. More recently, Levy et al.26 combined EEG and eye-tracking data to study attention regulation in students with ADHD, providing insights for targeted educational interventions.

Although physiological-based approaches offer robustness against visual disturbances, they often lack behavioral interpretability and contextual awareness when used in isolation.

Multimodal classroom behavior analysis

To overcome the limitations of single-modality systems, multimodal fusion strategies—ranging from data-level to feature-level and decision-level integration—have gained increasing attention27. Among these, feature-level fusion is particularly suitable for real-time classroom scenarios, as it balances representational richness and computational efficiency.

Representative studies include Guo3 who combined behavioral and knowledge modeling to construct learner profiles, and Hao28 who fused EEG and posture data using deep neural networks for attention assessment. Gao29 integrated audio and pose features through CNNs to identify learning states, while Asish et al.30 and Zhang31 demonstrated that combining physiological, visual, and motion cues significantly enhances behavioral sensing and identity recognition in educational settings.

Summary and research gap

In summary, existing classroom behavior analysis studies have achieved substantial progress in visual perception, physiological modeling, and multimodal fusion. Recently, multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have emerged as a promising paradigm for educational understanding and assessment by integrating visual, textual, and semantic reasoning. For example, Hang et al.32 proposed an MLLM-based framework for personalized vocabulary learning through image–text interaction, while Xiao et al.33 introduced EduVQA, a multimodal visual question answering system for intelligent educational assessment.

These studies demonstrate the strong potential of foundation models in supporting high-level cognitive analysis, adaptive feedback, and personalized learning. However, several critical gaps remain. First, many existing approaches still rely on single-modality data or shallow fusion strategies, limiting robustness under complex classroom conditions involving occlusion, illumination variation, and low-intensity behaviors. Second, although MLLM-based methods provide powerful semantic reasoning capabilities, they often require large-scale annotated datasets and substantial computational resources, which restrict their applicability in real-time classroom monitoring and edge deployment scenarios. Third, high computational complexity and limited portability continue to hinder the practical implementation of many multimodal systems in authentic teaching environments. Finally, relatively few studies effectively integrate multimodal perception with operational educational feedback mechanisms that directly support instructional adaptation and teaching reform.

Methodology

To enable real-time, accurate identification of student classroom behavior in complex educational settings, this study proposes a deep learning-based multimodal learning framework (Fig. 1). To protect the privacy of the participants, all figures containing human faces in this paper have been replaced with cartoon images. The framework integrates synchronized video and physiological data, capturing behavioral postures such as attentive head-up, head-down, talking, and sleeping, alongside physiological indicators including heart rate, body temperature, and blood pressure. These heterogeneous data sources provide complementary perspectives on students’ cognitive engagement and emotional states, forming a more robust basis for behavior analysis than visual or physiological signals alone.Fig. 1Overall architecture of the proposed MCSFN framework, illustrating multimodal data acquisition, three-branch feature extraction (visual, physiological, and enhancement), multi-level cross-self attention fusion, and classroom behavior classification.

Overall architecture of the proposed MCSFN framework, illustrating multimodal data acquisition, three-branch feature extraction (visual, physiological, and enhancement), multi-level cross-self attention fusion, and classroom behavior classification.

Video frames and physiological measurements were temporally aligned using shared timestamps, and individual students were identified through unique IMEI codes associated with each wristband. Upper-body regions were extracted from the video frames to emphasize behavior-relevant cues, and synchronized physiological values were embedded into the filenames, creating a structured dataset of multimodal samples. Physiological signals underwent outlier removal using the interquartile range (IQR) method and were standardized via z-scores, while the visual data were augmented using RandAugment34, which generates diverse training instances to improve resilience against variations in lighting, contrast, and pose.

Based on this processed dataset, we developed the Multi-Level Cross-Self Attention Fusion Network (MCSFN), a three-branch architecture designed to model and integrate multimodal information effectively. The network comprises a lightweight TA-MobileNetV3 backbone for spatial visual feature extraction, a SiLU-MLP module to capture non-linear dynamics in physiological signals, and a Restormer-ECA branch incorporating frequency-domain transformations and channel attention to strengthen image representation under challenging conditions such as occlusion or illumination changes. Features from these branches are integrated through a Multi-Level Cross-Attention Module (MMF), which dynamically models inter-modal dependencies and synthesizes a unified representation for classification.

The fused features are passed through fully connected layers and a Softmax classifier to generate predictions across four behavioral categories. In addition to classification, the system provides real-time monitoring functions, including behavior distribution statistics, accuracy curves, automated alerts for abnormal behaviors, and AI-derived engagement scores, offering instructors actionable insights into classroom dynamics.

Empirical evaluations in both vocational secondary and university classroom contexts demonstrate that the proposed framework achieves an average classification accuracy of 95.88%, substantially surpassing baseline single-modality and shallow fusion models. These results underscore the generalizability and deployment readiness of MCSFN, highlighting its potential as a scalable tool for data-driven teaching evaluation and adaptive instructional support.

Data collection and dataset construction

Given the absence of publicly available multimodal datasets that synchronously collect classroom video and physiological signals, and considering that existing datasets exhibit substantial differences in behavioral labeling systems, which limits their applicability to personalized classroom behavior analysis, this study independently constructed a multisource student classroom behavior dataset integrating visual and physiological information. In view of the involvement of portrait rights and privacy protection in the collection of classroom images and physiological data, the study was approved by the relevant institutional authority, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with applicable guidelines and regulations. The study underwent rigorous institutional ethical review prior to data collection (Approval Registration Number: 2024DY10401). Prior to data collection, all participants (or their legal guardians) provided explicit consent for their data and images to be published in an open-access online journal. All identifiable information has been removed from the dataset. The images presented in this manuscript have been carefully de-identified, and representative figures have been replaced or modified with non-identifiable illustrations to prevent participant recognition. For any questions or data-related requests, please contact the corresponding author, Xiaofeng An, at an.xf@jlenu.edu.cn.

All data in this study were collected in real time using smart wristbands and cameras. The smart wristbands recorded participants’ physiological information, including body temperature, heart rate, and blood pressure, while the cameras captured image data. As these data involve personal privacy, the dataset cannot be made publicly available. Researchers interested in accessing the dataset may contact the corresponding author.

The study involved 24 students from a vocational secondary school and 30 students from a university, whose behavioral videos and physiological signals were recorded during regular classes over a one-week period. Data collection was conducted in fixed classrooms under controlled environmental conditions to ensure sample consistency and validity. For the vocational class, all students participated, while in the university setting, participants were seated in the front five rows to ensure optimal image quality and completeness of multimodal data capture.

Classroom video data were collected using Ezviz C6C 4 K cameras, selected for their compact size, ease of installation, and minimal interference with natural classroom behaviors. Each camera was mounted centrally above the classroom blackboard, providing full-room coverage. The device is equipped with a 16 GB SD card and a data SIM card, supporting continuous recording and stable data storage, while being powered by the classroom mains to ensure uninterrupted operation.

Physiological data were captured using Oufu B2315 + smart wristbands, capable of real-time monitoring of five physiological indicators: heart rate, body temperature, wrist temperature, systolic blood pressure, and diastolic blood pressure. Data were uploaded to a secure cloud platform via the NB-IoT network, allowing researchers to access and manage measurements through a computer terminal. The system also supports remote configuration of sampling frequency, facilitating dynamic adjustments during experimental sessions.

Each student was assigned a unique device ID (linked via IMEI) to maintain accurate individual tracking. The wristbands, equipped with adjustable straps, were designed to be non-intrusive during lessons and allowed real-time monitoring via a companion mobile application. During the study, class monitors distributed the devices according to a pre-assigned schedule prior to each session, collected them post-class, and centralized data export occurred each evening. With a battery life exceeding 48 h per charge, the devices ensured continuous, high-quality data capture throughout the experiment.

This protocol enabled the construction of a synchronized multimodal dataset, integrating time-aligned video and physiological signals across diverse behavioral categories, thereby providing a robust foundation for subsequent model training and evaluation.

Data processing

This study collected more than 30 h of multimodal classroom data, including continuous video recordings and wristband-based physiological signals, from two distinct cohorts: one vocational school class and one university class. Data acquisition was conducted over a full week of regular classroom teaching to ensure ecological validity and to capture naturally occurring learning behaviors under authentic instructional conditions. The video data were recorded using fixed-position cameras covering the main classroom area, while physiological signals were acquired synchronously from wearable wristbands worn by participating students. To support multimodal learning and cross-modal fusion, precise temporal alignment was achieved using synchronized timestamps across data streams, ensuring frame-level consistency between visual observations and physiological measurements. This process resulted in time-consistent paired samples suitable for integrated analysis. All physiological data were anonymized at the feature level prior to analysis to protect student privacy. Missing samples were directly discarded, while abnormal values were identified based on the interquartile range (IQR) method. Any data points outside this interval were treated as outliers and removed prior to model training.

For the image modality, sample extraction relied on precise temporal matching. An OpenCV-based automated pipeline was developed to process video frames: full-class frames were first extracted, each target student was located using their unique IMEI-mapped wristband ID, and cropped upper-body regions were generated. Each image was systematically named following the format “timestamp + physiological features” (fields separated by “&”), ensuring traceable one-to-one correspondence between visual and physiological data.

All images underwent manual review to eliminate low-quality samples (blurred or occluded frames) and were standardized to 1:1 aspect ratio and 224 × 224 resolution for model compatibility. A total of 6,000 high-quality samples per class were retained, equally distributed across four behavioral categories—“looking up,” “looking down,” “talking,” and “sleeping.” These formed Dataset A (vocational class) and Dataset B (university class).

To improve model generalization across diverse classroom environments, we implemented RandAugment, a state-of-the-art automated augmentation strategy, for the visual modality. RandAugment constructs an operation pool comprising transformations such as color adjustment, histogram equalization, shearing, rotation, sharpness, and brightness modulation. A random subset of N operations is selected and applied with a uniform strength parameter M, generating more varied and challenging training samples. Unlike AutoAugment, RandAugment eliminates costly policy searches on proxy tasks, making it computationally efficient and well-suited for online augmentation during training.

Figure 2 illustrates the augmentation outcomes on classroom images, showing that RandAugment simulates common classroom imaging variations and substantially improves the model’s robustness to lighting variations, pose perturbations, and image quality inconsistencies. Model architecture

This study proposes a Multi-level Cross-Self Attention Fusion Network (MCSFN) for classifying students’ classroom behavioral states. The framework integrates visual data (classroom images) and physiological signals (e.g., heart rate, body temperature, and blood pressure collected from wearable devices) to achieve efficient and accurate behavior recognition. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the network comprises three main branches: an image feature extraction module, a physiological signal modeling module, and an image enhancement branch. Each branch processes its respective modality, and their outputs are deeply fused through a Multi-level Cross-Self Attention Fusion (MMF) module, followed by fully connected layers and a Softmax classifier to produce the final behavioral predictions.As shown in Fig. 3, the three-branch architecture explicitly decouples modality-specific feature extraction from cross-modal interaction and fusion.Fig. 3MCSFN network architecture.

MCSFN network architecture.

Unlike conventional two-branch concatenation or parallel fusion networks, the proposed three-branch design minimizes modality redundancy and bias. The hierarchical attention mechanism adaptively weights the relative importance and interactions of different modalities, thereby improving model generalization and robustness across diverse classroom scenarios.

Visual modality feature extraction

Students’ body movements, head poses, and gaze directions during class are critical indicators of their engagement and learning state. To effectively capture spatial behavioral cues from classroom images, we adopt TA-MobileNetV3 as the primary visual backbone. MobileNetV335, a lightweight convolutional neural network, achieves competitive accuracy with substantially reduced parameters, making it suitable for edge deployment and real-time applications. The integrated Triple Attention (TA) module enhances the backbone by modeling interdependencies across spatial dimensions (height and width) and the channel dimension36.

Within the TA module, the input feature tensor undergoes tensor rotation to generate three parallel sub-paths: channel–height (C–H), channel–width (C–W), and height–width (H–W) cross-attention mappings. Each path applies a lightweight attention mechanism to reweight features along the corresponding dimensions, followed by a cascaded fusion operation to produce the refined feature representation. This design enables the network to capture subtle postural variations and spatial distributions of students, particularly in multi-pose and low-motion intensity classroom scenarios.

Physiological modality feature modeling

Students’ levels of attention, focus, and emotional states during class are often indirectly reflected through physiological signals. To capture high-level representations from such data, we design a SiLU-MLP feature modeling module. The raw signals collected by the wearable device include heart rate (HR), wrist temperature (WT), arm temperature (AT), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP). These signals are first standardized using Z-score normalization and then fed into a two-layer multilayer perceptron (MLP) with SiLU (Sigmoid Linear Unit) activation37,38.

Compared to ReLU, SiLU offers smoother gradient continuity, which is advantageous for modeling nonlinear and fluctuating signals while avoiding instability due to gradient discontinuities. The resulting high-dimensional physiological embeddings are subsequently integrated into the cross-modal fusion module for joint representation learning.

Image enhancement module (Restormer-ECA branch)

To strengthen the model’s sensitivity to subtle behavioral variations in complex classroom environments, we design an additional image enhancement branch based on the Restormer architecture39, integrated with Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) and Efficient Channel Attention (ECA)40.

Restormer, a Transformer-based improvement, employs a local-window self-attention mechanism, making it particularly suitable for image restoration and enhancement tasks. The FFT module transforms the input image features from the spatial domain to the frequency domain, enabling the model to better capture low-frequency behavioral cues and background disturbances. The real and imaginary components of the frequency spectrum are separately extracted and embedded back into the backbone.

The ECA module models inter-channel dependencies via lightweight convolutional operations, avoiding the parameter explosion commonly seen in attention-based designs. The combination of FFT and ECA substantially enhances the model’s ability to represent behavior-related features under challenging conditions, such as motion blur, occlusions, and complex lighting variations.

Multi-level cross-self attention feature fusion (MMF module)

To achieve deep cross-modal feature integration, we develop the Multi-level Cross-Self Attention Fusion (MMF) module, which combines cross-attention and self-attention mechanisms in a multi-layer stacked architecture41,42.

After feature extraction, the visual, physiological, and enhanced image branches each output their respective feature vectors, which are first linearly projected into a shared embedding space. During the cross-attention phase, the visual features serve as queries, while physiological features act as keys and values, and vice versa, allowing bidirectional cross-modal interaction.

Subsequently, a self-attention layer captures intra-modal dependencies within the fused representation, further enhancing synergistic feature learning across modalities. Each fusion level is followed by residual connections and Layer Normalization to stabilize training and mitigate gradient vanishing.

The final MMF module comprises three cross-attention layers and two self-attention layers, forming a hierarchical fusion pathway that enables robust modeling of complementary features across the different modalities.

Classification output

The fused feature vectors are first processed through a fully connected (FC) layer for dimensionality reduction, followed by a Dropout layer (p = 0.3) to regularize training and mitigate overfitting. The final Softmax classifier outputs predictions across four classroom behavior categories: attentive (head up), looking down, talking, and sleeping.

The complete MCSFN network contains approximately 3.79 million parameters, with an average inference time of 2.335 ms per sample, ensuring real-time performance and deployment feasibility. This enables rapid monitoring and feedback of students’ behavioral states in real-world classroom environments.

Experiments

Experimental settings

In all experiments, the proposed framework was trained for 150 epochs with a batch size of 32 and an initial learning rate of 0.001 using the Adam optimizer. The Cross-Entropy Loss function was adopted for classification, and the random seed was fixed to 42 to ensure reproducibility.

During preliminary experiments, we evaluated multiple candidate values for key hyperparameters, including learning rate, batch size, and training epochs. The final configuration was selected based on validation performance and training stability. We observed that moderate variations in these parameters did not lead to significant performance fluctuations, indicating that the proposed framework does not rely on delicate parameter tuning.

In addition, we preliminarily investigated classical classifiers such as KNN with different neighborhood sizes. However, their performance was consistently inferior to deep multimodal models in handling high-dimensional heterogeneous features. Therefore, such methods were not included in the final comparative evaluation.

Overall, the experimental results demonstrate that the proposed MCSFN exhibits stable and robust performance under reasonable hyperparameter variations, supporting its practical applicability in real-world classroom scenarios.

In this study, four complementary metrics, including Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-score, are adopted to comprehensively evaluate classification performance. Accuracy reflects the overall prediction correctness, while Precision and Recall characterize class-wise discrimination and sensitivity. The F1-score provides a balanced measure of Precision and Recall, particularly under potential class imbalance.

Comparative experiments

To evaluate the practical effectiveness of the image feature extraction module within the proposed multimodal classroom behavior classification system, we systematically compared the performance of four widely used convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures on two datasets: Dataset A (vocational school classroom) and Dataset B (university classroom). The tested models include GoogLeNet, ResNet, MobileNetV3-S, and the proposed TA-MobileNetV3-S. These networks were selected due to their representative structural characteristics and their established applications in image recognition, behavior detection, and educational computing tasks, making them suitable baselines for performance comparison.

Specifically, GoogLeNet, as an early deep CNN representative, employs the Inception architecture to enhance multi-scale feature extraction. It has been applied in student facial expression recognition and posture detection tasks. For example, Tian et al.43 utilized a GoogLeNet-InceptionV variant for teacher facial expression recognition, achieving 90% accuracy on MOOC lecture recordings and an overall classification accuracy of 81.4%. ResNet, by incorporating residual connections, alleviates degradation issues in deep networks and is widely used for behavior recognition and posture estimation. Shiri et al.44 applied a ResNet + TCN framework to the DAiSEE engagement dataset, achieving a maximum accuracy of 63.9%. MobileNetV3-S, as a representative lightweight network, is particularly suited for deployment in resource-constrained educational environments. Liang et al.45 demonstrated its effectiveness for facial expression recognition, achieving 87.5% and 86.6% accuracy on the FERPlus and RAF-DB datasets, respectively.

These three architectures serve as strong benchmarks, representing deep convolutional, residual-based, and lightweight network categories. Comparing them with the proposed TA-MobileNetV3-S, which incorporates a triple-attention mechanism for improved spatial and channel feature modeling, allows a comprehensive assessment of its ability to extract and classify student behaviors under complex teaching conditions.

Ablation study

To evaluate the contribution of individual architectural components in the proposed Multi-branch Cross-Self Attention Fusion Network (MCSFN), structured ablation experiments were conducted under identical training and testing conditions. A baseline model and multiple hierarchical variants were designed to analyze the individual and joint effects of key modules.

The baseline configuration excludes the Restormer enhancement branch as well as the Efficient Channel Attention (ECA) and Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) modules, and relies solely on the TA-MobileNetV3 visual backbone and the parallel SiLU-MLP physiological branch, forming a lightweight bimodal fusion framework without frequency-domain enhancement or advanced attention mechanisms.

Based on this baseline, hierarchical ablation models were constructed by progressively introducing the Restormer branch, the ECA module, and FFT-based frequency enhancement. This incremental design enables interpretable evaluation of each component’s contribution to representation learning, cross-modal interaction, and classification performance, following widely adopted ablation practices in deep learning and multimodal research46,47,48.

Discussion and summary

Educational significance

With the rapid advancement of digital education, reliable sensing of students’ in-class states has become a core requirement for intelligent learning environments. Prior studies have predominantly relied on single-modality visual cues, such as facial expressions, head pose, or body posture, to infer student engagement14,16,17. While these approaches have demonstrated feasibility, their performance is often sensitive to occlusion, lighting variations, and low-intensity behaviors, limiting robustness in authentic classroom settings.

In contrast, the present study integrates visual behaviors with physiological signals, addressing limitations widely reported in earlier vision-only systems. Consistent with findings by Li et al.23 and Liu24, physiological indicators provide stable and complementary information related to attention and cognitive load. Unlike prior multimodal studies that primarily adopted feature concatenation or shallow fusion strategies28,29, this work introduces a multi-level cross-self attention mechanism, enabling deeper interaction and dynamic weighting between heterogeneous modalities. The observed performance gains across both vocational and university classrooms suggest that cross-attention fusion is particularly effective in modeling subtle behavioral states under complex instructional conditions.

From an educational perspective, the proposed system advances beyond offline behavior recognition commonly reported in the literature49,30 by embedding the model into a real-time, interactive classroom monitoring interface. The system visualizes engagement patterns and behavioral trends and further integrates physiological-aware feedback and automated alerts, such as sustained low attentiveness or increasing drowsiness. This capability aligns with recent calls in learning analytics research for actionable, teacher-facing feedback rather than post-hoc analysis alone.

The following sections critically examine the scope and limitations of this work, alongside strategies to ensure its broader applicability.Despite the promising results, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged and contextualized. First, the dataset was collected from two educational institutions within a relatively homogeneous cultural and instructional environment, which may constrain direct cross-cultural generalization. However, the dataset construction followed a protocol-driven design, including standardized classroom settings, consistent recording conditions, and unified annotation criteria, which emphasizes behavioral patterns rather than culture-specific content.

Second, although the number of participants is modest, the dataset captures a wide range of classroom behavioral variations across multiple sessions, enabling robust learning of modality-invariant features. Third, the behavioral annotations focus on coarse-grained engagement-related states rather than fine-grained cognitive processes. This design choice aligns with practical classroom monitoring scenarios and facilitates transfer to other educational contexts with similar observational objectives.

Due to privacy and ethical constraints associated with identifiable video and physiological signals, the dataset cannot be publicly released. Nevertheless, the methodological reproducibility of this work is supported through detailed documentation of the data collection protocol, preprocessing procedures, model architecture, and training strategy. These elements allow the proposed framework to be adapted and validated on other datasets with minimal modification, thereby supporting its potential generalizability beyond the specific data used in this study.

Importantly, this study supports a shift from teacher-centered observational judgment toward data-informed instructional adaptation, echoing pedagogical frameworks emphasizing adaptive teaching and formative assessment. By providing continuous, objective indicators of classroom dynamics, the system enables instructors to adjust pacing, interaction strategies, and instructional design in real time, thereby operationalizing the principle of “teaching adapted to learning” discussed in contemporary educational reform literature.

More broadly, this work contributes to the growing body of multimodal learning analytics by demonstrating that heterogeneous behavioral and physiological data can be jointly modeled in a lightweight yet effective architecture. Compared with existing multimodal systems that suffer from high computational complexity or limited deployability, the proposed framework strikes a balance between accuracy, efficiency, and practicality, supporting scalable adoption in real educational contexts.

Recent advances in Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have demonstrated strong capabilities in educational dialogue, semantic reasoning, and personalized feedback generation. In contrast, the proposed MCSFN framework focuses on multimodal perception and fine-grained behavioral state recognition based on visual and physiological signals. Rather than competing with MLLM-based systems, MCSFN is designed to complement them by providing reliable, structured behavioral representations.

High-quality perception constitutes a prerequisite for effective reasoning and feedback in educational AI systems. The outputs of MCSFN, such as attention level distributions, fatigue indicators, and engagement trajectories, can serve as informative inputs to MLLMs, enabling more context-aware and interpretable instructional support. Future work will explore the integration of MCSFN with MLLM-based reasoning modules to construct unified perception–reasoning–feedback pipelines for intelligent and personalized education.

Pedagogical interpretation and interface-supported decision making

Although the proposed framework can reliably identify observable behaviors such as “head-up,” “head-down,” “talking,” and “sleeping,” these categories should not be interpreted as direct proxies for students’ cognitive engagement or learning outcomes. Learning behaviors are inherently context-dependent and temporally dynamic.

To support meaningful pedagogical interpretation, the proposed system provides an integrated visual interface (Fig. 5) that combines real-time classroom video, behavior probability distributions, temporal trend curves, physiological indicators, and composite attention scores. This multi-dimensional visualization enables instructors to interpret behavioral patterns holistically rather than relying on isolated labels.

For example, a high “head-down” probability accompanied by elevated fatigue indicators and declining attention scores may suggest cognitive overload, whereas short-term head-down behavior during problem-solving activities may reflect focused engagement. Similarly, sustained “talking” combined with stable physiological signals may indicate peer collaboration rather than disengagement.

The alert module and trend visualization further assist teachers in identifying persistent behavioral changes, enabling timely instructional adjustments such as modifying instructional pace, incorporating interactive tasks, or providing individualized scaffolding.

Importantly, the system is designed to augment rather than replace professional pedagogical judgment. By integrating multimodal analytics with teachers’ experiential knowledge, the framework supports reflective, evidence-informed teaching while avoiding oversimplified interpretations of complex learning processes.

Privacy and ethical considerations

Given the sensitive nature of classroom video and physiological data, privacy preservation is a fundamental consideration in the design of the proposed framework. Rather than relying on centralized data storage, the system architecture supports local or edge-based inference, enabling most data processing to be performed on-site and reducing the risk of large-scale data exposure.

To minimize privacy risks, the framework focuses on compact feature representations instead of storing raw visual recordings. Visual inputs are transformed into abstract behavioral features, while physiological signals are normalized and anonymized prior to fusion, preventing direct personal identification.

Furthermore, the multimodal fusion strategy follows a modality minimization principle by incorporating only task-relevant information necessary for behavior recognition. This avoids excessive data collection and limits unnecessary access to sensitive attributes.

These privacy-aware design choices reflect a balance between analytical performance and ethical responsibility. By embedding privacy considerations into architectural decisions, the proposed system supports responsible deployment in real educational environments without imposing additional technical burden.

This study was conducted in accordance with relevant ethical guidelines. All participants or their legal guardians signed written informed consent forms regarding their participation in the study and the publication of their data in an open-access journal. To protect participant privacy, all data were anonymized prior to analysis, and this paper does not contain any personally identifiable information. Furthermore, all representative images have been modified or replaced with non-identifiable illustrations to ensure that no specific individuals can be identified.

Scalability and practical deployment

For large-scale adoption in realistic school environments, system scalability and operational feasibility are critical considerations. The proposed framework is designed as a lightweight and modular architecture, employing efficient visual backbones and compact multimodal fusion modules to limit computational overhead.

Owing to its low parameter count and fast inference speed, the system can support typical classroom sizes without requiring high-performance servers. Most processing can be conducted on local or edge devices, reducing network dependency and deployment costs.

In terms of device management, the modular integration of cameras and wearable sensors allows flexible configuration and incremental expansion. This design facilitates maintenance, replacement, and synchronization in multi-classroom scenarios, mitigating long-term operational burden.

Importantly, the system is positioned as a decision-support tool rather than an automated evaluator. It provides interpretable analytics, trend visualization, and alert mechanisms to assist instructors in monitoring classroom dynamics, while preserving teachers’ central role in pedagogical decision-making.

By emphasizing transparency, usability, and teacher-centered design, the proposed framework is expected to promote acceptance and sustainable adoption in diverse educational settings.

Conclusion

This study proposes and validates a multimodal fusion framework for classroom behavior classification based on synchronous video and physiological signals. By introducing a tri-branch architecture with multi-level cross-attention, the proposed model achieves deep integration of heterogeneous features and consistently outperforms traditional unimodal and shallow-fusion approaches reported in prior studies.

The results confirm and extend existing findings on the complementary nature of visual and physiological indicators of student engagement. While previous studies have demonstrated the feasibility of multimodal sensing, this work provides empirical evidence that attention-guided fusion mechanisms substantially enhance the recognition of complex, low-salience behaviors and improve robustness across diverse classroom conditions. In this sense, the proposed approach advances classroom behavior analysis from simple detection toward context-aware interpretation of learning states.

Beyond methodological contributions, the proposed system offers practical value for educational reform by translating multimodal analytics into real-time instructional support. It enables teachers to access actionable insights during instruction, supports early identification of disengagement, and lays a foundation for personalized learning interventions.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although the framework was evaluated on both vocational and university classroom datasets, the data were collected within geographically and culturally homogeneous environments. As a result, the direct transferability of the model to classrooms with different teaching styles, spatial layouts, or student age groups requires further validation.

Despite these limitations, the proposed approach emphasizes modality-invariant behavioral and physiological representations and employs attention-based fusion to dynamically adapt feature importance across heterogeneous inputs, which is expected to facilitate knowledge transfer across diverse instructional contexts.

Future work will focus on collecting multi-regional, cross-cultural, and multi-age-group datasets to systematically evaluate domain adaptation performance and enhance the robustness and scalability of the framework. In addition, incorporating additional modalities such as speech, affective signals, and interaction data, as well as exploring longitudinal modeling of learning trajectories, will further promote the transition from behavioral recognition to deeper learning understanding and strengthen the role of intelligent systems in supporting adaptive, student-centered education.
