Changes in health insurance coverage and healthcare utilization of middle-aged and older adults after the transition from Seguro Popular to INSABI
Authors: José Eduardo Cabrero-Castro, Mariana López-Ortega, Brian Downer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMexico replaced Seguro Popular—its public insurance for people without formal employment—with the Instituto de Salud para el Bienestar (INSABI) in 2019. How this policy change relates to insurance coverage and health-care use among older adults has not been examined with nationally representative longitudinal data.MethodsData came from 12 759 participants in the Mexican Health and Aging Study who were interviewed in 2018 and 2021 and were 50 years and older. Descriptive statistics documented changes in insurance status and provider type. Three multivariable models assessed: (1) factors associated with becoming uninsured (binary logistic regression), (2) the relation between insurance loss and provider type in 2021 (multinomial logistic regression), and (3) the likelihood of switching from public to private care between waves (binary logistic regression). Predictors included age, sex, community size, education, self-rated financial status, and six doctor-diagnosed conditions.ResultsThe share of respondents without insurance rose from 9% in 2018 to 26% in 2021, while coverage through Seguro Popular or its successor INSABI fell from 30% to 11%. More than half (53%) of 2018 Seguro Popular enrollees became uninsured by 2021. Living in a community with fewer than 2 500 inhabitants (odds ratio = 3.5), having at most six years of schooling (odds ratio = 1.7), and reporting poor finances (odds ratio = 2.5) were strong correlates of losing coverage. Among those who became uninsured, the relative likelihood of using private rather than Secretaría de Salud facilities increased markedly (relative risk ratio = 1/0.61). Among participants who used public providers in 2018, losing insurance was associated with an 80% higher probability of switching to private care by 2021.ConclusionsBetween 2018 and 2021 the proportion of Mexicans aged 50 years and older who lacked insurance nearly tripled, and health-care use shifted decisively toward the private sector, especially among rural, less-educated, and economically disadvantaged groups. Ensuring clear affiliation mechanisms and improving the quality and availability of public services are pressing priorities if Mexico is to prevent widening financial and access disparities under the post-Seguro Popular system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13673-x.

Introduction

Mexico’s health system is highly fragmented, combining public and private providers and generating marked disparities in access and outcomes [1]. As of 2018 it comprised three parallel pillars: (i) employment-based social security schemes; (ii) Secretaría de Salud services for people without social security—delivered by federal and state facilities and financed first by Seguro Popular (to 2019) and then INSABI; and (iii) a heterogeneous private sector of insurers and providers [2].

Employer-based social security is a major coverage source. The two largest schemes are the Mexican Social Security Institute (IMSS) and the Institute for Social Security and Services for State Workers (ISSSTE) [3, 4]. IMSS insures formally employed private-sector workers and their dependents (~40% of the population), while ISSSTE covers federal employees and families (~8%) [3]. Smaller schemes (e.g., Petróleos Mexicanos and armed-forces health services) cover their workforces. Social Security institutions provide comprehensive preventive, specialty, inpatient, and pharmaceutical benefits at little or no cost, but care is largely restricted to each insurer’s facilities and out-of-network services require out-of-pocket payment [2]. In the early 2000s, this model excluded an estimated 50 million people—predominantly informal workers, the unemployed, and rural residents [3].

To extend coverage, Seguro Popular (SP) was launched in 2003 [5] and grew to ~ 53 million beneficiaries (~45% of the population) by 2018 [6]. Emphasizing prevention and primary care, SP financed services through Secretaría de Salud facilities, providing access to nearly 300 interventions and > 600 essential medicines [7, 8]. SP enrollees typically faced no point-of-service charges, whereas uninsured patients paid income-adjusted fees. Evidence links SP to increased service use and reduced catastrophic health spending among previously uninsured groups [3, 9]. Nonetheless, SP drew criticism for gaps in coverage for certain conditions, inconsistent quality, and inefficiencies tied to decentralized financing, uneven state-level implementation, bureaucratic hurdles, and continued out-of-pocket costs for specialized care and medicines [3, 6, 10]. Even in 2018, an estimated 16% of Mexicans remained uninsured [11]. Limited public resources and administrative barriers encouraged private care, exacerbating disparities across programs [12, 13].

Amid critiques of inefficiency, fragmentation, and corruption, SP was replaced by the Instituto de Salud para el Bienestar (INSABI) in January 2020. INSABI centralized financing and regulatory oversight, eliminated formal enrollment, and introduced a unified financing approach intended to remove point-of-service fees [2, 3]. Early implementation was heterogeneous—26 states adopted the federal model while 6 maintained decentralized arrangements—and coincided with a ~ 17-percentage-point decline in self-reported coverage between 2018 and 2020 [4]. By 2022, only ~13% reported affiliation with INSABI, and private ambulatory care accounted for roughly two-thirds of visits, suggesting shortfalls in public provision [14].

In May 2023, a federal decree dissolved INSABI and reassigned its functions to IMSS-Bienestar, a service network distinct from the contributory IMSS insurer [15]. Although INSABI no longer exists, examining coverage and utilization during the 2018–2021 transition remains essential. This interval attempted to replace a decentralized, state-financed scheme with a centrally funded model and revealed persistent challenges—including drug-supply disruptions, shrinking coverage, and increased reliance on private providers [14]—that IMSS-Bienestar must now address. Documenting the magnitude and distribution of coverage and use during this period provides a baseline to evaluate whether IMSS-Bienestar can overcome constraints that limited both SP and INSABI and deliver stronger protection for Mexico’s aging population.

Focusing on older adults is policy-relevant because Mexico is aging rapidly and this group accounts for a growing share of health-care need [16]. The burden of chronic disease rises with age, implying frequent ambulatory contact and sustained need for medicines and monitoring [17]. Recent national surveys (2018–2022) document persistently low and unequal access to public care among adults ≥60 years, with sociodemographic gradients becoming more salient following the SP-INSABI transition [18]. Older adults often assemble hybrid care pathways across schemes and sectors; even among social-security affiliates, private services are common (e.g., in ENSANUT 2022, 33.5% of IMSS and 40.8% of ISSSTE enrollees reported regular private use) [3], citing shorter waits, convenience/proximity, and perceived medicine availability [19]. These strategies help navigate bottlenecks but shift costs to out-of-pocket payments and fragment continuity for chronic conditions.

Despite these patterns, longitudinal, population-based analyses of how older adults fared during this policy transition remain scarce. Using nationally representative MHAS data, we (1) quantify changes in self-reported insurance coverage and site of care between 2018 and 2021 among adults aged ≥50 years (2); identify sociodemographic and health-related correlates of losing coverage; and (3) estimate how loss of coverage relates to provider choice in 2021 and to switching from public to private providers between waves, controlling for age, sex, locality size, education, self-rated financial status, and physician-diagnosed conditions. We hypothesized that (H1) uninsurance increased during 2018–2021, with disproportionate losses among residents of small localities, individuals with low schooling, and those reporting poor finances; (H2) persons who became uninsured had markedly lower odds of using Social Security or Secretaría de Salud facilities—relative to private providers—in 2021; and (H3) among those who used public services in 2018, loss of insurance was associated with a higher probability of switching to private care by 2021.

Methods

Mexican Health and Aging Study (MHAS)

MHAS is an ongoing, nationally-representative cohort of adults in Mexico that began in 2001 with 15 186 respondents aged ≥ 50 years and their spouses/partners, irrespective of age [20]. Follow-up interviews were completed in 2003, 2012, 2015, 2018, and 2021. To preserve representativeness, MHAS added refresher cohorts of adults aged 50–59 in 2012 and 50–55 in 2018, along with their spouses. No additional sample refresh occurred in 2021.

Sample selection

We used data from Wave 5 (2018) and Wave 6 (2021) of the Mexican Health and Aging Study (MHAS). Wave 5 provides a pre-reform baseline at the end of the Seguro Popular period, whereas Wave 6 was fielded during INSABIs implementation. This pairing brackets the policy transition and allows us to observe within-person changes in insurance entitlement and provider use using identical question wording across waves.

Of the 17,114 respondents interviewed in 2018, 14,499 were successfully re-interviewed in 2021. Limiting the cohort to those who were aged ≥ 50 years at the 2021 wave left 14,085 individuals. We then excluded 1,326 cases with missing data on insurance status (in either wave) or on key covariates (education, hypertension, diabetes, heart attack, stroke, arthritis, COVID-19 history, or self-rated financial status). The resulting analytic cohort comprised 12,759 participants with complete information (Fig. S1).

Measures

Health-insurance status

At each wave, MHAS asks, “Do you have the right to medical services from … ?” and lists the principal public and private schemes. For descriptive analyses, we classify respondents who report a single entitlement into four categories: No insurance; Social Security (IMSS, ISSSTE, PEMEX, SEDENA/SEMAR); Secretaría de Salud coverage (Seguro Popular in 2018/INSABI in 2021); and Other (private insurance and not specified institutional schemes). For inferential analyses, the exposure is a binary indicator of entitlement: insured (any reported scheme—Seguro Popular or INSABI, IMSS, ISSSTE, PEMEX, SEDENA/SEMAR) vs uninsured. Because the Other category is heterogeneous and represents < 1% of observations, we report it in descriptives for transparency but exclude it from multivariable models to preserve model stability.

Healthcare provider type

Respondents were asked, “In the last 12 months, how many times have you visited or consulted a doctor or other health professional?” Those reporting ≥1 visit then indicated where they obtained that care. Our utilization outcome captures ambulatory (outpatient) care only. Use in analyses

For descriptive statistics we display all four categories. In Model 2, the outcome is provider type in 2021 with three levels—Private (reference), Social Security, Secretaría de Salud; the Other category is excluded due to low frequency and heterogeneity. In Model 3, we collapse Social Security and Secretaría de Salud into a single “Public” category and model the transition public to private provider between 2018 and 2021 (excluding Other and cases without visits).

Demographic and socioeconomic covariates

Age (continuous, 2021 interview), sex, locality size ( < 2 500; 2 500–14 999; 15 000–99 999; ≥ 100 000 inhabitants), educational attainment (no schooling; 1–6 years; ≥ 7 years), and self-rated financial status. Financial status derives from the item “How would you rate your current financial situation?” (very good/excellent, good, fair, poor, very poor) and is recoded as good (very good/excellent + good), fair, and poor (poor + very poor).

Health conditions

Self-reported, physician-diagnosed diabetes, hypertension, heart attack, stroke, arthritis, and COVID-19 (each coded yes/no). All covariates are taken from the 2021 interview so that they reflect participants’ status over the 2018–2021 interval during which insurance changes occurred.

Analysis plan

We conducted a longitudinal cohort study using panel data from MHAS Wave 5 (2018; baseline) and Wave 6 (2021; follow-up). The same respondents were re-interviewed at both waves, allowing estimation of within-person transitions in insurance entitlement (remained insured vs became uninsured) and changes in provider type between 2018 and 2021. We first produced weighted descriptive statistics—means or proportions—for insurance coverage, healthcare-provider use, sociodemographic variables, and health conditions, stratified by 2021 insurance category. We compared groups using ANOVA for continuous variables and Pearson χ2 tests for categorical variables.

We then exploited the longitudinal design by fitting three regression models of individual transitions between 2018 and 2021 to address our specific research questions: (a) determinants of losing insurance (Model 1), (b) the association between losing insurance and provider type in 2021 (Model 2), and (c) the association between insurance loss and switching from public to private care between 2018 and 2021 (Model 3).

Each regression model draws from the baseline cohort (12,759 participants) but has a model-specific N due to outcome definitions and visit requirements; these sample sizes are reported in each model’s section.

Model 1: Determinants of insurance loss

We fit a binary logistic regression among respondents insured in 2018 (N = 11,586). The outcome was loss of coverage by 2021. Covariates (sex, age, locality size, education, self-rated financial status, and physician-diagnosed hypertension, diabetes, COVID-19, heart attack, stroke, and arthritis) were taken from the 2021 interview so that predictors reflect status over the 2018–2021 exposure window during which insurance changes could occur.

Model 2: Loss of insurance and provider type in 2021

We fit a multinomial logistic regression to examine how losing insurance by 2021 is associated with the type of healthcare provider used in 2021. Provider categories were (i) private care (reference), (ii) Social-Security institutions, and (iii) Secretaría de Salud facilities. Covariates were identical to Model 1 (sex, age, locality size, education, financial status, and chronic conditions).

The analytic sample comprised 8,632 respondents who (a) were insured in 2018 and (b) reported at least one medical visit in 2021 (Fig. S2).

Model 3: Loss of insurance and shift from public to private care (2018 → 2021)

We estimated a binary logistic regression to evaluate whether losing insurance between 2018 and 2021 is associated with switching from public to private providers. Provider type in each wave was dichotomized as Public (care obtained in Social Security or Secretaría de Salud facilities) vs Private (all other nonpublic providers); the rare “Other” category was excluded. Covariates matched Models 1–2 and were measured in 2021. The analytic sample comprised 4,667 respondents who were insured in 2018, used public care in 2018, and reported ≥1 visit in 2021 (Fig. S3).

Results

Sample characteristics (2021 cross-section; Table 2)

In 2021, 26% of respondents reported no insurance (n = 3,303), 62% reported Social Security coverage (IMSS/ISSSTE/PEMEX/SEDENA-SEMAR; n = 7,897), 11% reported Secretaría de Salud coverage (INSABI; n = 1,397), and < 1% reported Other (n = 162). Mean age differed modestly across groups (ANOVA p < 0.01), with Social Security enrollees older on average than the uninsured. Women were the majority in all groups (χ2 p < 0.01).

Residence, schooling, and finances varied by insurance status (all χ2 p < 0.01). Uninsured respondents more often lived in small localities ( < 2,500 inhabitants: 35% vs 11% among Social Security) and had ≤6 years of education (71.8% vs 50.6% among Social Security). They also more frequently reported poor finances (10% vs 4.8% among Social Security). Chronic conditions were more commonly reported among insured groups (e.g., hypertension: 52% Social Security vs 36% uninsured; diabetes: 29% vs 21%).

Provider use in 2021 was patterned by entitlement. Among the uninsured, private care predominated (80.5%), with limited use of Secretaría de Salud facilities (12.5%). Among Social Security enrollees, 60.2% reported Social Security facilities and 36.5% private. Among INSABI, 41.1% reported Secretaría de Salud facilities, 45.0% private, and 10.8% Social Security (Table 2). Changes in coverage and provider use, 2018 → 2021 (Fig. 1)

From 2018 to 2021, the share uninsured rose from 9% to 26%, while Seguro Popular/INSABI coverage declined from 30% to 11%. Social Security coverage was relatively stable (59% → 62%). Over the same interval, private provider use increased from 31% to 48%, whereas use of Secretaría de Salud facilities decreased from 18% to 8%, and Social Security facility use moved from 49% to 41% (Fig. 1). Multivariable analyses (Table 3)

Model 1: Factors associated with losing insurance, 2018 → 2021

After adjustment, smaller locality size, lower schooling, poorer finances, and absence of selected chronic conditions were associated with higher odds of insurance loss (Panel A). Relative to residents of areas ≥100,000 inhabitants, those in smaller localities had progressively higher odds of becoming uninsured (AOR = 1.79 for 15,000– 99,999; 2.40 for 2,500– 14,999; 3.50 for < 2,500; all). Compared with no schooling, 1–6 years (AOR = 0.60) and ≥7 years (AOR = 0.29) were associated with lower odds. Older age was also associated with lower odds (per year AOR = 0.97). Fair (AOR = 1.58) and poor (AOR = 2.50) financial status were associated with higher odds. Reporting hypertension (AOR = 0.65) or diabetes (AOR = 0.76) was associated with lower odds of insurance loss. Other conditions were not statistically significant in this model.

Model 2: Losing insurance and provider utilization in 2021

Becoming uninsured was associated with lower relative risk of using public providers in 2021 compared with private care (Panel B). Versus private (reference), the RRR for Social Security facilities was 0.03 and for Secretaría de Salud facilities 0.61 among those who became uninsured. Smaller localities were associated with lower Social Security vs private use (RRR range 0.79–0.59) and higher Secretaría de Salud vs private use (RRR range 1.74–3.24). Higher schooling was associated with lower Secretaría de Salud vs private use (≥7 years RRR = 0.37). Poor finances were associated with higher Secretaría de Salud vs private use (RRR = 2.46). Older age showed a small inverse association with Secretaría de Salud vs private (RRR = 0.98 per year). Respondents with hypertension or diabetes showed a higher relative risk of using public providers in 2021. In contrast, having had COVID-19 was associated with a lower relative risk of using public providers vs private (RRR = 0.73 for Social Security; 0.62 for Secretaría de Salud).

Model 3: Losing insurance and switching from public to private care, 2018 → 2021

Among insured respondents who used public care in 2018, becoming uninsured by 2021 was associated with higher odds of Public → Private switching (AOR = 6.16; Panel C). Age had a small positive association (AOR = 1.01, p < 0.05). Hypertension (AOR = 0.67) and diabetes (AOR = 0.64) were associated with lower odds of switching, whereas a COVID-19 was associated with higher odds (AOR = 1.48). Locality size, sex, education, and financial status were not materially associated with switching in this restricted sample.

Discussion

This longitudinal assessment of the Mexican Health and Aging Study shows that replacing Seguro Popular with the Instituto de Salud para el Bienestar coincided with pronounced shifts in coverage and service use among adults ≥50 between 2018 and 2021. Two changes stand out. First, self-reported uninsurance more than doubled—from 9% to 26%—and 69% of the 2018 uninsured remained so three years later (Fig. 1; Table 2). Second, utilization shifted toward the private sector: private providers rose from 32% to 49%, while Secretaría de Salud visits fell from 20% to 9% and Social Security contacts declined from 46% to 39%.

Administrative records mirror these trends. CONEVAL reported that the share lacking access to public insurance increased from 16% (2018) to 28% (2020) and 39% (2022), leaving 50.4 million without formal coverage [21]. By 2022, 13.1% identified with INSABI or its predecessor SP, 46.5% with Social Security, and 0.9% with private insurance [21]. Our cohort shows the steepest decline among former SP affiliates: only 31% entered INSABI, whereas 53% were uninsured by 2021 (Fig. 2).

Part of the coverage drop is semantic/perceptual. SP conferred explicit affiliation and a printed identification document; INSABI offered an implicit right to services without a credential [22]. Qualitative interviews in 2021 found many older adults interpreted the absence of a card as loss of entitlement, especially without IMSS/ISSSTE numbers [23]. In Levesque’s access framework, credentialing affects approachability [24]. Affiliation yields realized access only if perceived; de jure eligibility unrecognized by users functions like no coverage. Lack of a credential is therefore consistent with higher reported uninsurance and lower Secretaría de Salud use.

Availability and affordability also weakened. The Strengthening Medical Care Program (FAM) recorded 2.3 million consultations in 2018 versus 1.2 million in 2021 (−49.7%), and specialty consultations fell from 362 to 168 per 1,000 inhabitants [23]. Public financing represented 50.1% of current health expenditure in 2021 [25], well below OECD comparators (62–86%) [26], consistent with fiscal constraints and medicine shortages. COVID-19 further strained public capacity; many patients turned to faster or more reliable private clinics despite higher costs [27]. Our multinomial model aligns with this behavior: losing insurance was associated with a markedly lower relative risk of using Social-Security or Secretaría de Salud facilities versus private care.

Provider choice varies by health need. ENSANUT 2021 indicates only about one-quarter of Mexicans seek public care for acute problems, whereas a larger share—especially with chronic conditions—continue using public services even without social security [28]. We observe the same: after losing insurance, acute visits shifted disproportionately to private clinics, while chronic-care contacts were more evenly split.

Insurance remains influential: a discrete-choice experiment attributed 36% of facility choice to insurance, alongside waiting times, distance, and service availability [29]. Related Mexican studies describe shorter waits, higher satisfaction, and better perceived medicine access in private settings, while documenting public-sector supply disruptions [19, 30]. Technical quality in pharmacy-adjacent clinics is mixed, implying trade-offs between rapid access and continuity/appropriateness [31]. Thus, the post-2018 turn to private providers reflects not only coverage loss but also perceived availability, convenience, and administrative simplicity.

Institutional design differentiates social security affiliates from those without social security. Social security schemes link comprehensive benefits to closed networks; those without social security rely on the Secretaría de Salud network funded by general revenues, where benefits and availability—particularly for high-cost tertiary care—are often thinner. Non-social-security populations therefore use private providers (including pharmacy-adjacent clinics) more often, citing proximity and speed, at the cost of out-of-pocket (OOP) payment [31]. National data show affiliates use their own networks more, while non-affiliates and higher-income groups rely more on private care; perceived lower public quality predicts greater private use [32]. These institutional contrasts shape opportunity sets.

Financial implications are substantial. Households without insurance are more prone to catastrophic, impoverishing, or otherwise excessive health spending [8]. Budget-incidence data show catastrophic/impoverishing expenditure rose from 3.0% (2018) to 3.6% (2020) and settled at 3.3% (2022) [33]—a ~10% relative increase affecting hundreds of thousands of households. Policies that cushion uninsured families (e.g., targeted subsidies, voucher schemes, or investments lowering OOP exposure in public services) are warranted.

Distributional changes were unequal. Residents of localities < 2,500, individuals with ≤6 years of schooling, and those reporting poor finances were more likely to lose insurance (Table 3). These groups had gained most from SP after 2004—when uninsurance among older Mexicans more than halved and catastrophic expenses declined [34]—and prior surveys consistently show higher OOP and catastrophic spending among uninsured versus insured households [8]. Relevance for older adults. Multimorbidity and medication-dependent care are common in later life; recent national analyses report cardiometabolic multimorbidity in 45% of adults ≥60 [35]. Access to public services among older adults remains modest (43.8% in 2022) and varies by urban residence, literacy, and partnership [18]. While private sites often offer shorter waits and higher satisfaction [19], reliance on private care exposes older adults to recurrent OOP spending [33], creating affordability risks that are especially consequential in later life.

International context. Reforms are most effective when entitlement is unambiguous (credentialed), benefits are explicit/enforceable, and public financing and delivery capacity expand alongside coverage. Thailand’s Universal Coverage Scheme paired near-universal entitlement with budget/payment reforms and reduced OOP spending [36]. Chile’s AUGE/GES embedded explicit guarantees (benefits, maximum waiting times, financial protection) and mobilized public funds, improving access/timeliness for prioritized conditions [37]. By contrast, Ghana’s NHIS and Indonesia’s JKN show that enrollment/renewal frictions, waits, medicine availability, and private-sector pull can sustain OOP exposure despite broad coverage [38, 39]. Turkey’s Health Transformation Program initially reduced catastrophic spending, with later partial reversals underscoring the need for stable financing and supply-side resilience [40]. These cases support our interpretation: perceived entitlement and public-sector availability drive realized use; clarifying affiliation, defining guaranteed benefits, securing medicines/appointments, and selectively contracting private providers are central post-reform tasks.

Limitations include self-reported utilization (recall error), focus on outpatient rather than hospital care, and lack of direct quality or distance metrics. Self-reported insurance should be read as perceived entitlement rather than verified enrollment; absence of an INSABI credential likely led some to report no insurance despite de jure eligibility, biasing coverage levels downward yet remaining behaviorally meaningful if such individuals sought care as uninsured (e.g., higher private use).

INSABIs functions were absorbed by IMSS-Bienestar in 2023 [41]. Early regulations signal a return to explicit credentialing and moves toward integrated purchasing. Whether perceived entitlement and public-sector availability improve will depend on stable financing, reliable drug supply, and clear communication of eligibility rules.

Conclusion

Between 2018 and 2021 the proportion of older Mexicans without formal insurance more than doubled, and provider choice shifted markedly toward the private sector—especially among rural, low-educated, and economically constrained adults. Policy priorities include: (i) restoring clear, verifiable affiliation mechanisms so eligibility is perceived and usable; (ii) sustaining public financing for first-contact/outpatient services with priority to small/rural communities; and (iii) addressing access barriers (communication about entitlement, medicine supply) to support equitable use of public services. These steps provide a path to regain lost ground toward universal coverage.
