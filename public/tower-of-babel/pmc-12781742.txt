Tetrahydromagnolol targets TRIM38 to mediate PANoptosis in cancer cells and has the potential for synergistic cancer therapy
Authors: Xiaoqing Gu, Lin Li, Ting Duan, Lijun Jin, Qinsong Sheng, Quan Gao, Mengting Chen, Xinbing Sui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTetrahydromagnolol (THM) is a compound isolated from Magnolia officinalis with unique chemical structure and composition. However, its anticancer effect of THM and the underlying molecular mechanisms remain unclear.MethodFirstly, the anticancer effects of THM on different cancer cell lines in vitro were investigated. Subsequently, the antitumor activity of THM was further evaluated in vivo and in vitro using colorectal and lung cancer models. This assessment involved the effects of THM on cell viability, apoptosis, proliferation, cell cycle progression, and tumor growth inhibition. In addition, the anticancer molecular mechanisms of THM were determined by RNA sequencing, western blot, immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, CETSA, and SPR. Meanwhile, the effects of THM on organelles were evaluated by measuring endoplasmic reticulum stress, mitochondrial membrane potential damage, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and calcium ion concentration. Finally, the efficacy of THM in combination with conventional anticancer drugs for colorectal cancer treatment was evaluated in vivo.ResultsThe results showed that THM had a significant anticancer activity in colorectal cancer and lung cancer both in vitro and in vivo. THM significantly inhibited cell proliferation and induced PANoptosis-like cell death through GSDME mediated pyroptosis, CASP3 mediated apoptosis, and MLKL mediated necroptosis. In addition, the anticancer potential of THM was also related to elevation of endoplasmic reticulum stress, mitochondrial membrane potential destruction, and increase of ROS and intracellular calcium concentration. Mechanically, we found that THM could directly bind to triplet motif-containing 38 (TRIM38) and induced its upregulation at mRNA and protein levels. Importantly, knockdown of TRIM38 remarkably rescued the anticancer effects of THM and PANoptosis induced by THM treatment, suggesting that TRIM38 played a key role in mediating the antitumor activity of THM. In addition, THM showed significant synergistic therapeutic effects when used in combination with conventional anticancer strategies (Cetuximab, FOLFOX, and FOLFIRI regimens) for colorectal cancer treatment.ConclusionOur data suggest that THM exerts its anticancer potential by inducing TRIM38-dependent PANoptosis and it also has synergistic antitumor effects in combination with conventional anticancer strategies. THM will be a promising candidate drug used alone or in combination with other anticancer regimens for cancer treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40164-025-00734-4.

Background

Cancer is a significant public health issue on a global scale, which seriously affects human life [1]. Over the years, plant-derived and other natural products are gaining increasing attention due to wide anticancer potentials, specifically being looked for development of alternative therapeutic intervention for advanced cancer types [2–4]. Houpo, derived from the bark of Magnolia officinalis, has been traditionally employed as a herbal medicine for the management of various diseases including cancer [5]. Magnolia officinalis serves as a treasure trove of numerous biologically active ingredients, with at least 255 distinct components reported to date [6, 7], among which the principal active ingredients—honokiol and magnolol—have been documented to exhibit anticancer activity in multiple cancer types [8–12]. Tetrahydromagnolol (THM) is also derived from Magnolia officinalis, but there have been no reports on its anticancer effects against colorectal cancer and lung cancer.

PANoptosis, as an inflammatory form of programmed cell death (PCD), is initiated by specific triggers and modulated by the PANoptosome complex. PANoptosis is generally believed to integrate features of Pyroptosis, Apoptosis, and Necroptosis, but cannot be fully comprehended by any of these pathways alone [13–17]. To date, several PANoptosomes are known, each equipped with unique sensors and regulatory proteins. The intricate PANoptosome network involves several proteins, including Z-DNA binding protein 1 (ZBP1), absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2), receptor-interacting serine-threonine kinase 1 (RIPK1), and NLR family pyrin domain containing 12 (NLRP12) [18–22]. These proteins differ from one another in terms of their composition and the downstream molecular pathways that govern the execution of cell death, and this difference results in various phenotypic outcomes [23, 24]. PANoptosis is a complex and multifaceted immune response that can use multiple cell death mechanisms to eliminate cancer cells and is expected to provide new approaches for the development of more effective anticancer strategies [25, 26].

This study aims to investigate the anticancer potential of THM, elucidate its possible antitumor mechanisms, and determine its synergistic therapeutic effects when used in combination with conventional anticancer strategies. Here, it was shown that THM had a broad anticancer activity and significantly inhibited cell proliferation and induced PANoptosis-like cell death through GSDME mediated pyroptosis, CASP3 mediated apoptosis, and MLKL mediated necroptosis. Mechanically, triplet motif-containing 38 (TRIM38) was found to be a key critical mediator for THM-induced PANoptosis. In addition, THM was demonstrated to show significant synergistic effects when combined with conventional anticancer strategies (Cetuximab, FOLFOX, and FOLFIRI regimens) in colorectal cancer (CRC) xenograft mice model. Taken together, these results suggested that THM targeted TRIM38 to mediate PANoptosis in colorectal cancer and lung cancer cells and had the synergistic anticancer potential.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and reagents

All cell lines were obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank (Shanghai, China). HT29 cells were maintained in McCoy’s 5 A medium; NCM460, HCT116, A549 and H1299 cells in RPMI-1640; HELF, Bcap-37, MDA-MB-231, CAPAN-1, SW1990, KU1919, U87, HGC27 and SNU449 cells in high-glucose DMEM. All culture media were fortified with 10% FBS and 100 µg/mL penicillin-streptomycin mixture. The cells were maintained in a 37 °C incubator with a humidified environment and 5% carbon dioxide supply. THM (C18H22O2) was purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Cetuximab was purchased from Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany). 5-Fluorouracil (5-FU) (HY-9006), Irinotecan (HY-16562), Calcium levofolinate (HY-13667), Oxaliplatin (HY-17371), Necrostatin-1 (Nec-1) (HY-15760), Z-VAD-FMK (HY-16658B), Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) (HY-100579), Chloroquine (CQ) (HY-175889A) were acquired from MCE (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA).

Organoids construction and maintenance experiments

Human colorectal tissue samples were removed from the primary preservation solution and washed three times in DPBS containing 4% penicillin–streptomycin. The tissues were then minced into ≈ 0.5 mm³ fragments with surgical scissors and digested gently on a shaker at 37 °C for 30–60 min to release single cells. Digestion was stopped by adding serum-containing DMEM, and the suspension was pipetted to homogeneity. After filtration through a 100 μm cell strainer, the resulting filtrate was subjected to centrifugation at 400 × g (≈ 2000 rpm), under 4 °C conditions for a duration of 5 min. Red-blood-cell lysis buffer was added, mixed by pipetting, and incubated on ice for 2 min; 4 mL DMEM with 10% FBS was then added to neutralise the lysis. Cells were pelleted, counted, and mixed with Matrigel on ice. Thirty-microlitre droplets were plated and allowed to polymerise at 37 °C for 10 min. Complete organoid culture medium was then carefully pipetted along the edge of each well to cover the droplets, and cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Medium was refreshed every 2 ~ 3 days and organoids were passaged weekly [27].

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

The CCK-8 reagent was procured from Meilunbio (MA0218-5, DaLian, China). The cell viability after treatment with various concentrations of THM was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. The Half inhibitory concentration (IC50) refers to the concentration of an agent that inhibited cell growth by 50% within 24 h and was determined through analysis with GraphPad Prism 9.5 software.

Cell apoptosis assay

After cells were treated with THM for 24 h, they were collected, stained with Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (40302ES60, Yeasen, Shanghai, China), and the cell death rate was determined by flow cytometry (Beckman, USA). The data from the flow cytometry were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Colony formation assay

Cells were evenly plated into 6 cm culture dishes at a seeding density of 5 × 10³ cells per dish, and after overnight adherence, they were exposed to THM for a 24 h treatment period, followed by further cultivation for approximately 14 days. At the end of this period, the cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde solution and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution. Then, each well was photographed with a flat-bed scanner at 600 dpi. Images were analyzed with ImageJ using the “Analyze Particles” function. The area (mm²) of every colony ≥ 0.02 mm² was measured and summed to give the “total colony area” for that well.

Calcein/PI cell activity and cytotoxicity assay

An appropriate number of cells and colorectal cancer organoids were seeded into 6-well and 24-well plates, respectively, and allowed to attach overnight. After exposure to the indicated concentrations of THM for 24 h (cells) or 48 h (organoids), the cultures were stained following the manufacturer’s instructions of the Calcein/PI Cell Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, C2015M). Following a 30 min incubation at 37 °C in the dark, the staining was visualized under a fluorescence microscope.

Western blot and antibodies

After THM treatment, RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013) containing PMSF (Beyotime, ST506) and phosphatase inhibitor (Coolaber, SL1087) were added to lyse the cells and obtain the cell protein extract. The concentration of proteins was meticulously quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, P0012). Following this, Subsequently, the proteins in the samples were denatured with SDS (Beyotime, P0015) and then subjected to western blot analysis. The antibodies involved in this study are as follows: β-actin (ABclonal, AC028, 1:2000), CDK6 (Cell Signaling Technology (CST), 13331S,1;1000), CDK4 (CST, 12790, 1:1000), Cyclin D1 (CST, 55506, 1:1000), GSDME (Abcam, ab215191, 1:1000), AIM2 (Proteintech, 20590-1-AP, 1:1000), ZBP1 (Proteintech, 13285-1-AP, 1:1000), p-MLKL (Affinity, AF7420, 1:500), p-RIPK1 (Proteintech, 28252-1-AP, 1:1000), PARP (CST, 9532, 1:1000), Cleaved-PARP (CST, 5625, 1:000), Cleaved-CASP3 (Abcam, ab2302, 1;500), TRIM38 (Proteintech, 13405-1-AP, 1:500), BIP (CST, 3177, 1:1000), CHOP (CST, 2895, 1:1000), Anti-mouse IgG (CST, 7076, 1:1000), and Anti-rabbit IgG (CST, 7074, 1:1000).

LDH release assay

According to the manufacturer’s instructions, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) detection kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, C0016) was used to detect the release of LDH after cells had been treated with various concentrations of THM for 24 h.

RNA extraction and sequencing

RNA was extracted from cells stimulated with or without THM using TRIzol (Accurate Biology, AG21101). Each treatment group consisted of three biologically independent replicates. The samples were submitted to Hangzhou Lianchuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for whole-transcriptome sequencing. All downstream analyses—including differential expression, GSEA and KEGG pathway enrichment—were performed on the same set of three biological replicates. The sequencing data were uploaded to the Lianchuan Biological Cloud Platform (https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool) for online analysis.

Immunofluorescence (IF) microscopy

For the colocalization analysis of PANoptosome components with ASC speck formations: HCT116 and A549 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well in a glass bottom cell culture dish (Biosharp, BS-20-GJM) and cultured overnight. After treatment with the specified concentration of THM, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Solarbio, T8200) for 30 min, and blocked with PBS solution containing 5% BSA for 1 h. Subsequently, the cells were incubated at 4 °C overnight with AlexaFluor594-conjugated anti-CASP8 antibody (Santa, sc-56070 AF594, 1:100), anti-RIPK3 (Proteintech, 17563-1-AP, 1:100), or anti-AIM2 (Proteintech, 20590-1-AP, 1:400). Except for the cells treated with the AlexaFluor594-conjugated anti-CASP8 antibody, the other cells were incubated with AlexaFluor555-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (CST, 4413S, 1:500) at room temperature for 1 h. The cells were then incubated at 4 °C overnight with anti-ASC antibody (Proteintech, 67494-1-1g, 1:100). On the next day, the cells were incubated with AlexaFluor488-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (CST, 4408S, 1:500) at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, the nuclei were stained with DAPI (Coolaber, SL7101, 1:1000). Fluorescence was examined using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Olympus FV3000).

For the colocalization analysis of THM-Cy5 with TRIM38 protein: Glass-bottom cell culture dishes were seeded with cells, which were then cultured for an overnight period. HCT116 and A549 cells were treated with 75 µM or 90 µM THM-Cy5 for 2 h, respectively. Following the previously outlined steps for fixation, permeabilization, and blocking, the cells were then subjected to overnight incubation at 4 °C with the anti-TRIM38 antibody (Proteintech, 13405-1-AP, 1:100). Next, the cells were incubated with AlexaFluor488-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (CST, 4412S, 1:500) at room temperature for 1 h and stained with DAPI for 15 min. Fluorescence was examined using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Olympus FV3000).

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

Isolation of total RNA was meticulously executed utilizing TRIzol reagent, strictly following the manufacturer’s protocol. The ensuing cDNA was synthesized employing TransScript kit (Vazyme, R312), in accordance with their detailed instructions. mRNA expression profiling was conducted using the SYBR Green PCR kit (Vazyme, Q711-02). The endogenous control was β-actin, and relative quantification was determined by applying the comparative ΔΔCt method. The sequences of the primers utilized in study are delineated in Table S2.

Mitochondrial membrane potential assay

The assessment of mitochondrial membrane potential was conducted utilizing the JC-1 assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, C2003S). After 24 h of THM treatment, the procedures were carried out according to the kit’s instructions and the detection was performed on a flow cytometer.

Reactive oxygen species assay

The reactive oxygen species (ROS) assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, S0033S) was used to measure intracellular ROS levels in cells after 24 h of THM treatment by flow cytometry. After incubation with the DCFH-DA staining solution, the acquired data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Measurement of intracellular free Ca2+ concentrations

Intracellular Ca²+ levels were measured using Fluo-Calcium Assay (Beyotime Biotechnology, S1061S). Cells at a density of 3 × 105 were inoculated and cultured overnight. After 24 h of THM treatment, staining working solution was prepared with detection buffer at a ratio of Fluo-4 AM to lysis enhancer of 1:500. After staining and washing of cells, staining results were evaluated by fluorescence microscopy (Fluo-4 AM exhibits green fluorescence, Ex/Em = 490/525nm) to determine Ca2+ content.

Molecular docking study

The structure file of the small-molecule compound tetrahydromagnolol was retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and converted from SDF to PDB format with Open Babel 2.3.2. The receptor protein TRIM38 was searched in the UniProt database, hydrogen atoms were added and charges were balanced by AutoDockTools, and both the receptor and the ligand were converted into pdbqt format. Molecular docking between the receptor and the ligand was performed with AutoDock Vina 1.1.2, the docking results were analyzed by Protein–Ligand Interaction Profiler (PLIP), and the resulting complex was visualized with PyMOL.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

The experiment was performed according to the method reported in the literature [28, 29]. Briefly, cells were seeded into two 10 cm dishes (∼3 × 10⁶ cells per dish) and grown to ~ 80% confluence. Monolayers were detached with a cell scraper, pooled into a 15 mL tube, and pelleted by centrifugation at 300 × g, 4 °C for 5 min. After discarding the supernatant, the cell pellet was reconstituted in 1 mL of pre-chilled 1× PBS supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Lysis was achieved by three rapid freeze–thaw cycles (30 s in liquid nitrogen followed by immediate thawing at room temperature). The lysate was transferred to a 1.5 mL tube and cleared by centrifugation at 20,000 × g, 4 °C for 20 min. The resulting supernatant was split into two equal aliquots and incubated with an equal volume of either DMSO or THM for 2 h at room temperature. Each mixture was then distributed into seven 0.2 mL PCR tubes and subjected to a temperature gradient on a PCR thermocycler: 43, 46, 49, 52, 55, 58 and 61 °C for HCT116 cells, or 55, 60, 65, 70, 75, 80 and 85 °C for A549 cells (3 min per temperature). After heating, tubes were held at room temperature for 3 min and immediately placed on ice. All samples were transferred to fresh 1.5mL tubes, centrifuged at 20,000 × g, 4 °C for 20 min, and the supernatants were mixed with SDS loading buffer. The protein samples were denatured at 100 °C for 10 min and stored at −80 °C prior to western blot analysis.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis

SPR analysis was conducted using the instrument BiacoreT200 (Cytiva), Series S Sensor Chip CM5 (Cytiva, 29149603), and Amine Coupling Kit (Cytiva, BR-1000-50) at 25 °C. The CM5 sensor chip was activated and coated with recombinant human TRIM38 protein (NCBI: 10475, designed and synthesized by IPODIX, China). THM at various concentrations (50, 25, 12.5, 6.25, and 0 µM) was injected into the chip to study its binding interactions. The association and dissociation phases were analyzed in a phosphate buffer with 5% DMSO. Data was processed using Biacore T200 software.

shRNA knockdown

The lentiviral vector containing TRIM38 shRNA was purchased from Tsingke Biotechnology (Beijing, China). 6 cm culture dishes were seeded with cells at a concentration of 1.5 × 10⁶. After overnight attachment, the cells were transfected with shRNA at a dosage of 2.5 µg using Lipomaster 2000 Transfection Reagent (Vazyme, TL201) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The sequences of shRNAs used in this study are shown in Table S3.

Tumor xenografts in mice assay

The mice involved in this study were female BALB/c nude mice (3 ~ 5 weeks old) provided by Shanghai Slack Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd.

For the treatment of subcutaneous xenograft BALB/c nude mouse models with THM alone, 5 × 106 HCT116 or A549 cells were subcutaneously implanted into the right side of the mice. Tumor incidence rate was 33/40 mice: ≈83%; 30/40 mice: ≈75%. Treatment initiation occurred when tumors reached an approximate volume of 30 mm³ (20–40 mm³), the mice were randomly assigned to control, 30 mg/kg THM, 60 mg/kg THM, or 120 mg/kg THM groups.

For the combined treatment of subcutaneous xenograft BALB/c nude mouse models with THM, 5 × 106 HCT116 cells were injected subcutaneously into the right side of the athymic nude mice to establish tumors. Tumor incidence rate was 74/80 mice: ≈92%. Treatment initiation occurred when tumors reached an approximate volume of 30 mm³ (20–40 mm³), the mice were randomized into eight treatment groups: control, mid-dose THM, Cetuximab, FOLFOX regimen (5-FU/Oxaliplatin/Calcium levofolinate), FOLFIRI regimen (5-FU/Irinotecan/Calcium levofolinate), THM + Cetuximab, THM + FOLFOX, and THM + FOLFIRI. The mid-dose THM group received 60 mg/kg every other day, and the Cetuximab group received 1 mg/mouse daily. The FOLFOX group received 5-FU (22.5 mg/kg) and Calcium levofolinate (33.75 mg/kg) on days 1 and 8, and Oxaliplatin (9.375 mg/kg) on days 2 and 9. The FOLFIRI group received 5-FU (22.5 mg/kg) and Calcium levofolinate (33.75 mg/kg) on days 1 and 8, and Irinotecan (15 mg/kg) on days 2 and 9 [30].

The above protocol involves measuring tumor volume and body weight every other day. Tumor volume was calculated as: (W2×L) / 2. According to the reported method, the average relative tumor volume (%) was calculated and presented as: (average tumor volume of the experimental group) / (average tumor volume of the control group); the combination index was calculated as: (average relative tumor volume A) × (average relative tumor volume B) / (average relative tumor volume A+B), a ratio > 1 indicates a synergistic effect, and a ratio < 1 indicates a less than additive effect [31].

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining assay

Tumor tissue samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and then subjected to gradient dehydration under a microscope, paraffin embedding, and slicing. Tumor sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval via pressure cooking in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% H₂O₂, followed by blocking non-specific binding with 10% goat serum. Primary antibodies were incubated with slides overnight at 4 °C, including Ki67 (CST, 9449, 1:1500), Cleaved-PARP (CST, 5625, 1:50), Cleaved-CASP3 (CST, 9664, 1:2000), AIM2 (Proteintech, 20590-1-AP, 1:100), p-MLKL (UpingBio, YP-Ab-10353, 1:200), p-RIPK1 (Proteintech, 28252-1-AP, 1:100) and TRIM38 (Proteintech, 13405-1-AP, 1:100). After washing, the sections were treated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. DAB (Solarbio, DA1010) staining was performed, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Images were captured using an automated slide scanning imaging system (Olympus VS200, Japan).

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining

Staining was performed using the Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining kit (Solarbio, G1120) following the instruction manual. Images were captured using an automated slide scanning imaging system.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis and graph plotting were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5. Differences between two groups were analyzed using a two-tailed unpaired t-test. Differences among three or more groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). For analyzing multiple groups and multiple factors, two-way ANOVA was employed. A significance level of p < 0.05 was assigned. Asterisks (*) were used to indicate the following p-values on the graphs: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

Results

THM possessed a significant anticancer activity in colorectal cancer and lung cancer both in vitro and in vivo

To identify the anticancer activity of THM, we initially assessed the antitumor efficacy of THM in several cancer cell lines. These cells included colorectal cancer (CRC) cells (HT29, HCT116), lung cancer cells (A549, H1299), breast cancer cells (Bcap-37, MDA-MB-231), pancreatic cancer cells (CAPAN-1, SW1990), bladder cancer cells (KU1919), glioma cells (U87), gastric cancer cells (HGC-27), hepatocellular carcinoma cells (SNU449). The results showed that THM possessed a broad-spectrum anticancer activity (Fig. S1, and Table S1). In this study, we further evaluated the anticancer potential of THM in CRC and lung cancer.

Next, to verify the antitumor activity of THM in vivo, we first established subcutaneous tumor-bearing nude mouse models of colorectal cancer using HCT116 cells in immuno-compromised athymic nude mice. THM groups were administered 30, 60, or 120 mg/kg via intraperitoneal injection once every two days (Fig. 1A). The results showed that after 17 days of THM treatment, the tumor growth rate in the treated groups significantly decreased in dose-dependent manner than that of their controls (Fig. 1B-C). The body weight of nude mice in each group did not significantly changes compared with that of the control group (Fig. 1D). Subsequently, adhering to the identical administration protocol, a subcutaneous tumor-bearing nude mouse model of lung cancer was established using A549 cells (Fig. 1E). Similarly, in THM administration group, the tumor growth rate significantly decreased in dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1F-H). During the treatment, the body weight of nude mice in each group did not significantly changes compared with that of the control group (Fig. 1I). Additionally, to further evaluate the safety of THM treatment in vivo, the H&E staining experiment (Fig. S2A) and blood biochemical analysis (Fig. S2B) were made. And the results suggested that THM had good and reliable safety in vivo.

Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) have been widely promoted as a drug screening platform for the development of novel cancer therapies. Furthermore, we established and maintained the organoids, which were obtained from two patients with diagnosed CRC. Next, the effect of THM on the growth of CRC organoids was observed in vitro. THM could significantly inhibit the viability of CRC organoids after the treatment with 0, 25 µM, 50 µM, and 75 µM THM for 24 and 48 h (Fig. S2C). Furthermore, the double staining experiment of live and dead cells was performed after 48 h of treatment with different concentrations of THM. As a result, the proportion of green fluorescence in organoids decreased and the proportion of red fluorescence increased, which indicated that THM inhibited the growth of CRC organoids (Fig. S2D).

Taken together, THM exhibited significant antitumor activity in a dose-dependent manner in nude mice bearing colorectal and lung cancer models, as well as CRC organoids.

THM suppressed cell viability in colorectal and lung cancer cells

To evaluate the effects of THM on the viability of colorectal cancer, lung cancer, and corresponding normal cells, CCK-8 assay results demonstrated that compared to human normal colonic epithelial cells (NCM460) and human embryonic lung fibroblasts (HELF), THM effectively inhibited the viability of colorectal cancer cells (HT29, HCT116) and lung cancer cells (A549, H1299) in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2A-B). THM has obvious cytotoxic and growth inhibition effects in CRC cells and lung cancer cells in a dose-dependent manner. HT29 and HCT116 were treated with 0, 25 µM, 50 µM, and 75 µM THM, A549 and H1299 were treated with 0, 30 µM, 60 µM, and 90 µM THM for 24 h. The gap between tumor cells became larger and cell death increased (Fig. 2C-D). Furthermore, the Calcein/PI cell activity and cytotoxicity kit were used to further investigate the effect of THM on cell viability. The green fluorescence ratio in HT29, HCT116, A549, and H1299 decreased while the red fluorescence ratio increased in response to THM treatment, which indicated that THM decreased cell viability in CRC and lung cancer cells (Fig. S3A-B). Flow cytometry showed the proportion of cell apoptosis was increased in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2E-H). Z-VAD-FMK is a membrane-transcending agent that acts as an irreversible pan-caspase inhibitor, which can attenuate the killing effect of drugs and reverse the occurrence of cell apoptosis by inhibiting caspase [32, 33]. Next, we used Z-VAD-FMK to determine whether THM-induced cell death could be reversed. The data showed that Z-VAD-FMK could only partially reverse THM-induced cancer cell death and slightly reduce the percentage of cell death (Fig. S3C). Therefore, we speculate that the occurrence of apoptosis is accompanied by other forms of cell death. In conclusion, THM significantly inhibited the proliferation and induced cell death of colorectal cancer cells and lung cancer cells.

THM induced cell cycle arrest and inhibited cell proliferation in colorectal and lung cancer cells.

The cell cycle is a fundamental biological process, indispensable for cellular development and multiplication. It coordinates the precise duplication and equitable distribution of cellular components to daughter cells, thereby ensuring the continuation of cell lineages [34].

To further evaluate the anti-proliferative effects of THM on CRC and lung cancer cells, colony formation assays were performed. Compared with the control group, the formation of cell colonies was significantly reduced with increasing concentrations of THM (Fig. 3A-B). This indicated that THM inhibited the proliferation of CRC and lung cancer cells in a dose-dependent manner. Cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) are core molecules that control the cell cycle, and cyclins are positive regulators of CDKs. Dysregulation of these proteins is closely related to cell proliferation. To investigate the effects of THM on these proteins, western blot analysis was performed. The results showed that treatment with THM downregulated the expression of CDK4, CDK6, and Cyclin D1, leading to cell cycle arrest in the G0/G1 phase (Fig. 3C). These findings suggest that THM may inhibit cell proliferation by arresting colorectal cancer cells and lung cancer cells in the G0/G1 phase, providing strong theoretical support for its potential as an anticancer agent.

THM triggered PANoptosis in colorectal and lung cancer cells

As we know, cell death has a profound impact on the formation and development of tumors. Different cell death patterns have different morphological characteristics. Apoptosis manifests through distinctive cellular changes, including membrane, shrinkage, chromatin compaction, nuclear disintegration, and the emergence of apoptotic bodies [35]. In contrast, pyroptosis and necroptosis often show distinct features of cell swelling and cell membrane disruption [36–38]. We further investigated the impact of THM on cell death. The morphological changes of CRC and lung cancer cells were observed by propidium iodide (PI) staining after the treatment with different concentrations of THM for 24 h. It was found that THM triggered the cell death of CRC and lung cancer cells in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4A). Interestingly, the cell death induced by THM exhibited various morphological forms, such as apoptotic-like cells with intact membranes, pyroptotic-like cell death with membrane swelling, and necrotic cell death with membrane disintegration (Fig. 4A). Subsequently, the LDH level was detected. In comparison to the control cells, the cells treated with THM exhibited significantly higher levels of lactate dehydrogenase in the culture supernatant (Fig. 4B). This data corroborated the phenomena of plasma membrane rupture and outflow of cell contents after cell death, which was distinct from those of apoptosis.

We next investigated the ways by which THM triggered cell death in CRC and lung cancer cells. So, the different inhibitors specific to diverse cell death pathways, including autophagy, ferroptosis, necroptosis and caspase, were used to confirm the cell death type induced by THM. Notably, the CCK-8 assay results revealed that both the pan-caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK and the necroptosis inhibitor Nec-1 could effectively reverse THM-triggered cell death (Fig. 4C). In contrast, the autophagy inhibitor CQ and the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 failed to protect against THM-induced cell death (Fig. S4). In addition, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further revealed that THM activated the pathways associated with PANoptosis, including apoptosis, necroptosis, and the related inflammatory pathways triggered by pyroptosis (Fig. 4D). In conclusion, these findings indicated that THM exerted its anticancer effect by inducing PANoptosis.

THM induced PANoptosis by assembling PANoptosome

To further confirm the PANoptosis induced by THM, western blot analysis was conducted to examine proteins linked to apoptosis, pyroptosis, and necroptosis. As a result, the pyroptosis marker protein Gasdermin E (GSDME), the apoptosis marker protein PARP, and Cleaved-CASP3 and Cleaved-CASP8 were all significantly cleaved after THM treatment. Moreover, the expression of the necroptosis markers p-RIPK1, phosphorylated receptor-interacting serine-threonine kinase 3 (p-RIPK3), and phosphorylated mixed lineage kinase-like protein (p-MLKL) were significantly increased (Fig. 5A). As widely recognized, PANoptosis represents a distinct form of programmed cell death within the domain of the innate immune system. It encompassed both cellular disintegration and inflammatory reactions, with this process being intricately governed by a sophisticated multi-protein assembly known as the PANoptosome complex [39–41]. Therefore, several classic PANoptosome sensor proteins were detected, including NLRP12, ZBP1, and AIM2. The results showed that the expression of AIM2 was upregulated in HT29, HCT116, A549, and H1299 cells, while the other sensors (NLRP12 and ZBP1) did not show an upward trend of activation (Fig. 5A and Figure S5A). Meanwhile, H&E staining of CRC and lung cancer tumor sections revealed nuclear condensation, apoptosis, and cytoplasmic depletion with indistinct cell boundaries in the THM-treated group (Fig. 5B). The immunohistochemical experiments revealed a significant elevation in the expressions of Cleaved-PARP, Cleaved-CASP3, AIM2, p-MLKL and p-RIPK1 but a significant reduction in the expression of the proliferation-linked gene Ki67 following THM treatment (Fig. 5B and Fig. S5B). Based on these findings, we speculate that THM may induce PANoptosis by forming the AIM2-PANoptosome.

Previous research has reported that the formation of the PANoptosome complex integrates crucial components from three cell death pathways: pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis. These components encompass apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC), CASP8, RIPK3, among others [39, 41]. ASC is a protein of great significance in cellular immunity and inflammatory responses. It contains a caspase recruitment domain and plays a crucial role in the assembly process of the inflammasome. To investigate the effect of THM on the assembly of PANoptosome, immunofluorescence experiments were performed in HCT116 and A549 cells by staining with ASC, CASP8, RIPK3, and AIM2 respectively. Confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) imaging revealed that in the absence of THM treatment, ASC, CASP8, RIPK3, and AIM2 were diffusely distributed throughout the cells. After THM treatment, ASC formed pairwise co-localization with CASP8, RIPK3, and AIM2 in the cells. Moreover, a high degree of co-localization was observed between the ASC specks and the aggregates of other indicators (Fig. 5C-D). This finding indicated that THM induced the assemblage of AIM2-associated PANoptosome within the cells. Collectively, THM could trigger the formation of PANoptosome, which was a molecular platform for PANoptotic signaling, through the assembly of AIM2.

The changes in cell homeostasis during PANoptosis induced by THM

PANoptosis is a sophisticated and balanced form of programmed cell death, encapsulating the core characteristics of pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis within a cohesive inflammatory response. Therefore, the process of PANoptosis often has the characteristics of multiple forms of cell death. Apoptosis, pyroptosis, and necrosis are typically accompanied by alterations in cellular homeostasis, including reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, Ca2+ imbalance, and organelle dysfunction, particularly affecting the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and mitochondria.

Through the analysis of gene expression differences, it was found that many genes were significantly down-regulated and up-regulated after THM treatment (p < 0.05, Fold change > 2) (Fig. 6A-B). The co-up-regulated genes with significant differences (p < 0.05, Fold change > 2) were interleaved in the two cell lines. There were 96 genes that were co-upregulated and 339 genes that were co-downregulated in both cell lines (Fig. S6A). Next, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed and it was found that the up-regulated genes were significantly enriched in endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and IL17 inflammatory signaling pathways (Fig.S6B), while the down-regulated genes were significantly enriched in cancer pathways (Fig. S6C). This result indicated that THM treatment affected the function of endoplasmic reticulum and inflammatory response, and the down-regulated genes suggested that cancer progression was significantly inhibited.

GSEA enrichment analysis of transcriptome sequencing data showed that the unfolded protein response (UPR) pathway was significantly enriched in HCT116 and A549 (Fig. 6C-D). And, ER stress-related genes CHOP, BIP, ATF6, IRE1α, PERK, and ATF4 were significantly up-regulated after treatment with different concentrations of THM for 24 h (Fig. 6E), which was consistent with the transcriptome sequencing data, suggesting that THM induced ER stress in tumor cells. In addition, the increased expression of CHOP, BIP, and PERK was demonstrated at the protein level (Fig. S6D). Given that ER stress may be caused by the accumulation of proteins, a modest reduction in protein synthesis may help alleviate ER stress. To sustain cellular homeostasis, ER and mitochondriathrough a direct structural coupling that facilitates molecular signaling [42]. Several studies have reported that mitochondrial dysfunction and mitochondrial DNA damage can lead to many forms of programmed cell death in cardiomyocytes under doxorubicin stimulation, especially PANoptosis [43]. By examining mitochondrial membrane potential, we found that THM could induce an imbalance of mitochondrial membrane potential, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction (Fig. 6F). ROS and Ca2+ is reported to play an important role as intracellular messenger molecules, and their numbers are in a dynamic balance within the cell. Moderate Ca2+ signaling is essential for cell proliferation, while a large amount of cytosolic Ca2+ influx or mitochondrial Ca2+ overload can trigger cell death [44]. A study reported that NFS1 deficiency triggered PANoptosis and acted synergistically with oxaliplatin treatment by increasing ROS levels [45]. Therefore, the intracellular ROS and Ca2+ levels were detected in this study, and the results showed that the intracellular ROS (Fig. 6G) and Ca2+ (Fig. 6H-I) levels were significantly increased.

Taken together, these results indicate that during the process of PANoptosis induced by THM, the changes in cellular homeostasis will be affected, mainly manifested as elevation of ROS and Ca2+ levels, ERS, and mitochondrial dysfunction.

TRIM38 was a direct target of THM

To identify the possible molecular targets of THM for its antitumor effect, a heatmap analysis was performed on the genes significantly associated with PANoptosis (Fig. 7A). Among them, we have focused on triplet motif-containing 38 (TRIM38) that plays an important role in immune regulation, inflammatory response [46, 47] and tumor suppression [48, 49]. Based on this, we examined the expression of TRIM38 in CRC and lung cancer tissues from the THM-treated and untreated groups by using immunohistochemistry. The results showed that, TRIM38 expression was significantly increased in the THM-treated group (Fig. 7B). TRIM38 is a protein containing a tripartite motif (TRIM) and belongs to the TRIM family. Members of the TRIM protein family commonly participate in diverse cellular processes, including cell cycle regulation, signal transduction, and protein degradation. TRIM38 may influence cellular functions by modulating the ubiquitination of proteins within the cell. Notably, some research reports have indicated that the dysregulation of TRIM family proteins is closely associated with the occurrence and progression of various cancers [50–53]. Considering this, we hypothesized that TRIM38 might be the direct binding target of THM in cancer cells. To confirm this hypothesis, the interaction between THM and TRIM38 was analyzed.

Firstly, molecular docking model prediction showed that THM could bind to the pocket of TRIM38 protein. It could form hydrogen bonds with SER424, TYR426, and THR436, and undergo hydrophobic interactions with LEU186, PHE189, PHE415, and VAL422. The minimum binding energy was − 7.4 kcal/mol (Fig. 7C). Subsequently, we employed biophysical methods to further validate the direct interaction between THM and TRIM38 protein. CETSA demonstrated that THM enhanced the thermal stability of TRIM38 (Fig. 7D-F). Next, SPR analysis was conducted by incrementally elevating THM concentrations (0, 6.25, 12.5, 25, 50 µM) to monitor the real-time binding/dissociation kinetics between THM and TRIM38, aiming to determine their binding affinity. The results indicated a strong binding affinity between THM and TRIM38, with a dissociation constant (KD) of 30.5 µM (Fig. 7G-H). In addition, to further investigate the drug target binding of TRIM38 in CRC and lung cancer cells, we synthesized a Cy5-labeled THM probe (THM-Cy5) for immunofluorescence analysis to observe its co-localization with TRIM38 in cells. The synthetic THM-Cy5 conjugate was firstly demonstrated to possess the same biological activity and target binding capacity as unmodified THM (Fig. S7A-B). Confocal microscopy showed significant co-localization (as indicated by arrows) between THM (red fluorescence) and TRIM38 (green fluorescence), suggesting a direct interaction between THM and TRIM38 at the cellular level (Fig. 7I-J). Taken together, these data collectively revealed that TRIM38 was a direct target of THM.

THM induced PANoptosis in colorectal and lung cancer cells by targeting TRIM38 protein

To investigate the effect of THM binding to TRIM38 on intracellular TRIM38 expression, we used qRT-PCR to detect TRIM38 mRNA levels in HT29, HCT116, A549, and H1299 cells. The results showed that THM treatment significantly up-regulated TRIM38 mRNA expression in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 8A). Additionally, western blot analysis indicated that THM treatment also significantly increased protein levels of TRIM38 (Fig. 8B), suggesting that THM could induce cell death by up-regulating TRIM38.

To further determine the role of TRIM38 in THM-induced tumor cell death, TRIM38 knockdown was achieved in HCT116 and A549 cells via shRNA. Among all the shRNAs, shTRIM38-3 with the best knockdown efficiency was selected for cytotoxicity assays (Fig. 8C). As expected, CCK-8 assay results showed that TRIM38 knockdown significantly enhanced cell viability after THM treatment (Fig. 8D). Western blot assays were then performed on TRIM38-knockdown cells to determine the effect of THM treatment on PANoptosis. As a result, TRIM38 knockdown reduced the cleavage of pyroptosis marker GSDME and the expression of AIM2 and decreased the phosphorylation of necroptosis markers RIPK1 as well as the cleavage of apoptosis markers CASP3 and PARP (Fig. 8E). These results confirmed the critical role of TRIM38 in THM-induced PANoptosis. Collectively, these data indicated that TRIM38 was the primary target of THM in the treatment of colorectal and lung cancers, and TRIM38 knockdown could effectively rescue THM-induced PANoptosis.

THM combined with clinical medications exhibited significant synergistic antitumor effect in colorectal cancer animal models

Colorectal cancer is one of the common and deadly malignant tumors worldwide, and anticancer drugs are widely used in clinical practice. Currently, FOLFOX and FOLFIRI chemotherapy regimens, as well as Cetuximab, are commonly used for clinical treatment [54, 55]. Although chemotherapy drugs can effectively control tumor progression, they still face many challenges, often accompanied by serious adverse reactions and acquired drug resistance. The molecular targeted drugs such as Cetuximab also face problems such as poor efficacy in some patients. In recent years, the role of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) in tumor treatment has attracted increasing attention. TCM has shown unique advantages in enhancing the efficacy of chemotherapy drugs, reducing adverse reactions, and improving patients’ immunity [56]. Therefore, combination therapy is considered a promising strategy.

To evaluate the synergistic therapeutic potential of THM in combination with clinical drugs in vivo, a xenograft model of HCT116 cells harboring the KRASG13D mutation (although this is a KRAS mutation, this specific subtype has been confirmed to be moderately sensitive to Cetuximab [57, 58]) was established in immunodeficient athymic nude mice. The combination therapy were administrated with the medium dose of THM in combination with Cetuximab, FOLFOX regimen, and FOLFIRI regimen, respectively (Fig. 9A). Compared with monotherapy, all combination therapies significantly enhanced anticancer efficacy (Fig. 9B-D). In our findings, Cetuximab monotherapy showed moderately low antitumor activity, whereas combination with THM significantly improved the therapeutic effect against colorectal cancer. Throughout the treatment period, no notable fluctuations in body weight were detected across any of the mice groups (Fig. 9E). Additionally, H&E staining and serum biochemical analysis revealed no obvious toxicity (Fig. S8A-B). Interestingly, we calculated the combination index (CI) using methods referenced from existing literature to evaluate synergistic effects [31]. The results showed CI values of 1.168, 1.421, and 2.656 for the THM + Cetuximab, THM + FOLFOX, and THM + FOLFIRI groups, respectively, indicating that all three combination therapies exhibited synergistic antitumor effects in vivo, with the THM + FOLFIRI group demonstrating the optimal efficacy (Fig. 9F-H). After that, the expression levels of TRIM38 and PANoptosis markers in tumor tissues were detected by immunohistochemical analysis. The results showed that THM combined with Cetuximab, FOLFOX and FOLFIRI enhanced the expression of TRIM38 and PANoptosis markers compared with the treatment groups alone (Fig. 9I and Fig.S8C). These results suggested that THM might enhance the antitumor efficacy in combination with conventional anticancer strategies in vivo through TRIM38-mediated PANoptosis. Taken together, our data revealed the synergistic anticancer potential of THM in CRC.

Discussion

Natural compounds have long been the main active ingredients of traditional medicines and are also valuable sources of new drug discovery. Due to their distinct structural diversity and high selectivity, natural compounds are regarded as superior sources for new drug development [59]. This study demonstrates the significant anticancer effects of THM, confirming through in vivo animal and in vitro cell studies that THM effectively inhibits the proliferation of colorectal and lung cancer cells while inducing cell death. Cell death is pivotal for organismal development, homeostasis maintenance, and immune responses. Among them, apoptosis, pyroptosis, and necroptosis represent the most genetically well-characterized forms of programmed cell death [14]. In 2019, American scholar Malireddi proposed the concept of PANoptosis [60]. PANoptosis plays a double-edged sword role in disease progression, it harms the prognosis of COVID-19 [61], but it can also protect the host from HSVI and other infections [62] as well as inhibit cancer progression [63–65]. Studies focusing on compounds and endogenous molecules involved in PANoptosis, which can act on PANoptosome-related members to modulate PANoptosis, are promising therapeutic strategies. Through transcriptomic analysis, we found that THM treatment significantly enriched pathways associated with PANoptosis. Furthermore, western blot analysis revealed that THM activates markers associated with pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis. Immunofluorescence studies observed colocalization of PANoptosomes.

TRIM38, as an important member of the TRIM family, not only participates in key biological functions such as protein degradation, immune regulation, and signaling pathway modulation through its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity, but also plays a complex role in the occurrence and progression of cancer [66]. Recent studies have shown that TRIM38 is downregulated in colorectal cancer due to promoter hypermethylation and exerts a tumor-suppressive effect through the TRIM38/CCT6A/c-Myc axis, providing a new potential target for targeted therapy of CRC [49]. Previous studies have also reported that in bladder cancer, TRIM38 can inhibit tumor malignancy and glycolysis through the ubiquitination and degradation of GLUT1 [48]. At the maternal-fetal interface, TRIM38-mediated K48-type ubiquitination of MITA can inhibit pyroptosis and maintain immune tolerance, offering new targets and ideas for the diagnosis and treatment of related diseases [67]. Another study has shown that TRIM38 also exhibits tumor-suppressive effects in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) by regulating the AMPK/NF-κB/NLRP3 pathway to inhibit tumor cell migration, invasion, and metastasis [68]. In this study, molecular docking between THM and TRIM38 protein showed favorable binding energy. In the further study, TRIM38 was demonstrated to be a direct target of THM by CESTA, SPR, Cy5-labeled THM probe, and shRNA experiments. We have identified TRIM38 as a regulator of PANoptosis in colorectal and lung cancers for the first time.

Moreover, the antitumor activity of THM was confirmed in colorectal and lung cancer cells both in vitro and in vivo, and the antitumor mechanisms of THM were further elucidated (Fig. 10), with combination therapies showing significant synergistic anticancer effects. Our data indicates that the natural compound THM holds great potential for future clinical cancer therapy.

However, there are still some limitations in this study. A distinctive feature of THM-induced tumor cell death is the cross-activation of pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis pathways [69]. This multi-pathway synergistic pattern also results in a pronounced dose-dependent interaction with different cell death inhibitors. This study reveals that the pan-caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK and the necroptosis inhibitor Nec-1 only demonstrate rescue effects under high-dose THM treatment, with no significant changes observed in low-dose THM groups. This finding prompts further inquiry: why do inhibitors fail to effectively block cell death at low THM doses? Considering pathway activation states and limitations of quantitative assays, the key mechanism can be summarized as following: Under low-dose THM treatment, these pathways are only weakly activated, with key molecules like caspases and RIPK1 showing limited activity. Meanwhile, both Z-VAD-FMK and Nec-1 rely on sufficient the pathway activation to work. When the pathway activation level is low, these inhibitors cannot effectively compete for binding sites nor block the small number of activated molecules. In addition, the multi-pathway network formed by cross-activation allows death signals to bypass partial inhibitory effects and continue to transmit, so no obvious cell viability rescue effect is observed in the low-dose group. When treated with high-dose THM, multiple pathways are strongly activated. At this time, these inhibitors can simultaneously block the key nodes of multiple pathways, disrupt the death signal transmission network, and thus effectively reverse the loss of cell viability.

Notably, although TRIM38 appears to differentially regulate PANoptosis-associated markers based on tissue context, the trends in these markers are consistent across colorectal and lung cancer tissues (Fig. 8E). PANoptosis is characterized by its combinatorial activation of pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis pathways, but cannot be fully comprehended by any of these pathways alone. if pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis pathways are activated together, it is considered that PANoptosis occurs. Regarding whether TRIM38 needs to regulate all markers, a large number of PANoptosis-related markers is reported, we do not think regulation of all markers is required. For example, in cardiovascular models, IRF1-mediated PANoptosis initiation requires only caspase-8 (apoptosis) and NLRP3 (pyroptosis) activation to drive pathological remodeling, while necroptosis markers (e.g., RIPK3) remain secondary [70]. Conversely, neuronal studies demonstrate that RIPK3-dominated PANoptosis can propagate inflammatory signals even with attenuated pyroptotic components [71].

While THM targeting TRIM38 may mediate the occurrence of PANoptosis, the specific mechanisms by which TRIM38 regulates the formation of the PANoptosome complex have not been clarified. Additionally, the relationship between TRIM38 and endoplasmic reticulum stress and mitochondrial dysfunction remains unclear, and further validation is needed to determine whether the knockdown of TRIM38 can rescue the cell homeostasis imbalance induced by THM. Since PANoptosis and PANoptosome complex have been widely implicated across the whole disease spectrum, we hope that future research on PANoptosis will be more in-depth, which will provide more possibilities for finding therapeutic targets and developing more effective treatment approaches, thus improving the prognosis of patients.
