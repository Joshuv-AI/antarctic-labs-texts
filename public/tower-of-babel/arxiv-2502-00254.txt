Even Hypergeometric Polynomials and Finite Free Commutators
Jacob CAMPBELL a, Rafael MORALES b and Daniel PERALES c

Abstract
We study in detail the class of even polynomials and their behavior with respect to finite free convolutions. To this end, we use some specific hypergeometric polynomials and a variation of the rectangular finite free convolution to understand even real-rooted polynomials in terms of positive-rooted polynomials. Then, we study some classes of even polynomials that are of interest in finite free probability, such as even hypergeometric polynomials, symmetrizations, and finite free commutators. Specifically, we provide many new examples of these objects, involving classical families of special polynomials (such as Laguerre, Hermite, and Jacobi). Finally, we relate the limiting root distributions of sequences of even polynomials with the corresponding symmetric measures that arise in free probability.

Introduction

In the past decade, the subject of finite free probability has grown considerably due to its connections with geometry of polynomials, combinatorics, random matrix theory, and free probability. The core objects of study in finite free probability are polynomials of a fixed degree
n
, and some convolution operations on such polynomials. These convolutions, called additive and multiplicative finite free convolutions and denoted by
\boxplus_{n}
and
\boxtimes_{n}
, were studied a century ago
[
30
,
32
]
but were recently rediscovered
[
19
]
as expected characteristic polynomials of certain random matrices. The finite free convolutions preserve various real-rootedness and interlacing properties, and when
n\to\infty
, they approximate the additive and multiplicative free convolutions of Voiculescu
[
31
]
, which are denoted by
\boxplus
and
\boxtimes
.

Since
[
19
]
first appeared in 2015, there have been several developments in finite free probability which expand on the parallel with free probability. In
[
18
]
, Marcus developed finite
R
- and
S
-transforms in analogy with the corresponding analytic functions in free probability. On the other hand, the combinatorial side of finite free probability was developed by Arizmendi, Garza-Vargas, and Perales
[
2
,
4
]
with a cumulant sequence for finite free convolutions, in analogy with the work of Nica and Speicher on free cumulants
[
26
]
.

There are two other operations on measures in free probability which have finite analogues that are relevant to this paper. One of them is the rectangular additive convolution studied by Gribinski and Marcus
[
13
]
, which is analogous to the rectangular free convolution defined by Benaych-Georges
[
6
]
. The other is the finite free commutator operation studied by Campbell
[
10
]
, which is analogous to the free commutator operation studied by Nica and Speicher
[
25
]
. A common thread between these two operations is their relation to
even
polynomials: these are the polynomials whose roots come in positive-negative pairs.

Thus far, the class of even polynomials has not been studied in general in the context of finite free probability. In this paper, we carry out a detailed study of the class of even polynomials and compile some basic but useful results concerning their behavior with respect to finite free convolutions. In many cases, convolutions of even real-rooted polynomials can be reinterpreted as convolutions of polynomials with all non-negative roots and some specific hypergeometric polynomials that were studied recently in
[
22
]
. We should also mention that while working on this project, we became aware of a recent preprint
[
9
]
that studies even entire functions in connection to finite free probability.

The basic tool in this paper is the map that takes an even polynomial, i.e., a polynomial of the form
\tilde{q}(x)=q\bigl(x^{2}\bigr)
, and returns the polynomial
q(x)
. We call this map
\bm{Q}_{m}
, where
m
is the degree of
q
. An important property of this map is that
\tilde{q}(x)
is real-rooted if and only if
q(x)
is positive-rooted; this is helpful because the behavior of positive-rooted polynomials with respect to finite free convolutions is well understood. However,
\bm{Q}_{m}
does not quite preserve finite free convolutions

\bm{Q}_{m}(p\boxplus_{2m}q)\neq\bm{Q}_{m}(p)\boxplus_{m}\bm{Q}_{m}(q)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bm{Q}_{m}(p\boxtimes_{2m}q)\neq\bm{Q}_{m}(p)\boxtimes_{m}\bm{Q}_{m}(q).

To make these into equalities, we modify the right-hand sides using some particular hypergeometric polynomials of the kind studied in
[
22
]
. In the additive case, the result is related to a generalization of the rectangular convolution of
[
13
]
. This generalization was first mentioned in a talk by Gribinski
[
12
]
, and was recently studied in
[
8
]
. We also study an interesting operation in finite free probability which takes a single polynomial
p
and returns the even polynomial
{p(x)\boxplus_{n}p(-x)}
, which we call the
symmetrization
of
p
. This operation appeared naturally in
[
10
]
in relation to the finite free commutator.

Then, we establish even versions of the algebraic results of
[
22
]
, which concern the behavior of even hypergeometric polynomials with respect to finite free convolutions. In particular, we use some known product identities concerning hypergeometric series to provide many non-trivial examples of symmetrizations of hypergeometric polynomials (such as Laguerre, Hermite, or Jacobi) which are of interest in finite free probability.

We also use our framework of even hypergeometric polynomials to provide some new insight into finite free commutators: the result of
[
10
]
can be phrased in terms of even hypergeometric polynomials, and we provide some partial results concerning real-rootedness. We work out many examples of finite free commutators, and connect them asymptotically with known examples of commutators in free probability.

Finally, we study the asymptotic behavior of even polynomials in connection to free probability. In some cases we obtain new results in free probability. For instance, in the limit, the symmetrization operation tends to the analogous operation on probability measures, which we call free symmetrization: given a probability measure
\mu
, its free symmetrization is the measure
{\mu\boxplus\widetilde{\mu}}
, where
\widetilde{\mu}
is just the pushforward of
\mu
by
x\mapsto-x
. To the best of our knowledge this type of operation has not been studied systematically in free probability, but it has appeared sporadically in different contexts (see Remark
6.5
). Our machinery allows us to compute the free symmetrizations of some special distributions, including the Marchenko–Pastur, reversed Marchenko–Pastur, and free beta distributions.

Besides this introductory section, the rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we review some preliminaries on polynomials, measures, and finite free probability. In Section
3
, we establish our basic framework for even polynomials and study its behavior in relation to finite free convolutions. We specialize to the study of even hypergeometric polynomials in Section
4
. In Section
5
, we use even hypergeometric polynomials to study the finite free commutator operation and provide many examples. In Section
6
, we study the asymptotic behavior of families of even polynomials and relate it to results in free probability. See Section
7
for a summary of the main results using standard hypergeometric series notation
{}_{i}F_{j}
.

Preliminaries

2.1
Polynomials and their coefficients

We start by introducing some notation. We denote by
\mathcal{P}_{n}
the set of monic polynomials (over the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
) of degree
n
. To specify that all the roots of a polynomial belong to a specific region
K\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, we use the notation
\mathcal{P}_{n}(K)
. For most of our results,
K
is going to be either the set of real numbers
\mathbb{R}
, the set positive real number
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, or the set of negative real numbers
\mathbb{R}_{<0}
.

Notation 2.1

(roots and coefficients)
.

Given a polynomial
p\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, we denote its roots by
\lambda_{1}(p),\dots,\lambda_{n}(p)
. Every polynomial
p\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
can be written in the form

p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}x^{n-k}(-1)^{k}\mathsf{e}_{k}(p)

(2.1)

for some coefficients
\mathsf{e}_{k}(p)
. Here,
\mathsf{e}_{0}(p)=1
as
p
is monic. There is a specific formula for these coefficients

\mathsf{e}_{k}(p)=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}\leq n}\lambda_{i_{1}}(p)\cdots\lambda_{i_{k}}(p)

for
0\leq k\leq n
. These are the so-called
elementary symmetric polynomials
in the roots of
p
.

Notation 2.2

(dilation)
.

The
dilation
of a polynomial
p
by a non-zero scalar
\alpha
is defined as
{[\operatorname{Dil}_{\alpha}p](x)=\alpha^{n}p\bigl(\alpha^{-1}x\bigr)}
.
The roots of
\operatorname{Dil}_{\alpha}p
are the roots of
p
scaled by
\alpha
:
\alpha\lambda_{1}(p),\dots,\allowbreak\alpha\lambda_{n}(p)
.

2.2
Measures and asymptotic empirical root distribution

We will be interested in sequences of polynomials with increasing degree, whose zero distribution tend in the limit to a probability measure. We denote by
\mathcal{M}
the set of probability measures on the complex plane. Similar to our notation for polynomials, for
K\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, we denote by
\mathcal{M}(K)
the set of probability measures supported on
K
. In particular,
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
is the set of probability measures supported on the real line. We also denote by
\mathcal{M}^{E}(\mathbb{R})
the set of symmetric probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
.

Notation 2.3

(Cauchy transform)
.

For a probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
, the
Cauchy transform
of
\mu
is defined by

G_{\mu}(z):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{z-t}{\rm d}\mu(t).

This is an analytic function from the upper half-plane to the lower half-plane. Among other things, the Cauchy transform encodes weak convergence: for a sequence
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}
in
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
and a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
, we have
\mu_{n}\to\mu
weakly if and only if
G_{\mu_{n}}(z)\to G_{\mu}(z)
pointwise. This result can be found, e.g., in
[
23
, Remark 12, Theorem 13, Lemma 3 of Section 3.1]
.

Notation 2.4
.

For
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{E}(\mathbb{R})
, let
Q(\mu)
be the pushforward of
\mu
along the map
\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\colon\allowbreak{t\mapsto t^{2}}
. This is a probability measure supported on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, and it has appeared before in the free probability literature
[
5
,
25
]
. A useful description of
Q(\mu)
was given in
[
5
, Proposition 5]
in terms of the Cauchy transform:

G_{\mu}(z)=zG_{Q(\mu)}\bigl(z^{2}\bigr)
.
In the other direction, for
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
, write

S(\nu)=\frac{1}{2}(S_{+}(\nu)+S_{-}(\nu))
,
where
S_{+}(\nu)
and
S_{-}(\nu)
are the pushforwards of
\nu
along the maps

\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\colon t\mapsto\sqrt{t}
and
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}\colon t\mapsto-\sqrt{t}
,
respectively.

Lemma 2.5
.

Q\colon\mathcal{M}^{E}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
is a homeomorphism with respect to weak convergence, with inverse
S
.

Proof.

The map
Q
is weakly continuous because it is a pushforward along a continuous map
[
7
, Theorem 2.7]
. One can check that
S
is the inverse of
Q
by integrating against bounded continuous functions, and
S
is weakly continuous for the same reason as
Q
.
∎

In this paper, we will often be concerned with root distributions of polynomials. For a non-zero polynomial
p
, the
empirical root distribution
(or
zero counting measure
) of
p
is the measure

\rho(p):=\frac{1}{\deg(p)}\sum_{\alpha\text{ root of }p}\delta_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{M},

where the roots in the sum are counted with multiplicity and
\delta_{\alpha}
is the Dirac delta (unit mass) placed at the point
\alpha
.

Definition 2.6
.

We say that a sequence
\mathfrak{p}=\left(p_{n_{k}}\right)_{k\geq 1}
of polynomials is
converging
if

•

(n_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is a strictly increasing subsequence of integers,

•

p_{n_{k}}
has degree
n_{k}
for
k\geq 1
, and

•

there is a measure, denoted by
\rho(\mathfrak{p})
, such that
\rho(p_{n_{k}})\to\rho(\mathfrak{p})
weakly as
k\to\infty
.

For our purposes, the subsequence
(n_{k})_{k\geq 1}
will usually be the full sequence of integers or the subsequence of even integers.

2.3
Free convolution

In this paper, we will occasionally use some tools from free probability. In this section we will briefly review the facts we need, following the references
[
23
,
26
]
.

Given a compactly supported measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
with moment sequence
(m_{k})_{k\geq 0}
, the
moment generating function
of
\mu
is given by

M_{\mu}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}m_{k}z^{k},

and the Cauchy transform
G_{\mu}
has the Laurent expansion

G_{\mu}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}m_{k}z^{-(k+1)}

in a neighborhood of
\infty
. This yields the relation
M_{\mu}(z)=z^{-1}G_{\mu}\bigl(z^{-1}\bigr)-1
.

The
R
-transform
and
S
-transform
of
\mu
are defined by

R_{\mu}(z)=G_{\mu}^{\langle-1\rangle}(z)-\frac{1}{z}\qquad\text{and}\qquad S_{\mu}(z)=\frac{z+1}{z}M_{\mu}^{\langle-1\rangle}(z).

Here,
\langle-1\rangle
denotes the compositional inverse, whose existence and analyticity around the origin for
G_{\mu}^{\langle-1\rangle}(z)
and
M_{\mu}^{\langle-1\rangle}(z)
are guaranteed provided that
m_{0}=1
and
m_{1}\neq 0
. The free additive and multiplicative convolutions correspond to the sum and product of free random variables; alternatively one can compute them using the
R
-transform
and
S
-transform
, see
[
23
, Definition 3.47 and Theorem 4.23]
.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
be compactly supported. Then

(1)

their
free additive convolution
is the unique measure
\mu\boxplus\nu
satisfying

R_{\mu\boxplus\nu}(z)=R_{\mu}(z)+R_{\nu}(z)
;

(2)

if
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
, their
free multiplicative convolution
is the unique measure
\mu\boxtimes\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
satisfying

S_{\mu\boxtimes\nu}(z)=S_{\mu}(z)S_{\nu}(z)
.

Finite free convolution of polynomials

In this subsection, we summarize some definitions and results on the finite free additive and multiplicative convolutions that will be used throughout this paper. First, let us establish some notation for rising and falling factorials.

Notation 2.8
.

For
a\in\mathbb{C}
and

and

We are now ready to introduce the multiplicative and additive convolutions, as defined in

Definition 2.9

(additive and multiplicative convolutions)
.

Consider polynomials
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
. We define the
finite free additive convolution
of
p
and

as the polynomial
p\boxplus_{n}q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
with coefficients given by

\mathsf{e}_{k}(p\boxplus_{n}q)=\left(n\right)^{\underline{k}}\sum_{i+j=k}\frac{\mathsf{e}_{i}(p)\mathsf{e}_{j}(q)}{\left(n\right)^{\underline{i}}\left(n\right)^{\underline{j}}}\qquad\text{for}\quad 0\leq k\leq n.

We define the
finite free multiplicative convolution
of
p
and
q
as the polynomial
p\boxtimes_{n}q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
with coefficients given by

\mathsf{e}_{k}(p\boxtimes_{n}q)=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\mathsf{e}_{k}(p)\mathsf{e}_{k}(q)\qquad\text{for}\quad 0\leq k\leq n.

Remark 2.10
.

We will also use an equivalent description of
\boxplus_{n}
in terms of certain differential operators, which was also given in
[
19
, equation (2)]
. Namely, if we can write
p(x)=P\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)x^{n}
and
q(x)=Q\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)x^{n}
for some polynomial
P
and
Q
, then

p(x)\boxplus_{n}q(x)=P\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)Q\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)x^{n}.

(2.3)

Notice that for a given
p
, there is a unique polynomial
P
of degree
n
satisfying
p(x)=P\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)x^{n}
, and is explicitly given by

P\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\frac{\mathsf{e}_{k}(p)}{\left(n\right)^{\underline{k}}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)^{k}.

To construct other differential operators that yield

\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)^{k}
with
{k>n}
, which vanish when applied to
x^{n}
.

Remark 2.11

(basic properties)
.

Directly from the definition, one can derive some basic properties of the binary operations
\boxplus_{n}
and
\boxtimes_{n}
. For example, they are bilinear, associative, and commutative. Another property of
\boxtimes_{n}
is related to scaling: for
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
and

, we have

p(x)\boxtimes_{n}(x-\alpha)^{n}=\operatorname{Dil}_{\alpha}p
.

The main property of finite free convolutions is that they preserve real-rootedness, in the following sense.

Theorem 2.12

(
[
30
,
32
]
)
.

Let
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. Then

(1)

p\boxplus_{n}q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
;

(2)

if either
p\in\mathcal{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
or
q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
, then
p\boxtimes_{n}q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
;

(3)

if both
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
, then
p\boxtimes_{n}q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})

Remark 2.13
.

It is easy to extend (3) in Theorem

•

and

Moreover, the good behavior of the roots under these convolutions goes beyond the real line. From the work of Walsh

2.5
Hypergeometric polynomials and examples

A large class of polynomials with real roots is contained in the class of hypergeometric polynomials; these were recently studied in connection with finite free probability in
[
22
]
. This class contains several important families, such as Bessel, Laguerre and Jacobi polynomials. These polynomials – and their specializations such as Hermite polynomials – constitute a rich class of examples in the theory of finite free probability.

Definition 2.16
.

For
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
n\geq 1
, let

a_{1},\dots,a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\left\{0,\frac{1}{n},\frac{2}{n},\dots,\frac{n-1}{n}\right\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad b_{1},\dots,b_{j}\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.4)

Define the polynomial
\mathcal{H}_{n}\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}b_{1},\dots,b_{j}\\
a_{1},\dots,a_{i}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
to be the unique monic polynomial of degree
n
with coefficients in representation (
2.1
) given by

\mathsf{e}_{k}\left(\mathcal{H}_{n}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{b_1,\dots,b_j}{a_1,\dots,a_i}\Bigl]}\right):=\binom{n}{k}\frac{\left(nb_{1}\right)^{\underline{k}}\cdots\left(nb_{j}\right)^{\underline{k}}}{\left(na_{1}\right)^{\underline{k}}\cdots\left(na_{i}\right)^{\underline{k}}}

for
1\leq k\leq n
.

To simplify notation, for a tuple
\bm{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{i})
and constants

\displaystyle\left(\bm{a}\right)^{\underline{k}}:=\prod_{s=1}^{i}\left(a_{s}\right)^{\underline{k}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad n\bm{a}+m:=(na_{1}+m,\dots,na_{i}+m).

Then, for tuples
\bm{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{i})

Remark 2.17
.

The reason we call

\mathcal{H}_{n}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{\bm b}{\bm a}\Bigl]}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{n}\left(\bm{b}n\right)^{\underline{n}}}{\left(\bm{a}n\right)^{\underline{n}}}{\ }_{i+1}F_{j}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n,\bm a n -n+1}{\bm b n -n+1};x\right)},

where
\bm{c}n-n+1
means that we multiply each entry of
\bm{c}
by

and then add
-n+1
, and we use the standard notation

for a generalized hypergeometric series. Namely, for tuples
{\bm{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{i}}
and
\bm{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{j})\in\mathbb{R}^{j}

{\ }{}_{i}F_{j}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{\bm a}{\bm b};x\right)}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\bm{a}\right)^{\overline{k}}}{\left(\bm{b}\right)^{\overline{k}}}\frac{x^{k}}{k!}.

In
[

]
, it was noticed that these hypergeometric polynomials behave well with respect to the finite free convolutions from Definition

For integers

Example 2.21

(identities)
.

The simplest cases of Definition
2.16
are the following:

\mathcal{H}_{n}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{\bm a}{\bm a}\Bigl]}(x)=\mathcal{H}_{n}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{\cdot}{\cdot}\Bigl]}(x)=(x-1)^{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{H}_{n}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{0}{\bm a}\Bigl]}(x)=x^{n}.

These polynomials are the identities for the operations

Example 2.22

(Laguerre and Hermite polynomials)
.

The analogues of the semicircular and free Poisson distributions in finite free probability are the Hermite and Laguerre polynomials respectively. These appear in finite free analogues of the central limit theorem and Poisson limit theorem
[
18
]
.

The Laguerre polynomials of interest in this paper can be written in terms of the hypergeometric polynomials
\mathcal{H}_{n}\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}b\\
\cdot\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
. There are some ranges of the parameter
b
which yield real-rooted polynomials:

•

if

, then
\mathcal{H}_{n}\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}b\\
\cdot\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
, and

is a root with multiplicity
(1-b)n
;

•

if
b\in\bigl(\frac{n-2}{n},\frac{n-1}{n}\bigr)
, then
\mathcal{H}_{n}\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}b\\
\cdot\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})

•

if

, then
\mathcal{H}_{n}\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}b\\
\cdot\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
.

See
[
22
, Table 1, (56)]
for more details.

We will be particularly interested in the following scaled version of a Laguerre polynomial, to which we give a special name: for
\lambda\geq 1
, write

L_{n}^{(\lambda)}=\operatorname{Dil}_{\frac{1}{n}}\mathcal{H}_{n}\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}\lambda\\
\cdot\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
.
This polynomial can be understood as the finite analogue of the free Poisson (also known as Marchenko–Pastur) distribution in free probability. Indeed, it appears as the limiting polynomial in the finite free Poisson limit theorem
[
18
]
and it converges in empirical root distribution to the free Poisson distribution with rate
\lambda

\rho\bigl(L_{n}^{(\lambda)}\bigr)\to\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\sqrt{(r_{+}-x)(x-r_{-})}}{x}\mathbf{1}_{[r_{-},r_{+}]}(x){\rm d}x

weakly as

, where

, and
\mathbf{1}_{[a,b]}
stands for the indicator function
\mathbf{1}_{[a,b]}(x)=1
if
x\in[a,b]
and 0 otherwise. This is a classical result; see
[
22
, Section 5.3]
and its references.

The Hermite polynomials used in this paper are defined as follows:

H_{2m}(x):=m^{-m}\mathcal{H}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{1-\frac{1}{2m}}{\cdot}\Bigl]}\bigl(mx^{2}\bigr)=\operatorname{Dil}_{\sqrt{\frac{1}{m}}}\mathcal{H}^{E}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{1-\frac{1}{2m}}{\cdot}\Bigl]}.

This polynomial is known to be the appropriate finite analogue of the semicircular distribution in free probability. It appears as the limiting polynomial in the finite free central limit theorem
[
18
]
and it converges in empirical root distribution to the semicircular distribution with radius
2
:

\rho(H_{2m})\to\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{4-x^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{[-2,2]}(x){\rm d}x

weakly as
n\to\infty
. This is also a well-known classical result, see, e.g.,
[
17
]
.

The resemblance between the definitions of
H
and
L^{(\lambda)}
is meaningful, and will be elaborated in Example
6.3
.

(Bessel polynomials)

The Bessel polynomials of interest in this paper can be written in terms of the hypergeometric polynomials

The Jacobi polynomials of interest in this paper can be written in terms of the hypergeometric polynomials

3
Even polynomials in finite free probability

In this section, we will study in detail the basic properties of even polynomials, with special emphasis on their behavior under finite free convolutions.

Definition 3.1
.

We say that a polynomial
p\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
is
even
if one of the following equivalent statements holds:

(1)

for every root
\alpha
of
p
, there is a root
\beta
of
p
with
\beta=-\alpha
and
\mathrm{mult}(\beta)=\mathrm{mult}(\alpha)
;

(2)

p
is an even function if
n
is even, or
p
is an odd function if
n
is odd;

(3)

\mathsf{e}_{k}(p)=0
for all odd
0\leq k\leq n
;

(4)

p=\operatorname{Dil}_{-1}(p)
.

We will denote by
\mathcal{P}_{2m}^{E}
the set of all even polynomials of even degree
2m
.

The equivalence between (1), (2) and (3) above is well known. The equivalence of (3) and (4) follows from noticing that
\operatorname{Dil}_{-1}
simply changes the sign of the roots.

Remark 3.2

(even polynomials of odd degree)
.

To simplify the presentation, throughout this paper we will focus on studying even polynomials of even degree. However, the reader should keep in mind that the case of odd degree is completely analogous to the case of even degree, except that we need to add a root at
0
. Indeed, notice that if
p
is an even polynomial of odd degree, then
p
must have a root at
0
, implying that it is of the form
p(x)=xq(x)
where
q
is an even polynomial of even degree.

Remark 3.3
.

From Definition
2.9
, it is immediate that the set
\mathcal{P}_{2m}^{E}
is closed under
\boxplus_{2m}
and
\boxtimes_{2m}
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{P}_{2m}^{E}
is absorbing with respect to multiplicative convolution. These claims can be nicely summarized as follows

\mathcal{P}_{2m}^{E}\boxplus_{2m}\mathcal{P}^{E}_{2m}\subset\mathcal{P}_{2m}^{E}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2m}^{E}\boxtimes_{2m}\mathcal{P}_{2m}\subset\mathcal{P}_{2m}^{E}
.

3.1
Degree doubling operation

A very simple way to construct even polynomials is by squaring the dummy variable. This is a very natural operation, and has appeared in the context of finite free probability
[
13
,
19
]
. Since we will extensively use this operation and its inverse, we will fix some notation.

Notation 3.4

(degree doubling operation)
.

Define
\bm{S}_{m}\colon\mathcal{P}_{m}\to\mathcal{P}_{2m}^{E}
by

\bm{S}_{m}(p)=p\bigl(x^{2}\bigr)

for
p\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
.

Notation 3.5

(even and odd parts)
.

Define
\bm{Q}_{m}\colon\mathcal{P}_{2m}\to\mathcal{P}_{m}
as follows: for
p\in\mathcal{P}_{2m}
, define
\bm{Q}_{m}(p)
by the coefficients

\mathsf{e}_{k}(\bm{Q}_{m}(p))=(-1)^{k}\mathsf{e}_{2k}(p).

(3.1)

Notice that the operations
\bm{S}_{m}
and
\bm{Q}_{m}
are linear, and for
p\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
, the roots of
\bm{S}_{m}(p)
are

-\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(p)},\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(p)},-\sqrt{\lambda_{2}(p)},\sqrt{\lambda_{2}(p)},\dots,-\sqrt{\lambda_{m}(p)},\sqrt{\lambda_{m}(p)}.

Another simple observation is that
\bm{Q}_{m}\circ\bm{S}_{m}\colon\mathcal{P}_{m}\to\mathcal{P}_{m}
is just the identity map, whereas the map
\bm{S}_{m}\circ\bm{Q}_{m}\colon\mathcal{P}_{2m}\to\mathcal{P}_{2m}^{E}
yields an even polynomial that has the same
even
coefficients as the original polynomial. These observations provide a bijection that we will use constantly throughout this paper.

Fact 3.6
.

\bm{S}_{m}
restricts to a bijection
\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})\to\mathcal{P}_{2m}^{E}(\mathbb{R})
, and the inverse of
\bm{S}_{m}
is
\bm{Q}_{m}
.

This is a finite free analogue of Lemma
2.5
. In Proposition
6.1
, we will check that this bijection behaves well with respect to limits of empirical root distributions.

3.2
Symmetrization

Another way to construct even polynomials is by taking the additive convolution of
p(x)
with
p(-x)
, yielding an even polynomial of the same degree. This operation appeared naturally in
[
10
]
when studying commutators in the context of finite free probability.

Notation 3.7
.

For
p\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, the
symmetrization
of
p
is the polynomial

\mathrm{Sym}(p):=p\boxplus_{n}(\operatorname{Dil}_{-1}p)
.

It follows directly from the definition that
\mathrm{Sym}(p)=\mathrm{Sym}(\operatorname{Dil}_{-1}p)
. Notice also that if
p\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{E}
, then
\mathrm{Sym}(p)=p\boxplus_{n}p
. In the following lemma, we collect more properties of the symmetrization.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
, and write
c_{\alpha}(x):=(x-\alpha)^{n}
. Then

(1)

\mathrm{Sym}(p)\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{E}
;

(2)

\mathrm{Sym}(p\boxplus_{n}q)=\mathrm{Sym}(p)\boxplus_{n}\mathrm{Sym}(q)
;

(3)

\mathrm{Sym}(\operatorname{Dil}_{\alpha}p)=\operatorname{Dil}_{\alpha}\mathrm{Sym}(p)
;

(4)

\mathrm{Sym}(p\boxplus_{n}c_{\alpha})=\mathrm{Sym}(p)
;

(5)

if
p\in\mathcal{P}_{2m}(\mathbb{R})
, then
\bm{Q}_{m}(\mathrm{Sym}(p))\in\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
.

Proof.

The proof of (1) follows from the observation that

\operatorname{Dil}_{-1}\mathrm{Sym}(p)=\operatorname{Dil}_{-1}(p\boxplus_{n}\operatorname{Dil}_{-1}p)=(\operatorname{Dil}_{-1}p)\boxplus_{n}p=\mathrm{Sym}(p).

Alternatively, one can use the formula for the coefficients of the additive convolution and notice that the negative signs of
\operatorname{Dil}_{-1}p
will generate cancellations, causing the odd coefficients of
\mathrm{Sym}(p)
to vanish.

For (2), observe that both sides are equal to

p\boxplus_{n}\operatorname{Dil}_{-1}p\boxplus_{n}q\boxplus_{n}\operatorname{Dil}_{-1}q
.
Part (3) is a direct consequence of the fact that dilation operation distributes over additive convolutions. For the proof of (4), first notice that

\mathrm{Sym}(c_{\alpha})=c_{\alpha}\boxplus_{n}c_{-\alpha}=c_{0}
.
So by (2), we have
\mathrm{Sym}(p\boxplus_{n}c_{\alpha})=\mathrm{Sym}(p)
. Finally, (5) follows from (1) and Fact
3.6
.
∎

3.3
Multiplicative convolution

The degree doubling operation behaves well with respect to multiplicative convolution.

Proposition 3.9
.

For
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{2m}
, we have

\bm{Q}_{m}(p\boxtimes_{2m}q)=\bm{Q}_{m}(p)\boxtimes_{m}\bm{Q}_{m}(q)\boxtimes_{m}\mathcal{H}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{-\frac{1}{2m}}{1-\frac{1}{2m}}\Bigl]}.

(3.2)

Equivalently, we can express this in terms of the degree doubling operation: for
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
, we have

\bm{S}_{m}(p\boxtimes_{m}q)=\bm{S}_{m}(p)\boxtimes_{2m}\bm{S}_{m}(q)\boxtimes_{2m}\bm{S}_{m}\Bigl(\mathcal{H}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{1-\frac{1}{2m}, 1-\frac{1}{2m}}{-\frac{1}{2m}, -\frac{1}{2m}}\Bigl]}\Bigr).

(3.3)

Proof.

To prove equation (
3.2
), we check the equality for every coefficient: for
0\leq k\leq m
the coefficient
\mathsf{e}_{k}(\bm{Q}_{m}(p\boxtimes_{2m}q))
of the left-hand side polynomial is given by

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}\mathsf{e}_{2k}(p\boxtimes_{2m}q)=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{\binom{2m}{2k}}\mathsf{e}_{2k}(p)\mathsf{e}_{2k}(q)

(Definition
2.9
)

\displaystyle\qquad=(-1)^{k}(-1)^{k}\mathsf{e}_{k}(\bm{Q}_{m}(p))(-1)^{k}\mathsf{e}_{k}(\bm{Q}_{m}(q))\frac{(2k)!}{\left(2m\right)^{\underline{2k}}}

(equation (
3.1
))

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathsf{e}_{k}(\bm{Q}_{m}(p))\mathsf{e}_{k}(\bm{Q}_{m}(q))\frac{(-1)^{k}k!\left(k-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\underline{k}}}{\left(m\right)^{\underline{k}}\left(m-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\underline{k}}}

(equation (
2.2
))

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{\mathsf{e}_{k}(\bm{Q}_{m}(p))\mathsf{e}_{k}(\bm{Q}_{m}(q))}{\binom{m}{k}\binom{m}{k}}\binom{m}{k}\frac{\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\underline{k}}}{\left(m-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\underline{k}}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathsf{e}_{k}\Bigl(\bm{Q}_{m}(p)\boxtimes_{m}\bm{Q}_{m}(q)\boxtimes_{m}\mathcal{H}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{-\frac{1}{2m}}{1-\frac{1}{2m}}\Bigl]}\Bigr).

(Definition
2.9
)

The proof of equation (
3.3
) is analogous.
∎

3.4
Additive and rectangular convolution

The effect of taking even parts of finite free convolutions is somewhat more complicated in the additive case. Here, one finds that a variation of the rectangular convolution of Gribinski and Marcus
[
13
]
plays a key role. We learned of the generalized rectangular convolution from a talk by Gribinski
[
12
]
. While working on this project, we became aware of another (equivalent) definition of generalized rectangular convolution given in
[

in terms of differential operators.

Definition 3.10

(Generalized rectangular convolution)
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{-1,\dots,-m\}
. For
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
, define the
(m,\alpha)
-rectangular convolution
p\boxplus_{m}^{\alpha}q\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
as the polynomial determined by the coefficients

\mathsf{e}_{k}(p\boxplus_{m}^{\alpha}q)=\left(m\right)^{\underline{k}}\left(m+\alpha\right)^{\underline{k}}\sum_{i+j=k}\frac{\mathsf{e}_{i}(p)}{\left(m\right)^{\underline{i}}\left(m+\alpha\right)^{\underline{i}}}\frac{\mathsf{e}_{j}(q)}{\left(m\right)^{\underline{j}}\left(m+\alpha\right)^{\underline{j}}}.

Remark 3.11
.

It is worth mentioning that Definition
3.10
is equivalent to the definitions presented in
[
12
]
and
[
8
, Definition 1.11]
. We prefer this presentation because one can readily rephrase it in terms of hypergeometric polynomials

\mathcal{H}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{\cdot}{1+\frac{\alpha}{m}}\Bigl]}\boxtimes_{m}\left(p\boxplus_{m}^{\alpha}q\right)=\left(\mathcal{H}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{\cdot}{1+\frac{\alpha}{m}}\Bigl]}\boxtimes_{m}p\right)\boxplus_{m}\left(\mathcal{H}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{\cdot}{1+\frac{\alpha}{m}}\Bigl]}\boxtimes_{m}q\right).

With this definition in hand we can prove that the effect of taking even parts of finite free additive convolutions is related to the rectangular convolution in the case where
\alpha=-\frac{1}{2}
. This relation was mentioned without proof in
[
12
]
; we include the proof here for the reader’s convenience.

Proposition 3.12
.

For
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{2m}^{E}
, we have

\bm{Q}_{m}(p\boxplus_{2m}q)=\bm{Q}_{m}(p)\boxplus_{m}^{-1/2}\bm{Q}_{m}(q).

(3.4)

Proof.

For
0\leq k\leq m
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{e}_{k}^{(m)}(\bm{Q}_{m}(p\boxplus_{2m}q)){}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\mathsf{e}_{2k}^{(2m)}(p\boxplus_{2m}q)=(-1)^{k}\left(2m\right)^{\underline{2k}}\sum_{i+j=k}\frac{\mathsf{e}_{2i}(p)}{\left(2m\right)^{\underline{2i}}}\frac{\mathsf{e}_{2j}(q)}{\left(2m\right)^{\underline{2j}}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}4^{k}\left(m\right)^{\underline{k}}\left(m-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\underline{k}}\sum_{i+j=k}\frac{(-1)^{i}\mathsf{e}_{i}(\bm{Q}_{m}(p))}{4^{i}\left(m\right)^{\underline{i}}\left(m-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\underline{i}}}\frac{(-1)^{j}\mathsf{e}_{j}(\bm{Q}_{m}(q))}{4^{j}\left(m\right)^{\underline{j}}\left(m-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\underline{j}}}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{e}_{k}\bigl(\bm{Q}_{m}(p)\boxplus_{m}^{-1/2}\bm{Q}_{m}(q)\bigr).

Since the coefficients match, the polynomials are the same.
∎

We should also mention that Gribinski
[
12
]
conjectured that the generalized rectangular convolution preserves positive real roots.

Conjecture 3.13
.

Given
\alpha>-1
, if
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
, then
p\boxplus_{m}^{\alpha}q\in\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
.

At the time of writing, the conjecture is only known to hold when
\alpha
is a non-negative integer. The case
\alpha=0
was proved in
[
19
, Theorem 3.1]
and the cases
\alpha=1,2,3,\dots
were proved in
[
13
, Theorem 2.3]
.

To finish this section, we mention how to prove the case
\alpha=-\frac{1}{2}
of Conjecture
3.13
using Proposition
3.12
.

Corollary 3.14
.

If
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
, then
p\boxplus_{m}^{-1/2}q\in\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
.

Proof.

Using (
3.4
), we can write

p\boxplus_{m}^{-1/2}q=\bm{Q}_{m}(\bm{S}_{m}(p)\boxplus_{2m}\bm{S}_{m}(q))
.
Since
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
, we have
\bm{S}_{m}(p),\bm{S}_{m}(q)\in\mathcal{P}_{2m}^{E}(\mathbb{R})
and
\bm{S}_{m}(p)\boxplus_{2m}\bm{S}_{m}(q)\in\mathcal{P}_{2m}^{E}(\mathbb{R})
. Therefore, after applying the degree halving operation we conclude that
p\boxplus_{m}^{-1/2}q=\bm{Q}_{m}(\bm{S}_{m}(p)\boxplus_{2m}\bm{S}_{m}(q))\in\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
.
∎

4
Even hypergeometric polynomials

The purpose of this section is to study the specifics of how even hypergeometric polynomials interact with the finite free convolution. Since this large class of polynomials contains several regions of parameters where the polynomials have all real, positive, or negative roots, understanding the multiplicative and additive convolutions in these cases will provide us with a large sample of even polynomials. For a summary of the results in this section using standard hypergeometric series notation
{}_{i}F_{j}
see Section
7
.

Our approach resembles that of
[
22
]
, with the difference that we want to study hypergeometric polynomials with the variable
x^{2}
rather than
x
. This requires some adjustment to the convolution formulas, because of the dependence on the degree, which is now
2m
instead of
m
.

Notation 4.1

(even hypergeometric polynomials)
.

Given
i,j,m\in\mathbb{N}
,
\bm{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{i}
and
{\bm{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{j})\in\mathbb{R}^{j}}
, write

\mathcal{H}_{m}^{E}\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}\bm{b}\\
\bm{a}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]:=\bm{S}_{m}\bigl(\mathcal{H}_{m}\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}\bm{b}\\
\bm{a}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\bigr)
,
where
\bm{S}_{m}
is the bijection from Notation
3.5
. In terms of coefficients, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{e}_{2k}\left(\mathcal{H}^{E}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{\bm b}{\bm a}\Bigl]}\right)=(-1)^{k}\mathsf{e}_{k}\left(\mathcal{H}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{\bm b}{\bm a}\Bigl]}\right)=(-1)^{k}\binom{m}{k}\frac{\left(\bm{b}m\right)^{\underline{k}}}{\left(\bm{a}m\right)^{\underline{k}}}=(-1)^{k}\binom{2m}{2k}\frac{\left(k-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\underline{k}}\left(\bm{b}m\right)^{\underline{k}}}{\left(m-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\underline{k}}\left(\bm{a}m\right)^{\underline{k}}}

for
0\leq k\leq m
.

Example 4.2

(Bernoulli polynomials)
.

The simplest even hypergeometric polynomial is

B_{2m}(x)=\mathcal{H}^{E}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{\cdot}{\cdot}\Bigl]}(x)=\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{m}.

We will call this a Bernoulli polynomial because its empirical root distribution is a Bernoulli distribution with equal weights at
1
and
-1
.

Example 4.3

(Hermite polynomials)
.

Another important sequence of even hypergeometric polynomials is the one we encountered in Example
2.22
:

H_{2m}=\operatorname{Dil}_{\sqrt{\frac{1}{m}}}\mathcal{H}^{E}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{1-\frac{1}{2m}}{\cdot}\Bigl]}.

4.1
Preliminary results

To study the convolution of even hypergeometric polynomials, we first need to understand how the polynomials look in differential form.

A formula to write polynomials
\mathcal{H}_{m}\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}\bm{b}\\
\bm{a}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
as differential operators was implicitly found in
[
22
]
. The formula to write
\mathcal{H}_{m}^{E}\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}\bm{b}\\
\bm{a}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
in terms of a differential operator can be derived in a similar way, and it can be generalized for larger powers. We will first prove a general lemma for an arbitrary power and then we specialize to the cases we are concerned.

Lemma 4.4
.

Given a constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
, integers
i,j,m,l\in\mathbb{N}
, and tuples of parameters
\bm{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{i}
and
\bm{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{j})\in\mathbb{R}^{j}
, if a polynomial has the following differential form

p(x)={\ }_{j}F_{i}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-m\bm b}{-m\bm a};c\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)^{l}\right)}x^{lm},

then we can express it as the following hypergeometric polynomial

p(x)=\bigl((-1)^{i+j+1}l^{l}c\bigr)^{m}\mathcal{H}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{\bm b, 1-\frac{1}{lm}, 1-\frac{2}{lm}, \dots, 1-\frac{l-1}{lm}}{\bm a}\Bigl]}\left(\frac{(-1)^{i+j+1}}{l^{l}c}x^{l}\right).

Proof.

We have

{\ }{}_{j}F_{i}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-\bm bm}{-\bm am};c\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)^{l}\right)}x^{lm}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(-m\bm{b}\right)^{\overline{k}}}{\left(-m\bm{a}\right)^{\overline{k}}}\frac{c^{k}}{k!}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)^{lk}x^{lm}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{m}(-1)^{(i+j)k}\frac{\left(m\bm{b}\right)^{\underline{k}}\left(lm\right)^{\underline{lk}}}{\left(m\bm{a}\right)^{\underline{k}}}\frac{c^{k}}{k!}x^{lm-lk}.

If
s
is not a multiple of
l
, then
\mathsf{e}_{s}(p)=0
. Otherwise, we have

\displaystyle(-1)^{lk}\mathsf{e}_{lk}(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(m\bm{b}\right)^{\underline{k}}}{\left(m\bm{a}\right)^{\underline{k}}}(-1)^{(i+j)k}\frac{c^{k}}{k!}\left(lm\right)^{\underline{lk}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(m\bm{b}\right)^{\underline{k}}}{\left(m\bm{a}\right)^{\underline{k}}}\frac{c^{k}}{k!}(-1)^{(i+j)k}l^{lk}\left(m\right)^{\underline{k}}\left(m-\frac{1}{l}\right)^{\underline{k}}\cdots\left(m-\frac{l-1}{l}\right)^{\underline{k}}

\displaystyle=\bigl((-1)^{i+j}l^{l}c\bigr)^{m}\binom{m}{k}\frac{\left(m\bm{b}\right)^{\underline{k}}\left(m-\frac{1}{l}\right)^{\underline{k}}\cdots\left(m-\frac{l-1}{l}\right)^{\underline{k}}}{\left(m\bm{a}\right)^{\underline{k}}}\left(\frac{(-1)^{i+j}}{l^{l}c}\right)^{m-k}.

So we have proven the claim

p(x)=\bigl((-1)^{i+j+1}l^{l}c\bigr)^{m}\mathcal{H}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{\bm b, 1-\frac{1}{lm}, 1-\frac{2}{lm}, \dots, 1-\frac{l-1}{lm}}{\bm a}\Bigl]}\left(\frac{(-1)^{i+j+1}}{l^{l}c}x^{l}\right).

As particular cases, we obtain the following.

Corollary 4.5
.

With the assumptions of Lemma
4.4
, in the case
l=1
, we have

{\ }{}_{j}F_{i}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-m\bm b}{-m\bm a};c\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)}x^{m}=\operatorname{Dil}_{c(-1)^{i+j+1}}\mathcal{H}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{\bm b}{\bm a}\Bigl]}(x).

In the case
l=2
, we have

{\ }_{j}F_{i}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-m\bm b}{-m\bm a};c\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)^{2}\right)}x^{2m}=\operatorname{Dil}_{2\sqrt{c(-1)^{i+j+1}}}\mathcal{H}^{E}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{\bm b, 1-\frac{1}{2m}}{\bm a}\Bigl]}(x).

(4.1)

It is worth emphasizing that in the right-hand side of (
4.1
), we first double the degree of the hypergeometric polynomial and then dilate it. Notice that the same polynomial can be obtained by first dilating (by the square of the constant) and then doubling the degree.

With these formulas in hand, we can now readily generalize the last part of Theorem
2.18
and relate the product of hypergeometric series (evaluated in any power of
x
) to the additive convolution of hypergeometric polynomials (evaluated on the corresponding powers of
x
). The idea is to use the definition of
\boxplus_{n}
in terms of differential operators and the fact that we just proved that hypergeometric series evaluated in differential operators applied to
x^{n}
yield hypergeometric polynomials. We first provide the result in its more general form, and then specialize to the cases that we are more interested in.

Theorem 4.6

(additive convolution of hypergeometric polynomials)
.

Let
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\in\mathbb{R}
be constants, and let
l_{1},l_{2},l_{3},n\in\mathbb{N}
be numbers such that
l_{k}
divides
n
for
k=1,2,3
. Consider tuples
\bm{a}_{1}
,
\bm{a}_{2}
,
\bm{a}_{3}
,
\bm{b}_{1}
,
\bm{b}_{2}
,
\bm{b}_{3}
of sizes
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}\in\mathbb{N}
, and assume that

{\ }{}_{j_{1}}F_{i_{1}}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n\bm b_1}{-n\bm a_1};c_{1}x^{l_{1}}\right)}{\ }_{j_{2}}F_{i_{2}}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n\bm b_2}{-n\bm a_2};c_{2}x^{l_{2}}\right)}={\ }_{j_{3}}F_{i_{3}}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n\bm b_3}{-n\bm a_3};c_{3}x^{l_{3}}\right)}.

Then, if for
k=1,2,3
we consider the polynomials

p_{k}(x)=\bigl((-1)^{i_{k}+j_{k}+1}l_{k}^{l_{k}}c_{k}\bigr)^{\frac{n}{l_{k}}}\mathcal{H}_{\frac{n}{l_{k}}}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{l_k \bm b_k, 1-\frac{1}{n}, \dots, 1-\frac{l_k-1}{n}}{l_k \bm a_k}\Bigl]}\left(\frac{(-1)^{i_{k}+j_{k}+1}}{l_{k}^{l_{k}}c_{k}}x^{l_{k}}\right),

we get that
p_{1}\boxplus_{n}p_{2}=p_{3}
.

Proof.

Fix
k\in\{1,2,3\}
. From Lemma
4.4
applied to
c_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
, integer values
i_{k},j_{k},\frac{n}{l_{k}},l_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
, and tuples of parameters
l_{k}\bm{a}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{i}
and
l_{k}\bm{b}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{j}
, we know that a polynomial written in differential form

{\ }{}_{j_{k}}F_{i_{k}}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-\frac{n}{l_k} l_k\bm b_k}{-\frac{n}{l_k} l_k\bm a_k};c_{k}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)^{l_{k}}\right)}x^{n}

is precisely the polynomial

p_{k}(x)=\bigl((-1)^{i_{k}+j_{k}+1}l_{k}^{l_{k}}c_{k}\bigr)^{\frac{n}{l_{k}}}\mathcal{H}_{\frac{n}{l_{k}}}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{l_k\bm b_k, 1-\frac{1}{n}, \dots, 1-\frac{l_k-1}{n}}{l_k\bm a_k}\Bigl]}\left(\frac{(-1)^{i_{k}+j_{k}+1}}{l_{k}^{l_{k}}c_{k}}x^{l_{k}}\right).

Then the result follows from the definition of additive convolution using differential operators.∎

4.2
Symmetrization

With the results from last section in hand, we are ready to study the symmetrization of some hypergeometric polynomials using some well-known results of products of hypergeometric functions.

Lemma 4.7
.

Consider tuples
\bm{a}
,
\bm{a}^{\prime}

\bm{b}

\bm{b}^{\prime}

4.3
Multiplicative convolution

The multiplicative convolution of two even hypergeometric polynomials has a very nice expression that follows from Proposition
3.9
and Theorem
2.18
.

Proposition 4.9

(multiplicative convolution of even hypergeometric polynomials)
.

Consider tuples
\bm{a}_{1}
,
\bm{a}_{2}
,
\bm{b}_{1}
,
\bm{b}_{2}
of sizes
i_{1}
,
i_{2}
,
j_{1}
,
j_{2}
. Then

\mathcal{H}^{E}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{\bm b_1}{\bm a_1 }\Bigl]}\boxtimes_{2m}\mathcal{H}^{E}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{\bm b_2}{\bm a_2}\Bigl]}=\mathcal{H}^{E}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{-\frac{1}{2m}, \bm b_1 , \bm b_2 }{1-\frac{1}{2m}, \bm a_1, \bm a_2}\Bigl]}.

Example 4.10

(multiplicative convolution of two Bernoulli polynomials)
.

By Proposition
4.9
, the multiplicative convolution of two Bernoulli polynomials
B_{2m}
from Example
4.2
is given by

B_{2m}\boxtimes_{2m}B_{2m}=\mathcal{H}^{E}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{-\frac{1}{2m}}{1-\frac{1}{2m}}\Bigl]}.

For

, this polynomial is not necessarily real-rooted; instead we can conclude that all the roots lie in the unit circle

. Indeed, since

has roots only at

), we conclude

B_{2m}\boxtimes_{2m}B_{2m}=\mathcal{H}^{E}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{-\frac{1}{2m}}{1-\frac{1}{2m}}\Bigl]}\in\mathcal{P}_{2m}(\mathbb{T}).

Example 4.11

(multiplicative convolution of two Hermite polynomials)
.

The multiplicative convolution of two Hermite polynomials

from Example

H_{2m}\boxtimes_{2m}H_{2m}=\operatorname{Dil}_{\frac{1}{m}}\mathcal{H}^{E}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{-\frac{1}{2m}, 1-\frac{1}{2m}}{\cdot}\Bigl]}.

This polynomial is not real-rooted in general.

Additive convolution

5
Finite free commutators

One of the main insights of
[
19
]
is that the operations
\boxplus_{n}
and
\boxtimes_{n}
, with their peculiar algebraic descriptions reviewed in Definition
2.9
, actually have very natural interpretations involving random matrices. Specifically,
p\boxplus_{n}q
is the expected characteristic polynomial of
A+UBU^{*}
, where
{p(x)=\det(xI_{n}-A)}
and
q(x)=\det(xI_{n}-B)
are the characteristic polynomials of some diagonal
n\times n
matrices
A
and
B
, and
U
is a random
n\times n
unitary matrix. Similarly,
p\boxtimes_{n}q
is the expected characteristic polynomial of
AUBU^{*}
.

A natural next step is to look at other polynomials in
A
and
UBU^{*}
, and try to extract algebraic descriptions of their expected characteristic polynomials. In particular, knowing the historical development of free probability, one might gravitate towards the self-adjoint commutator
i(AUBU^{*}-UBU^{*}A)
. The algebraic description of this commutator operation, due to
[
10
]
, can be set up in purely algebraic terms involving
\boxplus_{n}
,
\boxtimes_{n}
, and a particular special polynomial.

Notation 5.1
.

Let

z_{n}(x):=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}x^{n-2k}(-1)^{k}\binom{n}{2k}\left(n\right)^{\underline{k}}\frac{k!}{(2k)!}\frac{n+1-k}{n+1}

and for polynomials
p(x)
and
q(x)
with degree
n
, write

p\square_{n}q:=\mathrm{Sym}(p)\boxtimes_{n}\mathrm{Sym}(q)\boxtimes_{n}z_{n}.

(5.1)

The use of the symbol
\square
is inspired by its use in
[
25
]
.

With
n=2m
, one can write

z_{2m}=\operatorname{Dil}_{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{H}^{E}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{2, 2, 1-\frac{1}{2m}}{-\frac{1}{2m}, -\frac{1}{2m}, 2+\frac{1}{m}}\Bigl]}.

By Theorem
2.12
, it follows that
z_{2m}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{E}(\mathbb{R})
.

The point of the operation
\square_{n}
is that it encodes the expected characteristic polynomials of randomly rotated matrices.

Theorem 5.2

(
[
10
]
)
.

Let
A
and
B
be normal
n\times n
matrices with characteristic polynomials
{p(x)=\chi(A):=\det[xI-A]}
and
q(x)=\chi(B):=\det[xI-B]
. Then

\mathbb{E}_{U}\chi[i(AUBU^{*}-UBU^{*}A)]=p(x)\square_{n}q(x),

where
U
is a random
n\times n
unitary matrix.

The realization of
z_{n}
as a hypergeometric polynomial suggests a connection between special polynomials and analytic questions about finite free commutators. Our conjecture is that the commutator preserves real-rootedness in all cases.

Conjecture 5.3
.

For
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, we have
p\square_{n}q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
.

A general proof of this conjecture has turned out to be elusive, but we can provide some partial results. From now on, for the sake of simplicity we assume
n=2m
, see Remark
3.2
. First, we can rephrase the result of
[
10
]
in terms of our framework for even polynomials.

Proposition 5.4

(even part of commutator)
.

For
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{2m}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

\bm{Q}_{m}(p\square_{2m}q)=\bm{Q}_{m}(\mathrm{Sym}(p))\boxtimes_{m}\bm{Q}_{m}(\mathrm{Sym}(q))\boxtimes_{m}\operatorname{Dil}_{\frac{1}{4}}\mathcal{H}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{2, 2}{1-\frac{1}{2m}, 2+\frac{1}{m}}\Bigl]}.

Proof.

When one applies
\bm{Q}_{m}
to the right-hand side of equation (
5.1
) and uses Proposition
3.9
, one obtains the expression

\displaystyle\bm{Q}_{m}(\mathrm{Sym}(p))\hskip-0.95pt\boxtimes_{m}\hskip-0.95pt\bm{Q}_{m}(\mathrm{Sym}(q))\hskip-0.95pt\boxtimes_{m}\hskip-0.95pt\operatorname{Dil}_{\frac{1}{4}}\mathcal{H}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{2, 2, 1-\frac{1}{2m}}{-\frac{1}{2m}, -\frac{1}{2m}, 2+\frac{1}{m}}\Bigl]}\hskip-0.95pt\boxtimes_{m}\hskip-0.95pt\mathcal{H}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{-\frac{1}{2m}}{1-\frac{1}{2m}}\Bigl]}\hskip-0.95pt\boxtimes_{m}\hskip-0.95pt\mathcal{H}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{-\frac{1}{2m}}{1-\frac{1}{2m}}\Bigl]}.

By Theorem
2.18
, the hypergeometric polynomials can be combined, and cancellation of parameters leaves

\mathcal{H}_{m}\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}2,2\\
1-\frac{1}{2m},2+\frac{1}{m}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
,
hence the claim.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

It is very important to notice that the polynomial

\mathcal{H}_{m}\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}2,2\\
1-\frac{1}{2m},2+\frac{1}{m}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
,
appearing in Proposition
5.4
, does not belong to
\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
.

Actually, if this polynomial were to be in
\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
, then Conjecture
5.3
would follow from part (3) of Theorem
2.12
, after noticing that

\bm{Q}_{m}(\mathrm{Sym}(p))\in\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
and
\bm{Q}_{m}(\mathrm{Sym}(q))\in\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})

by part (5) of Lemma
3.8
.

This means that to prove Conjecture
5.3
, one must find some kind of “extra” real-rootedness in the symmetrizations
\bm{Q}_{m}(\mathrm{Sym}(p))
. It is unclear if this extra positivity holds in general. With this idea in mind, however, we can explicitly formulate a theorem that is similar to Conjecture
5.3
, but requires an extra assumption.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{2m}(\mathbb{R})
and suppose that

\bm{Q}_{m}(\mathrm{Sym}(q))=\mathcal{H}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{1-\frac{1}{2m}}{\cdot}\Bigl]}\boxtimes_{m}r

for some
r\in\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
. Then
p\square_{2m}q\in\mathcal{P}_{2m}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

Starting with the expression

\bm{Q}_{m}(p\square_{2m}q)=\bm{Q}_{m}(\mathrm{Sym}(p))\boxtimes_{m}\bm{Q}_{m}(\mathrm{Sym}(q))\boxtimes\operatorname{Dil}_{\frac{1}{4}}\mathcal{H}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{2, 2}{1-\frac{1}{2m}, 2+\frac{1}{m}}\Bigl]}

from Proposition
5.4
, it suffices to show the polynomial on the right-hand side has all its roots in
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
We can compute

\displaystyle\bm{Q}_{m}(p\square_{2m}q)

\displaystyle=\bm{Q}_{2m}(\mathrm{Sym}(p))\boxtimes_{m}r\boxtimes_{m}\mathcal{H}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{1-\frac{1}{2m}}{\cdot}\Bigl]}\boxtimes_{m}\operatorname{Dil}_{\frac{1}{4}}\mathcal{H}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{2, 2}{1-\frac{1}{2m}, 2+\frac{1}{m}}\Bigl]}

\displaystyle=\bm{Q}_{m}(\mathrm{Sym}(p))\boxtimes_{m}r\boxtimes_{m}\operatorname{Dil}_{\frac{1}{4}}\mathcal{H}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{2, 2}{2+\frac{1}{m}}\Bigl]}

and all three polynomials above are in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
. By Theorem
2.12
, this shows
\bm{Q}_{2m}(p\square_{2m}q)\in\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
, and in turn that
p\square_{2m}q\in\mathcal{P}_{2m}(\mathbb{R})
.
∎

and allows one to put
any

6
Asymptotics and connection to free probability

Most of the results from previous sections have counterparts in free probability, if we let the degree
n
tend to infinity while the empirical root distribution tends to a compact measure. Some of these results are well known in free probability, and in fact served as original motivation for the results in finite free probability. However, to the best of our knowledge, many examples are new, and might shed some light on future directions in this area.

We begin by explicitly proving the intuitive fact that when we let
m\to\infty
, the map
\bm{S}_{m}
from Notation
3.4
tends to the map
S
from Notation
2.4
.
Thus, the bijection between
\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
and
\mathcal{P}_{2m}^{E}(\mathbb{R})
from Fact
3.6
, in the limit becomes the bijection between
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
and
\mathcal{M}^{E}(\mathbb{R})
from Lemma
2.5
.

Proposition 6.1

(even polynomials approximate symmetric measures)
.

Let
\mathfrak{p}=(p_{m})_{m\geq 1}
be a converging sequence of positive-rooted polynomials. Then
\sqrt{\mathfrak{p}}:=(\bm{S}_{m}(p_{2m}))_{m\geq 1}
is a converging sequence of even real-rooted polynomials, with
\rho(\sqrt{\mathfrak{p}})=S(\rho(\mathfrak{p}))
.

Similarly, if
\mathfrak{q}=(q_{2m})_{m\geq 1}
is a converging sequence of even real-rooted polynomials, then
\mathfrak{q}^{2}:=(\bm{Q}_{m}(q_{2m}))_{m\geq 1}
is a converging sequence of positive-rooted polynomials with
\rho\bigl(\mathfrak{q}^{2}\bigr)=Q(\rho(\mathfrak{q}))
.

Proof.

The basic tool involved in the proof of this proposition is the fact
[
7
, Theorem 2.7]
that pushforwards along continuous functions preserve weak limits.

For the first claim, the assumptions amount to
\lim_{m\to\infty}\rho(p_{m})=\rho(\mathfrak{p})
. Then

\lim_{m\to\infty}S_{+}(\rho(p_{m}))=S_{+}(\rho(\mathfrak{p}))\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{m\to\infty}S_{-}(\rho(p_{m}))=S_{-}(\rho(\mathfrak{p}))

since
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\colon t\mapsto\sqrt{t}
and
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}\colon t\mapsto-\sqrt{t}
are continuous. This shows that

\lim_{m\to\infty}S(\rho(p_{m}))=S(\rho(\mathfrak{p})).

Since
S(\rho(p_{m}))=\rho(\bm{S}_{m}(p_{m}))
, we have

\lim_{m\to\infty}\rho(\bm{S}_{m}(p_{m}))=S(\rho(\mathfrak{p})),

hence
\sqrt{\mathfrak{p}}=(\bm{S}_{m}(p_{m})_{m\geq 1}
is a converging sequence with
\rho(\sqrt{\mathfrak{p}})=S(\rho(\mathfrak{p}))
.

For the second claim, suppose that
\mathfrak{q}=(q_{2m})_{m\geq 1}
is a converging sequence of even real-rooted polynomials, i.e.,
\lim_{m\to\infty}\rho(q_{2m})=\rho(\mathfrak{q})
. Then since
\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\colon t\mapsto t^{2}
is continuous, we have

\lim_{m\to\infty}Q(\rho(q_{2m}))=Q\bigl(\lim_{m\to\infty}\rho(q_{2m})\bigr)=Q(\rho(\mathfrak{q})).

Since
Q(\rho(q_{2m})=\rho(\bm{Q}_{m}(q_{2m}))
, this shows that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\rho(\bm{Q}_{m}(q_{2m}))=Q(\rho(\mathfrak{q})),

i.e.,
\mathfrak{q}^{2}=(\bm{Q}_{m}(q_{2m}))_{m\geq 1}
is a converging sequence with
\rho\bigl(\mathfrak{q}^{2}\bigr)=Q(\rho(\mathfrak{q}))
.
∎

Using this result, we can study the limiting root distributions of even polynomial. Specifically, the examples from Section
4
involving hypergeometric polynomials can be studied asymptotically, using free convolutions and even measures. Recall from Theorem
2.19
and
Notation

2.20
that the asymptotic root distribution of hypergeometric polynomials is given by a
S
-rational measure
\rho\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}b_{1},\dots,b_{j}\\
a_{1},\dots,a_{i}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
, determined by having an
S
-transform of the form
\frac{(z+a_{1})\cdots(z+a_{i})}{(z+b_{1})\cdots(z+b_{j})}
. Recall also that we denote the square root of an
S
-rational function by

\rho^{E}\bigl[\!\begin{smallmatrix}b_{1},\dots,b_{j}\\
a_{1},\dots,a_{i}\end{smallmatrix}\!\bigr]
.

We now state an analogue of Theorem
2.19
that holds for even hypergeometric polynomials.

Theorem 6.2
.

For non-negative integers
i
,
j
consider tuples of parameters
\bm{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{i})\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus[0,1))^{i}
and
\bm{B}=(B_{1},\dots,B_{j})\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})^{j}
. Assume that
\mathfrak{p}=(p_{m})_{m\geq 0}
is a sequence of polynomials given by

p_{m}=\operatorname{Dil}_{\sqrt{m^{i-j}}}\mathcal{H}^{E}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{\bm b_m}{\bm a_m}\Bigl]}\in\mathcal{P}^{E}_{2m}(\mathbb{R}),

where the tuples of parameters
\bm{a}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{i}
and
\bm{b}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{j}
have a limit given by

\lim_{m\to\infty}\bm{a}_{m}=\bm{A}
, and
\lim_{m\to\infty}\bm{b}_{m}=\bm{B}
.
Then
\mathfrak{p}
is a converging sequence
(
in the sense of Definition
2.6
)
that converges to the measure

\rho^{E}\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}B_{1},\dots,B_{j}\\
A_{1},\dots,A_{i}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\in\mathcal{M}^{E}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

Follows from Proposition
6.1
and Theorem
2.19
.
∎

Example 6.3

(Hermite polynomials and Laguerre polynomials)
.

In free probability, it is known
[
26
, Proposition 12.13]
that the square of a semicircular element has a free Poisson distribution with rate
1
. We can observe a similar phenomenon by applying our operations
\bm{Q}_{m}
and
\bm{S}_{m}
to Hermite and Laguerre polynomials; namely, it corresponds to the observation in Example
2.22
that Hermite polynomials are related to certain Laguerre polynomials with squared variables.

More precisely, recall that the respective finite analogues of the free Poisson distribution with rate
1
and the semicircular distribution with rate
2
are the polynomials

L_{n}^{(1)}(x)=n^{-n}\mathcal{H}_{n}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{1}{\cdot}\Bigl]}(nx)\qquad\text{and}\qquad H_{2m}(x)=m^{-m}\mathcal{H}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{1-\frac{1}{2m}}{\cdot}\Bigl]}\bigl(mx^{2}\bigr).

We have

\bm{Q}_{m}(H_{2m})=m^{-m}\mathcal{H}_{m}{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{1-\frac{1}{2m}}{\cdot}\Bigl]}(mx)

and since
\lim_{m\to\infty}(1-\frac{1}{2m})=1
, by Theorem
2.19
, we have

\lim_{m\to\infty}\rho(\bm{Q}_{m}(H_{2m}))=\rho{\Bigl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{1}{1}{\cdot}\Bigl]}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\rho\bigl(L_{n}^{(1)}\bigr).

We thus recover the aforementioned result concerning the square of a semicircular variable.

The symmetrization operation from Notation

Notation 6.4
.

For
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
, define the free symmetrization of
\mu
by

\mathrm{Sym}(\mu):=\mu\boxplus(\operatorname{Dil}_{-1}\mu)\in\mathcal{M}^{E}(\mathbb{R}).

Remark 6.5
.

Despite being a simple operation, to the best of our knowledge
\mathrm{Sym}(\mu)
has not been studied systematically in free probability. Some particular instances where this operation has appeared include
[
15
, Final remarks]
,
[
24
, p. 274]
,
[
28
, Example 25]
, and
[
3
, Example 7.9 (3)]
.

Notice also that the term of
free symmetrization
has appeared before in the literature, but denoting a different notion, like in
[
15

. It is unclear if there is a connection between both notions.

It would be interesting to study
\mathrm{Sym}(\mu)
in detail, due to its potential connections to the commutator. Below we present some basic properties, that are analogous to those of the symmetrization of polynomials.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, and let
\delta_{\alpha}
be the Dirac measure with and atom of mass one in
\alpha
. Then

(1)

\mathrm{Sym}(\mu)\in\mathcal{M}^{E}
;

(2)

\mathrm{Sym}(\mu\boxplus\nu)=\mathrm{Sym}(\mu)\boxplus\mathrm{Sym}(\nu)
;

(3)

\mathrm{Sym}(\operatorname{Dil}_{\alpha}\mu)=\operatorname{Dil}_{\alpha}\mathrm{Sym}(\mu)
;

(4)

\mathrm{Sym}(\mu\boxplus\delta_{\alpha})=\mathrm{Sym}(\mu)
;

(5)

\bm{Q}(\mathrm{Sym}(\mu))\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
.

Proof.

The proof is analogous to that of Lemma
3.8
; one just needs to substitute the polynomials for measures, and change
\boxplus_{n}
to
\boxplus
.
∎

6.6

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
\mathfrak{p}=(p_{m})_{m\geq 1}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
be a converging sequence of polynomials. Then
\mathrm{Sym}(\mathfrak{p}):=(\mathrm{Sym}(p_{m}))_{m\geq 1}\subset\mathcal{P}^{E}(\mathbb{R})
is also a converging sequence of polynomials, and
\rho(\operatorname{Sym}(\mathfrak{p}))\allowbreak=\operatorname{Sym}(\rho(\mathfrak{p}))

The claim follows from Theorem

(\operatorname{Dil}_{-1}p_{m})_{m\geq 1}
