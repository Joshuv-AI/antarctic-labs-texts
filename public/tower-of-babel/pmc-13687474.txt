Gender-stratified associations between resilience dimensions and big five personality traits among emerging adult college students in India
Authors: Shikha Verma, Nikita Singh, Ritika Bajpai, Indu Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmerging adulthood is a developmental stage marked by identity exploration, psychosocial transitions, and heightened vulnerability to stress. Resilience and personality traits are important for adaptive functioning during this period; however, limited research has examined their multidimensional associations in Indian emerging adults, particularly in relation to how these traits influence coping strategies and overall mental health outcomes during this critical developmental stage.ObjectiveThis study examined the relationships between multidimensional resilience and Big Five personality traits among emerging adult college students, with a focus on gender differences in resilience and personality dimensions.MethodsA descriptive cross-sectional correlational design was used with 335 college students (170 females, 165 males) aged 20–25 years from Kanpur Nagar, India. Participants were selected using disproportionate stratified random sampling. Personality traits were assessed using the Big Five Inventory-10 (BFI-10), and resilience was measured using the Multidimensional Scale of Resilience–Hindi (MDRS-H). Data were analysed using descriptive statistics, independent-samples t-tests, Pearson’s correlations, and regression analysis.ResultsSignificant gender differences were observed across several resilience dimensions and personality traits. Females scored higher on internal locus of control, forgiveness, sociability, and agreeableness, whereas males scored higher on emotional maturity, extraversion, neuroticism, openness to experience, total personality, and overall resilience. Gender-stratified analyses indicated distinct associations between resilience dimensions and personality traits among males and females. Total resilience was positively associated with overall personality traits in both genders. Among females, assertiveness was positively associated with extraversion, sociability was negatively associated with neuroticism, and hardiness was positively associated with openness to experience. Among males, self-acceptance, humour, and assertiveness were associated with extraversion; forgiveness and emotional maturity were associated with agreeableness; and self-acceptance was associated with openness to experience. Effect sizes were modest.ConclusionResilience dimensions were selectively associated with personality traits, and these associations varied across gender. Although some gender differences were observed in specific resilience dimensions and personality traits, the findings emphasise the multidimensional nature of resilience and personality during emerging adulthood. The study highlights the importance of culturally contextualised approaches to understanding psychological adaptation and supports the need for longitudinal research to further examine developmental patterns linking resilience and personality.

Introduction

Emerging adulthood is a distinct developmental stage, typically spanning ages 18–29, and characterised by substantial psychological, social, and biological transitions [1, 2]. During this period, individuals navigate identity exploration, self-focus, educational and occupational decision-making, changing interpersonal relationships, and increasing autonomy while often experiencing instability and uncertainty [1]. Arnett’s developmental framework identifies five defining features of emerging adulthood—identity exploration, instability, self-focus, feeling in-between, and optimism—which collectively reflect both opportunity and vulnerability [1]. This transitional period is increasingly recognised as a critical phase for understanding psychological adaptation because emerging adults must navigate developmental challenges while establishing long-term personal and professional trajectories.

From a neurodevelopmental perspective, emerging adulthood involves continued maturation of brain systems associated with executive functioning, emotional regulation, impulse control, and decision-making [3]. These ongoing biological developments interact with contextual demands such as academic competition, employment pressures, financial uncertainty, and evolving social roles. In the Indian sociocultural context, emerging adults often face additional pressures related to familial expectations, collectivistic values, career competition, and societal norms, which may intensify stress exposure and influence psychological adjustment [4]. Such culturally embedded developmental challenges make the study of adaptive capacities particularly relevant among Indian college students.

In this context, resilience has emerged as a critical framework for understanding how emerging adults adapt to developmental stressors and psychosocial challenges. Rather than conceptualising resilience solely as a fixed personality trait, contemporary perspectives increasingly define it as a multidimensional and dynamic process involving positive adaptation despite adversity [5, 6]. Resilience encompasses emotional regulation, cognitive flexibility, behavioural competence, social connectedness, and self-efficacy, all of which enable individuals to manage stress effectively and maintain psychological well-being [7, 8]. This multidimensional conceptualisation is especially important in emerging adulthood, where adaptive functioning depends on managing diverse academic, interpersonal, and identity-related challenges. Prior research has demonstrated that resilience is associated with improved mental health, reduced psychological distress, and enhanced coping under adversity [9].

Theoretical models examining resilience and personality suggest that resilience may be shaped by relatively stable dispositional characteristics while also functioning as an adaptive process responsive to environmental demands [10]. In this context, personality traits serve as significant indicators of resilience because they affect emotional reactivity, coping strategies, behaviour regulation, and social functioning. The Five-Factor Model (FFM) of personality—comprising openness to experience, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism—provides one of the most widely accepted frameworks for understanding individual differences in psychological functioning [11]. Although personality traits are generally considered relatively stable dispositions, they are also associated with patterns of coping and adaptation that may shape resilience capacities [12]. Accordingly, the present study does not assume that resilience causally determines personality; rather, it examines the pattern of associations between multidimensional resilience domains and personality traits to better understand their interrelationship in emerging adulthood.

A growing body of evidence suggests meaningful associations between personality traits and resilience. Neuroticism is consistently negatively associated with resilience due to heightened emotional instability, maladaptive coping, and vulnerability to stress [13, 14]. In contrast, extraversion, conscientiousness, agreeableness, and openness are generally positively associated with resilience because they facilitate social engagement, adaptive coping, goal-directed behaviour, and psychological flexibility [15–17]. For example, conscientious individuals often demonstrate better behavioural regulation and persistence, whereas extraverted individuals may benefit from stronger social support networks [18]. These associations point out the potential value of examining personality traits alongside resilience dimensions when studying adaptive functioning.

However, much of the existing literature has relied on global resilience scores or unidimensional conceptualisations, which may obscure important variations across resilience domains. Multidimensional resilience frameworks provide a more nuanced understanding by examining specific components such as self-efficacy, emotional regulation, adaptability, and social resources [7, 19]. This is particularly relevant because different personality traits may relate differently to distinct resilience dimensions rather than resilience as a singular construct.

Gender also represents an important contextual factor in resilience and personality research, though findings remain inconsistent across studies and cultural settings [20]. Some research suggests that males and females may differ in their emotional coping styles, socialisation patterns, and personality expressions, potentially influencing their resilience processes [21]. However, evidence regarding gender differences is mixed, and many prior studies have not adequately examined whether observed associations differ statistically by gender. Therefore, gender-stratified analyses should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory patterns rather than definitive gender-specific effects.

Most empirical studies have focused on Western populations, limiting cultural generalisability [22], despite the growing global interest in resilience and personality. Non-Western contexts such as India remain under-represented, even though sociocultural norms, educational systems, and family structures may meaningfully shape both resilience and personality development [4], which highlights the need for more inclusive research that considers these factors in understanding resilience and personality in diverse cultural settings. Furthermore, research among Indian emerging adults has rarely examined resilience as a multidimensional construct in relation to the Five-Factor Model. Addressing this gap is important for improving a culturally contextualised understanding of psychological adaptation during emerging adulthood.

Therefore, the present study investigates associations between multidimensional resilience dimensions and Big Five personality traits among Indian emerging adult college students, with separate analyses for male and female participants to explore gender-stratified patterns. By focusing on multidimensional resilience rather than solely total resilience scores, this study aims to provide a more theoretically nuanced and culturally relevant contribution to resilience-personality research while acknowledging the methodological limitations associated with brief personality measurement tools.

Rationale of the present study

Resilience is widely recognised as an important protective factor that supports psychological well-being, adaptive coping, and mental health during periods of developmental transitions and psychosocial stress [24, 25]. Emerging adulthood, particularly within higher education contexts, represents a period marked by academic demands, identity-related uncertainty, interpersonal transitions, and future-orientated pressures, all of which may heighten vulnerability to stress and psychological maladjustment [1, 24]. In this developmental context, understanding factors associated with resilience is essential for promoting mental health and adaptive functioning.

Personality traits are similarly relevant because they influence how individuals perceive, interpret, and respond to stressful situations through typical patterns of emotional regulation, behavioural tendencies, and coping strategies [26]. Existing literature suggests that personality traits are associated with resilience; however, much of this research has focused primarily on Western populations and has often relied on global resilience measures rather than multidimensional frameworks [13–17]. These limitations hinder our understanding of how specific dimensions of resilience may differentially relate to personality characteristics, particularly in culturally distinct contexts like India.

Furthermore, previous studies have often emphasised resilience as an outcome predicted by personality traits, given the relative stability of personality dispositions. In the present study, we acknowledge this conceptual framework while analytically examining resilience dimensions as associated variables within regression models to better understand their relationship with personality traits rather than to imply causal directionality. This distinction is important for theoretical clarity and addresses concerns regarding conceptual overreach.

Within India, emerging adult college students face unique sociocultural challenges, including competitive academic environments, family expectations, economic pressures, and rapidly changing social roles [4]. Despite these contextual factors, limited research has systematically examined multidimensional resilience and Big Five personality traits among Indian college populations. Additionally, while gender differences are frequently explored, prior findings are inconsistent, and many studies interpret gender-stratified findings too strongly without directly testing gender-based statistical differences, which raises questions about the validity of their conclusions and the need for more rigorous analysis in this area.

Therefore, the present study aims to examine associations between multidimensional resilience dimensions and Big Five personality traits among Indian emerging adult college students while also exploring gender-stratified patterns in these associations. Instead of claiming definitive gender-specific predictors, the study aims to uncover varying patterns between male and female subsamples in an exploratory fashion. By doing so, this research contributes to culturally relevant resilience literature and offers a more nuanced view of personality-resilience associations during emerging adulthood.

Methodology

Research design

A descriptive, cross-sectional, and correlational research design was employed to examine associations between multidimensional resilience and Big Five personality traits among emerging adult college students. This design was selected to identify patterns of association among variables in a naturalistic context without manipulation and does not permit causal inference. The correlational framework is appropriate for exploring psychological relationships while acknowledging that directionality cannot be definitively established.

Sample

The sample comprised 335 college students (170 females and 165 males) between the ages of 20 and 25 years from colleges in Kanpur Nagar, India. Participants were selected using disproportionate stratified random sampling. To improve methodological transparency, strata were formed on the basis of gender to ensure adequate representation of male and female participants for subgroup analyses. Participants were recruited from selected colleges through institutional access and voluntary participation procedures. Inclusion criteria required participants to be currently enrolled college students within the specified age range, while individuals outside the age range or not enrolled in college were excluded.

Although the sampling strategy was designed to facilitate gender comparisons, the disproportionate nature of the approach may limit broader population generalisability, which is acknowledged as a methodological limitation.

Measures

Big five personality traits

Personality traits were measured using the Big Five Inventory-10 (BFI-10; [27]), an ultra-brief self-report instrument designed for research contexts requiring efficient assessment of the Five-Factor Model under severe time constraints. The BFI-10 was derived from the original 44-item Big Five Inventory (BFI-44) through systematic psychometric selection procedures intended to preserve structural validity, broad construct representation, and cross-cultural applicability while substantially reducing administration time to approximately one minute [27].

The instrument comprises 10 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, which is a common survey format ranging from 1 (disagree strongly) to 5 (agree strongly). Each of the five personality dimensions is assessed using two items representing both poles of the construct, with reverse scoring applied where appropriate. Scoring was conducted as follows: Extraversion (Items 1R and 6), Agreeableness (Items 2 and 7R), Conscientiousness (Items 3R and 8), Neuroticism (Items 4R and 9), and Openness to Experience (Items 5R and 10) [27]. The scoring structure was specifically developed to maximise content breadth while minimising redundancies within each trait domain.

Psychometric evaluation by Rammstedt and John [27] demonstrated that the BFI-10 retains substantial reliability and validity despite its brevity. Across validation samples, the abbreviated scales demonstrated mean part-whole correlations of 0.83 with the full BFI-44 scales, indicating that the BFI-10 captures approximately 70% of the variance of the longer instrument. Test–retest reliability across 6–8 weeks ranged from 0.72 to 0.78, with an overall mean of 0.75, reflecting respectable temporal stability for brief personality assessment [27]. Structural validity analyses consistently supported the expected five-factor structure, with clear factor loadings, low intercorrelations among scales, and strong discriminant validity. Convergent validity with the NEO-PI-R domain scales averaged 0.67, while external validity based on peer ratings averaged 0.44 [27]. Collectively, these findings support the utility of the BFI-10 in large-scale or time-constrained psychological research settings.

In response to concerns regarding psychometric transparency and measurement adequacy, internal consistency for the BFI-10 was calculated for the present sample and demonstrated acceptable reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.78). The BFI-10 was deemed suitable for comprehensive personality evaluation in the present research context; however, its exceedingly concise format may hinder accuracy in nuanced trait-level analysis, especially when investigating subgroup or gender-stratified correlations. Accordingly, personality-related findings were interpreted cautiously, and this limitation is explicitly acknowledged as strengthening methodological rigour, transparency, and balance in interpretation [27].

Multidimensional resilience

Resilience was assessed using the Multidimensional Scale of Resilience–Hindi (MDRS-H) developed by Singh and Khullar [28], a culturally grounded self-report instrument specifically constructed to assess psychological resilience within the Indian population through a multidimensional and theoretically integrated framework. The MDRS-H was developed in response to the limitations identified in existing resilience measures, particularly their frequent overreliance on Western conceptualisations, unidimensional structures, and inadequate cultural applicability for Indian populations. The scale conceptualises resilience not merely as a fixed trait but as a dynamic and trainable psychological construct shaped by multiple protective factors that facilitate positive adaptation under adversity [28].

The development of the MDRS-H was based on extensive theoretical review and psychometric refinement. Singh and Khullar [28] identified ten protective dimensions central to resilience: the internal locus of control, self-acceptance, assertiveness, hardiness, forgiveness, sociability, optimism, emotional maturity, humour, and mindfulness. These domains were selected to represent resilience as the integration of cognitive, emotional, behavioural, and social capacities rather than as a single dispositional factor. This multidimensional structure is particularly relevant for emerging adulthood because it captures diverse adaptive processes associated with psychosocial adjustment, coping, and developmental transitions.

The MDRS-H consists of 46 self-report items presented in jumbled form to reduce response bias. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from Strongly Agree to Strongly Disagree [28]. Positive items are scored from 5 to 1, whereas negatively worded items are reverse scored. Total scores range from 46 to 230, with higher scores indicating greater resilience. In addition to generating a global resilience score, the scale permits dimension-wise interpretation, thereby allowing more nuanced analysis of resilience profiles.

The scale development process followed rigorous psychometric procedures. An initial pool of 178 items was generated from resilience theory and related constructs, followed by expert review, content validation, item-total correlation analysis, and pilot testing [28]. The final instrument was standardised on an Indian sample of 484 participants, thereby enhancing its cultural appropriateness and normative utility [28]. Content validity was established through expert evaluation, while concurrent validity was supported through correlation with the Psychological Hardiness Scale–Hindi (r = .658) [28, 29].

Psychometric analyses indicate that the MDRS-H possesses satisfactory reliability and validity. The original scale demonstrated strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.816), along with satisfactory split-half reliability (Spearman-Brown = 0.798; Guttman = 0.796) [28]. These findings support its appropriateness for both research and applied settings.

In the present study, the MDRS-H demonstrated strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.81), indicating satisfactory reliability within the current sample. This supports the adequacy of the scale for assessing multidimensional resilience among emerging adult college students in the present context. The use of a culturally relevant and multidimensional resilience measure also strengthens the conceptual and methodological framework of the study by enabling a more comprehensive assessment of resilience dimensions beyond global resilience scores [28].

The MDRS-H was selected because of its multidimensional structure, theoretical comprehensiveness, and cultural relevance to Indian emerging adults. Its emphasis on adaptive protective capacities rather than solely deficit-based approaches aligns with contemporary resilience theory and provides a nuanced framework for examining resilience-personality associations in higher education settings.

Procedure

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the relevant institutional authority prior to data collection. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before participation. Participants were informed about the purpose of the study, confidentiality of responses, voluntary participation, and their right to withdraw at any stage without penalty. Data were collected through standard group administration procedures in academic settings under uniform instructions. Consent to participate and consent to publish procedures are now separately clarified in the manuscript’s Declarations section in accordance with journal requirements.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS (Version 24.0) [30]. Descriptive statistics were computed for all study variables. Independent sample t-tests were used to examine gender differences in resilience dimensions and personality traits. Pearson product–moment correlations were conducted to examine associations between resilience dimensions and personality traits. Multiple regression analyses were performed separately for male and female groups to explore patterns of association.

In response to reviewer concerns, all statistical outputs were thoroughly rechecked for reporting accuracy, including p-values, t-values, and significance labels. Effect sizes were added where appropriate to improve interpretive clarity. Given the large number of statistical comparisons, findings were interpreted cautiously due to the increased possibility of Type I error, and this limitation is explicitly acknowledged.

No causal claims are made from these analyses, and regression findings are discussed as associative patterns rather than definitive predictive relationships.

Comparison of resilience between male and female emerging adults

At the dimensional level, significant gender differences were observed for internal locus of control, forgiveness, sociability, optimism, and emotional maturity. Female students scored significantly higher on internal locus of control, forgiveness, sociability, and optimism. These findings indicate that females reported stronger perceptions of personal control, greater capacity for forgiveness, higher social connectedness, and a more positive outlook toward future outcomes.

In contrast, male students scored significantly higher on emotional maturity suggesting comparatively greater emotional stability and regulation within this sample. No statistically significant gender differences were found for self-acceptance, assertiveness, hardiness, humour, or mindfulness (all p > .05), indicating that these resilience dimensions were broadly comparable across genders.

Overall, the findings suggest that gender differences in resilience are not uniform across all domains. Rather, females demonstrated advantages in several interpersonal and cognitive-emotional resilience resources, whereas males reported higher emotional maturity. These results highlight the multidimensional nature of resilience and underscore the importance of examining specific resilience components rather than relying solely on global resilience scores.

Gender differences in big five personality traits

Gender comparisons across the Big Five personality traits revealed several significant differences (Table 2; Fig. 2). Males scored significantly higher on extraversion, neuroticism, and openness to experience, whereas females reported significantly higher agreeableness. No significant gender difference was observed for conscientiousness.

The pattern of findings suggests that female participants tended to exhibit greater interpersonal warmth and cooperativeness, while male participants demonstrated higher levels of social expressiveness, emotional reactivity, and openness toward new experiences. At the dimensional level, assertiveness showed a significant positive correlation with extraversion (r = .254, p < .05), suggesting that females who reported greater assertiveness also tended to exhibit higher levels of sociability, confidence, and interpersonal engagement. Hardiness demonstrated a significant positive correlation with openness to experience (r = .260, p < .05), indicating that greater psychological endurance and adaptability were modestly associated with openness-related tendencies such as curiosity and flexibility.

Sociability demonstrated a significant negative correlation with neuroticism (r = –.294, p < .05), suggesting that females with stronger social connectedness reported comparatively lower emotional instability. Sociability was also negatively correlated with openness to experience (r = –.265, p < .05), indicating a more complex relationship in which socially orientated resilience may not uniformly align with exploratory or novelty-seeking tendencies, as individuals who are more sociable might prefer familiar social settings over new experiences.

Most other correlations between resilience dimensions and personality traits were weak and statistically non-significant, suggesting that associations were selective rather than global. These findings indicate that specific resilience components, rather than total resilience alone, may differentially relate to distinct personality dimensions. The observed effect sizes were modest, and therefore interpretations should remain cautious, particularly given the number of comparisons conducted. Overall, the results highlight nuanced associations between selected resilience dimensions and personality traits among female emerging adults rather than broad predictive patterns.

A simple linear regression analysis was conducted to examine whether assertiveness significantly predicted extraversion among female participants (Table 4). Assertiveness emerged as a statistically significant positive predictor of extraversion (β = 0.254, p < .05), explaining 6.5% of the variance in extraversion scores (R² = 0.065). This indicates that higher levels of assertiveness were modestly associated with higher levels of extraversion. Although statistically significant, the proportion of explained variance was relatively small, suggesting that assertiveness represents only one limited contributor to extraversion within this sample. Therefore, findings should be interpreted cautiously and not as evidence of strong predictive influence.

Discussion

The present study examined multidimensional resilience and Big Five personality traits among Indian emerging adult college students, with particular attention to gender-based patterns at both global and dimensional levels. Overall, the findings indicate that gender differences are more nuanced and dimension-specific than broad or uniform, which suggests interpreting resilience and personality within a multidimensional developmental framework rather than based on generalised categorical assumptions [1, 9].

Gender differences in resilience dimensions

The present study identified a statistically significant gender difference in overall resilience, with males reporting slightly higher total resilience scores than females. Although the magnitude of this difference was modest, it suggests that resilience may not be entirely comparable across gender within the present sample. Previous research has reported inconsistent findings regarding gender differences in resilience, with some studies indicating higher resilience among males and others reporting either female advantages or no significant differences, highlighting the contextual and culturally influenced nature of resilience development [20, 24].

At the dimensional level, females demonstrated significantly higher scores on internal locus of control, forgiveness, sociability, and optimism. These findings may reflect greater utilisation of interpersonal and cognitive-emotional resilience resources among female emerging adults. Higher levels of forgiveness and sociability are consistent with evidence suggesting that women are more likely to engage in relational coping strategies and seek social support during stressful situations [23, 31]. Similarly, higher optimism and internal locus of control may indicate stronger perceptions of personal agency and positive future orientation among female participants.

In contrast, males scored significantly higher on emotional maturity. This finding may suggest greater emotional regulation or perceived emotional stability among male participants in the present sample, although cultural expectations regarding emotional expression may also contribute to this pattern. No significant gender differences were observed for self-acceptance, assertiveness, hardiness, humour, or mindfulness, indicating that several core resilience resources were similarly distributed across male and female participants. Collectively, these findings support the multidimensional nature of resilience and suggest that gender differences are more evident within specific resilience components than as a uniform characteristic of resilience itself [24, 25].

Gender differences in big five personality traits

Gender differences were also evident across several personality dimensions. Females scored significantly higher on agreeableness, a finding consistent with previous research linking women with greater empathy, interpersonal sensitivity, cooperation, and prosocial orientation [32, 33]. This pattern may correspond with the higher levels of forgiveness and sociability observed among females, suggesting convergence between interpersonal resilience resources and socially oriented personality characteristics.

Males demonstrated significantly higher scores on extraversion, neuroticism, and openness to experience. Higher extraversion may reflect greater social assertiveness and behavioural engagement, whereas elevated openness to experience suggests stronger tendencies toward intellectual curiosity, exploration, and receptiveness to novel experiences. The finding of higher neuroticism among males diverges from some previous studies and therefore warrants cautious interpretation. Cultural influences, contextual stressors, or sample-specific characteristics may partially explain this pattern. In addition, the BFI-10 is an ultra-brief personality measure designed for efficient assessment of broad personality domains; consequently, trait-level gender differences should be interpreted conservatively due to the limited number of items representing each construct [27]. No significant gender difference was observed for conscientiousness, indicating comparable levels of organisation, responsibility, and goal-directed behaviour across male and female participants. Furthermore, the significant difference observed in overall personality scores suggests that gender-related variation may extend beyond isolated traits and reflect broader personality profile differences within this sample. Taken together, these findings indicate that gender differences among emerging adults are evident across selected personality domains, although the magnitude and interpretation of these differences should be considered within the cultural and methodological context of the study [34–38].

Gender-specific associations between resilience dimensions and big five personality traits

A key contribution of the present study is the identification of selective associations between resilience dimensions and personality traits across gender groups. These findings should be interpreted as patterns of association rather than evidence of causal relationships, given the cross-sectional nature of the study and the relatively stable nature of personality traits [6, 11].

Among females, assertiveness was positively associated with extraversion, suggesting that confidence in self-expression may be linked to greater social engagement. Sociability was negatively associated with neuroticism, supporting previous evidence that social connectedness may serve as a protective factor against emotional instability [7, 8]. Hardiness was positively associated with openness to experience, whereas sociability showed a negative association, indicating that different resilience dimensions may relate uniquely to openness-related tendencies. However, these associations were modest in magnitude and should be interpreted cautiously.

Among males, resilience dimensions demonstrated broader associations with personality traits. Self-acceptance, humour, and assertiveness were associated with extraversion, while forgiveness and emotional maturity were related to agreeableness. Internal locus of control contributed to conscientiousness, and self-acceptance showed a strong positive association with openness to experience. Although these findings suggest greater alignment between resilience dimensions and personality functioning among males, the observed effect sizes were generally modest to moderate.

The positive association between forgiveness and neuroticism among males was unexpected and warrants cautious interpretation. This finding may reflect contextual, cultural, or measurement-related influences and should be examined further in future longitudinal research.

Theoretical and cultural significance

The present results support resilience and personality research by extending investigation into a non-Western, Indian higher-education context using a multidimensional resilience framework. Much prior work has relied on Western samples and global resilience scores [12–14]. By examining culturally relevant resilience dimensions through the MDRS-H, the study offers more contextually grounded insight into how adaptive resources may intersect with personality among Indian emerging adults. This is particularly important given that emerging adulthood in India is often shaped by educational competition, familial interdependence, and sociocultural expectations that may influence both resilience processes and personality expression [2, 25], potentially leading to unique challenges and coping strategies that differ from those in other cultural contexts, such as the need to balance academic pressures with family obligations and societal norms.

At the same time, methodological limitations necessitate caution. The use of the BFI-10, while efficient and psychometrically acceptable for broad trait screening, may constrain precision for trait-specific and gender-stratified analyses [27]. Additionally, the cross-sectional correlational design precludes causal inference. Therefore, findings should be viewed as preliminary evidence of selective psychosocial associations rather than definitive gender-specific predictive models.

Conclusion

The present study highlights the complex and multidimensional relationships between resilience dimensions and personality traits among emerging adult college students. Significant gender differences were observed in overall resilience and several specific personality traits, indicating that resilience and personality functioning may vary across gender in nuanced ways. Females demonstrated higher levels of internal locus of control, forgiveness, sociability, optimism, and agreeableness, whereas males reported higher emotional maturity, extraversion, neuroticism, openness to experience, overall resilience, and total personality scores.

The findings further revealed that associations between resilience dimensions and personality traits differed across male and female groups, suggesting that adaptive psychosocial resources may relate to personality functioning through gender-specific patterns of psychological adjustment. Rather than reflecting broad or uniform differences, these relationships appeared selective and dimension-specific, underscoring the importance of examining resilience as a multidimensional construct.

Overall, the study supports the value of culturally contextualised and multidimensional approaches to understanding resilience and personality during emerging adulthood. Resilience resources such as internal locus of control, self-acceptance, assertiveness, sociability, forgiveness, and emotional maturity demonstrated meaningful associations with personality characteristics, highlighting their potential relevance for psychological well-being and positive adaptation. By providing evidence from an Indian emerging adult population, the study contributes to the growing literature on resilience and personality in non-Western contexts and offers a foundation for future longitudinal and cross-cultural investigations of factors that promote adaptive development and psychological flourishing.

Practical implications

The findings have implications for mental health promotion, counselling, and developmental interventions in higher education. Resilience-building initiatives in college settings may benefit from focusing on dimension-specific strengths such as assertiveness training, emotional regulation, self-acceptance, and adaptive social engagement. Given the modest and selective associations observed, interventions should avoid assuming universal or gender-specific pathways and instead prioritise individual psychosocial assessments.

Educational institutions may particularly benefit from incorporating culturally sensitive resilience enhancement programmes that strengthen coping flexibility, social connectedness, and self-regulatory capacities during emerging adulthood—a developmental stage marked by heightened transition and psychological vulnerability [24, 25].

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design restricts conclusions regarding causality or developmental directionality. Second, the use of an ultra-brief personality measure limits precision in detailed trait interpretation, which may lead to oversimplified conclusions about individual differences in personality traits. Third, the sample was regionally specific to college students in Kanpur Nagar, which may limit broader generalisability. Future studies should employ longitudinal designs, larger and more diverse samples, and fuller personality assessments to better clarify developmental and cultural pathways linking resilience and personality.

Further research should also examine whether observed gender-linked patterns remain stable across different socio-cultural settings, academic contexts, and developmental periods. Such work would contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of resilience-personality interplay across diverse populations.
