Tips and tricks for reducing radiation exposure during retrograde intrarenal surgery: A literature review by the European Association of Urology Section of Endourology
Authors: Otas Durutovic, Milos Petrovic, Vincent De Coninck, Laurian Dragos, Marcos Cepeda, Guido M Kamphuis, Panagiotis Kallidonis, Andreas Skolarikos, Amelia Pietropaolo, Bhaskar Somani, Vineet Gauhar, Thomas Tailly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFluoroscopy is an important component of retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS), as it facilitates safe and accurate access, guidance, and intraoperative navigation. Fluoroscopy during surgery exposes patients and medical personnel to the harmful effects of ionizing radiation. To reduce radiation exposure, multiple strategies have been proposed. The objective of this review is to analyze strategies for minimizing radiation exposure during RIRS and to provide recommendations for their implementation.MethodsA comprehensive literature review was performed by searching the PubMed, Cochrane Library, EMBASE, and Web of Science databases, as well as the ClinicalTrials.gov registry, for publications on strategies for reducing fluoroscopy use during RIRS, using various combinations of the following Medical Subject Headings: “fluoroscopy”, “radiation safety”, “radiation exposure”, “urology”, “endourology”, “flexible ureterorenoscopy”, “FURS”, “retrograde intrarenal surgery”, “RIRS”.ResultsA substantial corpus of evidence, encompassing original articles, clinical trials, and narrative and systematic reviews, was identified. Several preoperative measures, including thorough analysis of available imaging studies and the application of quality checklists to guarantee optimal patient positioning, C-arm settings, and the use of radiation protection equipment, have been associated with a significant reduction in radiation exposure. Intraoperatively, the use of fluoroless or fluoroscopy-free RIRS, along with equipment optimization techniques such as intermittent fluoroscopy, collimation, and last image hold, has been identified as a promising approach for radiation reduction. The implementation of radiation safety and simulation-based training can further decrease radiation exposure.ConclusionMinimizing radiation exposure during RIRS necessitates a comprehensive strategy that integrates careful preoperative planning, fluoroless or fluoroscopy-free techniques, equipment optimization, and radiation safety training and education. Current evidence suggests that these approaches can be performed safely and effectively in appropriately selected patients. However, further high-quality randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm these findings and guide their broader adoption.Keywords: Retrograde intrarenal surgery, Endourology, Radiation, Fluoroscopy, Fluoroless, Flexible ureteroscopy

Introduction

Urolithiasis is a common medical condition imposing a significant health and economic burden globally, with a prevalence of 10% in males and 7% in females [1]. Currently, the standard therapy for upper tract stone disease involves minimally invasive techniques, including extracorporeal shockwave lithotripsy, ureterorenoscopy (URS), retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS), and percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL). In recent years, there has been a significant increase in the utilization of RIRS for the management of upper tract stone disease, driven by advancements in endoscopic technology, improved laser systems, and enhanced surgical experience, while the reliance on shockwave lithotripsy has gradually diminished [2]. Moreover, due to advancements in laser technology, improved stone fragmentation efficiency, and acceptable stone-free rates and complication rates, RIRS has also been progressively employed for stones over 2 cm, establishing it as an appealing alternative to PCNL under appropriate indications [3].

Fluoroscopy has long been considered essential in RIRS, as it offers real-time imaging that ensures safe and precise access, guidance, and intraoperative navigation. During the initial stages of the procedure, fluoroscopy assists in the placement of guidewires and ureteral access sheaths (UASs) and in the confirmation of appropriate positioning within the collecting system, and is perceived as adding safety to these steps, preventing inadvertent ureteral injury. It can also provide additional information, especially in the setting of complex renal anatomy, by delineating the collecting system and detecting stone locations and residual fragments in difficult-to-reach intrarenal locations [4].

Fluoroscopy exposes medical staff and patients to harmful ionizing radiation and high-energy photons, which can disrupt chemical bonds and ionize atoms, resulting in the formation of free radicals that further cause DNA and cellular damage. Radiation-induced tissue injuries can be classified into deterministic and stochastic effects [5]. Deterministic effects emerge from the accumulated radiation exposure when a specific threshold is exceeded. These effects are dose-dependent, with severity escalating in correlation with radiation dose beyond the threshold. They are often observed following acute and substantial radiation exposure, most frequently resulting in erythema, cataracts, thyroid dysfunction, bone marrow suppression, hair loss, and acute radiation syndrome. Typically, these radiation thresholds are not exceeded during endourological procedures in standard settings, making deterministic effects rare among urological staff and patients [6]. In contrast, stochastic effects are not associated with a defined radiation dose threshold and occur in a linear manner at any level of exposure, including low doses, indicating that there is no safe lower limit for radiation exposure [7]. Stochastic effects may induce DNA mutations, which increase the risk of malignancies, particularly leukaemia, multiple myeloma, breast cancer, colon cancer, thyroid cancer, and ovarian cancer. During endourological procedures, the use of radiation also poses the potential risk of inducing stochastic effects [5].

Radiation measurements and monitoring are paramount for the safe application of fluoroscopy in endourological procedures like RIRS, facilitating the evaluation and reduction of exposure for both patients and personnel. Key parameters used in radiation metrics are fluoroscopy time (FT), dose-area product (DAP), and effective dose of radiation [8]. FT quantifies the duration, measured in seconds or minutes, during which the fluoroscopic beam is in active use. Although this is useful information, FT alone does not represent radiation intensity or distribution. In contrast, the DAP, quantified in Gy·cm2, provides a more comprehensive evaluation of radiation exposure by calculating the total radiation dose multiplied by the irradiated area, thus evaluating the total energy delivered to the patient. The effective dose, quantified in millisieverts (mSv), considers the radiation type and the sensitivity of the affected tissues, reflecting the stochastic risk associated with radiation exposure. Monitoring personnel exposure is mandatory during fluoroscopy, often conducted with personal dosimeters that quantify the cumulative dose received over time and are usually worn at the chest level (either above or beneath lead aprons) or on the extremities [8].

In light of the previously discussed radiation risks, the International Commission on Radiological Protection has advised that the annual occupational radiation dose limit for the whole body should be 20 mSv averaged over 5 consecutive years, or 50 mSv in a single year. The annual exposure limit for the extremities is 500 mSv; however, for the eyes, due to higher sensitivity, the suggested annual dose limit is 20 mSv [9]. For comparison, the mean radiation exposure during a CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis ranges from 9 mSv to 19 mSv, depending on the protocol employed [10]. In an attempt to limit and optimize the radiation exposure in clinical practice, the concept of “As Low As Reasonably Achievable” (ALARA) was introduced as early as the 1960s. The ALARA principle has been widely applied by endourologists during the entire process of patient care, from diagnosis to intraoperative management and follow-up, and is predicated on minimizing FT, employing protective shields, and maintaining maximum possible distance from the radiation source [5]. By adhering to these radiation safety principles, endourologists can stay within the recommended exposure limits for the chest, extremities, and the ocular lens [11].

A recent review on urologist radiation exposure during endourological procedures found great variability in the average exposure levels across studies. Prone PCNL was identified as the procedure with the highest radiation exposure, especially to the eyes and hands. Fortunately, using a thyroid shield and lead apron drastically reduced exposure by 94.1%–100%. The review also noted that educational courses on radiation safety helped lower exposure and raise awareness among endourologists [12]. Regarding patient radiation exposure, a review from 2024 found that radiation exposure was also the highest during PCNL, followed by shockwave lithotripsy and URS. The study found an inverse relationship between radiation dose and both surgeon experience and caseload. Patient exposure was minimized through strategies such as optimizing fluoroscopy settings, utilizing ultrasound, and following the ALARA principle [13].

The objective of this narrative review is to identify and evaluate the literature on current strategies for minimizing fluoroscopy usage during RIRS and prospective developments in this evolving field.

Materials and methods

A comprehensive literature review was conducted by searching the PubMed, Cochrane Library, EMBASE, and Web of Science databases, as well as the ClinicalTrials.gov registry, for publications on protocols and strategies for reducing fluoroscopy use during RIRS. The search encompassed studies published between January 2000 and July 2025. Further publications were discovered through the examination of reference lists from preliminarily identified articles. The search protocol encompassed different combinations of the following Medical Subject Headings: “fluoroscopy”, “radiation safety”, “radiation exposure”, “urology”, “endourology”, “flexible ureterorenoscopy”, “FURS”, “retrograde intrarenal surgery”, “RIRS”. Publications in non-English languages, case reports, editorials, letters, conference papers, and publications without fully available texts were omitted from subsequent analysis. The literature search was not confined to a particular study design and included both comparative and non-comparative studies, as well as prospective and retrospective studies. The identification, screening, and inclusion of studies in our review were performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [14] and presented in the PRISMA flow chart (Fig. 1). The quality and bias risk of the included randomized controlled trials were assessed using the Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool [15]. The assessment encompassed the following domains: bias arising from the randomization process, bias due to deviations from the intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in measurement of the outcome, bias in selection of the reported results, and overall risk of bias. The overall risk of bias for each study was categorized as low, some concerns, or high. The findings of the RoB 2 evaluation are displayed in Supplementary Table 1. The evaluation of non-randomized studies' quality was performed utilizing the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale [16]. Each study was evaluated in three domains: selection (up to 4 points), comparability (up to 2 points), and outcome (up to 3 points). Studies scoring 7–9 points were regarded as high quality; those scoring 5–6 points were regarded as moderate quality; and those scoring 0–4 points were regarded as low quality. The comprehensive findings of this evaluation are shown in Supplementary Table 2. Complications of RIRS across the studies were reclassified according to the Clavien–Dindo grading system as follows: Grade 1—fever requiring antipyretics, transient postoperative hematuria, mild flank pain resolving after oral analgesics, or minor mucosal abrasion not requiring intervention; Grade 2—febrile urinary tract infection requiring antibiotics or severe hematuria requiring transfusion or renal colic; Grade 3A—ureteral stent insertion for obstruction performed under local anesthesia or ureteral stent insertion for ureteral injury or percutaneous nephrostomy insertion for obstruction; Grade 3B—repeat ureteroscopy due to ureteral obstruction or migrated fragments, or surgical repair of ureteral injury requiring anesthesia; Grade 4—urosepsis; and Grade 5—death. Upon identification of relevant studies, a narrative synthesis was utilized.

Tips and tricks for reducing fluoroscopy exposure in RIRS

Preoperative planning

Radiation exposure during RIRS can vary significantly according to several factors, including stone burden and location, stone density, surgical experience, operative time, and ureteral stent placement [17,18]. To mitigate radiation exposure and prevent the detrimental consequences of radiation, several preoperative and intraoperative techniques have been developed and implemented over time. Such techniques must be optimized to comply with the ALARA principle, without compromising the effectiveness of the operation or raising complication rates.

Preoperatively, several measures may be used to decrease radiation exposure. First, a comprehensive examination of preoperative imaging, together with its immediate accessibility to the surgeon during the procedure, may accelerate stone identification and reduce fluoroscopy utilization in that segment of the operation [19]. Furthermore, the implementation of preoperative quality checklists facilitates the appropriate setup of the operating room [20]. Checklists ensure proper patient positioning, which is crucial for preventing interference artifacts in the X-ray field. In addition, checklists are beneficial for the correct implementation of radiation protection measures for all personnel (protective glasses, leaded trunk and neck aprons, and leaded gloves). An essential component for efficient preoperative planning in any low radiation exposure protocol is also the assurance of proper C-arm positioning and settings. The X-ray tube must be positioned at the lowest feasible height, with laser guidance employed to ensure ideal image centering. Additionally, the fluoroscopy dose must be adjusted according to the patient's weight [11]. In a prospective comparative study published in 2013, Kokorowski et al. [21] evaluated radiation exposure during ureteroscopy in two groups—one utilizing a pre-fluoroscopy checklist and the other managing without it. The study's results suggested that the implementation of a pre-fluoroscopy checklist led to a 67% reduction in FT and an 88% decrease in DAP. Based on published DAP-to-effective dose conversion coefficients [22], an approximate reduction in effective dose was observed after the implementation of the preoperative checklist, from 2.05 mSv to 0.25 mSv. For reference, the average effective radiation doses during RIRS have been reported to be approximately 4.4 mSv [23]. A retrospective comparative study conducted in 2011 found that the implementation of a pre-fluoroscopy checklist, which included a laser-guided C-arm operated by a dedicated radiological technician, readily accessible preoperative imaging, and ureteroscopic verification of stone and stent positions in the bladder, resulted in an 82% reduction in FT, without an increase in complication rates [19]. Although the outcomes of both studies were very encouraging, the limited patient sample size hinders the extrapolation of the results to larger patient populations, necessitating future investigations with larger cohorts. Nevertheless, it is apparent that the application of these straightforward, technically uncomplicated pre-fluoroscopy quality checklists holds substantial promise for reducing radiation exposure, without extending operation time or affecting patient outcomes. Intraoperative measures

Fluoroless RIRS

The maximum possible reduction of fluoroscopy or the implementation of entirely fluoroscopy-free procedures is a prominent strategy for minimizing radiation exposure during RIRS. Several fluoroless techniques have been developed over the years, with the primary goal of reducing FT, relying on visual and tactile cues and, sometimes, full endoscopic guidance. In a retrospective, non-comparative study conducted in 2017, Çimen and colleagues [24] assessed a full fluoroscopy-free technique in 93 patients undergoing RIRS with a UAS. The mean operative time was 72 min, with a stone-free rate (SFR) of 69.9% and clinically insignificant residual fragments seen in 13.9% of patients. The complication rate was minimal, at 5.4% [24]. Several prospective, non-comparative studies have also evaluated the feasibility and outcomes of fluoroless RIRS. Kirac et al. [25] described a fluoroless approach in which nearly all manipulations were conducted via visual and tactile cues, with single-shot fluoroscopy employed only to verify the location of the UAS. Supplementary fluoroscopic imaging was conducted in only 5% of patients, attributed to intricate renal architecture or difficult stone localization. The attained SFR was 82.9%, accompanied by a notable decrease in FT, which was 5.27 (standard deviation 1.8) s [25]. A similar SFR was reported in Aboutaleb's study [26], which also found that 95% of UAS placements were successful without fluoroscopy, and all double-J stent insertions were performed fluoroscopy-free. The 2017 multicenter study indicated that the continuous implementation of an ultralow radiation exposure strategy during RIRS (excluding complex patients) led to a substantial reduction in FT and DAP over time, while attaining a high SFR of 98.3%. Over a 7-year period, the mean FT decreased from 167.7 s in the first year to 7.4 s in the final year of the study. A similar reduction was also detected for DAP, from 318.4 cGy·cm2 to 6.4 cGy·cm2. Based on published DAP-to-effective dose conversion coefficients [22], a substantial reduction in effective dose was observed, from 0.795 mSv in 2009 to 0.016 mSv in 2015. The ultralow radiation exposure protocol employed in this study involved omitting retrograde ureteral studies, inserting guidewires under direct visualization without fluoroscopic guidance, inserting a UAS without fluoroscopy, and avoiding fluoroscopy when calyces were easily accessible via endoscopy [27]. In a large cohort study from 2019, comprising 576 patients, fluoroless RIRS was successfully performed in 96.7% of cases, with an SFR of 83.3% after the first session [28].

A variation of the zero-fluoroscopy protocol was also established, referred to as the "sheathless and fluoroless technique" [29,30]. This procedure involves initial ureteral exploration with a semirigid ureteroscope, after which a guidewire is retained in place to facilitate the passage of a flexible ureteroscope, followed by laser lithotripsy. Firdolaş et al. [29] observed a mean operation time of 56 min, an SFR of 78%, a clinically insignificant residual fragment rate of 17%, and a complication rate of 9% after using this approach in 598 RIRS cases. On the other hand, Mahmood and colleagues [30] observed a higher SFR (90.4%) alongside a complication rate of 17.4%, utilizing the identical technique.

Several studies have compared fluoroless RIRS with conventional RIRS. In a 2018 comparative, non-randomized study, 385 patients were assigned to the fluoroscopy group (n=284) and the fluoroscopy-free group (n=101) [31]. The mean FT in the fluoroscopy group was 52.61 (standard deviation 46.00) s. The mean operative time was statistically significantly shorter in the fluoroscopy group (49.85 min vs. 56.48 min, p<0.001). On the other hand, the SFR was significantly higher in the fluoroscopy-free group (98.02% vs. 80.85%, p<0.001), accompanied by a reduced complication rate (9% vs. 23%, p<0.001). The rate of postoperative double-J stenting was lower in the fluoroless group (61.39% vs. 79.85%, p<0.001). No significant difference in mean stone size was observed between the groups. Nonetheless, the study is constrained by its non-randomized methodology, selection bias, and several additional important limitations. First, patients in the fluoroscopy group had a significantly higher rate of lower calyx stones, compared with the fluoroless group (27.8% vs. 19.8%). In the fluoroless group, stones were significantly more prevalent in the renal pelvis (41.6% vs. 25%). Moreover, the rate of preoperative double-J stenting was significantly higher in the fluoroless group (21.8% vs. 8.1%, p<0.001), while collecting system anomalies were significantly more frequent in the fluoroscopy group (5.6% vs. 3.9%, p=0.004) [31]. Due to the previously indicated methodological limitations and confounding factors, the results of this study must be evaluated cautiously and cannot be considered indicative of the superiority of fluoroless RIRS. In three additional comparative, non-randomized studies, no statistically significant differences were seen between the fluoroless and fluoroscopy groups in operative time, SFR, or complication rate [[32], [33], [34]]. The noninferiority of fluoroless RIRS to conventional RIRS regarding success and complication rates was assessed in a prospective, multicenter, randomized controlled study published in 2024 [35]. No statistically significant difference in success rates was seen between conventional RIRS and fluoroless RIRS (80% vs. 78%, p=0.8). Likewise, complication rates, notably ureteral injuries, were comparable in both groups. Furthermore, the operation time, duration of hospital stay, or retreatment rates showed no significant differences between the groups [35].

The safety and efficacy of fluoroscopy-free RIRS were further evaluated in a systematic review and meta-analysis by Subiela et al. [36]. This systematic review from 2021, with 23 studies and 4029 patients, compared conventional URS and RIRS with fluoroless URS and RIRS in the treatment of ureteral and renal calculi. No statistically significant differences were identified in overall and subgroup analyses of SFR, intraoperative and postoperative complication rates, surgical time, or retreatment rates. The conversion rate from fluoroless to the conventional method employing fluoroscopy was 5%. Reasons influencing the conversion were failure of access sheath insertion, confirmation of double-J stent position, unlocalized stone, narrow ureter, ureteral stricture, failure to pass the ureteroscope, and bleeding [36]. Despite the findings of the aforementioned studies suggesting that fluoroless RIRS possesses comparable safety and efficacy to conventional RIRS, the paucity of randomized controlled trials, the presence of selection bias, significant variability in surgical techniques, and inconsistent definitions of SFR and residual fragment assessment across the studies necessitate further investigation. A further limitation is that the majority of research has been conducted at high-volume institutions with experienced endourologists, thus constraining the generalizability of this technique. The implementation of fluoroless RIRS should be individualized and conducted after a thorough evaluation of various patient factors. The ideal candidates for this method are those with normal ureteral, renal, and calyceal anatomy, with clearly defined stone locations. Patients with impacted calculi, ureteral strictures, and renal abnormalities, such as fusion anomalies, should have conventional rather than fluoroless RIRS. Additionally, patients with preoperative double-J stenting could also be good candidates for this approach. To achieve optimal outcomes and minimize complications, several key prerequisites are essential for the safe implementation of fluoroless and fluoroscopy-free RIRS. These include adequate surgeon experience with conventional fluoroscopically guided RIRS, a thorough understanding of endoscopic anatomy, access to appropriate equipment, formal training in radiation safety and radiation-reducing techniques, and sufficient institutional support [11]. Regarding the learning curve in conventional fluoroscopically guided RIRS, Silva et al. [37] reported that the learning curve plateau was reached after approximately 60 cases, while Şahin et al. [38] observed that the learning curve plateau was reached after around 50 procedures. On the other hand, Erdal and Ozman [39] reported that among inexperienced surgeons, proficiency in fluoroscopy-free RIRS may be achieved after approximately 35–50 cases. Additionally, fluoroscopy-free RIRS can be particularly beneficial for some vulnerable and radiation-sensitive populations such as pediatric, young, or pregnant patients [40,41]. Equipment optimization

Intermittent fluoroscopy (IF)

Contemporary C-arm fluoroscopy systems use sophisticated imaging technologies that provide IF with adjustable frame rates, a method aimed at markedly decreasing radiation exposure for both the patient and the surgical team while maintaining procedural accuracy. The most commonly used setting in C-arms is continuous fluoroscopy (CF), which, at a frame rate of 30 images per second, delivers a constant X-ray beam while the activation pedal or control is engaged, providing a real-time, uninterrupted image stream. This ensures smooth visualization of moving instruments or contrast flow but results in higher cumulative radiation exposure. IF, on the other hand, activates the X-ray beam only when the operator deliberately engages it in short bursts, capturing images or brief sequences as needed, thus significantly reducing radiation exposure. IF is characterized by a user-defined rate, ranging from 15 to 1 frame per second (FPS) [42]. The utilization of IF can lead to a significant decrease in radiation dose for PCNL patients [43,44]. Durutovic et al. [43] reported a significant decrease in FT, from 155.45 s to 76.8 s, following the introduction of IF at 2 FPS, in contrast to CF at 30 FPS, while preserving an equivalent SFR in 111 PCNL patients. Similar findings were noted in Sourial et al.'s 2019 study [44], which identified a decrease in FT of 175 s following the implementation of IF at 4 FPS and low-dose settings, characterized by a 50% decrease in milliamperage. The implementation of the IF protocol was also assessed in URS patients. In a 2012 comparative study, Elkoushy et al. [45] analyzed FT in CF and IF groups across 157 URS patients as well as 100 PCNL patients. RIRS was utilized in 54 patients; semirigid URS was utilized in 39 patients; and both techniques were applied in 64 cases. Among URS patients, the FT in the IF group, which utilized 4 FPS, was statistically significantly lower than that in the CF group (44.1 s vs. 109.1 s, p<0.001). A statistically significant decrease in FT was also seen in the PCNL patient group following the application of IF. The SFRs were similar in the IF and CF groups in both URS and PCNL patients [45]. A significant reduction in FT from 77 s to 16 s after switching from CF to IF with 1 FPS was also observed by Yecies et al. [42] in 151 URS cases. Furthermore, IF led to a monthly reduction in surgeon radiation exposure of 64%. Canales and colleagues [46] found a 30% decrease in radiation exposure across several different endourological procedures after using the IF method.

While the aforementioned studies suggest that IF could reduce radiation exposure without adversely affecting procedural safety or outcomes in URS and PCNL, high-quality evidence supporting its efficacy in RIRS remains limited. Consequently, there is a need for well-designed prospective studies and randomized controlled trials to evaluate the role of IF specifically in RIRS. Despite its promising benefits, a significant proportion of endourologists remain unaware of this technique or do not routinely incorporate it into their practice. This highlights the need for targeted education and training initiatives to increase awareness, promote the adoption of IF, and further enhance radiation safety in endourology.

C-arm collimation and last image hold

In fluoroscopy, collimation restricts the X-ray beam to a specific area of the body using adjustable lead shutters or diaphragms. Collimation facilitates the reduction of the irradiated field size, hence decreasing patient and medical staff radiation exposure. Furthermore, collimation reduces scattered radiation exposure, consequently enhancing image quality. A 2017 cadaver study found that using collimation during fluoroscopy reduced radiation exposure to the surgeon's hand and thyroid gland by 65% compared with non-collimated fluoroscopy [47]. The effectiveness of collimation in reducing radiation exposure was demonstrated in neurosurgical patients during endoscopic spine surgery [48]. In the previously mentioned study by Kokorowski et al. [21], which examined the use of pre-fluoroscopy checklists in pediatric ureteroscopy, collimation was one of the main components of the radiation reduction protocol, which led to a considerable decrease in FT (by 67%) and DAP (by 88%). Similar findings were observed in a study published in 2017 by Hein and colleagues [27], in which collimation was a standard component of an ultralow radiation exposure approach during RIRS.

Another essential aspect of C-arm settings optimization is the use of the last image hold option. This feature allows the operator to examine and analyze the most recent fluoroscopic image without the need for continuous X-ray exposure, thereby eliminating unnecessary repeated fluoroscopy and minimizing radiation exposure. To further enhance the radiation reduction protocol, fluoroscopic images should be captured toward the end of the patient's respiration, since this provides superior image quality and minimizes distortion [11]. In addition, in order to enhance fluoroscopic control, the C-arm pedal should be controlled by the surgeon rather than assistants or radiological technicians [49]. Stepping away from the fluoroscopy pedal when not engaged in imaging can further mitigate unnecessary radiation exposure by reducing scattered radiation and increasing the distance from the radiation source [11].

Radiation safety training and education

With the integration of fluoroscopy and ionizing radiation into contemporary endourological procedures, the cumulative lifetime radiation exposure for both patients and medical personnel has been progressively rising. As radiation exposure increases, a comprehensive understanding of the associated risks and dose reduction strategies is essential. Consequently, the implementation of thorough radiation safety training, emphasizing ALARA principles, has become critically important. However, in clinical practice, establishing radiation protection guidelines is challenging and often insufficient, and a large proportion of endourologists do not receive formal training in radiation exposure reduction during residency or fellowship [50]. In a 2024 review by Smith et al. [51] on radiation safety and practices during urological procedures, it was observed that compliance with the use of lead aprons and thyroid shields exceeded that of lead eyewear and gloves. Moreover, the review indicated that radiation safety training and education were scarce, and knowledge about the deterministic and stochastic effects of radiation was particularly deficient. A 2013 survey of urology residents revealed a high compliance rate for body and thyroid lead shields of 99% and 73%, respectively; however, the use of eyewear and gloves was nearly non-existent, mostly due to their unavailability. Only 53% of the residents perceived themselves as well-informed regarding radiation risks and protective measures [52]. In a similar study from 2019, only 46% of the residents had radiation safety as part of their curricula [53]. Based on this information, it can be concluded that there is a significant need to enhance current radiation protection training practices and to establish standardized and formal education for all urology residents, fellows, and other medical personnel in order to promote a radiation-conscious surgical culture. Developing recommendations for mandatory radiation safety training within international urological guidelines would further enhance this effort.

In addition to formal education on radiation safety, radiation exposure reduction can also be achieved through simulation-based training (SBT). By facilitating the training and enhancement of surgical skills in secure and regulated conditions, SBT has become an appealing strategy for education in urology and other surgical disciplines. SBT in urology is a rapidly evolving field, characterized by the development of simulators employing various modalities, including box-trainers, virtual reality platforms, and cadavers. Presently, simulators are accessible for robotic and laparoscopic surgery, transurethral resection of the prostate, URS, PCNL, and RIRS [54]. Technological advancements have enabled the creation of several training simulators for urolithiasis, effectively replicating patient anatomy and surgical scenarios for both URS and RIRS [55]. The standardization of training and educational methodologies, alongside the development of a curriculum featuring a urology bootcamp for URS and RIRS, as well as modular courses up to advanced levels, such as Advanced Robotic Training in Flexible Ureteroscopy (ART in Flex) or URO Berlin Skills Teaching and Training (UroBEST), offered by the European School of Urology, a division of the European Association of Urology, can further enhance patient and staff safety [54]. Studies have suggested that SBT in fluoroscopy-based procedures, used in interventional radiology and cardiology, can be associated with a significant decrease in radiation exposure [56,57]. A prospective investigation including 40 radiology residents and eight radiologists showed that SBT significantly reduced pedal usage, radiation exposure, and procedure duration [56]. Comparable beneficial outcomes of SBT might also be anticipated in RIRS. Nonetheless, it is essential to recognize the weaknesses of SBT, including the limitations of methods for evaluating acquired skills, as well as the unavailability and high cost of models. Future directions

Additional research is needed to identify the specific factors that influence radiation exposure, understand the impact on patient health, and establish standardized, procedure-specific reference levels for radiation doses. The existing evidence is limited by significant heterogeneity among studies and radiation-reduction protocols, along with the absence of standardized reporting for FT and DAP. Therefore, future research should emphasize the standardization of protocols and reporting of radiation metrics.

Improved adoption of ALARA principles can be achieved through the implementation of novel, emerging technologies such as real-time dosimetry. Passive dosimetry has considerable limitations owing to its restricted capacity to detect low-dose radiation, whereas radiation overexposure may only be determined retrospectively. Conversely, active, real-time dosimetry, which utilizes electronic data collection, allows rapid and continuous monitoring of radiation exposure during surgery, providing immediate feedback to the surgeon. This electronic dosimeter, worn by medical personnel, provides immediate, real-time radiation readings, facilitating on-site adjustments during procedures to minimize additional radiation exposure [58].

Artificial intelligence (AI) has been extensively researched across several urological fields, possibly providing significant applications in diagnosis, treatment planning, and intraoperative surgical guidance. In the treatment of urolithiasis, AI might enhance stone identification, predict treatment outcomes, optimize operative procedures, and improve stone composition analysis [59]. The ability of AI to enhance efficiency and decrease complication rates and radiation exposure is also applicable to RIRS [60]. During fluoroscopy, endourologists often focus on a restricted region of interest that is surrounded by a broader field of view, which is essential for orientation. As a result, tissues throughout this broader field of view are also subjected to substantial radiation doses, along with the regions of interest. With the application of AI algorithms and technology, AI-controlled collimators can focus radiation on the regions of interest, thus reducing radiation exposure to surrounding tissues and organs [61]. Moreover, AI-driven techniques utilizing deep learning and machine learning possess the capability to enhance the quality of X-ray images by diminishing noise, increasing contrast, and maintaining crucial anatomical information, while using a low radiation dose [62]. The remarkable capacity of AI to analyze extensive datasets might also enhance and refine existing radiation safety protocols. However, the current evidence is limited, necessitating validation in prospective clinical settings. Although current literature on the application of AI in RIRS remains limited, AI holds remarkable transformative potential and, given its rapidly evolving nature, is likely to play a significant role in shaping future clinical practice.

Conclusion

Reduction of radiation exposure during RIRS requires a multifaceted approach that combines preoperative planning, intraoperative measures, and continuous personnel training and education. Meticulous preoperative planning with a quality checklist can be an effective and feasible approach for reducing radiation exposure. Furthermore, growing evidence suggests the safety and efficacy of fluoroless and fluoroscopy-free RIRS, with equipment optimization, in appropriately selected patients. Finally, urological education and training regarding the risks of radiation exposure and the importance of adhering to ALARA principles should be considered a standard component of contemporary RIRS practice. However, further randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm these findings and guide broader adoption.
