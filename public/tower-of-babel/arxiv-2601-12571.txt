1.
Introduction

A
self-avoiding walk
(SAW) on a graph
G
is a path that visits no vertex more than once.
SAWs were introduced in the chemical theory of polymerization (see Flory
[
5
]
) and have
since been central objects in probability, combinatorics, and statistical physics; see, for
example, Madras and Slade
[
13
]
. For an infinite quasi-transitive graph
G
, the number

\sigma_{n}
of
n
-step SAWs grows exponentially, and the associated exponential growth rate

\mu=\mu(G)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sigma_{n}^{1/n}

is the
connective constant
of
G
. Exact values of connective constants are known only
in a small number of nontrivial cases.

This paper studies how SAWs and their asymptotic parameters behave under
local
transformations
of cubic graphs. Informally, a local transformation replaces every
degree-
3
vertex by a fixed finite
gadget
with three distinguished outer vertices
(ports) attached to the three incident edges, subject to a symmetry condition ensuring that
the three ports are indistinguishable from the point of view of the gadget (Definition 3.1).
A canonical example is the
Fisher transformation
, which replaces each vertex by a
triangle; Fisher-type transformations play an important role in exact mappings between
two-dimensional lattice models (e.g. Ising/dimer/vertex models) and have also been used in
the SAW literature.

Connective constants under local transformations.

Given a local transformation
\phi
, we associate to the gadget a two-port generating
function
g(x)
(Definition 3.2), defined by
counting SAWs that enter the gadget through one port-edge and exit through another, while
otherwise staying inside the gadget.
Our first main theorem (Theorem 3.3) shows that this local data determines the connective
constant of the transformed graph:
if
G
is infinite, connected, quasi-transitive and cubic, and
G_{1}=\phi(G)
is obtained by
applying
\phi
at every vertex, then

(1.1)

\mu(G)^{-1}=g\bigl(\mu(G_{1})^{-1}\bigr).

Equivalently,
\mu(G_{1})^{-1}
is the unique positive solution of
g(x)=\mu(G)^{-1}
.
This functional relation may be viewed as a substitution identity for SAW partition
functions, and it extends the familiar relation arising from the Fisher transformation.

Critical exponents.

Beyond exponential growth, SAWs are expected to exhibit universal power-law corrections,
captured by critical exponents.
Rather than assuming an ambient Euclidean dimension, we work with graph-theoretic
definitions of the exponents
\gamma,\eta,\nu
via the finiteness of natural SAW generating
series (Section 3).
Our second main theorem (Theorem 3.4) proves that local transformations preserve these
exponents under mild hypotheses:
\gamma
and
\eta
are invariant, and
\nu
is invariant
assuming the SAW counts satisfy a standard slowly varying correction (3.7) with a common
slowly varying function.

Transforming one colour class of a bipartite graph.

Our third main result concerns bipartite graphs. Let
G
be an infinite, connected,
quasi-transitive bipartite graph with colour classes (black/white), and suppose at least one
class is cubic. Let
G_{\mathrm{e}}
be obtained by applying a local transformation to all
vertices in one class (or, in the fully cubic case, applying possibly different local
transformations to the two classes).
Theorem 3.5 shows that the connective constants satisfy

(1.2)

\mu(G)^{-2}=h\bigl(\mu(G_{\mathrm{e}})^{-1}\bigr),

with
h(x)=xg(x)
if only one class is transformed, and

h(x)=g_{\phi_{1}}(x)\,g_{\phi_{2}}(x)
if both classes are transformed.
This yields new exact connective constants when combined with known exact values.

The special case in which each cubic vertex is replaced by a triangle is the

Fisher transformation
. The effect of the Fisher transformation on
self-avoiding walks was analyzed by Grimmett and Li
[
6
]
, who derived an explicit functional
relation between the connective constants of
G
and its Fisher transform and
proved invariance of the principal critical exponents under iteration.

The present paper extends
[
6
]
from Fisher triangles to
general
local transformations
in which each degree-
3
vertex is replaced
by an arbitrary finite three-port gadget satisfying a natural symmetry condition
(Definition
3.1
). Under this general framework we obtain corresponding
functional relations for connective constants (Theorems 3.3 and 3.5) and prove
invariance of the critical exponents under verifiable hypotheses (Theorem 3.4),
including the case where the transformation is applied to only one colour class
of a bipartite graph (Theorem 3.5).

3.
Local transformation

Definition 3.1

(Local transformation)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a simple graph, and let
v\in V
be a vertex of degree
3

A

\phi
acts at

by replacing

that satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

The gadget
\phi(v)
comes with three distinguished
outer vertices

each of which is attached to exactly one of the three edges incident to
v

(one outer vertex per incident edge).

(2)

For any degree-
3

\phi(v_{1})
and
\phi(v_{2})

are isomorphic (via an isomorphism that sends outer vertices to outer vertices).

(3)

Let

be the three outer vertices of
\phi(v)
. There exists a subgroup of the automorphism group
of the gadget that

•

acting transitively on
\{w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}\}
; equivalently, for any

w_{i},w_{j}\in\{w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}\}
there exists an automorphism of
\phi(v)
mapping
w_{i}
to
w_{j}
; and

•

preserving the set of distinguished outer vertices
\{w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}\}

The resulting graph is denoted by
\phi(G)
.

One special case of local transformations defined above is the so-called Fisher transformation; in which each vertex is replaced by a triangle.
This transformation has been valuable in the study of
the relations between Ising, dimer, and general vertex models
(see
[

4

11

on the Archimedean lattice denoted
(3,12^{2})
(see

10
]

In the remainder of this paper, we make use of the more general local transformation in the context of
SAWs and the connective constant. It will be applied to cubic graphs, of which the
hexagonal and square/octagon lattices are examples.

It is convenient to work with graphs with well-defined connective constants, and
to this end we assume

Obviously the automorphism group of

c_{n}
be the number of
n
-step SAWs on
G_{\mathrm{e}}
starting at mid-edges in
X

and ending at mid-edges in
E
(both viewed inside
G_{\mathrm{e}}
).
Clearly,

(5.1)

c_{n}\leq s_{n}\qquad(n\geq 0).

Step 1:
s_{n}
and
c_{n}
have the same exponential growth rate.

Let

\displaystyle N:=\max\{|V(\phi_{1}(v))|,\ |V(\phi_{2}(v))|\},

\displaystyle D:=\max\{\deg(u):u\text{ lies in a gadget of }\phi_{1}\text{ or }\phi_{2}\}.

Every
n
-step SAW counted by
s_{n}
falls into one of two classes:

•

it is entirely contained in a single gadget (a finite graph);

•

otherwise, it visits at least one mid-edge belonging to
E
.

In the first case
n\leq N
.
In the second case, trim each endpoint that is not a mid-edge of
E
along the walk
to the first encountered mid-edge of
E
. This removes at most
N
visited vertices
per endpoint, hence at most
2N
in total, producing a shorter SAW counted by

c_{n-i}
for some
i\in\{0,1,\dots,2N\}
.

Conversely, given a walk counted by
c_{n-i}
, the number of ways to extend its
endpoints by
i
additional vertices (inside gadgets) is bounded by
(i+1)D^{i}
.
Therefore there exists a constant
C_{0}<\infty
(depending only on the gadgets and
W
)
such that, with the convention
c_{m}=0
for
m<0
,

(5.2)

s_{n}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{2N}(i+1)D^{i}\,c_{n-i}\qquad(n\geq N+1).

Combining (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) yields

\limsup_{n\to\infty}s_{n}^{1/n}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}c_{n}^{1/n}.

Since
G_{\mathrm{e}}
is quasi-transitive, the connective constant
\mu_{\mathrm{e}}
exists and
equals this common growth rate. In particular, the power series

C(x):=\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}x^{n}

has radius of convergence
\mu_{\mathrm{e}}^{-1}
.

Step 2: even SAWs on
G
and the bivariate partition function.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be the set of SAWs on
G
starting at mid-edges in
X
,
and let
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{even}}\subset\mathcal{E}
be those of even length.
For
\pi\in\mathcal{E}
, write
|\pi_{\mathrm{black}}|
(resp.
|\pi_{\mathrm{white}}|
) for the number
of black (resp. white) vertices visited by
\pi
; then
|\pi|=|\pi_{\mathrm{black}}|+|\pi_{\mathrm{white}}|
.

For
x,y>0
, define

Z(x,y):=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{E}}x^{|\pi_{\mathrm{black}}|}y^{|\pi_{\mathrm{white}}|},\qquad Z_{\mathrm{even}}(x,y):=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{even}}}x^{|\pi_{\mathrm{black}}|}y^{|\pi_{\mathrm{white}}|}.

Clearly
Z_{\mathrm{even}}(x,y)\leq Z(x,y)
.
Let
\Delta:=\sup_{v\in V}\deg(v)<\infty
(here
\Delta=3
).
Every odd-length SAW in
\mathcal{E}
is obtained from an even-length SAW by appending
one extra step at one endpoint; the number of choices is bounded by
\Delta
, and the
one-step SAWs contribute a finite constant depending on
|X|
.
Consequently, for all
x,y>0
,

(5.3)

Z(x,y)-Z_{\mathrm{even}}(x,y)\ \leq\ (\Delta x+\Delta y)\,\bigl(|X|+Z_{\mathrm{even}}(x,y)\bigr),

and hence

(5.4)

Z(x,y)<\infty\iff Z_{\mathrm{even}}(x,y)<\infty.

Since
G
is bipartite, any even-length SAW alternates colors and satisfies

|\pi_{\mathrm{black}}|=|\pi_{\mathrm{white}}|=|\pi|/2
, and therefore

Z_{\mathrm{even}}(x,y)=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{even}}}(xy)^{|\pi|/2}=\sum_{m\geq 0}\sigma_{2m}\,(xy)^{m},

where
\sigma_{n}
is the number of
n
-step SAWs in
\mathcal{E}
.
Thus the radius of convergence of
Z_{\mathrm{even}}(x,y)
as a function of
t:=xy

equals
\mu^{-2}
, where
\mu=\mu(G)
is the connective constant of
G
.
In particular,

(5.5)

Z(x,y)<\infty\iff xy<\mu^{-2}.

Step 3: substitution identity and the connective-constant equation.

Let

g_{1}(x):=g_{\phi_{1}}(x),\qquad g_{2}(x):=g_{\phi_{2}}(x),

where each
g_{i}
is defined as in (
3.1
) (Definition 3.2) for the corresponding gadget.
Consider SAWs counted by
c_{n}
, i.e. SAWs in
G_{\mathrm{e}}
that start at a mid-edge of
X

and end at a mid-edge of
E
.
Contracting each gadget of
G_{\mathrm{e}}
to its original vertex yields a map from such SAWs
to SAWs
\pi\in\mathcal{E}
on
G
.
Conversely, given
\pi\in\mathcal{E}
, each time
\pi
visits a black vertex it uses two
incident edges, and the portion of the lifted walk inside the corresponding black gadget
is any SAW connecting the two corresponding incident mid-edges; the total contribution of
all choices is exactly
g_{1}(x)
.
Similarly each visited white vertex contributes a factor
g_{2}(x)
.
Since
\pi
is self-avoiding, these gadget-choices occur in disjoint gadgets and hence multiply.
Therefore we obtain the exact identity

(5.6)

C(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}x^{n}\;=\;\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{E}}g_{1}(x)^{|\pi_{\mathrm{black}}|}\,g_{2}(x)^{|\pi_{\mathrm{white}}|}\;=\;Z\bigl(g_{1}(x),g_{2}(x)\bigr).

By (
5.5
), we have

C(x)<\infty\iff g_{1}(x)\,g_{2}(x)<\mu^{-2}.

Hence the radius of convergence of
C(x)
is the unique positive solution
\rho
of

g_{1}(\rho)\,g_{2}(\rho)=\mu^{-2}.

Since the radius of
C(x)
is
\mu_{\mathrm{e}}^{-1}
by Step 1, this yields

\mu^{-2}=g_{\phi_{1}}(\mu_{\mathrm{e}}^{-1})\,g_{\phi_{2}}(\mu_{\mathrm{e}}^{-1}),

which is Theorem 3.5(2)(a).

For part (1)(a), take
\phi_{2}
to be the trivial transformation; in the mid-edge convention,
traversing an untransformed vertex contributes one visited vertex, hence
g_{\phi_{2}}(x)=x
,
and the identity above becomes
\mu^{-2}=x\,g_{\phi_{1}}(x)
with
x=\mu_{\mathrm{e}}^{-1}
.

Step 4: invariance of critical exponents (parts (1)(b) and (2)(b)).

Under the corresponding assumptions of Theorem 3.4, one repeats the arguments of
Section 4 with the contraction/expansion map used in (

The gadget lengths are uniformly bounded above and below, so the polynomial weights in

This implies that the exponents
\gamma,\eta

\nu

3.7

Example 6.3

(Compositions of local transformations)
.

Let
G
be a cubic graph. Suppose
\phi_{1}
is a local transformation such that

G_{1}:=\phi_{1}(G)
is again cubic, and let
\phi_{2}
be a local transformation on
G_{1}
with

G_{2}:=\phi_{2}(G_{1})
.
Write
g_{i}(x):=g_{\phi_{i}}(x)
and
\mu_{i}:=\mu(G_{i})
.
By Theorem 3.3,

\mu_{0}^{-1}=g_{1}(\mu_{1}^{-1}),\qquad\mu_{1}^{-1}=g_{2}(\mu_{2}^{-1}),

hence

\mu_{0}^{-1}=(g_{1}\circ g_{2})(\mu_{2}^{-1}).

Equivalently,
\mu_{2}^{-1}\in(0,1)
is the unique solution of
(g_{1}\circ g_{2})(x)=\mu_{0}^{-1}
.

More generally, for a sequence
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{r}
such that
G_{i}:=\phi_{i}(G_{i-1})
is cubic for

1\leq i\leq r
, one has

\mu_{0}^{-1}=(g_{1}\circ\cdots\circ g_{r})(\mu_{r}^{-1}),\qquad g_{\phi_{r}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{1}}=g_{1}\circ\cdots\circ g_{r}.

Remark 6.4

(Iterating a fixed transformation)
.

Assume
\phi
is a local transformation such that
G_{n}:=\phi^{n}(G)
is cubic for all
n\geq 0
.
Let
x_{n}:=\mu(G_{n})^{-1}
and
g(x):=g_{\phi}(x)
.
Then Theorem
3.3
gives

x_{n-1}=g(x_{n})\qquad(n\geq 1),

so
x_{n}=g^{-1}(x_{n-1})
.

Since
g
is a polynomial with nonnegative coefficients and no constant/linear term
(in particular
g(0)=g^{\prime}(0)=0
), it is strictly increasing on
(0,1]
and hence invertible
on its image.

Assume in addition that
g
is strictly convex on
(0,1)
and that
g(1)\geq 1
.
Then
h(x):=g(x)-x
is strictly convex with
h(0)=0
,
h^{\prime}(0)=-1
, and
h(1)\geq 0
,
so there exists a unique
x_{\ast}\in(0,1]
such that
g(x_{\ast})=x_{\ast}
.
Moreover,
(x_{n})
is monotone (increasing if
x_{0}<x_{\ast}
and decreasing if
x_{0}>x_{\ast}
)
and converges to
x_{\ast}
.
